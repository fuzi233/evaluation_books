The morning broke with a crisp clarity, and before breakfast was even finished, the family moved with purpose to the task at hand: trapping the birds. The mixture of rubber oil and turpentine, known as bird-lime, proved to be an effective adhesive. The boys took charge, smearing long rods with the sticky goo and propping them high in the canopy where the acorns grew thick and the flocks gathered.

The sheer volume of pigeons that descended upon the trees reminded Father of last year, though the circumstances were vastly different. Last season, they had witnessed only the final, straggling remnants of the migratory wave. This year, however, the branches bowed under the weight of massive, continuous swarms. Not only were the pigeons abundant, but the sweet acorns of the evergreens had also become a magnet, drawing vast flocks that roosted heavily on the limbs throughout the night. Observing this nightly congregation, Father decided to launch a nocturnal raid, employing torches—a tactic borrowed from the Virginian colonists—to flush them out.

Meanwhile, the stationary snares performed admirably. Birds alighting on the branches found themselves instantly ensnared by the tenacious glue. The more they flapped and squirmed, the thicker the coating became, eventually weighing them down to the earth amidst pitiful cries. Once captured, the birds were removed, fresh lime applied, and the traps reset. Soon, the boys mastered the technique independently, allowing Father to focus on preparing his torches from pine wood and turpentine for the upcoming night operation.

Just as evening approached, Jack trotted over with a particularly striking pigeon, distinct from the common brown varieties. Reluctant to harm it, he presented it to Father. Recognizing it as a European breed—one of the few they had brought with them—Father gently freed the trembling bird, cleaning the caked gum from its feet and wings using oil and ash. He placed it safely within a wicker cage, instructing Jack to capture any others of the same lineage. These were precious additions; having fled freely upon their initial landing, the original stock had become wild and untamable. By contrast, these new captures offered immediate utility, promising future meals of pigeon pie and roasted cottages whenever desired.

That evening, equipped with long bamboo poles, blazing torches, and sturdy canvas sacks, the party marched toward the grove of sweet acorns. To the children, their makeshift weaponry seemed absurdly primitive, yet the darkness fell swiftly, revealing the true scale of their quarry. As Father ignited the torches, the sudden glare shattered the birds' slumber. Confusion erupted among the terrified fowl; they fluttered blindly through the dense branches, dazzled and disoriented. Before the beating could truly commence, many had already fallen victim to the chaos and were scooped into the waiting sacks. The effort was immense; Mother and Franz strained to gather the deluge of birds that spilled onto the ground, filling the sacks with incredible speed. Upon returning to Falconhurst, they secured their bounty and retired to rest, eager for the culinary feast awaiting them.

The following day was consumed entirely by the labor of plucking, boiling, roasting, and stewing the pigeons. By the next morning, plans were already underway for a major expedition to "Woodlands," aimed at countering the persistent monkey invasions. Father hoped to corner the mischievous creatures in the act of destruction and administer a punishment severe enough to banish them from the neighborhood permanently. Anticipating an absence of several days, Mother packed provisions for the journey, staying behind with Franz and the dog Turk. Father assembled a formidable arsenal: super-strength birdlime, short wooden posts, coils of string, coconut shells, and gourds. Led by Father atop the donkey, accompanied by the buffalo and the boys, they ventured deep into the forest near Woodlands. There, concealed by thick undergrowth, they pitched a small tent and tethered the animals, ensuring the dogs were leashed to avoid alerting the intruders.

Upon arriving at the deserted cottage, Father wasted no time fortifying the perimeter. Driven stakes formed an irregular fence around the house, interlaced with heavy strings to create a labyrinthine barrier. Every surface was liberally coated with the potent birdlime, and basins filled with the mixture were scattered alongside piles of rice, maize, and other dainties intended as bait. Night fell without incident as they retired to their tent, confident in their trap.

Before dawn, a cacophony signaled the approach of the apes. Armed with heavy clubs, the family crept silently through the bushes, positioning themselves to observe the unfolding drama. The scene was chaotic and deafening: a riot of rustling leaves, cracking twigs, and horrid shrieks echoed through the woods. Out poured the rabble of monkeys, scrambling and leaping from the trees, racing across the grass toward the cottage. Attracted by the novelties of the setup, they dashed through the palings, some rushing for the food, others scaling the roof to pry at the wooden pegs.

Gradually, the tragedy of the situation became apparent. One by one, the apes became besmeared with birdlime on heads, paws, backs, and breasts. Their predicament worsened with every passing moment. Some sat frozen, attempting futile gestures to wipe away the sticky filth. Others were hopelessly tangled in the stakes and cords, trailing debris like bewildered victims. Groups tried to help one another, only to stick together in knots of mutual entrapment. Many dragged gourds and coconut shells, their paws caught as they reached for the bait. The most comical figure was an elderly ape who had discovered a calabash of palm wine; having drunk it, a shell adhered to his forehead and whiskers, masking his eyes and causing him to stumble wildly in a grotesque dance of confusion.

Panic spread as numbers attempted to flee, only to find themselves fixed to the surrounding trees by additional lime. The scene devolved into a ghastly battlefield. Father released the three dogs, whose pent-up aggression exploded into a frenzy of justice. The air filled with a din of howling, yelling, and barking, a symphony of rage and pain that slowly faded into an awful silence. Father and the boys surveyed the shocking aftermath: at least forty apes lay mangled and dead. The mood shifted from shock to somber reflection, but Father quickly rallied them, directing their energy toward the practical tasks of burying the dead, burning the evidence, and restoring order to the dismantled cottage.

While searching for the scattered livestock, they noticed something heavy falling from the trees. Investigating, they discovered three magnificent birds caught on loose, limed sticks. Two were identified as varieties of the Blue Molucca pigeon, while the third, with its resplendent green, bronze, and steely-blue plumage, was presumed to be a Nicobar pigeon. Father was delighted by the prospect of domesticating these exotic birds to establish them as the first tenants of a new dwelling near the cave.

"First tenants, Father!" Fritz exclaimed, sensing the gravity of the moment. "Do you expect to catch more like these?"

"Not exactly catch them," Father replied, a mysterious glint in his eye. "I intend to practice a secret art. Magic can accomplish wonders, Fritz!"

Days later, with Woodlands restored, the family returned to Falconhurst, where plans were immediately set for constructing a pigeon-house in the cliff face at Tentholm. Father envisioned a hollowed-out space facing the Jackal River, fitted with partitions, perches, and a large wooden front featuring sliding shutters and a broad platform. Weeks of labor followed, transforming the cavernous space into a functional aviary.

Once the construction was complete, Father summoned Fritz to stage the arrival of the new colonists. He instructed his son to mix soft clay, aniseed, and salt to create a "magic food" that would attract wild pigeons. Furthermore, he ordered an infusion of aniseed oil to be applied to the pigeonholes, creating a scent trail that would lure the wild birds to share the meal with the captives.

"All preparations complete," Father announced as the pigeons were installed and the slides closed. The European birds, now quite tame, mingled happily with the three beautiful strangers. When the other boys climbed the ladder to peer through the glass pane, they witnessed a tableau of contentment: birds picking up grain and pecking at the fragrant mixture.

On the third morning, Father awakened Fritz to perform the final ritual. A cord was rigged to the sliding door, allowing it to be opened from below. Fresh aniseed oil was poured around the entrance. The family gathered outside, bracing for the ceremony. Father waved a wand with mock solemnity, muttering a feigned incantation, and signaled Fritz to slide the panel open.

Pretty heads popped out, soft eyes scanning the surroundings. The birds ventured forth tentatively onto the "verandah," chirping softly. Suddenly, startled, they took wing with a shrill whizz, circling upward before darting out of sight. For a moment, hope faltered, but soon they returned, settling quietly on the dove-cot, confirming their acceptance of the new home. Yet, as the family congratulated themselves, the three blue pigeons—the noble foreigners—rose abruptly. Ignoring the familiar confines, they soared high and winged their way directly toward Falconhurst.

The departure seemed decisive, raising fears that they had been lost forever. Despair lingered as the family petted the four remaining birds, distracted by the emptiness of the coop. Then, mid-morning, Jack burst into the cavern, breathless with excitement.
