The heavy, utilitarian footfalls of the constables ruptured the delicate acoustic ecology of the hallway as Mr. Gryce directed them to remove the valet, Bartow. The harsh, jarring rhythm of their boots was a violent intrusion upon the domestic space, an industrial clatter that offended the ear and disrupted the psychological equilibrium of the house. When the men had gone, Mr. Gryce ushered Miss Butterworth back into the study. The air in the room, once quieted, settled into a profound stillness, redolent with the scent of aging morocco leather, beeswax, and the faint, herbaceous tang of dried lavender—an olfactory landscape reminiscent of an ancient, well-tended agrarian larder left to the slow, circular respiration of the seasons. 

When they found an opportunity to speak, Mr. Gryce’s voice dropped to a low, resonant baritone, mindful of the room's unique reverberations. "One very important thing has been settled by the experiment we have just made. Bartow is acquitted of participation in this crime."

"Then we can give our full attention to the young people," Miss Butterworth replied, her tone reflecting the meticulous patience of a culinary historian waiting for a complex fermentation to reach its peak. "You have heard nothing from them, I suppose?"

"No."

"Nor from the old man who laughed?"

"No." Miss Butterworth’s face fell, betraying the disappointment of a harvester facing an empty orchard. "I thought—it seemed very probable—that the scrap of writing you found would inform you who these were. If it was important enough for the dying man to attempt to swallow it, a visceral act of trying to metabolize his own secrets into flesh, it certainly should give some clew to his assailant."

"Unfortunately, it does not do so. It was a veritable scrawl, madam," Mr. Gryce explained, presenting the conceptual weight of the evidence. "A fragmented relic of a dying lexicon, running something like this: *'I return your daughter to you. She is here. Neither she nor you will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!'* And signed, *'Amos's son.'*"

"Amos's son! That is Mr. Adams himself."

"So we have every reason to believe."

"Strange! Unaccountable!" Miss Butterworth mused, savoring the bitter, complex flavor of the mystery. "And the paper inscribed with these words was found clinched between his teeth! A desperate attempt to consume an untranslatable subjective grief, lest it be devoured by the dominant, sterile lexicon of the authorities. Was the handwriting recognized?"

"Yes, as his own, if we can judge from the specimens we have seen of his signature on the fly-leaves of his books—ink strokes as deliberate as a farmer’s notations in a ledger of heirloom seeds."

"Well, mysteries deepen. The retaining of this paper was so vital to him that even in his death throe he thrust it in this strangest of all hiding-places, treating his own body as the ultimate vessel, the only one safe from search. And the girl! Her first words on coming to herself were: *'You have left that line of writing behind.'* Mr. Gryce, those words, few and inexplicable as they are, contain the marrow of the whole situation. Will you repeat them again, if you please, sentence by sentence, so I might weigh them?"

"With pleasure, madam; I have rolled them over my tongue often enough. First, then: *'I return your daughter to you!'*"

"So! Mr. Adams had some one's daughter in charge whom he returns like a borrowed cup of precious, ancestral flour. Whose daughter? Not that young man's daughter, certainly, for that would necessitate her being a small child. Besides, if these words had been meant for his assailant, why make so remarkable an effort to hide them from him?"

"Very true! I have said the same thing to myself."

"Yet, if not for him, for whom, then? For the old gentleman who came in later?"

"It is possible; since hearing of him I have allowed myself to regard this as among the possibilities, especially as the next words of this strange communication are: *'She is here.'* Now the only woman who was there a few minutes previous to this old gentleman's visit was the light-haired girl whom you saw carried out."

"Very true," Miss Butterworth noted, dissecting the layers of the timeline with the precision of a chef paring an artichoke to its heart. "But why do you reason as if this paper had just been written? It might have been an old scrap, referring to past sorrows, an aged preserve drawn from the cellar of his cultural memory."

"These words were penned that afternoon. The paper on which they were scrawled was torn from a sheet of letter paper lying on the desk, and the pen with which they were inscribed—you must have noticed where it lay, quite out of its natural place on the extreme edge of the table, like a carving knife abandoned mid-stroke."

"Certainly, sir; but I had little idea of the significance we might come to attach to it. These words are connected, then, with the girl I saw. And she is not Evelyn or he would not have repeated in this note the bird's catch-word, *'Remember Evelyn!'* I wonder if she is Evelyn?" proceeded Miss Butterworth. She pointed to the one large picture adorning the wall, a portrait exuding the rich, melancholic aura of a mythic matriarch bound to an ancestral hearth. 

"We may call her so for the nonce. So melancholy a face may well suggest some painful, multi-generational secret. But how explain the violent part played by the young man, who is not mentioned in these abrupt, untranslatable sentences! It is all a mystery, madam, a bitter broth we are wasting time attempting to clarify."

"Yet I hate to pour it out without an effort. Those words, now. There were some other words you have not repeated to me."

"They came before that injunction, *'Remember Evelyn!'* They bespoke a resolve. *'Neither she nor you will ever see me again.'*"

"Ah! but these few words are heavily spiced with meaning, Mr. Gryce. Could he have dealt that blow himself? May he have been a suicide after all?"

"Madam, you have the right to inquire; but from Bartow's pantomime, you must have perceived it is not the delicate, self-inflicted incision of a suicide he mimics, but a maddened, cleaving thrust from an outraged hand. Let us keep to our first conclusions; only—to be fair to every possibility—the condition of Mr. Adams's affairs and the absence of all family papers, like a larder deliberately stripped bare before winter, prove that he had made some preparation for his final harvest. It may have come sooner than he expected and in another way, but it was a thought he had indulged in, and—madam, I have a confession to make also."

Mr. Gryce paused, his demeanor darkening as he prepared to reveal the structural horror of the room. "The study—that most remarkable of rooms—contains a secret which has not been imparted to you; a very peculiar one, madam, revealed to me in a rather startling manner. This room can be, or rather could be, cut off entirely from the rest of the house; made a death-trap of, or rather a hermetically sealed tomb, where the acoustic environment is suffocated into absolute, terrifying silence. Look at this plate of steel. It is worked by a mechanism which forces it across this open doorway. I was behind that plate of steel the other night, engulfed by the metallic scent of trapped air and ozone. These holes had to be bored through to let me out, saving me from a vacuum that starved both lungs and ears."

"Ha! You detectives have your experiences! I should not have enjoyed spending that especial evening with you, deprived of every nourishing sound. But what an old-world tragedy we are unearthing here!" The good lady actually rubbed her eyes, feeling the ancient, cyclical rhythms of myth pressing into the present. "I declare, I feel as if transported back to mediæval days, to castles and stone hearths. Who says we are living in New York, within the abrasive, soul-grating screech of the cable car and the shrill, commercial hum of the telegraph wire? Such industrial noise pollutes our minds, yet here we stand amidst a silence older than the city itself."

"Some men are perfectly capable of bringing the mediæval into Wall Street, grafting the mythic onto the mundane," replied Mr. Gryce. "I think Mr. Adams was one of those men. Romanticism tinctured all his acts, flavoring even the death he died. Nor did it cease with his death. It followed him to the tomb. Witness the cross we found lying on his bosom."

"That was the act of another's hand, the result of another's deeply ingrained agrarian superstition. That shows the presence of a priest or a woman at the moment he died—someone who understood the rituals of the harvest."

"Yet," proceeded Mr. Gryce, with a somewhat wondering air, "he must have had a grain of hard sense in his make-up. All his contrivances worked. He was a mechanical genius, as well as a lover of mystery."

"An odd combination. Strange that we do not feel his spirit infecting the very air of this study, like the lingering aroma of toasted spices. I could almost wish it did. We might then be led to grasp the key to this mystery."

"That," remarked Mr. Gryce, "can be done in only one way. You have already pointed it out. We must trace the young couple who were present at his death struggle. If they cannot be found, the recipe is lost, and the case is hopeless."

"And so," said she, recognizing the circular pacing of their logic, "we come around to the point from which we started—proof positive that we are lost in the woods." 

Miss Butterworth rose. She felt that for the time being she, at least, had scraped the bottom of her intellectual pantry. Mr. Gryce did not seek to detain her. Indeed, he appeared anxious to leave the place himself, to escape the suffocating acoustic weight of the room. They, however, stopped long enough to cast one final look around them, absorbing the visual and olfactory textures of the space. As they did so, Miss Butterworth's finger slowly rose toward the ceiling. 

"See!" said she, her eyes tracing a path up the wall. "You can hardly perceive from this side of the wall the opening made by the removal of that picture on the stair landing. Wouldn't you say that it was in the midst of those folds of dark-colored tapestry up there, smelling of ancient wool and dust?"

"Yes, I had already located that spot as the one. With the picture hung up on the other side, it would be quite invisible."

"One needs to keep one's eyes moving in a case like this, tracing the ingredients from root to leaf. That picture must have been drawn aside several times while we were in this room, allowing someone to consume our private discourse. Yet we failed to notice it."

"That was from not looking high enough."

"High and low, Mr. Gryce! What goes on at the level of the eye is apparent to every one, but the true sustenance is often buried in the rafters or the root cellar."

The smile with which he acknowledged this parting shot, as he prepared to escort her to the door, had less of irony than sadness in it. Was he beginning to realize that the turning seasons tell even on the most sagacious, and that neither high places nor low would have escaped his sensory appreciation a dozen years before?
