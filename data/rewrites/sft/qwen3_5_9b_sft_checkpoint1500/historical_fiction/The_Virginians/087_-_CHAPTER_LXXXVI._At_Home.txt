A mother's plea, gentle and deep, might have melted a heart far harder than ours, yet we lingered. We spoke of a swift journey to Virginia, of securing the cabin aboard *Young Rachel*, but destiny held us back twice. First, our youngest child fell ill, rendering the ocean voyage perilous for her fragile frame; naturally, Mother could not leave her side, so *Young Rachel* sailed alone that year. The following season brought another obstacle: my beloved worship's first assault of gout, a malady that consumed me with pain yet, paradoxically, sharpened my wit and buoyed my spirit afterward. Finally, a sorrow far deeper intervened—the grim tidings from Jamaica.

Two years after settling at the Manor, our dear General returned from his governorship. He had amassed greater worldly wealth than when he departed, yet he bore a loss that riches could not heal, a grief so profound he saw no future remaining in the West Indies. His wife—my Theo's mother, the tenderest and kindest friend I have ever known save one—had succumbed to fever abroad. In her final moments, her deepest regret was that she would not live to witness our children and ourselves thriving in prosperity.

"She sees us, though we do not see her," my husband whispered, "and she thanks you, George, for having been good to her children."

Indeed, we feared he would not long join her. His entire existence had revolved around her love and his service to her; to exist apart from her felt like non-existence. Watching him sit with us, broken by this calamity, was heartbreaking. My wife, through her demeanor and countless gentle gestures, constantly summoned the memory of his lost beloved to soothe the widower's soul. We offered whatever cheer we could muster, yet little Hetty became, beneath heaven's eye, his primary anchor. She had declined numerous advantageous matches proposed within the island, and upon returning to England, Lord Wrotham's heir bowed low before her. Yet Hetty insisted she loved staying with her father best; as long as he needed her, she desired no husband.

"Nay," we urged when the final proposal for her hand was made, "let the General remain six months with us at the Manor, and you may have him at Oakhurst for the other six."

But Hetty declared her father could never endure Oakhurst again now that her mother was gone. She would wed no man for a coronet or gold—not she! The General, grave and thoughtful, noted that her lack of desire for marriage stemmed, perhaps, from an unspoken disappointment in her youth, a wound she never revealed. Respecting her silence, we remained mute. Meanwhile, my brother Lambert had secured a college living near Winchester and a wife to adorn his parsonage. While we professed a moderate affection for this lady, welcoming her warmly, her perspective on Hetty's determined celibacy differed sharply from mine. Mrs. Jack was a chatty woman, prone to speaking her mind with excessive liberty and taking pride in her ability to inflict pain upon her friends.

"My dear Sir George," she declared with a bright smile, "I have often told our dear Theo that I would not have a pretty sister in my house to make tea for Jack while I am upstairs, nor to hover in the kitchen or nursery, nor to appear neatly dressed whenever I am baking pies or attending to the children. I trust Jack implicitly, of course, but I should like to see him look at another woman! And so I do with Jemima, though they do not convene in my house when I am upstairs—I promise you! Thus, I warned my sister Warrington."

"Am I to understand," the General interjected, "that you have done my Lady Warrington the favor of warning her against her sister, my daughter Miss Hester?"

"Yes, pa, of course!" cried Mrs. Jack, flushed and voluble. "A duty is a duty, and a woman is a woman, and a man is a man, as I know very well. Do not tell me! He is a man. Every man is a man, with all his sanctified airs!"

"You yourself have a married sister, with whom you were staying when my son Jack first had the honor of making your acquaintance?" remarked the General.

"Yes, of course! Everyone knows that, and I have been as good as a mother to her children!"

"And shall I gather from your conversation that your charms proved a powerful temptation for your sister's husband?"

"Law, General! I know not how you can suggest I ever said such a thing!" Mrs. Jack cried, turning red.

"Do you not perceive, my dear madam, that it is you who have insinuated as much, not only regarding yourself, but concerning my own two daughters?"

"Never, never, never, as I am a Christian woman!" she shrieked, indignant. "It is most cruel of you to say so, sir. And I truly say a sister is best out of the house, that I do! And as Theo's time draws near, I warn her, that is all."

"Have you discovered, my good madam, whether my poor Hetty has stolen any of the spoons? When I came to breakfast this morning, my daughter was alone, and there must have been a dozen pieces of silver on the table."

"Law, sir! Who ever spoke of spoons? Did I ever accuse the poor dear? If I did, may I drop down dead upon this hearth-rug at this very instant!" She paused, offended. "And I am not accustomed to being spoken to thus. Both Jack and I have observed it, and I have fulfilled my duty!" With that, Mrs. Jack flounced from the room in tears.

"And has the woman the impudence to tell you this, my child?" the General asked when Theo, somewhat delicate, approached the tea-table.

"She has told me every day since her arrival," Theo replied softly. "She enters my dressing-room to remind me, then retreats to my nursery, saying, 'Ah, I would not have a sister prowling about my nursery, that I would not.' Ah, how pleasant it is to have amiable and well-bred relatives," she sighed sarcastically.

"Thy poor mother has been spared this woman," the General groaned.

"Our mother would have mended her ways, papa," Theo said, kissing him.

"Yes, dear." And I observed that both were at their prayers.

Yet, let it be admitted, loving one's relatives is not always easy, and living among neighbors can prove less amusing than anticipated. From Jack Lambert's sullen demeanor the following day, it was clear his wife had imparted her version of the exchange. Jack was sulky, though not dignified; his anger did not diminish his appetite. He delivered a sermon to us that was utterly dull. Little Miles, clad once more in sable, sat beside his grandfather, his small hand clasped in the kind old man's.

Should he stay to manage the house during our Virginian excursion? The housekeeper must submit to Hetty's full dominion, and the butler's keys passed to him. For Gumbo, I did not expect with too much grace, would accompany us to Virginia; having united himself with Mrs. Molly in matrimony, he had populated a cottage in my park with several tawny Gumbos. Under the guardianship of our General and his daughter, we left our home. Traveling to London, then to Bristol, our agent—who greeted us with obsequious prayers for our prosperity and vows that children as beautiful as ours (we now had an infant to accompany Miles) were unseen on any ship before—accompanied us. The voyage passed without accident. How strange the sensation of landing at Richmond! A coach and a host of negroes awaited to receive us; nearby, a gentleman on horseback, attended by men in our livery, dismounted and rushed to embrace us. Neither of us was little charmed to behold our dearest Hal. He rode with us to our mother's door. There she stood on the steps to welcome us, and Theo knelt to ask her blessing.

Harry rode in the coach as far as Mother's house but refused to enter further, claiming it would ruin the sport. "She sees me," he admitted, "and we are fairly good friends; but Fanny and she fare best apart. There is no love lost between them, I can promise you. Come to me at the Tavern when thou art free. Tomorrow, I shall have the honor of presenting her sister to Theo." Having sent a negro boy home to fetch his wife to pay respects to Lady Warrington, Harry leapt from the carriage, leaving us to meet our mother alone.

Having spent considerable time with fine company and paid our respects to the King and Queen at St. James's, I had encountered no stater than the man who welcomed us. Raising my wife from her knee, he embraced her and led her into the house. It was a plain, wood-framed dwelling with a gallery, typical of Virginian homes; yet, if it had been a palace housing a little empress, our reception could not have been more courteous. Old Nathan, still the major-domo, presided over a score of kind black faces, grinning with welcome. Names I remembered from childhood were forgotten by the buxom lads and lasses who had grown, while some I had left with dark complexions now sported snowy locks, and others born since my departure peered from doorways with wide eyes and bare feet. "I'm little Sip, Master George!" "I'm Dinah, Sir George!" "I'm Master Miles's boy!"
