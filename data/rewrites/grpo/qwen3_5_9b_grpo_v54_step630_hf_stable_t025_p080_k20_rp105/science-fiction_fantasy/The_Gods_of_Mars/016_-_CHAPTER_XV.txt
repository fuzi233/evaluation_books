Consciousness returned not with a jolt, but with a gradual, blinding wash of light. The corridor, previously shrouded in shadow, was now bathed in the harsh glare of a torch. The eyes that had watched me from the darkness were gone, extinguished by the sudden illumination. I rose, unharmed save for a dull ache on my forehead where I had struck the stone flagging. My instinct took over immediately; I scanned the gloom for the source of the light and found four green warriors advancing rapidly down the main passage. They were unaware of my presence. I did not hesitate. Slipping into the first intersecting corridor, I crouched low against the wall, careful not to retreat as far as I had on my previous attempt, knowing full well that distance had cost me Tars Tarkas and his guards before.

The torch-bearers swept past the opening of my hiding spot. Relief washed over me as I confirmed they were the same party I had followed into the pits: Tars Tarkas and his three loyal guards. I fell in behind them, keeping a respectful distance, until we reached the cell where the great Thark had been chained. Two warriors remained outside to guard the perimeter while the man with the keys entered to re-secure the Thark's irons. Once the door was barred, the two sentries began to stroll slowly toward the spiral runway leading to the upper floors, their heavy boots echoing softly before they vanished around a bend.

The torch had been wedged into a socket beside the door, casting a wide cone of light that illuminated both the corridor and the cell. Seeing the guards disappear, I moved closer to the entrance. My heart hammered against my ribs, but my resolve was ironclad. There was no other way for Tars Tarkas and myself to return safely to our camp in the hills.

I pressed close to the wall, positioning myself just inside the threshold. I raised my longsword high, gripping it with both hands, ready to bring it down in a single, decisive stroke upon the skull of the jailer the moment he emerged. The sound of approaching footsteps grew louder. I did not dwell on the violence of the act; it was a necessity. Within a minute or two, Tars Tarkas, clad in the metal insignia of a Warhoon chief, hurried down the corridor toward the spiral runway, the Warhoon's torch held high to light his path. A dozen paces behind him followed John Carter, Prince of Helium.

The two companions of the fallen jailer had just begun to ascend the runway as the Thark came into view.

"Why so long, Tan Gama?" one of the men called out.

"I had trouble with a lock," Tars Tarkas replied, his voice strained. "And now I find that I have left my short-sword in the Thark's cell. Go you on; I'll return and fetch it."

"As you will, Tan Gama," the other responded. "We shall see you above directly."

"Yes," Tars Tarkas said, turning as if to retrace his steps to the cell. But he merely waited until the two had disappeared onto the floor above. Then, I joined him. We extinguished the torch, plunging the corridor back into darkness, and together we crept toward the spiral incline leading to the upper levels.

At the first floor, we found the hallway terminated halfway, forcing us to cross a rear room teeming with green folk before we could reach the inner courtyard. Our only option was to ascend to the second floor and traverse the long hall I had used earlier. We climbed cautiously, hearing muffled conversations drifting from the room above, but the hall remained unlit and empty as we reached the top of the runway. We threaded the long corridor and reached the balcony overlooking the courtyard unseen.

To our right lay the window into the room where Tan Gama and his warriors had departed earlier. His companions had returned, and we overheard a fragment of their dialogue.

"What can be detaining Tan Gama?" one asked.

"He certainly could not be all this time fetching his shortsword from the Thark's cell," another replied.

"His short-sword?" a woman's voice questioned. "What mean you?"

"Tan Gama left his short-sword in the Thark's cell," the first speaker explained. "He left us at the runway to retrieve it."

"Tan Gama wore no short-sword this night," the woman said. "It was broken in today's battle with the Thark, and Tan Gama gave it to me to repair. See, I have it here." She drew the weapon from beneath her sleeping silks and furs.

The warriors sprang to their feet.

"There is something amiss here!" one cried.

"'Tis even what I myself thought when Tan Gama left us at the runway," said another. "Methought then that his voice sounded strangely."

"Come! Let us hasten to the pits."

We waited no longer. I slung my harness into a long single strap and lowered Tars Tarkas to the courtyard below, dropping to his side an instant later. Since felling Tan Gama at the cell door, we had exchanged scarcely a dozen words. The torchlight had revealed the utter bewilderment on the great Thark's face.

"By this time," he had muttered, "I should have learned to wonder at nothing which John Carter accomplishes." That was all. He needed no further explanation of the friendship that drove me to risk my life, nor did he need to express his gratitude. This fierce green warrior, the first to greet me twenty years ago with leveled spear and hatred in his heart as I stood beside the incubator of his horde on the dead sea bottom beyond Korad, was now, among the inhabitants of two worlds, the finest friend I possessed. Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of the Tharks.

Reaching the courtyard, we stood in the shadows beneath the balcony to formulate our next move.

"There be five now in the party, Tars Tarkas," I said. "Thuvia, Xodar, Carthoris, and ourselves. We shall need five thoats to bear us."

"Carthoris!" he cried. "Your son?"

"Yes. I found him in the prison of Shador, on the Sea of Omean, in the land of the First Born."

"I know not any of these places, John Carter. Be they upon Barsoom?"

"Upon and below, my friend; but wait until we have made good our escape, and you shall hear the strangest narrative that ever a Barsoomian of the outer world gave ear to. Now we must steal our thoats and be well away to the north before these fellows discover how we have tricked them."

In safety, we reached the great gates at the far end of the courtyard, through which we had to lead our mounts to the avenue beyond. Handling five of these great, fierce beasts was no easy feat; by nature, they were as wild and ferocious as their masters, held in subjection only by cruelty and brute force. As we approached, they sniffed our unfamiliar scents and circled us with squeals of rage, their long, massive necks uprearing, great gaping mouths high above our heads. Fearsome as they appeared at rest, they were fully as dangerous when aroused. The thoat stands nearly ten feet at the shoulder, its hide sleek and hairless, dark slate on the back and sides, shading down its eight legs to a vivid yellow at the huge, padded, nailless feet, while the belly is pure white. A broad, flat tail, larger at the tip than the root, completes the picture of this ferocious green Martian mount—a fit war steed for these warlike people.

Guided by telepathy alone, requiring no reins or bridles, our objective was to find two that would obey our unspoken commands. As they charged about us, squealing in agitation, we managed to master them sufficiently to prevent a concerted attack, though the din was certain to draw investigating warriors if it continued. Eventually, I reached the side of one great brute, and before he knew what I was doing, I was firmly seated astride his glossy back. A moment later, Tars Tarkas had caught and mounted another, and between us, we herded three or four more toward the great gates.

Tars Tarkas rode ahead, leaning down to throw open the barriers, while I held the loose thoats from breaking back to the herd. Together, we rode through into the avenue with our stolen mounts, hurrying off toward the southern boundary of the city without waiting to close the gates.

Our escape had been little short of marvelous, and our good fortune held. We passed the outer purlieus of the dead city and reached our camp without hearing even the faintest sound of pursuit. Here, a low whistle—the prearranged signal—apprised the rest of our party that I had returned. We were met by the three with every manifestation of enthusiastic rejoicing.

Little time was wasted in narration. Tars Tarkas and Carthoris exchanged the dignified, formal greetings common upon Barsoom, yet I sensed intuitively that the Thark loved my boy and that Carthoris reciprocated his affection. Xodar and the green Jeddak were formally introduced. Then Thuvia was lifted to the least fractious thoat, Xodar and Carthoris mounted two others, and we set out at a rapid pace toward the east. At the far extremity of the city, we circled north, and under the glorious rays of the two moons, we sped noiselessly across the dead sea bottom, away from the Warhoons and the First Born, though to what new dangers awaited us, we knew not.

Toward noon of the following day, we halted to rest our mounts and ourselves. We hobbled the beasts so they might move slowly, cropping the ochre moss-like vegetation that served as both food and drink. Thuvia volunteered to remain on watch while the rest of the party slept for an hour.

It seemed I had barely closed my eyes when I felt her hand upon my shoulder and heard her soft voice warning me of a new peril.

"Arise, O Prince," she whispered. "There be that behind us which has the appearance of a great body of pursuers."

The girl stood pointing toward the direction from whence we had come. As I arose and looked, I too detected a thin dark line on the far horizon. I awoke the others. Tars Tarkas, whose giant stature towered above the rest, could see the farthest.

"It is a great body of mounted men," he said, "and they are traveling at high speed."

There was no time to lose. We sprang to our hobbled thoats, freed them, and mounted. Turning our faces once more toward the north, we took flight again at the highest speed our slowest beast could manage. For the balance of the day and all the following night, we raced across the ochre wilderness with the pursuers at our back, ever gaining upon us. Slowly but surely, the distance lessened. Just before dark, they were close enough for us to plainly distinguish that they were green Martians, and throughout the long night, we distinctly heard the clanking of their accoutrements behind us.

As the sun rose on the second day of our flight, it disclosed the pursuing horde not a half-mile in our rear. As they saw us, a fiendish shout of triumph rose from their ranks.

Several miles ahead lay a range of hills—the farther shore of the dead sea we had been crossing. If we could reach these hills, our chances of escape would be greatly enhanced, but Thuvia's mount, although carrying the lightest burden, already showed signs of exhaustion. I rode beside her when suddenly her animal staggered and lurched against mine. I saw he was going down, but ere he fell, I snatched the girl from his back and swung her to a place upon my own thoat, behind me, where she clung with her arms about me.

This double burden soon proved too much for my already overtaxed beast, and our speed was terribly diminished, for the others would proceed no faster than the slowest of us could go. In that little party, there was not one who would desert another; yet we were of different countries, different colors, different races, different religions—and one of us was of a different world.

We were quite close to the hills, but the Warhoons were gaining so rapidly that we had given up all hope of reaching them in time. Thuvia and I were in the rear, for our beast was lagging more and more. Suddenly, I felt the girl's warm lips press a kiss upon my shoulder.

"For thy sake, O my Prince," she murmured. Then her arms slipped from about my waist, and she was gone.

I turned and saw that she had deliberately slipped to the ground in the very path of the cruel demons who pursued us, thinking that by lightening the burden of my mount, it might thus be enabled to bear me to the safety of the hills. Poor child! She should have known John Carter better than that.

Turning my thoat, I urged him after her, hoping to reach her side and bear her on again in our hopeless flight. Carthoris must have glanced behind him at about the same time and taken in the situation, for by the time I had reached Thuvia's side, he was there also. Springing from his mount, he threw her upon its back, turned the animal's head toward the hills, and gave the beast a sharp crack across the rump with the flat of his sword. Then he attempted to do the same with mine.

The brave boy's act of chivalrous self-sacrifice filled me with pride, nor did I care that it had wrested from us our last frail chance for escape. The Warhoons were now close upon us. Tars Tarkas and Xodar had discovered our absence and were charging rapidly to our support. Everything pointed toward a splendid ending of my second journey to Barsoom. I hated to go out without having seen my divine Princess, and I held her in my arms once again; but if it were not writ upon the book of Fate that such was to be, then would I take the most that was coming to me.
