Within the Duomo.

As Baldassarre forced his way behind the heavy curtain, his wrists bound tight and the rough hemp rope constricting his neck and torso, he halted abruptly. The interior of the cathedral did not match the hollow, dead expectation he had carried in his mind. He had imagined a cavern of silence, lined with lifeless idols and unlit candles, where only a few distant figures might murmur a monotone chant. Instead, he found himself confronted by a sea of living, breathing humanity.

The crowd was a tapestry of Florence itself: magistrates in fine wool, ladies of noble bearing, and the rough-hewn faces of artisans and peasants alike. They sat in breathless stillness, their gazes fixed upward toward the pulpit nestled between the nave and the choir. There, a Dominican friar with dark hair and stern features stood holding a crucifix, his voice cutting through the air.

For the first few moments, Baldassarre remained oblivious to the sermon. Though his entry had been silent, the rope around his neck marked him instantly as a fugitive. Yet, in a sanctuary, a prisoner was safe. His gaunt, aged face, etched with the lines of wild misery, invited pity rather than fear. As he stood frozen, his eyes drifting aimlessly from the vast congregation to the stone floor beneath him, the initial suspicion faded. The crowd, captivated by the preacher's rising power, turned their attention back to the pulpit, leaving Baldassarre to drift into the periphery of their collective awe.

Among those who had glanced at him was Romola. She had entered late through a side door, her position granting her a clear view of the main entrance. Her gaze lingered on Baldassarre with a profound intensity. The grey hairs framing his temples and the visible weight of suffering on his face struck a chord deep within her, awakening a lifelong sensitivity to the sorrows of the aged. She seemed certain that their eyes had met in that fleeting moment, but Baldassarre had seen nothing but a blur of faces. His consciousness was fractured, a mere flash of startled awareness lost in the violent turmoil of his own emotions. The memory of Tito burned like a fever dream, blending with a desperate thirst and physical anguish. No thought of the future could take root in this soil; only a bitter realization of his own weakening strength and a vague, all-encompassing distrust shaped his mind.

Suddenly, the air seemed to crackle with a sound that resonated like the thunder of his own inner rage. A voice, dripping with triumphant certainty, pierced his marrow: "The day of vengeance is at hand!"

Baldassarre shuddered, lifting his head. Too distant to discern the preacher's features clearly, he saw only an outstretched arm and the raised crucifix. Yet, he panted for those threatening words as if they were a promise of salvation. The preacher paused, slowly lowering his arm until he rested the cross upon the pulpit's edge. He crossed his arms over his chest and scanned the multitude, challenging every eye to meet his gaze.

"All ye in Florence are my witnesses," the voice boomed, echoing off the vaulted ceiling. "I spoke not in a corner. Four years ago, before the signs of war and tribulation appeared, I proclaimed the coming scourge. I lifted my voice like a trumpet to the princes and people of Italy, declaring that your cup of iniquity is full. Behold, the thunder of the Lord gathers to shatter it. What you drink as pleasant wine shall be poured out upon you as molten lead."

He turned his gaze specifically toward the clergy. "To you, O priests, who mock the Presence in the sanctuary, who declare the Mercy-seat bare and believe you may sin behind the veil unpunished: I say, God's presence shall return as a consuming fire. Your sacred vestments shall become winding-sheets of flame. Where there should be sweet music, there shall be shrieks; where soft couches, thorns; where the breath of wantons, pestilence. Trust not in your gold or silver, nor in your high fortresses. Though built of iron and adamant, the Most High shall instill terror into your hearts, causing you to flee like women. He shall break mighty men without number and replace them with others."

"And since Scripture declares that God reveals His will to His prophets, He has chosen me, His unworthy servant, to make His purpose known through the living Word and the deeds of Providence. By the ministry of angels, He has shown me visions. His word possesses me so completely that I am but a forest branch shaken by the wind of heaven; I cannot remain silent, even if I am mocked. For four years, in defiance of scoffers, I have preached three truths delivered by the Lord: that in these times God will regenerate His Church, that before this regeneration comes a scourge over all Italy, and that these things will come quickly."

"But hypocrites, cloaking their hatred of the truth with a show of love, have told me, 'Leave your prophecies; it is enough to teach virtue.' To them I answer: 'Yes, in your hearts you say God lives afar off, His word is a parchment written by dead men, and He does not rebuke nations as He did in the days of old. But I cry again in your ears: God is near, not afar. His judgments do not change. He is the God of armies; the storm, the fire, and the pestilence are His ministers, driven by the breath of His angels to strike the chosen land that has forsaken the covenant. And thou, O Italy, art that chosen land. Has not God placed His sanctuary within thee, and hast thou polluted it? Behold, the ministers of His wrath are upon thee—they are at thy very doors!'"

Savonarola's voice had climbed to a peak of impassioned force before falling suddenly into a profound silence. His hands dropped to his sides, then clasped quietly before him. This silence was not empty; it was a spell as potent as his voice. Men and women sat motionless, faces upturned like breathing statues, until the voice returned in clear, low tones.

"Yet there is a pause—even as in the days when Jerusalem was destroyed, there was a pause that the children of God might flee. There is stillness before the storm: lo, there is blackness above, yet not a leaf quakes. The winds are stayed that the voice of God's warning may be heard. Hear it now, O Florence, chosen city in the chosen land! Repent and forsake evil; do justice; love mercy; cast out all uncleanness from among you. Then the spirit of truth and holiness shall fill your souls, breathe through your streets, and the pestilence shall not enter, nor shall the sword pass over you unhurt."

"For the sword hangs from the sky; it quivers; it is about to fall! The sword of God upon the earth, swift and sudden! Did I not tell you years ago that I beheld the vision and heard the voice? And behold, it is fulfilled! Is there not a king with his army at your gates? Does not the earth shake with the tread of horses and the wheels of swift cannon? Is there not a fierce multitude that can lay bare the land as with a sharp razor? I tell you the French king with his army is the minister of God. God shall guide him as a hand guides a sharp sickle, and the joints of the wicked shall melt before him, mown down as stubble. He that flees shall not escape, he that escapes shall not be delivered. And the tyrants who built their thrones upon the vices of the multitude, and the unbelieving priests who traffic in souls and fill the sanctuary with fornication, shall be hurled from their soft couches into burning hell. The pagans and those who sinned under the old covenant shall stand aloof and say: 'Lo, these men have brought the stench of a new wickedness into the everlasting fire.'"

"But thou, O Florence, take the offered mercy. See! The Cross is held out to you; come and be healed. Which nation in Italy has received a token like yours? The tyrant is driven out; the men who held bribes in their left hand and rods in their right have gone forth, and no blood has been spilled. Now, cast away every other abomination, and you shall be strong in the strength of the living God. Wash yourselves from the black pitch of your vices, which have made you like heathens; put away the envy and hatred that have made your city a nest of wolves. Then no harm shall happen to you. The passage of armies shall be to you as a flight of birds, rebellious Pisa shall be restored to you, and famine and pestilence shall stay far from your gates. You shall be a beacon among the nations. But mark this: while you suffer the accursed thing to lie in the camp, you shall be afflicted and tormented, even though a remnant among you may be saved."

These warnings had been spoken with incisive authority, but in the next sentence, the preacher's voice melted into a strain of desperate entreaty.

"Listen, O people, over whom my heart yearns as a mother yearns over the children she has travailed for! God is my witness that but for your sakes I would willingly live as a turtle in the depths of the forest, singing low to my Beloved, who is mine and I am his. For you I toil, for you I languish, for you my nights are spent in watching, and my soul melts away for very heaviness. O Lord, thou knowest I am willing—I am ready. Take me, stretch me on thy cross. Let the wicked who delight in blood, who rob the poor, and who defile the temple of their bodies, wag their heads and sneer at me. Let the thorns press upon my brow, and let my sweat be anguish—I desire to be made like thee in thy great love. But let me see the fruit of my travail—let this people be saved! Let me see them clothed in purity; let me hear their voices rise in concord as the voices of angels; let them see no wisdom but in thy eternal law, no beauty but in holiness. Then they shall lead the way before the nations, and the people from the four winds shall follow them, gathered into the fold of the blessed. For it is thy will, O God, that the earth shall be converted unto thy law; it is thy will that wickedness shall cease and love shall reign. Come, O blessed promise; and behold, I am willing—lay me on the altar. Let my blood flow and the fire consume me; but let my witness be remembered among men, that iniquity shall not prosper forever."

During this final appeal, Savonarola stretched out his arms and lifted his eyes to heaven. His strong voice alternated between trembling emotion and renewed energy, but the passion with which he offered himself as a victim became too overwhelming for further speech, ending in a sob. Every shifting tone vibrated through the audience, shaking them into answering emotion. Even those with moderate faith in the Friar's prophetic mission, who in cooler moments loved him little, were swept along by the great wave of feeling rising from sympathies deeper than theory. A loud, responding sob rose instantly from the vast multitude as Savonarola fell to his knees, burying his face in his mantle. In that moment, he felt the rapture and glory of martyrdom without its agony.

In that great sob of the multitude, Baldassarre's joined theirs. Perhaps no other human present vibrated more strongly to the preacher's tones than he, yet his vibration was like a harp with all strings wrenched away save one. That threat of fiery, inexorable vengeance—a future where the hated sinner might be pursued and held by an avenger in an eternal grapple—came to him like the promise of an unquenchable fountain to an unquenchable thirst. The doctrines of the sages, his old contempt for priestly superstition, had fallen away from his soul like a forgotten language. If he could have remembered them, what answer could they have given to his great need compared to the answer given by this voice of energetic conviction?

The thunder of denunciation fell upon his passion-wrought nerves with the force of self-evidence. His thought never ventured beyond it into questions; he was possessed by it as a war-horse is possessed by the clash of sounds. No word that was not a threat touched his consciousness; he had no fiber to be thrilled by anything else. But the fierce, exultant delight he felt at the idea of perpetual vengeance found its climax and relieving outburst in the preacher's words of self-sacrifice. To Baldassarre, those words brought only a vague, triumphant sense that he too was devoting himself—signing with his own blood the deed by which he gave himself over to an unending fire, which would seem but coolness to his burning hatred.

"I rescued him—I cherished him—if I might clutch his heart-strings forever! Come, O blessed promise! Let my blood flow; let the fire consume me!"

The single remaining cord vibrated to its utmost. Baldassarre clutched his own palms, driving his long nails into the flesh, and burst into a sob with the rest.
