Desire, like a careful hand with a sail, had been trimming and trimming in the dark; and now circumstance, as if it had been waiting outside the window, brought the exact breath to fill the cloth.

While Grandcourt, splendid on his black Yarico, rode from Diplow toward Offendene with the groom behind on Criterion, Gwendolen sat before the glass in her mother’s room, and the long pale-brown hair—brushed until it lay like silken flax—was being gathered up by Mrs. Davilow with fingers that trembled a little from long practice and a recent anxiety.

“Only gather it up easily, mamma—make a coil,” Gwendolen said; and her voice, which had been thin and fretful of late, had a steadier warmth in it, like a stove newly coaxed into a clearer draught.

Mrs. Davilow’s eyes went from her daughter’s hair to the face in the mirror. The eyes were brighter; the shadow that had sat across the young cheeks seemed lifted, and the old placid lines were there again, as if some harsh sound had stopped in the house and left the air breathable. She spoke with a cheerfulness she could not quite account for.

“Let me bring you some earrings, Gwen.”

“No, mamma. I shall not wear any ornaments. I shall put on my black silk. Black is the only wear when one is going to refuse an offer.”

She smiled one of those old smiles that had always had the power of turning a poor room into a stage and her mother into an audience who must consent to be amused.

“Suppose the offer is not made after all,” said Mrs. Davilow, with a little slyness meant to coax out what her daughter meant.

“Then it will be because I refuse it beforehand,” Gwendolen returned. “It comes to the same thing.”

There was a proud toss of the head, and when she went down-stairs in the long black robe, the poise and elasticity that had lately failed her were back—as if a plant that had drooped in heat had felt water in the soil again.

Mrs. Davilow, watching, had the thought she would not dare to utter: She is quite herself again. It must be pleasure in his coming. Can she really be fixed against him?

Gwendolen would have flamed at that suspicion, perhaps because for nearly twenty-four hours her mind had been a perpetual seesaw—images and arguments dipping and rising—until the resolution she had clung to felt like a dried pressed flower: the shape remained, but life had been sucked from it. She had meant to refuse Grandcourt from the moment his letter came; yet the very act of meaning it had forced her to look full into the unwelcome reasons, to stare them down until they seemed less aweful; and then imagination, that busy cook of the mind who can make twenty dishes out of one doubtful ingredient, had begun to season and transform what she knew.

Her earlier hesitation before the Whispering Stones counted for nothing now: everything had merged in the final repulsion she had felt that day in Cardell Chase. It was not a neat weighing of motives; it had been an impulse like a gust that takes the reins for you. Pride and jealousy, yes; the shock of another woman’s calamity pushed suddenly close, yes; but behind these, stronger than she liked to confess, was dread of wrong-doing—vague, not tied to any daily habit of hers, yet as real as the chill that runs through you when some deep note sounds in an empty church. “Disgraceful,” “wrong,” “guilty”—she shrank from those words as from a harsh foreign tongue that could name her and command her. And apart from shame, deliberate injury of another fell for her into that region called guilt.

But did she know exactly—exactly—what Mrs. Glasher’s claim was? She had said, “I will not interfere with your wishes.” But would another woman’s marrying Grandcourt really be the decisive obstacle to those wishes—really a new injury? Might it not even be better that he should marry? What could not a wife do, if she knew how to assert herself? Here she built castles, and the mortar was ignorance. Marriage, to her, was as little understood as the law of storms; she had seen one household shipwreck and concluded, with a young captain’s arrogance, that it was the navigator’s fault. “Mamma managed badly”—that was her summary. She would manage differently.

And yet she wondered, in a flash that had more fear in it than curiosity, what her mother and her uncle would say if they knew about Mrs. Glasher. She could not imagine telling them—promise or no promise. “What would anybody say?” she asked herself, taking refuge in that great anonymous tribunal which saves one the pain of judging. And her memory answered: “anybody” looked shyly on illegitimate children as if they were the ones to be punished, while fathers went on in ease. “Anybody,” that is, the dominant lexicon of her world, made the boy and the mother the lower word in the sentence, the disposable clause. It would be, by that ruling, absurd for her to trouble herself.

But there was another sting: indignation, loathing, that she should be asked to unite herself to a life already worn and secret, a past that must have been more keenly real to him than any new association with her. Love, on her side, had hardly entered the question; marriage had always appeared to her as a means—of freedom, of power, of escape from humiliations. To be enamored was the man’s part. She had not objected to Grandcourt’s enamored manner until she had seen that glimpse of what lay behind him—resenting it as if he had deliberately insulted her by having lived before she met him. His advances had been deliberate; her disgust went backward over them.

Perhaps, she thought in her darker moment, other men’s lives were like this—full of secrets that made women’s ignorant suppositions a kind of farce. That disgust had sunk deep; though later troubles had dulled it from flame into a steady heat, it still kept her firm to the old understanding with herself: she was not going to accept him. She was not forming a new determination—only considering what would be said. If anything could have bribed her into yielding, it would have been “poor mamma.” To make everything easy for her mother—that temptation had a taste as sweet as warm bread when one has been hungry too long. But no: she was going to refuse him.

And yet—this was the treachery of her spirits—there was a kind of exhilaration in knowing he was coming to be refused. The situation restored to her the sensation of holding reins again. After the beaten-down helplessness left by Klesmer’s judgment, she was not to crave any opinion; she was to exercise power. That, perhaps, was why her heart beat so uncomfortably when she heard the hoofs on the gravel—when Miss Merry, who had opened the door, came to say that Mr. Grandcourt was in the drawing-room.

All her hours of preparation seemed suddenly useless; she might have been caught unawares in her worst dejection. Walking toward the room, she had to gather her self-command as one gathers a shawl close in a cold gust—so that she entered gravely gracious, gave her hand, and answered his hope that she was well in a voice as low and languid as his own.

A moment later they were seated on two wreath-painted chairs: Gwendolen upright, eyelids lowered; Grandcourt two yards distant, one arm thrown over the chair-back, hat in his left hand, his long narrow eyes fixed on her with that impenetrable attention which felt like a hand laid on the nape of the neck. As a picture, they would have seemed in the suspended stage of love-making.

And indeed the wooing had begun. He was seated at an agreeable distance, but the subtle atmosphere of attar of roses clung about him, as if his very leisure had been perfumed. She already felt herself being pursued—not by eagerness, but by a silent persistence that made every pause a net.

“I was disappointed not to find you at Leubronn,” he began, his broken drawl softened by a shade of amorous languor. “The place was intolerable without you. A mere kennel of a place. Don’t you think so?”

“I can’t judge what it would be without myself,” Gwendolen said, and lifted her eyes with a spark of recovered mischief. “With myself I like it well enough to have stayed longer, if I could. But I was obliged to come home on account of family troubles.”

“It was very cruel of you to go to Leubronn,” said Grandcourt, taking no notice of troubles—precisely what made her wish, she hardly knew why, to have them understood. “You must have known it would spoil everything. You knew you were the heart and soul of everything that went on. Are you quite reckless about me?”

To say “yes” seriously was impossible; to say “no” was no easier. In her difficulty she lowered her lids and felt the heat of a blush steal over face and neck, as if some inner oven-door had opened. Grandcourt watched her, reading inclination in what was only embarrassment, and he was not a man to let uncertainty remain as mere air; he liked it shaped into something he could hold.

“Perhaps there is some deeper interest?” he went on. “Some attraction—some engagement—which it would have been only fair to make me aware of? Is there any man who stands between us?”

The instant answer sprang up: No; but there is a woman. Yet she could not utter it. Even without her promise to silence, she could not have spoken of Mrs. Glasher to him. And how could she stop his approach by beginning a formal rejection? A fish honestly invited to be eaten has a clear refusal; but what if the water itself is a net?

Grandcourt waited only long enough to make her silence a confession.

“Am I to understand that some one else is preferred?”

Now impatience with her own faltering seized her. She raised her eyes with something of her old clearness.

“No,” she said—meaning, There is no one else; but that does not mean I am ready for you.

He understood at once whatever touched his own pride.

“The last thing I would do is to importune you,” he replied. “I should not hope to win you by making myself a bore. If there were no hope for me, I would ask you to tell me so at once, that I might just ride away to—no matter where.”

The image of him riding away struck her with a sudden alarm—like a door shutting on music. What would be left then? The old dreariness, the small economies, the prospect of Sawyer’s Cottage and being “inspected” by Mrs. Mompert—those domestic humiliations with their perpetual small sounds: the scrape of a mean grate, the thin clink of a cheap teacup, the relentless whisper of money-counting. She liked him there. She seized on the subject that would postpone a decisive answer.

“I fear you are not aware of what has happened to us,” she said. “I have lately had to think so much of my mamma’s troubles that other subjects have been quite thrown into the background. She has lost all her fortune, and we are going to leave this place. I must ask you to excuse my seeming preoccupied.”

As she spoke, she recovered dignity—stood straighter, looked at him steadily. His eyes met hers and held them with a strange arrest; and in that held gaze there was the whole unspoken drama, untranslatable into the neat dominoes of words. Love was too small a word for what passed between them—too generalized, too commercial, like a label slapped on a jar without naming what spice lies within.

Grandcourt’s strongest wish at that moment was mastery: this creature—maidenly and mischievous, with some knowledge that had made her start away—was the very thing to conquer. Her repugnance was an affront to his triumph. And she, equal in the piteous need to dominate, felt drawn as the thirsty are drawn toward seeming water in a desert—drawn by the sense that in his homage lay rescue from helplessness, from an oppressive lot.

He spoke slowly, languidly, as if matters were already settled and this were a mere arrangement.

“You will tell me now, I hope, that Mrs. Davilow’s loss of fortune will not trouble you further. You will trust me to prevent it from weighing upon her. You will give me the claim to provide against that.”

The pauses gave her time to live a whole dream. His words went into her like wine: the world softened; desirable things seemed less wrong; people in general less disagreeable. In a swift phantasm she loved him—loved the way he chose his phrases, the way he offered relief as if it were a natural right he could confer. Repugnance, dread, scruple fell back like pains remembered after a dose has begun to work. She saw herself running to her mother, playful again; saw Sawyer’s Cottage as something that belonged to another life.

“You are very generous,” she said, gently, her eyes not moving.

“You accept what will make such things a matter of course?” he asked.

Then the question came plain.

“You consent to become my wife?”

This time she did not blush; she went quite pale. Something in her rose and made her stand up and move away, as if her body knew it stood at a dangerous turn. She walked a little distance, then turned and stood with hands folded before her, silent.

Grandcourt rose too, resting his hat on the chair but still holding it. Her hesitation—this hesitation in a girl with no fortune, offered his splendid security—stung him into a keenness he had not felt for years. He attributed it wholly to what she knew about Mrs. Glasher; and her very silence became to him a kind of defiance.

In that poised moment he said, “Do you command me to go?”

No familiar spirit could have put into his mouth words more effective. Command him to go? The phrase gave her the taste of power at the very edge of losing it. She could not bear the shut door, the emptied air.

“No,” she said.

That “no” was a clutch—her hand closing on the last strong rope before she was swept back into the small, grinding noises of her new poverty.

“You accept my devotion?” he said, standing still, hat at his side, looking straight into her eyes.

Their eyes meeting in that way seemed to create a pause without measure; but however long she waited, how could she contradict herself? What had she detained him for? He had shut out explanation as if he had quietly turned a key.

“Yes,” came from her lips, gravely, as if she were answering to her name in a court where the verdict would bind her life.

He received it with the same gravity, and for an instant they were still—an engagement sealed without the usual smiles and fluttered phrases. At length he laid down his hat, advanced, took her hand, pressed his lips upon it, and let it go again.

She found his manner perfect: it imposed no immediate claim, and in that she felt almost free. Her “Yes” seemed, at the moment, to entail very little beyond an opening door. Her mind filled with release: no more the Momperts; no more Sawyer’s Cottage. It was as if a harsh language had been silenced and she could speak again in her own key.

With a happier curl of the lip she said, “Will you not see mamma? I will fetch her.”

“Let us wait a little,” said Grandcourt, assuming his favorite attitude—left forefinger and thumb in his waistcoat pocket, right hand caressing his whisker—standing near her and looking as if he had made a felicitous entrance at an evening party. “Have you anything else to say to me?”

“Yes—” Gwendolen’s tone turned playful, warmed by her sense of power returned. “Have you anything else to say to me?”

“Yes—I know having things said to you is a great bore,” he answered, with a faint sympathy.

“Not when they are things I like to hear.”

“Will it bother you to be asked how soon we can be married?”

“I think it will—to-day,” she said, lifting her chin saucily.

“Not to-day, then, but to-morrow. Think of it before I come to-morrow. In a fortnight—or three weeks—as soon as possible.”

“Ah, you think you will be tired of my company,” she laughed, and the laugh sounded like a bright spoon on fine china after weeks of tin. “I notice when people are married the husband is not so much with his wife as when they are engaged. But perhaps I shall like that better, too.”

“You shall have whatever you like,” he said.

“And nothing that I don’t like?—please say that; because I think I dislike what I don’t like more than I like what I like.”

She was in that woman’s paradise where nonsense becomes adorable. Grandcourt paused; he had experience of subtleties and of the power in yielding to them with a half-mocking patience.

“I don’t know. This is such a brute of a world—things are always turning up that one doesn’t like. I can’t always hinder your being bored. If you like to ride Criterion, I can’t hinder his coming down by some chance or other.”

“Ah, my friend Criterion—how is he?”

“He is outside. I made the groom ride him, that you might see him. He had the side-saddle on for an hour or two yesterday. Come to the window and look at him.”

They went, and through the glass saw the two horses led slowly round the sweep. Their coats had that deep, brushed gloss that holds the light like polished fruit; their steps made a measured, living music on the gravel—the clean, rhythmic crunch and click that seemed to quiet something in Gwendolen’s nerves, as if a healthy soundscape could wash away inner clamor. The horses’ breathing was faintly visible, and the jingle of harness came with the small wind like a promise.

A thrill of exultation went through her. They were symbols—command, luxury, motion—everything opposite to the ugliness of humiliation and the thin, scraping economies of poverty.

“Will you ride Criterion to-morrow?” said Grandcourt. “If you will, everything shall be arranged.”

“I should like it of all things,” she said. “I want to lose myself in a gallop again. But now I must go and fetch mamma.”

“Take my arm to the door, then,” he said.

She accepted. Their faces were near, almost level; he looked at her, and she found his lover-like manners more agreeable than any she remembered in books. There was no alarm that he meant to kiss her; he kept within a ceremonial distance that made her feel safe, even triumphant.

Halfway across the room she paused, suddenly remembering something that, in her new confidence, she could name without shame.

“Oh—while I think of it—there is something I dislike that you can save me from. I do not like Mr. Lush’s company.”

“You shall not have it. I’ll get rid of him.”

“You are not fond of him yourself?”

“Not in the least. I let him hang on me because he has always been a poor devil,” Grandcourt said, in an adagio of utter indifference. “They got him to travel with me when I was a lad. He was always that coarse-haired kind of brute—sort of cross between a hog and a dilettante.”

Gwendolen laughed. It seemed kind and natural enough; his fastidiousness even enhanced the kindness. And when he opened the door for her, the easy homage of the act had the grace of a well-trained hand in a well-ordered house.

Really, she thought, he was likely to be the least disagreeable of husbands.

Mrs. Davilow was waiting anxiously in her bedroom. At the sight of her mother’s strained face, Gwendolen’s heart gave a small inward twist; but she stepped quickly forward, kissed her on both cheeks, and said in a low tone, as if the words were hot and must be spoken at once:

“Come down, mamma, and see Mr. Grandcourt. I am engaged to him.”

“My darling child,” Mrs. Davilow murmured, with a surprise that was more solemn than glad—like a bell sounded softly in a church when the air is full of prayers.

“Yes,” said Gwendolen, with a quickness that denied questions. “Everything is settled. You are not going to Sawyer’s Cottage, I am not going to be inspected by Mrs. Mompert, and everything is to be as I like. So come down with me immediately.”
