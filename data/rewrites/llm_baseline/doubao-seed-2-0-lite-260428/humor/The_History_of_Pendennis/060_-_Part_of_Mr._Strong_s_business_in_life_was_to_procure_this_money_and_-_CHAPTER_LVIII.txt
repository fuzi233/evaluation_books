Our poor widow Helen Pendennis, ably assisted by her trusty Fairoaks factotum Martha – who’d spent the last fortnight snickering at all the weird German household quirks the boys had picked up on their continent jaunt, and side-eyeing their habit of stowing half-smoked pipes in every teacup and mantel nook – laid out what she considered a veritable feast in honour of Major Pendennis’s arrival. Trouble was, only the Major, Pen, and Warrington turned up to tuck in: Helen sent word she was too peaky to make it down to dinner, and Laura stayed glued to her side for moral support. The Major held court the whole meal, regaling everyone with third-hand anecdotes about his old White’s club cronies and how he’d once won 300 quid off a duke at whist, and didn’t notice – or chose not to notice – that Pen was scowling so hard he could’ve curdled the claret, and Warrington was staring into his port like it had personally insulted his entire family tree.

It wasn’t until the sun was dipping below the hedgerows that Helen and Laura shuffled into the parlour, Helen leaning all her weight on the girl’s arm, backlit by the waning dusk so Pen couldn’t see how deathly pale and tear-streaked her face was. She tottered straight over to her son, whom she hadn’t laid eyes on all day, draped her thin, trembling arms over his shoulders, and kissed him soft as a prayer. Laura immediately stepped back to the far side of the room to give them space. Pen noticed his mother’s whole frame was shaking like a leaf, her hand cold as a gravestone when she pressed it to his forehead, fussing over him like he was seven years old again and home from school with a fever.

Somehow, that display of abject misery only made 20-something idiot Pen’s temper flare harder. He barely bothered to return the kiss, and met her pleading gaze with a face like flint. *She’s playing the martyr*, he fumed to himself, *persecuting me just so she can look like the poor long-suffering mother*.
“You look dreadful, my love,” Helen said, voice wobbly. “I hate seeing you pull that face.” She staggered over to the sofa, still clinging to his passive hand like he was a life raft.
“I’ve had a devil of a lot to annoy me lately, mother,” Pen huffed, chest heaving so hard Helen’s own heart started hammering so fast she went half faint, too scared to speak.
Everyone else in the room froze, holding their breath. It was obvious the storm was about to blow the windows in.
“I got letters from London today,” Pen went on, not noticing his mom was about to keel over. “One of ’em’s given me worse pain than I’ve ever had in my whole life. Says my old letters were intercepted and stolen from me – that a young girl who cared for me more than anyone, who nursed me through my fever, got treated cruelly by – by you, mother.”
“For God’s sake, stop,” Warrington barked, jumping up. “Can’t you see she’s ill?”
“Let him speak,” Helen whispered, so quiet they almost didn’t hear her.
“Let him speak and kill her, why don’t you?” Laura shrieked, rushing to throw an arm around Helen. “Go on then, sir. Watch her drop dead right in front of you.”
That only made Pen angrier, even as his own soft heart was screaming at him that he was being a brute. “You’re the cruel one, all of you! You pin all this mess on me, hurl your wicked accusations, your stupid doubts, hound the people who actually love me, who’d brave anything for me, just because they weren’t born with a fancy name and a fancy estate like you! You want to know what I’m going to do now I know what you’ve done? I’m going straight back to that poor girl you threw out of my house, ask her to come live with me. I don’t care about your stupid pride, your stupid petty suspicions that insult both her and me.”
“Wait a second, Pen, you can’t mean –” Helen started, eyes wide, hands outstretched, but Laura clamped a hand over her mouth to hush her. Helen didn’t fight it, she was too desperate to hear the rest of what he had to say. “Go on, Arthur, go on,” she mumbled, half-slumped against the sofa cushions like she was about to pass out.
“By Gad, I say he *won’t* go on! I won’t stand for it, by Gad!” the Major exploded, jumping to his feet, face beet red. “If you want to insult your own mother, drag the good name of Pendennis through the mud by tying yourself to some low-born kitchen maid after everything we’ve done for you? Fine! Go do it, by Gad! But I wash my hands of you, sir, wash my hands of you! I’m an old man, I haven’t got long left on this earth. I come from one of the oldest, most honourable families in all of England, by Gad! I’d hoped before I kicked the bucket that the boy I doted on, looked after, nursed through every scrap of trouble, would do something to make sure our name stayed untarnished! But if you won’t, dammy, I say amen! By G–, my father and my brother Jack were the proudest men in all the kingdom, I never thought I’d live to see this disgrace attached to my name – never – and – and I’m ashamed to say you’re Arthur Pendennis!” The old boy’s voice cracked into a sob, the second time in all Pen’s life he’d seen tears on those wrinkled old cheeks.
That sob hit Pen like a punch to the gut, his anger vanishing instantly. He stopped pacing the room like a caged tiger, and everyone went quiet for a minute, the sky outside turning properly dark. Then Warrington’s deep rumbling voice cut through the dim, and every head turned to look at him.
“Might I tell you all a little story about myself?” he said, a little unsteady. “You’ve all been so kind to me, Mrs Pendennis, and Laura – I hope you don’t mind me calling you that – Pen and I have been thick as thieves for years, and I’ve wanted to tell you this for ages, only it’s a sad old tale, and involves someone else’s secret. But it might do Pen some good to hear it, and stop all of you tearing yourselves to pieces over this terrible misunderstanding. Mind if I go on?”
“Please do,” Helen said, though she was barely listening, her head swimming with that glorious new hope that Pen wasn’t actually about to run off and ruin his life.
Warrington poured himself a full bumper of port, downed the whole thing in one go, and started talking. “You all see me for what I am, right? A man who couldn’t care less about getting ahead in the world, couldn’t give a fig for reputation, living in a garret on pocket change even though I’ve got a good name and wits enough to make something of myself if I bothered. But I don’t bother. I’ll probably die up there all alone, in that garret. I sealed my own fate when I was barely out of boyhood. You know why I took such a shine to Pen the second we met at Oxbridge? The boys from our college were gossiping about that little early adventure he had with the Chatteris actress, the one that Major Pendennis stepped in to stop before it landed him married at nineteen to a woman ten years older who had nothing in common with him. What sort of a life would that have been for him? Misery, plain and simple. When he was yelling just now about threatening to do the same thing, that was just anger talking – perfectly understandable anger, mind you, after he acted so generous and honourable through this whole mess, and all he got back was suspicion and a pile of unfair treatment of a perfectly innocent girl who’s done all of us a good turn.”
Helen was already half out of her seat, giddy with relief. “Oh, I’m not tired at all – go on, go on!”
“I liked him for that, you see,” Warrington went on, glancing over at Laura with a little half smile. “I like a man daft enough to fall head over heels for someone, against all good sense. That’s why we became friends. He’s been the best companion a lonely old wretch like me ever had. I’m not complaining, mind you – up in my garret, with the flowers you all left me, my old books, and my pipe as a permanent wife, I’m pretty content. Only every now and then I catch myself envying other blokes, the ones with good careers, or the ones who get to have the thing fate and my own stupid choices stole from me – the love of a woman, or a child.” A soft little sigh drifted across the dark room, and a hand reached out for his, only to yank back immediately. Proper Victorian ladies, you see, are trained to blush at the very hint of unscripted kindness, so modesty kicked in, that spontaneous friendly gesture got stuffed back behind propriety, and Warrington just laughed softly and kept going. “My fate is all my own fault, and it wasn’t just miserable for me, it hurt other people too. Before I even went to college, when I was eighteen, I got tangled up in an adventure of my own. No one stepped in to save me the way the Major saved Pen. I was at a private tutor’s house, and like Pen, I got it in my head I was in love with a woman far below my station, several years older than me. I don’t blame you if you’re shocked, Laura –”
“I’m not shocked at all,” Laura said, and this time she reached out and laced her fingers through his, firm and steady, because she’d already guessed his secret from little hints he’d dropped months before.
“She was a yeoman’s daughter, lived on a farm nearby,” Warrington said, voice wobbly. “Her parents knew who my father was, and buttered me up with every cheap trick and flattery under the sun, to drag me into their trap. To give her credit, she never cared a fig for me – her family threatened and bullied her into it. I was young and daft, I wanted to believe I was in love, so I fell for it. What do you think that marriage was like? I found out soon enough I was hitched to a woman who couldn’t understand a single thing I cared about, whose dullness drove me up the wall so fast I started loathe to even come home. After months of sneaking around, hiding the marriage, I found letters that proved her heart belonged to some farm lad from her own village the whole time. When my father died, I paid off all my college debts, and sank every last penny I had left into an annuity for her and her family – on the strict condition they hid themselves away and never used my last name. They’ve kept the deal so far, but they’d break it tomorrow if someone offered them twice the money. If I ever made a name for myself, she’d have turned up out of nowhere to claim a share of it. So at twenty years old, I was stuck – hopeless, ruined, no way out. I was a stupid boy got conned by a bunch of greedy peasants, and it’s only lately I’ve figured out how hard it is to actually forgive ’em. There’s your moral, Pen – be careful who you marry, outside your own class. I think I could’ve had a better life than this, but it’s what I made, so here I am, happy to sit back and watch the rest of you get your happy endings, and not be too bitter about it.”
“By Gad, sir!” the Major cackled, already back in top form, his tears long dried. “I fully intended you to marry our Laura here, you know!”
“And by Gad, you old fool,” Warrington laughed, “you owe me a thousand pounds for that very joke.”
“A thousand? It was only a five-pound pony, you rascal!” the Major said, completely confused, making everyone snort.
Helen was so giddy with relief she jumped clean off the sofa, grabbed Warrington’s hands, and kissed both of them. “God bless you – God bless you forever, Mr. Warrington!” She ran straight over to Pen, and collapsed into his arms.
“I’m innocent, mother,” Pen said, holding her tight, all the anger gone, overflowing with tenderness. “You wronged me, my dearest, dearest mom.”
“Oh yes, my child, I wronged you, thank God, I wronged you!” Helen whispered, crying happy tears. “Come with me, Arthur – not here. I need to ask my boy to forgive me – and ask God to forgive me too, and bless you, and love you, my son.”
He led her, still shaky, into her bedroom, and shut the door behind them. The other three sat quiet, smiling through their tears, watching them go. For the rest of his life, Pen remembered that night: the moon rising through the window, lighting up his mother’s pale sweet face, their long soft talk where he told her every last detail of how he’d fought his temptation to run off with Fanny, how the threat he’d yelled earlier had just been stupid anger talking. Helen said he had every right to be cross with her, that she’d been proud and cruel, that she wanted him to go see Fanny, give her a little present, write to her and tell her no hard feelings. At nine o’clock, Helen reminded him of when he was a little boy, how she’d come up to his bedroom every night at that hour to hear him say the Lord’s Prayer. Pen knelt down at her feet, and sobbed through the old familiar prayer he’d not said in years. When he got to the last line, Helen’s head fell onto his shoulder, her arms wrapped tight around him, and they whispered “for ever and ever, amen” together.
A quarter of an hour later, Pen yelled “Laura! Laura!” at the top of his lungs. She ran into the room instantly, and found Pen still on his knees, holding Helen’s limp hand. Her head was tilted back, her face as white as a sheet. “Help, Laura, help!” he shrieked. “She fainted – she’s –”
Laura screamed, and dropped to her knees beside Helen. The noise brought Warrington, the Major, and all the servants rushing into the room. The kind, gentle woman was gone. Her last feeling on earth had been pure, uncomplicated joy. No more aches, no more doubts, no more grief, no more worries for her boy. Her last heartbeat was love, her last breath a blessing.
The small, grieving group arranged her funeral quickly, and laid her to rest next to her husband in the old Clavering church, the same one she’d prayed in every Sunday for decades. Laura went to stay with Dr Portman for a little while, and he read the funeral service over her, sobbing so hard he could barely get the words out, while a tiny handful of villagers who’d known her stood around the grave. Not many people in the neighbourhood cared to remember her, after that. The cottagers she’d spent years giving food and money to muttered a few kind words, then gossiped about how she’d died of a bad heart. The Clavering housewives spent three afternoons speculating about how much property she’d left, and wondering if Arthur would rent Fairoaks out or move in, all of them betting he’d blow through the whole inheritance in two years flat. By the next market day, almost everyone had forgotten she ever existed. Only three or four people who truly loved her kept her memory alive. The biggest gap she left was in Laura’s heart, who’d loved her more than anyone else in the world. “I’m glad she got to give me her blessing before she went,” Warrington told Pen later. Pen himself could barely believe how much love his mother had held for him, and spent the rest of his life trying to live up to it.
All of Helen’s papers were perfectly organised, her little fortune passed straight to Pen without a single hassle. There was a note in her own handwriting, a prayer asking God to let her die peacefully in the arms of her son – exactly how it happened. Pen and Laura went through all her things together, making sure every little last legacy was sent where she’d wanted it to go: the fancy vase she’d promised Dr Goodenough was shipped off the next week, her favourite silver coffee pot went to Dr Portman, the diamond ring set with her hair was given to Warrington. When Laura first went back to Helen’s empty bedroom, she broke down crying, looking at the old prayer cushion, the vanity chair, the mirror that would never again reflect her sweet sad face. Pen found her a little while later, brought her downstairs, gave her a glass of sherry, and told her “Nothing in your room will ever be moved, Laura. It’s always yours. It’s my sister’s room, forever.”
They found a sealed packet of old letters in Helen’s desk, marked “Letters from Laura’s father”, which Pen handed straight over to her. For the first time, Laura fully understood how deeply Helen had loved her own father, how tenderly she’d cherished that memory all those years.
Pen remembered one last wish of his mother’s that Laura didn’t know about: the gift for Fanny Bolton. He wrote her a kind letter, sent it enclosed to old Mr Bows, asking him to read it before he gave it to Fanny. He thanked her for nursing him through his fever, told her his mother’s last wish was that she’d accept a little sum of money, the interest paid to her every year until she married. It wasn’t a fortune, but it was enough to make Fanny’s life a little easier. Her parents were thrilled, her father saying Pen was a perfect gentleman. Old grumpy Bows just growled that plastering a broken heart with a banknote was the cheapest sort of sympathy, and poor Fanny knew perfectly well that the letter was a gentle, final farewell.
