_1st Gent._ What defines a worthy woman? Sir, you need only ask the taste of every unmarried man of standing. This planet’s store of iron, cotton, wool, chemicals—every raw substance bent to our craft, takes shape to answer what the market demands. The pulse of public preference rises and falls by a rule as sublime as any natural law. Our daughters must become wives, and wives must be exactly what men choose to value; a man’s taste is the true test of a woman’s worth. You mark that phrase? Is it not fine, its meaning light and sharp, poised perfectly between its t’s and s’s?
_2nd Gent._ Nay, turn it on its head, then—what test do we use for that so-called “fine taste”? Is a perfect menu today what the ancient Roman epicures insisted a gentleman must eat to earn the right to dine well?

Brackenshaw Park stretched its gentle green hilltops far over the neighboring valley, all the way to the hazy eastern downs and the broad, sun-drenched sweep of farmland that rose like a soft embroidered curtain to the west. The castle, perched on the highest rise of the clustered hills, was hewn from rough limestone, its walls streaked with dark lichen dust and rain-worn streaks that caught the light in shifting, dappled patterns. Masses of beech and fir sheltered its north face, spilling down the slopes in thick, dark clumps like flocks wandering toward the silver gleam of the lake below.

The archery ground occupied a sun-warmed table-land at the far end of the park, shielded from the blustery south-west by tall elms and a thick wall of hollies that kept the gravel promenade and newly mown turf where the targets stood wrapped in soft, golden afternoon shade. The white Archery Hall, with its pillared arcade out front, glowed like a little classical temple against the lush green backdrop to the north. It could not have made a lovelier frame for the groups of ladies drifting across the grass, bowing and turning their necks with the slow, easy grace of lilies that had suddenly decided to walk. Even after the Wanchester military band fell silent, the air hummed with sweet sound: layered musical laughs, a harmony of light, friendly chatter that rose in peaks of gentle excitement and sank to soft, warm murmurs.

No outdoor amusement could have been freer of the noisy, jostling crowds that spoiled most modern pleasures. This archery meeting was famously exclusive: only pre-vetted friends of club members were granted entry, all to keep numbers limited enough that the dinner and ball to be held that night in the castle would be perfectly comfortable. The only non-gentry guests admitted were Lord Brackenshaw’s tenants and their families, mostly the young mothers and girls, who brought their little ones along to gape. The tenant men passed the time laying silly, low-stakes bets on their favourite archers, but the young farm girls all daydreamed over which of the gaily dressed fine ladies they would choose to be, if fairy fortune granted their wish.

Most of those country girls would likely have picked the plump, pink-cheeked, fashionably fair-haired beauty they saw fluttering her fan across the lawn—but every unmarried man in the crowd immediately and unanimously agreed no girl there outshone Gwendolen Harleth.

Small wonder she was happier that July afternoon than she had ever been in her life. To the soul that craves pre-eminence, even small triumphs taste sweet: the slave proud to be bought first at market, the barn-door rooster preening over his superior strut, each feels that quiet, warm comfort of being the clear favourite. But for perfect joy, the world outside has to match the fire inside. That perfect alignment had come for Gwendolen that day.

Could any weapon on earth be prettier for a woman to hold than a bow and arrow? The poses it inspires are full of grace and quiet, coiled power, the sharp, bright focus of marksmanship stripped of all ugly association with bloodshed. The old British masculine urge to “kill something” no longer attaches to archery; invading armies don’t fight under clouds of arrows any more, poisoned darts survive only in rhetoric or far-off, harmless jungles. Archery has no stench of gunpowder, breaks no shins, breeds no brutish athletes. Its only risk is missing your mark, and the gentle sting of that disappointment is more than enough to make a spirited girl focus all her skill on the shot.

Even the prizes at Brackenshaw were perfectly designed to be honour, not vulgar gain: gold and silver arrows, gold and silver stars, to be pinned to gowns and worn as badges of skill, then later passed on to the next champion. They had all the romance of a victor’s wreath without the inconvenience of a heavy circlet of flowers wilting in the heat of the ballroom later that night. No ridiculous, awkward misstep could possibly mar the day.

The weather cooperated perfectly, too: soft mild warmth, not a breath of wind to ruffle a lady’s hair, tug at her gown, or send an arrow veering off course. Every shot got a fair, unhindered run, and when the whole group trooped out to the targets to pull their arrows free, that slow, laughing promenade of bright, happy young people moving as one toward a shared goal was a sight to make even the most cynical spectator sigh. Gwendolen moved through that crowd like a golden Calypso among her nymphs, every turn of her wrist, every tilt of her head, drawing admiring eyes.
“That girl is like a high-mettled racehorse,” Lord Brackenshaw murmured to young Clintock, a visiting guest, as he watched her.
“First chop, and devilishly pretty on top of that,” the elegant young man replied. “I’ve never seen her look half so good.”

She never had. Her face glowed with unshadowed young pleasure, no spark of discontent marring her mood. Sure of her own success, she felt warm and kind to everyone around her, fully satisfied with the entire world. The fact that she was not the highest-ranked guest there, not a wealthy heiress like Miss Arrowpoint, only made it sweeter that she was effortlessly outshining those advantages. She would not have traded her little family group for any other at the party: her mother looked gentle and dignified enough to pass for a duchess, her uncle and aunt Gascoigne and little cousin Anna made perfectly respectable, pleasant figures, and Gwendolen was too full of bright, unshakable confidence in herself to feel the slightest twinge of jealousy even when she saw that the skilled archeress Miss Arrowpoint was keeping pace with the best of the group.

Even the surprise reappearance of the formidable Herr Klesmer, the famed musician who had once dismissed her singing as trivial, amused her. What great maestro short of Apollo himself could look natural at a countryside archery meet? Gwendolen’s eyes glinted with playful satire as she watched the Arrowpoint party make their entrance, the contrast between Klesmer’s dramatic, foreign air and the staid, uniform reserve of the surrounding English gentry sharp enough to make even a far more serious person smile. The average English gentleman of the class all around them had a easy, unobtrusive way of dressing and moving, that avoided anything that could be labelled “inspired” as a mark of bad taste. They stared at Klesmer: wild thick mane of hair flowing back from his sharp, handsome features, his tall thin frame clad in deliberately dramatic foreign cuts of clothes that looked absurd under a standard stiff English top hat, which sat perched on his head as if he had put it on for a joke. Draped in a loose Renaissance robe and wearing a Florentine beretta, he could have stood beside Leonardo da Vinci himself—but in ill-fitting trousers that drew the wrong kind of eyes, and under that absurd top hat, his fiery, curious glances around the crowd looked more ridiculous than majestic. Many guests only ever saw Klesmer in candlelit concert halls, so this unexpected daylight appearance at an exclusive country party left some guests holding back laughs, and others side-eyeing the Arrowpoints for being so bad at manners as to bring an unorthodox genius into their polite set.
“Good lord, what a ridiculous figure those artistic types always cut!” young Clintock whispered to Gwendolen. “Look at him, bowing with his hand pressed to his heart to Lady Brackenshaw—Mrs. Arrowpoint’s feathered hat only just clears his shoulder.”
“You are one of the profane,” Gwendolen teased back, her eyes dancing. “You are blind to the majesty of genius. Herr Klesmer fills me with awe. I feel crushed in his presence, all my courage oozing right out of me.”
“Ah, right, you’re a patron of his music.”
“Not at all,” she laughed lightly. “It is he who knows all about mine, and thinks it pitiable.” The man’s sharp verdict on her singing had become a private joke to her, ever since he had complimented her rare, striking physical presence instead.
“Glad to hear it. His music isn’t for my ears, either.”
“Oh, you are far too kind. But look how well Miss Arrowpoint looks today! That gold dress would make a perfect allegorical painting.”
“A little too splendid, though, don’t you think?” Gwendolen said. “Too much like the personification of Wealth in a tableau.” It sounded a touch malicious, but it was nothing more than a bubble of playful fun. She had no wish for anyone else to fade into the background—she believed her own good fortune, and her own skill, would carry her right to the top.

That belief only grew stronger as the scoring went on. Her scores were startlingly high for a first-year member, and each new success lit a brighter spark under her. She trod on air, every pleasant possibility seeming within her reach. The day was perfect enough all on its own, she did not have to wonder what would come next to keep her life at that thrilling, sharp pitch.
“Where do the scores stand, do you suppose?” Lady Brackenshaw asked her husband, from her seat as lady paramount, flanked by her two small girls and a stout little boy. “It looks to me as if Miss Harleth is on track to win the gold arrow.”
“Gad, I think she will, if she keeps this pace!” Lord Brackenshaw exclaimed. “She’s running Juliet Fenn right hard. Unheard of for a first year. Catherine Arrowpoint isn’t hitting her usual mark,” he added, turning to the heiress’s mother sitting nearby. “But she won the gold arrow last year. Luck plays a part even in these games of skill, after all. Gives the rest of us a fighting chance.”
“Catherine would be overjoyed to see someone else win,” Mrs. Arrowpoint insisted, all simpering dignity. “She is so magnanimous. It was entirely her idea that we bring Herr Klesmer today instead of Canon Stopley, who was so looking forward to coming. She would have much rather brought the Canon for her own pleasure, but she is always thinking of others. I told her bringing someone so far outside our usual set wasn’t quite proper, but she said ‘Genius itself isn’t proper. It comes into the world to make new rules.’ And one has to admit that, of course.”
“Ay, to be sure,” Lord Brackenshaw said, waving the topic away dismissively. “For my part, I’m not magnanimous at all. I hate losing. But confound it, I never get the chance these days. I’m old and idle. The young ones beat me every time. As old Nestor said, the gods don’t give us everything at once. I was a young fellow once, and now I’m an old and supposedly wise one. At least I’m old, which is a gift everyone gets if they live long enough, so no one need be jealous of me.” He smiled comfortably at his wife.
“Oh, my lord, neighbours who have known each other twenty years don’t talk about age,” Mrs. Arrowpoint protested. “As the Tuscans say, years exist only to let houses be leased. But where is our new neighbour? I thought Mr. Grandcourt was supposed to be here today.”
“Ah, right, he was supposed to come,” Lord Brackenshaw said, glancing at his watch. “He only got to Diplow Manor the other day. He came to dine with us on Tuesday, said he’d been tied up with some tedious business. Might have been dragged off somewhere else. Why, Gascoigne!” he called out, spotting the rector walking across the lawn with Gwendolen on his arm. “You’re a downright traitor. You beat all us old hands at archery yourself, and then you train up your niece to beat every single archeress here.”
“It is quite scandalous of her to outdo all the senior members,” Mr. Gascoigne said, his short upper lip twitching with suppressed pride. “But it’s not my doing. I only wanted her to make a tolerable appearance, not surpass anyone.”
“It’s not my fault either,” Gwendolen put in, with pretty, teasing archness. “If I’m going to aim at a target, I can’t very well help hitting it.”
“Ay, ay, that can be a fatal sort of business for some people,” Lord Brackenshaw laughed good-naturedly. He glanced back at his watch, then addressed Mrs. Arrowpoint again. “You’re right, the time’s getting on. But Grandcourt is always late. I noticed in town he was never on time, and he’s no archer—doesn’t know a thing about the sport. But I told him he simply had to come. He’d get to see the very flower of the county gathered here. He asked about you, by the way. He saw your calling card. I don’t think you met him in town. He’s spent years travelling abroad, most people don’t know him well.”
“No, we’ve never been introduced,” Mrs. Arrowpoint said. “That’s strange, too, since he’s the nephew of Sir Hugo Mallinger, and he and I are such great old friends when we meet.”
“Uncles and nephews don’t haunt each other’s company the way uncles and nieces do, usually,” Lord Brackenshaw smiled, nodding at the rector. “Come with me a minute, will you, Gascoigne? I need a quick word about the upcoming clout-shooting competition.”

Gwendolen drifted back to the group with her mother and aunt Gascoigne while she waited for her next turn to shoot. The possibility that Mr. Grandcourt, the famously eligible, much whispered-about bachelor, might not show up at all had begun to nag at her, a tiny little shadow threatening to spoil the perfect completeness of her day. For all her saucy teasing, for all her mockery of her family’s obvious hope that he might take a fancy to her, she was far from indifferent to the idea of the impression she would make on him. She told herself he would never have the slightest power over her—she had not yet realised that the urge to conquer a man is itself a kind of quiet subjection. She pictured him as just another of the many admiring, assiduous gentlemen she had already encountered, all different coloured beards, all different styles of bearing, fawning over her. The knowledge that everyone around her expected her to think him delightful made her half-determined to find him ridiculous before they even met. But that was no reason she wanted him to stay away. Even the faint, absurd premonition that it would be a bother to reject him did not make her wish he would save her the trouble by never asking. The image of him ignoring her entirely, and shortly becoming engaged to Miss Arrowpoint, was not one that flattered her pride.

She hung on every word Lord Brackenshaw said about his late arrival, and when he finally stepped onto the grounds, no one was more sharply aware of his presence than Gwendolen—though she resolutely refused to turn her head to look for him. Not a single shift of her gaze would betray that the much-discussed Mr. Mallinger Grandcourt mattered to her in the slightest. She threw herself back into her shooting, so deliberately avoiding any curious glance that no one could possibly suspect she knew he was there. And all the while, the quiet, certain knowledge that he was watching her hummed like a bright thread through every other thought in her head.

It only made her shoot better. At last, she sent three arrows in a row dead centre into the gold. The whole crowd erupted in clapping and cheers, a special honour reserved for such a rare feat at the Brackenshaw meet: a extra fine gold star, meant to be pinned to the breast of the winner. The crowd made way for her, clearing a path for her to walk forward to receive it from Lady Brackenshaw’s hands, the long, soft afternoon light gilding the lines of her perfect, graceful figure as she moved. Every eye in the clearing was fixed on her. She was the centre of the beautiful scene, and she would not look anywhere but at Lady Brackenshaw, no matter how much she wanted to dart a quick glance at the new spectator at the edge of the crowd. Part of her pleasure was the thought that even the sharp, unimpressible Herr Klesmer was watching her, music far from his mind, his petty superiority completely overshadowed by the moment. Most of all, she knew Grandcourt was there, seeing her at the very height of her beauty and glory, admiring her unreservedly, no criticism in sight. She did not expect to admire him back, and that was just how she liked it.

She met Lady Brackenshaw’s gracious smile without blushing (she only blushed when she was caught off guard), her face glowing with bright, unforced joy, and bent with easy grace to let the star be fixed to her gown just at the curve of her shoulder. She had drifted off after the ceremony, exchanging playful congratulations with other guests, and was standing at the edge of the group, absently running her thumb over the sharp tip of an arrow, when Lord Brackenshaw strolled up to her, smiling.
“Miss Harleth, here is a gentleman who will not wait one moment longer for an introduction. He prevailed on your mamma to send me over with him. Will you allow me to introduce Mr. Mallinger Grandcourt?”
