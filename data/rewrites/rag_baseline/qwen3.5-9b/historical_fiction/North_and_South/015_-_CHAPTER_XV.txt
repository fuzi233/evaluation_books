The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the library windows, a rhythmic drumming that felt, to Margaret Hale, like the frantic, indifferent heartbeat of a city that refused to acknowledge the quiet suffocation of its visitors. She sat beside her father, Mr. Hale, her gaze fixed not on the book he held, but on the thick sheaf of letters that had arrived by the morning post, now folded neatly on the table. They were from Mr. Thornton, his script confident and sprawling, detailing his latest thoughts on industry and labor.

Margaret traced the edge of the parchment with a silver letter-opener, her mind drifting to the task ahead. Tomorrow, they must return Mrs. Thornton's call. Her mother was bedridden, a victim of a fever that had drained the color from her cheeks, leaving behind a pallor that seemed to echo the grey skies outside. Yet, Mr. Hale's voice remained steady, masking a deep, veiled anxiety that Margaret recognized instantly.

"Did you consult the doctor, Margaret?" he asked, his tone shifting from casual inquiry to urgent concern. "Did you send for him?"

"No, papa," Margaret replied, her voice clear and firm. "You spoke of his coming to see me when I was well. But if I only knew of a good physician, I would go this afternoon and ask him to come. I am sure Mamma is seriously indisposed."

She spoke the truth plainly, driven by a sudden realization that her father had previously shut his mind against the severity of her fears. Now, the landscape of their conversation had shifted. He answered in a despondent whisper, haunted by a terrifying possibility.

"Do you think she has any hidden complaint? Do you think she is really very ill? Has Dixon said anything? Oh, Margaret! I am haunted by the fear that our coming to Milton has killed her. My poor Maria!"

"Oh, papa! Don't imagine such things," Margaret said, shocked by the gravity of his despair. "She is not well, that is all. Many a one is not well for a time; and with good advice, gets better and stronger than ever."

"But has Dixon said anything about her?"

"No! You know Dixon enjoys making a mystery out of trifles; and she has been a little mysterious about Mamma's health, which has alarmed me rather—that is all. Without any reason, I dare say. You know, papa, you said the other day I was getting fanciful."

"I hope and trust you are," Mr. Hale said, his eyes lighting up with relief. "But don't think of what I said then. I like you to be fanciful about your mother's health! Don't be afraid of telling me your fancies. I like to hear them, though I dare say I spoke as if I was annoyed. But we will ask Mrs. Thornton if she can tell us of a good doctor. We won't throw away our money on anyone but someone first-rate. Stay, we turn up this street."

The street did not look as if it could contain a house large enough for Mrs. Thornton's habitation. Her son's presence never gave any impression as to the kind of house he lived in; but, unconsciously, Margaret had imagined that tall, massive, handsomely dressed Mrs. Thornton must reside in a mansion of equal grandeur. Now, Marlborough Street revealed itself as a long row of small, cramped dwellings, punctuated by blank walls.

"He told me he lived in Marlborough Street, I'm sure," said Mr. Hale, his brow furrowed in perplexity.

"Perhaps it is one of the economies he still practices, to live in a very small house. But here are plenty of people about; let me ask."

She accordingly inquired of a passer-by, who informed them that Mr. Thornton lived close to the mill, pointing out the factory lodge-door at the end of a long, dead wall they had noticed earlier.

The lodge-door was unremarkable, resembling a common garden gate, flanked by great closed iron gates designed for the ingress and egress of lorries and wagons. The lodge-keeper admitted them into a vast, oblong yard. On one side stood offices for the transaction of business; on the opposite, an immense, multi-windowed mill from which proceeded the continual, deafening clank of machinery and the long, groaning roar of the steam-engine. Opposite the wall, along the narrow side of the yard, stood a handsome stone-coped house. Blackened, to be sure, by the perpetual smoke, its paint, windows, and steps were kept with scrupulous cleanliness. It was evident that the house had been built some fifty or sixty years prior. The stone facings, the long narrow windows, the multiple flights of steps ascending from either side and guarded by railings—all testified to its age. Margaret wondered why people who could afford to live in such a substantial house, maintaining it in such pristine order, did not prefer a smaller dwelling in the countryside or a quieter suburb, escaping the continual whirl and din of the factory.

Her unaccustomed ears could hardly catch her father's voice as they stood on the steps awaiting the opening of the door. The yard, with the great doors in the dead wall serving as a boundary, was but a dismal outlook for the sitting-rooms of the house—a fact Margaret confirmed when they had mounted the old-fashioned stairs and been ushered into the drawing-room. The room featured three windows overlooking the front door and the passage on the right. There was no one inside. It seemed as though no one had entered since the day the furniture was bagged up with the meticulous care of someone preparing for a volcanic eruption, intending to discover the house a thousand years hence.

The walls were pink and gold; the pattern on the carpet depicted bunches of flowers on a light ground, yet it was carefully covered in the center by a linen drugget, glazed and colorless. The window curtains were lace; each chair and sofa wore its own particular veil of netting or knitting. Great alabaster groups occupied every flat surface, protected from dust beneath glass shades. In the middle of the room, directly under the bagged-up chandelier, stood a large circular table with smartly bound books arranged at regular intervals around its polished surface, like gaily colored spokes of a wheel. Everything reflected light; nothing absorbed it. The whole room possessed a painfully spotted, spangled, speckled appearance that impressed Margaret so unpleasantly that she was hardly conscious of the peculiar cleanliness required to keep everything so white and pure in such an atmosphere, or of the trouble that must be willingly expended to secure that effect of icy, snowy discomfort. Everywhere there was evidence of care and labor, but not care and labor to procure ease or to foster habits of tranquil home employment; solely to ornament and then to preserve ornament from dirt or destruction.

They had leisure to observe and to speak to each other in low voices before Mrs. Thornton appeared. They were discussing matters that the entire world might eventually hear, yet the room itself seemed to demand silence, as if unwilling to awaken its unused echoes.

Finally, Mrs. Thornton entered, rustling in handsome black silk, as was her wont. Her muslins and laces rivaled, though did not excel, the pure whiteness of the room's decor. Margaret explained how her mother could not accompany them to return the call, but in her anxiety not to revive her father's fears too vividly, she gave a bungling account. She left the impression on Mrs. Thornton's mind that Mrs. Hale's condition was some temporary or fanciful fine-ladyish indisposition, resolvable with a strong enough motive, or that if it was too severe to allow her to come out that day, the call might simply be deferred. Remembering the horses to her carriage, hired specifically for her own visit to the Hales, and recalling how Fanny had been ordered to go by Mr. Thornton to pay every respect to them, Mrs. Thornton drew up slightly offended. She gave Margaret no sympathy—indeed, hardly any credit for the statement of her mother's indisposition.

"How is Mr. Thornton?" asked Mr. Hale. "I was afraid he was not well, from his hurried note yesterday."

"My son is rarely ill; and when he is, he never speaks about it, or makes it an excuse for not doing anything. He told me he could not get leisure to read with you last night, sir. He regretted it, I am sure; he values the hours spent with you."

"I am sure they are equally agreeable to me," said Mr. Hale. "It makes me feel young again to see his enjoyment and appreciation of all that is fine in classical literature."

"I have no doubt that classics are very desirable for people who have leisure. But I confess, it was against my judgment that my son renewed his study of them. The time and place in which he lives seem to me to require all his energy and attention. Classics may do very well for men who loiter away their lives in the country or in colleges; but Milton men ought to have their thoughts and powers absorbed in the work of today. At least, that is my opinion." She delivered this final clause with the pride that apes humility.

"But, surely, if the mind is too long directed to one object only, it will get stiff and rigid, and unable to take in many interests," said Margaret.
