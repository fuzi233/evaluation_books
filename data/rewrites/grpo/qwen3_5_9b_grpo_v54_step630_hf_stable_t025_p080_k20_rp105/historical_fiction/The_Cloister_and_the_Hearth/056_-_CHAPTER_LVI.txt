Gerard secured a modest lodging on the west bank of the Tiber, embarking each day on a relentless quest for employment. He carried samples to every workshop rumored to commission such work, only to be met with icy disdain. "Our letters are somewhat thinner than this," one merchant scoffed. "How dark your ink is," another remarked. Yet the prevailing cry was dismissive: "What avail does this serve? Latin writ is scarce here now. Can you not write Greek?"

"Ay, but not nearly so well as Latin," Gerard admitted.

"Then you shall never earn your bread in Rome."

Undeterred, Gerard borrowed a magnificent Greek manuscript at a steep price, returning home with a hollow purse but an unbroken spirit. Within a fortnight, he had mastered the Greek character with remarkable speed. Eager to capitalize on this skill, he devoted his mornings to study and his afternoons to hunting clients. When he finally presented a Greek specimen superior to any they possessed, the traders merely informed him that both Greek and Latin were unsaleable; the city was flooded with works from across Europe. They advised him bluntly that he should have arrived the previous year.

Desperate, Gerard purchased a psaltery. His landlady, charmed by his appearance and demeanor, often offered him kind words. One day, she invited him to dine and, surprised by his despondency, asked what had dampened his spirits. He confessed his struggles. She offered her interpretation: "Those sly traders," she declared, "have writers in their pay, compensated nobly for their work while paying you poorly. No wonder they blow cold on you. I suspect you write too well. How do I know? Because you do not lock your door, like the churl Pietro, and women are naturally curious. Yes, you write too well for them."

Gerard requested clarification.

"Why," she explained, "your excellent work might blind those to whom they are selling inferior goods."

Gerard sighed. "Alas, dame, you read people on the wrong side, yet you are so kind and frank yourself."

"My dear little heart, these Romans are a subtle race. I am a Siennese, thanks to the Virgin."

"My mistake was leaving Augsburg," Gerard lamented.

"Augsburg?" she replied haughtily. "Is that a place to compete with Rome? I have never heard of it."

She then assured him that he would make his fortune despite the booksellers. "Seeing you as a stranger, they lie without sense or discretion. All the world knows that our great figures are bitten by the writing spider; they pour out their money like water, turning good land and houses into scribbled sheepskins to hide in chests. God help them, and has sent them safely through this fury, as He has through many others. Since the age of scribbling began, there has been less cutting and stabbing among rival factions, less vindictive eating of opponents' livers, minced and fried. Consider Cardinal Bassarion and the Pope himself—a pair who could keep a score like you writing night and day. I shall speak to Teresa; she hears the court gossip."

The following day, she reported seeing Teresa and learning of five additional signors afflicted by the writing spider. Gerard recorded their names, purchased parchment, and spent several days preparing specimens. He left one, bearing his name and address, at each of their doors, awaiting results in hopeful silence.

There were none.

Days turned into weeks, leaving him heartsick. Strangely, this was the same period when Margaret fought desperately against overwhelming odds to feed her male dependents in Rotterdam, only to be arrested for practicing medicine without a license rather than killing with it.

Gerard saw ruin staring him in the face.

He spent his afternoons collecting canzonets and mastering them, laying in playing cards for color, and striking off a meal each day. This final stroke of genius brought him fresh trouble. In these "camere locande," the landlady prepared all meals, though the lodgers purchased the provisions. His hostess quickly detected his deception. With a brusque opening that made him blush and look frightened, she pacified herself instantly, appealing to his common sense: "Adversity is not a thing to be overcome on an empty stomach."

"Patienza, my lad! Times will mend; meantime, I will feed you for the love of heaven."

"Nay, hostess," Gerard replied, "my purse is not yet quite void, and it would add to my trouble if true folk lost their due by me."

"Why, you are as mad as your neighbor Pietro, with his one bad picture."

"Why, how do you know 'tis a bad picture?"

"Because nobody will buy it. There is one who has no gift. He must don casque and glaive and carry his panel as a shield."

Gerard pricked up his ears. She elaborated: Pietro had arrived from Florence with money and an unfinished painting, renting an unfurnished room opposite Gerard's and furnishing it neatly. Upon completion, he received visitors and offers—liberal ones, in her opinion—but refused them so disdainfully that he made enemies. Since then, he had often taken the painting out to sell, only to return it defeated. Over the last month, she had watched his possessions vanish; now he wore but one suit and slept on a great chest. She had discovered this only by peering through the keyhole, for he locked his door vigilantly whenever he left. "Is he afraid we shall steal his chest or his picture, that no soul in all Rome is weak enough to buy?"

"Nay, sweet hostess; do you not see 'tis his poverty he seeks to screen from view?"

"And the more fool he! Are all our hearts as ill as his? A trial might suffice."

"How you speak of him. Why, his case is mine; and your countryman to boot."

"Oh, we Siennese love strangers. His case yours? Nay, 'tis just the contrary. You are the comeliest youth ever lodged in this house; hair like gold. He is a dark, sour-visaged loon. Besides, you know how to take a woman on her better side; not he. Nevertheless, I wish he would not starve to death in my house, earning me a bad name. Anyway, one starveling is enough in any house. You are far from home, and it is for me, the mistress here, to number your meals—for me and the Dutch wife, your mother, who is far away. We two women shall settle that matter. Mind your own business, being a man, and leave cooking and such to us, who are in the world for little else but roasting fowls, suckling men at starting, and sweeping grown-up cobwebs."

"Dear kind dame, in sooth you often remind me of my mother, who is far away."

"All the better; I'll put you more in mind of her before I have done with you." The honest soul beamed with pleasure.

Gerard, neither an egotist nor blinded by female partiality, saw his own grief reflected in poor, proud Pietro. The more he pondered it, the more he resolved to share his humble means with the unlucky artist; Pietro's sympathy would repay him. He attempted to intercept Pietro but failed.

One day, he heard a groan from the room. He knocked, receiving no answer, then knocked again. A surly voice commanded him to enter.

He obeyed timidly, stepping into a garret furnished with a chair, a picture facing the wall, an iron basin, an easel, and a long chest. Coiled upon the chest was a haggard young man with a wonderfully bright eye. Nothing resembled a coiled cobra ready to strike the first comer more than the sight.

"Good Signor Pietro," Gerard said, "forgive me that, weary of my own solitude, I intrude on yours. I am your nearest neighbor in this house, and methinks your brother in fortune. I am an artist too."

"You are a painter? Welcome, signer. Sit down on my bed."

Pietro jumped off and waved him into the vacant throne with magnificent courtesy.

Gerard bowed and smiled, yet hesitated. "I may not call myself a painter. I am a writer, a caligrapher. I copy Greek and Latin manuscripts when I can find patrons to pay for them."

"And you call that an artist?"

"Without offense to your superior merit, Signor Pietro."

"No offense, stranger, none. Only, it seems to me an artist is one who thinks and paints his thought. Now a caligrapher merely draws in black and white the thoughts of another."

"'Tis well distinguished, signor. But then, a writer can record the thoughts of the great ancients, matters of pure reason such as no man may paint: yes, and the thoughts of God, which angels could not paint. But let that pass. I am a painter as well; but a sorry one."

"The better thy luck. 'They will buy thy work in Rome.'"

"But seeking to commend myself to one of thy eminence, I thought it well rather to call myself a capable writer than a scurvy painter."

At that moment, footsteps echoed on the stairs. "Ah! 'tis the good dame," cried Gerard. "Hostess, I am here in conversation with Signor Pietro. I dare say he will let me have my humble dinner here."

The Italian bowed gravely.

The landlady brought in Gerard's dinner, smoking and savory. She set the dish down on the bed with a face divested of all expression and departed.

Gerard began to eat. But before he had consumed many mouthfuls, he stopped. "I am an ill-mannered churl, Signor Pietro. I never eat to my mind when eating alone. For our Lady's sake, put a spoon into this ragout with me; 'tis not unsavory, I promise you."

Pietro fixed his glittering eye on him.

"What, good youth, thou a stranger, and offerest me thy dinner?"

"Why, see, there is more than one can eat."

"Well, I accept," said Pietro, taking the dish with some appearance of calmness, then flinging the contents out the window.

He turned, trembling with mortification and ire. "Let that teach thee to offer alms to an artist thou knowest not, master writer."

Gerard's face flushed with anger, and it cost him a bitter struggle not to box this high-souled creature's ears. To go and destroy good food! His mother's milk curdled in his veins with horror at such impiety. Finally, pity for Pietro's petulance and egotism, mixed with a touch of respect for poverty-struck pride, prevailed.

However, he said coldly, "Likely what thou hast done might pass in a novel of thy countryman, Signor Boccaccio; but 'twas not honest."

"Make that good!" said the painter sullenly.

"I offered thee half my dinner; no more. But thou hast taken it all. Hadst a right to throw away thy share, but not mine. Pride is well, but justice is better."

Pietro stared, then reflected.

"'Tis well. I took thee for a fool, so transparent was thine artifice. Forgive me! And prithee leave me! Thou seest how 'tis with me. The world hath soured me. I hate mankind. I was not always so. Once more excuse that my discourtesy, and fare thee well."

Gerard sighed and moved for the door.

Suddenly, a thought struck him. "Signor Pietro," he said, "we Dutchmen are hard bargainers. We are the lads 'een eij scheeren,' that is, 'to shave an egg.' Therefore, I, for my lost dinner, do claim to feast mine eyes on your picture, whose face is toward the wall."

"Nay, nay," said the painter hastily. "Ask me not that; I have already misconducted myself enough towards thee. I would not shed thy blood."

"Saints forbid! My blood?"

"Stranger," said Pietro sullenly, "irritated by repeated insults to my picture, which is my child, my heart, I did in a moment of rage make a solemn vow to drive my dagger into the next one that should flout it, and the labor and love I have given to it."

"What, are all to be slain that will not praise this picture?" He looked at its back with curiosity.

"Nay, nay; if you would but look at it, and hold your parrot tongues. But you will be talking. So I have turned it to the wall forever. Would I were dead, and buried in it for my coffin!"

Gerard reflected.

"I accept the condition. Show me the picture! I can but hold my peace."

Pietro went and turned its face, placing it in the best light the room afforded, then coiled himself again on his chest, his eye and stiletto glittering.

The picture represented the Virgin and Christ flying through the air in a sort of cloud of shadowy cherubic faces; underneath was a landscape forty or fifty miles in extent, with a purple sky above.

Gerard stood and looked at it in silence. Then he stepped close, examined it, retired as far as possible, and looked again; but said not a word.

When he had been at this game half an hour, Pietro cried out querulously and somewhat inconsistently: "Well, have you not a word to say about it?"

Gerard started. "I cry your mercy; I forgot there were three of us here. Ay, I have much to say." And he drew his sword.

"Alas! Alas!" cried Pietro, jumping in terror from his lair. "What wouldst thou?"

"Marry, defend myself against thy bodkin, signor; and at due odds, being, as aforesaid, a Dutchman. Therefore, hold aloof, while I deliver judgment, or I will pin thee to the wall like a cockchafer."

"Oh! Is that all?" said Pietro, greatly relieved. "I feared you were going to stab my poor picture with your sword, already stabbed by so many foul tongues."
