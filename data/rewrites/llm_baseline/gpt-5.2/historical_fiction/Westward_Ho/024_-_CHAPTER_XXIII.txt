Nearly three years had gone by since they had knelt at evensong under the great tree of Guayra—three years that left little behind them but broken trails, scattered graves, and a few blotted names caught up by rumor and mis-spelt by strange tongues. They had ranged, like a needle hunting a lost thread, across a world of hill and forest—eight hundred miles one way and four hundred the other—always with Manoa in their minds: the Golden City that an Indian tale had set shining on the dark loom of the wilderness.

They had followed the long green banks of the Orinoco; they had labored beyond the roaring whiteness of Maypures where the river seemed to gnash its teeth; they had pushed into the upper waters that fed the mighty Amazon. They had climbed streams into Peru and walked under cinchona trees at Loxa, not knowing—as no one then knew—that the bitter bark held mercy against fevers. They had seen Chimborazo lift its virgin snows above thundercloud, and Cotopaxi brood like a black altar before it spilled fire. Foiled behind the Andes, they had turned again and plunged from cold cliff into the “green and misty ocean of the Montana,” and there, mile by mile, had wormed northward along the eastern foot of the inland Cordillera, until at last they lay camped on one of the many feeders of the Meta—rivers born of the Suma Paz and sliding down into forested plains.

There, under enormous trees and a tropic moon, the watch-fires threw a reddish tremble on the silent stream. Amyas and Will Cary sat with Jack Brimblecombe and old Salvation Yeo; near them, the Indian lad who had followed their fortunes as faithfully as a dog followed a master, alive still, lean and bright-eyed. They were as far from Manoa as ever—its fairy lake, its golden palaces, its wonders—though rumor had so often pricked their hopes that they had started a score of times in fresh directions only to meet fresh emptiness. Hope deferred had made their hearts sick, and yet they had gone on, because stopping felt too like dying.

Four-and-forty men sat there out of the four-score and four who had left Guayra.

The rest were scattered as the forest scatters what it has once taken. Drew the master lay by the Rio Negro with five brave fellows slain under poisoned arrows when they tried to force the gorges of Parima. Two had frozen among Andean valleys beneath hail that came down like slate shards from the condor’s height. Four had been swallowed at an Orinoco rapid. Five or six wounded men had been left among friendly Indians at another, to be fetched if they could—perhaps never. Fever had bitten them month by month; snakes and jaguars had taken their toll; alligators, cannibal fish, electric eels—every water and thicket had its tax. Of their march no path remained, only lonely mounds where English hands had dug into a foreign earth.

Yet the living were not broken. Sun-dried and spare, they were tough as seasoned oak, with the quiet fire of English courage unquenched, and that stubborn cheer which can make a jest of toil because it cannot afford to yield to it. Their beards lay on their breasts; their long hair was knotted like women’s to shade the nape from burning noon. Leggings of guazu-puti deer-skin hugged their shins; their shirts were patched with Indian cotton web; jaguar and puma, ape and deer, hung from their shoulders in ragged trophies. Their powder was long gone; their muskets, spoiled by the steaming vapor-bath of the woods, had been abandoned in a cave by some cataract of the Orinoco, rusty and useless. But their swords were bright still, and in the forest’s half-light flashed like teeth. They carried bows no Indian arm could bend, and arrows pointed with the remnants of their armor; many had learned the silent deadliness of the pocuna blowgun. So they had become, by sheer need, half-Indian in gear while remaining wholly English in temper—terrible to hostile tribes, but just and generous to those who dealt straight. Many a smooth-chinned Carib or Ature, Solimo or Guahiba, would recount with wonder the righteousness of the bearded strangers who called themselves the enemies of Spaniards, and spoke of a great queen oversea who would avenge and deliver the oppressed.

Most of the men slept among the trees: some on the ground, some in grass hammocks slung between trunks. The night had its own cookery of sound. Now the heavy plunge of a tapir broke the river as he tore up water-weeds for his supper; now a jaguar, climbing from tree-top to tree-top, disturbed the monkeys, and the monkeys roused the birds, and for ten minutes the forest turned into a madness of roars, howls, shrieks, and cacklings, like a lid lifted on pandemonium. Then it sank again into that listening hush which is never empty—only so crowded with small lives that the ear can scarcely sort them. In such a place a man learned that silence was not the absence of sound, but a balance of it: the difference between a mind soothed by river and leaf and insect-hum, and a mind driven raw by the clangor men make when they try to drown their own thoughts.

Beside the watch-fire, the four held their council of war. They had held a hundred such, and failed; likely this too would fail. Yet it was more solemn than most, because the two years for which they had vowed to seek Manoa were long spent. Another path must be chosen, unless they meant to die as forest-ghosts, drifting forever under green ceilings.

“Well,” said Will Cary, drawing his cigar from his mouth, “at least we have got something out of those last Indians. It is a comfort to have a puff at tobacco once more, after three weeks’ fasting.”

The tobacco had come as all food had come to them now: by barter, by courtesy, by cunning, by hardship—leaf bundled in palm-fibre, smelling of smoke and earth and a sweetness that made the mouth water, as if it promised meat. Cary’s fingers were careful with it as with a spice, and the glowing end breathed a warm, resinous perfume into the damp night.

“For me,” said Jack Brimblecombe, “Heaven forgive me! but when I get the magical leaf between my teeth again, I feel tempted to sit as still as a chimney and smoke till my dying day, without stirring hand or foot.”

“Then I shall forbid you tobacco, Master Parson,” said Amyas, “for we must be up and away again to-morrow. We have been idling here three mortal days, and nothing done.”

“Shall we ever do anything?” Jack answered, and the firelight touched the hollows in his cheek. “I think the gold of Manoa is like the gold that lies where the rainbow touches the ground—always a field beyond you.”

Amyas was silent awhile, and so were the rest. The river’s small noises—fish kissing the surface, some unseen creature slipping among reeds—seemed suddenly loud for want of speech. There was no denying the truth: in the immense circuit they had made, they had met nothing but disappointment.

“There is but one more chance,” Amyas said at length, and his voice sounded older than his years, “and that is the mountains to the east of the Orinoco, where we failed the first time. The Incas may have moved on to them when they escaped.”

“Why not?” Cary said. “They would so put all the forests, beside the Llanos and half-a-dozen great rivers, between them and those dogs of Spaniards.”

“Shall we try it once more?” Amyas asked. “This river ought to run into the Orinoco; and once there, we are again at the very foot of the mountains. What say you, Yeo?”

Old Salvation Yeo sat with his great hands on his knees, the fire making iron of his weathered face. “I cannot but mind, your worship,” he said slowly, “that when we came up the Orinoco the Indians told us terrible stories of those mountains—how far they stretched, and how difficult they were to cross, by reason of the cliffs aloft and the thick forests in the valleys. And have we not lost five good men there already?”

“What care we?” Cary broke in. “No forests can be thicker than those we have bored through already. Why, if one had had but a tail, like a monkey, for an extra warp, one might have gone a hundred miles on end along the tree-tops, and found it far pleasanter walking than tripping in withes, and being eaten up with creeping things from morn till night.”

“But remember, too,” said Jack, “how they told us to beware of the Amazons.”

“What, Jack—afraid of a parcel of women?” Amyas said, and would have laughed, but the sound would not fully come.

“Why not?” Jack returned. “I wouldn’t run from a man, as you know; but a woman—it’s not natural, like. They must be witches or devils. See how the Caribs feared them. And there were men there without necks, and with their eyes in their breasts, they said. Now how could a Christian tackle such customers as them?”

“He couldn’t cut off their heads, that’s certain,” said Cary; “but, I suppose, a poke in the ribs will do as much for them as for their neighbors.”

“Well,” Jack persisted, half in jest and half in dread of the unnameable, “if I fight, let me fight honest flesh and blood, that’s all, and none of these outlandish monsters. How do you know but that they are invulnerable by art-magic?”

“How do you know that they are?” Amyas answered. “And as for the Amazons—woman’s woman all the world over. I’ll wager you may wheedle them round with a compliment or two.”

Cary’s eyes sparkled. “Pity I have not a court-suit and a Spanish hat. I would have taken an orange in one hand and a handkerchief in the other, gone all alone to them as ambassador, and been in a week as great with Queen Blackfacealinda as ever Raleigh is at Whitehall.”

“Gentlemen,” said Yeo, and there was no laughter in him, “where you go, I go; and not only I, but every man of us, I doubt not. But we have lost now half our company, and spent our ammunition, so we are no better men—were it not for our swords—than these naked heathens round us. Now it was, as you all know, by the wonder and noise of their ordnance—let alone their horses, which is a break-neck beast I put no faith in—that both Cortez and Pizarro, those imps of Satan, made their golden conquests, with which if we could have astounded the people of Manoa—”

“Having first found the said people,” Amyas laughed at last, the sound sharp with fatigue. “It is like the old fable. Every craftsman thinks his own trade the one pillar of the commonweal.”

“Well! your worship,” Yeo said, stubborn as an old oak root, “it may be that being a gunner I overprize guns. But it don’t need slate and pencil to do this sum—are forty men without shot as good as eighty with?”

“Thou art right, old fellow—right enough,” Amyas answered, and his voice softened. “I was only jesting for very sorrow, and must needs laugh about it lest I weep about it. Our chance is over, I believe, though I dare not confess as much to the men.”

“Sir,” Yeo said, and the crackle of the fire seemed to listen, “I have a feeling on me that the Lord’s hand is against us in this matter. Whether He means to keep this wealth for worthier men than us, or whether it is His will to hide this great city in the secret place of His presence from the strife of tongues, and so to spare them from sinful man’s covetousness—and England from that sin and luxury which I have seen gold beget among the Spaniards—I know not, sir; for who knoweth the counsels of the Lord? But I have long had a voice within which saith, ‘Salvation Yeo, thou shalt never behold the Golden City which is on earth, where heathens worship sun and moon and the hosts of heaven; be content, therefore, to see that Golden City which is above, where is neither sun nor moon, but the Lord God and the Lamb are the light thereof.’”

There was a simple majesty in the old man’s outbreaks—something that made even bold companions stare as if he had stepped aside, for a moment, into another air. In the wilderness a man’s words were often all he had left to keep his own story from being stolen by fear; and Yeo’s words, with their antique turns and Scripture-thunder, sounded like a dialect that refused to die. Brimblecombe, whose gentle piety had long ceased to be offended by the Anabaptist’s roughness, answered softly:

“Amen! amen! my masters all. And it has been on my mind, too, this long time, that there is a providence against our going east; for see how this two years past, whenever we have pushed eastward, we have fallen into trouble, and lost good men; and whenever we went Westward-ho, we have prospered—and do prosper to this day.”

“And what is more, gentlemen,” Yeo went on, as if a hand had turned his thought like a page, “if, as Scripture says, dreams are from the Lord, I verily believe mine last night came from Him. For as I lay by the fire, sirs, I heard my little maid’s voice calling of me, as plain as ever I heard in my life; and the very same words, sirs, which she learned from me and my good comrade William Penberthy to say, ‘Westward-ho! jolly mariners all!’—a bit of an ungodly song, my masters, which we sang in our wild days. But she stood and called it as plain as ever mortal ears heard, and called again till I answered, ‘Coming! my maid, coming!’ and after that the dear chuck called no more—God grant I find her yet!—and so I woke.”

Cary had long since ceased to laugh at Yeo about the “little maid.” In a forest where a man might lose not only his way but his very words, the sound of a remembered voice could be as solid as bread.

“So let it be, Yeo, if the rest agree,” Amyas said at last. “But what shall we do to the westward?”

“Do?” Cary’s grin returned, quick as a spark in dry leaf. “There’s plenty to do; for there’s plenty of gold, and plenty of Spaniards too, they say, on the other side of these mountains—so that our swords will not rust for lack of adventures, my gay knights-errant all.”

They talked on into the night, the plan shaping itself between firelight and shadow. Desperate enough, certainly—but what cared those hearts for desperate counsels? They would cross the Cordillera to Santa Fe de Bogota, of whose wealth Amyas and Yeo had heard on the Pacific. There they would try to seize either the town itself or a convoy of gold coming from it. Then they would strike for the nearest great river said to run northward, build canoes, and make for the Northern Sea once more; and if Heaven favored them, they might take a Spanish ship and win home to England—not with Manoa’s unimaginable treasure, but with a fair booty of Spanish gold.

It was a wild dream; yet no wilder than Drake’s had been before it, and not so wild as Oxenham’s might have proved, but for the man’s own fatal folly.

Amyas sat up late, watching the coals crumble, and feeling the weight of what must be abandoned. To give up the dream of years was hard; to face his mother with empty hands was harder still. But men’s lives were in the balance; he had no right to feed them on a hope that had turned to mist.

Next day the new plan was put before the company and received with a shout, as if any road were better than the slow, sick wandering after an ever-receding marvel. They would go up to the mountains and rest awhile; if it might be done, bring up the wounded left behind; then try a new venture with new hopes and, likely, new dangers. They were long since hardened to danger, as the hand is hardened to the oar.

They started next morning cheerfully enough. For three hours and more they paddled easily up glassy, windless reaches between two green, flower-bespangled walls of forest. Birds and insects jeweled the air. Long trailers hung from branches to the water’s edge and seemed to admire their own blossoms in the stream’s clear mirror. River, trees, flowers—fairyland, if one chose to call it so; only the fairyland was colossal, and long familiarity had dulled the men’s wonder.

What once would have been miracle had become routine. Trees two hundred feet high stood massed with yellow and purple blossom to their topmost twigs; every stem was a hanging garden of orchids in crimson and orange, of vanilla vines whose pods—when you bruised them—gave a warm, dark perfume that made a man think, for a moment, of some kitchen far away where sweetness and smoke belonged to peace instead of peril. Strange birds flashed like dropped banners; fish glittered like new-minted coin. Monkeys marched along the tree-tops, keeping pace and chattering in whistles and grunts and howls; their noise, once comic, had grown as ordinary as rain. The roar of jaguar and the rustle of boa stirred no more fear than the creak of a ship’s timbers stirs a sailor used to the sea.

So when a brilliant green and rose-colored fish—flat-bodied like a bream, flab-finned like a salmon, saw-toothed like a shark—leapt clean into the canoe to escape the rush of a huge alligator (whose loathsome snout, unable to check itself, actually rattled against the canoe within a foot of Jack Brimblecombe’s hand), Jack did not turn pale as he once had at sharks upon another voyage. He coolly seized the fish, weighed it by habit in his palm, and said, “He’s four pound weight! If you can catch ‘pirai’ for us like that, old fellow, just keep in our wake, and we’ll give you the cleanings for wages.”

Food, even in jest, was never far from their minds now. The forest fed them, but on its own terms: smoked monkey, roasted iguana, fish split down the back and laid over greenwood coals till the skin blistered and the flesh steamed, bitter roots grated and washed to take out poison, palm hearts cut pale and crisp as a child’s bone. The very act of cooking had become a kind of religion—measuring salt scraped from palm-nut ashes, turning meat so it dried without burning, keeping smoke low so it would not call enemies, listening for the wrong bird-cry that meant strangers near. Here, taste and smell were not luxuries; they were warnings, promises, memories.

They paddled on, hugging the shadow of the southern bank while on their right the full sun-glare lay upon the northern wall of mimosas, figs, and laurels, broken by bamboo tufts and decked with a thousand gaudy parasites. Bank on bank of bloom piled upward toward the sky until, where its outline cut the blue, flowers and leaves too lofty to be distinguished formed a broken rainbow of hues quivering in ascending streams of azure mist, melting at last into heaven itself.

As the sun climbed, a great stillness fell upon the forest. Jaguars and monkeys withdrew into darker hollows. Birds’ notes died one by one. Butterflies ceased their flitting and slept with outspread wings upon glossy leaves, so perfectly matched to blossom that the eye could not pick them out. Now and then a hummingbird whirred down toward the water, hummed for a breath around a pendent flower, and vanished again into the inner shade where trunks stood like the pillars of some heathen shrine. A parrot swung and screamed at them from an overhanging bough. A thirsty monkey slid lazily down a liana, dipped water in his tiny hand, and scrambled back chattering when he met the stare of an alligator peering up through the clear depths.

In shaded nooks, capybaras—rabbits as large as sheep—paddled sleepily among blue water-lilies; black and purple water-hens ran on rafts of floating leaves. A freshwater dolphin’s shining snout rose, a jet of spray whirred up with a small hiss, and for a moment a rainbow hung upon it before the snout sank again. On shallow pebbly shores, scarlet flamingoes stood knee-deep on one leg like figures cut from cloth; crested cranes pranced, admiring their own finery; ibises and egrets dipped their bills for prey. But before noon even those slipped away, and there reigned a stillness that might be heard: not emptiness, but a delicate weave of sound—far-off water, near insect-hum, leaf’s faint rub on leaf—so that a man’s mind, if it had been bruised by harsher clamors, could find itself again in the balance of living noise. There, at least, the world was not shouting at itself for profit, nor selling music in order to drown the quiet truths that rose in quietness.

At last a soft, distant murmur began to grow through the air, swelling by degrees into a heavy roar. They were nearing some cataract. Turning a point where deep alluvial soil rose into a low cliff fringed with delicate ferns, they came full upon the sight—and every paddle paused, not in wonder, but in something like disgust.

“Rapids again!” grumbled one. “I thought we had had enough of them on the Orinoco.”

“We shall have to get out, and draw the canoes overland, I suppose. Three hours will be lost—and in the very hottest of the day, too.”

“There’s worse behind; don’t you see the spray behind the palms?”

“Stop grumbling, my masters, and don’t cry out before you are hurt,” Amyas called. “Paddle right up to the largest of those islands, and let us look about us.”
