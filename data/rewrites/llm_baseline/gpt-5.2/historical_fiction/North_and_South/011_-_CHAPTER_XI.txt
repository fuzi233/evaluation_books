“Margaret!” Mr. Hale said, coming back upstairs after seeing his pupil to the door. The last echo of boots on the stair had died into the close, woollen hush of the Milton house, where even silence felt dusted-over. “I watched your face with some anxiety when Mr. Thornton spoke of having been a shop-boy. I knew it from Mr. Bell, and yet I half expected you—impulsive creature—to rise and leave the room.”

Margaret had been gathering her mother’s work into its basket with a care that was almost tenderness made visible—folding the silk, smoothing the little strip of linen as if it could be comforted into lying flat. She looked up, and her colour came and went, not from shame, but from the remembered force of the man’s plain statement.

“Oh, papa! you cannot truly have thought me so foolish. That was the best thing he said—the only thing that did not set my teeth on edge. Everything else—his hardness—revolted me. But when he spoke of himself, there was no brag in it; no greasy pretence of gentility such as makes the vulgarity of shop-people. And his manner of speaking of his mother—so tender, so respectful—it was then I was least likely to leave the room. I could have risen when he boasted of Milton, as if there were no other place under heaven; or when he coolly despised people for being careless and improvident, as if it were not his duty—since he has had such training—to try and make them different, to give them some share of what his mother gave him. No! his being a shop-boy—that was what I liked best.”

Mrs. Hale’s hands had paused over her work. The lamplight showed the faint shadow under her eyes, and the way fatigue had begun to sit, like an unwelcome lodger, on her delicate features. Still, her voice held its old gentility of protest.

“I am surprised at you, Margaret. You, who were always calling people ‘shoppy’ at Helstone! And I do think, Mr. Hale, you have not quite done right to introduce such a person without telling us what he had been. I was afraid of showing him how much I was shocked at some parts. His father dying in miserable circumstances—why, it might have been in the workhouse.”

“I am not sure it was not worse,” Mr. Hale answered gravely. He drew a chair nearer the fire as if warmth could make the telling kinder. “Mr. Bell told me much of his early life before we came here. Since Mr. Thornton has told you part, I may fill what he left unsaid. His father speculated wildly, failed, and then—killed himself because he could not bear the disgrace. Friends fell away when the dishonest gambling was discovered; the struggles made with other people’s money to regain his own small portion. No one came forward to help the mother and the boy. There was another child too—a girl—too young to earn, but needing bread all the same. And Mrs. Thornton is not a woman to sit waiting until tardy kindness remembers her.”

Margaret’s fingers tightened on the work-basket handle as if she felt, in her own palm, the weight of years carried without complaint.

“So they left Milton. I knew he went into a shop, and that his earnings, with some fragment of property secured to his mother, kept them for a long while. Mr. Bell says they lived on water-porridge for years—how, he could not say. And then, long after the creditors had given up hope of payment—if indeed they ever hoped, after the suicide—the young man came back to Milton, went quietly to each creditor, and paid the first instalment. No noise. No summoning of men, no flourish. Very silently—and all was paid at last; helped, in good measure, by one creditor, a crabbed old fellow, who took him in as a kind of partner.”

“That really is fine,” Margaret said, and in the word there was genuine admiration—warm as bread drawn from an oven—before the familiar chill of Milton’s iron came over it. “What a pity such a nature should be tainted by his position as a manufacturer!”

“How tainted?” her father asked.

“Oh, papa—by testing everything by the standard of wealth. When he spoke of mechanical power, he seemed to see it only as a new way of extending trade and making money. And the poor men around him—they were poor because they were—because they had not his iron nature, because they could not be rich. They were out of the pale of his sympathies.”

“Not vicious,” Mr. Hale corrected gently. “He did not say that. Improvident and self-indulgent—those were his words.”

Margaret, now ready to leave, hesitated on the threshold—drawn by the wish to be honest with her father, and yet reluctant to speak what would trouble him. At last she gave it, like a small confession offered with both hands.

“Papa, I do think Mr. Thornton a very remarkable man; but personally I do not like him at all.”

“And I do!” Mr. Hale said, laughing in his quiet way. “Personally, and all. I don’t make him a hero. Good-night, child. Your mother looks sadly tired to-night, Margaret.”

The remark went with Margaret upstairs like a hand laid upon her heart. For some time now she had watched her mother’s strength ebb—first in little things: how she sat as if the very air pressed on her shoulders; how the close, soot-laden atmosphere of Milton seemed to thicken around her, robbing her breath of ease. At Helstone Mrs. Hale’s life had been in and out perpetually, the house opening on green and sun, the world full of bird-calls and leaves whispering. Here, even the street noises—cart-wheels on stone, distant factory pulses, the iron cough of the town—came to the ear like a perpetual wrong note, and the windows admitted not freshness but a dimmed light, as if daylight itself had been handled by grimy fingers.

There were other signs too. She and Dixon held mysterious consultations in her bedroom; Dixon would come out crying and cross, as she always did when her grief had no other language. Once Margaret, passing soon after Dixon’s heavy step withdrew, had looked in and found her mother on her knees; and, stealing away, caught a few words—broken prayer for strength and patience to endure severe bodily suffering.

Margaret longed to re-knit the old intimacy that had loosened during her years at her aunt Shaw’s. She pressed nearer with softened words, with gentle touches—bringing her mother a shawl before she asked for it, arranging the pillows as if comfort could be contrived by hands alone. Mrs. Hale met her with caresses and endearments enough—more, perhaps, than in former days—yet Margaret felt the withheld secret, like a closed drawer whose very lock proclaimed something precious and perilous within. She believed, with a dread that would not speak its name, that it concerned her mother’s health.

That night she lay awake long, listening—half unwillingly—to Milton’s soundscape through the thin walls: a late shout, a cart, the faint throb that never seemed wholly to cease, as if the town had a giant heart beating without repose. She made plans in the dark with the desperate industry of love. Dixon must have permanent assistance. A servant must be found if Margaret gave herself wholly to it. Then, at least, her mother might have the personal attention she had had all her life, and not be left to struggle against soot and worry with only faithful Dixon’s overburdened hands.

For days Margaret’s thoughts were taken up with what seemed, in Milton, as difficult as any moral problem—finding a servant. She went to register offices; she spoke to women who promised much and meant nothing; she saw faces that were sullen with suspicion or sharpened by hunger. She came home with her mind tired, and her senses full of the mingled odours of the streets: coal smoke that coated the tongue, stale beer from open doors, damp woollen clothing steaming on bodies that had no leisure to be clean.

One afternoon, as she returned, she met Bessy Higgins in the street—a pale, delicate creature, moving slowly as if each step were paid for with breath.

“Well, Bessy,” Margaret said, stopping at once, “how are you? Better, I hope, now the wind has changed.”

“Better and not better, if yo’ know what that means.”

“Not exactly,” Margaret replied, smiling a little.

“I’m better in not being torn to pieces by coughing o’ nights; but I’m weary and tired o’ Milton, and longing to get away to th’ land o’ Beulah; and when I think I’m farther and farther off, my heart sinks, and I’m no better—I’m worse.”

Margaret turned and walked beside her. For a minute or two she could find no words that were not too small. At last she spoke low, as if afraid the air might carry it to some listening ear.

“Bessy—do you wish to die?”

Bessy was silent, then answered in a voice that had the thin steadiness of someone who has lived too long with pain to be startled by its name.

“If yo’d led the life I have, and getten as weary on it as I have, and thought at times ‘maybe it’ll last for fifty or sixty years—it does wi’ some,’—and got dizzy and dazed, and sick, as each o’ them years seemed to spin about me and mock me wi’ its length o’ hours and minutes, and endless bits o’ time—oh, wench! I tell thee thou’d been glad enough when th’ doctor said he feared thou’d never see another winter.”

“Why, Bessy—what kind of life has yours been?”

“Nought worse than many others’, I reckon. Only I fretted again it, and they didn’t.”

“But what was it? You know—I’m a stranger here. Perhaps I don’t catch your meaning quick, as I might if I’d lived all my life in Milton.”

“If yo’d come to our house when yo’ said yo’ would, I could maybe ha’ told you. But father says yo’re just like th’ rest on ’em—out o’ sight out o’ mind wi’ you.”

“I don’t know who ‘the rest’ are,” Margaret said quietly. “And I’ve been very busy; and—to tell the truth—I had forgotten my promise.”

“Yo’ offered it; we asked none on it.”

“I had forgotten what I said for the time,” Margaret went on, without sharpness. “I should have thought of it again when I was less busy. May I go with you now?”

Bessy threw her a quick look, searching for pity that was not patronage. The keen glance softened into something wistful when it met Margaret’s steady kindness.

“I ha’ none so many to care for me; if yo’ care, yo’ may come.”

So they walked on together. Margaret heard, with a new attention, the sounds of the streets as if the town’s harsh music were part of Bessy’s illness: the slam of doors, the quarrelling voices, the clatter that never seemed to rest. When they turned into a small court off a squalid street, the noise altered—narrowed and echoed, as if every shout and every step struck back from the walls twice over.

“Yo’ll not be daunted if father’s at home,” Bessy said, breath failing a little. “He speaks a bit gruffish at first. He took a mind to ye, yo’ see, and he thought a deal o’ your coming; and just because he liked yo’ he were vexed and put about.”

“Don’t fear, Bessy.”

But Nicholas Higgins was not at home when they entered. A great slatternly girl—not so old as Bessy, but taller, stronger—was at a wash-tub, knocking about the furniture with rough capability, making so much noise that Margaret shrank, not from the girl, but from the assault on Bessy’s weary senses. The room smelt of soap-suds and damp clothes, of stale smoke embedded in walls that had never known clean air.

Margaret asked for a cup of water. While the sister went, tumbling over a chair and sending the fire-irons clattering, Margaret loosened Bessy’s bonnet-strings to ease her panting breath.

“Do you think such life as this is worth caring for?” Bessy gasped at last.

Margaret did not answer. She held the water to Bessy’s lips. Bessy drank long and feverishly, then fell back and shut her eyes. In the hush between one crash and the next, Margaret heard her murmur, as if repeating a taste she longed for but had never known in her mouth:

“They shall hunger no more, neither thirst any more; neither shall the sun light on them, nor any heat.”

Margaret bent over her. “Bessy, don’t be impatient with your life—whatever it is, or has been. Remember who gave it you, and made it what it is.”

She started; Nicholas Higgins’s voice came from behind. He had entered so quietly, in spite of his size, that she had not heard him—an odd mercy in such a noisy place, as if he carried his own silence.

“Now, I’ll not have my wench preached to,” he said. “She’s bad enough as it is, wi’ her dreams and her methodee fancies, and visions o’ cities wi’ goulden gates and precious stones. If it amuses her, I let it abe; but I’m none going to have more stuff poured into her.”

“But surely,” Margaret said, turning and meeting his rough gaze steadily, “you believe in what I said—that God gave her life, and ordered what kind of life it was to be?”

“I believe what I see, and no more,” he answered, blunt as a hammer. “That’s what I believe, young woman. I don’t believe all I hear—no! not by a big deal. I did hear a young lass make an ado about knowing where we lived, and coming to see us. And my wench here thought a deal about it—flushed up many a time, when hoo little knew I were looking at her, at sound o’ a strange step. But hoo’s come at last, and hoo’s welcome—so long as hoo’ll keep from preaching on what hoo knows nought about.”

Bessy had been watching Margaret’s face with anxious eyes; now she half raised herself and laid a thin hand on Margaret’s arm, pleading.

“Don’t be vexed wi’ him—there’s many a one thinks like him: many and many a one here. If yo’ could hear them speak, yo’d not be shocked at him. He’s a rare good man, is father—but oh!” She fell back with a despairing little motion. “What he says at times makes me long to die more than ever, for I want to know so many things, and am so tossed about wi’ wonder.”

“Poor wench—poor old wench,” Nicholas said, his harshness breaking into a rough sorrow. “I’m loth to vex thee, I am; but a man mun speak out for th’ truth. When I see th’ world going all wrong at this time o’ day—bothering itself wi’ things it knows nought about, and leaving undone all th’ things that lie in disorder close at its hand—why, I say, leave a’ this talk about religion alone, and set to work on what yo’ see and know. That’s my creed. It’s simple, and not far to fetch, nor hard to work.”

But Bessy only clung the more to Margaret’s sleeve, as if she feared the door would close and shut hope out with it.

“Don’t think hardly on him—he’s a good man, he is. I sometimes think I shall be moped wi’ sorrow even in th’ City o’ God, if father is not there.”

The feverish colour flamed into her cheek, into her eye. “But you will be there, father! you shall! Oh! my heart!”

She put her hand to her breast and turned ghastly pale. Margaret gathered her at once, strong young arms making a shelter where the world had made none. She laid the weary head on her bosom, lifted the thin soft hair from the temples, and bathed them with water. Nicholas, helpless in speech, understood every look and gesture with the quickness of love; even the round-eyed sister moved with clumsy gentleness at Margaret’s urgent “Hush!”

Presently the spasm that seemed to foreshadow death passed, leaving Bessy exhausted, like a flame that has dropped low for want of air. She roused herself and whispered:

“I’ll go to bed—it’s best place; but”—and she caught at Margaret’s gown with a child’s desperate insistence—“yo’ll come again. I know yo’ will—but just say it.”

“I will come again to-morrow,” Margaret said.

Bessy leaned back against her father, who prepared to carry her upstairs as if she were light as a bundle of clothes. As Margaret rose to go, Nicholas struggled with words that did not belong to his usual creed, but forced themselves up through the cracks of his grief.

“I could wish there were a God,” he said hoarsely, “if it were only to ask Him to bless thee.”

Margaret went away sad and thoughtful, and the street’s harsh din seemed to strike her more sharply after the close misery of that room. She was late for tea at home. In Helstone unpunctuality at meals had been a fault that roused her mother’s quick irritability; now, as with so many little things, the power of complaint appeared to have died away. Margaret almost longed for the old reproof, as one longs for a familiar bird-song in a place where the only music is machinery.

“Have you met with a servant, dear?” Mrs. Hale asked, her voice mild from weariness.

“No, mamma. That Anne Buckley would never have done.”

“Suppose I try,” Mr. Hale said. “Everybody else has had their turn at this great difficulty. Now let me try. I may be the Cinderella to put on the slipper after all.”

Margaret could scarcely smile; the image of Bessy’s pinched face, of that court’s echoing clamour, lay upon her too heavily.

“What would you do, papa? How would you set about it?”

“Why, I should apply to some good house-mother to recommend me one known to herself or her servants.”

“Very good. But we must first catch our house-mother.”

“You have caught her,” Mr. Hale answered, with a quiet twinkle. “Or rather she is coming into the snare, and you will catch her to-morrow, if you’re skilful.”

“What do you mean, Mr. Hale?” Mrs. Hale asked, roused by curiosity as if it were a draught of wine.

“Why—my paragon pupil (as Margaret calls him) has told me that his mother intends to call on Mrs. and Miss Hale to-morrow.”

“Mrs. Thornton!” Mrs. Hale exclaimed.

“The mother of whom he spoke to us?” Margaret said.

“Mrs. Thornton; the only mother he has, I believe,” Mr. Hale replied quietly.

“I shall like to see her,” Mrs. Hale said. “She must be an uncommon person. Perhaps she may have a relation who might suit us, and be glad of our place. She sounded such a careful economical person that I should like any one out of the same family.”

“My dear,” Mr. Hale said, with sudden alarm, “pray do not go off upon that idea. I fancy Mrs. Thornton is as haughty and proud in her way as our little Margaret here is in hers, and that she completely ignores that old time of trial and poverty and economy of which he speaks so openly. I am sure, at any rate, she would not like strangers to know anything about it.”

“Take notice that is not my kind of haughtiness, papa, if I have any at all—which I do not agree to, though you are always accusing me.”

“I don’t know positively that it is hers either,” he answered, “but from little things I have gathered, I fancy so.”

They cared too little to ask in what manner the son had spoken of the mother. Margaret’s mind was on the morrow for another reason: if Mrs. Thornton called, she must stay to receive her, and that would keep her from Bessy until late; yet her mother must not be left to bear alone the weight of entertaining a stranger. Outside, Milton went on sounding—wheel, step, distant throb—while within, in the quiet room, Margaret measured her duties as one measures ingredients, knowing that the smallest imbalance might ruin what little strength remained at the table.
