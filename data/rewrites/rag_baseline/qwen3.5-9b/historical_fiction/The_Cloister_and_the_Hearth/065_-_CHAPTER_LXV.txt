Two days after the turmoil I have sought to depict, Gerard wandered through one of Rome's most squalid thoroughfares, where the air itself seemed thick with the city's moral rot. A thunderstorm broke overhead, forcing him into a low, shadowed hostelry that smelled of stale beer and desperation. He ordered wine, and as the deluge continued outside, the alcohol quickly clouded his judgment, lulling him into a stupor. He dozed, his head resting heavily upon his hands, which were folded upon the scarred wooden table.

Time blurred until the room grew crowded with roughshod strangers, their raucous laughter and coarse speech tearing through the haze of his intoxication. It was then that his consciousness sharpened, drawn to the hushed, conspiratorial murmur of two figures seated nearby. One was a pardoner, a man whose very presence was a license to sin. Beside him sat another, dressed in garments that appeared clean yet modest enough to pass for a respectable tradesman. Yet, the manner in which he had slumped his wide-brimmed hat over his brow, obscuring every feature save for a bristling beard, betrayed his nature; he was a man who actively shunned the gaze of honest citizens and the scrutiny of the law.

Together, they were negotiating a transaction in the black market of salvation. Under the celestial contracts of that era, the crime to be absolved had not yet been committed—a loophole allowing the purchase of forgiveness before the act. The man with the slouched hat fretted over the escalating cost of these spiritual commodities. "If prices rise any further," he grumbled, "we poor souls shall be locked out of heaven entirely."

The pardoner dismissed his anxiety with a flat denial. "Indulgences have never been more affordable for prudent stewards of their souls."

"Who were they?" the buyer pressed, seeking clarity on the beneficiaries of such cheap grace.

"Why, those who sin according to the market logic of reasonable creatures," the pardoner replied, his tone dripping with condescension. "But should you be so perverse as to select a transgression the Pope has explicitly condemned, look to your own conscience, not mine!"

To demonstrate that even grave sins were within reach of all but the absolute dregs of society, the pardoner unrolled a parchment and recited the price list. It was a grotesque catalog of human misery, a document too vile for print here, yet preserved in the great libraries of history. Suffice it to say, the cost to extinguish a layman's life was remarkably lower than many offenses my readers might consider trivial. This revelation sealed the deal; with minor concessions from both sides, the transaction concluded. Money changed hands, and the aspiring soul was already absolved for the future murder of a commoner. The price for eliminating a cleric stood in stark, horrifying contrast. Not once did the word *assassination* leave either man's lips, sanitized by the euphemisms of commerce.

All this cacophony of vice drifted past Gerard's ears, but he remained buried beneath the table, his head unmoving, listening with a vacant, drunken stare. Only when the conspirators rose and dispersed did he lift his chin slightly, fixing a scowl upon the retreating silhouette of the purchaser.

"If Margaret lived," he muttered, the venom rising despite his foggy mind, "I would seize you by the throat and strangle you, you cowardly stabber. But she is dead. Dead. Dead. Let the whole world perish; it matters nothing to me. So let me die among the first."

When he finally stumbled home, a man wearing a slouched hat paced briskly back and forth on the opposite side of the street. "There goes that cur again," Gerard thought, his eyes narrowing. Yet, in his shattered mental state, the sight failed to register, leaving no mark upon his tormented soul.
