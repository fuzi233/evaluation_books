The eighteen months that followed—though shorter than anticipated—were perhaps the most tenderly joyful my father and mother had ever known. Not "happy" in the careless sense, but rich with a quiet, deepening love, as though Death had whispered, *Not yet. A little longer together.*

In those final days, pretense fell away. Petty concerns—keeping up appearances, fretting over neighbors’ opinions—vanished. What remained was pure and simple: their love for one another, their comfort in each other’s presence. We were poor, yes, but no longer straining under the weight of false pride.

Their hearts grew larger, their kindness wider. Even Aunt Fan, that bastion of prickliness, made a second attempt at amiability—an experiment she announced with characteristic bluntness.

"I'm a beast of an old woman," she declared one afternoon.

"Don’t say that, Fan," my mother protested.

"Why not? It’s true," Aunt Fan snapped. "People think I’m disagreeable."

"It’s your manner," my mother explained gently. "You don’t *mean* to be."

"Stuff and nonsense. I *like* being disagreeable. Watching people squirm—now *that’s* entertainment."

Yet, to everyone’s astonishment, Aunt Fan persisted in her newfound pleasantness—though her efforts often landed closer to sarcasm than sincerity.

"Your father’s stories!" she’d exclaim. "What wit! Such effortless humor!"

Or to my mother, admiring a retrimmed bonnet: "A *girl* in that! No, a saucy young thing—not a married lady with a son nearly grown!"

Her enthusiasm was so relentless it bordered on parody.

Meanwhile, a new figure entered our lives: Mr. James Wellington Gadley (or rather, Wellington James Gadley, though he’d long since reversed the order to spare himself Napoleonic comparisons). A portly, earnest solicitor’s clerk, he carried himself with the gravity of a man who’d witnessed the clandestine dramas of England’s elite—though he spoke only in veiled allusions to "a certain Lady X."

Aunt Fan, to everyone’s surprise, adored his stories. Where others nodded politely, she interjected with dark theories of hidden motives.

"Why did the husband return after eighteen years?" mused Gadley.

"To murder his wife—slow poison," Aunt Fan declared.

"Murder her? But why?"

"To marry the other woman, of course."

Gadley blinked. "How extraordinary! Though, in fact, the wife *did* die shortly after, and he *did* remarry..."

"Told you," said Aunt Fan.

Their debates became legendary. Gadley, enthralled by her macabre imagination, would lean closer—until Aunt Fan, wielding her darning needles like a knight’s lance, kept him at a safe distance.

Meanwhile, Amy, our cheerfully pragmatic maid, breezed through engagements with the ease of one changing gloves.

"Married in the spring? Oh, *that* one!" she’d say, waving away suitors like yesterday’s news. "Changed my mind. We can’t help our feelings, can we?"

And Barbara—bright, laughing Barbara—brought lightness where there might have been shadow. She coaxed my father into investments with the same ease she coaxed him into theater seats.

"Argentinos are rising!" she’d say.

"Are they?" my father would murmur. "I had a feeling about Argentinos..."

And so, on a close summer evening, we found ourselves waiting for the omnibus—my father, my mother, and I—bound for *A Voice from the Grave, or The Power of Love* (a drama in five acts and thirteen tableaux).

"Let’s ride on top," my father said, grinning.

My mother hesitated only a moment. "Very well. If we’re to be wicked, we may as well do it properly."

And with that, we climbed aboard—ready, for once, to let joy lead the way.
