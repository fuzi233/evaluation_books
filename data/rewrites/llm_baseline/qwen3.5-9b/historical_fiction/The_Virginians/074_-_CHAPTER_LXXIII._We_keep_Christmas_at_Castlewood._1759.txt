It is a familiar trope from our favorite fairy tales that at every christening and wedding, someone inevitably feels slighted and vows vengeance. Thus, we need not marvel that Cousin Will cursed and raged with such energy upon hearing of his brother's engagement to the colonial heiress. Initially, Will fled the house in a fury, swearing he would never return. Yet, neither his family nor the swearer himself took his oaths seriously. This unrepentant prodigal returned after a day or two, unable to resist the allure of the marriage feast. He could not bear to resign his knife and fork at the Castlewood table. He came back to drink and eat in token of revenge, pledging the young bride in a bumper while muttering prayers for her perdition beneath his breath. During the chapel ceremony, as her father gave her away and my lord vowed to love, honor, and cherish her, Will offered responses of smothered maledictions.

Will was hardly the only grumbler regarding the union. Mr. Warrington heard no end of abuse directed at my lady and her grandfather from various quarters. The old gentleman's city friends, his legal adviser, the Dissenting clergyman who had welcomed them to England, and even poor Jack Lambert—the orthodox young divine whose eloquence Mr. Warrington had hoped would sway her privately—were all bitter against the "little lady's" treachery. Each had a story of being enslaved, encouraged, and ultimately jilted by the American. The lawyer, who possessed an exhaustive inventory of her properties, estates, money, slaves, and ships, was ready to swear that her entire account was a fabrication; that places like New York and Virginia did not exist, or at least that Mr. Van den Bosch held no lands there. He claimed there was no such thing as the Guinea trade, suggesting the negroes were merely "black falsehoods" invented by a wily old planter. The Dissenting pastor mourned over his "stray lamb"—or rather, lamented that such a wily, mischievous monster could even be called a lamb. Poor Jack Lambert ruefully confessed to his mother that he possessed a lock of black hair, which he bedewed with tears and addressed in quite unclerical terms. As for Mr. William Esmond, he indulged in shrieks and curses even under his sister-in-law's very nose, calling upon Acheron to witness that no such artful devil existed outside the region of Miss Lydia. He swore she was an infernal female Cerberus and invoked all the wrath of heaven and hell upon his swindling brother, who had cajoled him with fair words and filched his prize.

"Why," Mr. Warrington asked when Will expatiated on these grievances, "if the girl is such a she-devil as you describe, you are all the better for losing her. If she intends to deceive her husband and poison him, as you claim, how fortunate for you that you are not the victim! You ought to thank the gods, Will, rather than curse them, for robbing you of such a fury. There can be no greater revenge on Castlewood than allowing him her sole possession."

"All this was very well," Will Esmond replied, yet he justifiably noted that his brother was no less a scoundrel for cheating him out of the fortune he had risked his life to win.

George Warrington was perplexed as to how his cousin had endangered his precious existence—a fate for which a rope's end might have been a fitting conclusion—and Will, with utmost candor, explained how the "little Cerbera" had orchestrated their meeting, only for it to be interrupted by Sir John Fielding's men. He recounted how Lydia constantly accused George of cowardice for sneering at Will, and vice versa, leading George to flee and nearly die of fright during Braddock's expedition. "Deuce take me," Will exclaimed, "I was never more surprised than when you stood so coolly in Tottenham Court Fields, offering to wager that you would never come!"

Mr. Warrington laughed, thanking Will for this assessment. "Though," he added, "it was lucky the constables arrived, or you would have whittled your sword through my body another minute. Did you not see how clumsy I stood before you? You actually turned white and shook with anger!"

"Yes, curse me," Will retorted, turning red, "that is my way of showing rage. I was confoundedly angry with you, cousin! But now it is my brother I hate, and that little devil of a Countess—a countess! A pretty countess, indeed!" With another rumbling volley of oaths, he saluted the reigning member of his family.

"Well, cousin," George said, staring him oddly, "you let me off easily. I dare say I owe my life to you, or at least a whole waistcoat. I admire your forbearance and spirit. What a pity such courage is wasted as a mere court usher! You are a loss to His Majesty's army, positively!"

"I never know if you jest or are serious, Mr. Warrington," Will growled.

"Few gentlemen would dare joke with you, cousin, if they valued their lives or ears!" cried Mr. Warrington, who loved this grave method of dealing with his noble kinsman, watching with droll interest as Will choked back his curses, ground his teeth in fear of biting, and smothered his cowardly anger.

"You should moderate your expressions regarding the dear Countess and my lord your brother," Warrington continued. "Of you, they speak most tenderly. Her ladyship has told me everything."

"What everything?" cried Will, aghast.

"As much as women ever do tell, cousin. She admitted she thought you had been a little *épris* with her. What woman can help liking a man who admires her?"

"She hates you, and says you were wild about her, Mr. Warrington!" protested Mr. Esmond.

"Spretae injuria formae, cousin!" Warrington quoted.

"For me? What's for me?" asked the other.

"I never cared for her, and hence, perhaps, she does not love me. Do you not recall the case of the wife of the Captain of the Guard?"

"Which guard?" asked Will.

"My Lord Potiphar," said Warrington.

"Lord Who? My Lord Falmouth is Captain of the Yeomen of the Guard, and my Lord Berkeley of the Pensioners. My Lord Hobart had them before. Perhaps you haven't been in England long enough to know who is who, cousin!" remarked William.

Warrington clarified that he meant the Captain of the Guard for the King of Egypt, whose wife had persecuted Joseph for not returning her affection. Will countered that since Egypt was a confounded long way off, and if Lord Whatever-it-was's wife lied about him, it was simply the nature of his sex, whom he supposed were identical everywhere.

In truth, when paying his marriage visit to Castlewood, Mr. Warrington had heard the little Countess's version of the dispute between Will Esmond and herself. While differing in some respects from Will's ingenuous narrative, it was far from more authentic. The lady expressed grief at being deceived by her brother-in-law. She feared his life at court and town had eroded the high principles inherent to the Esmonds. She suggested Will's words were unreliable, that a loose life and financial troubles had made him mercenary, blunting his honor and impairing the high chivalrous courage "which we Esmonds... must always possess." She tossed her head proudly, asserting that she, her lord, and her cousin Harry possessed this quality.

"Oh, cousin George! Must I confess that I was led to doubt yours?" she continued. "Without which, a man of ancient and noble family like ours isn't worthy to be called a man! I shall try, George, as a Christian lady and the head of one of the first families in this kingdom and the whole world, to forgive my brother William for speaking ill of a member of our family, though a younger branch and by the female side, and making me for a moment doubt you. He did so. Perhaps he told me countless bad things you had said of me."

"I, my dear lady!" cried Mr. Warrington.

"Which he said you said of me, cousin, and I hope you didn't, and heartily pray you didn't; and I can afford to despise them. And he paid me his court, that's a fact; and so have others, and that I'm used to; and he might have prospered better than he did perhaps—for I did not know my dear lord, nor come to value his great and eminent qualities, as I do out of the fulness of this grateful heart now!—but, oh! I found William deficient in courage. No man lacking that can ever have the esteem of Lydia, Countess of Castlewood! He said 'twas you who lacked spirit, cousin, and angered me by telling me you were always abusing me. But I forgive you, George, that I do! And when I tell you that it was he who was afraid—the mean skunk!—and actually summoned the constables to prevent the match between you and him, you won't wonder why I wouldn't value a fellow like that—not that much!" snapping her little fingers.
