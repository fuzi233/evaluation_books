"Betteredge!" I called out, gesturing to the familiar volume resting on his knees. "Has *Robinson Crusoe* informed you this evening that I might expect to see Franklin Blake?"

"By the Lord Harry, Mr. Franklin!" the old man cried, his voice trembling with delight. "That is precisely what *Robinson Crusoe* has done!"

With my assistance, he scrambled to his feet, standing for a moment to gaze back and forth between the book and me, seemingly unable to decide which of us had surprised him more. The verdict ultimately favored the book. Clutching it open in both hands, he stared at the pages with a look of unutterable anticipation, as if expecting Robinson Crusoe himself to stride out of the text to offer us a personal audience.

"Here is the passage, Mr. Franklin!" he exclaimed once he had regained his composure. "As I live by bread, sir, this is exactly what I was reading the moment before you arrived! Page one hundred and fifty-six reads: 'I stood like one Thunderstruck, or as if I had seen an Apparition.' If that does not mean 'Expect the sudden appearance of Mr. Franklin Blake,' then the English language holds no meaning!" Betteredge slammed the book shut, finally freeing one hand to grasp mine warmly.

I had naturally expected him to bombard me with questions. Yet, the hospitable impulse dominated the old servant's mind whenever a family member appeared, however unexpectedly, as a visitor.

"Walk in, Mr. Franklin," he said, bowing with his quaint, old-fashioned grace and opening the door behind him. "I shall inquire what brings you here afterward—I must first make you comfortable. Sad changes have occurred since you departed. The house is shuttered, and the servants have gone. Never mind that! I shall cook your dinner; the gardener's wife will prepare your bed; and if a bottle of our renowned Latour claret remains in the cellar, Mr. Franklin, it shall be poured down your throat. I bid you welcome, sir! I bid you a hearty welcome!" said the poor old fellow, battling bravely against the gloom of the deserted estate, receiving me with the sociable courtesy of a bygone era.

It pained me to disappoint him. Yet, this was Rachel's house now. Could I eat within its walls, or sleep upon its floors, after the events that unfolded in London? The most basic sense of self-respect—forbade me properly, strictly forbade me—to cross the threshold.

Taking Betteredge by the arm, I led him out into the garden. There was no alternative. I was compelled to tell him the truth. Caught between his devotion to Rachel and his affection for me, he was deeply perplexed and distressed by the turn of events. When he voiced his opinion, it came in his characteristic blunt manner, seasoned with the most positive philosophy I had ever encountered—the philosophy of the Betteredge school.

"Miss Rachel has her faults—I have never denied it," he began. "And riding the high horse, now and then, is one of them. She has been attempting to ride over *you*—and you have endured it. Lord, Mr. Franklin, do you not know women by this time better than that? You have heard me speak of the late Mrs. Betteredge?"

I had heard him speak of the late Mrs. Betteredge quite often—always presenting her as his undeniable proof of the innate frailty and perversity of the fair sex. In that role, he exhibited her now.

"Very well, Mr. Franklin. Listen to me. Different women ride the high horse in different ways. The late Mrs. Betteredge took her exercise on that favorite female animal whenever I happened to deny her something she had set her heart upon. Just as surely as I returned home from work on such occasions, just as surely did my wife call up the kitchen stairs to declare that, following my brutal treatment, she lacked the heart to cook my dinner. I endured it for some time—just as you are enduring it now from Miss Rachel. Finally, my patience wore thin. I descended the stairs, and I took Mrs. Betteredge—affectionately, you understand—into my arms and carried her, holus-bolus, into the best parlour where she received her guests. I declared, 'That is the proper place for you, my dear,' and returned to the kitchen. I locked myself in, removed my coat, rolled up my shirt-sleeves, and cooked my own dinner. When it was ready, I served it with my best manner and enjoyed it heartily. I smoked my pipe and had a drop of grog afterward; then I cleared the table, washed the crockery, scrubbed the knives and forks, put everything away, and swept the hearth. When everything was as bright and clean as possible, I opened the door and let Mrs. Betteredge in. 'I have had my dinner, my dear,' I said; 'and I hope you will find the kitchen left exactly as your fondest wishes desire.' For the remainder of that woman's life, Mr. Franklin, I never had to cook my dinner again! Moral: You have endured Miss Rachel in London; do not endure her in Yorkshire. Return to the house!"

Quite unanswerable! I could only assure my good friend that even *his* powers of persuasion were, in this instance, wasted on me.

"It is a lovely evening," I said. "I shall walk to Frizinghall, stay at the hotel, and you must come tomorrow morning to breakfast with me. I have something to say to you."

Betteredge shook his head gravely.

"I am heartily sorry for this," he said. "I had hoped, Mr. Franklin, to hear that matters were smooth and pleasant again between you and Miss Rachel. If you must have your own way, sir," he continued after a moment's reflection, "there is no need to travel to Frizinghall tonight for a bed. It can be found nearer than that. There is Hotherstone's Farm, barely two miles from here. You could hardly object to *that* on Miss Rachel's account," the old man added slyly. "Hotherstone lives, Mr. Franklin, on his own freehold."

The place flashed into my memory the moment Betteredge mentioned it. The farmhouse stood in a sheltered inland valley, on the banks of the prettiest stream in that part of Yorkshire; the farmer possessed a spare bedroom and parlour, which he was accustomed to rent to artists, anglers, and tourists alike. A more agreeable place of abode during my stay in the neighborhood, I could not have wished to find.

"Are the rooms available?" I inquired.

"Mrs. Hotherstone herself, sir, asked for my good word to recommend the rooms yesterday."

"I shall take them, Betteredge, with the greatest pleasure."

We returned to the yard where I had left my traveling bag. After threading a stick through the handle and swinging the bag over his shoulder, Betteredge appeared to relapse into the bewilderment my sudden arrival had caused when I surprised him in the beehive chair. He gazed incredulously at the house, then wheeled about to look even more incredulously at me.

"I have lived a goodish long time in the world," said this best and dearest of all old servants, "but the like of this, I never expected to witness. There stands the house, and here stands Mr. Franklin Blake—and, Damme, if one of them is not turning his back on the other to go sleep in a lodging!"

He led the way out, wagging his head and growling ominously. "There is only one more miracle that *can* happen," he said over his shoulder. "The next thing you will do, Mr. Franklin, will be to repay me that seven-and-sixpence you borrowed when you were a boy."

This stroke of sarcasm lifted his spirits toward himself and toward me. We left the house and passed through the lodge gates. Once clear of the grounds, the duties of hospitality (in Betteredge's moral code) ceased, and the privileges of curiosity began.

He fell back to walk beside me. "A fine evening for a stroll, Mr. Franklin," he said, as if we had merely encountered each other by chance at that moment. "Supposing you had gone to the hotel at Frizinghall, sir?"

"Yes?"

"I should have had the honor of breakfasting with you tomorrow morning."

"Come and breakfast with me at Hotherstone's Farm instead."

"Much obliged for your kindness, Mr. Franklin. But it was not exactly breakfast I had in mind. I believe you mentioned having something to say to me? If it is no secret, sir," Betteredge said, suddenly abandoning the crooked path for the straight one, "I am burning to know what brought you down here in such a sudden manner."

"What brought me here before?" I asked.

"The Moonstone, Mr. Franklin. But what brings you now, sir?"

"The Moonstone again, Betteredge."

The old man stopped abruptly, looking at me in the grey twilight as if he suspected his own ears were deceiving him.

"If that is a joke, sir," he said, "I fear I am becoming somewhat dull in my old age. I do not take it."

"It is no joke," I answered. "I have returned to resume the inquiry I abandoned when I left England. I have come here to do what no one has yet done—to discover who took the Diamond."

"Let the Diamond be, Mr. Franklin! Take my advice and let the Diamond be! That cursed Indian jewel has misled everyone who has approached it. Do not waste your money and your temper—in the prime of your life, sir—by meddling with the Moonstone. How can *you* possibly succeed (saving your presence), when Sergeant Cuff himself made a mess of it? Sergeant Cuff!" Betteredge repeated, shaking his forefinger at me sternly. "The greatest policeman in England!"

"My mind is made up, my old friend. Even Sergeant Cuff does not daunt me. By the way, I may wish to speak to him sooner or later. Have you heard anything of him recently?"

"The Sergeant will not assist you, Mr. Franklin."

"Why not?"

"There has been an event, sir, in police circles since you departed. The great Cuff has retired from business. He has acquired a small cottage at Dorking and is buried up to his eyes in the cultivation of roses. I have it in his own handwriting, Mr. Franklin. He has grown the white moss rose without budding it onto the dog-rose first. And Mr. Begbie the gardener is to go to Dorking to acknowledge that the Sergeant has finally bested him."

"It matters little," I said. "I must proceed without Sergeant Cuff's aid. And I must rely on you to begin."

It is likely I spoke somewhat carelessly.

At any rate, Betteredge seemed piqued by something in my reply. "You might trust to worse than me, Mr. Franklin—I can tell you that," he said sharply.

The tone of his retort, coupled with a certain disturbance I detected in his manner, suggested to me that he possessed information he hesitated to disclose.

"I expect you to help me," I said, "in gathering the fragments of evidence Sergeant Cuff has left behind. I know you can do that. Can you do no more?"

"What more can you expect from me, sir?" Betteredge asked with an appearance of utmost humility.

"I expect more—from what you said just now."

"Mere boasting, Mr. Franklin," the old man returned obstinately. "Some people are born boasters and never overcome it to their dying day. I am one of them."

There was only one approach to take with him. I appealed to his interest in Rachel and his affection for me.

"Betteredge, would you be glad to hear that Rachel and I are friends again?"

"I have served your family, sir, to mighty little purpose if you doubt it!"

"Do you remember how Rachel treated me before I left England?"

"As well as if it were yesterday! My lady herself wrote you a letter regarding it; and you were so kind as to show it to me. It stated that Miss Rachel was mortally offended with you for the part you played in attempting to recover her jewel. Neither my lady, nor you, nor anyone else could guess why.

"Quite true, Betteredge! And I return from my travels to find her still mortally offended with me. I knew last year that the Diamond lay at the bottom of it, and I know now that the Diamond lies at the bottom of it. I have tried to speak to her, and she will not see me. I have tried to write to her, and she will not answer me. How, in Heaven's name, am I to clear this matter up? The chance of investigating the loss of the Moonstone is the only chance of inquiry that Rachel herself has left me."

Those words evidently presented the case to him as he had not seen it before. He asked a question that satisfied me I had shaken him.

"There is no ill-feeling on your side in this, Mr. Franklin—is there?"

"There was some anger," I answered, "when I left London. But that is entirely worn out now. I wish to bring Rachel to an understanding with me—and I desire nothing more."

"You feel no fear, sir—supposing you make discoveries—in regard to what you may uncover about Miss Rachel?"

I understood the jealous belief in his young mistress that prompted those words.

"I am as certain of her as you are," I answered. "The fullest disclosure of her secret will reveal nothing that can alter her place in your estimation, or in mine."

Betteredge's last lingering scruples vanished instantly.

"If I am doing wrong to help you, Mr. Franklin," he exclaimed, "all I can say is—I am as innocent of seeing it as the babe unborn! I can set you on the road to discovery if you can only proceed alone. You remember that poor girl of ours—Rosanna Spearman?"

"Of course!"

"You always believed she held some sort of confession regarding this matter of the Moonstone, which she wished to make to you?"

"I certainly could not account for her strange conduct in any other way."

"You may set that doubt at rest, Mr. Franklin, whenever you please."
