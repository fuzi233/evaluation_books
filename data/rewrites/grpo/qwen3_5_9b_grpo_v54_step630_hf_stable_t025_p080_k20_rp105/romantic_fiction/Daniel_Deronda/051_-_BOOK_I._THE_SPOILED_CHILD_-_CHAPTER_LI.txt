The spindle spun, a rhythmic counterpoint to the heavy silence of Errina's chamber. With her thick-coiled raven hair and eyes the color of deep agate, she gazed at the surging visions of her destiny, destined to spin byssus in insect-like labor while gods and men wrought deeds worthy of song. But in the quiet of the *Italia*, the spindle was still, and the only movement was the approach of Daniel Deronda.

When Deronda stepped into the antechamber, the two servants exchanged glances, surprised that the doctor his lady had summoned was such a striking young gentleman, whose evening attire seemed adorned rather than burdened by its severity. Yet Deronda paid them no mind until the inner door swung open, revealing a figure waiting at the far end of the vast room.

She was draped in black lace that hung loosely from the summit of her whitening hair to a long train trailing behind her tall form. Her arms, bare to the elbow save for rich bracelets, were folded before her, and the fine poise of her head made her appear handsomer than her weary reality warranted. Deronda felt no pause for observation; he was already close, holding the hand she offered and raising it to his lips. She kept her hand in his, studying him with an examining gaze, while his own consciousness was seized by the piercing quality of her eyes and the mobile nature of her face, which seemed capable of shifting into a different person in the next breath. Even as she studied him, a play of her brow and nostrils formed a tacit language. Deronda dared not move, unable to conceive what manifestation her feeling demanded. He felt his cheeks flushing like a girl's, yet wondered at his own lack of profound emotion; he had lived through so many idealized meetings with his mother in his mind, and they had seemed more real than this stark encounter. He could not conjecture the language she would use. Suddenly, she released his hand, placing both palms on his shoulders. In that moment, her face flashed with admiration, every worn line vanishing to reveal a restored youth.

"You are a beautiful creature!" she said in a low, melodious voice, her syllables possessing a foreign yet agreeable outline. "I knew you would be." She kissed him on each cheek, and he returned the kisses. It was a greeting between royalties.

She paused as the lines of age returned to her face, then spoke in a colder tone. "I am your mother. But you can have no love for me."

"I have thought of you more than of any other being in the world," Deronda replied, his voice trembling.

"I am not like what you thought I was," the Princess said decisively, withdrawing her hands and folding her arms again, inviting him to observe her. He had often pictured her face in his imagination as bearing a likeness to his own; he saw some of it now, but amidst striking differences. She was a remarkable being. What gave her son a painful sense of aloofness? Her worn beauty possessed a strangeness, as if she were not quite a human mother, but a Melusina bound to a world independent of theirs.

"I used to think that you might be suffering," Deronda said, anxious not to wound her. "I wished to be a comfort."

"I _am_ suffering. But with a suffering you cannot comfort," the Princess said, her voice hardening as she moved to a sofa where cushions were carefully arranged. "Sit down." She pointed to a seat near her, then, seeing distress in his face, softened her tone. "I am not suffering at this moment. I am at ease. I am able to talk."

Deronda seated himself, waiting. It felt as though he stood before a mysterious Fate rather than the longed-for mother. He watched her with wonder, separated by a spiritual distance she had erected.

"No," she began. "I did not send for you to comfort me. I could not know beforehand what you would feel. I have no foolish notion that you can love me merely because I am your mother, when you have never seen or heard of me. But I thought I chose something better for you than being with me. I did not think I deprived you of anything worth having."

"You cannot wish me to believe that your affection would not have been worth having," Deronda said, noting her pause.

"I do not mean to speak ill of myself," the Princess said with proud impetuosity. "But I had not much affection to give. I did not want affection. I had been stifled with it. I wanted to live out the life that was in me, unhampered by other lives. You wonder what I was. I was no princess then." She rose suddenly, and Deronda followed, feeling breathless.

"No princess in this tame life I lead now. I was a great singer, and I acted as well as I sang. All the rest were poor beside me. Men followed me from country to country. I was living a myriad lives in one. I did not want a child."

There was a passionate self-defense in her tone. She had cast precedent aside; she sought justification only in the most intense words for her experience. She seemed to fling these words against any possible reproach in her son's mind, who stood clutching his coat-collar as if keeping himself above water. What else could she tell him? She continued with the same intensity, a pale illumination in her face.

"I did not want to marry. I was forced into marrying your father—forced by my father's wishes and commands. Besides, it was my best way of getting some freedom. I could rule my husband, but not my father. I had a right to be free. I had a right to seek freedom from a bondage I hated."

She seated herself again, while a subtle movement in her eyes and closed lips suggested suppressed speech. Deronda remained standing. After a moment, she looked up with less defiance, pleading softly.

"And the bondage I hated for myself, I wanted to keep you from. What better could the most loving mother do? I relieved you from the bondage of being born a Jew."

"Then I _am_ a Jew?" Deronda burst out with deep-voiced energy, causing his mother to shrink back against her cushions. "My father was a Jew, and you are a Jewess?"

"Yes, your father was my cousin," the mother said, watching him with a change in her look, as if fearing what she might have to confront.

"I am glad of it," Deronda said impetuously, in a veiled voice of passion. He could not have imagined beforehand how he would say this, never admitting it before. He could not have dreamed it would be in impulsive opposition to his mother. He was shaken by a mixed anger, against this mother who seemed to have borne him unwillingly, made herself a stranger, and was now making herself known unwillingly.

But the mother was equally shaken by a differently mixed anger, her frame less equal to repression. The shaking was visibly physical, her eyes appearing larger in her pallid excitement.

"Why do you say you are glad? You are an English gentleman. I secured you that."

"You did not know what you secured me. How could you choose my birthright for me?" Deronda said, throwing himself sideways into his chair, leaning his arm over the back while looking away.

He was fired with an intolerance foreign to him, yet he struggled to master himself, fearing he might say something too harsh in this epochal moment. A pause fell before his mother spoke again, her voice becoming more firmly resistant in its finely varied tones.

"I chose for you what I would have chosen for myself. How could I know that you would have the spirit of my father in you? How could I know that you would love what I hated—if you really love to be a Jew?" The last words carried such bitterness that any listener might have supposed hatred had arisen between them.

But Deronda had recovered his fuller self. He recalled his sensibilities to the life that had been and was for her, whose best years were gone and who was now exerting herself to tell him of a past that was not his alone. His habitual shame at accepting events as solely his own helped him here. As he looked at his mother silently after her last words, his face regained a penetrative calm, yet it seemed to have a strangely agitating influence over her. Her eyes were fixed on him with fascination, but not with the repose of maternal delight.

"Forgive me, if I speak hastily," he said with diffident gravity. "Why have you resolved now on disclosing to me what you took care to have me brought up in ignorance of? Why—since you seem angry that I should be glad?"

"Oh—the reasons of our actions!" the Princess said with a ring of sarcastic scorn. "When you are as old as I am, it will not seem so simple a question. People talk of their motives in a cut-and-dried way. Every woman is supposed to have the same set of motives, or else to be a monster. I am not a monster, but I have not felt exactly what other women feel—or say they feel, for fear of being thought unlike others. When you reproach me in your heart for sending you away, you mean that I ought to say I felt about you as other women say they feel about their children. I did _not_ feel that. I was glad to be freed from you. But I did well for you, and I gave you your father's fortune. Do I seem now to be revoking everything? Well, there are reasons. I feel many things I cannot understand. A fatal illness has been growing in me for a year. I shall very likely not live another year. I will not deny anything I have done. I will not pretend to love where I have no love. But shadows are rising round me. Sickness makes them. If I have wronged the dead, I have but little time to do what I left undone."

The varied transitions of tone in her speech were as perfect as any accomplished actress could make. It was a piece of sincere acting; this woman's nature was one in which all feeling, especially the tragic and real, immediately became matter of conscious representation. Experience passed into drama, and she acted her own emotions. In a minor degree, this is common, but in the Princess, the acting possessed a rare perfection of physiognomy, voice, and gesture. It would not be true to say she felt less because of this double consciousness; she felt all the more, but with a difference. Each nucleus of pain or pleasure had a deep atmosphere of spiritual intoxication that both exalted and deadened. But Deronda made no such reflection. All his thoughts hung on the purport of her words; her tones and wonderful face entered his agitation without being noted. What he longed for with an awed desire was to know as much as she would tell him of the strange mental conflict under which he had been brought into the world. His compassionate nature made the controlling idea within him the suffering and confession breathing through her later words, which forbade any further question when she paused, remaining silent with her brow knit, her head turned slightly away, and her large eyes fixed as if on something incorporeal. He must wait.

She spoke again with strange abruptness, turning her eyes upon him suddenly.

"Sir Hugo has written much about you. He tells me you have a wonderful mind—you comprehend everything—you are wiser than he is with all his sixty years. You say you are glad to know that you were born a Jew. I am not going to tell you that I have changed my mind about that. Your feelings are against mine. You don't thank me for what I did. Shall you comprehend your mother, or only blame her?"

"There is not a fibre within me but makes me wish to comprehend her," Deronda said, meeting her sharp gaze solemnly. "It is a bitter reversal of my longing to think of blaming her. What I have been most trying to do for fifteen years is to have some understanding of those who differ from myself."

"Then you have become unlike your grandfather in that," the mother said, "though you are a young copy of him in your face. He never comprehended me, or if he did, he only thought of fettering me into obedience. I was to be what he called 'the Jewish woman' under pain of his curse. I was to feel everything I did not feel, and believe everything I did not believe. I was to feel awe for the bit of parchment in the _mezuza_; to dread lest a bit of butter should touch a bit of meat; to think it beautiful that men should bind the _tephillin_ on them, and women not—to adore the wisdom of such laws, however silly they might seem to me. I was to love the long prayers in the ugly synagogue, and the howling, and the gabbling, and the dreadful fasts, and the tiresome feasts, and my father's endless discoursing about our people, which was a thunder without meaning in my ears. I was to care forever about what Israel had been; and I did not care at all. I cared for the wide world, and all that I could represent in it. I hated living under the shadow of my father's strictness. Teaching, teaching for everlasting—'this you must be,' 'that you must not be'—pressed on me like a frame that got tighter and tighter as I grew. I wanted to live a large life, with freedom to do what everyone else did, and be carried along in a great current, not obliged to care. Ah!" Her tone changed to one of bitter incisiveness. "You are glad to have been born a Jew. You say so. That is because you have not been brought up as a Jew. That separateness seems sweet to you because I saved you from it."

"When you resolved on that, you meant that I should never know my origin?" Deronda said impulsively. "You have at least changed in your feeling on that point."
