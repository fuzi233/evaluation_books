Tito Melema’s audience at San Marco had been arranged in advance, and Fra Niccolò, Savonarola’s quiet secretary, led him straight up the worn spiral stone stairs into long corridors lined with narrow monk’s cells. Half-hidden on the plaster walls, Fra Angelico’s frescoes glowed soft and fragile as rainbow light on melting cloud: sudden, ethereal flashes of a world beyond the monastery, where the Madonna sat crowned in radiant light, and the Divine Infant gazed out with the quiet promise of eternity.

It was the community’s hour of rest, and most cells stood empty. Thin gold light filtering through the slitted windows fell on nothing but bare whitewashed walls, hard straw pallets, and a small wooden crucifix nailed above each bed. At the far end of the longest corridor, behind the door of the inner private cell that opened off the antechamber where the Prior usually worked at his oak desk, that same slant of light caught only one other familiar monastic sight: the back of a figure draped in the crisp white Dominican tunic and scapulary, kneeling with bowed head before a crucifix propped on a stool. For a passing glance, he might have been any ordinary brother, fretting over trivial sins: laughing too loud in the choir, feeling petty satisfaction when a fellow monk spilled ink all over his hand-illuminated breviary. His only aim would seem to be climbing slowly up the narrow ladder of prayer, fasting, and obedience to earn his place in Paradise.

But under that plain white wool tunic, Girolamo Savonarola’s heart beat with a conflict no average monk could ever fathom. Old, irreversible mistakes, small lapses from the truth he had justified for the sake of his holy work, were tangled so tight with his noblest purposes and sincere, unshakable beliefs, that he could no longer untangle the two strands. Self-serving expediency was stitched deep into the fabric of the great Church reform he had spent his whole life building, a work he could no more abandon than his body could stop trembling before the things he hoped for, or feared. No matter what path he chose, his conscience would never know perfect rest.

He was muttering Latin prayers out loud, the words *Cor mundum crea in me* — create in me a clean heart — falling softly from his lips, but he was not truly praying. He had fallen to his knees to beg God for a rush of calm, some sign that the crowding, conflicting thoughts in his head were not drawing him away from divine support. But the premonitions and urges churning inside him were too strong. Even as he pressed his palms to his face, his mind was full of vivid images of the trap his enemies had laid for him, full of arguments he could use to defend himself against their taunts and accusations.

He knew his own followers were pushing for the Trial by Fire just as hard as his Franciscan opponents. They wanted him to accept the challenge, to perform the long-awaited miracle that would wipe away every last trace of doubt and crush his enemies for good. Had he not publicly declared that God would make his will plainly known at the fitting time? To hot-tempered, party-loyal Florentines sick of endless political squabbling, there was no time more fitting than now. Everyone agreed that if the earnest, devout Fra Domenico walked through flames unharmed, that would be a miracle plain enough for every man, woman, and child in the city to see. But Savonarola was acutely, painfully aware that none of the justifications he had given for refusing the challenge had quieted his followers’ secret, urgent longing to see him step into the fire.

Satisfying them was impossible. With bitter, heavy distress, he was forced to admit he could no longer resist the trial going forward under Fra Domenico’s name. He had never lied when he declared his belief that God would one day send a supernatural sign to validate his work. He could easily wrap his faith around a distant, undefined miracle, screened from plain view behind all the strong reasons he knew it must happen, and he had never doubted the inner miracles of his own prophetic inspiration and divinely guided intuitions. But a concrete, immediate miracle like walking unharmed through fire, every brutal detail of which pressed sharp on his imagination, demanding not just abstract belief but action? His mind recoiled from it completely.

Savonarola’s nature was built of two near-equal, opposing strands: passionate sensibility that flooded every idea with emotion and pulled him toward contemplative ecstasy, and a sharp, unforgiving eye for outward facts, a vigorous practical judgment of men and events. The second trait was amplified by an acutely sensitive physical constitution, that made the idea of searing pain and burning destruction feel vivid, unavoidable, rooted in every memory of past hurt. A man might tell himself theoretically that a red hot iron will not burn him, but no amount of that abstract belief will make him press his bare hand to it. For Savonarola, when the Trial by Fire was presented as an immediate, unavoidable demand, it was impossible for him to believe that any human being could walk through flames unhurt — impossible to believe that even if he forced himself to volunteer, he would not flinch and run back at the final step.

The Florentine public would never grasp those fine nuances. Ordinary people have always taken it as a sign of strong sense to reduce moral questions to simple, black-and-white choices, and judge a man’s conduct by the most concise alternative available. It would seem obvious to every one of them that a man who once swore God would give him a miracle to prove his work was true, and then drew back when the chance to test that promise came, had never meant what he said. Scores of his Piagnoni followers were ready to walk into the fire, they would say — why would they be braver than their leader, unless their faith was stronger? Savonarola could not have explained his conflict to his followers, not even if he had been able to untangle and make sense of it fully for himself. Raw, unnameable feeling quickly wraps itself in the handy, familiar phrases we keep stored in our minds, and true self-awareness takes far more than mere unfeigned sincerity to achieve.

Kneeling there, listening no longer to the prayers he spoke aloud, he shaped his justifications over and over, refining them for an audience. *To rashly accept the challenge and call on Heaven for a miracle set by my petty, ignoble foes would be to tempt God, and He would not answer. If the Pope’s legate comes, if ambassadors from every great European power swear that a successful miracle will force the calling of a General Council and the full reform of this corrupt Church, then I will step into the flames, trusting that God will not refuse to stamp His seal on that great work. Until then I must reserve myself for the higher duties laid on me. I cannot leap off the chariot of this sacred work just to wrestle with every loud-mouthed vaunter that crosses my path.*

But the second his imagination drew close to the blazing pyre they would build in the Piazza, the image of a human body stepping into the flames, his faith recoiled again. He felt the burn shiver all the way down to the tips of his sensitive fingers. The miracle could not happen. He told himself he could still find a way to stop the trial. The fuel would be stacked, the crowds would gather, all the formalities might go forward, but the Franciscan challenger would hang back at the last minute, and they could find a way to prevent Fra Domenico from entering the flames. If worst came to worst, he decided, Fra Domenico could carry the consecrated Host into the fire with him, and the sacred Mystery held in his hand would stay the ordinary destructive power of flame. Or the Franciscans would refuse to let that happen, and that would be final cause to call the whole trial off.

But none of those quiet intentions could be spoken aloud. He would have to appear to await the trial openly, to trust fully in its miraculous outcome. That gap between his private doubt and his public mask was a betrayal of that simple, unfeigned Christian innocence he had striven for all his life, and preached as the highest fruit of a life rooted in the divine. In the heat of the crowd, with shouts ringing in your ears and cheeks burning hot, who is so blessed that they remember the quiet, pure yearnings they held in the cool of the dawn, and know they have not betrayed them? *O God, it is for the sake of the people — they are blind, their whole faith depends on me. If I put on sackcloth and throw myself in the ashes in front of them, who will pick up the standard, who will lead the battle? Have I not been led down a path I never expected, to this great work no one else can fulfil if I abandon it?*

The conflict had no end. In his effort to pray, his restive, aching mind soothed its smart by clinging to the grandeur of the work no other man in all of Europe was fit to carry out. The words of prayer died away on his lips. He stayed kneeling, his mind full of visions of the changes his reform would spread across the whole continent, the small immediate difficulties fading into the warm glow of that grand future — until a soft, deliberate knock rapped on his cell door.

Savonarola pulled on his heavy black mantle over his tunic before stepping out, his habit when receiving visitors, and the moment he stepped into the antechamber his whole demeanor shifted to the calm, powerful, self-possessed energy he was known for, as if he had risen from quiet resolution instead of hours of tearing internal conflict. Tito did not kneel, only offered a bow of deep, deliberate deference, which Savonarola acknowledged quietly without formal sacerdotal pleasantries before gesturing to a chair.

“Your business is weighty, my son, that you could not send word through another?” he asked directly.

“Assuredly, Father,” Tito said, no unnecessary preamble. “I heard a remark from Messer Domenico Mazzinghi that you would be glad to make use of the Ten’s special courier leaving for France with official despatches. Messer Domenico is away at his villa today, so I came to warn you the courier departs for Lyons at dawn tomorrow. I hope I have not been over-officious in coming to tell you myself.”

Savonarola’s facial muscles were firmly under control, as they must be for any man whose power rests on the force of his personality, and he was habitually cautious about sharing his intentions with anyone unnecessarily. But under the spark of strong excitement, his eyes dilated and flared bright with a light no force of will could hide. He stared steadily at Tito for a long beat, considering if the news met a purpose he held. Tito, who never seemed to stare too intently but rarely missed the smallest detail, had seen that exact flash many times before. He noticed it now, and busied himself pretending to adjust the gold fibula pin on his tunic, looking the picture of patient waiting.

Savonarola had indeed been expecting that very message from Mazzinghi, one of his most loyal followers on the Ten, who had already been asked to pave the way for a private letter from Savonarola to the King of France, tucked in the desk beside him. That letter called on the king to help summon a General Council to reform the abuses of the Church, starting by deposing Pope Alexander — a corrupt, vicious man who had bought his way into the papacy through simony, and had no right to his throne. Tito did not know the exact contents of the letter, but his sharp, intuitive wits had guided him to guess its purpose, and he had come to make certain he was the one who carried it.

“It is true, my son,” Savonarola said evenly. “I do have letters I wish to send safely under cover to our Florentine ambassador. The San Marco community, as you know, holds the Medici library we purchased some years back, and we owe a debt to that wise and experienced Frenchman, Messer Philippe de Comines, related to that transaction, so there is legitimate reason for correspondence. But Messer Domenico will most likely return to the city before evening, and I would have more time to prepare the letters fully if I wait to give them to him myself.”

“Of course, Reverend Father, that would be ideal in every respect save one,” Tito replied smoothly. “If any accident delays Messer Domenico’s return, the letters would either force me to come back to San Marco at a very late hour, or force you to send them to the Palazzo via your secretary. I know you are very careful to avoid any false political gossip that might arise from too frequent communication between yourself and any official of the government.”

Savonarola fell silent, his jaw set firm, a faint flush rising to his cheeks as suppressed excitement built in his chest. This was a critical moment, the second he passed the letter out of his own hands.

“I am certain Messer Domenico will return in plenty of time,” Tito said, acting as if the Prior’s decision was settled, and rising from his chair as if to leave. “I will not trespass further on your time now. But as I may not get the chance for another private audience, I venture to share news not yet known to anyone save the magnificent Ten: I intend to resign my secretaryship, and leave Florence very soon. Am I presuming too much on your goodwill to share a matter that concerns mostly myself?”

“Speak on, my son,” Savonarola said. “I would know your prospects.”

“I realize I misjudged my true calling when I left the life of pure scholarly letters I was raised for. Florence’s politics are worthy of the greatest minds — minds like yours — when a man is free to carry out the ideas he believes in. But for a man like me, who can only ever be a tool for shifting, changing party schemes, you need the deep, rooted loyalty of a man born Florentine to stay here. My wife Romola’s profound unhappiness with life in Florence after the terrible unrest of last August has also influenced my choice. I intend to join her.”

Savonarola nodded approvingly.

“I will travel to the chief courts of Europe, widen my acquaintance with learned men across all the great universities, first stopping at the court of Hungary, where scholars are always warmly welcomed. I do not hide from you, Father, that I am no religious enthusiast, I do not share my wife’s ardor. But religious enthusiasm is not required, I think, to see the grandeur and justice of your vision for reforming nations and the Church. If you ever choose to entrust me with any commission that furthers the connections you wish to build abroad, I will feel deeply honored. May I take my leave now?”

“Stay, my son. When you leave Florence I will send a letter to your wife, to reassure her of my care for her spiritual welfare — she left my side in anger, and I would see that mended. As for the letters to France that I have ready now —” Savonarola rose and turned to his desk as he spoke, taking out a sealed letter addressed in his own tiny, exquisite handwriting, the same script that still survives covering the margins of his annotated Bibles. He tucked it into a larger sheet of heavy official paper, folded it, and sealed the outer wrap.

“Pardon me, Father,” Tito cut in, before he could speak. “Unless it is your absolute wish, I would rather not take the responsibility of carrying that packet. Messer Domenico will no doubt return in time, and if he does not, Fra Niccolò can bring it to me at the second hour this evening, when I place the rest of the despatches in the courier’s hands.”

“For the moment,” Savonarola said, brushing that protest aside, “I would ask you to write the outer address to our ambassador in your own handwriting. It will look far less suspicious than a note from my secretary.”

Tito sat to write the address while the Prior stood behind him, arms folded, the flush rising high on his cheeks, his lip finally trembling with suppressed, unnameable excitement. Tito rose to leave, when Savonarola spoke abruptly: “Take it, my son. There is no use waiting. I will not have Fra Niccolò trudge all the way to the Palazzo on an unnecessary errand.”

As Tito slipped the heavy sealed packet under his velvet mantle, Savonarola stood frozen in thrumming, suppressed excitement, unable to speak another word. A strange, heavy silence hung in the air, the kind that radiates unconsciously from passionate, overwrought minds when their thoughts are pulled far away from the world around them. Tito bowed deep, and slipped out of the monastery.

The letter was duly handed over to the official government courier, and carried out past Florence’s city gates. But long before that, a second messenger Tito had hired privately had already slipped a note in cipher out of the city, carried by a string of mounted relays to the armed agents of Ludovico Sforza, Duke of Milan, who were waiting on the Milanese border for the explicit purpose of intercepting Savonarola’s secret despatches.
