The Dashwood family had now settled fully into Barton Cottage, and their life there unfolded with a gentle, steady comfort they had not dared hope for after leaving Norland. The little white house, its sloping cottage garden, and all the winding lanes and wild hedgerows that surrounded them had grown familiar, and the small daily rituals that had once made half the joy of their old home—sketching wildflowers by the stream, poring over poetry beside the fire, tending to potted herbs on the windowsill—were resumed here with far more lightness than they had ever known at Norland after their father’s death. For the first fortnight after their arrival, Sir John Middleton called on them every single day; unused to seeing anyone keep themselves usefully occupied at home, he could not hide his open amazement at finding the three Dashwood girls and their mother always busy at some project, never idling away the hours.

They had very few other visitors outside of the Barton Park household. Though Sir John begged them repeatedly to mix more widely in the neighbourhood, swearing his carriage was always at their disposal for any call they wished to make, Mrs. Dashwood’s independent spirit outweighed any desire to force her daughters into uninviting social obligation. She was resolute that they would not call on any family further than a pleasant walk away, and there were very few such households within that short distance. On one of their very first rambles, the girls had wandered a mile and a half down the narrow, winding Allenham valley that branched off from Barton’s, and stumbled on an ancient, stately stone manor house whose gabled roofs and climbing old roses reminded them so strongly of Norland that they instantly longed to know more of its inhabitants. Upon enquiry, though, they learned it belonged to an elderly lady of excellent character who was sadly too infirm to ever leave her home or receive visitors.

The whole surrounding countryside was laced with beautiful walking paths. The high chalk downs rose almost directly from the cottage windows, their summits promising crisp, clean air and sweeping views of the valley below, the perfect escape when heavy mud in the lower lanes made their usual walks impassable. One showery morning, after two full days of steady, unrelenting rain that had left them cooped up indoors, Marianne and Margaret decided to head for one of those downs at once, lured by a fleeting patch of bright sunshine that peeked through the clouds. The weather was not fair enough to tempt Elinor and their mother away from their sketchbook and open book beside the parlour fire, even when Marianne insisted the day would clear entirely, every threatening cloud blown far off the hills. The two girls set off on their own, gaily climbing the path up the down, rejoicing over their good judgment every time a patch of bright blue sky peeked through the overcast. When the sharp, invigorating southwesterly gale hit their faces high on the slope, they laughed aloud, pitying Elinor and their mother for missing such pure, sparkling joy.

“Is there any happiness in all the world equal to this?” Marianne cried, turning to Margaret with glowing cheeks. “We will stay up here and walk at least two whole hours, I swear.”

Margaret agreed, and they pushed on against the wind, laughing as they leaned into its force for nearly twenty more minutes, before the clouds suddenly gathered thick and dark over their heads, and a driving, stinging rain lashed straight into their faces. Chagrined and caught completely unawares, they had no choice but to turn back, for there was not a single cottage or hedge thick enough to shelter them anywhere nearby, their only refuge the warm parlour of their own home. The quickest way down was the steep direct slope that led straight to their garden gate, and they began to run as fast as they could.

Marianne pulled ahead at first, but her foot slipped on the wet grass, and she tumbled hard to the ground. Margaret could not stop her own momentum in time to help, and she careened all the way down the hill to the bottom, arriving safe and breathless at the garden gate.

A gentleman carrying a shotgun, two fluffy pointers trotting and playing at his heels, had been making his way up the hill only a few yards from Marianne when she fell. He set his gun down instantly and ran to her side. Marianne had already lifted herself from the ground, but her ankle had twisted badly in the fall, and she could barely put any weight on it to stand. The gentleman offered his assistance at once, and seeing that her modesty made her hesitate to accept help she could not do without, he swept her gently up into his arms without further preamble and carried her all the way down the steep slope. He walked straight through the open garden gate that Margaret had left ajar, and carried her right into the cottage, not setting her down until he had seated her soft and safe in an armchair by the parlour fire.

Elinor and Mrs. Dashwood jumped to their feet in complete amazement at their entrance. Both stared at the stranger, their faces alight with equal parts wonder and quiet, unplanned admiration, drawn immediately to his handsome, bright face. He apologized gently for bursting in uninvited, explaining what had happened to Marianne with a frank, graceful ease that made his already striking looks even more charming, warmed by his soft voice and kind expression. Even if he had been old, plain, and coarse, Mrs. Dashwood would have been endlessly grateful to him for tending to her injured child; but his youth, his easy elegance, the warmth of his smile, made his small act of kindness feel like something far more precious.

She thanked him over and over, and with her usual gentle sweetness invited him to sit and rest a while. He declined, though, noting that he was soaked through with rain and covered in mud from the hill. When Mrs. Dashwood asked for his name, he told her it was Willoughby, that he was currently staying at Allenham Court, and that he hoped he might have the honour of calling on them the next day to enquire after Marianne’s sprained ankle. They granted his request at once, and he departed, stepping back out into the full force of the heavy pouring rain.

His manly beauty and uncommon grace instantly became the whole family’s topic of conversation. They laughed gently at Marianne’s tumble, and the story of her gallant rescuer felt all the more delightful for how handsome and charming he was. Marianne herself had barely gotten a clear look at him: the hot, crimson flush that had flooded her face the moment he lifted her off the ground had made her too flustered to meet his eyes for almost the whole walk into the house. But she had seen more than enough to agree with all their praise, lauding him with all the passionate warmth that was her usual way. His face and his bearing were exactly the kind of hero she had always daydreamed of finding in her favourite novels, and that quick, unhesitating eagerness to help her, no tedious formalities to get in the way, made his kindness feel all the more romantic. Every small detail of him felt interesting: his good name, his home at the old Allenham manor they had so admired, and Marianne soon declared that a hunting jacket was the most becoming garment any man could possibly wear. Her imagination ran wild, her thoughts soft and bright, and she barely noticed the throbbing ache in her sprained ankle at all.

Sir John called on them as soon as the sky cleared enough the next day for him to venture out. When they told him of Marianne’s accident, they eagerly asked if he knew any young man named Willoughby who was staying at Allenham.
“Willoughby!” Sir John cried, delighted. “What, he’s in the neighbourhood? That’s capital news! I’ll ride over to his place tomorrow and invite him to dinner here at the park on Thursday.”
“So you do know him, then?” Mrs. Dashwood said.
“Know him? Of course I do! He comes down to Allenham every single year without fail.”
“And what sort of young man is he?”
“The best fellow alive, I assure you. A crack shot, and there’s not a bolder rider in all of England.”
“Is that all you can say for him?” Marianne exclaimed, indignant. “What of his manners, when you know him better? What of his pursuits, his talents, his wit and genius?”

Sir John looked rather puzzled. “Upon my soul, I can’t say I’ve paid much mind to all of that. But he’s pleasant, good-humoured, and he’s got the sweetest little black bitch of a pointer I’ve ever seen. Was she out with him today?”
Marianne could no more tell him the colour of Mr. Willoughby’s pointer than he could describe the finer shades of his character to her.
“Wait, who exactly is he?” Elinor asked. “Where does he come from? Does he own property at Allenham?”

On that point, Sir John could give them far clearer answers. He told them Mr. Willoughby had no property of his own in Devonshire, that he was only staying at Allenham Court as a guest of the elderly lady who owned it, who was his relation, and whose entire estate he was heir to.
“Aye, aye, he’s a very good catch, let me tell you, Miss Dashwood,” he added, grinning. “He’s got a nice little private estate of his own in Somersetshire too. If I were you, I wouldn’t let your younger sister steal him away, not after tumbling down hills and getting carried off like that. Marianne can’t expect to have every nice young man all to herself, you know. Poor Colonel Brandon will be jealous, if she’s not careful.”
“I don’t think Mr. Willoughby will have any trouble from either of my daughters doing what you call ‘catching him,’” Mrs. Dashwood said with a good-humoured smile. “That’s not the sort of behaviour we raised our girls for. Men are perfectly safe with us, no matter how rich they are. I’m very glad to hear he’s a respectable young man, and that we’ll be happy to make his acquaintance.”
“He’s the best sort of fellow alive, I swear,” Sir John repeated. “Last Christmas, at the little hop we had here at the park, he danced from eight o’clock at night straight till four in the morning, and never once sat down.”
“Did he truly?” Marianne cried, her eyes sparkling. “And did he dance with elegance, with spirit?”
“He did! And he was up again by eight the next morning to go riding out to the hunt.”
“That’s exactly the kind of young man he ought to be,” Marianne said, breathless with delight. “Whatever a man chooses to pursue, he should throw his whole heart into it, no care for how tired he might get.”
“Aye, aye, I see how this will go,” Sir John laughed. “You’ll be setting your cap at him now, and never give poor old Brandon a second thought.”
“That is a phrase I detest above all others, Sir John,” Marianne said, colouring warmly. “I hate every silly, commonplace saying people use to try to sound clever, and ‘setting one’s cap at a man’ or ‘making a conquest’ are the worst of them all. They’re crude, they’re unkind, and even if they ever were clever, it’s been decades since they had any wit left to them.”

Sir John did not quite follow her rebuke, but he laughed heartily anyway, as if he did. “Well, you’ll make plenty of conquests either way, I don’t doubt. Poor Brandon’s already completely smitten with you, and he’s well worth setting your cap at, even if you have to tumble down a few more hills and sprain a few more ankles to get his attention.”
