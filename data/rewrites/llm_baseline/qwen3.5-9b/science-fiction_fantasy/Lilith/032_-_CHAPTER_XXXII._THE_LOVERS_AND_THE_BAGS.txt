The night turned bitterly cold, the chill seeping into my bones until my very body felt frozen. From the shadows, the cries of wolves drew near; I heard their soft-padded footsteps crunching on the rocks and their rapid panting filling the stagnant air. Through the impenetrable darkness, I saw a circle of glowing eyes contracting around me. My time had come. I sprang to my feet—alas, I possessed not even a stick to defend myself.

They surged forward in a rush, their eyes blazing with greedy fury, black throats gaping wide to devour me. I stood helpless, awaiting my end. Just as they halted over the horse, a cloud of green eyes descended upon their flank. Heads bearing these eyes shot toward the wolves with a cry feebler yet fiercer than their own snarls. By the sound, I recognized them: they were cats, led by a massive gray one. I could see nothing of him save his eyes, yet I knew him—and thus knew his color and immense size.

A terrible battle erupted, its details lost to me save for the sounds echoing through the night. I wanted to flee, certain this fight would cost me dearly, but where was the point? My first step would likely send me tumbling, and my foes of both kinds could see and scent me in the dark. Suddenly, the howling ceased, replaced by wilder caterwauling. Then came the soft padding again, signaling flight: the cats had won.

In an instant, the sharpest teeth bit into my legs. Moments later, the cats swarmed over me like a living cataract, biting wherever they could reach and scratching furiously across my entire form. A multitude clung to my body; escape was impossible. Desperately, I attacked the hateful swarm, tearing at them and strangling them in vain. When I tried to fling them off, they adhered to my hands like limpets. I trampled them underfoot, thrust fingers into their eyes, and clamped jaws stronger than theirs around them, yet I could not dislodge a single one. Without respite, they discovered new spaces on my flesh to feast, hauling at my skin with widely curved, claw-like pincers, hissing and spitting in my face—but never touching it.

Only when I threw myself onto the ground in despair did they abandon my body, darting instead to attack my face. As I rose, they immediately deserted it to return to my legs. In agony, I broke free and ran blindly into the solid dark, caring not where I went. They pursued me in a surrounding torrent, rubbing against me or leaping up, though they tormented me less violently now. Whenever I stumbled, they paused to let me rise; whenever fear slowed my pace, they lunged anew at my legs.

All that miserable night they forced me to run, driving me along a surprisingly smooth path. I tumbled into no gullies, and passing the Evil Wood unseen, I left it behind in the gloom. When morning finally broke, I found myself beyond the channels, standing on the edge of the orchard valley. Overcome with joy, I wished to make peace with my persecutors, but not a cat was to be seen. I collapsed onto the moss and fell fast asleep.

I was awakened by a kick, finding myself bound hand and foot once more, a thrall of the giants. "What fitter fate?" I muttered to myself. "To whom else should I belong?" I laughed in the triumph of self-disgust. A second kick silenced my false merriment. Thus aided by my captors, I eventually managed to stand. Six of them surrounded me. They untied the rope binding my legs, attached separate ropes to each limb, and dragged me away. I walked as best I could, but when they pulled both ropes simultaneously, I fell repeatedly, only to be kicked upright again.

Straight to my old labor they took me, tying my leg-ropes to a tree, undoing my arms, and placing the hateful flint in my left hand. They lay down and pelted me with fallen fruit and stones, rarely hitting me. If I could have freed my legs and seized the stick I spotted just a couple of yards away, I would have fallen upon all six of them! "But the Little Ones will come at night!" I told myself, finding comfort in the thought.

All day I labored hard. When darkness returned, they tied my hands and left me fastened to the tree. I slept deeply but woke often, each time dreaming of lying amidst a heap of children. At dawn, my enemies reappeared with their kicks and beastly companions. It was nearing noon when fatigue and hunger nearly overwhelmed me, then a sudden commotion erupted in the brushwood, followed by a burst of bell-like laughter so dear to my heart.

I cried out in delight and welcome. Immediately, there arose a trumpeting like baby elephants, a neighing like foals, and a bellowing like calves. Through the bushes poured a crowd of Little Ones riding diminutive horses, small elephants, and little bears—the noises emanating from the riders rather than the beasts. Mixed among them walked older boys and girls, including a woman holding a baby that crowed in her arms.

The giants sprang to their lumbering feet but were instantly assailed by a storm of sharp stones. Horses charged their legs; bears rose to hug them at the waist; elephants wrapped their trunks around their necks, pulled them down, and delivered a trampling unlike any they had ever received. In moments, my ropes were undone, and I was embraced by what seemed an innumerable sea of Little Ones.

For a while, I saw no more of the giants. They seated me, and my Lona arrived, silently beginning to feed me the loveliest red and yellow fruits. I sat and ate while the entire colony stood guard until I finished. Then, two of their largest elephants were brought forward, placed side by side, and hooked by trunk with their tails tied together. These docile creatures could have untied their tails with a simple shake or forgotten the hooks entirely, yet tails and trunks remained exactly as arranged. Clearly, the elephants understood they must keep their bodies parallel.

I rose and lay in the hollow between their two backs. As the wise animals countered the weight pushing them apart, leaning against one another, they created a most comfortable litter. My feet projected slightly beyond their tails, but my head rested pillowed on an ear of each. Smaller children mounted to serve as a bodyguard, lining up along the back of each bearer. The assembly formed a train, and the procession began to move.

Whither they carried me, I did not attempt to guess; I yielded myself to their pleasure, almost as happy as they. Chattering, laughing, and performing endless joyful tricks at first, the moment they sensed I was drifting to sleep, they became still as judges.

I woke to a sudden musical uproar greeting the opening of my eyes. We traveled through the forest where they found the babies—a stretch of woods reaching from the valley to the hot stream. A tiny girl sat with her little feet close to my face, looking down at me coaxingly for a moment before speaking, the others hanging on her words.

"We make a petisson to king," she said.
"What is it, my darling?" I asked.
"Shut eyes one minute," she answered.
"Certainly I will! Here goes!" I replied, closing my eyes tight.
"No, no! Not before I tell you!" she cried.

I opened them again, and we talked and laughed together for quite another hour.
"Close eyes!" she commanded suddenly.
I closed them, keeping them shut. The elephants stood still. I heard a soft scurry, a slight rustle, and then a profound silence—for in that world, SOME silences ARE heard.

"Open eyes!" twenty voices shouted in unison from a short distance away. But when I obeyed, not a creature was visible except the elephants bearing me. I knew the children were marvelously quick at clearing the way—the giants had taught them that—but when I raised myself and scanned the open, shrubless forest, seeing neither hand nor heel, I stared in blank astonishment.

The sun had set, and darkness was quickly falling, yet soon a multitude of birds began to sing. I lay listening, reasonably sure that if I left them alone, the hidden ones would emerge shortly. The singing grew into a little storm of avian voices. "Surely the children must have something to do with this! Yet how could they set the birds singing?" I wondered aloud as I lay listening.

Soon, however, glancing up into the tree beneath which my elephants stood, I thought I spied a slight motion among the leaves. Looking more keenly, sudden white spots appeared in the dark foliage. The music died down, a gale of childish laughter rippled through the air, and white spots emerged in every direction: the trees were full of children!

In wild merriment, they began to descend, some dropping from bough to bough so rapidly I could scarcely believe they hadn't fallen. I left my litter and was instantly surrounded—a target for all the artillery of their jubilant fun. With stately composure, the elephants walked away to bed.

"But," I said after their uproarious gladness had run its course, "how is it that I never before heard you sing like the birds? Even when I thought it must be you, I could hardly believe it!"
