When Buck dragged that thousand-pound load of flour a hundred yards across the frozen Yukon ice and earned John Thornton sixteen hundred dollars in five minutes flat, the gruff outdoorsman wasted no time paying off his old debts and loading his sled for the East: a months-long quest for the fabled lost mine, a legend older than the Yukon Territory itself. Hundreds of prospectors had chased its trail before, almost none returned, and those who did came back empty-handed or raving of a tumbledown forgotten cabin that marked the mine’s entrance. Dying men had sworn on the mine’s existence, clutching nuggets of gold purer than any ever panned in the North, but no living soul had ever laid claim to that hoard.

So Thornton, his partners Pete and Hans, Buck and a half-dozen other sled dogs struck out into uncharted wilderness, chasing the fortune that had broken every party that dared go looking for it. They sledded seventy miles up the Yukon, swung west onto the Stewart River, passed the mouths of the Mayo and McQuestion, and pushed on until the broad river shrank to a frothing trickle threading the jagged peaks that formed the continent’s backbone. Thornton feared nothing in the wild: with nothing but a sack of salt and his rifle he could vanish into the backcountry for months, hunting his meals as he traveled, no schedule, no rush. Their sleds carried little besides ammunition and tools, and their deadline was the endless, unmarked wild stretching out ahead of them.

For Buck, it was unadulterated, boundless joy. Weeks on end they trekked steadily day and night, then weeks on end they camped in random clearings, the dogs lazing in the sun while the men chopped holes in frozen gravel and panned dirt by the campfire light. Some days they feasted riotously on caribou and grouse, other days they went hungry when game vanished, no one rushing, no one complaining. When summer rolled around, they strapped packs to their backs, rafted across glacial lakes so blue they looked shards of fallen sky, and hacked slender dugout canoes out of standing spruce to drift up and down rivers no white man had ever mapped.

Months slipped past, and they twisted through vast, empty valleys where no footprints marked the mud, where you could hike for a week without seeing a single sign another human had ever passed through. They crossed mountain passes in mid-summer blizzards, shivered under the midnight sun on bare peaks between the treeline and permanent snow, waded through valleys thick enough with gnats to swallow a man whole, and picked wild strawberries growing in the shadow of glaciers that tasted sweeter than any fruit grown far down south. The next winter, they stumbled on a half-blazed trail cut through the pines that vanished as suddenly as it appeared, no start no end, no clue who had carved it. Later they found the rotting wreckage of an old trapper’s lodge, and tucked in the tattered remains of a blanket Thornton pulled out a long-barrelled Hudson’s Bay Company flintlock, the kind that once traded for its exact weight in flat-stacked beaver pelts. No other trace of the unknown trapper who’d left it there ever turned up.

When spring bloomed again, they didn’t find the lost mine at all. Instead, they stumbled on a shallow gravel placer in a broad sunlit valley, where pure gold glowed like melted butter across the bottom of every pan they pulled out. They didn’t need to wander another mile. Every shift of work earned them thousands of dollars in fine gold dust and rough nuggets, and they worked from dawn to dusk every day, piling 50-pound sacks of gold stitched out of moosehide like stacks of firewood outside their spruce-bough lodge.

The dogs had almost nothing to do but haul in the occasional deer carcass Thornton shot, and Buck spent hours every day dozing by the fire, half lost to dreams. That old, familiar vision of the short, shaggy caveman who’d lived in the trees long before men built cabins came to him more and more often. He’d watch the hairy man sleep restless by the fire, jerking awake every few minutes to peer into the dark and toss more wood on the flames, creep silent through the underbrush at his heels, and see him swing through tree limbs as easily as he walked on the ground, roosting high up in the boughs through the night to stay safe from predators.

And close on the heels of those old memories, the call began. It hummed deep in the dark heart of the forest, thrumming through his paws and down his spine, filling him with a vague, sharp, bright longing he could not name. He’d dash off chasing it for hours, barking softly into the trees, shoving his nose deep into cool moss to snort the rich smell of damp earth, or hunch motionless for hours behind a rotting log, ears pricked to catch the faint sound that was calling him. One mid-summer midnight, he snapped awake suddenly, eyes wild, mane bristling all down his back, and the call came clear as day: a long, rolling howl, not quite the howl of a husky, but something older, wilder.

He slipped silent through the sleeping camp and dashed into the trees, following the sound until he stepped out into an open clearing, where a lean, lanky timber wolf sat on its haunches, head tilted up to the moon. The wolf froze, spun around and fled when it spotted Buck, and Buck gave chase, bounding through brush and creek beds until he herded the smaller wolf into a blind channel blocked by a jam of fallen logs. The wolf pivoted, snarling and snapping its teeth, but Buck didn’t lunge. He circled slow, body loose, tail soft, until the wolf realized the big dog meant no harm at all. They sniffed noses, played like two half-grown pups for a while, then the wolf loped off west, clearly inviting Buck to follow.

Buck ran side by side with his wild brother for hours, racing through sun-warmed forests and across bare alpine meadows, every old memory of his pre-domestic ancestors rising up in his chest, until they stopped at a shallow stream to drink. The second his nose touched the cold water, he remembered John Thornton. He sat down, turned his back on the open wild, and started jogging back toward camp. The wolf whined, trotted after him for an hour, then stopped, threw his head back and howled a long, mournful note that faded away behind Buck as he ran.

Thornton was just sitting down to dinner when Buck crashed into the clearing, tackled him to the ground, licked his face, nipped his hands, and rolled around him like a giddy pup, while Thornton laughed so hard he could barely breathe and cursed Buck lovingly for being a total fool. For two days and two nights Buck never let Thornton out of his sight, following him to the river, watching him eat, curling up at his feet outside his blankets at night. But the call in the forest grew louder and more insistent than ever, and soon he was sneaking off for days at a time, ranging deep into the woods. He killed a massive blind black bear by the salmon river, scattered a pack of wolverenes fighting over the bear’s carcass like they were dry leaves, and ran for hours on end without tiring, a perfect apex predator: half St. Bernard, half shepherd, bigger than any wild wolf that ever roamed the Yukon, fast enough to catch a fleeing rabbit mid-leap, cunning enough to sneak up on a beaver mending its dam without making a sound.

“Never was there a dog like him,” Thornton said one day, watching Buck stride out of camp toward the trees.
“Broken the mould the second they finished making him,” Pete agreed.
“Py jingo, I t’ink dat mine own self,” Hans nodded.

They never saw how Buck transformed the second he slipped under the tree line. He didn’t march anymore. He melted into the shadows, silent as a cat, belly crawling across dirt like a snake, leaping to strike before his prey even knew he was there. When the fall moose herds moved down from the high peaks to the sheltered valleys, Buck set his sights on the biggest bull in the whole herd: a 1,200-pound monster, six feet tall at the shoulder, carrying 14 points of antlers that spanned seven feet across, with an arrow sticking out of his flank that had left him wounded and enraged. For half a day Buck harried the herd, darting in to snap at the bull’s heels, luring him far away from the rest of the group, until the other younger bulls grew tired of running to protect the old brute and abandoned him.

Buck chased that wounded bull for four straight days, never letting him stop to browse birch shoots, never letting him pause to drink from a stream, wearing the massive beast down until its great antlered head hung heavy, its legs shook, and it collapsed. Buck took his kill, feasted for a full day, rested, then turned his long easy lope straight back for camp and John Thornton. But the air felt wrong. The birds were quiet, the breeze carried a sharp, acrid tang of smoke and unfamiliar human scent. A cold, heavy dread settled in his chest, and he ran faster, heading down the last slope toward their valley, paws barely touching the ground, ready for anything.
