Even more disquieting than the mundane rhythm of a tap's drip mimicking the ambient air was the peculiar effect it had upon Oleron: it ignited an aberrant hypersensitivity to the cacophony of the ancient house. It is a noted truth that silence achieves its most profound weight only when fractured by the minutest intrusion; indeed, this dwelling was never truly silent. Perhaps the gentle warmth of the spring air had loosened the torpor of its aged timber bones, causing them to expand and settle; perhaps Oleron's own hearth fires had prompted the structure to stretch its weary anatomy. Certainly, a teeming legion of insect life drilled and burrowed within its beams and joists. Regardless of the cause, Oleron required only to sit quietly in his chair, waiting a mere minute or two, to perceive a shift in the auditory landscape akin to a man who, convinced the midsummer woods are motionless, suddenly finds his ears attuned to the crackling chorus of a million insects. A smile touched his lips as he considered man's arbitrary division between the living and the dead. Here, distinct from recognizable noises—the scuttling of mice, the crumbling of plaster behind paneling, the popping of embers from the fire—a whole house was speaking to him, provided he understood its dialect. Beams sighed with exhaustion into their mortises; creatures ticked within the walls; joints cracked, boards groaned; with no visible movement of air, window sashes shifted position with a soft knock against their frames. Whether the place possessed life in this manner or not, it undeniably held a charming personality. An hour of contemplation sufficed for Oleron to conceive that, just as his physical form stood in harmonious relation to his soul, his habitation might, by extension and attenuation, exist in some mysterious rapport with himself. He even amused himself with the fanciful notion that he might so fully identify with the place that a future tenant, taking possession, would regard it as haunted. It would be a cruel jest if he, a perfectly harmless author burdened only by the necessity of restarting a novel, were inadvertently laying the groundwork for a future specter.

Yet, in direct proportion to this burgeoning attachment to the very fabric of his home, Elsie Bengough transitioned from indifference to a pronounced aversion, which she voiced without hesitation. "It does not belong to today at all, and for you specifically, it is detrimental," she declared with firm conviction. "You are far too prone to relinquish your grasp on tangible realities and drift into apathy; *you* require a setting with concrete floors, a patent gas meter, and a tradesman's elevator. It would benefit you immensely to secure employment that forces you to scramble and jostle shoulders with your fellow men. Now, if I could procure a job for you—even for two or three days a week—that leaves ample time for your proper work, would you accept it?" Some part of Oleron resented being thus diagnosed. He thanked Miss Bengough, though without a smile. "Thank you, but I do not believe so. After all, each of us must live his own life," he added, unable to suppress the sentiment. "His own life to live!..." "How long has it been since you stepped outside, Paul?" "Approximately two hours." "I do not mean to purchase stamps or mail correspondence. How long has it been since you experienced anything resembling a genuine excursion?" "Oh, some time, perhaps. I am unsure." "Since my last visit?" "I have not ventured out much." "And has *Romilly* progressed significantly under these conditions of confinement?" "I believe she has. I am laying the foundations. I shall commence the actual writing shortly." It appeared Miss Bengough had momentarily forgotten their earlier struggle regarding the first *Romilly*. She frowned, turned partially away, and then swiftly reoriented herself. "Ah!... So you still harbor that ridiculous notion?" "If you refer," Oleron replied slowly, "to the fact that I have discarded the old *Romilly* and am engaged with a new iteration, you are correct. That idea remains firmly lodged in my mind." Something discordant in his tone struck her, yet she was a fighter. His own absurd sensitivities only hardened her resolve. She emitted a sharp "Pshaw!" of impatience. "Where is the original?" she demanded abruptly. "Why?" Oleron asked. "I wish to see it. I intend to show portions of it to you. I want, if you are not entirely lost in reverie, to restore you to your senses." This time, it was he who turned his back. Yet, upon turning again, his voice softened. "It is futile, Elsie. I am responsible for the trajectory of my journey, and you must permit me to proceed—even if it appears erroneous to you. Believe me, I am giving it serious consideration.... The manuscript? I was on the verge of incinerating it, but I refrained. It rests in that window seat, should you insist on viewing it." Miss Bengough hastened to the window seat and lifted the lid. Suddenly, she uttered a small exclamation and pressed the back of her hand to her mouth. Speaking over her shoulder, she said, "You ought to hammer those nails in, Paul." He strode to her side. "What? What is it? What troubles you?" he asked. "I did hammer them in—or, rather, I pulled them out." "You left enough protruding to cause scratches," she replied, displaying her hand. From the upper wrist to the knuckle of her little finger, a swelling red wound was visible. "Good—Gracious!" Oleron ejaculated. "Here, come to the bathroom and bathe it immediately—" He hurried her to the room, turned on the warm water, and washed and cleansed the grievous gash. Still holding her hand, he switched to cold water, uttering fragmented phrases of astonishment and concern. "Good Lord, how did that occur! As far as I knew, I had... is this water too cold? Does that hurt? I cannot imagine how on earth... there; that will suffice—" "No—one moment longer—I can endure it," she murmured, her eyes closed. Soon, he led her back to the sitting room and bound the hand with one of his handkerchiefs; yet his face retained an expression of perplexity. He had spent half a day opening and rendering serviceable the three window boxes, and he could not conceive how he had failed to notice an inch-and-a-half length of rusty nail remaining embedded in the wood. He himself had opened the lids of each box a dozen times without detecting any nail; yet there it was. "It shall be extracted now, at any rate," he muttered as he fetched a pair of pliers. There was no error in his action this time. Elsie Bengough had sunk into a chair, her face pale; but in her lap lay the manuscript of *Romilly*. She had not yet abandoned *Romilly*. Shortly thereafter, she resumed her charge. "Oh, Paul, it will be the greatest mistake you have ever, *ever* committed if you do not publish this!" she cried. He hung his head, genuinely distressed. He could not expel the image of the nail from his mind, and *Romilly* occupied a secondary place in his thoughts for the moment. Nevertheless, she persisted; and when he eventually spoke, it was as if he sought her pardon for some transgression. "What can I say, Elsie? I can only hope that when you view the new version, you will see how justified I am. And if, despite everything, you *do not* approve of her, well..." he made a gesture of despair. "Do you not realize that I *must* be guided solely by my own light?" She fell silent. "Come, Elsie," he said gently. "We have managed well thus far; let us not fracture over this." Hardly had the words passed his lips before he regretted them. She had been tending to her injured hand, her eyes once more closed; but her lips and eyelids quivered simultaneously. Her voice trembled as she spoke. "I cannot help saying it, Paul, but you have changed so profoundly." "Hush, Elsie," he murmured soothingly; "you have suffered a shock; rest for a while. How could I possibly change?" "I do not know, but you have. You have not been yourself since arriving here. I wish you had never seen this place. It has halted your work, it is transforming you into a stranger I barely recognize, and it has rendered me horribly anxious concerning you.... Oh, how my hand begins to throb!" "Poor child!" he murmured. "Shall I take you to a physician to have it properly dressed?" "No—I shall recover soon—I will keep it elevated—" She placed her elbow on the back of the chair, and the bandaged hand rested lightly upon his shoulder. At that contact, an entirely new anxiety surged within him. Hundreds of times previously, during their outings and excursions, she had slipped her hand into his arm as one might slip it into the arm of a brother, and he had accepted the affectionate gesture as a sibling might. But now, for the first time, a hundred startling questions rushed into his mind. Her eyes remained closed, and her head had fallen pathetically backward; there was a lost, ineffable smile upon her parted lips. The truth crashed upon him. Good God!... And he had never divined it!
