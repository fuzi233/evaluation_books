Of all the gifts Art offers her devotees, none are handed freely on a silver platter. Every heir to that promised land must fight tooth and nail to claim their patch of it; the grand prophecies of success we whisper to ourselves in the dark are no legal deed, no firm title to possession. Tucking a map of an unowned, unvisited estate into your coat pocket is no proof you will ever walk a single acre of it, no matter how vividly you daydream of casting your shoe over Eden’s gates to mark the land as yours.

The sturdiest, most unshakable beliefs we hold about ourselves are rarely rooted in fact, anyway. They spring from that constant, warm, spontaneous thrum of self-satisfaction beating in our chests — a tiny, hidden seed of wild, unproven madness, the quiet confidence that we can move the entire world without ever bothering to find a solid foothold or a proper lever.

“Go on to church this morning, mamma, please,” Gwendolen said over breakfast the next day. “I’d prefer to see Herr Klesmer alone.” He’d written back to her note the evening before to say he would call at eleven sharp.

“I hardly think that’s quite proper, dear,” Mrs. Davilow fretted, her brows drawn tight with anxiety.

“Our affairs are far too serious to waste time on silly little social rules,” Gwendolen said, dismissive, her tone sharp with contempt. “Those rules aren’t just ridiculous — they’re insulting, frankly.”

“Would you mind if Isabel sat with you? She’d just tuck herself in the corner with a book, she wouldn’t interrupt.”

“No, she won’t,” Gwendolen said, shaking her head. “She’ll pick at her nails and stare the whole time. It will drive me mad. Trust my judgment, mamma. I have to be alone. Take all the rest of them to church with you.”

Gwendolen, of course, got her way. Only Miss Merry and two of the younger girls stayed home, lingering by the dining room windows to make the house look lived-in rather than completely empty of guests.

It was a perfect, soft autumn Sunday. The pale, fading golden sun of late autumn spilled over the half-strewn grass outside, slanting gently through the windows in broad bands of light that gilded every old piece of furniture, the mirrored panel that reflected it all back, the tapestry-draped chairs strung with faded silk flower garlands, the dark, mysterious old oil paintings on the walls, the rickety old organ where Gwendolen had loved to pretend she was Saint Cecilia on the first bright, happy day they’d moved into the house. She could even see, through the open door to the antechamber, the shelves of pale, dust-furred knickknacks that had stood witness to the day she’d dressed up in her flowing Greek costume to play Hermione, posing for their amateur charades. That memory was looping through her head on repeat — hadn’t Klesmer been there that night, hadn’t he stopped short, struck breathless by the grace of her pose and the fire in her expression? Every word he’d said then, every little look she’d imagined him giving her, hummed with sharp, burning interest right now. For the first time in her life, she felt no unshakable, easy confidence. She was inwardly trembling, consciously, deliberately waiting for the verdict of another person. A new flutter of uncertainty had stirred in her chest, a new, careful self-doubt that had never touched her before, back when her self-esteem had been nothing but a blissful, unchallenged gift of intuition. Even so, she kept whispering to herself that Klesmer had only ever seen snippets of her life, only glimpsed tiny fractions of what she could do. Any unfair judgment he made would be based on far too little evidence. She really, truly felt clever enough to take on anything.

To pass the time before he arrived, she dragged out all her sheet music and her dog-eared volumes of plays, stacked them on top of the piano, and set about sorting through them. When she caught sight of her own reflection moving in the mirrored panel, she drifted over to stare at it. Dressed all in black, not a single jewel or ribbon to adorn her, the warm pale glow of her skin framed perfectly between her crown of light brown hair and the square cut of her bodice, she looked like a statue carved from blocks of black, white, and tawny marble, the kind old Roman artisans used to make for their temples. Watching her own image drift toward her, she thought, quiet and sure, I am beautiful. Not exultant, just grave and certain. Her beauty was the one thing she couldn’t prove through hard work, the one thing she needed the world to confirm for her. If anyone dared criticize the curve of her nose, or the line of her neck and jaw, she knew she could not magically practice those things into perfection.

There was no time to linger over her reflection much longer. The crunch of carriage wheels on the gravel drive, the loud, sharp ring of the doorbell, the sound of the footman drawing open the front door — all told her she would not be disappointed. A little flutter of panic leaped up in her chest. For all her usual swagger and self-assurance, she was still a little afraid of Klesmer. He was part of that unmanageable world that did not bend to her whims, something sharp as vitriolic acid that would burn just as hot no matter if she smiled at it or stamped her foot in rage. Poor girl! This moment was a higher turning point in her fate than the day she’d first been proposed to by Grandcourt. Back then, the only question she’d had to answer was whether to take a specific man as her husband. Now, the secret, thrumming heart of her question was whether she had to take any husband at all — whether she could carve out a life for herself on her own terms, sate her hungry ambition without ever bending to the bondage of marriage for security.

Klesmer bowed low, very deliberately, in the wide antechamber doorway, every inch the respected master: his fine grey kerseymere trousers pressed to perfection, his kid gloves immaculate, proof that even the greatest of the world’s artists are still very human about their presentation. Gwendolen stepped forward to meet him, her face unusually grave, and held out her hand. “It is so kind of you to come, Herr Klesmer. I hope you didn’t think me horribly presumptuous to send for you.”

“I took your request as a command that did me great honor,” Klesmer said, his tone just as grave as hers. He had rearranged his entire schedule for the day to give her his full, undivided attention, but his temper was still strung tight from the events of the day before, making every word he spoke sharper, more cutting than he might have intended.

Gwendolen was so strung tight with nerves that she forgot all the usual little formalities. She stayed standing near the piano, and Klesmer positioned himself at the other end, his back to the window light, his sharp, omniscient eyes fixed unblinkingly on her. There was no point in putting on airs, no room for her usual coquetry. She spoke at once, no preamble.

“I need your advice, Herr Klesmer. We’ve lost all our money. We have nothing left. I have to earn my own bread, and I want to make enough to support my mother, so she never has to want for anything or face any hardship. The only path I can think of — the only one I’d ever want to take — is to be an actress, to go on the stage. But of course I want to rise to the top, make a name for myself, and I thought… if you thought I had it in me… it would be even better if I studied singing too, so I could do both.”

Klesmer set his hat down carefully on the piano top, folded his arms across his chest, and settled into a still, focused stance.

“I know my singing method is very bad,” Gwendolen rushed on, flushing bright pink then draining pale in the space of a breath. “I had terrible teachers as a girl. I could learn properly, I could study hard. You understand what I want, don’t you? To sing and act both, like Grisi, that’s the highest possible rank. Naturally I want to climb as high as I possibly can. I trust your judgment completely. I know you’ll tell me the absolute truth.”

Somehow, she was fully convinced that now she’d laid this desperate, serious appeal at his feet, the truth would be kind. It would be on her side.

Still Klesmer said nothing. He pulled off his gloves quickly, tossed them into his hat, rested his hands on his hips, and paced to the far end of the room. His heart ached for this girl, and he was trying to choose his words carefully, to be gentle. When he turned back to her, there was a soft, troubled frown on his face, and he spoke quickly, but gently. “You’ve never spent any time around working artists, have you? Not musicians, not actors, not anyone who makes their living that way?”

“Oh no, of course not,” Gwendolen said, unruffled. It was obvious she’d grown up a wealthy, sheltered young lady, after all.

“Forgive me for asking,” Klesmer said, pausing again near the piano. “When you make a decision this big, every little detail counts. You’re twenty, I’d guess?”

“I’m twenty-one,” Gwendolen said, a flicker of fear pricking at her chest. “Do you think I’m too old?”

Klesmer pursed his lower lip, shook his long, thin fingers upward in a gesture that was completely unreadable.

“Plenty of people start later than that, you know,” Gwendolen jumped in, falling back on her usual habit of being sure she had the facts right.

Klesmer ignored the comment, speaking even more softly than before. “You never thought about taking this path before all this trouble hit you, did you? You never longed to be an actress, never daydreamed about it, until the day your fortune was gone.”

“Not exactly — but I’ve always loved acting. I’ve done it, you saw me, don’t you remember? You saw us do those charades right here, you saw me play Hermione,” Gwendolen said, suddenly terrified he’d forgotten that night entirely.

“Yes, yes,” he said quickly. “I remember. I remember every bit of it.” He paced back across the room again, a habit he couldn’t break when he was working through an argument out loud or inside his own head.

Gwendolen could feel him weighing her in the balance, judging her. The wait was agonizing. But she still couldn’t fathom that the scale might tip the wrong way against her. “Thank you so much for taking the trouble to advise me, whatever you end up saying,” she added, as gracefully as she could.

“Miss Harleth,” Klesmer said, turning to face her, his accent thickening a little with the gravity of what he had to say next. “I won’t lie to you, not an inch. I would count myself a wicked man if I painted this life brighter than it is, or darker than it is. The gods curse any man who intentionally sends another person down the wrong road. If I misled a girl as young, as beautiful as you — who deserves to find happiness on the right path — I would be no better than a villain.” The last word came out as a low, sharp whisper.

Gwendolen’s heart sank like a stone in her chest under that unexpected solemnity. She stared at him, transfixed, as he went on.

“You are a beautiful young lady. You’ve grown up in comfort, you’ve done exactly what you wanted your whole life. You’ve never once had to say to yourself, ‘I must know this perfectly,’ ‘I must understand this exactly,’ ‘I must do this flawlessly.’” As he spoke those three terrible, unyielding musts, he held up three long fingers one after another. “All your life, you’ve never had to be anything other than a charming young lady, the sort of girl it would be rude to find fault with.”

He paused for a beat, then set his hands back on his hips, thrusting out his strong, square jaw.

“Well then. With that preparation, you want to try the life of an artist. A life of grueling, unceasing toil, of praise that never comes easy, that has to be earned the same way your bread does — slow, scant, and often never coming at all.”

That discouraging tone, which Klesmer had hoped would be gentle enough to spare her feelings, sparked a hot little flare of defiance in Gwendolen. She turned her head away, cheeks pinking with pique. “I thought as an artist yourself, you’d think the life was one of the most honorable, delightful paths a person could take. And what if I can’t do anything better than that? I’m sure I can brave the same risks every other working person braves.”

“Do nothing better?” Klesmer said, his voice rising a little with fire. “No, my dear Miss Harleth, you could do nothing better — no man or woman on earth could do anything better — if you could produce work of the highest, truest quality. I’m not belittling the life of a real artist. I’m exalting it. It’s a life only open to those with the rarest constitutions: natures made to love perfection more than ease, to labor for it, ready like all true lovers to endure, to wait, to say ‘I am not yet worthy, but my mistress Art is worthy, and I will live to earn her.’ It is an honorable life, yes. But that honor comes from the secret, burning vocation in your bones and the hard-won achievements you bleed for. There is no honor in putting on that life like it’s a fancy livery coat, just because it looks grand.”

Some leftover fire from the day before had seized Klesmer, pulling him away from his original gentle purpose. He’d only wanted to delicately make her see how unfit she was for this perilous, brutal path, but he had never been able to stand the pretensions of people who thought they could skip all the work and claim the rewards of art anyway. He caught himself, paused abruptly, but Gwendolen only heard how deeply he revered art — it was a glamour he threw over everything he loved, she thought. All she cared to make clear was that she wasn’t afraid of a little early hardship. The unshakable belief that if she stepped on a public stage, the same charm that had always won her admiration in private would work its magic, was so much a part of her, she could no more rip it out of her chest than she could tear out her own flesh. “I’m fully prepared to bear hardships at first,” she said, insistent. “Of course no one becomes famous overnight. And not every actress or singer has to be a first-rate genius. If you’d only tell me what steps to take, I’ll take them. I’m not afraid of climbing uphill. It’s so much better than the dead, dull life of a governess. I’ll do anything you recommend.”

Klesmer knew then he had no choice but to speak plainly.

“I will tell you the steps that will not be recommended by anyone, but will be forced on you if you insist on going through with this. No matter what your goal is — excellence, fame, second-rate work, third-rate work — it’s all the same. You’ll have to go to London under the care of your mother. You’ll have to put yourself through full training: musical, dramatic, theatrical. Every single thing you want to do, you will have to learn from scratch.” Gwendolen opened her mouth to protest, but Klesmer lifted a hand, stopping her. “I know. You’ve ‘practiced’ your talents — you recite, you sing, in drawing rooms, for your friends. My dear young lady, you have to unlearn all of that. You don’t even understand what real excellence is yet. You have to unlearn all your silly, mistaken little ideas of what good performance is. You have to learn exactly what you are striving for, then bend every part of your mind and your body to unbroken, unforgiving discipline. Your mind first, mind you. You cannot think about fame. Blow that candle out of your eyes, and only look at the work, at perfection. You will not earn a penny, not get a single professional engagement, for years. You will need money to support yourself and your whole family. But that,” Klesmer frowned, waving his fingers as if swatting that trivial difficulty away, “that money could probably be found, if it came to it.”

Gwendolen flushed bright pink then went pale as death through that whole speech. Her pride had been sliced open by a sharp knife, and that last line about finding money made the sting burn even worse. She could feel how upset she looked, so she hurried to sit down on a nearby chair, then gestured for Klesmer to take the one across from her. He didn’t sit. He turned a little, leaning back against the piano to face her. At that exact moment, she wished she’d never sent for him. This was the first time in her whole life anyone had ever treated her as something other than a pretty, privileged young lady to be cosseted and flattered, and it tasted bitter on her tongue. Klesmer, focused entirely on the seriousness of what he had to tell her, kept speaking in the same steady tone.

“Now. What reasonable outcome could you possibly expect after all that years of self-denial? You will want to know. It is only fair you know the full truth. The outcome is uncertain — and the most likely outcome is that it will not be worth all the pain you’ve endured.”

At those ruthless words, Klesmer pushed his lower lip out, staring through his spectacles like some unyielding monster completely untouched by her beauty.

Gwendolen’s eyes began to burn with unshed tears, but the terror of looking weak made her clamp down on her emotions, holding them fast. She forced herself to speak in a hard, cold tone. “You think I don’t have the talent, or that I’m too old to start.”
