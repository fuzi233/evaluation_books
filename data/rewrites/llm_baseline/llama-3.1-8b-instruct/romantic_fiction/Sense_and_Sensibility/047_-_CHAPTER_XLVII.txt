The scent of freshly baked bread wafted through the air, mingling with the soft murmur of conversation as the Dashwood family sat together in the evening. Marianne, still visibly affected by the day's revelations, struggled to compose herself. Her mother, noticing her distress, would have rushed to offer comfort, but Elinor's gentle gesture stayed her hand.

"I wish to assure you both," Marianne began, her voice barely above a whisper, "that I see everything as you can desire me to do." The words were laced with a hint of effort, her rising color and unsteady tone betraying the turmoil within.

As the family listened intently, Marianne continued, her voice growing stronger with each passing moment. "It is a great relief to me, what Elinor told me this morning. I have now heard exactly what I wished to hear." For a moment, her words faltered, but she recovered, speaking with a newfound calm. "I am now perfectly satisfied. I wish for no change. I never could have been happy with him, after knowing, as sooner or later I must have known, all this. I should have had no confidence, no esteem."

Mrs. Dashwood's eyes welled up with tears as she nodded vigorously. "Happy with a man of libertine practices! With one who so injured the peace of the dearest of our friends, and the best of men! No, my Marianne has not a heart to be made happy with such a man! Her conscience, her sensitive conscience, would have felt all that the conscience of her husband ought to have felt."

Marianne's sigh was a testament to the weight of her emotions, but she spoke resolutely, "I wish for no change." Elinor watched her sister with a deep understanding, knowing that Marianne's rational mind had finally caught up with her feelings. "You consider the matter," Elinor said gently, "exactly as a good mind and a sound understanding must consider it. And I dare say you perceive, as well as myself, not only in this, but in many other circumstances, reason enough to be convinced that your marriage must have involved you in many certain troubles and disappointments, in which you would have been poorly supported by an affection, on his side, much less certain."
