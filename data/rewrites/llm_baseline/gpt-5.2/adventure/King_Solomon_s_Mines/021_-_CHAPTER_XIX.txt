Ten days after that morning which had very near been our last, we were back again in the huts at Loo. It is a strange thing to write, but we were not much the worse in body for what we had passed through. My beard, that had gone into the mountain black-stubbled, came out sprinkled as if dusted with ash, and I doubt it will ever quite lose that added grey. Good, too, carried a change no soap and razor could mend. Since Foulata’s death, something in him had fallen quiet—as though a cooking-fire had been quenched and the man sat still listening for the crackle that would not come again.

For my own part, speaking as an older hand who has seen what tangles men can make of their hearts, I will admit—coldly, perhaps—that her dying spared us trouble. She was no ordinary girl: there was a kind of proud grace in her, a delicacy of feeling that did not sit clumsily upon her as it often does where life is hard. Yet she herself had spoken the truth with a simplicity more bitter than any sermon: could the sun mate with darkness, or the white with the black? Some things do not translate; you can carry the words across, but the meaning breaks on the way.

We never again forced ourselves into Solomon’s treasure place. When we had slept away the worst of our exhaustion—forty-eight hours of it—we went down into the great pit where we had rolled like stones, hoping to find the hole by which we had crawled out of the mountain’s belly. Rain had fallen since then and wiped our spoor clean as a cook wipes a board before kneading. Besides, the walls of the pit were honeycombed with ant-bear tunnels and other holes, so that the cliff-face looked like a cheese riddled by mice. Which of those openings had been our deliverance? There was no telling.

On the day before we were to start back to Loo, a restlessness seized us, and we went once more through the cool, wet breath of the stalactite cave, even back into the Chamber of the Dead. The air there has a taste, thin and mineral, like water that has stood long in a stone jar. Passing beneath the spear of the White Death, we stood and looked at the mass of rock that had cut us off from life. Behind that rock lay the coffers—wealth piled up until it was no longer wealth but a kind of madness in stone; and there, crushed and flattened, were the remains of Gagool, that ancient malice which had clung to the land like a curse in an old song no one dares to sing aloud. And there, too—though we could not see it—was the tomb that had been the portal, and the girl’s bones, and all that unspent splendour waiting in the dark.

We examined the “rock” until our hands were sore with scraping, but we could find no seam, no join of the sliding door, no hint of the trick that moved it. It was mechanism, yes, but so plain, so massive, so perfectly concealed that it mocked us. The very age of it seemed to stare back—an age that could do such things without leaving any clue for another tongue, another mind, to read. At last we gave it up, half in anger, half in fear; for I will say honestly that if the stone had lifted of its own accord at that moment, I doubt we should have had the stomach to step over what was left of Gagool and go in again, even with the sure promise of diamonds as big as pigeons’ eggs.

And yet I could have wept at leaving it. The greatest treasure, most likely, that has ever been stored in one place since men first learned to covet, lay there beyond reach. Only dynamite would have eaten through five feet of living rock, and we had none. So we left it, and I dare say it will remain, for I have a feeling—call it fancy if you like—that those stones will never shine on any living woman’s throat. They, and Foulata’s bones, will keep cold company until the last silence.

Still, disappointment was ungrateful of us, for we had not come away empty-handed. By a lucky thought I had stuffed the broad pockets of my old shooting-coat and trousers with gems before we escaped, and filled Foulata’s basket as well, though the water-bottle took up part of its space. During our tumble down the pit’s side a good many stones shook loose and were lost—several of the largest, that I had foolishly jammed on top in my coat. But a great quantity remained. Ninety-three large diamonds we counted later, from over two hundred carats down to seventy, besides a multitude of smaller ones. Coat and basket together held enough to make us, if not American millionaires, at least men of very considerable means—and to leave each of us stones enough for the finest sets of jewels in Europe. It is odd to think of, when I remember the taste of that cave-air and the sound—no, the absence of sound—around those coffers. Wealth so vast, and yet the ears hear only dripping water and one’s own breath.

When we reached Loo we were welcomed by Ignosi with real warmth. We found him in good health, already busy as a man must be who has taken a throne by spear and must now keep it by law. He was consolidating his power, shifting captains, rebuilding regiments thinned in the struggle with Twala. He listened to our tale with eyes that held it as a pot holds broth, taking in every bit; but when we told him of Gagool’s end, his face darkened into thought.

“Come hither,” he called to a very old Induna who sat with others in a circle, just beyond earshot.

The ancient man rose slowly, like a tree that has been leaning into the wind for many seasons, came forward, saluted, and sat.

“Thou art aged,” said Ignosi.

“Ay, my lord the king! Thy father’s father and I were born on the same day.”

“Tell me. When thou wast little, didst thou know Gagaoola the witch doctress?”

“Ay, my lord the king!”

“How was she then—young, like thee?”

“Not so, my lord the king! She was even as she is now and as she was in the days of my great grandfather before me; old and dried, very ugly, and full of wickedness.”

“She is no more; she is dead.”

“So, O king! then is an ancient curse taken from the land.”

“Go!”

“Koom! I go, Black Puppy, who tore out the old dog’s throat. Koom!”

When the old man had gone, Ignosi looked at us gravely. “Ye see, my brothers,” he said, “this was a strange woman, and I rejoice that she is dead. She would have let you die in the dark place, and mayhap afterwards she had found a way to slay me, as she found a way to slay my father, and set up Twala, whom her black heart loved, in his place. Now go on with the tale; surely there never was its like!”

So I told it all—our escape, our climb, our fall, the blind groping that ended in air and day—because we had agreed amongst ourselves that I should speak for the three. And when I had done, I chose my time, as one chooses the moment to take bread from the fire before it burns.

“And now, Ignosi,” I said, “the time has come for us to bid thee farewell, and start to see our own land once more. Behold, Ignosi, thou camest with us a servant, and now we leave thee a mighty king. If thou art grateful to us, remember to do even as thou didst promise: to rule justly, to respect the law, and to put none to death without a cause. So shalt thou prosper. To-morrow, at break of day, Ignosi, thou wilt give us an escort who shall lead us across the mountains. Is it not so, O king?”

He covered his face with his hands for a while, and in that hush I became aware of the sounds outside the hut—the distant cough of cattle, the soft pad of feet on dust, a child’s thin laugh. Power sits in a kraal as it sits in a kitchen: among small noises and daily needs. At last he spoke.

“My heart is sore,” he said; “your words split my heart in twain. What have I done to you, Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, that ye should leave me desolate? Ye who stood by me in rebellion and in battle, will ye leave me in the day of peace and victory? What will ye—wives? Choose from among the maidens! A place to live in? Behold, the land is yours as far as ye can see. The white man’s houses? Ye shall teach my people how to build them. Cattle for beef and milk? Every married man shall bring you an ox or a cow. Wild game to hunt? Does not the elephant walk through my forests, and the river-horse sleep in the reeds? Would ye make war? My Impis wait your word. If there is anything more which I can give, that will I give you.”

“Nay, Ignosi,” I answered, “we want none of these things; we would seek our own place.”

Then he flamed, wounded pride turning quickly to accusation as a fire takes dry grass. “Now do I learn,” he said bitterly, “that ye love the bright stones more than me, your friend. Ye have the stones; now ye would go to Natal and across the moving black water and sell them, and be rich, as it is the desire of a white man’s heart to be. Cursed for your sake be the white stones, and cursed he who seeks them. Death shall it be to him who sets foot in the place of Death to find them. I have spoken. White men, ye can go.”

I put my hand upon his arm. He was trembling; the king and the lonely man were at war inside him.

“Ignosi,” I said, “tell us, when thou didst wander in Zululand, and among the white people of Natal, did not thine heart turn to the land thy mother told thee of, thy native place, where thou didst see the light, and play when thou wast little, the land where thy place was?”

“It was even so, Macumazahn.”

“In like manner, Ignosi, do our hearts turn to our land and to our own place.”

There was a silence, full as a pot held just below boiling. When he spoke again, his voice had changed, and with it the whole air in the hut.

“I do perceive that now as ever thy words are wise and full of reason, Macumazahn; that which flies in the air loves not to run along the ground; the white man loves not to live on the level of the black or to house among his kraals. Well, ye must go, and leave my heart sore, because ye will be as dead to me, since from where ye are no tidings can come to me.

“But listen, and let all your brothers know my words. No other white man shall cross the mountains, even if any man live to come so far. I will see no traders with their guns and gin. My people shall fight with the spear, and drink water, like their forefathers before them. I will have no praying-men to put a fear of death into men’s hearts, to stir them up against the law of the king, and make a path for the white folk who follow to run on. If a white man comes to my gates I will send him back; if a hundred come I will push them back; if armies come, I will make war on them with all my strength, and they shall not prevail against me. None shall ever seek for the shining stones: no, not an army, for if they come I will send a regiment and fill up the pit, and break down the white columns in the caves and choke them with rocks, so that none can reach even to that door of which ye speak, and whereof the way to move it is lost. But for you three, Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, the path is always open; for, behold, ye are dearer to me than aught that breathes.

“And ye would go. Infadoos, my uncle, and my Induna, shall take you by the hand and guide you with a regiment. There is, as I have learned, another way across the mountains that he shall show you. Farewell, my brothers, brave white men. See me no more, for I have no heart to bear it. Behold! I make a decree, and it shall be published from the mountains to the mountains; your names, Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, shall be hlonipa even as the names of dead kings, and he who speaks them shall die. So shall your memory be preserved in the land for ever.

“Go now, ere my eyes rain tears like a woman’s. At times as ye look back down the path of life, or when ye are old and gather yourselves together to crouch before the fire, because for you the sun has no more heat, ye will think of how we stood shoulder to shoulder, in that great battle which thy wise words planned, Macumazahn; of how thou wast the point of the horn that galled Twala’s flank, Bougwan; whilst thou stood in the ring of the Greys, Incubu, and men went down before thine axe like corn before a sickle; ay, and of how thou didst break that wild bull Twala’s strength, and bring his pride to dust. Fare ye well for ever, Incubu, Macumazahn, and Bougwan, my lords and my friends.”

He rose and looked at us a few seconds—long enough to fix our faces in his mind as one fixes a tune by humming it once through. Then he threw the corner of his karross over his head, hiding his face. We went out in silence.

At dawn next day we left Loo, escorted by our old friend Infadoos—heartsick at parting—and by the regiment of the Buffaloes. Though the light was barely up, the main street was lined with people. They gave us the royal salute as we passed at the regiment’s head; and the women blessed us for ridding the land of Twala, throwing flowers before our feet. The petals were crushed under sandals and boots and spear-butts, giving off that bruised green sweetness that lilies and other soft things have when they are broken—life’s brief fragrance trampled into the road.

One ludicrous thing happened, and I was glad of it, for laughter is a kind of food when the heart is tight. Just before we reached the edge of the town, a pretty young girl ran out with lilies in her hands and offered them to Good—somehow all the women liked Good; I believe his eye-glass and solitary whisker gave him a ridiculous glamour—and then she said she had a boon to ask.

“Speak on,” said Good.

“Let my lord show his servant his beautiful white legs, that his servant may look upon them, and remember them all her days, and tell of them to her children; his servant has travelled four days’ journey to see them, for the fame of them has gone throughout the land.”

“I’ll be hanged if I do!” cried Good, scandalised.

“Come, come, my dear fellow,” said Sir Henry, “you can’t refuse to oblige a lady.”

“I won’t,” Good insisted, stiff as a ramrod. “It is positively indecent.”

But in the end he yielded, red with vexation, and drew his trousers up to the knee. The women broke into notes of admiration so frank and joyous that the very air seemed to ring with it, and the girl looked as if she had been given a feast. In that absurd guise he had to walk until we were clear of the town. Good’s legs, I suspect, will never again enjoy such fame. The Kukuanas tired more or less of his “melting teeth” and even of his “transparent eye,” but of his legs—never.

As we travelled, Infadoos told us of another way across the mountains, north of Solomon’s Great Road: a place where one might climb down the cliff-wall that divides Kukuanaland from the desert, not far from where the huge shapes of Sheba’s Breasts rise like watchers. It seemed that a little over two years before, Kukuana hunters searching for ostriches—whose plumes they prize for their war head-dresses—had descended by this path and, led far from the mountains, were nearly undone by thirst. Seeing trees on the horizon, they had struggled toward them and found a wide oasis, fertile and well-watered. By way of this oasis, Infadoos said, we might return, escaping the worst rigours of the mountain pass. Some of those very hunters now came with us as guides, and they said that from the oasis other green spots could be seen far out in the waste.

As he spoke, I found myself thinking of Ignosi’s mother, wandering with her child—how any woman could survive that crossing. Perhaps, as with so many things in life, the story changes its meaning when told in another path’s language: she may have come this way, as Hagar went into the wilderness, and been found by hunters before thirst finished her. There is always some unrecorded kindness, some small domestic mercy—water carried, fire lit, a mouth fed—that saves a legend from ending as a death-song.

We travelled easily, and on the fourth night we found ourselves once more on the mountain crest. At our feet the desert rolled away in sandy billows, and we were, by Infadoos’ reckoning, some twenty-five miles north of Sheba’s Breasts. At dawn next day the guides led us to the lip of a fearful chasm down which we were to descend to the plain two thousand feet below. There we bade farewell to Infadoos, that true friend and tough old warrior. He wished us all good with great solemnity, and grief swelled in him until he near wept.

“Never, my lords,” he said, “shall mine old eyes see the like of you again. Ah! the way that Incubu cut his men down in the battle! Ah! for the sight of that stroke with which he swept off my brother Twala’s head! It was beautiful—beautiful! I may never hope to see such another, except perchance in happy dreams.”

We were sorry indeed to part from him. Good was so moved that he gave him, as a keepsake—what do you think?—an eye-glass. Later we discovered it was a spare. Infadoos was delighted, seeing at once how such a wonder would fatten his prestige. After several attempts, and much squinting and muttering, he managed to screw it into his own eye. Anything more absurd than that old warrior—leopard-skin cloak, black ostrich plumes, stern face—peering through a white man’s glass, I have never seen. The thing did not belong to his world, and yet he wore it as proudly as if it had grown there; a new word forced into an old mouth.

Then, after he had seen that our guides were properly laden with water and provisions, and after the Buffaloes had sent a thundering farewell salute crashing along the cliff, we wrung his hand and began the descent.

It was a hard business—hands scraping stone, muscles shaking, breath coming in hot pulls that tasted of dust and iron. But by evening we were at the bottom without accident, and made a small fire whose smoke rose straight up into the cold, still air beneath the beetling cliffs.

“Do you know,” Sir Henry said, as we sat watching the flames and looking up at that wall of rock, “I think there are worse places than Kukuanaland in the world, and I have known unhappier times than the last month or two, though I have never spent such queer ones. Eh, you fellows?”

“I almost wish I were back,” said Good, and sighed as if he could hear again the women’s admiration in Loo and, behind it, another voice that was gone.

As for myself, I reflected that all’s well that ends well; but in a long life of shaves I never had such shaves as those we had lately endured. The memory of that battle can still make me feel cold all over, and our time in the treasure chamber—there are things the tongue can name, but never properly convey, because the body remembers them first: the pressure of darkness, the thirst that turns the mouth to leather, the sound of one drop of water falling as if the whole world were reduced to that single note.

Next morning we set out across the desert. Our five guides carried a good supply of water, slung and stoppered, precious as blood. We marched until night and slept in the open, and marched again at dawn. The desert has its own acoustic tyranny: in the day, the hush is so wide it presses on the ears; at night, each small sound—foot on sand, the click of a pebble, the faint hiss of wind—seems magnified, as though the world had been emptied to make room for it. It is a relief, in its way, after the crowded noises of a town; and yet it can unman you, for silence, too, can be a kind of roar.

By noon on the third day we saw trees darkening the horizon—the oasis the hunters had promised. Within an hour of sundown we were walking on grass again, and the sound of running water reached us like a remembered language: not merely noise, but comfort, the old domestic assurance that life may be boiled, washed, drunk, and made clean.
