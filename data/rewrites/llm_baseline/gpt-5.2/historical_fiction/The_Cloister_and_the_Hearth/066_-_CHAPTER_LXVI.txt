Two nights after, Pietro Vanucci and Andrea sat with the board laid and supper cooling itself into reproach, waiting on Gerard.

The lamp made a small island of light on the table. Beyond it the lodging was all shadow and ticking stillness, broken only by the faint creak of the settling beams and the distant night-noises of Rome—an oar-charm from some late boatman far off, a dog that barked once and thought better of it, the thin cry of a seller packing up his tray. But here, within, the loudest thing was the food losing its voice: the crackle of a crust gone quiet, the steam no longer singing from the dish, the smell of warm bread turning from invitation to accusation.

Pietro had done what he always did when his mind was uncertain: he had made order. The trencher was set straight. The knife lay just so. A jug of common wine stood breathing out its tartness. He had even contrived a modest comfort—a little pan of onions softened in oil till they were sweet at the edge, and a handful of herbs bruised between finger and thumb for the scent, because Gerard, when he ate at all of late, had been won less by hunger than by fragrance. Andrea hovered about the table like a faithful hound, sniffing and listening in turns, as if the very air might bring news.

Nine o’clock went by, and the supper, once so full of promise, grew dull and stale, as if it too had lost heart.

Pietro’s patience, which was never fat, began to lean and show its bones.

“At this hour,” said he, drumming his fingers, “a man is either eating, or sleeping, or dead. Go—try his chamber. Perhaps the philosopher hath come in like a ghost and thinks none see him.”

Andrea shrugged, the gesture of one who obeys and doubts in the same breath, and went.

He came back without Gerard; yet he carried something between finger and thumb as carefully as a spice that costs dear. It was a slip of paper.

Andrea could not read—no more than the charity lads that stumble at their letters with a stick at their back—but his eye was quick, and he had learned the look of Pietro’s name upon a page as one learns the shape of a loaf before it is cut.

“That is for thee, I trow,” said he, with a kind of proud craft. “See—the words have thy face.”

Pietro snatched it, and as he read his voice tried to be light, as men do when they feel their insides go cold.

“‘Dear Pietro, dear Andrea, life is too great a burden.’”

He gave a short laugh, and made a jest of it as if jesting could salt away fear and keep it from rotting.

“‘So ’tis, my lad,’” said he aloud, turning the line as though he spoke to Gerard across the table, “‘but that is no reason for being abroad at supper-time. Supper is not a burden.’”

He read on; the lamp flame wavered as if it listened.

“‘Wear my habits!’”

Pietro’s lip curled; he would have mocked, but his cheek had gone pale and his teeth chattered, betraying his brave disdain.

“‘Said the poplar to the juniper bush.’”

He ran his eye downward.

“‘And thou, Andrea, mine amethyst ring; and me in both your hearts a month or two.’”

Pietro looked up sharply; there are gifts that are blessings, and gifts that are last wills. The mention of the ring—Gerard’s ring, so often turned and stared at in brooding—hit Andrea like a blow.

“Why, Andrea?” Pietro said, with a forced levity that cracked at the edge.

But he could not hold it; the paper seemed to grow heavier in his hand. He read the next words, and now the room, with its cooling supper and its obedient stillness, felt suddenly like a chamber where a corpse lies.

“‘For my body, ere this ye read, it will lie in Tiber. Trouble not to look for it. ’Tis not worth the pains. Oh unhappy day that it was born—oh happy night that rids me of it.’”

Pietro’s mouth opened, but no jest came out. Only a dry breath.

“‘Adieu! adieu!

“‘The broken-hearted Gerard.’”

He tried one more time to wrap his fear in sarcasm, as one wraps bitter herbs in bread.

“Here is a sorry jest of the peevish rogue,” said he.

Yet his face was the face of a man who has tasted ashes where he looked for salt; and Andrea, hearing those words, did not believe them. He burst like a jar struck by accident—sound pouring out of him, raw and uncontrollable.

“O, miserable day! O, calamity of calamities! Gerard, my friend, my sweet patron! Help! help! He is killing himself! Oh, good people, help me save him!”

He filled the house with cries. Doors opened. Feet shuffled. Voices rose, drowsy at first, then sharp with alarm. Andrea ran into the street bareheaded, his hair tossed by the night air, imploring every soul he met as if Christ might be found at any corner.

A knot of people gathered—some in nightcaps, some with cloaks flung over their shirts, all reluctant, as folk are reluctant to step from their own hearth into another man’s despair.

“Down to the river?” said one, and spat as if the word itself was unlucky. “No. What business is that of mine?”

“What part of the river?” said another. “It is a wild goose chase.”

“And after sunset?” muttered an old woman, crossing herself. “Too many ghosts walk those banks all night. The water takes what it takes.”

Andrea’s sobbing would not move them; grief is a noise that many men shut their ears against, lest it become their own.

But a lackey—one who had been standing some time opposite the house, watching with the patient stillness of paid eyes—stepped forward.

“I will go,” said he.

His readiness shamed three or four younger men into following, for youth is proud of courage when it costs little. So this small band set off toward the Tiber, Andrea leading like a hound on a scent, the lackey close behind him.

As they walked, the street-sounds altered. There was less laughter and less clatter of pots from taverns. The city’s voice thinned and grew wary. Andrea, gulping tears, told the lackey of the letter and of Gerard’s moody ways of late—the long silences, the untouched bread, the way he stared at the wine as if it were a medicine he dared not drink. Their Italian tongues went fast, words tumbling over one another, until they neared the river.

Then, at the first breath of the Tiber’s air, speech died in every mouth.

The river has its own smell: green and cold, with mud beneath, and a faint rot of reeds. It slid into their nostrils and into their minds, and with it came the hush that falls on men when the world’s great, indifferent things draw near. The night was a calm spring night, the moon bright and clean in a cloudless sky. Their feet, that had seemed ordinary a moment before, now sounded loud and ominous on the stones, as if each step were a knock on some locked door.

Presently, hurrying round a corner, they met a man.

He stopped at sight of them, hesitated as one caught in a net, and the moon showed his face and head. He was bareheaded; his hair clung in wet strands and glittered like fish-scales. His clothes were drenched, water darkening them from collar to hem, and drops fell from him to the ground with a steady patter.

“Here he is!” cried one of the young men, who did not know Gerard’s face and shape well enough to read them in a glance.

At the cry, the stranger turned and fled.

They ran after him with might and main, the sound of their chase striking off the walls. Andrea, desperate as hunger, gained on him; the lackey was next, silent and quick. The fugitive twisted suddenly into a narrow alley. Andrea, too hot and too swift to check himself, shot past the turning, and the others, stumbling after, found themselves in a little labyrinth of lanes where a quickfooted man who knew every stone could vanish like breath from a cup.

For a moment they tried to follow him by ear alone—listening for the slap of wet shoes, the rasp of a breath—but the city swallowed those sounds the way a market swallows a single voice.

They came back, panting, to the spot where the man had stood, and found those who had not run standing there, staring like men in a dream. Pietro, who had followed with the others as far as his fear would drive him, spoke now with a strained certainty.

“That was not he,” said he. “The fugitive had neither the features nor the stature of Gerard.”

“Are ye verily sure?” they demanded, uneasy. “He had been in the river. Why, in the saints’ names, fled he at our approach?”

Pietro’s wit, that usually pricked like vinegar, had gone flat; he looked from face to face, seeking sense where none lay ready.

“Friends,” said he, “methinks this has nought to do with him we seek.” Then, turning upon Andrea, whose eyes were swollen with weeping and whose mouth trembled, “What shall we do, Andrea?”

Here the lackey put in his word, calm as a man who has rehearsed his part.

“Let us track him to the water’s side, to make sure,” said he. “See, he hath come dripping all the way.”

The advice pleased them, for it required action and promised an end. They bent themselves to it. The drops were plain enough on the stones in that moonlight—dark coins spilled from a purse. With very little difficulty they followed the trail.

And soon they met a new enigma.

They had gone scarcely fifty yards when the drops turned away from the river and led them to the gate of a large gloomy building that loomed up like a black cliff: a monastery. They stopped before it, irresolute, and gazed at the dark pile. It seemed to them to hide some horrible mystery, as if men could wash their sins and their secrets alike in the same water.

For a moment none spoke. Even Andrea’s sobbing paused, as if the night itself listened.

But presently Andrea, peering down, gave a shout.

“Here be the drops again!” cried he. “And this road leadeth to the river.”

They resumed the track. Soon it became plain that the drops were now leading them back toward their own quarter, as if the man had run in circles like a hare that doubles. The closer they drew to the Tiber, the wetter the track grew, until the stones shone with it. At last it took them to the river’s edge.

There, upon the bank, a bucketful seemed to have been flung out of the stream—one heavy splash, and then trickles running back. The sight made them all cry out at once; hope is quick to leap at any shape that resembles its desire. But reflection came hard on its heels.

Certainly a man had been in the water, and had come out again in safety; but that man was not Gerard. The river lay as smooth as oil under the moon, giving back light without giving back truth. It made no sound but the small whisper of itself against stone and weed—voiceless, as if it took pride in keeping what it knew.

One of the men said he knew a fisherman hard by, a fellow with nets and drags.

They found him, roused him, and paid him liberally—silver now, and the promise of gold if he found the body. He listened, half asleep, with the slow, wary look of one who knows the river’s appetite and does not like to bargain with it. Yet money has its own voice, and he agreed.

So nets were sunk below the place, and the dragging went on above and below, ropes grating, water slapping, the fisherman’s men calling to one another in low tones that carried oddly over the calm surface. The sound of rope and water, of men’s short orders and answers, seemed to cut the night into strips.

Andrea and Pietro ran up and down the bank, calling and listening, as if Gerard might answer from behind a willow or from under a boat. The city behind them slept, and the river before them flowed on with that quiet that is not peace but indifference, holding this and a thousand more strange secrets. Now and then a night-bird gave a thin note; now and then the distant bell of a convent marked time with a soft iron mouth. Those sounds—so few, so clear—made their hearts feel larger and more empty, as if the whole world were a listening ear.

At last Andrea, with a cry of sudden hope, ran back toward the house. Pietro watched him go, helpless, as if hope were now something that must be chased on foot.

In less than half an hour Andrea returned. His face told the tale before his tongue could shape it.

“No,” he groaned, and wrung his hands. “No!”

The lackey, who had not spent his breath in running, asked coldly, “What is the hour?”

“Four hours past midnight,” said Andrea, as if each word were a stone laid on a grave.

The lackey drew himself up with a solemnity that might have been pity, or might have been performance.

“My pretty lad,” said he, “say a mass for thy friend’s soul: for he is not among living men.”

The morning broke at last, pale and reluctant. The moon’s white authority faded; the river took on the dull color of pewter. The city’s first sounds—carts beginning far away, a rooster somewhere daring to declare the day—came like insults to their vigil.

Worn out with fatigue, Andrea and Pietro went home, heart-sick. The supper on the table was cold now, its smells turned sour, as if the very food had forgotten its purpose. The days rolled on, mute as the Tiber as to Gerard’s fate.
