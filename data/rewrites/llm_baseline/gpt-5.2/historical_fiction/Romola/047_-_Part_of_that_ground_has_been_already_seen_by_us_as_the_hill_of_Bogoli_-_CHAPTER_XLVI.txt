The evening had come down early and sullen, with a thin rain that seemed less to fall than to breathe upon the stones. Romola was returning from San Matteo after vespers, Maso plodding at her side with the lantern held low so the wavering light ran along the wet walls and picked out the glisten of puddles like spilled oil. In the muffled streets, sound carried strangely: the soft slap of their steps, the lantern’s faint clink, a shutter banging once and then settling into a steady complaint, as if even the wood were uneasy under the weather.

Near San Marco she met Tito as he was coming out from the monastery. He had been in and out of the Via de’ Bardi all day without quite meeting her—so it had been for a long while now: two lives moving in the same house like parallel channels, kept from cutting into each other by careful banks. He proposed at once to see her home and, with the same easy command that made obedience look like courtesy, sent Maso away because the old man’s short steps “wearied” him.

It was the sort of attention Tito paid only when the street itself seemed to demand it. Between husband and wife there was no open quarrel, no storm that cleared the air. They had passed beyond the stage where remonstrance is still an attempt at agreement. They spoke of public matters and of household matters with the guarded precision of people reading from different copies of the same text. If Tito wished a supper in the old library—now made into a banqueting-room with pleasant hangings and polished boards—Romola assented and saw that the bread was warmed, the wine drawn, the knives set straight. If she needed money for her charities, it was found, as if by accident, among the accounts. In return she helped him with transcriptions and digests, laying out his notes with the careful hand of one bred among manuscripts and meanings.

And yet, throughout the day, he would avoid meeting her eyes, as if a glance were a kind of oath; he would pretend to suppose she was absent rather than seek her in her own room, and would jest—lightly, like someone tapping a thin crust to see if it would break—that she preferred solitude to his society. Once, after her return from that flight she had renounced, she had tried, timidly, to go back toward frankness. But Romola could not come to peace except by open speech—by laying the wound bare and asking what could heal it. Tito could only be brought back to her by a tenderness in her that would have required oblivion. He wanted the old fondness without the old truth. She could be gentle, submissive, exact in all duties; she could keep down the involuntary recoil. But she could not counterfeit warmth, any more than yesterday’s bread can be made new by polishing its crust.

She knew it, and he knew it. That knowledge made her, to him, a danger—one more reason to keep her outside of secrets. In politics he had sought to persuade her that the Medici cause was hopeless and that, both in theory and prudence, he served the popular government in which she now had a fervent stake. But impressions, subtle as odours caught in a passageway, made her uneasy about his relations with San Marco. She was torn between dreading to see what would confirm her suspicion and a restless impulse to watch, lest harm should be done that she might have stopped.

As they walked, Tito said, with that agreeable smoothness which could make even exertion sound like a compliment, “My business is not ended yet. I will conduct you to our door, Romola, and then I must fulfil another commission. It may take me an hour before I can return and rest—which I greatly need.”

He began to speak of Pisa, of things seen and people met, as if the mind could be fed on travellers’ anecdotes the way a household is fed on soups and stews: by small additions, by patient simmering, by a cook’s knack of making yesterday serve for to-day. Romola listened without answers, hearing beneath his words the faint percussion of rain and the distant mutter of a city settling into night.

They were nearing a loggia where a lamp burned before a painted Madonna. The light, caught in the damp, made a pale halo in the air; the smell there was of wet plaster and lamp-oil, and in the sheltered hollow of the arcade someone had lately stood with a cloak that still held the scent of smoke and wine. The street had been quiet, but now the sound of many footsteps swelled toward them, with voices blurred together as if the street itself had taken a mouthful of talk and was chewing it.

“We shall not reach home without a soaking,” Tito said quickly, and with a slight startled movement hurried Romola up into the loggia. “Let us shelter a moment.”

“It is only a drizzling rain,” Romola said, surprised. “Why should we wait?”

“No,” he answered. “I feel it thickening.”

But it was not the rain he had felt. With the habitual wakefulness of a mind trained to danger and advantage, he had caught, in the lamp’s glimmer, a red feather and the glitter of a sword-hilt among the approaching group—marks of a man he least wished to meet here, with Romola at his side.

He had already had one interview that day with Dolfo Spini, and the “commission” he spoke of was a second meeting with him—a sequel to Tito’s visit at San Marco. For Tito had long been shaping a plan with the delicacy of a falsifier trimming vellum to fit a stolen seal. He had been the bearer of letters to Savonarola—letters forged with consummate care; and one, by a stratagem bold enough to delight a cynical mind, bore the very name and seal of the Cardinal of Naples, that Cardinal who in Rome had exerted himself most for the Frate.

Those letters declared that the Cardinal, travelling from Pisa, was unwilling for strong reasons to enter Florence, yet eager at this juncture to take counsel with Savonarola. He would pause at San Casciano that very day—ten miles from the city—and at dawn would ride out in the plain garb of a priest and meet Savonarola, as if by chance, five miles along the Florence road, two hours after sunrise.

The plot that opened with those letters was simple enough in its cruelty. Dolfo Spini and a band of Compagnacci were to lie in ambush at a lonely spot beyond the gates. They were to seize Savonarola, along with the Dominican who must accompany him, and deliver both to a small detachment of Milanese horse waiting near San Casciano, to carry them into Roman territory. In Florence the Frate’s friends already thought it necessary he should be guarded when he preached; beyond the walls he would be on a road where even an honest cry can be swallowed by hedges and wet air.

There was always the chance Savonarola would suspect a trap. He had for some time avoided risking himself outside the city. On that, Tito’s scheme depended: giving him too little time to weigh and doubt, letting the Frate’s hunger—his eager longing to knit together forces within and without the Church toward a General Council, and his hope of immediate service from a powerful cardinal—overrun his caution.

Tito had insisted on putting the letters into Savonarola’s hand alone and, with art that could make inference sound like innocence, had admitted that he could guess their purport: a rendezvous outside the gates. He urged the Frate to ask the Signoria for an armed guard and offered—privately—to carry the request. Savonarola replied briefly that it was impossible: privacy and a guard could not go together. His eye flashed; Tito read the flash as resolve.

Whether Savonarola went or stayed, Tito believed himself secure. If the Frate fell, there were diamonds in question, and papal favour. If he did not, Tito’s adroitness would have strengthened him with Savonarola and with Spini; and any confidence wrung from either made him more valuable still to Medicean interests. He had the cold assurance of a man who tastes all dishes and is loyal to none, finding every flavour hollow except the one that feeds his own advantage.

Only Spini was an awkward companion. His cunning delighted in plots, but his mind lacked the tight-lidded control secrecy needs. He drank, and when wine loosened him he could become affectionate and loud—one of those Florentine “Beoni” whose laughter seems at first harmless until it begins to spill names and hints like overfilled cups. Though he and Tito had agreed to greet each other in public with coolness, there was always the risk that, in a sudden encounter, Spini would blurt and clasp and call him comrade before witnesses.

Now Tito’s hope was that Spini had not recognized him. The lamp had thrown Tito’s face into sudden light and shadow like a coin turned in the hand; and Tito, with his marked grace and foreign-bright features, was as easy to know as a captain with a red feather. Romola, in her dark shroud, had not been noticed; she stood behind Tito’s shoulder, tucked into the corner of the loggia where the air smelt of damp stone.

The hope lasted only a breath.

“Ha! my carrier-pigeon!” grated Spini’s harsh voice—meant for an undertone and all the more ugly for it. His hand came down on Tito’s shoulder with the familiarity of a blow. “Why did you run into hiding? You didn’t know comrades were coming. It’s well I caught you—it saves time. What of the chase to-morrow morning? Will the bald-headed game rise? Are the falcons ready?”

Tito felt his lips blanch. If rage had been native to him, it would have flamed now against this bull-faced accomplice—too clumsy to lead, too noisy to serve. But his excitement was the quick calculation of danger: how to stop Spini without making Romola’s suspicion blaze into certainty. To hush him outright might sharpen her watchfulness into action; to repel him angrily might make the wine-heavy Compagnaccio savage. Tito chose a third path—proof that Romola still held one power over him: the power of dread.

He pressed her hand, a small pressure meant as warning or command, and said, in a tone of hearty comradeship, “Yes, Dolfo, you may prepare in all security. But take no trumpets with you.”

“Don’t be afraid,” Spini answered, piqued. “No need to play Ser Saccente with me. I know where the devil keeps his tail as well as you. What—did he swallow the bait whole? The prophetic nose didn’t scent the hook?”

He lowered his voice a little, with the blundering air of a man who thinks secrecy consists in bending his head rather than in keeping his tongue still. Tito thought, with a cold inward disgust, He will never be satisfied till he has emptied the bag.

Aloud Tito said, “Swallowed all as easily as you swallow a cup of Trebbiano. Ha! I see torches—there must be a dead body coming. They say the pestilence spreads.”

The smell of torches—pitch and resin—was indeed beginning to taint the damp air, and their light flickered on the wet street like fire playing over fish-scales.

“Santiddio! I hate the sight of biers,” Spini muttered, and moved off in haste. “Good-night.”

The torches were real, but they did not escort a corpse. They went before a church dignitary returning homeward; Tito had used the hint of death and plague as one might throw an unpleasant spice into a pot to make a guest push it away.

When Spini had gone, Tito turned to Romola and spoke quietly, as if he were smoothing a wrinkled cloth. “Do not be alarmed by anything that _bestia_ has said. We can go on now—the rain has not increased.”

But Romola was already trembling with a resolute indignation that made her breath short. Tito’s unconcern only hardened her distrust; she felt every easy tone as a hand pressing her down.

“I will not go on,” she said. “I will not move nearer home until I have some security against this treachery being done.”

“Wait, at least, until these torches have passed,” Tito answered, perfectly self-possessed; and beneath that self-possession a new dislike rose—cold, hard, unimpassioned—at the thought that his wife might thwart him, not with tears but with will.

The torches came by with the Vicario dell’Arcivescovo. Tito did reverence; Romola saw only forms and light, heard the soft murmur of those who accompanied him. The city’s soundscape pressed close: footsteps on wet stone, the hiss of rain on flame, the distant call of a watchman, all making a net in which her thoughts struggled.

If, to prevent this betrayal she believed in with the force of long foreboding, she had needed at that instant to seize Tito and fling herself and him into a ravine, she felt she could have done it. Union with him? The discipline of two years, the daily self-quelling, seemed wiped away. In its place there was only a terrible clearness: they were divided.

They were nearly in darkness again. Only the Madonna’s lamp behind them gave a thin outline to Tito’s profile and left Romola’s face half-hidden under her veil.

“Tell me the truth, Tito,” she said, low and shaken. “This time tell me the truth. It will be safer for you.”

“Why should I desire to tell you anything else, my angry saint?” Tito replied, and a slight contempt ran under the words like bitter lees under clear wine. “Since the truth is precisely that which should make you rejoice—that my knowledge of a plot of Spini’s enables me to secure the Frate from falling victim to it.”

“What is the plot?”

“That I decline to tell,” he said. “It is enough that the Frate’s safety will be secured.”

“It is a plot to draw him beyond the gates, that Spini may murder him.”

“There has been no intention of murder,” Tito said smoothly. “It is a plot to compel him to obey the Pope’s summons to Rome. But as I serve the popular government, and believe the Frate’s presence here is necessary to maintain it at present, I choose to prevent his departure. You may sleep to-night with an easy mind.”

Romola was silent a moment. In the silence she heard the drip from the loggia’s edge and the far-off thrum of tavern voices—men warmed by wine and talk, careless of what their noise did to the night. Then she said, with anguish that made the words heavy, “Tito, it is of no use. I have no belief in you.”

He shrugged; even in dimness she saw the gesture: palms spread, as if to show empty hands. The cold anger of a man who does not burn but hardens was setting within him.

“If the Frate leaves the city—if any harm comes to him,” Romola said after a pause, her voice changing into a steadier iron, “I will declare what I have heard to the Signoria, and you will be disgraced. What if I am your wife?” The words came faster, as if once spoken they must be driven home. “I will be disgraced with you. If we are united, I am that part of you that will save you from crime. Others shall not be betrayed.”

“I am quite aware of what you would be likely to do, _anima mia_,” Tito answered in the coolest liquidity of his tone. “Therefore, if you have a small amount of reasoning at your disposal just now, consider that if you believe me in nothing else, you may believe me when I say I will take care of myself—and not put it in your power to ruin me.”

“Then you assure me the Frate is warned—he will not go beyond the gates?”

“He shall not go beyond the gates.”

Even then distrust did not loosen its grip. It sat in her mind like an unclean smell that clings after the pot is scoured.

“I will go back to San Marco now and find out,” Romola said, making a movement as if to turn.

“You shall not!” Tito whispered bitterly, seizing both her wrists with the full force of his grip. “I am master of you. You shall not set yourself in opposition to me.”

Footsteps were approaching. Tito had heard them; that was why he kept his voice low. Romola, mastered, did not struggle—partly because her pride recoiled from such a contest in the street, partly because she too heard the coming voices and felt the eyes that might turn upon them. The grasp on her wrists—this blunt assertion of his physical predominance—sent through her not a vulgar fury but a shuddering horror, as if she had been forced to touch something she could not wash from her skin.

They moved out from the loggia and walked on under the small, steady rain. For a while neither spoke. The first rush of alarm and indignation began to ebb in Romola, leaving behind a heavier mixture: habits of self-questioning, memories of impulse subdued, that proud reserve which discipline had never changed. In her earlier vehemence, open opposition to the husband from whom her soul revolted had seemed almost a temptation because it was so simple. Now she felt again the old check—the sense of boundaries, of consequences, of a dignity that would not stoop even to save itself.

She began to cling, as a starving person clings to the hope of a crust, to the probabilities that might spare her from a desperate course. Tito would not risk betrayal by her; now that she knew of the plot, whatever his first purpose, he must reckon with the fact. She needed only to keep over him the certainty that if he deceived her her lips would not remain closed. And it was possible—yes, she must hold to that possibility until it was shattered—that Tito had never meant to aid in betraying the Frate.

Tito, too, was busy within himself. He walked as if his feet had learned the streets by heart while his mind ran its own errands. He did not speak again until they were near home, where the familiar stones and the doorway’s shadow seemed to promise safety simply because they were known.

“Well, Romola,” he said then, “have you had time to recover calmness? If so, you can supply your want of belief in me by a little rational inference. You can see, I presume, that if I had intended to further Spini’s plot, I should now be aware that having a fair Piagnone for my wife—who knows the secret—would be a serious obstacle.”

He chose the tone easiest to him now: not pleading, which he guessed would fail, but cool reasoning, as if arranging accounts on a table.

“Yes, Tito,” she said in a low voice, “I think you believe that I would guard the Republic from further treachery. You are right. If the Frate is betrayed, I will denounce you.”

She paused, and the effort to soften came like swallowing something dry. “But it was not so. I have perhaps spoken too hastily—you never meant it. Only…why will you seem to be that man’s comrade?”

“Such relations are inevitable to practical men, Romola,” he replied, pleased to see her struggling back toward control. “You fair creatures live in the clouds. Pray go to rest with an easy heart.”

He opened the door for her, and the house received them with its familiar quiet—quiet that, like a well-managed kitchen, can hide the heat and the sharp tools that make its order possible.
