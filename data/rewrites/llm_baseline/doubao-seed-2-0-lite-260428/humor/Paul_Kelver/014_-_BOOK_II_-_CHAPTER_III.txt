The sun blared through my garret window like a nosy neighbour shoving their face against the glass at full volume, and I jolted awake so fast I nearly launched myself right off the lumpy straw mattress. The low roar of London streets below was already in full swing, loud and unapologetic, reminding me the rest of the world had already finished their first cuppa, ticked off two errands, and was openly judging me for still horizontal. I fumbled for my watch on the wobbly dressing table that looked one sneeze away from collapsing entirely, only to find it had vanished. My clothes were strewn across the floor like a toddler’s discarded toy dump, one boot perched snootily on the armchair by the fireplace like a retired aristocrat, the other nowhere to be seen – almost certainly hiding to avoid being associated with whatever mess I’d made the night before.

My skull felt like it had swollen to twice its normal size overnight. For half a second I idly wondered if I’d accidentally swapped heads with Minikin while drunk, and forgotten to grab my own back; this new top-heavy monstrosity was throbbing so hard, every pulse felt like a small man with a hammer was doing construction work behind my eyebrows.

Then the previous night’s memories hit me all at once, and I snapped fully awake. The nearby church steeple started chiming, and I counted carefully. Eleven o’clock. I leapt out of bed – and immediately plopped straight down onto the floor. The whole room was still waltzing, you see. Not whirling, not violently spinning, just staggering slowly in circles like it was also nursing a hangover and trying not to knock over the furniture after a 12-hour all-night dance party. I crawled to the washbasin, missed twice, finally dunked my throbbing head in cold water, hauled my trousers on with three failed attempts, and collapsed into the beat-up armchair to untangle the mess, starting from the here and now and working backwards.

I was very obviously ill. Something had definitely disagreed with me.
“That terrible strong cigar,” I whispered to myself weakly. “I never should’ve touched it. And that stuffy little crowded room. Also I’ve been working far too hard. I definitely just need more exercise, that’s all.”
I congratulated myself briefly for being so sharp I couldn’t be bamboozled by my own bad decisions – until my inner snark cut in.
“Nonsense, you berk,” it snapped. “Stop lying. You were completely, utterly, disgracefully drunk.”
“Not *drunk*,” I whined. “That’s such a rude, coarse word! I’ve heard some people just can’t handle sweet champagne. It’s my liver. My liver’s delicate. Let’s call it a liver issue, please.”
“Drunk,” my inner voice insisted, unrelenting. “Hopelessly, vulgarly drunk. Drunker than any bloke named ‘Arry stumbling home after a Bank Holiday fair.”
“It’s my first time,” I mumbled pitifully.
“It’s your first ever chance to get properly wasted, you mean,” it shot back.
“I’ll never touch a drop again!” I promised.
“Sure you will,” it retorted. “That’s the exact line every drunk in every temperance pamphlet uses right before they sneak a flask out of their coat pocket next weekend. You’re 19, you don’t even have the excuse of being an old, tired man. What if this becomes a habit? What if you end up a ragged, staggering sot dying alone in a ditch?”

I dropped my heavy head into my hands and groaned. Every single Sunday school temperance tale I’d ever sat through came flooding back, and I could vividly picture myself stumbling down a path of ruin: disappointing my mom, making my dad’s face go pale, getting that scornful, withering look from Barbara that made her whole mouth curl, and having Hal mock me so mercilessly for being a weak idiot I’d never live it down. Shame and panic mixed together to make me feel even worse.
“I’m leaving this house right now!” I yelped out loud, dramatic as only a 19 year old can be. “I’ll get far away from this terrible unwholesome vibe, wipe all of last night from my memory, start a whole new fresh life –”
And then the memory that had been skulking at the back of my brain stepped front and centre. Miss Rosina Sellars. The barmaid. 160 pounds of very determined woman. What was she doing here? What right did she have to ruin my new, sober, regenerated life?
“The right of your brand new fiancée,” my inner voice said, with a dry little cackle.
“Did I actually propose to her?” I whispered, horrified.
“Let’s not go into the details,” it replied. “We don’t want to relive them. But that’s the end result.”
“I was not myself! I didn’t know what I was doing!”
“Yeah, well no one’s ever fully in their right mind when they do stupid things, but you still have to deal with the consequences. Half a dozen people saw you do it, and she’s not the kind of woman who’ll let a perfectly good ‘foolish proposal’ slip through her fingers. You’re gonna end up married, living in two tiny rooms, meeting all her weird relatives who all think she’s the fanciest member of the family – which tells you everything you need to know about them. Great start to your grand ambitious career as a famous writer, genius.”
“What the hell do I do?” I asked.
“Beats me,” it answered.

I spent the entire rest of the day holed up in my room, door locked, too ashamed to even face Mrs Peedles or the 12 year old slavey who brought my bread and butter round. At dusk, when I felt slightly less like I’d been run over by a cart, I snuck out to the nearby coffee shop for a sad meal of unsugared tea and a kipper. But then another guy sat down opposite me and ordered hot buttered toast, which smelled far too rich for my fragile stomach, so I bolted before the waiter even brought it out.
At 8pm Minikin turned up from the office to see what the hell had happened. I was too ashamed to lie, so I told him the whole sordid truth.
“Thought as much,” he said, nodding. “Looks like you had an A1 bender, mate.”
“I’m glad it’s over,” I told him earnestly. “It’s a lesson I’ll never forget. Nothing’ll make me touch a drink again.”
“Right,” Minikin said, grinning. “Nothing beats a proper all-night drunk to make you feel holier than a preacher fresh off a three-hour sermon. I’ve done it myself, I know.”
I was desperate for advice, and even though he was younger than me, Minikin was shockingly worldly. “The worst part isn’t the hangover,” I confessed. “I accidentally got engaged.”
“Quiet little you? No one’s more feral than the shy ones when they finally cut loose,” he commented. “Nice girl?”
“I don’t know!” I moaned. “I just know I don’t want to marry her. How do I get out of this?”
Minikin plucked his removable glass left eye out and started polishing it on his handkerchief – his little ritual for when he was thinking hard, like he could pull inspiration right off the glass. “Is she the sort of girl who’ll fight tooth and nail to make you keep your promise?”
I nodded. That was a perfect description of Rosina Sellars.
“Does she know how little you earn?”
“She knows I live up here in an attic and boil my own eggs for dinner half the time,” I said.
Minikin glanced around the tiny, messy room. “Wow. She must really fancy you, then.”
“She thinks I’m a genius who’s gonna be famous one day,” I explained. “She’s willing to wait for me.”
Minikin popped his polished glass eye back into his head. “Then just let her wait. There’s ages before you have to do anything about it.”
“But she’s a barmaid!” I wailed. “She’s gonna make me walk her round the park on Sundays, meet all her cousins, go to her family tea parties! I can’t do that. And when I actually do get successful? She’s never letting go. This is a disaster!”
“How’d you even propose anyway?” he asked.
“I have no idea,” I groaned. “I don’t remember a single second of it until I woke up today.”
“Any witnesses?”
“Half a dozen of them, all of her friends.”
The door flew open without a knock – Jarman, obviously, the man who had never heard of a door that required prior warning. He didn’t do his usual loud hello, just crossed the room, shook my hand so hard my arm wiggled, and looked at me sombre. “You look like you survived a shipwreck,” he said. “This your mate?”
I introduced them.
“Pleased to meet you,” Jarman said.
“Glad to hear it,” Minikin replied. “Doesn’t look like you’ve got much else to be pleased about in life.”
“Don’t mind him,” I told Jarman. “He was born that rude. It’s a medical condition.”
“Gifted, that is,” Jarman said, nodding. “I should hire myself out as a professional party disrupter, that talent’d make me a fortune tramping round suburban evening dos. Shame you weren’t here last night, you could’ve saved our lad a whole world of trouble. He told you he accidentally got engaged?”
I nodded, confirming I’d spilled everything.
“Alright then, you’ve got a sharp practical eye,” Jarman said, leaning forward to stare at Minikin’s glass eye. “What d’you reckon we oughta do with him? Dose of poison and bury him in the back garden? Or tie a brick to his ankle and toss him off Waterloo Bridge?”
“Wait a second,” I cut in. “There’s no way she actually thinks we’re properly engaged, is there?”
“Not a single tiny sliver of a doubt, mate,” Jarman said cheerfully. “As far as she’s concerned, you two are already picking out the china for your wedding registry.”
“I’ll just tell her straight I was completely wasted at the time,” I said. “She’ll see that makes the proposal invalid, right?”
Jarman blinked at me like I’d just suggested I could fly to the moon. “And how d’you plan on proving that? You think telling her you loved her is proof you were too drunk to know your own name? Any normal person might buy it, but not her! You think she spent three months hunting for a clever, upwardly mobile fiancée just to let him wiggle out of it because he was a bit tipsy? If every girl gave their man back the engagement ring just because he was too drunk to propose properly, all the engagement shops in London would close down tomorrow, wouldn’t they?”
Minikin nodded in complete agreement.
Jarman, now that he’d successfully bantered with Minikin and decided he liked him, pulled his chair right up next to him and started plotting like they were planning a bank heist. “Look, this ain’t some ordinary bloke who can just tell her to get lost and never see her again. This lad’s gonna be the next Poet Laureate one day, right? You think when the Prince of Wales invites him over to dinner at Marlborough House he wants to turn up with a 160 pound barmaid who drops aitches left and right and doesn’t know which end of the soup spoon to drink from?”
“Fair point,” Minikin said. “That does put a spanner in the works.”
“So we need to get him out of this,” Jarman said, frowning. “And I swear to God, I almost can’t think of how.”
“Minikin suggested he do a runner,” I offered.
“Not a bad plan!” Jarman mused. “But where you gonna run? She’s sharp, that one. She’d track you down across three counties no problem. She’d sniff you out like a bloodhound.”
“Change his name? Get a new job?” Minikin suggested.
“Need references for jobs,” Jarman pointed out. “He’s got none. She’d find him in a week.” He jumped up so fast his chair scraped the floor. “I’ve got it! THE STAGE!”
“Can he act?” Minikin asked.
“Course he can! You only need to not make a total fool of yourself, that’s it! No references needed, no one asks you what you did last month, everyone there’s running away from something! Perfect hiding spot!” Jarman yelled, excited now. “Can you sing?”
I said I could warble a little, if no one was listening.
“Sing something now!” Jarman said. “Quick! Wait a second –” He snuck out of the room, tiptoed down the landing, knocked on Rosina’s door, and came back grinning. “She’s not home yet! Sing like your freedom depends on it, because it does!”
I sang *Sally in Our Alley*, but every time the name Sally came out of my mouth I pictured Rosina’s broad, flushed face, and my voice got wobbly. But Jarman gave me a thumbs up at the end. “It’s not grand opera, but it’s more than good enough for the back row of a cheap economy chorus where they don’t care what you sound like as long as you don’t fall off the stage. Tomorrow you go find O’Kelly, the theatre guy I know. He’s a good sort, he’ll sort you out with a spot in the next show. She’ll never find you in that crowd.”
I was too tired and hungover to feel hopeful, but I thanked them both, and promised I’d think about it properly the next day when my head stopped hurting enough to make rational decisions. They said goodnight, Minikin heading down first, and Jarman snuck back in one more second.
“Consols are a bit low this week,” he whispered, hand on the door. “If you need a few quid for train fare or a new hat, don’t go selling your whole book collection. I’ve got a couple of pounds spare I can lend you. Suck a couple lemons tonight, you’ll feel brand new tomorrow. See you later, mate.”
I did exactly what he said, and by morning my head was almost clear. I turned up to the office on time, and my boss Mr Lott let me off with a two shilling deduction from my weekly pay for missing half the previous day – far better than getting fired. I’d planned to go see O’Kelly that evening, sent him a note saying I was coming round, but got a reply back that he was out, and I’d have to wait till next Friday. I moped back to the lodgings, feeling suddenly lonely and miserable, the old familiar dark hollow homesickness creeping back. The thought of no one on earth caring if I lived or died suddenly made Rosina, my eager new fiancée, seem weirdly like a lifeline. At least she cared about me, even if it was mostly because she wanted to marry a famous writer and move her family up in the world.
That’s when there was a tiny tap at my door. The little slavey was standing in the dim passage holding a crumpled pink, scented envelope. It was addressed to “Paul Kelver Esquire”, handwriting messy but surprisingly neat, full of crossed out words. I lit my candle, and read it:
“Dear Mr Paul,
I heard you got took ill right after the party. I fear you work too hard and you will make yourself poorly and then I will be very cross with you. If you are better now I am going for a walk and you can come with me if you are a good boy. Lots of love.
Your affectionate ROSIE.”
For half a second I felt all soft and stupid. My first ever love letter! A little mist swam in my eyes, and I pictured her face, all warm and soft, her big white fleshy arms held out to me. I grabbed my coat, pulled my boots on in a panic, and banged on the door of her second floor back room, hands shaking.
“Who’s there?” her sharp voice called.
“It’s me! Paul.”
“Oh, wait a minute, dear!” Her voice went all sweet and syrupy. There was a sound of scurrying feet, drawers banging shut, rustling fabric, then she called out “You can come in now, dear.”
I walked in. The small room smelled of smoky lamp, and all I could see at first was Rosina, standing with her arms over her head, pinning her hat to her straw coloured hair.
And all of a sudden, every last bit of my gushy soft feeling died. She wasn’t even ugly, really, but the whole room, her whole vibe, reeked of that cheap common coarseness that made me cringe. The same finicky snobbery that made me send a plate of chops back if the waitress who served them had dirty fingernails was now fixated entirely on her rough red hands, dotted with two or three little warts that were impossible to miss, and the caked-on powder she’d dabbed on her face in a hurry.
“You’re a very naughty boy,” she said, finishing pinning her hat on. “Why didn’t you come visit me at dinner time? I’ve half a mind not to kiss you.”
I prayed she’d skip the kiss, but no, she leaned in and pressed her flowery, powdery cheek to mine. It tasted exactly like those pale, thin, cardboardy dough cakes the school porter’s wife used to sell us at four a penny back when I was a kid.
We trundled down the stairs to the kitchen, where she paused and yelled shrilly for Mrs Peedles. “Me and Mr Kelver are just going for a little walk, I won’t need any supper tonight! Good night!”
“Oh good night dear,” Mrs Peedles panted. “Hope you two have fun. Is Mr Kelver there?”
“He’s just round the corner being shy,” Rosina sniggered.
“Aww, the shy ones are always the wildest once you get them home, that’s my experience,” Mrs Peedles whispered back.
