When Maso opened the door again, the cool hush of the house was broken by the small, familiar noises of welcome: the scrape of a shoe on brick, the soft clap of a cloak shaken straight, and Nello’s voice—always ready to fill an empty space as readily as steam fills a kitchen.

He entered first, bowing low to Romola with the flourish of a man who had bowed to women all his life and meant every bow to be a compliment to himself. Yet he did not come alone. With a hand laid lightly, almost coaxingly, upon the stranger’s sleeve, he guided him forward as if ushering a prized dish from the fire to the table.

“Messer Bardo,” said Nello, and in this room, before blindness and learning, even Nello’s tone grew measured, “I have the honour of presenting to you a Greek scholar, eager to have speech of you—not less from what I have told him of your learning and your priceless collections, than because your patronage may further him in the transient need to which he has been reduced by shipwreck. His name is Tito Melema, at your service.”

Romola’s astonishment sprang up so quick it was almost a physical thing—like the sudden scent of crushed mint where one expected only dust. Nello had said nothing of the Greek’s youth. Those who came to her father were mostly men with brows scored by years of dispute and parchment-light; this new face, fresh as a spring fig split open, belonged rather to the world of sunlight and sea-winds than to the shadowed mines of books.

Only once before had a youthful masculine beauty been stamped upon her memory with that deep, unreasoning fidelity which clings to the smell of a mother’s bread: the face of her brother, long lost—fair, with sunny hair like her own. That recollection rose now like a ghost at the edge of her sight. But the habit of her soul toward strangers—proud self-dependence, the refusal to ask for anything, even by a smile—stood firm as a white embankment. Tito bowed; she answered with her usual pallor and composure, though her heart made a quick, rebellious movement behind it.

His black sajo fell plain and unadorned to the knee; the austerity of the garment only threw into stronger relief the rich-tinted life of his face. When he lifted his eyes, there was in them a gentle, beseeching admiration—an appeal without demand. If he had been too handsome, that look was his atonement: it asked to be allowed near, not to conquer. Romola felt the heat rise in her cheeks, a brief flush like embers coaxed into colour; then it vanished as quickly, as if her will had set a lid upon it.

“Messere, I give you welcome,” said Bardo, with the grave condescension of a man who had long been accustomed to be asked and not to ask. “Misfortune wedded to learning, and especially to Greek learning, is a letter of credit that should win the ear of every instructed Florentine; for since the period when your countryman, Manuelo Crisolora, diffused the light of his teaching in the chief cities of Italy, no man is held worthy of the name of scholar who has acquired merely the transplanted and derivative literature of the Latins. Such inert students are stigmatised as opici—barbarians—according to the phrase of the Romans themselves, who frankly replenished their urns at the fountain-head.

“I am, as you perceive, and as Nello has doubtless forewarned you, totally blind: a calamity to which we Florentines are held especially liable—whether owing to the cold winds rushing upon us in spring from the passes of the Apennines, or to that sudden transition from the cool gloom of our houses to the dazzling brightness of our summer sun, by which the lippi are said to have been made so numerous among the ancient Romans; or to some occult cause that eludes our surmises. But I pray you be seated. Nello, my friend, be seated.”

He paused, as always, until his fine ear had taken the measure of the room—the settling of bodies, the soft creak of a chair leg, the slight brush of Romola’s gown as she took her usual place at his right hand. Blindness had trained him to live by sound as others lived by sight; the air of the house itself seemed tuned to his hearing, keeping away the clatter of the street as a cook keeps away flies from cooling milk.

“From what part of Greece do you come, Messere?” he continued. “I had thought your unhappy country almost exhausted of sons who could cherish any image of her original glory, though indeed the barbarous Sultans have shown themselves not indisposed to engraft on their wild stock the precious vine their own fierce bands have hewn down. From what part of Greece do you come?”

“I sailed last from Nauplia,” said Tito; “but I have resided both at Constantinople and Thessalonica, and travelled in various parts little visited by Western Christians since the triumph of the Turkish arms. I should tell you, however, Messere, that I was not born in Greece, but at Bari. I spent the first sixteen years of my life in Southern Italy and Sicily.”

At the name and the cadence of these places, something passed over Bardo’s delicate features like a shadow over still water. He leaned forward, stretched his right hand a little toward Romola, and turned his head as if to speak to her; then, as though remembering himself, he drew back, and his voice fell.

“Excuse me—is it not true—you are young?”

“I am three-and-twenty,” said Tito.

“Ah,” said Bardo, and there was a subdued excitement in the single syllable. “And you had, doubtless, a father who cared for your early instruction—who, perhaps, was himself a scholar?”

A pause—small, but it quivered in the room like a sudden silence in a marketplace. Tito’s lip trembled for an instant, as if the remembrance he touched were tender and raw.

“Yes,” he said at last, “at least a father by adoption. He was a Neapolitan, and of accomplished scholarship, both Latin and Greek. But”—another pause, like a hand briefly tightening—“he is lost to me—was lost on a voyage he too rashly undertook to Delos.”

Bardo sank backward, too finely wrought to press upon pain that sounded so near. Romola, who knew the secret fibres in her father that such words had struck, felt with an odd, startled certainty that this stranger had slipped at once within the boundary that lay between their shut-in lives and the rest of men.

Nello, smelling a pause as a cook smells a pot in danger of boiling over, took it upon himself to stir.

“In truth,” he said, “it is as clear as Venetian glass that this fine young man has had the best training; for the two Cennini have set him to work at their Greek sheets already, and it seems to me they are not men to begin cutting before they have felt the edge of their tools. They tested him well, we may be sure. And if there are two things not to be hidden—love and a cough—I say there is a third, and that is ignorance, when once a man is obliged to do something besides wagging his head. The tonsor inequalis is inevitably betrayed when he takes the shears in his hand; is it not true, Messer Bardo? I speak after the fashion of a barber, but, as Luigi Pulci says—

“‘Perdonimi s’io fallo: chi m’ascolta
Intenda il mio volgar col suo latino.’”

“Nay, my good Nello,” said Bardo, with friendly severity, “you are not altogether illiterate, and might doubtless have made a more respectable progress in learning if you had abstained somewhat from the cicalata and gossip of the street-corner, to which our Florentines are excessively addicted. But still more if you had not clogged your memory with those frivolous productions of which Luigi Pulci has furnished the most peccant exemplar—a compendium of extravagances and incongruities, removed farthest from the models of a pure age, resembling rather the grylli—the conceits—of a period when mystic meaning was held a warrant for monstrosity of form; with this difference, that while the monstrosity is retained, the mystic meaning is absent. In contemptible contrast stands the great poem of Virgil, who—so I long held with Filelfo before Landino had taken upon him to expound the same—embodied the deepest lessons of philosophy in a graceful and well-knit fable.

“I cannot but regard the multiplication of these babbling, lawless productions, albeit countenanced by patronage and in some degree by the example of Lorenzo himself, otherwise a friend to true learning, as a sign that the glorious hopes of this century are to be quenched in gloom—nay, that they have been the delusive prologue to an age worse than that of iron: an age of tinsel and gossamer, in which no thought has substance enough to be moulded into consistent and lasting form.”

Nello spread his palms and shrugged, as if the rebuke had slipped from him like oil from a ladle.

“Once more, pardon. I find myself knowing so many things in good Tuscan before I have time to think of the Latin for them; and Messer Luigi’s rhymes are always slipping off the lips of my customers—that is what corrupts me. And, indeed, talking of customers, I have left my shop and my reputation too long in the custody of my slow Sandro, who does not deserve even to be called a tonsor inequalis, but rather to be pronounced simply a bungler in the vulgar tongue. So, with your permission, Messer Bardo, I will take my leave—well understood that I am at your service whenever Maso calls upon me. It seems a thousand years till I dress and perfume the damigella’s hair, which deserves to shine in the heavens as a constellation—though indeed it were a pity for it ever to go so far out of reach.”

Three voices answered him in a small fugue of farewell as he retreated, bowing to Romola, beckoning Tito with a flick of his fingers, and taking with him the scent of street-air and shop-sachet. He went satisfied: the pretty youngster whom he had brought like a gift had been received with favour, and Nello knew well when a dish had taken its seasoning.

When the door closed and the house settled again into its quieter breath, the little flare of wrath that Nello’s quotation had kindled in Bardo seemed already burned down to a steady ember. He addressed Tito anew with his customary calm, as if the mind, once diverted into the channel of scholarship, flowed there willingly.

“Ah, young man, you are happy in having been able to unite the advantages of travel with those of study. You will be welcome among us as a bringer of fresh tidings from a land that has become sadly strange to us except through the agents of a now restricted commerce and the reports of hasty pilgrims. Those days are far distant that I myself witnessed, when men like Aurispa and Guarino went out to Greece as to a storehouse and came back laden with manuscripts which every scholar was eager to borrow—and be it owned with shame, not always willing to restore. Nay, even the days when erudite Greeks flocked to our shores for refuge seem far-off now—farther off than the on-coming of my blindness.

“But doubtless, young man, research after the treasures of antiquity was not alien to the purpose of your travels?”

“Assuredly not,” said Tito. “On the contrary, my companion—my father—was willing to risk his life in his zeal for the discovery of inscriptions and other traces of ancient civilisation.”

“And I trust there is a record of his researches and their results,” said Bardo, and his eagerness sharpened the words, “since they must be even more precious than those of Ciriaco, which I have diligently availed myself of, though they are not always illuminated by adequate learning.”

“There was such a record,” said Tito; “but it was lost, like everything else, in the shipwreck I suffered below Ancona. The only record left is such as remains in our—in my memory.”

“You must lose no time in committing it to paper,” said Bardo, interest growing as yeast grows when it finds warmth. “Doubtless you remember much, if you aided in transcription; for when I was your age, words wrought themselves into my mind as if fixed by the tool of the graver. Wherefore I constantly marvel at the capriciousness of my daughter’s memory, which grasps certain objects with tenacity, and lets fall all those minutiae whereon depends accuracy—the very soul of scholarship. But I apprehend no such danger with you, young man, if your will has seconded your training.”

At the mention of Romola, Tito ventured to look toward her; and when he smiled at the charge against her memory, his face opened into its brightest light. The effect upon her was as immediate as a draught of warm broth taken in the chill of fasting. She had been made to feel a culprit so often for that very deficiency that the simple human sweetness of amusement instead of rebuke was like the dawning of a new sense—the sense of comradeship.

They did not look away at once, as if they had stolen something. Their eyes met and held, and the smile passed between them frankly.

“She is not really so cold and proud,” thought Tito.

“Does he forget too, I wonder?” thought Romola; “yet I hope not, else he will vex my father.”

But he turned back, compelled by Bardo’s waiting attention.

“I have had much practice in transcription,” he said; “but in the case of inscriptions copied in memorable scenes, rendered doubly impressive by the sense of risk and adventure, it may have happened that my retention of written characters has been weakened. On the plain of the Eurotas, or among the gigantic stones of Mycenae and Tyrins—especially when the fear of the Turk hovers over one like a vulture—the mind wanders, even though the hand writes faithfully what the eye dictates. But something doubtless I have retained,” he added, with modesty that was not false—only lightly seasoned with policy—“something that might be of service if illustrated and corrected by a wider learning than my own.”

“That is well spoken,” said Bardo, delighted. “And I will not withhold from you such aid as I can give, if you like to communicate with me concerning your recollections. I foresee a work that will be a useful supplement to the Isolario of Christoforo Buondelmonte, and may take rank with the Itineraria of Ciriaco and the admirable Ambrogio Traversari.

“But we must prepare ourselves for calumny, young man,” Bardo went on, energy rising as if the book were already bound and handed about. “If your writing contains novelties you will be charged with forgery; if my elucidations should clash with any principles of interpretation adopted by another scholar, our personal characters will be attacked. We shall be impeached with foul actions. You must prepare yourself to be told that your mother was a fish-woman, and that your father was a renegade priest or a hanged malefactor. I myself, for having shown error in a single preposition, had an invective written against me wherein I was taxed with treachery, fraud, indecency, and even hideous crimes. Such—such are the flowers with which the glorious path of scholarship is strewed!

“But tell me: I learned much concerning Byzantium and Thessalonica long ago from Demetrio Calcondila, who has but lately departed from Florence; but you, it seems, have visited less familiar scenes?”

“Yes,” said Tito, readily, “we made what I may call a pilgrimage full of danger, for the sake of visiting places which have almost died out of the memory of the West, for they lie away from the track of pilgrims; and my father used to say that scholars themselves hardly imagine them to have any existence out of books. He held that a new and more glorious era would open for learning when men should begin to look for their commentaries on the ancient writers in the remains of cities and temples—nay, in the paths of rivers, and on the face of valleys and mountains.”

“Ah!” said Bardo, fervidly. “Your father, then, was not a common man. Was he fortunate—may I ask? Had he many friends?” The last words were weighted; in them Romola heard other names unspoken, other memories kept like old spices in a jar and opened only with trembling.

“No,” said Tito. “He made enemies—chiefly, I believe, by a certain impetuous candour; and they hindered his advancement, so that he lived in obscurity. And he would never stoop to conciliate: he could never forget an injury.”

“Ah!” Bardo’s voice drew the syllable out, long and deep.

Tito, wishing to turn aside from that too-personal road, continued quickly.

“Among our hazardous expeditions I remember with particular vividness a hastily snatched visit to Athens. Our hurry, and the double danger of being seized as prisoners by the Turks, and of our galley raising anchor before we could return, made it seem like a fevered vision of the night—the wide plain, the girdling mountains, the ruined porticos and columns, either standing far aloof as if receding from our hurried footsteps, or else jammed confusedly among the dwellings of Christians degraded into servitude, or among the forts and turrets of their Moslem conquerors, who have their stronghold on the Acropolis.”

“You fill me with surprise,” said Bardo. “Athens, then, is not utterly destroyed and swept away, as I had imagined?”

“No wonder you should be under that mistake,” said Tito, “for few even of the Greeks themselves, who live beyond the mountain boundary of Attica, know anything about the present condition of Athens—or Setine, as the sailors call it. I remember, as we were rounding the promontory of Sunium, the Greek pilot on board our Venetian galley pointed to the mighty columns standing on the summit of the rock—the remains, as you know well, of the great temple erected to the goddess Athena, who looked down from that high shrine with triumph at her conquered rival Poseidon. And our pilot, pointing to those columns, said, ‘That was the school of the great philosopher Aristotle.’ And at Athens itself, the monk who acted as our guide in the hasty view we snatched, insisted most on showing us the spot where Saint Philip baptised the Ethiopian eunuch, or some such legend.”

“Talk not of monks and their legends!” said Bardo, breaking in with sudden heat. “It is enough to overlay human hope and enterprise with an eternal frost to think that the ground trodden by philosophers and poets is crawled over by insect-swarms of besotted fanatics or howling hypocrites.”

“Perdio, I have no affection for them,” said Tito, with a shrug. “Servitude agrees well with a religion like theirs, which lies in renunciation of all that makes life precious to other men. And they carry the yoke that befits them: their matin chant is drowned by the voice of the muezzin, who from the gallery of the high tower on the Acropolis calls every Mussulman to prayers. That tower springs from the Parthenon itself; and every time we paused and directed our eyes towards it, our guide set up a wail that a temple once won from the diabolical uses of the pagans to become the temple of another virgin than Pallas—the Virgin Mother of God—was now again perverted to the accursed ends of the Moslem.

“It was the sight of those walls of the Acropolis, disclosed in the distance as we leaned over the side of our galley when it was forced by contrary winds to anchor in the Piraeus, that fired my father’s mind with the determination to see Athens at all risks, in spite of the sailors’ warnings that if we lingered till a change of wind they would depart without us. But after all it was impossible for us to venture near the Acropolis; the sight of men eager in examining old stones raised suspicion that we were Venetian spies, and we had to hurry back to the harbour.”

“We will talk more of these things,” said Bardo, eagerness returning like a tide. “You must recall everything, to the minutest trace left in your memory. You will win the gratitude of after-times by leaving a record of the aspect Greece bore while yet the barbarians had not swept away every trace of the structures Pausanias and Pliny described. You will take those great writers as your models; and such contribution of criticism and suggestion as my riper mind can supply shall not be wanting.

“There will be much to tell; for you have travelled—you said—in the Peloponnesus?”

“Yes; and in Boeotia also,” said Tito. “I have rested in the groves of Helicon, and tasted of the fountain Hippocrene. But on every memorable spot in Greece, conquest after conquest has set its seal, till there is a confusion of ownership even in ruins, that only close study and comparison could unravel. High over every fastness, from the plains of Lacedaemon to the straits of Thermopylae, there towers some huge Frankish fortress, once inhabited by a French or Italian marquis, now either abandoned or held by Turkish bands.”

“Stay!” cried Bardo, whose mind was now too fully possessed by the shape of the future book to follow the thread of Tito’s landscape. “Do you think of writing in Latin or Greek? Doubtless Greek is the readier clothing for your thoughts, and it is the nobler language. But, on the other hand, Latin is the tongue in which we shall measure ourselves with the larger and more famous number of modern rivals. And if you are less at ease in it, I will aid you—yes, I will spend on you that long-accumulated study which was to have been thrown into the channel of another work—a work in which I myself was to have had a helpmate.”

He paused, as if the word helpmate had rung like a struck bell in some hidden chamber.

“But who knows whether that work may not be executed yet? For you, too, young man, have been brought up by a father who poured into your mind the long-gathered stream of his knowledge and experience. Our aid might be mutual.”

Romola watched her father’s growing excitement and felt, beneath it, the deeper currents she had lived beside all her life: hope rising with pain braided through it, the yearning for what had been broken. The room itself seemed to listen, held by the fine web of Bardo’s expectation. She kept turning her eyes toward Tito, anxious lest he should, by any sign of reluctance, scatter the vision of co-operation that made her father’s face look strangely younger. If he knew about her brother—if he understood why certain questions trembled—he would be gentler still. An eighteen-year-old heart readily weaves belief from the youth and sweetness that has moved it: it imagines depths behind a sympathetic face as easily as a child imagines gods in weather.

And Tito, in truth, was far from impatience. He liked sitting there, liked the sense that Romola’s attention returned to him again and again like a hand feeling for something warm. He was pleased that Bardo took interest in him. The prospect of a learned labour to be shared did not press on his imagination with weight enough to trouble his ease; it lay before him like a path bordered by olive trees—something to be walked when the day came.

“I shall be proud and happy,” he said, answering Bardo’s last words, “if my services can be held a meet offering to the matured scholarship of Messere. But doubtless”—and here he glanced toward Romola—“the lovely damigella, your daughter, makes all other aid superfluous; for I have learned from Nello that she has been nourished on the highest studies from her earliest years.”

“You are mistaken,” said Romola. “I am by no means sufficient to my father: I have not the gifts that are necessary for scholarship.”

She spoke not with anxious humility, but with a proud gravity—as if even in self-distrust she would not beg to be contradicted.
