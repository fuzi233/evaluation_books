"The unwatched garden bough sways,
And tender blossoms flutter down;
The unloved beech gathers browns,
While maples burn themselves away..."

So sang Tennyson, a fitting prelude to the final day in Helstone. The house was a hive of activity, filled with packing cases being carted to the railway station. Even the once-pristine lawn lay trampled and untidy beneath the straw drifting from open windows. Inside, the rooms echoed strangely, bathed in a harsh, uncurtained light that rendered them already alien.

In her dressing-room, Mrs. Hale and Dixon worked in a slow, sentimental rhythm, pausing frequently to coo over forgotten treasures—relics of the children from years past. Their progress was negligible, lost to the weight of sentiment. Downstairs, Margaret stood a figure of calm resolve amidst the chaos, directing the men hired to assist the cook and Charlotte. To the servants, who wept intermittently, she appeared unnervingly composed. They speculated that a young lady raised in London could not possibly care deeply for Helstone, failing to perceive the heavy, suffocating ache within her. They did not see the constant strain required to keep her composure, the mental fortitude needed to prevent a cry of pain. If she faltered, who would lead? Her father was locked in the vestry with the clerk, buried in papers and registers, preparing his own books for transport—a task requiring a solitary focus she dared not disturb. Nor would she yield to strangers, let alone the familiar grief of the cook and Charlotte.

Finally, the packers retired to the kitchen for tea. Margaret moved stiffly from the hall, traversing the bare, echoing drawing-room and stepping out into the twilight of an early November. A film of soft, dull mist obscured the world, lending it a lilac hue as the sun dipped below the horizon. A robin sang somewhere near, perhaps the same bird her father had called his "winter pet," for whom he had crafted a wooden house beside his study window. The leaves outside were gorgeously colored, amber and gold against the low, slanting rays, waiting for the first frost to lay them low. Already, a few drifted down.

She walked along the path beneath the pear-tree wall, a route untouched since she had paced it at Henry Lennox's side. There, amidst the bed of thyme, he had begun to speak of things she must not dwell upon. Her eyes had lingered on a late-blooming rose as she answered him, distracted by the vivid beauty of carrot leaves he mentioned. It had been only a fortnight ago! And now, everything was transformed. Where was he? Likely in London, navigating the same social circles, dining with the Harley Street set or his younger friends. Perhaps even now, as she trudged through the damp, dreary garden, he was enjoying a run in the Temple Gardens, escaping the roar of the city and the hum of thousands of busy lives, freshening himself after a day of legal toil. He had often described these hasty walks, snatched between study and supper, and the thought of them had haunted her imagination.

Here, the garden was silent. The robin had fled into the vast stillness of night. Occasionally, a distant cottage door creaked shut, admitting a weary laborer to his home, sounding impossibly far away. Beyond the garden, a stealthy, crunching sound emerged from the crisp fallen leaves of the forest. It sounded close, menacing.

Margaret recognized it. Earlier that autumn, sitting in her darkened bedroom, she had watched the poachers' silent leaps over the fence, their quick tramps across the moonlit lawn, their vanishing into the black shadows. Then, their wild, adventurous freedom had fascinated her; she had wished them success, harboring no fear. But tonight, a primal terror seized her. She did not know why.

She heard Charlotte closing the windows, fastening them for the night, unaware that someone had ventured into the garden. Suddenly, a heavy crash echoed nearby—a branch, perhaps rotted or snapped by force, falling from the tree line. Panic surged through Margaret. She sprinted, swift as Camilla, to the window and tapped it with a trembling urgency that jolted Charlotte awake.

"Let me in! Let me in! It is only me, Charlotte!"

Her heart hammered until she was safely inside the drawing-room, surrounded by the familiar, hemmed-in walls. She collapsed onto a packing case. The room was cheerless and chill, devoid of fire or light save for Charlotte's long, unsnuffed candle. Charlotte stared at her with surprise, sensing something wrong before seeing it. Margaret rose, feeling the shock more than witnessing it.

"I was afraid you were shutting me out altogether, Charlotte," she said, managing a faint, strained smile. "And then you would never have heard me in the kitchen, where the doors to the lane and churchyard are locked long ago."

"Oh, miss, I would surely have missed you soon. The men would have asked you for directions. And I've put tea in Master's study; it's the most comfortable room, so to speak."

"Thank you, Charlotte. You are a kind girl. I shall be sorry to leave you. Try to write to me, if I can offer any help or advice. I shall always welcome a letter from Helstone. I will send you my address as soon as I know it."

The study was prepared, a fire blazing brightly, unlit candles arranged on the table. Margaret sat on the rug, seeking warmth as the damp evening clung to her dress and exhaustion chilled her bones. She balanced herself by clasping her hands tightly around her knees, her head drooping slightly, an posture of desolation that masked the turmoil within.

But when her father's steps echoed on the gravel outside, she sprang up. Hastily smoothing back her heavy black hair and wiping away tears she had not noticed falling, she went to open the door.

He looked far more depressed than she did. She struggled to find words, forcing herself to speak of topics that might interest him, knowing each effort might be her last.

"Have you had a very long walk today?" she asked, noting his refusal to touch any food.

"As far as Fordham Beeches. I went to see Widow Maltby. She is deeply grieved at not having wished you goodbye. She says little Susan has kept watch down the lane for days past."

"Nay, Margaret, what is the matter, dear?" His voice cracked. The image of the little child watching endlessly, continually disappointed—not from forgetfulness, but from sheer inability to leave home—was the final drop in Margaret's cup. She sobbed, as if her heart would break.

Mr. Hale was distressingly perplexed. He rose and paced the room nervously. Margaret tried to stifle her emotions, waiting until she could speak with firmness. She heard him muttering to himself.

"I cannot bear it. I cannot bear to see the sufferings of others. I think I could endure my own with patience. Oh, is there no going back?"

"No, father," she said, looking straight at him, her voice low and steady. "It is bad enough to believe you in error. It would be infinitely worse to have known you a hypocrite." She dropped her voice at the end, shuddering at the implication of such a thought regarding her father.

"Besides," she continued, "it is simply that I am tired tonight. Do not think I am suffering because of what you have done, dear papa. We cannot talk about it tonight, I believe." Tears and sobs threatened to overwhelm her again. "I had better go and bring Mother this cup of tea. She had hers very early, when I was too busy to attend to her, and I am certain she will need it now."

By railroad time, they were inexorably torn away from the beloved Helstone the next morning. They departed, casting one last glance at the long, low parsonage, half-hidden behind China-roses and pyracanthus, glowing with a homely warmth in the morning sun. Before they could settle into the carriage sent from Southampton, it was gone, returning no more. A sharp sting pierced Margaret's heart as she strained to catch the last glimpse of the old church tower above the forest trees at the bend. Her father remembered, too, and silently yielded the single view to him. She leaned back, shutting her eyes, and tears welled, hanging for an instant on her lashes before rolling slowly down her cheeks, unheeded, onto her dress.

They stopped in London all night at a quiet hotel. Poor Mrs. Hale had wept nearly the whole day, while Dixon expressed her sorrow through extreme irritability, constantly trying to avoid touching the unconscious Mr. Hale, whom she blamed for all their suffering.

As they traveled through the familiar streets, passing houses they had often visited and shops where she had once waited patiently by her aunt's side, they found themselves almost entirely alone. Though the journey had felt interminable, it was the height of the afternoon bustle in London. Long since removed from the capital, Mrs. Hale revived with the curiosity of a child, gazing at the shops and carriages.

"Oh, there's Harrison's, where I bought so many of my wedding things! Dear, how altered! They have immense plate-glass windows, larger than Crawford's in Southampton. And there, I declare... yes, it is—they have just passed Mr. Henry Lennox. Where could he be going, among all these shops?"
