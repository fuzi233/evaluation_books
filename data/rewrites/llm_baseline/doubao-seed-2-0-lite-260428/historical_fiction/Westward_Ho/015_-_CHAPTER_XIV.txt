“Ignorance and evil, even in full flight, deal terrible backhanded strokes at their pursuers.”--HELPS.

It is no credit to the England of Elizabeth’s day, I must admit, that the overland road from Plymouth to the north of Devon was no safe journey for any traveller. Unless you were willing to waste days looping far out of your way, you had no choice but to cross the territory of a foreign, hostile potentate: a man who had repeatedly raided royal lands, and defeated detachments of the Queen’s own soldiers, known behind his back by the mocking title the King of the Gubbings. As the old historian Fuller once wrote, “I dare name them now, secure in my distance, when no braver man would dare breathe the word to their face for fear of drawing their fury down on his head. No man I have met yet can give a good account of where their name came from. We call the worthless little shavings left from cleaning fish gubbings, and the folk who bear the name well know it carries a taint of shame.
My learned friend Joseph Maynard suggests it comes from *inhabitare montes gibberosos*, “the dwellers on hunchbacked hills”, but most men will smile at that clever etymology and doubt its truth. I have read of an England that lies beyond Wales, but the land of the Gubbings is a little Scythia right inside Devon, and they are pure heathens in its bounds. It sits on Dartmoor’s edge, near Brent. Tradition says some two hundred years ago, two pregnant fugitive women fled there to hide, and a crowd of idle, lewd men followed them: that was the start of their line. They have made their own little world, answerable to no bishop, no archdeacon, no civil law of any kind. They live in hovels more like holes in the ground than houses, as swine do, sharing all goods in common, multiplying without the formality of marriage until they number in their hundreds. Their speech is the dregs of the dregs of Devonian dialect, and the more learned a man is, the less he will understand a word of it. During the civil wars, no commander dared quarter soldiers on them, for fear his men would end up quartered among them as captives. Their wealth is made of other men’s goods: they live by stealing moorland sheep, no constable has the power to search their dens, and no sheriff thinks the task worth the trouble. They are faster than most horses, longer-lived than most men, untouched by the soft luxury that shortens civilized lives. They hold together like a hive of bees: offend one, and all will turn out to avenge the wrong.” Fuller closes his account with a dry quip about the dissenting Anabaptists of his day, glad to note the Gubbings have lately begun to send their children for baptism and rejoin the rest of Christian humanity.

It was this wild, lawless land that Amyas Leigh and his small armed party approached at dusk, Amyas himself clad in full steel plate astride a strong warhorse, with lance, broadsword, and two heavy horse pistols at his saddle. Behind him rode Salvation Yeo, grim old Puritan arquebusier, and five or six north Devon veterans who had served under Amyas in Ireland, all wearing steel headpieces and thick quilted jerkins, each armed with pike and sword. Two shaggy moorland ponies carried their baggage, as they pushed hard past Tavistock to reach Lydford and a safe inn before nightfall. But delays buying fresh horses had kept them from starting out till noon, and full dark fell the very moment they rode over the border of Gubbing territory.

It was a desolate stretch under the lurid glow of the last sunset: a high open heath plateau, Dartmoor’s jagged crags rising to their right, sloping away west to the great cone of Brent Tor, that ancient extinct volcano, its peak crowned by the tiny votive church a long-dead Plymouth merchant had vowed to build when he first sighted English land after a storm at sea. Far down the waste slopes below them, thin threads of blue smoke curled up from the hidden hollows where the Gubbings made their dens. More than once the party halted, staring hard at clumps of gorse and stray grazing ponies, half convinced they were enemy sentinels. It is easy to laugh at their caution now, but in that day it was no jest, as they would soon learn.

A mile further on, they found the only wayside inn for miles around: Rogues’ Harbor, a desolate lump of lichen-crusted granite, its windows patched with scraped paper, its rotting thatch weighted down with heavy stones to keep the wind from tearing it off. Pigs and barefoot grubby children loitered in the rambling muddy back yard, grunting in unholy communion of dirt. In the doorway stood the innkeeper, six feet of brawny, blear-eyed brutishness, holding his hose up with one hand for want of proper points, scratching his matted elf-locked hair where stray goose feathers stuck thick: proof he had only just stumbled out of bed after a night spent out stealing sheep, and thought the feathers made him look as if he slept on soft down. He spotted Amyas’s party, counted them, shook his head at the sight of Amyas’s full armor, and let out a sleepy howl for his daughter Mary.

The strapping red-cheeked lass, clad only in a thin green bodice and scanty red petticoat, brought out his fishing rod and basket. He fumbled with the line, swore when he found the tiny fly was missing, and roared that the old gander must have eaten it. When Mary dodged the blow he aimed at her head, she clouted him back hard across his own, and he shambled off yelping, yelling to go fetch the Patrico. Mary tidied her hair, slipped on her best gown and a pair of stockings over her dirt-smudged legs, and greeted the arriving guests. The men pulled faces at how mucky the place was, but agreed it was far better to sleep under its leaky roof than bivouac out on the heath within raiding distance of the Gubbings camps.

The old hen that had swallowed the missing fishing fly was soon plucked, roasted, and set on the table, alongside cuts of “black Dartmoor mutton” that wafted the distinct, rich scent of red deer venison. Amyas stabled his horse and the pack ponies in a shed that had neither lock nor key, and came back grumbling, already worried his fine war steed would be stolen in the night. Yeo, ever suspicious, stationed himself by the front door, while the rest of the men lingered by the fire to “superintend” Mary’s cooking.

Presently Yeo stepped back inside, all a-twitter.
“Sir, there’s a gentleman just riding up, alone.”
“Ask him to join us, tell him he’s welcome to supper,” Amyas said.

Yeo trotted out, and came back five minutes later. “He slipped round the back, through the yard, sir, strange as can be.”
“Odd tastes he has, to make himself at home in this den,” Amyas shrugged.

Yeo vanished again, and burst back in moments later, quivering with excitement. “Come out, sir! For the Lord’s sake come quick! I’ve got him, safe as a rat in a trap!”
“Got who?” Amyas asked.
“A Jesuit, sir! I saw the way he trod, soft on his toes like he feared he’d offend God with every step, I knew he was one of them Popish villains the moment he came over the hill. I spied through the crack in the stable gate, saw him slip up to the back door, call Mary’s name all quiet, give her one of them Popish Agnus Dei charms. She kissed it, crossed herself, he blessed her like a priest, then he tiptoed right into the stable. I shut the door behind him, wedged a full cart against it, posted one of the men on guard. That wench is out there crying fit to burst.”

Amyas huffed that he might have locked up an honest traveller by mistake, but Yeo was unyielding. “Honest men don’t hand out Popish charms to maids. If the Lord’s enemies are delivered into my hand, I’m bound to hold them, sir.”
Amyas followed him out, grumbling, dragged the heavy cart away, and pulled open the stable door. Trapped inside, staring at him half foolish, half venomous, like a fox caught digging its way out of a trap, was his own cousin Eustace Leigh.

For a long minute neither spoke. At last Amyas laughed. “Well, cousin, have you added horse theft to your other tricks now?”
“I am here only for the Lord’s work,” Eustace said meekly. “I came to minister to these poor neglected folk, whom your Protestant Church leaves in heathen ignorance.”
Eustace’s temper snapped suddenly when Yeo sneered that he was wasting his blessings on thieves. “You would leave these poor souls to rot untaught? You can hand me over to hang at Exeter jail if you wish to disgrace your own family, but no man, not all the Queen’s soldiers, shall drive me away while there is one soul here left unsaved.”

Amyas sighed, and waved Yeo off. “Come out of the stable, man. I won’t inform on you. I promise you guest’s protection. Come in, eat supper with us, let bygones be bygones.”
Eustace agreed, for he saw a chance to turn Amyas’s soft heart to the good of his own Church, and the two cousins sat by the fire, talking of old childhood days in Bideford, their aunts and uncles, old play haunts, until the tension between them thawed. When Eustace asked why Amyas was heading north, Amyas told him the whole story of Rose Salterne’s disappearance, of her flight with the mysterious Spanish sailor.

Eustace shrieked aloud, leaping to his feet, stamping up and down the room in a wild frenzy, clutching at empty air. “Fool that I was! That villain broke his promise to me on Lundy Island! I loved her! I had as much right to love her as any of your puffed-up Brotherhood of the Rose!” He had betrayed far more than he meant to, and he suddenly checked himself, glowering at Amyas from under his brows, sharp and defensive.

Amyas offered to team up with him to find Rose, but Eustace snapped back that their paths were too far apart, and hinted darkly that Amyas was now deep in the territory of men who answered to him, a fellow Catholic, not the Queen’s Protestant laws. Amyas’s temper flared, but he remembered his promise of safe conduct, and Frank’s old kindness to Eustace back in Clovelly, and he only opened the front door and gestured him out, telling him to go his own way. Eustace vanished into the dark.

Amyas noticed Yeo was missing, and assumed the old Puritan had wandered off to say his prayers. He told his men to bed down around the fire, and stretched out on the wooden settle, using his saddle as a pillow.

At midnight, a deafening racket jolted him awake: thousands of panicking geese squawking and flapping around the house, then Yeo’s voice, yelling at the top of his lungs for help. The men grabbed their swords, burst out the back door into the courtyard, and found Yeo backed up against the stable door, fending off a dozen Gubbing raiders single-handed, sword in one hand, buckler in the other. The raiders turned and fled the second they saw Amyas and his armed party come pouring out of the inn.

“Not a scratch on me, praise the Lord!” Yeo panted. “I got the two ringleaders. Your warhorse kicked the other one dead before he could run. This big brute here, I ran him through.”
They lifted the big man up: he was the King of the Gubbings, their feared Patrico, the raider chief the whole tribe obeyed. He was bleeding out fast, gasping for breath. The second man was already cold in the stable straw, trampled to death by the panicked horses. They stumbled back through the low kitchen door to carry the wounded chief inside, and found the rest of the raider plot unspooling: the innkeeper, the Jesuit Father Parsons in half-dressed disarray, and Eustace Leigh, all caught trying to slip out the front door with Mary’s help, bound for the moor to rouse the rest of the Gubbings tribe.

The wounded chief lying on the floor heard Parsons’ voice, and moaned out loud for his Patrico. Parsons preened, stepping forward, his eyes full of smug triumph. “You see, sir. The sheep know the voice of their shepherd, and I shall prepare this my wounded lamb for the account your man’s cruelty has untimely sent him to render.”
