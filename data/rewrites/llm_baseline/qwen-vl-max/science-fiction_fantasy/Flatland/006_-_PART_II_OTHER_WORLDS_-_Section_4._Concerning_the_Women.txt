In Flatland, where geometry defines both form and fate, the most perilous beings are not the soldiers or the nobles, but the women. While a Soldier-Triangle is sharp and dangerous at its apex, a woman is far more so—she is a line with two points, a needle of pure extremity. And she possesses a power beyond mere shape: the ability to vanish from sight at will. This makes her, in our world, a force neither to be ignored nor trifled with.

To understand this invisibility, imagine placing a needle upon a table. View it from the side, and you see its full length. But turn your gaze directly toward one end, and all you perceive is a tiny point—so small, so faint, that it might as well not exist. That is precisely how a woman appears to us. When her side faces us, we see a straight line, clear and unmistakable. But when she turns her front—a single point housing both eye and mouth, for in Flatland these organs are one—we see only a gleaming dot. And when her back is turned, her dim, almost lifeless rear becomes like an invisible cap, shielding her presence entirely.

This is why collisions with women are not merely accidents—they are disasters. A man might survive a brush with a working-class Triangle, though he may bleed. A soldier’s angle can wound deeply. But a woman? To meet her in motion is to invite instant annihilation. Her stinger-like tip can pierce through flesh and bone without hesitation. And since she can become nearly invisible, even the most careful traveler risks disaster. The laws of Flatland have long sought to mitigate this danger, especially in regions where gravity pulls harder and movement is less controlled.

Here are the core rules governing women in many of our states:

1. Every home must have a separate eastern entrance for women only. They must enter “in a becoming and respectful manner,” never using the western door reserved for men.
2. No woman may walk in public without continuously shouting her Peace-Cry, under penalty of death.
3. Any woman suffering from involuntary movements—such as St. Vitus’s Dance, seizures, or violent sneezing—must be destroyed immediately.

Some states add further restrictions: requiring women to sway their backs constantly to signal their presence, mandating that they be followed by a son or servant when traveling, or confining them entirely to their homes except during religious festivals. But over time, even the wisest Circles—the highest thinkers and rulers—have realized that too many laws breed more harm than good. Over-restricting women leads to resentment, which often erupts into sudden, devastating violence. Entire villages have been wiped out in moments when enraged women, denied freedom, turned on their families.

Thus, the Three Laws above are generally sufficient for well-ordered societies. Yet the real protection lies not in law, but in nature and custom. Though a woman can kill instantly with a backward thrust, she cannot withdraw her stinger if her victim resists. In such struggles, her fragile body is easily shattered. So, despite their lethal potential, they are not invincible.

And then there is fashion—our silent ally. In civilized lands, no lady of standing walks without swaying her back gently from side to side. This has become a mark of refinement, passed down through generations. The wives of high-ranking Circles move with rhythm and grace; those of Equilateral Triangles manage only a monotonous swing, like a pendulum ticking. Even lower-class Isosceles women mimic this motion, not because they need it, but because it is expected.

In every household of status, this “back-motion” is as natural as breathing. It keeps husbands safe from surprise attacks. And once inside their chambers—designed specifically to prevent turning—women lose all power. Their rage fades quickly. They forget insults, promises, threats. Minutes later, they may ask what happened, unaware of their own fury.

Yet this does not mean women lack affection. They do love—but their emotions are raw, unfiltered, ruled by impulse. This is not weakness of character, but consequence of design. With no angles to speak of—worse even than the lowest Isosceles—they possess no brainpower. They think not, judge not, remember not. In moments of anger, they know no bounds, recognize no kinship. I’ve seen a woman slaughter her entire family, then, calm again, inquire where her husband and children had gone.

So caution is key. Never provoke a woman when she can turn. But lock her in her room—where rotation is impossible—and you may say anything, do anything. She will soon forget. The wisdom of our upper classes rests on this balance: safety over comfort, order over harmony.

Still, even in the best households, peace is rare. The wife of a Circle or Polygon always faces forward, eyes and mouth fixed on her husband and his guests. To turn her back would be scandalous, a loss of dignity. So the stream of conversation flows endlessly—sharp, constant, relentless. There is no silence. Not even light is so persistent as the voice of a woman in Flatland.

And here lies a cruel irony: while her sting is deadly, her tongue is worse. For though she has nothing meaningful to say, she says it anyway—without wit, without conscience, without restraint. Some men, weary of endless chatter, have confessed they’d rather risk the silent, lethal thrust than endure the noise.

To you in Spaceland, this may seem tragic. And indeed, it is. Men—even the humblest Isosceles—may hope for improvement, for evolution, for a future where their angles grow broader, their minds sharper. But not women. Once a woman, always a woman. Nature has sealed their fate. Evolution seems to pause for them, as if the universe itself refuses to grant them advancement.

Yet perhaps there is mercy in this. Since they cannot hope, they cannot mourn. Without memory, they do not recall past pain. Without foresight, they do not fear the future. Their existence is narrow, harsh, and brief—but they are spared the burden of reflection. And so, in a strange way, they are free. Not free to rise, but free from regret. A terrible freedom, perhaps. But still, a kind of peace.
