I rose before dawn, the grey light of pre-sunrise filtering through the dormitory windows, and knelt by the centre stand to complete my final vigil. The night-lamp flickered weakly, casting long, dancing shadows across the floor as I worked. Every bead, every thread of silk was spent before the chain reached the desired length and opulence. By the rule of contraries, I knew that to truly suit the specific taste I sought to gratify, an overwhelming display was essential. Thus, I crafted it double. To finish the ornament, I required a gold clasp; luckily, I possessed one from the fastening of my sole necklace. I detached it, re-attached it, coiled the completed *garde-corps* tightly, and nestled it inside a small box I had purchased for its brilliant hue. It was carved from a tropic shell of "nacarat" color, adorned with a tiny coronet of sparkling blue stones. Inside the lid, with the precise tip of my scissors, I carefully engraved the initials: P.C.D.E.

Recall, if you will, the grandeur of Madame Beck's annual fête. At these gatherings, the school subscribed to a handsome present for the matron, a tradition observed solely for her and, in modified form, for her kinsman and counselor, M. Emanuel. While it was an honor spontaneously awarded to him—an accolade reflecting the genuine esteem in which the Professor of Literature was held despite his frequent irritabilities and prejudices—no valuable articles were ever offered. He had made it clear that plate or jewelry were unwelcome. Yet, he craved a slight tribute, not for its monetary worth, but for the sincerity behind it. A diamond ring or a gold snuff-box presented with pomp would have palled upon him; a flower, or a simple sketch offered with heartfelt emotion, would have delighted him. Such was his nature; a man perhaps not wise by the standards of his generation, yet possessing a filial sympathy with the "dayspring on high."

M. Paul's fête fell on the first of March, a Thursday. It was a fine, sunny day, coinciding with the customary morning for Mass and the half-holiday that allowed for afternoon strolls, shopping, or visits. These factors combined to induce a general smartness and freshness of dress. Clean collars were in vogue, replacing the ordinary, dingy woolen classes dresses with lighter, clearer fabrics. Mademoiselle Zélie St. Pierre, on this particular Thursday, even ventured into a "robe de soie"—an item deemed in economical Labassecour an article of hazardous splendour. Nay, it was remarked that she had summoned a *coiffeur* to dress her hair that morning; the pupils were acute enough to detect that she had bedewed her handkerchief and fingers with a new, fashionable perfume. Poor Zélie! She was wont to declare around this time that she was tired to death of her secluded, laborious life, longing for the means and leisure for relaxation, for a husband who would pay her debts—which were woeful—and supply her wardrobe, leaving her at liberty to "goûter un peu les plaisirs." Rumors abounded that her eye was fixed upon M. Emanuel, and certainly, his eye was often fixed upon her. He would sit and watch her perseveringly for minutes at a time. I have seen him give her a quarter-hour's gaze while the class sat silently composing, he throned upon his estrade, unoccupied.

Aware of this basilisk attention, she would writhe beneath it, half-flattered, half-puzzled. And M. Paul would follow her sensations, sometimes looking appallingly acute. For in some instances, he possessed the terrible, unerring penetration of instinct, piercing the hiding-places of the heart's last lurking thoughts, discerning under florid veils the bare, barren places of the spirit. Yes, he could perceive its perverted tendencies and hidden false curves—all that men and women would not have known—the twisted spine, the malformed limb born with them, and far worse, the stains or disfigurements they might bring upon themselves. No calamity so accursed that M. Emanuel could not pity and forgive, if acknowledged candidly. But where his questioning eyes met dishonest denial, where his ruthless researches found deceitful concealment, oh, then he could be cruel. I thought him wicked! He would exultantly snatch the screen from poor, shrinking wretches, passionately hurrying them to the summit of the mount of exposure, showing them all naked, all false—poor living lies, the spawn of that horrid Truth which cannot be looked upon unveiled. He thought he did justice; yet, in my opinion, I doubt whether man has a right to do such justice on man. More than once in these visitations, I have felt compelled to give tears to his victims and spare no ire or keen reproach to himself. He deserved it, though it was difficult to shake him in his firm conviction that his work was righteous and needed.

Breakfast concluded, Mass attended, and the school-bell rang, filling the rooms. A very pretty spectacle was presented in *classe*. Pupils and teachers sat neatly arrayed, orderly and expectant, each bearing in hand a bouquet of felicitation—the prettiest spring-flowers, fresh and fragrant. Alas, I alone had no bouquet. I like to see flowers growing, but when they are gathered, they cease to please me. I regard them as things rootless and perishable; their likeness to fleeting life makes me sad. I never offer flowers to those I love, nor do I wish to receive them from hands dear to me. Mademoiselle St. Pierre marked my empty hands; she could not believe I had been so remiss. With avidity, her eye roved over and round me, surely expecting me to possess some solitary symbolic flower, some small knot of violets to win myself praise for taste and ingenuity. The unimaginative "Anglaise" proved superior to the Parisienne's fears: I sat literally unprovided, as bare of bloom or leaf as a winter tree. This ascertained, Zélie smiled, well pleased.

"How wisely you have acted to keep your money, Miss Lucie!" she exclaimed. "Silly I have gone and thrown away two francs on a bouquet of hot-house flowers!" And she displayed with pride her splendid nosegay.

But hush! A step—*the* step. It came promptly, as usual, but with a promptitude that inspired us to flatter ourselves, driven by feelings other than mere excitability of nerve or vehemence of intent. We believed our Professor's "foot-fall" (to speak romantically) carried a friendly promise this morning; and so it did.

He entered in a mood that made him as good as a new sunbeam to the already well-lit first *classe*. The morning light played amongst our plants and laughed upon our walls, catching an added lustre from M. Paul's all-benignant salute. Like a true Frenchman (though I know not why I should say so, for he was of strain neither French nor Labassecourien), he had dressed for the "situation" and the occasion. Not obscured by the vague folds, sinister and conspirator-like, of his soot-dark *paletôt*, were the outlines of his person. On the contrary, his figure (such as it was, I do not boast of it) was well set off by a civilized coat and a silken vest quite pretty to behold. The defiant and pagan *bonnet-grec* had vanished; bare-headed, he came upon us, carrying a Christian hat in his gloved hand. The little man looked well, very well. There was a clearness of amity in his blue eye, and a glow of good feeling on his dark complexion, which passed perfectly in the place of beauty. One did not care to observe that his nose, though far from small, was of no particular shape, his cheek thin, his brow marked and square, his mouth no rosebud. One accepted him as he was, and felt his presence the reverse of damping or insignificant.

He passed to his desk, placing his hat and gloves upon it. "Bon jour, mes amies," he said, in a tone that somehow made amends to some amongst us for many a sharp snap and savage snarl. Not a jocund, good-fellow tone, still less an unctuous priestly accent, but a voice belonging to himself—a voice used when his heart passed the words to his lips. That same heart did speak sometimes; though irritable, it was not an ossified organ. In its core was a place tender beyond a man's tenderness, a place that humbled him to little children, that bound him to girls and women to whom, rebel as he might, he could not disown his affinity, nor quite deny that, on the whole, he was better with them than with his own sex.

"We all wish Monsieur a good day, and present to him our congratulations on the anniversary of his fête," said Mademoiselle Zélie, constituting herself spokeswoman of the assembly. Advancing with no more twists of affectation than were with her indispensable to the achievement of motion, she laid her costly bouquet before him. He bowed over it.

The long train of offerings followed. All the pupils, sweeping past with the gliding step foreigners practice, left their tributes as they went by.
