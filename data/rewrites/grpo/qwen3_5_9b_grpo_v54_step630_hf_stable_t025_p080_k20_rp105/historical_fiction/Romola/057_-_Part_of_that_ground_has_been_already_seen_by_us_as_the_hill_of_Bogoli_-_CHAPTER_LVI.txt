The morning sun had grown heavy and oppressive to Romola by the time she turned from the walls of San Marco back into the intersecting lanes near Santa Croce. The Borgo La Croce lay in a profound, sun-drenched silence, so still that she could hear the rhythm of her own footsteps on the pavement. As she rounded a bend, a small figure emerged from the glare—a toddler, no older than three, clad only in a white shirt. He paused mid-waddle, his back square and sturdy beneath a cloud of reddish-brown curls.

When he turned, Romola saw dark eyes wide with tears and a lower lip trembling as he clutched his shirt in helpless fists. A tall black figure casting a shadow over him seemed to trigger a climax of bewildered fear, sending a loud sob rolling from his throat.

Acting on a maternal instinct that stirred deep within her, Romola uncovered her head, stooped low, and wrapped her arms around the child, pressing her cheek against his. At first, his cries grew louder, but he made no attempt to flee. Soon, the outburst ceased with the strange abruptness characteristic of childhood emotions; his face smoothed out, fixed in an open-mouthed stare at her.

"You have lost yourself, little one," she said softly, kissing him. "Never mind! We will find the house again. Perhaps your mother will meet us."

She sensed he had escaped while his mother's attention was elsewhere and believed he would soon be pursued.

"Oh, what a heavy, heavy boy!" she murmured, attempting to lift him. "I cannot carry you. Come, then, you must toddle back by my side."

The parted lips remained motionless in awed silence, one brown fist still gripping the shirt with tenacity, while the other yielded willingly to her soft, strong hand.

"Do you have a mother?" Romola asked, looking down at the boy with a yearning she could not suppress. He offered no answer. A girl might have chirped, but this square-shouldered little man with his big cloud of curls remained mute.

He was, however, keenly aware of his destination. At the turn by San Ambrogio, he dragged Romola toward it, looking up at her.

"Ah, that is the way home, is it?" she smiled. He merely thrust his head forward and pulled, urging her to hurry.

After another turn, Romola found herself in a short street opening onto garden grounds. The boy paused before a house at the end, pulling her toward stone stairs. He showed no desire to release her hand, and she refused to leave him without ensuring he was delivered to his guardians. They ascended the stairs, emerging from the sunlight into dimness, until a stream of light spilled from an open doorway. Passing through a small lobby, they entered another room where Romola paused. No sound greeted her arrival.

At the far end of the room, opposite the light, sat Tessa with one hand on the edge of a cradle, her head drooping slightly as she slept. Near a window, with her back to the door, Monna Lisa sat preparing salad in a deaf, unconscious state. Romola had only an instant to take in the stillness before Lillo snatched his hand away and ran to his mother's side, leaning his head back against her arm to survey Romola seriously from a distance.

As Lillo pushed against her, Tessa opened her eyes in bewilderment. But her gaze rested on the figure at the doorway, and she started up, blushing deeply and trembling, unable to speak or move forward.

"Ah! We have seen each other before," Romola said, smiling and stepping forward. "I am glad it was your little boy. He was crying in the street; I suppose he had run away. So we walked together a little way, and then he knew where he was and brought me here. But you had not missed him? That is well, else you would have been frightened."

The shock of discovering Lillo had wandered away overwhelmed Tessa. Her color drained, and seizing the boy's arm, she ran to Monna Lisa, whispering loudly in the old woman's ear with a half-sob:

"Oh, Lisa, you are wicked! Why did you stand with your back to the door? Lillo ran away ever so far into the street."

"Holy Mother!" Monna Lisa exclaimed in her meek, thick tone, dropping her spoon. "Where were *you*, then? I thought you were there, watching him."

"But you *know* I go to sleep when I rock," Tessa retorted pettishly.

"Well, well, we must keep the outer door shut, or tie him up," Monna Lisa advised. "For he'll be as cunning as Satan before long, and that's the holy truth. But how did he come back?"

This question brought Tessa back to the present. Without answering, she turned toward Romola, blushing and timid again, while Monna Lisa's eyes followed her. The old woman made a low reverence and said, "Doubtless the most noble lady brought him back." Advancing slightly, she added, "It is my shame that he was found with only his shirt on; but he kicked and wouldn't wear his other clothes this morning, and the mother, poor thing, will never hear of him being beaten. But what can an old woman do without a stick when the lad's legs get so strong? Let your nobleness look at his legs."

Lillo, conscious his legs were being inspected, pulled his shirt up higher and looked down at their olive roundness with a dispassionate curiosity. Romola laughed, stooping to give him a caressing shake and a kiss. This action helped reassure Tessa, who had already begun to feel relief from Monna Lisa's respectful address. For when Naldo had been told of the adventure at the Carnival, and Tessa had asked who the heavenly lady who appeared just when needed might be—perhaps the Holy Madonna herself?—he had replied, "Not exactly, my Tessa; only one of the saints," choosing not to elaborate. Thus, in Tessa's dreamlike combination of small experiences, Romola remained confusedly associated with church pictures. When she reappeared, the grateful memory of her protection was slightly tinged with religious awe—not deeply, for Tessa's dread was chiefly of ugly and evil beings. It seemed unlikely that good beings would be angry and punish her, as Nofri and the devil were wont to do. Now that Monna Lisa spoke freely of Lillo's legs and Romola laughed, Tessa felt more at ease.

"Ninna is in the cradle," she said. "She's pretty too."

Romola went to look at the sleeping Ninna, and Monna Lisa, one of the exceptionally meek deaf who never expected to be spoken to, returned to her salad.

"Ah! She is waking; she has opened her blue eyes," Romola noted. "You must take her up, and I will sit down in this chair—may I?—and nurse Lillo. Come, Lillo!"

She sat in Tito's chair and extended her arms toward the lad, whose eyes had followed her. He hesitated, pointing his small fingers at her with a half-puzzled, half-angry expression. "That's Babbo's chair," he said, not seeing how to resolve the difficulty if Babbo returned to find Romola in his place.

"But Babbo is not here, and I shall go soon. Come, let me nurse you as he does," Romola said, wondering for the first time what sort of Babbo he was whose wife was dressed in contadina fashion yet possessed a certain daintiness indicating idleness and plenty. Lillo consented to be lifted, and finding the lap exceedingly comfortable, began to explore her dress and hands, searching for ornaments beside the rosary.

Tessa, having coaxed Ninna out of her waking peevishness, sat down in her low chair near Romola's knee, arranging Ninna's tiny person with jealousy that the strange lady seemed to notice the boy as much as Naldo did.

"Lillo was going to be angry with me because I sat in Babbo's chair," Romola said, bending to kiss Ninna's little foot. "Will he come soon and want it?"

"Ah, no!" Tessa replied. "You can sit in it a long while. I shall be sorry when you go. When you first came to take care of me at the Carnival, I thought it wonderful; you came and went away so fast. And Naldo said, perhaps you were a saint, and that made me tremble a little, though the saints are very good, I know; and you were good to me, and now you have taken care of Lillo. Perhaps you will always come and take care of me. That was how Naldo did a long while ago; he came and took care of me when I was frightened, one San Giovanni. I couldn't think where he came from—he was so beautiful and good. And so are you," Tessa concluded, looking up at Romola with devout admiration.

"Naldo is your husband. His eyes are like Lillo's," Romola said, examining the boy's darkly pencilled eyebrows, unusual for his age. She spoke not interrogatively, but with a quiet certainty of inference that was necessarily mysterious to Tessa.

"Ah! You know him!" Tessa said, pausing in wonder. "Perhaps you know Nofri and Peretola, and our house on the hill, and everything. Yes, like Lillo's; but not his hair. His hair is dark and long—" she continued, growing excited. "Ah! If you know it, ecco!"

She placed her hand on a thin red silk cord around her neck and drew from her bosom the tiny old parchment *Breve*, a horn of red coral, and a long dark curl carefully tied at one end and suspended with those mystic treasures. She held them toward Romola, keeping them away from Ninna's snatching hand.

"It is a fresh one. I cut it lately. See how bright it is!" she said, laying it against the white background of Romola's fingers. "They get dim, and then he lets me cut another when his hair grows; and I put it with the *Breve*, because sometimes he is away a long while, and then I think it helps to take care of me."

A slight shiver passed through Romola as the curl was laid across her fingers. At Tessa's first mention of her husband arriving mysteriously from unknown quarters, a possibility had risen before Romola that made her heart beat faster; for one anxiously searching for a certain object finds peculiar significance in the faintest suggestions. And when the curl was held toward her, it seemed for an instant like a mocking phantom of the lock she herself had cut five years ago. Yet she preserved her outward calmness, bent not only on knowing the truth but on arriving at that knowledge in a way that would not pain this poor, trusting, ignorant creature with the child's mind in a woman's body. "Foolish and helpless:" yes; so far she corresponded to Baldassarre's account.

"It is a beautiful curl," she said, resisting the impulse to withdraw her hand. "Lillo's curls will be like it, perhaps, for his cheek, too, is dark. And you never know where your husband goes when he leaves you?"

"No," Tessa said, putting back her treasures out of the children's way. "But I know Messer San Michele takes care of him, for he gave him a beautiful coat, all made of little chains; and if he puts that on, nobody can kill him. And perhaps, if—"

Tessa hesitated, under a recurrence of that original dreamy wonder about Romola which had been dispelled by chatting contact—"if you *were* a saint, you would take care of him, too, because you have taken care of me and Lillo."

An agitated flush came over Romola's face in the first moment of certainty, but she had bent her cheek against Lillo's head. The feeling that leaped out in that flush was something like exultation at the thought that the wife's burden might be about to slip from her overladen shoulders; that this little ignorant creature might prove to be Tito's lawful wife. A strange exultation for a proud and high-born woman to experience! But it seemed to Romola as if that were the only issue that would make duty anything other than an insoluble problem. Yet she was not deaf to Tessa's last appealing words; she raised her head and said, in her clearest tones—

"I will always take care of you if I see you need me. But that beautiful coat? Your husband did not wear it when you were first married? Perhaps he used not to be so long away from you then?"

"Ah, yes! He was. Much—much longer. So long, I thought he would never come back. I used to cry. Oh me! I was beaten then; a long, long while ago at Peretola, where we had the goats and mules."

"And how long had you been married before your husband had that chain-coat?" Romola asked, her heart beating faster and faster.

Tessa looked meditative and began counting on her fingers, while Romola watched them as if they would reveal the secret of her destiny.

"The chestnuts were ripe when we were married," Tessa said, marking off her thumb and fingers again as she spoke; "and then again they were ripe at Peretola before he came back, and then again, after that, on the hill. And soon the soldiers came, and we heard the trumpets, and then Naldo had the coat."

"You had been married more than two years. In which church were you married?" Romola asked, too entirely absorbed by one thought to pose any question less direct. Perhaps before the next morning she might go to her godfather and declare she was not Tito Melema's lawful wife—that the vows binding her to strive after an impossible union had been rendered void beforehand.

Tessa gave a slight start at Romola's new tone of inquiry and looked up with a hesitating expression. Hitherto she had prattled on without consciousness that she was making revelations, any more than when she repeated old things to Monna Lisa.
