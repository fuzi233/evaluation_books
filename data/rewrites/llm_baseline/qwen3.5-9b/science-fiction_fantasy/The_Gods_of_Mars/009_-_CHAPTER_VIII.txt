I finally understood why the black pirate had held me captive with his bizarre narrative. For miles, he had sensed the approach of rescue, and but for that single, betraying glance, the battleship would have been directly overhead moments ago. The boarding party, surely already hanging from the vessel's keel, would have swarmed our deck, extinguishing my fleeting hope of escape in an instant. Being a veteran of aerial combat, I knew exactly how to react. Simultaneously, I reversed the engines and dove the small craft a full hundred feet downward. Above me, I watched the dangling figures of the boarding party as the massive battleship raced over our heads. Then, I surged upward at a sharp angle, pushing the throttle to its limit. Like a bolt fired from a crossbow, my sleek vessel shot its steel prow straight at the whirring propellers of the giant above. If I could merely strike them, the behemoth would be crippled for hours, and my chance of flight restored.

At that precise moment, the sun crested the horizon, revealing a hundred grim, black faces peering over the stern of the pursuing ship. Upon seeing us, a roar of fury erupted from a hundred throats. Commands were barked, but it was too late to save the great propellers. With a deafening crash, we collided. Instinctively, I reversed my engine, but my nose was instantly wedged into the hole I had torn in the battleship's stern. I managed to tear free after only a second, but that brief interval was sufficient for the black devils to swarm my deck. There was no resistance offered. There was simply no space to fight; we were overwhelmed by sheer numbers. Just as swords threatened me, a command from Xodar halted his men. "Secure them," he ordered, "but do not injure them."

Several pirates had already freed Xodar. He personally supervised my disarmament and ensured I was properly bound. He believed his work thorough, yet he underestimated me. While the bindings would have held a Martian firm, they were mere cotton strings around my wrists. When the time came, I could snap them as easily as thread. The girl was bound as well, and then they fastened us together. Meanwhile, they maneuvered our craft alongside the disabled battleship, and soon we were hoisted onto its deck.

Fully a thousand black men manned the great engine of destruction. Their decks were packed as they pressed forward, limited only by discipline, to catch a glimpse of their captives. The girl's beauty drew forth brutal comments and vulgar jests. It was clear that these self-proclaimed supermen were far inferior to the red men of Barsoom in both refinement and chivalry. My close-cropped black hair and thern complexion became topics of much speculation. When Xodar recounted my fighting prowess and strange origins to his fellow nobles, they crowded around me with endless questions. The fact that I wore the harness and metal of a thern slain by a member of my party convinced them I was an enemy of their hereditary foes, placing me on favorable terms in their eyes.

Without exception, the blacks were handsome and well-built. The officers stood out amidst the wondrous magnificence of their resplendent gear. Many harnesses were encrusted with gold, platinum, silver, and precious stones, completely obscuring the leather beneath. The commanding officer's harness was a solid mass of diamonds. Against the ebony backdrop of his skin, they blazed with a peculiar, accentuated brilliance. The entire scene was enchanting: the handsome men, the barbaric splendor of their accouterments, the polished skeel wood of the deck, the gloriously grained sorapus of the cabins inlaid with priceless jewels and metals in intricate designs, the burnished gold of the handrails, and the shining metal of the guns.

Phaidor and I were taken below decks. Still bound, we were thrown into a small compartment containing a single porthole. As our escort departed, they barred the door behind them. We could hear the men working on the broken propellers, and through the porthole, we saw the vessel drifting lazily toward the south. For some time, neither of us spoke, each lost in private thoughts. I wondered about the fate of Tars Tarkas and the girl, Thuvia. Even if they evaded pursuit, they must eventually fall into the hands of either red men or greens, and as fugitives from the Valley Dor, they could expect little else but a swift and terrible death. How I wished I had accompanied them! It seemed I could not fail to impress the intelligent red men of Barsoom with the truth—that a cruel, senseless superstition had deceived them. Tardos Mors would believe me; I was certain of it. So would Dejah Thoris. And a thousand of my red and green warrior friends would gladly face eternal damnation for my sake. Like Tars Tarkas, wherever I led, they would follow. My only danger lay in escaping the blacks only to fall into the hands of unfriendly red or green men, which would mean a short, harsh sentence. Yet, the likelihood of ever escaping the blacks seemed remote, rendering that fear moot.

Linked by a rope allowing movement of only three or four feet apart, we sat on a low bench beneath the porthole. The bench was the sole furniture, carved from sorapus wood. The floor, ceiling, and walls were constructed of carborundum aluminum—a light, impenetrable material widely used in Martian fighting ships. As I sat contemplating the future, my gaze remained fixed on the porthole. Suddenly, I turned to Phaidor. She regarded me with a strange expression I had never seen before. She was breathtakingly beautiful in that moment. Instantly, her white lids lowered, veiling her eyes, and I thought I spotted a delicate flush tingling her cheeks. Clearly, she was embarrassed at being caught staring at a "lesser" creature, I assumed.

"Do you find the study of the lower orders interesting?" I asked, laughing.

She looked up again with a nervous but relieved chuckle. "Oh, very," she said, "especially when they possess such excellent profiles."

It was my turn to blush, though I did not hide it. I felt she was teasing me, and I admired a brave heart capable of finding humor on the road to death. So, I laughed with her.

"Do you know where we are going?" she asked.

"To solve the mystery of the eternal hereafter, I imagine," I replied.

"I am going to a fate worse than that," she said, shivering slightly.

"What do you mean?"

"I can only guess," she replied, "since no thern damsel of the millions stolen by black pirates throughout the ages has ever returned to recount her experiences among them. That they never take male prisoners strengthens the belief that the fate of the girls they steal is worse than death."

"Is it not just retribution?" I couldn't help asking.

"What do you mean?"

"Do not the therns themselves do likewise with the poor creatures who voluntarily pilgrimage down the River of Mystery? Was not Thuvia for fifteen years a plaything and a slave? Is it less than just that you should suffer as you have caused others to suffer?"

"You do not understand," she replied softly. "We therns are a holy race. It is an honor for a lesser creature to be a slave among us. Did we not occasionally save a few of the lower orders who foolishly float down an unknown river to an unknown end, sparing them from becoming prey to the plant men and the apes?"

"But do you not actively encourage this superstition among the outside world?" I argued. "That is the wickedest of your deeds. Can you tell me why you foster this cruel deception?"

"All life on Barsoom," she said, "is created solely for the support of the race of therns. How else could we live if the outer world did not furnish our labor and our food? Would a thern demean himself by labor?"

"It is true then that you eat human flesh?" I asked in horror.

She looked at me with pitying commiseration for my ignorance. "Truly, we eat the flesh of the lower orders. Do you not also?"

"The flesh of beasts, yes," I replied, "but not the flesh of man."

"As man may eat the flesh of beasts, so may gods eat the flesh of man. The Holy Therns are the gods of Barsoom."

Disgust washed over me, and I suppose it showed on my face.

"You are an unbeliever now," she continued gently, "but should we be fortunate enough to escape the black pirates and return to the court of Matai Shang, I think we shall find arguments to convince you of your errors. And—" she hesitated, "perhaps we shall find a way to keep you as... one of us."

Again, her eyes dropped to the floor, and a faint color suffused her cheeks. I could not comprehend her meaning, nor did I for a long time. Dejah Thoris often called me a simpleton in certain matters, and I suspected she was right.

"I fear I would ill requite your father's hospitality," I answered. "For the first thing I should do, were I a thern, would be to set an armed guard at the mouth of the River Iss to escort the poor, deluded voyagers back to the outer world. Furthermore, I would dedicate my life to the extermination of the hideous plant men and their horrible companions, the great white apes."
