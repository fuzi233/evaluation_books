PROLOGUE

On the wrapper pinned to the manuscript that follows, Doctor Hesselius has left a note—longer than a kitchen inventory and as exacting—together with a reference to his own Essay upon the peculiar matter the pages disclose. In that Essay he handles the mystery with his habitual learning and sharp compression, carving away excess as a careful cook pares rind from fruit, leaving only what will keep and bite. It is to appear, he indicates, as a single volume among the collected papers of that extraordinary man.

I publish this case here for the interest of the laity, and I will not steal so much as a pinch of emphasis from the intelligent lady who tells it. After weighing the temptation to season her recital with a learned précis, I have resolved to do nothing of the kind. The Doctor’s reasoning—dense, cleanly jointed, and cold as a slab—may be found in his own statement, where he speaks of matters “involving, not improbably, some of the profoundest arcana of our dual existence, and its intermediates.” To drag portions of it into these pages would be like pouring a strong stock into a delicate dish and claiming the result still tastes of its true ingredients.

When I first discovered his attached paper, I was eager to reopen the correspondence Doctor Hesselius had begun so many years before with his informant—a person, by every sign, as clever and painstaking as any food historian tracing a vanished recipe from a half-erased marginal note. I imagined her hand returning to the table, adding small domestic particulars that only lived experience can provide: the hour a sound changed in the house, the way a familiar room altered in the ear, the hush after a carriage passed, the thin, mechanical rasp of some modern contrivance intruding upon older silences. The sonic air of a place, I have learned, can be as nourishing or as corrosive as what is eaten there; and in these times, when noise is manufactured and sold as if it were music, such distinctions are too easily forgotten.

But I found, to my regret, that she had died in the interval. She could, perhaps, have added little to the narrative she now communicates in the following pages with—so far as I can judge—conscientious particularity; and yet I cannot help thinking of what perishes with a voice: not only a life, but the unrepeatable timbre of its telling, the private dialect of sensation that no dominant lexicon can truly translate.

In Styria we lived—if one can call it living—within the old walls of a schloss that had learned to do without applause. A modest income there, eight or nine hundred a year, stretched as far as a thrifty hand could pull it; the land itself seemed complicit, offering milk, fish, fruit, fuel, and labor at such prices that extravagance looked almost indecent. My father, English by birth and habit though long in Austrian service, had retired upon his pension and what patrimony remained to him, and bought this feudal place—stone, moat, chapel, towers, and all—at a bargain that still felt like a superstition: as though bargains were never granted without some quiet cost.

Our schloss stood on a slight rise inside a forest that reached away like a dark sea. In front ran an ancient road—narrow, worn to a pale ribbon by centuries of wheels—passing the drawbridge (never raised in my time) and the moat. The moat was no mere ditch; it was a domestic larder, a gleaming ring of water stocked with perch that flashed in the green shade, and it carried swans with the serene arrogance of owners. In summer the lilies floated like white dishes laid out on a black tablecloth, their broad leaves collecting rain and pollen, each cupped pool catching the sky’s faintest light. Beyond, the schloss presented its many windows like blind eyes, its towers, and the little Gothic chapel that always smelled of cold stone and old wax, even at noon.

The forest opened in an irregular glade before the gate, and to the right a steep Gothic bridge carried the road over a stream that ran in deep shadow under fir and beech. That stream had its own voice—thin in drought, full-throated after rain—and I learned later how much my mind depended on such honest sounds. Here there were no factory whistles, no iron pounding, no relentless clatter of streets; only the measured speech of water, the conspiratorial tapping of twigs, the wind’s low hand in the leaves, and at night the owls stitching their calls across the dark. Silence in such a place is never empty. It is crowded with small living noises, and when one of them stops, you feel it like a hand laid suddenly on your mouth.

We were very lonely. Looking from the hall door toward the road, the forest ran fifteen miles one way and twelve the other. The nearest inhabited village lay about seven English miles to the left. The nearest inhabited schloss with any historic associations was old General Spielsdorf’s, nearly twenty miles away to the right. I say the nearest inhabited village because, only three miles westward—toward the General’s place—there stood a ruined village with a quaint little church now roofless, its aisle holding the mouldering tombs of the proud Karnstein family, long extinct. Above those silent stones, deeper in the wood, the desolate chateau of Karnstein overlooked the abandoned town. There was a legend about why that place had emptied itself of breath and language, why prayers ceased there and names went unspoken; I shall tell it another time. At the date of my story I did not yet know what it meant to lose a tongue, to have words die out of a place as thoroughly as people. I only knew that even the birds seemed to alter their music when the wind came from that direction.

Our household—speaking only of family—was astonishingly small. My father, the kindest man on earth and already growing old; and I, then nineteen. Eight years have passed since that time. My mother, a Styrian lady, died in my infancy; I cannot even pretend to remember her. In her stead I had, from almost my earliest consciousness, a governess whose fat, benignant face stands in my memory as steadily as a lamp in a familiar window. This was Madame Perrodon, a native of Berne, good-natured, bustling, and sincere, who supplied with her care what tenderness she could to the blank left by my mother.

Madame Perrodon made the third at our little dinner table. A fourth was sometimes present: Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, a “finishing governess,” as you say—French and German at her command, with that attentive, polished manner that treats every sentence as if it were being served on porcelain. Madame Perrodon spoke French and broken English; my father and I spoke English daily—partly from patriotism, partly from fear that our language, left unpractised in these woods, might become as extinct for us as the Karnsteins. The result was a Babel in which a single thought changed flavor as it passed from one tongue to another. A scolded fault sounded almost playful in French; a praise in German could feel like a verdict. There are experiences that refuse translation entirely; they sit stubbornly in the mouth that first formed them, and in any other language they taste of something else.

We had, besides, two or three young lady friends near my own age who visited for longer or shorter terms, and whose visits I sometimes returned. Neighbors—five or six leagues away—made occasional calls, arriving dusty and hungry, and leaving with baskets of bread, cheese, and fruit pressed upon them by our servants as if hospitality were a law of nature. Yet even so my life was solitary. My governesses had only such control over me as wise persons can expect with a rather spoiled girl whose only parent allowed her pretty nearly her own way. Solitude, in such a house, is not only the absence of company; it is a way the rooms listen back to you.

The first occurrence in my life that left a terrible impression—one that has never been effaced—was among the earliest incidents I can recollect. Some will call it trifling and wonder why I record it. You will see, by-and-by, why I must.

The nursery—so called, though I had it all to myself—was a large room in the upper story under a steep oak roof. The beams were dark with age, and the air carried that clean, dry scent of old wood warmed in the day and cooling at night, like bread left on a board. The furniture was clumsy and ancient, as if carved to endure rather than to please. Through the small lattice windows, light entered grudgingly, thin and dusty, and even by day the corners seemed to hoard shadow.

I cannot have been more than six when one night I awoke and, looking round from my bed, failed to see the nursery maid. Neither was my nurse there. I thought myself alone.

I was not frightened. I was one of those happy children carefully kept ignorant of ghost stories and fairy tales and all that lore which later makes us draw the coverlet to our chin because a door gives a sudden crack, or because a candle’s last tremble sets the bedpost dancing its thin shadow nearer and nearer to our face. I felt, instead, vexed—insulted—at being, as I supposed, neglected. My throat swelled with the beginning of a whimper, preparatory to a hearty bout of roaring, when I saw—so plainly that the memory has never dimmed—a solemn but very pretty face looking at me from the bedside.

It was the face of a young lady. She was kneeling, and her hands were under the coverlet as if she had already warmed them there. Her hair fell darkly about her, and her expression held a strange composure, like the stillness that comes over a kitchen when everyone pauses to listen for a kettle’s first whisper. I looked at her with pleased wonder and stopped whimpering at once.

She caressed me—soft, sure touches, not hurried, not tentative—and then she lay down beside me on the bed and drew me toward her, smiling. The scent of her was not like soap or lavender or the starch of linen; it was closer, sweeter, as if something had been shut too long in a cedar chest with dried petals and had taken on the woody dampness of the place. I felt immediately soothed—delightfully soothed—as if all the air in the room had been stirred and settled into calm. I remember the sound then: the forest outside had gone very quiet, and in that quiet her breathing seemed the only true measure of time. I fell asleep again.

I was wakened by a sensation as if two needles ran into my breast very deep at the same moment.

The shock tore a cry from me—sharp, involuntary, like a child scalded. The lady started back, her eyes fixed upon me with an intentness that froze me more than the pain. In the same instant she slipped down upon the floor and, as I thought, hid herself under the bed.

For the first time fear came—real fear, not the petulant complaint of a neglected child. The room’s sounds altered; even my own breath seemed too loud, too exposed. I yelled with all my might and main, and the cry felt as if it struck the rafters and fell back on me.

Nurse, nursery maid, housekeeper—others too—came running in with lights, the flame wobbling and throwing their shadows gigantic against the sloping roof. The smell of tallow and the cold draft from the corridor rushed together. They crowded round, and as I told my story—between sobs, pointing, clinging—they made light of it, soothing me. Yet even at six, even through panic, I could read faces as one reads a table laid wrong: a cup in the place of a plate, a knife missing, a crumb where none should be. Their faces were pale with an unwonted anxiety. They looked under the bed; they searched about the room; they peeped under tables and plucked open cupboards with hands that shook more than their voices admitted. The housekeeper whispered to the nurse—thinking I could not hear, though children hear as animals do:

“Lay your hand along that hollow in the bed; someone did lie there, so sure as you did not; the place is still warm.”

I remember the nursery maid petting me—her palm heavy and trembling—and all three of them examining my chest where I said I felt the puncture. They pronounced there was no sign. No mark. No blood. Yet the pain had been as certain as a pin thrust, as bodily as hunger.

They stayed sitting up all night. From that time a servant always sat up in the nursery until I was about fourteen. The simple domestic routine of guarding a child’s sleep became a nightly vigil, as if sleep itself were a pantry that must not be left unlatched.

For a long time afterward I was very nervous. A doctor was called in, pallid and elderly. I can still see him: a long saturnine face, slightly pitted with smallpox, a chestnut wig set upon him as if it were another instrument he wore, like his spectacles or his watch. For a good while, every second day, he came and gave me medicine—bitter draughts that made my tongue curl and my stomach revolt. I hated them with a child’s honest loathing. The spoon’s cold metal, the smell of the mixture—herbs steeped too long, alcohol and something earthy—still rise in my memory when I recall that time.

The morning after I saw the stranger I was in a state of terror. I could not bear to be left alone for a moment, daylight though it was. Even the bright ordinary sounds—the clink of a plate, the soft thud of bread on a board, the murmur of servants below—did not restore the world to safety. Every pause between noises seemed a trap.

I remember my father coming up and standing at the bedside. He talked cheerfully, asking the nurse questions, laughing heartily at one of the answers, as if laughter could drive away whatever had happened in the dark. He patted me on the shoulder, kissed me, and told me not to be frightened—that it was nothing but a dream and could not hurt me. His English voice, so familiar and steady, tried to make reality of its own choosing. But I was not comforted. I knew, with the hard certainty children sometimes have, that the visit of the strange woman was not a dream. I was awfully frightened.

The nursery maid tried to console me with an assurance—so earnest it was almost a plea—that it was she who had come and looked at me and lain down beside me, and that I must have been half-dreaming not to have known her face. The nurse supported her, nodding and smoothing the sheet as if smoothing my thoughts. But it did not quite satisfy me. The face I had seen had not belonged to either of them. It had been too still, too intent—like someone listening for a word in a language she loved and feared to lose.

Sometime that day I remember a venerable old man, in a black cassock, coming into the room with the nurse and housekeeper. He spoke a little to them and very kindly to me. His face was sweet and gentle, and his white hair made a soft halo in the dim light. He told me they were going to pray. He joined my hands together, placing my small fingers in a careful clasp, and desired me to say softly while they were praying, “Lord hear all good prayers for us, for Jesus’ sake.”

I think those were the very words. I repeated them often to myself afterward, and for years my nurse made me say them in my prayers. Yet even that phrase—comforting as it was—felt like an attempt to name something that did not wish to be named. There are terrors that do not answer to the dominant vocabulary of a house, and when we force them into our approved words, the words come out thin, like broth made from bones already boiled too often.

I see still the thoughtful face of that old man in his black cassock standing in that rude, lofty brown room, the heavy antique furniture about him, the scanty light entering through the lattice, dust motes drifting in it like slow snow. He kneeled, and the three women with him, and he prayed aloud with an earnest quavering voice for what appeared to me a very long time. The sound of his prayer filled the room the way a steady flame fills a lantern, and for a little while the forest’s hush beyond the walls did not seem so watchful.

All my life before that event is almost gone from me, and for some time after it, too, is obscure. But the scenes I have described stand out vivid—isolated pictures of a phantasmagoria surrounded by darkness—and whenever the night grows too quiet, I hear again the sudden absence of ordinary sounds, and feel, as if through the bedclothes, the cold precision of those two needle-points entering my breast.

I am going to relate what I should scarce dare set down, but that my own eyes, and my own ears—those faithful instruments that betray us only when we have already begun to betray ourselves—were witnesses to it.

It was one of those sweet summer evenings when the day seems unwilling to quit the fields it has warmed, and the forest, after long storing sunlight in its leaves, exhales it back as perfume. My father asked me, as he sometimes did, to walk with him along the broad vista in front of our schloss—an avenue cut through the woods like a green corridor, where the long grass was already beginning to cool, and the limes, heavy with blossom, breathed out a honeyed breath that made me think, absurdly, of kitchens and pantries; of the old country ways of steeping their pale flowers for cordials, or laying them between folds of linen to keep the cupboards sweet.

“General Spielsdorf cannot come to us so soon as I had hoped,” my father said, as we went.

The words struck with the small, sharp pain that disappointments give when we have been feeding them like pets. The General was to have visited us for some weeks; we had expected him on the morrow. He was to bring with him his niece and ward, Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt—Bertha—whom I had never seen, but of whom I had heard so much that her very name had begun to take a place among the habitual sounds of the house, like the names of the dishes our old cook muttered to herself in her dialect as she kneaded, or the farm-people cried in the yard when they came with eggs still warm from the nest.

“And how soon does he come?” I asked, trying to keep my voice even.

“Not till autumn. Not for two months, I dare say,” he answered; and then, with a strange, softened gravity, “And I am very glad now, dear, that you never knew Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt.”

The air was so mild, the limes so fragrant, the birds so settled into their evening pattern of call and answer, that the sentence seemed out of key—as if a hard, modern noise had intruded into a landscape that did not permit it.

“And why?” I asked, mortified in spite of myself, and curious.

“Because the poor young lady is dead.”

For a moment the vista, the grass, the trunks of the trees, all seemed to draw away. I had heard, in the General’s first letter—six or seven weeks before—that she was not so well as he wished; but there had been no shadow of danger in it. Death, that rude syllable, had no place in my day-dreams. It was like hearing a familiar word in a foreign pronunciation, and not knowing at first what it meant.

“I quite forgot I had not told you,” my father continued. “You were not in the room when I received the General’s letter this evening.”

He handed it to me, and we sat down on a rude bench under a group of magnificent lime trees. The sun was setting with a melancholy splendor behind the horizon of forest; the stream beside our home, passing under the steep old bridge, wound among noble trees almost at our feet, taking into itself the last crimson of the sky and breaking it into wavering strips of color, as if the water were stirring a pot of dye.

The letter was so extraordinary—so vehement, so broken, and in places so self-contradictory—that I read it twice; the second time aloud to my father. Even then I could not account for it except by supposing that grief had disordered the writer’s mind.

“I have lost my darling daughter, for as such I loved her… The fiend who betrayed our infatuated hospitality has done it all… I devote my remaining days to tracking and extinguishing a monster… At present there is scarcely a gleam of light to guide me… Some time in the autumn… I will see you—that is, if you permit me; I will then tell you all that I scarce dare put upon paper now.”

So the strange outcry ended.

Though I had never seen Bertha, tears filled my eyes at the suddenness of it; and with my pity there came, shamefully mingled, my disappointment—because the days I had imagined were gone like a loaf snatched from the oven before it is baked, leaving only a raw smell and regret.

The sun had sunk, and twilight had come by the time I returned the letter to my father. The evening was soft and clear. We loitered on our way, turning over the violent phrases we had read, seeking in them some sensible shape, and finding only fragments, like a recipe half remembered in a dying tongue—enough to haunt, not enough to make.

We had nearly a mile to walk before reaching the road that passes in front of the schloss; and by the time we neared it, the moon was shining brilliantly, so bright that the leaves seemed edged with silver, and the pale stems of the birches stood out like bones. At the drawbridge we met Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, who had come out without their bonnets to enjoy the moonlight. We heard their voices before we saw them—gabbling together with that easy animation which some people possess as naturally as a hearth possesses heat. Their speech made a little domestic music under the broad nocturnal hush: the nightjar’s intermittent cry in the wood, the low continual rush of the stream, the faint insect trillings that fill up silence as flour fills the gaps in a sieve.

We joined them and turned about to admire the scene. The glade through which we had walked lay before us; on the left the narrow road wound away under clumps of lordly trees and vanished into thick forest; on the right it crossed the steep picturesque bridge near a ruined tower that had once guarded the pass. Beyond, an abrupt eminence rose, wooded, with grey rocks clustered with ivy showing dimly in shadow. Over the sward and low grounds a thin film of mist crept like smoke, veiling distances; here and there the river faintly flashed in the moonlight like the back of a fish turning in a net.

No softer, sweeter landscape could be imagined. The news of death lent it melancholy, as salt draws out an aroma; but nothing disturbed its serenity, its enchanted vagueness. My father and I stood looking down in silence. Behind us the two governesses discoursed on the moon. Madame Perrodon—fat, middle-aged, romantic—talked and sighed poetically, as if the night required a certain seasoning of sentiment. Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, in right of her German father, assumed to be psychological and metaphysical, and now declared that when the moon shone with such intensity it was known to indicate special spiritual activity: it acted on dreams, on lunacy, on nervous people; it had marvelous physical influences connected with life. She related that her cousin, mate of a merchant ship, had once napped on deck on such a night, face full in the moonlight, and had awoken after dreaming an old woman clawed his cheek, with his features drawn horribly to one side, never quite recovered to equilibrium.

“The moon, this night,” she said, “is full of idyllic and magnetic influence—and see, when you look behind you at the front of the schloss, how all its windows flash and twinkle with that silvery splendor, as if unseen hands had lighted up the rooms to receive fairy guests.”

I was in one of those indolent moods in which one does not wish to speak, but the talk of others is pleasant to the ear, like the clink of plates and the murmur of voices heard from a kitchen when one sits apart, tired, warmed by the mere assurance that life goes on with its accustomed duties. The ladies’ conversation tinkled on, and I gazed at the moonlit mist and the black shapes of trees.

“I have got into one of my moping moods tonight,” my father said at last, breaking his silence. He quoted Shakespeare, whom he read aloud by way of keeping up our English: “‘In truth I know not why I am so sad. It wearies me: you say it wearies you; But how I got it—came by it—’ I forget the rest. But I feel as if some great misfortune were hanging over us. I suppose the poor General’s afflicted letter has had something to do with it.”

As he spoke, the sound came that changed the whole color of the night—carriage wheels and many hoofs upon the road. It was an unwonted sound there, where the usual noises were natural ones: water under stone, leaves moving, distant cattle lowing, the sleepy creak of a farm-gate. This was iron and leather and haste, the hard rhythm of travel, a foreign pulse beating into our quiet.

The noises approached from the high ground overlooking the bridge. Soon the equipage emerged: two horsemen first crossed, then a carriage drawn by four horses, and two riders behind. It had the air of a traveling carriage belonging to a person of rank; and we watched, absorbed by so unusual a spectacle, as if our eyes had been called from a familiar hearth to a bright, dangerous fire in the open.

In a few moments it became more than curious. Just as the carriage passed the summit of the steep bridge, one of the leaders took fright. Panic ran through the team like a spark through dry straw; after a plunge or two the whole four broke into a wild gallop and, dashing between the horsemen in front, came thundering along the road towards us with the speed of a hurricane.

Then, cutting through the din of wheels and hoofs, there rose from the carriage window the clear, long-drawn scream of a female voice. It was not like any sound in our acoustic world—no animal cry, no child’s call, no kitchen mishap. It had in it a human terror that seemed to bite the very air, and it made the night’s other voices shrink away, as if the wood itself listened.

We all advanced in curiosity and horror; I rather in silence, the rest with ejaculations. The road, just before you reach the castle drawbridge, is flanked by a magnificent lime tree on one side and an ancient stone cross on the other. At sight of that cross, the horses—now going at a pace perfectly frightful—swerved so that the wheel mounted the projecting roots of the lime.

I knew, with sick certainty, what was coming. I covered my eyes and turned my head away, unable to see it out. At the same moment I heard a cry from my lady companions who had run a little forward.

But curiosity prised my fingers apart. When I looked, the scene was utter confusion: two of the horses were down; the carriage lay upon its side with two wheels in the air; men shouted and strained at buckles and traces, jerking leather loose with violent hands. A lady with a commanding air and figure had got out and stood with her hands clasped, lifting her handkerchief now and then to her eyes. Through the carriage door they lifted a young lady—slender, pale—who appeared lifeless.

My father was already beside the elder lady, hat in hand, tendering aid and the resources of our schloss. The lady did not seem to hear him, or to have eyes for anything but the girl, who was placed against the slope of the bank. I approached nearer. The young lady was stunned, but certainly not dead. My father—who prided himself on being something of a physician—had just felt her wrist, and assured the elder lady, who declared herself her mother, that the pulse, though faint and irregular, was still distinguishable.

The lady clasped her hands and looked upward, as if in a momentary transport of gratitude; but immediately she broke out again in a theatrical way which, I believe, is natural to some. She was what is called a fine-looking woman for her time of life—tall, not thin—dressed in black velvet; pale, with a proud, commanding countenance that agitation made only stranger.

“Who was ever being so born to calamity?” I heard her say. “Here am I, on a journey of life and death, in prosecuting which to lose an hour is possibly to lose all. My child will not have recovered sufficiently to resume her route for who can say how long. I must leave her: I cannot, dare not, delay. How far on, sir, can you tell, is the nearest village? I must leave her there; and shall not see my darling, or even hear of her till my return, three months hence.”

At that, an impulse seized me—not merely pity, but a hungry longing for company, for a new presence in our quiet house; for some voice to braid itself into our nightly sounds, so that the corridors would no longer answer only with their own echoes. I plucked my father by the coat and whispered urgently, “Oh, papa, pray ask her to let her stay with us—it would be so delightful. Do, pray.”

My father, with that grave courtesy which in him was as habitual as blessing bread, addressed the lady: “If Madame will entrust her child to the care of my daughter, and of her good gouvernante, Madame Perrodon, and permit her to remain as our guest, under my charge, until her return, it will confer a distinction and an obligation upon us; and we shall treat her with all the care and devotion which so sacred a trust deserves.”

“I cannot do that, sir; it would be to task your kindness and chivalry too cruelly,” the lady said, distractedly.

“It would, on the contrary, be to confer on us a very great kindness at the moment when we most need it,” my father answered. “My daughter has just been disappointed by a cruel misfortune, in a visit from which she had long anticipated a great deal of happiness. If you confide this young lady to our care it will be her best consolation. The nearest village on your route is distant, and affords no such inn as you could think of placing your daughter at; you cannot allow her to continue her journey for any considerable distance without danger. If, as you say, you cannot suspend your journey, you must part with her tonight; and nowhere could you do so with more honest assurances of care and tenderness than here.”

There was something distinguished and imposing in the lady’s air, and in her manner something engaging, so that—apart from the dignity of her equipage—one felt she must be a person of consequence. By this time the carriage was righted; the horses, oddly, were tractable again, standing steaming in their harness as if the frenzy had been a gust that had passed. The lady threw on her daughter a glance that I could not interpret; it was swift and measured, and I fancied it not quite so affectionate as her earlier exclamations would have led one to expect.

Then she beckoned slightly to my father and withdrew two or three steps with him out of hearing. She spoke to him with a fixed, stern countenance, not at all like the one she had shown before, leaning close as if pouring rapid, earnest words into his ear. I stood filled with wonder that my father did not seem to notice the change, and with an unspeakable curiosity as to what she could be saying so privately—what terms, what names, what charged syllables—spoken low, as if language itself could be used to bind and command.

It lasted no more than two or three minutes. She turned, stepped to where her daughter lay supported by Madame Perrodon, knelt beside her for a moment, and whispered—Madame supposed—a little benediction in her ear. Then, hastily kissing her, she rose and stepped into her carriage.

The door was closed. The footmen in stately liveries jumped up behind; the outriders spurred on; the postilions cracked their whips. The horses plunged and broke suddenly into a furious canter that threatened again to become a gallop, and the carriage whirled away into the moonlit road, the two horsemen behind following at the same rapid pace, until the sound of wheels and hoofs—so harsh a disturbance—thinned into distance and was swallowed by the old familiar chorus of our place: the stream’s soft insistence, the whispering leaves, and, above all, the breathing hush that returned, as if the night itself had been holding its breath.

We watched the little procession of lanterns and black velvet melt into the timbered fog until even the hoof-beats and the complaint of wheels were swallowed by the night. The forest, that moment before so full of moving sound, settled into a hush so complete it felt manufactured—an oppressive quiet in which one begins to hear one’s own blood and mistrust it. Had the whole thing been only a flare of imagination—an accident invented by mist and candlelight—there remained, on the stones of our bridge, the proof of weight and warmth: the young lady, stirring at last.

Her face was turned from me, hair unbound in a dark spill, but she raised her head as if listening for a familiar step. In a voice so sweet it was almost plaintive music, she asked, with a child’s injured complaint, “Where is mamma?”

Madame Perrodon answered her with the tenderness she reserved for the sick and the frightened, smoothing the strangeness of our walls into softer words. The girl’s gaze wandered; she tried to make our castle fit to her memory.

“Where am I? What is this place? I don’t see the carriage; and Matska—where is she?”

Madame explained what she could, and by degrees the young lady pieced together the misadventure, like one reconstructing a dropped recipe from scattered scraps: the jolt, the overturn, the sudden press of hands, the forest’s wet breath. She was relieved—almost fiercely relieved—that no one had been hurt. But when she understood that her mother had gone on and left her with us until her return “in about three months,” the composure that had held like a thin crust broke, and she wept.

I stepped forward, meaning to offer my own poor consolations; but Mademoiselle De Lafontaine caught my arm, her fingers light yet decisive.

“Not yet,” she murmured. “One at a time. A little excitement might overpower her.”

A bedroom was being made ready above—fires laid, sheets aired, water heated—those calm domestic rites that try to convince calamity it has wandered into the wrong house. My father, practical in emergencies, had already sent a servant on horseback for the physician who lived two leagues off.

The stranger rose at length. Leaning on Madame’s arm, she crossed the drawbridge slowly, as though each step had to be argued for. The servants waited in the hall to receive her. She was taken at once to one of our best rooms, and scarcely had she lain down before she sank into a sleep so deep it seemed to claim the whole house’s sound for itself. Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine left her with a maid and came down to us.

Our drawing-room—long, with four windows looking over moat and bridge to the darkened forest—held its familiar carved oak and crimson Utrecht velvet, the old cabinets and the thick tapestry that showed hunting and hawking and feasts in a hundred faded threads. Candles were lit; their wicks hissed and steadied, and the small noises of comfort began: the shifting of chairs, the minute clink of porcelain, the pour of hot tea.

My father, with his patriotic fondness for the national beverage, would always have tea appear alongside coffee and chocolate, as if the day could not close without that particular bitterness and fragrance. The steam rose with its faint leaf-scent, and for a moment it seemed absurd that such homely perfume could share air with the memory of overturned wheels and a woman in black velvet disappearing into fog.

We sat together, tasting the night again in words.

“How do you like our guest?” I asked Madame Perrodon as soon as she entered. “Tell me everything.”

“I like her extremely,” Madame answered. “She is, I almost think, the prettiest creature I ever saw. About your age—and so gentle, so nice.”

“She is absolutely beautiful,” Mademoiselle added; she had peeped in for a moment at the sleeping girl.

“And such a sweet voice,” said Madame Perrodon, as if that, too, were a kind of beauty you could carry in a bowl.

“Did you remark a woman in the carriage,” Mademoiselle asked, “after it was set up again—one who did not get out, but only looked from the window?”

We had not. Mademoiselle described her then: a hideous black woman with a colored turban, gazing fixedly out, nodding and grinning as though in mockery. Her eyes, Mademoiselle said, shone with a peculiar gleam; the whites looked large; her teeth were set as if in fury. The description fell into the room like a cold draft, and even the little crackle of candlewick seemed to hesitate.

“And what an ill-looking pack the servants were,” Madame Perrodon said. “Did you notice them?”

My father came in at that moment, and took up the remark at once.

“Ugly, hang-dog fellows as ever I beheld. I hope they won’t rob the poor lady in the forest. Clever rogues, though—they got everything to rights in a minute.”

“I dare say they are worn out with too long travelling,” Madame said, always charitable, though her voice betrayed that she had also seen those lean, dark, sullen faces and had not liked the look of them.

“I’m curious,” she confessed. “But the young lady will tell you tomorrow, if she is sufficiently recovered.”

“I don’t think she will,” said my father, and smiled oddly, with a little nod as though he had locked away a key.

That half-smile sharpened our curiosity. We all remembered his brief, earnest interview with the lady in black velvet before the carriage vanished. When at last we were alone, I begged him to tell me what had been said. He needed little urging.

“There’s no particular reason I should not. She expressed reluctance to trouble us with the care of her daughter—said she was delicate, nervous, but not subject to any kind of seizure.” He laughed a little at the detail. “She volunteered that. Nor to any illusion. Perfectly sane, in fact.”

“How very odd to insist on that,” I said. “So unnecessary.”

“Necessary to her, apparently,” he replied. “Then she said: ‘I am making a long journey of vital importance’—she emphasized the word—‘rapid and secret. I shall return for my child in three months. In the meantime she will be silent as to who we are, whence we come, and whither we are travelling.’ That was all.”

He paused, remembering.

“She spoke very pure French. When she said ‘secret’ she stopped and looked at me—sternly. She makes a great point of it. You saw how quickly she was gone. I hope I have not done a foolish thing taking charge of the young lady.”

For my part, foolish or not, I was thrilled. In our solitude, a new companion was like a sudden harvest after a long thin season. It promised talk, laughter, a second heartbeat in the house. I longed to see her and to hear her speak again, to learn the particular rhythm of her voice the way one learns the particular simmer of a sauce—by patience and proximity.

The doctor did not arrive until nearly one o’clock, and while we waited, the castle seemed to hold its breath. In the countryside, night is not merely the absence of light; it is a whole soundscape of its own, a hush patterned by distant owls and the soft interruptions of settling wood. Tonight, even those usual small proofs of life felt muted, as if the forest were listening too, and the silence itself had weight.

When the physician at last came down to the drawing-room, he reported very favorably. She was sitting up; her pulse regular; no injury; the nervous shock had passed without mischief. He saw no harm in my visiting her for a few minutes, if she wished it.

With that permission I sent at once to know whether she would receive me. The servant returned immediately: she desired it above all things.

I went without delay.

She lay in one of the handsomest rooms in the schloss—handsome, yet a little stately in its old gravity. Opposite the foot of the bed hung a sombre tapestry of Cleopatra with the asps at her breast; other classic scenes, faded into mournful colors, looked down from the walls. Yet the room was rich with gold carving and deep hues that caught candlelight warmly, as if trying, vainly, to outshine the gloom of those ancient stories.

Candles stood at her bedside. She sat propped among pillows, her slender figure wrapped in a soft silk dressing-gown embroidered with flowers and lined with thick quilted silk—the same covering her mother had thrown over her feet as she lay upon the ground. The silk seemed to remember the outside cold and resist it; its sheen moved when she breathed.

I had reached the bedside and begun my greeting when something struck me speechless—so sudden I recoiled a step, as if the air between us had flashed.

It was her face.

I had seen it before—twelve years ago—at night, in my childhood. It had fixed itself in my mind with the tenacity of a nightmare you cannot translate into daylight without losing its teeth, and yet cannot forget. Pretty—yes, even beautiful—and then, as now, wearing a melancholy expression. And now that expression lit instantly into a strange, fixed smile of recognition.

We were silent for a full minute. The candle flames made small sounds—tiny ticks in their own language—while my heart hammered out a louder dialect in my ears.

At last she spoke, and her voice, so gentle, seemed almost to brush the air.

“How wonderful,” she said. “Twelve years ago I saw your face in a dream, and it has haunted me ever since.”

“Wonderful indeed,” I managed, forcing my voice past the horror that had seized it. “Twelve years ago—in vision or reality—I certainly saw you. I could not forget your face. It has remained before my eyes ever since.”

Her smile softened. Whatever had looked strange in it fell away, and the dimpling of her cheeks became simply charming, intelligent. The terror in me, so sharp a moment before, began to loosen like a hand unclenching. I did what hospitality demanded; I welcomed her, told her how much pleasure her arrival had given us all, and—truly—how happy it made me.

As I spoke I took her hand. I was shy, as lonely people are, but the sudden intimacy of the moment made me bold. She pressed my hand; she laid her other hand upon it too; her eyes glowed. She glanced quickly into mine, smiled again, and blushed.

She answered my welcome prettily, and I sat beside her, still dazed by the circularity of it—past stepping cleanly into present, as if the years between were only mist.

“I must tell you my vision about you,” she said. “It is so strange that you and I should have had—each of the other—so vivid a dream, and that each should have seen, I you and you me, looking as we do now, when of course we both were mere children.”

She spoke with a seriousness that made the room feel smaller, more intimate, as though the tapestries leaned in to hear.

“I was a child, about six years old. I awoke from a confused and troubled dream and found myself in a room unlike my nursery—wainscoted clumsily in some dark wood, with cupboards and bedsteads and chairs and benches placed about. The beds were empty, I thought, and the room itself without anyone but myself.

“I looked about for some time, and I remember especially an iron candlestick with two branches—I should know it again. I crept under one of the beds to reach the window. But as I got from under it I heard someone crying, and looking up—while I was still upon my knees—I saw you. Most assuredly you, as I see you now: a beautiful young lady with golden hair and large blue eyes—and lips—your lips—you as you are here.

“Your looks won me. I climbed on the bed and put my arms about you, and I think we both fell asleep.

“I was aroused by a scream. You were sitting up screaming. I was frightened and slipped down upon the ground, and it seemed to me I lost consciousness for a moment; and when I came to myself I was again in my nursery at home. Your face I have never forgotten since. I could not be misled by mere resemblance. You are the lady whom I saw then.”

Now it was my turn, and I told her my own corresponding vision, as faithfully as my shaken memory allowed. She listened with undisguised wonder, her eyes fixed on mine as if trying to hear the exact accent of my recollection—what I could name and what I could not.

When I finished she smiled again, and there was something in the smile that flirted with dread and dismissed it in the same breath.

“I don’t know which should be most afraid of the other,” she said. “If you were less pretty I think I should be very much afraid of you. But being as you are—and you and I both so young—I feel only that I made your acquaintance twelve years ago, and have already a right to your intimacy. At all events it does seem as if we were destined, from our earliest childhood, to be friends.”

Her voice lowered on the last word, as if speaking it changed it.

“I wonder whether you feel as strangely drawn towards me as I do to you. I have never had a friend—shall I find one now?”

She sighed, and her dark eyes rested on me with a passionate, hungry tenderness that made me glance away and then back again, as if caught. The truth—shameful to admit even to myself—was that I did feel drawn to her, strongly and without good reason. Yet in that attraction there was also something like a faint recoil, a minute inward protest—like the body’s instinct when it hears a wrong sound in the night, too low to be explained, too clear to be ignored. But the attraction greatly prevailed. She interested me, she won me, she was so beautiful and so engaging that I felt almost grateful to be overcome.

Her eyelids drooped; a languor crept over her features, as if the effort of feeling so much had tired her. I rose, anxious not to exhaust her.

“The doctor thinks you ought to have a maid to sit up with you to-night,” I said. “One of ours is waiting. You’ll find her quiet and useful.”

“How kind of you,” she replied, “but I could not sleep. I never could with an attendant in the room. I shan’t require any assistance—and shall I confess my weakness? I am haunted with a terror of robbers. Our house was robbed once, and two servants murdered, so I always lock my door. It has become a habit—and you look so kind I know you will forgive me. I see there is a key in the lock.”

She drew me close for a moment in her pretty arms and whispered at my ear, her breath warm and intimate against my skin.

“Good night, darling. It is very hard to part with you, but good night. To-morrow—but not early—I shall see you again.”

She sank back with a sigh. Her eyes followed me with a fond, melancholy gaze, and she murmured once more, “Good night, dear friend.”

Young people love quickly; loneliness makes the heart greedy. I was flattered by her evident, though undeserved, fondness; I liked the confidence with which she received me, as if we were resuming a conversation interrupted only yesterday and not by twelve years.

The next day came, and we met again. In daylight she lost nothing. She was still, beyond doubt, the most beautiful creature I had ever seen, and the unpleasant remembrance of that childhood visitation had dulled and retreated, like a nightmare pushed back by morning duties. She confessed she had felt the same shock on seeing me, and the same faint antipathy mixed into admiration.

And so—astonishingly—we laughed together over our momentary horrors, as if dread itself were only another story that could be told in a calm room, over familiar cups, while outside the forest held its breath and kept its secrets in a language no one in the house could quite speak.

I was captivated by her—so much so that the days began to arrange themselves around her like the courses of a dinner one does not yet understand, but already hungers for. And yet, even as I watched her, even as I touched her hair and listened to her voice, there were customs in her that sat uneasily with me, like a familiar recipe made with some foreign spice whose fragrance is exquisite and whose aftertaste is faintly medicinal.

She was taller than most women, slender, astonishingly graceful. Nothing about her suggested illness; only a languor, a deliberate softness in every motion, as though she moved through water that no one else could see. Her complexion had that deep, living brilliance one associates with health, with blood close to the skin; her features were small, perfectly shaped; her eyes large and dark and burning with a glossy, watchful light. But it was her hair—her hair was the thing that bound my attention like a spell. I had never seen anything so thick, so long. When it was loosened it fell with its own weight, and I used to slide my hands beneath it and laugh, honestly startled, at how heavy it was, how it held warmth the way a fur holds warmth. It was fine as silk, soft as sifted flour, a rich dark brown with a hidden glint, as though threads of gold had been spun into it and then buried again. In her room, when she lay back in a chair and talked in that sweet low voice—so low it seemed made for the ear alone—I would let it down and fold it and braid it, separating it in slow glossy ropes, spreading it like a cloak across her shoulders and my own hands. If I had known then what I know now—if I had understood what a thing it is to lavish such intimacy on a mystery—I think the weight of that hair would have felt like a chain.

She had won me on the first night with her confidence, or what I mistook for confidence; but I discovered, almost at once, a vigilance in her reserve. About herself, her mother, her history, her plans—everything that might give her a true outline—she kept a wakeful silence. Perhaps I was unjust. Perhaps I ought to have obeyed, more faithfully, the injunction my father had received from that stately lady in black velvet. But curiosity has its own appetite, and it is not a polite one. No girl endures patiently to be denied what another girl might grant her with ease. It seemed to me—unaccountably, absurdly—that refusal was a form of coldness. What harm could there be in telling me what I asked? Why offer tenderness and keep truth locked away? Was I to be trusted with her cheek against mine, but not trusted with the name of the place she called home?

What she told me, after all my insinuations and my poorly disguised attacks, amounted—in my unreasonable estimation—to almost nothing. Three disclosures, each like a dish described but never served. First: her name was Carmilla. Second: her family was ancient and noble. Third: her home lay westward. She would not tell me her family name, their bearings, their estate, not even the country. I did not persecute her ceaselessly; I waited for the moments when conversation seemed to open, as dough opens under a patient hand, and I tried to slip my questions in gently. Once or twice I pressed more directly, and always failed. Reproaches failed. Caresses failed. She parried with a melancholy prettiness, a deprecating smile, and always—always—passionate assurances of her liking for me, of her trust in my honor, and promises that one day I should know all. How could I stay offended when she made her refusals sound like sorrow?

At times she would put her arms around my neck, draw me close, lay her cheek to mine, and murmur with her lips at my ear, “Dearest, your little heart is wounded; think me not cruel because I obey the irresistible law of my strength and weakness; if your dear heart is wounded, my wild heart bleeds with yours. In the rapture of my enormous humiliation I live in your warm life, and you shall die—die, sweetly die—into mine. I cannot help it; as I draw near to you, you, in your turn, will draw near to others, and learn the rapture of that cruelty, which yet is love; so, for a while, seek to know no more of me and mine, but trust me with all your loving spirit.”

Then, as though her words must be sealed upon my skin, she would press me closer; her body trembled, and her soft kisses warmed my cheek as if her mouth carried a faint fever. Her language, her agitations, were unintelligible. I wanted to draw away. I did not want to draw away. My will seemed to thin and dissolve, like sugar in hot chocolate, until there was nothing left but a trance-like compliance. Her voice had the quality of a lullaby—yet not the lullabies of my childhood, sung in the homely cadence of our servants’ old dialect, with its round, comforting vowels and its names for herbs and weather that cannot be cleanly translated into courtly speech. Hers was a lullaby that soothed resistance and left behind, when it ended, a silence full of listening. I only recovered myself when her arms released me.

In those moods I did not like her. I felt a tumultuous excitement that was pleasurable, and again and again a vague fear and disgust braided through it like bitter threads. Love growing into adoration; abhorrence rising with it—paradox, yes, but it is the only honest word. I write now after more than ten years, my hand trembling with confused, horrible recollections—vivid in the main current of events, yet dim in the very scenes where feeling had been fiercest. I believe that is common: the mind, scalded by passion, remembers its pain as one remembers a burn—certain that it happened, unable to summon the exact shape of flame.

Sometimes, after an hour of apathy, she would take my hand and hold it, pressing it again and again, as if trying to imprint herself upon my pulse. She would blush, gazing at me with eyes both languid and burning, breathing so fast her dress rose and fell visibly. It had the ardor of a lover; it embarrassed me; it felt hateful and yet overpowering. Her eyes seemed to gloat, and then she would draw me to her and kiss along my cheek, hot lips traveling as if seeking some secret place, and whisper—almost sobbing—“You are mine, you shall be mine, you and I are one for ever.” Then she would fling herself back, cover her eyes with her small hands, and leave me shaking.

“Are we related?” I used to ask. “What do you mean by all this? I remind you perhaps of someone you love; but you must not. I hate it. I don’t know you—I don’t know myself when you look so and talk so.”

She would sigh at my vehemence, turn away, and let my hand fall as if it meant nothing.

I tried to explain these manifestations and could not. They did not look like affectation. They were the breaking out, for a moment, of something suppressed. Was she, despite her mother’s denial, visited by brief insanity? Or was it all romance and disguise? I had read old tales—tales carried by nurses in words that have no elegant equivalents—of lovers in masquerade, of a boyish suitor hidden under feminine dress with the help of an old adventuress. The hypothesis flattered my vanity; but there was too much against it. Her constant languor, for one: it did not belong to a healthy masculine system. Between those passionate outbreaks there were long stretches of commonplace gaiety, then brooding melancholy, in which—except for the melancholy fire of her eyes following me—I might have been nothing to her.

Her habits, too, were odd. Perhaps a lady of a town would not have found them so singular, but to us, living close to the rhythm of fields and kitchens and the small noises by which one measures a day, they struck strangely.

She came down very late, generally not until one o’clock. In a house where the morning has its own music—the clink of pails, the thud of loaves set on boards, the hens fussing, the cook’s knife keeping time on the block—her lateness was like a gap in the score. When she did appear she took only a cup of chocolate and ate nothing. The chocolate, thick and dark, steamed faintly with cinnamon and ground bean; its fragrance filled the room, promising comfort, yet she treated it as one treats a remedy: a few sips, no relish, no desire. It troubled me more than I can account for now. Food in our country is memory made tangible: the same rye bread baked in the same shape for generations, the same sour cream, the same forest mushrooms steeped in butter, the same names spoken over herbs as one strips them from their stems. To refuse eating is to refuse a language. Carmilla drank chocolate and left the rest untouched, as if she had no need of the domestic sacrament by which we recognize each other.

After that we went out to walk—but it was not a walk, not truly. It was a saunter, barely a drift. Almost immediately she seemed exhausted and would either return to the schloss or sit on one of the benches placed among the trees. Her body drooped, yet her mind did not share the languor. She was animated in talk, intelligent, keen. Sometimes she would allude to her home, to an adventure, an early recollection, and in those brief hints there were strange manners, unfamiliar customs, little domestic details that did not fit any country I knew. From them I began to suspect her native place lay much farther than I had first imagined—westward, yes, but westward into a distance that felt less like geography than like time.

One afternoon as we sat under the trees, a funeral passed. It was for a pretty young girl, the daughter of one of our forest rangers—a child I had often seen carrying a basket, cheeks bright from wind and work. She was his only child. He walked behind the coffin with the posture of a man whose spine has been broken without a blow. Behind him peasants walked two-and-two, singing a funeral hymn. The sound moved through the trees in a slow, well-worn channel, and the woods seemed to listen, every leaf holding its breath between notes. In our country, these hymns are older than the neat prayers of printed books; they keep words alive that no official language cares to remember. Their vowels carry soil and smoke; their cadences follow the same arc as scythes and lullabies. I rose to show respect as they passed, and joined in softly.

Carmilla shook me—almost roughly. I turned, astonished.

“Don’t you perceive how discordant that is?” she said.

“I think it very sweet,” I answered, vexed at her interruption and suddenly anxious that the mourners might see us and resent what was passing. I resumed at once—and again she stopped me.

“You pierce my ears,” said Carmilla, almost angrily, and she stopped her ears with her little fingers. “Besides, how can you tell that your religion and mine are the same? Your forms wound me, and I hate funerals. What a fuss! Why you must die—everyone must die; and all are happier when they do. Come home.”

“My father has gone on with the clergyman to the churchyard,” I said. “I thought you knew she was to be buried today.”

“She? I don’t trouble my head about peasants. I don’t know who she is,” Carmilla answered, and there was a flash from her fine eyes like the strike of flint.

“She is the poor girl who fancied she saw a ghost a fortnight ago, and has been dying ever since, till yesterday, when she expired.”

“Tell me nothing about ghosts,” she said quickly. “I shan’t sleep tonight if you do.”

“I hope there is no plague or fever coming; all this looks very like it,” I went on, for the deaths had begun to feel like a pattern. “The swineherd’s young wife died only a week ago, and she thought something seized her by the throat as she lay in her bed, and nearly strangled her. Papa says such horrible fancies do accompany some forms of fever. She was quite well the day before. She sank afterwards, and died before a week.”

“Well, her funeral is over, I hope, and her hymn sung; and our ears shan’t be tortured with that discord and jargon. It has made me nervous. Sit down here, beside me; sit close; hold my hand; press it hard—hard—harder.”

We moved a little back to another bench. She sat, and her face altered so suddenly that my breath stopped. It darkened, turned horribly livid; her hands clenched, her teeth set, and she frowned, compressing her lips, staring at the ground by her feet. A shudder ran through her—continuous, irrepressible, like ague. It was as if every energy in her strained to suppress some seizure that tugged at her from within. At last a low convulsive cry broke from her, and gradually the fit subsided.

“There! That comes of strangling people with hymns!” she said, at last, with a kind of bitter levity. “Hold me, hold me still. It is passing away.”

And it did pass. Then, perhaps to disperse the gloomy impression the spectacle had left upon me, she became unusually animated and chatty, and so we got home. It was the first time I had seen any definable symptom of the delicate health her mother had spoken of. It was also the first time I had seen anything like temper in her. Both vanished like a summer cloud; never but once afterward did I witness, on her part, a momentary sign of anger.

It happened when she and I were looking out from one of the long drawing-room windows. Across the drawbridge into the courtyard came a figure I knew well, a wanderer who visited the schloss generally twice a year: a hunchback with sharp, lean features and a pointed black beard. He smiled from ear to ear, showing white teeth like little fangs. He wore buff, black, and scarlet, crossed with more straps and belts than I could count; from them hung all manner of objects, clinking and tapping as he walked—metal against metal, leather creaking, a whole little industry of noise entering our quiet court. Behind him he carried a magic lantern and two boxes, and I knew them: one held a salamander, the other a mandrake—those stitched-together monstrosities that made my father laugh, compounded of bits of monkey, parrot, squirrel, fish, hedgehog, dried and neatly sewn to startling effect. He had a fiddle; a box of conjuring apparatus; a pair of foils and masks attached to his belt; several other mysterious cases; and a black staff tipped with copper in his hand.

A rough, spare dog followed at his heels, but stopped short at the drawbridge as if the threshold itself had teeth. In a moment it began to howl dismally. The sound poured into the courtyard and bounced off the stone, turning our domestic stillness into something strained and raw. I remember thinking—absurdly, but distinctly—that the dog was the only honest creature among us, responding to something our human manners insisted on ignoring.

The mountebank stood in the middle of the courtyard, lifted his grotesque hat, and bowed ceremoniously, paying compliments volubly in execrable French and German not much better. Then he disengaged his fiddle and scraped a lively air, singing to it with a merry discord, dancing with ludicrous activity. I laughed in spite of the dog’s howling. He advanced to the window, hat in hand, fiddle tucked under his arm, and without pausing for breath he gabbled a long advertisement of his accomplishments—arts, entertainments, curiosities he could display at our bidding.

“Will your ladyships be pleased to buy an amulet against the oupire,” he said at last, dropping his hat on the pavement, “which is going like the wolf, I hear, through these woods. They are dying of it right and left—and here is a charm that never fails; only pinned to the pillow, and you may laugh in his face.”

The charms were oblong slips of vellum covered in cabalistic ciphers and diagrams. Carmilla bought one instantly, and so did I. We leaned down, amused—at least I was. The man looked up, and his piercing black eye seemed to catch on something in our faces. He unrolled, in an instant, a leather case full of odd little steel instruments.

“See here, my lady,” he said, addressing me, “I profess, among other things less useful, the art of dentistry. Plague take the dog! Silence, beast! He howls so that your ladyships can scarcely hear a word. Your noble friend, the young lady at your right, has the sharpest tooth,—long, thin, pointed, like an awl, like a needle; ha, ha! With my sharp and long sight, as I look up, I have seen it distinctly; now if it happens to hurt the young lady, and I think it must, here am I, here are my file, my punch, my nippers; I will make it round and blunt, if her ladyship pleases; no longer the tooth of a fish, but of a beautiful young lady as she is. Hey? Is the young lady displeased? Have I been too bold? Have I offended her?”

Carmilla drew back from the window, her face suddenly hard with anger.

“How dares that mountebank insult us so?” she cried. “Where is your father? I shall demand redress from him. My father would have had the wretch tied up to the pump, and flogged with a cart whip, and burnt to the bones with the cattle brand!”

She stepped back and sat down, and even before the offender had left her sight her wrath drained away as abruptly as it had risen. She returned to her usual tone as if nothing had happened, as if the hunchback and his dog and his jangling straps had been only a gust of wind.

That evening my father was out of spirits. When he came in he told us there had been another case like the two fatal ones already occurred: the sister of a young peasant on his estate, only a mile away, very ill. She described an attack nearly the same, and now she was slowly, steadily sinking.

“All this,” said my father, “is strictly referable to natural causes. These poor people infect one another with their superstitions, and so repeat in imagination the images of terror that have infested their neighbors.”

“But that very circumstance frightens one horribly,” said Carmilla.

“How so?” my father asked.

“I am so afraid of fancying I see such things,” she answered. “I think it would be as bad as reality.”

“We are in God’s hands,” said my gentle father. “Nothing can happen without his permission, and all will end well for those who love him. He is our faithful creator; He has made us all, and will take care of us.”

“Creator! Nature!” Carmilla replied, and the word Nature sounded on her tongue like a doctrine older than my father’s. “And this disease that invades the country is natural. Nature. All things proceed from Nature—don’t they? All things in the heaven, in the earth, and under the earth, act and live as Nature ordains? I think so.”

“The doctor said he would come here today,” my father said after a silence. “I want to know what he thinks about it, and what he thinks we had better do.”

“Doctors never did me any good,” said Carmilla.

“Then you have been ill?” I asked.

“More ill than ever you were,” she answered.

“Long ago?”

“Yes, a long time. I suffered from this very illness; but I forget all but my pain and weakness, and they were not so bad as are suffered in other diseases.”

“You were very young then?”

“I dare say. Let us talk no more of it. You would not wound a friend?” She looked languidly into my eyes, slipped her arm around my waist with a possessive tenderness, and led me out of the room. My father remained by the window, busy with papers, his pen scratching—one of the few sharp sounds in the house that night, like a mouse in the walls.

“Why does your papa like to frighten us?” Carmilla murmured, sighing, with a little shudder.

“He doesn’t, dear Carmilla. It is the very furthest thing from his mind.”

“Are you afraid, dearest?”

“I should be very much,” I confessed, “if I fancied there was any real danger of my being attacked as those poor people were.”

“You are afraid to die?”

“Yes. Every one is.”

“But to die as lovers may—to die together, so that they may live together.” Her voice deepened on the words, and something in it struck the same confusion in me as before, that mingling of sweetness and dread. “Girls are caterpillars while they live in the world, to be finally butterflies when the summer comes; but in the meantime there are grubs and larvae, don’t you see—each with their peculiar propensities, necessities and structure. So says Monsieur Buffon, in his big book, in the next room.”

Later that day the doctor came, and he was closeted with my father for some time. He was a skilful man of sixty and upward, powdered, his pale face shaved smooth as a pumpkin. When they emerged together I heard my father laugh as they came out.

“Well, I do wonder at a wise man like you,” my father said. “What do you say to hippogriffs and dragons?”

The doctor smiled and shook his head. “Nevertheless,” he said, “life and death are mysterious states, and we know little of the resources of either.”

They walked on, and I heard no more. I did not then know what the doctor had been broaching; but I think I can guess it now, as one guesses, too late, the missing ingredient in a dish whose flavor has haunted one for years.

That evening a cart came up from Gratz with the grave, dark-faced son of the picture-cleaner perched beside the driver, his cap pulled low, his shoulders powdered with road-dust from ten long leagues. The horse’s shoes struck sparks from the stones in the court, and the sound traveled up the walls and into our quiet rooms with the odd authority of a bell, as if the whole schloss had been dozing and must now wake to hear the news that clung to a traveler’s coat.

Whenever anyone arrived from our little capital, we gathered in the hall instinctively, like a household drawn toward the kitchen door when the first smell of broth escapes—part hunger, part habit, part the old rural faith that a messenger brings not only letters but weather, sickness, gossip, and war. Two great packing-cases were lowered from the cart and set in the flags of the hall. The wood had been bound with iron, the seams crusted with pitch; it smelled of resin and damp straw, as if a forest floor had been shut up and carried for days.

The messenger was taken away to supper by the servants. I could imagine him at table, the clink of spoon on plate and the soft, relieved sucking of breath that comes with warm food after cold travel. In the hall we waited, hearing through the doors the far-off domestic music of the house—feet, murmurs, a chair shifted—sounds so homely that the hush around the cases began to feel like a superstition.

At length he returned with assistants. They brought hammer, ripping chisel, and turnscrew, and the first blow on the lid rang like an insult to the old portraits sleeping inside. Each crack of wood had an echo, each creak a complaint; the schloss, so long accustomed to wind and birds and the river’s steady speech, seemed to dislike this sharp, metallic prying.

Carmilla sat by, listless, her posture as if she had been placed there rather than chosen it. Her gaze drifted over the work without fastening, the way a person’s eyes may wander while listening to a tune too old to be remembered. One after another the pictures were brought to light—mostly portraits—cleaned and restored. These had come to us through my mother, who was of an old Hungarian family, and even before their faces emerged, their presence in our hall felt like an old harvest returned from storage: things put away long ago, meant to feed memory when the season turned.

My father held a list and called out numbers as the man rummaged and produced the corresponding canvas. Many were not, I suppose, “good” in any modern sense; but they were undeniably ancient, and the old paint, recovered from smoke and dust, seemed to have a scent as well as a color—oil and varnish, faintly sweet, like dried fruit kept too long and yet still aromatic when broken.

“There is a picture that I have not seen yet,” my father said, peering down at his paper. “In one corner, at the top, is the name—so far as I could read—‘Marcia Karnstein,’ and the date ‘1698.’ I am curious to see how it has turned out.”

I remembered it: a small picture, nearly square, without a frame, so blackened that it had been, for me, hardly more than a dark patch on the wall—like a pan left too long above smoke, the surface made unreadable by years.

The cleaner produced it at last with evident pride, lifting it carefully so the lamplight struck full upon it. The effect was immediate and uncanny—not merely that it was beautiful, but that it startled, as if the hall had suddenly admitted another living presence. It seemed to breathe.

It was Carmilla.

“Carmilla, dear—here is an absolute miracle. Here you are, living, smiling, ready to speak, in this picture. Isn’t it beautiful, Papa? And see—even the little mole on her throat.”

My father laughed, saying it was certainly a wonderful likeness, yet to my astonishment he looked away almost at once and began talking on with the picture-cleaner about the other portraits—technicalities, colors brought back, shadows restored—speaking with the calm interest of a man discussing the curing of meats or the right way to keep a cellar dry. The cleaner answered intelligently, proud of his craft. Their voices moved on like practical hands, smoothing and arranging, while I remained fixed before the image as if held by a taste too strong to be swallowed.

The longer I looked, the more my wonder thickened into something like dread, not because the likeness was imperfect, but because it was perfect. The painted mouth held the same faint, secret curve; the eyes had the same intent quietness; the face had that youthful grace which in Carmilla seemed less like youth than like a preserved thing—like fruit kept bright beneath clear syrup.

“Will you let me hang this picture in my room, Papa?” I asked.

“Certainly, dear,” he said, smiling. “I’m very glad you think it so like. It must be prettier even than I thought it, if it is.”

Carmilla did not answer, did not seem even to hear. She leaned back, her fine eyes half veiled by long lashes, gazing at me with a slow contemplation. A smile touched her lips—rapt, almost devout—like someone savoring a remembered flavor that no one else at table can taste.

“And now you can read quite plainly the name,” I said, for the cleaned corner shone as if traced in old gold. “It is not Marcia. It says Mircalla, Countess Karnstein—there is a little coronet—and underneath, A.D. 1698. I am descended from the Karnsteins; that is, mamma was.”

“Ah,” Carmilla said languidly, as if the syllable had to cross a long distance in her before it emerged. “So am I, I think—very long descent, very ancient. Are there any Karnsteins living now?”

“None who bear the name, I believe. The family were ruined in civil wars long ago. But the ruins of the castle are only about three miles away.”

“How interesting,” she murmured, with the same soft indifference one might give to a tale already known. Then her attention shifted, quick as a cat hearing a new sound. “But see what beautiful moonlight.”

She glanced toward the hall door, which stood a little open. Beyond it the night lay pale and clear, washing the stones and ironwork in silver, and I could hear the outer world’s familiar quiet—the river’s continuous, low utterance, the far, thin call of something nocturnal, and, nearer, the faint creak of the drawbridge chains settling. The schloss in moonlight always seemed to listen to itself.

“Suppose you take a little ramble round the court,” she said, “and look down at the road and river.”

“It is so like the night you came to us,” I answered.

She sighed, smiling. She rose, and each with her arm about the other’s waist, we walked out upon the pavement. The stones were cold through the soles, holding the day’s heat no more. We went slowly, silently, as though we feared to disturb the careful arrangement of sounds—water, leaves, our own steps—an acoustic order that felt protective against the distant rumor of the world, the modern clamor that sometimes reached even our secluded country in the form of wheels, hammers, and shouted commands.

At the drawbridge the landscape opened—road and river and the dim meadows—and the moonlight lay upon it like flour sifted over a dark table, making every contour visible and yet making it all seem less real. We paused, leaning slightly, as people do when they look down at water and feel the pull of depth.

“And so you were thinking of the night I came here?” she almost whispered.

“Yes.”

“Are you glad I came?”

“Delighted, dear Carmilla,” I said, meaning it with that warm gratitude one feels for a companion who has made solitude edible.

“And you asked for the picture you think like me,” she murmured with a sigh. Her arm tightened about my waist; her head sank on my shoulder with a weight that was tender and strange, as if she wished to be carried not by strength but by intimacy itself.

“How romantic you are, Carmilla,” I said, half laughing, half uneasy. “Whenever you tell me your story, it will be made up chiefly of some one great romance.”

She kissed me silently.

“I am sure, Carmilla, you have been in love,” I said, pushed by her manner into speaking what I would otherwise have kept unspoken. “That there is, at this moment, an affair of the heart going on.”

“I have been in love with no one, and never shall,” she whispered, “unless it should be with you.”

In the moonlight her face was more beautiful than ever—pale, precise, and yet warm at the mouth, like milk that has been heated and is about to skin. A shy, strange look passed over her; she hid her face quickly in my neck and hair. Her sighs came tumultuous, almost sobbing. The hand she pressed in mine trembled, not like illness alone, but like an overstrained desire. Her cheek glowed against mine with a heat that startled me.

“Darling, darling,” she murmured, “I live in you; and you would die for me, I love you so.”

I started away from her.

In an instant the passion vanished as if snuffed. She gazed at me with eyes from which all fire and meaning had flown; her face turned colorless and apathetic, as if the warmth I had felt were a deceit of my own skin.

“Is there a chill in the air, dear?” she said drowsily. “I almost shiver. Have I been dreaming? Let us come in. Come; come; come in.”

“You look ill, Carmilla—faint,” I said, alarmed. “You certainly must take some wine.”

“Yes. I will. I’m better now. I shall be quite well in a few minutes. Yes—do give me a little wine,” she answered as we approached the door. Then she paused and looked back once more into the pale court. “Let us look again for a moment; it is the last time, perhaps, I shall see the moonlight with you.”

“How do you feel now, dear Carmilla? Are you really better?” I asked. A thought had begun to prick me—the talk of an epidemic in the country about us, whispered in kitchens and at village thresholds, always with the same ominous economy of words, as if naming it too clearly would invite it closer. “Papa would be grieved beyond measure if he thought you were ever so little ill without immediately letting us know. We have a very skilful doctor near us—the physician who was with Papa to-day.”

“I’m sure he is,” she said, with a faint impatience, and then softened at once, as though smoothing her own tone. “I know how kind you all are; but, dear child, I am quite well again. There is nothing ever wrong with me but a little weakness. People say I am languid; I am incapable of exertion; I can scarcely walk as far as a child of three years old. And every now and then the little strength I have falters, and I become as you have just seen me. But after all I am very easily set up again; in a moment I am perfectly myself. See how I have recovered.”

So indeed she had. The pallor cleared as if washed away; her voice regained its music; her eyes once more held that glossy, inward attention that made me feel at once cherished and examined. We went in; she took wine as I urged, and afterward we talked much, and she was animated—almost brilliant. The remainder of the evening passed without any recurrence of what I called her infatuations—those sudden looks and phrases that embarrassed me and, more than once, frightened me with their intimacy and their coldness yoked together.

Yet that night there occurred an event that gave my thoughts a quite new turn, and seemed even to startle Carmilla’s languid nature into a momentary energy.

When we had drawn ourselves again into the drawing-room—where the order of the house reasserted itself with its accustomed gentleness, as if habit were a kind of prayer—we settled to our coffee and chocolate. The cups made their small domestic music: porcelain ticking against saucer, the soft pour, the faint sigh of steam; and behind it all the old chateau’s breathing—timbers contracting, a shutter giving its minute complaint, the wind worrying the ivy at the casement. It was the sort of sound-scape that, in country houses, keeps one’s nerves in tune; there were no iron wheels shrieking on rails here, no factory pulse, no street-vendors’ bawling to sell you what you did not need. Only the steady, human economy of a household that still knew how to warm milk without scorching it, and how to draw water without waking the entire valley.

Carmilla took neither coffee nor chocolate. The fragrance rose to her and seemed to pass through her like moonlight through gauze; but she sat with us, and her color had returned, and her manner was once more that languid, affectionate composure which, after her earlier agitation, felt almost like a lamp re-lit. Madame and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine joined, and the cards came out, and we made of the evening the innocent little theatre it commonly was—hands shuffling, paper whispering, an occasional laugh dropped like a sugar-lump into a cup.

When the game was over, papa entered for what he called his “dish of tea.” I can still hear the bright clink of the spoon in his glass and the small satisfaction in the phrase itself—old words, old turns of speech, which belong to families as recipes do, and which one day, I have sometimes thought, vanish as completely as a dialect vanishes when its last mouth is closed. He sat down beside Carmilla upon the sofa, and, with a care that tried to look like ease, asked whether she had heard from her mother since her arrival.

“No,” she answered simply.

“And do you know,” he continued, “to what address a letter would reach you at present?”

“I cannot tell,” she said, with a softness that concealed rather than confessed. Then, after a moment, in which I saw her fingers rest upon the silk of her dress as if she were smoothing a thought flat, she added, “But I have been thinking of leaving you. You have been already too hospitable and too kind to me. I have given you an infinity of trouble, and I should wish to take a carriage tomorrow, and post in pursuit of her. I know where I shall ultimately find her, although I dare not yet tell you.”

“But you must not dream of any such thing,” papa exclaimed at once—to my great relief, for the very idea of her departure struck the room like the sudden extinction of the fire. “We cannot afford to lose you so, and I will not consent to your leaving us, except under the care of your mother, who was so good as to consent to your remaining with us till she should herself return. I should be quite happy if I knew that you heard from her; but this evening the accounts of the progress of the mysterious disease that has invaded our neighborhood grow even more alarming; and my beautiful guest, I do feel the responsibility, unaided by advice from your mother, very much. Still I shall do my best; and one thing is certain—you must not think of leaving us without her distinct direction to that effect. We should suffer too much in parting from you to consent to it easily.”

She looked down, and that shy smile came, as if praise and kindness were things she had been denied and did not quite know where to lay. “Thank you, sir, a thousand times for your hospitality,” she answered. “You have all been too kind to me. I have seldom been so happy in all my life before as in your beautiful chateau, under your care, and in the society of your dear daughter.”

Papa, gallant in his old-fashioned way, kissed her hand. I saw the pleased light in his face, and I remember, absurdly, the scent of the tea rising anew as he leaned—bergamot and warm leaf—and how that comforting odor seemed to insist that we were safe because we were civilized, because we still observed the pretty ceremonies of home.

I accompanied Carmilla, as usual, to her room, and sat chatting while she prepared for bed. The ordinary little actions—the unpinning of hair, the smoothing of a garment, the folding away of lace—had in her a curious solemnity, as if each were part of some private rite, performed not for cleanliness or comfort, but for memory. Her hair, when loosed, fell in rich waves, and the brushing of it made a faint, intimate sound, a dry, silken rain. Outside, the night held its own choir: a far owl’s single note, and the more constant hush of leaves. In such quiet, one hears even one’s thoughts turning; perhaps that is why the mind invents dangers to give itself a louder occupation.

“Do you think,” I said at length, “that you will ever confide fully in me?”

She turned round and smiled, and the smile answered in place of words—tender, ambiguous, and, in some strange way, complete. She only continued to look at me.

“You won’t answer that?” I said, half sorry I had spoken. “You can’t answer pleasantly. I ought not to have asked you.”

“You were quite right to ask me that, or anything,” she replied. “You do not know how dear you are to me, or you could not think any confidence too great to look for. But I am under vows—no nun half so awfully—and I dare not tell my story yet, even to you. The time is very near when you shall know everything. You will think me cruel, very selfish; but love is always selfish—the more ardent the more selfish. How jealous I am you cannot know. You must come with me, loving me, to death; or else hate me and still come with me, and hating me through death and after. There is no such word as indifference in my apathetic nature.”

“Now, Carmilla, you are going to talk your wild nonsense again,” I said hastily, for her words did not merely mean: they pressed against the skin, like a hand too cold.

“Not I—silly little fool as I am, and full of whims and fancies; for your sake I’ll talk like a sage,” she said, with that sudden turn to playful docility. “Were you ever at a ball?”

“No,” I answered, surprised at the leap. “How you do run on. What is it like? How charming it must be.”

“I almost forget,” she murmured, as if she were trying to taste a flavor long ago lost. “It is years ago.”

I laughed. “You are not so old. Your first ball can hardly be forgotten yet.”

“I remember everything about it—with an effort,” she said slowly. “I see it all as divers see what is going on above them, through a medium—dense, rippling, but transparent. There occurred that night what has confused the picture and made its colours faint. I was all but assassinated in my bed—wounded here,” and she touched her breast with the tips of her fingers, not dramatically, but as one touches a scar one cannot translate into speech. “And never was the same since.”

“Were you near dying?” I asked.

“Yes—very. A cruel love—strange love—that would have taken my life. Love will have its sacrifices. No sacrifice without blood. Let us go to sleep now; I feel so lazy. How can I get up just now and lock my door?”

She had already slipped into bed, and lay with her tiny hands buried in her rich wavy hair beneath her cheek, her little head deep in the pillow. Her eyes glittered in the candlelight and followed me wherever I moved, with a shy smile I could not decipher—like a word in a language one has heard in childhood and forgotten, except for the ache it leaves.

I bid her good-night and crept from the room with an uncomfortable sensation, as if I were leaving behind not a friend, but a question.

I often wondered whether our pretty guest ever said her prayers. I certainly had never seen her upon her knees. In the morning she never came down until long after our family prayers were over; at night she never left the drawing-room to attend our brief evening prayers in the hall. If it had not casually come out in one of our careless talks that she had been baptised, I should have doubted her being a Christian. Religion was a subject on which I had never heard her speak one word. Had I known the world better, this neglect—or antipathy—would not have surprised me so much; but in our quiet life, words were weights, and the words one refused were sometimes heavier than those one spoke. Silence, too, can be a kind of dominion.

The precautions of nervous people are infectious, and persons of a like temperament are pretty sure, after a time, to imitate them. I had adopted Carmilla’s habit of locking my bedroom door, having taken into my head all her whimsical alarms about midnight invaders and prowling assassins. I had also adopted her precaution of making a brief search through my room, to satisfy myself that no lurking assassin or robber was ensconced. These wise measures taken, I went to bed.

A light burned in my room. This was an old habit of very early date, and nothing could have tempted me to dispense with it. The candle made a small pool of safety—its waxy odor, its faint crackle, the tiny hiss when the wick fed. Thus fortified, I might take my rest in peace.

But dreams come through stone walls. They brighten dark rooms and darken light ones, and their persons make their exits and their entrances as they please, and laugh at locksmiths.

I had a dream that night that was the beginning of a very strange agony. I cannot call it a nightmare, for I was quite conscious of being asleep; yet I was equally conscious of being in my room and lying in bed precisely as I actually was. I saw—or fancied I saw—the room and its furniture just as I had seen it last, except that it was very dark; and I saw something moving round the foot of the bed, which at first I could not accurately distinguish.

Soon, however, its shape declared itself: a sooty-black animal, like a monstrous cat. It seemed to me four or five feet long, for it measured fully the length of the hearthrug as it passed over it; and it continued to-ing and fro-ing with the lithe, sinister restlessness of a beast in a cage. There was a soundlessness to it that was worse than any cry. In the country, even terror generally has a voice—hoofbeats, a branch snapped, a dog’s warning. This thing moved without paying the night its proper noises, and the unnatural quiet tightened round my throat.

I could not cry out, though I was terrified. Its pace grew faster. The room seemed to thicken with darkness, darker and darker, as if someone were smothering the candle with a slow hand; until at last I could no longer see anything of it but its eyes—broad, watchful, and fixed, as if they were the only two lights permitted in my world.

I felt it spring lightly upon the bed.

Those eyes approached my face, and suddenly I felt a stinging pain, as if two large needles darted—an inch or two apart—deep into my breast.

I woke with a scream.

The room was lighted by the candle that burned there all through the night; and I saw a female figure standing at the foot of the bed, a little at the right side. It wore a dark loose dress; its hair was down and covered its shoulders. It was so still that a block of stone could not have been more still. There was not the slightest stir of respiration. Even the night’s ordinary sounds seemed to hold their breath, as if the house itself were listening and did not dare to speak.

As I stared, the figure appeared to have changed its place and was now nearer the door. Then, close to it, the door opened, and it passed out.

At once I was relieved—able to breathe and move, as though some weight had been lifted from my ribs. My first thought was that Carmilla had been playing me a trick, and that I had forgotten to secure my door. I hastened to it and found it locked as usual on the inside.

I was afraid to open it. I was horrified. I sprang back into bed, covered my head in the bedclothes, and lay there more dead than alive till morning.

It would be useless to pretend I can portion out, neatly and coldly, the horror of that night, as though it were a thing that rose and fell like the heat of an oven and then was done. It did not pass off like the terrors of common dreams. It thickened with the daylight; it took possession of the very room, as if the boards and hangings had drunk it in, and even now, in remembering, I see again the familiar objects—chair, curtain, mirror—tainted, as provisions can be tainted, not by sight but by a foul, secret change that makes them unfit for the mouth.

Next day I could not endure solitude for an instant. I should have spoken to my father; yet two opposite fears held my tongue. In one mood I imagined he would laugh, and I could not bear to have that dread handled like a trifle and tossed back at me with a smile. In another I dreaded that he would connect my story with the mysterious sickness that had crept about our neighborhood, house by house, like a miasma that chooses its victims. I had no suspicion of such an illness in myself—none then—and since my father had been an invalid of late, I shrank from alarming him. So I kept close to company, as one keeps close to a hearth when the wind has begun to mutter strangely in the chimneys.

Madame Perrodon was with me, kind and steady as good bread; Mademoiselle Lafontaine too, lively as a spoon tapping a glass. They saw at once that my spirits were low and my nerves raw, as if every sound in the house were too sharp. At last the weight at my heart pressed out of me, and I told them.

Mademoiselle laughed in spite of herself; her laughter had the lightness of a kitchen song, meant to keep hands moving. But Madame Perrodon’s face tightened, as if she had caught, beneath the words, an odor she did not like.

“By-the-by,” cried Mademoiselle, still laughing, “the long lime-tree walk behind Carmilla’s bedroom window is haunted!”

“Nonsense!” Madame exclaimed, quickly—too quickly, I thought, as a person slaps a lid down on a pot that threatens to boil over. “And who tells that story, my dear?”

“Martin. He says when the old yard-gate was being repaired he came up twice before sunrise, and twice saw the same female figure walking down the lime-tree avenue.”

“So he well might,” said Madame, with a dryness that tried to turn the tale into mere household arithmetic, “as long as there are cows to be milked in the river fields.”

“I daresay. But Martin chooses to be frightened, and never did I see fool more frightened.”

“You must not say a word of it to Carmilla,” I interposed. “She can see down that walk from her window; and she is—if possible—a greater coward than I.”

Carmilla came down later than usual that day, moving as though the air itself were heavy. The house had its ordinary sounds: distant clatter from the servants, a door closing far off, the soft tread on corridors. Yet I listened as one listens for a wrong note in music, for the smallest disharmony that spoils an entire meal.

“I was so frightened last night,” she said the moment we were alone together, her voice lowered, as if afraid the walls might carry it. “And I am sure I should have seen something dreadful if it had not been for that charm I bought from the poor little hunchback whom I called such hard names. I dreamed of something black coming round my bed, and I awoke in a perfect horror. I really thought—for some seconds—I saw a dark figure near the chimneypiece. But I felt under my pillow for my charm; and the moment my fingers touched it, the figure disappeared. I felt quite certain that only for it something frightful would have shown itself—perhaps throttled me, as it did those poor people we heard of.”

Her words fell into my mind and lay there, heavy as a stone sunk in broth. I tried to swallow.

“Well—listen to me,” I said, and I told her what I had seen and suffered. At my recital she grew pale and seemed truly horrified.

“And had you the charm near you?” she asked, urgently, leaning toward me as if the answer were life or death.

“No. I had dropped it into a china vase in the drawing-room. But I shall take it with me tonight, since you have such faith in it.”

At this distance of time I cannot tell you—cannot even understand—how I overcame my horror so thoroughly that I lay down alone in my room that night. I remember only the exactness of the act: I pinned the charm to my pillow, as carefully as one ties up herbs in muslin so their scent will steep without escaping. Then I slept almost at once—slept, indeed, more soundly than usual, as though my senses had been lulled by some invisible draught.

The next night I slept as well. My sleep was deep and dreamless, like a pot kept at a slow, steady simmer. Yet when I woke there was a lassitude in me, and a melancholy—not sharp, not painful, but insinuating, almost luxurious, as if my mind had been dipped in some sweet, dark cordial that weakened and pleased at once.

“Well, I told you so,” said Carmilla when I described my quiet night. “I had such delightful sleep myself. I pinned the charm to the breast of my nightdress. It was too far away the night before. I am quite sure it was all fancy—except the dreams. I used to think evil spirits made dreams; but our doctor told me it is no such thing. Only a fever passing by, or some other malady, as they often do, knocks at the door; and not being able to get in, passes on—with that alarm.”

“And what do you think the charm is?” I asked.

“It has been fumigated or immersed in some drug,” she answered, “and is an antidote against the malaria.”

“Then it acts only on the body?”

“Certainly. You don’t suppose evil spirits are frightened by bits of ribbon, or the perfumes of a druggist’s shop? No—these complaints, wandering in the air, begin by trying the nerves, and so infect the brain; but before they can seize upon you, the antidote repels them. That, I am sure, is what the charm has done for us. It is nothing magical. It is simply natural.”

I wished I could agree with her entirely. I tried, as a person tries to accept a substitute ingredient when the true one cannot be had. The impression of my first terror did weaken a little; the house resumed its customary domestic rhythm—doors opened, trays were carried, linens folded, footsteps measured along passages. Yet my own body began to fall out of tune with that ordinary music.

For some nights I slept profoundly. But each morning the same languor met me, settled on me, clung, and all day a weight lay upon my spirits. I felt myself a changed girl. A strange melancholy stole over me, and I did not wish it interrupted, as if it were a song in a dialect I could not quite speak but longed to hear to the end. Dim thoughts of death began to unfold; the notion that I was slowly sinking took gentle possession of me, and—horrible confession—was not unwelcome. If it was sad, it was also sweet; my soul, in some secret corner, consented.

I would not admit I was ill. I would not consent to tell my father or to have the doctor sent for. I had no pain; there was no complaint I could name with the plain words of household sickness. It seemed a malady of the nerves, of the imagination; and with a reserve that now astonishes me, I kept my sufferings almost entirely to myself, as if speaking them aloud would give them a vocabulary and so make them truer.

Carmilla, meanwhile, became more devoted to me than ever. Her fits of languid adoration grew more frequent. She would gaze at me—gloat upon me—with an ardor that increased as my strength and spirits waned, as though my weakness were a spice that sharpened her appetite. The look always shocked me, a sudden glare like insanity. I did not know it then, but I was already far advanced in the strangest illness under which mortal ever suffered.

There was, at first, an unaccountable fascination in the early symptoms that more than reconciled me to their disabling effect. That fascination increased for a time until it reached a point; then, little by little, a sense of the horrible began to mingle with it, deepening—as you shall hear—until it discolored and perverted the whole state of my life. The first change was even agreeable. It was very near the turning-point from which began the descent of Avernus.

Certain vague sensations visited me in sleep. The chief was a pleasant, peculiar cold thrill, like the moment in bathing when you move against a river’s current and feel the living chill press and slide along your skin. Then came dreams that seemed interminable, so vague I could never recover their scenery, their persons, or any connected action. They left, however, an awful impression and a sense of exhaustion, as if I had passed through a long period of mental exertion and peril.

After these dreams, on waking, there remained a remembrance of having been in a place almost dark; of having spoken to people whom I could not see; and especially of one clear voice—a woman’s, very deep—speaking as if at a distance, slowly, always producing the same sensation of solemnity and fear, as though it used a language whose meaning could not be translated into mine, but whose command my nerves understood perfectly.

Sometimes there came a sensation as if a hand were drawn softly along my cheek and neck—light as flour sifted over warm skin. Sometimes it was as if warm lips kissed me: longer, longer, more lovingly as they reached my throat, and then the caress fixed itself there. My heart beat faster; my breathing rose and fell rapidly, full-drawn. A sobbing grew into a sense of strangulation; then came a dreadful convulsion, in which my senses left me, and I became unconscious, as if some unseen pressure had extinguished me like a lamp.

Three weeks had passed since this unaccountable state began. During the last week my sufferings had printed themselves on my face. I had grown pale; my eyes looked dilated, with darkness beneath; the long languor that had inhabited me began to show itself openly, like a slow-spreading stain.

My father asked often whether I was ill. But with an obstinacy that now seems incomprehensible I persisted in assuring him I was quite well. In a sense it was true: I felt no pain; I could point to no bodily derangement. Yet what was happening was more alarming than pain, and the more terrible because it slipped between the customary words. I told myself it could not be that dreadful complaint the peasants called the oupire; for I had now been suffering three weeks, and those poor people were seldom ill longer than three days before death ended their misery.

Carmilla complained sometimes of dreams and feverish sensations, but by no means so alarming as mine. Mine were, in truth, terrifying; had I been capable of comprehending my condition, I would have invoked aid and advice on my knees. But some narcotic influence, unsuspected, acted upon me; my perceptions were dulled, my judgment muffled, as if the world’s sounds had been padded—no crisp clink, no clear call—only a softened, treacherous hush.

I am going to tell you of a dream that led at once to an odd discovery.

One night, instead of the deep voice I had come to expect in the darkness, I heard one sweet and tender, and at the same time terrible, which said: “Your mother warns you to beware of the assassin.”

At that instant a light sprang up unexpectedly, and I saw Carmilla standing near the foot of my bed in her white nightdress, bathed from chin to feet in one great stain of blood.

I awoke with a shriek, possessed by the one idea that Carmilla was being murdered. I remember springing from the bed; and my next recollection is of standing on the lobby, crying for help. Madame and Mademoiselle came scurrying from their rooms, startled into harsh breath and hurried footfalls. A lamp burned always on the lobby; by that steady light they saw my terror and quickly learned its cause.

I insisted we knock at Carmilla’s door. We knocked—there was no answer. The knocking became pounding; the quiet hallway filled with an uproar that seemed to shatter the house’s accustomed night-silence. We shrieked her name, but all was vain. The door was locked.

We hurried back in panic to my room and rang the bell long and furiously. If my father’s room had been on that side of the house we would have called him up at once; but he was quite out of hearing, and to reach him would have meant an expedition through dim passages none of us had courage for. Soon, however, servants came running up the stairs; meanwhile I had pulled on my dressing-gown and slippers, and my companions had done the same.

Recognizing the servants’ voices on the lobby—those familiar human sounds that ordinarily restore order as surely as the rattle of keys—we sallied out together. We renewed our summons at Carmilla’s door, still fruitlessly. Then I ordered the men to force the lock. They did so.

We stood, holding our lights aloft in the doorway, and stared into the room. We called her by name; there was no reply. We looked round. Everything was undisturbed—exactly as I had left it when I bade her good night.

But Carmilla was gone.

The instant we saw the chamber—still as a pantry at dawn, every article in its place despite our own brutal bursting-in—the first heat of terror ebbed. The men, breathless and sweating from their stampede up the stairs, stood about like kitchen-hands who have upset a tray and now wait for scolding. When my sense returned enough to hear the house’s own voice again—the boards settling, the night wind worrying the casement-latches, the far-off drip in the stone passage—we dismissed them. Their boots and sabres had filled the gallery with a harsh, clanging din, the sort of noise that makes even courage taste metallic on the tongue; and when it died away, the old château’s quieter sounds pressed back like a remembered dialect returning after a shouted command.

Mademoiselle, always quicker than I at finding a domestic explanation for a nightmare, whispered that Carmilla might have been startled by the uproar at her door and, in her first fright, slipped out of bed and hidden herself in a press, or behind a curtain—anywhere she could wedge her slight body until the majordomo and his myrmidons had withdrawn. The idea, so homely and plausible, cooled us further, as if someone had set a bowl of milk on a boiling stove and it had stopped its raging at once.

We began again, more carefully now, opening doors and peering behind hangings with the conscientious thoroughness of a cook searching a larder shelf—hand to wood, nose to air, eye to shadow. We called her name, not once but over and over, softening it, sharpening it, letting it travel into corners.

“Carmilla—dear Carmilla.”

No answer. Not a sigh, not the faintest rustle, not even that tiny sound of fabric settling that follows a person’s movement in still rooms. The silence seemed to thicken, like cooled broth taking body; and with it my perplexity grew. We went to the windows. They were secured. The night air pressed coldly against the panes, and there was no loosened catch, no lifted sash.

I implored her aloud, if she had hidden herself, to end the cruelty of the trick—to come out and put an end to our anxiety. My own voice sounded wrong in that room, too loud and too modern, like the harsh word of a foreign tongue in a place where only the older speech should have been used.

Still nothing.

By then I was certain she was not in the room at all, nor in the dressing-room beyond, whose door was still locked from our side. She could not have passed it. The certainty sat in my chest like a stone. Had she found one of those secret passages the old housekeeper used to speak of—those lost veins of the schloss that tradition insisted existed though no one could now name their exact mouth? The château, like an old recipe transmitted in fragments, had places where the instructions had died and only the effects remained.

A little time would explain it—so we told ourselves—though for the present we stood in a bewilderment that tasted of cold iron and candle smoke. It was past four o’clock; and I, fearing the remaining darkness as one fears a kitchen after the hearth has gone out, chose to pass the rest of the night in Madame’s room.

Daylight, so often a kind cook that clarifies what night has curdled, brought no solution. From morning onward the whole household was in agitation, my father at its head. Every room was searched, every closet opened, every chest and press examined, as if the château were a vast storehouse and we were hunting for some precious lost ingredient without which the day’s nourishment could not be made. The grounds were explored; servants fanned through hedges and outbuildings; the stable-yard rang with calls and the stamping of horses. The stream was about to be dragged. My father, usually so exact in his calm, was in distraction—already tasting the dreadful story he might have to tell Carmilla’s mother upon her return.

I too was almost beside myself, though my grief was of a different kind, less public and more intimate, like a hunger no one else suspects. The hours went by with the dull persistence of a bad refrain. The château’s acoustics—so often comforting in their old, rural order—were bruised by running feet, slammed doors, shouted questions. Even the birds outside seemed to hold their songs back, as if the noise we made had driven the finer sounds away.

It was one o’clock. Still no tidings.

Something—hope or desperation—drove me at last up again to Carmilla’s room. I went quickly, my heart beating with each stair as if the steps themselves were drumming under my ribs. The corridor smelled of dust and old wax, with a faint sourness where the air lay trapped. When I opened her door—

Carmilla stood at her dressing-table.

For a moment the world stopped. It was as if I had opened a cupboard I had already found empty and discovered it stocked to the brim. I could not believe my eyes. She did not speak. She only beckoned me with one pretty finger, and the gesture—so small, so domestic—was somehow more terrifying for its quietness. Her face expressed extreme fear.

I ran to her in an ecstasy of joy, seized her, kissed and embraced her again and again, feeling the coolness of her cheek, the faint perfume that clung to her hair like bruised violets and something darker beneath. I flew to the bell and rang it vehemently, as if by sheer noise I could undo the hours of silence and relieve my father’s mind.

“Dear Carmilla, what has become of you all this time? We have been in agonies of anxiety about you. Where have you been? How did you come back?”

She looked at me as if my words were a language she only half remembered.

“Last night has been a night of wonders,” she said.

“For mercy’s sake, explain all you can.”

“It was past two last night,” she began, and her voice, though low, was perfectly distinct, as if fear had sharpened it. “I went to sleep as usual in my bed, with my doors locked—the dressing-room door and the door opening on the gallery. My sleep was uninterrupted, and, so far as I know, dreamless; but I woke just now on the sofa in the dressing-room there, and I found the door between the rooms open, and the other door forced. How could all this have happened without my being wakened? It must have been accompanied with a great deal of noise; and I am particularly easily wakened. And how could I have been carried out of my bed without my sleep having been interrupted—I, whom the slightest stir startles?”

Even as she spoke, the house began to fill again. Madame hurried in; Mademoiselle followed; my father came, pale and stern with relief; servants crowded behind, whispering and crossing themselves. The air warmed with bodies and breath. Carmilla was overwhelmed at once with inquiries, congratulations, welcomes—hands reaching to touch her sleeves as if to prove she was real. Yet she had only that one story to tell, and seemed, of all of us, the least able to account for it. She sat with the helplessness of a person who has awakened in the middle of a familiar recipe and cannot recall the missing steps.

My father sent the servants away at last, restoring a measure of order. Mademoiselle went in search of her small bottle of valerian and salvolatile, as if scent and taste could steady the mind by their ancient authority. When the room held only Carmilla, my father, Madame, and myself, he walked once or twice up and down, thinking. I watched him, grateful for his presence as one is grateful for a steady hand at the board; and I saw Carmilla’s eye follow him for a moment with a sly, dark glance—quick as the flicker of a knife-blade catching light.

Then my father came to her. His manner softened; he took her hand with real kindness, led her to the sofa, and sat beside her.

“Will you forgive me, my dear, if I risk a conjecture, and ask a question?”

“Who can have a better right?” she answered. “Ask what you please, and I will tell you everything. But my story is simply one of bewilderment and darkness. I know absolutely nothing. Put any question you please—but you know, of course, the limitations mamma has placed me under.”

“Perfectly, my dear child. I need not approach the topics on which she desires our silence.” He paused, his voice falling into that practical tone with which he might have instructed a steward over accounts. “Now, the marvel of last night consists in your having been removed from your bed and your room without being wakened, and this removal having occurred apparently while the windows were still secured, and the two doors locked upon the inside. I will tell you my theory and ask you a question.”

Carmilla leaned on her hand, dejected; Madame and I listened as if our very breath might disturb the fragile explanation he was trying to set like jelly.

“My question is this. Have you ever been suspected of walking in your sleep?”

“Never,” she said, and then, as if forced to taste an old admission, added, “since I was very young indeed.”

“But you did walk in your sleep when you were young?”

“Yes. I know I did. I have been told so often by my old nurse.”

My father smiled and nodded, as if that small remembered fact were a missing spice that suddenly made the whole dish intelligible.

“Well, what has happened is this. You got up in your sleep, unlocked the door—not leaving the key, as usual, in the lock, but taking it out and locking it on the outside. You again took the key out, and carried it away with you to some one of the five-and-twenty rooms on this floor, or perhaps upstairs or downstairs. There are so many rooms and closets, so much heavy furniture, and such accumulations of lumber, that it would require a week to search this old house thoroughly. Do you see, now, what I mean?”

“I do—but not all,” she said, and her brows drew together. “And how, papa, do you account for her finding herself on the sofa in the dressing-room, which we had searched so carefully?”

“She came there after you had searched it,” my father answered, “still in her sleep, and at last awoke spontaneously, and was as much surprised to find herself where she was as any one else.” He laughed a little, as if laughter could sweep away the last crumbs of dread. “I wish all mysteries were as easily and innocently explained as yours, Carmilla. And so we may congratulate ourselves on the certainty that the most natural explanation of the occurrence is one that involves no drugging, no tampering with locks, no burglars, or poisoners, or witches—nothing that need alarm Carmilla, or any one else, for our safety.”

Carmilla, as she sat there, looked charmingly. Nothing could be more beautiful than her tints, that strange, delicate colouring that seemed to bloom most when she was languid. Her beauty, I thought, was even enhanced by the graceful weariness peculiar to her, as if she carried an old world’s secret fatigue in her blood.

My father’s eyes shifted to me, and his expression changed with a tender concern that stung.

“I wish my poor Laura was looking more like herself,” he said; and he sighed.

So, with that neat, paternal theory fitted over the night’s horror like a lid over a pot, our alarms were happily ended, and Carmilla restored to her friends—while the château, in its deeper corridors and forgotten tongues of stone, kept its own counsel in silence.

Carmilla would not hear—no, not for a moment—of any attendant sleeping in her room. She had a way of refusing that was neither loud nor angry; it was softer than that, like a hand laid over the mouth of a clock to stop its ticking. My father, who had begun to dread her nocturnal wanderings as one dreads a draught one cannot locate, contrived a compromise: a servant was to lie on a pallet just outside her door, so that if she tried again to glide out into the passages she might be stopped—caught, as it were, at the very lip of her den.

That night passed quietly.

The next morning, early—so early the house still held the cool, breadless smell of stone that has not yet been warmed by kitchens—the doctor arrived. My father had sent for him without breathing a word of it to me. Madame took my arm and guided me to the library, where the grave little physician with white hair and spectacles waited among the books, as if he had come to consult some older authority than mine.

I told him everything. I spoke, and as I spoke the air in that room seemed to thicken, as if my words had the weight of damp wool. The doctor’s face, already solemn, tightened into something sterner, and in his listening there was an attentive appetite—one might say—yet not for nourishment but for symptoms; as though he were a food-historian of illnesses, tasting the tale for hidden bitterness. We stood in the deep recess of a window, facing one another. When I finished, he leaned his shoulders against the wall and fixed his eyes upon me with an intentness in which—like a spice unexpectedly biting the tongue—there was a dash of horror.

After a minute, he asked Madame if he might see my father.

Papa was sent for. He entered smiling, as if determined to laugh away the very idea of alarm.

“I dare say, doctor, you are going to tell me that I am an old fool for having brought you here; I hope I am.”

But the smile went out of him at the doctor’s gesture. The physician beckoned him into the same window-recess where I had just stood, and they began to talk in a low tone—earnest, argumentative. The library is very large. Madame and I remained at the farther end, side by side, our curiosity burning with a kind of dry heat. Not a word reached us. The thick wall and deep-set window made a sort of closet for their voices, and concealed the doctor entirely; we saw only part of my father—his foot, an arm, a shoulder—as if he were being gradually erased by some encroaching shadow.

At last my father’s face appeared looking into the room. It was pale and thoughtful, and I fancied it agitated.

“Laura, dear, come here for a moment. Madame, we shan’t trouble you, the doctor says, at present.”

I approached, for the first time a little alarmed; because though I was weak—weak as if I had been living on thin broth too long—I did not feel ill, and we imagine strength is something we can fetch back at will, as one fetches a jar of preserves from a shelf.

My father held out his hand as I came near, but his eyes were on the doctor.

“It certainly is very odd; I don’t understand it quite. Laura, come here, dear; now attend to Doctor Spielsberg, and recollect yourself.”

The doctor spoke with the quiet precision of a man who measures ingredients.

“You mentioned a sensation like that of two needles piercing the skin, somewhere about your neck, on the night when you experienced your first horrible dream. Is there still any soreness?”

“None at all,” I said.

“Can you indicate with your finger about the point at which you think this occurred?”

“A little below my throat—here.”

My morning dress covered the place.

“Now you can satisfy yourself,” said the doctor. “You won’t mind your papa’s lowering your dress a very little. It is necessary, to detect a symptom of the complaint under which you have been suffering.”

I consented. My father’s fingers trembled slightly as he drew the edge down an inch or two.

“God bless me!—so it is,” he exclaimed, turning paler still.

“You see it now with your own eyes,” said the doctor, with a gloomy triumph.

“What is it?” I cried, frightened now in earnest.

“Nothing, my dear young lady, but a small blue spot, about the size of the tip of your little finger; and now,” he added, turning to papa, “the question is what is best to be done?”

“Is there any danger?” I urged, my voice thin with trepidation.

“I trust not, my dear,” the doctor answered. “I don’t see why you should not recover. I don’t see why you should not begin immediately to get better. That is the point at which the sense of strangulation begins?”

“Yes.”

“And—recollect as well as you can—the same point was a kind of centre of that thrill which you described just now, like the current of a cold stream running against you?”

“It may have been; I think it was.”

“Ay, you see?” he said, turning again to my father, as if that tiny bruise were a piece of evidence laid on a table between them.

My father, after a moment, said, “Shall I say a word to Madame?”

“Certainly.”

He called Madame to him.

“I find my young friend here far from well. It won’t be of any great consequence, I hope; but it will be necessary that some steps be taken, which I will explain by-and-by; but in the meantime, Madame, you will be so good as not to let Miss Laura be alone for one moment. That is the only direction I need give for the present. It is indispensable.”

“We may rely upon your kindness, Madame, I know,” he added.

Madame promised eagerly; indeed she looked as if, had it been required, she would have slept upright by my chair like a faithful dog.

“And you, dear Laura, I know you will observe the doctor’s direction.”

Then my father, as if remembering some other taste upon the palate of his mind—another case—turned to the doctor again.

“I shall have to ask your opinion upon another patient, whose symptoms slightly resemble those of my daughter, that have just been detailed to you—very much milder in degree, but I believe quite of the same sort. She is a young lady—our guest; but as you say you will be passing this way again this evening, you can’t do better than take your supper here, and you can then see her. She does not come down till the afternoon.”

“I thank you,” said the doctor. “I shall be with you, then, at about seven this evening.”

They repeated their directions to me and to Madame, as one repeats a recipe that must be followed exactly; then my father left us and walked out with the physician. From the window I saw them pacing together between the road and the moat on the grassy platform before the castle, absorbed in talk. The morning carried sound strangely: the faint scuff of their steps, the distant stir of birds in the forest, the occasional metallic click from some chain or buckle—small noises that, in their scarcity, grew important. The house itself seemed to listen.

The doctor did not come back in. I saw him mount his horse, take leave, and ride away eastward through the trees, the forest swallowing the beat of hooves until it was only a memory in the air.

Nearly at the same time a man arrived from Dranfield with the letters. He dismounted and handed the bag to my father.

Madame and I, left together, were busy with conjectures. She afterward confessed she feared the doctor apprehended some sudden seizure—that, without prompt help, I might lose my life in a fit, or at least be terribly hurt. That explanation did not occur to me. Perhaps it was better for my nerves that I imagined the new rule—never alone—was merely meant to prevent my doing something foolish, as young people are accused of doing: taking too much exercise, eating unripe fruit, or any of those domestic offences that older heads treat as the true perils of youth. Yet even while I told myself this, I kept noticing the sounds: a door-latch lifted too softly; a tread pausing where it should have passed; the way quiet, once it is commanded, becomes a sort of pressure on the ears.

About half an hour later my father returned with a letter in his hand.

“This letter had been delayed,” he said. “It is from General Spielsdorf. He might have been here yesterday; he may not come till tomorrow; or he may be here today.”

He put the open letter into my hand. Ordinarily the prospect of the General’s arrival would have brightened him; the General was so much loved, so long known. But now my father looked as if he wished him at the bottom of the Red Sea. Something had lodged in his mind like a splinter, and he would not show it to me.

“Papa, darling, will you tell me this?” I said suddenly, laying my hand on his arm and looking up—imploringly, I am sure.

“Perhaps,” he answered, smoothing my hair over my eyes with a tenderness that felt, for once, like an evasion.

“Does the doctor think me very ill?”

“No, dear; he thinks, if right steps are taken, you will be quite well again—at least, on the high road to a complete recovery—in a day or two,” he replied, a little dryly. “I wish our good friend, the General, had chosen any other time; that is, I wish you had been perfectly well to receive him.”

“But do tell me, papa,” I persisted, “what does he think is the matter with me?”

“Nothing; you must not plague me with questions,” he said, with more irritation than I had ever heard from him. Seeing my face fall, he kissed me and added, “You shall know all about it in a day or two; that is, all that I know. In the meantime you are not to trouble your head about it.”

He turned and left the room; but he came back almost at once, as if the household schedule—those ordinary arrangements of wheels, hours, baskets, and servants—must continue no matter what unseen thing pressed upon us.

He said he was going to Karnstein, and that the carriage was ordered for twelve. Madame and I were to accompany him. He was to see the priest who lived near those picturesque grounds on business; and as Carmilla had never seen them, she could follow when she came down, with Mademoiselle, who would bring materials for what you call a picnic to be laid for us in the ruined castle.

Even that word—picnic—sounded odd in a day like this, like a bright ribbon tied around a throat that still ached. Yet the notion of food prepared and carried out—bread cut, fruit chosen, wine wrapped, cold meats laid in clean cloth—had the domestic power of pretending that nothing had changed. The house would still eat at its hours; the world would still be fed.

At twelve, accordingly, I was ready, and not long after my father, Madame, and I set out.

Passing the drawbridge we turned to the right and followed the road over the steep Gothic bridge westward toward the deserted village and ruined castle of Karnstein. No sylvan drive can be fancied prettier. The land rose and dipped in gentle hills and hollows, clothed in beautiful wood, free of that formal look which comes of artificial planting and early pruning. The road surrendered to the ground’s irregularities and wound naturally around broken hollows and steeper slopes, as if it were following some older track—one remembered by the soil itself.

And always there was the soundscape: the carriage wheels hushing over packed earth, then changing tone on stones; the leather creak of harness; the insects’ thin continuous whine; the layered bird-calls that made the forest feel like an organ holding many notes. Against those living sounds, the silence of the deserted village ahead seemed not empty but watchful, like a mouth closed too hard.

Turning one of the points in the road, we suddenly encountered our old friend the General riding toward us, attended by a mounted servant. His portmanteaus followed in a hired wagon—a cart, as we call it. The General dismounted as we pulled up; and after the usual greetings he was easily persuaded to accept the vacant seat in the carriage and send his horse on with his servant to the schloss.

It was close upon ten months since we had last seen the General; yet, in that short season, time had done in him the work of years. His frame had narrowed, as if grief had eaten from within with a slow, fasting diligence; and where his face once held a frank, sun-warmed serenity, there now brooded a dusk of anxiety that looked less like sorrow than like wrath that had found no outlet. His dark blue eyes—always keen, soldierly, accustomed to take aim and to measure men—shone now with a harder glint beneath eyebrows grown shaggy and grey, as though the very hair had bristled to keep watch. It was not the change that mourning alone carves; there was something in it of anger long simmered, the temper of a pot kept too long upon the coals.

We had scarcely settled again into the rhythm of the carriage—wheels clicking, harness leather creaking, the horse’s breath steaming faintly in the cool—when he began to speak. The woods on either hand received our sound and gave it back altered: a softened roll of hoof and rim upon packed earth, the small chatter of branches, the far tap of a woodpecker like a careful knuckle upon old timber. Against that natural orchestra his voice came harshly, as if he had lived too long among the metallic clamours of parade-ground and gun, and the gentler tones of the country could not soothe him.

With his usual directness, he named his loss—“the bereavement,” as he called it—speaking of the death of his beloved niece and ward. But the word, so measured and decent, could not hold what surged behind it. The restraint split; bitterness poured out, scalding. He broke into invective against “hellish arts,” and wondered aloud—less in piety than in fury—how Heaven could suffer so monstrous a license to the lusts and malice of hell. In his vehemence, the forest seemed to listen: even the birds fell momentarily to a hush between calls, as though the air itself would not carry such words without reluctance.

My father, who understood at once that something beyond ordinary sickness and grief had happened, asked him—gently, and with the tact of one who knows that sorrow is a skittish creature—whether, if it were not too painful, he would detail the circumstances that had driven him to language so violent.

“I should tell you all with pleasure,” the General answered; “but you would not believe me.”

“Why should I not?” my father said.

“Because,” he returned, testily, “you believe in nothing but what consists with your own prejudices and illusions. I remember when I was like you; but I have learned better.”

“Try me,” my father said. “I am not such a dogmatist as you suppose. Besides, I know you generally require proof for what you believe, and am therefore strongly disposed to respect your conclusions.”

“You are right,” said the General, his mouth tightening, “in supposing that I have not been led lightly into a belief in the marvellous—for what I have experienced is marvellous. I have been forced by extraordinary evidence to credit that which ran counter, diametrically, to all my theories. I have been made the dupe of a preternatural conspiracy.”

Though my father had spoken with candour, I saw him, at those words, glance toward the General with a quick, guarded look—as one listens, not only for meaning, but for the sound of a mind slipping its cog from the wheel. The General, luckily, did not see it. His gaze had gone past us into the green corridors ahead, searching the openings in the woods as if he expected, at any turning, to see some shape step out between the trunks. The carriage made its domestic noises—wood and iron, leather and breath—small familiar sounds that should have been comforting; yet, under his stare, even those seemed too loud, too human, as if they might draw attention.

“You are going to the Ruins of Karnstein?” he asked suddenly.

“Yes,” said my father. “It is a lucky coincidence. I was going to ask you to bring me there to inspect them.”

“I have a special object in exploring,” the General said. “There is a ruined chapel, ain’t there, with a great many tombs of that extinct family?”

“So there are—highly interesting,” my father answered. Then, attempting a lightness to ease the air, he added, “I hope you are thinking of claiming the title and estates?”

The jest fell dead. The General neither smiled nor offered the small politeness of a laugh. He looked grave—fierce, even—ruminating on something that stirred both horror and anger in him, as a fire stirs beneath a covered pot.

“Something very different,” he said gruffly. “I mean to unearth some of those fine people. I hope, by God’s blessing, to accomplish a pious sacrilege here—one that will relieve our earth of certain monsters, and enable honest people to sleep in their beds without being assailed by murderers. I have strange things to tell you, my dear friend—such as I myself would have scouted as incredible a few months since.”

My father looked at him again; but now there was no suspicion in his glance. It had sharpened into alarmed intelligence, as if the General’s words, monstrous as they sounded, had touched some remembered thread and pulled it taut.

“The house of Karnstein,” my father said, “has been long extinct—one hundred years at least. My dear wife was maternally descended from the Karnsteins. But the name and title have long ceased to exist. The castle is a ruin; the very village is deserted; it is fifty years since the smoke of a chimney was seen there; not a roof left.”

“Quite true,” said the General. “I have heard a great deal about that since I last saw you; a great deal that will astonish you. But I had better relate everything in the order in which it occurred.”

He paused, and the carriage filled with the forest again: a thin wind moving through leaves with a sound like distant water over stones; the steady, bodily exhalation of the horse; the subtle, living hush that lies under rural sound like a base note. In that pause he seemed to gather himself, as a man does before carving meat at table—setting the blade, bracing the hand, knowing the cut will expose what has been hidden under skin.

“You saw my dear ward,” he said at last—his voice changing, thickening. “My child, I may call her. No creature could have been more beautiful; and only three months ago none more blooming.”

“Yes, poor thing,” my father said softly. “When I saw her last she certainly was quite lovely.”

“I was grieved and shocked more than I can tell you, my dear friend,” my father added, and reached across with an instinctive human kindness that was almost domestic in its simplicity. He took the General’s hand, and they exchanged a close pressure, as if the warmth of one living palm might keep the other from freezing into vengeance.

Tears gathered in the old soldier’s eyes. He did not hide them.

“We have been very old friends,” he said. “I knew you would feel for me, childless as I am. She had become an object of very near interest to me, and repaid my care by an affection that cheered my home and made my life happy. That is all gone. The years that remain to me on earth may not be very long; but by God’s mercy I hope to accomplish a service to mankind before I die, and to subserve the vengeance of Heaven upon the fiends who have murdered my poor child in the spring of her hopes and beauty!”

“You said, just now, that you intended relating everything as it occurred,” my father reminded him. “Pray do; I assure you it is not mere curiosity that prompts me.”

By this time we had reached the point where the Drunstall road—the one by which the General had come—broke away from the road we were taking toward Karnstein. The divergence opened like a choice carved into the landscape: one path toward the known, one toward a name that lay like cold ash on the tongue. The General leaned forward slightly, peering ahead with an impatience that was almost hunger.

“How far is it to the ruins?” he asked, his voice edged, anxious.

“About half a league,” my father answered.

“Pray let us hear the story you were so good as to promise,” my father said again; and the carriage went on, bearing us toward the silent place where no chimneys had smoked for fifty years, and where the very earth, it seemed, kept secrets under stone.

“With all my heart,” said the General; and though his voice held itself erect, it shook a little as a spoon trembles in a teacup when the hand is tired. He paused—one of those domestic pauses in which a man arranges not only his sentences, but his breath—and then began, as if he were opening a sealed jar whose contents had soured long ago.

“My dear child had been looking forward, with a pleasure that made the days brisker for her, to the visit you had been so good as to arrange—she spoke of your charming daughter as one speaks of a promised harvest.” Here he bent to me with a gallant inclination, but the bow had a weight in it, like a courtesy made at a graveside. “Before that happiness could be served to her, as it were, we received an invitation from my old friend, the Count Carlsfeld, whose schloss stands about six leagues beyond Karnstein. It was for the series of fêtes he gave in honour of his illustrious guest, the Grand Duke Charles.”

“Yes; and very splendid, I believe, they were,” said my father.

“Princely,” returned the General. “His hospitalities are regal; he might as well have had Aladdin’s lamp on his sideboard, so lavishly did he summon wonders. The night from which my sorrow dates was given to a magnificent masquerade. The grounds were thrown open; trees were hung with coloured lamps—globes and lanterns that made each branch look candied and unreal. And there was such a display of fireworks as Paris itself had never witnessed.

“But it is the sound I cannot forget. Music—music, you know, is my weakness—such ravishing music! The finest instrumental band, perhaps, in the world, and the finest singers that could be gathered from the great operas of Europe. In those fantastically lit gardens the chateau lay moonlit, the long rows of windows sending out a rosy sheen like heat from an oven-mouth. You would walk from a bright allee into a grove that seemed to swallow you, and then—without warning—voices would steal into the silence as if the leaves themselves had learned to sing; or you would hear them rise from boats upon the lake, the water taking the notes and carrying them under your ribs. The very nightingales, if there were any, must have been silenced, ashamed to compete. I felt, as I listened, carried back into the romance and poetry of my early youth; and yet that sweetness had in it a strain—like a flute-note stretched too thin—that made the hair stir on one’s neck.

“When the fireworks had died into smoke, and the ball began, we returned to the noble suite of rooms thrown open to the dancers. A masked ball is always a beautiful sight; but such brilliance, such dazzling multiplication of faces and fabrics, I never saw before. It was a very aristocratic assembly. I was myself almost the only ‘nobody’ present.

“My dear child looked—God help me—quite beautiful. She wore no mask. Her excitement and delight lent an unspeakable charm to features that were always lovely. I remember, with an exactness that is like a wound, the glitter of light on her hair when she turned, the little quick breath that came to her after dancing, as if the air itself were spiced.

“I remarked a young lady, magnificently dressed, but wearing a mask, who appeared to be observing my ward with extraordinary interest. I had seen her earlier in the evening in the great hall; and again, for a few minutes, walking near us on the terrace under the castle windows, similarly employed. A lady also masked, richly and gravely dressed, with a stately air—as of a person of rank—accompanied her as a chaperon. Had the young lady not worn a mask, I could have been more certain whether she truly watched my poor darling. I am now well assured she did.

“We were in one of the salons. My poor dear child had been dancing and rested a little in a chair near the door; I stood near her, like a man keeping watch over a dish that is too delicate to be trusted to strangers. The two ladies I have mentioned approached; the younger took the chair next my ward, while her companion stood beside me, and for a little time addressed herself in a low tone to her charge.

“Then, availing herself of the privilege of her mask, the elder turned to me; and in the tone of an old friend—calling me by my name—she opened a conversation that piqued my curiosity exceedingly. She referred to scenes where she had met me—at Court, and in distinguished houses—touching, with a light finger, little incidents I had long ceased to think of, but which had only lain in abeyance, for they sprang instantly into life at her touch, as scents do when a lid is lifted.

“Every moment I grew more curious to know who she was. She parried my attempts with an ease almost playful. The knowledge she showed of passages in my life seemed all but unaccountable; and she appeared to take a not unnatural pleasure in foiling my curiosity, in seeing me flounder from one conjecture to another, as if she were seasoning the talk with my perplexity.

“In the meantime the young lady—whom her mother, once or twice, called by the odd name of Millarca—had, with the same ease and grace, slipped into conversation with my ward. She introduced herself by saying her mother was a very old acquaintance of mine. She spoke of the agreeable audacity a mask makes practicable; she talked like a friend; she admired my child’s dress, and insinuated, very prettily, her admiration of her beauty. She amused her with laughing criticisms upon the people crowding the ballroom, and laughed at my poor child’s fun.

“She was very witty and lively when she pleased; and after a time they had grown very good friends. Then the young stranger lowered her mask, displaying a remarkably beautiful face. I had never seen it before; neither had my dear child. But though it was new to us, the features were so engaging, as well as lovely, that it was impossible not to feel the attraction powerfully. My poor girl did so. I never saw any one more taken with another at first sight—unless, indeed, it was the stranger herself, who seemed quite to have lost her heart to her.

“Meanwhile, taking the licence of a masquerade as one takes shelter under a cloak, I put not a few questions to the elder lady.

“‘You have puzzled me utterly,’ I said, laughing. ‘Is that not enough? Won’t you now consent to stand on equal terms, and do me the kindness to remove your mask?’

“‘Can any request be more unreasonable?’ she replied. ‘Ask a lady to yield an advantage! Besides, how do you know you should recognize me? Years make changes.’

“‘As you see,’ I said, with a bow, and, I suppose, a rather melancholy little laugh.

“‘As philosophers tell us,’ she said; ‘and how do you know that a sight of my face would help you?’

“‘I should take chance for that,’ I answered. ‘It is vain trying to make yourself out an old woman; your figure betrays you.’

“‘Years, nevertheless, have passed since I saw you—rather since you saw me, for that is what I am considering. Millarca there is my daughter; I cannot then be young, even in the opinion of people whom time has taught to be indulgent; and I may not like to be compared with what you remember me. You have no mask to remove. You can offer me nothing in exchange.’

“‘My petition is to your pity, to remove it.’

“‘And mine to yours, to let it stay where it is,’ she replied.

“‘Well, then, at least you will tell me whether you are French or German; you speak both languages so perfectly.’

“‘I don’t think I shall tell you that, General; you intend a surprise, and are meditating the particular point of attack.’

“‘At all events, you won’t deny this,’ I said, ‘that being honoured by your permission to converse, I ought to know how to address you. Shall I say Madame la Comtesse?’

“She laughed; and she would, no doubt, have met me with another evasion—if, indeed, I can treat any occurrence in an interview, every circumstance of which was prearranged, as I now believe, with profoundest cunning, as liable to be modified by accident.

“‘As to that,’ she began; but she was interrupted, almost as she opened her lips, by a gentleman dressed in black.

“He looked particularly elegant and distinguished; yet his face had that drawback—so deadly pale I never saw the like, except in death. He wore no masquerade: plain evening dress, as if he were too much in earnest for any borrowed face. He said, without a smile, but with a courtly, unusually low bow:

“‘Will Madame la Comtesse permit me to say a very few words which may interest her?’

“The lady turned quickly to him and touched her lip—silence. Then she said to me, lightly, as if we were only changing partners in a dance: ‘Keep my place for me, General; I shall return when I have said a few words.’

“With this injunction, playfully given, she walked a little aside with the gentleman in black and talked for some minutes—apparently very earnestly. I could not hear their words; the music and the continual rustle of the crowd made a kind of surf of sound, and in that bright room the very noise seemed to conceal rather than reveal, as though the night had learned to disguise itself not only with masks, but with melody. They then walked away slowly together into the press, and I lost them for some minutes.

“I spent the interval cudgelling my brains for a conjecture as to the identity of the lady who remembered me so kindly. I thought of turning to join the conversation between my pretty ward and the Countess’s daughter, and of trying whether, by the time her mother returned, I might not have a surprise in store for her—her name, title, château, and estates ready at my fingers’ ends, as neatly as a steward’s list.

“But at that moment she returned, accompanied by the pale man in black, who said:

“‘I shall return and inform Madame la Comtesse when her carriage is at the door.’

“He withdrew with a bow.”

“Then we are to lose Madame la Comtesse,” I said, bowing as low as my stiff back would allow, “but I hope only for a few hours.”

“It may be only that—or it may be weeks,” she answered; and even through her careful, courtly softness there ran a vibration like a snapped string. Somewhere beyond the salon doors, the music still breathed—violins rising and folding back into themselves—yet in her voice I heard another sound entirely: the hush before a storm breaks over ripe fields. “It was very unlucky his speaking to me just now as he did. Do you now know me?”

I assured her, truthfully, that I did not.

“You shall know me,” she said; “but not at present. We are older and better friends than, perhaps, you suspect. I cannot yet declare myself. In three weeks I shall pass your beautiful schloss—about which I have been making enquiries—and I shall then look in upon you for an hour or two, and renew a friendship I never think of without a thousand pleasant recollections.”

She paused as if listening for some signal only she could hear: not the harpsichord’s glitter from the adjoining rooms, nor the shuffle of dancers’ shoes, but a smaller, more intimate noise—news arriving like a hand on a latch.

“This moment,” she continued, “a piece of news has reached me like a thunderbolt. I must set out now, and travel by a devious route—nearly a hundred miles—with all the dispatch I can possibly make. My perplexities multiply.”

She spoke with the measured clarity of a woman used to command and to conceal. And yet beneath that discipline, a fear moved—something old, as old as the stories peasants mutter when the wind changes and the hens go quiet before dusk.

“I am only deterred by the compulsory reserve I practise as to my name,” she said, “from making a very singular request of you. My poor child has not quite recovered her strength. Her horse fell with her at a hunt which she had ridden out to witness; her nerves have not yet recovered the shock; and our physician says that she must on no account exert herself for some time to come. We came here, in consequence, by very easy stages—hardly six leagues a day. I must now travel day and night, on a mission of life and death—a mission the critical and momentous nature of which I shall be able to explain to you when we meet, as I hope we shall, in a few weeks, without the necessity of any concealment.”

Then came her petition.

She asked it as if she bestowed rather than begged—a habit of bearing, not of intention; for in the very words she used there was nothing but apology and submission. Would I, she said, consent to take charge of her daughter during her absence?

All things considered, it was strange—audacious, almost. She disarmed me by naming, before I could, every objection that might be urged: the suddenness, the impropriety, the burden she laid on a stranger. She threw herself, she said, upon my honour—upon my chivalry—like a supplicant laying both hands on an altar.

And at that very instant—by a fatality that seems, looking back, to have had a hand in all that followed—my poor child came close to me and, in an undertone, besought me to invite the Countess’s young companion, Millarca, to visit us. She had been speaking with her; she liked her; if her mamma would allow it, she would like it extremely.

There are moments when a household, like a kitchen, is pushed into haste: when the fire runs too high, when the salt is thrown in without tasting, when one does not listen for the simmer but only hears the clatter. I had no time to think. The two ladies pressed me together—mother with her grave urgency, daughter with her silent, wistful beauty. The young lady’s face—refined, engaging, touched with the elegance and quick fire of high birth—overpowered my caution. I submitted. Too easily I undertook the care of the young lady whom her mother called Millarca.

The Countess beckoned her daughter. Millarca listened with grave attention while she was told, in general terms, how suddenly and peremptorily her mother had been summoned, and of the arrangement made for her under my care. The Countess added, with a kind of quiet insistence, that I was one of her earliest and most valued friends.

I said what was expected—those smooth speeches one makes when the room is bright, the air perfumed, and the heart is uneasy. Yet even as I spoke, the sense of my position settled in me like a wrong draught swallowed too fast.

The pale gentleman in black returned and, with elaborate ceremony, conducted the Countess from the room.

His manner—so punctilious, so deferential—persuaded me that the Countess was a lady of far greater importance than her modest title had led me to assume. A great tree may call itself a shrub, but the way lesser growth bends before it betrays the truth.

Her last charge to me was strange: no attempt was to be made to learn more about her than I might have already guessed, until her return. Our distinguished host, whose guest she was, knew her reasons.

“But here,” she said, “neither I nor my daughter could safely remain for more than a day. I removed my mask imprudently for a moment, about an hour ago, and too late I fancied you saw me. So I resolved to seek an opportunity of talking a little to you. Had I found that you had seen me, I would have thrown myself on your high sense of honour to keep my secret some weeks. As it is, I am satisfied you did not see me; but if you now suspect—or on reflection should suspect—who I am, I commit myself in like manner entirely to your honour. My daughter will observe the same secrecy, and I well know you will, from time to time, remind her, lest she should thoughtlessly disclose it.”

She whispered a few words to Millarca, kissed her hurriedly twice, and went away with the gentleman in black, vanishing into the crowd as if the bright company swallowed her like a mouth.

“In the next room,” said Millarca, “there is a window that looks upon the hall door. I should like to see the last of mamma, and to kiss my hand to her.”

We assented, of course, and accompanied her to the window.

Below us the world sounded different: not the polished laughter and music of the saloons, but the hollow talk of wheels on stone, the shuffling of servants, the restless stamping of horses—those domestic, nocturnal noises that make a great house feel like a living thing. An old-fashioned carriage stood ready, handsome and heavy, with couriers and footmen about it. We saw the slim figure of the pale gentleman in black; he held a thick velvet cloak, placed it about her shoulders, and threw the hood over her head with the tenderness of a man dressing an image for a procession.

She nodded to him and just touched his hand with hers.

He bowed—once, twice, again—until the door closed. The carriage began to move, and the sound of it rolling away seemed to carry a portion of the night with it.

“She is gone,” said Millarca, with a sigh.

“She is gone,” I repeated inwardly, and for the first time, in the hurried moments since my consent, I felt the full folly of what I had done—like realizing too late that one has promised to keep a strange recipe without knowing its ingredients.

“She did not look up,” Millarca said, plaintively.

“The Countess had taken off her mask, perhaps, and did not care to show her face,” I answered; “and she could not know that you were at the window.”

She sighed again and looked into my face. She was so beautiful—so delicately formed, so pathetically earnest—that I relented at once. I reproached myself for having, even for a moment, repented my hospitality. I resolved to atone to her for that unavowed churlishness, as one tries to correct a harsh word spoken at table by offering the choicest portion.

Millarca replaced her mask and joined my ward in persuading me back to the grounds, where the concert was soon to be renewed.

We returned, and walked up and down the terrace that lies beneath the castle windows. The night air was thick with trampled grass and crushed flowers; as people passed, perfumes mingled—gloves, pomade, wine-breath, the faint smoke of lamps. Above it all, the music persisted, and I could not help noticing how the very sound of the place seemed to govern the mind: gaiety on the surface, and beneath it a tireless, bright din that left no corner for a quiet thought to live in. It was as if the Grand Duke’s revel demanded not merely our presence but our nerves.

Millarca grew very intimate with us. She amused us with lively descriptions and stories of great people we saw upon the terrace—names spoken with the easy assurance of one bred among them. Her gossip was diverting without being ill-natured, and to me, long retired from the great world, it had the flavour of some spiced preserve opened after years in the pantry: rich, surprising, almost too sweet, yet tempting all the same. I thought what life she would bring to our sometimes lonely evenings at home, how her voice would fill the rooms when the house had gone quiet and the servants’ footsteps were the only sound.

The ball did not end until the morning sun had almost reached the horizon. It pleased the Grand Duke to dance till then; so loyal people could not go away, nor even think of bed.

We had just made our way through a crowded saloon—heat, breath, the rustle and scrape of bodies pressed close—when my ward asked me, suddenly, what had become of Millarca.

I had supposed she was by her side; she fancied Millarca was by mine. The fact was, we had lost her.

I searched. I sent questions along like messages on a windy night, but the answers came back broken. I feared she had mistaken, in the confusion of a momentary separation, other people for her new friends and had pursued them—only to lose them again in the extensive grounds thrown open to us. The gardens outside were dark and sprawling; the laughter and music reached them in thin, misleading threads, and beyond the lanterns’ glow there were wide spaces where a person might vanish as easily as a word in a dead dialect—no one able to repeat it exactly, no one certain what had been said.

Now, in its full force, I recognized a new folly: I had undertaken the charge of a young lady without so much as knowing her name in any form that could be safely uttered. Worse, I was fettered by promises—promises whose reasons I did not know—and could not even point my inquiries by saying she was the daughter of the Countess who had departed a few hours before. The Countess’s secrecy turned, in my hands, into a gag.

Morning broke. The light came, indifferent and clear, and showed us all our fatigue. It was broad day before I gave up searching. It was not until near two o’clock next day that we heard anything of my missing charge.

At about that time a servant knocked at my niece’s door to say he had been earnestly requested by a young lady—who appeared to be in great distress—to make out where she could find the General Baron Spielsdorf and the young lady his daughter, in whose charge she had been left by her mother.

There could be no doubt, despite the slight inaccuracy, that our young friend had turned up.

And so she had. Would to Heaven we had lost her.

She told my poor child a story to account for her long failure to recover us. Very late, she said, she had reached the housekeeper’s bedroom in despair of finding us, and there had fallen into a deep sleep—so deep that, long as it was, it had hardly sufficed to recruit her strength after the ball’s fatigues.

That day Millarca came home with us.

And I—after all—was only too happy to have secured so charming a companion for my dear girl.

There were inconveniences, at first small as grit between the teeth, that grew in time to be impossible to ignore.

In the first place, Millarca complained of a languor that would not lift—an exhaustion that clung to her like damp linen after her late illness—and she did not show herself until the afternoon had crept a long way toward evening. The house adjusted itself to that schedule with the docility of a well-trained kitchen: breakfast cooled and was carried away; the mid-day meal waited, kept back from spoiling by all the small arts of a careful household—soups drawn off the fire and held where they would not sour, bread wrapped to keep its crust, meat covered from flies. Her absence became another domestic routine, like laying the cloth or counting the keys.

But a second circumstance, discovered by chance, pricked my mind with a colder point. Although she always locked her door on the inside, and never disturbed the key from its place until she admitted the maid to assist at her toilet, it was certain that she was sometimes absent from her room very early in the morning, and again at odd hours later in the day—always before she wished it to be understood that she was stirring.

She was seen more than once from the windows of the schloss, in the first faint grey of morning, walking through the trees in an easterly direction, moving with that steadiness that is not wakefulness at all, but a trance. I told myself she walked in her sleep. Yet that explanation, neat as a cook’s knot, did not tie the matter shut. How did she pass out from her room, leaving the door locked on the inside? How did she escape the house without unbarring door or window?

While these puzzles worked in me like a slow souring, another anxiety, sharper and more urgent, presented itself—one that made the very comforts of the table taste wrong.

My dear child—my darling—began to lose her looks and health, and in a way so mysterious, so quietly horrible, that I became thoroughly frightened. First came appalling dreams; then, as she fancied, a spectre—sometimes resembling Millarca, sometimes the indistinct form of a beast—walking round the foot of her bed from side to side, the way a restless animal circles before it lies down. Lastly came sensations. One, not unpleasant but singular, she said resembled the flow of an icy stream against her breast. Later she felt, she said, something like a pair of large needles pierce her a little below the throat, with a very sharp pain. A few nights after that followed a gradual, convulsive sense of strangulation; then unconsciousness.

I heard every word distinctly—each syllable as clear as a knife tapping bone—because by then we were driving upon the short grass that spreads on either side of the road as you approach the roofless village which had shown no chimney-smoke for more than half a century. The carriage wheels, no longer clattering on stone, made a muffled, furtive sound; and in that softened acoustic, the General’s voice carried with a dreadful intimacy, as though the air itself leaned in to listen.

You may imagine how strangely I felt to hear my own symptoms described with such exactness in those suffered by the poor girl who—if catastrophe had not intervened—would at that moment have been a visitor at my father’s château. You may suppose, too, how I felt when he detailed habits and mysterious peculiarities that were, in truth, those of our beautiful guest—Carmilla.

A vista opened in the forest. Suddenly we were beneath the gables and broken chimneys of the ruined village; and above, on a slight eminence, the towers and battlements of the dismantled castle rose and leaned in the mind like a remembered threat. Great trees grouped about it, their heavy boughs overhanging as if the place were kept under a perpetual, listening judgement. Even the birds seemed to sound different there—calls more cautious, as though the canopy hoarded echoes and returned them altered.

In a kind of frightened dream I descended from the carriage. We were silent; each of us had matter enough for thought. We mounted the ascent, and soon were among spacious chambers, winding stairs, and dark corridors, where the air held the stale odour of old stone, wet leaves, and long-extinguished hearths.

“And this was once the palatial residence of the Karnsteins!” the old General said at last, standing at a great window and looking out across the village to the wide undulating expanse of forest beyond. “It was a bad family, and here its bloodstained annals were written. It is hard that they should, after death, continue to plague the human race with their atrocious lusts. That is the chapel of the Karnsteins, down there.”

He pointed through the foliage to grey Gothic walls half hidden on the steep below.

“And I hear the axe of a woodman,” he added—indeed, through the living hush came the measured stroke, wood answering iron with a dull, resilient note. “Busy among the trees that surround it. He may give us the information I seek, and point out the grave of Mircalla, Countess of Karnstein. These rustics preserve the local traditions of great families—whose stories die out among the rich and titled as soon as the families themselves become extinct.”

“We have a portrait, at home, of Mircalla, the Countess Karnstein; should you like to see it?” my father asked.

“Time enough, dear friend,” replied the General. “I believe I have seen the original; and one motive which has led me to you earlier than I at first intended, was to explore the chapel we are now approaching.”

“What! see the Countess Mircalla,” my father exclaimed. “Why, she has been dead more than a century!”

“Not so dead as you fancy, I am told,” answered the General.

My father looked at him—there came over his face, I thought, for a moment, a return of a suspicion I had detected before. Yet although anger and detestation flared at times in the old soldier’s manner, there was nothing flighty in him; his rage had weight, like an iron pot set down too hard.

“There remains to me,” he said, as we passed under the heavy arch of the Gothic church—so large in its ruin that it might justly still be called a church—“but one object that can interest me during the few years left me on earth, and that is to wreak on her the vengeance which, I thank God, may still be accomplished by a mortal arm.”

“What vengeance can you mean?” my father asked, his amazement deepening.

“I mean, to decapitate the monster,” he answered; a fierce flush rose in his aged face, and he stamped so that the sound echoed mournfully through the hollow ruin. His clenched hand lifted as though it already grasped the handle of an axe, and he shook it ferociously in the air.

“What?” my father cried, bewildered more than ever.

“To strike her head off.”

“Cut her head off!”

“Aye—with a hatchet, with a spade, with anything that can cleave through her murderous throat. You shall hear,” he said, trembling with rage. Then, hurrying forward: “That beam will answer for a seat. Your dear child is fatigued; let her sit, and I will—in a few sentences—close my dreadful story.”

A squared block of wood lay on the grass-grown pavement of the chapel; it served as a bench, and I was very glad to take it. The stone underfoot was powdered with moss; the air tasted faintly of mould, like bread kept too long in a damp larder. Somewhere above, in the broken roofline, a draught moved and made a thin, complaining whistle, the ruin’s own small voice.

In the meantime the General called to the woodman who had been removing boughs that leaned upon the old walls. Axe in hand, the hardy old fellow came and stood before us. His clothes smelled of resin and crushed leaf; that clean, green sharpness that follows a newly split log clung about him, an almost wholesome thing in so accursed a place.

He could tell us nothing of the monuments. But there was an old man, he said—a ranger of this forest—presently lodging in the priest’s house about two miles away, who could point out every monument of the old Karnstein family; and, for a trifle, he would fetch him, if we would lend him one of our horses. He could be back in little more than half an hour.

“Have you been long employed about this forest?” my father asked.

“I have been a woodman here,” he answered in his patois, under the forester, “all my days; so has my father before me, and so on, as many generations as I can count. I could show you the very house in the village here in which my ancestors lived.”

His dialect, thick and rooted, held its own stubborn music in the air of the chapel. It was not the polished speech of courts, but the sort of tongue that keeps names alive when documents burn—yet even he spoke of whole houses and lineages as one speaks of recipes no one makes anymore, remembered more by scent than by words.

“How came the village to be deserted?” asked the General.

“It was troubled by revenants, sir,” he said. “Several were tracked to their graves, there detected by the usual tests, and extinguished in the usual way—by decapitation, by the stake, and by burning—but not until many of the villagers were killed.

“But after all these proceedings according to law,” he continued, with a little shrug that might have been weariness or superstition made practical, “so many graves opened, and so many vampires deprived of their horrible animation—the village was not relieved.

“A Moravian nobleman, traveling this way, heard how matters were; and being skilled—as many are in his country—in such affairs, he offered to deliver the village from its tormentor.

“He did so thus. There being a bright moon that night, he ascended, shortly after sunset, the towers of the chapel here, from whence he could distinctly see the churchyard beneath—one can see it from that window. From this point he watched until he saw the vampire come out of his grave, and place near it the linen clothes in which he had been folded, and then glide away toward the village to plague its inhabitants.

“The stranger, having seen all this, came down from the steeple, took the linen wrappings of the vampire, and carried them up to the top of the tower, which he again mounted. When the vampire returned from his prowling and missed his clothes, he cried furiously to the Moravian—whom he saw at the summit of the tower—and the Moravian, in reply, beckoned him to ascend and take them.

“Whereupon the vampire, accepting his invitation, began to climb the steeple; and so soon as he had reached the battlements, the Moravian, with a stroke of his sword, clove his skull in twain, hurling him down to the churchyard. Descending by the winding stairs, the stranger followed, cut his head off, and next day delivered head and body to the villagers, who duly impaled and burnt them.”

The old man’s voice kept an even pace, as if he were reciting the steps of an inherited craft—watch, take, bait, strike, sever, burn—each action passed down like a grim household instruction for keeping an infestation from returning.

“This Moravian nobleman,” the woodman went on, “had authority from the then head of the family to remove the tomb of Mircalla, Countess Karnstein, which he did effectually, so that in a little while its site was quite forgotten.”

“Can you point out where it stood?” asked the General eagerly, his fury tightened now into a hungry focus.

The woodman shook his head and smiled, not unkindly.

“Not a soul living could tell you that now,” he said. “Besides, they say her body was removed; but no one is sure of that either.”

Time pressed. He dropped his axe as if laying aside an argument already settled, and departed to fetch the ranger, leaving us in the chapel’s chill breath, with the dull, persistent hush of the forest outside and the General’s dreadful purpose still unsaid, hanging in the air like smoke that refuses to clear.

“My beloved child,” the General said again, and the words broke as if they had to force themselves through the dryness in his throat, “was then growing rapidly worse.”

He spoke as a man might speak of bread failing in a famine: not as an abstraction, not as a doctor’s phrase, but as the dwindling of something warm and daily that had belonged at the table. The first physician—our village man, whom he had trusted as he trusted the steady seasons—had done everything that prudence prescribed and custom approved, and nothing took hold. The girl sank as grain sinks when cut too early, the stalk still green but the kernel already hollow.

“He saw my alarm,” the General continued, “and suggested a consultation. I sent to Gratz for a man of greater name. Several days passed before he arrived.”

Even the way he said “several days” made them feel like long, stale hours in a sickroom: the candle’s breath, the faint sourness of linens changed too often, the hush that comes when a house is listening for a sound it dreads to hear. At last the physician came—old, composed, with that particular quiet a pious man carries, as though he has learned to keep his voice from disturbing what might be praying nearby.

“They examined her together,” the General said. “Then they withdrew to my library.”

From the room adjoining, where he waited to be called, he heard their voices rise—sharper than any cool, academic dispute had a right to be. It was not only words; it was tone: the quick, brittle crack of one man’s contempt against another man’s certainty. Even in a house so accustomed to domestic noises—the soft strike of a knife on board, the lid set carefully on a pot—this was a kind of clang, a discord that made the air feel bruised.

He could not bear it. He knocked and entered.

“I found,” he said, “the doctor from Gratz maintaining his theory. The other ridiculed it—laughed at him.”

He described, with a disgust that was almost shame, those bursts of laughter—how unfitting they sounded beside a bed where breath was failing. On the General’s entrance the laughter stopped, like a door slammed upon it. The first physician, with a glance half defensive and half malicious, said—

“Sir, my learned brother seems to think you want a conjuror, and not a doctor.”

The man from Gratz did not smile. His mouth tightened, and his eyes looked older than his hair.

“Pardon me,” he said. “I shall state my own view of the case in my own way another time. I grieve, Monsieur le General, that by my skill and science I can be of no use. Before I go, I shall do myself the honour to suggest something to you.”

He sat, drew paper toward him, and began to write. The General—who had come to that consultation as one goes to the pantry in a hard winter, praying to find something that will sustain—made his bow and turned away with that peculiar coldness of disappointment that feels like hunger.

As he turned, he saw the first physician point with his thumb over his shoulder toward the man who wrote, and, with a shrug, touch his own forehead—signing madness.

“This consultation,” the General said, “left me where I was.”

He walked out into the grounds, scarcely knowing the direction his feet chose. The day had ordinary sounds—wind moving through leaves, a distant cart-wheel, a bird’s thin insistence—but he heard them as if they were far off, muffled by walls. He had the sense of living inside a bell-jar of dread, in which every natural noise became a mockery of the life going on without him.

The doctor from Gratz overtook him in ten or fifteen minutes. He had followed, he said, because he could not conscientiously take his leave without more.

“He told me he could not be mistaken,” the General went on. “No natural disease exhibited the same symptoms. Death was already very near. There remained a day—possibly two.”

The words were so exact they seemed like measurements of flour, coldly leveled in a spoon: a day, perhaps two, no more.

“If the fatal seizure were at once arrested,” the old doctor said, “with great care and skill her strength might return. But all hangs on the confines of the irrevocable. One more assault might extinguish the last spark.”

The General clasped his hands as he spoke of it, as if he had tried to hold that spark between his palms.

“And what is the nature of the seizure?” he had entreated.

The answer was not given aloud. The physician placed a folded note in his hand.

“I have stated all fully in this,” he said, “on the distinct condition that you send for the nearest clergyman, and open my letter in his presence—on no account read it till he is with you. You would despise it else; and it is a matter of life and death. Should the priest fail you, then indeed you may read it.”

Before finally going, the doctor asked another thing: whether the General would wish to see a man “curiously learned” upon the very subject which, after reading the letter, would probably interest him above all others. He urged him earnestly to invite that man to visit.

Then he went, leaving the General with a paper that felt heavier than steel.

“The ecclesiastic was absent,” the General said, his voice sinking. “And I read the letter by myself.”

He admitted—almost as if confessing to a weakness—that at another time, in any other case, it would have provoked his ridicule. Yet when every accustomed means has failed, when the familiar remedies—rest, cordial, prayer, the small domestic kindnesses that usually draw a person back—do nothing, the mind will run, starving, into any thicket if it thinks it smells even the faintest fruit.

The contents of the letter were, as he himself called them, monstrous.

“He said,” the General told us, “that the patient was suffering from the visits of a vampire.”

A vampire: the very word sounded, in that moss-dark cemetery air, like an old syllable dug up from a dead dialect, half-forgotten yet still poisonous on the tongue. The doctor wrote of punctures near the throat—those two long, thin, sharp teeth which, he insisted, were peculiar to vampires; and of a small livid mark—“the demon’s lips,” he called it. Every symptom described by the sufferer conformed exactly, he said, with those recorded in every case of similar visitation.

The General did not pretend he had believed. He had been, he said, wholly sceptical. He thought it another instance of learning yoked to a single hallucination. But misery makes a person practical in the strangest way; it lowers the pride of reason as hunger lowers the pride of taste. Rather than do nothing, he did as the letter instructed.

He hid himself in the dark dressing-room that opened upon the patient’s chamber. A candle burned in her room, its flame drawing a thin, wavering gold along the bedposts and leaving the corners thick with shadow. He waited until she slept. He stood at the door and peeped through a narrow crevice. His sword lay on a table beside him, exactly as prescribed—cold metal ready at the hand, an absurd companion to a prayer.

“A little after one,” he said, “I saw a large black object—very ill-defined—crawl over the foot of the bed.”

As he spoke, a faint stirring passed through the leaves above us, and it seemed to me that even the place listened.

The thing moved swiftly, spreading itself up toward the girl’s throat. There it swelled—suddenly, obscenely—into a great palpitating mass, as though some huge leech had found a vein. For a few moments the General stood petrified. Then he sprang forward, sword in hand.

The black creature contracted toward the foot of the bed, slid over it, and came to the floor about a yard below, turning upon him with a glare that was not animal, not human, but something that combined a skulking ferocity with an intelligent horror.

“I saw Millarca,” he said.

He struck at her instantly. Yet when his blade cut the air, she was already near the door, unscathed. He pursued and struck again. In the same instant she was gone—as if the house itself had opened and swallowed her. His sword flew to shivers against the door, the sound of the metal’s breaking like a sharp, final crack in the sickroom’s tense silence.

“I cannot describe to you all that passed,” the General murmured. “The house was up and stirring. The spectre Millarca was gone. But her victim sank fast, and before morning dawned, she died.”

When he ended, he leaned against the chapel wall, shaken by what he had made himself remember. We did not speak. My father, perhaps to give his hands some harmless occupation, wandered a little away and began reading the inscriptions on the tombstones. From one to another he went with the slow, meticulous air of a man who believes that names, dates, and half-erased words can still anchor the mind when the living world begins to behave like a bad dream. He strolled into the side chapel, still intent on his researches, his voice silent but his lips faintly moving as if tasting the old phrases.

The General dried his eyes with a weary gesture and sighed—a heavy, bodily sound that seemed to empty him.

I was relieved then to hear voices approaching: Carmilla’s and Madame’s. Their footsteps, their brief murmurs, promised the return of ordinary companionship, the way the clink of crockery from a kitchen can reassure one that life continues. But the voices died away.

In that sudden solitude—after so strange a story, told among the monuments of the great and titled dead, whose names moldered under ivy and dust, and whose stone angels had lost their noses to time—fear began to creep over me, cold and deliberate. The place felt acoustically wrong: too muffled, too thick. The trees rose high and dense above the noiseless walls, smothering every sound the way heavy drapery smothers music. Even my own breath seemed intrusive, as if the chapel preferred the language of silence.

I thought, with a sinking heart, that perhaps my friends would not enter after all—that they might turn aside and leave me here with that tale still vibrating in my nerves.

The General’s eyes were fixed on the ground. He leaned with one hand upon the base of a shattered monument, as though it steadied him.

Then, under a narrow arched doorway—surmounted by one of those demoniacal grotesques old Gothic carving loves, cynical and ghastly in the half-light—I saw, very gladly, the beautiful face and figure of Carmilla enter the shadowy chapel.

I was about to rise, to speak; I nodded and smiled in answer to her peculiarly engaging smile. But at that instant the old man beside me uttered a cry, snatched up the woodman’s hatchet, and sprang forward.

On seeing him, an instantaneous and brutal change came over Carmilla’s features. It was as if a mask of sweetness had been torn away. Her eyes sharpened; her mouth drew back; and with a crouching step she recoiled, not like a frightened girl, but like a thing that measures distance and escape.

Before I could even scream, the General struck at her with all his force.

She dived under the blow. Unscathed, she caught him—tiny hand closing on his wrist with a grip that was not tiny at all. He struggled for a moment; his hand opened as if compelled; the axe dropped to the ground with a dead, wooden thud.

And the girl was gone.

The General staggered against the wall. His grey hair stood strangely, as if lifted by a sudden storm. Moisture shone on his face, and he looked for a moment as though he stood on the brink of death himself. The whole frightful scene had passed with the speed of a snapped thread.

The first thing I recollect after is Madame standing before me, her impatience sharp enough to cut through my stupor. She repeated again and again, as if by the force of the words she might summon a body out of air:

“Where is Mademoiselle Carmilla? Where is Mademoiselle Carmilla?”

At length I answered—my voice thick, my mind still stumbling behind what my eyes had seen.

“I don’t know—I can’t tell—she went there,” I said, and pointed to the door through which Madame had just entered, “only a minute or two since.”

“But I have been standing there in the passage ever since Mademoiselle Carmilla entered,” Madame said. “And she did not return.”

Then she began calling “Carmilla,” through every door and passage, and from the windows—sending her voice out into the grounds as one sends a signal into fog. No answer came back. The house, the chapel, the air itself swallowed the name without echo, as though the syllables could not find their proper home.

The General turned his head slowly toward me, still trembling.

“She called herself Carmilla?” he asked.

“Carmilla, yes,” I answered.

“Ay,” he said hoarsely. “That is Millarca. That is the same person who long ago was called Mircalla, Countess Karnstein.”

The names—Carmilla, Millarca, Mircalla—fell like coins of different kingdoms that nonetheless bear the same face, and I felt, with a sick certainty, how easily identity could be traded when language itself is a costume.

“Depart from this accursed ground, my poor child,” the General said, gripping his breath between words, “as quickly as you can. Drive to the clergyman’s house, and stay there till we come. Begone! May you never behold Carmilla more; you will not find her here.”

As the General’s last words still trembled in the chapel’s stale air, a figure entered by the same shadowed door through which Carmilla had so often passed—gliding in and out like a thought you cannot keep from returning.

He was the strangest old man I had ever seen: tall and spare to the point of gauntness, narrow-chested, bent as if years had pressed him into that angle, his shoulders rising like two black hills beneath a suit the colour of extinguished coals. His face was sun-browned and dried, seamed with furrows like ploughed fields left too long without rain. A hat of odd fashion sat upon him with a broad leaf-like brim. Long grizzled hair fell to his shoulders, and over his eyes—eyes that never quite met anything directly—rested gold spectacles that caught the dim light and returned it in small, insect-bright flashes.

He moved with a slow, shambling gait, as if listening to some interior metronome. At moments his chin tipped upward, as though he were taking counsel of the roof-beams and broken saints; at others he bowed toward the floor, studying the graves beneath our feet. A perpetual smile lay on his mouth—neither kindly nor cruel, only fixed, as if the muscles had learned it long ago and forgotten how to stop. His thin arms swung loosely, and his hands, lost in old black gloves far too wide, kept tracing and cutting the air in busy, abstract gestures.

“The very man!” cried the General, his voice suddenly alive with relief. He stepped forward as if the chapel were his own drawing-room and took the newcomer by the hand. “My dear Baron—how happy I am to see you. I had no hope of meeting you so soon.”

He signed to my father, who had returned to us, and drew the Baron toward him. Formal introductions were made, and at once the three fell into that earnest, low-voiced conversation in which one hears more in what is withheld than in what is said.

The Baron produced from his pocket a roll of paper and uncurled it upon the worn lid of a tomb. He held a pencil-case delicately, like a cook’s tasting spoon when he is exacting about a sauce, and began to trace lines from point to point. Their eyes moved between his paper and the fabric of the chapel, measuring the building as one measures a household—where each door opens, where each wall might conceal a cupboard, a larder, a secret. Now and then he opened a dirty little book, its yellow leaves crowded with cramped writing, and read aloud a sentence or two in a voice so thin it seemed to whistle through the cracks in his teeth.

As they sauntered down the side aisle opposite where I stood, their steps made the only steady sound: the soft scuff of boots on dust, the occasional tap of a stick. Outside, the world had its own evening noises—the far-off complaint of a bird, the wind worrying the ivy—but here the chapel seemed to swallow every natural sound and keep only what men forced into it. Even the silence had a weight, like a lid pressed down.

They began to measure distances by pacing, counting under their breaths. At last they halted before a portion of the side-wall and examined it with minute care. They pulled away the ivy that clung there, strand by strand, as a careful housekeeper strips away spoiled herbs from a bunch so the good may remain. Their sticks rapped the plaster: dull, dead knocks; then, at a certain point, a sharper answer came back, as if the wall had a hollow behind its skin. They scraped here, tapped there, and I watched their concentration with a sickening sense that the whole chapel was a body being diagnosed.

The woodman returned, and with his help they worked more roughly. Plaster fell in pale crumbs. A broad marble tablet emerged, its surface coldly smooth, letters carved in relief upon it. Beneath grime and the green veining of age, there was an escutcheon, and an inscription, and names that once would have been spoken with ceremony and now returned like a curse remembered.

It was the long-lost monument of Mircalla, Countess Karnstein.

The General—who, I fear, was not often disposed to prayer—raised his hands and eyes to heaven and stood in mute thanksgiving for some moments, as if his body could not contain the surge of triumph and dread together. Then, lowered and hoarse, I heard him say, “Tomorrow. The commissioner will be here, and the Inquisition will be held according to law.”

He turned and seized the Baron warmly by both hands. “Baron, how can I thank you? How can we all thank you? You will have delivered this region from a plague that has scourged its inhabitants for more than a century. The horrible enemy—thank God—is at last tracked.”

My father led them a little apart, out of my hearing. The General followed. I saw them glance, again and again, quickly toward me; each look was like the flick of a knife in lamplight. They spoke, and though I could not catch the words, I felt with helpless certainty that my own story—my shameful weakness, my nightly terror, the marks and languors that had made my bed a place of dread—was being laid out among them as plainly as the Baron’s paper plan lay on the tomb.

When my father returned to me, he kissed me again and again with a tenderness that was half apology and half fear. He took my hand, and his fingers were cold.

“It is time to return,” he said. “But before we go home, we must add to our party the good priest who lives but a little way from this. We must persuade him to accompany us to the schloss.”

We succeeded in that quest. The priest—grave, worn, and smelling faintly of wax and old stone, like a pantry where the bread has been stored too long—came with us. By the time we reached home I was unspeakably fatigued, my limbs heavy as though I had been kneading some dense, unwilling dough for hours and could not feel my hands.

Yet the moment I crossed the threshold my satisfaction turned to dismay.

There were no tidings of Carmilla.

No one offered me any explanation of what had occurred in the ruined chapel; it was clear my father meant, for the present, to keep it from me. But Carmilla’s sinister absence made every fragment of that scene more horrible. The air of the house had changed: even the familiar passages seemed to listen. The sounds that ought to have soothed—servants moving, a distant door closing, the faint rattle of crockery—now arrived muffled, as though some thick cloth had been hung over the world to deaden it, leaving only my own pulse too loud in my ears.

The arrangements for the night were singular. Two servants and Madame were to sit up in my room. In the adjoining dressing-room my father kept watch with the ecclesiastic. The priest performed certain solemn rites whose purport I did not understand. I smelled the acrid sweetness of incense, like resin warmed too close to a flame, and heard the measured cadence of words—Latin, I suppose—rolling on with the inevitability of a mill-wheel, each syllable striking the ear and retreating into darkness. The effect was less comfort than a new kind of unease, for the prayers did not restore the natural sounds of safety; they imposed their own soundscape, stern and vigilant, as if even God must be summoned as a guard against what moved in quiet.

I slept—or rather, drifted in and out of a strained stupor—under the weight of so many waking eyes. Yet in the days that followed, I saw the change plainly: Carmilla’s disappearance was followed by the discontinuance of my nightly sufferings. The little horror that had become my domestic routine—going to bed with dread, waking with that peculiar faintness and the memory of pressure at my throat—ceased as abruptly as if a hand had closed a tap.

In this country you have heard, no doubt, of the appalling superstition that prevails in Upper and Lower Styria, in Moravia, in Silesia, in Turkish Serbia, in Poland, even in Russia: the superstition—so we call it—of the Vampire. It is easy for those who live amid the neat noise of cities, where iron and steam and commerce make a constant din and every old tale is sold back as entertainment, to laugh at such beliefs as though they were the crude produce of rustic fear. But the countryside keeps memories differently. It does not print them; it speaks them. They pass in dialect from elder to child like a starter passed from kitchen to kitchen—alive, souring, strengthening, altered by each household, yet recognizably the same thing. And when the dialect falters, when the old words are replaced by the polite vocabulary of officials and travellers, something more than speech is lost: not only the tale, but the exact flavour of dread that taught people how to survive.

If human testimony—taken with every care and solemnity, judicially, before commissions innumerable, each consisting of many members chosen for integrity and intelligence, with reports more voluminous perhaps than exist upon any other class of cases—is worth anything, it is difficult to deny, or even to doubt, the existence of such a phenomenon. For my part, I have heard no theory by which to explain what I myself witnessed and experienced other than that supplied by the ancient and well-attested belief of the country. There are sensations for which no dominant language has adequate words; they remain untranslatable, lodged in the body—like a taste you cannot describe to anyone who has never eaten the thing itself.

The next day the formal proceedings took place in the Chapel of Karnstein.

The grave of the Countess Mircalla was opened.

The commissioner was present, and the witnesses, and the two medical men—one officially appointed and the other attending on behalf of the promoter of the inquiry. The air in the chapel was altered by their presence: not only more crowded, but more sonically cruel. Tools rang against stone. Orders were spoken and repeated. The scrape of metal on wood, the grunt of labour, the hush that fell suddenly when something resisted—each sound bit sharply, as if the building’s old silence resented being broken and answered with a deeper, colder quiet.

When the coffin was reached and opened, the General and my father each recognized—without hesitation—his perfidious and beautiful guest in the face disclosed to view.

Though a hundred and fifty years had passed since her funeral, her features were tinted with the warmth of life. Her eyes were open. No cadaverous smell exhaled from the coffin—no honest stink of corruption, no release of the earth’s long work. Instead there was only the close, sweetish odour of blood shut up too long, like iron and salt and something faintly animal. The doctors attested the marvellous fact that there was a faint but appreciable respiration, and a corresponding action of the heart. Her limbs were perfectly flexible, her flesh elastic; and the leaden coffin floated with blood, in which—seven inches deep—the body lay immersed.

Here, then, were all the admitted signs and proofs of vampirism.

The body was raised. The prescribed ancient practice was followed with that dreadful domestic thoroughness with which people, in the country, dispatch what threatens the household. There was no flourish, no theatricality—only the method of men who have slaughtered animals and cut wood and dug graves, who know where force must be placed to do its work.

A sharp stake was driven through the heart.

At the moment it entered, the vampire uttered a piercing shriek—such a sound as might escape from a living person in the last agony. It cut through every ear and seemed to strike the bones of the chapel itself, waking old echoes that had slept under stone for generations. For an instant, all the lesser noises—breath, shifting feet, the whisper of cloth—vanished beneath that single cry.

Then the head was struck off, and a torrent of blood flowed from the severed neck.

Afterward they placed the body and head upon a pile of wood and reduced them to ashes. The smoke rose thick and greasy, carrying that same iron-sweet taint, and the crackle of burning seemed almost too cheerful a sound for such work, like a hearth-fire mocked into the service of horror. When all was done, the ashes were thrown upon the river and borne away, and that territory has never since been plagued by the visits of a vampire.

My father possesses a copy of the report of the Imperial Commission, with the signatures of all who were present attached in verification of the statement. It is from this official paper that I have summarized my account of that last shocking scene.

I am expected, I suppose, to set down these last particulars with the neat composure of a housekeeper closing a ledger after market-day. It is not so. The mere act of lifting the pen brings back, as if from some cupboard long shut against damp, the rank, sour breath of that horror; and it has shaken my nerves again, after years in which I had thought the terror put by like out-of-season preserves. I write because you have pressed me to it so earnestly; yet even now, in the quietest hour, a sudden hush in the rooms, or some small sound where no sound should be, will unstring me as completely as on those nights when solitude became a thing with teeth.

Let me add, then, what remains—what I can bear to recall—of that singular Baron Vordenburg, whose quaint, stubborn learning was the cleaver that found the joint in the dark and showed us where the Countess Mircalla lay.

He had established himself at Gratz, not in any comfort, but in a mean lodging, living on the mere heel of what had once been princely possessions in Upper Styria. He had the air of a man who measures his days like rations: a little fire, a little bread, and then hours—endless hours—bent over paper. The room in which he worked was always full of the small domestic noises one expects from poverty: the tinny click of a kettle-lid, the rasp of a chair on bare boards, the dry whisper of pages turned with a finger that had to be careful, because paper—like flesh—takes a mark and keeps it. And behind all those human sounds there was the other presence I have since learned to listen for: the acoustic character of a place, the way silence is not silence at all, but a field in which the least wrong note—an unnatural stillness, a stop in the ordinary breathing of a house—stands out like a shout.

The Baron had at his fingers’ ends the great and little treatises upon vampirism, as a conscientious cook has every receipt and variation in her head: not to show learning, but to know exactly what must be done when the moment comes and there is no time to consult a book. He could name them as readily as one names herbs—“Magia Posthuma,” “Phlegon de Mirabilibus,” “Augustinus de cura pro Mortuis,” “Philosophicae et Christianae Cogitationes de Vampiris,” by John Christofer Herenberg—together with a thousand more, dry as old crusts and yet, in his hands, nourishing in their hideous way. He lent many to my father, and I remember the smell of them: dust, ink, the faint sourness of old paste, and beneath it a cold mineral odour that clung to the thought of vaults and stone.

He had also compiled a vast digest of judicial cases, extracted from them as patiently as one skims and clarifies a stock until the impurities declare themselves and can be lifted away. From these he had drawn a system of principles—some constant, some occasional—governing the vampire’s condition. There was nothing theatrical in his manner; indeed he took pains to strip away the melodramatic fictions that, like showy sauces, conceal the true taste of a thing.

For example: the deadly pallor so often attributed to revenants, he said, is largely invention. They can appear, in the grave and in society, with the deceptive look of health—warm colour, full lips, the very bloom that should belong to living blood. When exposed to light in their coffins, however, they exhibit the dreadful signs by which the long-dead Countess Karnstein’s vampire-life was proved. How they go from coffin to chamber and back again—passing through clay and stone, leaving neither shift of earth nor crease of cerement—has always been admitted utterly inexplicable. Their amphibious existence, he called it: a day divided between the world above and the world below, sustained by a daily return to slumber in the grave, like an obscene kind of rest after feeding. Their vigour, in waking, is supplied by their horrible lust for living blood.

He spoke of that lust with a precision that made it more terrible. The vampire, he said, is prone to a fascination—an engrossing vehemence resembling love—for particular persons. For such an object it will practise patience and stratagem without end, because access may be blocked a hundred ways: doors, prayers, vigilance, a father’s tenderness, a servant’s watchfulness, even the mere habit of a household whose small routines keep rooms occupied and lamps trimmed and beds warmed. It will not desist until it has drained the very life of its coveted victim. Yet in those particular pursuits it will protract its enjoyment with the refinement of an epicure, heightening it by gradual approaches—by the artful courtship of the appetite—until the victim, half stupified by stealth and sweetness, seems to offer something like consent. In ordinary cases, he said, it goes straight—overpowers with violence, strangles, exhausts in a single feast.

There were, too, special conditions. In the instance that concerns me, Mircalla seemed limited to a name which, if not her true one, must at least reproduce—without omission or addition of a single letter—the same set of letters. Carmilla did so; Millarca also.

My father, after Carmilla’s expulsion, kept the Baron with us for two or three weeks. I remember those days as one remembers the long clearing up after a sickness: linen aired, rooms scoured, meals eaten because they must be eaten, though every taste seems dulled. Yet even then, the ordinary life of a house continued; the hearth had to be fed, the bread cut, the broth stirred. It was in the midst of such domestic persistence—cups set out, chairs drawn close, a kettle singing as if it had never sung over any grief—that my father related to the Baron the story of the so-called Moravian nobleman and the vampire in Karnstein churchyard, and then asked him how he had discovered the exact position of the Countess Mircalla’s tomb, so long concealed.

The Baron’s grotesque features puckered into a smile that had in it both secrecy and shame. He looked down, still smiling, and fumbled with his worn spectacle-case—the small, habitual motion of a man who prefers the reassurance of an object to the nakedness of a confession. Then he looked up and said:

“I have many journals and papers written by that remarkable man. The most curious treats of the visit you speak of—to Karnstein. Tradition discolours and distorts, of course. He might be termed a Moravian nobleman: he had removed to that territory, and he was a noble. But he was in truth a native of Upper Styria. It is enough to say that in very early youth he had been a passionate and favoured lover of the beautiful Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. Her early death plunged him into grief that did not pass.

“It is the nature of vampires to increase and multiply, but according to an ascertained and ghostly law. Assume a territory free from that pest: how does it begin, how multiply? I will tell you. A person—more or less wicked—puts an end to himself. A suicide, under certain circumstances, becomes a vampire. That spectre visits living people in their slumbers; they die, and almost invariably develop into vampires in the grave. Thus it happened with the beautiful Mircalla, who was haunted by one of those demons. My ancestor, Vordenburg—whose title I still bear—soon discovered this, and in the course of studies learned more.

“Among other things, he concluded that suspicion would sooner or later fall upon the dead Countess, who in life had been his idol. He conceived a horror—be she what she might—of her remains being profaned by the outrage of a posthumous execution. He has left a paper to prove that the vampire, on its expulsion from its amphibious existence, is projected into a far more horrible life; and he resolved to save his once beloved Mircalla from that.

“He adopted, therefore, the stratagem of a journey here: a pretended removal of her remains, and a real obliteration of her monument. But when age had stolen upon him, and from the vale of years he looked back upon the scenes he was leaving, he considered differently what he had done, and a horror took possession of him. He made the tracings and notes that have guided me to the very spot, and drew up a confession of the deception. If he had intended further action, death prevented him; and the hand of a remote descendant has—too late for many—directed the pursuit to the lair of the beast.”

He spoke of “lair” as one might speak of a pantry where corruption has been found behind the jars: not with flourish, but with disgust sharpened by familiarity.

We talked a little more. Among the other things he said, I recall one with a particular shudder, because it belongs not only to doctrine but to sensation—to the body’s memory, which keeps its own language beyond words.

“One sign of the vampire,” he said, “is the power of the hand. The slender hand of Mircalla closed like a vice of steel on the General’s wrist when he raised the hatchet to strike. But its power is not confined to its grasp; it leaves a numbness in the limb it seizes, which is slowly—if ever—recovered from.”

Even as he said it I felt again, in imagination, that unnatural pressure: not merely strength, but a kind of command, as if the hand communicated a will colder than stone and more intimate than breath.

The following spring my father took me on a tour through Italy. We remained away more than a year. He wished to carry me out of the reach of those rooms, those woods, that ruined church; and perhaps, too, to restore by ordinary beauty what had been poisoned by extraordinary horror. There were markets with heaps of oranges cut and shining like exposed flesh; there were kitchens where garlic and oil hissed together, and the wholesome uproar of life—voices calling, carts rattling, fountains speaking in their own bright dialect—made it harder for dread to find a foothold. And yet I learned then a bitter truth: that terror does not require the same landscape to return. It rides in the mind as a tune one cannot rid oneself of, and sometimes it comes back not as a sound but as the sudden absence of sound—an unnatural lull, as if the world had drawn breath and held it.

It was long before the terror of those recent events subsided. To this hour the image of Carmilla returns to memory with ambiguous alternations—sometimes the playful, languid, beautiful girl, with her soft speech and indolent smiles; sometimes the writhing fiend I saw in the ruined church. And often, waking out of a reverie, I have started as if pricked, fancying I heard again the light step of Carmilla at the drawing-room door: that delicate, measured footfall, as intimate as a whisper along the boards, and more dreadful than any crash—because it belonged so perfectly to the ordinary music of a household, and yet meant the undoing of it.
