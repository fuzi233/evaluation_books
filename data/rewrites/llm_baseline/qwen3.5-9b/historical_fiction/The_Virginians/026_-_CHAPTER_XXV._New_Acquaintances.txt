Cousin Maria made her entrance, flanked by two gardeners' boys carrying baskets brimming with fresh blooms, intended to adorn Madame de Bernstein's drawing-room for the anticipated arrival of her guests. Three footmen, resplendent in liveries gorgeously laced with worsted, arranged double the number of card tables. Following them was a major-domo dressed in black and white, sporting fine lace ruffles and carrying a bag; he moved with the air of a man who ought to bear a sword at his hip. He trailed behind the lackeys carrying bundles of wax candles, placing a pair upon each card table and setting others in the silver sconces mounted on the wainscoting. Sunlight streamed through the windows, gilding the walls and casting slanting rays across the scene, mirroring the view outside: the green common dotted with rocks, tree clusters, and distant houses twinkling in the warmth.

Groups of figures in hoops, powdered wigs, and brocade sauntered across the lawn, dappling the grass with their shadows. Beyond the Baroness's windows lay the Pantiles, where a perpetual fair was in full swing, filled with the clamor and buzz of society. A band played music specifically for the benefit of the visitors to the spa town. While Madame Bernstein's main sitting-room might seem ill-suited for a recluse or a scholar, it was perfect for those who preferred bustle, gaiety, bright cross-lighting, and a panoramic view of the cheerful, busy place. Once the windows were lit, passersby below would know her ladyship was hosting a card assembly, accessible to anyone with an introduction.

It bears mentioning that the nightlife of society a century prior was considerably darker than today. There was scarcely one wax candle for every ten we now see in a modern drawing-room, let alone gas or electric illumination. Horrible, guttering tallow smoke and odor permeated the corridors. The candle-snuffer was a notorious figure in theaters. Recall Hogarth's paintings: how dim they are, how begrimed with tallow drippings! In "Marriage à la Mode," within Lord Viscount Squanderfield's grand saloons, where he and his wife yawn listlessly before a horrified steward after their party, there are merely eight candles—one per card table and half a dozen in a brass chandelier. If Jack Briefless had invited friends to oysters and beer in his chambers at Pump Court, he would have used twice as many. Let us find comfort in knowing that even Louis XIV, in all his glory, held his revels in the dark, and thank Mr. Price and other luminous benefactors for banishing the abominable gloom of our ancestors.

Maria, the fairest flower herself, diligently arranged her roses, sweet-williams, and other blossoms into vases scattered throughout the apartment, decorating the space to the best of her ability. She lingered fondly over one bowl or another dragon-shaped jar, casting only sly, timid glances toward young cousin Harry. His own blush would have suited any young woman, and one might have suspected she intended to stay longer than her aunt allowed. However, the Baroness, seated comfortably in her armchair with a crooked tortoiseshell stick in hand, imperiously directed the servants to their duties. She scolded Tom for a darn in his stocking and John for greasing his locks excessively from the candle box, maintaining a stern dominion over the household.

Another observation regarding the humble conditions of a hundred years ago: James slept two to a bed, four to a room—often a cellar—and washed in a trough rarely seen in London outside the barracks of Her Majesty's Foot Guards. If Maria hoped for a private moment, her fond heart was soon disappointed.

"Where are you going to dine, Harry?" asked Madame de Bernstein.

"My niece Maria and I shall have a chicken in the little parlour," she replied. "I think you should go to the best ordinary. There is one at the White Horse in a few moments; you'll hear his bell shortly. Understand, sir, you ought not to spare expense, but behave like the son of Princess Pocahontas. Your trunks have been transferred to the lodging I secured for you. It is not becoming for a lad to constantly hover around the aprons of two elderly women, is it, Maria?"

"No," said her ladyship, lowering her meek eyes, while the other lady glared in triumph.

"I believe Andromeda has been significantly exposed to the Dragon during the last five or six days," the narrator muses. "If Perseus had severed the latter's cruel head, he would have committed no unjustifiable murder. Yet, he drew neither sword nor shield; he merely looked mechanically at the lackeys in tawny and blue as they creaked about the room."

"There are excellent mercers and tailors from London always here to serve the company at the Wells," the lady continued. "You had better visit them, my dear, for your suit lacks the very latest fashion—a bit of lace, perhaps—"

"I cannot leave mourning, ma'am," the young man replied, looking down at his sable garments.

"Ho, sir!" cried the lady, rising abruptly on her cane. "Wear black for your brother until you are as old as Methuselah, if you wish. I am certain I do not wish to prevent you. I simply want you to dress properly, act like others, and make a figure worthy of your name."

"Madam," said Mr. Warrington with great formality, "I have done nothing to disgrace it that I know."

Why did the old woman pause and start as if struck? Let bygones be bygones. They had shared numerous brief exchanges where swords were metaphorically crossed and thrusts rapidly parried. She liked Harry no less for his courage in facing her.

"Surely a finer linen shirt than the one you wear would not be a disgrace to you, sir," she said with a forced laugh.

Harry bowed and blushed. This was one of the homely gifts of his Oakhurst friends. He felt strangely pleased to wear it, thinking of his new acquaintances—who were so good, pure, simple, and kindly—with immense tenderness. Clad in this garment, he felt as though evil could not touch him. He stated he would return to his lodging and ensure he appeared in the best linen available.

"Come back here, sir," commanded Madame Bernstein. "If our company has not yet arrived, Maria and I will find some ruffles for you!"

Guided by a footman, the young man departed for his new quarters. Harry discovered not only handsome, spacious apartments but also a groom standing ready to be hired, along with a second valet should he choose to employ one to attend to Mr. Gumbo. Before many minutes had passed, emissaries from a London tailor and bootmaker arrived with cards and compliments from their employers, Messrs. Regnier and Tull. Gumbo laid out the finest articles from Harry's modest wardrobe alongside the exquisite linens provided by his thrifty Virginian mother.

Visions of his snow-covered homeland arose: the crackling logs, the trim, quiet ladies working by the fire. For the first time, a fleeting thought crossed his mind that his homely clothes were not quite smart enough, that the home-worked linen lacked fineness. Could he feel shame for anything belonging to him or to Castlewood? It seemed strange. The simple folk there were entirely content with everything done, said, or produced at Castlewood; and Madam Esmond, when sending her son forth on his travels, believed no young nobleman needed superior provisions. The clothes might have fit better or been of a later fashion, certainly—but still, the young fellow presented a comely figure once his toilette was complete. Gumbo hailed a chair, marching beside it until they reached the ordinary where Harry was to dine.

Here, he expected to find the beau he had met earlier at his aunt's lodging, who had indicated the White Horse as the most fashionable dining spot. He mentioned his friend's name to the host, but the landlord and waiters, greeting him with many smiles and bows, assured his honor that no further introduction was needed. They helped him hang his coat and sword, offered choices between Burgundy, Pontac, or champagne, and guided him to a table. Although the most fashionable ordinary in the village, the White Horse was not crowded that day. Monsieur Barbeau, the landlord, informed Harry that a great entertainment at Summer Hill had drawn away most of the company. Indeed, upon entering, only four other gentlemen remained. Two were finishing wine after their meals; the other two were mid-course, to whom the landlord must have whispered the newcomer's name, for they looked at him with apparent interest and offered a slight bow as the smiling host bustled away to serve Harry.

Mr. Warrington returned the salutes of the two gentlemen, who welcomed him to Tunbridge and expressed hope he would enjoy the place upon closer acquaintance. Then, they exchanged waggish glances with each other, meanings unclear to Harry, while casting knowing looks at the two other guests over their wine. One wore a somewhat tarnished velvet coat with a massive queue, bag, voluminous ruffles, and embroidery. The other was a little, beetle-browed, hook-nosed, high-shouldered gentleman whom his companion addressed as "milord" or "my lord" in a very high voice.

"And so you know the nephew of the old woman—the Croesus who is arriving?" asked the little Baron.

"You're thrown out there, Jack!" said one young gentleman to the other.
