As Tito stepped into the elegant pavilion, his sharp eyes quickly took in the gathering—its guests, their manner, the air of quiet purpose that hung over them like a veil. He had guessed rightly: this was no mere social affair. Though the evening wore the guise of conviviality, its true aim lay deeper—in the strengthening of political bonds through shared wine and conversation. In Florence, where moderation was prized, such gatherings were rare but potent. Good food, fine wine, and the warmth of fellowship were believed to sharpen the mind’s grasp of principle, especially when the spirits were high and reason softened by pleasure. The Florentines valued thrift, yet even among them, indulgence had its devotees—those who bowed to Madonna della Gozzoviglia, goddess of feasting, and San Buonvino, patron of good cheer. The Rucellai family stood apart, known for their lavish hospitality and refined taste. Tonight, they hosted not just a dinner, but a subtle meeting of minds and ambitions.

There would be no lofty discourse on philosophy here—not tonight. Yet there was no need to remove the bust of Plato from the wall, nor erase the frescoes of the cardinal virtues. These symbols had long watched over more intellectual banquets. This very bust had once presided over the Platonic Academy, brought here after Lorenzo de’ Medici’s death, when the philosophers migrated from his villa to these gardens. On each November 13th—the anniversary of Plato’s passing—a select group of scholars would gather beneath laurel leaves, dining with restraint and debating the master’s teachings with fervor. Among them had been Pico della Mirandola, once a brilliant, reckless youth whose bold theses startled Rome, later transformed into a man seeking inner peace; Marsilio Ficino, nurtured from youth on Platonism until his mind seemed almost too saturated with it; Angelo Poliziano, poet and scholar alike, whose words still stirred readers centuries later; and Leon Battista Alberti, a towering intellect—artist, scientist, inventor, thinker—whose breadth dwarfed even the most gifted of his peers. Others, less famed but no less vital, had shaped the intellectual currents of their age, leaving unseen legacies as essential as the laborers who tilled the land before us.

Bernardo Rucellai himself would have earned a place in that circle long before he became its host. Still in his prime, barely forty-four, he carried himself with quiet dignity, a man of refined Latin speech so pure that Erasmus once noted he could not be tricked into speaking it—even under pressure. He greeted Tito with unusual warmth, placing him between Lorenzo Tornabuoni and Giannozzo Pucci, both young men of the Medicean faction, accomplished and discreet.

At first, the talk remained light. As the brass bowl of scented water passed around, fingers adorned with rings gleamed under wax-lit candles, and the scent of fresh white damask—imported from France—drifted through the room. Someone mused aloud: what would Dante’s old Florentine, stiff in leather belt and bone clasp, think if he could see silver forks at table? All agreed that the customs of later generations would astonish their ancestors—if only those ancestors could witness them.

While the forks danced lightly over appetizers—tiny morsels of liver cooked to melt upon the tongue—conversation turned to the artistry of the table. The enamel designs on the brass service drew admiration, and someone remarked again on the silver dish for confetti, a masterpiece by Antonio Pollajuolo, whom popes had lured away from Florence to Rome, seduced by greater splendor.

“I remember,” said Niccolò Ridolfi, a man of middle years with an air of effortless ease born of lifelong status, “Antonio growling about his metalwork. He took up painting in a rage, saying, ‘The artist who puts his work into gold and silver puts his brains into the melting-pot.’”

“Not unlikely,” replied Giannozzo Pucci. “If this war with Pisa continues, and unrest spreads to our other towns, it won’t just be our silver dishes that vanish. I fear even Antonio’s silver saints at San Giovanni may one day disappear—melted down into coin.”

“The Frate is already preparing us for that,” said Tornabuoni. “He tells the people God will not accept silver crucifixes while stomachs starve. The church, he says, is best adorned with the gems of holiness and the fine gold of brotherly love.”

“A useful doctrine for funding war,” Bernardo Rucellai observed dryly. “But politics come after the confetti, Lorenzo—when we’ve drunk enough wine to wash them down. They’re too solid for roast and boiled.”

“Indeed,” Ridolfi added. “Luigi Pulci would have said this delicate kid must be eaten with an impartial mind. Once, at Careggi, when Luigi was in full flow, he insisted nothing corrupted the palate like opinion. ‘Opinion,’ he declared, ‘corrupts the saliva—that’s why men took to pepper. Scepticism is the only philosophy that doesn’t bring a taste in the mouth.’ ‘Nay,’ poor Lorenzo de’ Medici replied, ‘you’re wrong. Look at Matteo Franco—untainted sceptic—who wants hotter sauce than any of us.’ ‘Because he has a strong opinion of himself,’ Luigi shot back. That’s the original egg of all opinion. _He_ a sceptic? He believes in the immortality of his own verses. He’s like that friar who described the pavement of the bottomless pit. Poor Luigi—his mind was steel, sharp enough to cut anything it touched.”

“And yet gentle-hearted,” Pucci said. “His talk seemed like soap-bubbles. He raved about food and drink—but lived as temperately as a butterfly.”

The feast lingered. After meats came capon and game, then the crowning glory: a peacock, prepared according to Apicius’s recipe—feathers intact, arranged to resemble a living bird at rest. Only the skilled carver dared turn it, exposing the plucked breast to serve delicate slices. Few chose it. Tito quoted Horace and scattered his portion across the plate. Rucellai offered a learned note on ancient peacock eggs but declined to eat. Ridolfi held a piece on his fork, telling a story of a Sienese man who, wanting to impress without expense, bought a wild goose, severed its beak and feet, boiled it in its feathers, and served it as a pea-hen.

In truth, little peacock was eaten. But the spectacle mattered—proof of wealth, of taste, of belonging to a world beyond reach for most. To speak ill of such luxuries now would be folly, especially when Fra Girolamo preached that the rich should not live opulently for the sake of the poor.

Outside, in the cold dark, another man walked in slow circles. Baldassarre, disowned and alone, paused among the trees, watching through the windows. He saw Tito laughing, gesturing, heard his voice rise and fall in the warm chatter. His heart burned with ambition—this moment, this night, was his chance to break into that world. And he took grim pleasure in seeing Tito so carefree, unaware of the trap being set.

Inside, the light masked everything. The glow of candles and laughter hid the pale face in the shadows. Light, like darkness, could be a curtain.

As the meal advanced, the talk grew sharper. The sense of civic duty pressed heavily on all. Fra Girolamo’s sermons, his influence, dominated the thoughts of every guest. Before the stewed fish was removed, and sweets still awaited, the friar’s name surfaced—despite Rucellai’s earlier warning. Politics crept back in.

At first, it was gossip: how the Great Council vote had gone, how hot-tempered Francesco Valori had been, how Soderini’s speeches echoed the friar’s sermons—both, it seemed, fed from the same source.

“My view,” Ridolfi said, “is that the Frate has a longer head for public affairs than Soderini or any Piagnone. Soderini is merely his mouthpiece.”

“No,” Rucellai countered, “the Frate is clever, yes—but Soderini is no fool. He’s studied Venice’s councils. He won’t borrow ideas from a monk. He loads the cannon; the Frate brings the powder and lights the match. The people are growing stronger—and they’re beginning to rule us. Ecco!”

When servants left, wine flowed freely. Then Tornabuoni spoke: “Whether Soderini owes the Frate or not, we owe him the amnesty. We might have done without moral reform voted by black beans—but the fact that our heads remain on our shoulders, and our property spared, is a gift we welcome. And I believe only the Frate’s power could have secured that. I’ve spoken with him.”

A murmur rose. Rucellai simply nodded—no surprise, no comment.

“Yes,” Tornabuoni continued, “I visited him in his cell—an uncommon favor. Even Valori rarely sees him alone. But perhaps he welcomed me because I wasn’t already his follower—I had to be converted. And now I see clearly: the safest path for us Mediceans is to support the Frate’s party. We’re too weak to stand alone. If the Frate falls, who will rise? Nerli, Alberti, Pazzi—the Arrabbiati—men who’d tear us apart like mad dogs, not content until half of us are banished.”

Agreement rippled through the table.

“A wise dissimulation,” Tornabuoni went on, “is the only course for rational men in times of turmoil. I needn’t say my real loyalties here—many share them. But beyond personal ties, I agree with my friends: the only lasting peace for Florence is the dominance of one family. The Frate’s dream—a popular government where no party exists—is fit for some remote island found by Cristoforo Colombo, not for quarrelsome Florence. Change is coming. With patience, we can shape it. For now, let the Frate’s flag fly. If another rises, it will be black for us.”

“It’s true,” Ridolfi said bluntly. “You’re right, Lorenzo. I’m too old to pretend I’ve changed. Some of us—like Bernardo del Nero—would never take another’s shield. But we can lie still, like old dogs. Barking won’t help now. As for these psalm-singers, who vote only for God’s glory and pretend we can all love each other—well, their time won’t last. There are only two kinds of government: one where men show teeth, one where they lick the strongest. They’ll get their Great Council tomorrow—certain. They’ll think they’ve invented something new. But as sure as every lucco in the Council has human skin, their plan will end in snarling or licking. That’s my view. Not that I think noble families should chase reasons like law doctors. I’ll stay true to my alliances. No chalk-mark tells me which reason is true. But Bernardo here—Rucellai—he’s a man of reasons. I’ll listen to fine arguments, so long as they don’t interfere with my duties.”

“If you appeal to me, Niccolò,” Rucellai replied with formal dignity, “I must say: while I am shaped by long-standing ties, I cannot commit to schemes I do not control. I might accept a return to the old order—with changes. But I fully agree with Lorenzo: our best policy is to align with the popular party. I make no pretense. I do not hide my doubts. Of the twenty men I work with, I disagree with every one. No single scheme commands my full assent.”

Ridolfi shrugged. The conversation shifted.

As wine deepened, voices grew bolder. The company split into smaller groups. Tornabuoni and Pucci, aware of this, soon moved toward Tito, seated between them, drawing chairs away from the table.

“In truth, Melema,” Tornabuoni said, crossing one leg over the other, “I know no man in Florence better suited to serve our cause. You see what others are—men who cannot conceal their bias, like dogs that bark in their natural tone. Or those whose loyalty is obvious, and thus suspect. Giannozzo and I—we can overcome suspicion. We have finesse, discretion. But you—except you—there is no one else we can trust.”

“Yes,” Pucci said, laying a hand on Tito’s shoulder, “you could help us more than Ulysses himself. I believe Ulysses often made enemies. To manage men, you need a sharp mind wrapped in velvet. And no one in Florence could undertake a journey to Rome with the safety you possess. Your scholarship gives cover. But your talent—your charm—outshines even that. Macchiavelli might have served us, but not as well. He’s too tied to ideas, lacks your grace. He’s lost a great opportunity. You’ve gained it.”

Tornabuoni lowered his voice. “Play your part well, Melema, and the future is yours. The Medici will keep a foothold in Rome as well as Florence. Soon, they may offer even greater rewards than before. Why not take holy orders? A cardinal’s hat awaits. You wouldn’t be the first Greek to wear it.”

Tito laughed. He understood the flattery, but enjoyed it.

“My joints aren’t stiff enough,” he said, “to run for such a prize. A few abbeys held *in commendam*, without tonsure—now that would satisfy me.”

“I was serious,” Tornabuoni said calmly. “A scholar benefits from ordination. But we’ll speak of that later. First: you must win the confidence of those around San Marco. Giannozzo and I will work on that—but you can go farther, since you’re less noticed. You’ll learn their plans, become a screen for our actions. Nothing can be done before your trip to Rome—Piero’s business with the French nobles must happen now. When you return… well, no need to say more. You could become the favorite of San Marco, if you wished. But you know: effective deception is never excessive.”

“If it weren’t for your need to appear loyal to the popular side,” Pucci said gently, “I’d wish your talents used elsewhere—where they’d shine even brighter. But now, we must find someone to infiltrate the Arrabbiati. We need to know their moves more than the Frate’s, who act openly. Still, it would have been hard for you—given your past ties to the Medici, your wife’s connection to Bernardo del Nero. We need someone unconnected, neutral.”

Tito pushed his hair back, his lips curling slightly.

“No need to look elsewhere,” he said. “I can handle it all. I’ll make myself Dolfo Spini’s closest confidant—know his plans before he does.”

He rarely spoke so confidently. But now, a new path opened—broader, more powerful than he’d imagined. Success had once meant favor; now it meant power—power available to the untethered, the unbound. Each party that saw him as a tool might soon depend on him. His alien status, his lack of belief, became advantages. He felt anew the thrill of skill in play. And the motives that might have made him recoil from deceit—loyalty, conscience—had long since been worn thin by the lies of his life.
