"Here we go up, up, up;
And here we go down, down, downee!"

The air above Milton was thick with smoke, a gray shroud rising from chimneys that belched their burden into a sky already heavy with the promise of winter. Below, the factory floor was a cacophony of ceaseless roar, mighty beat, and dazzling whirr. Wood, iron, and steam labored in an endless, senseless cycle, driven by a persistence that rivaled the tireless endurance of the crowds moving through the streets. Yet, unlike the machinery, the people possessed sense and purpose, though their destination remained a frantic, unanswered question. There were no loiterers, no strollers seeking mere pleasure; every face was etched with lines of eagerness or anxiety. In the Mart and the Exchange, men jostled one another aside with the deep, selfish desperation of competitors fighting for survival.

A gloom hung over the town, heavier than the fog. Trade had stalled; buyers were few, and those who ventured to purchase were viewed with suspicion by sellers who knew that credit was a fragile thing. The stability of even the most established houses was threatened by the sweeping failures in the great neighboring port, where shipping houses had crumbled like sandcastles. While Milton had hitherto known no such disaster, the shadow of American speculation had fallen heavily upon it. Whispers circulated with fierce avidity: "What news? Who is gone? How will it affect me?" Men spoke in hushed tones, dwelling cautiously on the names of those who seemed safe, terrified that idle breath might drag a neighbor down into the abyss. "Thornton is safe," they murmured, praising his vast business and his prudent daring. But behind their backs, others leaned in closer, whispering the darker truth: "Thornton's business is large, but he has spent his profits extending it. He holds no capital laid by. His machinery is new, costing him a fortune we dare not speak aloud."

Mr. Harrison, a man who had inherited his father's trade and feared change, grumbled these criticisms, grudging every penny earned by the more daring souls around him. Yet the truth was that Mr. Thornton was hard pressed, and he felt the pressure acutely in his most vulnerable point: his pride. He had built his commercial character from the ground up, believing that commerce offered a unique path for the brave and honest to rise above the ordinary. He dreamed of a name known in the East and West, of a word that passed like gold across distant seas. His mother had once read to him, "Her merchants be like princes," a trumpet call that had invited him into this struggle. He had sought the influence of a global firm, a legacy for generations, and it had taken him long, silent years to reach even a glimmer of that vision, only to find himself now trapped in his own town, among the very people he had once kept at a distance.

For years, he and his workers had lived parallel lives—close, yet never touching—until the accident of his acquaintance with Higgins. That meeting, shifting the dynamic from master and workman to man and man, had revealed a shared humanity. But now, the fear of losing contact with the few workmen he had come to know as individuals gnawed at him. The thought of his experimental plans being nipped off without trial added a new poignancy to his anxiety. He realized too late how deeply he had invested in his role as a manufacturer, not just for profit, but for the rare opportunity to exercise power and influence among a race of people full of character and strong feeling.

He reviewed his history. The strike a year and a half prior, occurring in an untimely winter, had prevented him from fulfilling large orders. He had locked his capital into expensive new machinery and purchased vast quantities of cotton, only to find the Irish hands he imported lacked the necessary skill, damaging the very quality his house prided itself upon. The embarrassment of that period had festered, and often, when his gaze fell on Higgins, he felt a surge of angry resentment. But he curbed it, resolving to prove his mastery over his own temper by allowing Higgins access whenever business permitted. Slowly, the resentment faded, replaced by a wonder at how two men living the same trade could view their duties so differently. This newfound understanding fostered a charity between them, revealing facts previously hidden from one side or the other.

But now, the market had turned. The value of his large stocks had plummeted to nearly half. Orders ceased, interest on his capital vanished, and payment for completed goods became difficult to secure. Bills for cotton piled up, forcing him to borrow at exorbitant rates while remaining unable to realize any of his property. Yet, he did not despair. He worked day and night, foreseeing emergencies with a calm gentleness that surprised even his own household. To the women in his home, he was as tender as ever; to the workmen, he spoke little, but his demeanor had shifted. Where once there was smoldering antagonism, there was now sympathy for the visible weight pressing upon him.

"Higgins," the overseer noted one day, hearing Mr. Thornton's sharp inquiry followed by a suppressed sigh, "I reckon I know who'd ha' been sorry to see our measter sitting so like a piece o' grey calico! The old parson would ha' fretted his woman's heart out if he'd seen the woeful looks on our measter's face." Higgins and another man stayed over hours that night, unseen, to finish neglected work, ensuring the overseer received the credit.

Later, as Higgins approached Mr. Thornton in Marlborough Street, he watched his employer walk with a quick, resolved stride, his face frozen in a wintry frost of care.

"Measter," Higgins called, stopping him. The interruption caused Mr. Thornton to start, his eyes clearing as if a soft summer gale had blown away the anxiety.

"Have ye heard aught of Miss Marget lately?"

"Miss—who?" Mr. Thornton asked, momentarily confused.

"Miss Marget—Miss Hale—the old parson's daughter. Ye know who I mean, if ye'll only think a bit."

"Oh yes!" Suddenly, the grim look vanished from Mr. Thornton's face. Though his mouth remained compressed, his eyes smiled benignly. "She's my landlord now, you know, Higgins. I hear of her through her agent. She's well and among friends—thank you, Higgins." The "thank you" lingered, warm and laden with feeling, sending a signal to the astute Higgins.

"And she's not gotten married, measter?"

"Not yet." The clouds returned to Mr. Thornton's face. "There is some talk of it, with a connection of the family."

"Then she'll not be for coming to Milton again, I reckon."

"No!"

"Stop a minute, measter." Higgins moved closer, confidential. "Is the young gentleman cleared?" He winked, adding mystery to his words. "The young gentleman, I mean—Master Frederick, they called him—her brother who was over here."

"Over here."

"Ay, to be sure, at the missus's death. Ye need na be feared of my telling; for Mary and me, we knew it all along, only we held our peace."

"And he was over. It was her brother."

"Sure enough. I reckoned ye knew, or I'd never ha' let on. Ye knew she had a brother?"

"Yes, I know all about him. And he was over at Mrs. Hale's death?"

"Nay! I'm not going to tell more. I've maybe gotten them into mischief already. I nobbut wanted to know if they'd gotten him cleared?"

"Not that I know of. I know nothing. I only hear of Miss Hale, now, as my landlord, and through her lawyer."

Mr. Thornton broke away, leaving Higgins baffled. Alone, he reflected, "It was her brother. I am glad. I may never see her again; but it is a comfort—a relief—to know that much. I knew she could not be unmaidenly; and yet I yearned for conviction. Now I am glad!"

It was a golden thread running through the dark web of his fortunes, which grew ever gloomier. His agent had trusted a house in the American trade that collapsed alongside others, triggering a chain reaction of failures. Could he stand?

Night after night, Mr. Thornton took books and papers into his private room, staying up long after his family had retired. One morning, as daylight stole through the crevices of his shutters, he sat in hopeless indifference, thinking he could do without the rest he desperately needed. The door opened, and his mother stood there, dressed as she had been the day before. She had not slept either. Their eyes met, cold and rigid, wan from long watching.

"Mother! Why are you not in bed?"

"Son John," she said, "do you think I can sleep with an easy mind while you keep awake full of care? You have not told me what your trouble is; but sore trouble you have had these many days past."

"Trade is bad."

"And you dread—"

"I dread nothing," he replied, drawing up his head erect. "I know that no man will suffer by me. That was my anxiety."

"But how do you stand? Shall you—will it be a failure?" Her steady voice trembled.

"Not a failure. I must give up business, but I pay all men. I might redeem myself—I am sorely tempted—"

"How? Oh, John! Keep up your name—try all risks for that. How redeem it?"

"By a speculation offered to me, but full of risk. If successful, it places me high above water-mark, so that no one need ever know the strait I am in. Still, if it fails—"

"And if it fails," she advanced, laying her hand on his arm, her eyes full of eager light. She held her breath.

"Honest men are ruined by a rogue," he said gloomily. "As I stand now, my creditors' money is safe—every farthing of it; but I don't know where to find my own—it may be all gone, and I penniless at this moment. Therefore, it is my creditors' money that I should risk."

"But if it succeeded, they need never know. Is it so desperate a speculation? I am sure it is not, or you would never have thought of it. If it succeeded—"

"I should be a rich man, and my peace of conscience would be gone!"

"Why! You would have injured no one."

"No; but I should have run the risk of ruining many for my own paltry aggrandisement. Mother, I have decided! You won't much grieve over our leaving this house, shall you, dear mother?"

"No! but to have you other than what you are will break my heart. What can you do?"

"Be always the same John Thornton in whatever circumstances; endeavouring to do right, and making great blunders; and then trying to be brave in setting to afresh. But it is hard, mother. I have worked and planned. I have discovered new powers in my situation too late—and now all is over. I am too old to begin again with the same heart. It is hard, mother."

He turned away, covering his face with his hands.

"I can't think," she said with gloomy defiance, "how it comes about. Here is my boy—good son, just man, tender heart—and he fails in all he sets his mind upon: he finds a woman to love, and she cares no more for his affection than if he had been a common man; he labours, and his labour comes to nought. Other people grow rich, and hold their paltry names high and dry above shame."

"Shame never touched me," he said in a low tone.

"I sometimes have wondered where justice was gone to, and now I don't believe there is such a thing in the world—now you are come to this; you, my own John Thornton, though you and I may be beggars together—my own dear son!"

She fell upon his neck, kissing him through her tears.

"Mother!" he said, holding her gently, "who has sent me my lot in life, both of good and evil?"

She shook her head, refusing to speak of religion in that moment.

"Mother," he continued, seeing her silence. "I, too, have been rebellious; but I'm striving to be so no longer. Help me, as you helped me when I was a child. Then you said many good words—when my father died, and we were sometimes sorely short of comforts—which we shall never be now; you said brave, noble, trustful words then, mother, which I have never forgotten, though they may have lain dormant. Speak to me again in the old way, mother. Do not let us have to think that the world has too much hardened our hearts. If you would say the old good words, it would make me feel something of the pious simplicity of my childhood. I say them to myself, but they would come differently from you, remembering all the cares and trials you have had to bear."

"I have had a many," she sobbed, "but none so sore as this. To see you cast down from your rightful place! I could say it for myself, John, but not for you. Not for you! God has seen fit to be very hard on you, very."

She shook with convulsive sobs. The silence around her finally struck her, and she quieted herself to listen. No sound. She looked. Her son sat by the table, arms thrown half across it, head bent face downwards.

"Oh, John!" she said, lifting his face up. A strange, pallid look of gloom was on it, striking her for a moment as the forerunner of death. But as the rigidity melted and natural color returned, she saw he was himself once again. All worldly mortification sank to nothing before the consciousness of the great blessing that his simple existence was to her. She thanked God for this, with a fervor that swept away all rebellious feelings.

He did not speak readily, but he went and opened the shutters, letting the ruddy light of dawn flood the room.
