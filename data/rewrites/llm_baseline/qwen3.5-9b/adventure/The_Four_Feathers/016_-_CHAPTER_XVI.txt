Ethne had completely erased Colonel Durrance from her thoughts. Since the moment Captain Willoughby placed the soiled, yellowed feather—once pure white—into her hand, her entire world had narrowed to focus solely on Harry Feversham. She had guided Willoughby into the secluded enclosure, and his tale had consumed her, filling the gaps in his narrative with vivid recollections of Harry's gestures, voice, and gaze. In doing so, she had been swept away from that August garden of sunlight and vibrant blooms. The five grueling years she had endured, holding her head high and masking her sorrow with a brave smile, vanished from her immediate consciousness. Perhaps she never truly understood how exhausting those years had been until she lifted her head and spotted Durrance standing at the hedge's entrance.

"Hush!" she whispered to Willoughby. Her face drained of color, and her eyes squeezed shut for a fleeting second against a sharp pang of agony. Yet, there was no room for lingering emotion. Their brief conversation with Willoughby had been a respite, but the holiday was over. She must immediately resume the heavy responsibilities those five years had imposed. If she could not achieve the impossible task of forgetting—and she now knew with certainty that she never would—she must do the next best thing: offer no sign that she had not forgotten. Durrance must continue to believe she felt nothing deeper than friendship toward him.

He stood right at the threshold of the enclosure and stepped inside. He was too perceptive; it would be unwise for him to encounter Captain Willoughby or learn of his presence. Ethne scanned her surroundings for an exit, knowing full well she would find none. A creek lay ahead, and three walls of dense, towering hedges enclosed them behind and to the sides. There was only one entrance, and Durrance himself blocked the path.

"Stay still," she murmured. "Do you recognize him?"

"Of course. We served together for three years at Suakin. I heard rumors he had gone blind, but I'm relieved to see his stride is steady."

"Lower your voice," Ethne warned urgently. "It is true. He *is* blind."

"One would never have guessed. Comforts seem so pointless. What words could possibly help him?"

"Say nothing!"

Durrance remained just inside the enclosure, appearing to stare directly at the two figures seated on the bench.

"Ethne," he said—not shouting, but speaking with a calm, unquestioning tone that created a powerful illusion of perfect vision.

"It's impossible he is blind," Willoughby insisted. "He sees us."

"He sees nothing."

Again, Durrance called out, "Ethne!" but this time with a note of uncertainty in his voice.

"Do you hear? He isn't sure," Ethne whispered, gripping Willoughby's arm tightly to prevent him from moving. "Stay perfectly still."

"Why?"

"Because he mustn't realize you are here."

Willoughby fell silent under the weight of Ethne's demeanor. She sat motionless, rigid as stone, holding her breath, her face strained forward. Her eyes fixed on Durrance with palpable terror, yet not a single muscle twitched. Observing her extraordinary apprehension, Willoughby began to fear for himself and Ethne.

"He is approaching," he whispered. "Not a word, not a movement."

"Ethne!" Durrance cried again, advancing further into the clearing toward the bench. Both women and the captain sat frozen, watching him intently. He walked past the bench and halted directly facing them. Willoughby thought surely he must see them; Durrance stood relaxed, poised as if ready to speak. Even Ethne, certain of his blindness, began to harbor doubts.

"Ethne!" he called once more, using that same quiet, confident tone. When no reply came, he shrugged and turned toward the creek. His back was now to them, but Ethne, trained to read the subtlest shifts in his posture, sensed he was listening intensely. The tilt of his head, the casual swing of his cane—all signaled deep attention. Her grip on Willoughby's arm tightened.

They held this position for a minute before Durrance spun around and took a sudden step toward the seat. Anticipating his tricks, Ethne did not flinch; not a rustle disturbed her skirts, and her hold on Willoughby remained firm.

"I wonder where in the world she could be," Durrance muttered aloud, walking backward out of the enclosure.

Ethne did not release Willoughby's arm until Durrance vanished from sight.

"That was a narrow escape," Willoughby breathed once he was permitted to speak. "Suppose Durrance had sat right on top of us?"

"Why suppose, since he didn't?" Ethne replied calmly. "Did you tell me everything?"

"So far as I recall."

"And all of this happened in the spring?"

"The spring of last year," Willoughby confirmed.

"Yes. I have a question. Why didn't you bring this feather to me last summer?"

"Last summer my leave was brief. I spent it hunting ibex in the hills north of Suakin."

"I see," Ethne said quietly. "I trust you had good sport."

"It wasn't bad."

If Willoughby had returned with news of victory instead of tales of mountain goats, it would have altered the course of Ethne's life entirely. The cry echoed loudly in her heart: *Why didn't you come?* Yet outwardly, she displayed the composure of a woman hardened by trial, speaking with such nonchalance that Willoughby assumed her inquiry stemmed from mere curiosity.

"You might have written," she suggested gently.

"Feversham did not indicate any urgency. Explaining matters via letter would have been arduous. He instructed me to visit him when I had the chance, and I lacked opportunity until now. Truthfully, I feared finding he had returned before me."

"Oh, no," Ethne interjected smoothly. "There was no possibility of that. Two feathers still remain unredeemed before he dares ask me to return mine."

Willoughby shook his head. "Feversham can never convince Castleton and Trench to retract their accusations as he convinced me."

"Why not?"

"Major Castleton was killed when the square broke at Tamai."

"Killed?" Ethne exclaimed, laughing briefly and with satisfaction.

Willoughby turned, stunned by her reaction, but her face revealed her delight. As a Celt, Ethne viewed death as less significant than others perceived. She could hate fiercely and be as hard as iron toward those she despised. These three men were objects of her intense hatred. While she had technically handed Harry the fourth feather to signify her agreement with their judgment, she cared little for that formality. She was genuinely pleased to hear Major Castleton was finally removed from the world.

"And Colonel Trench?" she asked.

"No," Willoughby answered gravely. "You are disappointed? He fares even worse. He was captured during a reconnaissance mission and is currently a prisoner in Omdurman."

"Ah!" Ethne responded.

"I don't believe you comprehend what imprisonment in Omdurman entails," Willoughby said sternly. "Even if you disliked the captive, you ought to feel pity."

"Not I," Ethne stated stubbornly. "Let me tell you what it implies."

"No. I prefer not to hear about Colonel Trench. Besides, you must go. Before you leave, I want to know one thing: What became of Mr. Feversham after he gave you that feather?"

"I advised him he had done all that could reasonably be expected, and he accepted my counsel. He boarded the first steamer to Suakin en route to Suez and left the Sudan."

"I must locate him. He must return. Did he require funds?"

"No. He still receives an allowance from his father and claimed to have ample resources."

"I am glad to hear that," Ethne said, dismissing Willoughby to wait within the enclosure while she exited alone to ensure the coast was clear.

The garden was deserted. Durrance had departed, and the great stone terrace and the house, with its striped blinds, appeared asleep. Approaching midday, the birds were unusually quiet. The stillness struck Ethne as peculiar; only the drowsy hum of bees drifted from the flowerbeds, accompanied by the distant call of a boy from across the creek.

Returning to Willoughby, she said, "You may depart now. I cannot feign friendship for you, Captain Willoughby, but I am grateful you sought me out and shared your story. Are you returning immediately to Kingsbridge? I hope so. I do not wish Colonel Durrance to know of your visit or what you have told me."

"Durrance was Feversham's greatest friend," Willoughby protested.

"He is unaware any feathers were sent to Mr. Feversham, nor does he need to know one has been reclaimed," Ethne explained. "He doesn't know why our engagement ended. I do not wish him to know. Your revelation would expose him, and he must remain ignorant."

"Why?" Willoughby demanded, his nature being obstinate; he required a reason before promising secrecy.

Ethne answered simply. "I am engaged to Colonel Durrance."
