"It will hardly be denied that even in this frail and corrupted world, we sometimes meet persons who, in their very mien and aspect, as well as in the whole habit of life, manifest such a signature and stamp of virtue, as to make our judgment of them a matter of intuition rather than the result of continued examination." —Alexander Knox: quoted in Southey's Life of Wesley.

Mirah claimed she had slept well that night. When she descended in Mab's black dress, her dark hair curling in fresh, damp fibrils as it dried from its recent bath, she possessed the appearance of someone reclaiming comfort after a long season of sorrow and vigilance that had pale her cheeks and etched blue semicircles beneath her eyes. It was Mab who carried her breakfast and escorted her downstairs, wearing a distinct pride in the effect of a pair of tiny felt slippers she had hurried out to purchase, for there were no shoes in the house small enough for Mirah. Her borrowed dress ended just above her ankles, exposing the cheap garments that molded themselves to her feet, appearing to Mab as an adornment as exquisite as the sheaths of budding flowers. The farthing buckles were jewels.

"Oh, if you please, mamma?" cried Mab, clasping her hands and bowing toward Mirah's feet as they entered the parlor. "Look at the slippers, how beautifully they fit! I declare she is like Queen Budoor—'two delicate feet, the work of the protecting and all-recompensing Creator, support her; and I wonder how they can sustain what is above them.'"

Mirah glanced down at her own feet with a childlike curiosity and then offered a smile to Mrs. Meyrick. The mother said inwardly, *One could hardly imagine this creature harboring an evil thought. Yet, wise counsel dictates caution.* She returned Mirah's smile and replied, "I fear the feet have borne their burden a little too often lately. But today she shall rest and be my companion."

"And she will tell you so many things, and I shall not hear them," grumbled Mab, feeling herself thrust into the first volume of a delightful romance, forced to skip chapters because she had duties to attend to among her pupils.

Kate had already departed to sketch along the river, and Amy was away on business errands. This separation was precisely what the mother desired: solitude with this stranger, whose narrative must inevitably be one of sorrow, yet one that demanded to be heard.

The small front parlor stood that morning as a sanctuary. Sunlight spilled onto the river, and a soft breeze drifted through the open window. The walls bore witness to a glorious, silent cloud of observers: the Virgin soaring amidst her cherubic escort; Grand Melancholia with her solemn universe; the Prophets and Sibyls; the School of Athens; the Last Supper; mystic groups where distant ages condensed into a single breath; the grave heads of Holbein and Rembrandt; the Tragic Muse; children of the previous century lost in reverie or play; Italian poets—all present through the medium of black and white. The neat mother, having weathered her trials and emerged with a face still cheerful, sorted colored wools for her embroidery. Hafiz purred on the window-ledge; the clock on the mantelpiece ticked without haste; and the occasional rumble of wheels seemed to lie beyond the more massive, central quiet. Mrs. Meyrick perceived this silence as the most potent invitation for her companion to speak, choosing not to disrupt it with remark.

Mirah sat opposite in her former posture, hands clasped on her lap, ankles crossed, her eyes initially drifting slowly over the surrounding objects before settling with a sort of placid reverence upon Mrs. Meyrick. Finally, she began to speak softly.

"I remember my mother's face better than anything else; yet I was not seven when I was taken away, and I am nineteen now."

"I can understand that," said Mrs. Meyrick. "Some earliest impressions endure the longest."

"Oh, yes, it was the earliest. I think my life began with waking up and loving my mother's face; it was so near to me, and her arms were round me, and she sang to me. One hymn she sang so often, so often: and then she taught me to sing it with her; it was the first song I ever knew. They were always Hebrew hymns she sang; and because I never knew the meaning of the words, they seemed filled with nothing but our love and happiness. When I lay in my little bed and it was all white above me, she would bend over me, between me and the white ceiling, and sing in a sweet, low voice. I can dream myself back into that time when I am awake, and it often returns to me in my sleep—my hand is very small; I raise it to her face, and she kisses it. Sometimes in my dreams I begin to tremble and think that we are both dead; but then I wake up, and my hand lies like this, and for a moment I hardly know myself. But if I could see my mother again, I should know her instantly."

"You must expect some change after twelve years," said Mrs. Meyrick, gently. "See my grey hair: ten years ago it was bright brown. Days and months pace over us like restless little birds, leaving the marks of their feet backward and forward; especially when they are birds with heavy hearts—then they tread heavily."

"Ah, I am sure her heart has been heavy for want of me. But to feel her joy if we could meet again, and I could make her know I love her and give her deep comfort after all her mourning! If that were possible, I should mind nothing; I should be glad that I have lived through my trouble. I did despair. The world seemed miserable and wicked; no one helped me so that I could bear their looks and words; I felt that my mother was dead, and death was the only path to her. But then, in the last moment—yesterday, when I longed for the water to close over me—and I thought that death was the best image of mercy—goodness came to me alive, and I felt trust in the living. And—it is strange—but I began to hope that she was living too. And now, with you—here—this morning, peace and hope have flooded into me. I want nothing; I can wait; because I hope and believe and am grateful—oh, so grateful! You have not thought evil of me—you have not despised me."

Mirah spoke with low-toned fervor, sitting as still as a painting throughout.

"Many others would have felt as we do, my dear," said Mrs. Meyrick, feeling a mist rise in her eyes as she looked at her embroidery.

"But I did not meet them—they did not come to me."

"How was it that you were taken from your mother?"

"Ah, I am a long while coming to that. It is dreadful to speak of, yet I must tell you—I must tell you everything. My father—he was the one who took me away. I thought we were only going on a little journey; and I was pleased. There was a box containing all my little things. But we boarded a ship and drifted farther and farther from the land. Then I became ill; and I thought it would never end—it was the first misery, and it seemed endless. But at last we landed. I knew nothing then, and believed whatever my father said. He comforted me, telling me I should return to my mother. But it was America we had reached, and it was many long years before we returned to Europe. At first, I often asked my father when we were going back; and I tried to learn writing quickly, for I wanted to write to my mother. But one day, when he found me attempting to compose a letter, he lifted me onto his knee and told me that my mother and brother were dead—that was why we did not return. I remember my brother a little; he carried me once, but he was not always at home. I believed my father when he said they were dead. I saw them under the earth when he said they were there, with their eyes forever closed. I never doubted its truth; and I used to cry every night in my bed for a long while. Then, when she visited me so often in my sleep, I thought she must be living nearby, though I could not always see her, and that comforted me. I was never afraid in the dark because of that; and very often during the day I would shut my eyes, bury my face, and try to see her and hear her singing. I eventually came to do that without closing my eyes."

Mirah paused, her face bearing a sweet contentment, as if she were inhabiting her happy vision while gazing out toward the river.

"Still, your father was not unkind to you, I hope," said Mrs. Meyrick after a moment, anxious to draw her back from the memory.

"No; he petted me and took pains to teach me. He was an actor; and I discovered later that the 'Coburg' I used to hear him mention at home was a theater. His acting was not particularly good, I think, but he managed the stage, wrote, and translated plays. An Italian lady, a singer, lived with us for a long time.
