Mrs. Jennings turned to Elinor the moment Colonel Brandon had taken his leave, her eyes bright with knowing delight. "Well, Miss Dashwood, I shan’t pretend ignorance—though I made a valiant effort not to eavesdrop, one can’t help catching a word or two. And let me say, I’ve seldom been more pleased in all my days! You have my sincerest congratulations, my dear."

Elinor’s cheeks warmed, though she kept her composure. "You are very kind, ma’am. It is indeed a most generous offer, and I am deeply sensible of Colonel Brandon’s goodness. Such compassion is rare—I confess I was quite astonished."

"Modesty becomes you, but really, my dear, I’ve thought it likely for some time now. A man of his disposition doesn’t act without purpose."

"You anticipated his kindness, perhaps, but surely not the immediacy of the opportunity?"

"Opportunity!" Mrs. Jennings laughed. "When a man sets his mind to something, opportunity follows. But enough—I shan’t keep you any longer. Only think, soon there’ll be no happier pair in all of England!"

Elinor smiled faintly. "I suppose you mean to visit them at Delaford?"

"Indeed I shall! And as for the house being unfit, I cannot fathom his meaning—it’s as fine a parsonage as any I’ve seen."

"He mentioned repairs."

"Well, whose place is it to see to them, if not his own?"

Their conversation was cut short by the arrival of the carriage. Mrs. Jennings rose briskly. "I’ve scarcely said half I wished, but no matter—we’ll finish our talk this evening. You’ll want to share the news with Marianne, no doubt. And I daresay you’ve little desire for company just now."

Marianne had already withdrawn, leaving Elinor to her thoughts. "I will tell her, of course," Elinor said, "but for the present, I would rather no one else knew."

Mrs. Jennings looked slightly put out. "Not even Lucy? I had thought to call on her today."

"Not yet, if you please. I must write to Mr. Ferrars first—there are matters he must attend to without delay."

For a moment, Mrs. Jennings looked puzzled. Then her face cleared. "Ah! Mr. Ferrars is to be the man, is he? Well, that is good news indeed! Though I must say, oughtn’t the Colonel to write himself?"

Elinor, unsure of her meaning but unwilling to inquire further, replied only to the latter remark. "Colonel Brandon preferred that another should convey his intentions. He is of a delicate mind in such matters."

"How very odd! But there—I shan’t keep you from your letter." She turned to go, then paused. "Oh! I’ve just thought of Betty’s sister—she might make a fine lady’s maid, if she suits. But you’ll consider that in good time."

Elinor, barely attending, murmured assent. Once alone, she took up her pen, her thoughts racing. How to phrase her letter to Edward? The situation demanded both delicacy and clarity, and she hesitated over each word—until Edward himself entered, startling her utterly.

Mrs. Jennings, meeting him at the door, had insisted he come up, claiming Elinor had pressing business with him. Now here he stood, and Elinor’s carefully composed letter was rendered unnecessary.

His appearance flustered her. She had not seen him since his engagement had become public knowledge—nor since he had learned she knew of it. The weight of her news and the memory of their last parting made the moment painfully awkward.

Edward, too, seemed ill at ease. He apologized stiffly for intruding. "Mrs. Jennings led me to believe you wished to speak with me—otherwise I should not have presumed. Though I could not leave London without seeing you and your sister."

"You need not have gone without our good wishes," Elinor said, rallying. "But since you are here, I may deliver them in person. Colonel Brandon was with me not half an hour ago. He has asked me to inform you that—knowing you intend to take orders—he wishes to offer you the living of Delaford. It is not as valuable as he might wish, but it is a mark of his esteem."

Edward’s astonishment was plain. "Colonel Brandon offers *me* a living?"

"Yes. He acts out of concern for your present difficulties—and from respect for your character."

Edward was silent a long moment. "I cannot account for such kindness—except that it must come through you."

Elinor shook her head. "You mistake. The offer is entirely his own. I had no hand in it."

Still, Edward seemed unconvinced. At last, he spoke quietly. "He is a man of excellent reputation. Your brother thinks highly of him."

"He is all that is honorable," Elinor agreed. "And as you will be neighbors, it is all the better."

Edward’s expression darkened briefly, but he said nothing. Rising, he asked for Colonel Brandon’s direction. "I must thank him—since you will not allow me to thank *you*."

Elinor did not detain him. They parted with mutual wishes for happiness—hers earnest, his halting.

As the door closed, Elinor sighed. The next time she saw Edward, he would be Lucy’s husband. The thought was bittersweet.

When Mrs. Jennings returned, her curiosity was undiminished. "Well? Did he accept?"

"Without hesitation."

"And when shall he take orders?"

"In a few months, I imagine."

"Months!" Mrs. Jennings exclaimed. "The Colonel won’t wait so long! Surely another candidate could be found."

Elinor laughed. "The Colonel’s sole aim is to assist Mr. Ferrars."

Mrs. Jennings stared. "You don’t mean he’s marrying you just to give Edward a living?"

The misunderstanding was swiftly corrected, much to Mrs. Jennings’ amusement. "Well! The parsonage may be small, but it’s a fine house. We must urge the Colonel to make it fit for Lucy before she settles there."

Elinor agreed—privately certain that Lucy would not wait for improvements.
