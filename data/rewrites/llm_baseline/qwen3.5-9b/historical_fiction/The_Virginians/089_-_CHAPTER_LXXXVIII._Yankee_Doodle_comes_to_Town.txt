In the days when America was still a possession of Britain, one of its primary functions served as a sanctuary for our nation's outcasts. Alongside the convicts and indentured servants we shipped across the Atlantic, we dispatched to these distant shores the "black sheep" of society—scapegraces, younger sons with no inheritance, and those driven away by debt, despair, and relentless bailiffs. Much as Captain Cook presented English fauna to the indigenous peoples of his newly charted islands, we took care to export samples of our domestic failures to the colonies, leaving them to struggle and multiply in the wilderness as best they could.

I confess I played a small role in this exodus regarding the worthy Mr. Hagan, husband of my kinswoman. When England offered him no livelihood, we gladly shipped him to Virginia, providing him with a colonial pulpit to occupy his hands and mind. In Virginia, he conducted himself as a brave and honest gentleman, fulfilling his duties to both his congregation and his King with thoroughness, thereby earning credit for my own patronage. My cousin, Madam Theo, always defended my decision, insisting that my actions were guided by the highest principles of morality and honor. Yet, if I had truly believed in his worthiness, would I have kept him at home? Would I have chosen him and his wife to serve our own parish? I fear not. While I never doubted Cousin Maria's sincerity in seeking redemption, I admit I felt a secret relief watching her work out her salvation in the untamed frontier. I acknowledge this as a flaw born of pride.

Twice, when I needed support most desperately, this same Maria offered me her sympathy and friendship. She endured her own trials with courage and soothed the sorrows of others with admirable devotion. And yet, despite her virtues, I and several of my kin looked down upon her. Oh, the shame of our arrogance!

Maria was not alone among our family in being banished to the American wilderness. After exhausting his ability to borrow, steal, and cheat within the bounds of polite society, the Honourable William Esquire was relocated to New York. His noble brother and royal master expressed a heartfelt desire to never see him again. When the troubles erupted, news reached us of William's exploits in his new home. Deceit and malice seemed to precede him wherever he traveled. Lord Dunmore informed me that Mr. William had boldly declared to the public that our estate of Castlewood belonged solely to his brother's pleasure; that his father, acting out of kindness toward Madam Esmond (Lord Dunmore's half-sister), had granted her the estate for life; and that William was currently negotiating with the current Lord Castlewood to purchase the remainder interest.

We possessed the original deed of gift locked safely in our strongroom at Castlewood, and it was duly registered in Williamsburg. On paper, we were secure. However, intent is often more powerful than law. Hal and I resolved that upon meeting Mr. William, we would demand confirmation of this fabrication. The reaction of Madam Esmond upon hearing such slander needs no elaboration.

"What!" she cried, her voice trembling. "My father, the Marquis of Esmond, was a liar, and I am a cheat?" She feared he intended to seize her son's property upon her death. She prepared letters to Lord Castlewood in England and even to His Majesty at St. James's, only to be stopped by my assurance that Mr. William's reputation for falsehood was notorious among all who knew him. We dared not hope he would suddenly become consistent enough to tell the truth.

Soon, we heard of him as a leading Loyalist in New York, rising rapidly from Captain to Major of a volunteer corps. They sent addresses to sympathetic figures throughout the colonies, proclaiming their readiness to die for the Mother Country. Unable to remain in a house where windows were effectively boarded up by the atmosphere of distrust, Madam Esmond abandoned our rented home in Williamsburg. My family retreated to Richmond, which was likewise deserted by the members of the dissolved Assembly. Captain Hal and his wife returned promptly to their plantation. I, however, torn between annoyance at the unfolding chaos and a sense of duty, split my time between my own household and that of our Governor, who eagerly sought my counsel and companionship.

Political divisions ran deep, yet no personal enmity existed between the factions. Even after the dissolution of our House of Assembly—which adjourned to a local tavern to hold that famous meeting where the idea of a continental congress was reportedly conceived—the staunchest opponents of the Governor remained his friends, shared his hospitality, and joined him in leisurely excursions. Once the session ended, the gentry dispersed to their counties to hold their own meetings. With assemblies abruptly dissolved in most other provinces as well, a consensus formed: a general congress must be convened. Philadelphia, the largest and most significant city on the continent, was chosen as the venue. These celebrated conferences began, serving merely as the angry prelude to war.

We still sang "God Save the King"; we still submitted humble petitions to the throne. Yet, fate intervened. I had ridden out to visit my brother Harry at Fanny's Mount earlier that day. His new plantation lay near ours, separated only by the Rappahannock and bordering the Mattaponey River. That afternoon, I rode out again. A groom informed me that Master Harry had departed toward Willis's Ordinary. Thinking nothing of it, I followed.

Not far from Willis's, emerging from the tree line onto a clearing, I encountered Captain Hal on horseback. Surrounding him were thirty or forty countrymen, armed with a motley array of weapons: pikes, scythes, fowling-pieces, and muskets. The Captain, flanked by two or three promising young officers, was drilling the men.

As I approached, a peculiar expression crossed Hal's face.

"Present arms!" he commanded. The makeshift army attempted the salute with varying degrees of success.

"Captain Cade," Hal announced, gesturing to me, "this is my brother, Sir George Warrington."

"As a relation of yours, Colonel," replied the man addressed as Captain, "the gentleman is welcome." He extended his hand.

"And—a true friend to Virginia," Hal added, his face flushing slightly.

"Yes, please God, gentlemen!" I exclaimed. The regiment responded with a hearty hurrah for the Colonel and his brother.

Once the drill concluded, the officers and men moved to Willis's Ordinary for refreshments. Colonel Hal declined to join them that afternoon. Instead, we trotted homeward together.

"So, Hal, the cat is out of the bag!" I remarked.

He shot me a hard look. "I suspect there are wilder cats in this bag than you imagine. It must come to this, George. Listen, you must not tell Madam."

"Good heavens!" I cried. "Do you mean that with fellows like those I saw yonder, you intend to make a stand against the greatest nation and finest army in the world?"

"I guess we'll get an awful whipping," Hal admitted grimly. "That is the fact. But then, George," he continued, flashing his characteristic sweet, kind smile, "we are young. Perhaps a few whippings will do us good. Won't it do us good, Dolly, you old slut?" He playfully touched the whip against an old mongrel dog trotting alongside him.

I refrained from pressing him further on the British side of the argument, the perspective I deemed superior. He had grown accustomed to dismissing my reasoning with, "All mighty well, brother, you speak as an Englishman and have cast your lot with your country, just as I have with mine." Against such logic, there is no defense; the dispute ultimately resolves itself in battle, where the stronger party prevails. Who held the right in the wars of the last century? The King or Parliament? History showed that whoever stood atop the hill was considered right, and generally proved more humane in victory than the Cavaliers might have been had they won. Consider, moreover, the horror of a Tory triumph in America: the imagined ropes, scaffolds, and noble heads laid low. It is a strange paradox, I confess: I stood on the Loyalist side, yet I wished for the Whigs to win.

My brother Hal, conversely, distinguished himself greatly with his regiment while maintaining absolute decorum toward the enemy. "The officers of the British army," he often stated, "are gentlemen. At least, I have not heard they have changed much since my time. There may be scoundrels and ruffians among the enemy's ranks; I dare say we could find similar characters among our own. Our business is to defeat His Majesty's forces, not to insult them; any rascal can hurl names."

It was because of a name given to my brother by Mr. Lee—one that many of his rough horsemen failed to comprehend—that Harry was frequently dubbed "Chevalier Baird" within the Continental Army. He was, indeed, a knight without fear or reproach.

When I asked, "What could such people as those you were drilling possibly do against the British army?" Hal answered with unshakeable confidence. "They can beat them, Mr. George. That is what they can do."

"Great heavens!" I protested. "Do you mean that with your company of Wolfe's, you would hesitate to attack five hundred of them?"
