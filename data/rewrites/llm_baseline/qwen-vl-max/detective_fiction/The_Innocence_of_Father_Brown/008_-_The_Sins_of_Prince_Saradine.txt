The Sins of Prince Saradine

When Flambeau took his month-long break from his office in Westminster, he did so not in a grand hotel or a seaside resort, but aboard a small sailing boat—so modest in size that it often served more as a rowboat than a vessel for the open water. He navigated the quiet rivers of eastern England, waters so narrow and winding that the boat seemed to glide through meadows and cornfields as if sailing across land. It was just large enough for two people, with only room for essentials. Flambeau had stocked it carefully, according to his own philosophy: tins of salmon for sustenance, loaded revolvers for protection, a bottle of brandy for emergencies—perhaps fainting—and a priest, in case death came uninvited. With this sparse cargo, he drifted down the gentle Norfolk streams, aiming eventually for the Broads, but enjoying every moment along the way—the overhanging gardens, the mirrored reflections of manor houses and villages, the quiet pools where he paused to fish, and the peaceful closeness of the riverbank.

Flambeau, like any true philosopher, had no real goal for his holiday—only an excuse. He carried a half-formed intention, one he treated lightly enough that failure wouldn’t spoil the trip, yet seriously enough that success would make it memorable. Years before, during his days as the most notorious thief in Paris, he had received many strange letters—some admiring, some accusing, even one expressing love. But one message stood out: a visiting card, postmarked from England, with a note written in French and green ink on the back: “If you ever retire and become respectable, come and see me. I want to meet you, for I have met all the other great men of my time. That trick of yours—getting one detective to arrest the other—was the most splendid scene in French history.” On the front, engraved in formal script: “Prince Saradine, Reed House, Reed Island, Norfolk.”

At the time, Flambeau had paid little attention to the prince beyond confirming that he had once been a dazzling figure in southern Italy—known for eloping with a married woman of high rank. Though such affairs were common among the elite, this one had taken a tragic turn: the husband, insulted, supposedly threw himself off a cliff in Sicily. The prince later lived in Vienna, then vanished into years of restless travel. When Flambeau himself left the world of crime and settled in England, he thought of reaching out to this forgotten exile. He didn’t expect to find him—or even know where Reed Island might be—but fate brought them together sooner than expected.

One night, they moored their boat under a bank thick with tall grass and short pollarded trees. After a long day of rowing, sleep came early, and by chance, they woke before dawn. Not quite dawn, though—a large, pale moon hung low in the sky, just beginning to set behind the reeds above them. The sky was a vivid violet-blue, bright but still dark. Both men suddenly remembered childhood dreams: the feeling of being surrounded by towering weeds like ancient woods. Standing upright, the daisies looked giant, the dandelions enormous. The riverbed dipped slightly beneath them, making them feel as though they were looking up from underground at a forest of flowers. “By Jove!” Flambeau exclaimed. “It’s like being in fairyland.”

Father Brown sat up sharply and made the sign of the cross. His sudden movement startled his companion, who asked, mildly, what was wrong.

“The people who wrote the medieval ballads,” replied the priest, “knew more about fairies than you do. Fairyland isn’t just full of nice things.”

“Oh, nonsense,” said Flambeau. “Only good things could happen under such a gentle moon. I say we push on and see what happens. We may never again see such a moon or such a mood.”

“All right,” said Father Brown. “I’m not saying it’s always wrong to enter fairyland. Just that it’s always dangerous.”

They moved slowly upstream as the sky lightened. The violet and gold of the moon faded into the colorless haze that precedes sunrise. By the time the first streaks of red and gold split the horizon, they were nearing a village or town perched on the riverbank ahead. Twilight had fallen, but everything was visible. The houses, with their long, sloping roofs, leaned over the water like cattle drinking. The dawn turned to daylight before they saw anyone. Then, finally, a calm, prosperous man in shirt sleeves appeared—his face round as the moon, his whiskers red and radiating like sunbeams—leaning on a post above the slow tide. Without thinking, Flambeau rose in the swaying boat and called out, asking if he knew Reed Island or Reed House.

The man smiled wider and pointed upstream. Flambeau nodded and steered onward without another word.

The boat wound through grassy bends and silent reaches until, around a sharp corner, they entered a quiet pool or lake, ringed with rushes. In the center floated a long, low island, upon which stood a house built of bamboo or tropical cane. Its walls were pale yellow, its roof darker brown or red, and the structure repeated itself in simple, almost monotonous lines. The morning breeze rustled the reeds and hummed through the ribbed walls like wind through a giant panpipe.

“By George!” shouted Flambeau. “Here it is! This must be Reed Island. And that’s Reed House. I swear that fat fellow with the whiskers was a fairy.”

“Perhaps,” said Father Brown quietly. “If he was, he was a bad one.”

Even as he spoke, Flambeau had already run the boat ashore through the rattling reeds. They stepped onto the island beside the strange, silent house.

The house faced away from the river and landing stage; the main entrance opened toward the long garden. The visitors walked along a narrow path under the low eaves, circling nearly three sides of the building. Through windows on different angles, they glimpsed the same long, well-lit room—panelled in light wood, filled with mirrors, and set for an elegant lunch. At last, they reached the front door, flanked by two turquoise flowerpots. It was opened by a butler—tall, thin, grey, and listless—who murmured that Prince Saradine was away but expected soon. The house was kept ready for him and his guests. When Flambeau showed the visiting card with the green ink message, the butler’s face flickered with life. He offered them a seat with stiff courtesy. “His Highness may arrive any minute,” he said. “He’d be distressed to miss anyone he invited. We always keep cold lunch ready. I’m sure he’d want you offered it.”

Curious, Flambeau accepted. The butler led them into the mirror-lined room. Nothing was remarkable except the unusual pattern of long windows alternating with long oblong mirrors, giving the space a dreamlike, airy quality—almost as if dining outdoors. A few quiet pictures hung in the corners: a large grey photograph of a young man in uniform, and a red chalk sketch of two boys with long hair. When Flambeau asked if the soldier was the prince, the butler replied curtly: “No. That’s Captain Stephen Saradine, the prince’s younger brother.” Then he fell silent.

After lunch, with coffee and liqueurs, they were shown the garden, library, and the housekeeper—a dark, dignified woman with a presence like a noble Madonna. She and the butler were the only remnants of the prince’s original household; the others were new, hired locally. She went by Mrs. Anthony, but her Italian accent hinted she’d adopted a British name. Mr. Paul, the butler, also had a faint foreign air, though he spoke English with polished precision—common among servants of cosmopolitan nobility.

Though beautiful, the place felt strangely luminous and sad. Hours stretched like days. The rooms were flooded with daylight, but it felt dead. Over everything, the sound of the river flowed—melancholy, constant, like a distant lament.

“We’ve taken a wrong turn,” said Father Brown, staring out at the silver flood and grey-green reeds. “But sometimes, being the right person in the wrong place can do good.”

Father Brown, usually quiet, had a gift for listening. In those endless hours, he drew out secrets from the household more deeply than Flambeau, despite saying little. The butler, Paul, was sullen and loyal to the prince, whom he claimed had been cruelly treated. The chief offender, he insisted, was the prince’s brother, Captain Stephen—a ne’er-do-well who had drained his brother of fortune and forced him into exile. Paul refused to say more, clearly biased.

Mrs. Anthony, however, was more revealing—though not without bitterness. As the men studied the red sketch of the two boys, she swept in on business. The mirrors reflected her image four or five times. Father Brown stopped mid-sentence. Flambeau, close to the drawing, said aloud, “The brothers Saradine, I suppose. Both look innocent enough. Hard to tell which is good and which is bad.” Then, realizing she was there, he changed the subject and stepped outside.

But Father Brown remained, gazing at the sketch. Mrs. Anthony stared at him.

She had large, sorrowful eyes and a face glowing with painful curiosity—as if questioning his identity. Perhaps his clerical clothes stirred memories of confession, or perhaps she sensed he knew more than he let on. In a low voice, like a conspirator, she whispered, “Your friend is right in one way. It would be hard to pick the good brother. Oh, it would be hard—very hard—to pick the good one.”

“I don’t understand,” said Brown, turning to leave.

She stepped closer, brows furrowed, body tensed like a bull lowering its head.

“There isn’t a good one,” she hissed. “Captain Stephen took money, yes—that’s bad. But I don’t think the prince was much better. Giving it away? That wasn’t goodness. The captain’s not the only one with something against him.”

A light sparked in the priest’s mind. He silently formed the word *blackmail*. Before he could speak, she whirled around, pale as death. The door opened silently. Paul stood there, ghost-like. The mirrors multiplied him—five Pauls entering at once.

“His Highness has arrived,” he said.

Outside the first window, a man passed, crossing the glass like a figure on stage. Then the second window—again, the same profile, the same stride. The man was tall and alert, but his hair was white, his skin an odd ivory yellow. He had a Roman nose, long lean cheeks masked by moustache and beard—the moustache darker, giving a theatrical effect. He wore a white top hat, an orchid, a yellow waistcoat, yellow gloves, which he swung as he walked. At the front door, Paul opened it, and the newcomer said cheerfully, “Well, you see I’ve come.” Paul bowed, spoke softly, then announced, “Everything is at your disposal.”

The glove-flapping Prince Saradine entered, laughing. Five princes seemed to walk in from five doors.

He placed his hat and gloves on the table and shook hands warmly.

“Delighted to see you here, Mr. Flambeau,” he said. “I know you by reputation—if that’s not too bold.”

“Not at all,” answered Flambeau, smiling. “I’m not easily offended. Very few reputations are built on pure virtue.”

The prince glanced sharply at him, then laughed and offered chairs.

“Pleasant little place,” he said, coolly. “Not much to do, I fear—but the fishing is excellent.”

Father Brown watched him with a baby’s solemn gaze. He studied the white curls, yellow face, slim figure—foppish, perhaps, like a stage actor. But something else nagged at him—the shape of the face. It felt familiar. Then he recalled the mirrors and dismissed it as illusion.

Prince Saradine charmed both guests. He guided Flambeau to the best fishing spot, returning swiftly to join Brown in the library. He spoke several languages—mostly slang—and told stories of opium dens, bushrangers, brigands. He seemed worldly, brilliant, yet restless. His eye was wild, his manner nervous. He relied entirely on Paul, who managed the household like a steward or chamberlain. Paul dined privately, consulted with the prince formally, and commanded the staff. The housekeeper was a shadow compared to him. Brown heard nothing more of the blackmail rumors. But the prince’s secrecy made the story plausible.

Later, as evening fell, they returned to the mirror-room. Yellow light touched the water and willow banks. A bittern called in the distance. The same sense of eerie sadness returned. “I wish Flambeau were back,” muttered Brown.

“Do you believe in doom?” asked the prince suddenly.

“No,” said Brown. “I believe in Doomsday.”

The prince turned, his face in shadow against the sunset. “What do you mean?”

“I mean we’re on the wrong side of the tapestry,” said Brown. “The events here don’t seem to mean anything. But somewhere else—somewhere else—retribution will fall on the true offender. Here, it often falls on the wrong person.”

The prince made a strange, animal-like noise. His eyes shone oddly in the dimness. A new thought struck Brown: Was the prince truly sane? He repeated, “The wrong person… the wrong person…” far too many times for mere conversation.

Then Brown noticed the mirror: the door stood open, and Paul stood there, silent and pale.

“I thought it better to announce at once,” said Paul, with the stiffness of a family lawyer. “A boat with six men has arrived at the landing-stage. A gentleman sits in the stern.”

“A boat?” repeated the prince. “A gentleman?” He rose.

Silence followed, broken only by the bird’s call. Then, as before, a new figure passed the windows—taller, sharper, with an aquiline profile. But unlike Saradine’s flashy style, this man wore an old-fashioned black hat, a shabby blue coat, a red waistcoat, coarse white trousers—clothes from another age. His face was olive, young, clean-shaven, with a chin like Napoleon’s. He looked sincere, almost terrifyingly so.

“The deuce!” said the prince. He grabbed his white hat and rushed to the door.

On the lawn stood the newcomer and his six men, arranged like soldiers. The boatmen had pulled the craft high onto shore, oars held upright like spears. Swarthy, some with earrings. One stood forward, carrying a large black case.

“Your name,” said the young man, “is Saradine?”

The prince nodded, casually.

“My name is Antonelli,” said the young man.

“Antonelli,” repeated the prince. “Somehow I remember that name.”

“Allow me to introduce myself,” said the Italian.

With his left hand, he removed his hat politely. With his right, he struck the prince across the face with a force that sent the white hat rolling down the steps and rocked one of the blue pots.

The prince lunged, but the young man twisted away with a strange politeness.

“That is all right,” he panted, in halting English. “I have insulted. I will give satisfaction. Marco, open the case.”
