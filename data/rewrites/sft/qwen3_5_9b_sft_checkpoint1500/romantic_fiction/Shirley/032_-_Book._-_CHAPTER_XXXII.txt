Because Briarmains stood closer than the Hollow, Mr. Yorke had guided his young companion thither. He had witnessed him laid upon the finest bed in the house, tended with a meticulous care that mirrored the attention he would have given to his own son. The sight of the blood, welling from that treacherously inflicted wound, stirred within the Yorkshire gentleman a paternal affection he had rarely known. To behold the sudden collapse of such a tall, straight figure—prostrated in its pride across the road—the fine southern head now laid low in the dust, the youth in prime flung forth before him pallid, lifeless, and helpless—this tragic tableau seized Mr. Yorke's heart with immediate and fierce tenderness.

With no other hand to raise, no other voice to offer kindness, no other mind to devise a plan, he was forced to act alone. This absolute dependence of the speechless, bleeding boy on his benevolence ensured that benevolence was exercised with unyielding efficiency. Mr. Yorke, who relished the exercise of power, now held dominion over a fellow creature's life in the palm of his hand. It suited him perfectly.

Equally, the incident appealed to his saturnine better half. While lesser women might have been stricken with terror by a gory man dragged over their threshold to lie in the "howe of the night," Mrs. Yorke found in such drama a source of exhilaration. Where others would have succumbed to hysteria, she remained composed, her spirits buoyed by the crisis. An attempted murder near her door, a half-dead man resting in her best bed, set her cap askew with a dash akin to a turban and filled her with a strange, vigorous energy.

Mrs. Yorke possessed a peculiar constitution; she could render the drudging existence of a simple maid miserable, yet nurse a hospital full of plague victims like a heroic saint. She developed an almost tender regard for Moore. When she found him committed to her charge, left in her arms as dependent as her youngest child, her tough heart yearned for him. If she had seen a domestic or one of her daughters approach him with water or to smooth his pillow, she would have boxed their ears. Consequently, she banished Jessie and Rose from the upper realms of the house and strictly forbade the maids from entering.

Had the accident occurred at the rectory gates and Old Helstone had taken in the martyr, neither Yorke nor his wife would have pitied him; they would have deemed him rightly served for his tyranny and meddling. But as it stood, he became the apple of their eye.

Strangely, Louis Moore was permitted to enter—to sit on the edge of the bed, lean over the pillow, hold his brother's hand, and press his pale forehead with fraternal lips—and Mrs. Yorke endured it with remarkable patience. She allowed him to remain half the day, even suffering him to sit up all night. She rose at five on a wet November morning, lit the kitchen fire herself, prepared breakfast, and served it to the brothers with majestic indifference, arrayed in a boundless flannel wrapper, a shawl, and her nightcap, watching them eat as complacently as a hen observing her chicks. Yet, she promptly reprimanded the cook for daring to bring up a basin of sago-gruel and stripped the housemaid of her favor for airing Moore's surtout and helping him on with it, accepting only a perfunctory "Thank you" and a shilling. When two pale, anxious ladies begged to see Moore, Mrs. Yorke hardened her heart and sent them packing, not without a sense of opprobrium.

But when Hortense Moore arrived, the situation shifted. The entire family of the Moores seemed to suit Mrs. Yorke better than any other. Hortense and she shared an exhaustless mutual fascination with the corrupt propensities of servants, viewing them with identical suspicion and severity. Hortense, from the start, displayed no jealousy of Mrs. Yorke's attentions to Robert; instead, she kept to her station with little interference, finding ceaseless occupation in fidgeting about the house, surveilling the kitchen, reporting on its activities, and generally making herself useful. Together, they sedulously excluded visitors from the sickroom, holding the young mill-owner captive, allowing hardly a breath of air or a ray of sunshine to reach him.

Dr. MacTurk, to whom Moore's case was entrusted, pronounced the wound dangerous, though hopefully not hopeless. Initially, he desired to assign a nurse of his own selection, but neither Mrs. Yorke nor Hortense would consent. Promising faithful adherence to his directions, he left them in charge.

Undoubtedly, they executed the trust to the best of their ability, yet something went awry. The bandages were displaced or tampered with, resulting in a catastrophic loss of blood. Dr. MacTurk, summoned with a steed foaming at the mouth, proved to be one of those surgeons dangerous to vex; abrupt in his best moods and savage in his worst. Upon witnessing Moore's state, he relieved his feelings with a flowery vocabulary unnecessary to recount here. A bouquet of the choicest blossoms fell upon the unperturbed head of Mr. Graves, a stony young assistant he usually carried with him; another nosegay landed on another young gentleman in his train—an interesting facsimile of himself, indeed his own son—but the full corbeille of blushing blooms fell to the lot of meddling womankind, en masse.

For most of a winter night, the doctor and his satellites labored around Moore. Shuttered alone with him in his chamber, they wrought and wrangled over his exhausted frame. Three stood on one side of the bed, and Death on the other. The conflict was sharp, lasting until daybreak, when the balance between the belligerents appeared so equal that either party might have claimed victory.

At dawn, Graves and the younger MacTurk remained in charge, while the senior departed to secure additional strength in the person of Mrs. Horsfall, the best nurse on his staff. With stern injunctions regarding the heavy responsibility laid upon her shoulders, he handed Moore over to her. She accepted this burden stolidly, taking likewise the easy-chair at the bedhead. That moment, she began her reign.

Mrs. Horsfall possessed one virtue: she obeyed orders received from MacTurk to the letter. In her eyes, the Ten Commandments were less binding than her surgeon's dictum. In every other respect, she was no woman but a dragon. Hortense Moore fell effaced before her; Mrs. Yorke withdrew, crushed. Yet both women were personages of some dignity in their own estimation and considerable bulk in the eyes of others. Perfectly cowed by the breadth, height, bone, and brawn of Mrs. Horsfall, they retreated to the back parlor. She, for her part, sat upstairs when she pleased and downstairs when she preferred it, consuming three drams daily and four pipes of tobacco.

As for Moore, no one dared inquire about him. Mrs. Horsfall had him at dry-nurse; it was she who was to do for him, and the general conjecture ran that she did so accordingly.

Morning and evening, MacTurk visited him. Complicated by this new mischance, his case had become an object of intense interest to the surgeon, who regarded him as a damaged piece of clockwork, crediting his skill with the ability to set it going again. Graves and the young MacTurk—Moore's sole other visitors—contemplated him as they might the occupant of a dissecting table at Stilbro' Infirmary.

Robert Moore enjoyed a peculiar existence—in pain, in danger, too weak to move, almost too weak to speak, with a sort of giantess as his keeper and the three surgeons as his only company. Thus he lay through the diminishing days and lengthening nights of the dreary month of November.

Initially, Moore feebly resisted Mrs. Horsfall. He hated the sight of her rough bulk and dreaded the contact of her hard hands, but she taught him docility in a trice. She made no account whatsoever of his six feet, his manly thews, and sinews; she turned him in his bed as another woman would turn a babe in its cradle. When he was good, she addressed him as "my dear" and "honey"; when he was bad, she sometimes shook him. If he attempted to speak when MacTurk was present, she lifted her hand and bade him "Hush!" like a nurse checking a forward child. If she had not smoked, and if she had not taken gin, it would have been better, he thought. Once, in her absence, he intimated to MacTurk that "that woman was a dram-drinker."

"Pooh! my dear sir, they are all so," replied the surgeon. "But Horsfall has this virtue," he added, "drunk or sober, she always remembers to obey *me*."

By the time the latter autumn passed, England had shed its mourning fogs and rains. Its winds swept onward to sigh over distant lands. Behind November came deep winter—clarity, stillness, frost accompanying.

A calm day settled into a crystalline evening. The world wore a North Pole coloring; all its lights and tints resembled the reflections of white, violet, or pale green gems.
