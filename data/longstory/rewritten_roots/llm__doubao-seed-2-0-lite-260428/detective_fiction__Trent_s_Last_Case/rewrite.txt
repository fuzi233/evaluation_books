Between what the world judges as meaningful, and what only seems to matter, how can any jury of ordinary people hope to see clearly? When Sigsbee Manderson — the indomitable, scheming financial titan — was gunned down by an unknown hand, the wider world lost nothing worth shedding a single tear for. All it gained was a harsh, unforgettable reminder of the vanity of the vast fortune he had spent his life hoarding: he died without one loyal friend to mourn him, without one single public act that might have left his memory even a scrap of honor. But when word of his death first spread, the men who lived and worked in the great churning vortex of global finance felt as if the earth itself had shuddered under their feet.

No figure in all the lurid, bloody history of American commerce had ever imposed itself so completely on the mind of the trading world. He had a niche all his own in the temples of high finance. Financial giants before him had grown great by directing and multiplying the power of capital, pocketing legitimate fortunes in millions for their labor. But Manderson was different: a pale, incongruous halo of piratical romance clung to him for decades, the kind of romance his countrymen loved most, even as he was universally seen as the unshakable guardian of market stability, the man who stamped out manipulated panics, the sworn enemy of the raiding chieftains who lurked at the borders of Wall Street.

His fortune had begun with his grandfather, one of those early pirate traders from the Gold Rush era, and passed down untouched through his father, who lived a quiet, long life lending money and never once margining a single stock. Manderson never knew a time in his life when he did not have huge sums of money at his disposal, and he should have fit perfectly into the newer American plutocracy, steady and grounded in generations of inherited wealth. But that was not the case. His upbringing and education had given him European ideas of how a rich man ought to live: a taste for quiet magnificence, the kind of grandeur that never shouts its own presence. But he had inherited too much of his Forty-Niner, financial buccaneer ancestor. In the first wild stretch of his career, dubbed his “early bad manners” period, he was little more than a genius gambler, his hand turned against every man on the Street: an infant prodigy who brought a sharper, more capable brain to the thrall of speculation than any opponent who dared face him. Napoleon had called war a glorious occupation on Saint Helena, and young Manderson felt exactly the same about the chaotic, dog-eat-dog brawl of the New York Stock Exchange.

Then came the shift. When his father died, and Manderson was thirty years old, he seemed to experience a sudden, blinding revelation of the power and glory of the god of finance he served. With the elastic, quick adaptability so typical of his country, he turned his back on the Street’s wild battles and settled down to steady, sober labor in his father’s old banking firm. Within a few years he controlled every part of the great institution, whose unimpeachable conservatism and financial weight stood like a solid cliff above the roiling, angry sea of the markets. All old mistrust of his wild youth faded. He was plainly a new man. No one could say for certain how the change had come about, though stories circulated of final words spoken by his father — the only man Manderson had ever respected, and perhaps loved.

He began to tower over the entire global financial system. Soon his name was spoken on every bourse in the world. To say the name Manderson was to evoke every broad, unshakable pillar of America’s vast national wealth. He built great combined capital trusts, centralized industries spanning entire continents, and financed large state and private enterprise projects with unerring judgement. More than once, when he “took hold” to smash a labor strike or federate ownership of an entire major industry, he sent ruin crashing down on thousands of small working homes. If miners, steelworkers, or cattlemen defied him and sparked unrest, he could be more ruthless and lawless than any of them. But this was all done in the name of legitimate business. Tens of thousands of poor people might curse his name, but financiers and speculators no longer feared or hated him. He stretched a hand to protect or manipulate the levers of wealth in every corner of the country. Forcible, cold, and unerring, he fed the national American hunger for bigness — and a grateful public dubbed him the Colossus.

But there was a side of Manderson in this later era that no one knew about outside a tiny circle: his secretaries, lieutenants, and a handful of old associates from his wilder, younger days. That small circle knew that Manderson, the great pillar of stable, sound business, had bouts of nostalgia for the old wild days when the entire Street shook at his name. One of them compared it to Blackbeard settling down as a respectable merchant in Bristol, living off plunder from the Main. Every so often, the pirate would glare out from behind the respectable mask, knife clamped in his teeth, sulphur matches sputtering in his hatband. During these brief lapses, he and his inner circle would draft plans for a dozen massive, tempestuous market raids in the back room of Manderson, Colefax and Company. But he never carried a single one of them out. Blackbeard would quell the mutiny of his old wild self, and go back soberly to his accounts — maybe humming a line or two of the old sea shanty *Spanish Ladies* under his breath. He allowed himself one harmless indulgence: once the window for action had passed, he would point out to some hotshot market raider that a single coup could have netted him a million dollars. “Seems to me,” he would say, almost wistfully, “the Street’s gotten a mighty dull place since I quit.”

Slowly, the financial world learned of this gentle quirk of the Colossus, and loved the story for it.

The news of his death hit the markets like a hurricane, and it came at the worst possible moment. Prices tottered and crashed like towers in an earthquake. For two full days Wall Street was a clamorous inferno of pale, desperate despair. All across the United States, wherever speculators gathered, a wave of ruin spread, and a plague of suicides followed. Even in Europe, dozens of men took their own lives, their fates pitifully tangled to the destiny of a financier most of them had never seen. In Paris, a well-known banker walked calmly out of the Bourse and dropped dead on the broad steps, a crushed phial of poison in his hand, surrounded by raving crowds. In Frankfort, a man jumped from the top of the Cathedral, leaving a dark red stain on the red stone tower where he landed. Men stabbed, shot, or strangled themselves, drank poison or breathed gas to escape, all because in a quiet corner of rural England, the life had left a single cold heart that had spent itself in service to greed.

The blow could not have fallen at a more disastrous time. Wall Street had already been holding its breath for days, just barely suppressing a coming slump. The great interests known to work for the Colossus had been fighting to contain the damage from Lucas Hahn’s sudden arrest, and the exposure of his massive embezzlement from the Hahn banks. That bombshell had dropped right when the market had been artificially inflated far beyond its real strength. In Street slang, a slump was long overdue. Corn harvest reports were poor, and two or three railway earnings statements had been far worse than investors had expected. But every time the first faint tremors of a market crash rippled through speculation, “the Manderson crowd” stepped in and propped prices up. For four feverish days, the shallow, quick-witted, greed-addicted speculators assumed the great Manderson himself was pulling all the strings from his holiday home. Newspapers all sang the same tune: Manderson was in constant hourly communication with his lieutenants in New York. One paper even published exact, round figures for the amount of money spent on transatlantic cables between New York and Manderson’s holiday home in Marlstone, England in the last 24 hours, claiming the Post Office had sent a full team of expert telegraph operators down to the tiny village to handle the flood of messages. Another revealed that Manderson had planned to abandon his holiday and sail home on the Lusitania when the Hahn crash broke, but that he had gotten the situation so well under control he’d decided to stay.

All of it was a lie, elaborated by finance editors at the prompting of Manderson’s inner circle, who knew the illusion of the great hero watching over their shoulders was the best possible tool to stop panic. The truth was, none of them had heard a single word from Manderson in days. Howard B. Jeffrey, the steel and iron tycoon, was the one running the whole rescue operation. By Saturday, Jeffrey thought his work was almost done. The market was firm, and slowly climbing again. Wall Street went to its Sunday rest, worn out but relieved, at peace.

At the start of trading on Monday, a hideous rumor spread across the sixty acres of the financial district out of nowhere, like lightning. Most suspected it had first been whispered over a telephone by a cable service employee, who passed it along right alongside a massive urgent sell order. Share prices convulsed in a single sharp spasm. In five minutes, the low murmur of the curb market in Broad Street had risen to a frantic, high-pitched din of confused terror. Inside the New York Stock Exchange itself, a droning hubbub of fear swelled, and men rushed hatless in and out of the building. Was it true? every man asked, and every trembling man answered that it was a lie, planted by some unscrupulous short seller trying to cover their own losses.

Fifteen minutes later, news arrived that share prices in London had collapsed completely right at the end of their trading day. That was all it took. New York still had four full hours of trading left. The whole strategy of propping the market up with the illusion of Manderson as their savior backfired completely, annihilating all of Jeffrey’s hard work. He stood with his ear pressed to his private telephone, jaw set, listening to the story of total disaster unfold. The new Napoleon had lost his Marengo. He watched the entire financial landscape slide and crumble into chaos before his eyes. Half an hour later, the news that Manderson’s body had been found, paired with the inevitable rumor of suicide, was rolling off the presses at a dozen newspaper offices — but before a single copy could reach Wall Street, the full tornado of panic was raging, and Jeffrey and his team were being swept away like dry leaves in the gale.

All of it had sprung from nothing. Nothing in the texture of ordinary, real life had changed. The corn still ripened in the sun. Rivers carried barges and turned the wheels of a million engines. Flocks fattened on pastures, and herds numbered in the millions grazed undisturbed. Men labored at their work all across the world, no more chafing at their lot than they had the week before. The gods of war tossed and murmured restlessly, but still slept. To all the world but a million or two half-crazed, reality-blind gamblers, Manderson’s death meant nothing at all. The work of the world went on. Weeks before he died, strong hands had already been in control of every wire in the vast network of commerce and industry he had once overseen. Before his corpse was even buried, his countrymen had made a strange discovery: the existence of the great monopoly engine known as Sigsbee Manderson had never been a requirement for national prosperity, not even of the financial kind.

The panic burned itself out in two days. The pieces were picked up, the bankrupts vanished out of public sight, and the market recovered its normal tone. Even as the brief delirium was still fading, a domestic scandal in England broke that grabbed the full attention of two continents. The next morning, the Chicago Limited passenger train wrecked, and the same day a prominent politician was shot dead in the street in New Orleans by his wife’s brother. Within a week of breaking, editors across the entire United States agreed that “the Manderson story” was cold, no longer worth front page space. The tide of American tourists pouring through Europe stopped to wander the memorials and tombs of great men who died penniless, and never once spared a thought for their most famous plutocrat. Like the poet Keats, who died young and poor in Rome a hundred years before, Manderson was buried far from his native land. But where crowds of Manderson’s countrymen flock to stand around Keats’s grave in the cemetery under Monte Testaccio, there is not one visitor, nor ever will be, to stand before the rich man’s grave beside the small country church of Marlstone.

The rest of the *Record* office reeked of damp newsprint and chilled ink, drafty and cluttered, but Sir James Molloy’s private sanctum was the only room in the whole warren that felt lived-in, stuffed with leather chairs, a half-smoked Havana smoldering in an ashtray brimming with crumpled galley slips. The telephone on his mahogany desk buzzed once, sharp as a startled wasp. Sir James waved his pen without looking up, and his secretary Mr Silver abandoned his typewriter to cross the room and lift the receiver.

“Who’s that? I can barely hear you—oh, it’s Mr Bunner, is it? Sir James is swamped this afternoon, couldn’t you possibly—wait, really? Grave news? All right, hold on one moment.” He set the receiver down in front of his boss. “Calvin Bunner, Sigsbee Manderson’s right-hand man. He’s calling straight from the millionaire’s country house outside Bishopsbridge, says he has to speak to you personally.”

Sir James glowered at the phone like it had personally interrupted his lunch, then took the receiver in one big calloused hand. “Well?” he barked, his broad Irish baritone cutting through the line. For half a minute he only nodded, grunting out half-syllable replies of “yes” as he listened. Then the colour drained from his swart, black-moustached face. “Good God,” he breathed. He leaned in, clutching the plastic casing hard enough to crack it, his eyes fixed unblinking on the wall. A quick glance at the clock, and he snapped over his shoulder at Silver without taking his ear off the line: “Go find Figgis and young Williams. Run.”

Silver bolted. The man he’d left behind was a Fleet Street legend at fifty: sharp, tireless, half-cynical but entirely unpretentious, no fake mystique, no performative wit, the managing director who ran both the powerful morning *Record* and the chaotic, high-selling evening *Sun* across the street. His staff respected him more than almost any man in a industry that worshipped nothing but a good scoop, and when his brow furrowed like this, the whole office went quiet.

A few more minutes of listening, a few sharp questions, and he finally hung up. “You’re sure the police haven’t leaked a word yet? The servants haven’t gossiped to the village? Right. Bunner, I owe you one for this—come see me first time you’re in town, I mean it.” He slammed the receiver down, grabbed a tattered railway timetable off the rack, flicked through it, and swore so loud the walls rattled. Silver burst back in seconds later, trailed by Figgis, the grizzled crime reporter with spectacles and a face hewn from granite, and Williams, the sharp-eyed cub reporter who could outrun a newsboy with a bundle of extras.

“Figgis, jot this down fast,” Sir James said, all trace of shock gone, his voice clipped and steady as a machine gun. “We’re spinning a special *Sun* edition that will hit the streets before any other outlet even hears the rumour. You’ll have the copy written before the clock hits half past three.” Figgis nodded, flipped open his shorthand notebook, and pulled a chair up to the desk, face impassive as if he’d been told it was going to rain. “Silver, wire our Marlstone local correspondent right now, tell him to drop every single story he’s working on and get to Manderson’s estate, no questions. Don’t put a single word of the news in the telegram, no leaks allowed. Williams, run across the street to the *Sun* offices, find Anthony, tell him to prep a two-column front page splash that’ll knock London flat. Tell him it’s the scoop of the decade, Figgis will be over in five minutes with all the facts. On your way back, send Miss Morgan in to me, and tell the switchboard to get Mr Trent on the line as fast as humanly possible. Report back here when you’re done.” Williams vanished like a smoke wisp.

He turned straight back to Figgis, pacing the floor with his hands clasped behind his back. “Sigsbee Manderson is dead. Murdered. Shot straight through the left eye, point blank range. He, his wife, and his two secretaries have been staying at his Marlstone estate White Gables for a fortnight. He turned in at half past eleven last night as usual, no one saw him leave the house. A gardener found his body at ten this morning, laying next to a toolshed in the grounds. Death was instant. His pockets weren’t touched, no valuables stolen, but there were deep bruise marks on his wrists—he fought his killer. The local doctor is doing the post-mortem, the Bishopsbridge cops are on site and won’t say a word, they don’t have a single lead on who did it.”

Figgis scribbled a line of shorthand. “We can add that a top Scotland Yard detective is already assigned, that’s a safe bet,” he suggested. “And Mrs Manderson—prostrate with shock, refusing to see anyone, human interest beat.”

“Leave that part out,” said a quiet, calm voice. Miss Morgan, the pale, sharp, gracefully efficient head of the newsroom morgue, had slipped into the room while they talked, no one had heard her. “I met Mrs Manderson last year. She’s a woman of iron, not the fainting type. She won’t be prostrate. She’s probably down at the shed right now, helping the police pick through the clues.”

Sir James huffed a quiet laugh. “You’re right, Figgis. Cut it. Off you go, run. Miss Morgan, you know what we need.”

“Our full Manderson biography is already fully updated,” she said, flipping through the mental index of the morgue she carried in her head. “I reviewed it three months ago, it’s ready for tomorrow’s *Record* front page. The *Sun* can run the shorter profile we put out when he sorted the Berlin potash cartel dispute two years back, it was tight, well written, perfect for their space. We have two exclusive portraits, one a charcoal sketch Mr Trent drew of Manderson on a transatlantic crossing three years ago, far better than any press photo. I’ll send them up to your desk in ten minutes to pick which one to run. The only gap we have: the last fast train down to Marlstone left half an hour ago, we can’t get a special correspondent down there in time for tomorrow’s first edition.”

Sir James groaned, leaning back in his chair. “This woman knows Bradshaw by heart,” he complained to Silver. “None of us have a single trick she doesn’t see coming. Make a mistake someday, Miss Morgan, a big silly one, just so the rest of us don’t feel like utter fools.” She gave him the flicker of a smile and vanished back to the morgue.

Sir James picked up the phone to talk to Anthony across the street, the firebrand editor who called himself the Murat of Fleet Street, who lived for beating the clock on a breaking story. Five minutes later, a copy boy poked his head in to say Mr Trent was on the line. Sir James cut his conversation short.

“Trent!” he barked into the receiver.

The voice on the other end was half amused, half annoyed. “Hullo be blowed, Molloy. I’m mid-canvas, right in the middle of the best part of a new landscape. This better be important.”

“It is. Sigsbee Manderson’s been murdered. Shot through the head, body found this morning by his gardener, cops have no leads. I want you down there on the case for us.”

There was a long grunting pause on the other end. “Look, Molloy,” Trent said, voice half-querulous, half-serious. “It might be a big clever mystery, it might be dead simple. Could be some tramp he chased off the grounds at two in the morning, who pulled a gun, knew enough not to steal anything so no one would suspect robbery. And for the record? I wouldn’t lift a finger to hang a man who put a bullet in that greedy, brutal old tyrant. Half the financial world wanted Manderson dead.”

Sir James smiled, he already knew he’d won. “Come off it. You’re dying to go poke around the scene. If it bores you, you can walk away no strings attached. The only train we can catch leaves Paddington at midnight. My fast car’s in the garage, but even that won’t get you there before dark tonight. You can crash on the slow one, we’ll wire ahead to get you a hotel room near White Gables.”

“Fine,” Trent grumbled. “I’ll be at your office in forty minutes. And for the record? I love slow trains. I’m the master of the railway nap, the king of the dining car snack. Don’t rush me.”

Sir James hung up. Just then, a high, screaming din erupted from the street below. He leaned out the open window, and saw a swarm of grinning, ragged newsboys pouring down the steps of the *Sun* building, bundles of extra editions under their arms, waving big placards scrawled in thick black ink:

The boys were already yelling the headline at the top of their lungs as they swarmed towards Fleet Street, and the first passersby were stopping mid-step, pulling out pennies to grab a copy. Sir James jingled the loose silver in his pocket, grinning. “Best headline we’ve run in ten years,” he said to Silver at his elbow.
It was Sigsbee Manderson’s epitaph.

At eight o’clock sharp the morning after Manderson’s body was discovered, Nathaniel Burton Cupples stood on the veranda of the Marlstone Hotel, giving the matter of his morning meal his full, deliberate attention. This was no turn of phrase: Cupples approached every conscious action of his life with the careful deliberation he had honed over decades as a banker, and he reflected that the prior day’s chaos and shock had left him far short of his usual nourishment. Now, up and pottering for a full hour, his stomach made its demands clear. He settled his plan: one extra egg, a third slice of toast, all other courses taken at their usual measured portions. The remaining deficit would be addressed over luncheon, a meal he could plan for later.

With that small decision settled, he turned his connoisseur’s eye to the view spread before him. The rugged Jurassic coast stretched out to a glass-smooth summer sea, a great sea-worn arch piercing the headland rock like a natural gateway, while rolling tiers of pasture, ploughed fields, and coppice sloped gently up from the cliff tops toward the hazy purple of the distant moors. Cupples loved quiet landscapes, with the soft, unshowy passion he brought to all his small, consistent joys.

Nearing sixty, spare and wiry despite a lifelong delicate constitution, he had the unmistakeable gentle air of a clergyman, though he had never taken orders. A thin, straggling beard and moustache did little to hide his kind, mild-mouthed expression; his eyes were sharp and pleasant, his nose and jaw angular, the whole effect amplified by his plain dark suit and soft black hat. A widower with no children, now retired from the bank, he was a respected stalwart of the London Positivist Society. His days were spent poring over obscure books and haunting museum galleries, his weirdly wide, disconnected stores of knowledge making him a beloved fixture at the quiet, unhurried dinner parties of curators and research professors. Montaigne was his favourite author, and he had escaped his grim, strictly devout childhood home with two unbroken gifts: a bottomless well of quiet kindness, and a knack for unforced, unhumorous innocent gaiety.

He was halfway through his meal at a small veranda table when a great purring motor car swung up the hotel drive. “Who is that, I wonder?” he asked the listless young waiter.
“Das is der hotel manager,” the man mumbled. “He go to meet der gendleman on der early train.”

When the car pulled up, a long, lanky, loosely built man in rumpled tweeds bounded up the veranda steps, flinging his hat onto an empty chair with the easy, unselfconscious energy of a great overgrown boy. Cupples’s face lit up in unfeigned pleasure.
“Cupples, by all that’s impossible!” the newcomer cried, pouncing before Cupples could stand to greet him, seizing his hand in a vice-like grip. “My luck’s holding today—this is the second pleasant surprise I’ve stumbled on in an hour. How are you, you best of all old friends? What on earth are you doing here, mooning over a half-eaten breakfast like a Romantic poet mourning the ruins of a feast? I swear I could not be happier to see you.”
“I half expected you, Trent,” Cupples smiled back, beaming. “You look in wonderful form. But you’ve not had breakfast yet, I’ll wager. Pull up a chair at my table, won’t you?”
“Will I? An enormous breakfast, with refined conversation and tears of recognition, exactly what I need,” Trent laughed. “Send that young Siegfried of a waiter to lay a place, I’ll go wash my hands in two minutes flat.”

He was back before Cupples had finished phoning the office to arrange the extra setting, already pouring himself a cup of tea and eyeing the menu like a man who knew he would get no chance to eat again for hours. “I expect this is going to be a brutal day,” he said in that characteristic jerky, staccato way he spoke when his mind was racing ahead. “I probably won’t get another bite till this evening. You know why I’m here, right?”
“Undoubtedly,” Cupples nodded. “You’ve come down to cover the Manderson murder.”
“Bit of a colourless way to put it,” Trent grinned, dissecting a fried sole with sharp, efficient movements. “I prefer to think of myself as the avenger of blood, sent to hunt the guilty down and vindicate the honour of society. The *Record* wired me last night, the manager was waiting for me at the station at seven a.m. with a motor the size of a haystack. He’s a huge fan, knows all my old cases better than I do. Before we even got to the hotel, he asked if I wanted to see the body—they’re doing the post-mortem this morning, I was just in time. Drove me straight down to Dr Stock’s surgery in the village, reeled off every detail of the case on the way. The constable on duty didn’t even kick up a fuss, as long as I didn’t name him in the paper.”
“I saw the body before they moved it,” Cupples put in mildly. “I thought it was most unusual the bullet to the eye scarcely disfigured his face at all, barely any blood. His wrists were scratched and bruised. With your trained eye, I’m sure you picked up on far more suggestive little details.”
“Details, yes, though I can’t say they add up to anything yet,” Trent said through a mouthful of fish. “Odd, contradictory little things. For starters—you saw the bruises on his wrists? Manderson never showed his wrists to anyone, did he? He always wore stiff starched cuffs that came well down over his hands, the manager confirmed it. But his cuffs were pulled right up inside his coat sleeves, like he’d hurried his jacket on without stopping to yank them down. That’s why you saw the scratches.”
“That’s very suggestive,” Cupples said. “One would infer he was in a terrible hurry to get dressed.”
“Or was he?” Trent shot back. “The manager said exactly the same thing. Manderson was famously fussy about his clothes, he told me—look at his shoe laces, they’re tied all lopsided, done in a rush. He left his false teeth on his bedroom nightstand, too. That all screams hurry, right? But wait—his hair is parted perfectly, every strand laid exactly where he always put it. He had on a full set of underclothes, polished studs in his shirt, sock suspenders, his watch, keys, all his usual pocket clutter. Why would a man rush through his shoes, forget his teeth, but spend three minutes carefully parting his hair? He didn’t even wash that morning, which for a man as neat as Manderson, that looks like he was in a panic from the second he woke up. But his watch? He always kept his gold watch in the special wash-leather lined pocket on the right side of his waistcoat. It was in the left. No settled, habitual man does that by accident. Half the clues say violent agitation, half say careful deliberation. I’m not guessing yet. I need to get the lay of the land first, win the household over to my side.”

Cupples smiled at him, benevolent. “That, actually, is where I can help you. I told you I half expected you here. Mrs. Manderson is my niece.”
Trent dropped his knife and fork with a loud clatter. “Cupples, you’re pulling my leg.”
“Not at all, I assure you,” Cupples said earnestly. “Her father John Peter Domecq was my wife’s older brother. I never mentioned her or the marriage before, it was always a painful subject for me, so I never brought it up. The house, White Gables, you passed it on the drive from the station—red roof among the poplars, three hundred yards from here, separate from the village. Last night when I was over there, Bunner, one of Manderson’s two secretaries, was saying how much he wished the *Record* would send you, since the local police seemed completely stumped. Mabel—my niece—read some of your old reports, she remembered your Abinger case coverage. She’s been dreading the horde of reporters descending on the house, she begged me last night to keep them all away, but when I told her you were an old personal friend of mine, someone who always treated people’s feelings with tact, she said she would put no barriers in your way at all. The whole house and grounds are open to you, you can question anyone you like. She’s keeping to her own sitting room, too upset to meet you, that’s all.”

Trent reached across the table and shook Cupples’s hand silently, his face serious. He filled his pipe slowly once he finished breakfast, leaning against the veranda rail staring out at the blue sea. “Cupples,” he said quietly, “is there anything about this whole situation that you know, and have been holding back from me?”
Cupples gave a little start, blinking at him in surprise. “What do you mean?”
“I mean—no one around here seems particularly cut up that Manderson was murdered,” Trent said gently. “The manager talked about him like he was a total stranger, even though they’ve been summer neighbours for years. You’re talking about his death like it’s a minor museum theft, not a man gunned down in the garden. And his own wife, apparently, is calm enough to arrange full access for a detective instead of falling apart. Was there something very wrong with Manderson, Cupples? I shared a boat with him once, never spoke to him, only knew his public reputation was vile. But this could change everything about the case, that’s the only reason I ask.”

Cupples gazed out over the sea for a long minute, fingering his thin beard, before he turned back. “I see no reason not to tell you, strictly between us. No one ever liked Manderson. The people who lived with him liked him least of all.”
“Why?”
“It’s hard to put into words. He was never rude, or cruel in public, even could be charming when he wanted to be. But you could feel there wasn’t a scrap of sympathy in him. He would have sacrificed any living thing that got in the way of his plans, no hesitation. But the real trouble was with Mabel. She’s the sweetest, most honourable woman alive, I’ve known her since she was a baby. For months before he died, Manderson had shut himself off from her completely. He never explained what was wrong, never spoke a civil word, nursed some quiet, unspoken grievance against her. She was miserable, too proud to beg him to explain, too proud to tell anyone what was happening. A week ago she wrote to me, the only relative she has left, and I came straight down. I couldn’t bring myself to stay in his house, you know how I feel about men like him, the way he crushed the Pennsylvania coal miners three years ago. I set up at this hotel, met her here every day. Finally last Sunday morning, I ran into him as he walked past the gate, and I called him over. I told him straight that he owed my niece an explanation for treating her like that.”

Trent smiled a little to himself, charmed by the image of mild Cupples confronting the ruthless millionaire. “How did he take it?”
“Badly. He told me not to butt in, that his wife could look after herself. The tone of it—there was something menacing there. I lost my temper, for the first time in decades. I yelled at him, right there in full view of half a dozen hotel guests on the veranda. I told him the law gave wives rights against treatment like that, that men like him were unfit to walk the earth. He never raised his voice. Just stared at me, then smiled that cold little smile of his, and turned to walk back to White Gables. That was the last time I spoke to him. I saw him once more on the golf course later that day, but we didn’t exchange a word. Next morning he was dead.”

The two men sat in silence for a long minute, as a group of chattering bathers trooped up the steps and took a table nearby. Cupples stood, took Trent’s arm, and led him down the veranda steps to the long empty tennis lawn at the side of the hotel, where no one could overhear them.
“I have a very specific reason for telling you all this,” he began.
Trent struck a match, holding it to his pipe and puffing till the tobacco caught. “I think I can guess,” he said, blowing a thin cloud of smoke into the warm June air. “You knew perfectly well I’d work out sooner or later that there was no happy marriage here, that the obvious first line of inquiry for any detective would be to look at the grieving widow. You didn’t want me wasting days chasing the wrong lead, speculating on absurd theories about her being involved. So you laid the whole truth out for me, and made very sure I knew exactly what kind of woman your niece is. Am I close?”

Philip Trent, the son of a painter and a celebrated artist in his own right by his late twenties, had carved out a respected, profitable niche for himself in the English art world before he ever stumbled into detection. He owed that success not just to his original, vigorous style and his habit of slow, steady work punctuated by bursts of fiery creative inspiration, but to the quiet magic that made people instinctively like him. His father’s reputation had opened doors, and a comfortable inheritance spared him the tedious pity reserved for “starving artists” – but the real secret was the man himself: high-spirited, sharp-witted, with a genuine, unobtrusive curiosity about everyone around him, who never forced anyone to stand on ceremony. At thirty-two, he still had a healthy appetite for laughter and unscripted adventure, his knowledge of art and its history paired with a wide, unstructured cultural education anchored by a lifelong love of poetry.

His leap to national fame, a hundred times bigger than his art ever earned him, started on a random rainy afternoon. He’d picked up a newspaper to find the whole country fixated on a rare British railway murder, its circumstances so baffling two innocent men had already been taken in as suspects. Trent, who’d never cared for crime news before, found himself digging through every newspaper account he could get his hands on. The facts grabbed his imagination the same way a half-finished canvas did, thrumming with that same electric high he only ever felt in the thick of inspiration. By the end of the day, he’d dashed off a long, detailed letter to the editor of the *Record*, the paper that had run the most thorough coverage, laying out gaps in the public evidence exactly as Poe had done for the Mary Rogers case decades before, pointing to a seemingly random witness as the real killer.

Sir James Molloy, the *Record*’s legendary editor, printed the letter in bold leaded type – and that very same evening, the police arrested the man Trent had named, who confessed immediately. Molloy tracked Trent down fast, the two hit it off instantly, and soon Trent was spending hours wandering the *Record*’s roaring basement press rooms, so charmed by the spectacle of the machines that he painted a “machinery-scape” in the style of Heinrich Kley that Molloy bought on sight. A few months later, the Ilkley mystery broke, and Molloy invited Trent to dinner, laid out an absurdly large paycheck, and talked him into working as the paper’s special correspondent on the case, pointing out he could write, talk to people, and had that rare mix of wild imagination and steady good judgement that made for great detection.

Trent took the gig, mostly out of stubborn pride – he hated being afraid of the unfamiliar. And he pulled it off, beating the police to the full truth a second time, his name on every front page in the country. He went back to painting after that, no interest in full-time journalism, but over the next few years Molloy called him in roughly thirty times to crack similar cases across the country and abroad. Sometimes he turned the jobs down if he was wrapped up in a canvas, sometimes the police beat him to the punch – but his irregular work for the paper turned him into one of the most famous public figures in England. Characteristically, he’d made the Molloy papers promise never to print a single personal detail about him, so the public knew nothing beyond his name.

As he strode up the sloping road to White Gables, the Manderson estate, he told himself the case might turn out to be disarmingly simple. Old Cupples, Mrs. Manderson’s uncle, was a sensible man, but he was clearly biased about his niece – though the Marlstone hotel manager, who’d gushed about her to Trent that morning, had been even more emphatic about her goodness than he was about her beauty. “Not a kid in the village doesn’t light up when they hear her voice,” the man had said, “and the grown-ups too. Everyone’s been waiting for her summer visits for years. She’s not all soft kindness, either – got backbone, plenty of grit. There’s not a soul in Marlstone that’s not sorry for her in her trouble, even if a few of us think she’s finally better off.” Trent was very eager to meet her.

Past a wide, neatly kept lawn and hedge, the house came into view: two stories of dull red brick, topped with the pair of grand gables that gave it its name, barely ten years old, the kind of quiet, opulent home that wrapped itself in gentle peace even in the busiest season. Beyond the road, rich meadow sloped down to the cliff edge, and behind it, wooded hills stretched across a broad valley to the open moors. It seemed absurd that a violent murder could have happened in such a perfectly ordered, well-cared-for place – except that forty yards from the front steps, tucked between the garden hedge and the sun-bleached white road, stood the little wooden gardener’s toolshed against which Manderson’s body had been found.

Trent walked past the main drive gate, followed the road to the small unmarked white service gate at the edge of the property, which swung open easily on well-oiled hinges. He followed the narrow path between the boundary hedge and a tall wall of rhododendrons until he reached the shed, searching the wide stretch of uncut grass where the body had lain on the side facing away from the house. The grass was trampled flat there, but no footprints, no stray bullet, no discarded weapon turned up, no matter how carefully he knelt and combed through the turf with his fingers.

He stood, brushing grass off his trousers, at the sound of the front door slamming. A young man was hurrying down the drive toward the main gate, and when he heard Trent’s step on the gravel, he spun around so fast his whole body tensed. His face was startlingly pale, haggard and drawn, though no older than thirty, bright blue eyes heavy with days of exhaustion. Trent noted his broad shoulders, lithe strong build, the easy, polished bearing that spoke of an Oxford education.

“If you’re Mr. Trent,” the young man said pleasantly, “you’re expected. Mr. Cupples phoned over from the hotel. My name’s Marlowe – I was Mr. Manderson’s secretary.”

Trent liked him immediately. Even half-collapsed with fatigue, he radiated that quiet, clean confidence of his class, but there was a sharp, thoughtful glint in his tired eyes that suggested he was constantly turning over facts no one else had noticed. “Terrible business for all of you,” Trent said. “I’m sorry it’s knocked you so off balance.”

“Just a little run ragged, that’s all,” Marlowe said, wearily. “I was driving Manderson’s car all Sunday night and most of yesterday, and didn’t sleep a wink after hearing the news. I’m off down to the doctor’s office now to sort out the inquest – it’ll be tomorrow, probably. If you head up to the house and ask for Mr. Bunner, he’s the other secretary, an American, great fellow, he’ll show you around. Oh, and there’s a detective already here – Inspector Murch, from Scotland Yard. Got here yesterday before I got back from Southampton, interviewing everyone. He’s in the library now, the room with the big French windows facing the lawn over there.”

“Murch?” Trent laughed out loud. “He’s an old friend of mine. How on earth did he get here so fast? Did Scotland Yard start a secret flying corps without telling anyone?”

Marlowe shook his head, waved, and headed off down the drive. Trent padded across the thick, noiseless lawn, and a moment later was leaning in the open French window, grinning at the sight of Inspector Murch’s broad back hunched over a table stacked with papers, his grizzled short hair sticking up in tufts.

“‘Twas ever thus,” Trent intoned in a mock-melancholic tone, and Murch spun around so fast the papers scattered. “From childhood’s hour I’ve seen my fondest hopes decay. I thought for once I’d beat Scotland Yard to a fresh crime scene, and now the biggest man in the entire Met is already camped out at the desk.”

Murch smiled grimly, stepping over to shake his hand. “I was expecting you. This is exactly your kind of case.”

“Since you were all thinking of me,” Trent said, stepping into the sunlit library, “you could have kept my old rival locked up somewhere. You’ve got a full day’s head start on me already, I know. How did you make it here so quick? Did you make a deal with the devil? The Home Secretary ought to release a public statement about police magic at once.”

“Nothing so exciting,” Murch said, his usual professional stolidity unshaken. “I was on leave with my wife at Havley, twelve miles down the coast. The local force heard about the murder, found me, I wired the Commissioner, and he put me on the case immediately. I bicycled over last evening, been at it ever since.”

“Speaking of your wife,” Trent said, glancing idly around the room, “how is she?”

“Never better, thanks,” Murch said. “She still talks about the silly games you used to play with our boys. But don’t think you can distract me, Trent. I know your tricks – you’re already scanning every inch of the room while you’re chatting. I know you have Mrs. Manderson’s full permission to investigate the grounds and the house.”

“Guilty,” Trent said, grinning. “And I’m going to beat you to the answer this time, old man. I still owe you one for beating me to the Abinger case. But if we’re done with the pleasantries, let’s play the game like we always do.”

The game, the two men’s long-running unspoken understanding, was famous between them. Murch was one of the most shrewd, brave officers in the CID, a broad, generous man with a string of takedowns of some of London’s most dangerous criminals to his name, and the two had clicked instantly the first time they’d worked a case together. They’d agreed long ago on the rules of “detective sportsmanship”: Trent would never publish a lead he got exclusively from police sources, and neither of them would hide a breakthrough they deemed vital to the case – but both reserved the right to keep quiet about small, unconfirmed inspirations until they could prove them, competing fairly for the credit, the press and the police pitting Trent’s fast, imaginative brain against Murch’s steady, methodical experience.

Standing on either side of the open French window, the soft golden haze of the summer countryside spread out before them, they began to lay out all the known facts of the case. Trent pulled out his thin notebook, and began sketching a rough floor plan of the library, a habit he’d picked up as an artist that had solved more than one case for him. It was a big, bright corner room, windows spanning two walls: the roll-top desk stood to the left of the main table by the window, the inner door was on the far left wall facing a wide casement window, a carved old oak corner cupboard stood against the far wall, and the rest of the wall space was crammed with rows of the great English novelists, essayists and poets, bought by the yard and clearly never opened, their sober leather bindings untouched. A few old oak chairs, a modern armchair, a swivel office chair by the desk, and the only small portable items: a striking blue porcelain bowl on the table, a clock and cigar boxes on the mantel, and a standing telephone on top of the desk.

“Seen the body?” Murch asked. Trent nodded. “My first thought was a tramp robbery murder, though that’s almost unheard of around here. But that theory doesn’t hold water at all. No marks of forced entry anywhere on the house, the body wasn’t robbed of anything – and the whole thing looks like a textbook suicide, except for three huge problems. For one thing, there’s no gun anywhere, not a trace of it within a hundred yards of the body. For another, Manderson had fresh scratches and bruises on his wrists, the kind you get fighting with someone. And last – no one shoots themselves in the eye.”

“Three good points,” Trent said, leaning against the window frame and clasping his knees. “And I’ve got a fourth, from the hotel manager. Manderson got fully dressed, put on his coat and shoes, everything before he went out that morning. But he left his false teeth on his nightstand. Who the hell takes the time to get all dressed up to shoot themselves, so they look respectable when they’re found, but forget their dentures?”

“Never thought of that,” Murch admitted. “That’s a solid point. I ruled out suicide before you even said that. I went over every inch of this house this morning. What do you say we apply full general suspicion to everyone who lives here? I suspect Mrs. Manderson. I suspect both the secretaries – which one of them looks the shadiest to you? I suspect the butler, the lady’s maid, all the other servants, and most of all I suspect the boot-boy.”

“Nice try,” Murch snorted. “There is no boot-boy. The staff is just the butler, lady’s maid, cook, three maids, one of them a young teen, and a chauffeur who’s off sick with a broken wrist.”

“Then I suspect the gardener,” Trent said. “The mysterious shadowy gardener who no one’s mentioned yet. He’s hiding in the bushes, I know it. When was he last here?”

“He lives in the village, comes twice a week. Was here last Friday. Already talked to him.”

“Then he’s definitely a suspect. Now, what about the bedrooms? You said you went over them?”

“Both of them. Nothing there. Manderson’s was almost a monk’s cell, bare, no valet, he insisted on doing everything for himself, hated frills. Not a mark or a clue anywhere. His bedroom connects straight to Mrs. Manderson’s – hers is full of pretty, nice things, nothing like his. She moved out the morning they found the body, said she couldn’t bear to sleep in a room that opened into her murdered husband’s. She’s staying in a spare guest room now, totally understandable for a woman. And for weeks before he died, the whole house staff noticed Manderson was acting strange, cold and silent to his wife, barely spoke to her at all the last week. Everyone said he looked like he was waiting for something terrible to happen.”

Trent scribbled the note down, a small smile tugging at his mouth. He knew that flat, deliberately casual tone in Murch’s voice. Either the inspector had already found something that pointed straight at Mrs. Manderson, and didn’t want Trent chasing it, or he’d already decided she was completely innocent, and didn’t mind if Trent wasted half his day investigating her.

Either way, the game was on, and it was far more interesting than he’d expected.

There are quiet, unaccountable moments in life when the hidden, half-busy part of ourselves slips a flash of foreknowledge into our waking minds: that unshakable, unforced certainty that everything is about to break right, not the desperate bravado of a man staring down fate, not the daft persistent optimism of a fool, but a little wild spark of conviction that rises unbidden like a bird flushing out of heather. The general knows at dawn his battle will turn to victory; the golfer on the green knows before he swings that the long putt will drop. As Trent climbed the narrow staircase to the second floor outside the library, that exact certainty settled over him, thick and warm. A jumble of stray observations and half-formed inferences he’d not yet been able to weave together suddenly hummed in his head; the tiny, seemingly meaningless details he’d noted earlier now felt like pieces of a puzzle that were about to slot straight into place. He did not know what the full truth was yet, but he knew, beyond any doubt, that the light was about to break.

The bedrooms lined both sides of a wide, carpeted upper hall, lit by a tall sash window at the far end, where the corridor turned sharp right to lead to the servants’ wing. The butler Martin’s room was a quirk, tucked off a tiny half-landing halfway to the attic floor. Trent glanced in as he passed: a cramped, spotless, completely unremarkable little room. The rest of the stairs he climbed with exaggerated, deliberate care, pressing close to the wall and setting each foot down slow, but the old dry timber still let out a series of loud, unapologetic creaks that echoed down the empty hall.

He made straight for Manderson’s room, the first door on the right when he reached the landing. He tested the latch and lock, both working smoothly, ran a finger over the key’s wards, then turned to take in the space. It was shockingly small, almost bare, a far cry from the opulence most plutocrats surrounded themselves with. The bed was unmade, sheets and blankets tangled and thrown back as if the man had torn himself out of it in a hurry that morning the body was found. Bright sunlight spilled through the window over the rumpled bedding, glinting off the gold work of a set of dentures soaking in a shallow glass bowl on the plain little bedside table, next to a dented wrought-iron candlestick. A few items of discarded clothing lay draped over one of the two rush-seated chairs, and the top of the chest of drawers pressed into service as a dressing table was strewn with the half-ordered mess of a man who’d dressed in a terrible rush. Trent’s eye picked out at once that the dead man had never washed, never shaved before he left the room at dawn. He poked the dental plate gently with a fingertip, frowning at the odd, unexplainable detail of it left half-submerged there, while the sun-warmed empty air of the room began to hum with a faint, gruesome weight. He could almost see the man there, haggard, moving by the dim first light of day, glancing over his shoulder every few seconds at the connecting door behind which his wife slept, his eyes wide with some nameless terror.

Trent shivered, and turned to yank open the two tall wall cupboards flanking the bed to ground himself in hard facts. They were stuffed full of fine clothing, a huge, varied selection that was clearly one of the only small luxuries the grim millionaire had ever allowed himself. The same ran to his shoes: rows and rows of them, carefully treed and polished, ranged along two low shelves against the far wall, not a single heavy work boot among the lot. Trent, an amateur of fine leather footwear himself, ran an appreciative eye over the collection: it was clear Manderson had been proud of his small, neat foot, every pair narrow, round-toed, exquisitely stitched, all cut from the exact same last.

Then his eyes narrowed, locking on a pair of scuffed patent-leather shoes on the top shelf. These were the exact pair Inspector Murch had described earlier, the ones Manderson had been wearing the night he died. They were well worn, but they’d been polished not long ago. Something about their uppers snagged his attention. He leaned in, comparing their finish to the pairs on either side, then lifted them to run a finger along the seam where the uppers joined the soles.

Unconsciously, he began to whistle, soft and precise, a short melodic phrase Murch would have recognized instantly. Most men who are fiercely self-controlled have a tiny, involuntary tell for when they’re holding back excitement: Murch had long ago noticed that whenever Trent picked up a hot new scent in a case, he whistled that exact bar of Mendelssohn’s *Songs Without Words* in A Major.

He turned the shoes over, measured them with a marked tape he pulled from his pocket, then squinted hard at the soles. Tucked in the narrow angle between the heel and the instep on each shoe was a faint, smudged trace of the same red gravel that lined the drive and the paths near the copse where Manderson’s body had been found.

Trent set the shoes down on the floor, paced over to the window, and stared out at the lawn, still whistling under his breath, his eyes unfocused. Once his lips parted and he muttered a sharp, soft expletive of pure, sudden enlightenment. When he turned back, he moved fast, checking every single pair of shoes on the shelves one after the other, every last one.

That done, he picked up the clothes draped over the chair, examined every seam, then folded and replaced them. He hunted through both wardrobes a second time, pored over the mess on the dressing table for a second long stretch, then sank down onto the empty chair, dropped his head in his hands, and sat staring at the carpet for several full minutes. Finally he stood, and pulled open the inner connecting door that led straight to Mrs. Manderson’s bedroom.

One glance told him the big room had been stripped bare in a tearing hurry, all traces of the lady’s personal life cleared out. The usual array of cosmetics, perfumes, and trinkets that crowded a woman’s dressing table was gone. No hats, gloves, veils, or half-hidden handkerchiefs peeked out from under pillows or tumbled out of drawers. The space looked as neat and impersonal as an unused guest room, but every detail of the furniture and soft, muted colour scheme spoke to an unconventional, exacting artistic taste. Trent, his eye trained to pick up on small clues about a person’s character, could easily picture the quiet, lonely woman who’d lived here, and his brow drew tight as he thought of the quiet burdens laid on her life, and the story of the crime that was slowly taking solid, unexpected shape in his head.

He walked over to the tall French window facing the door, opened it, and stepped out onto a small iron-railed balcony. Below him a stretch of broad lawn ran straight down, only separated from the house wall by a narrow flower bed, sloping sharply at the far end toward the old orchard. The room’s other sash window looked out directly above the library’s garden entrance, and a second door in the far corner of the room opened onto the main upper hall, the door the maid used to enter each morning.

Trent sat down on the empty bed, pulled his notebook out, and quickly sketched a rough floor plan of both bedrooms. The bed sat tucked in the corner between the connecting door and the sash window, its headboard pressed up against the shared wall between the two rooms. He stared at the pillows for a long moment, then lay down deliberately full length on the bed, and looked straight through the open connecting door into Manderson’s empty room.

Satisfied with that observation, he stood, noted down on his plan that each side of the bed held a small covered side table, the one furthest from the door holding a graceful copper electric lamp strung on a long loose wire to the wall. He studied the lamp thoughtfully, then checked the wall switches for the rest of the room’s lights, all mounted by the main door, far out of reach of anyone lying in bed. He confirmed every fixture was working, then turned on his heel, walked straight back into Manderson’s room, and rang the bell pull by the door.

“I need a favour, Martin,” he said, as the impassive butler appeared in the doorway. “I’d like you to send Mrs. Manderson’s maid up to speak with me.”

“Certainly, sir.” Martin nodded.

“What sort of woman is she? Sharp, on the ball?”

“She’s French, sir,” Martin said succinctly, pausing for a beat before adding, “She hasn’t been with us long, but I’d say the girl has more than enough worldly sense to look after herself.”

Trent grinned. “Good, I’m not easily flustered. I just have a few questions for her.”

“I’ll send her up at once, sir.” Martin withdrew, and Trent wandered the small room, hands behind his back.

The maid, Célestine, appeared sooner than he expected, a tiny neat figure in a black uniform. She’d spotted Trent crossing the lawn from her window earlier that day, and had been desperately hoping the famous mystery-solver—whose reputation was as legendary in the servants’ quarters as it was anywhere else—would send for her. Her nerves were frayed to breaking point after the murder; the stuffy Inspector Murch had chilled her into silence with his official gruffness, but Trent had not struck her as the sort of policeman who crushed dramatic scenes, and from a distance he’d seemed perfectly *sympathique*.

Still, instinct told her coquetry would be out of place here. She entered with an air of open, amiable candour. “Monsieur wished to speak to me?” she said, adding helpfully, “My name is Célestine.”

“Naturally,” Trent said, all business. “Tell me, Célestine: when you brought your mistress her tea at seven yesterday morning, was the door between these two bedrooms open?”

Célestine lit up like a lamp at once. “Oh oui! It was open as it always is, monsieur, and I shut it as I always do! But you must understand, listen—come with me, I will show you, it is simple!” She tugged gently at his arm, pulling him through the connecting door into the big bedroom. “See! I come in from the hall door there, carrying the tray, I walk toward the bed. Before I reach the bed, the door to Monsieur Manderson’s room is right here on my right, wide open—but the door swings in toward the bed, not toward me! I never see into his room at all when I push it shut. It is madame’s strict order to shut it first thing, every morning. Yesterday it was exactly the same! Madame was still fast asleep, she saw nothing, I shut the door, set down the tray, opened the curtains, laid out her things, and left—that is all!” She paused, breathless, and spread her hands wide.

Trent nodded, his face grave. “I see exactly how it was. Thank you, Célestine. So Mr. Manderson was supposed to still be in his room the whole time your mistress was getting dressed and eating her breakfast in her sitting room, right? No one noticed he was gone at all?”

“Oui, monsieur.”

“Nobody missed him, in fact,” Trent said. “Thank you, you’ve been a great help.”

Célestine turned at the outer door, her hand on the knob, and a sudden fierce passion flooded her small dark face. “It is nothing, monsieur. I hope you catch the man who killed him. But I do not regret Monsieur Manderson, not at all!” She switched into rapid, fiery French, her words spilling out. “I would throw myself into the fire for madame, she is so sweet, so gentle! But that man—sour, silent, cold! I could not bear him one minute longer! The very idea that people like him walk the earth—!”

“Enough of that racket, Célestine!” Trent cut in sharply, his own fluent student’s French coming back to him in a rush. “That’s quite the scene you’re putting on, it’s getting tedious, you know. Pull yourself together, mademoiselle, it’s terribly reckless. If the inspector downstairs heard you talking like that you’d find yourself in real trouble. And stop waving your fists around, you’ll knock something over.” He softened a little as she calmed down under his steady gaze. “I see you’re far more relieved than the rest of us that Mr. Manderson is gone. I almost suspect he never gave you so much as a second glance, did he?”

“He barely ever looked at me at all!” Célestine said simply.

“Now that is truly the last straw,” Trent said, grinning. “You’d be quite the entertainment at a small tea party, that’s for sure. Well, I’m very busy. Good day, Célestine. You are quite a beauty, you know.”

Célestine took the line as an unexpected compliment, her balance restored. She flashed a quick bright smile over her shoulder and slipped out the door, the lock clicking softly behind her.

Left alone, Trent muttered a couple of equally fiery French curses under his breath, then turned back to his problem. He fetched the pair of patent leather shoes he’d examined earlier, set one on each of the two chairs, and sat down opposite them, hands in his pockets, eyes fixed unblinkingly on the two dumb, leather witnesses. Now and then he whistled a few almost inaudible bars of that Mendelssohn tune. The house was dead quiet outside, save for the faint chirp of birds in the trees beyond the open window, and the soft rustle of the climbing ivy on the sill. Trent’s face had gone hard and sombre with thought, and he did not move for half an hour straight.

Finally he stood fast, replaced the shoes carefully back on their shelf, and stepped out onto the landing. Directly opposite him were two more bedroom doors. He opened the first one he came to, and stepped into a messy, lived-in space that was clearly not Manderson’s. Fishing rods and golf clubs leaned tangled in one corner, a stack of books teetered in another. The dressing table and mantelpiece were heaped with the jumble of a man who never bothered to tidy: pipes, penknives, golf balls, old letters, photos, tins of tobacco. Fine etchings hung on the walls, a row of shoes and boots lined up under the far window. Trent studied the shoes intently for a long minute, measured several of them with his tape, whistling very softly all the while. Then he sank down on the edge of the unmade bed, his gaze roving gloomily over the room.

His eyes landed on the photos on the mantel first. He picked up one of Marlowe and Manderson out riding, two more of famous Alpine peaks, a faded old print of three ragged young men dressed in costume as 16th century foot soldiers, one of them unmistakeably the sharp-eyed secretary Marlowe. A framed portrait of a stately old woman, who looked a little like Marlowe, sat in the corner. Trent pulled a cigarette out of an open box on the mantel, lit it, then picked up a flat leather case sitting right next to the box.

It flipped open easily, revealing a small, exquisitely made light revolver, and a loose handful of cartridges. The initials *J.M.* were engraved deep into the stock.

A footstep sounded on the stairs. Trent had just swung open the revolver’s breech to peer down the barrel when Inspector Murch appeared in the doorway. “I was wondering where you’d got to—” he began, then stopped short when he saw what Trent was holding. “Whose is that pistol, Mr. Trent?” he asked, mild as ever.

“Looks like it belongs to the man who lives in this room, John Marlowe,” Trent said lightly, tapping the engraved initials. “Found it on the mantel there. Seems like a handy little thing. Looks like it was very carefully cleaned not too long ago, but I don’t know much about firearms.”

“Oh, I do,” Murch said quietly, taking the pistol gently from his hand. “Firearms are a bit of a speciality of mine, as you know. And you don’t need an expert to spot what’s important here.” He set the revolver back in its case on the mantel, fished a cartridge out of the box, laid it on his big palm, then pulled a small leaden bullet out of his waistcoat pocket and set it next to the cartridge. The bullet was slightly dented at the nose, scratched with bright new fresh marks across its sides.

“That’s the one they pulled from Manderson’s head?” Trent murmured, leaning in.

“That’s him,” Murch nodded. “Lodged in the bone at the back of his skull. Dr. Stock dug it out less than an hour ago, sent it right up to me. The bright new scratches are from his surgical tools. The rest of the marks? Those are from the rifling grooves of a gun barrel—exactly like the one you’re holding. Same make, same calibre. No other gun makes that exact pattern on a bullet.”

For a long quiet moment, the two men stared at each other across the case that held the revolver. “This whole case is backwards,” Trent said finally. “It makes no sense. Let’s lay out what we know for certain. We were both sure Manderson sent Marlowe off in the car to Southampton, and that Marlowe didn’t get back until very late last night, hours after the murder happened.”

“There’s no doubt at all about that,” Murch said, putting sharp emphasis on the verb.

“And now,” Trent went on, “this neat little pistol is asking us to believe the most ridiculous story possible. That Marlowe never went to Southampton at all. That he snuck back to the house in the dead of night, somehow convinced Manderson to get dressed and go out into the grounds without making a sound that would wake his wife or any of the servants, shot him with his own gun, then carefully cleaned the gun, snuck back in to leave it sitting right out on his mantelpiece where the police would find it, then hid somewhere for the rest of the day with a whole large motor car, and drove back up to the house pretending he’d just arrived from Southampton at what, nine last night?”

“A little after nine,” Murch said, staring glumly at Trent. “And as you say, that theory sounds totally wild—until you realize it falls apart completely before it even gets started. Marlowe was fifty to a hundred miles away when that shot was fired. He definitely did go to Southampton.”

“Calvin C. Bunner, at your service,” the newcomer amended with a flick of punctilio, pulling the unlit cigar from between his teeth. He’d long since learned Englishmen moved slow and formal around strangers, and Trent’s quick, half-joking greeting had thrown him just a little off stride. “You’re Mr. Trent, I take it,” he added, nodding. “Mrs. Manderson mentioned you not five minutes back. Captain Murch, good morning.”
Murch gave a gruff nod in return, taking the outlandish Yankee courtesy in stride. “I was heading up to my room when I heard voices in here, thought I’d pop my head in. Caught a snippet about a pistol—this one, I reckon? Figured you might wonder if I was lingering outside the door eavesdropping.” Bunner laughed easy, no trace of discomfort.

The man was thin, slightly under average height, with a clean-shaven, pale, sharp-boned face so fine it looked almost girlish, and big dark eyes sharp as shards of glass that missed nothing. His wavy dark hair was parted dead center, and his lips—perpetually wrapped around a cigar on normal days—hung half-open now with that quiet, hungry look of a man always ready to pounce on new information. The second he popped a cigar back between those lips, that eager softness vanished: he looked every inch the sharp, unflappable Connecticut Yankee he was.
He’d left college straight for a broker’s office, and handled so much of Manderson’s account that the great financial Colossus had singled him out, watched him for months, and poached him for his private secretary post. Bunner was the model of a perfect employee: reliable, far-sighted, methodical to a fault. Manderson could have hired a dozen men with those qualities alone. But he’d picked Bunner for two extra things no one else offered: he was fast, he was discreet, and he had a gut instinct for the stock market that bordered on the supernatural.

Trent and Bunner sized each other up for a long quiet second, both clearly satisfied with what they saw.
“I was just being told,” Trent said amiably, “that my little discovery of this pistol, the one that might have fired the shot that killed Manderson, doesn’t count for much. Apparently it’s a favorite over in the States, and it’s caught on fast over here too.”
Bunner reached out his bony hand, lifted the pistol from its case, and hefted it like he’d handled a hundred just like it. “You’re dead right, Captain. Back home we call these Little Arthurs, and I’d bet there’s a hundred thousand of them riding in hip pockets right this second. Too light for my taste, though.” He fished an ugly, heavy-caliber revolver out from under his jacket, passing it over to Trent. “Feel that. Careful, it’s loaded. This little toy? Marlowe bought it right before we sailed over this year, just to humor the old man. Manderson was dead set that no self-respecting man went around unarmed in the 20th century. So Marlowe ran out and bought whatever the shop handed him, never even consulted me. Not that it’s a bad gun, mind you.” Bunner squinted down the sights for a second. “Marlowe was terrible at first, but I coached him the last month or so, he’s got a pretty good shot now. But he never could get in the habit of carrying it. Me? Carrying a gun’s as natural as putting on pants in the morning. I’ve had one on me for years, what with all the folks laying for Manderson. And look what happened. They got him when I wasn’t around.” He sighed, heavy and quiet. “Gentlemen, you’ll have to excuse me. I’m heading into Bishopsbridge, got a stack of cables to send off big enough to choke a cow, whole office is on fire right now.”
“Then I’m off too,” Trent said, checking his pocket watch. “I’ve got an appointment at the Three Tuns inn.”
“Hop in my car, I’ll drop you,” Bunner offered warmly. “Passes right by the place. Say, Captain, you heading that way? No? C’mon then, Trent, help me roll the car out. Chauffeur’s laid up, we’re doing everything but wiping the mud off the body ourselves these days.”

Bunner drawled on, easy and unhurried, as he led Trent downstairs, through the empty hall, and out to the detached garage a little ways behind the house, cool as a cave after the blazing midday sun. He made no move to turn the engine over, though. He passed Trent a cigar, lit his own, then perched on the car’s footboard, thin hands laced between his knees, and fixed Trent with that sharp dark stare.
“Look here, Trent,” he said after a long drag. “I know your record. You’re a sharp man, I like working with sharp folks. That local detective, Murch? He strikes me as a total mutt. I’d answer any question he was smart enough to ask me, already did, matter of fact, but I’m not going out of my way to hand him theories he never thought to ask for. You get that?”
Trent nodded. “Most people feel that way about police,” he said. “Official stiffness, I suppose. But don’t write Murch off. He’s one of the shrewdest officers in Europe. Not fast on the uptake, but he never slips. Years of experience no man can match. My whole trick is imagination, but in detective work? Experience beats it hollow, nine times out of ten.”
“Beats nothing,” Bunner shot back crisply. “This ain’t some regular break-and-enter case. Let me tell you why. I’m convinced the old man knew someone was coming for him. Worse: he knew there was no way to outrun it.”
Trent dragged a wooden crate over to sit across from him, leaning forward. “Now that’s the kind of lead I need. Spit it out.”
“I know that because of how he’s acted these last few weeks. Everyone says Manderson was the most controlled man on the planet. That’s no lie. I’ve worked with every big name in finance, and he had the steadiest nerve I ever saw. I knew him better than anyone else alive. Better than his wife, poor woman. Better than Marlowe, who never saw Manderson in the middle of a high-stakes trade that could make or break the whole firm. He never showed that side to anyone but me.
“Then a few months back? He changed. Gloomy, sullen, brooding nonstop over something he couldn’t fix. It followed him everywhere, in the office, out here at the house. Then a few weeks ago? That iron self-control of his snapped. And Trent?” Bunner tapped a bony finger on Trent’s knee. “I’m the only one who ever saw that. To everyone else, he was just quiet, a little grumpy. But alone with me? The tiniest thing going wrong would send him flying off the handle. Right here in this library once, he opened a letter that rubbed him the wrong way, and he was raging around the room like a wild man, screaming what he’d do to the guy who wrote it, till it was pitiful. I never saw anything like it.
“A week before he died? He stopped working. First time in the six years I’ve known him. Wouldn’t answer a letter, wouldn’t send a cable, even when our holdings back home were falling apart. I told him he ought to see a doctor once, and he told me to go straight to hell. No one else ever saw that side. If he was mid-rage in the library and Mrs. Manderson walked in? He’d snap back to that cold, dead calm in a single heartbeat.”
“So you’re saying that secret weight on his mind, the thing he was panicking over, was that someone was out to kill him?” Trent asked.
Bunner nodded slow.
“First thought I had, though,” Trent went on, “is that it was a breakdown. Overwork, snapped his mind. That’s the cliche for all your American big business tycoons, right? The newspapers are full of that story.”
“Bunk,” Bunner said, earnest now. “Only the guys who get rich too fast, can’t cut it, go crazy. Think of all the real big men, the ones in Manderson’s league? You never heard of any of them losing their minds. People say every man’s got his breaking point, sure, but that’s not full-blown insanity. That’s just a weird little quirk, like hating cats, or my own stupid thing where I can’t stand to eat any kind of seafood at all.”
“What was Manderson’s quirk?”
“Loads of them. Hated all the fancy useless nonsense rich folks throw money at. No ornaments, no expensive trinkets lying around. Hated having servants hovering around doing little trivial stuff for him. Even though he was more careful with his clothes than any man I knew, spent a fortune on custom shoes? He never hired a valet. Never let anyone touch him. Shaved himself every single day of his life.”
“I heard that story,” Trent said. “Any idea why?”
“Just that Manderson family suspicion, I guess. His dad and granddad were exactly the same. Like a dog with a bone, convinced the whole world’s waiting to steal it off him. He didn’t actually think the barber was going to cut his throat, but he didn’t want to take even the tiniest risk. Same in business, he was convinced everyone was out to steal his deal, and half the time they were right. Made him the most secretive, careful man on Wall Street, that’s half the reason he was so successful. But that’s not madness. Not even close. He wasn’t crazy. He was just worn thin, worrying himself sick, lost his nerve.”
Trent smoked in silence for a minute. He wondered how much Bunner knew about the rift between Manderson and his wife, decided to test the waters. “I heard there was trouble between him and Mrs. Manderson.”
Bunner laughed short. “Please. You think a domestic tiff was going to break a man like Sig Manderson? No way. He was too big to get torn up over something that small.”
Trent looked at the young Yankee, half skeptical, and saw nothing but honest, unworldly conviction behind those sharp eyes. Bunner truly thought a fractured marriage was a trivial inconvenience for a great man. “What was the trouble between them, anyway?”
“Search me,” Bunner said bluntly. He puffed his cigar for a second. “Marlowe and I talked it over a hundred times, never could figure it out. At first I thought the old man was mad they didn’t have kids, but Marlowe said it was probably her that was disappointed, not him. Got that bit from Mrs. Manderson’s French maid, Célestine. She gossips to him all the time, he speaks French perfect, she’s taken a real shine to him. French servants are nothing like English ones, they’ll chat your ear off. I still can’t imagine a lady talking about that kind of private stuff to a man, but the French are a law unto themselves.”
Trent’s head snapped up. So that’s what Célestine was hinting at, he thought to himself.
“Back to what you were saying,” Trent said, steering them back. “You think Manderson knew someone was coming for him. Who? I’m drawing a complete blank here.”
“Terror’s too strong a word,” Bunner said, thoughtful. “Suspense, that’s the right one. Manderson was almost impossible to scare. And what’s weird? He wasn’t taking any precautions at all. He was avoiding them. Like he was almost asking for it. He’d sit in that library window at night, the whole front of his white shirt a perfect target for someone with a rifle. And who’d want him dead? Trent, you’ve never lived in the States. The Pennsylvania coal consolidation he pulled last year? Thirty thousand men, their wives and kids, all facing starvation or total surrender to his terms. Thirty thousand of the toughest, most brutal immigrant miners in the country. Those guys will lay for you for ten years, track you across three states to get even for a slight no one else even remembers. They’ve dynamited men in Idaho for something they did back in New Jersey a decade before. You think the Atlantic Ocean’s going to stop them? It takes real guts to be a top tier businessman in the US. Manderson knew that, he’d always known there were hundreds of dangerous men spread across the country gunning for him. My guess? He just got word that a group of them had crossed the ocean to get him. The thing that’s eating at me? Why he didn’t run. Why he walked out into that garden yesterday morning, sat down on that seat, and practically presented himself to the killer.”
Neither man spoke for a long minute, the blue smoke of their cigars curling up toward the garage rafters, both lost in the same puzzle. Then Trent stood up, brushing dust off his trousers.
“Your theory’s entirely new to me,” he said. “It’s perfectly sound. All we have to do is check if it fits the facts. I can’t reveal my newspaper’s work, but I will say this much: I’ve already proven this was premeditated, and pulled off with a level of cunning I’ve rarely seen. I owe you a huge debt for this. We have to talk again soon.” He checked his watch. “My friend’s been waiting for ages. Let’s go.”
Bunner checked his own watch, standing up. “Two o’clock. That’s ten a.m. on Wall Street. You’ve never seen that place, Trent. Let’s both hope we never get any closer to hell than the chaos that’s raging down there right now.”

A stiff offshore wind drove the waves raging against the cliff foot, while bright sun poured over the land from a dappled blue English sky. Bad sleep had left John Trent restless, so before eight he hiked down to the hidden rock pool the inn staff had pointed him to, and dove deep into the crisp, clear water. He swam out between massive grey boulders into the choppy open sea, forced himself a short stretch against the coast-hugging current, then returned to the shelter of the rocks, winded but sharper than he’d felt in days.

Ten minutes later he was clambering back up the cliff, the heavy, gnawing disgust he’d carried for the Manderson murder case temporarily burned away by sea and wind. It was the day of the inquest, barely twenty four hours since he’d arrived in the small coastal town. He’d barely advanced his investigation at all after parting ways with the American, Bunner, on the road to Bishopsbridge the day before. That afternoon he’d wandered into town with Mr. Cupples, the dead millionaire’s lawyer, to make a quiet purchase at a chemist’s, confer in private with a local photographer, send a reply-paid telegram, and run a quick check at the town telephone exchange. He’d shared almost no details of his leads with the mild-mannered Cupples, who seemed perfectly happy to leave the detecting to him. Back from Bishopsbridge, he’d banged out a long, detailed dispatch for his paper *The Record* and sent it off to the local correspondent to wire through, then dined with Cupples before spending the rest of the evening alone on the inn veranda, turning the case over in his head until sleep eluded him entirely.

Halfway up the cliff now, Trent realized he’d never taken on a case he hated more, or one that had him so completely hooked. In the golden new sunlight, the whole affair felt darker, and more fiercely challenging, than ever. All his half-formed suspicions, the scraps of fact that had kept him awake for hours, glowed sharp and clear now, washed clean by the sea’s cold purity. He could almost see the shape of the guilt he was convinced was there, and the ugly motive he guessed at turned his stomach, but the thrill of the hunt was back, bright and hot. No holding back now, no unnecessary compunction. By the end of the day, he hoped, every thread of his net would be tied tight. He waited, half-excited, not quite daring to hope, for the answer to that telegram he’d sent the day before.

The path back to the inn wound along the cliff top, and when Trent drew near the spot he’d spotted from the water level – an old, long-ago landslip where the cliff face had fallen away – he leaned over the edge, expecting to watch the soft wash of small waves over broken rock below.

There was no broken rock. A few feet beneath him jutted a broad flat ledge, as big as a large sitting room, carpeted with wiry grass, walled in by steep stone on three sides. Right at the far edge, where the cliff dropped straight down to the sea, a woman sat, arms looped around her drawn-up knees, her gaze fixed on the thin trailing smoke of a distant liner, lost to the world in some private daydream.

To Trent, who had trained his eye to drink in every detail without staring, she was the most perfect thing he had ever seen. Southern pale skin, wind-kissed pink on the cheeks, a profile of delicate, unforgivingly regular lines softened by nothing but the full, gentle curve of her mouth. Dark thick brows slanted down to almost meet above her nose, giving her resting face a faint, severe look that vanished the second her lips curved. Thick dark hair escaped the two bandeaux meant to pin it back from her forehead, a mass of loose curls catching the wind at her nape. Everything she wore was matte, fine black – suede shoes, the hat pinned to the grass beside her, her dress that fell to her bare throat. The cut had the quiet elegance of French tailoring, and she looked every inch the sharp modern woman, until you looked at her face and caught that bright, unselfconscious glow of someone fully alive, drinking in sun and wind and sea. Trent thought she could not be English, certainly not American – that unapologetic, unthinking vigour of womanhood was something rarer.

He paused only a moment, then passed along the cliff path above her, unseen on the soft turf, every detail of her burned indelibly into his memory. Then she moved: uncurled her long hands from around her knees, stretched her whole body with the easy grace of a cat, then slowly lifted her head and spread her arms wide, fingers curved open, as if she meant to gather all the glory and unshakable sanity of the morning right into her chest. It was the gesture of a woman who had just claimed her own freedom, resolved to live, to move forward, to take what was coming to her.

Trent did not turn. He knew exactly who she was, and a thick curtain of gloom seemed to drop between him and the bright blue day.

Over breakfast at the inn, Cupples was bright as a sparrow, gushing about the coming inquest. He rambled on about the ancient history of the coroner’s court, that rare old tribunal unshackled by stuffy legal rules and precedents, before steering the conversation straight back to Manderson’s murder.
“Young Bunner was at my rooms last night,” he said, buttering his toast placidly. “He laid out his theory of the crime. A very sharp young man, Trent. He thinks this was an industrial vendetta – Manderson made so many enemies in the labour movement back in the States, it’s perfectly plausible a union hitman tracked him down. He showed me dozens of cases of exactly that sort of secret, violent revenge. Terrible times we live in, eh? All the fancy machinery science gives us, and we’ve no energy left for the old moral virtues. Agricultural machines killed the Harvest Home festival. Fast trains killed the old roadside inn. No wonder the US is falling apart at the seams.”
“Wait, I thought Puritanism was almost as strong there as the obsession with making money,” Trent said, half-listless. Cupples corrected him gently on the exact meaning of the word, then drifted back to his point, and Trent stood up to go.
“Right,” he said. “That philosophy needs a snappy slogan, you’d have crowds dying for it. But you were heading over to White Gables before the inquest, right? We can walk together, I’ll grab my camera first.”

The dull red roof of White Gables glowered through the dark trees, matching Trent’s heavy, troubled mood. If his clues all panned out, the first blow would land on that radiant, alive woman he’d just seen stretching in the sun on the cliff ledge. His mother had raised him to an old, chivalrous respect for women, and now the artist in him revolted at the thought of hurting something so perfectly, fiercely beautiful. But what was he supposed to do? Throw the whole case away? He was sure he was the only person within a hundred miles who was close to the truth. Today, he’d find out if he was chasing a ghost, or a killer. He’d trample down his compunction until he knew for certain it was deserved.

As they walked through the drive gate, they spotted Marlowe and Bunner chatting on the front porch steps. The lady in black stood in the shadow of the porch, and stepped forward to meet them, her walk straight, light, perfectly balanced – just as Trent had known it would be. When Cupples introduced them, her golden-flecked brown eyes regarded him kindly, her face a mask of pale, composed grief, no trace left of that wild, free gesture he’d seen on the cliff.
“I do hope you’ll succeed,” she said to him, earnest and quiet. “Do you think you will?”
Trent made up his mind in a split second. “I believe I will, Mrs. Manderson. When I have the case fully pieced together, I’d like to meet with you privately to go over the facts before we publish anything. I may need to consult you first.”
She looked puzzled, a flicker of distress crossing her face. “Of course, if it’s necessary.”
Trent hesitated. He remembered she’d been reluctant to talk to the police the day before, but there was a question that had been niggling at him, a loose thread that didn’t fit anywhere in the pattern of clues he’d built so far. “I wonder if I might ask you a small question, nothing that would distress you, I think.”
She sighed, tired. “There’s no point in me refusing. Ask, Mr. Trent.”
“It’s just this: your husband recently withdrew an unusually large sum of cash from his London bank, and was keeping it here in the house. Have you any idea why he would do that?”
She stared at him, completely shocked. “I had no idea. I can’t imagine why he would do that – my husband almost never kept cash in the house at all, he always carried a hundred pounds or so in his note case. On Sunday night, right before he left for that supposed moonlight motor ride with Mr. Marlowe, he came into the drawing-room where I was sitting, irritated about something, and asked me for every bit of cash I had on me until the next day. I unlocked my desk and gave him almost thirty pounds. He never explained why he needed it, just said Marlowe had talked him into the drive to help him sleep, since he’d had bad insomnia. I thought it odd he needed money that late, but I forgot all about it until this second.”
“Very odd, indeed,” Trent said, staring off into the distance. Cupples called his niece over to sort out inquest arrangements, and Trent wandered over to join Marlowe pacing the lawn. The young man laughed tiredly as he described the local police’s pomposity and Dr. Stock’s overinflated self-importance, until Trent steered the conversation back to the case.
“Bunner’s vendetta theory?” Marlowe said, shaking his head. “I’m not convinced. But I’ve lived in the US long enough to know that kind of over-the-top, dramatic revenge is exactly the sort of thing they love. Have you read *Huckleberry Finn*? Tom Sawyer wastes days cooking up this absurdly complicated, secret plan to free Jim, when he could have just unlocked the door and walked him out in twenty minutes. That’s the American brain. All the secret societies, the Ku Klux Klan, the Mormons’ penny-dreadful bloody tyranny in Utah – it’s all the same impulse.”
“Did it ever strike you that Manderson was paranoid, scared of that exact threat Bunner talks about?” Trent asked. “It was very strange to send you tearing off to London in the middle of the night.”
“10 PM, exactly,” Marlowe corrected. “And Manderson loved dramatic, unexpected moves. He told me he needed me to find a man called George Harris, collect a secret message from him that he couldn’t wire for. No one even knows who Harris is. Bunner’s never heard of him. I was told to book a deck cabin for him on the Monday steamer, that’s all.”
Trent leaned in, making sure no one was listening. “The butler Martin heard a snippet of your conversation with Manderson in the orchard before you drove off. He heard Manderson say, ‘If Harris is there, every moment is of importance.’ Are you sure you had no idea what that business was?”
“Absolutely none,” Marlowe said, calm. “He told me straight up he couldn’t tell me what it was about. I was just supposed to find Harris, bring back his note. That’s it.”
“Why did he lie to his wife about the moonlight drive, then?” Trent muttered, half to himself.
“Lie to the staff too,” Marlowe added. “He told the same story to Martin.”
Trent pulled two small, blank slips of fresh paper out of his breast pocket, holding them out. “Have you ever seen these before?”
Marlowe turned them over curiously. “They look like they were cut out of a 192X pocket diary, the October pages. No writing on them. I’ve never seen them around the house, anyway.”
Before Trent could reply, Mrs. Manderson walked over. “My uncle says we should leave for the inquest now,” she said.
“Cupples and Bunner have gone on ahead to sort out some business,” Trent said. “I’m not going to the inquest just yet – I wanted to poke around the house a little longer for some clues I think I might find.”
“Of course, do whatever you need to,” she said. “We’re all relying on you. I’ll just go get my things, Mr. Marlowe.” She stepped back into the house.
Trent nodded towards the distant road, where a small boy was trotting towards them, an unmistakable bright orange telegram envelope flapping in his hand. “That’s for me, I think.”
The boy panted to a stop. “Telegram for you, sir, just arrived.”
Trent tore the envelope open, and his face lit up so bright Marlowe’s tired face broke into a smile. “Good news?” Marlowe said.
“Not news,” Trent said, tucking the slip back into his pocket, his eyes sharp and bright. “Just confirmation that one more of my little guesses was dead right.”

The provincial coroner, a jovial, large-framed local solicitor who knew full well this was his one shot at global attention, was determined to make the most of his fleeting moment in the sun. He had a natural knack for staging dramatic moments in his line of work, and the news that millionaire Sigsbee Manderson had turned up dead on his patch had made him the most elated coroner in all of England. He had a sharp head for arranging facts, paired with a gift for rolling, weighty phrasing that could bend a jury to his will and even smooth over messy gaps in evidence procedure.

The inquest was held in a long, bare new extension tacked onto the local hotel, built to double as a future ballroom or concert hall. A whole phalanx of reporters hunkered down in the front row seats, their notebooks at the ready. Witnesses sat in a line along one side of the coroner’s table, while the jury, hair plastered flat and putting on a show of unflappable solemnity, filled two rows on his other flank. The rest of the space was packed with quiet, awed local onlookers, listening in dead silence to the slow, weighty opening formalities; the veteran newspaper men, far less impressed, muttered quiet asides to each other, and those who recognized Trent on sight confirmed for the rest that the detective had not yet arrived.

The first witness was Manderson’s widow, called to formally identify the body. The coroner, whose every instinct leaned toward gentle sympathy for the grieving young woman, walked her through the basics of her late husband’s health and daily habits before asking her to recount the last time she had seen him alive. She lifted her heavy black veil before she began to speak, and the sight of her marble-pale face and unshaken composure sent a quiet ripple through the room. It was not the cold composure of a hard woman: she radiated soft, vivid femininity, no trace of evasion or mystery about her, only the quiet, obvious force of a strong character fighting to keep her emotions under iron control. Once or twice she dabbed at her eyes with a handkerchief, but her voice stayed low and steady to the end.

Her account was simple. Her husband had come up to their suite at his usual hour on Sunday night. He had always preferred a tiny, simple dressing room that connected directly to her bedroom, the door between them usually left open overnight. Both rooms had separate doors leading out to the main hallway, too. She had been half-asleep when he came in, woken only by the click of his bedroom light switch. She’d mumbled something to him, too drowsy to remember the exact words, only recalling he’d planned a moonlight drive in the car. She thought she’d asked if he’d enjoyed the run, and what the time was — she’d only felt like she’d been asleep a few minutes, and had expected him to be out much later. He’d told her it was half-past eleven, and said he’d changed his mind about the drive.
“Did he say why?” the coroner asked gently.
“Yes,” she replied, and paused for half a beat, a flicker of faint confusion crossing her face. “It stuck with me, because he almost never talked about his business affairs. He thought they’d bore me, he rarely mentioned them at all. That’s why I was so surprised when he said he’d sent Mr. Marlowe down to Southampton, to catch a man leaving for Paris on the ferry the next day and bring back some critical information. He said Marlowe could pull it off easily if he didn’t have an accident. He’d started the car, then decided to walk home the last mile or so, and said the fresh air had done him good.”
She had no more details to add. She’d fallen back asleep almost right away, only remembered him switching his light off, and that was the last she’d seen of him alive. She’d slept straight through the night, woken only at seven when her maid brought her morning tea, who’d closed the connecting door between their rooms as she always did. She’d assumed he was still sleeping in, as he often did — he needed far more rest than most people. She’d had breakfast in her private sitting room, and it wasn’t until ten o’clock that someone had come to tell her his body had been found. She dropped her head, waiting to be dismissed.

But the coroner wasn’t done with her. His voice stayed soft, but carried a firm, unyielding edge.
“Mrs. Manderson, the question I must ask you now is deeply painful, but it is my legal duty to put it to you. Is it true that for some months before his death, your relations with your husband were no longer marked by mutual affection and trust? That there was an estrangement between you?”
She straightened her shoulders, lifting her face to meet his gaze, a flush rising high on her pale cheeks.
“If the question must be asked,” she said, clear and cold, “I will answer it plainly so there can be no misunderstanding. The last few months of my husband’s life, his attitude toward me caused me terrible grief and worry. He changed. He grew distant, mistrustful. I saw far less of him than I ever had, he seemed to prefer being alone. I have no explanation at all for the shift. I tried to bridge the gap, as much as I could without surrendering my own self-respect. There was something standing between us, something I never knew the name of, and he never told me what it was. My own stupid pride kept me from prying it out of him. I just decided to act exactly as I always had toward him, for as long as he would let me. I suppose I will never know what it was, now.”
Her voice trembled despite all her control as she finished. She pulled her veil back down, standing straight and still, and when one of the jury asked if they had ever had a shouting match, a fight, she answered the single word “Never” so flat and final that every person in the room recognized the question as a clumsy, foolish mistake. She knew of no other troubles weighing on her husband’s mind, she said, and the coroner at last released her from the stand.

The whole room’s attention had just shifted to the next witness, Martin, when Trent edged through the doorway into the packed hall. He did not glance at the witness stand. His gaze fixed on Mrs. Manderson, moving quickly toward the exit down the narrow gap in the crowd, and his expression darkened. He stepped aside to let her pass, half-bowing, when to his shock she spoke his name in a low, unsteady voice, drawing him a few steps away into the outer hall.
“I have to ask you,” she said, her voice faint and broken, “would you give me your arm to walk part of the way back to the house? I can’t see my uncle waiting by the door, and I suddenly feel so faint — the air will help. No, I can’t stay here a moment longer, please Mr. Trent,” she cut him off when he offered to fetch a car. “I have to get to the house.”
Her hand tightened hard on his arm for a second, like she could drag him away against his will, before she leaned most of her weight on it, head bowed, as they walked slow along the oak-shaded path back to White Gables. Trent said nothing, his mind spinning, yelling at himself over and over that he was a fool. Every fact, every hunch he held alone about Manderson’s death surged through his head in a chaotic rush, but he could not stop feeling the weight of her hand on his arm, a weird, giddy exaltation twisting through him that he hated and could not shake.

He walked her all the way to the house, helped her sink onto a couch in the morning room, and she lifted her veil to thank him, her eyes bright with unfeigned gratitude. She was already feeling better, she said, a cup of tea would fix her completely. She apologized for dragging him away from the inquest, she had thought she could get through all the testimony, but she never expected those last questions about the rift between her and her husband. “I’m glad you didn’t have to hear me say it all,” she told him when he explained he had only just arrived. “It took every last bit of my self-control not to break down in front of all those staring men. Thank you again. I knew I could ask you for help,” she finished, with a small, tired smile. Trent left the house a few minutes later, his fingers still tingling where hers had brushed his when he helped her sit down.

Back at the inquest, the rest of the testimony yielded nothing new for the reporters’ notebooks. The servants, the workman who had found the body, the local police all gave bland, unremarkable statements — then Mr. Bunner took the stand, and stole the entire show. The young American secretary recounted every threat Manderson had received, every story of targeted violence against powerful industrialists back home, every odd, anxious shift in his boss’s behaviour in his final days. The reporters scribbled every word, and the full unedited account ran on the front page of every major newspaper across Britain and the United States the next morning.

The coroner, in his final summing up to the jury, dismissed the faint, token suggestion of suicide he was legally obliged to raise. He reminded them no weapon had ever been found anywhere near the body. “That fact is the core of this case, gentlemen,” he told the jury. He walked them through the medical evidence from Dr. Stock: Manderson had died six to eight hours before his body was found, a single bullet to his left eye that had shattered the base of his brain. The wound showed no signs the gun had been pressed to or even held close to his face, and while it was technically possible he had shot himself from a short distance, the fresh, distinct scratches and bruising on his wrists and lower arms were clear signs of a struggle.
He went on to flag Bunner’s testimony as deeply relevant. Manderson, he pointed out, was not an ordinary man — he had received very real, very credible death threats from radical labour activists back in the States, threats that Bunner confirmed had left him jittery, secretive, and consumed by anxiety in his last weeks alive. He made it very clear he thought Bunner’s line of reasoning was correct, and told the jury they were free to weigh all that evidence as they arrived at their verdict. The jury withdrew to consider their finding, the room buzzing with quiet certainty that the official ruling would name an unknown killer.

“Come in!” Trent called out, not lifting his eyes from the enamel developing dish he was tilting slowly back and forth in the golden light of the hotel sitting room window.

Mr. Cupples stepped over the threshold. It was the early evening of the very day the coroner’s jury, without even stepping out of their jury box, had handed down the expected open verdict: death at the hands of some unknown person or persons. Trent’s face was pale, his movements jittery with contained, thrumming energy as he studied the negative bobbing in the dish. “Take the sofa,” he advised, glancing up for half a second. “Those hard chairs over there are job lot stock from the auction after the Spanish Inquisition got shut down. You do not want to test them.” He held the negative up to the light, tilting his head to squint at the detail. “This one came out great. Done washing, I think. Let it hang to dry, and we can clear this mess off the table.”

Cupples wandered over as Trent bustled to stack basins, processing racks, boxes and bottles out of sight, picking up each stray item to peer at it with gentle, unoffending curiosity. “That’s hypo eliminator,” Trent said as Cupples uncorked a frosted bottle and sniffed it. “Handy if you’re in a rush to get a negative processed. Don’t drink it, though. It eats through sodium hypophosphite real efficient, and I’d put good money it’d eat through a human just as fast.” He tucked the last of the clutter onto the overcrowded mantelpiece, then perched on the edge of the table right across from Cupples.

“Best thing about these generic hotel sitting rooms? Their total lack of charm never distracts you from work. No space here for any silly little idle pleasure of someone not chasing a lead. You been in this exact room before, Cupples? I swear I’ve had it hundreds of times, all across England. It’s followed me for years. I’d be lost if some random hotel somewhere gave me a different sitting room. Look at this tablecloth: that’s the ink stain I spilled on it when I stayed here in Halifax. That burnt hole in the carpet? Ipswich, three years back. But hey, they finally mended the cracked glass over that terrible *Silent Sympathy* print I threw a boot at in Banbury. I do my best work in these things. This afternoon, right after the inquest? I knocked out half a dozen perfect negatives. The basement here has a great dark room.”

“The inquest—speaking of which,” Cupples cut in, well familiar with this chattering, playful energy from Trent that always meant he was closing in on something big, “I came to thank you for looking after Mabel this morning. I never expected her to get faint stepping out of the courtroom. She seemed totally calm, and she’s got better self-control than almost any woman I know, I thought I could leave her for a minute to finish hearing the evidence, which I knew I couldn’t miss. She’s back to normal now, and very grateful to you.”

Trent, hands stuffed deep in his pockets, frowned slightly and didn’t reply for a beat. “You know what,” he said after a short pause, “you walked in right as I got to the really good part of this case. C’mon—want to see some top-tier detective work? The exact sort of thing old Inspector Murch should be digging into right now. I’m praying to heaven he hasn’t got to it yet.”

He hopped off the table and vanished into his bedroom, reappearing a minute later with a large drawing board covered in scattered, unrelated little items. “First let me show off my loot,” he said, setting the board down on the clear table. “Big ivory paperknife, two pages torn out of my own old diary, a tube of toothpaste, a little polished walnut case. Half of this stuff belongs in bedrooms at White Gables, and I’m sneaking them all back before sundown. No stopping me, obviously. I borrowed the whole lot this morning when every last soul was down at the inquest. Most people would call that pretty weird if they knew. Now, there’s one last thing left on this board. Don’t touch it, tell me what you see.”

“Easy,” Cupples said, leaning in, fascinated. “It’s a plain glass finger bowl. Nothing odd about it at all.”

“Neither did I, at first,” Trent grinned. “That’s where the fun starts. Grab that little fat bottle there, Cupples, yank the cork out. Recognize the powder inside? You’ve swallowed pounds of the stuff, I’d bet. They give it to teething babies. Grey mercury and chalk, everyone calls it grey powder. Works absolute wonders. Now, I’ll tilt the bowl sideways over this sheet of white paper—you pour a little of that powder right over this spot on the curve, just there… Perfect. Sir Edward Henry himself, the head of the fingerprint bureau, couldn’t have done that neater. You’re a natural.”

“I am absolutely not,” Cupples said, dead serious, as Trent brushed the excess powder back into the bottle. “This is all complete mystery to me. What did I just do?”

Trent ran a soft camel-hair brush lightly over the dusted spot on the glass. “Look again. You thought there was nothing on there a minute ago. See it now?”

Cupples squinted, and his eyes went wide. “Good heavens! Two big grey smudges shaped like fingerprints. They weren’t there before.”

“Your friendly neighbourhood detective at work,” Trent said. “Let me give you a quick lecture on glass finger bowls. Any time you pick one up, you leave a trace you almost never see, and it can stay there for days, even months. The human hand, even when you think it’s totally clean, is never fully dry. And if you’re under a lot of stress, say, Cupples, if you’re terrified or panicking? Your hand gets damp. It leaves a perfect print on any cold, smooth surface it touches. Someone with a pretty sweaty, nervous hand moved that bowl very recently.”

He sprinkled a little more grey powder on the other side of the bowl, brushing it clean to reveal a large, distinct thumb print. His voice stayed even, but Cupples could see he was burning, electric with excitement as he stared at the faint grey marks. “That one there’s the index finger. I don’t need to tell a man as sharp as you that it’s a single spiral whorl, perfectly symmetrical deltas on both sides. The one next to it is the second finger, a simple loop, fifteen distinct ridge counts. I know there’s fifteen, because I already photographed this exact pair of prints earlier today, and pored over every detail. Look.” He held up the negative to the dying sunlight, tracing the ridges with the tip of a pencil. “You can see they’re identical. That ridge bifurcation there? Exact same spot on the print on the bowl. That tiny little scar near the centre? Both of them have it. There’s a dozen tiny unique little ridge details any forensics expert would swear to under oath: the prints on this bowl and the prints on that negative came from the exact same hand.”

“Where did you photograph them?” Cupples blurted, eyes wide. “What does this all mean?”

“I found them on the inside of the left pane of the front window in Mrs. Manderson’s bedroom. I couldn’t very well yank the whole window out and bring it back with me, so I stuck a bit of black paper on the other side of the glass and took the photo. The bowl? That’s the little glass dish Manderson kept his false teeth in overnight. I could carry that, so I did.”

“Those can’t be Mabel’s fingerprints. They’re far too big.”
“Damn right they’re not,” Trent said firmly. “They’re twice the size of any print she could possibly leave.”
“Then they must be her husband’s, right?”
“Maybe they are.”

Trent’s face was chalk white now, he whistled softly under his breath as he reached for another small squat bottle full of dense black powder. “Lampblack,” he explained. “Hold a piece of paper in your hand for two seconds, this stuff will pull the full pattern of your fingers right out of it.” He picked up one of the torn diary pages with a pair of tweezers, held it up for Cupples to see it was completely blank, then sprinkled the black powder over both sides, shook off the loose dust, and held it out.

The two fingerprints they’d just seen on the bowl blazed clear in sharp black ink on one side of the paper. On the reverse, the big thumb print from the bowl stared back at them, unmistakeable.

“Same man,” Trent said, with a short, bitter little laugh. “I suspected it had to be, and now I know for certain.” He turned on his heel and walked to the window, staring out at the darkening street. “Now I know,” he repeated, quiet, almost talking to himself, his tone sharp with a kind of exhausted pain.

Cupples, completely lost, stared at his motionless back for a long moment. “I still have no idea what this means,” he said finally. “I’ve heard of fingerprint work my whole life, but I can’t for the life of me see how proving these are Manderson’s prints gets us any closer to who killed him—”

“I’m sorry, Cupples,” Trent cut him off, spinning back to the table, his face tight and hard. “When I started this investigation I promised I’d bring you along every single step of the way. Don’t think I don’t trust your discretion, but I have to hold my tongue about all of this, just for a little while. All I can say right now: I just found a fact so explosive, if the wrong people get wind of it before I’m ready, the consequences will be devastating for everyone involved. Up until ten minutes ago I was still hoping against hope I was wrong. I might still be wrong about what it all adds up to. But there’s only one test left to run, and I have to nerve myself up to do it.” He suddenly softened, and grinned at Cupples’s shocked, confused face. “No more dramatic speeches, I promise. We’re not half done playing with the powder bottles yet.” He dragged one of those terrible hard Inquisition-era chairs over to the table, sat down, and picked up the big ivory paperknife to test the blade’s surface. Cupples, still reeling, leaned forward, utterly rapt, and held out the bottle of lampblack to him.

Mabel Manderson stood at the sill of her first-floor sitting room at White Gables, staring out at a landscape dissolving into streaks of fine, wind-lashed rain and thick sea mist. The weather had broken with a ferocity almost unheard of for that stretch of the English coast in June: wispy white fog coiled up the sloping fields from the sullen, leaden Atlantic, and the sky hung low as an unbroken sheet of dead grey, spitting pinprick droplets that rattled against the windowpanes like tiny, hopeless sighs. Her face was hollow with grief as she watched the dim, chilling scene outside. There could not have been a bleaker day for a woman newly widowed, adrift, and stripped of every last sense of purpose.

A soft knock pulled her out of her reverie. “Come in,” she called, straightening her shoulders unconsciously, the automatic gesture she made whenever she caught herself letting the world see how deep her weariness ran. The maid announced Mr. Trent, who apologized for calling so early, and said he hoped Mrs. Manderson would grant him a few minutes on a matter of urgent, private importance.

Mrs. Manderson said she would see him. She stepped to the mirror, stared at her own haggard olive reflection, flickered a grim little smile of self-reproach, and turned to greet him as he was shown in. She noted right away how different he looked: the easy, half-fixed good-humor he’d carried through the first days of the investigation was gone, replaced by the jaded, raw-eyed exhaustion of someone who had not slept for 24 hours, and a new, tight guardedness that pricked at her sharp intuition as something deeply unpropitious.

“May I come straight to the point?” he said, when she had offered him her hand. “I have a train to catch out of Bishopsbridge at noon, but I will not leave until I settle this. It concerns no one but you, Mrs. Manderson. I worked half the night on the case, and spent the rest of the hours turning it over in my head, and I know now exactly what I owe it to you to do.”

“You look terribly tired,” she said gently, gesturing to a deep, overstuffed armchair. “Sit here, it’s the most restful spot in the house. Of course this is about the terrible business of my husband’s death, and your work as a correspondent. Ask me anything I can properly tell you. I know you will not make this harder for me than you have to, even while you do your duty. If you say you had to come to me about this, I know it must be critical.”

“Mrs. Manderson,” Trent said, weighing every word slow and deliberate, “I will not make this worse than I have to. But what I have to say will hurt you, badly — and I intend for it to stay entirely between us, if it goes the way I hope. As for what you can properly tell me: I give you my word of honor, I will only ask questions that will decide whether I publish, or permanently suppress, the grave new truths I have uncovered about your husband’s death. No one else — not the police, not the press, no soul — suspects these things exist. What I have found out, what I have all but proven, will shock you no matter what. But if you confirm the possibility I have worked out in my head, I will burn every word of it.” He laid a long sealed envelope on the small polished side table between them. “Inside is a short private note to my editor, followed by a full, 3,000-word dispatch for the *Record*. You are free to refuse to say a single word to me. If you do, my duty to my employers, as I see it, is to take that envelope back to London this afternoon and leave it in my editor’s hands to do with as he sees fit. I cannot justify suppressing a scoop this big on the strength of a mere guess I pulled out of thin air. But if you confirm that the thing I suspect is real — something no one but you can confirm — then I will not print a line. There are lines I will not cross, even for the police. Have you followed me that far?”

A flicker of anxious tension seeped into his otherwise cold, careful tone. She was as still as the marble figures on the mantel, her face pale, hands clasped tight in her lap, shoulders braced back in the exact rigid, composed posture she had held throughout the coroner’s inquest. “I understand perfectly,” she said, her voice low and steady. She drew a deep breath. “I have no idea what dreadful secret you’ve uncovered, or what possibility you’re referring to, but it was honorable of you to come to me first before you ruined lives with it. Tell me what you need to know.”

“I cannot show you the manuscript yet,” Trent replied. “If it is not your secret, it is my newspaper’s. If you confirm what I suspect, the whole envelope is yours to burn, no questions asked.” A flicker of his old warmth broke through, sharp and frustrated. “I hate this secrecy more than I can say. But it was not me who wove this mystery. This is the most painful hour of my life. The first question I have to ask you is this: did you lie, when you told the inquest you had no idea why your husband grew distant, mistrustful, and cold toward you in the last months of his life?”

Mrs. Manderson’s dark brows shot up, her eyes flamed with sudden, hot anger, and she shot out of her chair. Trent stood at once, reaching for the envelope on the table, his whole demeanor signaling the interview was over. But she held up a hand to stop him, color flooding her cheeks, her breath coming fast. “Do you know what you are asking me, Mr. Trent? You are calling me a perjurer.”

“I am,” he said, unflinching. After a short pause, he added, “You knew before you let me in that I had no use for polite fictions here. The idea that no respectable person could ever hold back a part of the truth under oath, no matter the cost? That is nothing but a lie we all agree to pretend to believe.”

He waited for her to dismiss him, but she said nothing. She walked to the rain-streaked window, and Trent stood there, miserable, watching the small, tight tremor in her shoulders until it subsided. When she spoke at last, her face still turned away from him, her voice was clear and unhurried. “Mr. Trent, you make people want to trust you. I know nothing I say here will ever leave this room. And I know you would not ask this of me if you did not have a serious, good reason. I will tell you the truth. But to make sense of it, you have to understand what my marriage was, from the very start.”

She told him of being 20, dazzled by the force, certainty, and almost mythic wealth of the man 20 years her senior who courted her, how she had convinced herself she was in love, only to realize too late that he had married her to be a social trophy: the perfect, charming hostess who would elevate his name and his fortune into the highest circles of London and New York society. She told him of the empty, hollow world of the ultra-rich he had dragged her into, a place where money was the only measure of worth, where the men were either burnt out from decades of ruthless business or useless idle drones, the women lived for display and petty cruelty and empty, meaningless amusements. She told him of the secret little holidays she stole, slipping away to travel cheap in Italy with her old school friends, or crashing on the sofas of her old middle-class childhood friends in London, sharing cheap theater seats and gossiping about dressmakers, the only moments of real happiness she had in years of marriage. Eventually, she said, Manderson had seen through her pretense of enjoying the life he’d built for her. He’d realized she hated every minute of it, that she despised the luxury and the crowds and the status he had poured so much of his ambition into. He never said a word to her, but the warm, easy companionship they’d once shared died overnight. For months before his death, they had been nothing more than two polite strangers sharing a roof.

She sank back into the deep cushions of the window seat, as if all the strength had drained out of her after speaking for so long. For a long minute, neither of them said a word. Trent’s head was reeling. He had never seen this side of her: not the quiet, disciplined grieving widow the world saw, but a woman of raw, fierce intensity, full of fire and deep, unspoken feeling. A wild, unexpected lurch in his chest caught him completely off guard. He had never been drawn to anyone like this before, not even the wittiest, most brilliant women he’d known back in London. The pull was so strong it felt like a physical ache.

He crushed that feeling down, hard, bracing himself like a man about to jump into icy water. “All of that makes perfect sense,” he said, his voice rough. “But there is one last, brutal question that is the entire point of this enquiry. Mrs. Manderson, will you assure me that your husband’s change toward you had nothing at all to do with John Marlowe?”

What he had dreaded came to pass. She cried out, a sound of pure, raw anguish, throwing her hands up in front of her as if begging him to take the words back, before pressing her palms hard to her burning face, and collapsing sideways into the sofa cushions, her whole body wracked with great, heaving sobs. The rigid, self-possessed woman who had faced the coroner, faced the press, faced all the scrutiny of the investigation, had crumbled like a tower struck by lightning.

Trent’s face went deathly white. He set the sealed envelope exactly in the center of the polished table, as if he was moving in a trance. He walked to the door, pulled it shut noiselessly behind him, and within minutes he was tramping across the rain-soaked fields away from White Gables, with no destination, seeing nothing of the mist and the cold driving rain that soaked through his coat. Every part of him was locked in a ferocious, violent fight to stamp out the raving, mad impulse that had seized him the second he saw her break down: the impulse to run back, fall on his knees at her feet, pour out the words that were straining at his lips, to tell her he cared for her, that none of it mattered, to ruin every last shred of his self-respect, and destroy every chance of doing the right thing, for a woman who he now knew, beyond all doubt, loved another man.

This note is for if I miss you at the office. I’ve cracked who killed Manderson, and the dispatch attached lays out the full proof. It names a man no one on the force, no reporter, no member of the household even suspects, and the case against him is damning. I don’t need to tell you you can’t run this before his arrest, and it’s illegal to publish a full accusation before a verdict is handed down — but once that comes down, you’ve got the exclusive of a lifetime. Do what you can with the snippets to move papers between now and then, that’s your call. In the meantime, get this whole document over to Scotland Yard immediately. As for me? I’m done with the Manderson mystery. God help me, I wish I’d never laid eyes on it.

Yours,

Marlstone, June 16th

This is my third, and almost certainly my final, dispatch to the *Record* on the Manderson murder, and I write it with two warring feelings in my chest. The first is immense relief: for my first two dispatches, I hid critical facts that would have tipped off a man of unusual nerve and cleverness, and let him slip the noose before we even got close. Today, I can lay all those cards on the table. The second feeling? Nothing but distaste. This is a story of cold treachery, of twisted cleverness that turned every small, ordinary quirk of a quiet household into a tool for murder. It leaves a foul taste in the mouth, and I believe I’ve untangled the last dark thread of motive beneath the crime itself.

You will recall from my first dispatch that I arrived at the Marlstone house at dawn on the Tuesday after the murder, found the body laid out by the garden shed, described the baffling lack of any obvious culprit, walked through Manderson’s quiet, estranged household, and laid out his movements the night he died. I also mentioned a small, seemingly trivial detail: more than twice the amount of whisky Manderson ever drank after dinner was missing from his private decanter by the time he was found. At the inquest the next day, I only wired over a transcript of the coroner’s proceedings, which our stenographers captured in full at my request. Now, before this same day is out, I have finished the investigation that leads straight to the man who will now have to prove he is innocent of Manderson’s death.

Before I got to the key clue, two tiny, jarring contradictions niggled at me — points that must have stuck in the mind of every reader who followed the case. First: the body was found less than thirty yards from the back door. Manderson was not gagged. There were clear struggle marks on his wrists. A shot had been fired, at the very least one, more likely two if the killer fumbled his aim mid-fight. Yet not a single soul in the house heard a scream, a scuffle, or a gunshot. Worse, the butler Martin was a notoriously light sleeper, his bedroom window propped open every night, his line of sight and hearing pointed directly at that very shed. It should have been impossible for him to miss it.

The second oddity: Manderson’s upper dental plate, the one he wore every single waking hour, the one that held all his visible top teeth, sat forgotten in a bowl by his bedside. He’d dressed fully, necktie knotted, watch and chain fastened, and left the house without it. Anyone who wears a denture like that knows putting it in is second nature the second you wake up. He’d never leave the house without it, not even in a panic. He couldn’t speak clearly, couldn’t eat, would look like a stranger to everyone who knew him. That, too, made no sense.

Neither of those two details gave up an answer right away. They just hung there, like shadows in the corner of every room I walked through, hinting at some secret the killer had gone to extraordinary lengths to hide.

That secret cracked open in Manderson’s dressing room, on the long upper shelf where he kept his hundreds of pairs of custom shoes. I was glancing over them, not looking for clues — I’ve always been a judge of fine leather, and every single pair there was masterfully made — when my eye caught his evening patent lace-ups, the pair he’d worn to dinner the night before. They were well-worn, polished to a shine, fitted to their shoe trees, but two tiny splits gaped on the vamp of both shoes, right where the laces meet the toe. The reinforcing stitching there had snapped, and the leather tore no more than an eighth of an inch — a mark so small no one but a man who handles fine shoes would ever notice it. Even harder to spot, a faint strain in the stitching that joins the upper to the sole, tugged loose along the toe and outer edge of both shoes.

That could only mean one thing. Someone had worn those shoes who was at least a full size bigger in the foot than Manderson.

A quick check of every other pair in the house confirmed it. Manderson, vain of his small, narrow feet, would never squeeze himself into a shoe too tight, never risk those splits. Someone else had worn them, and recently: the torn edges of the leather were still fresh. No one had slipped them on after the body was found, the whole household had been in a panic for 26 hours straight, no one had time to borrow a dead man’s evening shoes for fun. That left one, unthinkable possibility.

I still can’t explain the exact jump of logic that followed, the way every scattered little oddity I’d noted that day crashed together in my head all at once. It was not Manderson’s heavy bedtime whisky. It was not Manderson, the immaculate, meticulous millionaire, who’d been found dead with his cuffs dragged up inside his sleeves, his shoes unevenly laced, wearing last night’s unwashed shirt, who’d forgotten to wash his face before leaving the house, who’d stuffed his watch in the waistcoat pocket not lined for it. It was not Manderson, who never spoke more than two words to his wife after dinner, who had roused her half-asleep to chat about his evening plans. It was not Manderson who left the house without his denture.

For one wild second, the thought hit me like a punch: the man who everyone swore had come home at 10 o’clock, who’d talked to Mrs. Manderson, who’d sat at the library telephone, who’d “gone to bed” in Manderson’s room — that man was not Manderson.

It sounded like utter nonsense. People had seen Manderson leave the house after dinner, climb into the car with Marlowe. But who came back?

I didn’t take long to puzzle out the imposter’s game. He didn’t just not want to leave his own footprints. He wanted to leave Manderson’s. He wanted every scrap of evidence to scream that John Manderson was alive, well, and sleeping in that bedroom until the early hours of the morning. That explained the denture, too. The imposter had brought it with him, taken from the dead man’s mouth, to drop by the bedside. That meant the real Manderson was already dead before the fake Manderson ever stepped foot in the house.

All the rest snapped into place. The man in Manderson’s broad-brimmed hat, his unmistakable tweed shooting jacket, sat hunched over the telephone with his back to the door. Martin never saw his face. Mrs. Manderson, half-asleep behind the half-open connecting door, never saw him at all. She’d only heard a voice she thought she recognized, picking up the thread of a conversation she’d had with her real husband an hour earlier. The imposter never needed a full face disguise. All he needed was a good ear for mimicry, the right build, and a strong enough nerve.

He’d slipped in through the library garden window, not the front door, so the light-sleeping Martin in his pantry across the hall would never hear him. He’d helped himself to Manderson’s decanter of whisky to steady his hands, still shaking from whatever he’d already done to kill the real Manderson, miles away where no one would hear a shot. Then he’d crept up to Manderson’s bedroom, and froze. Mrs. Manderson had spoken to him.

And the imposter, playing the part of Manderson, didn’t just mumble a reply. He told her, casually, that he’d just sent Marlowe off in the car to Southampton, on urgent business to catch a man sailing for Paris on the morning steamer.

Why? Why would a man who never spoke to his wife tell her that specific lie, all about Marlowe?

By then I had my full theory laid out. Here is what happened, as best I can piece it together:

Sometime after Manderson climbed into that car with Marlowe, before 11pm, he was shot dead somewhere far enough from the house that no one heard the shot. The killer drove the body back, left it by the garden shed, stripped it of its outer clothes. Then he donned Manderson’s too-tight shoes, his jacket, his hat, stuffed the stolen dental plate and the murder weapon in his pockets, slipped through the library window, rang for Martin, and stayed hunched over the telephone so he never had to turn his face. He snuck up the stairs, slipped into Marlowe’s bedroom, put the very revolver he’d used to kill Manderson back in its case on the mantelpiece, where it was supposed to belong. Then he went back to Manderson’s room, left the shoes outside the door for the maid to polish, dumped the dead man’s dinner clothes over a chair, set the denture by the bed, and dressed himself in a new set of Manderson’s clothes to slip out of the house unseen before dawn.

Now the only question left: who was he?

Five facts were certain about the imposter:
1. He knew Manderson extremely well, well enough to copy his voice, his mannerisms, his habits down to the smallest detail without slipping up in front of the people who knew him best.
2. He was roughly Manderson’s height and build, broad-shouldered, so that anyone seeing his back would never spot the difference — but his feet were noticeably, if not drastically, larger than Manderson’s.
3. He had real talent for mimicry, and stage experience.
4. He knew every tiny quirk, every unspoken routine of the Manderson household, inside and out.
5. He absolutely, desperately needed every single person to believe Manderson was alive and in that house, until long after midnight that Sunday.

Now cross-reference that list against John Marlowe, Manderson’s private secretary, who’d worked for him for four years:
1. He’d lived in the house as part of the family for nearly four years, closer to Manderson than any other living soul.
2. They stood almost identical heights, five foot eleven, both broad and powerfully built. Marlowe was 20 years younger, a little slimmer in the torso — and his shoes, which I checked later, were a full size longer and wider than Manderson’s.
3. I’d spotted a cast photo on his mantelpiece, of him and two friends dressed as Falstaff’s cronies from *The Merry Wives of Windsor*, marked with an Oxford photography studio stamp. I sent an urgent, confidential wire to a friend at Oxford who ran the dramatic society, asking for Marlowe’s acting record. His reply came back the morning of the inquest: Marlowe was in the OUDS for three years, served as president, played Bardolph, Cleon, Mercutio, was famous for dead-on character impersonations, and was the talk of the university for legendary pranks and hoaxes.
4. No one outside the servants knew the ins and outs of the household half as well as he did.
5. We have unshakable witness proof that Marlowe checked into a Southampton hotel at 6:30am that Monday, exactly as he claimed he’d been told to do by Manderson on that late night mission, before he drove back to Marlstone to put on a perfect show of shock and horror at the news his employer was dead.

We can now hold that fifth fact, and line it up against the fifth, unshakable conclusion about the imposter the whole world thought was Manderson that night.

Trent posted his letter to Sir James Molloy from Munich, the ink faintly smudged where his hand had jerked mid-line. He’d just left the *Record* offices, where he’d handed in a terse, unremarkable dispatch closing out his work on the Manderson case, and bolted for the continent before the full weight of what he’d uncovered could pin him down.

“I am returning the cheque you sent for my work on the Manderson matter,” he wrote. “What I delivered was not worth a tenth of that sum. Ordinarily I’d not hesitate to pocket the fee, but for this business, for reasons I won’t elaborate, I cannot touch a penny of it. If you’ve no objection, bill the space at your standard correspondent rate, and donate the proceeds to any charity you know of that doesn’t make a habit of hectoring the people it claims to help. I’m here to see old friends and untangle the mess in my head, and the one clear thought that’s stuck fast is that I need active, unrelenting work right now. I can’t bring myself to paint, not even a plain wooden fence. Will you send me out as your roving special correspondent? Give me a good adventure, and I’ll send you dispatches worth every penny you pay me. Once that’s done, I daresay I can settle back to quiet work again.”

Sir James’s reply arrived by telegram 48 hours later: he was to proceed immediately to Kurland and Livonia, where Citizen Browning’s revolt was spilling through towns and countryside in fire and blood. It was a roving, unshackled assignment, and for two months Trent chased stories across the war-torn region, his luck holding as steady as it ever had. He was the only foreign correspondent on the street in Volmar the day an 18-year-old girl shot General Dragilew dead mid-parade. He saw whole blocks burn, public lynchings, random fusillades, men strung up from lampposts for crimes they never committed, and each day the senseless cruelty of misrule turned his stomach anew. He slept in ditches under gunfire more nights than he could count, went two days without food more than once, but no matter what chaos surrounded him, morning and night, the face of the woman he loved hopelessly haunted his every thought.

He found a bitter, perverse sort of pride in how unshakable this infatuation was. It fascinated him, like a strange new phenomenon he was observing in his own bones. He was 32, no stranger to the world of romance, his past experiences gentle and unburdened by bitter memories, but he’d always held a quiet, lukewarm faith that if the great, consuming love people wrote about ever came for him, he would choose its timing, not the other way around. He’d mocked the idea of permanent unrequited passion as a silly teenage delusion, right up until the second he rounded that clifftop path and saw Mabel Manderson throw her head back in that vast, unguarded gesture of joy — the joy of a woman who had just been set free from a marriage that had eaten her alive. That moment had blown every assumption he’d ever held about love to smithereens.

Worse, that gesture had confirmed the terrible, unspoken suspicion that had been niggling at the back of his mind for days: her widowhood was not a tragedy, but a passport to happiness with the man she actually loved. He traced the seed of that thought back to his first meeting with John Marlowe, the tall, sharp young secretary with an easy grace and quiet confidence that any woman starved for affection would lean on. Once he’d worked out who the killer was, he’d fought tooth and nail not to land on the obvious motive: that Marlowe, driven mad by the same consuming passion that held Trent now, had murdered Manderson to set Mabel free. Try as he might, he could find no other reason for the crime. Marlowe wasn’t insane, wasn’t a cruel man by nature, but Trent knew well enough that love could rot a man’s scruples away to nothing, that two people starved for each other could be reckless enough to kill for it.

The thought that would not leave him alone, though, was that Mabel had known. That she had known what Marlowe planned to do to her husband, and had said nothing. When he’d confronted her bluntly about her feelings for Marlowe that day in her drawing room, she’d collapsed completely, and that reaction had swept away every last hope Trent had that the two of them weren’t in love. She’d burned the manuscript he’d left her, he knew, to protect Marlowe from being hanged for murder. But could she have gone further? Could she have known the plot from the start, helped it along? The escape route led straight from Manderson’s bedroom, through her private chambers, out her window. The first time he’d considered that, he’d pictured her as a fierce, hysterical creature, ruthless in love and hate, the mastermind behind the whole crime. But then he’d met her, talked to her, seen the soft goodness in her eyes, and that idea had felt like an insult, a vile slander. Still, the doubts crept back, unbidden, every single night.

Six months of brutal war reporting later, Trent returned to Paris, his energy restored, his chest a little lighter. He fell in with a raucous, mismatched crowd of artists, poets, journalists, beat cops, hotel keepers, lawyers and drifters from half a dozen countries, and won their easy affection the way he had in his student days — the kind of warm welcome almost no Englishman ever got in Parisian private circles. He was invited into family dinners, let in on all the grand, ridiculous secrets the new generation of young artists swore they’d uncovered about life and art, until one night he ran into a man he’d known in his own wild youth, one of the self-styled “Hermits of the New Parnassus” who used to rant on cafe terraces about destroying all old rules of verse. That man was now a decorated bureaucrat in the Ministry of the Interior, telling Trent the country needed an iron fist, and quoting exact prices for government betrayals Trent had never even heard of. The old illusion that the new generation was different shattered: Trent was the one who had changed, he realized. The only thing he’d lost, he thought, was his old easy, uncomplicated high spirits.

He was halfway down the sloped Rue des Martyrs one June morning when he spotted Mr. Bunner heading straight for him. He ducked, tried to slip into a doorway to avoid the man — his wound was finally healing, he was thinking less and less of Mabel, and he didn’t want any old memories from the Manderson case ripping that fragile peace open. But the narrow street gave him nowhere to hide, and Bunner spotted him before he could turn away. The American’s warm, unforced friendliness made Trent feel petty for trying to run, and they settled in at a nearby restaurant for a long, leisurely lunch. Bunner was now the Manderson firm’s head Continental agent, perfectly happy with his post, and rambled cheerfully about his work for twenty minutes before shifting to news from England. Jack Marlowe, he said, had joined his father’s business right after Manderson died, turned the failing firm completely around, and was now all but running the whole operation. The two of them were still close friends, planning a summer holiday together.

“Jack Marlowe’s got a first-rate business head on his shoulders,” Bunner enthused. “Give him a little more experience, and he’d outmaneuver me every single time, no question.”

Trent listened, a cold, confused perplexity creeping over him. None of this lined up with the theory he’d carried for a year. There was no mention of Mabel. Then Bunner offhandedly mentioned that Marlowe was newly engaged to a wealthy Irish girl, a beauty he’d met at a country house party.

Trent’s hands curled tight under the table. His entire construction of the murder, the motive, everything, was sliding out from under his feet. What the hell had happened? Bunner didn’t know much more: Mabel had left England right after Manderson’s estate was settled, spent a year in Italy, and only recently come back to London. She’d refused to move back into the old Mayfair house, bought a small quiet place in Hampstead and a cottage in the countryside, and barely ever went out into society.

“All that money just sitting there, going to waste,” Bunner said, a soft note of real regret in his voice. “Old Man Manderson left her more than half his fortune. She could be one of the most prominent women in London, she’s beautiful, and the kindest soul I ever met — but she just won’t spend a penny of it, not the way she ought to.”

Trent made an excuse to leave soon after, and half an hour later he was in his studio, packing up his canvases and books on autopilot. He had to go back to England. He had to find out what had really happened. He would never approach Mabel directly, never force that terrible last encounter back into her life, but he couldn’t stay away. Twenty-four hours later, he’d torn up every last weak root he’d put down in Paris, and was staring across the grey English Channel at the chalk glow of the Dover cliffs.

Mr. Cupples, the one man who could fill him in on all the missing details, was travelling abroad and wouldn’t be back for a month. Trent refused to do the stupid, shameful thing: lurk outside Mabel’s Hampstead house, hoping to catch a glimpse of her. Instead, he remembered her offhand comment at their last meeting about loving opera. He bought a season ticket, and went to every performance, alone, slipping through the foyer crowds as fast as he could, hoping he might spot her by chance, no intrusion required. For weeks he left each night disappointed, but the music wrapped around him like a blanket, soothing the restlessness in his bones.

One night, hurrying through the glittering crush of the opera house foyer, he felt a light, familiar touch on his arm.

He turned, his heart leaping before he even saw her face. It was Mabel. No trace of grief, no shadow of fear on her face, glowing in the soft light of the chandeliers, dressed for the evening, smiling at him so brightly he could not find his voice. She was breathing a little fast, a spark of brave mischief in her eyes and cheeks.

“I wouldn’t miss a single note of *Tristan*,” she said, “and you mustn’t either. Come find me in my box during the interval.” She gave him the number, and the rest of the noise of the crowd faded away to nothing.

For two full months after his visit to White Gables, Trent lived through a stretch of time he would never recall afterward without a quiet shudder. He crossed paths with Mrs. Manderson half a dozen times in that window, and every single encounter left him unmoored: her manner was a perfectly balanced, unassailable blend of cool friendliness, calibrated exactly to land halfway between distant acquaintance and the first soft edge of casual intimacy, and it baffled him, and gnawed at him, more than any hostile confrontation ever could.

At the opera, he stumbled on her sitting in a box with Mrs. Wallace, a giddy, frisky matron he’d known from early childhood. To his stunned surprise, he learned Mabel Manderson, fresh back from Italy, had settled right in the middle of the London social circles he’d been born and raised in, with a dozen of his closest friends as near neighbours. That night he remembered, with a hot, crawling embarrassment, he’d been nothing like himself: fidgety, face burning, chattering like an idiot about trivial adventures in the Baltic provinces, only to catch himself, over and over, directing all his ramble exclusively to Mrs. Wallace, too cowardly to meet Mabel’s eye. When he joined their group, all trace of the quiet agitation she’d shown stopping him in the opera vestibule had vanished. She spoke pleasantly, easily, of her travels, her new home in London, of shared friends they both knew, as if that terrible conversation at White Gables had never happened.

He stayed in their box for the entire second half of the opera, but he heard none of the music. He sat behind the two women, completely fixated on the soft curve of Mabel’s cheek, the thick dark mass of her hair, the clean lines of her shoulder and arm, the slight weight of her hand resting on the edge of the velvet cushion. That black hair felt, by the final act, like an endless, pathless enchanted forest, luring him toward a ruin he could not bring himself to turn away from. When the curtain fell he was pale, quiet, and said his goodbyes to both women with stiff, formality.

The next time they met, as guests at a sprawling country house weekend, and every meeting after that, he kept a tight rein on himself. He matched her light, easy manner, thought he was acquitting himself decently, all things considered—considered that he was living in a constant, roiling agony of bewilderment, remorse, and sharp, unnameable longing. He could not parse her attitude at all. There was no possible doubt she had read his completed manuscript, that she understood exactly what dark suspicion he’d implied in that final, brutal question he’d put to her at White Gables. How could she treat him so openly, so naturally, the same way she treated every other man who had never wronged her?

His sharp intuition picked up, despite the complete lack of any overt shift in her outward behaviour, that she had felt that wrong, keenly. Several times, during the rare, snatched moments they found themselves talking alone, he caught a quiet warning hum in the air, that she was about to broach the subject he dreaded more than anything. Every single time, he twisted the conversation away, his ingenuity sharpened by raw fear. He made two iron resolutions that week. The first: the second he finished the high-paid commercial illustration commission tying him to London, he would leave the country, and stay gone for good. The strain was unendurable. He no longer burned to uncover the final truth of Manderson’s death. He wanted nothing to contradict the quiet, unshakable conviction he’d nursed in his chest for weeks: that he had blundered, misread every sign, misinterpreted her tears at White Gables, written himself down a slanderous, arrogant fool. He stopped speculating about John Marlowe’s motive for killing Manderson. When Mr. Cupples returned to London, Trent asked him not a single question about the case. He thought back to Cupples’ words, the heavy, urgent emphasis in his voice: “So long as she considered herself bound to him… no power on earth could have persuaded her.” He met Mabel for dinner not long after at her uncle’s vast, tomb-quiet Bloomsbury townhouse, and spent the entire rest of the evening deep in conversation with a visiting professor of archaeology from Berlin.

His second resolution: he would never, under any circumstances, be left alone with her.

But when, a few days later, a handwritten note arrived asking him to call on her the following afternoon, he did not even attempt to dream up an excuse. It was a formal challenge, one he could not run from.

For the whole tea service, and a long while after, she chatted with such easy, natural lightness about the trivial news of the day that Trent began to let himself hope she had abandoned whatever plan he was certain she had, to corner him and confront him gravely about their unresolved past. She looked completely unburdened, smiling so wide he recalled an old line about a Princess of Brunswick he’d read once, “Her mouth has ten thousand charms that touch the soul.” She led him on a tour of her sunlit drawing room, picking up this trinket, that small treasure from her collection of bric-à-brac, laughing as she told stories of the bargain hunts and serendipitous finds that had filled the room. When he asked if she would play the piano piece he’d heard her perform at a friend’s house a few months prior, the one that had stuck in his head ever since, she agreed at once.

She played with a perfect blend of technical mastery and quiet feeling that moved him just as deeply as it had the first time. When the last note faded to silence, he said softly, “You were born a musician. I knew that before I ever heard you play.”

“I’ve been playing since I was small,” she said simply, half turning on the piano stool to smile at him. “It’s been such a comfort, through hard times. When did you first tell yourself I had it in me? At the opera, I suppose? But anyone can pretend to care about music at the opera, can’t they?”

“No,” he said abstractedly, his mind still lingering in the echoes of the last bars. “I think I knew it the very first time I saw you.” The second the words left his mouth, he went rigid. He had said it. The past was no longer locked away.

A short, thick silence fell. Mabel looked at him directly, then darted her gaze away. A faint pink flush crept up her cheeks, and she pursed her lips for a second as if to whistle. Then she gave that sharp, defiant little shrug he remembered from their first meeting, rose from the piano, and sank into the armchair directly across from him.

“That line works as well as anything else to get us to what I’ve been dying to say,” she began slowly, staring at the point of her shoe. “I asked you here today on purpose, Mr. Trent, because I couldn’t stand this quiet, unspoken thing between us a single day longer. Ever since the day you left me at White Gables, I told myself it shouldn’t matter what you thought of me about the murder. That you weren’t the kind of man who would spread a story like that to anyone else, not after you told me you were going to bury your whole manuscript to keep my name out of the mud. I told myself it didn’t matter. But of course it did. It mattered horribly. Because what you thought was completely, utterly wrong.” She lifted her eyes and met his gaze steadily. Trent, his face completely blank of expression, held her look.

“Since I got to know you better,” he said, “I stopped believing it, a long time ago.”

“Thank you,” she said, and blushed bright, sudden red. She twisted a soft kid glove between her fingers as she spoke. “But I need you to know what really happened. I didn’t know if I’d ever see you again, after that day. But I knew if I did, I had to clear this up. I thought it wouldn’t be so hard—you struck me as someone who could understand. And a married woman doesn’t have to stumble over herself talking about things like this the way a young girl would, right? But then we kept running into each other, and I found it was harder than I could ever have imagined. You made it hard.”

“How?” he asked, quiet.

“I don’t know,” she said, then laughed softly. “No, I do know. You treated me exactly like someone who had never suspected a single terrible thing about me. I’d braced myself, the whole time, that the next time I saw you you’d turn that cold, hard, sharp look on me that you had when you asked me that last question, do you remember? At White Gables? Instead, you were just… nice. Exactly like any other casual friend. After that first opera meeting I went home wondering if you’d even recognized me. I thought maybe you’d seen my face, but forgotten who I was entirely.”

Trent barked out a short, involuntary laugh, but said nothing. “I never could remember if you’d said my name that night,” she added, apologetic. “So I thought it was possible. Then the next time, at the Iretons’ house party, you did say my name, and I knew you remembered perfectly. A dozen times that week I almost worked up the courage to talk about it, and never quite managed it. I started to think you wouldn’t let me, that the second I steered the conversation that way you’d slip right out of it. Wasn’t I right? Tell me.” He nodded. “Why?” He stayed silent.

“Fine,” she said. “I’ll finish the story, then you can tell me why you made it so impossible. Once I realized you were dodging the subject, it only made me more determined. I suppose you never thought I’d actually force the conversation, did you? I dare say I never could have, if what you suspected was true. You walked in here today completely sure I’d never dare bring it up. Well, now you see.”

All traces of hesitation vanished from her face. She talked herself right into a bright, determined focus, utterly intent on wiping out the misunderstanding that had hung over her for months, and she was completely in control of the room.

“I’m going to tell you the full story of the mistake you made,” she went on, as Trent sat with his hands clasped between his knees, his face still unreadable. “You’ll have to believe it. It’s messy, full of crossed wires and hidden misunderstandings and tiny, stupid natural mistakes that everyone just takes for fact. Please know I never, not once, blamed you for jumping to the conclusion you did. You knew my husband and I were estranged, and you know what everyone assumes that means. You guessed, before I even said it out loud, that he’d turned cold and resentful toward me. I was silly enough, back at White Gables, to lie and say he was only disappointed I wouldn’t play the society hostess for him. That was part of the truth, but I could see you didn’t buy it. You guessed the thing it took me months to work out myself: he was insanely, irrationally jealous of John Marlowe.

“When you let me see that you’d figured that out, I fell apart completely. It was such a blow. I’d thought all that quiet humiliation and strain was over, that his stupid delusion had died with him. You practically asked me if his secretary was my lover, Mr. Trent— I have to say it out loud, so you understand why I broke down sobbing. You thought that was a confession. You thought I was guilty of that. I think you even thought I was part of the murder, that I’d agreed to it. That hurt worse than anything I can say. But I don’t know, maybe you couldn’t have thought anything else, given what you saw.”

Trent, who had not looked away from her face the whole time, dropped his head, staring at his shoes. He did not lift it as she kept talking. “It was nothing but shock, and the memory of all those months of hidden, miserable suspicion, that made me lose control. And by the time I pulled myself back together, you were gone.”

She rose, walked over to the escritoire by the window, unlocked a drawer, and pulled out a long, sealed envelope. “This is the full manuscript you left with me,” she said. “I read it over and over again. Everyone who knows you knows how brilliant you are at unravelling these kinds of cases.” A faint, mischievous little smile flickered across her face for half a second. “I thought it was magnificent, Mr. Trent. I got so wrapped up in the mystery I almost forgot the story was my own. And I want to tell you, holding this in my hand, how grateful I am that you did that chivalrous, generous thing, giving up the biggest scoop of your career to keep a woman’s reputation out of the mud. If all had been as you thought, the truth would have spilled out when the police picked up the leads you handed them. I understood exactly what you risked to do that, and I was grateful for it even at the worst points, when I felt crushed under the weight of what you suspected.”

Her voice shook a little as she said the words, and her eyes were bright. Trent noticed none of it, still staring down at his knees. She laid the envelope gently in his open, upturned palm, resting on his knee. The soft, gentle gesture made him look up, at last.

“Can you—” he began, slowly.

She held up a hand to stop him, standing right in front of him. “No, Mr. Trent, let me finish before you say anything. It’s such an unspeakable relief to get this out at last, and I want to finish the whole story before the courage leaves me.” She sank back onto the sofa. “I’m telling you something no other person alive knows. Everyone could tell, I suppose, that something had come between my husband and I, no matter how hard I tried to hide it. But I don’t think a single soul ever guessed what his stupid, absurd suspicion was. It was so wildly, ridiculously far from the truth. Mr. Marlowe and I were perfectly friendly, ever since he came to work for us. For all his cleverness—my husband used to say he had the sharpest brain of any man he ever met—I always thought of him as practically a boy. I’m a few years older than him, and he had that easy, unambitious softness about him that made me feel even more that way. One day my husband asked me what I thought Marlowe’s best quality was, and I said, without thinking at all, ‘His manners.’ He scowled black as thunder, and after a long silence, he said, ‘Yes, Marlowe is a gentleman, that’s so’—and he wouldn’t look at me.

“We never spoke of it again, until a year ago. That was when Marlowe fell head over heels in love with a rich American girl. To my absolute disgust, he picked the worst possible woman you could imagine: a beautiful, brilliant, sporty flirt who did nothing but amuse herself, who broke hearts for fun and got away with every bit of it. Everyone knew how unprincipled she was, but she wrapped Marlowe around her little finger, clever as he was. One day, at our Lake George summer house, I asked him to row me out on the lake. We’d never been alone for any stretch of time before. I tried to talk some sense into him, gently. He was polite, but he wouldn’t believe a word I said about her. Not long after that, he got a note from her, asking him to congratulate her on her engagement to another man. He looked so broken at breakfast that day I thought he was sick, so I went to his study afterward to ask what was wrong. He just handed me the note, and turned to stare out the window at the garden. I put my hand on his arm, to comfort him. That was the exact second my husband stepped into the doorway, with a stack of papers in his hand. He glanced at us once, turned, and walked quietly back to his own study. I thought he’d heard me trying to cheer Marlowe up, and was being kind to leave us alone. Marlowe never saw him. My husband left the house for a business trip out west that same morning, while I was out walking. He did that all the time, no warning, so I didn’t think a thing of it.

“It wasn’t until he came back a week later, pale and strange, and asked me where Marlowe was, that the whole truth hit me in a single flash. I almost gasped. I was so angry I could barely see straight. I don’t think I would have cared a bit if someone had thought I’d left my husband openly, run off with someone I loved. That I could own. But that coarse, stupid, hidden suspicion… of a man he trusted, behind his back… it made my blood boil. I swore to myself right then that I would never, ever let on that I knew what he thought. I would act exactly as I always had, no difference at all, and that’s what I did, right up to the day he died. I knew there was an unbreakable wall between us now, that even if he apologized a thousand times we’d never get past it, but I never once showed I noticed he was treating me differently.

“And that’s how it went, month after month. I never could survive going through that again. He was always perfectly cold, perfectly polite to me, only speaking to me when he had to. He never once said a word about his suspicion, but I could feel it hanging in the air between us, and he knew I could. Both of us were too stubborn to break first. He was nicer to Marlowe, if anything, than he’d ever been before—heaven only knows why. I thought he might be planning some secret revenge, but I was never sure. Marlowe never had the faintest idea what my husband thought of him. We stayed good friends, but we never spoke of anything personal after that heartbreak of his. Then we came to England, moved to White Gables, and then… my husband died, the way he did. You know the rest, more than any other person alive,” she added, and glanced up at him with a soft, odd little smile.

Trent stared back at her, his chest flooding with a wave of deep, unnameable relief. All the tight, strained tension drained from his face, and his natural vivacity flooded back. Long before she’d finished her story, he knew it was the unvarnished truth, the same way he’d known, from the very first of their meetings after White Gables, that the theory he’d built up piece by piece back then was nothing but arrogant nonsense.

“If you insist,” Philip Trent said, sinking back into the armchair by the fire with a dramatic, exaggerated sigh, “I suppose I must bend to your will. But I would far rather scrawl this thing when I’m not sitting three feet away from you, every scrap of my wits scattered to the winds of newly announced affection. Hand me a sheet of paper, then—something whiter than a star, something no hymning angel would blush to write on, no, not that note-paper stamped with your address in that faint silver script. You don’t understand the sacrifice I’m making. I have never in my life felt less inclined to put pen to paper for anyone but a love note.”

Mabel rewarded that complaint with a quick, soft smile, leaning her elbow on the oak escritoire. “What do you want the letter to say?” Trent asked, hovering his nib over the blank page as if it might bite. “Shall I compare the man to a summer’s day? What on earth am I supposed to put here, under *Dear Mr. Marlowe*?”

“Say exactly what you mean to,” she suggested.

Trent shook his head, a faint, rueful glint in his eye. “What I mean to say—what I’ve been dying to yell at every stranger I’ve passed in the last twenty-four hours—is that Mabel and I are betrothed, and everything in the whole wide world is all gas and gaiters. That makes for a terrible opening to a letter that’s supposed to be strictly formal, not to say vaguely menacing. I’ve got as far as *Dear Mr. Marlowe*. That’s as far as I’ve got.”

“I’m sending you a manuscript,” Mabel prompted, “which I thought you might like to see.”

“D’you realize,” Trent said, lifting his pen to squint at her, “that sentence only has two words of more than one syllable? This letter isn’t supposed to make him comfortable. It’s supposed to jolt him. We need long words. Lots of them.”

“I don’t see why,” Mabel frowned. “I’ve got dozens of letters from lawyers and businessmen, all starting with *with reference to our recent communication* and that sort of mouthful, going on like that for pages. But when I meet those same men in person, they never talk like that. It’s absurd.”

“Not to them,” Trent set his pen down with an audible click and stood up, stretching his long limbs. “Let me explain. We Britons are a nation that hates straining its mind if it doesn’t have to. We get by day to day with a tiny, simple vocabulary. Any long word is abnormal, same as any other odd thing—it’s either hilarious, or so heavy with gravity it makes you sit up straight. Look at that phrase *intelligent anticipation*. On the continent, no one would have batted an eye. Over here? It’s a national joke. Every time a politician says it, or a leader column prints it, everyone grins. Why? The idea’s as boring and ordinary as cold leftover mutton. It’s just two long, fancy words. And then there’s *terminological inexactitude*—we still roar laughing at that one, years later. The whole joke’s the big words, nothing else. It works the other way, too. When we need to be deadly serious, we dump the simple words and haul out the long ones. A solicitor who opens a sentence with *pursuant to the instructions communicated to our representative* knows he’s earned his six and eightpence, just for the sound of the thing. Don’t laugh, it’s true. Continental folks don’t work that way. They’re used to playing with ideas all the time, so a Paris shopkeeper or a cabman has a daily vocabulary that would sound like Greek to nine out of ten Londoners. I had dinner once with a friend of mine who drives a hansom in Paris, in this grubby little spot opposite the central post office, full of cabbies and porters yelling at each other over stew. Words like functionary, unforgettable, exterminate, independence were flying across the table faster than the bread rolls. A London cabman would’ve felt completely out of his depth there. Mind you,” he added hurriedly as Mabel crossed the room and picked his pen up off the floor, “I’m not saying we should all be intellectuals. Keats was right—happy is England, sweet her artless cabmen, enough their simple loveliness for me. But when you’re dealing with the industrial, professional class that runs the country—”

“No, no, no!” Mabel laughed, pressing a hand over his mouth for half a second. “I don’t care about that right now. You’re stalling, and we will not stop for a half hour lecture on national vocabularies. You promised me that letter. Come on.” She tucked the pen back into his hand.

Trent looked at the nib like it was a dead rat. “I warn you, don’t stop me talking,” he said glumly. “Silent men are far worse to live with than chatty ones. Beware of the quiet ones, they’re all harbouring terrible secrets. I am stalling, you know. Writing this cold, stern note while I’m sitting here in the same room as you, all soft and happy? It feels almost indecent, mixing these two completely different moods.”

She guided him back to the hard wooden chair in front of the escritoire and pushed him down gently. “Just try, please. I want to see it finished, and posted at once. I’d be perfectly happy to leave all the old horror buried, but you say you have to get to the bottom of the truth. If that’s the case, I want it done as fast as possible. You can do it if you stop avoiding it. Don’t you ever get that feeling? The ache to stuff that worrying letter in the post, where you can’t get it back no matter how much you fuss over it, and it’s out of your hands for good?”

“I’ll do it,” Trent said, and turned to the paper, dating it from his hotel, not Mabel’s house. Mabel stood behind him, a soft, tender light in her eyes, her hand half raised to smooth down his messy, unkempt crop of hair—then she let it fall, not touching him, slipping soundlessly over to the piano in the corner. She began to play very softly, half a wordless melody, for ten full minutes while the only other sound was the scratch of his pen.

“What if he writes back saying he won’t speak?” Trent said finally, lifting his head.

Mabel glanced over her shoulder, her fingers still hovering over the keys. “He won’t dare. He’ll have to talk, to stop you from denouncing him.”

“But I’m not going to denounce him,” Trent said. “You wouldn’t let me, first off—and even if you did, I wouldn’t. The whole thing’s far too tangled, far too uncertain now, to go throwing accusations around.”

Mabel laughed. “Poor Mr. Marlowe doesn’t know you won’t, does he?”

Trent sighed, leaning back in his chair. “Odd things, codes of honour. There are things I do without a second thought that you’d think were disgraceful—punching a man who insults me, swearing loud when I bark my shin in the dark. Now you’re telling me to bluff Marlowe with a threat I never, ever intend to carry out. Even the worst fiend in hell, drunk on guilt, would think twice about that trick.” He went back to writing, and Mabel smiled indulgently, turning back to her soft piano playing.

A few minutes later, he laid down his pen. “There. Faithfully yours, Philip Trent. Wanna see it?”

Mabel ran across the twilight room, flicking on the green shaded reading lamp on the escritoire, and leaned on his shoulder as she read:

> You will perhaps remember that we met under very unhappy circumstances last June, at Marlstone.
> On that occasion, I was working as a newspaper representative, tasked with running an independent investigation into the death of the late Sigsbee Manderson. I completed that investigation, and arrived at a set of clear conclusions. The enclosed manuscript, which I originally drafted as a dispatch for my paper, lays out those conclusions in full. For reasons I do not need to explain here, I chose at the very last minute not to publish them, nor to tell you about them. No one but two people besides myself know the full contents of that document.

Mabel’s dark brows drew together, her eyes lifting from the page. “Two people?”

“Your uncle Cupples is the other,” Trent said. “I went to see him last night, and told him the whole truth. You don’t mind, do you? I always felt awful keeping it from him, after I’d promised him I’d share everything I found out. My silence just looked like I was playing at being mysterious. Now that we’re done with hiding the truth to protect you, I wanted him to know everything. He’s a sharp, clever man, and I want him there with me when I meet Marlowe. Two heads are better than one, for this interview.”

Mabel sighed, squeezing his free hand. “Of course he should know. I just hope no one else finds out. I want all that old horror buried deep, six feet under. I’m so happy right now, Philip, but I’ll be happier still when you’ve satisfied that curious mind of yours, unearthed every last bit of the truth, and stamped the earth down over all of it for good.” She went back to the letter.

> Quite recently, however, new facts have come to my attention that have led me to change my original decision. I do not intend to publish what I discovered, but I have determined to approach you directly, to ask for a full private statement of your side of the story. If there is anything you can say that will cast this whole affair in a different light, I can see no reason for you to withhold it.
> I expect to hear from you shortly, to arrange a time and place I may call on you—unless you would prefer the interview to take place at my hotel. In either case, I request that Mr. Cupples, whom you will remember, and who has read the enclosed manuscript in full, be present for the conversation.
> Faithfully yours,
> Philip Trent.

“It’s the stiffest, coldest thing I’ve ever read,” Mabel said, half laughing. “You couldn’t have written anything more formal if you’d locked yourself in your hotel room for three hours.”

Trent slipped the letter and the thick manuscript enclosure into a long, heavy envelope. “It’ll make him sit up so fast he knocks his tea over. This can’t go wrong. We can’t risk it getting lost, or left with a servant if he’s out. We ought to send a special messenger, with strict orders to hand it directly to Marlowe himself, no one else.”

Mabel nodded. “I’ll arrange that. Wait here, I’ll be back in a minute.”

When she returned, Trent was rummaging through the music cabinet by the piano. She sank onto the carpet at his feet, the hem of her dark brown dress spreading out around her. “Philip, tell me something,” she said.

“Almost anything,” he said, pausing in his rummage.

“When you saw Uncle last night, did you tell him… about us?”

“I didn’t,” Trent said, quiet. “I remembered you hadn’t said anything about telling anyone yet. It’s your choice, isn’t it? We can keep it to ourselves a little longer, or tell the whole world tomorrow, whatever you want.”

“Then will you tell him?” Mabel stared down at her clasped hands. “I want you to be the one to tell him. I think you’ll guess why, without me saying it. That’s settled, then.” She lifted her eyes to his, and for a long moment neither of them said a word, the soft golden light of the lamp wrapping around them both.

Trent leaned back into the deep armchair at last. “What a strange, beautiful world this is,” he said. “Mabel—will you play something for me? Something that’s just pure, uncomplicated joy. No feverish storm and stress, no snappy little bits that sound like thorns crackling under a pot. Just the kind of joy that’s decided the whole universe is worth being happy about. That mood never lasts long, you know. We ought to soak up all we can of it, while we’ve got it.”

Mabel walked over to the piano, struck a few soft experimental chords while she thought, then settled in, her fingers moving across the keys like she was pouring all her happiness into the sound. It was the theme from the final movement of the Ninth Symphony—the one that sounds exactly like the great gates of Paradise swinging open, slow and wide, to let all the world’s joy pour in.

Beneath a high ceiling lined with well-loved leather-bound books, a deep-bellied antique oak desk sat pulled close to the tall sash windows overlooking the green sweep of St James’s Park. The spacious, tastefully decorated room bore the unmistakeable mark of a long-time bachelor: a half-smoked cigar smouldered in a crystal ashtray, a pair of well-worn riding boots stood propped by the door, and no stray decorative frill cluttered the clean lines of the furniture. John Marlowe slid a brass key in the desk’s lock, reached deep into its farthest compartment, and drew out a long, thick manila envelope.

“I gather you’ve already gone over this,” he said, nodding at Mr. Cupples, who was perched on a deep velvet sofa, his round, benevolent face scanning the room like a curious sparrow.
“Read it for the first time 48 hours ago,” Cupples replied. “We’ve picked over every line between us.”

Marlowe crossed to the table and set the envelope down in front of Trent. “That’s your full manuscript. I’ve read it three times cover to cover. I don’t think another living soul could have dug up half as much of the truth as you’ve set down here.”

Trent ignored the compliment. He sat rigid by the hearth, staring hard into the leaping flames, his long legs twisted awkwardly under his chair. “You mean there’s still the rest of the truth to lay out,” he said, tugging the envelope toward him. “We’re all ears whenever you’re ready. The longer the story, the better as far as I’m concerned— I want to understand every last thread. First, though, we’d both like a full picture of who Sigsbee Manderson really was, and what your relationship to him was. It struck me from day one that the dead man’s strange character had to be the hidden engine driving this whole mess.”

“You were dead right,” Marlowe said grimly. He crossed the room and settled on the edge of the tall, tufted fender, his lean frame loose and perfectly controlled, a far cry from the haggard, jittery wreck Trent had met at the Marlstone estate 18 months prior. His brow was open, his bright blue eyes steady—though they still held that faint, unplaceable edge of wariness that had pricked at Trent the first time they shook hands. Only the tight, set lines of his mouth betrayed that he knew he was standing on dangerous ground, and intended to face it straight on.

“Sigsbee Manderson was never a normal man,” Marlowe began in his soft, even voice. “Most of the wildly rich men I met in America made their fortunes off abnormal greed, abnormal grind, abnormal brute force of will, or just abnormal luck. None of them had the raw brainpower to back it up. Manderson hoarded wealth like a dragon, worked 18 hour days chasing it, had a will you could bend steel around, and had more luck than any three of his rivals put together— but what made him one of a kind was his intellect. Back home, the papers called him the Napoleon of Wall Street, and everyone who knew him thought his ruthlessness was his defining trait. But hundreds of other men would have burned every last bridge to get what they wanted, if they’d been clever enough to dream up Manderson’s schemes.
“Americans aren’t stupid, far from it— as a nation they’re ten times sharper than us. But I never met another one who had that level of icy sagacity, that foresight, that perfect memory and sheer relentless mental grit behind every move he made. He never forgot a single useful fact. He ran his business the same way Napoleon ran his campaigns, he’d devour prepped digests of coal market data, wheat futures, railway holdings in every spare minute he could snatch. He’d dream up bolder, cleverer plays than any man on the Street. Everyone learned to expect Manderson to never take the obvious path, but no one could ever guess what he’d do instead. The whole market would rattle like a loose tin when word got out the old man was out hunting, and half his opponents would surrender before he even made his move. A scheme that would take most people months to plot out in full? Manderson could map it down to the last tiny detail while he shaved.
“I’ve always suspected that faint strain of Iroquois blood in him explained that unholy mix of cunning and cruelty. Hardly anyone even knew that line existed— only he and me. He asked me once, because I dabbled in genealogy, to dig into his obscure family history, and that’s how I found he was a direct descendant of Montour, the legendary Iroquois chief, and his French wife, that wild, fearsome woman who ruled tribal politics across the Wilderness 200 years back. The Mandersons ran fur trading posts on the Pennsylvania border back then, and more than a few married Indigenous women. That secret landed a year before he died, and he was horrified. He was so terrified of anyone finding out he had mixed blood, he made me swear to take it to my grave. I kept that promise while he lived, but I’m convinced that’s when he started turning against me.”

“Did Manderson have any definable religious beliefs?” Mr. Cupples asked so sharply the other two men jumped.
Marlowe paused for a beat. “None I ever saw. He never prayed, he never set foot in a church, never even mentioned God. But he’d had a strict religious upbringing as a child, and for all that, he was almost ascetic— no drinking, no women, nothing but cigars for a vice. I lived with him four years, and in all that time, I never once heard him utter an outright verbal lie. He’d lie through every business trick, every misleading market rumour, every deception he could pull off— but he would never, ever say a direct falsehood over the smallest trivial thing. Can you wrap your head around that? It’s like a soldier who’s scrupulously honest face to face, but will burn villages and fake dispatches to fool the enemy. For him, the whole business world was a war zone. The rules of the game let him lie through action, but never through his words.”

“It’s a sad old world,” Cupples observed.
“Couldn’t agree more,” Marlowe nodded. “And that unbreakable habit of telling no direct lie? The first time I ever heard him say a straight-up falsehood was the night he died. And hearing that lie is what saved me from hanging for his murder.”

Trent shifted restlessly in his chair. “Before we get to that part,” he cut in, “tell us exactly what your position was with Manderson for all those years.”
“We got on perfectly, right up until the end. It was never friendship— he was the sort who never let anyone get close enough to be a friend. But I was his most trusted secretary. I came to him straight after I finished my Oxford degree. I was supposed to go into my father’s business back home, but my dad suggested I take a couple of years off and see the world, and this secretary post popped up, and I stayed four years. The whole thing happened because of chess, of all things.”

At the word, Trent smacked his palms together, letting out a sharp exclamation. The other two stared at him.
“Chess!” Trent repeated, standing up and stepping toward Marlowe. “You know what the first thing I noticed about you the day we met? Your eyes. I couldn’t place it then, but now I know exactly where I’d seen that exact look before. It was on Nikolay Korchagin, the grandmaster, the man I sat across from in a railway carriage for two whole days once. That cold, faraway, calculating chess-player’s eye. I couldn’t put a name to it when I saw it on you, that’s all.”

“I’ve played since I was a kid, ran in the Oxford chess circles,” Marlowe said simply. “Dr. Munro at Queen’s, the one I never once beat, sent for me my last term. Told me some rich American businessman was looking for an English secretary, and the only hard requirements were chess, a hunting licence, and an Oxford education. That’s how I got the job.
“I loved it, at first. You don’t get a lot of dull days trailing a vibrant, at-the-top-of-his-powers American plutocrat. And then my father’s business hit a rough patch not long after I started, so I was grateful to not need an allowance from him. A year in, Manderson doubled my salary. ‘Big money,’ he told me, ‘but I don’t lose a penny of it.’ By then I was doing far more than ride horses with him in the morning and play chess after dinner. I ran his houses, his Ohio farm, his Maine hunting lodge, his stable, his cars, his yacht. I could recite every British railway timetable from memory, I knew every cigar shop in London by name. The work was fun, varied, I had plenty of time to go out and enjoy myself, plenty of money to spend. I made a fool of myself over a girl once, back then— that was the hard patch, and it was Mrs. Manderson who helped me through it. You can ask her about it, if she wants to tell you. But Manderson? He never treated me anything but fair, nothing but generous. I never had the faintest inkling he was anything but happy with the work I did for him. Right up until the night he died. That night, he showed me the depths of the raving, crazy hatred he’d been hiding for me all that time.”

Trent and Cupples exchanged a long, quiet look.
“You never suspected he hated you before that?” Trent asked.
“To what end do you think that hatred was aimed?” Cupples added at the same time.

“I never had a clue,” Marlowe said. “How long he’d felt that way, I have no idea. I still can’t work out why. I thought, in those terrible days right after his death, that he must have been in the grip of some madman’s delusion, convinced I was plotting against him. You can’t plumb the depths of a lunatic’s mind. Can you even imagine a state of mind where a man will plot his own death, just to make sure the person he hates gets hanged for his murder?”

Cupples jolted upright in his chair. “You’re saying Manderson killed himself?”
Trent shot him an impatient glance, his gaze never leaving Marlowe’s face.
“I am,” Marlowe said flatly, meeting his eye. Cupples nodded.

“Wait, hold on,” Trent cut in gently, laying a hand on Cupples’s arm before the old man could launch into a philosophical tangent. “Tell us the facts of that Sunday night, step by step.”

Marlowe flushed at the faint emphasis Trent put on the word “facts”. He sat up straighter.
“Bunner and I dined with Mr. and Mrs. Manderson that Sunday evening. It was exactly like every other quiet dinner the four of us had. Manderson was quiet, sullen, the way he’d been the last few months. The rest of us made conversation, we finished dinner around nine. Mrs. Manderson went to the drawing room, Bunner headed up to the little hotel nearby to meet a friend. Manderson asked me to walk out to the orchard behind the house with him, said he needed to talk to me. We paced up and down the path, far enough from the house that no one could overhear us, and he talked to me in that cool, calm, deliberate tone he used for big business. He never seemed saner, or more friendly. He said he had a secret big deal going, no one knew about it, not even Bunner, and he needed me to do exactly what he told me, no questions asked.
“That was normal for him, that’s how he always operated. I told him I was ready. He told me there was a man named George Harris, using that alias, who was supposed to take the noon Southampton to Havre ferry to Paris the next day. He’d had me book that very berth in Harris’s name a week earlier. But Harris couldn’t leave now, he said. He needed that man to stay in England. Someone else had to take the wallet of papers to Paris instead, otherwise the whole multi-million dollar deal would collapse. He wanted me to go.
“I didn’t love the idea, but I was never going to let him down. I said sure, I’d do it. He told me my face was known to all his rivals, so I’d have to disguise myself, travel the whole way as George Harris, no contact with him at all, no letters, no phone calls, until I’d handed over the wallet in Paris. I was to drive his car all through the night straight to Southampton, leave it at a garage under a fake name, meet Harris at the Bedford Hotel if he was there, if not just board the ferry, check into the Hotel St Petersbourg in Paris, wait for a note addressed to George Harris telling me where to drop the locked wallet off.
“I ran back to my room, stuffed a few things in a kit bag, my head spinning. Manderson loved this over-the-top, secretive melodrama stuff, so I thought nothing of it. I grabbed the car from the garage behind the house, and just as I was pulling it around to the front of the house, I realised I had barely a few shillings in my pocket.
“I was flat broke. A few months earlier, I’d tried dabbling in stock speculation, lost every penny, ended up deep in debt. I’d gone to Manderson, told him the whole mess, and he’d advanced me a huge chunk of my future salary to clear the debts. He knew I had no cash, he knew I’d borrowed pocket change off Bunner to get by until my next paycheque came in.
“I walked back into the library and told him I needed cash for the journey. That’s when I saw the first thing that made me stop and feel like something was very, very wrong. He went to reach for his left hip pocket, the one he always kept his hundred quid in notes in— a habit so automatic he did it every time he paid for anything. Then he froze mid-movement, and swore under his breath. I’d never heard him curse before. I thought he’d lost his little note case. But a week earlier, when I’d been up in London running errands for him, I’d drawn a thousand pounds in small notes from his bank for him, and I’d seen him fingering that thick stack of cash on his desk earlier that same day.”

The heavy wrought-iron gate of the west-end flat complex clanged shut behind them as Mr. Cupples squinted up at Trent, his mild brow creased. “What’s this you were saying about a half-past seven appointment? Did we ever agree to such a thing?”
“Of course we did,” Trent replied, stepping out with an uncharacteristic, almost giddy lightness to his stride that made a passing constable smile indulgently, the look on his face so bright the man assumed he’d had more than a few tots of gin to celebrate. “You’re dining on my tab. This case, the one that’s been gnawing at my brain for over a year, is finally wrapped up tight. That’s reason enough to splash out on a feast, no arguments. We’re skipping my club, by the way. Last thing I want is for the rest of the stuffy old members to see me grinning like a schoolboy over a solved mystery, that’d tank my reputation for good. And besides, their roast mutton tastes like boilerplate, every single night, no exceptions. No, we’re going to Sheppard’s.”
“Who on earth is Sheppard?” Cupples asked, trailing after him up Victoria Street.
Trent let out a dramatic groan. “That question, Cupples, is the perfect example of this modern age’s useless appetite for aimless fact-checking. You want to interrogate the poor bloke before you even set foot in his dining room? I’ve never met Sheppard, for all I know he’s a myth the place made up. All I care about is that his saddle of mutton is so tender and juicy, I’ve seen visiting Americans sob into their plates and curse Christopher Columbus for ever sailing to the New World and making them miss it. Taxi!”
A black cab glided to the kerb, the driver nodding majestically when Trent rattled off the address. He lit a cigarette, his fingers still jittery with leftover excitement, and leaned forward. “Also, I have another bit of news. I’m going to marry Mabel. I assume the connection between that and my need for a very good dinner is obvious.”
“Mabel?” Cupples stopped short, then grabbed Trent’s hand to shake it so hard his knuckles whitened. “My dear boy, that’s the best news I’ve heard in ten years! I could not be happier for you two. I’ve been hoping this would happen since that dinner I hosted, when you sat half-listening to old Professor Peppmuller ramble on about geology, and stared at Mabel like the sun rose and set in her pocket. Us old fogeys notice these things, you know.”
“Mabel says she knew I was smitten even before that night,” Trent said, looking slightly sheepish. “I thought I was hiding it perfectly, too. Turns out I’m terrible at pretending I don’t care. But never mind that, I’m too happy to be embarrassed. I’m going to be unbearable tonight, fair warning. I might even sing you that old music hall number you loved so much, the one that goes-“ He started stamping his feet on the cab floor in a terrible impression of a clog dance, belting out the silly ditty about the old man with the wooden leg and the tobacco box, until Cupples protested he’d never heard the song in his life, and certainly never sang it. Trent just laughed it off, saying the fullness of his heart was spilling out any which way it could.
The cab swung around a corner, and pulled up outside the familiar panelled front of Sheppard’s. The dining room hummed with the noise of a hundred different dinner conversations, three bookmakers tucking into roast pork at Trent’s usual favourite table, so he steered Cupples to an empty corner booth by the crackling open fire.
“Order whatever you want,” Trent told him, winking at the waiter who knew his order off by heart. “The wine’s actually made from grapes here, not whatever swill they serve at my club.”
Cupples blinked at him. “I think I’ll have milk and soda water, please.”
“Shhh!” Trent hissed, mock horrified. “The head waiter has a weak heart, he’ll drop dead on the spot if he hears you asking for that abomination. But fine, fine, it’s your drink, don’t let me stop you. I won’t be the one to stand between a man and his terrible life choices.”
Once the waiter had brought Cupples his bizarre non-alcoholic drink and darted off to attend to another table, Trent leaned back, cutting into his mutton with a huge appetite. “We can talk freely here, no one’s listening. What did you make of Marlowe’s confession this afternoon?”
Cupples chewed his food slowly, dicing every piece of meat into tiny neat cubes before he swallowed. “The most ironic part of the whole thing, to my mind, is that we knew the one secret Marlowe never learned: that Manderson was eaten alive by insane, irrational jealousy of him, for no real reason at all. We didn’t say a word about it out of respect for Mabel, of course. How strange it is, isn’t it? We all move through life completely unaware of the wild, wrong assumptions other people hold about us. I once found out a whole group of my friends thought I’d secretly converted to Catholicism, all because I offhand said it might be nice to skip eating meat one day a week. Manderson’s conviction that Marlowe had betrayed him was almost certainly built on just as tiny, meaningless a scrap of nothing. And Marlowe’s story? It’s all perfectly straightforward, once you accept that Manderson was not a well man.”
Trent laughed out loud. “You think that whole wild plot, where he impersonated Manderson to fake his own alibi, is straightforward?”
“Only the details are unusual,” Cupples replied calmly. “A madman cooks up a plot to ruin an innocent man, and accidentally gets himself killed in the process. That sort of thing happens all the time. You want to hear something that’s actually remarkable? The life cycle of the liver fluke, as documented by Poulton. That’s bizarre, far more fascinating than any silly murder plot.”
Trent blinked at him. “I can’t argue that, I’ve never even heard of the liver fluke.”
“My point is,” Cupples said, sipping his milk and soda, “we’re all so quick to gawk at sensational crime drama and miss the far weirder ordinary things happening all around us. Marlowe’s impersonation trick was clever, but it wasn’t genius. He already sounded just like Manderson, he was a decent amateur actor, he knew every inch of that house. The idea was practically lying on the table in front of him. The only reason the case got so complicated was because you had two sharp strategic minds playing against each other, not just one. That sort of thing almost never happens in cases of real crime. Criminals, even the very clever ones like Crippen, are usually only good for one simple trick. They never think two steps ahead.”
He paused, his face growing serious. “But there is one thing that’s been bothering me, ever since we talked to Marlowe today. If he hadn’t caught on to Manderson’s trap in time, he would almost certainly have hanged for a murder he didn’t commit. How many innocent men have already been hanged, convicted on nothing but circumstantial evidence? I’ll never trust a death sentence based on that sort of proof ever again.”
“Couldn’t agree more,” Trent nodded. “I once read an old case, the Campden Murder, where a man named John Perry swore he helped his mother and brother kill a local man. All three were hanged, based purely on his word. Two years later, the ‘victim’ walked back into town, he’d been kidnapped by pirates. Perry had sacrificed his own life in his testimony, just to make the lie seem believable. If Marlowe had told the full truth to a jury, no one on earth would have believed him. The prosecution would have torn his story to shreds. We both thought he was guilty, right up until this afternoon.”
“Wait a moment,” Cupples cut in, laying down his knife and fork very gently. “I never thought he was guilty. I was certain, from the very start, that he was innocent.”
Trent stared at him, confused. “What? After all the evidence we went through, all the circumstantial proof that pointed straight at him? That’s not reasonable, Cupples.”
“I am certain,” Cupples said firmly, folding his hands on the table in front of his plate. “I never doubted him for a second, because I knew something you didn’t. You said just now there isn’t a single scrap of evidence that backs up Marlowe’s story. There is. My evidence. And it’s completely conclusive.”
Trent’s face blanched white as a sheet, the giddy warmth draining out of him in a single rush. He gaped across the table at his old friend, too shocked to speak.
Cupples wiped a stray crumb from his sparse moustache, leaned forward, and spoke in a quiet, even tone.
“It’s very simple. I shot Manderson myself.”
