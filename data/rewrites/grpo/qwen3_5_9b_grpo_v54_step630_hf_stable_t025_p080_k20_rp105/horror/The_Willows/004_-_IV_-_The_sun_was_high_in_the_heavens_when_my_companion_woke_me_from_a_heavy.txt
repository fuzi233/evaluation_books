The sun burned high and white in the heavens when my companion roused me from a heavy, dreamless sleep. He announced that the porridge was ready and that there was just enough time to bathe before the river claimed us. The scent of frizzling bacon drifted through the tent flap, a savory promise of survival.

"River still rising," he said, his voice tight. "Several islands have vanished into the flood. Our own is shrinking by the hour."

"Any wood left?" I asked, my voice thick with grogginess.

"The wood and the island will finish tomorrow in a dead heat," he laughed, but the sound lacked its usual warmth. "Enough to last us till then."

I plunged into the water at the island's point, which had already shifted in shape and size during the night. The current swept me instantly to the landing opposite the tent. The water was ice-cold, and the rushing banks blurred past like scenery from a speeding train. Bathing in such conditions was a shock to the system, an exhilarating purge that seemed to wash the terror of the night from my brain. Yet, as I climbed out, the sun blazing hot against a cloudless sky, I felt a chill that had nothing to do with the water. The wind had not abated; it roared with a relentless, mechanical fury.

Suddenly, the implication of his words struck me. "Enough to last till tomorrow"—he meant we were staying another night. It was a drastic change from the urgency of the previous evening. How had his resolve crumbled so quickly?

Breakfast was a chaotic affair. Great chunks of the riverbank crumbled into the frying pan, sending clouds of spray into our food, while he talked incessantly about the impossibility of the Vienna-Pesth steamers navigating the flood. But his state of mind troubled me far more than the rising water. He had changed. His manner was agitated, shy, laced with a suspicion that made his gestures jerky and his voice uncertain. I couldn't quite define it then, but I knew with absolute certainty that he was frightened.

He ate little and omitted his pipe. Instead, he spread the map open beside him, staring at the markings with a furrowed brow.

"We'd better get off sharp in an hour," I said, sensing an opening for a confession.

"Rather! If they'll let us," he replied, his eyes fixed on the paper.

"Who'll let us? The elements?" I asked, feigning indifference.

"The powers of this awful place, whoever they are," he said, not looking up. "The gods are here, if they are anywhere at all in the world."

"The elements are always the true immortals," I replied, forcing a laugh that felt brittle in my throat.

"We shall be fortunate if we get away without further disaster," he said gravely, finally meeting my gaze across the smoke.

That was exactly what I had dreaded. I forced myself to ask the direct question. It was like consenting to a tooth extraction; the pain was inevitable, and the rest was just pretense.

"Further disaster! What's happened?"

"For one thing—the steering paddle's gone," he said quietly.

"The steering paddle gone!" I repeated, my excitement spiking. Without a rudder, the Danube in flood was suicide. "But what—"

"And there's a tear in the bottom of the canoe," he added, a genuine tremor shaking his voice.

I stared at him, repeating the words in my mind. There, on the burning sand, a freezing atmosphere descended upon us. He nodded gravely and led the way toward the tent. The canoe lay there as I had left her, ribs uppermost, and beside her sat the single paddle.

"There's only one," he said, stooping to pick it up. "And here's the rent in the base-board."

I almost told him I had seen two paddles an hour ago, but a second impulse silenced me. I approached to inspect the damage.

There was a long, cleanly made tear in the bottom of the canoe where a sliver of wood had been neatly gouged out. It looked as if a sharp rock had eaten down her length, and investigation confirmed the hole went all the way through. Had we launched without noticing, we would have foundered instantly.

"There," he murmured, more to himself than to me, "an attempt to prepare a victim for the sacrifice. Two victims, rather," he added, running his fingers along the slit.

I began to whistle—a habit I had when utterly nonplussed—and pretended not to hear. I was determined to dismiss his words as foolishness.

"It wasn't there last night," he said, looking anywhere but at me.

"We must have scratched her in landing," I said, stopping my whistle. "The stones are very sharp."

I stopped abruptly. He turned and met my eye squarely. I knew as well as he did that my explanation was impossible. There were no stones.

"And then there's this to explain too," he added, handing me the paddle and pointing to the blade.

A new, curdling emotion spread over me as I examined it. The blade was scraped down all over, beautifully smoothed, as if someone had sanded it with care until it was so thin that the first vigorous stroke would snap it off.

"One of us walked in his sleep and did this," I said feebly. "Or perhaps the wind blew sand against it."

"Ah," said the Swede, turning away with a bitter laugh. "You can explain everything."

"The same wind that caught the paddle and flung it near the bank," I called out, desperate to maintain the illusion of rationality.

"I see," he shouted back before disappearing among the willow bushes.

Alone with the evidence, my first thought was that one of us had done it, and certainly not I. But my second thought dismissed that too. My companion, the trusted friend of a dozen expeditions, could not have knowingly sabotaged us. Nor could this practical man have suddenly gone insane.

Yet, the certainty remained: his mind had altered. He was nervous, suspicious, aware of events he refused to speak of, waiting for a climax he expected imminently.

I inspected the tent and surroundings. The measurements of the night remained unchanged, but I noticed deep, basin-shaped hollows in the sand, varying from tea-cup sizes to large bowls. The wind must have created these miniature craters, just as it had tossed the paddle. The rent in the canoe was the only inexplicable thing, though I clung to the theory of a sharp snag with the diminishing portion of my intelligence I called "reason." An explanation was an absolute necessity for sanity.

I set the pitch melting, and the Swede joined me. Under the best conditions, the canoe could not travel safely until the next day. I casually drew his attention to the hollows.

"Yes," he said. "They're all over the island. But *you* can explain them, no doubt!"

"Wind, of course," I answered without hesitation. "Little whirlwinds in the street twist everything into circles. This sand is loose enough."

He made no reply. We worked in silence. I watched him surreptitiously; I felt he was watching me. He seemed to be listening to something I could not hear, turning his head toward the bushes, the sky, and the water through the willow gaps. Sometimes he pressed his hand to his ear, holding it there for minutes. He said nothing, and I asked no questions. While he mended the torn canoe with the skill of a red Indian, I was glad of his absorption, fearing he would speak of the changed aspect of the willows. If he had noticed that, my imagination would no longer suffice.

At length, he began to talk in a hurried voice.

"Queer thing," he said, eager to get it over. "About that otter last night."

I had expected something else entirely. "Shows how lonely this place is. Otters are shy—"

"I don't mean that. Did you think it really was an otter?"

"What else, in the name of Heaven?"

"You saw it before me. It seemed so much bigger. It had extraordinary yellow eyes."

"That was the sun," I laughed boisterously.

He looked at me absently. "I wondered what that thing in the boat was. I remember thinking it was not a man. The whole business rose suddenly out of the water."

I laughed again, but with impatience and anger. "Look here, this place is queer enough without imagining things! That was an ordinary boat, an ordinary man, and an otter. Don't play the fool!"

He looked steadily at me, grave and unannoyed. "You fool! That's just the way all victims talk. As if you didn't understand just as well as I do!" he sneered, a tone of resignation in his voice. "Keep quiet and hold your mind firm. Self-deception only makes the truth harder."

My little effort was over. I knew his words were true. Up to this point, he had kept ahead of me, proving himself more sensitive to these extraordinary happenings. I had missed the point about the necessity of a victim, that we ourselves were destined to satisfy the want. I dropped all pretense, and my fear increased steadily to a climax.

"But you're right about one thing," he added. "We're wiser not to talk about it, or even think about it, because what one *thinks* finds expression in words, and what one *says*, happens."

That afternoon, while the canoe dried, we fished, tested the leak, and collected wood. The island grew perceptibly smaller as the banks were torn away. The weather remained fine until four o'clock, when the wind finally showed signs of abating. Clouds gathered in the southwest.

This lessening of the wind was a relief, yet the silence that came at five o'clock was oppressive. The booming of the river filled the air with deep, monotonous murmurs, sounding like the music of doom. The withdrawal of sunlight stripped the landscape of cheerfulness. The willows stood densely together, rustling among themselves when no wind stirred, shaking oddly from the roots upward. In the darkening light, they assumed a bizarre grotesquerie, appearing as purposeful, living creatures. Their very ordinariness masked a malignancy. The forces of the region were focusing upon our island, and upon ourselves.

I had slept in the afternoon, recovering somewhat from the exhausted night, but this only made me more susceptible to the haunting spell. I fought against it, laughing at my feelings as absurd, yet I dreaded the approaching night as a child lost in a forest dreads the dark.

The canoe was covered with a waterproof sheet, and the single paddle was tied securely to a tree. From five o'clock, I prepared dinner. We had potatoes, onions, bacon fat, and a residue of former stews, mixed with black bread. My companion sat lazily watching me, cleaning his pipe and offering useless advice. He had been quiet all afternoon, re-caulking the canoe and fishing for driftwood. No more talk of undesirable things had passed between us.

The pot began to bubble when I heard his voice calling from the bank.

"Come and listen," he said, holding his hand cupwise to his ear. "See what you make of it."

"Now do you hear anything?" he asked curiously.

We stood there, listening. At first, there was only the deep note of the water and the hiss of turbulence. Then, a peculiar sound reached my ears—a humming like a distant gong. It came from the waste of swamps and willows opposite, repeated at regular intervals. It was neither a bell nor a steamer's hoot. It sounded like an immense gong suspended in the sky, striking a muffled, metallic note. My heart quickened.

"I've heard it all day," he said. "While you slept, it came all round the island. Sometimes overhead, sometimes under the water. Once, I could have sworn it was *within myself*—like a sound in the fourth dimension."

I was too puzzled to pay much attention. "The wind in the sand-funnels, or the bushes rubbing together?"

"It comes from the whole swamp. From everywhere at once. It comes from the willow bushes somehow."

"But the wind has dropped," I objected.

"It is *because* the wind has dropped we now hear it. It was drowned before. It is the cry of the—"

I dashed back to the fire, warned by the bubbling stew, determined to escape further conversation. I dreaded that he would begin about the gods or elemental forces.

"Come and cut up bread," I called, stirring the mixture.

He came over slowly, taking the provision sack from the tree and emptying its contents onto the ground-sheet.

"Hurry up! It's boiling."

The Swede burst into a roar of laughter that startled me. It was forced, mirthless.

"There's nothing here!" he shouted, holding his sides.

"Bread, I mean."

"It's gone. They've taken it!"

I dropped the spoon and ran up. Everything lay on the sheet, but there was no loaf.

The whole dead weight of my growing fear fell upon me. Then I burst out laughing too. It was the only thing to do. The sound of my laughter made me understand his. It was an explosion of psychical pressure, a safety-valve for repressed forces. With both of us, it ceased suddenly.

"How criminally stupid of me!" I cried, determined to find an explanation. "I forgot to buy a loaf at Pressburg. That chattering woman put everything out of my head."

"The oatmeal, too, is much less than this morning," the Swede interrupted.

"Why draw attention to it?" I thought angrily.

"There's enough for tomorrow," I said, stirring vigorously. "We can get more at Komorn or Gran. In twenty-four hours we'll be miles from here."

"I hope so—to God," he muttered, putting the things back, "unless we're claimed first as victims for the sacrifice," he added with a foolish laugh. He dragged the sack into the tent, and I heard him mumbling indistinctly.
