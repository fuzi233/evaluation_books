The shadows of Cartagena clung to the stone walls like a second skin, heavy and suffocating after three weeks of silence. Deep within a subterranean wing of the city, where the air smelled of damp earth and unwashed bodies, lay a row of cells. In one, the iron bar of the door hung ajar, revealing a gloom illuminated only by the sputtering flame of a tallow lamp.

Standing there were two figures wrapped in the same grey habit of the Holy Office. One was Eustace Leigh, his face a mask of rigid composure though his hands trembled slightly behind his back. Beside him stood his superior, a man whose eyes held the cold, calculating glint of a shark. On the straw mattress beneath the lamp lay Frank, the young man who had been dragged from the savagery of the jungle, beaten nearly to death, and now reserved for a different kind of torment—the slow, agonizing unmaking of the spirit.

Frank had undergone the *question* that afternoon. Now, the Inquisition sought not merely his confession, but his recantation, while his mind was still reeling from the physical trauma. "It is necessary," Eustace muttered to himself, the words tasting like ash. "Duty calls."

Yet, as he gazed at the pale, broken form of his cousin, a terrible contradiction gnawed at him. He loved Frank. He always had. But love, he reminded himself, was a luxury for the innocent. Here, in the theater of divine justice, emotion was a weakness to be excised. He stepped forward, raising the lamp. The flickering light caught Frank's high forehead, the delicate features twisted in sleep. For a fleeting second, Eustace thought he saw a ghost of a smile play upon those lips—a sign of the peace that transcends understanding. Then he heard it: a faint, muffled whisper drifting from the depths of the dream. A mother's name. And something far older, holier, and more terrifying than any god the world knew.

Eustace froze. The lamp slipped from his fingers, catching the straw with a soft thud, but he did not bend to retrieve it. To wake him now, while the boy's heart was still tender from the lash, would be murder. The mercy of the Church required patience, not haste.

"Let him rest," Eustace whispered, his voice cracking just enough to betray the storm inside. "I fear my words will be of little use."

"The boy is an iron saint," the superior replied, his tone bored. "He laughed in our faces when we first brought him here. Open defiance."

"Great is the pravity of the human heart," Eustace murmured, bowing his head. "And the power of Satan. Forgive me, Father. We shall return when the time is right."

"And the Sorceress?" the superior asked suddenly, his gaze sharp. "Where is she?"

"The Elder? Or the Younger?"

"The Younger. The Señora de Soto."

"A pity," the superior mused, a cruel smirk playing on his lips. "One could almost feel compassion for her, were it not for the stain of heresy upon her soul." He gestured casually toward a heavy wooden door further down the corridor. "She is currently with the Notary. Perhaps you should hear the proceedings, Señor Leigh. It might jog your memory regarding the nature of truth."

Eustace hesitated. A cold sweat broke out across his brow, making his clothes cling to his skin. Fear, primal and ancient, seized him. Yet, a morbid curiosity, born of a conscience that felt increasingly dead, pulled him forward. He moved toward the door, his knees knocking together, his heart hammering against his ribs as if trying to burst free. He pressed his back against the rough stone wall, hiding his convulsed face from his companion.

Inside, voices drifted out, low but clear. The Notary was speaking, laying out the standard charges of witchcraft—the old, convenient fabrications used to justify the bloodshed of the Inquisition. And then, cut through the haze, came the voice of the accused woman. It was sharp, filled with a raw, animalistic indignation.

"Witchcraft against Don Guzmán?" she cried, her voice rising in a crescendo of agony. "What need of that, Oh God! What need!"

"You deny it, Señora?" the Notary's voice was smooth, oily. "We are sorry for you... but the evidence is overwhelming."

A confused, choking murmur followed, mixed with words that made no sense to Eustace, sounds of pure terror and confusion. Then, silence fell, broken only by a sudden, piercing wail.

"She has confessed!" Eustace gasped, the words tearing from his throat. "Saints preserve us! She—she..."

The sound that followed shattered him. It was a wail of such depth and despair that it rang in his ears, his brain, and his very soul. It was the sound of a spirit breaking under the weight of false accusation. Eustace lunged for the door, his hand grasping at the wood, desperate to tear it open and stop the horror. But the superior grabbed his arm, dragging him back with brute force.

"They must finish the work," the superior said coldly. "Do not interfere."

Another wail. And another. Like the cry of an avenging angel turned madman, the sound pursued Eustace as he fled down the corridor and burst out into the night.

Outside, the air was thick with the scent of jasmine and the warmth of the tropics. Above, the moon poured a golden, silvery light over a garden that looked, in its stillness, like a fragment of Eden. But as Eustace stumbled into the cool air, the screams followed him, echoing through his mind, turning paradise into hell.

"Oh, misery! Misery, misery!" he groaned, clutching his head, his teeth ground together in silent anguish. "I have brought her to this! I have had to bring her to it! What else could I do? Who dares blame me? And yet, what devilish sin have I committed that requires punishment of this magnitude? Was there no one else? No one? And yet... it may save her soul. It may bring her to repentance!"

"It may, indeed," the superior's voice followed him, calm and detached. "For she is delicate, and cannot endure much. You ought to know, Señor, the merciful disposition of the Holy Office."

"I know it," Eustace stammered, his voice trembling now with a different kind of fear. "All in love—all in love. A paternal chastisement."

"And consider the proof of heresy," the superior continued, stepping closer. "Patent, undeniable. Alongside the strong suspicion of enchantment, and the notorious reputation of the Elder Sorceress. You yourself admitted, did you not, that she was a witch in England before the Señora brought her here as an attendant?"

"Yes," Eustace choked out, tears streaming silently down his face. "Of course she was. Of course. There was no other course open. And though the flesh may be weak in my case, Sir, let it be known that the spirit remains willing!"

With those final, hollow words, Eustace walked away. The garden, the moon, the beauty of the Indies—it all faded into a blur. By the time the sun dipped below the horizon, he had gone to the principal of the Jesuits. He had laid bare his soul—or whatever remained of it—and begged to be allowed to complete his novitiate, with the promise of immediate return to Europe. "Otherwise," he had pleaded, his voice breaking, "I shall go mad, even if I am not already."

The Jesuit, a man of gentle countenance, had interceded. He spoke of Eustace's past valor, framing his actions as zealous service rather than moral failure. The Inquisitors, satisfied, released him. That same night, Eustace Leigh left Cartagena. He sailed the following week, bound for a destination unknown, a ship carrying a cargo of a man who had died twice.

In the pages of this history, Eustace Leigh vanished. He might have risen to become a General of the Order, a conqueror of souls in the tropics, reducing both body and spirit to dust. Or he might have returned to England, only to hang alongside Ballard three years later for his part in treason. Perhaps he lived out his days in some forgotten forest, a slave to the will of the Society.

But it mattered not. Eustace was no longer a man. He was a tool, a mechanism of the Church, a vessel that carried out orders without question, indifferent to the good or evil they wrought. By an act of moral suicide, he had traded his soul for the illusion of salvation, shedding his conscience, his will, and his humanity to serve "The Society." As he himself had declared, he was dead. And as the old proverb went, let the dead bury their dead. There was no story left to tell about Eustace Leigh, only the shadow he cast on the future of those who followed.
