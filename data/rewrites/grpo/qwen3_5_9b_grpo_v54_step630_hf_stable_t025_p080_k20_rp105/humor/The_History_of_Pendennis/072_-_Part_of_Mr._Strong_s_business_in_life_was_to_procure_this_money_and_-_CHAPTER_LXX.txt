Arthur Pendennis had just touched down in London, though his stay was merely a fleeting stopover. After dropping off his fellow travelers at a hotel he'd escorted them to from the West, he hurried straight to the Chambers in Lamb Court. The building was dreary, yet not entirely devoid of comfort, basking in whatever patches of sunlight dared to visit its gloomy corridors. In such chambers, freedom itself serves as the sun; the Templars may grumble, but they eventually settle in to enjoy their ease.

His domestic announced that Warrington was also present. Naturally, Arthur bolted up the stairs to his friend's room. As always, the air was thick with the scent of pipe tobacco, and George was already deep in his newspapers and reviews. The two greeted one another with that specific, rough cordiality reserved for young Englishmen—a facade of rudeness hiding a furnace of warmth and affection.

"Well, young one!" Warrington grinned, pulling his pipe from his mouth.
"How are you, old boy?" Arthur replied, extending a hand.

It was a greeting between friends who hadn't seen each other in months. Imagine Alphonse and Frederic rushing into each other's arms, shrieking *Ce bon cœur!* over one another's shoulders; or Max and Wilhelm exchanging a dozen kisses scented with Havana on their mustaches. But Britons? "Well, young one!" "How are you, old boy!" is all they say, perhaps after saving each other's lives the day before. Tomorrow, the handshakes cease, replaced by head-wags at breakfast. Yet, beneath this stoical decorum lies a bond of absolute trust: they would share their purses, defend each other with the loudest enthusiasm, and yet part with a perfunctory "Good-bye" and meet with a casual "How-d'you-do?" in the interim. It is a curious, modest, strangely stoical form of friendship. "Yes, we aren't demonstrative like those confounded foreigners," Hardman might say, a man who showed no friendship and felt none in his entire life.

"Been in Switzerland?" Pen asked.
"Yes," Warrington confirmed.
"Couldn't find a bit of tobacco fit to smoke until we hit Strasbourg, where I finally got some caporal."

The man's mind was undoubtedly filled with the grand sights and the vast emotions of nature, yet his enthusiasm remained coy, veiled behind a cloud of tobacco smoke. He would save his true confessions for confidential evenings and pour his ardor into his writings instead. His travels would enrich his prose with pregnant allusions and brilliant illustrations, coloring his eloquence and sharpening his wit, even if he never displayed them in conversation.

The elder gave a rapid, gloomy account of his tour: Switzerland, North Italy, the Tyrol, Vienna, Dresden, and the Rhine. He spoke of these places in a sulky voice, as if mentioning them made him miserable. Then the young man began. He had been in the country, bored stiff, canvassing at a glacial pace. He was only in town for a day or two before heading to Tunbridge Wells to visit friends who would be equally slow. How difficult it is for an Englishman to admit he is happy!

"And the seat in Parliament, Pen? Have you made it all right?" Warrington asked.
"All right," Pen said. "As soon as Parliament meets and a new writ is issued, Clavering retires, and I step into his shoes."
"And under which king does Bezonian speak or die?" Warrington pressed. "Are we Liberals, Conservatives, Government men, or flying our own flag?"
"Hem!" Pen sighed. "There are no real politics now; everyone's politics are pretty much the same. I haven't got enough acres to be a Protectionist, nor could I be one even if I owned the county. I'll go pretty much with the Government, though I plan to lead them on some social questions I've been working on during the vacation—don't grin, you old cynic. I've been compiling the Blue Books and intend to be strong on Sanitary and Colonisation issues."
"We reserve the liberty of voting against the Government, though we're generally friendly. We are friends of the people *avant tout*. We give lectures at the Clavering Institute and shake hands with intelligent mechanics. We believe the franchise should be greatly enlarged, yet we are free to accept office once the House has listened to our crack speeches and the Administration sees our merit."
"I am not Moses," Pen said, with his usual touch of melancholy. "I have no laws from Heaven to bring down from the mountain. I don't belong to the mountain, nor do I set myself up as a leader of mankind. My faith isn't strong enough for that, nor my vanity, nor my hypocrisy. I won't tell any lies, George, I promise you; I'll only coincide with those necessary ones that keep circulation flowing. Give a man the advantage of his skeptical turn. If I find a good thing to say in the House, I'll say it; a good measure, I'll support it; a fair place, I'll take it, and be glad of my luck. But I wouldn't flatter a great man more than a mob. Now you know as much about my politics as I do. What call have I to be a Whig? Whiggism isn't a divine institution. Why not vote with the Liberal Conservatives? They've done for the nation what the Whigs never could without them. Who converted both? The Radicals and the country outside. I think the *Morning Post* is often right, and *Punch* is often wrong. I don't profess a calling; I just take advantage of a chance. *Parlons d'autre chose*."

"The next thing at your heart, after ambition, is love, I suppose?" Warrington asked. "How have our young loves prospered? Are we changing conditions and leaving the chambers? Are you going to divorce me, Arthur, and take a wife?"
"I suppose so," Pen said. "She's very good-natured and lively. She sings, and she doesn't mind smoking. She'll have a fair fortune—I don't know exactly how much—but my uncle augurs everything from the Begum's generosity and says she'll come down very handsomely. And I think Blanche is devilish fond of me," Arthur added, sighing.
"That means we accept her caresses and her money."
"Haven't we said before that life is a transaction?" Pendennis countered. "I don't pretend to break my heart over her. I've told her pretty fairly what my feelings are—and I've engaged myself to her. Since I last saw her, especially over the last two months in the country, I think she's grown fonder and fonder. Her letters, and especially hers to Laura, show it. Mine have been simple—no raptures, no vows, you understand—but I've treated it as an *affaire faite*, neither hastening nor deferring the completion."
"And Laura? How is she?" Warrington asked frankly.
"Laura, George," Pen said, staring hard at his friend—"by heaven, Laura is the best, noblest, and dearest girl the sun ever shone upon." His voice faltered; he seemed unable to utter the words. He stretched out his hand to his comrade, who took it and nodded.
"Have you only found out that now, young un?" Warrington asked after a pause.
"Who hasn't learned things too late, George?" Arthur cried, gathering words and emotion in his impetuous rush. "Whose life isn't a disappointment? Who carries his heart entire to the grave without mutilation? I never knew anyone who was truly happy, or who hasn't had to ransom themselves from Fate's grasp with the payment of some dearest treasure. Lucky if we're left alone afterwards, having paid our fine, if the tyrant visits us no more. Suppose I've found out I've lost the greatest prize in the world, now that it can't be mine—that for years I had an angel under my tent and let her go? Am I the only one—ah, dear old boy, am I the only one? And do you think my lot is easier to bear because I own that I deserve it? She's gone from us. God's blessing be with her! She might have stayed, and I lost her; it's like Undine, isn't it, George?"
"She was in this room once," George said softly.
He saw her there—he heard the sweet low voice, the kind shining eyes, the face remembered in night-watches, blest and loved always—now gone! A glass that had held a nosegay, a Bible with Helen's handwriting, were all that remained of that brief flower of his life. Tell it was a dream; tell it passes. Better the recollection of a dream than an aimless waking from a blank stupor.

The two sat in silence, each lost in their own thoughts, aware of the other's presence. Pen broke it soon after, saying he must go seek his uncle and report progress. The Major had written in a very bad humor; the Major was getting old. "I should like to see you in Parliament, and snugly settled with a comfortable house and an heir to the name before I make my bow," the Major had written. "Show me these, and then let old Arthur Pendennis make room for the younger fellows; he has walked the Pall Mall pave long enough."
"There is a kindness about the old heathen," Warrington observed. "He cares for somebody besides himself, at least for some other part of himself besides that buttoned into his own coat—for you and your race. He wants to see the progeny of the Pendennises multiplying and increasing, hoping they may inherit the land. The old patriarch blesses you from the Club window of Bays's, and is carried off and buried under the flags of St. James's Church, in sight of Piccadilly, the cabstand, and the carriages going to the levee. It is an edifying ending."
"The new blood I bring into the family," Pen mused, "is rather tainted. If I had chosen, I think my father-in-law Amory wouldn't have been the progenitor I desired for my race; nor my grandfather-in-law Snell; nor our Oriental ancestors. By the way, who was Amory? Amory was lieutenant of an Indiaman. Blanche wrote some verses about him, about the storm, the mountain wave, the seaman's grave, the gallant father, and that sort of thing. Amory was drowned commanding a country ship between Calcutta and Sydney; Amory and the Begum weren't happy together. She has been unlucky in her selection of husbands, the good old lady, for, between ourselves, a more despicable creature than Sir Francis Clavering, of Clavering Park, Baronet, never—"
"Never legislated for his country," Warrington broke in, causing Pen to blush slightly.
"By the way, at Baden," Warrington continued, "I found our friend the Chevalier Strong in great state, wearing his orders. He told me he'd quarreled with Clavering, of whom he seemed to have almost as bad an opinion as you do, and in fact, I think, though I won't be certain, he confided to me that Clavering was an utter scoundrel. That fellow Bloundell, who taught you card-playing at Oxbridge, was with Strong; and time, I think, has brought out his valuable qualities, rendering him a more accomplished rascal than he was during your undergraduateship. But the king of the place was the famous Colonel Altamont, who was carrying all before him, giving flies to the whole society, and breaking the bank, it was said."
"My uncle knows something about that fellow—Clavering knows something about him. There's something *louche* regarding him. But come! I must go to Bury Street, like a dutiful nephew." Taking his hat, Pen prepared to leave.
"I will walk, too," Warrington said. They descended the stairs, stopping briefly at Pen's chambers, which, as the reader knows, were now on the lower story.

Here Pen began sprinkling himself with eau-de-Cologne, carefully scenting his hair and whiskers with that odoriferous water.
"What's the matter? You've not been smoking. Has my pipe poisoned you?" Warrington growled.
"I'm going to call upon some women," Pen said. "I'm—I'm going to dine with 'em. They're passing through town and are at an hotel in Jermyn Street."
Warrington looked with good-natured interest at the young fellow dandifying himself to a pitch of completeness, emerging in a gorgeous shirt-front and neckcloth, fresh gloves, and glistening boots. George wore thick high-lows, his old shirt torn about the breast and ragged at the collar where his blue beard had worn it.
"Well, young un," he said simply, "I like you to be a buck; somehow. When I walk about with you, it's as if I had a rose in my button-hole. And you're still affable. I don't think there's any young fellow in the Temple who turns out like you; and I don't believe you were ever ashamed of walking with me yet."
"Don't laugh at me, George," Pen said.
"I say, Pen," the other continued, sadly, "if you write—if you write to Laura, I wish you would say 'God bless her' from me."
Pen blushed, looked at Warrington, and then—then burst into an uncontrollable fit of laughter.
"I'm going to dine with her," he said. "I brought her and Lady Rockminster up from the country today—made two days of it—slept last night at Bath—I say, George, come and dine, too. I may ask anyone I please, and the old lady is constantly talking about you."
