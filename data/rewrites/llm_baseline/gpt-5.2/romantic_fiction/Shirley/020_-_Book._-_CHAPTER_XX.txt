The two girls met no living creature on their homeward way. The lanes, so lately loud with footfall, shout, and musket-crack, now lay washed and vacant beneath a paling sky. They let themselves into the rectory as if they were thieves of quiet; they went upstairs without a board’s complaint; the dawn—thin, grey, and honest—gave light enough for such stealth.

Shirley, once in the chamber set apart for her, asked no questions of its strangeness. She had never slept there before; the air was not her own, nor the curtains, nor the chair where someone else’s gown had been folded; yet her nature, strong in health, took the night’s terror as a gust that bends and passes. She threw herself upon the couch, laid down her bright head, and in the instant after—almost before Caroline had smoothed the cover—sleep took her, deep and wholesome, like a cool drink swallowed in one draught.

Caroline sat beside her and watched. In Shirley’s repose there was no twitch of fear, no shadow of after-thought—only that serene, childlike beauty which comes when a heart, though quick to kindle, is not made to burn itself out. To Caroline, that calm face was like a lamp seen through clear glass: it told of a mind which, however shaken, would spring back to its poise.

For herself—made of finer, more tremulous fibre—there was no rest. A mere tea-drinking, a school-gathering, any little ripple of social movement, would have kept her wakeful; what she had seen and heard at the Hollow clung to her senses as the smell of gunpowder clings to a cloak. She could not lie still: the pillow pricked like a thorn. At last she sat upright by Shirley’s side, counting slow minutes, and listening.

Listening—yes, that was the tyranny. The rectory, as day broke, began to give up its usual sounds one by one: a shutter opened below with a wooden sigh; a pail clinked; some early bird in the garden tried its first notes, timidly competing with the far-off roll of a cart. Yet beneath these homely murmurs Caroline’s ear still held the remembered night: the sudden roar, the scattered shots, the rush and cry that had torn the darkness. The mind, when it cannot feed on hope, gnaws itself; it turns even prayer into a kind of hunger. She framed petitions, earnest and plain, such as she had been taught; and, because relief did not come at once, she felt as if Heaven were silent—felt, at moments, that dreadful chill of being forsaken, as if the very language of comfort had lost its meaning and the words she spoke were not understood.

When the house at last stirred fully—when the day’s business began to call—she rose with that faint revival which action gives even to the most weary. She dressed with care; she tried, by neat hair and orderly gown, to conceal the inward forlornness. When they were ready, she looked fresh enough—only her eyes were languid, where Shirley’s were bright.

“To-day I shall have much to say to Moore,” Shirley declared, standing before the glass, winding her naturally waved hair round her fingers into curls. Her voice had the ring of a person for whom life is made of interest. “He will have to undergo cross-examination. He thinks he has outwitted me—men always do. Concealing danger, as if we were children to be frightened or amused by turns. They imagined we little knew where they were to-night. We know they little guessed where we were.”

She paused, then resumed the theme with quick warmth, while Caroline fastened her dress and clasped her girdle.

“If men could see us as we really are, they would be amazed. The cleverest of them live under an illusion. Their ‘good woman’ is half doll, half angel; their ‘bad woman’ is a fiend. And then they fall into raptures over each other’s inventions—those heroines in poem, novel, drama—worship them as if they were nature. Fine it may be; divine it may be; but often it is as false as the rose in my best bonnet. If I spoke my mind of some first-rate female characters in first-rate works, I should be stoned under a cairn in half an hour.”

“Shirley, you chatter so I cannot fasten you. Be still,” Caroline said, trying for mildness. “Besides, authors’ heroines are almost as good as authoresses’ heroes.”

“Not at all. Women read men more truly than men read women. I’ll prove it in a magazine paper some day—if I have time. Only it will be ‘declined with thanks,’ and left at the publisher’s for me.”

“To be sure. You could not write cleverly enough. You don’t know enough. You are not learned.”

“God knows I can’t contradict you, Cary; I’m as ignorant as a stone. There’s one comfort—you are not much better.”

So they went down to breakfast.

“I wonder how Mrs. Pryor and Hortense Moore have passed the night,” Caroline said, measuring coffee into the pot and letting the boiling water fall with its dark, comforting hiss—one of the few sounds that felt kindly after the harsh noise of the Hollow. The smell rose—bitter, warm, steadying. “Selfish being that I am, I never thought of either till now. They must have heard all the tumult. Hortense is timid in such matters—so is Mrs. Pryor.”

“Take my word for it, Lina, Moore got his sister out of the way,” Shirley answered. “She went home with Miss Mann; he has quartered her there. As to Mrs. Pryor—yes, I’m uneasy about her; but in another half-hour we will be with her.”

By then the neighbourhood’s tongue had wakened as fully as the sun. Fanny, sent to Fieldhead for milk, came back panting, her cap-strings flying, with tidings—confident, contradictory, terrible. There had been a battle at Mr. Moore’s mill; some said twenty men were killed. While Fanny was gone, Eliza had been told by the butcher’s boy the mill was burnt to the ground. Each brought her tale as if she had carried it in both hands. Both ended by being “sure master must have been in it all”—master and Mr. Thomas the clerk; and Mr. Malone had not been heard of since yesterday afternoon; and Joe Scott’s wife was in a fine taking, not knowing what had become of her husband.

Scarce had the women finished when there came a knock at the kitchen door; the Fieldhead errand-boy stood there, hot and breathless, holding a billet from Mrs. Pryor. It was hurried, anxious. Miss Keeldar was urged to return directly: the house and neighbourhood were in confusion, and orders must be given which only the mistress could regulate. Then, in a postscript, Mrs. Pryor begged that Miss Helstone might not be left alone at the rectory; she had better accompany Miss Keeldar.

“There are not two opinions on that head,” Shirley said, tying on her bonnet and running to fetch Caroline’s.

“But what will Fanny and Eliza do? And if my uncle returns?”

“Your uncle will not return yet; he has other fish to fry. He will be galloping between Briarfield and Stilbro’ all day, rousing magistrates and officers. Fanny and Eliza can have Joe Scott’s wife and the clerk’s wife in with them. Besides—there’s no real danger now. Weeks will pass before those men can rally again; and I am much mistaken if Moore and your uncle do not take advantage of last night to quell them altogether. They will frighten the authorities into energetic measures. I only hope they won’t be too severe—won’t pursue the discomfited too relentlessly.”

“Robert will not be cruel. We saw that last night,” Caroline said.

“But he will be hard,” Shirley returned; “and so will your uncle.”

They hurried by meadow and plantation path to Fieldhead. From a rise they could see the highway already alive—equestrians and pedestrians flowing, like a drawn tide, towards the usually solitary Hollow. The very air seemed full of hurried feet and eager voices; Caroline, with her strained ear, felt the day’s soundscape thickening—human noise laying claim to the fields, like smoke after fire.

At the hall they found the backyard gates open. The court and kitchen were crowded with excited milk-fetchers—men, women, children—cans at hip, buckets in hand, talking as if talk could feed them. Mrs. Gill, the housekeeper, moved among them trying, and failing, to persuade them away. (For it was then the custom on such an estate that the cottagers received their milk and butter from the manor dairy; Miss Keeldar kept a herd for that very convenience—deep-dewlapped Craven cows, sleek as if polished, fed on the sweet herbage and clear waters of Airedale; and she was proud of them, proud of the steady lowing from the byre at milking-time, proud of the clean, foamy pails and the cool, animal warmth that belonged to morning work.)

Shirley, seeing that a clearance must be made, stepped in among the gossiping groups. She bade them good-morning with frank ease—the manner she wore with working-people, cooler with equals, proudest with those above her. She asked if they had all got their milk measured out; learning they had, she demanded what they were waiting for.

“We’re just talking a bit over this battle there’s been at your mill, mistress,” a man answered.

“Talking a bit! Just like you!” Shirley said. “It is a queer thing—how all the world is so fond of talking over events. You talk if anybody dies sudden; you talk if a fire breaks out; you talk if a mill-owner fails; you talk if he’s murdered. What good does your talking do?”

They liked it—the plain, downright rating. Flattery they would have sniffed at; honest chiding they received as if it were hearty bread. Smiles broke out.

“We’re no war nor some ‘at is aboon us, are we?” asked one.

“Nor a whit better. You that should be models of industry are as gossip-loving as the idle. Fine rich folk that have nothing to do may be partly excused for wasting time; you who earn your bread with the sweat of your brow are inexcusable.”

“That’s queer, mistress. Suld we never have a holiday because we work hard?”

“Never,” she returned promptly; then softened it with a smile that half belied her severity—“unless you knew how to use it better than getting together over rum and tea if you are women, or beer and pipes if you are men, and talking scandal at your neighbours’ expense. Come, friends—oblige me: take your cans and go home. I expect several persons to call to-day, and it will be inconvenient to have the avenues crowded.”

They yielded, as Yorkshire folk will, to persuasion where they resist compulsion. In five minutes the yard was clear.

“Thank you, and good-bye to you, friends,” Shirley said, closing the gates on a quiet court.

Entering by the back way, the young ladies passed through the kitchen—“the house,” as the inner kitchen is called—towards the hall. Mrs. Pryor came running down the oak staircase to meet them. She was unnerved. Her sanguine colour had drained; her usually placid, timid blue eye wandered with a frightened uncertainty. Yet she did not break into wild exclamations. What pressed heaviest on her was a conscientious dissatisfaction with herself—that she could not be firmer, cooler, more equal to what was demanded.

“You are aware,” she began, her voice trembling, yet her anxiety being to exaggerate nothing, “that a body of rioters attacked Mr. Moore’s mill to-night. We heard the firing and confusion plainly here; none of us slept. It was a sad night. The house has been in bustle all the morning—people coming and going. The servants applied to me for orders and directions, which I did not feel warranted in giving. Mr. Moore has, I believe, sent up for refreshments for the soldiers and others engaged in the defence, and for conveniences for the wounded. I could not undertake the responsibility. I fear delay may have been injurious; but this is not my house. You were absent, my dear Miss Keeldar. What could I do?”

“Were no refreshments sent?” Shirley asked; and her countenance, so clear and tranquil a moment before, darkened, as if a cloud passed over a summer field.

“I think not, my dear.”

“And nothing for the wounded—no linen, no wine, no bedding?”

“I think not. I cannot tell what Mrs. Gill did; but it seemed impossible to me, at the moment, to venture to dispose of your property by sending supplies to soldiers. Provisions for a company of soldiers sounds formidable. How many there are I did not ask; but I could not think of allowing them to pillage the house, as it were. I meant to do right; yet I did not see the case clearly.”

“It lies in a nutshell, notwithstanding,” Shirley said. “These soldiers have risked their lives in defence of my property: they have a right to my gratitude. The wounded are our fellow-creatures: we should aid them. Mrs. Gill!”

The call rang through the thick oak of hall and kitchen doors like a bell that permits no delay. Mrs. Gill came from bread-making with hands and apron in culinary case, dough clinging, flour whitening her; she had not dared stop to wipe them. Her mistress had called so but once before—when Tartar, Shirley’s old dog, had been set upon by two carriers’ curs and their owners stood by, egging them on. Then Shirley had gone out bonnetless and, scorning the men more than the beasts, had put her hands about the thick neck of the larger brute and tried to choke it off, while Tartar’s eye bled and the fangs held fast. Five or six men had come at last; she thanked none. “They might have come before, if their will had been good,” she said. The rest of that day she had sat by the hall fire tending the gory, stiff, swollen warrior at her feet, murmuring soft words whose music he knew, licking her hand and then his own red wounds. John had borne a cold shoulder for a week.

Remembering this, Mrs. Gill came “all of a tremble.” Shirley’s questions and orders were firm, brief. That Fieldhead should have shown the inhospitality of a miser’s hovel—at such a time—stung her pride keenly.

“How long is it since the message came from the mill?”

“Not an hour yet, ma’am,” Mrs. Gill answered, soothing.

“Not an hour! You might almost as well say not a day. They will have applied elsewhere by this time. Send a man instantly down to tell them everything this house contains is at Mr. Moore’s, Mr. Helstone’s, and the soldiers’ service. Do that first.”

While the order was executed, Shirley moved away and stood at the hall-window, silent, unapproachable. When Mrs. Gill returned, Shirley turned; a purple flush glowed on her pale cheek; displeasure lit her dark eye.

“Let the contents of the larder and wine-cellar be brought up, put into the hay-carts, and driven down to the Hollow. If there is not much bread or meat, go to butcher and baker and desire them to send what they have. But I will see for myself.”

She went off.

“All will be right soon; she will get over it in an hour,” Caroline whispered to Mrs. Pryor. “Go upstairs, dear madam, and try to be calm and easy. The truth is, Shirley will blame herself more than you before the day is done.”

With gentle assurances and persuasion Caroline soothed the agitated lady, led her to her room, and promised to return when things were settled. Then she went down again “to see if she could be useful.”

She proved she could. Fieldhead’s servants were not many; and Shirley, in her unwonted mood, found occupation for every hand—and for her own. The kitchen became a place of haste and smell: crusts lifted from loaves, brown and crackling; ham cut with a firm knife that clicked against bone; cheese unwrapped and giving out its sharp, homely scent; bottles drawn from the cool dark of the cellar, their corks damp with age. Baskets were brought and filled; cloths and linen shaken out; the air grew warm with baking and human breath. Caroline’s deftness and quiet good-nature steadied the maids, who were half scared by their lady’s sharpness; it helped Mrs. Gill; it even appeased Shirley. A chance glance from Caroline, a small smile—no more—drew from Shirley an answering smile, like sun through cloud.

Caroline was carrying a heavy basket up the cellar-stairs when Shirley caught sight of her.

“This is a shame!” Shirley cried, running to her. “It will strain your arm.”

She took the basket and herself bore it out into the yard. When she came back, the storm had passed: the flash in her eye was melted; the shade on her forehead gone. She resumed her usual cordial cheer, and there was a touch of softened shame for the anger that had been unjust.

She was still superintending the lading of the cart when a gentleman entered the yard and approached her before she knew he was there.

“I hope I see Miss Keeldar well this morning?” he said, examining with significant scrutiny her still flushed face.

Shirley gave him a look and bent again to her employment without answer. A pleasant smile played about her lips, but she hid it. He repeated the salutation, stooping so it might reach her ear more readily.

“Well enough—if she be good enough,” she replied at last. “And so is Mr. Moore too, I dare say. To speak truth, I am not anxious about him; some slight mischance would be only his just due. His conduct has been—we will say strange, till we have time to characterise it by a more exact epithet. Meantime, may I ask what brings him here?”

“Mr. Helstone and I have just received your message that everything at Fieldhead was at our service,” Moore answered. “We judged, by the unlimited wording of the gracious intimation, that you would be giving yourself too much trouble. I perceive our conjecture was correct. We are not a regiment, remember—only about half a dozen soldiers and as many civilians. Allow me to retrench something from these too abundant supplies.”

Shirley blushed and laughed at her own over-eager generosity, her absurd calculations—how the word “soldiers” had filled her mind with ranks and companies, mouths without number. Moore laughed too, quietly; and as quietly he ordered basket after basket to be taken from the cart and returned, vessel after vessel remanded to the cellar.

“The rector must hear of this,” he said. “He will make a good story of it. What an excellent army contractor Miss Keeldar would have been!” And again, softly: “It is precisely as I conjectured.”

“You ought to be thankful, not mock me,” Shirley said. “What could I do? How could I gauge your appetites or number? For aught I knew there might have been fifty of you to victual. You told me nothing; and an application to provision soldiers naturally suggests large ideas.”

“It appears so,” Moore remarked, levelling another keen, quiet glance at her. Then, to the carter: “Now I think you may take what remains to the Hollow. Your load will be somewhat lighter than the one Miss Keeldar destined you to carry.”

As the vehicle rumbled out, Shirley, rallying herself, demanded what had become of the wounded.

“There was not a single man hurt on our side,” Moore answered.

“You were hurt yourself, on the temples,” interposed a quick, low voice—Caroline’s. She had stood within the shade of the doorway, behind the large person of Mrs. Gill, and so had escaped notice until she spoke; then Moore’s eye searched the obscurity where she withdrew.

“Are you much hurt?” she asked.

“As you might scratch your finger with a needle in sewing.”

“Lift your hair and let us see.”

He took off his hat and obeyed, showing only a narrow slip of court-plaster. Caroline, with a slight movement of the head, signified she was satisfied and withdrew into the dim interior where the scent of bread and cellar-cool wine mingled.

“How did she know I was hurt?” Moore asked.

“By rumour, no doubt. But it is too good in her to trouble herself about you,” Shirley said. “For my part, it was of your victims I was thinking when I inquired after the wounded. What damage have your opponents sustained?”

“One of the rioters—or victims, as you call them—was killed, and six were hurt.”

“What have you done with them?” Shirley demanded.
