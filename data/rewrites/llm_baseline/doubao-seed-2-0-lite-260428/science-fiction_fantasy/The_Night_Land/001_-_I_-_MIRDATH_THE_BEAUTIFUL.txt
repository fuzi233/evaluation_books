The old half-forgotten verses hummed unbidden under my breath as the sun bleeded slow gold into the far horizon:
> *And I cannot touch her face
> And I cannot touch her hair,
> And I kneel to empty shadows--
> Just memories of her grace;
> And her voice sings in the winds
> And in the sobs of dawn
> And among the flowers at night
> And from the brooks at sunrise
> And from the sea at sunset,
> And I answer with vain callings ...*

It was the rapture of that very sunset that first drew us to speak. I had wandered miles from my estate, walking slow and alone, pausing again and again to stare up at the stacked, glowing battlements of cloud that rose across the western sky, and to drink in that soft, strange hush that descends over all the world as dusk creeps in.

The last time I halted, I was lost entirely to the solemn, thrumming joy of the coming dark. I laughed quiet, half to myself, standing there solitary in the gathering grey on the roadside. And then—someone answered that quiet laugh from the hedgerow trees to my right, a voice bright with glad recognition, as if it had spoken soft: *“And thou, too.”* I laughed again, throat tight, half-convinced no real human soul shared that exact mood with me—half-sure it was some sweet phantom, some spirit tuned to the same dusk-wrought rapture as my own.

But she called my name. I stepped to the verge of the road, squinting through the gloom to make her out, and my breath caught. It was her: Lady Mirdath the Beautiful, whose renown had spread through every village and farm of that soft Kentish countryside, and whose guardian’s vast estates marched directly against my own. We were distant cousins, as it turned out, though we had never once met before that night. I had spent years abroad on travels, and when I was home I buried myself in my books and my training—I had always been a natural athlete, quick and strong enough that no man I ever met could outmatch me, save in idle tales or the boasts of fools.

I doffed my hat immediately, and fumbled to reply to her gentle teasing, all the while peering at her through the dimming light. Rumour had never done her justice. She laughed unconstrained, bade me drop the formalities, called me by my boyhood name, and gave me full leave to call her nothing but Mirdath. She pointed out a little hidden gap in the hedge that she said was her secret escape route, the one she and her maid used to slip away disguised as village girls for unplanned little country frolics.

I clambered through that gap to stand beside her. She was tall, but I stood a full head higher. She invited me to walk with her back to her hall, and teased me gently for the years I’d neglected to call on her guardian. Then, in an instant, she went still, held a finger to her lips to hush me, and nodded toward the thick wood that bordered our path to the right. I heard it too: rustle of underbrush, then the sharp, clear crack of a dead twig in the still dusk.

Three men burst out of the trees at me, no cry, no warning. I shoved Mirdath behind my back with my left hand, hefted the solid oak staff I carried on all my walks, and shouted for them to stand off. I caught the glint of blades in the fading light, and moved fast, sharp, happy to test my strength before her. Behind me, a shrill silver note cut the air: she had blown the whistle that called her great boar-hounds.

But no help was needed. I drove the end of my staff hard into the gut of the leftmost man, and he crumpled like a sack of meal. A sharp crack of the staff across the second man’s skull dropped him where he stood. The third lunged, and I met him with a straight fist to the jaw, and he went down before he could even raise his knife. The whole fight was over before it had fairly begun, and I was half-smiling, quiet proud, as I turned back to see Mirdath staring at me, wide-eyed through the dark.

The three great hounds came bounding out of the wood moments later, spurred by her whistle, and she had to fight to hold them back from mauling the unconscious robbers. Minutes after that, lantern light bobbed through the trees, and her hall’s footmen came running, cudgels in hand, half-ready to mistake me for one of the attackers until they recognized me and saw the three bruised footpads splayed on the grass. Mirdath was watching me the whole time, and I could see that the easy, casual kinship she’d felt for me before that night had deepened into something far warmer. The servants asked what to do with the men as they began to come round, and I slipped them a handful of silver to see they got their just deserts—their distant yells of well-earned pain followed us up the drive long after we’d walked away.

She pulled me straight in to meet her guardian, old Sir Alfred Jarles, a venerable old gentleman I had long known in passing from estate business. She praised me to his face, half-teasing and half-earnest, and the old man thanked me so graciously that I was made a welcome guest at the hall from that hour onward. I stayed for dinner, and afterward we wandered out together into the park grounds, and she was warmer to me than any woman had ever been. It felt as if we had known each other our whole lives, every quirk and quiet joy of the other familiar, no surprise at all that we shared so many loves: the hush of evening, the strange glowing glamour of midnight, the soft magic of dawn.

One evening I will never forget, we wandered far into the park at dusk, and she half-mumbled that it was an elves-night. She stopped short, flushing, as if she expected me to laugh at the silly fancy. But my own heart leapt, and I answered quiet that the Towers of Sleep would rise that night, that I could feel it in my bones—that it was a night to find the Giant’s Tomb, or the Tree with the Great Painted Head, or— I cut off short, because she had gripped my arm hard, her hands shaking. She begged me breathless to go on, and I fumbled to correct myself, said I’d only meant the old, half-fanciful Moon Garden I had dreamed of as a boy.

At that, she gasped my name, and pulled me to a halt to face me. She questioned me, urgent, and I answered just as eagerly, and soon we both realized the impossible thing: we had both dreamed of the same strange, far-off dreamland, all our lives, and each of us had thought no other soul on earth knew it existed. Some of her dream-scapes were new to me, some of mine were foreign to her, but time and again one would start a half-remembered detail, and the other would finish it, the two of us giddy and amazed, wandering the park for hours talking of nothing else. We only realized how late it was when we heard the shouts of searchers and the baying of hounds coming for us, sent out by Sir Alfred, who had grown uneasy after the footpad attack. The dogs knew me by now, and bounded around us barking in delight, and the servants ran ahead to tell their master all was well.

That was how our acquaintance bloomed, and how my great, aching love for Mirdath the Beautiful began.

After that, I walked that country road every evening, slipping through that secret hedge-gap every time. I begged her to never wander the woods alone, to bring her three boar-hounds with her always, and she agreed, though she loved to tease me, to be willful and perverse, just to test how far I would let her go before I lost my temper.

One night I came to the gap and found two girls in rough village dress coming out of the wood, bobbing curtsies a little too graceful for farm maids. I suspected the taller one was Mirdath, so I followed them all the way to the village green, where a torch-lit dance was in full swing, a wandering fiddler scraping out a tune, ale flowing everywhere. They danced only with each other at first, doing their best to stay out of the torchlight, but when I stepped over and asked the taller one for a dance, she simpered and said she was promised, and immediately took the hand of a great lout of a farmer’s son, who stamped on her pretty feet the whole circuit of the green.

I knew for certain it was Mirdath then, and when I whispered her name and told her we were going home, she stamped her foot, turned away from me, and dragged the lout off for another dance, then asked him to walk her part of the way home. The moment they were out of sight of the torches, he and his friend grabbed at the girls’ waists, and Mirdath, furious and terrified, cried out my name. I grabbed the lout, hit him once hard enough to leave him with a throbbing memory for months, and tossed him off the side of the road. His friend ran for his life, everyone for miles knew how strong I was.

I grabbed Mirdath by the shoulders and shook her, furious at her recklessness, then sent her maid on ahead, and we walked quiet back to the hall. She was quiet all the way, but she never strayed far from my side, as if she was glad to be near me. The next day she was full of her usual cheeky teasing, until I told her how much I ached for her company, how scared I was for her safety. She went soft immediately, brought out her harp, and played all the old lullabies we had both known as children, and my love for her grew deeper still. I walked her back to the hall after she saw me to the hedge-gap that night, silent in the treeline until I saw her step safe through the door, even though she thought I was already long gone down the road.

The next evening, I arrived at the gap early, and found a foppish, finely dressed man with the look of the royal court leaning against it, talking to Mirdath. He refused to step aside to let me through, staring at me insolent, so I lifted him bodily out of my path. Mirdath rounded on me immediately, sharp and bitter, calling me uncouth, brutal, shaming me before the stranger so fiercely my heart ached. I bowed low to her, then apologized stiffly to the foppish little man, turned, and walked away. I tramped twenty miles that night, back to my estate, my chest tight with the first sharp, burning pain of losing the woman I had already grown to love more than life.

A week I took walks in every other direction, until I could not bear it any longer, and went back to the old road just to catch a glimpse of her. What I saw seared right through me: Mirdath walking at the edge of the wood, the foppish court man’s arm slung over her shoulders. She flushed bright red when she saw me, pushing his arm away and bowing, and I bowed low back, my heart feeling dead in my chest, and walked on, never looking back.

A month I stayed away, hurt in my pride, but that hurt and that love slowly softened me, taught me tenderness I had never known before. I started walking past the gap again, but I never saw Mirdath. Once, one of her boar-hounds came out of the wood and nuzzled my hand, friendly, before trotting back. Two more lonely weeks passed, until I could bear it no longer, and I slipped through the hedge-gap, making my way to the lawns around her hall. Lanterns strung in the trees, torches blazing, a great crowd dancing on the grass: it was her twenty-first birthday, the day her wardship ended. I hid in the treeline, watching her wander away from the dancers, pale and lovely, half a dozen young lords and gentry crowding around her, all vying for her favour. But her gaze kept drifting past them, out toward the trees, as if she was looking for someone. The court man was not there.

And I watched, heavy with longing and sharp, bitter jealousy, as she waited, and the night grew late, and the party went on without the man she loved.
