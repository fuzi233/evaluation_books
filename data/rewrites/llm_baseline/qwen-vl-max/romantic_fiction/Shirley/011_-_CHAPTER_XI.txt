Caroline could not surrender without a fight. There was a quiet strength within her—a fire that burned low but refused to go out. And she used it. People never struggle so fiercely as when they are alone, with no one to witness their pain, no confidant to comfort them, no voice to guide or console. She stood in that silence, her suffering the only fuel for her will. It was sharp, real, and unrelenting—yet it stoked her resolve. Determined to conquer this ache that gnawed at her soul, she threw herself into every task, every movement, every breath. Never had she been so busy, so absorbed, so restless. She walked long distances in all weather, through empty lanes and over open moors, returning each evening pale and drained, yet never truly tired. As soon as she removed her bonnet and shawl, she would begin pacing her room—not to rest, but to exhaust herself. Sometimes she wouldn’t sit until she felt faint. She claimed she did it to sleep better. But sleep eluded her. While others rested, she tossed beneath her sheets, or sat upright in the dark, forgotten by the night, lost in thoughts too heavy for tears.

And yet—when the weight became unbearable—she wept. Oh, how she wept! Not just with sorrow, but with a grief so deep it broke her spirit, leaving her trembling and helpless, like a child who has lost everything. In those moments, whispers crept into her mind: *Write to Robert. Tell him you’re hurting. Say you miss seeing him, Hortense—say you fear he’ll forget you entirely. Ask him to remember you. To write back.* She wrote such letters—more than once—but never sent them. Shame held her hand; reason pulled her back.

At last, she reached the edge. The life she lived was no longer bearable. She needed change—or else her heart and mind would break under the strain. She longed to leave Briarfield, to vanish somewhere far away. But deeper still, beneath that longing, stirred another—quiet, persistent, almost shameful: the desire to know her mother. Each day, it grew stronger. Yet with it came dread. What if she found her—and didn’t love her? Her mother had never been spoken of with warmth. Her uncle spoke of her sister-in-law with a cold reserve. An old servant, who had served Mrs. James Helstone briefly after her marriage, called her “queer,” said she “didn’t understand her.” Those words were like ice in Caroline’s veins. If she knew her mother, would she despise her? Was it better never to know?

But one plan remained—one that seemed possible, even hopeful: becoming a governess. It was the only path left open to her. A small event brought her to the brink of speaking her decision aloud. Her walks—long, solitary, often late—always led her near the Hollow. She rarely descended into the valley, but she visited its edge at twilight, as regularly as the stars rose above the hills. Her favorite spot was a stile beneath an ancient thorn tree, where she could see the cottage, the mill, the dewy garden, and the still, deep dam. From there, she watched the counting-house window. At a certain hour, the lamp inside would flare to life—bright, sudden, familiar. That light was her anchor. When it appeared, she felt a spark of hope. If a shadow passed across it, her heart leapt—it was Robert. She’d return home comforted, carrying in her mind a clearer image of his face, his voice, his smile. Sometimes, she believed—if only she could be near him—he might still welcome her. He might reach out his hand, draw her close, shelter her as he once had. That night, though she might cry, the tears would feel less hot. The pillow softer. The ache in her temples, lighter.

The shortest route from the Hollow to the rectory passed near Fieldhead—the old, abandoned mansion where Malone had once wandered on a moonlit night. Though vacant for ten years, it was not ruined. Mr. Yorke had ensured it stayed well kept—an old gardener and his wife lived there, tending the grounds, maintaining the house. It wasn’t grand, but it was striking. Time had painted its stone gray and mossy, its windows latticed, its porch carved, its roof and chimneys rich in shadow and texture. Behind it rose bold trees; before it, a majestic cedar. Granite urns lined the wall; a fretted arch framed the gate. For an artist, it was a feast for the eye.

One soft May evening, near moonrise, Caroline lingered near the gate. Though weary, she couldn’t bring herself to go home—where only sorrow waited. She sat on the mossy ground, gazing toward the cedar and the house. The night was calm, dewy, cloudless. The west-facing gables glowed amber, reflecting the horizon. The oaks behind were black; the cedar, darker still. Beneath its thick boughs, a patch of sky shimmered blue. The moon hung high, solemn and gentle, watching over her.

She found the scene mournfully beautiful. She wished she could be happy. She wished peace would come. Why did Providence seem so indifferent? Why offer no comfort? Memories of lovers’ trysts from old ballads flooded her mind—how sweet they must have been in such a place. Where was Robert now? She hadn’t seen his lamp. Had he gone away? Would she ever speak to him again?

Then—suddenly—the door of the porch opened. Two men stepped out. One older, white-haired; the other young, tall, dark-haired. They crossed the lawn, passed through the garden gate, and walked down the road. Caroline saw them pass the stile, descend the field, disappear. Robert Moore had walked past her—with Mr. Yorke. Neither had seen her.

The moment was fleeting—gone before she could grasp it. But it lit her blood. It left her shaken, feverish, furious. It found her despairing; it left her desperate.

“Oh, had he but been alone! Had he but seen me!” she cried inwardly. “He would have said something. He would have taken my hand. He _must_ care for me a little. I would have read comfort in his eyes, in his lips. But it’s gone. He passed like wind, like shadow—silent, empty. I’ve been mocked. And Heaven is cruel.”

With that, she turned and walked home, her heart shattered.

The next morning, at breakfast, she looked pale, hollow-eyed, as if she had seen a ghost. She asked her uncle, “Uncle, do you object if I look for a position in a family?”

Mr. Helstone, unaware of her inner torment, stared in disbelief.

“What nonsense is this?” he said. “Are you bewitched?”

“I’m not well,” she replied. “I need a change.”

He studied her. Without knowing it, he saw what she had become: the rose had faded to a snowdrop, bloom vanished, flesh thinned. She sat before him drooping, colorless, fragile. Only her eyes—soft and brown—and her flowing hair saved her from being plain.

“What on earth is wrong with you?” he demanded. “What ails you?”

No answer. Just a tremble in her lips, a swelling in her eyes.

“Look for a situation, indeed!” he scoffed. “What kind? What have you done with yourself? You’re not well.”

“I would be well if I left home.”

“These women!” he muttered. “They startle you with surprises. One day they’re bright and rosy, the next, shriveled and broken. What causes it? Who knows? She eats, she’s free, she has a good house and clothes. Once it was enough. Now she’s a poor, pale, whimpering thing. Provoking! What shall I do? I suppose I must call a doctor.”

“No, uncle,” she said quietly. “A doctor can’t help. I just need a change of air and scene.”

“Well, if it’s a whim, it shall be granted. You shall go to Cliff Bridge. Fanny will go with you.”

“But uncle,” she said, “one day I must work. I have no fortune. I should begin now.”

“You shall not become a governess, Caroline,” he declared. “I won’t have it said my niece is a governess.”

“But the later one begins such a change,” she said gently, “the harder it is. I’d rather grow used to it before habits of ease take root.”

“Don’t harass me,” he said sharply. “I will provide for you. I’ll buy an annuity. I’m fifty-five—healthy, strong. Plenty of time. Don’t worry about the future.”

“It’s not the future,” she whispered. “It’s the need for change.”

He laughed. “There speaks the woman!” he said. “Always fantastical! Always wanting a change. Well, it’s in her nature.”

“But it’s not fantasy,” she insisted.

“What then?”

“Necessity,” she said. “I feel weaker. I believe I should have more to do.”

“Admirable!” he said. “She feels weak—so she should be set to hard labor. ‘Clair comme le jour,’ as Moore—confound Moore! You shall go to Cliff Bridge. Two guineas for a new dress. Come, Cary, don’t fear. We’ll find balm in Gilead.”

“Uncle,” she began, “I wish you were less generous and more—”

“More what?”

“Sympathizing,” she meant to say—but stopped. She knew he’d laugh. So she said nothing.

He took her silence as agreement. “The truth is,” he said, “you don’t know what you want.”

“I do,” she said. “I want to be a governess.”

“Pooh! Nonsense! I won’t hear of it. Don’t mention it again. It’s too feminine. I’ve finished breakfast. Ring the bell. Put your foolish ideas aside and amuse yourself.”

“What with?” she thought, as she left the room. “My doll?”

A week passed. Her health neither improved nor worsened. She was exactly at the point where, if she carried seeds of illness—consumption, slow fever—those would now rage unchecked. People don’t die of grief alone, but grief can awaken hidden diseases and send them spiraling. Strong people endure agony—bodies break, beauty fades—but life persists. They grow pale, thin, frail. Others think they’ll soon fall ill, die quietly. But they live on. Like a blossom nipped by March winds but not swept away—it may hang on, a withered fruit, into autumn. Having braved spring frost, it may also brave winter’s first chill.

Everyone noticed the change in Miss Helstone. Most said she was dying. She didn’t believe it. She felt no sickness, no pain. Her appetite dwindled—because she wept so much at night. Her strength waned—because sleep fled, dreams haunted her. In the distance, she still imagined a time when this pain would end, when she might be calm again—though perhaps never happy.

Her uncle urged her to visit, to accept invitations. She avoided them. She couldn’t pretend cheerfulness. She felt watched—curiosity, not kindness. Old ladies offered advice, remedies. Young ones looked at her with pity, with understanding. Their eyes said: *She’s been disappointed.* By whom? They weren’t sure.

Ordinary young women can be as harsh as ordinary young men—as worldly, as selfish. Sufferers should avoid them. They scorn grief, see it as divine punishment for weakness. To them, “love” is a scheme for a good match. To be “disappointed” is to have that scheme fail. They assume everyone thinks like them, and judge accordingly.

Caroline knew this—by instinct, by observation. She withdrew. She kept her pale face and thin figure hidden. Living in seclusion, she lost touch with the world.

One morning, her uncle entered the parlour where she sat painting wildflowers gathered at the top of the Hollow fields.

“Come, child,” he said abruptly. “You’re always bent over palette or book. Leave that. By-the-bye—do you put your pencil to your lips when you paint?”

“Sometimes, uncle,” she said, “when I forget.”

“Then that’s poisoning you,” he declared. “Those paints are deadly—white lead, red lead, verdigris, gamboge—poisons all. Lock them up! Get your bonnet on. I want you to make a call with me.”

"With *me*?" she asked, surprised. She never went anywhere with him.

“Quick! I have no time to lose.”

She gathered her things, asking where they were going.

“To Fieldhead.”

“Fieldhead? To see James Booth, the gardener?”

“No. To see Miss Shirley Keeldar.”

“Miss Keeldar? Is she here? At Fieldhead?”

“She is. She’s been there a week. I met her last night—at that party you refused to attend. I liked her. I want you to meet her. It will do you good.”

“She’s come of age?”

“She is. She’ll stay for a while. I lectured her on duty. She’s not stubborn. Rather a lively girl. Nothing dull about her.”

“I don’t think she’ll care to see me. What can I offer her?”

“Pshaw! Put your bonnet on.”

“Is she proud, uncle?”

“Don’t know. Do you think she’d show pride to *me*? A girl like that wouldn’t presume to give airs to the rector of her parish.”

“No. But how did she behave?”

“Didn’t notice. She holds her head high. Probably saucy when she dares. But she’s a woman—what else?”

“Very well. But I’m not eager.”

“Enough! Away!”

Though naturally shy, weakened and discouraged, Caroline felt her spirits sink as they walked up the broad, paved path to Fieldhead’s porch. She followed her uncle reluctantly into the dim, vast hall beyond.

It was sombre—long, dark, shadowed. A single latticed window cast feeble light. The great fireplace, filled with willow branches, held no fire. The gallery above was barely visible. Carved deer heads with real antlers stared down grotesquely. The house was old, rambling, impractical. Yet it held a thousand pounds a year—a fortune passed to a woman, since no sons remained. Merchant families nearby earned twice as much, but the Keeldars, by birth and title, claimed precedence.

They were shown into a parlour. Of course, in such a Gothic dwelling, it was lined with oak—dark, polished, imposing. Beautiful, yes—but oppressive. Anyone who’s seen servants scrubbing these panels in summer heat knows the horror. Yet someone had painted the drawing-room pink-white—a rebellious act, perhaps, but one that brought light and relief.

This parlour was furnished in old style—real antique furniture. On either side of the mantel stood two solid oak chairs. In one sat a woman—matronly, with auburn hair, young features, but no youth in her bearing. Her gown was fine but outdated, arranged in stiff folds. She looked eccentric.

She greeted them with English diffidence—polite, uncertain, anxious to please. Miss Helstone felt it, and sympathized. She sat quietly beside her, offering gentle conversation. The woman had a clear, soft voice—surprisingly musical for her age. Caroline liked it. They would have gotten along well—had not Mr. Helstone stood rigid on the rug, his eyes sharp, impatient, disapproving. His presence made the woman flustered. She tried to talk of the weather, the country—but he pretended not to hear. She repeated herself. Then, just as she rose nervously to search for Miss Keeldar, the younger woman appeared.

She came through a glass door from the garden—slight, erect, holding a silk apron full of flowers. She gave Mr. Helstone her hand and said pleasantly, “I knew you’d come to see me, though you *do* think Mr. Yorke has made me a Jacobin. Good morning.”

“But we won’t have you a Jacobin,” he said. “No, Miss Shirley. I’ll teach you sound doctrine—both political and religious. Now that you’re among us, you’ll be my pupil.”

She smiled. “I’ll try not to disappoint you.”
