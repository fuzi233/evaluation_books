"Pardon, my lord—I speak for Sigismund."

"For him?" cried Fronsberg, his countenance lighting with a mischievous glint. "Ay, for him I always hold a pardon safe in bank, sure he will draw sooner or later on me. What is his need? Some mad project broken? Fine mechanic wings that would not fly? Durance, assault on watch, a bill for Epernay, not a crust to eat?"

"Oh, none of these, my lord," Aspern replied smoothly. "He has escaped from Circe's herd and seeks to win the love of your fair ward, Cecilia. But he would win first your consent."

"You frown," observed Aspern.

"Distinguish words," Fronsberg retorted sharply. "I said I held a *pardon*, not *consent*."

***

In spite of Deronda's urgent reasons for wishing to return to town—motives inextricably blended with his deepening anxiety for Mirah and a burning curiosity regarding the enigmatic Mordecai—he did not manage to ascend before Sir Hugo, who preceded his family to ensure readiness for Parliament's opening on the sixth of February. Instead, Deronda took up his quarters in Park Lane, fully aware that his chambers were sufficiently tenanted by Hans Meyrick. This arrangement, he had anticipated, yet he discovered circumstances not altogether according to his expectations.

Most of us recall Retzsch's drawing of Destiny in the guise of Mephistopheles playing chess with Man for his soul, wherein the clever adversary feints unintended moves to lure the beguiled mortal away from the true point of attack. The Fiend mocks preparation above all, fatally persuading us to forego waterproofs when the sky is destined to clear, foreseeing that the imbecile will transform this delusion into a prejudice against rain-coats rather than studying weather signs. It is a peculiar test of a man's mettle when, after painfully adjusting to what seems a wise provision, he finds all his mental precautions slightly astray, his excellent intentions reduced to miscalculated dovetails cut from a wrong starting-point. His magnanimity prepares to meet misbehavior, only to find a quite different summons. Something akin to this befell Deronda.

His first impression was one of pure pleasure and amusement at discovering his sitting-room transformed into an *atelier*. It was strewn with miscellaneous drawings and the contents of two chests brought from Rome; the lower half of the windows were darkened with baize, and the blonde Hans, in his weird youth, presided over the littered space as its genius. Hans's hair was longer than of old, his face more whimsically creased, and his high voice rose higher under the excitement of rapid talk. The friendship between the two had been maintained warmly since their memorable days at Cambridge, sustained not merely by correspondence but by little episodes of companionship abroad and in England. The original relation of confidence on one side and indulgence on the other had, as is wont to be the case where such spiritual borrowing begins well, been further developed in practice.

"I knew you would like to see my casts and antiquities," Hans announced after the first hearty greetings and inquiries, "so I didn't scruple to unload my chests here. But I've found two rooms at Chelsea, not many hundred yards from my mother and sisters, and I shall soon be ready to hang out there—once they've scraped the walls and put in some new lights. That is all I'm waiting for. But you see, I don't wait to begin work: you can't conceive what a great fellow I'm going to be. The seed of immortality has sprouted within me."

"Only a fungoid growth, I dare say—a growing disease in the lungs," Deronda remarked, treating Hans with his customary brotherly fashion. He walked toward some drawings propped on the ledge of his bookcases: five rapidly sketched heads, different aspects of the same face. He stood at a convenient distance, making no immediate remark. Hans, equally silent for a minute, took up his palette and began touching the picture on his easel.

"What do you think of them?" Hans asked finally.

"The full face looks too massive; otherwise, the likenesses are good," Deronda said, more coldly than was usual with him.

"No, it is not too massive," Hans decided. "I have noted that. There is always a little surprise when one passes from the profile to the full face. But I shall enlarge her scale for *Berenice*. I am making a *Berenice* series—look at the sketches along there—and now, thinking of it, you are just the model I want for the *Agrippa*." Hans, still holding pencil and palette, had moved to Deronda's side as he spoke, but he added hastily, as if conscious of a mistake, "No, no, I forgot; you don't like sitting for your portrait, confound you! However, I've picked up a capital *Titus*. There are to be five in the series. The first is *Berenice* clasping the knees of Gessius Florus and beseeching him to spare her people; I've got that on the easel. Then, this, where she stands on the Xystus with *Agrippa*, entreating the people not to injure themselves by resistance."

"*Agrippa*'s legs will never do," Deronda interjected.

"The legs are good realistically," Hans replied, his face creasing drolly. "Public men are often shaky about the legs—'Their legs, the emblem of their various thought,' as somebody says in the *Rehearsal*."

"But these are as impossible as the legs of Raphael's *Alcibiades*," Deronda countered.

"Then they are good ideally," Hans insisted. "*Agrippa*'s legs were possibly bad; I idealize that and make them impossibly bad. Art, my Eugenius, must intensify. But never mind the legs now: the third sketch in the series is *Berenice* exulting in the prospects of being Empress of Rome, when the news has come that Vespasian is declared Emperor and her lover *Titus* his successor."

"You must put a scroll in her mouth, else people will not understand that. You can't tell that in a picture."

"It will make them feel their ignorance then—an excellent aesthetic effect. The fourth is *Titus* sending *Berenice* away from Rome after she has shared his palace for ten years—both reluctant, both sad—*invitus invitam*, as Suetonius hath it. I've found a model for the Roman brute."

"Shall you make *Berenice* look fifty? She must have been that."

"No, no; a few mature touches to show the lapse of time. Dark-eyed beauty wears well, hers particularly. But now, here is the fifth: *Berenice* seated lonely on the ruins of Jerusalem. That is pure imagination. That is what ought to have been—perhaps was. Now, see how I tell a pathetic negative. Nobody knows what became of her—that is finely indicated by the series coming to a close. There is no sixth picture." Hans pretended to speak with a gasping sense of sublimity, drawing back his head with a frown as if seeking a like impression on Deronda. "I break off in the Homeric style. The story is chipped off, so to speak, and passes with a ragged edge into nothing—*le néant*; can anything be more sublime, especially in French? The vulgar would desire to see her corpse and burial—perhaps her will read and her linen distributed. But now, come and look at this on the easel. I have made some way there."

"That beseeching attitude is really good," Deronda said after a moment's contemplation. "You have been very industrious in the Christmas holidays; for I suppose you have taken up the subject since you came to London." Neither of them had yet mentioned Mirah.

"No," Hans said, applying touches to his picture, "I made up my mind to the subject before. I take that lucky chance for an augury that I am going to burst on the world as a great painter. I saw a splendid woman in the Trastevere—the grandest women there are half Jewesses—and she set me hunting for a fine situation of a Jewess at Rome. Like other men of vast learning, I ended by taking what lay on the surface. I'll show you a sketch of the Trasteverina's head when I can lay my hands on it."

"I should think she would be a more suitable model for *Berenice*," Deronda said, struggling to express his discontent.

"Not a bit of it. The model ought to be the most beautiful Jewess in the world, and I have found her."

"Have you made yourself sure that she would like to figure in that character? I should think no woman would be more abhorrent to her. Does she quite know what you are doing?"

"Certainly. I got her to throw herself precisely into this attitude. Little mother sat for Gessius Florus, and Mirah clasped her knees." Hans stepped back to consider the effect of his touches.

"I dare say she knows nothing about *Berenice*'s history," Deronda said, feeling more indignation than he could justify.
