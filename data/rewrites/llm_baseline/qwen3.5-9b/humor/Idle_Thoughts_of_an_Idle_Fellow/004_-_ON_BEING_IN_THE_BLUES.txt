I suppose I could enjoy feeling melancholy; there is a certain satisfaction in being thoroughly miserable. But nobody actually *likes* a fit of the blues. Still, everyone gets them, and yet, nobody seems to understand why. It's impossible to account for them. You're just as likely to hit a slump the day after winning a fortune as the day after losing your brand-new silk umbrella on a train.

The effect is roughly equivalent to suffering from a simultaneous attack of toothache, indigestion, and a severe cold. Suddenly, you become stupid, restless, and irritable—rude to strangers and potentially dangerous to friends. You're clumsy, maudlin, and quarrelsome, turning into a nuisance to everyone within a three-mile radius.

When the blues strike, you feel paralyzed by the need to act while simultaneously unable to think. You can't sit still, so you grab your hat and stride out for a walk. But before you've even reached the corner of the street, you regret coming outside and turn right back. You try reading a book, hoping for distraction, but suddenly Shakespeare sounds trite, Dickens is boringly prosaic, Thackeray is unbearable, and Carlyle is overly sentimental. You toss the book aside and start calling authors names.

Then you try to shoo the cat out of the room, only to kick the door shut on her tail. You decide to write a letter, starting with, *"Dearest Auntie: I find I have five minutes to spare..."* After fifteen minutes of staring at the blank page without generating another thought, you crumple the paper, slam the wet pen onto the tablecloth, and resolve to visit the Thompsons. As soon as you pull on your gloves, however, it hits you: the Thompsons are idiots, they don't have supper, and you'll be expected to hold the baby. You curse them, cancel the trip, and feel utterly crushed.

Now you bury your face in your hands, wishing you were dead and in heaven. You visualize your own sickbed, surrounded by weeping friends and relatives. You bless them all, especially the young and pretty ones, telling yourself they'll finally appreciate you then. You realize too late what they've lost, bitterly contrasting their future reverence with their current indifference. This makes you feel slightly better—for a second. Then you remember how foolish it is to think anyone would care if you blew up, got hanged, married, or drowned. Nobody cares. You've never been truly appreciated. You review your entire life and conclude you've been mistreated since birth.

Half an hour of this thinking ramps you up into a state of savage fury against everyone and everything, particularly yourself—the only person you can't physically kick thanks to anatomy.

Finally, bedtime arrives to stop you from doing something rash. You sprint upstairs, strip naked, leave your clothes scattered everywhere, blow out the candle, and jump into bed as if racing against the clock. For two hours, you toss and tumble, occasionally stripping off your sheets and putting them back on just to break the monotony. Eventually, you drift into an uneasy sleep, plagued by nightmares, waking up late the next morning. That's basically the routine for single men in the blues.

Married men handle it differently: they bully their wives, complain about dinner, and force the kids to bed. This creates quite a commotion in the house, which must be a huge relief to a man in the blues, since rowdy arguments are the only entertainment he can tolerate.

The symptoms are consistent, but people name the illness differently. Poets call it "a feeling of sadness"; 'Arry tells Jimee he's "got the blooming hump"; your sister claims she's just "out of sorts"; and the average guy says, "Old chap, I feel dreadfully miserable tonight." My usual line is, "I have a strange, unsettled feeling tonight... I think I'll go out." Though, strangely, it never happens during the day.

In broad daylight, when the world is buzzing with energy, we can't afford to sigh and sulk. The noise of work drowns out the tiny spirits singing their sad songs in our heads. During the day, we might be angry or indignant, but we aren't "in the blues." If something goes wrong at 10 AM, we swear and bang furniture. If it happens at 10 PM, we retreat to the shadows, read poetry, and ponder how hollow the world is.

Usually, trouble isn't the cause of the blues. Real misery is too harsh for sentimentality. We cry over pictures of tragedy, but we'd turn away immediately from the real thing. There's no luxury in genuine grief. We don't choose to play with sharp swords or cuddle a starving fox. Yet, many elderly ladies will call me a cynical brute for suggesting otherwise. They gaze at tiny shoes in lavender drawers, weeping for toddlers who have grown up, or clutch locks of hair from sons lost at sea. They'd say I'm wrong, but if they asked themselves honestly if dwelling on such sorrows feels pleasant, they'd admit it does. To some, tears taste as sweet as laughter.

As the old chronicler Froissart noted, the Englishman takes his pleasures sadly, and the Englishwoman takes her pleasures *in* sadness itself. I'm not mocking this. Sentiment keeps hearts tender in a cruel world. Men are cold and practical enough already; we wouldn't want women to be the same. Ladies, please remain soft and sentimental—you are the soothing butter to our coarse bread. Besides, sentiment is to women what fun is to us. They deserve their grief just as we deserve our humor.

Who can claim that a contorted face screaming in agony represents a higher form of happiness than a pensive face resting gently on a hand, gazing through time at a fading past? I am glad when I see Regret walking alongside me as a friend. I know the salt has washed from the tears and the sting has been removed from Sorrow's face before we kiss her pale lips. Time heals the wound until we can look back on our pain without bitterness. The burden becomes light, mixed with a sweet pity, much like when Colonel Newcome answers "adsum" to the roll call, or when Tom and Maggie Tulliver clasp hands beneath the swollen waters of the Floss.

Thinking of Tom and Maggie reminds me of George Eliot's phrase, "the sadness of a summer's evening." How perfectly true! Who hasn't felt the enchanting sorrow of a lingering sunset? The world belongs to Melancholy then—a thoughtful, deep-eyed maiden who avoids the glare of noon. She emerges only when "light thickens and the crow flies to the rocky wood." Her palace is twilight land.

There, she meets us. At her shadowy gate, she takes our hand and leads us through her mystical realm. We see no form, only hear the rustle of her wings. Even in the busy city, her spirit lingers: a somber presence in long, dull streets; a dark river creeping ghost-like under black arches, hiding secrets in its mud. In the quiet countryside, when trees blur against the night and bats flutter near our faces, the spell deepens. We stand by an unseen deathbed, hearing the sigh of the dying day in the swaying elms.

A solemn sadness reigns, bringing a great peace. Our daily worries shrink to insignificance. Bread, cheese, and even kisses cease to be the only goals. Unspoken thoughts flood in. Standing under Earth's darkening dome, we feel greater than our petty lives. Surrounded by dusky curtains, the world transforms from a dingy workshop into a stately temple, where, in the dimness, our groping hands might finally touch God.
