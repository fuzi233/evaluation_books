"Let China's earth, enriched with coloured stains,
Pencil'd with gold, and streaked with azure veins,
The grateful flavour of the Indian leaf,
Or Mocha's sunburnt berry glad receive."

The following day, Mr. Hale ascended the stairs to the small drawing-room at an hour that was decidedly unconventional. He moved among the furnishings with a distracted air, his hands hovering over objects as if inspecting them, yet Margaret perceived the gesture for what it truly was: a nervous deferral, a stalling tactic for words he desired to utter but dreaded to speak. Finally, the truth emerged.

"My dear," he announced, "I have invited Mr. Thornton to tea tonight."

Mrs. Hale reclined in her armchair, eyes closed, her face bearing a habitual mask of suffering. Yet, at her husband's announcement, she sprang upright, her demeanor shifting instantly to querulous agitation.

"Mr. Thornton! And tonight! What on earth could possibly bring that man here? Besides, Dixon is scrubbing my muslins and laces, and there is no soft water to be found amidst these dreadful eastern winds, which I suspect will plague us in Milton for the remainder of the year."

"The wind is turning, my love," Mr. Hale replied, gazing out at the smoke drifting squarely from the east, though he had not yet mastered the cardinal points and often rearranged them to suit his convenience.

"Don't bother telling me!" Mrs. Hale shuddered, tightening her shawl around her shoulders. "Be that as it may, I suppose the man is coming."

"Oh, Mother, that merely proves you have never truly met Mr. Thornton," Margaret interjected. "He is the sort of person who finds joy in battling every adversity life throws his way—whether enemies, storms, or misfortune. The harder the rain and wind blow, the more certain it is he will arrive. I shall go and assist Dixon; I am becoming quite proficient at starching. And Dixon will require nothing more than conversation with Father. Father, I am genuinely eager to introduce the Pythias to your Damon. You recall how awkward our first meeting was, how we struggled to find common ground?"

"I cannot say I expect you to find him agreeable, Margaret. He is certainly not a 'lady's man.'"

Margaret twisted her neck in a gesture of disdainful amusement.

"I hold no particular admiration for 'lady's men,' Father. However, Mr. Thornton has come as your friend, one who has recognized your worth—"

"The only person in Milton," Mrs. Hale muttered.

"Then let us offer him a warm welcome and some coconut cakes. Dixon would be delighted to prepare them, and I shall happily iron your caps, Mother."

That morning, Margaret had wished Mr. Thornton a thousand miles away. She had envisioned other pursuits: a letter to Edith, a passage of Dante, a visit to the Higgins household. Instead, she stood ironing, listening to Dixon's grievances, hoping that an overflow of sympathy might keep the laundry maid from recounting her sorrows to Mrs. Hale. Periodically, Margaret had to summon her father's high regard for Mr. Thornton to quell the creeping irritation and the onset of the severe headaches that plagued her lately. By the time she finally sat down to inform her mother that she was no longer Peggy the laundry-maid but Margaret Hale, the lady, she intended it as a jest. She was chagrined when her mother took the remark with deadly seriousness.

"Yes! If anyone had told me, when I was Miss Beresford and one of the country's belles, that my child would spend half a day standing in a cramped kitchen, laboring like a servant to prepare properly for the reception of a tradesman—and that this tradesman should be the only—"

"Oh, Mother!" Margaret interrupted, rising abruptly. "Do not punish me for such a careless slip. I do not mind ironing, or any work for you and Father. I remain a born and bred lady through it all, even if it means scouring floors or washing dishes. I am merely tired for a moment; in half an hour, I shall be ready to begin again. As for Mr. Thornton's trade, well, he cannot help that now, poor fellow. I doubt his education would qualify him for much else."

She rose slowly and retreated to her own room, unable to endure any further strain.

Simultaneously, in the Thornton household, a scene of stark contrast unfolded. In a grand, grimly furnished dining room, a large-boned woman, well past middle age, sat at work. Her features, matching her sturdy frame, were strong and massive rather than heavy. Her countenance shifted slowly between distinct expressions of resolve. There was little variety in her face, yet those who saw it once were compelled to look again; even passersby in the street turned their heads to gaze a moment longer at the firm, severe, dignified woman who never yielded in street courtesy nor paused from her straight path toward her clearly defined ends.

She wore handsome black silk, stout and immaculate, not a thread worn or discolored. She mended a vast tablecloth of the finest texture, holding it up against the light to inspect thin spots requiring her delicate attention. No books cluttered the room save for Matthew Henry's Bible commentaries, six volumes resting on the massive sideboard, flanked by a tea-urn and a lamp. In a distant room, the piano played an exercise piece. Someone practiced a salon morceau with frantic speed, missing every third note on average, rendering many chords indistinct or entirely absent, yet the performer remained entirely satisfied with the result. Mrs. Thornton heard a step, decisive and familiar, pass the dining-room door.

"John! Is that you?"

Her son appeared in the doorway.

"What brings you home so early? I thought you were going to tea with that friend of Mr. Bell's, Mr. Hale?"

"So I am, Mother. I have returned to dress."

"Dress! Humph! When I was a girl, young men were content to dress once a day. Why dress to take a cup of tea with an old parson?"

"Mr. Hale is a gentleman, and his wife and daughter are ladies."

"Wife and daughter! Do they teach? What do they do? You have never mentioned them."

"No, Mother, because I have never seen Mrs. Hale; I have seen Miss Hale for only half an hour."

"Take care you do not get caught by a penniless girl, John."

"I am not easily caught, Mother, as I believe you know. But I must not have Miss Hale spoken of in that manner, which you know is offensive to me. I have never been aware of any young lady attempting to catch me, nor do I believe anyone has ever given themselves such useless trouble."

Mrs. Thornton refused to yield the point, driven perhaps by pride or the general dignity of her sex.

"Well! I only say, take care. Perhaps our Milton girls possess too much spirit and good feeling to go angling for husbands; but this Miss Hale comes from the aristocratic counties, where, if all tales are true, rich husbands are considered prizes."

Mr. Thornton's brow furrowed, and he stepped forward into the room.

"Mother" (with a short, scornful laugh), "you will force me to confess. The only time I saw Miss Hale, she treated me with a haughty civility thick with contempt. She held herself aloof as if she were a queen and I her humble, unwashed vassal. Be easy, Mother."

"No! I am neither easy nor content. What business had she, a renegade clergyman's daughter, to turn her nose up at you! I would not dress for any of them—a saucy set! If I were you." As he left the room, he added:

"Mr. Hale is good, gentle, and learned. He is not saucy. As for Mrs. Hale, I shall tell you what she is like tonight, if you care to hear."

He closed the door and departed.

"Despise my son! Treat him as her vassal, indeed! Humph! I should like to know where she could find such another! Boy and man, he is the noblest, stoutest heart I have ever known. I do not care if I am his mother; I can see what is what and not be blind. I know what Fanny is, and I know what John is. Despise him! I hate her!"
