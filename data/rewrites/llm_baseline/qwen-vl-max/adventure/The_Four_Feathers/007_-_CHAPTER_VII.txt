“No one,” said Durrance, slipping his field-glasses into their leather case at his side.
“No one, sir,” Captain Mather echoed. “We’ll move forward.”

The scouts advanced ahead, the troops reformed into line, and the two seven-pounder mountain guns closed up behind. Durrance’s detachment of the Camel Corps descended from the shadowed ridge of Khor Gwob—thirty-five miles southwest of Suakin—into the open plateau of Sinkat. This was the final reconnaissance in strength before the evacuation of the eastern Sudan.

All morning, the camels had trudged slowly through a narrow ravine of shale, flanked by steep red cliffs. As the rocks receded, they gave way to scattered heaps of broken stone, a desolate landscape where not even a blade of grass or a tattered mimosa branch broke the barren monotony. Yet when they reached the valley below, the green shrubs of Sinkat offered a sudden relief—a verdant park amid the waste. The troopers sat up straighter in their saddles, alert now, as they crossed the plateau diagonally toward the distant mountains of Erkoweet. A silent column moved across a land that seemed to hold its breath.

It was eleven o’clock. The sun climbed high in a pale, cloudless sky, casting sharp, shrinking shadows on the sand. The ground shimmered white, like a beach in the Scilly Isles. There was no wind—no whisper through the foliage, no rustle in the leaves. Shadows lay motionless, so crisp and still they might have been left behind by some ancient storm. Only the clank of weapons, the soft thud of camel hooves, and the occasional flutter of pigeons disturbed the quiet.

But life stirred here—quietly, unseen. As the riders followed the smooth curves of sand between the bushes, like carriage paths through a garden, they would sometimes glimpse a herd of gazelle springing up from the earth, racing silently toward the hills—a flash of dappled brown and white vanishing into the distance.

“It’s strange,” Durrance murmured. “This is the very route the caravans once took southward to Erkoweet and the Khor Baraka. The Suakis built summer houses here.”
“And there,” Mather added, pointing ahead, “Tewfik fought—and died with four hundred men.”

For three hours they rode across the plateau. It was May, and the sun beat down with unbearable intensity. Alertness faded. Men rocked drowsily in their saddles, longing for evening and the cool glow of stars. The camels moved with their peculiar, smirking gait, heads bobbing rhythmically—until suddenly, a hundred yards ahead, Durrance saw a ruined wall pierced by window openings framing the sky.

“The fort,” he said.

Three years had passed since Osman Digna captured and razed it. But in that time, nature had reclaimed the ruin. Trees had grown thick around it—on the rear, right, and left—so that it appeared only when you were upon it, much like Childe Roland discovering the Dark Tower. In front, the sand stretched clear to the wells, where three great Gemeiza trees stood like sentinels, their dark branches spreading wide.

In the shade near the fort’s right front, the soldiers dismounted and prepared their meal. Durrance and Mather walked around the structure. At the southern corner, Durrance stopped.

“Hallo!” he said.

“Some Arab has camped here,” Mather observed, halting beside him.

A small pile of grey ashes rested on a blackened stone.
“And lately,” Durrance said.

Mather stepped into the crumbling archway and wandered through the roofless corridors. Durrance kicked the ashes with his boot. A charred twig glowed faintly red. He pressed down on it—tiny smoke curled upward.

“Very lately,” he muttered, then followed Mather inside.

Young saplings sprouted from every crack in the mud walls, from fissures in the floor, from the corners. Behind the fort rose a steep glacis and a deep ditch. Durrance sat on the parapet above the glacis, watching pigeons wheel overhead. He thought of Tewfik Bey, who must have stood here each day, scanning the pass from Suakin, hoping—like another general far to the south—for the gleam of sunlight on weapons that never came.

Mather sat beside him, lost in different thoughts.
“The Guards are already steaming out past the coral reefs toward Suez. In a week, it’ll be our turn,” he said. “What a cursed place this is.”

“I’ll come back,” Durrance replied simply.

“Why?” Mather asked.

“I like it. I like the people.”

Mather found the sentiment odd—but he knew it explained Durrance’s rise. Sympathy had given him patience, understanding. While others rushed ahead, Durrance had listened, learned, and earned the trust of the tribes. He understood their hatred of Turkish rule, their fervor under Osman Digna’s tyranny—real or feigned. That empathy had carried him farther than talent alone.

“I’ll return,” he said, “in three months. Because we all know—every Englishman in Egypt knows—this isn’t the end. I want to be here when the work begins again. I can’t stand unfinished things.”

The sun blazed. Soldiers lay in the shade, sleeping. Afternoon was as still as morning. For a long while, neither man spoke—drawn into silence by the surrounding hush.

Then Durrance’s gaze shifted from the hills. His eyes sharpened, fixed on the thickets beyond the glacis. He wasn’t thinking of Tewfik anymore. Without turning, he saw Mather looking in the same direction.

“What are you thinking about?” Durrance asked.

Mather laughed softly. “I was planning my first dinner back in London. Alone. At Epitaux. Watermelon to start. And you?”

“I was wondering why the pigeons keep circling one particular tree—beyond the ditch, to the right of two small bushes. Don’t point to it. Just watch.”

Around them, pigeons perched quietly, purple dots among the leaves. But over that one tree, they wheeled nervously, calling in alarm.

“We’ll draw that covert,” Durrance said. “Take a dozen men, surround it quietly.”

He stayed on the glacis, watching. Six soldiers crept in from the left, six from the right. Before they could close the ring, the branches shook violently—and an Arab burst from the undergrowth. Wearing a yellowish dammar roll around his waist, armed with a flat spear and a hide shield, he dashed out between the soldiers and sprinted a few yards across the plain.

Then Mather barked a command. The Arab stopped instantly—as if he’d heard it. No rifle was raised. He turned and walked calmly back to Mather.

They brought him to the glacis. He stood before Durrance with no arrogance, no fear.

“I am Abou Fatma, of the Kabbabish tribe,” he said in Arabic. “I am friendly to the English. I’m going to Suakin.”

“Why did you hide?” Durrance asked.

“It was safer. I knew you were my friends. But… did you know I was yours?”

Durrance paused. Then, swiftly: “You speak English.”

“Yes,” the Arab replied without hesitation. “I know a few words.”

“Where did you learn them?”

“In Khartum.”

After that, Durrance left the others and talked with Abou Fatma alone on the glacis. They spoke for nearly an hour. When it ended, the Arab descended the glacis, crossed the trench, and headed toward the hills.

Durrance ordered the march to resume. Water tanks were filled. Men refilled their zamshyehs—the thirst of afternoon was the worst of all. Camels groaned and snarled as they mounted. The detachment turned northwest, angling sharply from its morning path. It skirted the hills opposite the pass it had descended earlier.

The bushes thinned. The land grew black with stones, dotted sparsely with yellow-tasseled mimosas.

Durrance called Mather to his side.
“That Arab had a strange tale. He was Gordon’s servant in Khartum. In early 1884—eighteen months ago—Gordon gave him a letter to take to Berber. From there, it was to be telegraphed to Cairo. But Berber had just fallen when he arrived. He was arrested the next day. But during that single free day, he hid the letter in a house wall. As far as he knows, it’s still there.”

“He’d have been questioned if it had been found,” Mather said.

“Exactly. And he wasn’t. He escaped three weeks ago. Curious, isn’t it?”

“And the letter remains buried?”

“Possibly. But he had the chain marks on his ankles.”

The cavalcade turned left, climbing into the northern hills, over shale again.

“A letter from Gordon,” Durrance mused. “Maybe scribbled on the rooftop of his palace, beside his telescope—written in haste, his eye already back to the lens, scanning the palm trees for the smoke of steamers. And it travels down the Nile to be hidden in a mud wall in Berber. Yes—it’s curious.”

He turned his face westward, watching the sun dip behind the hills. The sky darkened rapidly to violet; in the west, fire and gold exploded into a blaze of color. Then the fury softened, blending into a delicate rose. That too faded, leaving a sky of pure emerald green, lit from beneath the world’s rim.

“If only they had let us go west last year—to the Nile,” Durrance said, almost pleading. “Before Khartum fell. Before Berber surrendered. But they wouldn’t.”

The sunset’s beauty meant nothing to him now. The letter’s story had struck deep. He thought of Gordon—the honest, great, impractical soldier—despised by officials, undermined by intrigue, a man of few ties, yet relentless in his duty, knowing full well that every step forward was undone the moment he turned away.

Darkness settled. The camels quickened their pace. Cicadas shrieked from every tuft of grass. The detachment moved down toward the well of Disibil.

That night, Durrance lay awake on his camp bed beneath the stars. He forgot the letter. Southward, the Southern Cross hung slanted in the sky. Above, the Great Bear curved like a silver arc. In a week, he would sail for England. He counted the years since the packet had left Dover Pier—and found the tally satisfying.

Kassassin. Tel-el-Kebir. The rush down the Red Sea. Tokar. Tamai. Tamanieb. The moments crowded in—vivid, real. He felt again the thrill of the Hadendowas charging through McNeil’s zareba six miles from Suakin. He remembered the Berkshires holding firm, the Marines steady, the broken troops rallying.

Years of war—years of purpose—had brought him to brevet lieutenant-colonel.

“A week more—only a week,” Mather murmured, drifting off.

“I’ll come back,” Durrance said, laughing softly.

“You have no friends?”

There was a pause.

“Yes,” he said. “I have friends. Three months to see them.”

He hadn’t written to Harry Feversham in years. Writing letters was not his nature. Now, he imagined surprising his friends in Donegal—or finding them in London. He might ride once more in the Row. But in the end, he would return. His friend was married—Ethne Eustace. And Durrance’s own life’s work lay in the Sudan.

He would come back.

And so, turning on his side, he slept dreamlessly beneath the stars, while the heavens rolled above him.

* * * * *

At that very moment, Abou Fatma of the Kabbabish tribe slept beneath a boulder on Khor Gwob. He rose early and continued across the broad plains to the white city of Suakin.

There, he told his story to Captain Willoughby, acting deputy-governor. Later, at the Palace, he repeated it—then again in the native bazaar, speaking in Arabic. A Greek seated outside a nearby café overheard part of it.

The Greek approached Abou Fatma, offered him merissa—enough to intoxicate—and persuaded him to recount the tale a fourth time, slowly and clearly.

“Could you find the house again?” the Greek asked.

Abou Fatma had no doubt. He drew diagrams in the dust—unaware that Berber had been systematically destroyed and rebuilt northward by the Mahdists.

“It will be wise to speak of this to no one but me,” the Greek said, jingling a handful of dollars.

For a long while, the two men talked in secret.

The Greek was Harry Feversham—the very man Durrance planned to visit in Donegal.
Captain Willoughby was Deputy-Governor of Suakin.
And after three years of waiting, one of Harry Feversham’s chances had finally come.
