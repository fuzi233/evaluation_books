“And it’s hame, hame, hame,
Hame fain wad I be.”

The soft, pale papering of the rooms was all that could make the house at Milton seem tolerable. But even that was not enough—there was something else missing, something that could never be replaced. The thick, yellow November fogs had settled in, swallowing the view of the valley and the sweeping curve of the river, leaving only a dull, smothered world outside the windows. When Mrs. Hale arrived, she found no comfort in her new home. Margaret and Dixon had been unpacking for two days, but inside, everything still felt chaotic and incomplete. Outside, the fog crept up to the very glass, swirling through every open door in choking waves of damp, unclean mist.

“Oh, Margaret!” Mrs. Hale exclaimed, her voice trembling with dismay. “Are we really to live here?”

Margaret’s heart echoed the sorrow in her mother’s tone. She struggled to reply, finally managing, “Oh, the fogs in London are often worse.”

“But then,” her mother whispered, “you knew London. You knew your friends were near. Here—here we are alone. Oh, Dixon, what a place this is!”

“Indeed, ma'am,” Dixon said, her face drawn with concern, “I’m sure it’ll be your death before long—and then I know who’ll—” She stopped herself abruptly. “Miss Hale, that’s far too heavy for you to lift.”

“No, thank you, Dixon,” Margaret replied, her voice cold and distant. “The best thing we can do now is get Mother’s room ready so she can rest. I’ll go fetch her some coffee.”

Mr. Hale, too, was disheartened. He sought Margaret out, seeking solace. “Margaret,” he sighed, standing by the window, “I truly believe this place is unhealthy. Suppose either your mother or you fall ill? I wish I had taken a post in the countryside—Wales, perhaps. This is unbearable.”

There was no comfort to give. They were here. They had no choice. The move, the fortnight spent at Heston—it had drained nearly all their savings. There was no turning back. That night, as Margaret lay awake, the weight of their situation pressed upon her like the smoky air that clung to her bedroom. Her room was narrow, tucked into a long projection at the rear of the house. The window looked directly across to another similar wall, just ten feet away. Through the fog, it loomed like a silent barrier between her and hope.

Inside, chaos reigned. All efforts had gone toward making her mother comfortable. Margaret sat on a box, its direction card from Helstone catching her eye—Helstone, beautiful, beloved Helstone! A wave of grief washed over her. Then, suddenly, she remembered a letter from Edith, half-read earlier in the day’s rush.

She opened it again. Edith wrote of their arrival in Corfu, of their voyage along the Mediterranean, of music and dancing on deck, of life unfolding in vibrant colour. She described her house with its trellised balcony, the white cliffs, the deep blue sea. She wrote fluently, if not vividly—enough detail to let Margaret see it all in her mind.

Captain Lennox and another newly married officer shared a villa high above the sea. Their days, even so late in the year, were filled with boating, picnics, outdoor pleasures—life lived freely, joyfully. Edith’s existence seemed as clear and boundless as the sky above her. Her husband attended drills; she, the most musical wife among them, copied popular English tunes for the bandmaster—a duty she treated with cheerful seriousness.

She hoped, if the regiment stayed another year, that Margaret might come visit. And she asked if Margaret remembered the day twelve months ago when she wrote—how it rained all day in Harley Street, how she refused to wear her new gown to a dinner party, afraid of getting it wet, and how it was at that very dinner they first met Captain Lennox.

Yes, Margaret remembered well. Edith and Mrs. Shaw had gone to dinner. Margaret had joined later. The memory flooded back—the grandeur of the house, the elegance of the furniture, the ease of the guests. It contrasted sharply with the present. That old life had closed behind them like a smooth, quiet sea, leaving no trace. The dinners, the visits, the dances—they continued, untouched, though Edith and Aunt Shaw were gone. And Margaret? She was barely missed.

She doubted anyone from that world thought of her—except Henry Lennox. He would try to forget her, she knew. He always claimed he could push away painful thoughts with ease. But the truth was, she had never loved him as a lover. If she had, and if their lives had changed as they had, she could not doubt that Mr. Lennox would have resented the shift in her father’s position. It would have wounded his pride.

Yet, though the thought stung, she could bear it. She believed in her father’s integrity. His mistakes, though serious, were rooted in good intent. Still, the world’s judgment—its rough, blunt dismissal of her father as fallen—would have galled Mr. Lennox deeply. As she imagined what might have been, she found herself grateful for what was.

They were at rock bottom. They could not sink lower. Edith’s astonishment, Aunt Shaw’s shock—when their letters came, she would meet them bravely.

She rose slowly, began undressing, allowing herself the luxury of time, though it was late. She fell asleep, hoping for light—some sign of warmth or hope. But if she had known how long the darkness would last, her heart would have broken.

The season was cruel to both body and spirit. Her mother caught a severe cold. Dixon, too, was clearly unwell, though Margaret dared not offer help—she would only insult her dignity. No girl could be found to assist. All young women worked in the factories. Those who applied were scolded by Dixon, who scorned the idea that such girls could ever serve in a gentleman’s household.

So they kept a charwoman almost constantly. Margaret longed to send for Charlotte—but the distance was too great, and Charlotte was more skilled than they could afford now.

Mr. Hale found pupils, recommended by Mr. Bell or Mr. Thornton. Most were boys just past school age. In Milton, it was believed a boy must be trained early to become a good tradesman. Sending him to university, especially Oxford or Cambridge, risked losing him to academic dreams. So sons were placed in mills, offices, warehouses at fourteen or fifteen, their education cut short to focus entirely on commerce.

Still, there were exceptions. Some parents saw their own shortcomings and tried to correct them. A few men, already in middle age, acknowledged their ignorance and began learning late in life.

Mr. Thornton was perhaps the oldest of Mr. Hale’s students—and certainly his favourite. Mr. Hale often quoted his opinions with such respect that it became a joke in the household: how much real teaching could fit into an hour when so much time was spent in conversation?

Margaret encouraged this lightness, knowing her mother watched Mr. Thornton’s friendship with unease. Once, her father’s attention belonged solely to his books and parishioners. Now, he eagerly awaited each meeting with Mr. Thornton. To her mother, it felt like neglect.

Mr. Hale’s praise had its usual effect: admiration turned to mild rebellion. After twenty years in a quiet country parsonage, the energy and power of Milton dazzled him. The machinery, the men—it all spoke of greatness, and he accepted it without questioning its cost.

But Margaret saw differently. She did not walk among the machines or the crowds. Instead, she encountered those who bore the brunt of progress—the ones trampled underfoot in the march of industry. The question, she thought, was not whether progress was made, but whether care had been taken to lessen the suffering of those left behind. Had they been gently moved aside, or crushed beneath the wheels?

It fell to Margaret to find a servant to help Dixon. At first, Dixon had promised to find exactly the right girl—one who would do the heavy work. But Dixon’s idea of a good servant was shaped by memories of tidy schoolgirls from Helstone, proud to be allowed into the parsonage, respectful almost to fear.

Dixon enjoyed that reverence, just as Louis XIV enjoyed the flattery of his courtiers. But in Milton, the girls who applied were blunt, independent, even bold. They questioned Dixon back, doubting the family’s means—how could they live in a house worth thirty pounds a year and yet keep two servants, one so haughty?

Mr. Hale was no longer seen as Vicar of Helstone, but as a man with a certain income. Margaret grew weary of Dixon’s endless reports of these applicants’ rudeness. She was repelled by their manners, offended by their directness, their curiosity about a family’s wealth. Yet, the more she felt insulted, the less she spoke. She preferred to handle the search herself, sparing her mother the pain of repeated disappointments.

So she went out, visiting butchers and grocers, searching for the perfect girl. Each week, her hopes sank lower. In a town like Milton, where factory work offered better pay and greater freedom, few wanted to be domestic servants.

It was hard for Margaret to walk alone in such a busy place. Her aunt’s strict rules had always required a footman when going beyond Harley Street. Though she had rebelled silently against those limits, enjoying the freedom of the forest walks—running through the trees, pausing to watch birds or listen to the wind—now she had to adjust to the rigid pace of city streets.

But the real challenge was not the pace—it was the people. The side of town where Crampton stood was crowded with factory workers. From the mills nearby poured streams of men and women twice or thrice a day.

At first, Margaret was startled by their boldness. They laughed loudly, joked openly, especially at anyone who seemed above them. Their voices rang out, unbound by polite restraint. The girls, with their rough but friendly ways, would comment on her dress, even touch her shawl to feel the fabric. Once, a girl asked her about a particular article she admired.

There was such simple trust in their questions, such belief in her kindness, that Margaret found herself answering, even smiling back.

She didn’t mind the girls. But the men—those she feared and resented. They didn’t comment on her clothes, but on her face, in the same open, fearless way.

She, who had always considered any remark on her appearance an impertinence, now had to endure undisguised admiration. But their bluntness revealed innocence—not malice. Their words were honest, not meant to wound.

One day, after passing several men who called her “my sweetheart,” one lingered and said, “Your bonny face, my lass, makes the day look brighter.”

Another day, as she smiled to herself, a poorly-dressed, middle-aged worker said, “You may well smile, my lass; many a one would smile to have such a bonny face.”

He looked so worn that she smiled back, glad her looks could bring him joy. He seemed to understand, and from then on, whenever they met, a silent understanding passed between them. They never spoke again—only that one compliment—but Margaret found herself looking at him with more interest than anyone else in Milton.

On Sundays, she sometimes saw him walking with a girl—his daughter, thin and pale, clearly unwell.

One spring morning, Margaret and her father had walked to the fields beyond the town. She gathered wildflowers—dog-violets, lesser celandines—her heart heavy with longing for the south. Her father left her to attend business in Milton. On her way home, she met the man and his daughter.

The girl stared at the flowers. Without thinking, Margaret offered them.

Her eyes lit up. Her father spoke for her. “Thank yo, Miss. Bessy’ll think a deal o’ them flowers; that hoo will. And I shall think a deal o’ yor kindness. Yo’re not of this country, I reckon?”

“No,” Margaret said softly. “I come from the South—from Hampshire.”

“That’s beyond London, I reckon? And I come fro’ Burnleyways, forty miles to th’ North. And yet, yo see, North and South has both met and made kind o’ friends in this big smoky place.”

She slowed her pace to walk beside them, the girl’s steps weak and slow. Margaret spoke to the girl, her voice full of gentle pity.

“I’m afraid you are not very strong.”

“No,” the girl said quietly, “nor never will be.”

“Spring is coming,” Margaret said, trying to offer hope.

“Spring nor summer will do me good,” the girl replied.

Margaret looked at the man, expecting him to contradict her. But he said, “I’m afeared hoo speaks the truth. I’m afeared hoo’s too far gone in a waste.”

“I shall have a spring where I am boun’ to, and flowers, and amaranths, and shining robes besides.”

“Poor lass, poor lass,” the father murmured. “I’m none so sure o’ that; but it’s a comfort to thee, poor lass, poor lass. Poor father! it’ll be soon.”

Margaret was shocked—but not repelled. She felt drawn to them.

“Where do you live? We must be neighbours—we meet so often.”

“We put up at nine Frances Street, second turn to th’ left after yo’ve past th’ Goulden Dragon.”

“And your name? I must not forget that.”

“I’m none ashamed o’ my name. It’s Nicholas Higgins. Hoo’s called Bessy Higgins. Whatten yo’ asking for?”

Margaret hesitated. At Helstone, it would have been understood that she would call upon a neighbour whose name and address she’d asked for.

“I thought—I meant to come and see you.”

She felt shy, unsure. Was it an impertinence?

“I’m none so fond of having strange folk in my house,” the man said.

Then, seeing her flush, he relented. “Yo’re a foreigner, as one may say, and maybe don’t know many folk here. And yo’ve given my wench here flowers out of yo’r own hand—yo may come if yo like.”

Margaret was amused and nettled. She wasn’t sure she wanted to accept permission given as a favour.

But when they reached the turn into Frances Street, the girl stopped. “Yo’ll not forget yo’re to come and see us.”

“Aye, aye,” the father snapped. “Hoo’ll come. Hoo’s a bit set up now, because hoo thinks I might ha’ spoken more civilly; but hoo’ll think better on it, and come. I can read her proud bonny face like a book. Come along, Bess; there’s the mill bell ringing.”

Margaret returned home, wondering at her new acquaintances, smiling at the man’s insight into her thoughts.

From that day on, Milton began to feel less bleak. It was not the sun, nor time, nor habit. It was the human connection she had found—a spark of warmth in the smoke and fog.
