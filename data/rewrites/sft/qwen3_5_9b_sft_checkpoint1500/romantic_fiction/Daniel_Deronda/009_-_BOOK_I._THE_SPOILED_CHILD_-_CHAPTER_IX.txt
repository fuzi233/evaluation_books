Eight months after the family's arrival at Offendene, during the heat of late June, a whisper rippled through the local countryside, sparking excitement among those who listened. It bore no connection to the wars across the Atlantic, yet it resonated deeply with everyone from the corn merchants and brewers to the humble blacksmith of Diplow. To the laborers' wives, it promised employment for their nimble sons; to the farmers, a chance for better trade; and to the gentry, it offered a vision of elevated status. Above all, it stirred the dormant dreams of matrimony. Just as a royal visit ignites fantasies of knighthood, so this news awakened a fluttering vision of marriage in the minds of the well-born.

The news centered on Diplow Hall, Sir Hugo Mallinger's estate, which had stood silently watching its elms and beeches with shuttered windows for nearly two years. Now, preparations were underway to welcome a tenant in a fitting style for the remainder of the summer and the hunting season. The guest was not Sir Hugo, but his nephew, Mr. Mallinger Grandcourt—the presumptive heir to the baronetcy, as Sir Hugo's marriage had yielded only daughters. Fortune smiled upon young Grandcourt doubly: the baronetcy hung in the balance through his father, while his mother possessed a baronial lineage. Should certain distant relatives pass away, he might ascend to the peerage. Nature, it seemed, reserved the tuft of feathers for the male bird alone, yet the philosophy of copying nature blindly was not yet embraced by the wise. If Grandcourt secured a title, his wife would share it—an advantage that made the prospect of finding a bride for him a matter of considerable interest to many.

Yet, it is hard for some to believe that people would build matrimonial futures on mere rumors of a wealthy bachelor's arrival, dismissing such thoughts as cynical gall. They claim their minds are not so unbridged, that human nature knows better than to speculate on fallacies. However, this history does not concern general human nature, but rather a specific corner of Wessex, where reputations were unimpeached and visitors mingled freely with the ranks.

Consider the Arrowpoints, residing at their beautiful home, Quetcham. Though no one could accuse them of sordid motives regarding their daughter Catherine—who had stubbornly refused Lord Slogan, an Irish peer whose estate lacked drainage and population—their hearts were filled with anxious rumination. They wondered if Mr. Grandcourt was handsome, of sound constitution, virtuous, or perhaps a reformed man of liberal-conservative leanings. Whether his succession to the title was an event to be desired, they hoped he would be fit to secure Catherine's happiness.

These thoughts were mirrored in Mr. Gascoigne, the clergyman, who, despite his vocation, felt the heavy burden of a parent and guardian. He and his wife, Mrs. Gascoigne, began to feel that the young creatures under their care were beyond the reach of any metaphorical bit or bridle. Naturally, people did not openly confess their deepest fears or calculations concerning Grandcourt. On no subject is total transparency prudent, not even regarding the mysteries of chemistry or astronomy. One might forestall another with superior insight, or worse, incur ill will due to differing opinions. Mr. Gascoigne did not ask Mr. Arrowpoint for sources on Grandcourt as a husband, nor did Mrs. Arrowpoint voice to Mrs. Davilow her expectation that the prospective peer would seek Catherine, adding that she would not accept him unless he proved worthy. Even to his wife, the rector remained silent about the possibility of matrimony, fearing the implications of Grandcourt meeting Gwendolen at the upcoming Archery Meeting, though Mrs. Gascoigne's mind raced along similar paths. She had once exclaimed, "It would be a mercy, Fanny, if that girl were well married!" To which Mrs. Davilow, sensing a critique of her darling, replied inwardly, "You will not get her to marry for your pleasure."

To her husband, Mrs. Gascoigne asked if she had heard what sort of young man Grandcourt was, hoping he would set a good example. Mr. Gascoigne admitted he knew little. His acquaintances may have gossiped, but he deemed it futile to inquire into the past of a young man whose wealth and leisure rendered many habits venial. If Grandcourt had gambled, leaving others ruined, he would be a reformed character indeed, for true reformation requires the strength to stop when ruin is near. Yet, Mr. Gascoigne suspected no gambling. A landed proprietor with noble blood need not be scrutinized like a reformed servant offering himself as a footman. Reformation, when affordable, is rarely anything but genuine. Moreover, there was no evidence suggesting Grandcourt needed reform more than other youths of twenty-five, and ultimately, his current character mattered more than his history.

Mrs. Davilow, though outwardly composed, harbored her own speculations. An image of a handsome, accomplished, and excellent young man formed instantly in her mind, suitable for Gwendolen. But soon, a second, darker thought intruded: *Would Gwendolen be satisfied?* There was no guarantee that excellence aligned with Gwendolen's tastes or affections. Perhaps it required something else entirely. Thus, the image of the perfect suitor dissolved into a fluctuating blend of qualities that might win Gwendolen's heart. In this difficulty, Mrs. Davilow whispered to herself, "It would not signify about her being in love, if she would only accept the right person." For herself, marriage had been a necessity born of misfortune, yet she dared not dwell on its desirability for Gwendolen, fearing a response akin to that of the future Madame Roland: "Yes, Mother, like you."

Least of all would Mrs. Davilow allow hints of her "aerial castle-building" to slip out, aware that such thoughts might poison Gwendolen's view of a desirable husband before she even met him. Since Rex's farewell, the mother had felt a new sense of peril in probing the mystery of her child's feelings, yet she could conceive of welfare only in the shape of marriage.

The conversation shifted to Gwendolen's attire for the Archery Meeting. Having decided on a pale green accent for her white cashmere, Mrs. Davilow felt her ears tingle when Gwendolen suddenly threw herself into the stance of drawing a bow, her expression one of comic enjoyment. "How I pity all the other girls at the Archery Meeting!" she declared. "All thinking of Mr. Grandcourt! And they have not a shadow of a chance."

Startled, Mrs. Davilow hesitated, but Gwendolen turned sharply, her tone wicked. "Now you know they have not, Mamma. You and my Uncle and Aunt—you all intend him to fall in love with me."

Piqued, Mrs. Davilow feigned surprise. "Oh, my dear, that is not so certain. Miss Arrowpoint has charms which you have not."

"I know," Gwendolen retorted, "but they demand thought. My arrow will pierce him before he has time for thought. He will declare himself my slave—I shall send him round the world to bring me back the wedding ring of a happy woman—in the meantime, all the men who stand between him and the title will die of different diseases. He will return as Lord Grandcourt, without the ring, and fall at my feet. I shall laugh at him; he will rise in resentment; I shall laugh more. He will summon his steed and ride to Quetcham, where he will find Miss Arrowpoint newly wed to a needy musician, Mrs. Arrowpoint tearing her cap off, and Mr. Arrowpoint standing by. Exit Lord Grandcourt, who returns to Diplow, and, like M. Jabot, *changes his linen*."

Had there ever been a young witch more like this? One might try to hide secrets from her, sit upon them looking innocent, only to realize she knew precisely how much money lay beneath the cushion by the corner of her eye. To turn the key against the damp would be futile. By dint of divination, she likely already knew more of Mr. Grandcourt than anyone else. Driven by this suspicion, Mrs. Davilow asked, almost without thinking, "What kind of a man do you imagine him to be, Gwendolen?"

"Let me see!" said the witch, pressing her forefinger to her lips with a thoughtful frown, then stretching it out decisively. "Short—just above my shoulder—trying to appear taller by turning up his mustache and keeping his beard long. A glass in his right eye to lend him an air of distinction. A strong opinion about his waistcoat, but uncertain and trim about the weather, on which he will try to draw me out. He will stare at me constantly, and that glass in his eye will make him pull horrible faces, especially when he smiles flatteringly. I shall cast down my eyes, and he will perceive I am not indifferent. I shall dream that night that I am gazing at the magnified face of an insect—and the next morning he will make an offer. The sequel, as before."

"That is a portrait of someone you have seen already, Gwen," Mrs. Davilow said gently. "Mr. Grandcourt may prove a delightful young man."
