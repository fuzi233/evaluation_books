Buck’s first day on Dyea beach did not unfold like a day at all, but like a whole bad season crammed into a handful of hours—each moment startling, each breath edged with a cold so clean it seemed to scrape the inside of his nose. He had been lifted from warm lawns and the soft, human clatter of comfort and dropped into a camp that lived by different sounds: the flat crack of clubs on hide, the thin whine of a lash, the iron click of teeth meeting, and the constant undertone of men shouting in tongues that did not bother to translate themselves for a newcomer.

Here, there was no gentleness in the air. Even the domestic things—tents, fires, sacks of provisions—were wrapped in urgency. Smoke smelled of green wood half-wet with snow. Fish, split and dried hard as boards, gave off a sharp, briny tang that clung to fur and mitten alike. Life moved fast, and if you failed to read it you were simply erased. Buck learned that law before he had a chance to pretend he didn’t.

It was Curly who paid the first price in front of his eyes.

They were camped near the log store. Curly, friendly as she had always been, went up to a big husky—wolf-sized in manner, if not in bulk—with the same open-hearted confidence a house dog might bring to a neighbor’s porch. There was no growl to warn her, no ritual, no polite testing. The attack arrived like a thrown blade: a leap in, a metallic clip, and a leap out again so swift it seemed the air itself had bitten her. Her face opened from eye to jaw.

Curly lunged back, brave and outraged, trying to fight as she knew how—front-on, honest, trusting that effort and courage would be answered in kind. But the other dog fought in the wolf way: strike and vanish, strike and vanish, letting her spend herself against emptiness and cold. Then he met her with his chest at an angle—an ugly, practiced trick—and toppled her. The moment her feet lost the ground, the camp’s sound changed.

Thirty or forty huskies rushed in, but not with the noisy chaos Buck expected. They formed a ring that was intent and terribly quiet, a hush full of licking mouths and waiting throats. Buck did not yet understand that silence—how it held hunger the way a covered pot holds steam—but he saw their tongues slide over their chops, saw their bodies quiver for the instant when cruelty would be permitted.

When Curly went down, they poured over her as if a trapdoor had opened beneath the snow and released them. The circle collapsed into a bristling heap of bodies—snarling, yelping, tearing. Curly screamed once, twice, and the screams were swallowed under fur and fang. The sound of it struck Buck harder than the sight: agony beaten into a muffled, wet ruin.

Spitz stood close enough to see. He did not help. He merely ran out his scarlet tongue in a way that looked like laughter.

François, the swart half-breed, burst in swinging an axe, and three men came with clubs. The blows landed with dull, meaty thuds; the pack scattered in grudging fragments. It was over quickly—two minutes from Curly’s fall to the last assailant being clubbed away—but Curly lay trampled and torn, limp in bloodied snow, almost literally in pieces. François stood over her, cursing so fiercely the words became more sound than sense, a rough music of rage that belonged to the North as surely as the wind.

That night and many nights after, Buck would wake with the memory of that circle—its quiet, its sudden closing—staring at him from the dark.

So that was the rule: no fairness. No pause for pity. Once down, you were finished. Buck decided without ceremony that he would never go down.

And when Spitz laughed again, Buck’s hatred began—bitter and clean, like a knife laid on ice.

Before the shock had cooled inside him, another lesson arrived in leather and buckles.

François fitted straps around Buck’s chest and shoulders. The harness felt wrong—too intimate, too demanding—as if it claimed him. It was like what Buck had seen grooms put on horses at home, except there was no stable smell here, no sweet hay, no familiar creak of stalls. Only snowlight and the sharp scent of men’s mittens, tar, and fish.

He was put to work at once, hauling François on a sled to the dark fringe of forest and back with a load of firewood. The logs carried their own perfume—resin and sap, crushed green life locked under frost. Buck’s pride flared at being made a draught animal, but wisdom, newly learned by pain, kept him from rebellion. He bent to it, pulled hard, and tried to read the language being spoken to him: not a language of words, but of whip, teeth, and weight in the traces.

François demanded instant obedience, and the whip made it sensible. Dave, an experienced wheeler, corrected Buck with quick nips whenever he blundered. Spitz, leader and veteran, couldn’t always reach him, but when he could, he growled sharp reproof, or slyly leaned his body in the harness to jerk Buck into line. Their corrections came like a stern apprenticeship in some craft where mistakes cost blood.

Buck learned fast. “Ho” meant stop. “Mush” meant go. On bends he must swing wide. On descents he must keep clear of the sled that came shooting downhill behind them like a pursued thing. The work began to make a strange kind of sense—rhythm in the strain, a grim satisfaction in doing it correctly.

“T’ree vair’ good dogs,” François told Perrault. “Dat Buck, heem pool lak hell. I tich heem queek as anyt’ing.”

By afternoon, Perrault returned in a hurry, and with him came two more dogs, brothers.

“Billee” and “Joe,” he called them—true huskies, sons of the same mother, yet as different as day and night. Billee overflowed with good nature; his tail seemed always ready to negotiate peace. Joe was sour, inward, with a perpetual snarl that looked less like anger than a settled distrust of the world.

Buck greeted them as comrades. Dave ignored them. Spitz, on the other hand, set about asserting himself, thrashing first one and then the other. Billee tried appeasement—tail wagging, whines of submission—and when that failed he turned to run, still crying softly as Spitz’s teeth scored his flank. Joe would not run. He spun on his heels to face Spitz, mane up, ears flat, lips writhing back from snapping jaws, eyes gleaming with a terrible promise—the belligerence of fear turned into a weapon. Joe’s display was so formidable Spitz chose to save face by turning again on Billee, driving the yelping, inoffensive brother to the edge of camp.

By evening, Perrault added another: an old husky, long and lean and gaunt, with a scarred face and a single eye that flashed warning. He was called Sol-leks—the Angry One. Like Dave, he asked nothing and offered nothing; he moved into their midst with deliberate slowness, as if the world owed him space. Even Spitz left him alone.

Sol-leks had one peculiarity Buck discovered with a sudden sting. Approaching him on his blind side, Buck meant no offense—only curiosity—but Sol-leks whirled and slashed Buck’s shoulder to the bone, three inches up and down. The pain was bright and instructive. After that, Buck kept clear of the blind side, and never again gave Sol-leks cause.

That night brought Buck his next great problem: where to sleep.

The tent glowed warmly, candlelit, a domestic little pocket in the white waste. Buck, following the habit of his former life, stepped in as naturally as he might have entered Judge Miller’s house. Perrault and François answered him not with welcome but with curses and flying cooking utensils. A battered pot clanged off the ground near his paws; the sound alone told him he was trespassing in a language he did not speak.

He fled back into the cold, his wounded shoulder bitten by wind that slid under fur like a thin blade. He lay on the snow, tried to sleep, and the frost drove him up shivering. He wandered among tents where other dogs rushed him savagely. Buck bristled and snarled—learning quickly the posture that kept teeth at a distance—and they let him pass.

At last, desperate and puzzled, he returned to his own camp to see how his team-mates managed. They had vanished. He circled the tent, tail low, body trembling, feeling more forlorn than he had ever been on Judge Miller’s land. Then the snow gave way under his forelegs and he sank.

Something wriggled beneath him.

Buck sprang back, hackles up, snarling at what he could not see. A friendly yelp answered him, and a puff of warm air rose to his nostrils—air that smelled of dog and damp heat, of life conserving itself. In the hollow under the snow lay Billee, curled in a snug ball. He whined, wiggled, and, as a bribe for peace, licked Buck’s face with a warm wet tongue.

Another lesson. So that was how they did it.

Buck chose a spot and began to dig, clumsy at first, wasting effort with too much motion and too little knowledge. But soon his body heat began to fill the small space, and the snow became walls that held warmth the way an oven holds it once the door is shut. He slept at once, deeply—growling and barking in dreams, wrestling phantoms that smelled of blood and heard like Curly’s scream.

He did not open his eyes until the waking camp’s noises reached him. At first he did not know where he was. Snow had fallen in the night, burying him completely. Pressed in on every side, he felt a sudden, primitive terror—the fear of the wild thing caught in a trap. It rose from somewhere older than his own memories, a knowledge belonging to ancestors who had never seen a house or heard a civilized street. His muscles contracted; his hair stood. With a ferocious snarl he bounded straight up into blinding daylight, snow exploding around him in a flashing cloud.

Before he landed, he saw the camp spread white and busy. The memory of everything—from his stroll with Manuel to the hole he had dug—returned in a rush.

François shouted in triumph. “Wot I say? Dat Buck for sure learn queek as anyt’ing.”

Perrault nodded gravely. As courier for the Canadian Government, carrying important despatches, he wanted the best dogs, and Buck pleased him.

Within an hour three more huskies were added, making nine in all. A quarter of an hour later they were in harness and swinging up the trail toward Dyea Cañon.

Buck was glad to leave the beach behind. The work was hard, but he did not despise it as he had expected. The eagerness of the team possessed him—an energy that ran along the traces like a current. More surprising still was the change in Dave and Sol-leks. Out of harness they had been passive, uninterested; in harness they became new creatures—alert, intent, fiercely irritated by any delay. The toil of the traces was their pleasure and their meaning, as if they were built for this single function and became fully alive only when straining against it.

Dave ran as wheeler at the sled. Buck pulled just ahead of him, then Sol-leks, and the rest strung out single file up to Spitz at lead. Buck had been placed between Dave and Sol-leks to be taught. They were good teachers, enforcing their lessons with teeth. Dave was fair and wise; he never nipped without cause, and he never failed to nip when correction was due. With François’s whip behind them, Buck learned it was cheaper to improve than to fight back.

Once, during a brief halt, Buck tangled the traces and delayed the start. Dave and Sol-leks flew at him and administered a trouncing. Their attack worsened the tangle, but the pain went into Buck’s memory like a nail. After that he kept the traces clear. By day’s end he had improved so much his mates almost ceased nagging him. François’s whip snapped less. Perrault even honored Buck by lifting each foot and examining it carefully.

They ran hard—up the Cañon, through Sheep Camp, past the Scales and the timber line, across glaciers and snowdrifts hundreds of feet deep, and over the great Chilcoot Divide that stands between salt water and fresh, guarding the sad North with a forbidding shoulder of ice. They made good time down the chain of lakes filling the craters of extinct volcanoes, and late that night pulled into the huge camp at the head of Lake Bennett, where thousands of goldseekers were building boats for the spring break-up.

Buck dug his hole and slept the sleep of the exhausted just. But before dawn fully owned the sky he was routed out into the cold darkness and harnessed again.

That day they made forty miles on a packed trail. Then came many days of breaking their own path—harder work, poorer time. Perrault often travelled ahead on webbed shoes, packing snow so the dogs could pull. François guided at the gee-pole, sometimes exchanging places, though not often. Perrault was in a hurry, proud of his knowledge of ice—a knowledge that mattered, because fall ice was thin and swift water held none at all.

Day after day, miles unending, Buck toiled. They broke camp in darkness; the first gray of dawn found them already moving. They pitched camp after dark, ate their ration, and crawled into the snow to sleep. The camp’s nightly music became familiar: the rasp of knives on frozen fish, the crackle of fire, the groan of sled runners cooling, the low, satisfied grumbles of dogs curling into holes.

Buck was ravenous. His ration—pound and a half of sun-dried salmon—seemed to vanish without reaching his belly, as if his body were a furnace that ate and ate yet never held heat. The fish was tough and leathery, salted by the sea and dried until it resisted the teeth. When it warmed briefly in the mouth it released a rich, oily smell—life preserved against winter—but it was never enough. The other dogs, smaller and born to this, received a pound and kept good condition. Buck’s hunger was a constant gnaw, a voice louder than fatigue.

In his old life he had been fastidious. Here fastidiousness was a way to be robbed. If he ate slowly, his mates—finishing first—would steal what remained. There was no defending it. While he fought off one thief, another would gulp down his portion. So he learned to eat fast, ripping and swallowing without pause, letting hunger drive him past delicacy. And hunger drove him further: he was not above taking what did not belong to him.

He watched and learned.

Pike, one of the new dogs, was a clever malingerer and thief. Buck saw him steal a slice of bacon when Perrault’s back was turned—bacon that smelled impossibly rich, smoky and fatty, the kind of food that haunted the air with its promise. The next day Buck duplicated the performance, but better: he got away with the whole chunk. The camp erupted in accusation and noise, men’s voices harsh, dogs’ snarls answering. Buck, careful and silent, was unsuspected. Dub, awkward and always caught, was punished for Buck’s theft.

That act marked Buck. Not because theft was noble, but because it was necessary. In this Northland, where the law was club and fang, moral niceties did not keep a body alive. Buck did not reason it out; he simply adapted. What had been called “good” in the Southland—respecting property, honoring feelings—was here a handicap, a soft language that could not be spoken without being bitten.

He did not steal for joy. He stole because his stomach clamored like an empty pot left on the fire too long. He did not steal openly; he did it slyly, with respect for club and fang. It was easier to do than not to do, and ease here meant survival.

His development—his stripping-down—was rapid. His muscles hardened like iron. Ordinary pain became something he could carry without complaint. He grew economical inside and out: he could eat anything, however loathsome, and his stomach would take it apart and salvage the last particle of nourishment. His blood delivered that salvaged strength to every reach of him, building tough tissue and enduring sinew.

His senses sharpened. Smell became a map: smoke, fish, fur, old blood, the faint mineral scent of ice. Sight took in movement at the edge of snowlight. Hearing became so keen that in sleep he registered the smallest sound and knew whether it promised peace or peril. He learned to bite ice from between his toes, to rear and strike through a scum of frozen water when thirst demanded. Most of all, he learned the wind—how to scent it a night ahead, how to choose a place to dig so that when the gale finally arrived it would find him sheltered on the lee, snug and warm.

And with each day, something older stirred.

The domesticated generations fell away. In vague, unbidden ways he remembered back—not as a thought he could name, but as a bodily certainty—to the youth of the breed, when wild dogs ran in packs through primeval forest and killed meat as they ran it down. Fighting came to him in the cut-and-slash and quick wolf snap, not as a lesson learned but as a skill waking from sleep. The tricks stamped into heredity arrived without effort, as if he had always known them.

On still, cold nights, when camp finally quieted and the noises of men dulled into distance, Buck would lift his nose to the hard stars and howl long and wolflike. The sound did not feel like something he invented; it felt like something passing through him—an ancient cadence carried down the centuries, a voice that belonged to forgotten ancestors who had howled under different skies yet the same indifferent cold. In that cry lived the meaning of stiffness and darkness, of hunger and endurance—things no human word could fully translate.

And thus the old song surged through him, domestic comfort loosening its hold, while the world hurried him north because men had found a yellow metal in the ground, and because Manuel, a gardener’s helper, needed money for the needs of his wife and the small copies of himself.
