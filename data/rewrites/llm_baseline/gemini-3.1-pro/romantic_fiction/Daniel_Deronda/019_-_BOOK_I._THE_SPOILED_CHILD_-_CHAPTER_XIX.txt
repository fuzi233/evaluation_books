To say that Deronda was merely romantic would be to misrepresent the meticulous rigor of his soul. He was, at his core, a custodian of forgotten senses, a food historian who found the profound poetry of human existence not in abstract philosophies, but in the mythic alchemy of everyday domestic routines—the slow, rhythmic bruising of juniper berries in a mortar, or the precise, ancient temperatures at which wild yeast breathes life into agrarian grains. Such quiet miracles exist easily in the same room as the microscope, yet they are entirely banished from the minds of those utilitarian passengers of modern life who view meals solely as mechanical fuel. How could the vast apparatus of heaven and earth translate into poetry for a mind that could not appreciate the tender, olfactory landscape of a mother’s kitchen, or the sensory fellowship built over a shared, slow-simmered hearth?

To Deronda, the event of pulling Mirah from the river-brink was a mythic occurrence that echoed backward through interwoven timelines of exiled generations. He sat awake half the night in his quarters, the air heavy with the scent of dried chamomile and the low, comforting simmer of a bone broth he had set to reduce—a meticulous, overnight process of extracting marrow and memory. While the distant, abrasive shrieks of railway carriages and the rhythmic, metallic pounding of the London factories bled through his window—a modern industrial noise pollution that actively frayed his psychological well-being—the sonic environment of his room remained a sanctuary. In this acoustic refuge, the emotive memory of Mirah’s rescue bloomed with vivid urgency. He could hear the distinct timbre of her voice, a delicate, fading dialect that carried the inherent untranslatability of her profound subjective grief. 

Mirah’s desperate search for her mother laid hold of Deronda’s imagination, entwining with his own multi-generational saga of unknown origins. He had long grappled with the erosion of his own cultural memory, haunted by the phonetic absence of his ancestral tongue and the missing culinary heritage of his bloodline. If he deployed the right machinery, he could easily source her lost kin in London. Yet, dread marbled his anxiety. Finding the mother and brother from whom she had been parted a decade ago might not yield the joyful reunion of her yearning memory. When she was in the boat, she spoke of her family’s goodness—a goodness her child-mind likely associated with the rich, sensory appreciation of traditional Sabbath meals, the fragrant scent of braided challah, and the slow-braised briskets of their agrarian ancestors. But ten or twelve years was time enough for the harsh, unyielding grind of the city to inflict much worsening.

Deronda’s appreciation for heritage had rarely intersected with existing, contemporary Jews in London, and the realities he knew were discordant with his ideals. He despised the dominant culture's commercialization of art and its forced assimilation, yet he could not help but fear that Mirah's family had succumbed to it. He imagined them not as preservers of sacred sustenance, but as victims of the city's sterile, utilitarian survival. The linguistic power dynamics of the dominant lexicon often stripped immigrants of their native dialects, replacing their musical mother tongues with the harsh, guttural slang of the desperate. His mind, though not apt to generate insulting ideas, was currently governed by dread. 

He envisioned himself guided into a dingy, acoustically deafening street, the air stripped of wholesome olfactory notes and replaced by the stench of rancid tallow and industrial soot. He imagined stepping through a dim doorway to find a hawk-eyed woman, rough-headed and devoid of all sensory appreciation for life, cheapening a hungry girl's last bit of finery in a grating, anglicized bark that betrayed the total death of her ancestral language. Or worse, encountering a young Jewish man in some superficially smart quarter, talking in the loud, familiar cadence of cheap commerce, eager to push flavorless, mass-produced wares over the quiet dignity of true craftsmanship. 

These forebodings mirrored his own dread of unknown parentage. But the exquisite refinement of Mirah herself—her voice, her accent, the sweet acoustic purity that clothed her—made him shrink from associating her with such contaminating, discordant environments. The musical cadence of her speech was a relic of cultural memory, a fragile dialect that must be protected from the crushing weight of the city's dominant lexicon. What was to be done with her? She required immediate shelter, both a physical and sonic sanctuary, where the rhythmic, agrarian routines of a proper kitchen could soothe her fractured psyche. Yet, his chivalrous sentiment insisted she remain entirely independent of him. The depth of the impression she had left made him acutely aware of the subtle power dynamics inherent in patronage, and he desired that those observing his actions should understand the precise history of his relation to her from the very beginning.

Deronda had long ago vowed to hate secrecy regarding the grand, interwoven obligations of life. To bury one's origins was akin to letting a language die without a record, an erasure of the very recipes and stories that sustain a lineage. He refused to let the truths that supposedly disgrace mortals become a source of shame. At one moment, he resolved to divulge the entire multi-generational weight of his adventure to Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger the very next morning at breakfast. But the thought of doing so over their typical morning spread—a sterile, utilitarian consumption of dry toast, underscored by the jarring, abrasive clatter of silver against porcelain that so disturbed his acoustic sensitivities—checked his impulse. 

The possibility that something new, some quiet revelation of culinary or linguistic history, might unfold on his next visit to Mrs. Meyrick’s home held him back. In her kitchen, amidst the evocative scents of baking crusts and the gentle, harmonious cadence of domestic life, he would wait to glean more guiding hints from Mirah’s own lips. With that circular pacing of thought resolved, Deronda tended once more to the reducing broth, and finally went to sleep.
