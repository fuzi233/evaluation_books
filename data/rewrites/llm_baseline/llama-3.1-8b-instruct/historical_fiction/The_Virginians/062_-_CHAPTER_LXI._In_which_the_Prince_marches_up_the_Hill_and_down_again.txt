As the news of Mr. George Warrington's encounter with the Prince spread throughout the castle, a chorus of disapproval arose from the loyal subjects of the British crown. It seemed that the young man had committed a grave offense against the noble Prince, the beloved son of the king and captain-general of the British army. Will Esmond, known for his sharp tongue and penchant for strong language, took great pleasure in lambasting his cousin's behavior, declaring that George had squandered a golden opportunity for advancement. Cousin Castlewood chimed in, suggesting that perhaps George had simply grown weary of the war and its cacophony, much like the war-whoops he had heard during Braddock's campaign.

As the debate raged on, Lady Maria intervened, cautioning Will to temper his words lest they fall on the ears of George's brother, Harry. Will, undeterred, continued to express his disdain for his cousin, prompting Maria to remark that perhaps one warrior in the family was enough. My lord, amused by the exchange, interjected with a wry comment, suggesting that Will was the family's champion and that George, on the other hand, was more suited to quieter pursuits. The conversation devolved into a lighthearted game of verbal sparring, with my lord displaying his skill at piquet and Lady Castlewood playfully jibing about his gloved hand, which, she claimed, was better suited to cards than combat.

Meanwhile, Madame de Bernstein seethed with indignation at George's behavior, venting her frustration upon her household staff and, though she stopped short of scolding her nephew directly, her words carried a hint of disappointment and concern. When George paid his respects to her soon after the incident, she cautioned him that his refusal of the Duke's offer would likely hinder his prospects at court, where princes were not fond of having their favors declined. George acknowledged his mistake, confessing that he had never envisioned himself as a courtier and had doubted his ability to excel in such a role. His aunt, taking snuff, seemed to acknowledge the truth of his words, and George pressed on, explaining that his sole desire was to support his brother, Harry, who possessed the qualities necessary to succeed as a soldier.
