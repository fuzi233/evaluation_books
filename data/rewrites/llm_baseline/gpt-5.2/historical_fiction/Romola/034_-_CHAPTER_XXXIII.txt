Baldassarre had drifted through Florence as a leaf drifts between walls, seeking not charity—he had long since ceased to believe in that word—but the cheapest corner of shelter where a man might lie down and not be driven off with curses. The city pressed on him with its stony passages and its thin, trapped strip of sky; every turn seemed to repeat itself, and the very sameness made his broken memory ache, as if it were being forced to name streets it could not recall.

His feet, without clear bidding, carried him toward the one uneven rise within the walls, where a man could climb above tiled roofs and see, beyond them, the ring of hills and the wide valley that the Arno alone opened to view. He had been told—by Bratti, whose words came back in scraps like stones in a pocket—that Tito’s dwelling lay in the Via de’ Bardi. Baldassarre had looked along that street, then turned up behind it to the slope commonly called San Giorgio, glad of any ground that was not flat and hemming, glad of air that did not sound so boxed-in.

The hill was mostly gardens, thinly peopled; the soil smelled different there—less of damp stone, more of turned earth and leaf-mould—and the soundscape shifted too. The city’s clatter thinned, and in its place came small domestic noises: a bucket chain creaking, a goat’s impatient bleat, the soft crack of someone snapping kindling. Even in his misery, the change steadied him, as if the hill kept a memory of older Florence when life had been measured by water carried, beans shelled, bread baked, and not by the metallic shouts of crowded streets.

There was one scarred patch of ground, jagged with great stones, that seemed to remember an old hurt: a landslip, long ago, had broken houses there and left the place uncultivated ever since. Just above the edge of that roughness stood a queer little square building like a tower cut short and capped with fluted tiles. Close by, an outhouse leaned against a piece of ruined wall. Under a large mulberry-tree—half-dead now, shaking its last leaves into the open doorways—a small, dried, hard old woman was untying a goat with two kids.

Baldassarre saw at once that one part of the outbuilding was for beasts, but the other—its door hanging open—held nothing but straw and a few tools. It was the sort of shelter a man who wanted no questions would choose.

He spoke to the old woman. She did not look up. He came closer and raised his voice until it seemed to hurt his own throat. Then she stared, blinking, and answered only with a slow shake of the head and the muffled monotony of the deaf.

“No—no lodging.”

He persisted, shaping the matter with his hands and his few serviceable Italian words: poor stranger, far over seas; no money for hostels; only straw; a quattrino or two each week. She watched him as one watches a dog that might bite, muttering to herself in a thick undertone. Then, as if a calculation had ripened, she disappeared into the little house and returned with a hatchet. Pointing with it to a heavy chump of wood half-buried under litter, she asked if he would chop it up. If he would, he might lie in the outhouse for one night.

He took the hatchet and set to work. The blows fell clean; his arms still knew what his mind had lost. The old woman—Monna Lisa—stood with her arms akimbo, pleased in a narrow, thrifty way, talking low to herself as if her thoughts were another room she could lock.

It had lain there since her old man died, she muttered; might as well have been a stone on the fire. This stranger chopped well, though he spoke with a foreign tongue and looked odd. Cheaper than hiring a lad. And if he only wanted straw, he could earn it with errands up and down the hill. Who would know? A sin kept hidden was half forgiven.

The “her” she named without naming had eyes pressed, at that moment, to a round hole cut in the shutter of an upper window. The shutter was not closed for punishment; it was closed because glass was a luxury and draughts were free. A hole four inches wide served for looking out, and it required a low stool—rickety, but faithful—to reach it. On that stool stood Tessa.

She had darted to the opening at the first loud foreign voice, hungry for any change in her days. She watched the old man’s bent back, the lift and fall of his arms, the pale chips flying, the mulberry leaves shivering down like tired birds. When dusk gathered and she saw him go into the outhouse and sit on the straw, an irresistible thought took hold of her like the smell of supper taking hold of a hungry child.

She would bring him part of her food. She would talk to him.

He was not deaf like Monna Lisa. And there were things a girl could say that were wasted if you had to shout them at someone who already knew your whole small history. Besides, he was a stranger; strangers came from far away and went away again, belonging nowhere in particular. It was naughty—obedience was the largest part of Tessa’s notion of duty—but naughtiness could be confessed at Easter. What else did she have to confess? That she sometimes fell asleep over her beads? that she had been a little cross because Monna Lisa’s deafness made life feel like talking into wool?

Her pretty, pouting face brightened with the pleasure of the transgression. She still wore the simple dress of a contadina, but one made ready for a festa: dark-green serge, clean and neatly set; a red girdle; red glass beads at her throat; her brown hair, rough with its own curl, knotted and pinned with silver. On her hand was a new ornament she could not stop admiring: a fine gold ring that caught light like a secret.

She sat for a minute on her stool, hugging her knees, hovering on the edge of disobedience as if it were the edge of a warm hearth. She had been commanded to make no acquaintances. She had been warned—half in jest, half in threat—that if she did, her new happiness would vanish like treasure turned to lead when brought into daylight. And she had been so obedient that even when she went to church she shaded her face with her hood and pursed her lips tight, as if keeping her very breath from escaping to the world.

Her obedience, too, had been sharpened by fear: the memory of Nofri, the stepfather who beat and drove her, lurked like a shadow behind any unfamiliar footstep. But this old man—this old man was not Nofri. And he likely knew nothing of Nofri. If she fed him, he would be kind; he would not betray her. Monna Lisa would say no. Therefore Monna Lisa must not be asked.

Supper was being prepared downstairs—she knew it by the smell rising through the boards: macaroni piled like a small mountain, steamed until it went soft at the heart, then tossed so the strands shone; cheese grated over it until the scent was sharp and nutty, melting in the heat and clinging in little threads. The fragrance could have gentled a thief.

Tessa tripped down with solemn purpose, put on an innocent face, asked what the noise of talking had been, and before Monna Lisa could gather an answer, knitted her brow with a tiny peremptory air—like a kitten trying to look dangerous—and declared she chose to eat her supper below. Then she sent the old woman upstairs.

Three minutes later she had a lantern in one hand and a wooden bowl of macaroni in the other. She went out to the outhouse and kicked softly at the door.

Baldassarre, roused from a heavy reverie, opened to the lantern-light and saw—impossibly—this small, bright creature in clean festival dress, her eyes wide with delight as if she had found a song in a ruined place. For a moment he doubted whether he was awake.

“I’ve brought you some supper,” she said, lifting her mouth to his ear and shouting, as if he too were deaf like Monna Lisa. “Sit down and eat it, while I stay with you.”

Surprise and distrust rose first in him. Yet he had no impulse to push her away; the kindness struck him like a blow that had no place to land. He sank back on the straw. Tessa came close, set the bowl on his lap, placed the lantern before them so the light fell warm on the steam, crossed her hands, and nodded at the food with a smile that said, You may—yes, truly—you may eat.

In the hurry of doing her daring deed, she had forgotten that a stranger might hear. She fell into her old habit of gesture and loudness, the way one does when the house you live in has swallowed speech into deafness.

The macaroni was rich with cheese, and there was something in its plain warmth that reached past thought. Baldassarre had been gnawing dry bread. He ate.

Tessa watched two or three mouthfuls vanish before she dared to speak again; his eyes had seemed fierce at first. Then she leaned close and cried, “I like my supper, don’t you?”

The look he turned on her was not a smile—his face seemed carved to forget how—but it softened, like a dog’s eyes softening when a hand that might strike instead strokes.

“Yes,” he said. “But I can hear well. I’m not deaf.”

“It is true; I forgot,” Tessa said, clasping her hands. “But Monna Lisa is deaf, and I live with her. She’s kind, and I’m not frightened at her. We live very well—we have plenty of nice things. I can have nuts if I like. And I’m not obliged to work now.”

She spoke quickly, as if pouring out what had been dammed up. “I used to have to work, and I didn’t like it; but I liked feeding the mules. I should like to see poor Giannetta again—the little mule. We’ve only got a goat and two kids, and I used to talk to the goat a good deal, because there was nobody else but Monna Lisa. But now I’ve got something else—can you guess what it is?”

She drew back and looked at him with a challenging smile, as if she had offered a riddle with a prize inside it.

“No,” Baldassarre said, putting aside the bowl, staring at her as one stares at a half-remembered face from one’s own youth.

“You like me to talk to you, don’t you?” Tessa said. “But you must not tell anybody. Shall I fetch you a bit of cold sausage?”

He shook his head. He did not stop her with words, but his refusal was gentle. His gentleness made her bolder.

“Well, then—I’ve got a little baby,” she said, her voice dropping into awe, as if the word itself were holy. “Such a pretty bambinetto, with little fingers and nails! Not old yet; it was born at the Natività, Monna Lisa says. I was married one Natività—a long, long while ago—and nobody knew. O Santa Madonna! I didn’t mean to tell you that!”

She hunched her shoulders and bit her lip, staring at him as if expecting thunder for the secret spilled. But the stranger’s face did not change much; perhaps strangers did not understand what was grave and what was play in a girl’s life.

“Yes,” she went on, half to herself, “you are a stranger. You don’t live anywhere or know anybody, do you?”

“No,” Baldassarre said, speaking as if he were answering his own thought more than hers. “I only know one man.”

“His name is not Nofri, is it?” Tessa asked, sudden fear tightening her.

“No,” Baldassarre said, noticing it. “Is that your husband’s name?”

Tessa laughed, clapping her hands—pure relief turned into amusement. “No, indeed! But I must not tell you anything about my husband. You would never think what he is—not at all like Nofri!”

She laughed again at the very idea, as if the name Nofri could only belong to heaviness and blows, while the thought of her husband belonged to brightness.

“But I don’t see him very often,” she added, more gravely. “Sometimes I pray to the Holy Madonna to send him oftener, and once she did. But I must go back to my bimbo now. I’ll bring it to show you to-morrow. You would like to see it. Sometimes it cries and makes a face, but only when it’s hungry, Monna Lisa says. You wouldn’t think it, but Monna Lisa had babies once, and they are all dead old men. My husband says she will never die now, because she’s so well dried. I’m glad of that, for I’m fond of her.”

She paused, listening to something beyond the door—the hill’s nighttime hush, the goat’s soft shifting, the far city noise like a sea inside a shell. Then she asked, “You would like to stay here to-morrow, shouldn’t you?”

“I should like to have this place to come and rest in—that’s all,” Baldassarre said. “I would pay for it, and harm nobody.”

“No, indeed; I think you are not a bad old man,” Tessa said with earnest judgment. “But you look sorry about something. Tell me, is there anything you shall cry about when I leave you by yourself? I used to cry once.”

“No, child,” he answered. “I think I shall cry no more.”

“That’s right,” she said, pleased, as if he had promised her a gift. “And I’ll bring you some breakfast, and show you the bimbo. Good-night.”

She took up her bowl and lantern and shut the door behind her.

To Baldassarre the visit had been only a pale, brief rainbow against a blackness: a gleam that did not change the deep water he struggled in. When his waking drifted into the harsher images of sleep, he scarcely held her in mind.

But Tessa held him.

No sooner had she gone inside than she told Monna Lisa what she had done and insisted that the stranger must be allowed to come and rest in the outhouse when he liked. Monna Lisa, whose thrift had already begun to imagine him carrying burdens and fetching water, made a show of reluctance, shook her head, and said Messer Naldo would be angry if any man were allowed about the house.

Tessa refused to believe it. Naldo had said nothing against strangers who lived nowhere. This one knew nobody except one person—and that person was not Nofri.

At last Monna Lisa gave way with the air of yielding to sin rather than choosing profit. “Well, if I let him stay for a while and carry things up the hill for me,” she said in her thick undertone, “thou must keep thy counsel and tell nobody.”

“No,” said Tessa. “I’ll only tell the bimbo.”

“And then,” Monna Lisa went on, leaning closer as if even the walls had ears, “God may love us well enough not to let Messer Naldo find out anything about it. For he never comes here but at dark; and since he was here two days ago, it’s likely he’ll never come at all till the old man’s gone away again.”

“Oh me, Monna,” Tessa said, clasping her hands, longing and complaint knotted together, “I wish Naldo had not to go such a long, long way sometimes before he comes back again.”

“Ah, child,” Monna Lisa answered, with the solemnity of the old who keep the world’s distances like proverbs, “the world’s big, they say. There are places behind the mountains; and if people go night and day, night and day, they get to Rome and see the Holy Father.”

Tessa fell silent before that mystery, rocking her baby and singing syllables with no clear words, yet full of meaning the way a lullaby is—sound shaping comfort where language cannot. Her voice tried to imitate the sweet certainty of a triple chime, as if bells could keep absence from growing.

The next morning she was unusually industrious, driven by the promise of more talk and the pleasure of making the poor old stranger glad with breakfast and the sight of her baby. But when she was ready to carry the food, she found Monna Lisa had already put him to work drawing water. The chain and bucket made their slow, familiar complaint; the water answered with its cool slap, and that little domestic rhythm—pull, creak, splash—filled the air more kindly than the city’s shout.

Tessa postponed her paternosters and hurried down, indignant on his behalf, insisting he must sit on his straw so she could sit by him while he ate. That posture delighted her all the more because it was an old posture from her other life—straw shared with goats and mules, warmth made where there was none.

“I will not let Monna Lisa give you too much work,” she said, bringing him steaming broth and soft bread. The broth carried the scent of simmered bones and herbs, simple and honest; the bread yielded under the fingers, not like yesterday’s stone. “I don’t like much work, and I daresay you don’t. I like sitting in the sunshine and feeding things. Monna Lisa says work is good, but she does it all herself, so I don’t mind. She’s not cross; you needn’t be afraid of her being cross. And now—eat that, and I’ll go and fetch my baby and show it you.”

She returned with the small mummy-case bundled close in her arms. Yet the little thing inside seemed lively enough, with unusually large dark eyes that looked out solemnly, and only the faintest promise of a nose.

“This is my baby,” Tessa said, sitting close to Baldassarre so the lantern-light touched the child’s face. “You didn’t think it was so pretty, did you? It is like the little Gesù, and I should think the Santa Madonna would be kinder to me now—is it not true? But I have not much to ask for, because I have everything now—only that I should see my husband oftener.”

She hesitated, then offered the bundle with careful pride. “You may hold the bambino a little if you like—but I think you must not kiss him, because you might hurt him.”

Her prohibition came with a soothing excuse, as if she feared he would feel slighted. Baldassarre took the child, the weight of it scarcely more than a loaf, and yet it changed the air between them.

“Poor thing! poor thing!” he said, and the depth of his voice made the pity sound like a threat. To him it did not seem that this guileless, loving young creature reconciled him to the world; rather it seemed she stood beside him against it—something tender that the world would injure, something that would need avenging.

“Oh, don’t you be sorry for me,” Tessa said quickly. “Though I don’t see him often, he is more beautiful and good than anybody in the world. I say prayers to him when he’s away. You couldn’t think what he is!”

She took the baby back with a wide, mysterious glance, almost wishing he would question her, almost craving the weight of being asked to speak more.

“Yes, I could,” Baldassarre said, and there was bitterness in him like old wine turned sour.

“No, I’m sure you never could,” Tessa said, earnest as a vow. “You thought he might be Nofri,” she added, with triumph, as if that settled everything forever. “But never mind—you couldn’t know. What is your name?”

At the question, Baldassarre’s hand went over his knitted brow. He looked at her blankly, and the blankness was not a trick. It was as if a door in his mind had been slammed and bolted, and he stood outside it.

“Ah, child—what is it?” he said.

Sometimes he remembered his name well enough; sometimes, had he been able to summon it now, he would have chosen not to give it. But a sudden demand on his broken memory struck him into helplessness. In that moment he felt nothing but the emptiness where certainty ought to have been.

Tessa, ignorant as she was, learned pity by the look on his face. “Never mind,” she said softly. “You are a stranger; it is no matter about your having a name. Good-bye now, because I want my breakfast. You will come here and rest when you like—Monna Lisa says you may. And don’t you be unhappy, for we’ll be good to you.”

“Poor thing!” Baldassarre said again—whether of the baby, of Tessa, or of something in himself that could not be named in any tongue that still lived whole on his lips.
