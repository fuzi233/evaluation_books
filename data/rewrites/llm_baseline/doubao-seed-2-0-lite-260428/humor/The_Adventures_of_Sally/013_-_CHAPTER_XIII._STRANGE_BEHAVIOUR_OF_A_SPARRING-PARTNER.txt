1
Sally plopped down on her NYC apartment couch the morning she got back, feeling exactly like the person who’s stood shivering on the edge of a public pool for 20 minutes hyping themselves up to jump in mid-March, then finally did the plunge. Her bones ached, she’d had a garbage week of messy breakups and rushed transatlantic crossings, but for the first time in months, she didn’t feel like hiding under a blanket forever. She’d stopped waffling, she’d done the hard thing, and she was ready to deal with whatever chaos New York threw at her. The sting of it wasn’t the mushy, draggy sad kind that makes you eat a whole jar of pickles and cry at cat photos—it was the zingy, alive, “I could take on a taxi cab right now” kind of sharp.

She finished unpacking her overstuffed hatboxes and tidying up, and the only thing she could think of was that she needed Ginger, stat. His whole vibe of unflappable, no-nonsense solid friend was exactly the safety net she needed right now. She kicked herself for not cabling him to meet her at the dock, where the customs guys yelled at her for 10 minutes about a jar of homemade jam she’d stuffed in her suitcase and the whole echoing shed made her feel smaller than a gum wrapper under a shoe.

She glanced at her watch, shocked it was only 9 a.m. She could swing by the Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises office, drag him out for pancakes for lunch, no questions asked. She grabbed her hat and bolted out the door.

New York’s obsession with swapping out every single employee every other month hadn’t spared the office, that was for sure. The friendly pimply teen she’d chattered with during her last visit was gone, replaced by a new pimply teen who looked like he was one bad papercut away from throwing the entire office typewriter out the window. He squinted at her like he’d caught her sneaking the office’s last pack of gum, and snapped at her to state her business.

“I’m here to see Mr. Kemp,” Sally said.

The teen scratched his cheek with a ruler, face so stern you’d never guess that 10 seconds before she walked in, he’d almost nailed his three-paperweight juggling trick while balancing that exact ruler on his chin—his whole life’s dream was to go into vaudeville, and he was this close to quitting to go do auditions full time.

“Name?” he said, ice cold.

“Nicholas. I’m Mr. Nicholas’s sister.”

The line hit him like a flying rubber band. He jolted so hard he dropped the ruler, fumbling to pick it up off the floor. His whole plan to take out his frustration over blowing that juggling trick on random visitors evaporated faster than cheap office coffee. His scowl flipped into a grin so bright it was like the sun popping through London fog. He jumped up, dusted the nearest hard chair off with his coat sleeve, and purred, “Take a seat, miss! Beautiful weather we’re having, right?”

“Thanks,” Sally said, amused. “Can you tell Mr. Kemp I’m here?”

The teen’s face fell instantly, oozing gentlemanly regret. “Ah, shoot, miss. Mr. Nicholas is out, headed to White Plains in his car half an hour ago.”

“I don’t want Mr. Nicholas,” Sally corrected. “I want Mr. Kemp. Red hair, looks like he’s never once successfully set a alarm clock in his life?”

The boy’s expression shifted into the kind of sad apologetic face you pull when someone asks you where the last slice of pizza went and you already ate it. “Never heard of no Mr. Kemp working here, sorry, ma’am.”

A cold little bad feeling crept over Sally. “How long have you worked here, exactly?”

“Just over a month!” he chirped, delighted to have a chance to chat. Normally he hated people interrupting his juggling practice, but the boss’s sister got a pass.

No. No way. Fillmore wouldn’t fire Ginger out of nowhere—except, wait, no, of course he would, if Ginger had somehow messed up so bad even Fillmore, the guy who once hired a drunk clown for a children’s show, couldn’t keep him around. “What’s he doing in White Plains?” she asked.

“Checking in on Bugs Butler, the boxer!” the teen said, lighting up. He leaned in, like he was sharing top secret government intel. “Bugs is fighting the lightweight champ Lew Lucas tomorrow. Everybody’s talking about it. You think Bugs stands a chance? I got two bucks right here in my vest pocket that says he couldn’t last six rounds. Guy couldn’t punch a hole through a nut sundae, I swear. Cyclone Mullins only beat him by a referee’s call after 15 rounds, for crying out loud! K-leg Binns knocked Cyclone out in five! Bugs is a stiff, through and through.”

Sally blinked, putting the pieces together. “So it’s a training camp there? Anybody can go find it?”

“Sure! Catch the next train out, you’ll be there in 45 minutes, anyone’ll point you right to it!” he said, holding the door open for her with old-school politeness, then immediately darted back to his desk to hoist the ruler onto his chin and get back to juggling the paperweights the second she was out of sight.

2
Fillmore was seconds away from tiptoeing out of the gymnasium-slash-barn, sighing so hard his shoulders ached. He’d been cornered by Bugs Butler’s hyper, tweed-clad manager Lester Burrowes for 20 whole minutes, and if he had to listen to one more second of Burrowes gushing about Bugs’s secret new left hook, he was going to gnaw through the ring ropes and eat the canvas. The public got to gawk at the training for 50 cents a pop, which meant 40 people were loitering around the ropes, half of them newspaper guys there for free passes writing flowery articles about how Bugs was a surefire champion, when everyone who knew anything knew Bugs couldn’t punch his way out of a wet paper bag.

Burrowes had just run off to yell at someone about something or other, and Fillmore was 10 feet from the door, about to sprint to the nearest diner and eat three hamburgers, when a soft voice said “Hi, Fillmore!” behind his shoulder.

He leapt so high he smacked the bottom of a hanging punching bag, sending it swinging back and forth. His nervous system felt like someone had stirred it with a pool cue. He’d left Sally in Shropshire, England, a whole week prior, fully convinced she was going to stay there and marry millionaire Bruce Carmyle, the guy with the fancy estate and the bank account thick enough to buy a private island. She hadn’t mentioned a single plan to cross the Atlantic, let alone show up out of nowhere in a White Plains boxing training camp. His brain short circuited.

“Sally!” he croaked. “What are you doing here? You’re supposed to be in England!”

“I decided I wanted to come home,” Sally said, glancing past him at the ring. “Things didn’t work out at Monk’s Crofton. Let’s not talk about it, okay?”

“Wait a second—you turned down Carmyle? The millionaire?” Fillmore squawked, horrified. The perfect, ideal brother-in-law, the guy who would’ve happily funded all Fillmore’s terrible theatrical ideas for the rest of their lives, and she’d dumped him? He felt the kind of despair you feel when you drop a whole full pint of ice cream on the sidewalk. “Why? He’s a great guy!”

“I didn’t like him enough to marry him, Fill, that’s the obvious reason,” Sally said, already annoyed.

Just then, Burrowes came hustling back, grinning, and clamped a hand on Fillmore’s coat button so tight he couldn’t escape. “Hey! You ain’t leaving! Bugs is about to spar! You gotta see his new left shift, that guy’s a wizard, a total natural! The best lightweight since Joe Gans, I swear!”

“Quick question,” Sally cut in. “Can he make 135 ringside without being weak as a dishrag?”

Burrowes dropped his hand off Fillmore’s coat button like he’d touched a hot stove. His jaw hung open. No rich nice lady who looked like she owned 12 fancy hats was supposed to know boxing lingo! Fillmore didn’t waste a second—he slipped away like a cat escaping a bath, already halfway to the diner. If he didn’t eat a sandwich in the next 10 minutes, he was going to pass out.

“Wait—wait a second!” Burrowes spluttered. “You got that all wrong! Cyclone Mullins was down for half a minute in that fight and they refused to count him out! The ref was dead set against us! Bugs is a genius, look, I’ll show you his photo!” He fished a postcard out of his pocket and shoved it at her. The picture was of Bugs crouched half naked, looking like he was mid-gastritis attack. “I’ll call him over, he’ll sign it for you! Bugs! C’mere!”

A guy in a ratty bathrobe wandered over, smirking so hard his cheeks creased. Bugs Butler, in the flesh, was even less charming than his photo—he had that mean little lip curl, that arrogant glint in his eye that said he thought every woman within 100 feet was swooning over him. He gave Sally the full smolder like he was the main character in a bad silent film romance, and Sally had to fight not to snort.

“Nice to meet you,” Sally said, polite enough. “Hope you win tomorrow.”

“Bet your boots I will,” Bugs said, puffing out his chest under the bathrobe. He glanced over the crowd, disappointed there weren’t more paying customers, then perked up again. “I’m gonna spar a couple rounds with Reddy first. Guy’s been getting too fresh lately, I’m gonna show him who’s boss.”

Burrowes hustled off to grab the sparring partner. Bugs winked at Sally, climbed under the ring ropes, and plopped down in his corner. Seconds later, Burrowes herded a sturdily built guy in a thick gray sweater over. The guy was yanking the sweater over his head, his broad shoulders visible as he pulled it off.

Tousled, bright tomato-red hair popped into view.

Sally gasped so loud half the newspaper guys turned to stare. The red-headed guy, who’d been bending down to grab his gloves, jerked upright so fast he almost knocked himself out on the top rope. He stared at her, face turning so bright crimson it perfectly matched his hair, looking like he’d just been caught red-handed stealing cookies off the boss’s desk.

“C’mon, speed it up, Reddy!” Burrowes yelled, checking his watch like he was late for a train.

Ginger Kemp jolted like he’d just woken up from a nap, and slowly started tugging his gloves on, his face still bright red. He looked like he wanted to dissolve straight through the canvas floor.

Sally tugged Burrowes’s elbow, whispering, “Wait—who is that guy?”

“Oh, him? He’s just Bugs’s sparring partner!” Burrowes said, distracted, checking the clock. “Sorry, miss, I gotta time the rounds, no time to chat!” He darted off, leaving Sally standing there, gawking.

She felt like an outsider who’d wandered into some weird sacred boys’ club ritual. All she could do was stare at Ginger, who was pointedly not looking at her, picking at the laces on his glove like they held the secrets of the universe. The stolid, reliable Ginger she’d left working in Fillmore’s office, who was supposed to have a nice boring desk job, was now a human punching bag for a lunkheaded boxer who couldn’t punch a hole in a nut sundae. She had zero words.
