"In prison, sir? Good heavens, for what crime?"

"She is a witness, and may prove essential to the case."

"Why, Messire Bailiff," interjected Denys, "you do not lay all your witnesses by the heels, I trow."

The alderman, pleased to be addressed as bailiff, grew conversational. "In cases involving bloodshed, we detain all testimony likely to secure bail and thus frustrate justice; hence, we still hold the womenfolk. A man may hide in one mindset for a week, but a woman, if she fulfills her duty to the realm on Friday, will undo it before Sunday comes around. Could you see that wench now, you would find her sobbing bitterly over having betrayed five men to the gallows. Had they been women, we might have trusted a subpoena; they despise one another, showing some sense in that regard. But now, thinking on it, there were other reasons for holding this one captive. Hand me those depositions, young sir." He adjusted his spectacles. "Ah! She was implicated; she was one of the band."

A loud denial erupted simultaneously from Denys and Gerard.

"No need to deafen me," retorted the alderman. "It is written in black and white. 'Jean Hardy (one of the thieves), when questioned, confessed that—hmm? Yes, here it is. 'And that the girl Manon was the decoy, and her sweetheart was Georges Vipont, one of the band; and hanged last month; and that she has been depressed ever since, openly blaming the band for his death, stating that if they had not been rank cowards, he would never have been caught, and it is his opinion she betrayed them out of pure spite—and'

"His opinion!" cried Gerard indignantly. "What significance does the opinion of a cutthroat hold, one burning for revenge against her who delivered him to justice? If you accept that, what value is his testimony? Is a thief never a liar? Is he not always a liar? And here is a motive to lie: revenge, which is the strongest of all passions. Oh, sir, what madness to question a detected felon and listen to him fabricating an honest life—as if he were a truthful man swearing in broad daylight with his true hand on the Gospel!"

"Young man," said the alderman, "restrain your heat in the presence of authority! By your tone, I find you are a stranger. Know then that in this land we question everyone. We are not so weak as to hope to discover the truth by closing either our left or right ear."

"And so you would listen to Satan bellying the saints!"

"Nonsense! The law deals only with men and women, and these cannot utter a story entirely composed of falsehoods, however hard they try. Therefore, we do not shut the barn door against any man's grain. Once admitted, we merely winnow and sift. Who told you I swallowed the thief's story whole like clear water? Not so. I credited only as much as was corroborated by superior evidence."

"Superior evidence?" Gerard looked bewildered. "Who else but the thieves would speak against her?"

"Marry, herself."

"Herself, sir? Did you question her as well?"

"I tell you we question everyone. Here is her deposition; can you read? Read it yourself, then."

Gerard glanced at Denys and read Manon's statement aloud.

"I am a native of Epinal. Two years ago I left my home due to misfortune; I could not tolerate the man they forced upon me. My father beat me, and I fled. I went into service, but left when the mistress became jealous of me. Her stated reason for dismissing me was my sauciness. Last year, I stood in the marketplace seeking employment alongside other girls. The landlord of 'The Fair Star' hired me. I remained with him for eleven months. A young man courted me, and I loved him. I discovered that travelers arrived and never departed again. I informed my lover. He commanded silence and threatened me. I learned my lover belonged to a band of thieves. When travelers were to be robbed, the landlord would leave and signal the band to emerge. Then I wept and prayed for the travelers' souls. I never spoke. A month ago, my lover died.

"The soldier reminded me of my lover; he was bearded just like the one I lost. I cannot say whether I should have intervened had he lacked a beard. I regret telling now."

The paper nearly slipped from Gerard's grasp. For the first time, he perceived that Manon's life hung by a thread. Knowing the rigid laws and the relentless men who enforced them, he threw himself suddenly to his knees before the alderman. "Oh, sir! Consider the difference between those cruel men and this poor, weak woman! Could you have the heart to send her to the same death as them? Could you condemn us to watch her slaughter, someone who, risking her life for ours, is now in peril? Alas, sir! Show me and my companion some pity, if you have none for her, poor soul. Denys and I are true men, and you will rend our hearts if you kill that innocent, simple girl. What can we do? What remains for us but to cut our throats at the foot of her gallows?"

The alderman was tough yet mortal; Gerard's prayers and agitation first astonished, then touched him. He displayed this in a peculiar manner, becoming peevish and fretful. "There, rise, do," he said. "I doubt anyone would speak so many words for me. Ho, Daniel! Fetch the town clerk."

As the functionary entered from an adjacent room, the alderman continued, "Here is a foolish lad fretting about that girl. Can we stretch a point? Shall we admit her to bear witness and question her favorably?"

The town clerk was a man of strict impossibilities.

"Nay, sir, we cannot do that; she was not involved in this affair. Had she been an accessory, we might have offered a pardon in exchange for her testimony."

Gerard burst in, "But she did better! Instead of being an accessory, she stayed the crime; she volunteered as a witness by rushing here with the tale."

"Tush, young man, 'tis a matter of law." The alderman and the clerk engaged in a lengthy debate, the one insisting, the other denying, that she stood legally as sound as if she had been an accomplice to the attempted robbery of the travelers. Fortunately for Manon, the alderman, irritated by the clerk's repetition of his inability to act, declared, "I will show him I can do whatever I choose."

Consequently, he released Manon, securing her release through the landlord of "The White Hart" acting as her bondsman, and Denys depositing five gold pieces. The girl promised, though requiring some coaxing from Denys, to appear as a witness. The alderman liberated her but eased his conscience by explaining his leniency in his own terms: "The town must buy a new rope for every execution and present it to the executioner, or compensate him in money; in his estimation, she was not worth this municipal expense, whereas determined characters like her late confederates certainly were."

Thus, Denys and Gerard escorted her away. Gerard danced around her in joy, while Denys bolstered her spirits by assuring her of the removal of a troublesome figurehead. Yet, she wept ominously. On the road to "The White Hart," the public discovered her story through the archers, who naturally recounted it passionately in her favor. Following her with cheers and encouragement, the crowd swelled until, backed by numbers, she regained her courage. The landlord of the inn, seeing at a glance that her presence would attract custom, received her politely and assigned her an upper chamber. There, she secluded herself, and being alone, she poured out her tears anew.

Poor little mind, it was like a ripple, up and down, down and up, endlessly oscillating. Bidding the landlord treat her kindly while keeping her effectively imprisoned without her realizing it, the friends departed. Lo! As they stepped onto the street, they witnessed two processions approaching from opposite directions. One was large, accompanied by noise, howls, and those indescribable cries by which rough natures occasionally reveal a kinship to the beasts of the field and forest—a connection usually hidden. The other procession was sparse, attended by a few nuns and friars, moving slowly and silently.

These were the prisoners heading to exposure in the marketplace, and the gathered bones of the victims being transported to the churchyard.

The two met in the narrow street nearly at the inn door, unable to pass each other for a considerable time. The bier bearing the relics of mortality became wedged against the cart carrying the men who had turned those bones into what they were, destined within hours to meet the same fate themselves. The mob lacked the quick wit to immediately grasp the grim irony of this encounter. Finally, a woman cried out, "Look at your work, ye dogs!" The crowd seized upon this like wildfire, erupting in a horrific yell. The culprits groaned, attempting to hide their heads on their chests, but failed, their hands bound. They stood there, images of pale, hollow-eyed despair, gazing longingly at the bier and envying those they had sent ahead on the dark path they were now forced to tread.
