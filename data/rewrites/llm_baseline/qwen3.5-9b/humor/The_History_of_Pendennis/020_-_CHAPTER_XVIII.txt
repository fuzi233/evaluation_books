Every man, no matter how short or forgettable his time at university may have been, retains a special, tender spot in his heart for his old academic chums and those golden days. Life is just kicking off for the young man; the baby ropes are finally cut, and he's tasting the dizzying, brand-new flavors of total freedom. He hasn't met "tomorrow" yet, nor has he encountered worry, sickness, mischief, pennilessness, or crushing disappointments. The show hasn't been played enough times to get boring. Sure, the hangover you get later on tastes stale and bitter when you're stuck doing it over and over, but oh, how clear and sparkling that first sip of fun really was! How the lad lunges for the cup and gulps it down with wild, desperate eagerness!

Even old foodies who've been kicked out of the kitchen and are surviving on boiled eggs and tap water love watching people with big appetites. Besides, if seeing yourself in a comedy isn't possible, seeing your kids enjoy theirs is the next best thing. So, I'm hoping there isn't a single age or amount of wisdom a human can reach without still feeling a twinge of joy when looking at happy youth.

A few weeks ago, I returned from a quick trip to Oxford and Cambridge, where my friend Arthur Pendennis spent some time. I traveled by train alongside a young guy currently studying at Saint Boniface. He'd managed to sneak out early ("get an exeat") and was determined to spend a day chasing thrills in London. From the moment we boarded until we dropped off (which was way too soon for me—I never tire of listening to an honest kid's jokes and bright laughter), he wouldn't stop rattling on. At the station, nothing satisfied him but a hansom cab so he could rush into town and dive straight into whatever fun awaited him. Off he went, spinning away with joy lighting up his honest face. As for me, carrying only a small carpet-bag, I climbed onto the outside of the omnibus and sat quite happily sandwiched between a Jewish peddler puffing terrible cigars and a gentleman's servant guarding a poodle. Once we gathered our full load of passengers and boxes, the driver took it slow. We weren't in a rush to hit the smoky Babylon of the city, nor did we plan on dining at the Club or dancing at the Casino that night. Yet, in a few years, my young railway buddy won't be any less eager.

There were no trains when Arthur Pendennis attended the famous University of Oxbridge. Instead, he rode in a well-furnished coach, packed tight with dons, gown-wearing students, fresh freshmen ready to arrive, and their guardians escorting them. Inside sat a portly old gentleman in grey stockings from the City, traveling with his pale-faced son. They were terrified when they learned the coach had been driven for several miles by young Mr. Foker of Saint Boniface. This fellow was friends with everyone, including drivers, and could handle a whip as well as the legendary Tom Hicks. Meanwhile, Pen sat on the roof, inspecting the coach, the crowd, and the countryside with wide-eyed delight. His heart skipped a beat as the great university appeared, revealing a magnificent view of ancient towers, spires, towering elms, and the gleaming river.

Before leaving, Pen had stayed a few days with his uncle at the Major's lodgings in Bury Street. The Major felt the lad needed new clothes, and Arthur couldn't resist any plan involving fresh coats and waistcoats. His self-sacrificing uncle spared no expense for the youth. London felt terribly empty. The Pall Mall pavement was deserted; even the red-coated guards had left town. Scarcely a face could be seen in the club windows. The Major escorted his nephew into a couple of these ghostly mansions and signed his name on the candidate list of one. Arthur's delight at this gesture from his guardian was overwhelming. He read his name and title in the parchment ledger—"Arthur Pendennis, Esquire, of Fairoaks Lodge"—with a thrill of intense satisfaction.

"You'll be eligible for election in about three years, by which time you'll have your degree," the guardian noted. Pen wished those three years would vanish instantly, staring at the stucco halls, vast libraries, and drawing rooms as if they were already his private property. The Major chuckled softly at the pompous air the simple young man adopted as he strutted out of the building.

Later, the Major and Foker drove down to the Grey Friars in the latter's cab to renew old friendships. Boys crowded around the cab as it waited by the gates, admiring the chestnut horse and the impressive uniform of Stoopid, the tiger. Just as they were strutting about the playground chatting with old cronies, the bell for afternoon school rang. The terrifying Doctor marched into the school holding his grammar book. Foker slunk away nervously at his approach, but Pen stepped forward, blushing, to shake the dignitary's hand. He laughed thinking of how that same Latin Grammar had boxed his ears countless times in the past. Generous, good-natured, and utterly conceited, Pen was completely satisfied with himself.

Next, they headed to the parental brewery. Foker's Entire was built on an enormous site near the Grey Friars, with the firm's name gilded on countless pub signs throughout the neighborhood. The venerable junior partner treated the young lord of the vats and his friend to honor, serving them silver flagons of brown stout so potent that it seemed the young men, and even the horse Mr. Harry Foker was driving, were affected. Stoopid galloped home at a breakneck pace, endangering pie stalls, colliding with crossing women, and slamming cab steps against street posts, causing the horse to swing wildly on his boards.

The Major was delighted to see Pen with his young acquaintance. He listened intently to Mr. Foker's straightforward stories, treated the two boys to a fine dinner at a Covent Garden coffee house, and took them to the theater. But he was happiest when Mr. and Lady Agnes Foker, visiting London, invited the Major and Arthur Pendennis to dinner at Grosvenor Street.

"Once you gain entry into Lady Agnes Foker's house," he told Pen with affectionate seriousness, fitting the gravity of the moment, "you must guard it. Never neglect calling on Grosvenor Street when you return to London. I advise you to study Debrett thoroughly regarding the alliances and genealogy of the Earls of Rosherville. If you can, make some small, neat, complimentary historical references to the family—something a poet like you can handle easily. Mr. Foker himself is a decent man, though not of high birth or great education. He insists on serving some of the family porter after dinner; you must never refuse it, and neither will I, though all beer disagrees with me terribly." True to his word, the heroic martyr sacrificed himself on the day of the dinner. When the worthy old gentleman made his traditional joke about Foker's Entire, the Major grinned broadly.

Lady Agnes, wrapped entirely around her son Harry, was the kindest of mothers and one of the most good-natured (if not wise) women. She welcomed her son's friend warmly and surprised Pen by detailing the rigorous studies her darling boy was undertaking, fearing they might harm his delicate health. Foker the Elder burst into loud laughter at some of these remarks, while the heir winked knowingly at his friend. Lady Agnes then recounted her son's history from infancy, listing his miraculous escapes from measles, hooping-cough, and drowning, and the brutal treatment he endured at the dreadful school Mr. Foker had chosen for him simply because he'd attended it himself. She vowed never to forgive that disagreeable doctor. After an hour of non-stop chatter about her son, she declared the two Messieurs Pendennis delightful company. When pheasants arrived with the second course—which the Major correctly identified as coming from Logwood—she hoped they would visit her there at Christmas or during Harry's vacation.

"God bless you, my dear boy," Pendennis whispered to Arthur as they lit candles in Bury Street to retire. "That little nod to Agincourt, where a Rosherville hero shone, was very neat, even if Lady Agnes didn't fully grasp it. Excellent for a beginner—but don't blush so. Remember this forever: with a good introduction ('entree'), securing good society is just as easy as avoiding bad, and keeping your footing in the best London houses costs no more effort than dining with a lawyer in Bedford Square. Keep this in mind while you study at Oxbridge, and be extremely careful about the friends you make. The first step in life is the most crucial. Did you write to your mother today? No? Well, do it before you go, and ask Mr. Foker for a frank stamp—they like them. Goodnight. God bless you."

Pen wrote a humorous account of his London adventures, the play, the visit to the Friars, the brewery, and the party at Mr. Foker's to his dearest mother, who was praying alone in the lonely house at Fairoaks, her heart overflowing with unspoken love for her boy. She and Laura read those letters, and the ones that followed, many times, brooding over them as women do. It was the first step in life for Pen. Ah! What a perilous journey it is, and how even the bravest can stumble and the strongest fail. Fellow traveler, may you find a kind arm to support yours and a friendly hand to help those who fall.
