Upon the precipice of my exile, I stood alone against the chill of a March night in eighteen-hundred-and-eighty-six, the Hudson River sprawling below like a spectral corpse, grey and silent, severed from the pulse of life. Yet, even here, amidst the desolation of terrestrial winter, the magnetic pull of Mars—the Red God of War, my beloved sovereign—constricted my soul with a familiar, terrible intimacy. For ten long years, I had stretched my arms toward the void, imploring the crimson star to reclaim me, to drag me back to the woman whose loss had hollowed my existence.

It was a sensation identical to that other March night in eighteen-hundred-and-sixty-six, when I had knelt before the Arizona cave, my body wrapped in the shroud of simulated death, feeling the irresistible, gravitational tug of the deity of my profession. With arms flung wide toward the burning eye of the Great Star, I invoked the return of that supernatural potency which had twice before torn me through the infinite abyss of space. I prayed as I had prayed a thousand times during the decade of my waiting, my lips moving in a silent liturgy of desperation.

Then, a wave of nausea, visceral and crushing, seized me. My vision blurred into a kaleidoscope of gray, my knees buckled, and I pitched forward, crashing onto the very edge of the dizzying bluff.

Instantly, clarity returned, slicing through the fog like a laser. The horrific tableau of the Arizona cave flooded my memory with renewed, agonizing vividness. Just as on that distant night, my muscles betrayed my will, paralyzed by a reflexive terror. Even upon the banks of the placid Hudson, I could hear the phantom moans and the rustling of the fearsome entity that had lurked in the cave's dark recesses. Summoning a strength that defied mortal limits, I fought the bonds of this strange anesthesia. Again, the sharp *click* resonated—a sound like a taut wire snapping—and I stood naked and free, trembling beside the lifeless husk of the thing that had recently pulsed with the warm, red blood of John Carter.

With barely a glance backward, I turned my gaze to Mars, lifting my hands to catch his lurid rays, and waited.

I did not have to wait long. Scarcely had I turned when I was hurled with the velocity of thought into the awful void before me. There followed that same instant of unthinkable cold and absolute darkness I had endured twenty years prior, and then, abruptly, I opened my eyes in a new world, bathed in the searing heat of a sun that poured through a tiny aperture in the canopy of a mighty forest.

The scene that greeted me was so profoundly non-Martian that my heart hammered against my ribs, a sudden, primal fear seizing me that I had been aimlessly cast upon some alien sphere by a cruel fate. Why not? What compass had guided me through the trackless wastes of interplanetary space? Was there no guarantee that I might not have been hurled to some distant star of another solar system rather than to Mars?

I lay upon a carpet of close-cropped, red grass-like vegetation, surrounded by a grove of strange and breathtaking trees, draped in huge, gorgeous blossoms and teeming with brilliant, voiceless avians. I termed them birds because they possessed wings, though no mortal eye had ever rested upon such odd, unearthly forms. The flora bore a resemblance to the lawns of the great waterways of the red Martians, yet the trees and birds were unlike anything I had witnessed on Barsoom. Through the deeper layers of the woods, I glimpsed the most un-Martian sight of all: an open sea, its azure waters shimmering beneath the brazen glare of the sun.

As I attempted to rise to investigate, I suffered the same ridiculous catastrophe that had plagued my first attempt to walk under Martian conditions. The lesser gravitational attraction of this smaller planet, combined with the rarified density of its atmosphere, offered negligible resistance to my earthly musculature. The mere exertion of rising sent me soaring several feet into the air before I crashed face-first into the soft, brilliant grass of this strange world.

Yet, this stumble provided a sliver of reassurance. Perhaps I was indeed in an unknown corner of Mars, a possibility heightened by the fact that during my ten-year residency, I had explored but a fraction of the planet's vast expanse. Rising again, laughing at my own forgetfulness, I soon mastered the art of attuning my terrestrial sinews to these altered physical laws.

Walking slowly down the imperceptible slope toward the sea, I could not help but observe the park-like perfection of the landscape. The grass was clipped to a uniform, carpet-like precision reminiscent of an ancient English lawn, while the trees displayed evidence of meticulous pruning, capped at a consistent height of approximately fifteen feet. From a distance, the forest resembled a vast, high-ceilinged chamber, a testament to systematic cultivation. These signs convinced me that I had entered Mars through the domain of a civilized people, and that upon finding them, my rank as a Prince of the House of Tardos Mors would ensure me courtesy and protection.

My admiration for the forest deepened as I proceeded. Their colossal stems, some reaching a hundred feet in diameter, attested to prodigious heights I could only guess at, blocked from view by dense foliage that rose no higher than sixty or eighty feet. As far as my sight could penetrate, the trunks and branches were smooth and highly polished, akin to the finest American pianos. Some timber glowed black as ebony; others gleamed white as fine china; still others burned in hues of azure, scarlet, yellow, or deepest purple. The foliage matched this chromatic riot, and the blooms clustering thick upon the branches defied description in any earthly tongue, challenging the very lexicon of the gods.

As I neared the forest's confines, a broad expanse of meadowland revealed itself between the grove and the open sea. Emerging from the shadows, a sight banished all romantic reflection upon the landscape's beauty. To my left, the sea stretched to the horizon; ahead, a vague, dim line marked its further shore; and to my right, a mighty river, broad, placid, and majestic, flowed between scarlet banks to empty into the quiet sea. At a distance upriver, perpendicular bluffs rose from the very base of the water, appearing as if the river itself erupted from the cliff's foundation.

Yet, it was not these inspiring evidences of Nature's grandeur that captured my immediate attention. It was the sight of a dozen figures moving slowly along the meadow near the riverbank. Odd, grotesque shapes they were; unlike anything I had ever seen on Mars, yet, from afar, possessing a disturbingly human silhouette. The larger specimens stood ten or twelve feet tall, proportioned in torso and lower extremities precisely as earthly man. However, their arms were abominably short, resembling elephant trunks, moving in sinuous, snakelike undulations devoid of visible bone structure, suggesting a vertebral composition hidden beneath skin.

Watching from behind the stem of a giant tree, one of the creatures advanced, engaged in the principal occupation of its kind: running its oddly shaped hands over the surface of the sward, the purpose of which remained inscrutable. As it drew closer, I obtained a perfect view, and though I would later learn more of its kin, I can say that this single cursory examination of this awful travesty of Nature would have been sufficient to drive any sane being mad. The fastest flyer of the Heliumetic Navy could not have carried me far enough from this hideous monstrosity.

Its hairless body was a ghastly, strange blue, save for a broad band of white encircling its protruding, solitary eye—an orb of pure, dead white, pupil, iris, and ball indistinguishable. Its nose was a ragged, inflamed, circular hole in the center of its blank face, resembling nothing so much as a fresh bullet wound that had not yet begun to bleed. Below this repulsive orifice, the face was utterly blank to the chin; the creature possessed no mouth that I could discern.

The head, excluding the face, was crowned by a tangled mass of jet-black hair, eight or ten inches in length. Each strand was as thick as a large angleworm, and as the creature moved, the muscles of its scalp caused this awful covering to writhe, wriggle, and crawl about the fearsome visage, as if each separate hair possessed independent life. The body and legs were symmetrically human, as Nature could fashion them, and the feet, too, were human in shape but of monstrous proportions. From heel to toe, they spanned three feet, flat and broad.

As it approached, I realized its strange movements—running its odd hands over the turf—were the result of a peculiar feeding method. It cropped off the tender vegetation with razorlike talons and sucked it up from two mouths located in the palms of its hands, drawing sustenance through arm-like throats. In addition to these features, the beast sported a massive tail, six feet in length, round where it joined the body but tapering to a flat, thin blade at the end, trailing at right angles to the ground.

By far the most remarkable attribute of this creature were the two tiny replicas, each about six inches long, dangling from its armpits—one on either side. Suspended by a small stem growing from the top of their heads to connect with the adult's body, they hung motionless. Whether they were offspring or merely appendages of a composite organism, I could not determine.
