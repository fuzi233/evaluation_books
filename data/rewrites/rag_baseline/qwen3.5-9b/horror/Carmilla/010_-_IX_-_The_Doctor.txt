The atmosphere within the castle shifted palpably once the decision was made to deny Carmilla the solitude of her chamber. My father, driven by a paternal instinct that bordered on paranoia, arranged for a servant to sleep directly outside her door, creating a sentinel barrier designed to intercept any further nocturnal excursions before they could culminate in catastrophe. That night passed in a silence so profound it felt heavy, pressing against the walls. By dawn, before the light had fully claimed the room, the doctor arrived—a summons issued by my father without so much as a whisper to me.

Madame escorted me to the library, where the physician awaited. He was a figure of stark gravity, his white hair stark against the dim light, spectacles perched firmly on his nose. I recounted the terrors of the previous nights, and as my voice faltered, his expression hardened into a mask of somber intensity. We stood in the deep recess of a window, framed by stone and shadow, facing one another. When my confession ended, he leaned back, his shoulders resting against the cold wall, his eyes locking onto mine with a gaze that burned with a mixture of clinical interest and primal horror. After a moment of silent contemplation, he signaled for my father.

When my father entered, his customary smile was already in place, a defensive shield against whatever dread lay ahead. "I dare say, doctor," he began, his tone light despite the gravity of the summons, "you are going to tell me that I am an old fool for having brought you here; I hope I am." But the smile died instantly, swallowed by the shadow of the doctor's stern countenance as he gestured imperiously. For some time, the two men engaged in a hushed, fervent debate within that same window niche. From afar, where Madame and I stood burning with anxious curiosity, we could see nothing but the faint outline of my father's arm and shoulder, obscured by the thick masonry. Their voices were reduced to indistinguishable whispers, muffled by the acoustic prison of the alcove.

Finally, my father's face emerged into the main room, transformed. It was pale, etched with thought, and undeniably agitated. "Laura, dear, come here for a moment," he commanded softly. "Madame, we shan't trouble you; the doctor requires privacy." Trembling with a weakness that defied reason—I felt drained, yet strangely lucid—I approached. My father extended his hand, but his gaze remained fixed on the physician. "It certainly is very odd," he admitted, his voice tight. "I don't understand it quite. Laura, listen to Doctor Spielsberg, and compose yourself."

"You mentioned a sensation," the doctor intoned, his voice devoid of warmth, "like two needles piercing the skin, somewhere about your neck, on the night of your first nightmare. Is there still any soreness?"

"None at all," I replied, trying to steady my breath.

"Can you indicate with your finger the precise point?"

"Very little below my throat—here," I answered, lifting my sleeve. I wore a morning dress that covered the area, but the fabric parted easily.

"Now you can satisfy yourself," the doctor said, his tone dropping to a conspiratorial urgency. "You won't mind your papa lowering your dress a very little. It is necessary to detect the symptom."

My father hesitated, then complied. The fabric fell just an inch or two below the collarbone. A collective gasp seemed to fill the room. "God bless me!" my father exclaimed, his face draining of color. "So it is."

"You see it now with your own eyes," the doctor declared, a grim triumph coloring his features.

"What is it?" I cried, panic rising in my chest.

"Nothing, my dear young lady," he lied smoothly, "but a small blue spot, about the size of the tip of your little finger." He turned sharply to my father. "The question is, what is best to be done? Is there any danger?"

"I urge you," I pleaded, my voice trembling, "to tell me if there is danger."

"I trust not, my dear," the doctor answered, his eyes darting between us. "I don't see why you should not recover. In fact, I see every reason for you to begin improving immediately. That is the exact point where the sense of strangulation begins, isn't it?"

"Yes," I whispered.

"And recall," he pressed, "that same point was the center of that thrill you described earlier, like the current of a cold stream running against you?"

"It may have been; I think it was."

"Ay, you see?" he muttered, addressing my father. "Shall I speak to Madame?"

"Certainly," my father said, his composure cracking. He summoned Madame forward. "I find my young friend here far from well. It won't be of great consequence, I hope, but immediate steps are required. For the present, Madame, you will ensure Miss Laura is not left alone for even a single moment. That is the only instruction I need give, and it is indispensable."

"We may rely upon your kindness, Madame, I know," my father added, his voice strained.

Madame nodded eagerly, her relief evident. "And you, dear Laura, I know you will obey the doctor's directions."

"I shall have to ask your opinion upon another patient," the doctor interjected, shifting tactics. "A young lady, our guest, whose symptoms slightly resemble those of my daughter—much milder in degree, but undoubtedly of the same sort. Since you are scheduled to pass this way again this evening, you cannot do better than take your supper here. You will see her then. She does not descend until the afternoon."

"I thank you," the doctor said curtly. "I shall be with you at about seven this evening."

With these final instructions delivered to both of us, my father departed. I watched him walk out with the doctor, their strides synchronized as they paced up and down the grassy platform between the road and the moat, completely absorbed in their urgent discourse. The doctor eventually mounted his horse, took his leave, and rode eastward through the forest, disappearing into the trees.

Almost simultaneously, a rider arrived from Dranfield bearing a bag of letters for my father. While the household buzzed with speculation regarding the doctor's ominous decree—that we feared a sudden seizure that could cost me my life—or perhaps, as Madame later suggested, a precautionary measure to curb youthful indiscretions like overexertion or unripe fruit, my father returned. He carried a letter in his hand.

"This letter had been delayed," he announced, his demeanor sour. "It is from General Spielsdorf. He might have been here yesterday, or tomorrow, or perhaps today." He thrust the open letter into my hands. Yet, there was no joy in his delivery. If anything, he looked as though he wished the General were lost at sea. Something weighed heavily on his mind, a secret he refused to divulge.

"Papa, darling, will you tell me this?" I implored, seizing his arm and looking up with wide, pleading eyes.

"Perhaps," he answered, smoothing my hair over my forehead with a gesture that felt increasingly mechanical. "Does the doctor think me very ill?"

"No, dear," he replied, his voice dry and brittle. "He thinks, if the right steps are taken, you will be quite well again, at least on the high road to a complete recovery, in a day or two."

"I wish our good friend, the General, had chosen any other time," he continued, avoiding my gaze. "That is, I wish you had been perfectly well to receive him."

"But do tell me, papa," I insisted, my frustration mounting. "What does he think is the matter with me?"

"Nothing," he snapped, his irritation flaring brighter than I had ever witnessed. "You must not plague me with questions." Seeing my wounded expression, he softened slightly, kissing my brow. "You shall know all about it in a day or two; that is, all that I know. In the meantime, you are not to trouble your head about it."

He turned to leave but returned moments later, his resolve hardening. "I am going to Karnstein," he stated abruptly. "I have ordered the carriage to be ready at twelve. You and Madame will accompany me. I am going to see the priest who lives near those picturesque grounds upon business. Since Carmilla has never seen them, she may follow when she comes down, accompanied by Mademoiselle, who will bring materials for what you call a picnic, which we shall lay out in the ruined castle."

At twelve o'clock, prepared and anxious, we set out. We crossed the drawbridge, turned right, and followed the road over the steep Gothic bridge, heading westward toward the deserted village and the looming silhouette of the ruined castle of Karnstein. No sylvan drive could be imagined more picturesque. The ground broke into gentle hills and hollows, draped in wild, untamed woods that lacked the stiff formality of cultivated gardens. The terrain forced the road to wind unpredictably, snaking around broken hollows and climbing the steep slopes of the hills, a path of natural irregularity.

As we rounded a bend, we suddenly encountered our old friend, the General, riding toward us, attended by a mounted servant. His portmanteaus trailed behind in a hired wagon. The General dismounted as we pulled up. After exchanging the usual greetings, he was easily persuaded to abandon his horse and accept the vacant seat in the carriage, sending his servant and the horses onward to the *schloss*.
