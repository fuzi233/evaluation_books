“Great was the crying, the running and riding, Which at that season was made in the place; The beacons were fired, as need then required, To save their great treasure they had little space.”

—Winning of Cales.

The crew would have lingered near Margarita and Cubagua, hoping for another pearl prize. But Amyas, having, as he put it, “fleshed his dogs,” was unwilling to tarry after the alarm had been raised. So they sailed south-west across the mouth of the vast bay stretching from the Peninsula of Paria to Cape Codera, leaving Tortuga to starboard and the low green islands of the Piritoos to port—mere lines of land barely above the tideless sea. Yeo and Drew knew every inch of this coast, having once attempted trade here with Hawkins, long before any Englishman had dared to tread these shores. Ahead, rising abruptly from the ocean like a wall of stone, loomed the towering Caracas mountains—so immense that even the hills most of the men had known seemed mere hillocks beside them. Frank had seen the Alps; Amyas, the Andes; but Cary’s idea of height was bounded by M’Gillicuddy’s Reeks, while Brimblecombe could not fathom such grandeur. He spent an entire day clinging to the rigging, staring upward with his chin higher than his nose, until he developed a stiff neck.

As they neared Cape Codera, the sea grew choppy despite a fair breeze. By the time they passed the cape, they felt the strong eastward current that, during winter months, often thwarted mariners seeking to sail west. All night, they battled through heavy swells, with the sheer cliff of Cape Codera towering a thousand feet above them to the left, and beyond it, layer upon layer of mountain peaks glowing under the yellow moonlight.

At dawn, they sighted a large ship that had passed them during the night, now ten miles to the east. Yeo insisted on returning to take her. It was feasible—the wind blew directly off the land, offering a "soldier's wind" for both ships. But Amyas and Frank hesitated.

“Why, Yeo,” said Amyas, “you said one more day would bring us to La Guayra.”

“Then all the more reason,” replied Yeo, “to do the Lord’s work thoroughly when He has brought us so far.”

“She can pass safely enough,” said Amyas. “No harm done.”

“Ah, sirs,” Yeo urged, “she is delivered into your hands. You will have to give account of her.”

“My good Yeo,” Frank interjected, “I trust we’ll give good account of many a Spaniard before we return. But you know well that La Guayra—and the salvation of one we believe dwells there—was our first purpose in this journey.”

Yeo shook his head sorrowfully. “Ah, sirs, a lady brought Captain Oxenham to ruin.”

“You do not dare compare her with this one?” Frank and Cary said together.

“God forbid, gentlemen,” Yeo replied. “But no venture prospers unless it is guided by a single eye to the Lord’s work—to weaken the Spaniard and exalt Her Majesty the Queen. And I thought nothing dearer to Captain Leigh’s heart than that.”

Amyas stood uncertain. His duty to the queen called him to pursue the Spanish vessel; his vow, to reach La Guayra. It may seem a paradox—but to him, it was real.

Yet Frank’s counsel prevailed. They pressed onward toward La Guayra. Amyas half hoped the Spaniards would spot them and attack. But they did not. Had he turned back, the tale might have ended very differently.

By midday, a canoe approached under a large triangular sail. Two Indians stood aboard.

“Metal floats in these seas,” quipped Cary. “A fresh marvel, Frank.”

“Expound,” said Frank, ever eager for wonders.

“How else could two bronze statues dare set out to sea in such a frail shell? Have I given you the dor now, master courtier?”

“I’m past dors, Will. But what noble creatures! And how boldly they come alongside! Could they know we’re English—avengers of the Indians?”

“I suspect they think we’re Spaniards,” Cary said, “and want to sell coconuts. See—her canoe is full of vegetables.”

“Hail them, Yeo!” Amyas ordered. “You speak Spanish best. I want speech with one.”

Yeo called out. The canoe ran alongside, lowered its sail, and a tall, graceful Indian leapt aboard like a cat.

He stood nearly six feet tall, bold and dignified as Frank or Amyas himself. At first he smiled, showing white teeth. Then his face changed. Springing to the side, he shouted in Spanish:

“Treachery! Not a Spaniard!”

He tried to leap overboard, but a dozen men seized him.

It took some effort to subdue him—his limbs were strong and slippery. Amyas urged the men not to harm him, and the Indian to calm down. After five minutes of chaos, the stranger yielded sullenly.

“Don’t bind him,” Amyas said. “Let him loose. Form a ring around him. Now, my man—here’s a dollar.”

The Indian’s eyes lit up. He took the coin.

“All I ask,” Amyas said, “is first, to know what ships are in La Guayra, and second, to go with me there and show me the governor’s house and the custom-house.”

The Indian placed the coin on deck, crossed himself, and looked Amyas straight in the eye.

“No, senor. I am a freeman, a cavalier, a Christian Guayqueria. My ancestors, the first of all Indians, swore fealty to the King of Spain. He calls us still his most faithful, loyal, noble Guayquerias. God forbid I should betray them to their enemies—my enemies too.”

A growl rose from those who understood. One muttered that a rope round the head or a match between the fingers would soon extract truth.

“God forbid!” Amyas said. “A brave and loyal man—he shall be treated as such. Tell me, noble fellow—how do you know we are the king’s enemies?”

The Indian grinned shrewdly, pointing to various objects: “Not Spanish.”

“Well?” asked Amyas.

“Only Spaniards and free Guayquerias have right to sail these waters.”

Amyas laughed. “Thou art a valiant bit of copper. Take thy dollar and go in peace. Make room for him, men. We can learn what we need without him.”

The Indian paused, stunned. “Overboard with you!” Amyas said. “Don’t you know when you’re well off?”

“Most illustrious senor,” the Indian began, in the slow, deliberate manner of his people, “I have been deceived. I heard you heretics roast and eat true Catholics—as we Guayquerias are—and that your padres have tails.”

“Plague on you, sirrah!” Jack Brimblecombe squeaked. “Have I a tail? Look here!”

“Quien sabe? Who knows?” the Indian sniffed.

“How do you know we’re heretics?” Amyas asked.

“Humph. But in repayment for your kindness, I warn you, illustrious senor—not to go to La Guayra. Warships wait there. And yesterday, Don Guzman sailed east to seek you. I wonder you did not meet him.”

“To seek us? On the watch for us?” Cary scoffed. “Impossible! Lies! Amyas, this is a trick to frighten us away.”

“Don Guzman sailed only yesterday?” Amyas asked sharply.

“As I live, senor. He and another ship—I mistook yours for that.”

Amyas stamped on deck. That must have been the ship they’d passed!

“Fool that I was,” he muttered, “to let my chance slip! If I’d done my duty, all would have gone right!”

But it was too late. Still, the Indian’s tale might be false.

“Off with you!” Amyas said. The Indian leapt back into his canoe, leaving the crew amazed at his nobility and grace.

They sailed on beneath the mighty northern wall—the highest cliff on earth, seven thousand feet high, separated from the sea by a narrow strip of bright green lowland. Sugar cane and coconut palms marked the tropics, but above, the cliffs were bare and savage, like Alpine precipices. Deep clefts allowed sunlight to pour down, revealing strange trees and perpetual mist veiling the summits. Clouds hung motionless in the heat, deepening the sense of vastness and solemn stillness.

“Within those mountains,” Drew said, “three thousand feet above us, lies Saint Yago de Leon—the city the Spaniards founded fifteen years ago.”

“Is it rich?” asked Cary.

“Very, they say.”

“Strong?” asked Amyas.

“No forts, they say. The Spaniards boast Heaven built them walls enough—no man needs to build more.”

“I don’t know,” Amyas mused. “Lads—could you climb those hills?”

“Higher than Harty Point, sir,” came the reply. “But it depends on what’s behind.”

Now the final point rounded. Before them lay La Guayra—the destination of their four-thousand-mile voyage. A black cliff crowned with a wall, batteries at each end. Inside, narrow white streets ran parallel to the sea, on a strip of land less than two hundred yards wide. Behind, the mountain wall cast deep shadow. How one ascended into the interior remained a mystery—until Drew pointed out a narrow path winding up a glen, seemingly perpendicular. That was the road to the capital—if anyone dared.

Though shaded, the town looked dusty and glaring. The air coming off the land was stifling. No wonder—La Guayra, heated by its massive rock, was one of the hottest places on earth.

Where was the harbor? There was none. Only an open roadstead, where five vessels tossed at anchor. Two small merchant caravels sat outside. Behind them, two long, low, ugly galleys. Yeo whistled.

“Galleys, by my soul! And what’s that big one inside? Robert Drew—she’s got more than hawseholes in her black sides, I think.”

“We’ll see her astern of the galleys in a moment,” Amyas said. “Look out, Cary—your eyes are better than mine.”

“Six round portholes on the main deck,” Will observed.

“And I see brass patararoes glittering on her poop,” Amyas added. “We’re in for it.”

“In for it we are, captain.”

“Farewell, farewell, my parents dear. I never shall see you more, I fear.”

“Let’s go in, nevertheless, and pound the Don’s ribs, my old lad of Smerwick. Eh? Three to one is fair odds.”

“Not underneath those fort guns,” Yeo cautioned. “If the Philistines will come out unto us, we’ll make them like Zeba and Zalmunna.”

“Quite true,” Amyas agreed. “Game cocks are game cocks—but reason’s reason.”

“If the Philistines won’t come out,” Cary said, “they’ll send a messenger instead.” He pointed. “Look out, all thin skulls!”

A puff of smoke burst from the eastern fort. A cannonball splashed into the water between fort and ship.

“I don’t altogether like this,” Amyas said. “Why fire without warning? What are these warships doing here? Drew—you said the armadas never came here.”

“They don’t, sir,” Drew replied. “The anchorage is too bad. I’m afraid that rascal told the truth—the Dons have learned of our coming.”

“Run up a white flag, at least,” Amyas said. “If they expect us, they’ve known for days. How else could they have gotten ships here?”

“True,” Drew said. “They must have come from Santa Marta—or Cartagena. That would take a month.”

Amyas suddenly remembered Eustace’s threat in the inn. Could he have betrayed them? Impossible.

“Let’s hold a council,” Amyas said to Frank.

But Frank was lost in thought. Half a mile east of the town, perched three hundred feet up the mountainside, stood a large, low, white house surrounded by trees and gardens. No other house like it existed nearby. And wasn’t that the Spanish royal flag flying before it? It must be the governor’s house. It must be the home of the Rose of Torridge! Frank stared wildly, convinced he saw a woman’s figure on the terrace—her own.

Amyas struggled to pull him away from the view. The council was grim, though not unkind.

The three adventurers—Amyas, Frank, and Cary—gathered with Brimblecombe, Yeo, and Drew on the poop. Each met the others’ eyes.

“What are we to do?” Amyas asked at last. “Months of planning—ruined in an hour.”

“It’s impossible,” he said, “to surprise the town by land while those ships are here. If we land men, our ship is defenseless.”

“As impossible as challenging Don Guzman while he’s not here,” Cary said.

“I wonder why they haven’t opened fire yet,” Drew said.

“Perhaps they respect our flag of truce,” Cary guessed.

“Or perhaps they don’t care,” Yeo said. “Trust them not. They kept no faith with Captain Hawkins at San Juan d’Ulloa. We could have taken the plate fleet—two hundred thousand pounds of gold—and didn’t. We asked only to trade. They granted license, lured us ashore, then attacked five to one—no mercy. No, sir. They’ll attack only when their crews are aboard.”

“They’ll be soon enough,” Amyas said. “I see soldiers descending the landing stairs.”

Indeed, boats filled with armed men pushed off toward the warships.

“We may thank Heaven,” Drew said, “we weren’t here two hours ago. The sun will set before they’re ready. They won’t hunt us at night.”

“So much the worse for us,” Amyas said. “If they stay, we may slip past and try again. I cannot leave without one dash.”

Yeo shook his head. “There are other towns along the coast—more worth trying. But Heaven’s will be done.”

As they spoke, the sun sank into the sea. Darkness fell.

At last, they agreed to anchor and wait till midnight. If the warships left, they would attempt to run in past them, land west of the town, take it in flank, plunder the government storehouses near the shore, fight back to their boats, and escape. Two hours would suffice if the armada cleared.

Amyas called the men forward, explained the plan. It was not welcomed cheerfully—but what else could they do?

They sailed a mile and a half west, anchored.

Night wore on. No movement among the ships. Their lights—easily identified by height—remained fixed. The men fretted, seeing a prize within reach, yet unreachable.

Let Amyas and his men have patience. In five short years, the Great Armada would come and go. Then the storm—of which they, like Oxenham, Hawkins, and Drake, were the vanguard—would break upon every Spanish port, from Corunna to Cadiz, from the Canaries to Havana. La Guayra and St. Yago de Leon would not escape.

Captain Amyas Preston and Captain Sommers, colonist of the Bermudas, would land with a tiny force—larger than Leigh’s, yet where Leigh dared not go. They would seize the fort of Guayra, find, as Leigh had, that their arrival was expected. The Pass of the Venta, three thousand feet high, would be fortified with barricades, abattis, and cannons, making the capital impregnable—except to Englishmen or Zouaves.

Up that seven-thousand-foot precipice, stair upon stair behind the town, those fierce adventurers would climb hand over hand through rain and fog, while men begged their officers to kill them—they could go no farther. Yet they would go, cutting paths through wild plantains and rhododendron thickets, over the saddle of the Silla,* down upon the astonished “Mantuanos” of St. Jago, driving all before them. They would burn the city in default of ransom, return triumphantly by the proper route, and sail along the coast—masters of the sea.

* Humboldt notes a smuggler’s path between the peaks from Caravellada to St. Jago. This may be the “unknowen way of the Indians” Preston used.

I know not if any men still live descended from those two captains. But if they do, let them remember: the history of the English navy tells no greater feat of courage and endurance than that forgotten raid of Amyas Preston and his comrade in 1595.

Yet though the town was beyond reach, another venture stirred in Frank’s heart. A light burned brightly in one window of the governor’s house—the lodestar of his soul. Sitting in the cabin with Amyas, Cary, and Jack, he opened his heart.

“And are we,” he asked mournfully, “to go without doing the very thing we came for?”

Silence. Then John Brimblecombe spoke.

“Show me the way, Mr. Frank, and I’ll go.”

“My dearest man,” Amyas said, “what would you have? Any attempt to see her—though she be here—would mean certain death.”

“And what if it were?” Frank replied. “What if it were, my brother Amyas? Listen. I have long ceased to fear Death. But until I came to these magic lands, I never knew the beauty of his face.”

“Of death?” Cary asked. “I should say of life. God forgive me—but man might wish to live forever in such a world.”

“And do you forget,” Frank said, “that the more beautiful this passing world, the more glorious the eternal one—where all here is but shadow and dream? Where the four mystic beasts stand before His throne, crying, ‘Holy, holy, holy, Lord God of hosts!’ He has made all things—for His pleasure. I have done with shadows. It is better to depart and be with Him, where there is no desire, no anger, no pretense—only eternal truth. One thing remains before I die—God has laid it on me. Let it be done tonight. Then, farewell!”

“Frank! Frank! Remember our mother!”

“I do remember her. I’ve spoken of this with her many times. She sent me forth with my virgin honor, as the Spartan mother sent her son with the shield—‘Return with this, or upon it.’ I must do one or the other—this life or the next. But hear me: my life is God’s. I promise not to cast it away rashly.”

“What would you do?”

“Go to that house, Amyas, and speak with her—if Heaven grants me the chance. And I feel sure it will.”

“And do you call that no rashness?”

“Is any duty rashness? Is it rash to stand amid bullets when your queen sends you? Or to seek Christ’s lost lamb when God and your oath send you? John Brimblecombe answered that long ago.”

“If you go,” the others said in unison, “I go with you!”

“No,” Frank said. “Amyas, you owe duty to our mother and your ship. Cary, you inherit estates—bound to country and tenants. John Brimblecombe—”

“Ay!” Jack squeaked. “And what have you to say, Mr. Frank, against my going? I have neither ship nor estate—only that I am not worthy to travel in such company?”

“Think of your parents, John—your sisters.”

“I thought of them before I started,” Jack said. “I came to keep my vow. I will not turn renegade at the foot of the cross.”

“Someone must go with you, Frank,” Amyas said. “In case—” He faltered.

“In case I fall,” Frank said, smiling. “I’ll finish your sentence. I’m not afraid. But someone must go. Unhappy me—that I cannot risk my worthless life without risking yours!”

“Not so, Mr. Frank!” Jack declared. “Your oath is ours. Your duty is ours! We three will draw lots for the honor of going with you.”

“Lots?” Amyas asked. “I don’t like leaving such matters to chance, friend John.”

“Chance, sir?” Jack replied. “When you’ve used all your wit and it fails, isn’t drawing lots simply handing the matter from your weak hands to God’s strong ones?”
