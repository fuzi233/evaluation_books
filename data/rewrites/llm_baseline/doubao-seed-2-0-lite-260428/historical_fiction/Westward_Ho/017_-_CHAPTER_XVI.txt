Let us climb aboard a small wherry at Whitehall Stairs, just as Amyas Leigh did, and slip downstream ahead of him, gliding beneath the weathered arches of old London Bridge, all the way to Deptford Creek. There, preserved almost as if embalmed in the Thames mist, rests the famous *Pelican* — the stout little ship in which Francis Drake had sailed clean around the whole circumference of the world. She sits drawn high and dry on the reed-fringed riverbank, like a grizzled old veteran finally resting after decades of hard campaigning. Nailed to her mainmast are verses and epigrams honouring both the vessel and her captain, three of them penned by a scholar of Winchester College that the historian Camden later recorded in his chronicles. Queen Elizabeth herself had consecrated the ship solemnly, feasted aboard her decks, and there and then bestowed the honour of knighthood on Drake. On that very day, the plank bridge the royal party had used to board her collapsed under the press of cheering onlookers, pitching a hundred men into the mud below — yet not a single soul was harmed. It was said ever after that the *Pelican* had been born under a lucky, protective star.

For years now she has stood there as a public curiosity, and a favourite dining hall for jovial groups of London citizens. This afternoon, it is clear a grand feast is underway: bright banners flutter from her masts, the roar of revelry and the rich scent of spiced roasted meat drift from the hatches that once served as gun ports, and white-aproned waiters from the neighbouring Pelican Inn rush back and forth across that famous lucky plank bridge, alongside liveried serving men bearing the City of London’s badge. The Lord Mayor himself is hosting a feast for a party of gentlemen from the Earl of Leicester’s household, all of them invested in new overseas discovery — and what possible venue could be more fitting than Drake’s own circumnavigating ship?

Look around at the guests gathered at that long table: you will seldom see a nobler, bolder company in one room. And if you are American, pause to look long at their faces and hold them in reverence, for their wisdom, their daring, and their sacrifices laid the very foundations of your mighty nation.

At the head of the table sits the Lord Mayor, whom every reader will recognise at once: Sir Edward Osborne, the famous cloth worker, ancestor to the Dukes of Leeds, whose romantic story of daring is still told by every fireside. He has aged little in the decades since he leapt from a London Bridge window into the roaring, frothing tide below to rescue the infant who would grow up to be his wife. The quick, chivalrous courage of that young apprentice has hardened into the thoughtful, steady daring of a wealthy merchant adventurer. Beside him sits the Earl of Cumberland on his right, and Walter Raleigh on his left, the three of them talking low of the vast, wealthy undiscovered lands that are rumoured to stretch between Florida and the mouth of the great Canadian river. Raleigh’s half-scientific speculations, full of quotations from the learned alchemist Doctor Dee, carry far more weight with Cumberland (who has backed dozens of foreign adventures, and failed at all of them, simply because he never sailed on any himself, sending other men in his stead) than they do with the practical Osborne. Raleigh soon turns to draw the quiet Oxford scholar at his left hand into the conversation: Richard Hakluyt, the great collector of voyage narratives. But Hakluyt is deep in talk with a white-bearded old elder whose beard falls nearly to his waist, whose face is crisscrossed with the lines of a thousand gales: Anthony Jenkinson, the legendary Asiatic traveller, who is regaling him with tales of reindeer sleds streaking across Siberian steppes, and the fossil ivory the Tungus people dig out of Arctic sea ice cliffs, plain proof of Noah’s flood. Next to them sits Christopher Carlile, Walsingham’s son-in-law (as Philip Sidney also is now), a valiant captain who would later lead the land forces in Drake’s triumphant 1585 West Indian raid, where he would share a very fine bottle of wine with the Bishop of Carthagena himself. He is deep in conversation with Alderman Hart the grocer, Sheriff Spencer the cloth worker, Amyas’s own merchant cousin Charles Leigh, the Mayor of Bristol Aldworth, and William Salterne — alderman of Bristol, and cousin to the Rose Salterne the Leighs know in Bideford. For these men, alongside Secretary Walsingham, have spent two years pooling funds to back Humphrey and Adrian Gilbert’s grand North-West Passage expeditions, ventures Carlile had once planned to sail on himself, before circumstances (the exact reason no one ever uncovered) kept him ashore.

Across the table sits a group no less fascinating. Martin Frobisher and John Davis, the two trailblazers of the North-West Passage, are chatting with Alderman Sanderson, the great geographer and maker of globes, alongside Mr. Towerson, Sir Gilbert Peckham, their old acquaintance Captain John Winter — and last, but far from least, Philip Sidney himself. With his usual generous courtesy, Sidney has given up his rightful place near the head of the table so he can cluster here with this knot of fellow exploration enthusiasts, bringing his two closest intimates Edward Dyer and Francis Leigh along with him. They too are deep in talk of the North-West Passage: Sidney is lamenting that he is tied to court diplomacy, and gushing with affectionate, flattering envy of old Martin Frobisher’s adventures at sea. Frobisher just snorts and replies, “It’s all very well talking of it here, sitting on dry land with a good glass of wine before you, Sir Philip. You’d find it a very different sort of business bashing through icebergs with your beard frozen fast to your ruff, especially if you had a weak stomach.”
“That would be a trivial hardship to endure, my good man, if I could win half the honour Her Majesty bestowed on you, when she waved that ivory white handkerchief from Greenwich Palace windows to farewell your ship,” Sidney laughs back.
“Well sir, folks say you’ve no cause to complain of lack of royal favour, and no cause to deserve less of it,” Frobisher grins. “If you can win honours staying ashore, don’t go chasing them out on the ocean, that’s what I say, eh Master Towerson?”
Towerson’s gray beard, weathered by decades of fair and foul foreign voyages, nods in grim agreement. But at that very moment, a waiter hurries into the cabin.
“By your leave, my Lord Mayor’s worship, there’s a tall gentleman outside begging a word with the Right Honourable Sir Walter Raleigh.”
“Show him in at once. Sir Walter’s friends are our friends,” Osborne calls.

Amyas steps across the threshold, pausing hesitantly in the doorway.
“Captain Leigh!” half a dozen voices cry out at once.
“Whyever did you hang back out there?” Osborne asks warmly. “You know your way about these decks well enough.”
“Well enough, my lords and gentlemen. But — Sir Walter, you must excuse me,” Amyas says, giving Raleigh a sharp, meaningful look that the quick-witted explorer understands instantly. Raleigh blanches, stands, and follows Amyas out into a side cabin. The two are gone for five minutes, before Amyas steps back out alone.
In a few plain, heavy words he tells the whole company the terrible news they had not yet heard: Sir Humphrey Gilbert had gone down with his ship *Squirrel* in the mid-Atlantic, no survivors. Before he finishes speaking, noble tears glisten on the faces of these hardened, battle-scarred men.
“The ancient Egyptians,” Sir Edward Osborne says heavily, “would set a corpse out among their guests at feasts, to remind them of the fragility of human life. Have we forgotten God and our own weakness in our celebration today, that He Himself has sent us this message from the dead?”
“Nay, my Lord Mayor,” Sidney says softly, “it is no message from the dead, but a greeting from the realm of everlasting life.”
“Amen,” Osborne answers. “But gentlemen, our feast is done. There are men here brave enough to drink on merrily in spite of private losses, but not a single one of us can raise a toast now with the news of such a great, good man’s death still ringing in our ears.”
It was true. Many of these men had lost great sums of money backing Gilbert’s expedition, but all of that vanished from their minds in the shadow of the tragedy. The party breaks up sadly and hurriedly, every man turning to his neighbour to mutter, “What will the Queen say?”
Raleigh returns a few minutes later, silent, pressing the hand of every friend he passes before he slips out to hail a wherry, beckoning Amyas to follow. Sidney, Cumberland, and Frank Leigh take another boat behind them, leaving Raleigh and Amyas to sort through the sad details of the wreck.

They land back at Whitehall Stairs. Raleigh, Sidney, and Cumberland head into the palace to break the news to Elizabeth, while the two Leigh brothers make their way to their mother’s lodgings.
Amyas had rehearsed what he would say to Frank about Rose Salterne, but now that the moment is here, he cannot bring himself to start, wishing Frank would broach the subject first. Frank too shrinks from the difficult conversation, all the more so because he has no idea Amyas passed through Bideford on his way to London, and already knows all about Rose’s disappearance.
When they climb the stairs, both of them are relieved to find their mother is out visiting Westminster Abbey. They drift over to the deep bay window overlooking the Thames, making small talk of trivial things, gazing at each other’s faces long in the fading dusk: it has been three whole years since they last met.
The years and their wildly different lives have only deepened the contrast between the two brothers. Frank smiles with affectionate, proud admiration as he looks up at Amyas, and sees he is no longer the rollicking, quick-handed young sailor lad he remembered, but a stately, self-assured warrior, every line of his face and gesture bearing the marks of “the reason firm, the temperate will, endurance, foresight, strength, and skill” one would expect of a man tutored by Drake and Grenville, then mentored by Raleigh and Gilbert. His long wild locks are cropped close to his head, offset by a rich golden beard covering his lips and chin. His skin is tanned dark by a thousand tropical suns and Atlantic gales, a long scar from a fight in Ireland cuts across his right temple, his great broad frame has filled out with hard muscle, and the hand he rests on the windowsill is as heavy and calloused as a blacksmith’s. Frank lays his own slender hand on top of it and sighs, and Amyas looks down, shocked at the contrast: Frank’s delicate fingers are thin, bloodless, almost translucent. Amyas stares anxiously into his brother’s face. He has changed drastically in their years apart. The bright red flush still burns on his two cheeks, but the skin around it is dull and pale, his lips are colourless, his features sharpened, and his eyes glint with an unnatural, feverish brightness. When Frank teases him for looking far older than his years, Amyas cannot help but think the remark applies far more truly to Frank himself.
Amyas tries to steer the conversation to lighter things, pointing at every new building along the riverbank to ask its name.
“So this is old Father Thames, lined all along with palaces?”
“Aye,” Frank says softly. “His banks are stately enough, but you see, he does not stop to look at them. He hurries down to the sea, the sea flows on to the ocean, and the ocean — westward ho, forever. Everything moves westward ho. Perhaps we will move that way ourselves one day, Amyas.”
“What strange talk is that?”
“Only that the great tides of the world follow the primum mobile of the heavens, and flow forever from east to west. Is it so strange to think of that, after we spent all afternoon toasting success to all our westward ventures?”
“And much good that toast has done!” Amyas grunts. “I lost the best friend and noblest captain on God’s green earth, not to mention all my life’s savings, swallowed up in that same cursed westward gulf.”
“Aye, Sir Humphrey Gilbert’s star set in the west — why not?” Frank says. “The sun, moon, and all the planets sink below the western horizon. Why not the meteors of this lower world? Why not a will-o’-the-wisp like me, Amyas?”
“God forbid that should happen to you, Frank!”
“Why not? Is not the west the land of peace, the land of dreams? Does not our own heart tell us that, every time we watch the sun sink, and long to float away on those golden clouds with him? They bury men facing east. I would rather my face was turned to the west when I die, for I think some divine instinct made the old ancient poets guess that Elysium lay beneath the setting sun. That longing for the west is woven deep into every human heart. I do not blame any man who sails out beyond the farthest sea to find it, as David once wished to flee, to find peace.”
“You do not blame anyone who flees westward?” Amyas asks, sharply. “I do.”
Frank stares at him inquiringly, then smiles. “If I was to blame anyone just now, it would be you, for seeming tired of chasing that westward dream.”
“Then you would have me go?”
“God knows,” Frank says, after a long pause. “But I suppose I must tell you all, once and for all. Things have happened back in Bideford that —”
“Spare us both, Frank. I know everything. I passed through Bideford on my way here. I came to London not just to see you and our mother, but to ask for your advice, and her permission.”
“True, noble heart!” Frank cries, throwing his arms around Amyas. “I knew you would be true to your vow.”
“Westward ho it is, then?”
“We have no choice, do we?”
“We?” Amyas repeats, shocked.
“Amyas, does not the same oath that binds you bind me too?”
Amyas staggers back, holding Frank by the shoulders at arm’s length, and feels beneath his clothes that his brother’s arms are little more than skin and bone.
“You? Dearest heart, a single month of that hard voyage would kill you!”
Frank laughs softly, tossing his head in that familiar, graceful way of his. “I follow the school of Thales, who said the ocean is the mother of all life. I have no more fear of returning to her bosom than Humphrey Gilbert did.”
“But Frank — what of our mother?”
“Our mother knows all. She would never have us prove unworthy of her.”
“Impossible! She will never let you go.”
“All things are possible for those who believe in God, brother, and she believes. This past summer, Doctor Dee the wise astrologer gave her all sorts of readings about me, you see. It seems I was born under a cold, watery planet, and if I want to live a long life, I must travel closer to the warm, life-giving sun, to bask out my short little life there like a fly on a sunlit wall. To tell you the truth, he ordered me to stop spending cold winters here in the east, and go back to the sea breezes of our home shores to heal my frozen lungs. He filled my mother’s head with stories of sick men everyone thought were dying in Germany and France, who got their youth back, like serpents or eagles shedding their old skin, by travelling to Italy, Spain, and the Canary Islands. She will be more eager to let me go than I will be to leave her all alone. And yet I must go, Amyas. It is not just that my heart burns, as Philip Sidney’s does, as every gallant man’s should, to join that noble band of Argonauts who are spreading God’s word and claiming new lands for our queen. It is not just that love calls me — tyrannous, uncontrollable love, that grows stronger with absence, deeper with despair. Honour binds me too, Amyas — my oath —”
He breaks off, gasping for breath, and bursts into a violent fit of coughing, leaning heavily on his brother’s shoulder.
“Fools that we were — fools that I was — to make that ridiculous, fantastical vow!” Amyas groans.
“Not so,” a soft voice says from behind them. “You made that vow for the sake of peace on earth, and goodwill to all men, and ‘Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called the children of God.’ No, my sons, be sure that such self-sacrifice as you have shown will meet its full reward at the hand of Him who sacrificed Himself for you.”
“Mother! Mother!” Amyas cries. “Do you not hate the very sight of me, coming here to take your first-born son away from you?”
“My boy, it is God who is taking him, not you. If I dare put my faith in such predictions, Doctor Dee assured me that a great, extraordinary honour awaits both of you in the west, each according to your deserts.”
“Ah,” Amyas says, grinning a little sadly. “I suppose my blessing will be like Esau’s, to live by my sword out there. And you, Jacob the gentle spiritual man, will inherit the kingdom of heaven, and an angel’s crown.”
“Be it what it may, it will be a blessing so long as you stay the good, honourable boys you have always been. At the very least, my Frank will be safe from court intrigues and the temptations of the city. Oh, how I wish I could come with you, and share in your glory!” she says, laying her head on Amyas’s chest and looking up with that familiar winning smile. “I have heard of heroic mothers who marched to battle beside their sons, and cheered them on to victory. Why should I not come with you on a far more peaceful errand? I could nurse the sick, if any fall ill. I could speak to that poor lost girl, and win her back far more easily than any of you men could.
