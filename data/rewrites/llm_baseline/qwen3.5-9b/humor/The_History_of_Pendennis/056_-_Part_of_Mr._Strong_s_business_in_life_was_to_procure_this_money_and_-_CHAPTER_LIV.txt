It is time to record a rather embarrassing fact about Pendennis. While it might seem undignified to admit such a flaw in the biography of a novel's protagonist and godfather, the truth demands to be told to the public reading his genuine memoirs. After falling ill with fever and succumbing to the pangs of love, Pendennis underwent a rigorous medical regimen: he was bled, blistered, had his head shaved, and subjected to whatever treatments the doctor prescribed. Once his body recovered, his mind followed suit. Just as surely as the fever broke, the infatuation vanished. He was no longer smitten with Fanny Bolton; he was as indifferent to her as anyone else who is too wise—or too moral—to let their hearts wander after the daughters of porters.

Lying on his pillow, Pendennis chuckled at himself, reflecting on this "second cure." He couldn't fathom why he'd ever cared for Fanny. Dissecting his own dead passion for his poor little nurse, he concluded that neither her wit, breeding, nor beauty held the charm he once attributed to her. Hundreds of women were prettier. The fault wasn't in her; it was in his own feverish imagination. She remained unchanged, but the eyes that viewed her had shifted. He felt a friendly disposition toward the "little thing," but the violent adoration of weeks prior had fled, vanquished by the pill and lancet that cured his flesh. A profound sense of relief washed over him (though he admitted a touch of selfishness to this feeling); he realized he had resisted temptation precisely when the danger was greatest, leaving him free of guilt regarding his conduct toward the young girl. Looking back at the "Fanny Bolton snare" from which he had escaped, he felt a strange mix of comfort and shame at the ease with which he had stopped loving.

Amidst this internal shift, the kind smiles and tender vigilance of his mother at his bedside filled him with peace. Seeing health return was all the unwearied nurse required; executing his slightest whim was her supreme joy. Surrounded by her love, he felt a gratitude akin to that of a child recovering from weakness.

Though Pen vaguely recalled Fanny nursing him during his delirium, the memory was so foggy he couldn't distinguish reality from fever-dreams. Consequently, just as he hadn't previously confided in his mother about Fanny, he now withheld his current sentiments from her. It was an unfortunate lack of trust on both sides; a few timely words might have spared his mother and her circle considerable anguish.

Mrs. Pendennis, seeing Miss Bolton installed as nurse and caring for her son, naturally interpreted the intimacy between the two young people as proof of Arthur's alleged misconduct. Why stop to inquire? Stories damaging to a man's reputation tend to find fertile ground among those who adore him most. Wasn't a wife often the first to suspect her husband? Poor Pen absorbed a heavy dose of this suspicious affection from his nurse. The pure-hearted woman believed her boy had suffered a malady far worse than physical fever—one that stained his character as well as his strength. She bore this silent terror, masking her dread with a forced cheerfulness.

Meanwhile, at Boulogne, Captain Shandon read the latest *Pall Mall Gazette* and remarked to Mrs. Shandon that Jack Finucane's handwriting was absent from the leading articles, replaced by Mr. Warrington. "I know the crack of his whip," Shandon observed, using a brutal metaphor comparing Warrington's criticism to a butcher's cleaver. Mrs. Shandon protested, defending Warrington's kindness to the children, but Shandon retorted that while he was gentle with kids, he was savage with men. He urged his wife to avoid understanding newspaper politics, preferring the easy life at Boulogne where wine flowed freely. "Mix us another tumbler, Mary," he commanded, dismissing the grim reality with a Latin quote and a toast to future endeavors.

Indeed, Warrington stepped in with vigor to write Pen's share of the paper. He penned articles, reviews, and theatrical critiques with his characteristic ferocity. Though his hand was too powerful for such delicate subjects, he delighted in telling Arthur's mother, uncle, and sister that no pen was more graceful or elegant than Arthur's. "The people here don't understand style," he explained to Mrs. Pendennis, claiming Arthur as his own charge. Despite admitting a bit of wilfulness and dandyism in the youth, Warrington swore he knew no more honest or loyal creature, noting that while Arthur's pen could be wicked, his heart was as kind as a young lady.

Helen sighed deeply, and Laura felt wounded, yet both were grateful for Warrington's high praise of Arthur. Major Pendennis echoed these sentiments with unusual enthusiasm, declaring Warrington a true gentleman of the ancient Suffolk lineage, a man of immense talent destined for greatness. Laura blushed as the Major spoke, secretly speculating that Warrington's melancholy demeanor hid a tragic romantic attachment.

Warrington secured chambers near Grenier's in Flag Court. After working tirelessly in the mornings, he spent his afternoons visiting the sick man and the household. He even escorted Miss Bell for walks in the Temple Gardens. When Laura asked her mother's permission, the Major eagerly approved, assuring her that everyone walked there safely under the watch of beadles. With the moral arbiter's blessing, Helen allowed her daughter the fresh air of the river, delighted to see her return rosy-cheeked and spirited.

Prior to this, Laura and Helen had reached a fragile truce. When news of Pen's critical illness arrived, Laura insisted on traveling to London with the terrified mother. Even after Helen initially refused, and later when hope seemed lost due to Pen's scandalous behavior, Laura tearfully confessed her secret love. Sharing their grief softened the blow, allowing them to endure the journey together.

Now, consider the precarious position of Fanny Bolton. Facing judgment from such stern observers, she faced swift condemnation. Women are notoriously harsh critics in matters of morality; they form a vigilant network to protect their own, ready to tear apart any who stray. The narrator, adopting the persona of a Turkish judge, warns these female "Mahmouds" to exercise extreme caution before sentencing. Ensure the accused truly deserves the sack before casting them into the Bosphorus. If she is guilty, let justice prevail; if not, spare her.

Despite the tension surrounding Fanny, the Major encouraged Warrington's friendship with Laura. Their interactions were innocent and refreshing. Warrington, encountering a woman of purity and high mind for the first time, found her captivating. Similarly, Laura, isolated and longing for connection, discovered in Warrington a man of natural power, humor, and sincerity—a stark contrast to Pen's dandyish indifference and languid arrogance. Where Pen was a spoiled despot, cloyed by obedient slaves, Warrington offered energy, respect, and a hearty humanity.

How, then, did Laura develop such a rapturous devotion for Pen? During the journey from Fairoaks to London, she recounted endless tales of his heroic childhood deeds—braving tooth extractions, sparing birds' nests, sharing food with beggars. These stories, sung through tears, painted a picture of a saintly figure. Yet, the narrator points out that Pen is merely an ordinary man. Why does a sensible girl love him? Because she sees no one else at Fairoaks, hears him constantly praised, and possesses an innate human need to attach herself to someone. Love, the narrator suggests, is often less about the beloved and more about the lover's own nature and thirst for connection. Laura fell for Pen because he was available, praised, and gentlemanly, and because her heart needed an anchor. Once she formed this image, she cherished it fiercely.

Later, when fate scattered the group across Lamb Court, they might have reflected on how happy those days were. The Major developed a newfound appreciation for London in September, finding the "dead season" surprisingly pleasant. He frequently visited the Temple, climbed stairs with benevolent energy, and arranged special meals at Bays's restaurant—jellies, clear soups, and aspics suitable for invalids. Thanks to Dr. Goodenough's leniency regarding alcohol, the Major orchestrated a grand celebration involving rare Amontillado sent by the Marquis of Steyne. The widow and Laura drank respectfully despite disliking the taste, while Pen, invigorated, cheered feebly. Warrington toasted the Major and the aristocracy, teaching Laura to shout "Hear! hear!" as they clattered glasses and cards.

Inside the rooms, life hummed gently. Warrington, knowing Sibwright, accepted the offer of his chambers and furniture. A piano stood in the room, belonging to Sibwright, a talented amateur musician. Here, Laura, trembling slightly at first, played simple airs and sang old home songs. Her rich contralto voice captivated Warrington, who sat rapt, following the rhythm even if he couldn't grasp the harmony. He watched her with growing enthusiasm, admiring the pure, tender creature creating the music.

Outside, a pale girl in a black bonnet occasionally stood at the lamp-post, listening to the melodies drifting from the open windows. When Pen retired, the music ceased, lights flickered in the upper room, and the Major, Warrington, and Laura gathered for games of ecarte or backgammon. Sometimes Laura worked on a pair of worsted slippers, perhaps intended for Arthur, George, or the Major—all of whom would have given anything for them.

As the evening wound down, a shabby old gentleman led the pale girl away. The street musicians dispersed.
