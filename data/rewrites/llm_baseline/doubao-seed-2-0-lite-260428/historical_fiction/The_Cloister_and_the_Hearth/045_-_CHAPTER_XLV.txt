The small household gathered around the hosier’s scrubbed oak dining table, passing mugs of spiced ale and crusty rye bread as they chattered over the strange, troubling news that had come to the doorstep that day. No one had seen Catherine return, until the heavy door thudded shut behind her, and she cast a piercing, unblinking glance over every face in the little circle. She laid Gerard’s crumpled letter flat on the tabletop, and traced its lines with a calloused, work-worn finger as she recited every single word from memory — a small, stubborn ruse to fool herself, and the rest of them, into believing she could read the script that had come all the way from Italy.

Then she lifted her head, and her sharp gaze locked straight on Cornelis and Sybrandt. The two brothers flinched, and their eyes fell to their laps.

The storm that had been brewing, quiet and smoldering, for months, burst over their heads at last. Catherine swelled like an angry mother hen ruffling all her feathers to shield her chicks from harm, and a torrent of words poured from her lips — a rushing current of sharp wisdom, tangled trivialities, blistering invective that no man alive could ever have uttered in one unbroken stream, and very few women. The unbroken monologue ran on, breathless and unstopping:

“I’ve had my suspicions for months now, that you two were the ones stoking the strife between Gerard and your father, that you set that old rogue Ghysbrecht Van Swieten on him to do your dirty work. And now Gerard’s own words, written with his own hand, prove every last bit of it. You drove your own flesh and blood halfway across the world, to a foreign land full of strangers, and robbed the mother that bore you of her dearest darling — the pride of my eye, the joy of my heart.

And you two have always been of a piece, through and through. Back when you were all small boys together, the rest of my brood were a comfort to me, but you two were nothing but a curse: sly, mischievous, tearing your clothes to rags so bad it took me half a day’s mending to stitch them whole, for work wears cloth thin slow, but petty malice cuts right through it. You’ve grown beards now, but prudence never grew with them. Still the last to bed, the last to drag yourselves out of it at dawn — don’t you know honest folks turn in early, and industry rises with the sun? Two layabouts in one house make a pair of ne’er-do-wells, and no mistake. I’ve sat and watched your ways a hundred times, wondering where on earth you could have come from. You don’t take after your father, and you are no more like me than a wasp is like an ant. I swear you were swapped in the cradle, or a cuckoo dropped you on my doorstep, for you have none of our calloused, hardworking hands, and none of our soft hearts. No one else of all my blood ever mocked the folks God saw fit to afflict, but I’ve heard you, a hundred times when my back was turned, snickering at poor hunchbacked Giles for being smaller than the other boys, and teasing my sweet Kate for not being as strong as a Flanders work mare. After that, you may as well go rob a church! You can’t be any worse in the eyes of the God who made Kate and Giles, nor in mine, who suffered through every ache of their birth just as I did through yours, you paltry, unfeeling, traitorous curs.

Hush, now, Kate, don’t try to calm me. Their cup is full to overflowing. It takes a great deal to turn a mother’s heart against the sons she nursed at her own knee. For years I bit my tongue, I winked at their tricks, I pretended I didn’t see half of what they did, lest your father find out exactly how bad they are — he would have thrown them out on the street that very minute. But now they have gone too far. Where did you get all that money you’ve been rolling in for the last month? You never wrought a single hour’s honest work for it. I would rather die than hear the story of how you stole it from some other poor soul out of another mouth. It all started that night you stumbled home long after dark, Cornelis, your head all swelled up like you’d won some great prize. Sloth and greed make a wretched pair, my masters. A man who loves money too much will either sweat for it honestly, or steal it. And if you robbed anyone, I’ll wager it was some helpless woman — any man would have chased you off with his bare fists. No matter. That money showed me plain what you would do with this shop, this house, and all our gear if it ever fell into your hands. I’ve watched you today: the two of you spent a whole groat between you, while I scarce spend a groat a week to feed and keep a roof over all of you, good and bad.

I will not wait for the day you might walk behind my coffin to claim what is mine. This shop, this house, none of it will ever belong to you two. Gerard is our heir — poor Gerard, the boy you banished, the boy you tried your best to kill. You’ve made him ten times dearer to me than he ever was before. My poor lost boy! I will see him soon, hold him in my arms, set him on my knee just like when he was small. Aye, you may stare all you like. You thought you were so clever, but you were not clever enough. You cut the flowering stalk right off, but you left the seed — the seed that will outgrow you, outlive you, outshine you. Margaret Brandt is quick with child, and it is Gerard’s. What is Gerard’s is mine. I have prayed to the saints a hundred times that it will be a boy, and it will, it must. When I first found that news, my bowels ached for that unborn child as if it were my own flesh and blood. He is our heir. He will outlive all of us. You two will not — a wicked heart in a man’s breast is like a worm inside a nut, it eats the whole shell away to dust long before its time.

Kate, go take down Gerard’s old bibs and little linen tuckers from the drawer you know of, the one at the back of the chest. One of these fine days soon we will borrow Peter Buyskens’ cart, ride out to Sevenbergen, and go comfort Gerard’s wife through her confinement. She is his wife. Who is Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, to come between a man and a woman standing before the altar, to tear apart what God and the priest have bound as one? She is my daughter now, and I am as proud of her as I am of you, Kate, almost. And as for you two — keep out of my sight for as long as you can. You are as welcome to me as a black ill-omened dog on my hearth.”

Cornelis and Sybrandt did not wait to be told twice. They slunk out of the house at once, every inch of them stinging with a hot, jumbled mess of half-hearted remorse, stubborn impenitence, and a slow, burning hate. For days after that, they dodged her gaze whenever they could, and Catherine never spoke a single word to either of them — not good, not bad, not even to ask them to pass the bread. She had unburdened every last grief and rage from her soul at last.
