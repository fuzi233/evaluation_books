Mrs. Jennings was a widow of comfortable means, blessed with a generous jointure and two daughters who had both found respectable husbands. With her domestic duties fulfilled, she now devoted herself wholeheartedly to the noble pursuit of matchmaking—her mission in life being to unite every eligible young person within her circle. She possessed an uncanny knack for detecting hidden affections, often delighting in teasing young ladies with hints that such-and-such a gentleman was deeply smitten with them. Her keen eye did not fail her at Barton: no sooner had Colonel Brandon arrived than she declared, without hesitation, that he was utterly captivated by Marianne Dashwood.

Her conviction formed almost instantly—on the very first evening, when he listened so intently to Marianne’s singing, his gaze fixed and thoughtful. When the Middletons returned the visit and dined at the cottage, the evidence was confirmed: again he sat rapt, as if each note stirred something deep within him. There could be no doubt. Mrs. Jennings was certain. It would be a splendid match—Colonel Brandon was wealthy, and Marianne was undeniably beautiful.

She had long wished to see Colonel Brandon settled in marriage, ever since their acquaintance began through Sir John. And she always took pleasure in securing good matches for charming young women. The amusement it brought her was a bonus—she relished teasing them both, laughing at the colonel at the park and poking fun at Marianne in the cottage. To him, her jests likely meant nothing; but to Marianne, they were baffling at first. Once she understood their target, she struggled to decide whether to laugh at their absurdity or resent their impertinence. She felt they mocked the colonel’s age and his solitary state as an older man, which seemed unkind and insensitive.

Mrs. Dashwood, though aware that five years separated her from the colonel, found it hard to view him as ancient. “Marianne,” she gently said, “you cannot deny that your own feelings are influenced by youth. Colonel Brandon may appear old to you, but he is not so aged as you imagine. He is still a man of strength and dignity.”

“Yet, Mama,” Marianne replied, “he is old enough to be my father! If he ever truly loved, surely such emotions have long since faded. It is ridiculous to suppose he could feel anything like passion now. When does a man become safe from such jokes? Not even illness or frailty seems to shield him!”

Elinor stepped in, defending the colonel’s health. “Do you really consider him infirm?” she asked. “He moves with ease and vitality. His age may seem greater to you than to others, but I see no sign of weakness.”

“Did you not hear him speak of rheumatism?” Marianne countered. “That is the hallmark of declining years.”

“Oh, my dear,” Mrs. Dashwood laughed, “if you apply that logic to me, I must live in constant fear of my own decay. Forty is hardly ancient, and yet you treat me as if I were on the brink of collapse!”

“But thirty-five,” Marianne insisted, “has nothing to do with love. No woman should expect affection from a man of such an age. Marriage at that point is merely a transaction—a matter of convenience, not feeling.”

Elinamin smiled faintly. “You believe no woman over twenty-seven can truly love a man of thirty-five? But what if that woman has never married? Then perhaps age becomes less of an obstacle. And why should Colonel Brandon be confined to a sickroom simply because he mentioned a slight ache in his shoulder one cold day?”

“He spoke of flannel waistcoats,” Marianne retorted. “To me, those are symbols of frailty—of cramps, stiffness, and all the ills that come with age.”

“Had he been feverish,” Elinor teased, “you might have found him more interesting. Admit it—there is something compelling about a flushed face, sunken eyes, and a rapid pulse. Even illness can stir emotion.”

Later, after Elinor had left the room, Marianne turned to her mother with a troubled expression. “Mama, I have a troubling thought I cannot keep to myself. I am certain Edward Ferrars is unwell. We’ve been here nearly two weeks, and he hasn’t come. Nothing but genuine sickness could explain such a delay. What else could hold him back?”

“Did you think he would arrive so soon?” Mrs. Dashwood asked.

“I didn’t expect it,” Marianne admitted. “But I feared he might not come at all. He seemed hesitant when I invited him. Still, I believed he would eventually make the journey. Does Elinor anticipate his arrival?”

“I don’t think so,” Mrs. Dashwood said. “Yesterday, when I mentioned getting a new grate for the spare bedroom, she said there was no need—it wouldn’t be used for some time.”

Marianne frowned. “How strange. Why does he delay? Their behavior toward each other has been so unnatural. Their farewell was so cold, so reserved. Their last conversation lacked warmth. Edward’s goodbye was equally kind to both of us—as if we were no different in his heart. I left them alone twice that morning, and both times he followed me out. And Elinor—she didn’t weep when leaving Norland, as I did. She remains composed, calm. Never once does she show sadness. She never avoids people, never seems restless. When will she finally break down?”
