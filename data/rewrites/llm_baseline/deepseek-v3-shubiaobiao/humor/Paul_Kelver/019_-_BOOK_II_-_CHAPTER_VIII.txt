I left London with drums beating in my heart and flags of triumph waving in my brain. A little over a year later, I crept back to it on a damp, fog-choked December evening—defeated, disgraced, a whipped cur slinking to its kennel. I had meant to be a great man, or at the very least a decent one. Instead, I returned as something best hidden in the crowd until I could muster the courage to end the sorry farce of my existence.

Ambition? Oh, I’d had that in spades. I was going to carve my name into the world with a chisel of genius and integrity. But ambition without backbone is just vanity in a waistcoat. I’d fancied myself a Galahad, pure and untouchable. Turned out I was more of a Launcelot—if Launcelot had tripped over his own scruples and landed face-first in the mud.

Urban Vane, my partner in crime (literal and metaphorical), was a swindler of the first order. The play we’d been touring wasn’t his. It wasn’t even mine. He’d lifted it straight from the desk of a half-dead dramatist he’d charmed in some literary scullery. When the poor devil recovered enough to crawl back from Italy, Vane broke the news to me over a bottle of wine, grinning around his cigar.

“Your language, my dear Kelver,” he said, when I ran out of indignant spluttering, “might wound me if I thought you had any standing to lecture on morality. You’ve been perfectly happy to take credit for work that wasn’t yours. ‘Drama, in five acts, by Mr. Horace Moncrieff’—how many of those lines were actually yours? Two hundred? And none of them the good ones.”

The worst of it was, he wasn’t wrong. I’d been eager to slap my name on anything that might float me into fame. Now the tide was going out, and I was left stranded in the stinking shallows.

What followed was a masterclass in moral gymnastics. Vane, ever the pragmatist, proposed we rewrite the play just enough to dodge outright theft. “He’ll think he mislaid the manuscript,” he assured me. “Or maybe he’ll assume he only dreamed of writing it. Artists are terribly forgetful.”

So I became a literary counterfeiter, stitching together a Frankenstein’s monster of stolen scraps and my own feeble embellishments. The irony wasn’t lost on me—here I was, the would-be knight of artistic purity, sweating to make a crime look like inspiration.

Vane was a peculiar sort of villain. Not the mustache-twirling kind, but the sort who genuinely believed villainy was just advanced thinking. He lectured me on the absurdity of morality, the pettiness of honesty. “The world’s only rule,” he’d say, “is *don’t get caught*. Everything else is sentimental drivel.”

I wish I could say I resisted. Instead, I let him sand down my conscience till it fit neatly into his philosophy. Worse, I started to *like* him. The man was a walking moral vacuum, yet he had a way of making depravity sound like higher wisdom.

Our leading lady was a Juno-esque creature with the charm of a sledgehammer and the subtlety of a music-hall ballad. She’d married a sensitive novelist, ruined him, and now regarded our tour as a stepping stone to better things. Vane, ever the matchmaker, saw in her the perfect instrument for my “artistic education.”

“All great men are libertines,” he declared. “Suppression is for shopkeepers. You need *experience*.”

I tried very hard to want her. Not out of passion—God knows there was none—but because it seemed the literary thing to do. If I was to be a proper artist, surely I ought to be dissolute? I rehearsed my debauchery like a bad actor stumbling through a part.

Fate, in her blunt way, saved me from myself. Our plagiarized play was recognized. Rumors spread. Vane, ever the strategist, vanished one evening with the week’s takings and a breezy note: *Life’s a comedy—laugh it off.*

I stood in the theater, surrounded by a chorus of outraged actors, all suddenly very concerned with artistic integrity. Their salaries were gone. Their trust—what little they’d had—was gone. And I was left holding the bag.

That night, our leading lady paid me a visit. She’d come, she cheerfully informed me, to accept my drunken proposal of elopement. “Damned lucky I took a day to think it over!” she laughed on her way out.

I walked thirty miles to Belfast that night. By the time I reached London, I was a ghost in my own life. I took a room in Deptford and gave singing lessons for sixpence a half-hour. I taught French and German to ambitious shopboys (may they never discover the crimes I committed against their subjunctives). I existed in a fog, moving through streets that felt like a dream I couldn’t wake from.

The oddest thing? I remember every detail of those months—the grime, the chatter, the thousand little tragedies of the streets—and yet I have no memory of *seeing* any of it. I was a spectator in my own ruin, watching from somewhere outside myself.

Defeat is one thing. Disgrace is another. But the real tragedy is realizing you’ve become the villain of your own story—and not even an interesting one.
