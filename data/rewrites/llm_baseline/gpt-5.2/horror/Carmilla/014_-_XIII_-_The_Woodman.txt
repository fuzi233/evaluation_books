There were inconveniences, at first small as grit between the teeth, that grew in time to be impossible to ignore.

In the first place, Millarca complained of a languor that would not lift—an exhaustion that clung to her like damp linen after her late illness—and she did not show herself until the afternoon had crept a long way toward evening. The house adjusted itself to that schedule with the docility of a well-trained kitchen: breakfast cooled and was carried away; the mid-day meal waited, kept back from spoiling by all the small arts of a careful household—soups drawn off the fire and held where they would not sour, bread wrapped to keep its crust, meat covered from flies. Her absence became another domestic routine, like laying the cloth or counting the keys.

But a second circumstance, discovered by chance, pricked my mind with a colder point. Although she always locked her door on the inside, and never disturbed the key from its place until she admitted the maid to assist at her toilet, it was certain that she was sometimes absent from her room very early in the morning, and again at odd hours later in the day—always before she wished it to be understood that she was stirring.

She was seen more than once from the windows of the schloss, in the first faint grey of morning, walking through the trees in an easterly direction, moving with that steadiness that is not wakefulness at all, but a trance. I told myself she walked in her sleep. Yet that explanation, neat as a cook’s knot, did not tie the matter shut. How did she pass out from her room, leaving the door locked on the inside? How did she escape the house without unbarring door or window?

While these puzzles worked in me like a slow souring, another anxiety, sharper and more urgent, presented itself—one that made the very comforts of the table taste wrong.

My dear child—my darling—began to lose her looks and health, and in a way so mysterious, so quietly horrible, that I became thoroughly frightened. First came appalling dreams; then, as she fancied, a spectre—sometimes resembling Millarca, sometimes the indistinct form of a beast—walking round the foot of her bed from side to side, the way a restless animal circles before it lies down. Lastly came sensations. One, not unpleasant but singular, she said resembled the flow of an icy stream against her breast. Later she felt, she said, something like a pair of large needles pierce her a little below the throat, with a very sharp pain. A few nights after that followed a gradual, convulsive sense of strangulation; then unconsciousness.

I heard every word distinctly—each syllable as clear as a knife tapping bone—because by then we were driving upon the short grass that spreads on either side of the road as you approach the roofless village which had shown no chimney-smoke for more than half a century. The carriage wheels, no longer clattering on stone, made a muffled, furtive sound; and in that softened acoustic, the General’s voice carried with a dreadful intimacy, as though the air itself leaned in to listen.

You may imagine how strangely I felt to hear my own symptoms described with such exactness in those suffered by the poor girl who—if catastrophe had not intervened—would at that moment have been a visitor at my father’s château. You may suppose, too, how I felt when he detailed habits and mysterious peculiarities that were, in truth, those of our beautiful guest—Carmilla.

A vista opened in the forest. Suddenly we were beneath the gables and broken chimneys of the ruined village; and above, on a slight eminence, the towers and battlements of the dismantled castle rose and leaned in the mind like a remembered threat. Great trees grouped about it, their heavy boughs overhanging as if the place were kept under a perpetual, listening judgement. Even the birds seemed to sound different there—calls more cautious, as though the canopy hoarded echoes and returned them altered.

In a kind of frightened dream I descended from the carriage. We were silent; each of us had matter enough for thought. We mounted the ascent, and soon were among spacious chambers, winding stairs, and dark corridors, where the air held the stale odour of old stone, wet leaves, and long-extinguished hearths.

“And this was once the palatial residence of the Karnsteins!” the old General said at last, standing at a great window and looking out across the village to the wide undulating expanse of forest beyond. “It was a bad family, and here its bloodstained annals were written. It is hard that they should, after death, continue to plague the human race with their atrocious lusts. That is the chapel of the Karnsteins, down there.”

He pointed through the foliage to grey Gothic walls half hidden on the steep below.

“And I hear the axe of a woodman,” he added—indeed, through the living hush came the measured stroke, wood answering iron with a dull, resilient note. “Busy among the trees that surround it. He may give us the information I seek, and point out the grave of Mircalla, Countess of Karnstein. These rustics preserve the local traditions of great families—whose stories die out among the rich and titled as soon as the families themselves become extinct.”

“We have a portrait, at home, of Mircalla, the Countess Karnstein; should you like to see it?” my father asked.

“Time enough, dear friend,” replied the General. “I believe I have seen the original; and one motive which has led me to you earlier than I at first intended, was to explore the chapel we are now approaching.”

“What! see the Countess Mircalla,” my father exclaimed. “Why, she has been dead more than a century!”

“Not so dead as you fancy, I am told,” answered the General.

My father looked at him—there came over his face, I thought, for a moment, a return of a suspicion I had detected before. Yet although anger and detestation flared at times in the old soldier’s manner, there was nothing flighty in him; his rage had weight, like an iron pot set down too hard.

“There remains to me,” he said, as we passed under the heavy arch of the Gothic church—so large in its ruin that it might justly still be called a church—“but one object that can interest me during the few years left me on earth, and that is to wreak on her the vengeance which, I thank God, may still be accomplished by a mortal arm.”

“What vengeance can you mean?” my father asked, his amazement deepening.

“I mean, to decapitate the monster,” he answered; a fierce flush rose in his aged face, and he stamped so that the sound echoed mournfully through the hollow ruin. His clenched hand lifted as though it already grasped the handle of an axe, and he shook it ferociously in the air.

“What?” my father cried, bewildered more than ever.

“To strike her head off.”

“Cut her head off!”

“Aye—with a hatchet, with a spade, with anything that can cleave through her murderous throat. You shall hear,” he said, trembling with rage. Then, hurrying forward: “That beam will answer for a seat. Your dear child is fatigued; let her sit, and I will—in a few sentences—close my dreadful story.”

A squared block of wood lay on the grass-grown pavement of the chapel; it served as a bench, and I was very glad to take it. The stone underfoot was powdered with moss; the air tasted faintly of mould, like bread kept too long in a damp larder. Somewhere above, in the broken roofline, a draught moved and made a thin, complaining whistle, the ruin’s own small voice.

In the meantime the General called to the woodman who had been removing boughs that leaned upon the old walls. Axe in hand, the hardy old fellow came and stood before us. His clothes smelled of resin and crushed leaf; that clean, green sharpness that follows a newly split log clung about him, an almost wholesome thing in so accursed a place.

He could tell us nothing of the monuments. But there was an old man, he said—a ranger of this forest—presently lodging in the priest’s house about two miles away, who could point out every monument of the old Karnstein family; and, for a trifle, he would fetch him, if we would lend him one of our horses. He could be back in little more than half an hour.

“Have you been long employed about this forest?” my father asked.

“I have been a woodman here,” he answered in his patois, under the forester, “all my days; so has my father before me, and so on, as many generations as I can count. I could show you the very house in the village here in which my ancestors lived.”

His dialect, thick and rooted, held its own stubborn music in the air of the chapel. It was not the polished speech of courts, but the sort of tongue that keeps names alive when documents burn—yet even he spoke of whole houses and lineages as one speaks of recipes no one makes anymore, remembered more by scent than by words.

“How came the village to be deserted?” asked the General.

“It was troubled by revenants, sir,” he said. “Several were tracked to their graves, there detected by the usual tests, and extinguished in the usual way—by decapitation, by the stake, and by burning—but not until many of the villagers were killed.

“But after all these proceedings according to law,” he continued, with a little shrug that might have been weariness or superstition made practical, “so many graves opened, and so many vampires deprived of their horrible animation—the village was not relieved.

“A Moravian nobleman, traveling this way, heard how matters were; and being skilled—as many are in his country—in such affairs, he offered to deliver the village from its tormentor.

“He did so thus. There being a bright moon that night, he ascended, shortly after sunset, the towers of the chapel here, from whence he could distinctly see the churchyard beneath—one can see it from that window. From this point he watched until he saw the vampire come out of his grave, and place near it the linen clothes in which he had been folded, and then glide away toward the village to plague its inhabitants.

“The stranger, having seen all this, came down from the steeple, took the linen wrappings of the vampire, and carried them up to the top of the tower, which he again mounted. When the vampire returned from his prowling and missed his clothes, he cried furiously to the Moravian—whom he saw at the summit of the tower—and the Moravian, in reply, beckoned him to ascend and take them.

“Whereupon the vampire, accepting his invitation, began to climb the steeple; and so soon as he had reached the battlements, the Moravian, with a stroke of his sword, clove his skull in twain, hurling him down to the churchyard. Descending by the winding stairs, the stranger followed, cut his head off, and next day delivered head and body to the villagers, who duly impaled and burnt them.”

The old man’s voice kept an even pace, as if he were reciting the steps of an inherited craft—watch, take, bait, strike, sever, burn—each action passed down like a grim household instruction for keeping an infestation from returning.

“This Moravian nobleman,” the woodman went on, “had authority from the then head of the family to remove the tomb of Mircalla, Countess Karnstein, which he did effectually, so that in a little while its site was quite forgotten.”

“Can you point out where it stood?” asked the General eagerly, his fury tightened now into a hungry focus.

The woodman shook his head and smiled, not unkindly.

“Not a soul living could tell you that now,” he said. “Besides, they say her body was removed; but no one is sure of that either.”

Time pressed. He dropped his axe as if laying aside an argument already settled, and departed to fetch the ranger, leaving us in the chapel’s chill breath, with the dull, persistent hush of the forest outside and the General’s dreadful purpose still unsaid, hanging in the air like smoke that refuses to clear.
