I turned and followed the spotted leopardess, catching only a fleeting glimpse of her as she vanished over the hillside toward the palace gate. By the time I reached the entrance hall, the princess was in the midst of pulling on the robe she’d left discarded on the floor. Her wounds had stopped bleeding, the blood now crusted and dry from the wind that had chased her across the open ground. When she saw me, a flicker of anger crossed her face—then she looked away. But after a moment, she forced a smile and said, “A small mishap! I heard the cat-woman was back in the city, so I went down to drive her off. But she brought one of her monstrous creatures with her—it leapt at me before I could react, and its claws dug into my neck!”

She shivered, and though I knew she lied, I couldn’t help feeling pity. The wounds were real, and the hollow look in her eyes reminded me of how she’d appeared in the cave beneath the mountain. A pang of guilt stirred within me—why hadn’t I stayed to help her? My expression must have given it away.

“Child of folly,” she said, her voice edged with false sweetness, “you’re not crying, are you? Wait here. I’ll be just a moment. I need something for these scratches.”

But I didn’t wait. I followed close behind. Out of sight, I feared what she might do.

The instant she stepped into the black hall, a low buzzing filled the air—as if hundreds of voices murmured in unison. Then, like a beam of light sweeping across a darkened stage, illumination began to move through the chamber. One section would glow brightly, then fade into shadow, while another emerged, shimmering with sudden clarity. Groups of figures danced, fought, or stood still—scenes I recognized, scenes I had lived or witnessed. And there, in each tableau, was the princess: watching, urging, participating.

The skull-headed dancers moved across the grass in the forest-hall—there she stood at the doorway. In the Evil Wood, the battle raged—she was there, inciting the clash. Yet all the while, she remained motionless before me, head bowed, shoulders slumped.

The shifting light continued, revealing more fragments: the heath, the hollow, the mist curling around her feet as she walked in circles, trying to embrace the vapour that slipped through her grasp. Then, abruptly, the old librarian approached. He paused, stared at her. She collapsed—not physically, but in spirit—her limbs dissolving, her form vanishing into thin air. A scream tore through the chamber, echoing from every wall. At the same moment, the real princess—still standing before me—crumpled to the ground, unconscious.

I turned and fled. No longer would I try to save her.

Standing trembling beside the cage, I understood: in that black ellipsoid, I had entered her mind. I saw the tip of the leopardess’s tail twitch once—then nothing.

Still trying to steady myself, I heard the princess speak beside me. “Come,” she said. “I’ll show you what I need.”

She led me into the courtyard. I followed, numb, half-dreaming. The moon hung high, near the zenith, its silver light brighter than the sun’s absent gold. Through the trees, we came to the tallest—the central tree, unlike the others. Its branches converged at the top, forming a tight cone that seemed to point directly at the sky.

She stood beneath it, gazing upward. “On the very top,” she whispered, almost to herself, “grows a tiny blossom. It would heal my wounds instantly. If only I could become a dove for a moment—I could fly up and fetch it. But look—there’s a serpent among the leaves. Its bite would kill a dove far worse than a tiger’s fang would harm me.” She spat the words out. “How I loathe that cat-woman!”

Then she turned to me, her face softening into one of her most radiant smiles. “Can you climb?”

The smile vanished as quickly as it came. Her expression twisted into sorrow, pain etched deep. I should have walked away. But when she touched her wounded neck, I felt my resolve break.

I studied the tree. Along its trunk, stubs jutted out—remnants of fallen leaves, like the scars of a palm. “I can climb that,” I said.

“Not barefoot,” she insisted. “You’ll fall.”

I had left my sandals in my room, chasing the leopardess. “It doesn’t matter,” I replied. “I’ve walked barefoot for years.”

She knelt, tore the wide embroidered borders from her robe, and bound them tightly around my feet—one at a time. “You’ve left the ends loose,” I said.

“I have no knife to cut them,” she answered. “But they won’t tangle.”

I turned to the tree. The cold wasn’t severe in Bulika after sunset—especially compared to the chill near the sexton’s cottage—but as I climbed, the temperature dropped. Higher I went, colder I grew. Among the branches, my hands and feet turned numb. There was no wind, yet the tree felt unstable—each limb trembled under my touch, as if about to snap.

When I rose above the canopy, bathed in moonlight, I searched for the blossom. Then—suddenly—I was drenched. Water poured over me from nowhere. The next instant, I was thrown violently—spun, tossed, rolled—like a leaf in a storm. I sank, gasping, choking, pulled downward by invisible currents. I tumbled, caught, spun again, until finally I crashed onto solid ground.

“Told you so,” croaked a voice beside my ear.
