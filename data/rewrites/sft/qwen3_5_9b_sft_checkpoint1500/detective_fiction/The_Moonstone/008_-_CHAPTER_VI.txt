Reserving my private thoughts, I respectfully urged Mr. Franklin to proceed. "Do not fidget, Betteredge," he replied, and continued.

His opening remarks informed me that his inquiries regarding the nefarious Colonel and the Diamond commenced with a visit he had paid prior to joining us to the family solicitor at Hampstead. A casual remark dropped by Mr. Franklin, overheard when the two were alone following dinner, disclosed that his father had entrusted him with a birthday gift intended for Miss Rachel. Circumstances unfolded naturally, culminating in the lawyer revealing the true nature of the present and the origins of the friendship between the late Colonel and Mr. Blake, senior. The facts were so extraordinary that I doubted my ability to render justice to them through language alone; thus, I preferred to report Mr. Franklin's findings in his own words.

"You recall the time, Betteredge," he said, "when my father sought to establish his claim to that ill-fated Dukedom? It coincided with my uncle Herncastle's return from India. My father discerned that his brother-in-law held specific documents potentially vital to his litigation. He visited the Colonel under the guise of welcoming him home. However, the Colonel was not easily deceived. 'You seek something,' he declared, 'otherwise you would never compromise your reputation by visiting *me*.' Realizing this was his only leverage, my father immediately admitted his desire for the papers. The Colonel requested a day to deliberate. His response arrived in the form of a most peculiar letter, which my friend the lawyer subsequently showed me.

"The Colonel began by stating his need for something from my father, proposing an exchange of friendly services. The fortunes of war, he noted, had placed him in possession of one of the world's largest diamonds. He harbored grave suspicions that neither he nor his precious jewel remained safe within any household or region where they coexisted. Under these alarming circumstances, he resolved to entrust the Diamond to another individual. This recipient would incur no risk; he might deposit the stone in any heavily fortified location reserved for high-value assets, such as a banker's or jeweler's vault. His sole personal obligation would be passive. Either personally or via a trusted agent, he was to deliver an annual note to a prearranged address on specific dates, merely certifying that he was a living man. Should the date pass without receipt of this note, silence would signify the Colonel's death by murder. In that singular event, and none other, certain sealed instructions regarding the Diamond's disposition would be opened and executed. If my father accepted this strange charge, the Colonel's papers would become his property."

"What action did your father take, sir?" I inquired.

"Do?" Mr. Franklin exclaimed. "I will tell you. He applied the invaluable faculty known as common sense to the Colonel's proposal. To him, the entire arrangement was sheer absurdity. Somewhere during his travels in India, the Colonel had stumbled upon some wretched crystal mistaken for a diamond. Regarding the alleged threat of murder and the elaborate precautions designed to preserve both his life and his piece of glass, he reasoned that we lived in the nineteenth century; any sane man would simply consult the police. Furthermore, the Colonel was a notorious opium addict. If acquiring the valuable papers necessitated accepting a matter involving opium as fact, my father was entirely willing to shoulder this ridiculous responsibility, especially as it entailed no trouble for himself. Consequently, the Diamond and the sealed instructions were deposited in his banker's strongroom, while the Colonel's periodic letters confirming his survival were received and opened by our family lawyer, Mr. Bruff, acting as my father's representative. No sensible person in a similar position could have viewed the matter differently. Nothing in this world, Betteredge, is probable unless it aligns with our own trite experience; we only embrace a romance when we witness it in a newspaper."

It was evident to me that Mr. Franklin regarded his father's judgment of the Colonel as hasty and erroneous.

"What is your own private opinion of the matter, sir?" I asked.

"Let us first conclude the story of the Colonel," Mr. Franklin suggested. "There is a curious lack of systematic thought in the English mind, Betteredge, and your question, my old friend, serves as an example thereof. When we are not engaged in constructing machinery, we are, mentally speaking, the most slovenly people in the universe."

"So much," I reflected internally, "for a foreign education! He has presumably learned that manner of gazing at us from France."

Mr. Franklin resumed the interrupted narrative.

"My father obtained the papers he desired and never saw his brother-in-law again thereafter. For years, on the prearranged dates, the prearranged letter arrived from the Colonel and was opened by Mr. Bruff. I have seen the pile of letters, all composed in the same brief, business-like phrasing: 'Sir,—This is to certify that I am still a living man. Let the Diamond be. John Herncastle.' That was the entirety of his correspondence, delivered regularly until some six or eight months ago, when the format varied for the first time. It read: 'Sir,—They inform me I am dying. Come to me, and assist me in drafting my will.' Mr. Bruff proceeded to the little suburban villa, surrounded by gardens wherein the Colonel had resided alone since returning from India. The estate was populated by dogs, cats, and birds for companionship, yet devoid of any human soul save the domestic worker who came daily and the physician at the bedside. The will was remarkably simple. The Colonel had squandered the majority of his fortune on chemical investigations. The will consisted of three clauses dictated from his bed, his faculties perfectly intact. The first provision ensured the safekeeping and support of his animals. The second established a professorship of experimental chemistry at a northern university. The third bequeathed the Moonstone as a birthday present to his niece, contingent upon my father acting as executor."

"At first, my father declined to serve as executor," I noted. "On second thought, however, he relented, partly because he was assured the role would entail no trouble, and partly because Mr. Bruff suggested, in Rachel's interest, that the Diamond might indeed possess value after all."

"Did the Colonel provide any reason, sir," I inquired, "for bequeathing the Diamond to Miss Rachel?"

"He not only gave the reason; he inscribed it in his will," said Mr. Franklin. "I possess an extract which I shall show you presently. Do not be slovenly-minded, Betteredge! Attend to one thing at a time. You have heard of the Colonel's Will; now you must learn what transpired following his demise. Formally, the Diamond required valuation before the Will could be proven. Every jeweler consulted immediately corroborated the Colonel's assertion that he held one of the largest diamonds in the world. Accurately valuing it presented significant difficulties. Its size rendered it a phenomenon in the diamond market; its color placed it in a unique category; furthermore, to compound these uncertainties, there existed a defect—a flaw—in the very heart of the stone. Yet, despite this serious drawback, the lowest estimate offered was twenty thousand pounds. Imagine my father's astonishment! He had stood on the brink of refusing the executorship and allowing this magnificent jewel to vanish from the family. The keen interest he now took in the affair compelled him to open the sealed instructions deposited alongside the Diamond. Mr. Bruff displayed this document to me, along with the other papers, and it suggests, to my mind, a clue to the nature of the conspiracy that menaced the Colonel's life."

"Then you do believe, sir," I said, "that a conspiracy existed?"

"Not lacking my father's excellent common sense," answered Mr. Franklin, "I believe the Colonel's life was threatened precisely as he claimed. The sealed instructions, in my estimation, elucidate how he ultimately died quietly in his bed. Had his death resulted from violence—that is to say, had the regular letter failed to arrive on the appointed date—my father would have been directed to send the Moonstone secretly to Amsterdam. There, it was to be deposited with a renowned diamond cutter, sliced into four to six separate stones. These fragments were then to be sold for whatever sum they fetched, and the proceeds applied to funding the aforementioned professorship of experimental chemistry, which the Colonel subsequently endowed via his Will. Now, Betteredge, deploy those sharp wits of yours and observe the conclusion to which the Colonel's instructions point!"

I immediately applied my intellect. It was of the slovenly English variety; consequently, it muddled the situation until Mr. Franklin intervened and guided it toward clarity.

"Observe," said Mr. Franklin, "how the integrity of the Diamond as a whole stone is artfully made dependent upon the preservation of the Colonel's life from violence. He is not content merely to declare to his enemies, 'Slay me—and you will remain no closer to the Diamond than you are now; it lies where you cannot reach it—in the guarded strongroom of a bank.' Instead, he declares, 'Slay me—and the Diamond shall cease to be the Diamond; its identity will be obliterated.' What does this signify?"

Here, I believed, flashed the wonderful foreign brilliance.

"I comprehend," I stated. "It implies diminishing the value of the stone and defrauding the rogues thereby!"

"Absolutely not," Mr. Franklin countered. "I have investigated this thoroughly. A flawed diamond, cut into pieces, would actually command a higher price than the diamond in its current state. Consider this obvious reason: from four to six perfect brilliants could be cut from it, whose collective value would exceed that of the large, albeit imperfect, single stone.
