III. It always unfolded the same way. On the evening of her annual ball, Mrs. Julius Beaufort never missed a performance at the Opera. In fact, she timed her grand event precisely to coincide with an opera night—proof, as society saw it, of her effortless grace and supreme command over domestic affairs. Her household ran so flawlessly that she could leave everything in capable hands, trusting her staff to manage every detail while she graced the stage boxes with her presence. The Beauforts’ home was one of the few in New York to boast a dedicated ballroom—one older even than Mrs. Manson Mingott’s or the Headly Chiverses’. At a time when hosting a ball meant hastily covering floors with "crash" paper and hauling furniture upstairs, the Beauforts maintained a room reserved solely for dancing, left dark and still for all but one night a year. Its gilt chairs stood stacked in corners, its chandelier wrapped in cloth—yet this very extravagance was seen as a mark of distinction, a quiet triumph over the modesty others now deemed fashionable.

Mrs. Archer, known for distilling social truths into neat aphorisms, once remarked: “We all have our pet common people.” Though bold, the sentiment resonated quietly in many privileged hearts. But the Beauforts were not merely common—they were something else entirely. Some whispered they were worse. Still, Mrs. Beaufort came from one of America’s most respected lineages. She had been Regina Dallas, a breathtaking beauty from South Carolina, introduced to New York society by her cousin Medora Manson—a woman famed for her reckless kindness. To be linked to the Mansons and Rushworths granted one a *droit de cité* in elite circles, as Mr. Sillerton Jackson once put it, recalling his days at the Tuileries. But did that right vanish upon marrying Julius Beaufort?

Who, then, was Beaufort? He passed as English—charming, handsome, sharp-tongued, generous, and quick-witted. He arrived in America with letters from Mrs. Manson Mingott’s English son-in-law, a banker, and swiftly carved out a powerful role in finance. Yet he lived recklessly, spoke bitterly, and carried secrets like shadows. When Medora announced her cousin’s engagement to him, it was another entry in her long ledger of imprudent acts. But time often vindicates folly. Two years after their marriage, young Mrs. Beaufort presided over what was widely acknowledged as the most celebrated house in New York. No one knew exactly how she achieved it. She seemed idle, passive—some called her dull—but dressed like a goddess, draped in pearls, growing ever more radiant with each passing season, she reigned in her husband’s imposing brownstone, drawing the city’s elite without lifting a finger.

The truth, those in the know suspected, was that Beaufort himself managed everything—the staff, the chef, the gardeners, the guest list, even the punch and the notes his wife sent. But he performed these tasks behind closed doors, presenting instead the image of a careless millionaire strolling into his own drawing-room as if he were a visitor, remarking casually, “My wife’s gloxinias are a marvel, aren’t they? I believe she gets them from Kew.” His secret, everyone agreed, was his flair for making things seem effortless. Rumors swirled—he’d fled England under scandalous circumstances, perhaps aided by the international bank where he’d once worked. But Beaufort swept such whispers aside as lightly as any other. With his charm and confidence, he carried the world along with him. For over two decades, saying “going to the Beauforts” had become as natural as visiting Mrs. Manson Mingott, though with the added pleasure of savoring hot canvas-back ducks and aged wines instead of lukewarm champagne and reheated croquettes.

That evening, Mrs. Beaufort appeared in her box just before the Jewel Song, as she always did. And when, as expected, she rose at the end of the third act, wrapped herself in her opera cloak, and vanished into the wings, New York understood: in half an hour, the ball would begin.

The Beaufort mansion was a source of pride for New Yorkers, especially on this night. They were among the first to own their own red velvet carpet, rolled down the steps by their own footmen beneath their own awning—no longer renting it with supper and chairs. They also pioneered letting ladies remove their cloaks in the hall, rather than shuffling up to the hostess’s bedroom to re-curl their hair under gaslight. Beaufort reportedly said he assumed all his wife’s friends had maids who ensured proper coiffures before leaving home.

Their house was boldly designed around the ballroom, allowing guests to march through a sequence of elegant rooms—the sea-green, the crimson, and the bouton d'or—each leading deeper into opulence. From afar, the polished parquetry reflected the many-candled lustres, and beyond lay a conservatory where camellias and tree-ferns formed a lush canopy over black-and-gold bamboo seats.

Newland Archer arrived somewhat late, as befitted his station. He handed his overcoat to the silk-stockinged footmen—though the stockings were one of Beaufort’s minor vanities—and lingered a moment in the library, lined with Spanish leather and furnished with Buhl and malachite, where men were chatting and adjusting their dancing gloves. Finally, he joined the line of guests being received by Mrs. Beaufort at the threshold of the crimson drawing-room.

Archer felt distinctly uneasy. He hadn’t returned to his club after the opera—as most young men did—but had walked partway up Fifth Avenue before turning back. He feared the Mingotts might have gone too far—perhaps even brought Granny Mingott’s orders to bring the Countess Olenska to the ball. The tone in the club box had made clear how disastrous that would be. Though he remained resolved to “see the thing through,” he no longer felt quite so noble about defending his fiancée’s cousin after their brief exchange at the opera.

He wandered into the bouton d'or drawing-room, where Beaufort had dared hang *Love Victorious*, Bouguereau’s controversial nude. There, he found Mrs. Welland and her daughter near the ballroom door. Couples already glided across the floor beyond—tulle skirts swirling, candlelight catching on delicate blossoms in girls’ hair, on the sparkling aigrettes of newly married women, on polished shirt-fronts and fresh *glacé* gloves.

Miss Welland stood at the threshold, lilies-of-the-valley in hand (she carried nothing else), her face pale, her eyes alight with honest excitement. A group of young people gathered around her, laughing, clasping hands, exchanging teasing remarks. Mrs. Welland stood slightly apart, casting a look of measured approval. It was clear Miss Welland was announcing her engagement, while her mother played the part of reluctant parent, as tradition demanded.

Archer paused. It had been his wish that the announcement be made—but not like this. To declare their happiness in the clamor of a crowded ballroom felt like stripping it of its sacred privacy. His joy was deep enough that the surface mattered little—but he wished the surface had remained untouched. He was pleased to see May shared this feeling. Her eyes met his, pleading: *Remember—we’re doing this because it’s right.* No appeal could stir him more deeply. Yet he wished their reason had been something pure, something ideal—not merely poor Ellen Olenska.

The group parted for him with knowing smiles. After accepting their congratulations, he led his betrothed into the center of the ballroom and slipped his arm around her waist. “Now we won’t have to talk,” he said, smiling into her candid eyes as they drifted to the rhythm of the Blue Danube.

She didn’t speak. Her lips curved into a smile, but her gaze remained distant, fixed on some unseen vision. “Dear,” he whispered, pulling her closer. It struck him then that even in a ballroom, the first hours of engagement held a solemnity, almost a sacrament. What a new life it would be—with this purity, this light, this goodness beside him!

When the dance ended, the couple moved into the conservatory. Seated behind a screen of ferns and camellias, Archer pressed her gloved hand to his lips. “You see,” she said softly, “I did as you asked.”

“Yes,” he replied, smiling. “I couldn’t wait.” After a pause, he added, “Only I wish it hadn’t had to be here—at a ball.”

“Yes, I know,” she answered, meeting his glance with understanding. “But after all—even here, we’re alone, aren’t we?”

“Oh, dearest—always!” he exclaimed. Clearly, she would always understand; she would always say the perfect thing. The realization flooded him with joy. “The worst of it is,” he went on cheerfully, “that I want to kiss you and I can’t.”

As he spoke, he glanced around the conservatory, confirmed their privacy, and drew her close, pressing a fleeting kiss to her lips. To balance the daring, he led her to a bamboo sofa in a less secluded corner and sat beside her, plucking a lily-of-the-valley from her bouquet.

She remained silent. The world stretched out before them like a sunlit valley.

“Did you tell my cousin Ellen?” she asked after a moment, as if speaking through a dream.

He blinked, remembering he hadn’t. An instinctive reluctance to speak of such matters to the foreign woman had kept the words from forming. “No—I didn’t get the chance,” he lied quickly.

“Oh.” She looked disappointed, but resolute. “You must. I didn’t either, and I wouldn’t want her to think—”

“Of course not,” he said. “But shouldn’t you be the one to tell her?”

She considered. “If I’d done it at the right time, yes. But now that there’s been a delay, I think you should explain that I asked you to tell her at the opera—before we told everyone here. Otherwise, she might think I forgot her. You see, she’s family. And she’s been away so long… she’s rather sensitive.”

Archer looked at her with warmth. “Dear and great angel! Of course I’ll tell her.” He glanced toward the crowded ballroom, a hint of unease crossing his face. “But I haven’t seen her yet. Has she come?”

“No,” May replied. “At the last minute, she decided not to.”

“At the last minute?” he echoed, surprised she’d ever considered coming.

“Yes,” she said simply. “She loves dancing. But suddenly she decided her dress wasn’t smart enough for a ball—though we thought it was lovely. So Auntie had to take her home.”

“Oh, well,” Archer said, brushing it off with cheerful indifference. Nothing pleased him more than her steadfast adherence to the ritual of overlooking the “unpleasant”—a habit they both cherished. *She knows the real reason Ellen stayed away,* he thought, *but I’ll never let her see that I’m aware of the shadow hanging over her cousin’s reputation.*
