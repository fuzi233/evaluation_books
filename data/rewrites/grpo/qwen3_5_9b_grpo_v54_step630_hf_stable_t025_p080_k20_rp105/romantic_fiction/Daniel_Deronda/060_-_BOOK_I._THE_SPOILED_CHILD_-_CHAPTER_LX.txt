"Let us bind love with duty; for duty is the love of law; and law is the nature of the Eternal."

These words echoed in Daniel Deronda's mind as he stood before the white-bearded man in the quiet room of the Schuster Strasse in Mainz. The air smelled of old paper and the faint, dusty scent of travel. Joseph Kalonymos sat at a table scattered with open letters, his hands moving with a practiced rhythm until the knock on the door broke the silence. When he saw Deronda, the older man did not immediately rise or offer a handshake. Instead, he looked up with small, penetrating eyes that glittered like black gems against his pale, yellowish skin.

"It is now you who seek me, young man," Kalonymos said in German, his voice sharp yet warm.

"Yes," Deronda replied, feeling the weight of gratitude settle in his chest. "I seek you as a friend of my grandfather's. I owe you a debt for the trouble you have taken on my behalf." He spoke the language with ease, the foreign accents of his upbringing melting into the fluid German tongue.

Kalonymos smiled, extending a hand this time. "Then you are no longer angry at being something more than an Englishman?"

"On the contrary," Deronda said, accepting the gesture. "Thank you for saving me from ignorance of my parentage, and for guarding the chest my grandfather left in trust."

"Sit," Kalonymos urged, gesturing to a chair nearby. He removed his hat, revealing a head thick with white hair, and stroked his beard as he examined Deronda's face. The moment struck Deronda with the force of an electric shock. To be in the presence of a man who had sworn loyalty to his grandfather—a man who, though dead, seemed to speak through these written memorials—felt like touching the very chain of his ancestry. He bore Kalonymos's scrutinizing gaze with a mixture of delight and awe, a sensation akin to witnessing a sacred ritual.

In Hebrew, Kalonymos quoted a hymn: "As thy goodness has been great to the former generations, even so may it be to the latter." He paused, then continued, his voice trembling slightly with emotion. "Young man, I rejoice that I was not yet gone on my travels when you arrived. I see the image of my friend as he was in his youth—no longer perverted from the fellowship of your people, no longer shrinking in proud wrath from the touch of one who claimed you as a Jew. You come with thankfulness to claim the kindred and heritage that wicked contrivance would have stolen. You declare, 'I am the grandson of Daniel Charisi.' Is it not so?"

"Assuredly it is," Deronda confirmed. "But let me say, I would never have treated a Jew with incivility simply because he was a Jew. I merely shrank from telling a stranger, 'I know nothing of my mother.'"

"A sin, a sin!" Kalonymos exclaimed, closing his eyes in disgust. "A robbery of our people! But it is frustrated. I have frustrated it. When Daniel Charisi was a stripling and I a boy little above his shoulder, we made a solemn vow to be friends. He said, 'Let us bind ourselves with duty, as if we were sons of the same mother.' That was his bent—to fortify his soul with bonds. He believed duty was the love of law, and law the nature of the Eternal. We bound ourselves. Though we drifted apart in later life, the bond never broke. When they sought to rob him, they could not rob him of me. I rescued the remainder of him he prized for his offspring, and I have restored the offspring they stole."

Kalonymos left the room briefly and returned with a clerk carrying a chest. It was not large, but heavy with ornamental gilt iron bracers and handles. The wood was beautifully incised with Arabic lettering.

"So!" Kalonymos said, returning to his seat. "And here is the curious key." He produced it from a small leather bag and handed it to Deronda with a monitory, slightly suspicious look—the way age trusts youth with fragile treasures. "Bestow it carefully. I trust you are methodic and wary."

"I shall be more careful of this than of any other property," Deronda smiled, tucking the key into his breast pocket. "I have never possessed anything that signifies so much cherished hope and effort. And I shall never forget that this effort was partly yours."

"Do you have time to hear more of my grandfather? Or am I trespassing?" Deronda asked.

"Stay yet a while," Kalonymos said, glancing at his watch. "In an hour and eighteen minutes I depart for Trieste. My sons will soon expect my attention. Would you like me to introduce you to them? They dwell here in ease and luxury, though I choose to be a wanderer."

"I would be glad if you commended me to their acquaintance for a future opportunity," Deronda replied. "Pressing claims call me to England—friends in need of my presence. I have been away too long by circumstance. But to know more of you and your family would be motive enough to return to Mainz."

"Good! You will hardly find me, for I am beyond my threescore years and ten, a wanderer carrying my shroud with me. But my sons and their children dwell here in wealth and unity. The days have changed since Karl the Great brought my ancestors from Italy to share knowledge with our rough German brethren. We fought for it. Our youth fell on evil days, but we won. We increase our wealth in safety, and the learning of all Germany is fed by Jewish brains—even if they do not always keep their Jewish hearts. Have you been left ignorant of your people's life, young man?"

"No," Deronda admitted. "Before I suspected my parentage, I was led to study everything belonging to their history with more interest than any other subject. It turns out I have been making myself ready to understand my grandfather." He felt a surge of relief; the conversation was circling back to what mattered most.

"So," Kalonymos mused, studying Deronda's face. "You would perhaps have been such a man as he if your education had not hindered. You are like him in features, yet not altogether. He had an iron will in his face; it braced everyone around him. Even young, he had a deep upright line in his brow. I see none of that in you."

Daniel Charisi used to say, "Better a wrong will than a wavering; better a steadfast enemy than an uncertain friend; better a false belief than no belief at all." He despised indifference. He had longer reasons than I can give you."

"Yet his knowledge was not narrow?" Deronda asked, thinking of the common excuse for indecision—that it stems from knowing too much.

"Narrow? No," Kalonymos shook his head with a compassionate smile. "From childhood, he drank in learning as easily as a plant sucks up water. But he early took to medicine and theories about life and health. He traveled to many countries, spending much of his substance to see and know. What he insisted on was that the strength of mankind depended on the balance of separateness and communication. He was bitterly against our people losing themselves among the Gentiles. 'It's no better,' he said, 'than many sorts of grain going back from their variety into sameness.' He mingled all sorts of learning, like our Arabic writers in the golden time. We studied together, but he went beyond me. Though we were bosom friends, we were as different as the inside and outside of a bowl. I stood by two notions of my own; I took Charisi's sayings as one takes the shape of trees—they were there, not to be disputed. It came to the same thing in both of us: we were both faithful Jews, thankful not to be Gentiles."

Kalonymos leaned forward, his voice softening. "Since I was a ripe man, I have been what I am now, for all but age—loving to wander, loving transactions, loving to behold all things, and caring nothing about hardship. Charisi thought continually of our people's future; he went with all his soul into that part of our religion. I, not so. So we have freedom; I am content. Our people wandered before they were driven. When I am in the East, I lie on the deck and watch the greater stars. The sight satisfies me. I know them as they rise, and hunger not to know more. Charisi was satisfied with no sight; he pieced it out with what had been before and what would come after. Yet we loved each other. As he said, he bound our love with duty; we solemnly pledged to help and defend each other to the last. I have fulfilled my pledge."

Kalonymos rose, and Deronda followed suit. "And in being faithful to him, you have caused justice to be done to me. It would have been a robbery of me too to never know of the inheritance he prepared. I thank you with my whole soul."

"Be worthy of him, young man. What is your vocation?" Kalonymos asked abruptly, a question that made Deronda hesitate. He did not feel entirely honest to claim law-reading as a vocation yet.

"I cannot say that I have any," Deronda answered.

"Get one, get one. The Jew must be diligent. You will call yourself a Jew and profess the faith of your fathers?" Kalonymos placed a hand on Deronda's shoulder, his gaze piercing.

"I shall call myself a Jew," Deronda said deliberately, feeling his cheeks grow pale under the scrutiny. "But I will not say I shall profess to believe exactly as my fathers have believed. Our fathers themselves changed the horizon of their belief and learned of other races. But I think I can maintain my grandfather's notion of separateness with communication. I hold that my first duty is to my own people, and if there is anything to be done toward restoring or perfecting their common life, I shall make that my vocation."

At that moment, the need for speech crystallized into resolve. His respect for the questioner prevented him from declining, and in the necessity of answering, he found the truth for himself.

"Ah, you argue and you look forward—you are Daniel Charisi's grandson," Kalonymos said, adding a benediction in Hebrew.

With that, they parted. Almost as soon as Deronda was back in London, the aged man was once again on shipboard, greeting the friendly stars without any eager curiosity, content in his wandering solitude.
