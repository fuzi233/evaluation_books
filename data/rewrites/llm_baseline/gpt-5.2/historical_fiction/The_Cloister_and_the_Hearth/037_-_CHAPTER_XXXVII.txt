They set the three of them up in the market-place, pinioned like felons on a painter’s board, arms strained back, shoulders wrenched, so that the very posture made confession seem to creep out of the bones. The town had ordained two full hours of this misery, not for punishment alone, but for recognition: that any man or woman who had met these night-prowlers on a lonely road, and carried the bruise of it in memory or flesh, might come forward and add fresh crimes to the count.

Yet the hours wore through, measured by the marketplace’s own small noises—the slap of sandals on cobble, the creak of carts, the shrill calls of hawkers, the sparrows quarreling in the eaves—and no new voice rose to accuse. It was not so strange: such men hunted weary strangers, and strangers, once plundered, went on, nursing their fear in another parish and another tongue.

At last the officers stepped in to take them down.

Then, as the ropes were loosened, a scream cleft the air—one of those cries that makes every sound after it seem thin and guilty. A woman in the crowd thrust out her hand and pointed, eyes wide as if they would leave her skull; her lips shaped a name, a word, a whole tale—and she fell like a sack, senseless on the stones.

People surged to her, some with pity, more with that sharp appetite a crowd has for calamity. As she came back to herself, their guesses bubbled and collided.

“’Twas him she marked.”

“Nay, this one.”

“No, no,” cried another, “yon hangdog with the hair down round his neck.”

The woman’s mind cleared in a flash—cleared and broke. She sprang up and flew, not at the pinioned man’s face but at his throat, like a mother-beast. “My child!” she cried. “Man! man! give me back my child!” And with both hands she seized the glossy golden hair the officers had hung about the culprit’s neck—some trophy of the road—and tore it free. She pressed it to her mouth, kissed it again and again as if warmth might return to it by sheer will.

Then the token told its own truth: hair without the head, a relic without the living. She sank down shrieking, clutching it to her breast, sobbing till her whole body shook over those poor locks as over a grave.

The market, that had been a market an instant before, turned wolf. A low growl ran through it; men’s hands went to their girdles. In those days every man carried death close, and the difference between justice and slaughter was often no thicker than a breath.

The assassin would have been torn and hacked where he stood, but Denys was on them like steel drawn from a sheath. He whipped out his sword, planted himself, and shouted, “A moi, camarades!” Other archers sprang in, and their blades made a hedge. “Who lays a finger on him dies,” Denys cried, and his voice, hard as a struck bell, rang above the snarling.

“What vengeance is this?” he called to them. “Would you be so mad as rob the wheel, and give the vermin an easy death?”

Words did little; naked iron did more. The crowd held back, lips pulled from teeth, eyes bright with the lust to strike. Seeing how the temper ran, the municipal officers drew close; with archers and staves they made a guard and carried the prisoners back toward gaol, prudently, before the market-place could boil over.

They went amid hoots that struck officer and culprit alike. Denys watched them safely lodged, and then, thinking of Gerard and the strange mishap of that dawn, turned his steps toward the White Hart; he expected to find his comrade there.

On the way he glanced up and saw two girls at a first-floor window, hands busy at their work. Their cheeks were high-colored, their laughter ready. Denys saluted; they smiled; words flew as easily as swallows under an eave. Their voices braided with the street’s music—hoofbeats, a distant hammer, a peddler’s chant—as if they belonged to the town’s own soundscape.

He invited them to eat at the White Hart. They refused. He accepted the refusal with the art of a man who knows refusal is sometimes merely the first seasoning. They consented.

And in that bright, unguarded company he let the thought of Gerard slip away like a coin through a careless finger.

Meanwhile Gerard, the very man forgotten, was marched off toward gaol. His heart had been a trapped bird at first; then, step by step, it beat itself steadier. He stopped short in the street and demanded, with such dignity as fear allows, by what authority he was taken.

“By the vice-baillie’s,” said the constable.

“The vice-baillie?” Gerard’s voice faltered into bewilderment. “Alas! what have I, a stranger, done to offend a vice-baillie? This charge of sorcery must be a blind. No sorcerer am I; but a poor true lad far from his home.”

The vagueness displeased the officer. “Show him the capias, Jacques,” he said.

Jacques held the writ out in both hands at arm’s length as if paper itself might scorch him; at the same moment the big constable pinned Gerard, sudden and rough. Both were on edge, guarding the document with superstitious care, fearing he might snatch it and work some devilry by touching it.

Gerard had no such thought. Indeed, he looked on it as on a snake. He only craned his neck and read. To his astonishment, the name signing it was the very alderman who had spoken him fair before. Courage stirred. He told them there must be error and demanded to be taken before the alderman.

“What say you to that, Jacques?” asked the constable, hesitating.

“Impossible. We have no orders to take him before his worship. Read the writ!”

“Nay, but good kind fellows,” Gerard pleaded, “what harm can it be? I will give you each an ecu.”

The constable’s eyes flicked. “Jacques, what say you to that?”

“Humph! I say we have no orders not to take him to his worship. Read the writ!”

“Then say we take him to prison round by his worship.”

It was agreed with the speed that silver gives. The money passed; they told Gerard, with the air of men performing a kindness, that he should be grateful too. He tried to show it, but gratitude, feigned, stuck in his throat like dry crust. He could not season his face with falsehood to please another man’s appetite.

So he came again into the alderman’s presence, heart thudding like a pestle in a mortar, and begged in a voice that shook to know what he had done since he had left that very room with Manon and Denys.

“Nought that I know of,” said the alderman.

When the writ was shown, he nodded. “I signed it at daybreak. I get old, and my memory faileth me: in discussing of the girl I quite forgot your own offence. But I remember now. All is well. You are he I committed for sorcery. Stay! ere you go to gaol, you shall hear what your accuser says: run and fetch him, you.”

The messenger fumbled about and could not find the man at once. The alderman, growing impatient, turned back to Gerard and told him bluntly the charge: he was said to have set a dead body burning with diabolical fire—flame that shone and yet did not consume. “And if ’tis true, young man,” the alderman added, “I’m sorry for thee; for thou wilt assuredly burn with fire of good pine logs in the market-place of Neufchasteau.”

The words brought in their own smell—the resinous bite of pitch, the thick smoke that catches in the throat. Gerard’s mouth went dry. “Oh, sir, for pity’s sake let me have speech with his reverence the curé.”

The alderman advised against it. “The Church is harder upon sorcerers than is the corporation.”

“But, sir, I am innocent,” Gerard burst out, somewhere between a snarl and a whine, shame and terror wrestling on one tongue.

“Oh, if you think you are innocent—officer, go with him to the curé; but see he ’scape you not. Innocent, quotha?”

They found the curé not in stole and dignity but in his doublet, mending a wheelbarrow as calmly as if no man in his parish ever faced a stake. Gerard poured out the whole matter—his phosphorus, his fright, his desperate trick—and pleaded as if pleading could season fate. “Just for using a little phosphorus in self-defence against cut-throats they are going to hang.”

It stood Gerard in good stead that he had already told the tale at length; the priest had the facts in hand, and could grasp them where the corporation clutched at mere terror. The curé put on a grave face and said aloud, “I must question you privately on this untoward business.” He led Gerard to a small room, bade the officer stand without and guard the door, and be ready if called.

The big constable remained outside, quaking, as though the room might lift away on a gust of brimstone and leave only a stench behind.

The moment the door closed, the curé’s countenance unlocked; his eyes brightened with pure curiosity. “Show me the trick on’t,” he said.

“I cannot, sir, unless the room be darkened.”

The curé shut the wooden shutter and made twilight. “Now, then.”

“But on what shall I put it?” Gerard asked. “Here is no dead face. ’Twas that made it look so dire.” The priest groped, found a carved figure. “Good; here is an image: ’tis my patron saint.”

“Heaven forbid!” Gerard recoiled. “That were profanation.”

“Pshaw! ’twill rub off, will’t not?”

“Ay, but it goes against me to take such liberty with a saint,” Gerard said, and his scruple was honest—his conscience had a dialect of its own, not easily translated into another man’s impatience.

“Fiddlestick!” said the divine.

“To be sure,” Gerard argued, trying to make devotion serve his case, “by putting it on his holiness will show your reverence it is no Satanic art.”

“Mayhap ’twas for that I did propose it,” the curé replied, shrewd as any lawyer under a cassock.

Thus heartened, Gerard took his bottle and, with careful finger, laid the substance where he had laid it before—eyes, nostrils, hair. In the half-dark the saint’s gaze sprang into a sharp, eerie light; the nostrils flared as if breathing fire; patches of hair kindled and shone, and then the whole face glimmered like a glow-worm pinned to wood. The curé jumped back, and even in his readiness to laugh he crossed himself.

“By’r Lady,” he cried, “’tis strange; and small my wonder they took you for a magician, seeing a dead face thus fired. Now come thy ways with me!”

He flung on his grey gown and great hat, and in a few minutes they stood again before the alderman. Beside him—like a spice too bitter for any stew—stood the accuser: a singular figure in red hose and red shoes, black gown with blue bands, and cocked hat. His very clothes seemed to shout, as if he tried to win authority by loud colors when his truth was pale.

The curé saluted the alderman, then turned on the man with a good-humored contempt. “So, Mangis, at thy work again, babbling away honest men’s lives! Come, your worship, this is the old tale! Two of a trade can ne’er agree. Here is Mangis, who professes sorcery, and would sell himself to Satan to-night, but that Satan is not so weak as buy what he can have gratis—this Mangis, who would be a sorcerer, but is only a quacksalver—accuses of magic a true lad, who did but use in self-defence a secret of chemistry well-known to me and all churchmen.”

“But he is no churchman,” objected the alderman, “to dabble in such mysteries.”

“He is more churchman than layman,” the curé returned instantly, “being convent bred, and in the lesser orders. Therefore, sorcerer, withdraw thy plaint without more words!”

Mangis stiffened. “That I will not, your reverence. A sorcerer I am, but a white one, not a black one. I make no pact with Satan, but on the contrary still battle him with lawful and necessary arts. I ne’er profane the sacraments, as do the black sorcerers, nor turn myself into a cat and go sucking infants’ blood, nor e’en their breath, nor set dead men o’ fire. I but tell the peasants when their cattle and their hens are possessed, and at what time of the moon to plant rye, and what days in each month are lucky for wooing of women and selling of bullocks and so forth: above all, it is my art and my trade to detect the black magicians—as I did that whole tribe of them who were burnt at Dol but last year.”

“Ay, Mangis,” said the curé, and his voice sharpened like a knife on a stone. “And what is the upshot of that famous fire thy tongue did kindle?”

“Why, their ashes were cast to the wind.”

“Ay. But the true end of thy comedy is this. The parliament of Dijon hath since sifted the matter, and found they were no sorcerers, but good and peaceful citizens; and but last week did order masses to be said for their souls, and expiatory farces and mysteries to be played for them in seven towns of Burgundy; all which will not of those cinders make men and women again.”

The curé leaned forward, staff in hand. “Now ’tis our custom in this land, when we have slain the innocent by hearkening false knaves like thee, not to blame our credulous ears, but the false tongue that gulled them. Therefore bethink thee that, at a word from me to my lord bishop, thou wilt smell burning pine nearer than e’er knave smelt it and lived, and wilt travel on a smoky cloud to him whose heart thou bearest—for the word devil in the Latin it meaneth ‘false accuser’—and whose livery thou wearest.”

He pointed his staff straight at Mangis.

“That is true i’fegs,” said the alderman, eager to join the music of authority, “for red and black be the foul fiendys colours.”

Mangis’s cheek went suddenly the color of parchment, so that his own gaudy hose seemed to mock him. He stammered, “I respect Holy Church and her will; he shall fire the churchyard, and all in it, for me. I do withdraw the plaint.”

“Then withdraw thyself,” said the vice-baillie.

When Mangis had slunk away, the curé’s tone softened into business. He told the alderman that the accused had received the chemical substance from Holy Church and had restored it, by giving it all to him.

“Then ’tis in good hands,” said the alderman. “Young man, you are free. Let me have your reverence’s prayers.”

“Doubt it not! Humph! Vice-baillie,” the curé added, as if recalling a recipe’s missing pinch of salt, “the town owes me four silver franks, this three months and more.”

“They shall be paid, curé, ay, ere the week be out.”

So Church and State parted in decent amity, each having tasted a morsel of power and found it to their liking.

Once in the street, Gerard seized the priest’s hand and kissed it. “Oh, sir—oh, your reverence—you have saved me from the fiery stake. What can I say, what do? what?”

“Nought, foolish lad,” the curé said. “Bounty rewards itself. Natheless—Humph?—I wish I had done’t without leasing. It ill becomes my function to utter falsehoods.”

“Falsehood, sir?” Gerard stared, lost.

“Didst not hear me say thou hadst given me that same phosphorus? ’Twill cost me a fortnight’s penance, that light word.” He sighed, but his eye twinkled with a cunning that knew how to keep accounts between Heaven and earth.

“Nay, nay,” cried Gerard, eager as a man bringing bread to a hungry table. “Now Heaven forbid! That was no falsehood, father: well you knew the phosphorus was yours, is yours.” And he thrust the bottle into the curé’s hand. “But alas, ’tis too poor a gift: will you not take from my purse somewhat for Holy Church?” His eyes shone as he held it out.

“Nay,” said the priest brusquely, and put his hands behind him too quickly. “Not a doit. Fie! fie! art pauper et exul. Come thou rather each day at noon and take thy diet with me; for my heart warms to thee.” And he went off abruptly, hands clasped behind his back.

They itched.

But they itched in vain.

Where there’s a heart there’s a Rubicon.

Gerard, still shaken, hastened to the White Hart to relieve Denys of what he must suppose to be anxiety at so long and strange an absence. The inn breathed out its usual odors—smoke, sour wine, damp straw, meat-fat clinging to the rafters like old gossip. In the common room, Denys sat at his ease, dice rattling, playing with two young women—those same high-colored girls—whose laughter rose and fell with the click of bone on wood.

Something in Gerard tightened. “N’oubliez point la Jeanneton!” he said, coloring.

“What of her?” Denys answered, gayly, shaking the dice as if they were his whole creed.

“She said, ‘Le peu que sont les femmes.’”

“Oh, did she?” Denys turned, smiling at the girls. “And what say you to that, mesdemoiselles?”

“We say that none run women down,” said one, “but such as are too old, or too ill-favoured, or too witless to please them.”

“Witless, quotha?” Gerard flared, lofty with indignation and wounded loyalty. “Wise men have not folly enough to please them, nor madness enough to desire to please them; but ’tis to my comrade I speak, not to you, you brazen toads, that make so free with a man at first sight.”

Denys laughed. “Preach away, comrade. Fling a byword or two at our heads. Know, girls, that he is a very Solomon for bywords. Methinks he was brought up by hand on ’em.”

“Be thy friendship a byword!” Gerard shot back. “The friendship that melts to nought at sight of a farthingale.”

“Malheureux!” cried Denys. “I speak but pellets, and thou answerest daggers.”

“Would I could,” said Gerard. “Adieu.”

“What a little savage!” said one of the girls, with the easy cruelty of the uninvested.

Gerard opened the door, put his head back in, and tossed one more sentence like a stone into their laughter. “I have thought of a byword,” he said spitefully:

“Qui hante femmes et dez
Il mourra en pauvretez.”

“There.” And he slammed the door before any of them could season a reply.

The day’s fear had hollowed him; the quarrel had drained what was left. He went to the bar for a morsel of bread and a cup of wine, craving something simple to anchor him—crust under teeth, warmth down the throat, the humble, faithful comfort that asks no questions.

The landlord would sell nothing less than a pint bottle. Gerard agreed; but when the bottle came, its body seemed to promise more than it gave. He held it up, eyed the glass; saw the cheat: thin where it tapered, thick and treacherous at the bottom.

He pointed it out.

The landlord answered with a supercilious shrug. He did not make bottles; he was not accountable for their shape.

“That we will see presently,” said Gerard. “I will take this thy pint to the vice-baillie.”

“Nay, nay, for Heaven’s sake,” cried the landlord, and his tone changed as swiftly as a sauce saved from burning. “I love to content my customers. If by chance this pint be short, we will charge it and its fellow three sous instead of two sous each.”

“So be it,” Gerard said. “But much I admire that you, the host of so fair an inn, should practise thus. The wine, too, smacketh strongly of spring water.”

“Young sir,” said the landlord, leaning in with the sour frankness of a man long pinched by law and life, “we cut no travellers’ throats at this inn, as they do at most. However, you know all about that. The White Hart is no lion nor bear. Whatever masterful robbery is done here, is done upon the poor host. How then could he live at all if he dealt not a little crooked with the few who pay?”

Gerard objected root and branch: honest trade, small profits, quick returns; neither to cheat nor be cheated.

The landlord sighed as if Gerard had described an inn in paradise where every measure was true and every guest paid gladly. “So might one keep an inn in heaven, but not in Burgundy. When foot soldiers going to the wars are quartered on me, how can I but lose? Two sous per day is their pay, and they eat two sous’ worth, and drink into the bargain. The pardoners are my good friends; but palmers and pilgrims—what think you I gain by them? Marry, a loss. Minstrels and jongleurs draw custom and so claim to pay no score, save for liquor. By the secular monks I neither gain nor lose; but the black and grey friars have made vow of poverty, but not of famine: eat like wolves and give the poor host nought but their prayers—and mayhap not them: how can he tell?”

His grievances spilled like thin broth from an overfilled pot. Weddings no longer filled inns; gentry hired out plate and benches, so even mechanics feasted at home. Wine sold by the cup in the market-place now, under privilege, undercutting those who must buy from growers. The law forbade innkeepers to go into market the first hour; so they bought only refuse. They might buy but three bushels of meal at a time though market came but once a week. Butchers demanded bribes to kill for them.

“Courage,” Gerard said gently, hearing under the rant the worn music of a trade trapped between hunger and statute. “The shoe pinches every trader somewhere.”

“Ay,” groaned the landlord, “but not as it pinches us. If we pay not the merchants we buy of, they can cast us into prison and keep us there till we pay or die. But we cannot cast into prison those who buy our victuals. A traveller’s horse we may keep for his debt—but where? In our stable, eating his head off at our cost. The traveller himself we may keep—but where? Not in gaol; in our own house—and then must we lodge and feed him gratis. Merci; no. Our honestest customers are the thieves. Would to Heaven there were more of them. They look not too close into the shape of the canakin, nor into the host’s reckoning: lightly come, lightly go. Also they spend freely, not knowing but each carouse may be their last.”

He told too of provost’s men who pretended suspicion of noble travellers and demanded searches, forcing the host to “stop their mouths with meat and wine”; and of excisemen—town, royal, parliament—who swarmed like flies, each with a wolf in the belly and a sponge in the gullet. Prices rose; yet inns might not raise their own. Mustard, pepper, salt, firewood—no charge allowed, though none were free. Candle a sou the pound. Salt five sous the stone. Pepper four sous the pound. Mustard twenty deniers the pint. A cook at a hundred sous a year. A waiter thirty, and richer than his master by perquisites.

Even sermons lashed innkeepers: godless for this, godless for that—opening doors during mass—though law forced them to admit travellers at all hours.

A man speaking of himself in general is a babbling brook; of his wrongs, a shining river—rolling on and on.

“Labitur et labetur in omne volubilis aevum.”

The river was cut short by a new flood.

Denys burst in, face livid, voice cracking the air. “I am robbed!” he swore, cursing as if each oath might summon back the coins. “Did those hussies pass this way? Who are they? Where do they bide? They have ta’en my purse and fifteen golden pieces. Raise the hue and cry! Ah! traitresses! vipers! These inns are all guet-apens.”

“There now,” cried the landlord triumphantly to Gerard, as if the loss proved his philosophy as a burnt crust proves a careless oven.

Gerard caught Denys’s arm and begged him to be calm and tell how it happened.

“First one went out on some pretence,” Denys snapped; “then after a while the other went to fetch her back, and neither returning, I clapped hand to purse and found it empty. Ungrateful creatures! I was letting them win it in a gallop; but loaded dice were not quick enough—they must claw it all in a lump.”

Gerard, who still had the alderman’s bench and the curé’s shuttered room in his bones, was for running straight to authority and setting officers on their track.

“Not I,” Denys said. “I hate the law. No: as it came so let it go.”

Gerard would not let it go. At a hint from the landlord—who had his own reasons for wanting thieves chased away from his doorstep—he forced Denys along to the provost-marshal.

That dignitary listened and shook his head. “We have no clue to occasional thieves—girls that work honestly at their needles, till some gull comes and tempts them with an easy booty, and then they pluck him.”

Denys flung up his hands. “Come away!” he cried furiously. “I knew what use a bourgeois would be to me at a pinch.” And he marched off, rage making his steps loud on the boards.

“They are clear of the town ere this,” Gerard said, bitterly.

“Speak no more on’t if you prize my friendship,” Denys answered. Then, mastering himself a little, he added, “I have five pieces with the bailiff, and ten I left with Manon, luckily; or these traitresses had feathered their nest with my last plume.”

Gerard stared at him, and Denys’s anger softened into confession. “What dost gape for so? Nay, I do ill to vent my choler on thee. I’ll tell thee all. Art wiser than I. What saidst thou at the door? No matter. Well, then—I did offer marriage to that Manon.”

Gerard stopped as if struck. “What? You offered her what?”

“Marriage. Is that such a mighty strange thing to offer a wench?”

“’Tis a strange thing to offer to a strange girl in passing.”

“Nay, I am not such a sot as you opine,” Denys said, and there was a kind of grim pride in it. “I saw the corn in all that chaff. I knew I could not get her by fair means, so I was fain to try foul. ‘Mademoiselle,’ said I, ‘marriage is not one of my habits, but struck by your qualities I make an exception; deign to bestow this hand on me.’”

“And she bestowed it on thine ear.”
