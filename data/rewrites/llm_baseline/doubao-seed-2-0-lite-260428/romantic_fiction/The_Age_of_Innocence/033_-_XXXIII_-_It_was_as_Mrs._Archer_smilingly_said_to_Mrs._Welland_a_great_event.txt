It was, as Mrs. Archer smilingly said to Mrs. Welland over their teacups that golden autumn afternoon, a landmark event in the life of any young New York couple: hosting their very first grand formal dinner.

The Newland Archers, in the months since setting up their little household, had welcomed friends often enough in casual, unplanned ways. Newland loved dragging three or four of his legal or club pals home for impromptu suppers, and May greeted them all with that bright, unflinching warmth her own mother had perfected as the mark of a good wife. Newland sometimes wondered if left entirely to her own devices, she would ever have invited a soul across their threshold unprompted, but he’d long since stopped trying to dig for the “real” May beneath the layers of tradition and social training that had shaped her since girlhood. It was simply expected that wealthy, well-connected young couples in New York did their share of informal entertaining — and a Welland married to an Archer was doubly bound to uphold that unwritten rule.

But a grand dinner, with a hired French chef, two borrowed footmen from the van der Luydens’ staff, a serving of that legendary Roman punch no casual party ever dared to serve, roses flown in specially from Henderson’s nursery, and menus printed on thick, gilt-edged Tiffany bristol card? That was something else entirely. As Mrs. Archer never tired of pointing out, the Roman punch made all the difference: not for its taste, but for what it signaled. It meant you’d served canvasback duck or terrapin, two distinct soups, both a hot and cold sweet, every woman in the room turned out in low-cut gowns with bare short sleeves, and a guest list of such gravity even the most sought-after socialites would never dream of turning you down.

It was always a thrill when a young couple sent out their first formal third-person invitations, and their summons was almost never refused. Still, everyone agreed it was a staggering triumph that the van der Luydens themselves — the highest arbiters of old New York’s respectability — had agreed to delay their long-planned trip up to their Skuytercliff estate just to attend May’s farewell dinner for her cousin, Countess Ellen Olenska.

The two matriarchs had ensconced themselves in May’s drawing-room since midday. Mrs. Archer hunched over the dining table writing out the menus in her neat, slanted hand with a sharp gold fountain pen, while Mrs. Welland supervised the placement of potted palms and polished brass standard lamps, tweaking every inch of the room for perfect effect.

Archer stumbled in late from his office, his overcoat still dusted with the crisp autumn street grime, and found them still at their tasks. Mrs. Archer had moved on to place cards for the dinner table, and Mrs. Welland was debating the merits of dragging the big gilded sofa a foot away from the wall to create a cozy private nook between the piano and the bay window.

May, they told him, was in the dining room, inspecting the massive mound of deep red Jacqueminot roses and trailing maidenhair fern piled at the center of the long table, and the arrangement of dainty Maillard bonbons set out in openwork silver baskets between the crystal candelabra. On the piano sat a huge wicker basket of rare orchids Mr. van der Luyden had sent down specially from Skuytercliff. Everything, in short, was exactly as it should be for such a momentous occasion.

Mrs. Archer ran a thoughtful eye down the guest list, tapping each name with the tip of her pen. “Henry van der Luyden — Louisa — the Lovell Mingotts — the Reggie Chiverses — Lawrence Lefferts and Gertrude — (yes, I suppose May was right to include them) — the Selfridge Merrys, Sillerton Jackson, Van Newland and his wife. (How time flies! It feels like only yesterday he was your best man, Newland) — and Countess Olenska. That’s all, I think.”

Mrs. Welland gazed at her son-in-law with soft, affectionate approval. “No one can ever say, Newland, that you and May aren’t giving Ellen the most handsome send-off she could possibly ask for.”

“Ah, well,” Mrs. Archer smiled as she tucked the list into her apron pocket, “I’m sure May wants her cousin to tell all their acquaintances abroad that we’re not quite the barbarians they make New York out to be.”

“I know Ellen will adore it,” Mrs. Welland chirped cheerfully. “She was due to arrive back this morning, wasn’t she? It will leave her with the loveliest last impression. The night before you sail is always such a dreary, lonely thing, otherwise.”

Archer turned for the door, ignoring his mother-in-law’s call for him to go peek at the dining table and make sure May didn’t tire herself out. He bounded up the stairs to his library, and found the room had been ruthlessly tidied: his legal files stacked neatly, his half-finished manuscripts tucked away, ash trays and cedar cigar boxes set out in every corner to make it perfect for the men to smoke after dinner. “It won’t be long now,” he told himself, quiet and resolute, before ducking into his dressing room to change.

Ten days had passed since Ellen had slipped out of New York. In all that time, he’d gotten no word from her save for a small sealed envelope addressed in her handwriting, left on his office desk, holding his old brass door key wrapped in soft tissue paper. It would have been easy to write it off as a polite, formal gesture — but Archer had chosen to read deeper into it. She was still fighting against the fate everyone was pressing her into: a quiet life alone in Paris, far from her cruel husband, but also far from him. Nothing, he’d reasoned, stood in his way of following her. Once he took that irrevocable step, once he showed her he’d burn every bridge here for her, he knew she wouldn’t turn him away.

That quiet confidence in their future had steeled him to play his part in the tedious performances of the present. He hadn’t written to her, hadn’t let a single slip of his tongue or flicker of his face betray the raw, miserable longing gnawing at him. To his mind, they were playing a deadly silent game of cards, and he still held all the trumps. He would wait.

There had been hard moments, of course. The day after Ellen left, his senior partner Mr. Letterblair had summoned him to his office to go over the fine print of the generous trust old Mrs. Manson Mingott had arranged to set up for her granddaughter. They’d pored over the deed for two hours, and all the while Archer could feel there was a hidden, unspoken reason he’d been asked to handle the file, far beyond his family connection to Ellen.

“Well, no one can deny that’s a very handsome arrangement for the lady,” Mr. Letterblair had said, closing the thick leather binder. “I’d go so far as to say she’s been treated very fairly, all around.”

“All around?” Archer had echoed, sharp with quiet derision. “You mean to her husband’s ‘generous’ offer to give her back access to her own money, that he’s been hoarding this whole time?”

Mr. Letterblair had lifted his bushy gray eyebrows a fraction of an inch. “My dear boy, French law is French law. She married under it, one presumes she knew what she was signing.”

“Even if she did, everything that happened after —” Archer had started, then stopped short. Mr. Letterblair had set his pen against the bridge of his wrinkled nose, looking at him with that specific sanctimonious expression virtuous old men used when they wanted young people to know they were far more worldly than they let on. “My dear sir, I’ve no wish to defend the Count’s vices. But on the other hand… I wouldn’t swear on my life there wasn’t a little tit for tat, with that young champion of hers…” He’d pulled a folded confidential report out of his desk drawer and slid it across the table. Archer had never looked at it. He’d known exactly what it contained: the lies, the carefully planted rumors, all to paint Ellen as no better than her scoundrel husband, to justify the family pushing her out of New York to save face. “It’s eminently satisfactory for all parties that we’ve reached this dignified solution,” Letterblair had finished.

A day or two later, his soul had been tested even further, when he’d gone to call on old Mrs. Mingott. She’d been in a querulous, weepy mood. “You know she’s deserted me?” she’d blurted before he could even say hello. “Don’t ask me why, she gave me a dozen different excuses. I think she just couldn’t stand the boredom of Fifth Avenue. And I can’t even blame her, not really. Olenski’s a total scoundrel, but life with him must have been far more fun than the endless dinner parties here. Not that the rest of the family will admit it — they think Fifth Avenue is heaven on earth, with the Rue de la Paix thrown in for good measure. And Ellen will never go back to him, not if you tied her down. She’s going to settle in Paris with that silly old fool Medora Manson. Well, Paris is Paris, you can keep a carriage there on almost no money. But she was as bright and gay as a little bird, and I’m going to miss her.” Two dry, parched old tears rolled down her puffy cheeks and vanished into the lace of her bodice. “All I ask is that they stop pestering me about it. I’d like to be left in peace to digest my gruel,” she’d said, winking at Archer wistfully.

That very night, back at home, May had come to him with a stack of crisp formal invitations in her hand. She hadn’t spoken Ellen’s name since that night she’d gone rushing off to Washington to find her. “I’m throwing a farewell dinner for Ellen,” she’d said, her cheeks pink. “I thought you’d be pleased, you’ve always liked her.” Archer had stared at the list of names in her hand, and suddenly he didn’t just see his gentle, predictable wife — he saw the entire, unstoppable weight of the Welland, Mingott, and Archer families, all standing behind her, solid as a brick wall. He’d given in, quiet and numb, and told her it was a wonderful idea.

When he walked into the drawing room right before dinner, May was kneeling by the hearth, prodding at the logs to make them catch fire on their new, spotless white tile surround. All the tall cut-glass lamps were lit, and Mr. van der Luyden’s orchids had been arranged in vases of pale modern porcelain and heavy knobby silver all around the room. Everyone agreed May’s drawing room was a masterpiece: the gilt bamboo jardinière brimming with primulas and cinerarias, blocking access to the bay window where old-fashioned folks would have put a bronze copy of the Venus de Milo, the pale brocade sofas and armchairs clustered around little velvet tables heaped with silver trinkets, porcelain figurines, and gilded photograph frames, the tall lamps with rosy silk shades glowing like tropical flowers among the potted palms.

“I don’t think Ellen has ever seen this room all lit up,” May said, rising flushed from the hearth, glancing around her space with unashamed pride. The brass fire tongs she’d propped against the chimney clattered to the floor with a crash, drowning out whatever Archer might have said, before the butler announced Mr. and Mrs. van der Luyden.

The rest of the guests streamed in quickly, everyone knowing the van der Luydens hated being kept waiting for dinner. Archer was making polite conversation with Mrs. Selfridge Merry, showing her the tiny, highly varnished Verbeckhoven painting of sheep Mr. Welland had given May for Christmas, when he suddenly realized Ellen Olenska was standing right at his elbow.

She was so pale her dark hair looked twice as thick and dark as he remembered, strung with several loops of chunky amber beads around her throat. It hit him all at once: this was the same little Ellen Mingott he’d danced with at children’s costume parties, all those years ago, when Medora Manson first brought her back to New York from Europe. The amber beads did nothing for her complexion, or maybe her plain dark gown was unflattering, but her face looked almost lustreless, soft and tired, and he had never loved her more than he did in that exact moment. Their hands brushed, and he heard her murmur, “Yes, we sail tomorrow on the *Russia* —” before the butler threw open the dining room doors, and May’s clear, bright voice cut through the noise: “Newland! Dinner’s been announced. Won’t you please take Ellen in?”

Ellen rested her ungloved hand on his arm, and Archer stared down at it, remembering that night they’d sat together in her tiny Twenty-third Street drawing room, his eyes glued to that exact hand for hours. All the beauty that seemed to have drained from her face had taken refuge in those long pale fingers, the faint dimples on her knuckles pressed to his sleeve. He thought to himself: I would cross an entire ocean just to hold that hand again.

Only at a dinner ostensibly thrown for a foreign visitor would Mrs. van der Luyden, the undisputed queen of New York society, accept being seated to the left of the host, the spot reserved for guests of honor. By seating Ellen in the prime seat at Archer’s right, they’d made her foreign status, her soon departure from their tight little circle, the most unmissable tribute possible. In the unwritten code of old New York, it was simply how things were done: no messy scenes, no public anger, the whole tribe rallying around a kinswoman who was about to be gently exiled, to make it look like nothing but love and loyalty. The Wellands and Mingotts would have moved heaven and earth to make everyone see how much they adored Ellen Olenska, now that she’d agreed to leave for Europe of her own free will. Archer sat at the head of his table, watching it all unfold in a daze, marveling at how smoothly they’d rewritten history: all the old grievances against Ellen silenced, her past forgiven, her reputation polished to a perfect shine by the full force of family approval.

Archer felt almost weightless, as if he was floating somewhere between the glittering crystal chandelier and the ceiling, watching the whole scene play out below him. He glanced around the table at all those soft, well-fed, placid faces, and suddenly he saw it in a flash: every single one of these people knew. They’d known about him and Ellen, for months. Every observing eye, every listening ear, they’d all quietly worked together to drive them apart, no blood spilled, no scandal, no fuss. Now the whole tribe was rallying around May, all silently agreeing to pretend none of them had ever suspected a thing. This dinner was nothing more than a performance, a pretty lie, to make sure Newland never dared to break free of the life they’d all mapped out for him.

It was the old New York way, taking life without effusion of blood: a place that dreaded scandal more than disease, that valued decency over courage, that thought nothing was more vulgar than a public scene, save for the people who caused them. Archer felt like a prisoner surrounded by an armed camp, and when he heard them all calmly gossiping about the disgraced Beaufort and his wife Regina, over plates of fresh Florida asparagus, he knew what they were doing. They were warning him, wordlessly, what would happen to him if he stepped out of line. A cold, heavy feeling settled over him, as if the heavy stone doors of the family vault were closing around him. He laughed out loud, and met Mrs. van der Luyden’s startled eyes.

“You think that’s funny?” she asked, with a tight little smile. “Of course poor Regina’s idea of staying on in New York is rather ridiculous, I suppose.” Archer muttered a quick apology.

He noticed then that the man seated on Ellen’s other side had been chatting nonstop to the woman next to him, leaving her in silence, and he saw May glance down the table quickly, making sure everything was running smoothly. The host couldn’t leave the woman beside him hanging in silence, it would be unforgivably rude. He turned to Ellen, and her pale, soft smile met his, as if she was saying, Let’s just get through this, all right?

“Did you find the journey back tiring?” he asked, surprised at how natural and calm his voice sounded. She said no, she’d barely had any discomforts at all on the train, save for that terrible unexpected heat. Archer leaned in, intense. “I’ve never been more frozen in my life than one April day on the train between Calais and Paris.” She said it was nothing, you could always carry an extra rug, every form of travel had its small hardships. He shook his head, sharp and urgent. “None of it matters. Nothing matters at all, compared to the blessedness of getting away from here.” Her face flushed, and he raised his voice loud enough for half the table to hear. “I’m planning to do a whole lot of travelling myself, very soon.” A tiny tremor ran across her face. He turned and called over to Reggie Chivers at the far end of the table: “I say, Reggie! What do you say to a trip around the world? Next month, no delays. I’m game if you are!” Mrs. Reggie piped up immediately that she couldn’t possibly let her husband leave before the Martha Washington Ball she was throwing for the Blind Asylum over Easter week, and Reggie placidly replied that he’d be busy training for the International Polo Match by then anyway.

Mr. Selfridge Merry, who’d once circled the whole globe on his private steam yacht, caught the phrase “around the world” and launched into a long monologue about the terrible shallow harbors in the Mediterranean, how once you’d seen Athens and Smyrna and Constantinople, there was nothing else worth seeing. His wife added that she was forever grateful to their doctor for making them promise never to stop in Naples, what with all the dangerous fevers there. Before long, the ladies were all rising from the table, trailing their silk gowns toward the drawing room.
