“At the court of the Tuileries,” said Mr. Sillerton Jackson, his eyes crinkling with that soft, reminiscent smirk of a man who had hoarded decades of society’s juiciest secrets, “such things were pretty openly tolerated.”

The heavy, beeswax-polished dark walnut dining room of the van der Luydens’ Madison Avenue townhouse hummed with the hush of unassailable old New York decorum, and it was barely twenty-four hours since Newland Archer had wandered the quiet galleries of the Metropolitan Museum, staring at faded medieval tapestries that had tangled his thoughts tight around Ellen Olenska. Mr. and Mrs. van der Luyden had only just drifted back to the city from their rural Skuytercliff estate, where they’d fled precipitately the second news of Beaufort’s catastrophic financial failure had broken. Society had begged them to return: when the entire fabric of their narrow, ordered world was fraying, their quiet, unassailable authority was the only thing that could stop vulgar upstarts like Mrs. Lemuel Struthers from scrambling up the social ladder to claim Regina Beaufort’s vacant place. As Mrs. Archer had put it, they “owed it to society” to show their faces at the Opera, to open their doors, to stand in the breach as they always had. So they had unpacked their linen, unmuffled the long-shuttered rooms, and sent out invitations for two small dinners and one evening reception.

This particular evening, their guests were Sillerton Jackson, Mrs. Archer, Newland, and his wife May, all bound for a winter premiere of *Faust*. No dinner under the van der Luydens’ roof ever deviated from ritual: even with only four guests, the first course was served sharp at seven, every subsequent course unfolding at a deliberate, unrushed pace so the gentlemen would have time to linger over their cigars before the opera’s first act. Archer had barely seen May since the previous night: he’d left at dawn for his office, buried under a pile of trivial, tedious paperwork, then been detained by a senior partner long past his usual departure time, arriving home so late that May had already driven ahead to the van der Luydens’ and sent the carriage back empty. Now, across the bank of soft pink Skuytercliff carnations and the glint of their massive, heirloom silver plate, she looked pale, almost languid, but her eyes were bright, and she chattered with a bright, exaggerated animation that felt forced to his watchful eye.

The subject that had prompted Jackson’s Tuileries allusion had been brought up, Archer suspected, very much on purpose by their hostess. The Beaufort failure, and Regina Beaufort’s unapologetic refusal to shrink into obscurity after it, was every drawing-room moralist’s favorite topic that winter, and once the whole sorry affair had been thoroughly picked over and condemned, Mrs. van der Luyden turned her soft, disapproving gaze on May. “Is it possible, dear, that what I hear is true? I was told your grandmother Mingott’s carriage was seen idling outside Mrs. Beaufort’s door this week.” She did not use Regina’s first name, as if the disgraced woman no longer merited the courtesy.

May’s cheeks flushed bright pink, and Mrs. Archer cut in hastily. “If it was there, I am certain it was without poor Catherine’s knowledge.”

“Ah, you think—?” Mrs. van der Luyden paused, sighed, and glanced over at her husband.

“I am afraid,” Mr. van der Luyden said gently, “that Madame Olenska’s kind heart may have led her into the imprudence of calling on Mrs. Beaufort.”

“Or her taste for peculiar, unorthodox people,” Mrs. Archer added dryly, her eyes drifting, all innocent, to rest on her son’s face.

“I am so sorry to think it of Madame Olenska,” Mrs. van der Luyden murmured, and Mrs. Archer sighed right back. “Ah, my dear—after you invited her to stay at Skuytercliff not once, but twice!”

That was the exact moment Sillerton Jackson leaned in, pleased to have the floor. “At the Tuileries,” he repeated, as every eye in the room turned to him, “the standard was excessively lax in some respects; if you’d asked where Morny’s money came from, or who paid the debts of half the court beauties, no one would have batted an eye.”

“I hope, dear Sillerton,” Mrs. Archer said, a little sharp, “you are not suggesting we should adopt such loose standards here?”

“I never suggest anything,” Jackson returned, imperturbable. “But Madame Olenska’s foreign upbringing may well make her less particular about small social lines than the rest of us.” The two elder ladies sighed in unison.

“Still, to have kept her grandmother’s carriage waiting on the doorstep of a known defaulter!” Mr. van der Luyden protested, and Archer guessed he was remembering, and resenting, the hampers of rare carnations he’d himself sent to Ellen’s little Twenty-Third Street house not long before.

“Of course I have always said she looks at the whole world quite differently than we do,” Mrs. Archer summed up.

A hot flush rose to May’s forehead. She looked straight across the table at her husband, and spoke before anyone else could. “I am sure Ellen only meant to be kind.”

“Imprudent people are often kind,” Mrs. Archer replied, as if kindness was no excuse at all, and Mrs. van der Luyden murmured, “If only she had consulted someone before she acted—”

“Ah, that she never did!” Mrs. Archer rejoined.

A small signal passed between the van der Luydens: their hostess nodded slightly at Mrs. Archer, and the three ladies, their silk skirts glimmering in the gaslight, swept out of the dining room to retire to the drawing room. The gentlemen stayed behind to light their special short cigars, reserved for opera nights—they were so fine that even Archer found himself mourning the strict punctuality that would force them to stub them out in less than an hour.

Once they reached the opera house and the first act of *Faust* wrapped, Archer slipped away from their party, weaving to the back of the gentlemen’s club box. From there, he peered over the shoulders of the Chivers, Mingott, and Rushworth men packed in the rows ahead, staring at the exact same horseshoe curve of boxes he’d gazed at two years prior, on the night he had first met Ellen Olenska. He’d half-expected her to appear in old Mrs. Mingott’s usual box, but the velvet seat stayed empty, and he sat motionless, his eyes fixed on the empty space, until Madame Nilsson’s pure, soaring soprano broke out into “*M’ama, non m’ama…*”

He turned to the stage, where the same familiar set of giant painted roses and frilly pansies framed the scene: the same large, blonde Marguerite succumbing to the small, dark, persuasive Faust. His eyes drifted away from the singers, down the curve of the horseshoe to where May sat between Mrs. van der Luyden and his own mother, exactly as she had sat, two years prior, between Mrs. Lovell Mingott and her newly arrived “foreign” cousin. She was all in white, and he suddenly recognized the fabric: the blue-white satin and hand-stitched old lace of her wedding dress.

It was custom in old New York for brides to wear their wedding gowns to formal events for the first year or two of marriage; his own mother still kept hers wrapped in tissue paper, hoping Janey would wear it one day, though poor Janey was fast approaching the age where pearl grey poplin and no bridesmaids would be deemed far more “appropriate.” Archer realized May had barely pulled the gown out of her closet since their return from their European honeymoon, and the shock of seeing her in it made him compare the woman sitting there to the bright, blushing girl he had watched two years prior, all soft blissful anticipation, playing with her bouquet of lilies of the valley. Her outline was a little fuller now, as her statuesque frame had always promised it would be, but her upright, athletic carriage and the transparent, uncomplicated innocence of her expression had not changed a bit. The quiet languor he’d noticed creeping over her these last weeks made her seem even more fragile, more childlike, and the memory of the passionate generosity hidden under that calm stillness hit him hard. He remembered the way she’d looked at him when he’d begged to announce their engagement at the Beaufort ball, the soft, steady voice in the Mission garden, saying “I couldn’t have my happiness made out of a wrong—a wrong to someone else.”

A wild, uncontrollable longing seized him. He would tell her the truth. He would throw himself on that legendary generosity, beg for the freedom he had once been too cowardly to take, and run away with Ellen.

Newland Archer was a quiet, self-controlled man, who had spent his whole life conforming to the strict unwritten rules of his small, closed society. The thought of making a spectacle, of doing something “bad form” that would make Mr. van der Luyden frown and every man in the club box whisper behind his back, usually made him recoil. But in that moment, he forgot the club box, he forgot the van der Luydens, he forgot every single habit and convention that had wrapped around him like a soft, warm blanket his whole life. He walked along the semi-circular passage at the back of the opera house, and opened the door of the van der Luydens’ box like he was stepping through a gate into an unknown new world. “*M’ama!*” the triumphant Marguerite trilled from the stage, and everyone in the box looked up in shocked surprise—no one ever entered a box mid-solo, it was one of the most unbreakable social rules there was.

He slipped between Mr. van der Luyden and Sillerton Jackson, leaned over his wife, and whispered low in her ear: “I’ve got a beastly headache. Don’t tell anyone, but come home with me, won’t you?”

May gave him one quick, perfectly comprehending glance. He watched her whisper an excuse to his mother, who nodded sympathetically, then murmur her apologies to Mrs. van der Luyden, and rise from her seat just as Marguerite fell into Faust’s arms. As he helped her on with her opera cloak, he caught the shared, knowing little smile that passed between the three older ladies.

On the drive back to their house, May laid her hand shyly over his. “I’m so sorry you don’t feel well. I do wish they wouldn’t overwork you so terribly at the office.”

“It’s not that,” he said, confused, letting the carriage window down on his side. He sat staring out at the gas-lit brownstones passing by, feeling her presence beside him like a quiet, watchful question, and refused to tear his eyes away from the street. When they reached their door, she caught the hem of her dress in the carriage step, and stumbled against him. “Did you hurt yourself?” he asked, steadying her with his arm.

“No, but my poor dress—look, it’s torn!” she exclaimed, bending to brush mud off the stained satin hem, then following him up the steps into the hall. The servants had not expected them home so early, and only a single glimmer of gas lit the upper landing. Archer climbed the stairs, turned the light up, and lit the two gas brackets flanking the library mantelpiece. The curtains were drawn, and the warm, familiar glow of the room wrapped around him, like running into the face of an old friend on a secret errand. He noticed how pale May was, and asked if he should fetch her a glass of brandy.

“Oh no,” she said, a faint flush rising to her cheeks as she took off her opera cloak. “But hadn’t you better go to bed at once?” she added, when he opened the silver cigarette box on the table and pulled out a cigarette.

Archer threw the cigarette down on the table, and walked over to his usual spot by the fireplace. “No. My head isn’t that bad.” He paused, the weight of what he was about to say hanging thick in the air. “There’s something I want to say. Something important, that I have to tell you right now.”

She dropped into an armchair, and lifted her head to look at him. “Yes, dear?” she said, so gently that he was surprised she did not look even a little confused by his sudden urgency.

“May—” he began, standing a few feet away from her, as if that small stretch of floor between them was an uncrossable abyss. His voice echoed weirdly in the soft, quiet hush of the room, and he repeated, “There is something I have to tell you… about myself.”

She sat perfectly still, not a single lash flickering. She was still very pale, but her face had a strange, serene tranquility, as if she was drawing strength from some secret, inner source he had never suspected. Archer pushed aside all the stock, conventional lines of self-accusation that were crowding to his lips. He would be blunt, no excuses, no lies. “Madame Olenska—” he started.

At the name, May lifted her hand, as if to silence him. The gaslight glinted off the simple gold band of her wedding ring. “Oh, why should we talk about Ellen tonight?” she asked, with a tiny, soft pout of impatience.

“Because I ought to have spoken a long time ago.”

Her face stayed perfectly calm. “Is it really worth the trouble, dear? I know I haven’t always been fair to her—none of us have, I think. You’ve understood her better than the rest of us, of course. You’ve always been so kind to her. But what does any of it matter, now that it’s all over?”

Archer stared at her, blank and reeling. Could the fog of unreality that had wrapped around his own brain somehow spread to hers? “All over? What do you mean?” he stammered, his words thick and indistinct.

May looked back at him, her eyes clear and bright as glass. “Why—since she’s going back to Europe so soon. Granny approves, and understands, and has arranged to make her completely independent of her husband, no strings attached.” She paused, and Archer grabbed the corner of the mantelpiece in a convulsed hand, holding himself up as his whole world tilted off its axis. “I thought you’d been kept late at the office this evening about all the business arrangements. It was all settled this morning, I believe.” She lowered her eyes under his unseeing, stunned gaze, and a faint, fleeting flush crossed her face.

He understood that his own face must look unbearable, and he turned away, resting his elbows on the mantel shelf and covering his face with his hands. Something thundered and clanged in his ears; he could not tell if it was the rush of his own blood, or the slow, steady tick of the mantel clock. May did not move, did not speak, for five full minutes while the clock ticked on. A lump of coal fell forward in the grate, and when he heard her rise to push it back into the fire, Archer finally turned to face her.

“It’s impossible,” he burst out.

“Impossible?”

“How do you know—what you just told me?”

“I saw Ellen yesterday. I told you I met her at Granny’s house.”

“It wasn’t then that she told you?”

“No. I got a note from her this afternoon. Do you want to see it?”

He could not find his voice, and she walked out of the room, coming back almost immediately. “I thought you knew,” she said simply, laying a single sheet of paper down on the table.

Archer reached for it, his hands shaking. The letter was only a few short lines.
*May dear, I have at last made Granny understand that my visit to her could be no more than a visit, and she has been as kind and generous as ever. She sees now that if I return to Europe I must live by myself, or rather with poor Aunt Medora, who is coming with me. I am hurrying back to Washington to pack up, and we sail next week. You must be very good to Granny when I’m gone—as good as you’ve always been to me. Ellen.*

Archer read the letter two, three times over, then dropped it on the table and burst out laughing. The sound of his own wild, hollow laugh startled him, and he remembered the night years ago when Janey had woken up terrified, catching him rocking with the same incomprehensible, helpless mirth over May’s telegram announcing that their wedding date had been moved forward.

“Why did she write this?” he asked, forcing the laugh to die in his throat.

May met his question with unshaken, soft candor. “I suppose because we talked things over yesterday—”

“What things?”

“I told her I was afraid I hadn’t been fair to her, hadn’t always understood how hard it must have been for her here, alone among so many people who were relations and yet strangers, who felt they had every right to criticize her without ever knowing the full story.” She paused. “I knew you’d always been the one friend she could count on. And I wanted her to know that you and I were the same—when it came to all the things that matter most.” She hesitated, waiting for him to speak, then added slowly, “She understood exactly why I wanted to tell her that. I think she understands everything.”

She walked up to Archer, took one of his cold hands, and pressed it quickly to her soft cheek. “My head aches too. Goodnight, dear,” she said, turning for the door, her torn, mud-stained wedding dress dragging behind her across the library rug, leaving him alone, staring at the crumpled letter on the table, every last shred of the future he’d dared to imagine for himself dissolving into smoke.
