My early fortune led me to form a friendship with a gentleman of standing, born on an island often hailed by impartial critics as the "first gem of the ocean." The tales this man recounted concerning his kin—those inhabitants of the aforementioned jewel—were sufficient to curdle the blood of youth. He described a lineage where every conceivable crime was committed; the Ten Commandments shattered in a single sweep, accompanied by such audacious frauds and villainy that no fiction writer could conceive, and murders and robberies surpassing even the exploits of Thurtell or Turpin. Yet, despite this catalogue of depravity, the family somehow managed to survive without ending up in the hulks.

My brother Tim had brought his father's gray hairs to the grave with sorrow; my brother Mick had repeatedly robbed the parish church; and my sister Annamaroia had jilted a captain, eloped with an ensign, forged her grandmother's will, and stolen the silver spoons—an offense for which Larry the knife-boy eventually hung. Compared to the infamous House of Atreus, this clan of O'What-d'ye-call-'em was merely a shadow, yet I dare not name their homeland. What struck me as naive astonishment was witnessing these murderers, parricides, and habitual forgers correspond with one another as "dearest brother" and "dearest sister," maintaining amicable relations for months on end. Tim, whose hands reeked of patricide, would mix a screeching tumbler for Maria; Mick, whose lips were blackened by perjury regarding his grandmother's will and the murder of his brother Thady's orphans, would kiss Julia's cheek. They would share a jolly night, weeping and reminiscing about the old castle, the quarreling Onetyoneth, the Major's proposal for Cyaroloine, and the tomb of their scent-filled mother who had disinherited them.

They embraced with such fervor that one forgot their bloody histories and the threats to hang each other. What is finer than forgiveness? After hurling every insult under the sun, apologizing for hasty words, withdrawing the decanter once flung at an enemy's head, and becoming friends anew—is there anything more rational? Some possess this angelic gift. It was beautiful to observe our two ladies at Tunbridge Wells forgiving one another, smiling and fondling despite yesterday's harsh words, forgetting bygones while perfectly remembering them. Can you and I achieve such heights? We must strive for this pacable, Christian spirit. While I believe one can learn to forgive bad language through practice, embracing after a quarrel renders insults meaningless.

Thus, Aunt Bernstein and her niece played cards amiably, toasted each other's health, shared wings of chicken, and even scratched neighbors' eyes out rather than each other's. This mirrors the behavior of Peninsular warriors who fraternized over tobacco and wine during truces, only to resume combat instantly afterward. Similarly, our veterans laid down their weapons to hobnob gaily. Of course, while drinking with a Frenchman, one keeps a pistol handy, yet amidst the *A votre santé*, everything remains pleasant.

Regarding Aunt Bernstein's threatened gout, the twinges had vanished. Maria's fainting fits? None returned, save for a slight recurrence the prior night, prompting the Baroness's concern and offers of bottled waters. Yet, private whispers suggest the old lady dismissed these fits as epilepsy inherited from a "scrofulous German mother," while Lady Maria might have ordered her maid to test every medicinal glass for poison. Such precautions are like the loaded firelocks of old soldiers: ready, but currently resting quietly on the grass.

Possessing Harry's bond in her pocket, the veteran Maria refrained from pressing for immediate payment. Knowing the world too well, she granted him ample grace, understanding his youth and need for diversion. "Take your ease, cousin," she advised. Even the cat, Grey Grimalkin, purred contentedly in the corner, watching with green eyes.

Lady Maria and her aunt discussed Harry's anticipated arrival in London with frankness typical of the era. In his absence, the wily Calypso sought diversions, finding joy in the company of Mr. Jack Morris, a gentleman who delighted in associating with titled figures and losing money to an earl's daughter. Since Lady Maria was eager to win, she secured permission to invite Morris to the Countess of Yarmouth's assembly, ensuring everyone's satisfaction.

The first eighty-four hours following Mr. Warrington's departure passed cheerfully at Tunbridge Wells until Friday arrived, marking the day of Mr. Sampson's sermon. The company was eager to attend; if there were no breakfasts, conjurers, or dancing bears, the Wells folk would endure a sermon. Sampson knew Lady Yarmouth was present, wielding immense influence over livings and bishoprics, and thus prepared his finest discourse.

When the Great Man visits the local church, bringing dukes and cabinet ministers, the curate becomes acutely aware of the audience. He watches the Great Pew nervously, fearing judgment on his future prospects. His wife and daughters join the gaze, hoping for a favorable reaction. Once the initial nervousness fades, the noble voice warms, kindling into passion until he reaches the exquisite passage that moves the congregation to tears. Then comes the humming noise—the Right Honourable Lord Naseby snoring loudly in the front pew. Poor Trotter's vision of a mitre vanishes with the snores.

Sampson, the domestic chaplain to Madame Bernstein's nephew and favored by the Esmond ladies, appeared that morning rosy and handsome in a fresh wig and a new cassock bought on credit. Accompanied by lacquemen carrying gilt prayer books, he marched from breakfast to church. The Baroness looked dazzling, laughing warmly with her niece, her tortoiseshell cane in hand. Outside, a dazzling confluence of ranks gathered, including Lady Yarmouth in flame-colored taffeta. Among the crowd were shabbier figures, notably two men in ragged coats entering as the organ ceased. One, with carrot hair peeking from a scratch-wig, mechanically crossed himself, muttering, "Bedad, Tim, I forgawt!" Clearly, he hailed from the island mentioned earlier. Whisky followed them inside.

Even Catholics and ancient Hebrews paid homage. Two men with hooked noses and sallow complexions pushed past the beadle's objections, bribing him with coin and promising conversion. The doors closed, the organ began, and the sermon commenced.

Suddenly, as Sampson rose from his pulpit cushion, he turned pale as death. Glancing toward the west door, he saw the "horrible Hebrew caryatides." Looking toward the vestry near the rector's pew, he noticed two perfumed Irish gentlemen seated nearby, eyes cast meekly downward. How could he deliver his sermon when his mind was elsewhere?

Despite his distraction, the sermon was deemed excellent. Upon its conclusion, the ladies praised it effusively. Madame Walmoden declared it bewitching; Madame Bernstein called it excellent. Lady Maria waved a handkerchief, attempting to catch the reverend's eye, but he remained transfixed, staring toward the font where the two Hebrews still sat. As the congregation filed out, the two men remained by the door.

Inside the rector's pew, the Baroness conversed deeply with a gentleman while Lady Maria fretted. "Who are those horrible men at the door? And what a smell of spirits!" cried Lady Maria to her maid, Mrs. Brett. Meanwhile, the Baroness bid farewell to a doctor, asking about his son's clerical ambitions, before insisting that Lady Maria exit the pew first.

As the younger lady stepped forward, the two men advanced. One produced a long strip of paper. Lady Maria started, turning pale, and fled toward the vestry, hoping to escape. However, the men intercepted her. "At the suit of Mistress Pincott, mercer of Kensington, I arrest your ladyship," declared one, introducing himself as Michael Costigan, a poor Irishman forced into a disagreeable profession. "Will you walk, or shall my man fetch a cheer?"

In reply, Lady Maria shrieked three times and swooned. "Keep the door, Mick!" shouted Costigan, politely informing the Baroness that the lady had fainted and required rest. "Unlace her, Brett!" commanded the old lady, her eyes twinkling strangely. Seizing a small bag hanging from Lady Maria's necklace, the Baroness cut the chain. "Dash cold water on her face!" she instructed. Turning to Costigan, she asked, "How much is your suit?"

"The debt stands at one hundred and thirty-two pounds," Costigan replied.

Where was Reverend Sampson? Like the opossum who admits defeat when spotted by a hunter, he surrendered immediately. "At whose suit, Simons?" he asked sadly. "Buckleby Cordwainer," came the reply. "Forty-eight pounds and charges," Sampson sighed. Lacking funds, he was ushered into two chairs by Mr. Lyons, who offered his house as accommodation. Thus, Lady Maria and Sampson were escorted to Mr. Lyons's residence.

Shortly thereafter, the Baroness sent her servant, Mr. Case, with a note expressing ardent affection but explaining that heavy card losses made repayment impossible. She wrote to Mrs. Pincott requesting time, promising to settle once an answer was received. Meanwhile, Mrs. Betty attempted to raise funds for a carriage to London.
