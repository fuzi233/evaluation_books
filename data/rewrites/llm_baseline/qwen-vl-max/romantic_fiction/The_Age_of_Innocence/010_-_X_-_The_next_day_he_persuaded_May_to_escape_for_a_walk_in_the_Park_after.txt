X. The next day, he persuaded May to slip away for a walk in the Park after luncheon. As was customary among old-fashioned Episcopalian families in New York, she usually attended church with her parents on Sunday afternoons. But Mrs. Welland had quietly allowed this small rebellion—having that very morning convinced her daughter of the necessity of a long engagement, so they might have time to prepare a hand-embroidered trousseau with all the proper number of dozens.

The day was radiant. The bare arches of trees lining the Mall were set against a sky like lapis lazuli, and snow glittered beneath them like shattered crystal. It was the kind of weather that brought out May’s natural glow, and she shone like a young maple touched by frost. Archer felt proud under the admiring glances they drew, and the simple joy of being with her washed over his deeper doubts.

“It’s so delicious—waking every morning to smell lilies-of-the-valley in my room!” she said, smiling. “Yesterday they came late. I didn’t have time in the morning—”

“But your remembering each day to send them makes me love them more than if you’d just given a standing order,” he replied, “like one’s music teacher—just as I know Gertrude Lefferts’s did when she and Lawrence were engaged.”

“Oh, they would!” Archer laughed, amused by her sharpness. He glanced sideways at her rosy cheek and, feeling rich and secure, added: “When I sent your lilies yesterday afternoon, I saw some magnificent yellow roses and sent them off to Madame Olenska. Was that right?”

“How sweet of you! She adores flowers like that. It’s odd she didn’t mention it—she lunched with us today and spoke of Mr. Beaufort sending her orchids, and cousin Henry van der Luyden a whole hamper of carnations from Skuytercliff. She seems so surprised to receive them. Don’t people send flowers in Europe? She thinks it such a lovely custom.”

“Oh, well, no wonder mine were overshadowed by Beaufort’s,” Archer muttered, irritated. Then he remembered—he hadn’t included a card. He regretted speaking of the roses at all. He wanted to say, *I called on your cousin yesterday*, but hesitated. If Madame Olenska hadn’t mentioned it, it might seem awkward for him to bring it up. Yet not saying anything gave the whole matter an air of secrecy he disliked. To distract himself, he turned to their future—plans, engagements, Mrs. Welland’s insistence on a long courtship.

“If you call it long!” she teased. “Isabel Chivers and Reggie were engaged two years. Grace and Thorley nearly a year and a half. Why aren’t we doing quite well as we are?”

It was the classic maidenly retort, and he felt embarrassed at how childish it sounded. No doubt she’d simply repeated what she’d heard—but she was nearing twenty-two, and he wondered when “nice” women began to think for themselves.

“Never, if we won’t let them,” he mused, recalling his impulsive words to Mr. Sillerton Jackson: *Women ought to be as free as we are—* Soon, he would have to lift the veil from this young woman’s eyes and let her see the world. But how many generations of women had been buried blindfolded in family vaults? He shivered, thinking of the scientific theories he’d read—the Kentucky cave-fish, born without eyes because they never needed them. What if, when he asked May to open hers, they could only stare into emptiness?

“We might be better off,” he said. “We might be together—travel.”

Her face lit up. “That would be lovely,” she admitted. She’d love to travel. But her mother wouldn’t understand wanting to do things differently.

“As if the mere ‘differently’ didn’t explain everything!” he insisted.

“Newland! You’re so original!” she exclaimed, delighted.

His heart sank. He realized he was saying exactly what every young man in his position was expected to say—and she was responding exactly as tradition dictated—even calling him original.

“Original?” he said, stopping and facing her. “We’re all cut from the same paper, like those dolls folded from the same sheet. We’re patterns stenciled on a wall. Can’t you and I break free, May?”

She looked at him with bright, unclouded admiration.

“Mercy—shall we elope?” she laughed.

“If you would—”

“You *do* love me, Newland. I’m so happy.”

“But then—why not be happier?”

“We can’t behave like characters in novels, can we?” she said gently.

“Why not—why not—why not?” he pressed.

She looked slightly bored. She knew they couldn’t—she just couldn’t come up with a reason. “I’m not clever enough to argue with you,” she said, relieved to find a word that would end the conversation. “But that kind of thing is rather… vulgar, isn’t it?”

“Are you so afraid of being vulgar?” he asked sharply.

She blinked. “Of course I would hate it—so would you.”

He stood silent, tapping his stick against his boot-top. She seemed to sense she’d found the perfect way to close the subject and continued cheerfully: “Oh, did I tell you I showed Ellen my ring? She thought it the most beautiful setting she’d ever seen. Nothing like it in the rue de la Paix, she said. I love you, Newland, for being so artistic!”

The next afternoon, before dinner, Archer sat smoking sullenly in his study. He hadn’t stopped at his club on the way home from the office—where he practiced law in the unhurried manner typical of wealthy New Yorkers. He was in low spirits, restless, haunted by the dread of repeating the same routine every day at the same hour.

“Sameness—sameness!” he muttered, the word echoing like a tuneless refrain. He imagined the familiar figures in tall hats lounging behind the plate-glass windows of the club. He knew precisely what they’d be discussing—probably the Duke, or the golden-haired lady who’d appeared in Fifth Avenue in a canary-yellow brougham drawn by black cobs (widely believed to be Beaufort’s doing). Such women—called “women” in society circles—were rare in New York; even rarer were those who drove their own carriages. Miss Fanny Ring’s sudden appearance had caused a stir. Only the day before, her carriage had passed Mrs. Lovell Mingott’s, and the latter had immediately rung the bell and ordered her coachman to take her home.

“What if it had happened to Mrs. van der Luyden?” people whispered, horrified.

Archer could already hear Lawrence Lefferts ranting about the collapse of society. He raised his head irritably as his sister Janey entered, then quickly bent over his book—Swinburne’s *Chastelard*, newly published—as if he hadn’t noticed her.

She glanced at the table piled with books, opened a volume of the *Contes Drolatiques*, made a face at the old French, and sighed: “What learned things you read!”

“Well—?” he asked, watching her hover like Cassandra.

“Mother’s furious.”

“Furious? With whom? About what?”

“Miss Sophy Jackson was here. She said her brother would come after dinner—she couldn’t say much, because he forbade her. He wants to give all the details himself. He’s with cousin Louisa van der Luyden now.”

“For heaven’s sake, try a fresh start,” he groaned. “It would take an omniscient God to make sense of this.”

“It’s not the time to be profane, Newland,” she said, eyes wide. “Mother feels badly enough about your not going to church…”

With a sigh, he returned to his book.

“Newland! Listen. Your friend Madame Olenska was at Mrs. Lemuel Struthers’s party last night. She went there with the Duke and Mr. Beaufort.”

At the last part, a wave of irrational anger surged through him. He laughed to cover it.

“Well, what of it? I knew she meant to.”

Janey paled. “You knew—and you didn’t try to stop her? Warn her?”

“Stop her? Warn her?” He laughed again. “I’m not engaged to the Countess Olenska!”

The words sounded absurd even to himself.

“You’re marrying into her family.”

“Oh, family—family!” he sneered.

“Newland—don’t you care about Family?”

“Not a brass farthing.”

“Nor about what cousin Louisa van der Luyden will think?”

“Not half a one—if she thinks such old-maid nonsense.”

“Mother is not an old maid,” Janey said, lips pinched.

He wanted to shout: *Yes, she is—and so are the van der Luydens—and so are we all, when it comes to touching reality.* But he saw her face begin to crumple, and felt ashamed.

“Hang Countess Olenska! Don’t be a goose, Janey—I’m not her keeper.”

“No,” she said, “but you *did* ask the Wellands to announce your engagement sooner so we could all support her. And if it hadn’t been for that, cousin Louisa would never have invited her to the dinner for the Duke.”

“Well—what harm was there in inviting her? She was the most beautiful woman in the room. She made the dinner less funereal than usual.”

“You know cousin Henry asked her to please you. He persuaded cousin Louisa. Now they’re so upset, they’re leaving for Skuytercliff tomorrow. I think, Newland, you’d better go down. You don’t seem to understand how mother feels.”

In the drawing-room, he found his mother. She looked up from her embroidery. “Has Janey told you?”

“Yes.” He tried to keep his tone calm. “But I can’t take it seriously.”

“Not the fact that we’ve offended cousin Louisa and cousin Henry?”

“The fact that they can be offended by something as trivial as Countess Olenska attending a party at a woman they consider common.”

“Consider—!”

“Well, who is—but who has good music and entertains people on Sundays, when the whole city is dying of boredom?”

“Good music? All I know is, there was a woman who stood on a table and sang songs like they sing in Paris. There was smoking and champagne.”

“Well—that happens elsewhere. The world still turns.”

“I don’t suppose you’re really defending the French Sunday?”

“I’ve heard you complain often enough about the English Sunday when we were in London.”

“New York is neither Paris nor London.”

“Oh, no, it’s not!” he groaned. “You mean society here isn’t as brilliant? Probably true—but we belong here. People should respect our customs when they come among us. Especially Ellen Olenska—she came back to escape the kind of life they lead in brilliant societies.”

He said nothing.

After a pause, his mother ventured: “I was going to put on my bonnet and ask you to take me to see cousin Louisa before dinner.”

He frowned.

“I thought you might explain to her what you just said—about how society abroad is different, how people aren’t as particular, and that Madame Olenska may not have realized how we feel. It would be, you know, dear,” she added with innocent tact, “in Madame Olenska’s interest if you did.”

“Dearest mother, I really don’t see how we’re involved. The Duke took Madame Olenska to Mrs. Struthers’s—in fact, he brought Mrs. Struthers to call on her. I was there when they arrived. If the van der Luydens want to quarrel with someone, the real culprit is under their own roof.”

“Quarrel? Newland, have you ever known cousin Henry to quarrel? Besides, the Duke is his guest—and a stranger. Strangers don’t discriminate. How should they? Countess Olenska is a New Yorker—she should have respected New York’s feelings.”

“Well, then, if they must have a victim, you have my permission to throw Madame Olenska to them,” he snapped. “I don’t see myself—or you—offering up to expiate her sins.”

“Oh, of course you see only the Mingott side,” his mother said, voice tinged with hurt.

The butler drew back the portières and announced: “Mr. Henry van der Luyden.”

Mrs. Archer dropped her needle and pushed her chair back. “Another lamp,” she called to the servant, while Janey adjusted her mother’s cap.

Mr. van der Luyden stepped into the room, and Archer moved forward.

“We were just talking about you, sir,” he said.

Mr. van der Luyden seemed taken aback. He removed his glove to shake hands, smoothed his hat shyly, and Janey offered him a chair.

“And the Countess Olenska,” Archer added.

Mrs. Archer paled.

“Oh—a charming woman,” Mr. van der Luyden said, regaining composure. “I just visited her. She has a real gift for arranging flowers. I sent her carnations from Skuytercliff, and I was astonished. Instead of grouping them in big bunches like our gardener does, she scattered them loosely—here and there. I can’t describe it. The Duke told me: ‘Go see how cleverly she’s arranged her drawing-room.’ And she has. I’d really like to take Louisa to see her—if the neighborhood weren’t so… unpleasant.”

A silence followed.

Mrs. Archer pulled her embroidery from the basket, and Archer leaned against the fireplace, twisting a hummingbird-feather screen in his hand. Janey’s face lit up as the second lamp was lit.

“The fact is,” Mr. van der Luyden continued, stroking his grey leg with a pale hand heavy with the Patroon’s signet ring, “the fact is, I came to thank her for her lovely note about my flowers—and also—to give her a friendly warning about letting the Duke carry her off to parties. I don’t know if you’ve heard—”

Mrs. Archer smiled indulgently. “Has the Duke been carrying her off to parties?”

“You know what these English grandees are. They’re all alike. Louisa and I adore our cousin—but it’s hopeless to expect people used to European courts to care about our little republican distinctions. The Duke goes where he’s amused.”

He paused. No one spoke.

“Yes—it seems he took her to Mrs. Struthers’s last night. Sillerton Jackson just came to us with the story, and Louisa was rather troubled. So I thought the best way was to go straight to Countess Olenska and explain—by the merest hint, you understand—how we feel in New York about certain matters. I felt I might, without impropriety, because the evening she dined with us, she rather suggested… she let me see that she would appreciate guidance. And she *did*.”

Mr. van der Luyden looked around the room with a mild benevolence that Mrs. Archer dutifully mirrored.

“How kind you both are, dear Henry—always! Newland will especially appreciate what you’ve done, for dear May and their new relations.”

She shot her son a warning glance. He said: “Immensely, sir. But I was sure you’d like Madame Olenska.”

Mr. van der Luyden looked at him gently. “I never invite anyone to my house, my dear Newland,” he said, “whom I do not like. And so I’ve just told Sillerton Jackson.”

He glanced at the clock. “But Louisa will be waiting. We’re dining early to take the Duke to the Opera.”

After the portières closed behind him, silence fell.

“Gracious—how romantic!” Janey burst out.

No one knew what she meant. Her family had long since stopped trying.

Mrs. Archer shook her head. “Provided it all turns out for the best,” she said, as if knowing it wouldn’t.

“Newland, you must stay and see Sillerton Jackson when he comes tonight. I really don’t know what to say to him.”

“Poor mother! But he won’t come—” her son laughed, bending to kiss her frown away.
