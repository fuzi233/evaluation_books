"Good heavens," Mrs. Jennings declared, her eyes crinkling with a sagacious, predatory gleam the moment the gentleman withdrew, "I do not ask you what the Colonel has been saying to you. Though, upon my honour, I *tried* to keep out of hearing, I could not help catching enough to understand his business. And I assure you, I have never been better pleased in my life. I wish you joy of it with all my heart."

Elinor bowed, her composure holding firm despite the storm of emotion raging within. "Thank you, ma'am. It *is* a matter of great joy to me; and I feel the goodness of Colonel Brandon most sensibly. There are not many men who would act as he has done. Few possess such a compassionate heart! I have never been more astonished in my life."

"Lord! My dear, you are very modest. I am not the least astonished in the world. I have often thought of late there was nothing more likely to happen."

"You judged from your knowledge of the Colonel's general benevolence; but at least you could not foresee that the opportunity would occur so very soon."

"Opportunity!" Mrs. Jennings repeated, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "Oh! As to that, when a man has once made up his mind to such a thing, somehow or other he will soon find an opening. Well, my dear, I wish you joy of it again and again. If ever there was a happy couple in the world, I think I shall soon know where to look for them."

"You mean to go to Delaford after them, I suppose?" Elinor asked, a faint smile touching her lips.

"Aye, my dear, that I do, indeed. And as to the house being a bad one, I do not know what the Colonel would be at. It is as good a one as ever I saw."

"He spoke of its being out of repair."

"Well, and whose fault is that? Why doesn't he repair it? Who should do it but himself?"

Their conversation was abruptly cut short by the servant entering to announce the carriage at the door. Mrs. Jennings prepared to depart, yet lingered with the urgency of a hawk spotting prey.

"Well, my dear, I must be gone before I have had half my talk out. However, we may have it all over in the evening; for we shall be quite alone. I do not ask you to come with me; your mind is surely too full of this matter to care for company, and besides, you must long to tell your sister all about it."

Marianne had already fled the room before the exchange began.

"Certainly, ma'am, I shall tell Marianne of it; but I shall not mention it to anyone else at present."

"Oh! Very well," Mrs. Jennings said, her disappointment palpable. "Then you would not have me tell it to Lucy? For I intend to travel as far as Holborn today."

"No, ma'am, not even Lucy, if you please. One day's delay will not be material; and until I have written to Mr. Ferrars, I think it ought not to be mentioned to anyone else. I shall do *that* directly. It is of paramount importance that no time be lost with him, for he will, of course, have much to attend to regarding his ordination."

This declaration baffled Mrs. Jennings initially. Why write to Mr. Ferrars in such haste? Yet, a moment's reflection sparked a brilliant, mischievous idea. She exclaimed, "Oh, ho! I understand you. Mr. Ferrars is to be the man. Well, so much the better for him. Ay, to be sure, he must be ordained in readiness. I am very glad to find things are so forward between you. But, my dear, is this not rather out of character? Should not the Colonel write himself? Surely, he is the proper person."

Elinor did not grasp the initial premise of Mrs. Jennings's speech, nor did she deem it worthy of inquiry, replying only to the conclusion. "Colonel Brandon is so delicate a man that he rather wished anyone to announce his intentions to Mr. Ferrars than himself."

"And so *you* are forced to do it. Well, *that* is an odd kind of delicacy! However, I will not disturb you (seeing her prepare to write). You know your own concerns best. So goodby, my dear. I have not heard of anything to please me so well since Charlotte was brought to bed."

She departed, only to return instantly. "I have just been thinking of Betty's sister, my dear. I should be very glad to get her such a good mistress. But whether she would do for a lady's maid, I am sure I can't tell. She is an excellent housemaid and works very well at her needle. However, you will think of all that at your leisure."

"Certainly, ma'am," replied Elinor, barely registering the words, her anxiety focused solely on solitude. How she should begin—how she should phrase her note to Edward—was now her sole concern. The peculiar circumstances surrounding them complicated a task that would be trivial for any other pair. She feared saying too much or too little, sitting deliberatively over her paper, pen poised, until the room fell silent save for the scratching of ink.

Her concentration was shattered by Edward's sudden entrance. He had encountered Mrs. Jennings at the threshold on his way to leave his farewell card; she, after apologizing for her absence, had insisted he enter, claiming Miss Dashwood awaited him on urgent business. Elinor had just congratulated herself that writing was preferable to speaking when her visitor arrived, forcing her into this greatest of exertions.

Her astonishment and confusion were profound. She had not seen him since his engagement became public, and thus not since he knew she was aware of it. Combined with the weight of what she needed to convey, the encounter left her uncomfortably flustered for several minutes. Edward, too, was visibly distressed. They sat together in a state of promising embarrassment. Whether he had apologized upon entry, he could not recall; but deciding caution was safer, he formally excused himself as soon as he could speak, settling into a chair.

"Mrs. Jennings told me," he said, "that you wished to speak with me—at least, I understood her so—or I certainly should not have intruded in such a manner. Though at the same time, I should have been extremely sorry to leave London without seeing you and your sister, especially as it will most likely be some time before I have the pleasure of meeting you again. I go to Oxford tomorrow."

"You would not have gone, however," Elinor replied, regaining her composure and determined to dispel the dread quickly, "without receiving our good wishes, even if we had not been able to give them in person. Mrs. Jennings was quite right. I have something of consequence to inform you of, which I was on the point of communicating by paper. I am charged with a most agreeable office..." She breathed slightly faster than usual. "Colonel Brandon, who was here only ten minutes ago, has desired me to say that, understanding you mean to take orders, he has great pleasure in offering you the living of Delaford, now just vacant. He only wishes it were more valuable. Allow me to congratulate you on having so respectable and well-judging a friend, and to join in his wish that the living—it is about two hundred a year—were much more considerable, and such as might better enable you to... as might be more than a temporary accommodation to yourself—such, in short, as might establish all your views of happiness."

What Edward felt, unable to articulate it himself, remains unsaid by others. He *looked* all the astonishment such unexpected, unimagined news could inspire, uttering only two words: "Colonel Brandon!"

"Yes," Elinor continued, gathering resolution as the worst of the shock faded, "Colonel Brandon means it as a testimony of his concern for the cruel situation in which the unjustifiable conduct of your family has placed you—a concern which I am sure Marianne, myself, and all your friends must share; and likewise as a proof of his high esteem for your general character and his particular approbation of your behavior on this occasion."

"Colonel Brandon gives *me* a living! Can it be possible?"

"The unkindness of your own relations has made you astonished to find friendship anywhere."

"No," he replied, struck by sudden realization, "not to find it in *you*; for I cannot be ignorant that to you, to your goodness, I owe it all. I feel it—I would express it if I could—but, as you well know, I am no orator."

"You are very much mistaken. I do assure you that you owe it entirely, at least almost entirely, to your own merit and Colonel Brandon's discernment of it. I have had no hand in it. I did not even know, until I understood his design, that the living was vacant; nor had it ever occurred to me that he might have such a living in his gift. As a friend of mine, of my family, he may, perhaps—in fact, I know he *has*—still greater pleasure in bestowing it; but, upon my word, you owe nothing to my solicitation."

Truth obliged her to acknowledge a small share in the action, yet she was so unwilling to appear as Edward's benefactress that she admitted it with hesitation. This ambiguity likely fixed the suspicion in his mind that had recently taken root.
