The planned outing to Whitwell turned out far differently than Elinor had anticipated. She had braced herself for damp clothes, exhaustion, and perhaps even a touch of fear—but what actually happened was worse: they didn’t go at all. By ten o’clock, the entire party had gathered at the park, ready to enjoy breakfast before setting off. Though rain had fallen through the night, the morning offered some relief—the clouds were breaking apart, and the sun peeked through now and then. Everyone was in high spirits, eager to make the most of the day, determined to endure any discomfort rather than miss this rare opportunity for joy.

As they sat down to eat, the letters arrived. Among them was one addressed to Colonel Brandon. He took it, glanced at the handwriting, paled slightly, and immediately excused himself from the table.

“What’s wrong with Brandon?” Sir John asked, puzzled.
No one could say.
“I hope he hasn’t received bad news,” Lady Middleton murmured.
“It must be something extraordinary,” Sir John added, “to make Colonel Brandon leave my breakfast table so abruptly.”

After a few minutes, Brandon returned.
“No bad news, Colonel?” Mrs. Jennings asked as soon as he stepped back into the room.
“None at all, ma'am, I thank you.”
“Was it from Avignon? I hope your sister isn’t worse?”
“No, ma'am. It came from town—it’s only business.”
“But why did you turn so pale? If it’s just business, why such a reaction?” she pressed. “Come now, Colonel—let us hear the truth.”

“Dear madam,” Lady Middleton interjected gently, “do remember your manners.”

But Mrs. Jennings paid no heed. “Perhaps it’s to tell you that your cousin Fanny is married?” she guessed.
“No indeed,” Brandon replied.
“Well, then, I know who it’s from,” she declared. “And I hope she’s well.”

“Who do you mean, ma'am?” he asked, his cheeks warming slightly.
“Oh! You know exactly who I mean.”

“I am truly sorry,” he said, turning to Lady Middleton, “that I should receive this letter today. It concerns matters that require my immediate presence in London.”

“In London!” Mrs. Jennings exclaimed. “What on earth could you possibly need there at this time of year?”

“My loss is great,” he continued, “in having to leave such a pleasant gathering. But I’m especially regretful, because I fear my absence may prevent you from gaining admittance to Whitwell.”

It was a devastating blow. The disappointment was palpable.

“But if you write a note to the housekeeper,” Marianne suggested eagerly, “wouldn’t that suffice?”

Brandon shook his head.
“We must go,” Sir John insisted. “We’re too close now to give up. You can’t possibly leave until tomorrow, Brandon—that’s all.”

“I wish it were so simple,” Brandon replied. “But I cannot delay my journey by even a single day.”

“If you’d only let us know what your business is,” Mrs. Jennings urged, “we might see if it could be postponed.”

“You wouldn’t be six hours later,” Willoughby said calmly, “if you waited until we returned.”

“I cannot afford to lose *one* hour,” Brandon repeated firmly.

At that moment, Elinor overheard Willoughby whisper to Marianne, “There are some people who simply can’t stand a good time. Brandon is one of them. He probably just wanted to avoid getting cold—and made up this excuse to get out of it. I’d bet fifty guineas that he wrote the letter himself.”

“I have no doubt of it,” Marianne replied without hesitation.

“Brandon,” Sir John said with a sigh, “you’ve always been stubborn when you set your mind to something. But still, think about it—here are the two Miss Careys come all the way from Newton, the three Miss Dashwoods walked up from the cottage, and Mr. Willoughby rose two hours earlier than usual just to join us at Whitwell.”

Brandon once again expressed his sorrow at causing such dismay, but stood firm in his decision.

“And when will you return?” Lady Middleton asked.
“I hope to see you at Barton soon,” she added, “as soon as you can conveniently leave town. We’ll have to postpone our visit to Whitwell until then.”

“You are very kind,” Brandon replied. “But I cannot promise when I might return—it’s uncertain.”

“Oh, he *must* and *shall* come back!” Sir John declared. “If he’s not here by the end of the week, I’ll go after him myself.”

“Do go, Sir John,” Mrs. Jennings chimed in. “Then maybe you’ll discover what his real business is.”

“I don’t want to pry into other men’s affairs,” Sir John said. “I suppose it’s something he’s ashamed of.”

Just then, the announcement came that Colonel Brandon’s horses were ready.
“You’re not riding all the way to town, are you?” Sir John asked.
“No. Only to Honiton. From there, I’ll take the post-chaise.”

“Well, since you’re resolved to go,” Sir John said, “I wish you a safe journey. But you really ought to reconsider.”

“I assure you,” Brandon said, “it’s beyond my control.”

He then bade farewell to everyone. Turning to Elinor, he said, “Is there any chance I might see you and your sisters in town this winter?”
“I’m afraid not,” she replied.
“Then I must bid you farewell for longer than I would like,” he said quietly.

To Marianne, he merely bowed and said nothing.

“Come, Colonel,” Mrs. Jennings called after him, “before you go—do tell us what this urgent matter is.”

He wished her a good morning and, escorted by Sir John, left the room.

Now, with no restraint of politeness, the full weight of their disappointment poured forth. Every person at the table lamented the ruined plan, repeating over and over how unjust it was to be denied such a day.

“I can guess what his business is,” Mrs. Jennings announced triumphantly.
“Can you, ma'am?” several voices asked.
“Yes,” she declared. “It’s about Miss Williams—I’m certain of it.”

“And who is Miss Williams?” Marianne asked.
“What! Don’t you know who Miss Williams is? You must have heard of her. She’s a relation of the Colonel’s—a very close one. We won’t say just how close, for fear of shocking the young ladies.”

She lowered her voice slightly and leaned toward Elinor. “She’s his natural daughter.”

“Indeed!” Elinor murmured.
“Oh, yes,” Mrs. Jennings confirmed. “And as like him as she can stare. I daresay the Colonel will leave her everything.”

When Sir John returned, he joined in the general regret but quickly shifted the mood. “Since we’re all together,” he said, “we must find some way to enjoy ourselves. Let’s not waste the day.” After brief discussion, they decided that while happiness couldn’t be found at Whitwell, they could at least seek comfort in a country drive.

The carriages were summoned. Willoughby’s curricle was first to depart, and Marianne looked more radiant than ever as she climbed inside. He drove swiftly through the park, and soon they vanished from sight. Not until much later did they return—after all the others had already come back.

Both seemed thoroughly delighted with their ride, though they spoke only in vague terms about keeping to the lanes while the others explored the downs.

That evening, a dance was arranged, and everyone vowed to be exceptionally merry. More of the Carey family arrived for dinner, swelling the company to nearly twenty—a number Sir John found immensely satisfying.

Willoughby took his usual seat between the two elder Miss Dashwoods. Mrs. Jennings sat beside Elinor, and not long after they were seated, she leaned behind them and addressed Marianne loudly enough for both sisters to hear:
“I’ve found you out, despite all your tricks. I know exactly where you spent the morning.”

Marianne flushed and replied sharply, “Where, pray?”

“Didn’t you know,” Willoughby said coolly, “that we went out in my curricle?”

“Yes, yes, Mr. Impudence,” Mrs. Jennings snapped. “I know that well enough. But I was determined to learn *where* you went. I hope you’re enjoying your house, Miss Marianne. It’s quite large, I understand. And when I visit you, I hope you’ll have furnished it properly—because it certainly needed it when I was there six years ago.”

Marianne turned away, deeply embarrassed. Mrs. Jennings laughed heartily.

Later, Elinor discovered that Mrs. Jennings had sent her maid to question Willoughby’s groom—and learned that they had gone to Allenham, spending a considerable time walking through the garden and touring the house.

Elinor found this hard to believe. It seemed unlikely that Willoughby would suggest entering the house while Mrs. Smith was still living there—someone Marianne barely knew.

As soon as they left the dining room, Elinor questioned her sister. Her surprise was immense when Marianne confirmed every detail.

“You doubted me?” Marianne said, annoyed.
“Why should I imagine we didn’t go there—or that we didn’t see the house? Isn’t that something you’ve often wanted to do yourself?”

“Yes,” Elinor admitted, “but I wouldn’t go while Mrs. Smith was there—especially with no one but Mr. Willoughby.”

“But Mr. Willoughby is the only one who has the right to show the house,” Marianne argued. “And since he traveled in an open carriage, we couldn perfection had another companion. I never spent a more delightful morning in my life.”

“I’m afraid,” Elinor replied gently, “that pleasure doesn’t always prove propriety.”

“On the contrary,” Marianne insisted, “nothing could better prove it. If I had done anything truly wrong, I would have felt it at the time—we always know when we’re acting improperly. And if I truly believed I was doing something wrong, I could not have enjoyed it.”

“But,” Elinor said, “since it’s already brought you such impertinent remarks, don’t you now question your own judgment?”

“If Mrs. Jennings’ opinions are to define what’s proper,” Marianne retorted, “then we’re all guilty every minute. I care not for her approval—or her disapproval. I feel no guilt for walking through Mrs. Smith’s grounds or seeing her house. One day, they’ll belong to Mr. Willoughby—and—”

“If they were to become yours someday, Marianne,” Elinor interrupted softly, “would you still consider your actions justified?”

Marianne blushed—but there was a flicker of satisfaction in her eyes. After a pause, she returned to her sister, smiling brightly.
“Perhaps,” she said, “it *was* rather unwise of me to go to Allenham. But Mr. Willoughby was eager to show me the place—and it is truly charming. There’s one remarkable sitting room upstairs—just the right size for daily use, and with modern furnishings, it would be perfect. It’s a corner room with windows on two sides. On one side, you look across the bowling green, behind the house, to a lovely hanging wood. On the other, you see the church and village, and beyond them, those magnificent hills we’ve admired so often. I didn’t see it at its best—the furniture was dreadful—but if it were refurnished—say, two hundred pounds, Willoughby says—it could be one of the most delightful summer rooms in England.”

If Elinor had been able to listen without interruption, Marianne would have described every room in the house with equal enthusiasm.
