*"On earth is known to none
The smile that is not sister to a tear."*

Margaret and her father walked home under a canopy of stars. The air was crisp and invigorating, carrying the scent of summer evenings, and Margaret, dressed in a gown of white silk that fluttered softly against her legs, felt a lightness of spirit. Beside her, Mr. Hale moved with a gentle step, his hand occasionally resting on her shoulder.

"I believe Mr. Thornton is troubled," Margaret observed, looking ahead. "He seemed quite uneasy tonight."

"He was thoughtful, perhaps," Mr. Hale replied, his tone measured. "Yet, when addressing the others, he maintained his customary composure. That stoicism, however, often masks deeper currents within men of his nature."

"His countenance, Father, speaks of anxiety," Margaret persisted. "And I think it right that it does. If I were in his place, knowing the simmering resentment of his workers—the way they view him as a 'hard man,' devoid of feeling, rigid in his adherence to principle—I would not expect calm. I recall the words of Mr. Boucher, half-mad with grief and injustice. To imagine Mr. Thornton remaining so serene amidst such storm is difficult for me to accept."

"My dear girl, you judge him by the surface of the storm, whereas I see the rock beneath. Mr. Thornton is a man of immense pride, Margaret, a quality that shields his vulnerabilities. He is not easily swayed from his judgments, nor does he permit his emotions to dictate his actions openly. This is a trait I have always admired in him. He possesses a strength of character forged by a practical life, a necessity for constant judgment and self-restraint from youth. While he lacks the breadth of historical knowledge that comes from leisure, he is acutely aware of this deficit, and that awareness alone proves a fertile mind. You are merely seeing the shield, not the warrior within."

"But I am not entirely convinced," Margaret murmured, her brow furrowing slightly. "To me, he remains a stranger, a figure of great intellect but perhaps lacking warmth. Yet, I found myself drawn to the conversation of the gentlemen tonight, despite my confusion. And I was truly discomfited when Miss Thornton removed me from the circle, fearing I would be isolated among so many men. The company of the ladies, I confess, was tedious—though they did possess a peculiar wit. They played a game of sorts, weaving sentences around nouns that signified wealth: *housekeepers*, *under-gardeners*, *glass*, *lace*. It was a strange exercise, reminding me of our childhood games, yet it highlighted the gulf between their world and ours."

"And yet, you felt a kinship?" Mr. Hale asked gently.

"Yes," she admitted, a soft smile touching her lips. "A sense of goodwill. I know he is kind in his own way, and slowly, I begin to appreciate that kindness."

"Then you shall grow to admire him, I am sure." Mr. Hale smiled, a quiet, paternal expression. "Though, I must say, I was mistaken in my assessment of your appearance tonight. You carried yourself with the grace of a lady."

Margaret laughed, a bright, clear sound. "Did you really think so? I felt like a fraud, Father. Sitting there with my idle hands, draped in silk, while I thought of the hard labor the servants performed today. They treated me as a princess, and I felt guilty for the moment."

"You were a lady, my dear," Mr. Hale said warmly. "And they served you as such."

Their conversation dissolved as Dixon opened the front door, her face pale with terror.

"Oh, Master Hale! Oh, Miss Margaret!" Dixon gasped, clutching her apron. "Thank God you are home! Dr. Donaldson is here. The charwoman has returned, but the woman next door sent for him immediately. The servant who usually watches is gone. She... she was dying, sir! I thought she'd pass away an hour ago!"

Mr. Hale stepped forward, steadying himself, his face draining of color. He looked at Margaret, searching her eyes for any sign of dread, but found instead a mixture of shock and profound sorrow. She understood more than he did, yet she stood frozen, her expression one of awed, helpless apprehension.

"Oh! I should not have left her!" Margaret cried, supporting her father as he stumbled toward the stairs. "Wicked daughter that I am!"

Dr. Donaldson awaited them on the landing. "She is recovering now," he whispered urgently. "The opiate has worked. The spasms were severe, naturally frightening the maid, but she will rally."

"This time!" Mr. Hale breathed, his vision blurring, his limbs trembling as if age had suddenly overtaken him. "Let me go to her!"

Dr. Donaldson took hold of his arm, guiding him into the bedroom. Margaret followed closely. Lying in the bed was her mother, her face etched with an unmistakable pallor. Though she slept, the silence between her and death was thin. It was evident that the Reaper was waiting, poised to claim her again. Mr. Hale stared at her for a long, agonizing minute, unable to speak. Then, a shiver racked his frame, and he turned away from the doctor, groping blindly for the door. The candles, hastily lit in the panic, burned brightly, yet he could not find the handle. He staggered into the drawing-room, collapsing onto a chair.

"Speak to him, Miss Hale," Dr. Donaldson urged, his voice firm. "We must rouse him."

"Papa!" Margaret called out, her voice breaking with raw emotion. "Papa! Speak to me!"

Slowly, the fog in his eyes cleared. With a Herculean effort, he lifted his gaze. "Margaret," he croaked, tears streaming down his cheeks. "Did you know of this? Oh, it was cruel of you!"

"No, sir! It was not cruel!" Dr. Donaldson interjected swiftly. "Miss Hale acted under my instructions. There may have been an error in judgment, but it was not cruelty. Your wife will be altered tomorrow, I trust. The spasms, as I predicted, required sedation. She will sleep deeply, and that look of terror will fade."

"But not the illness?" Mr. Hale whispered, his voice barely audible.

Dr. Donaldson glanced at Margaret. Her bowed head, her silent plea, indicated that she wished the full truth spoken. "Not the disease," the doctor said solemnly. "Our skill, however vaunted, cannot cure the malady. We can only delay its march and alleviate the suffering. Be a man, sir—a Christian. Have faith in the immortality of the soul, which no earthly pain or sickness can touch."

The response Mr. Hale offered was a strangled sob, followed by deep, manly weeping that resonated through the silent house like a heavy drumbeat.

Margaret knelt beside him, her hands clasping his, her tears falling freely. Time seemed to stretch and contract in equal measure. Finally, Mr. Hale, the first to regain his composure, broke the silence. "What must we do? Tell us both. Margaret is my staff, my right hand."

Dr. Donaldson laid out his plan with clear, sensible authority. Fear for the night was over; peace for the morrow, and likely for many days to follow, was assured. However, there was no promise of ultimate recovery. He advised Mr. Hale to retire, suggesting that only one guard was needed for the night, leaving ample time for rest. He promised to return early in the morning. With a kind handshake, he departed.

Words were scarce between the parents; exhaustion from the terror left them with little energy for discourse, save for the necessary decisions. Mr. Hale resolved to sit up all night, but Margaret, with a gentle firmness, prevailed upon him to lie down on the drawing-room sofa. Dixon, stubborn and blunt, refused to go to bed, and as for Margaret, it was impossible for her to abandon her mother. Thus, Dixon sat, staring, blinking, drooping, and jerking awake, until eventually, she succumbed to a heavy snore.

Margaret, having discarded her gown with impatient disgust, wrapped herself in her dressing-gown. Sleep eluded her completely. Every sensation seemed amplified, every nerve ending hyper-vigilant. For over two hours, she heard her father's restless movements in the adjacent room. He drifted constantly to her mother's door, pausing to listen intently, prompting her to open it and report the stillness of the vigil, answering the broken questions his parched lips could scarcely form. Eventually, he too fell into a fitful slumber, and the house sank into a profound silence.

Sitting behind the curtain, Margaret thought. In the vast expanse of time and space, the concerns of recent days faded. Less than thirty-six hours prior, her heart had ached for Bessy Higgins and Boucher; now, those sorrows seemed like a dream from a forgotten life. Everything that had happened outside the house felt severed from her mother, rendered unreal by distance. Even Harley Street, once vibrant with the news of home, seemed distant. Helstone itself receded into a dim, gray past. The monotonous winter and spring, previously uneventful, now held a strange association with what she cherished most.
