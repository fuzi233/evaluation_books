Harry Warrington sat in the dim, musty room of the lock-up house, his spirits a tumult of shame, anger, and unease. The reality of being arrested—of being locked away like a common debtor—struck him with sudden force. He had always moved through life as if above such indignities, a young man of fortune, charm, and consequence. Now he was confined, not by any crime, but by debt—a fate that seemed absurd to him, yet one he could not deny.

He felt genuine remorse for his reckless spending, his gambling habits, his indulgence in pleasures without thought of consequences. Yet he also resented the humiliation of it all. To be dragged from his chambers, surrounded by bailiffs and clerks, while the world looked on—this was not how a gentleman should live or fall. But Harry was certain this would be brief. Twenty friends stood ready to help him: Mr. Draper, the shrewd businessman who had always been attentive; his uncle, the Baronet, who had offered him a home and treated him like a son; his cousin Castlewood, who had won vast sums from him at cards; the noble gentlemen at White’s Club; even his Aunt Bernstein, whose affection he sometimes doubted but always cherished.

Any one of them would step forward, though perhaps with a scolding for his folly. The key, however, was discretion. Mr. Warrington cared deeply about appearances. He did not want the public to know that a man of his standing had been reduced to this. As if the world didn’t already know everything it wished to learn! As if the hole in his stocking, visible now beneath his boot, or the dinner he would eat later, could remain hidden from some curious observer. The truth was, secrets were fragile things. The world prided itself on knowing what it wanted to know—and then promptly forgot it.

People took their reputations too seriously, he mused. A man falls into prison? He thinks himself ruined. Bankrupt? He believes society will cast him out. Exposed in a bargain gone wrong? He imagines he’ll be punished. But no—society yawns. It talks, dines, laughs, dances, as if nothing had happened. If you tumble, brush off the dirt, smile, and reappear—no one notices. You are not the center of the universe, mi fili. And yet here he was, fuming as if every soul in London had felt the weight of that hand laid upon his shoulder.

“A fine sensation my arrest must have caused at the club,” he muttered. “Selwyn will no doubt be making jokes about it. March will tremble over our bet—I wonder if I’ll ever be able to pay him when I lose. They’ll be cheering at the Savage, calling me ‘the Savage’ now that I’m behind bars. How can I face anyone again?”

Yet, despite his pride and distress, Harry displayed a strange mix of cunning and simplicity. He decided not to turn immediately to family or friends. Instead, he chose Sampson—the chaplain, a man who had once found himself in similar straits and might know how to navigate the maze of legal troubles.

As dawn broke, weak and grey, Harry sent a message to Sampson in Long Acre, pleading for advice and comfort. The hours stretched slowly. The light crept into the cell-like room, the silence broken only by the occasional shuffle of feet from other inmates. When breakfast arrived, Harry ordered it eaten in silence, refusing to dine at Mrs. Bailiff’s table with the others. “I’ll be gone before dinner,” he declared to the attendant, who likely thought this a familiar boast from another young gentleman who believed escape was just around the corner.

“Very well,” the man replied, “but if you stay, there’s good beef and carrots at two o’clock.” And with that, he left Harry alone with his thoughts.

But no Sampson came. Not at ten, nor eleven, nor noon. Instead, just after midday, Gumbo burst in—his master’s servant—carrying a portmanteau and weeping uncontrollably. He flung himself at Harry’s feet, sobbing, swearing eternal loyalty, promising to sell himself into slavery again if it meant freeing his beloved master.

Harry, touched by the display, gently lifted him up. “All you need do is get me dressed and keep your mouth shut. Not a word to anyone, understand?”

“Oh no, sir, never to nobody!” Gumbo vowed, eyes wide and solemn.

And so he began dressing his master—powdering his hair, brushing his coat, arranging his cravat—until Harry looked every inch the gentleman, polished and poised as if preparing for a visit to St. James’s Palace.

Gumbo served his master with devotion, but there were limits to what even love could achieve. He could not undo what had been done. He could not stop himself from talking—especially when grief and excitement stirred his tongue. For though he swore secrecy, Gumbo had already told the tale to half of London.

He had carried the news to Colonel Lambert’s and Lord Wrotham’s servants. He’d shared it at the footman’s club, where noblemen’s retainers gathered. He’d drunk ale with Sir Miles Warrington’s butler, embellishing the story with each retelling. Then he’d visited Madame Bernstein’s household, and finally Lord Castlewood’s, spreading the news like wildfire.

So when Gumbo stood before Harry, hand on heart, tears streaming, and promised never to speak of it, the reader may judge how credible that oath truly was.

At last, late in the afternoon, a letter arrived—not from Sampson, but from him. Sealed with wet wax, the ink still fresh, it read:

> “Oh, sir, dear sir, I have done all a man can do for his patron! You did not know what you were asking of me. Else, why would I not share your prison, if I am to go to one? Indeed, I am in one myself—three doors down, at Naboth’s.
>
> As I hurried to you, knowing the danger, I was seized by two men with a writ against me. They’ve locked me up close by—so near, we could almost hear each other across the garden walls.
>
> I had much to say about your affairs, which I cannot write down. May they mend! May my cursed fortunes improve!
>
> Your afflicted Chaplain-in-Ordinary,

Thus, Sampson refused to speak further. But now, let us reveal what he dared not write.

Gumbo had not reached Long Acre, so Sampson remained unaware of the full extent of Harry’s plight until he received the message from Chancery Lane. Still grateful for the kindness Harry had shown him—having recently helped him financially—Sampson rushed to Lord Castlewood’s house, hoping to move his noble cousin to mercy.

He spoke passionately, with real emotion, of Harry’s misfortune, of his generosity, of his downfall. But Lord Castlewood cut him short.

“Did I not tell you two days ago,” he said sharply, “that I am as poor as a beggar? Has anyone left me a fortune since? The money I won from him was spent long ago. I cannot help him—and indeed, I had written this morning asking *him* for aid.”

A letter to this effect had already reached Harry’s lodgings via the penny post.

“I must find him money,” Sampson insisted. “I know he had little left after helping me. I’ll pawn my cassock, my bands—anything!”

“Go ahead,” said Castlewood, unimpressed. “Your enthusiasm is admirable. But I have no use for it.” And with that, he returned to his reading, leaving the chaplain crushed.

Meanwhile, Lady Maria, hearing of Sampson’s visit, confronted him as he left her brother’s house. Her face showed deep concern.

“What has happened to Mr. Warrington?” she asked.

“You know?” Sampson replied.

“I’ve been in agony since Gumbo brought the news last night,” she said. “We heard it at the opera—from Lady Yarmouth’s box. My lord, I, and Lady Castlewood.”

“And my lord knew?” Sampson asked.

“Benson told us when we returned from the playhouse,” she confirmed.

The chaplain exploded. “This is outrageous!” he exclaimed. He recounted his failed appeal to Castlewood—the refusal, the hypocrisy, the cruelty of winning from Harry and then turning his back when he was in need.

Lady Maria, usually reserved, erupted in a torrent of words. She spoke bitterly of her family, of their greed, their lack of honor, their betrayal of kinship. With tears and fury, she accused her brother of selfishness, of using Harry as a pawn. She called him cowardly, dishonorable, unworthy of respect.

She stormed toward Castlewood’s library door, shrieking, until the terrified chaplain fled outside.

Castlewood looked up, surprised, and launched a sharp curse at her.

But Maria was fearless now. “You sit with him at cards, take his last shilling, and refuse to give him a penny when he’s penniless!” she shouted.

“So the chaplain has been gossiping?” Castlewood snapped.

“Dismiss him! Pay him and send him away—he’ll be glad enough to leave!”

“I keep him for when one of my sisters needs marrying,” Castlewood growled.

“What kind of women are we, when our brothers are like this?” Maria demanded.

“Effectivement,” Castlewood shrugged.

“We are bad enough—but what are *you*? No courage, no honor, no decency. Since your equals won’t play with you, you take a poor lad from Virginia—your own cousin—and fleece him! It’s a disgrace!”

“We all play our own games,” Castlewood retorted. “Haven’t you played yours, Maria? Are you suddenly squeamish about the boy? Has he rejected you? Or have you found a better match?”

“I’d be a fool to expect you to act like an honest man,” Maria said coldly. “You wouldn’t help a relative in distress. You’d fleece him.”

“Fleece him? Nonsense. Look at his behavior—how could he avoid this end? If I hadn’t taken his money, someone else would. I didn’t begrudge you your schemes. Why fly into a rage because I took what he was offering freely?”

“You think this money belongs to you? It’s not yours to spend. In three months, every shilling would’ve vanished into White’s gaming tables. Better it pays your debts.”

“My good brother might have given more than kind words to the boy from whom he just took hundreds,” Maria interjected.

“Great heavens, Maria! Don’t you see this could be a chance for a clever woman?” Castlewood said, smirking. “Suppose I know of a situation—another family, another young man. A green lad comes to town, a country cousin. An elderly female relative, lonely and ambitious, extracts a marriage promise from him. He doesn’t want it. He’ll claim his mother refuses. He’ll lie. He’ll break it.”

“My lord, are you insulting me as well as your cousin?”

“Not at all. I said nothing about cheating. I simply tell a story. The aunt gets a promise—then he breaks it. That’s what happens. Like Tunbridge—your Aunt Bernstein handled it perfectly. She got the letters, hid them, and said nothing. Wise woman. You still pine for your Cherokee. Soit. A woman knows the value of a husband. What’s the loss of two or three hundred pounds?”

“Not more than three hundred?” Maria asked.

“Eh? Never mind a hundred or two. It’s a bagatelle. You’re playing for a principality. You want your kingdom in Virginia. And this little misfortune? It may be great luck for you.”

“I don’t understand.”

“C’est possible. But sit down—I’ll explain.” And so, the furious lioness collapsed into a gentle lamb at her brother’s feet.

Madame de Bernstein, upon hearing the news from Gumbo, was disturbed. She had not slept, consumed by worry. Was Harry in serious trouble? How much would it cost to free him? Had he spent all his own funds?

She liked his extravagance. He was the only person in years who had drawn her affection. She sent a messenger to Mr. Draper, her man of business, ordering him to investigate Harry’s debts and report immediately.

Draper discovered the claims: Mr. Sparks, the jeweller, for unpaid goods; the landlord in Bond Street, for board and lodging. These were manageable. He could settle them at once—with her guarantee.

“There’s no difficulty in raising a thousand pounds more or less for a gentleman of Mr. Warrington’s rank,” she said.

Draper suggested settling the matter quickly, especially the jeweller’s account—Harry had taken items on credit and pledged them to a pawnbroker. If made public, it would look dishonorable.

But the Baroness had a different idea. “I think,” she said, “my young gentleman has sown enough wild oats. When he comes out, I want him clean—no debts, no liabilities.”

“No gentleman ever tells all his debts,” Draper reminded her.

“True,” she replied. “But I want him free.”

And so, while Harry waited in silence, the web of influence, money, and family politics tightened around him—each player acting not from duty, but from self-interest, ambition, or pity. The chains of debt bound him, but the greater chains were those of expectation, blood, and social order.
