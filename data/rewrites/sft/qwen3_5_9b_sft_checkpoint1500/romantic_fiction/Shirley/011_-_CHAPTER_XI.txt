Caroline did not yield easily. Within the quiet chambers of her girlhood, she harbored a strength that was rarely summoned, yet when called upon, it proved formidable. It is a peculiar truth of human nature that men and women fight hardest when they stand alone—without witnesses, counselors, or confidants, unaided, unadvised, and unpitied. Miss Helstone found herself in this solitary arena. Her suffering was the only spur that drove her forward; it was acute and piercing, striking a nerve that woke her spirit to a feverish vigilance. Determined to conquer a mortal pain, she threw herself into activity with a zeal previously unknown to her. She walked in every season, venturing long distances into solitude. Each evening she returned, pale and worn, yet seemingly devoid of fatigue. As soon as she removed her bonnet and shawl, instead of resting, she began to pace her room. At times, she did not sit until she was physically faint. She claimed this was necessary to tire her body completely so that she might sleep soundly; but if this were her goal, it remained unachieved. For while others slept, she tossed restlessly on her pillow or sat in the darkness at the foot of her couch, utterly forgetting the need for repose. Often, unhappy girl! she wept—a weeping born of an intolerable despair that, when it swept over her, shattered her strength and reduced her to a childlike state of helplessness.

When laid low in this manner, temptation assailed her. Whispers in her weary heart suggested writing to Robert, confessing her unhappiness caused by the prohibition against seeing him and Hortense, fearing he would withdraw his friendship—and perhaps his love—and forget her entirely. Begging him to remember her, to write again... she drafted one or two such letters. Yet, shame and common sense prevented their sending.

Finally, the life she led reached a breaking point. It seemed she could bear it no longer; her heart and mind threatened to collapse under the strain. She longed to leave Briarfield, to flee to some distant land. Coupled with this desire was a deep, secret, anxious yearning to discover and know her mother, a longing that grew stronger daily. However, it was shadowed by doubt and dread: if she knew her mother, could she love her? There was cause for hesitation. In her entire life, Caroline had never heard her mother praised. Every mention of Mrs. James Helstone was delivered with a cool, detached reserve. Her uncle seemed to regard his sister-in-law with a tacit antipathy. An old servant, who had briefly served the mistress after her marriage, spoke of her former employer with chilling caution—calling her "queer," or stating simply that she did not understand her. To Caroline, these words were ice. They suggested a terrifying possibility: that it might be better never to know one's parent than to know them and find oneself unable to love them.

Among all the possibilities, one project offered a semblance of hope: becoming a governess. It was the only thing she could do. A small incident finally gave her the courage to reveal this design to her uncle. Her long, late walks invariably traversed lonely roads—whether along the dreary skirts of Stilbro' Moor or the sunlit expanse of Nunnely Common—the homeward path was always contrived to pass near the Hollow. Though she rarely descended into the valley itself, she visited its brink at twilight almost as regularly as the stars rose over the hilltops. Her favorite spot was a specific stile beneath an ancient thorn. From there, she could gaze down upon the cottage, the mill, the dew-laden gardens, and the still, deep dam. Most importantly, she could see the familiar window of the counting-house. At a fixed hour, a sudden beam of light would shoot forth from its panes, bright and clear, or shimmering dimly through mist, or flashing brokenly between slanting lines of rain—for she came in all weathers.

Her mission was to watch for this ray; her reward was catching it. Some nights it failed to appear. She knew then that Robert was away, and departed doubly sorrowful. Conversely, its kindling made her elated, as though she saw within it the promise of some indefinite hope. If, while she gazed, a shadow bent between the light and the lattice, her heart leaped. That eclipse was Robert; she had seen him. Returning home comforted, she carried a clearer vision of his countenance, a distinct recollection of his voice, smile, and bearing. Blended with these impressions was often a sweet persuasion that if she could draw near, his heart might yet welcome her presence—that at this very moment, he might be willing to extend his hand and draw her close, to shelter her as he once had. That night, though she might weep as usual, she fancied her tears less scalding; the pillow seemed a little softer; the temples pressed against it ached less.

The shortest path from the Hollow to the rectory wound past a certain mansion, the same under whose lone walls Malone had journeyed that fateful night mentioned in an earlier chapter—the old, tenantless dwelling known as Fieldhead. Though vacant by the proprietor for ten years, it was no ruin. Mr. Yorke had ensured it was kept in good repair, and an old gardener and his wife resided there, cultivating the grounds and maintaining the house in habitable condition.

If Fieldhead possessed few other merits as a building, it could at least be termed picturesque. Its regular architecture, combined with the gray and mossy coloring imparted by time, earned it a rightful claim to this epithet. The old latticed windows, the stone porch, the walls, the roof, and the chimneys were rich in crayon touches and sepia lights and shadows. The trees behind were fine, bold, and spreading; the cedar on the lawn in front was grand; and the granite urns on the garden wall, together with the fretted arch of the gateway, were, for an artist, the very desire of the eye.

On one mild May evening, Caroline, passing near about moonrise and feeling, though weary, unwilling yet to return home where awaited only the bed of thorns and the night of grief, sat down on the mossy ground near the gate and gazed toward the cedar and the mansion. It was a still night—calm, dewy, cloudless. The gables, turned westward, reflected the clear amber of the horizon they faced; the oaks behind were black; the cedar was blacker. Beneath its dense, raven boughs, a glimpse of sky opened, gravely blue, full of the moon, which looked solemnly and mildly down upon Caroline from beneath that sombre canopy.

She felt this night and prospect mournfully lovely. She wished she could be happy; she wished she could know inward peace. She wondered if Providence had no pity for her, refusing to help or console. Recollections of happy trysts of lovers, commemorated in old ballads, returned to her mind; she thought such a tryst in such a scene would be blissful. *Where now was Robert?* she asked herself. Not at the Hollow; she had watched for his lamp long and had not seen it. She questioned within herself whether she and Moore were ever destined to meet and speak again. Suddenly, the door within the stone porch of the hall opened, and two men emerged—one elderly and white-headed, the other young, dark-haired, and tall. They passed across the lawn, out through a portal in the garden wall. Caroline saw them cross the road, pass the stile, descend the fields, and disappear. Robert Moore had passed before her with his friend, Mr. Yorke. Neither had seen her.

The apparition had been transient—scarce seen ere gone—but its electric passage left her veins kindled, her soul insurgent. It found her despairing, it left her desperate—two vastly different states.

*"Oh, had he but been alone! Had he but seen me!"* was her cry. *"He would have said something. He would have given me his hand. He _does_, he _must_, love me a little. He would have shown some token of affection. In his eye, on his lips, I should have read comfort; but the chance is lost. The wind, the shadow of a cloud, does not pass more silently, more emptily than he. I have been mocked, and Heaven is cruel!"*

Thus, in the utter sickness of longing and disappointment, she went home.

The next morning, at breakfast, she appeared white-cheeked and miserable-looking, as if she had seen a ghost. She inquired of Mr. Helstone, "Have you any objection, Uncle, to my inquiring for a situation in a family?"

Her uncle, as ignorant as the table supporting his coffee-cup of all his niece had undergone and was enduring, scarcely believed his ears.

"What whim now?" he asked. "Are you bewitched? What can you mean?"

"I am not well, and I need a change," she said.

He examined her closely. He discovered she had _experienced_ a change, at any rate. Without his awareness, the rose had dwindled and faded to a mere snowdrop; bloom had vanished, flesh wasted. She sat before him drooping, colorless, and thin.
