Shepherd's Inn, tucked away somewhere behind the soot-stained gables and grim chimneys of Wych Street and Holywell Street, sits right next to Lincoln's-Inn Fields and the Temple. It's a place the sun seems to have given up on, accessible only through winding, smoke-filled alleyways where shadows stretch endlessly. Here, the air smells of brimstone and cheap perfume. Slop-sellers hawk their wares alongside vendors of hard-bake and brandy balls, while peddlers of lurid theatrical prints and dusty furniture crowd the narrow lanes. The doors are battered and bell-ridden, surrounded by gangs of dirty children clamoring over shellfish trays on damp, mud-slicked pavement. Ballad singers drone out scathing tunes against the Whigs, the bishops, and even the German relatives of royalty, while Punch sets up a makeshift theater hoping to coax a halfpenny from the passersby. Amidst the screaming mothers chasing loitering kids and drunken husbands stumbling home from the gin shop, there is a chaotic, ceaseless noise—a stark contrast to the quiet, old-world courtyard of Shepherd's Inn itself.

In the center of this courtyard stands a patch of ragged grass, guarded by iron rails, holding the statue of Shepherd. Around it lie the Inn's halls and ancient chambers. Once a haven for lawyers, it has long since become a hub for the general public. Today, the ground floors house the offices of the Polwheedle and Tredyddlum Copper Mines and the "Registry of Patent Inventions and Union of Genius and Capital Company." The only real lawyer left is Mr. Campion, a man with prominent mustachios who arrives in his cab twice a week. While known as Mr. Somerset Campion in Mayfair, where his wife hosts high society, here in the Inn he operates as "Campion & Co." His uniform includes a tuft of hair on his chin shared by his entire firm. It is a spectacle worth seeing: his cab-horse, adorned with blinding heraldic crests, foams at the mouth as the groom, dressed in gleaming white, guides him to the door.

Our old friend, Captain Costigan, spends his afternoons inspecting Campion's flashy carriage, trailing through the court in his carpet slippers and dressing gown. After breakfast, he visits the porter's lodge, patting the heads of the children and chatting with Mrs. Bolton about theaters and her daughter, Leedy Mirabel. Mrs. Bolton, formerly a dancer at the Wells Theatre, knows a thing or two about show business. She lives upstairs with Mr. Bows, a former tenor singer, and Captain Costigan. They share the rooms once belonged to Charley Podmore. When the windows are open, you can hear Mr. Bows practicing his piano, teaching Fanny Bolton—the porter's daughter—who dreams of following in her mother's footsteps. Despite Mr. Bows' tendency toward drink, Fanny sees the best in everyone, especially the Captain, whom she believes to be the finest gentleman alive.

Captain Costigan has certainly hosted distinguished visitors in the past. One might imagine, listening to him in the mornings, that Sir Charles and Lady Mirabel drop by daily to visit "poor old Jack." The reality is far less romantic. Lady Mirabel stopped visiting years ago. Upon marrying Sir Charles, she settled a small pension on her father, who initially tried to maintain dignity in high society, using grandiloquent language and formal manners that baffled his guests. However, his caution quickly evaporated. By the third or fourth visit, Costigan would succumb to premature intoxication, disgracefully embarrassing his daughter-in-law. He would eventually be driven away, returning later to lament his fate to his friends at the Back Kitchen, comparing himself to King Lear and claiming his daughter was ungrateful.

When his funds ran dry, Costigan turned to begging. He once wrote a desperate letter asking for three pounds and five shillings to avoid jail, which Lady Mirabel promptly sent along with a stern warning to be more economical. Another time, he accidentally smashed a plate-glass window in the Strand. Again, she paid the bill, sending the money via her servant to Costigan's "aide-de-camp"—a fellow Irishman who actually took the cash to the Roscius Head pub, where Costigan was already in debt for rounds of rum-and-water.

On a particularly dire occasion, Costigan claimed illness and needed money for a doctor. Lady Mirabel, worried and perhaps feeling guilty for neglecting him, drove to Shepherd's Inn. She found the door marked "Podmore" opening to reveal Costigan in his shirt sleeves, preparing a gridiron for chops bought by Mrs. Bolton. Meanwhile, Sir Charles was suffering greatly. Letters addressed to him at Brookes's announced Costigan's presence in the hall; invitations to clubs required him to rush out of his carriage lest his father-in-law intercept him. Sir Charles, a frail and aging man, grew hysterical, threatening to move abroad or die if he ever saw Costigan again. Only after Lady Mirabel visited Costigan and issued a final ultimatum—that any further harassment or loan requests would result in the total withdrawal of her allowance—was peace restored. She also reprimanded Mr. Bows for failing to curb the Captain's drinking habits.

Next door, at number three, reside two other characters: Colonel Altamont, agent to the Nawab of Lucknow, and Captain Chevalier Edward Strong. Unlike their neighbors, they keep their names off the door. Strong prefers the anonymity of a Jermyn Street hotel address, while Altamont claims to be on a confidential mission unrelated to the East India Company. In reality, Altamont is trying to sell some of the Nawab's precious diamonds and rubies directly to private capitalists.

Strong's chambers are surprisingly comfortable, furnished nicely since Sir Francis Clavering arrived in London. The space includes an office, bedrooms, and a private staircase leading to upper apartments used by Altamont and the cook, Mr. Grady. The three men living there—Strong, Altamont, and Grady—are all accomplished cooks. Grady excels at Irish stew, the Colonel at pilau and curry, and Strong is a master of French and Spanish cuisine. When money is tight, Strong becomes incredibly hospitable, offering friends excellent dinners, fine wine, and loud, raucous singing. Poor Costigan often envies these gatherings from his room nearby, though he is rarely invited due to his drinking problems and maudlin storytelling.

The group surrounding Strong is a motley collection of schemers and dreamers. Jack Holt, a veteran of Don Carlos's army, plans to smuggle tobacco into London. Tom Diver, ex-Mexican navy, boasts knowledge of a sunken ship loaded with gold and silver, promising a return to Parliament within ten months. Keightley manages the underwater copper mines and dreams of sponge and quicksilver ventures. Filby has a mysterious inheritance from his deceased father, the location of which remains unknown. And then there is Sir Francis Clavering, a wealthy baronet who enjoys their company despite his own checkered past involving prisons and stamped papers.

Clavering brought Altamont to stay at Strong's chambers after meeting him in Paris. However, Strong finds the Colonel unbearable. He complains bitterly to Clavering about Altamont's lack of gentility, his garish dress, and his behavior at the theater, where he screamed and cried during *The Brigand*. Strong suspects Altamont is a criminal, possibly linked to church robberies, and urges Clavering to cut ties. Yet, Clavering merely replies, "Hang him! I wish he was dead!" before falling silent, leaving Strong to wonder about the secret bond connecting his patron and the troublesome Colonel.
