At Fairoaks, the household was beginning to wonder where Major Pendennis had gotten to. Dr. Portman and his wife, on their way back to Clavering, stopped by Helen’s lodge gate with a short note from the Major—just one of those polite little missives that say more than they mean. It read something like: “Dear Helen—staying in Chatteris another day. Must talk with Messrs. Tatham about some business. Will be home soon. Love.” No mention of the morning’s drama, naturally. The note had been written mid-battle, when the Major was still reeling from the verbal thrashing he’d just taken. He didn’t feel like going into town while his uncle was around—especially not if the man in the polished boots might be lurking somewhere near the Dean’s grass-plat, spying on him as he tried to flirt in Miss Costigan’s drawing-room. Pen wasn’t exactly shy, but he was deeply, profoundly embarrassed by the idea of being seen making eyes at a girl by someone who thought he should be wearing a wig and reading the Bible.

Love, for Pen, was a silent affair—except for the eyes, which did all the shouting. And even then, it was only in private. In public? Forget it. Love-talk, especially when overheard by strangers, is the most absurd kind of nonsense in existence. Try reading your old love letters again, dear reader—you’ll see what we mean. Those once-magical lines now look like gibberish scribbled by a madman. “I shall never forget you,” says one. “You are my everything.” But really? You probably forgot them ten minutes after writing them. Yet there was a time when you’d have kissed those very words with reverence, lived on them like bread, and felt utterly lost until the next dose arrived. That’s why we’re not publishing any of Pen’s poems or love notes. They’re too sweet, too dramatic, too full of teenage delusion. Let young ladies keep their own romantic dreams intact. We’ll leave these early effusions tied up in newspaper bundles with Miss Fotheringay’s string and sealed with Captain Costigan’s silver seal. For posterity. And discretion.

The Major left Captain Costigan’s house in such a state of rage that he looked like a volcano about to erupt. “The impudent, bog-trotting scamp!” he muttered under his breath. “Dare threaten me? Say I’m a coward? Send me a challenge? If the fool can find a gentleman to carry it, I’ll give him the fight of his life. Psha! What would people say if I fought a drunken mountebank over an actress in a barn?” So when Dr. Portman asked how the “dragon-slaying” had gone, the Major merely said it was unpleasant and far from over. He begged the doctor and his wife to keep quiet about it—no gossip at Fairoaks—and sent off the note we’ve already mentioned. Then he stomped back to his hotel, where he vented his fury on Mr. Morgan, his valet, “dammin’ and cussin’ upstairs and downstairs,” as Morgan later told Mr. Foker’s man during dinner in the servants’ room at the George.

The news spread fast. Mr. Foker, having just finished breakfast (it was two o’clock), remembered he wanted to know how the showdown between his two friends had gone. He checked the Major’s room number and, wrapped in his brocade dressing-gown and topped with an embroidered skull-cap (a gift from a devoted mother), marched over and knocked.

Inside, the Major was busy with papers and red tape, discussing a lease for the widow’s property with old Mr. Tatham, the family lawyer. Seeing the visitor, the modest youth Foker hesitated. “Oh, you’re busy—call another time,” he said, backing away.

But the Major insisted. “Come in, come in!” he said with a smile. So Foker removed his tarboosh (a fez, though no one called it that), bowed grandly, and sat down in an armchair, spreading out his crimson robes like a peacock showing off its tail feathers. “You seem to like my dressing-gown, sir,” he said to Mr. Tatham. “A pretty thing, isn’t it? Neat, but not gaudy. And how do you do, Major Pendennis, sir? How does the world treat you?”

There was something about Foker’s presence—his charm, his ridiculousness—that could cheer up even a monk. It smoothed the wrinkles beneath the Major’s hairline. “I’ve had an interview with that Irishman,” he said. “And it was… not satisfactory. He refuses to believe my nephew is poor. Says we’re both liars. And before I left, he had the nerve to suggest I was a coward. I thought, when I heard the knock, that you might be the gentleman carrying a challenge from Mr. Costigan. That’s how the world treats me, Mr. Foker.”

“Not the Irishman—the actress’s father?” gasped Mr. Tatham, who disliked the theatre and considered actors morally suspect.

“The very man. Haven’t you heard what a fool my nephew has made of himself over the girl?” Mr. Tatham hadn’t—he never set foot in a theatre. So the Major recounted the whole story, while Foker added comments in his usual breezy fashion. Tatham was horrified. Why hadn’t Mrs. Pendennis married a respectable man—like himself, a widower? Why had she raised this boy to ruin?

As for Miss Costigan—well, her profession spoke for itself. “I’ve known some decent folk in the booths,” Foker interjected. “They call it the Temple of the Muses.”

“Perhaps,” said Tatham, “but the father—I know him personally. Wine-bibber, idler, billiard-hall regular, and a notorious bankrupt. And here’s the kicker: we’ve got a writ against him and another actor—Mr. Garbetts—for a bill given to Mr. Skinner, a fine Quaker grocer and spirit merchant. Costigan came crying into Skinner’s shop, begging for mercy. We haven’t pursued it—neither man was worth the cost of powder and shot.”

Just then, a third knock came. In walked a shabby-looking man in a braided frock coat, holding a letter with a large, blotchy red seal. “May I speak with Major Pendennis privately?” he began. “I have a message from Captain Costigan.”

But then he froze. His face turned as pale as milk. He saw Mr. Tatham. “Hullo, Garbetts, speak up!” cried Foker, delighted.

“Why, bless my soul—it’s the other party to the bill!” said Tatham.

“Stop!” said Garbetts, gasping. Then he fled, like a rabbit caught in a spotlight.

Everyone burst out laughing. The Major laughed. Foker laughed. Even the solemn lawyer chuckled. “I don’t think there’ll be any fight, Major,” said Foker, mimicking a tragedian. “If there is, the old gentleman—your name Tatham?—may send the bailiffs to separate the men.” Tatham promised he would.

The Major was actually relieved. “It seems to me, sir,” he said to Foker, “that you always arrive just in time to put me in good humour.”

And indeed, Foker was about to become the hero of the Pendennis family that day. He’d been invited into Captain Costigan’s lodgings, so he decided to pay a visit and hear the General’s side of the story. But Costigan wasn’t home—he’d gone to the Magpie Hotel, where he was doubtless bragging about wanting to murder a certain ruffian. He wasn’t just brave—he knew it, and liked to show it off.

But Miss Fotheringay was there, washing tea cups while Mr. Bows sat across from her. “Just done breakfast, I see—how do?” said Foker, popping his head in.

“Get out, you funny little man,” she snapped.

“I mean *come in*,” he replied. “Here we are!” And with that, he folded his arms and began spinning his head around like Harlequin breaking free from his cocoon. Miss Fotheringay roared with laughter. One wink from Foker could make her laugh; Bows’s bitterest joke couldn’t even get a smirk.

When the spin ended, Foker dropped to one knee and kissed her hand. “You’re the drollest little man,” she said, giving him a hearty slap.

Pen would have died if anyone slapped him. He trembled just kissing her hand.

After the preliminaries, the three began chatting. Foker recounted the scene with Garbetts, revealing just how furious Costigan had been. He defended the Major’s character fiercely—calling him a “tip-top swell,” a man of impeccable honesty, and certainly not someone who’d deceive a lovely young woman like Miss Fotheringay.

He touched lightly on marriage, though he couldn’t help hinting that he thought Pen was a bit of a dreamer. “I knew it wouldn’t do, Miss Foth,” he said, nodding wisely. “Couldn’t do. Didn’t like to put my hand in the bag, but I knew it couldn’t do. Too young. Too green. A deal too green. And now he’s poor as Job. Can’t have him at no price, can she, Mr. Bo?”

“Indeed, he’s a nice poor boy,” said Miss Fotheringay, sadly.

“Poor little beggar,” said Bows, hands in pockets, stealing a glance at her. Maybe he was wondering how women play with men—coax them, win them, then drop them.

But Bows admitted the match was absurd. Miss Costigan agreed—only she couldn’t turn down two thousand a year. “It all comes of believing Papa’s silly stories,” she said. “Faith, I’ll choose for meself another time.” And perhaps, right then, Lieutenant Sir Derby Oaks flashed through her mind.

After praising the Major—“smelling of lavender, neat as a pin”—Foker suddenly suggested they all meet him for dinner that evening at his apartment at the George. “He agreed to dine with me,” he said. “And after the—after the little shindy this morning, in which I must say the General was wrong, it would look kind, you know. I know the Major fell in love with you, Miss Foth—he said so.”

“So she may be Mrs. Pendennis still,” sneered Bows.

“No, thank you, Mr. F.—I’ve dined.”

“Sure, that was at three o’clock,” said Miss Costigan, “and I can’t go without you.”

“We’ll have lobster-salad and champagne,” said Foker, who couldn’t parse Latin or do long division.

Now, for lobster-salad and champagne—especially served by the little monster who could barely spell—Miss Costigan would have walked through fire. So at seven o’clock, Major Pendennis found himself seated at a table with Mr. Bows, a professional fiddler, and Miss Costigan—whose father had nearly blown his brains out just hours before.

To complete the miracle, Foker sent Stoopid to fetch Costigan from the Magpie, where the General was singing a sentimental ballad. When he arrived, he found his daughter and Bows at the table. He was stunned. The Major laughed and shook his hand warmly. The General was quite drunk and had already been sobbing over his song before joining the party. He burst into tears more than once, calling the Major his “dearest friend.”

Stoopid and Foker walked him home. The Major gallantly gave his arm to Miss Costigan.

The next day, the Major returned to Fairoaks, saying nothing about the previous night. He invited Mr. Smirke to dinner, and anyone watching might have noticed something odd—his usual joviality seemed forced, his manners unusually careful. He gave Pen a firm “God bless you” before bed, and as they parted, he seemed about to say something to Mrs. Pendennis—but thought better of it. Better not disturb her sleep.

Next morning, he was down in the breakfast room earlier than usual, greeting everyone with great warmth. The post arrived just as the meal ended. John, the old servant, dumped the mail on the table. The Major watched Pen pick up his letter. Arthur blushed and set it down quickly. He knew the handwriting—it was Costigan’s. And he didn’t want to read it in front of everyone.

The Major knew the letter too. He’d posted it himself the day before. He sent little Laura out of the room—she hated him—and took Mrs. Pendennis’s hand. With a meaningful look, he pointed to the letter under the newspaper Pen was pretending to read.

“Will you come into the drawing-room?” he asked. “I want to speak to you.”

She followed, puzzled.

“What is it?” she whispered nervously.

“The affair is over,” said the Major. “He’s received his dismissal. I dictated it myself yesterday. There’s a few lines from the lady, too, bidding him farewell. It’s all over.”

Helen ran back to the dining room. Pen had snatched up the letter the moment they were gone. He read it with a blank stare. It said what the Major had said: Costigan was grateful for Arthur’s kindness, but had just learned of his financial situation. Marriage was impossible. Given the age difference, a future union was unthinkable. With deep regret, he bid Arthur farewell and asked him to stop visiting.

Enclosed was a note from Miss Costigan. She agreed with her father. She was older than Arthur, and an engagement was not feasible. She would always be grateful for his kindness, and hoped to remain friends. But for now, until the pain passed, they should not meet.

Pen read it mechanically. He looked up, wild-eyed, seeing his mother and uncle watching him with sorrow. Helen’s face was full of maternal concern.

“What—what is this?” he stammered. “It’s a joke. This isn’t her writing. Some servant wrote this. Who’s playing tricks on me?”

“It came in her father’s envelope,” said the Major. “Those letters you got before weren’t in her hand. That one is.”

“How do you know?” Pen snapped.

“I saw her write it,” the Major replied. Pen jumped up. His mother stepped forward and took his hand. He pushed her away.

“How could you see her? How dare you interfere between me and her? What have I ever done to you? Oh, it’s not true! It’s not true!” he shouted.

“She can’t have done it of her own accord,” he cried. “She can’t mean it. She’s pledged to me. Who’s lied to her?”

“Lies aren’t told in our family, Arthur,” said the Major. “I told her the truth—that you have no money. Her father had told her you were rich. When she learned the truth, she withdrew—without any pressure from me. She was right. She’s ten years older than you. She’s not suited to be your wife. Look at that handwriting—ask yourself, is such a woman fit to be your mother’s companion?”

“I’ll hear it from her,” said Arthur, crumpling the paper. “I’ll ask her to marry me. Once more. No one shall stop me.”

“What, a woman who spells affection with one ‘f’? Nonsense, sir. Be a man. Remember your mother is a lady. She was never meant to associate with that tipsy swindler or his daughter. Be a man. Forget her. As she has forgotten you.”

“Be a man and comfort your mother, my Arthur,” Helen said, embracing him.

Seeing them both shaken, the Major quietly left the room, closing the door behind him. He’d won. He’d even brought Pen’s love letters back in his portmanteau—returning them to Costigan with a little promissory note, which he settled with Mr. Tatham.

Pen rushed to Chatteris that day, desperate to see Miss Fotheringay. But she wasn’t there. He left a letter, enclosed to her father. It was returned. Costigan begged all correspondence cease. After a few more attempts, the General cut Pen off entirely. He ignored him in the street.

One day, walking with Foker, Arthur saw Emily on her father’s arm. She passed without a nod. Foker felt Pen trembling beside him.

His uncle wanted him to travel. His mother urged it too—Arthur was ill, suffering badly. But he refused. “I won’t go,” he said flatly. “I won’t obey.” His mother was too fond, his uncle too wise, to force him.

Whenever Miss Fotheringay performed, he rode to the Chatteris Theatre. One night, the house was nearly empty. The manager refunded their money. Pen went home, went to bed at eight, and developed a fever.

“If this goes on,” the Major thought, “I’ll have to go fetch the girl myself.”

As for Pen, he believed he was dying.

We won’t describe his feelings. We won’t bore you with a journal of despair. Have not others been rejected in love? Yes. But few die of it.
