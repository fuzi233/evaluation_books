The following afternoon found Gerard no longer dizzy, though he was plagued by irritability and strange whims. In one such mood, he demanded a lemon to suck upon. Denys, transformed from a rough soldier into a doting grandfather figure by their shared bond, rose quickly. "Do not worry," he assured him, promising lemons within moments. He rushed out to scour the local shops. Lemons were rare in the north then, and his search took a long time. Gerard grew increasingly impatient until the door finally creaked open.

However, it was not Denys who entered. Instead, a formidable figure glided softly inside: an elderly gentleman clad in a long, sober gown trimmed with rich fur, wearing cherry-colored hose and pointed shoes. A sword hung at his side in a fine leather sheath, and around his neck sat a ruff, starched so severely it was held rigid by hidden wooden stays. On his head rested a four-cornered cap bordered with fur, and a magnificent white beard framed his chin and bosom. Gerard instantly recognized the visitor's profession; barring the weapon, this was the full ceremonial attire of a physician. Trailing behind him was a boy carrying a basket brimming with phials, lint, and surgical instruments.

The old doctor approached the bedside gently, asking in a hushed tone, "How fares my son?"

Gerard replied gratefully that his wound caused little pain, though his throat felt parched and his head heavy.

"A wound!" the doctor exclaimed, feigning surprise. "They told me nothing of that. Let me inspect it. Yes, yes—a clean, deep bite. The mastiff certainly had sharp teeth capable of such a feat." His sympathy seemed to drift entirely toward the imaginary beast he had conjured. "This requires immediate cauterization, or you will find yourself unable to drink water, tumbling somersaults in bed. It is the season of rabid dogs; one has already done your business, but we shall thwart him. Boy, heat the iron."

"But, sir," Gerard protested weakly, "'twas no dog, but a bear."

"A bear!" the senior physician remonstrated sternly. "Think before you speak! It is disrespectful to joke with a healer who brings his gray hair and lifelong studies to mend you. A bear, you say? Had you dissected as many bears as I have—or even a tenth—you would know that no bear's jaw creates such a trivial wound. I tell you it was a dog. Since you force my hand, I deny it was even a large one, but merely one of those furious curs running rampant today, biting legs and bringing men low unless stopped by my blade and cautery."

"Alas, sir!" Gerard corrected him, "When did I say a bear's *jaw*? I said 'bear'; it was his *paw*, now."

"And why did you not tell me immediately?"

"Because you kept insisting otherwise."

"Never hide anything from your leech, young man," the doctor continued, proving himself a skilled speaker but a terrible listener. "Well, this is unfortunate. Claws of tigers, panthers, badgers, cats, and bears, along with deer horns and human fingernails—especially children's—are steeped in deadly poison. You would have been safer bitten by a stray cur than gored by a bull or scratched by a bear. Nevertheless, I shall apply a soothing poultice to your leg. Meanwhile, keep the body cool. Stick out your tongue—good! Fever. Let me check your pulse—good! Fever. I order bloodletting, and immediately."

"Bloodletting! Humph! Very well. If it guarantees a cure, I shall not remain idle." The doctor assured him that the procedure was infallible, particularly in this instance.

"Hans, fetch the necessary items, and I shall explain the reasoning to the patient."

The physician then lectured Gerard on the nature of disease, claiming that blood becomes hot, disturbed, and poisonous. Removing this corrupt fluid allowed Nature to replace it with pure substance. Therefore, bleeding acted as both a coolant and a purifier, curing all ailments because all diseases were essentially fevers, regardless of what other practitioners claimed.

"Do not suppose," he said earnestly, "that mere bleeding suffices. Any barber can cut a vein (though not all can stitch it closed). True art lies in knowing exactly which vein to drain for a specific illness. Recently, a man suffered from severe earache. I bled his right thigh, and his pain vanished instantly. Regrettably, he passed away shortly after. Another arrived with toothache; I bled behind his ear, relieving him immediately. He too has since died. I bled our bailiff between the thumb and forefinger for rheumatism. Soon afterward, he returned complaining of headaches and ringing in his ears, holding his hand over a basin. I smiled at his folly, bled his left ankle against his will, and cleared his head completely."

Branching off into enthusiastic tangents, the revered teacher continued:

"Know, young man, that two schools of medicine currently vie for dominance across Europe. The Arabian school, rooted in the ancient oracles of Avicenna, Rhazes, and Albucasis, revived by Chauliac and Lanfranc; and the Greek school, championed by modern figures like Bessarion, Platinus, and Marsilius Ficinus, whose original masters were medicine's supreme prophets: Apollo, Chiron, Asclepius, and his sons Podalinus and Machaon, alongside Pythagoras, Democritus, Praxagoras—who discovered the arteries—and Diocles, 'who first gave the soul to urine.' These teachings were oral until Hippocrates, the eighteenth descendant of Asclepius, appeared. From him we possess manuscripts; to him we owe the concept of the 'vital principle.' He invented the bandage, tapped chests for water, and above all, performed dissections—though only on quadrupeds, as pagan prejudice forbade cutting the human body. He was followed by Aristotle, who identified the aorta, the largest vessel in the human body."

"Certainly, sir," Gerard objected humbly, "the Almighty gave us all that exists within our bodies, not Aristotle or any Greek scholar."

"Child! Of course He created the organ," the doctor retorted, flushing slightly. "But Aristotle gave it its name. Yet, youths always chatter. The next great luminary was Galen, who studied in Alexandria, the center of science. Dissatisfied with quadrupeds, he dissected apes, believing them closer to man, and bled like a Trojan. Then came Theophilus, who revealed the nerves, lacteal vessels, and pia mater."

This troubled Gerard. "I cannot lie still hearing mortals claim they bestowed upon Adam the very parts God formed from clay and breathed into his sons."

"Is there such perversity?" the doctor snapped, his color rising. "Who truly donates a thing to man: the one who planted it secretly in the dark recesses of the body, or the learned sage who reveals it to the intellect, thereby enriching the mind? Understanding is your only true possession. Is that answer sufficient?"

"I am silenced, sir."

"That is preferable; garrulous patients are difficult to cure, especially when febrile. I maintain that Eristratus discovered the cerebral nerves and milk vessels; indeed, he invented lithotomy. Then came another whom I forget; you disturb me with your petty objections. Next was Ammonius, author of lithotrity. Here comes Hans with the basin—to curb your verbosity. Blow the charcoal, boy, and bring the basin. Excellent. Arabs, you say? What are they but a sect of yesterday who, around the year 1000, stumbled upon Greek writings and misread them, lacking their own light? Their demi-god, camel-driver Mahound—an impostor in science as in religion—strictly forbade them anatomy, even of lower animals. To separate medicine from anatomy is to remove the sun from the world, as Cicero said. Do not marvel at my fervor, good youth; when public welfare is at risk, a little warmth is civic, humane, and honorable. Lately, a pestilential Arabist has settled here, a mere empiric who despises anatomy, barely distinguishes Greek from Hebrew, yet has stolen half my patients. I tremble for the rest. Extend your ankle, and you, Hans, fan the charcoal."

Just then, Denys entered with the lemons, looking bewildered. "What sport is this?" he asked, raising his eyebrows.

Gerard blushed slightly and explained that the learned doctor intended to bleed and cauterize him—that was all.

"Yes! Indeed; and what is that imp blowing hot coals for?"

"For what else," the doctor addressed Gerard, "than to cauterize the vein once opened and the poisonous blood released? It is the only safe method. Avicenna recommends tying the vein, but he offers no instructions on how, nor did he know himself, nor any of the descendants of Ishmael. I have no faith in such tricky expedients. Take this as a safe principle: 'Whatever an Arab or Arabist says is correct, must be wrong.'"
