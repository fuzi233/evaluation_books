A shadow fell across the white house at Pennicote, a chill that settled not just in the air, but in the hearts of those who lived within its walls. Beyond the estate, news traveled swiftly of Lord Grandcourt's demise, stirring a turmoil that demanded both expression and suppression. Within the household of Mrs. Meyrick, the atmosphere shifted with a peculiar, jagged tension.

Hans Meyrick had a ritual: delivering the *Times* to his mother, who devoured news from high politics to the latest matrimonials, finding comfort in the fates of fictional heroes and real-life strangers alike. Usually, Hans arrived precisely when Mirah finished teaching Mab her weekly lesson, claiming he wished to hear his sister sing. But today, the rhythm was broken. Hans entered the parlor with a jarring clatter, shaking the newspaper until it cracked like dry timber, shattering Mab's delicate rendition of "Lascia ch'io pianga."

The music died instantly. Mirah, her fingers hovering over the keys, spun around, startled by the harsh intrusion.

"Oh, Hans!" Mab cried, her voice trembling. "Why do you bring a noise more terrible than my singing?"

"What miracle is in the paper?" Mrs. Meyrick asked, leaning forward, her eyes wide. "Politics? A shift in the Austrians?"

"Nothing about politics," Hans replied, his voice dropping into a conspiratorial murmur that sent a shiver down his mother's spine. "Something from Italy."

Mirah's breath hitched. Her gaze darted toward Hans, then to her mother, searching for reassurance. Hans placed the paper before his mother, pointing a finger at a specific paragraph.

"Duchess Alfonso is alive," Hans announced, a grin twisting his features. "Her husband... well, he's drowned. But the best part? Deronda was there. At the same hotel. He saw her pulled from the sea by fisherman. They say she jumped in after him. A bit rash, don't you think? But Deronda was there to catch her."

Mirah sank back onto her stool, her hands clenching into fists, her eyelids lowering as if to block out the light. Mrs. Meyrick handed the paper to Mab, her face pale. "Poor soul," she whispered. "To leap into the water..."

"It was a slip of the mind," Hans dismissed, lounging in a chair near Mirah, his expression merry. "A jealous baritone, always glaring, always singing his own laments—it's hardly surprising the sea claimed him. Now the Duchess is free to marry a man with hair like silk and eyes that warm instead of freeze. And I'll bet I'm invited to the wedding!"

The air in the room grew heavy, suffocating. Mirah sprang up from her seat, her eyes blazing with a fierce, indignant fire.

"Hans!" she cried, her voice shaking with a depth of anguish she rarely showed. "Do not speak so lightly of life and death! You cannot call it 'luck.' If Deronda loved Mrs. Grandcourt, it would not be luck for anyone. It would be a tragedy for my brother! She would tear him away from me, Hans. You do not understand what it means to lose him!"

Silence crashed down upon them. The three siblings stood frozen. Mirah's face, usually flushed with health, was drained of color, her lips tight and white. Hans sat stunned, his playful mask shattered, his cheeks burning red beneath the weight of her accusation.

"I am a fool!" Hans stammered, rising abruptly. "A brute! I take back every word. I deserve whatever punishment comes." He looked at Mirah, his eyes wet with sudden remorse, and added with a faint, tragic smile, "I'd hang myself like Judas if I could."

Mirah did not soften. The pain within her was raw, a wound that bled openly. She sat back down at the piano, pressing the sheet music flat as if trying to pin down the chaos in her mind.

"Mirah speaks truth," Mab said softly, her voice thick with sympathy. "You toy with dangerous words, Hans. To joke of Deronda marrying her... it shows a darkness in your heart."

Hans bowed his head, shame etching lines into his forehead. "Forgive me, dears."

"We must finish the lesson," Mirah said, her voice regaining a measure of command, though it rang hollow. "Sing again, Mab."

Mab nodded eagerly, eager to escape the gravity of the moment. Mirah began to sing, pouring her sorrow into the melody, her voice swelling with a new, terrifying fullness. As she played, Hans lingered by the mantelpiece, watching her with a mixture of guilt and admiration, careful not to meet his mother's gaze.

When the final note faded, Mirah stood, clutching her shawl. "I must go home," she said. "Ezra waits."

She offered her hand to Mrs. Meyrick, hesitating before kissing her cheek, a gesture of apology mingled with fear. The older woman drew her close, murmuring, "God bless you, my dear." Mirah felt a sting of guilt for her harshness toward Hans, a pride that felt wrong, a shadow of ingratitude settling over her conscience.

Outside, the street air was crisp. Hans caught up with her, opening the door. "Shall I walk you home?" he asked, his voice tentative.

Mirah said nothing, allowing him to lead her. Inside the carriage, the space between them hummed with unspoken things. Hans watched her profile, his mind racing. He realized with a jolt that he had been a fool. He had suspected an attachment between Deronda and Mrs. Grandcourt, piecing together fragments of observation and whispers from Anna Gascoigne, but Mirah's anger had laid bare the truth: she feared losing him to her brother, to the world, to the inevitable distance of fate.

They parted at her door. Hans extended a hand, seeking forgiveness. "Come in," Mirah whispered, her voice melodic with a strange, softening resolve. "See my brother."

Inside, Mordecai sat by the window, his countenance radiant, holding a letter that held his entire world. He looked like a man who had conquered the grave.

"A letter from Daniel," he told them, his eyes shining. "He returns soon. Unexpected delays kept him away, but the promise remains."

Mirah slipped away to her room, closing the door gently. She did not weep. Instead, she sat, pressing her hands to her temples, as if trying to stem a flood. She splashed cold water on her face, watching the droplets fall, and for a moment, the old habits of her childhood returned—the need to endure, to hide, to perform. But beneath the surface, a new current ran.

She thought of Deronda, of the woman who had followed him, and the terrible, ugly knot of jealousy that twisted in her chest. It was not the noble, self-sacrificing love Mordecai spoke of in stories of maidens who died for kings. It was a selfish, burning desire to possess, to keep, to prevent the loss. It was a realization that shocked her: the love she had thought so pure, so reverent, had curdled into something sharp and painful.

"You have read too many plays," Mordecai's voice echoed from the other room, cutting through her thoughts. "You judge by fiction, not by your own heart."

Mirah stood still, listening to the silence, feeling the weight of a secret that threatened to swallow her whole. The future, with its uncertainties and the looming shadow of Deronda's return, stretched out before her, vast and unwieldy. She touched her brow, drying the dampness, and prepared to face the coming days, armed with a sorrow that was uniquely, painfully her own.
