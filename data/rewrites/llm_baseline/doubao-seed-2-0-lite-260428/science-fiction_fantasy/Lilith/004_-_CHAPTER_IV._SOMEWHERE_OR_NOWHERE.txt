The sun blazed sharp and gilded over the lawn, but a prickle of premonition told me the fine spell would not hold. I twisted the milky sapphire heirloom on my finger, squinting at the faint, starlike core trapped in its cloudy blue depths: it was far dimmer, far more blurred than I had ever seen it.
I pushed back my breakfast chair and crossed to the window to glance again at the odd standing stone at the edge of my property. Heavy rain had lashed the manor overnight, and a thrush was perched on a patch of moss, hammering stubbornly at a snail shell to crack it open. I twisted my ring further to catch the full sun on its star core, when a glossy, keen black eye blinked directly back at me from the gem’s milky mist.
The shock jolted the ring right out of my hand. When I fumbled to pick it up, the eye was gone.
At that exact second the sun winked out, swallowed whole by a roiling dark vapour. In less than a minute the entire sky was clogged with storm clouds, the air turned thick and sultry, and a gust of wind slammed the window curtain against my shoulder. Lightning seared the hedgerows, one single, sharp thunderclap rattled the china on my breakfast table, and then the rain fell in solid, torrential sheets, blurring every line of the landscape beyond the glass.
I left the window propped open, leaning against the frame to watch the downpour, when I spotted a raven striding across the soaked grass toward me. His gait was slow, solemn, not a single feather ruffled, and he moved through the deluge like it did not exist at all. I recognised him at once from my earlier strange encounters, and congratulated myself I was on the ground floor with no winding staircase to tempt me after him. Even so, a quiet, unshakable certainty settled over me that if I let my guard slip, something would shift that I could never undo.
He drew close, bowed so deep his beak nearly brushed the grass, then launched in a sudden winged leap straight to the windowsill. He stepped over the ledge, hopped down onto my floor, and walked purposefully toward the back of the house. I followed, half expecting him to head for the library or the stairs, already resolved I would not set a single foot on that upward path after him. But he turned not toward the library or the landing, but to the tiny, seldom-used side door that opened onto an overgrown grass nook wedged between two sprawling wings of my old rambling manor. I hurried to pull the door open for him.
He stepped out onto the creeper-draped porch, and stood staring out at the rain, which fell like a vast, thin silver cataract. I lingered in the doorway behind him. A second flash of lightning split the sky, followed by a long, rolling peal of distant thunder. He tilted his head back over his shoulder to glance at me, as if to say *You hear that?*, then swivelled his gaze back to the storm, his posture almost human in its quiet approval.
The gesture was so uncannily familiar that I spoke before I thought. “Fine weather for the worms, Mr. Raven!”
“Fine, indeed,” he rumbled in that rough, croaky voice I had already learned to recognise. “The ground’s soft enough for them to burrow in and out easy. Must be grand times on the steppes of Uranus right now! I’d wager it’s pouring there too. It’s rained solid for a whole week.”
“Why would that be grand?” I asked.
“Every creature there is a burrower,” he explained. “Just like your field mice and moles here. That whole world’s still young, deep in its evolutionary spring. You’d think a beast heaving up out of the mud was a hairy elephant, or some long-lost deinotherium — though none of them match anything that’s ever lived on Earth. The first time I saw a dry-bog-serpent wallow out of the mud, all thick mane and great glowing eyes, I nearly jumped out of my own feathers. But the shower’s nearly over. It’ll stop the second after the next thunderclap.”
A flash burst as he spoke, and half a minute later the thunder boomed. The rain ceased instantly, as if someone had turned off a tap.
“Now it’s time we go,” the raven said, stepping to the edge of the porch.
“Go where?” I asked.
“Where we have to go,” he said. “You didn’t truly think you were home, did you? I told you no one can come and go as they please until they find the real home.”
“I don’t want to go anywhere,” I said.
“That doesn’t change much, not even a little,” he croaked. “This way.”
“I’m perfectly content right where I am.”
“You think you are. But you aren’t. Come along.” He hopped down onto the soaked grass, and turned to wait for me.
“I won’t leave the house today,” I said, stubborn.
“Then just step into the garden,” he bargained.
I sighed, and gave in, stepping out past him onto the lawn. The sun broke through the clouds, and every raindrop on the grass glinted like scattered diamond.
“You’ll wet your feet,” I warned him.
“And mire my beak,” he answered, and plunged that black, sharp beak deep into the soft turf, yanking out a fat, wriggling red worm. He tossed it high in the air.
The worm unfurled great, iridescent wings of red and black, and soared straight up into the sky.
“Tut tut,” I laughed. “Worms don’t turn into butterflies, Mr. Raven.”
“Never mind. It’ll do for once,” he croaked. “I’m not much of a reader these days. I’m sexton at a graveyard — a cemetery, more properly — somewhere far past the edges of where you think the world ends. Dig things up sometimes, when there’s nothing to bury.”
“You can’t go digging things up and making them fly off,” I said. “No creature should ever forget where it came from.”
“Why not?” he asked.
“Because they’ll grow proud. They’ll stop recognising their superiors.”
I did not know then that I was making a perfect fool of myself.
“Where do worms come from, anyway?” he asked, tilting his head like he was genuinely curious.
“Why the earth, obviously,” I said.
“Last, sure,” he answered. “But they didn’t come from the earth first. The earth will never get them all back, either.” He nodded up at the sky.
I looked up, and saw nothing but a small dark cloud, its edges glowing red like it was lit by sunset — impossible, the day was barely half done.
“The sun can’t be setting already,” I gasped.
“It isn’t,” he said. “That red glow belongs to the worm we just let fly.”
“See? That’s what happens when you make creatures forget their origins!” I said, heated.
“Only if forgetting lets them rise higher,” he said. “I’m not making them lose their roots. I’m teaching them to find them. You wouldn’t have the whole sky full of worms, anyway. That’s just the sexton’s work. If the rest of the clergy understood half that much, the worlds would be a far better place.”
He drove his beak into the turf a second time, pulled out another wriggling worm, and tossed it. It spread its own pair of glowing wings and soared away.
I turned back toward the house, and a cry of dismay tore out of my throat. I had sworn not ten minutes earlier I would never leave it. Now I was standing in the middle of a dark, fragrant pine forest, with no sign of my manor anywhere.
“What right do you have to drag me here against my will, Mr. Raven?” I snapped, stung. “Am I not a free agent?”
“A man is exactly as free as he chooses to make himself. Not an atom more,” the raven answered. “No one can force a choice on a full individual. But you are only just beginning to become one. So I can.”
I stared around at the endless pines, searching for a glint of the familiar, any path back to the life I knew. How could I ever call that house home again, when every door, every window opened out into somewhere else, and even my garden was no longer mine to keep inside?
“Maybe that eases it a little,” the raven said, watching my face. “You haven’t left your house. Your house hasn’t left you. It just can’t contain you, not any more. You can’t inhabit it the way you did.”
“I don’t understand you,” I said. “Where am I?”
“The region of seven dimensions,” he said, making a curious low rumble in his throat and flicking his tail feathers. “You’d better follow very closely now. You might step right through someone you can’t see, and hurt them bad.”
“There’s no one here to hurt but you, and half the time I’d happily do it,” I said.
“Not seeing them is the danger,” he said. “You see that big pine tree, thirty yards off to your left?”
“Of course I see it,” I said, testy.
“Ten minutes ago you didn’t. And right now you have no idea where it actually stands. That tree is growing straight up through your kitchen hearth, right out your kitchen chimney.”
I laughed out loud. “Now I know you’re making fun of me!”
“Was I making fun of you when I winked at you out of that sapphire ring of yours, not an hour ago?” he asked.
“You’re just messing with me,” I said, turning away.
“No one can make a fool of you but yourself,” he said. “You make yourself one by refusing to accept what’s true, and then you punish yourself for it by believing what’s completely false. Those hyacinth blooms tangled in the strings of your piano? They’re what make the playing so sweet. Your housekeeper’s niece is sitting right there at the keys, playing Grieg’s Wedding March, right next to that wild rose bush you see growing at the foot of the pine. You just can’t see any of it, not yet.”
I walked over to the rose bush. I could not hear a single note of music, but the scent that rose from its petals was not just rose — it was rose, tangled with the bright, thrumming echo of the march, something stranger and sweeter than any scent I had ever breathed.
“Mr. Raven,” I said, softening. “I’m sorry I was rude. I was just angry, scared. Please show me the way home. I have an appointment with my bailiff. I can’t break faith with my servants.”
“The path back doesn’t go through any door,” he said. “It goes through you. No one can walk that path for you. No one can show it to you.”
There was no arguing, no pleading. Entreaty was useless. But I took a deep breath, and the despair loosened in my chest. It was an adventure. I had never done a single meaningful thing back in my old world, had not left it a single bit better than I found it. But here, I would have to earn my keep, learn new rules, live a new life. At the very least, I would be one of the rare few to know two whole worlds.
“There’s no rush here,” the raven said, watching my face. “We don’t run by clocks. But the sooner you start the work you were meant to do, the easier it goes. I’ll take you to meet my wife.”
“Thank you,” I said. “Lead on.”
He hopped off through the pine needles, his black wings barely brushing the misty air, and I followed.
