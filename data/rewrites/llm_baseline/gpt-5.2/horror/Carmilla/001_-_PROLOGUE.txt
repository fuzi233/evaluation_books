PROLOGUE

On the wrapper pinned to the manuscript that follows, Doctor Hesselius has left a note—longer than a kitchen inventory and as exacting—together with a reference to his own Essay upon the peculiar matter the pages disclose. In that Essay he handles the mystery with his habitual learning and sharp compression, carving away excess as a careful cook pares rind from fruit, leaving only what will keep and bite. It is to appear, he indicates, as a single volume among the collected papers of that extraordinary man.

I publish this case here for the interest of the laity, and I will not steal so much as a pinch of emphasis from the intelligent lady who tells it. After weighing the temptation to season her recital with a learned précis, I have resolved to do nothing of the kind. The Doctor’s reasoning—dense, cleanly jointed, and cold as a slab—may be found in his own statement, where he speaks of matters “involving, not improbably, some of the profoundest arcana of our dual existence, and its intermediates.” To drag portions of it into these pages would be like pouring a strong stock into a delicate dish and claiming the result still tastes of its true ingredients.

When I first discovered his attached paper, I was eager to reopen the correspondence Doctor Hesselius had begun so many years before with his informant—a person, by every sign, as clever and painstaking as any food historian tracing a vanished recipe from a half-erased marginal note. I imagined her hand returning to the table, adding small domestic particulars that only lived experience can provide: the hour a sound changed in the house, the way a familiar room altered in the ear, the hush after a carriage passed, the thin, mechanical rasp of some modern contrivance intruding upon older silences. The sonic air of a place, I have learned, can be as nourishing or as corrosive as what is eaten there; and in these times, when noise is manufactured and sold as if it were music, such distinctions are too easily forgotten.

But I found, to my regret, that she had died in the interval. She could, perhaps, have added little to the narrative she now communicates in the following pages with—so far as I can judge—conscientious particularity; and yet I cannot help thinking of what perishes with a voice: not only a life, but the unrepeatable timbre of its telling, the private dialect of sensation that no dominant lexicon can truly translate.
