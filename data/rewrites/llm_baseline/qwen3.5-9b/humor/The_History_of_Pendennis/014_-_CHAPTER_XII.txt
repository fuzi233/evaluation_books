Early in this chronicle, we introduced Mr. Garbetts, the Principal Tragedian—a promising, athletic youth of jovial habits and erratic tendencies. He shared a deep camaraderie with Mr. Costigan, serving as the chief decoration alongside him at the convivial gatherings of the Magpie Hotel. These two gentlemen frequently assisted one another in various financial maneuvers, lending each other their valuable signatures with reckless abandon. They were, in essence, fast friends; though Mr. Garbetts rarely ventured to Costigan's residence, largely because he was disliked by Miss Fotheringay, a fact that bred considerable jealousy in Mrs. Garbetts. The truth of the matter was that Garbetts had once courted Miss Fotheringay, only to be rejected before he ever considered proposing to Mrs. Garbetts. However, their romantic entanglements are irrelevant to our current narrative. Suffice it to say, Captain Costigan summoned Mr. Garbetts immediately after his daughter and Mr. Bows departed, seeking a confidant fit for the delicate situation at hand.

Mr. Garbetts was a towering figure with a booming voice and a ferocious demeanor. He possessed the finest legs in the entire company and could snap a poker in half merely for sport, crushing it against his muscular arm. "Run, Tommy," commanded Captain Costigan, dispatching the small messenger. "Fetch Mr. Garbetts from his lodgings above the tripe shop, and instruct them to bring two hot glasses of whisky-and-water from the Grapes."

Tommy obeyed promptly. Soon, Mr. Garbetts arrived, accompanied by the requested spirits. Captain Costigan withheld the full details of recent events from the actor, relying instead on the alcohol to soften his resolve. Under the influence, Garbetts drafted a menacing letter addressed to Major Pendennis. The note demanded that the Major cease all interference with the planned union between Arthur Pendennis and Miss Fotheringay, setting a date for the wedding forthwith. Alternatively, it threatened to grant the Captain the customary satisfaction due between honorable gentlemen. Should the Major refuse, the Captain ominously hinted that he would compel compliance using a horsewhip. The exact wording remains unknown to us, though it undoubtedly bore the Captain's finest rhetorical flourish and was sealed with the grand silver seal of the Costigan family—the sole piece of heirloom plate the Captain owned.

Garbetts was dispatched with this missive. After squeezing his ambassador's hand and wishing divine blessings upon him, the General retrieved his venerable, lethal flintlock pistols—weapons that had claimed the lives of many handsome youths in Dublin. Satisfied with their condition, he proceeded to the drawer where he kept Arthur Pendennis's letters and poems, items he invariably reviewed before allowing his daughter to read them.

Within twenty minutes, Garbetts returned, his face etched with anxiety and defeat. "Have you seen him?" asked the Captain.
"Yes," Garbetts replied.
"And when is the meeting?" Costigan inquired, testing the lock of an antique pistol and raising it toward his bloodshot eye.
"When is *what* for?" Garbetts asked, bewildered.
"The duel, my dear fellow?"
"You don't mean mortal combat, Captain!" Garbetts exclaimed, horrified.
"What the devil else do I mean, Garbetts? I intend to shoot the man who has betrayed my honor, or become a martyr myself!"
"Damn it, I won't carry challenges," Garbetts declared firmly. "I'm a family man, Captain, and I have no part in pistol duels. Take back your letter." To the astonishment and outrage of Captain Costigan, the actor threw the letter onto the floor, its sprawling script and blotched seal landing amidst the debris.

"You don't mean you saw him and didn't deliver the letter?" the Captain roared in fury.
"I saw him, but I could not speak with him, Captain," Garbetts explained calmly.
"Why the devil not?"
"There was someone present I wished to avoid, and surely you would too," the tragedian answered in a sepulchral tone. "Minion Tatham was there, Captain."
"The cowardly scoundrel!" Costigan screamed. "He's frightened and already plotting to swear the peace against me."
"I have nothing to do with fighting, mark my words," Garbetts insisted stubbornly. "Nor do I wish to have seen Tatham, nor that piece of..."
"Hold your tongue, Bob Acres. I believe you are no better than a coward," Costigan retorted, quoting Sir Lucius O'Trigger—a role he had mastered both on and off the stage. After further heated debate, the two parted ways in ill humor. This exchange has been summarized here, as the reader understands the core issue. Now, however, we must explain why it is impossible to provide a full account of the letter sent to Major Pendennis: the gentleman never even opened it.

When Miss Costigan returned from rehearsal, accompanied by her loyal servant Mr. Bows, she found her father pacing their apartment in a state of high agitation, surrounded by the potent scent of whisky-and-water, which evidently failed to soothe his troubled mind. Papers belonging to the Pendennis family lay scattered on the table around empty goblets and a discarded teaspoon used for mixing the Captain's drink. As Emily entered, he grabbed her in a tight embrace, crying out, "Prepare yourself, my child, my blessed child," his voice trembling with agony and his eyes filled with tears.

"You're tipsy again, Papa," Miss Fotheringay chided, pushing him away gently. "You promised you wouldn't drink before dinner."
"It is to forget my sorrows, my poor girl, that I've taken just a drop," the bereaved father wailed. "To drown my cares, I drain the bowl."
"Your cares require a great deal of drowning, Captain dear," Mr. Bows mocked, imitating his friend's heavy accent. "What has happened? Did that soft-spoken gentleman in the wig annoy you?"
"The oily miscreant! I'll have his blood!" roared Costigan.

Miss Milly had already retreated to her room to remove her bonnet and shawl. "I thought he meant mischief. He was so unusually civil," she noted later. "What did he want?"
"Oh, Bows! He has overwhelmed me," the Captain lamented. "There is a diabolical conspiracy against my poor girl. In my opinion, both the Pendennises—the nephew and the uncle—are infernal traitors and scoundrels who ought to be consumed from the face of the earth."
"What is it? What occurred?" Mr. Bows asked, growing increasingly agitated.

Costigan recounted the Major's assertion that young Arthur earned neither two thousand nor two hundred pounds annually. He expressed his rage that he had allowed such an impostor to woo and deceive his innocent daughter, nurturing a viper within his own household. "I have shaken the reptile free," Costigan declared. "As for his uncle, I shall exact a revenge so severe he will regret the day he insulted a Costigan."
"What do you mean, General?" Bows asked.
"I mean to take his life, Bows—his villainous, skulking life," the Captain growled, tapping the battered old pistol case ominously.

Bows had often heard him threaten to use that box of death against enemies, but since the Captain hadn't mentioned writing a challenge, Bows largely ignored the pistols at that moment. Just then, Miss Fotheringay returned to the sitting room, appearing perfectly healthy, happy, and unconcerned—a stark contrast to her father's delirious tremor of grief and anger. She carried a pair of nearly white satin shoes, which she intended to polish with breadcrumbs before wearing them the following Tuesday evening in the role of Ophelia.

She glanced at the papers on the table, paused as if to ask a question, but decided against it. Instead, she fetched a suitable piece of bread from the cupboard to clean her slippers. Returning to the table, she sat comfortably with the shoes and asked her father in her honest Irish brogue, "What are you doing with Master Arthur's letters, poems, and rubbish? Surely you don't intend to read that nonsense again?"

"Oh, Emilee!" cried the Captain. "That boy whom I loved as the apple of my eye is merely a scoundrel and a deceiver, my poor girl!" He cast a tragic look at Mr. Bows, who returned an anxious gaze.
"He! Pooh! That poor lad is as simple as a schoolboy," she dismissed lightly. "Children write verses and nonsense."
"He has acted as a viper at this hearth and a traitor in this family!" the Captain shouted. "Tell you what, he is no better than an impostor."
"What exactly has the poor fellow done, Papa?" Emily asked.
"Done? He has deceived us in the most atrocious manner," her father declared. "He trifled with your affections and outraged my noble feelings. He presented himself as a man of substance, only to reveal he is no better than a beggar. Didn't I often tell you he had two thousand a year? He is a pauper, I tell you, Miss Costigan; dependent entirely on his mother's bounty. A decent woman, true, who may remarry and likely live forever, earning only five hundred a year.
