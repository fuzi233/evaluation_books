St. Bertrand de Comminges is a decayed town perched on the spurs of the Pyrenees, situated not far from Toulouse and even closer to Bagnères-de-Luchon. Once the site of a bishopric until the Revolution, it retains a cathedral that attracts a steady stream of tourists. In the spring of 1883, an Englishman arrived at this ancient, dying place. I hesitate to dignify it with the title of "city," for its population barely numbers a thousand souls.

He was a scholar from Cambridge, having traveled specifically from Toulouse to inspect St. Bertrand's Church. He had left two companions behind in their hotel at Toulouse, promising to reunite with them the following morning. Those two friends required only a brief thirty-minute tour of the church; thereafter, the trio intended to continue their journey toward Auch. However, our Englishman had arrived early on this particular day. His ambition was grander: he planned to fill a notebook and utilize dozens of photographic plates to document every corner of the magnificent church dominating the little hill of Comminges. To execute this project thoroughly, he needed exclusive access to the church for the entire day. Consequently, he dispatched the *verger*—or, more accurately, the sacristan—to the innkeeper of the *Chapeau Rouge*, a somewhat brusque lady. When the sacristan arrived, the Englishman found himself confronted by an unexpectedly fascinating subject.

The intrigue did not lie in the man's physical appearance. He was a small, dry, wizened old fellow, indistinguishable from hundreds of other French church guardians. Instead, the fascination stemmed from a peculiar, furtive demeanor—an air of being hunted and oppressed. He was perpetually casting half-glances over his shoulder. The muscles of his back and shoulders remained in a constant, nervous contraction, as if he awaited the imminent grasp of an unseen enemy. The Englishman struggled to categorize the man: was he a victim of a fixed delusion, crushed by a guilty conscience, or perhaps an unbearably henpecked husband? While the odds favored the domestic explanation, the overall impression suggested a persecutor far more formidable than any nagging wife.

Let us call the Englishman Dennistoun. Soon, however, he became so engrossed in his notebook and camera that he could spare only occasional glances for the sacristan. Whenever he did look, the old man was found at a close distance, either huddled against a wall or crouching within one of the church's gorgeous stalls. After a while, Dennistoun grew restless. Suspicions mingled in his mind: Was the old man being kept from his breakfast? Did Dennistoun suspect theft of St. Bertrand's ivory crozier or the dusty stuffed crocodile hanging over the font? These thoughts tormented him.

"Will you not go home?" Dennistoun asked finally. "I am quite capable of finishing my notes alone; you may lock me in if you wish. I require at least two more hours here, and it must be cold for you, is it not?"

"Good heavens!" exclaimed the little man, thrown into a state of inexplicable terror by the suggestion. "Such a thing cannot be considered for a moment. Leave *monsieur* alone in the church? No, no; two hours, three hours, it matters not to me. I have eaten breakfast, I am not cold, thank you, *monsieur*."

"Very well, my little man," Dennistoun muttered to himself. "You have been warned; you must bear the consequences."

Within two hours, the stalls, the massive dilapidated organ, the choir screen of Bishop John de Mauléon, the fragments of stained glass and tapestry, and the artifacts in the treasury had been meticulously examined. The sacristan trailed at Dennistoun's heels, occasionally whipping around as if stung whenever one of the strange noises plaguing large, empty buildings drifted into the space. These were curious sounds indeed.

"Once," Dennistoun recounted to me later, "I could have sworn I heard a thin, metallic voice laughing high up in the tower. I darted an inquiring glance at my sacristan. He was white-lipped. 'It is he—that is—it is no one; the door is locked,' was all he said. We stared at each other for a full minute."

Another incident deeply puzzled Dennistoun. He was examining a large, dark painting hanging behind the altar, part of a series depicting the miracles of St. Bertrand. The composition was nearly indecipherable, yet a Latin legend beneath it read: *Qualiter S. Bertrandus liberavit hominem quem diabolus diu volebat strangulare* ("How St. Bertrand delivered a man whom the Devil long sought to strangle").

Dennistoun turned to the sacristan with a smile, intending a jocular remark, only to be confounded by the sight of the old man kneeling before the image. The sacristan gazed at the painting with the desperate eyes of a suppliant in agony, his hands tightly clasped, tears streaming down his cheeks. Dennistoun feigned ignorance, but the question gnawed at him: *Why should such a crude painting affect anyone so profoundly?* It seemed he was finally grasping the key to the strange behavior that had plagued him all day. The man must be a monomaniac, but what was his obsession?

It was nearly five o'clock. The short day was waning, and the church began to fill with shadows. The curious noises—the muffled footsteps and distant murmuring voices audible throughout the day—seemed, due to the failing light and heightened senses, to grow more frequent and insistent.

For the first time, the sacristan displayed signs of haste and impatience. He sighed with relief when the camera and notebook were packed away and hurriedly beckoned Dennistoun to the western door beneath the tower. It was time to ring the Angelus. A few pulls on the reluctant rope, and the great bell, Bertrande, high in the tower, began to toll. Its voice soared among the pines and descended into the valleys, loud with the sound of mountain streams, calling the dwellers on those lonely hills to repeat the angel's salutation to Her whom he calls Blessed among Women. With that, a profound silence fell upon the little town for the first time that day. Dennistoun and the sacristan exited the church.

On the doorstep, conversation resumed.

"*Monsieur* seemed to take an interest in the old choir-books in the sacristy," the sacristan noted.

"Indeed. I was going to ask if there is a library in the town."

"No, *monsieur*; perhaps there once belonged to the Chapter, but now it is such a small place—" Here came a strange pause of irresolution. Then, with a sudden plunge, he continued: "But if *monsieur* is an amateur of old books, I have something at home that might interest you. It is not a hundred yards away."

Instantly, Dennistoun's cherished dream of discovering priceless manuscripts in untouched French corners ignited, only to dim a moment later. It was likely a dull missal printed by Plantin around 1580. How probable was it that a place so near Toulouse had not already been ransacked by collectors? Yet, it would be foolish not to go; he would reproach himself forever if he declined. Thus, they set off. Along the way, the sacristan's erratic shift from irresolution to determination returned to Dennistoun's notice. In a shamefaced manner, he wondered if he was being led into a secluded spot to be murdered as a presumed wealthy Englishman. He managed to initiate conversation, clumsily mentioning that he expected two friends to join him early the next morning. To his surprise, this announcement seemed to relieve the sacristan of some of his oppressive anxiety.

"That is well," the sacristan said brightly. "That is very well. *Monsieur* will travel with his friends; they will always be near him. It is a good thing to travel in company—sometimes."

The final word appeared to be an afterthought, bringing with it a relapse into gloom for the poor little man.

Soon they arrived at the house. It was slightly larger than its neighbors, built of stone, with a shield carved over the door bearing the arms of Alberic de Mauléon, a collateral descendant of Bishop John de Mauléon. This Alberic served as Canon of Comminges from 1680 to 1701. The upper windows were boarded up, and the entire property bore the aspect of decaying age common to Comminges.

Upon reaching the doorstep, the sacristan paused.

"Perhaps," he hesitated, "perhaps, after all, *monsieur* lacks the time?"

"Not at all—plenty of time—nothing to do until tomorrow. Let us see what you have."

The door opened, revealing a face far younger than the sacristan's, yet bearing a similar distressing expression. Here, however, the look seemed less fear for personal safety and more acute anxiety regarding another. Clearly, this was the sacristan's daughter. Aside from the expression, she was a handsome girl. She brightened considerably upon seeing her father accompanied by a robust stranger. A few words exchanged between father and daughter escaped Dennistoun's ears, save for the sacristan's phrase, "He was laughing in the church," which drew only a look of terror from the girl.
