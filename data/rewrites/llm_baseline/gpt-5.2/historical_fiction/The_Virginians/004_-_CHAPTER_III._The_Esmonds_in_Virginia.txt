When the last cannon of Queen Anne’s wars had cooled, and the last toasts to her memory had been drowned in new loyalties, Henry Esmond—who had carried a colonel’s commission and a gentleman’s conscience with equal stiffness—found himself pinched between what he had attempted and what the kingdom would forgive. He had played, however briefly, at the dangerous game of restoring a fallen family; and though the nation, with a practical appetite, chose another dynasty, some of those who had erred out of devotion—or vanity, or old habit—were advised to slip away from the three kingdoms until the heat of accusation abated.

Esmond went, not with the romantic air of an exile, but with the tired exactness of a man who had mismeasured a recipe and did not care to taste the result again. His friends, who understood how mild the new sovereign’s temper could be when properly approached, soon contrived his peace; and had he cared to return, the way lay open. But there were tangles at home—of family, of memory, of a private hurt that had steeped itself into his days like bitter bark left too long in a pot—and he chose instead the broad, slow-breathing country across the Atlantic, where an old grant from King Charles I. waited like an uncut loaf.

So he took possession of the Virginia estate that had belonged, in law and in story, to his line, and named his American house Castlewood, after the patrimonial home in Hampshire. Virginia, in those years, loved to model its manners on England as faithfully as it could manage with different air and different soil. The very sound of it tried to be English: the clop of horses along red roads, the church-bell’s sober tongue on Sunday, the creak of wharf-planks as hogsheads of tobacco rolled toward the river, the drawled salutations under great trees. Those who called themselves gentry held their heads above the Dutch traders to the north and the industrious dissenters farther up the coast. No people in the world seemed less republican than these planters who would, in another generation, be foremost in revolt.

The great estates were run after a patriarchal fashion: broad fields roughly coaxed into yield; smokehouses and dairies, stables and kitchens; and—under it all, like the steady undertone in a piece of music—labor compelled. There were purchased and assigned servants from Europe, and there were black servants born to bondage; and in that time the question of slavery had not yet been forced into the daylight of argument among such people. The land gave corn and meat; the woods gave game; the rivers, with their thick living swish and splash at dawn, gave fish that needed only net and hand. Ships came up to private wharves along the Potomac or the James, taking away tobacco heavy with the smell of its own cured leaf, and bringing back English goods—cloth and metalwork and china, spices and tea—so that the tables of Virginia, though fed by maize and pig and river, still held the taste of London and Bristol in their cupboards.

Hospitality was not merely a virtue but a habit, like salting meat for winter. A stranger did not go unfed from a gate. The gentry visited one another with an almost feudal certainty—arriving with servants and trunks, staying as long as the larder and conversation held, leaving behind the fading acoustics of laughter in the hall and the softer after-sounds of domestic work resuming its usual rhythm.

It was in this world that Esmond’s daughter and grandsons were born, and his wife died. The lady he had married was widow to his kinsman, the unlucky Viscount Castlewood, killed in a duel by Lord Mohun at the end of King William’s reign. Grief, in Esmond, did not explode; it settled, like lees at the bottom of a bottle, leaving the upper part clear enough for daily use but forever altered in taste. He was not unkind—indeed, to those around him he was gentle and considerate, even obsequious to the women of his family, whom he scarcely ever contradicted. Yet something in him had failed and never repaired: a bankruptcy of heart, as though the currency of his feelings had been called in and replaced with quieter coin. He submitted to life more than he enjoyed it, and was never in better spirits than when he spoke of laying it down.

After his wife’s death, his daughter took the management of him and of Castlewood with brisk capability. He surrendered both to her with a sort of grateful acquiescence. So long as he had his books and his quiet, he asked little more. When company came, he entertained handsomely—there was always a table fit to make a hungry man reverent, and always drink enough to persuade a guest that his host was the most genial of fellows. Esmond had a pleasant, sarcastical turn, and could season a story as neatly as a cook seasons stew—without overdoing it, so that the flavor came through. But when the guests went away, he was not sorry.

“My love,” he said once to his daughter, with that calm irony which was almost tenderness, “I shall not be sorry to go myself; and you, though the most affectionate of daughters, will console yourself after a while. Why should I, who am so old, be romantic? You may, who are still a young creature.”

He did not mean all he said, for the lady he addressed was a matter-of-fact little person, with very little romance in her nature. Her imagination, when it kindled, burned chiefly in one direction: lineage, precedence, the ordering of the world into ranks as carefully as she ordered her household stores. After fifteen years of good profit from Virginia soil and tobacco, she pressed him to build a mansion grander and more durable than the plain wooden house in which he had been content. Neighbors had raised handsome brick dwellings; she would not have Castlewood seem mean by comparison. Esmond, who cared neither for quarters nor quarterings, yielded with a shrug, and even helped her.

For he did possess antiquarian learning, and a painter’s skill besides. A century earlier, heraldry had been part of polite education; and during a visit to Europe, his daughter had hunted pedigrees with a hunger that had nothing to do with appetite and everything to do with pride. She returned to Virginia with documents and printed volumes from France and England, treating them as sacred receipts that proved, beyond doubt, the ingredients of her own importance. They satisfied her entirely that the Esmonds sprang from noble Norman warriors, and farther still—from native English royalty. Esmond, indulgent and amused, put brush to paper and painted two magnificent heraldic trees for her: on one side Charlemagne, in plate armour with imperial mantle and diadem; on the other Queen Boadicea, whom he insisted upon giving the light costume of an ancient British queen, with a prodigious gilded crown, a trifling fur mantle, and a symmetrical person tastefully marked with brilliant blue figures. From these two roots the family rose, in painted branches and gold-lettered names, until the stocks united somewhere in the thirteenth century in a fortunate Esmond who claimed both.

Of the Warrington family, into which she married, good Madam Rachel thought little. She wrote herself Esmond Warrington, but in the country she was Madam Esmond of Castlewood, and after her father’s death she would rule the domain accordingly. Indeed, she was sometimes so certain of her own grandeur that the colony’s assemblies at Jamestown were disturbed by quarrels for precedence—sharp words, and even a scuffle or two, preserved in her notes with indignation. King James II. and his son had once offered her father and grandfather the title of Marquis; he had burned and disowned the patent, but she behaved at times as though it lay still in some strong-box and needed only to be unfolded. She considered the English Esmonds of an inferior dignity to her own branch; and as for the colonial aristocracy, she made no scruple of asserting her superiority over the whole body.

Yet this same colony, for all its provincial jealousies, was as loyal in profession as any shire at home. Virginians boasted that Charles II. had been king in Virginia before he had been king in England. English king and English church were honored with equal steadiness; and the two young twins at Castlewood—bred on family pride as regularly as they were fed on hominy and bacon—were faithful little subjects of the crown.

When the boys’ grandfather died, their mother proclaimed in great state her eldest son George successor and heir of the estate. Harry, younger by half an hour, was always enjoined to respect his senior, and the whole household was instructed accordingly—black servants, of whom there was a large, noisy, contented family, and assigned servants from Europe, whose lot was made as bearable as it might be under the lady’s government. In all that busy little kingdom, there was scarcely a rebel save Mrs. Esmond’s faithful companion Madam Mountain, and Harry’s foster-mother, a devoted black woman who could never be brought to understand why her child should not be first when he was, as she swore, handsomer, stronger, and cleverer than his brother. In truth, there was scarcely any difference in beauty, strength, or stature between the twins. Their faces matched as closely as two loaves from the same batch, and when they lay in their beds with their heads swallowed by enormous ribboned nightcaps, only a nurse or mother could tell which was which—except for the color of their hair.

In temper, however, they were unlike. George, the elder, was peaceful, studious, and silent. Harry was warlike and noisy. Harry could learn quickly when he began, but was slow to begin; no threats of the ferule could force him into a lesson during an idle fit. George, by contrast, would sit and labor without complaint, and would even help his brother over a hard passage when Harry at last applied himself. Where George’s mind brightened in the library, Harry’s came alive in the stable-yard and the woods. He drilled the little negroes on the estate and caned them like a corporal, sparring with them in earnest boxing-matches; and if he was worsted, he bore no malice, only a hotter desire to win next time. George was sparing of blows and gentle with all about him, with a softness that could become stubbornness when he believed a thing unjust.

As was customary, each boy had a special little servant assigned to him. It became known through the house, and was told with different emphases in kitchen and parlor, that George once found his little black attendant asleep on his master’s bed. Instead of calling out or striking, George sat down beside the sleeping child and brushed the flies away with a feather fan. Old Gumbo, the boy’s father, came upon the scene and was horrified to see his young master performing such offices; Madam Esmond, hearing of it, was indignant at the “insolence” of a servant in such a place and ordered the young negro to the proper officer for a whipping.

George implored and entreated. He burst into passionate tears, and begged remission of the sentence. His mother was inflexible. The little culprit went off beseeching his young master not to cry. Then George, trembling with rebellion against what he conceived to be injustice, turned his anger on his mother. He declared the punishment a shame—a shame; he said he was master of the boy, and no one—no, not his mother—had a right to touch him. If correction were needed, let it fall on him; he and Harry had often endured it. But no one should lay a hand on his boy. And in his fury he shrieked out an oath—an ugly word from the mouth of the usually gentle child—which shocked his mother and his governor, who had never before heard such language from him. He vowed that when he came of age he would set young Gumbo free, and that very day he went down to the quarters, sought out the child, and gave him one of his own toys as if to salt a wound with kindness.

The young black “martyr,” as some called him in later telling, was in fact an impudent, lazy, saucy little personage, and would likely be none the worse for a whipping, as the old Colonel thought. Esmond acquiesced when his daughter insisted upon punishment, and laughed in his good-natured way when his indignant grandson flung at him, “You let mamma rule you in everything, grandpapa.”

“Why, so I do,” the old man answered easily. “Rachel, my love, the way in which I am petticoat-ridden is so evident that even this baby has found it out.”

“Then why don’t you stand up like a man?” cried little Harry, always ready to abet his brother in any fray.

Esmond looked queerly at that—his eyes narrowing as if some private sound had passed through him, like a distant drum heard in sleep. But he only said, “Because I like sitting down best, my dear. I am an old gentleman, and standing fatigues me.”

It was George, for all his quietness, who was his grandfather’s companion. There was a kind of apish drollery and humor in the boy that pleased the old man, and an early liking for some of Esmond’s own pursuits. George, demure in company, would grow animated in the library; his senses seemed to wake there as if books gave off a scent he could breathe. He knew volumes before he could well carry them, and read in them long before he could understand them—moving his lips over the lines with the seriousness of a priest over a prayer. Harry, in that same room, would sit gloomy and restless, as though the muffled hush of paper and leather oppressed him. But set him among saddles and bridles, among the clean animal heat of horses and the sharp tang of oiled tack; set him where the woods spoke in birdsong and wind, and where a river made its own music against the bank—and he was all life and appetite.

Once, when their grandfather’s ship was sailing for Europe, the boys were asked what present Captain Franks should bring back. George wavered between books and a fiddle. Harry declared instantly for a little gun. Their mother was hurt that her elder son should choose what she deemed low tastes, and she applauded the younger’s desire as more worthy of the name she guarded so jealously.

“Books, papa, I can fancy to be a good choice,” she said to her father, trying to keep her tone respectful while her pride bristled, “though I am sure you must have pretty nigh all the books in the world already. But I never can desire—I may be wrong, but I never can desire—that my son, and the grandson of the Marquis of Esmond, should be a fiddler.”

“Should be a fiddlestick, my dear,” the old Colonel answered. “Remember that Heaven’s ways are not ours, and that each creature born has a little kingdom of thought of his own, which it is a sin in us to invade. Suppose George loves music? You can no more stop him than you can order a rose not to smell sweet, or a bird not to sing.”

“A bird! A bird sings from nature; George did not come into the world with a fiddle in his hand,” Mrs. Warrington replied, tossing her head. “I am sure I hated the harpsichord when a chit at Kensington School, and only learned it to please my mamma. Say what you will, dear sir, I cannot believe that this fiddling is work for persons of fashion.”

“And King David who played the harp, my dear?”

“I wish my papa would read him more, and not speak about him in that way,” she said sharply; for in her ear, sacred words belonged to sermons and not to arguments about instruments.

“Nay, my dear, it was but by way of illustration,” he answered gently.

It was Esmond’s nature, as he himself had owned elsewhere, always to be led by a woman. With his wife dead, he coaxed, dandled, and spoiled his daughter; laughing at her caprices, but humoring them; making a jest of her prejudices, but letting them stand. He held that meddling could not change dispositions—only teach children hypocrisy by commanding them overmuch. So he let Rachel rule the outward order of Castlewood while he sat with his quiet, and with the inward knowledge that what mattered most in a life was untranslatable: no pedigree chart could express it, no title could carry it, no proud word could keep it from slipping away.

At length the time came when he was done with the affairs of this world. He laid them down almost as if glad to be rid of their burden. There was no need to begin a history with tolling bells, yet the colony would have its solemnities. Parson Broadbent of Jamestown preached a funeral discourse that caused listeners to wonder where such eloquence and Latin had been found. Perhaps Mr. Dempster knew—the boys’ Scotch tutor, who corrected the proofs of the oration, later printed, at the desire of his Excellency and many persons of honor, at Mr. Franklin’s press in Philadelphia.

No such sumptuous funeral had ever been seen in the country as that which Madam Esmond Warrington ordained for her father—pompous grief that the dead man himself would have been the first to smile at. The twins, almost smothered in black trains and hatbands, headed the procession. Behind them came my Lord Fairfax from Greenway Court; his Excellency the Governor of Virginia in his coach; the Randolphs, the Careys, the Harrisons, the Washingtons, and many more, for the whole county esteemed the departed gentleman—his goodness, his talents, his benevolence, and that unobtrusive urbanity which had made even his sarcasm feel like wit rather than wound.

News crossed the water to England, to the family of Colonel Esmond’s stepson, the Lord Castlewood of Hampshire. They offered to bear the charges of a marble slab to record the names and virtues of his lordship’s mother and her husband. In due time the monument was set up, displaying the arms and coronet of the Esmonds, supported by a chubby group of weeping cherubs, and inscribed with an epitaph which, for once, did not tell falsehoods.
