Some two weeks later, Newland Archer slouched in the quiet dim of his tiny private nook at the law offices of Letterblair, Lamson and Low, half lost to idle, wandering thoughts, when the clerk rapped at his door. The head of the firm wanted to see him at once.

Old Mr. Letterblair, the longstanding legal custodian of three full generations of New York gentility, was throned behind his heavy mahogany desk, his tight-clipped white whiskers stroked absently as he ran a hand through the rumpled grey hair above his jutting brows. To his irreverent young junior, he looked exactly like the old family physician, thoroughly vexed by a patient whose symptoms refused to fit any known, neat classification.

“My dear sir” — he always addressed Archer as “sir,” in formal deference to their shared clientele — “I’ve sent for you to handle a delicate matter. One I’d prefer not to mention to Mr. Skipworth or Mr. Redwood for the time being.” Those two were the other named senior partners, of course, long dead these thirty years, a quirk of old New York’s oldest legal firms that made Mr. Letterblair, professionally speaking, his own grandson.

He leaned back, brow furrowed. “It’s a family matter.” Archer looked up. “The Mingott family. Mrs. Manson Mingott called on me yesterday. Her granddaughter, the Countess Olenska, wishes to sue her husband for divorce. Certain papers have been placed in my hands.” He drummed his fingers lightly on the polished desk. “Given your forthcoming alliance with the family, I’d like to consult you, talk this case through before we take any further steps.”

Warm blood surged hot to Archer’s temples. He had only seen Ellen Olenska once since that quiet visit to her fire-lit parlor, and that had been at the opera, tucked away in the Mingott family box. Over these past weeks, the sharp, vivid weight of her had faded from the forefront of his mind, pushed gently back to make space for May Welland, bright and radiant and uncomplicated, reclaiming her rightful place. He had written off the little whispers of her wanting a divorce as silly, unfounded gossip. Theoretically, the very idea of divorce sat as poorly with him as it did with his own mother, and he felt a flare of quiet irritation that old Mrs. Mingott had clearly prodded Letterblair to rope him into this business, before he was even formally a member of the Mingott clan by marriage. Surely there were plenty of Mingott men better suited to it. He waited for the older man to go on.

Mr. Letterblair unlocked a drawer and pulled out a sealed packet. “If you would run your eye over these papers —”

Archer frowned. “I beg your pardon, sir. But precisely because of that prospective family tie, I would far rather you turned this over to Mr. Skipworth or Mr. Redwood.”

Mr. Letterblair looked surprised, faintly offended. No junior ever turned down a favour of this sort from the head of the firm. “I respect your scruple, sir. But in this case, true delicacy demands that you take this on. The suggestion wasn’t mine, you see — it came from Mrs. Manson Mingott, and her son Lovell Mingott. I spoke to Mr. Welland, too. They all named you, specifically.”

Archer felt his temper rising. He had drifted along lazy and unresisting for the last fortnight, letting May’s soft, sweet beauty blot out the Mingott clan’s pressing, unspoken claims on him, but this new demand made him suddenly acutely aware of exactly what the family thought they were entitled to extract from a soon-to-be son-in-law, and he chafed at the assigned role.

“Her uncles ought to handle this,” he said.

“They have. The whole family has gone over the matter. They are firmly opposed to the Countess’s plan — but she refuses to back down, and insists on getting a formal legal opinion.”

Archer went quiet, the packet still unopened in his hand. “Does she intend to remarry?”

“Word has been passed around that that is the case, but she denies it.”

“Then —”

“Oblige me, Mr. Archer, by reading through these documents first. Once we have talked the case through, I will give you my full opinion.”

Archer left his superior’s office reluctantly, the unwelcome packet tucked under his arm. He had spent the past fortnight half-consciously working to shake free of the lingering burden of Madame Olenska. That quiet, intimate hour they had shared by her fireside had drawn them into a momentary closeness that the Duke of St. Austrey’s perfectly timed arrival, accompanied by Mrs. Lemuel Struthers, had providentially broken. Two days later, he had watched the farce of her being welcomed back into the van der Luydens’ good graces, and told himself, with a faint sharpness, that a woman so skilled at thanking the most powerful of New York’s elders for a bouquet of flowers had no need of a young man of his modest standing to champion her or offer her private comfort. That neat, detached perspective had simplified his own life immeasurably, shining a new, warm light on all the quiet domestic virtues he valued. He could not imagine May Welland, in any possible crisis, parading her private hurts about the world and pouring her confessions out to strange men, and she had never seemed finer, more perfect, than in the week that followed. He had even given in to her wish for a long engagement, after she gave him that one unanswerable, gentle reply to his pleas to marry soon.

“You know your parents have always let you have your way, ever since you were a tiny girl,” he had argued, and she had looked at him with that clear, soft smile of hers and said: “Yes. That’s why it feels so cruel to refuse them the very last little thing they will ever ask of me as their little girl.”

That was the true, old New York note, the kind of answer he had always wanted to know his wife would give. When you had breathed that rarefied, crystal air your whole life, anything less pure seemed stifling.

The papers he sat down to read told him very little on the surface, but they plunged him into an atmosphere so thick and ugly he felt as if he could barely breathe. They were mostly a back-and-forth of letters between Count Olenski’s solicitors and the French legal firm the Countess had approached to sort out her financial affairs, plus one short, brutal note from the Count himself to his wife. The second he finished it, Newland Archer jumped to his feet, stuffed all the papers back into their envelope, and walked straight back into Mr. Letterblair’s office.

“Here are the letters, sir. If you wish, I will go and see Madame Olenska,” he said, his voice tight and constrained.

“Thank you — thank you, Mr. Archer. Come dine with me tonight, if you are free, and we can go over the matter afterwards. That way, you can call on our client tomorrow, if you wish.”

Archer walked straight home that afternoon, no detours. It was a winter evening of perfect, transparent clarity, a soft pale new moon hanging innocent above the brownstone rooftops, and he wanted to breathe deep of that clear, cold radiance, not speak a single word to anyone until he and Mr. Letterblair were closeted together after dinner. There was no other possible choice he could have made. He had to see Madame Olenska himself, rather than let her most private, painful secrets be bared to the eyes of strangers. A great wave of compassion had swept away all his former indifference and irritation. She stood before his mind now as a raw, exposed, pitiable figure, who had to be saved at any cost from wounding herself further in her reckless fight against fate.

He remembered Mrs. Welland’s request, that the family be spared any hint of anything “unpleasant” in Ellen’s history, and winced, suddenly wondering if that very attitude was the thing that kept New York’s air so artificially pure. “Are we nothing but a crowd of Pharisees, after all?” he wondered, lost, struggling to reconcile his instinctive revulsion at the ugliness of the Count’s crimes with his equal instinct to pity her for her weakness.

For the first time, he realized how shockingly simple, how unexamined, his own core principles had always been. He had the reputation of being a young man unafraid of a little risk, and his secret brief affair with silly, vain little Mrs. Thorley Rushworth had never been quite secret enough, lending him a very becoming air of gentle adventure. But Mrs. Rushworth was “that sort of woman,” naturally clandestine, far more charmed by the thrill of sneaking around and the danger of being caught than she had ever been by any charm he possessed. When that truth had dawned on him it had nearly broken his heart, but now it felt like the only redeeming part of the whole affair. It was the exact kind of trivial, foolish dalliance nearly every young man of his acquaintance had gone through, walking away with perfectly clear consciences, still fully believing in the vast, uncrossable chasm between the women you loved and respected, and the women you enjoyed — and pitied. Their mothers, aunts, and all the other older women of their circle had carefully nurtured that belief, all sharing Mrs. Archer’s quiet certainty that when “such things” happened, the man was only foolish, but the woman was always, fundamentally, guilty. Every elderly lady Archer knew regarded any woman who fell in love imprudently as cunning and unscrupulous, and the poor simple man as helpless in her clutches. The only sensible thing to do was to get him married off to a nice girl as soon as possible, and trust her to keep him out of trouble.

Archer found himself guessing now, that love problems in the old, tangled communities of Europe could never be so simple, never so easily sorted into neat little categories. Rich, idle, decorative old societies must breed so many more messy, unfair situations like this one — situations where a naturally sensitive, reserved woman could, through nothing but utter defencelessness and overwhelming loneliness, get tangled up in a bond that conventional society would never, ever forgive.

When he got home, he scribbled a short note to the Countess Olenska, asking what hour the next day she might be free to see him, and sent it off with a messenger boy. The boy returned a short while later with a reply: she was leaving for Skuytercliff the next morning to spend the weekend with the van der Luydens, but he could find her home and all alone that very evening, right after dinner. The note was written on a slightly crumpled half-sheet of paper, no date, no address, but her handwriting was bold, firm, unapologetically free. He smiled to himself, amused by the thought of her spending a whole weekend in the stately, frigid silence of Skuytercliff — and then immediately felt a sharp twinge of sympathy. That, of all places, was where she would feel the chill of a dozen minds all deliberately averted, all determined not to see anything “unpleasant” at all.

He showed up at Mr. Letterblair’s house promptly at seven, glad of the excuse to slip away as soon as dinner was over. He had already formed his own full opinion from the papers he had been given, and did not particularly want to hash the whole thing out with his senior partner. Mr. Letterblair was a widower, so they dined alone, slowly and copiously, in a dark, shabby dining room hung with yellowing old prints of *The Death of Chatham* and *The Coronation of Napoleon*. On the sideboard, between two fluted Sheraton knife cases, stood a decanter of Haut Brion, and another of the famous old Lanning port — a gift from a client, that Tom Lanning the wastrel had sold off only a year or two before his mysterious, disgraceful death in San Francisco, a scandal the family had found far less humiliating than the sale of their entire wine cellar.

After a smooth, velvety oyster soup came shad and cucumbers, then a young broiled turkey served with corn fritters, followed by canvasback duck with currant jelly and celery mayonnaise. Mr. Letterblair, who only ever ate a sandwich and tea for lunch, took his dinner slowly and very seriously, and insisted his guest do the same. When the last of the courses were cleared away, the tablecloth removed, and their cigars lit, Mr. Letterblair leaned back in his chair, pushed the decanter of port toward Archer, and settled his back comfortably against the glowing coal fire behind him.

“The whole family is against this divorce. And I think they are entirely right.”

Archer felt himself bristle, instantly taking the opposite side of the argument. “But why, sir? If there was ever a case that justified it —”

“Well, what good would it do? She’s here, he’s there, the Atlantic is between them. She will never get a single extra dollar of her own money back than what he has already voluntarily given her. Those damned heathen European marriage settlements see to that. As things stand over there, Olenski has behaved generously. He could have turned her out of the house with nothing at all.”

Archer knew that was true, and fell silent.

“From what I understand, though,” Mr. Letterblair went on, “she doesn’t care a thing about the money. So as the family says, why not leave well enough alone?”

An hour before, Archer had walked into this house in full agreement with that exact sentiment. But hearing it spoken out loud by this well-fed, supremely indifferent, selfish old man, it suddenly turned into the very voice of the Pharisees, a society entirely focused on building walls to block out anything unpleasant.

“I think that ought to be her decision to make.”

“Hmm. Have you thought through what will happen if she does choose divorce?”

“You mean the threats in her husband’s letter? How much weight do those carry? It is nothing more than the rambling accusations of an angry blackguard.”

“Yes. But he might choose to formally contest the suit, and that would stir up a great deal of very unpleasant talk.”

“Unpleasant!” Archer burst out, before he could stop himself.

Mr. Letterblair lifted his eyebrows at him, curious. Archer, aware it was useless to try to explain the mess of tangled, roiling feelings in his head, bowed his head in apparent acquiescence as his senior went on. “Divorce is always unpleasant.”

“You agree with me, then?” Mr. Letterblair asked, after a short pause.

“Naturally,” Archer said.

“Well then. I can count on you. The Mingotts can count on you, to use your influence to talk her out of this idea?”

Archer hesitated. “I cannot promise you anything until I have spoken to the Countess Olenska,” he said finally.

“Mr. Archer, I do not understand you. Do you want to marry into a family with a messy, scandalous divorce suit hanging over its head?”

“I do not think that has any bearing on this case.”

Mr. Letterblair set down his port glass, fixing his young partner with a careful, wary stare. Archer knew he risked having his mandate to see Ellen taken away from him, and for some unnameable reason, he hated the thought. Now that the job had been put in his hands, he had no intention of giving it up. To make sure that did not happen, he knew he had to reassure this unimaginative old man, who was the Mingotts’ entire living legal conscience.

“You may rest assured, sir, I will not commit to any course of action before I report back to you. All I meant was that I would prefer not to give a final opinion until I have heard exactly what Madame Olenska has to say.”

Mr. Letterblair nodded approvingly, at that excess of caution perfectly in line with the very best old New York traditions. Archer glanced at his watch, pleaded a prior engagement, and took his leave, hurrying out into the crisp cold night, his heart already light with the quiet, unspoken thrill of seeing Ellen Olenska again.
