"Fortune favors the fool," the adage goes, or perhaps it is some other witless bard who penned it. The sentiment remains constant: a chap is often at the zenith of his confidence, bristling with invincibility, precisely when Fate decides to deliver a lead pipe to his skull. I am convinced this axiom holds water. Consider the current predicament regarding Lady Malvern and her offspring, Wilmot. Just moments prior to their arrival, I was basking in the glow of absolute domestic tranquility.

It was one of those pristine mornings, fresh from a cold ablution, feeling revitalized and buoyant. In truth, I was particularly emboldened because the previous evening I had successfully asserted my dominance over Jeeves—a feat of considerable magnitude. The dynamic between us had been deteriorating; I risked becoming a mere serf under his thumb. While I accepted the forfeiture of one of my new suits, given his impeccable sartorial acumen, I nearly revolted when he forbade me from wearing a specific pair of cloth-top boots I cherished above all else. Furthermore, his attempt to dictate my choice of headwear—the Longacre style favored by John Drew versus the Country Gentleman I desired—crossed the line. After a heated confrontation, I secured the Country Gentleman. Thus, I entered the morning feeling robust, independent, and entirely in control.

I was in the bathroom, rubbing my spine with a coarse towel and humming a tune, when a sharp rap echoed at the door. I halted my song and cracked the door ajar.

"What ho without!"

"Lady Malvern requests your presence, sir," Jeeves intoned.

"Eh?"

"Lady Malvern, sir. She awaits you in the sitting-room."

"Compose yourself, Jeeves," I commanded sternly, for pranks before breakfast are strictly prohibited. "You are aware there is no one awaiting me. It is barely ten o'clock."

"I surmised from Her Ladyship that she disembarked from an ocean liner at an early hour this morning."

This rendered the scenario marginally plausible. I recalled my own arrival in America a year prior, where proceedings commenced at a ghastly hour, depositing me on a foreign shore before eight.

"Who exactly is Lady Malvern, Jeeves?"

"She did not disclose her identity to me, sir."

"Is she alone?"

"She is accompanied by a Lord Pershore, sir. I presume he is her son."

"Oh, well. Prepare a suitable ensemble, and I shall dress."

"Our heather-mixture lounge suit is ready, sir."

"Lead the way."

As I dressed, my mind raced to identify this intruder. It was only upon pulling my shirt over my head and grasping for my studs that the memory surfaced.

"I've located her, Jeeves. She is an acquaintance of my Aunt Agatha."

"Indeed, sir?"

"Yes. I encountered her at luncheon one Sunday prior to departing London. A formidable specimen. An author. She penned a treatise on social conditions in India following her return from the Durbar."

"Yes, sir? Pardon me, sir, but not that tie!"

"Eh?"

"Not that tie accompanying the heather-mixture lounge, sir!"

A shock rippled through me. I believed I had neutralized the issue. The atmosphere grew somber. If I faltered now, the progress achieved the previous night would evaporate. I steel-ed my resolve.

"What is the objection to this tie? I recall you scrutinizing it previously. Speak plainly! What is the matter?"

"Excessively ornate, sir."

"Nonsense! A cheerful pink. Nothing more."

"Unsuited, sir."

"Jeeves, this is the tie I wear!"

"Very good, sir."

Disagreeable. I perceived the man was wounded. Yet, I remained firm. I fastened the tie, donned the coat and waistcoat, and proceeded to the sitting-room.

"Halloa! Halloa! Halloa!" I exclaimed. "What?"

"Ah! Pray, how do you do, Mr. Wooster? I believe you have never met my son, Wilmot? Motty, darling, allow me to introduce Mr. Wooster."

Lady Malvern was a hearty, vibrant, and overwhelmingly powerful female entity. Though not towering in height, she possessed a presence extending approximately six feet from the opening post to the prompt side. She occupied my largest armchair as if it had been custom-fitted by an architect anticipating hip compression. Her eyes were bright and bulging, framed by a profusion of yellow hair. Upon speaking, she revealed roughly fifty-seven anterior teeth. She was the type of woman capable of numbing a gentleman's faculties instantly, reducing him to the status of a ten-year-old clad in Sunday attire, summoned to offer perfunctory greetings. Entirely unsuitable for a sitting-room visit before breakfast.

"Motty," the son, was approximately twenty-three, tall, slender, and possessing a meek demeanor. He shared his mother's yellow hair, though styled flat and parted centrally. His eyes also bulged, yet lacked brightness, appearing dull grey with pink rims. His chin surrendered its structural integrity halfway down, and he appeared devoid of eyelashes. In summary, a mild, furtive, sheepish individual.

"Delighted to meet you," I stated. "So you have arrived? Extending a prolonged stay in America?"

"For approximately a month. Your aunt provided your address and instructed me to ensure I called upon you."

I welcomed this news, indicating Aunt Agatha was thawing. A year prior, discord had erupted when she dispatched me to New York to extricate my cousin Gussie from the grasp of a music-hall performer. By the time I concluded my interventions, Gussie had not only wedded the actress but ascended the stage himself, achieving success. Consequently, Aunt Agatha was profoundly distressed. I had avoided returning to face her, finding solace in learning that time had sufficiently healed the rift for her to request introductions among her peers. Essentially, while I appreciated America, I harbored no desire for England to remain inaccessible due to Aunt Agatha's wrath. England is undeniably cramped for those navigating her fury. Thus, hearing her kind words bolstered my spirits, and I smiled genially at the assembly.

"Your aunt indicated you would render every assistance within your power."

"Certainly? Oh, certainly! Absolutely!"

"Thank you immensely. I require you to accommodate dear Motty temporarily."

I failed to comprehend this initially.

"Acommodate whom? At my clubs?"

"No, no! Dear Motty is inherently domesticated. Is he not, Motty darling?"

Motty, currently gnawing on the knob of his cane, finally uncorked himself.

"Yes, Mother," he murmured, promptly resealing his reticence.

"I would prefer he not resort to clubs. I mean, reside here. Have him live with you during my absence."

These dreadful words dripped forth like honey. The woman seemingly failed to grasp the calamitous nature of her proposition. I delivered a swift glance to Motty. He sat with his mouth nuzzling the stick, staring blankly at the wall. The prospect of hosting this appendage indefinitely terrified me. Absolutely terrified. I was on the verge of declaring the arrangement impossible, threatening to summon the police at the slightest sign of nesting behavior, when she continued, rolling placidly over my objections.

Something about this woman eroded a gentleman's willpower.

"I depart New York via the midday train, necessitating a visit to Sing-Sing prison. I possess a keen interest in American penal conditions. Subsequently, I traverse gradually toward the coast, observing points of interest en route. Observe, Mr. Wooster, I am in America primarily for professional reasons. Presumably you have read my tome, *India and the Indians*? My publishers eagerly anticipate a companion volume on the United States. I cannot remain longer than a month, requiring a return for the season, yet a month suffices. I spent less than a month in India, and my esteemed friend Sir Roger Cremorne authored *America from Within* following a fortnight's residence. I would delight in taking dear Motty along, though the poor youth succumbs to illness during rail travel. I shall retrieve him upon my return."

From my vantage point, I observed Jeeves in the dining room, arranging the breakfast table. I wished fervently for a private moment with him, confident he could devise a solution to halt this intrusion.

"It will prove such a relief knowing Motty is secure with you, Mr. Wooster. I am cognizant of the temptations inherent in a great metropolis. Hitherto, dear Motty has been shielded from them. He has resided quietly in the countryside with me. I trust you will attend to him diligently, Mr. Wooster. He requires minimal attention." She discussed the unfortunate youth as though absent. Motty, however, seemed indifferent. Having ceased chewing his cane, he sat with his mouth agape. "He is a vegetarian and abstains from alcohol, devoted to literature. Provide him with a suitable volume, and he shall be content." Rising, she added, "Thank you exceedingly, Mr. Wooster! I am uncertain how I managed without your aid. Come, Motty! We possess sufficient time to view several attractions before my departure. However, I shall depend upon you for the majority of my insights regarding New York, darling. Ensure you observe keenly and document your impressions! It will be invaluable. Farewell, Mr. Wooster
