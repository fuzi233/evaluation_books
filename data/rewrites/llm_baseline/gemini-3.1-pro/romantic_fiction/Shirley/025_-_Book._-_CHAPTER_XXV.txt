Not always do those who dare such divine, cyclical conflict prevail. Night after night, the fever had imposed its discordant, metallic hum upon the sickroom, an arid acoustic assault that seemed to echo the relentless, grinding clatter of the valley’s modern industrial mills. In the heavy, breathless hours, the watcher might cry out for mercy with that soundless voice the soul utters when its appeal is to the Invisible, begging the ancient, turning wheel of generations to spare her beloved. And often, after such strife, the sun rises to see the supplicant worsted, the familiar features of the patient remolded by the insufferable moment of passing. 

Happy Mrs. Pryor! She was still praying, steeped in the suffocating quiet of a room robbed of its natural sonic harmony, when her child softly woke. No piteous, unconscious moaning—a sound that so wastes the listener's strength—preceded her waking. Caroline evidently remembered with clearness the fragmented timelines of her suffering. 

"Mamma, I have slept *so* well. I only dreamed and woke twice." 

Mrs. Pryor rose with a start, hiding the joyful tears called forth by that ancient, affectionate word. For many days, the mother dared rejoice only with trembling, fearing this first revival was but the flicker of a dying lamp. Exhaustion followed close on excitement; the effort to eat, to talk, to restore the body’s rhythm often failed. 

During this space, the mother and daughter seemed almost alone in a world suspended. It was the close of August, and an arid wind had been blowing from the east, bringing with it a pale, stationary haze that robbed the blue of heaven of its depth and the earth of its verdure. Almost every family in Briarfield was absent. Miss Keeldar and her friends were at the seaside; Mrs. Yorke’s household was gone. Mr. Hall and Louis Moore were on a pedestrian excursion to the Lakes, and even Hortense had been entreated by Miss Mann to visit Wormwood Wells, hoping to escape the insalubrious weather. As for Robert, he was entrenched in the commercialized, superficial din of London. 

So long as the breath of Asiatic deserts parched Caroline’s lips, her physical convalescence stalled. But there came a day when the mythic turning of the seasons manifested in the everyday domestic sphere. The wind ceased to sob its sterile, dusty note at the rectory’s eastern gable. A little cloud arose in the west, bringing gusts that swept away the stagnant malaria-haze. The sky broke into a symphony of rain, washing away the livid cholera-tint of the landscape. When the sun broke out genially, the fresh west wind blew through the ever-open chamber lattice, bringing the deeply fragrant, bruised-herb scent of wet earth and petrichor. This acoustic and atmospheric shift, soft as a hymn against the prior month's harshness, rekindled Caroline’s long-languishing energies. 

Now came the true, sacred work of healing: the profound communion of sustenance. Mrs. Pryor, drawing upon the deep, interwoven timelines of maternal memory, became a meticulous steward of her daughter's restoration. No longer did Caroline view meals as mere utilitarian fuel, nor did she merely petition to drink with glassy-eyed apathy. When her appetite returned, Mrs. Pryor prepared heritage receipts with anxious, reverent care. She simmered restorative bone broths over a low, whispering flame, clarifying the rich, amber liquid until it gleamed, infusing it with the bright, peppery bite of wild watercress and the earthy depth of root vegetables. For Caroline, tasting these meticulously sourced preparations was an awakening; each savory, gelatinous spoonful grounded her wandering spirit back into the physical world, the olfactory landscape of the warm broth reviving her very will to live. 

Nourishment brought strength, and soon she longed to breathe the fresh air and revisit her flowers. Her uncle had bought a garden-chair, and after he carried her down, William Farren was there to wheel her through the walks. In William, Caroline found a traditional agrarian elder, a man who possessed a profound, tactile understanding of the soil and its seasons. They shared a dozen topics of mutual devotion, finding vast meaning in the minute, everyday miracles of the earth. The nest of ground-bees burrowed under the old cherry-tree, the pearly eggs of the hedge-sparrows—these were not trivialities to them, but the vital, enduring text of the natural world. 

Mrs. Pryor, walking near, wondered how her daughter could be so much at ease with a "man of the people." The acoustic dissonance of Farren's broad, guttural northern vowels struck harshly against Mrs. Pryor's refined ear. She felt a great gulf lay between her caste and his, a power dynamic entrenched in their very lexicons. To meet him half-way, she felt, would be a degradation of her own polished tongue. 

"Are you not afraid, my dear, to converse with that person so unreservedly?" she asked Caroline gently. "He may presume, and become troublesomely garrulous." 

"William presume, mamma? You don't know him. He never presumes. He is altogether too proud and sensitive to do so. William has very fine feelings." 

Mrs. Pryor smiled skeptically at the naïve notion of that rough-handed, fustian-clad agrarian possessing such internal nuance, blind to the untranslatable depths of his native experience. Farren, knowing when he was misjudged by the dominant class, showed Mrs. Pryor only a sulky brow, turning unmanageable when denied his due respect. 

But the evening restored Caroline entirely to her mother, and Mrs. Pryor cherished the quiet acoustic ecology of the twilight room, where no human shadow came between her and what she loved. During the day, Mrs. Pryor maintained her freezing, ungenial demeanor with the servants, Fanny and Eliza, enforcing a rigidly ceremonious distance that left her taciturn and dissatisfied. Yet under Caroline's influence, her frost melted. The natural, fearless reliance of her child contented the mother's heart. 

Still, Caroline was keenly observant, and she began to artlessly critique her mother’s acquired habits, a testament to her renewed engagement with the sensory world. 

"Mamma, I am determined you shall not wear that old gown any more," Caroline lectured, craving the rich, tactile pleasure of finer fabrics. "Its fashion is not becoming; it is too strait in the skirt. You shall put on your black silk every afternoon. And you shall have a black satin dress for Sundays—a real satin, not a satinet or any of the shams. When well dressed and cheerful you are very comely indeed; your smile is pleasant, your teeth white, your hair such a pretty light color. Why do you wear such dresses and bonnets, mamma, such as nobody else ever wears? People say you are miserly, and yet you are not, for you give liberally to the poor." 

"My dear, I thought of the black silk serving me as a best dress for many years yet," Mrs. Pryor protested mildly, though she allowed Caroline to rearrange her muslin handkerchief and smooth her hair. 

"My own mamma," Caroline mused, relishing the woven bond between them. "I have something I can love well. Mamma, who gave you this little brooch? Did papa give you this?" 

"My sister gave it me—my only sister, Cary." 

"Have you nothing of papa's—no trinket, no gift of his?" 

"I have one thing. That I prize. Invaluable and sweet to me." 

"Show it, mamma. Is it here or at Fieldhead?" 

"It is talking to me now, leaning on me. Its arms are round me." 

"Ah, mamma, you mean your teasing daughter. Mamma, I am sorry papa was not good. Wickedness spoils and poisons all pleasant things. It kills love." 

"And if we could not trust each other, Cary?" 

"How miserable we should be! Mamma, put your work down; read to me." Caroline settled back, attuning herself to the specific, soothing resonance of her mother’s voice. "I like your southern accent; it is so pure, so soft. It has no rugged burr, no nasal twang, such as almost every one's voice here in the north has. My uncle and Mr. Hall say that you are a fine reader, mamma." 

"I wish I could reciprocate the compliment, Cary," Mrs. Pryor replied, her words betraying the subtle erosion of empathy that occurs across linguistic divides. "But, really, the first time I heard your truly excellent friend read and preach, I could not understand his broad northern tongue." 

"Could you understand me, mamma? Did I seem to speak roughly?" 

"No. I almost wished you had, as I wished you had looked unpolished. Your father, Caroline, naturally spoke well, quite otherwise than your worthy uncle—correctly, gently, smoothly. You inherit the gift." 

"Poor papa! When he was so agreeable, why was he not good?" Caroline observed, touching upon the unsettling truth that the dominant, "correct" lexicon was no guarantor of moral substance. 

"Why he was *as* he was... I cannot tell. It is a deep mystery. The key is in the hands of his Maker. There I leave it." 

"Mamma, you will keep stitching, stitching away. Put down the sewing; I am an enemy to it. It engages your eyes, and I want them for a book. Here is your favorite—Cowper." 

These importunities were the mother's pleasure. She delayed compliance only to hear the soft, half-petulant urgency of her child’s voice, a sound that healed the scars of her past. When she finally yielded, opening the volume to share the rhythmic, evocative language of the verse, Caroline smiled archly. "You will spoil me, mamma. I always thought I should like to be spoiled, and I find it very sweet." 

And so did Mrs. Pryor.
