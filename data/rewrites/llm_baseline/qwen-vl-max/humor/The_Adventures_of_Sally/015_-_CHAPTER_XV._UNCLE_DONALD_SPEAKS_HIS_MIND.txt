There are men—Bruce Carmyle being a prime example—who possess an almost supernatural ability to bounce back from defeat, like rubber balls that refuse to stay down no matter how hard they’re dropped. This resilience, useful in business, politics, or any other arena where one might be expected to lose gracefully, was particularly effective when applied to matters of the heart. Bruce Carmyle, though not exactly a romantic hero, was built for persistence. And right now, he was determined to win Sally.

She had said no at Monk’s Crofton—clearly, firmly, with the kind of finality usually reserved for rejecting bad investments or unpalatable dinner invitations. But Bruce didn’t believe in finality. He believed in persistence. He believed in effort. He believed in himself. And most importantly, he believed that if he simply kept trying long enough, Sally would eventually say yes.

He’d had doubts—brief, fleeting ones—about her wild streak, her tendency to wear hats at angles that defied gravity and convention alike, her habit of quoting poetry during breakfast. But those doubts had been quickly smothered by the memory of her laughing at him once, just once, in a way that made his chest feel warm and slightly dangerous. That laugh, combined with her chin (which always seemed to be tilted up as if daring the world to tell her what to do), had become a beacon in the dark, guiding him toward a future he couldn’t yet see but desperately wanted.

So he packed his bags. Quietly. Secretly. With all the stealth of a man who knows he’s about to do something very unwise but also very necessary.

Unfortunately, secrecy is not a strong suit in families where every member seems to communicate via telepathic whispers passed through the walls, the wallpaper, and even the family silver.

And so, on Bruce’s last night in London, Uncle Donald arrived.

Uncle Donald was not a man one could ignore. He was two hundred and forty pounds of solid, breathing evidence that indulgence has consequences. When he sat down in Bruce’s chair, it groaned like a shipwrecked galleon. His moustache, thick and walrus-like, moved with each breath, as though it were a separate organism struggling to survive beneath layers of fat and mildew. He had climbed several flights of stairs—no small feat for someone whose heart had been considered weak since childhood, which meant he’d avoided exercise for fifty years and eaten everything within arm’s reach for nearly as long.

Bruce greeted him with the usual polite reserve, but inwardly, he felt a familiar pang of irritation. There was something about Uncle Donald that brought out the worst in people. Even Bruce, usually composed and dignified, found himself wishing he’d taken the train to America earlier.

“Wha’ this?” Uncle Donald wheezed, peering into the open suitcase. “You packin’?”

“Yes,” Bruce replied, trying to sound calm.

“You goin’ away?”

“Yes.”

“Where you goin’?”

“America.”

“When?”

“Tomorrow.”

“Why?”

The exchange, though short on paper, lasted nearly three minutes in real time because between each question, Uncle Donald paused to inhale deeply, exhale slowly, adjust his moustache, and mutter things like “Ah, bless my soul” and “This is serious.” It was exhausting.

“You’re goin’ after that girl,” Uncle Donald finally declared, voice low and accusing.

Bruce flushed. For a moment, he thought of Ginger—his cousin, known for his inability to control his tongue—and realized, with a jolt, that Ginger wasn’t *that* bad. In fact, under the circumstances, Ginger might have handled this better.

“Would you like a whisky and soda?” Bruce asked, hoping to change the subject.

“Gimme!” Uncle Donald bellowed, already leaning forward.

Bruce poured one. Then another. Then a third, because Uncle Donald had a rule: never refuse a drink. Ever. Especially not when it came from someone who might otherwise be inconvenient.

After the second glass, Uncle Donald leaned back, sighed, and resumed.

“Never thought you were a fool before,” he said, shaking his head slowly. “But now… you’re a damn fool.”

Bruce bristled. He wasn’t used to being called names by anyone, let alone a man who looked like a walking sofa with eyebrows.

“I’m not a fool.”

“You are. A complete idiot. Don’t like the girl. Never did. She’s not nice. Not proper. Not suitable.”

“Must we discuss this?” Bruce asked, dropping into his most aristocratic tone.

“We must,” Uncle Donald snapped. “I climbed these blasted stairs with a weak heart! I nearly died! For this! You think I did it for fun? Gimme another!”

Bruce poured the fourth.

“Shocking business,” Uncle Donald muttered, sipping slowly. “If your poor father were alive, what would he say? He’d say… What kind of whisky is this?”

“O’Rafferty Special.”

“New to me. Not bad. Mellow. Smooth. Wherej get it?”

“Bilby’s, Oxford Street.”

“Must order some. Mellow. He’d say… well, God knows what he’d say. But he wouldn’t approve. Whatch doing it for? Why? Nobody understands. Puzzles George. Baffles Geraldine. All of us.”

“She’s not after my money,” Bruce said stiffly.

“Oh, really? Then why did she refuse you at Monk’s Crofton?”

“Because she wasn’t ready.”

“Drawing you on,” Uncle Donald said immediately. “Luring you. Classic trick. Happened to me in 1881. Girl tried to lure me. If I hadn’t had sense and a weak heart…”

Bruce didn’t know whether to laugh or cry.

“What do you know about her?” Uncle Donald demanded.

“I met her in France. At Roville.”

“Travelling with her family?”

“No. Alone.”

“Not even with that brother of hers? Bad! Very bad!”

“American girls travel independently,” Bruce said, defending.

“Her brother will be fat soon,” Uncle Donald predicted. “Traveling alone? What did you do? Catch her eye on the pier?”

“Really, Uncle Donald—”

“Well, you must’ve met somehow.”

“I was introduced by Lancelot. She was a friend of his.”

“Lancelot!” Uncle Donald exploded, turning red as a boiled lobster. “Lancelot! That shows you what kind of girl she is. Any girl who’d be friends with *him*—Unpack!”

“Excuse me?”

“Unpack! Can’t let you go off like this. Nonsense. Find yourself a decent girl. Aunt Mary’s been meeting the Bassington-Bassingtons. Kent branch. Eldest daughter—charming. Perfect match.”

Bruce stared. He felt something inside him crack.

“I shall do nothing of the kind,” he said coldly. “I sail tomorrow.”

Uncle Donald blinked. He’d never been defied before—not properly. It was disorienting. Like being told the sun doesn’t rise in the east.

“Eh?” he said.

Bruce stood straighter. “I am over twenty-one. Financially independent. I make my own decisions.”

“But consider!” Uncle Donald pleaded, voice cracking. “Reflect!”

“I have reflected.”

“Your position in the county…”

“I’ve thought of that.”

“You could marry anyone!”

“I’m going to.”

“You’re determined to run off to God-knows-where after this Miss-I-can’t-even-remember-her-name?”

“Yes.”

“And you haven’t considered,” Uncle Donald said, lowering his voice dramatically, “that you owe a duty to the Family?”

Bruce’s patience snapped.

“Oh, *damn* the Family!” he shouted.

Silence. The only sound was the creak of the chair as Uncle Donald rose, slowly, painfully.

“After that,” he said, “I have nothing more to say.”

“Good,” Bruce replied, rudely. “Then don’t.”

“Except this,” Uncle Donald added, hand on the door handle. “If you come back married to that girl… I’ll cut you in Piccadilly. By George, I will.”

Bruce didn’t respond. He just looked down his nose, expression unreadable.

A tense silence.

Then Uncle Donald turned.

“What,” he asked, fingers still on the handle, “did you say it was called?”

“What was what called?”

“That whisky.”

“O’Rafferty Special.”

“And wherj get it?”

“Bilby’s, Oxford Street.”

“I’ll make a note of it,” Uncle Donald said, and walked out.

Bruce watched him go. Then he picked up the bottle and stared at it.

“Mellow,” he muttered. “Mellow, indeed.”
