The Dashwood sisters had now resided in London for over two months, and Marianne's longing to depart grew with every passing day. She sighed for the fresh air, the freedom, and the quietude of the countryside, convinced that no place could offer her the peace she craved like Barton. Elinor shared her desire to leave, though she tempered her urgency with a practical awareness of the difficulties inherent in such a long journey—a reality Marianne refused to acknowledge. Yet, Elinor began to seriously formulate plans for their departure, having already voiced their wishes to their kind hostess, who resisted with all the eloquence of her goodwill. Then, a new proposal emerged: the Palmers were preparing to move to Cleveland near the end of March for the Easter holidays, and Mrs. Jennings, along with her friends, received a fervent invitation to accompany them. While this alone might not have satisfied Elinor's delicate sensibilities, it was reinforced by the genuine politeness of Mr. Palmer himself. Coupled with the marked improvement in his manners since learning of his sister's unhappiness, this gesture induced Elinor to accept the invitation with pleasure.

When she revealed her decision to Marianne, the younger sister's reaction was far from auspicious.

"Cleveland!" she cried, agitated. "No, I cannot go to Cleveland."

"You forget," Elinor said gently, "that its location is not—that it is not near—"

"But it is in Somersetshire," Marianne interrupted. "I cannot go into Somersetshire. There, where I had hoped to go... No, Elinor, you cannot expect me to go there."

Elinor saw no point in arguing the propriety of suppressing such feelings; instead, she sought to counteract them by appealing to others. She framed the move as a way to secure a more comfortable and timely reunion with their beloved mother, whom Marianne cherished deeply. From Cleveland, merely a few miles from Bristol, the journey to Barton would take no more than a day, albeit a long one. Their mother's servant could easily accompany them, and since their stay at Cleveland would likely be brief, they could be back home in little over three weeks. Knowing Marianne's love for her mother was sincere, Elinor believed it would triumph over the imaginary terrors Marianne conjured.

Mrs. Jennings, far from weary of her guests, pressed them earnestly to return with her from Cleveland. Elinor appreciated the kindness, yet her resolve remained unshaken. With their mother's consent readily obtained, arrangements for their return were finalized. Marianne found solace in calculating the remaining hours until she would once again be in Barton.

"Ah, Colonel," Mrs. Jennings addressed him upon his first visit after their departure was settled, "I do not know what you and I shall do without the Miss Dashwoods! They are quite resolved to go home from the Palmers. And how forlorn we shall be when I return! Lord, we shall sit and stare at one another as dull as two cats."

Perhaps Mrs. Jennings hoped this vivid sketch of future boredom would provoke an offer from the Colonel, offering him an escape from it. If so, she soon had reason to believe her aim was achieved. As Elinor moved to the window to quickly measure a print she intended to copy, the Colonel followed, his gaze laden with particular meaning. He conversed with her for several minutes. The effect on Elinor was evident; though Mrs. Jennings, too honorable to eavesdrop, had deliberately shifted her seat to the piano where Marianne played, she could not help noticing Elinor's flushed cheeks and agitated demeanor. She was too absorbed in the Colonel's words to continue her work.

Further confirmation of her hopes arrived during a pause in Marianne's practice. Words from the Colonel drifted to Mrs. Jennings' ear, in which he seemed to apologize for the inadequacies of his house. This settled the matter beyond doubt. She marveled that he would feel the need to do so, assuming it was mere etiquette. Unable to distinguish Elinor's reply, she judged from the motion of her lips that the Colonel's comment was not a material objection. Mrs. Jennings commended Elinor silently for her honesty. They spoke for a few more minutes without her catching a syllable, until another fortunate break in Marianne's performance allowed her to hear the Colonel's calm voice clearly:

"I am afraid it cannot take place very soon."

Startled and shocked by such an unloving remark, she nearly cried out, "Lord, what could possibly hinder it?" but checked herself, settling for a silent gasp.

"This is very strange! Surely he need not wait to be older."

However, this apparent delay did not seem to offend or mortify his fair companion. When the conversation ended and they parted ways, Mrs. Jennings distinctly heard Elinor say, with a voice betraying her feelings, "I shall always think myself very much obliged to you." Mrs. Jennings was delighted by this gratitude, wondering only why the Colonel could depart with such composure, making no reply, after hearing such a sentence. She had not expected her old friend to consider him such an indifferent suitor.

What truly transpired between them was as follows:

"I have heard," the Colonel said with deep compassion, "of the injustice your friend Mr. Ferrars has suffered from his family. If I understand correctly, he has been entirely cast off by them for persevering in his engagement with a very deserving young woman. Have I been rightly informed? Is it so?"

Elinor confirmed it was true.

"The cruelty, the impolitic cruelty," he replied with great feeling, "of separating, or attempting to separate, two young people long attached to each other, is terrible. Mrs. Ferrars does not know what she is doing—what she may drive her son to. I have seen Mr. Ferrars two or three times in Harley Street and am much pleased with him. He is not a man with whom one can become intimately acquainted in a short time, but I have seen enough to wish him well for his own sake, and as a friend of yours, I wish it all the more. I understand he intends to take orders. Will you be so kind as to tell him that the living of Delaford, now just vacant as I learned from today's post, is his, if he deems it worthy of acceptance? But perhaps, given his unfortunate circumstances, it may seem foolish to appear to doubt; I only wish it were more valuable. It is a rectory, but a small one; the late incumbent earned no more than £200 per annum. Though it is certainly capable of improvement, I fear not to such an extent as to provide a very comfortable income. Nevertheless, my pleasure in presenting it to him will be great. Please assure him of this."

Elinor's astonishment at this commission was profound, had the Colonel been genuinely making her an offer of marriage. The preferment she had considered hopeless for Edward just two days prior was now provided to enable him to marry, and she, of all people, was chosen to bestow it. Her emotion was such that Mrs. Jennings attributed it to a completely different cause. Whatever lesser, less pure feelings might have contributed to her agitation, her esteem for the Colonel's general benevolence and gratitude for his particular friendship, which prompted this act, were strongly felt and warmly expressed.

She thanked him from the bottom of her heart, praised Edward's principles and disposition with the accuracy she knew they deserved, and promised to undertake the commission with pleasure if he truly wished to delegate such an agreeable office to another. Yet, she could not help thinking that no one could perform it better than he. It was an office from which, unwilling to cause Edward pain by accepting an obligation from her, she would have gladly been spared. However, Colonel Brandon, driven by motives of equal delicacy, declined it as well. Still, he seemed so eager for it to be conveyed through her that she would not further oppose him. She believed Edward was still in town and, fortunately, had heard of his address from Miss Steele. Thus, she undertook to inform him of the news later that day.

Once this was settled, Colonel Brandon began discussing his advantage in securing such a respectable and agreeable neighbor. It was then that he mentioned, with regret, that the house was small and indifferent. As Mrs. Jennings had suspected, Elinor made very light of this, particularly regarding its size.

"The smallness of the house," she said, "I cannot imagine any inconvenience for them, as it will be proportional to their family and income."

The Colonel was surprised to find that she considered Mr. Ferrars's marriage a certain consequence of the presentation. He did not suppose the Delaford living could supply an income sufficient for anyone of his station to settle upon, and he stated as much.

"This little rectory can do no more than make Mr. Ferrars comfortable as a bachelor; it cannot enable him to marry," he said sorrowfully. "I am sorry to say that my patronage ends here; my influence is hardly more extensive. If, however, by some unforeseen chance it falls within my power to serve him further, I must think very differently of him than I do now, if I am not as ready to be useful to him then as I sincerely wish I could be at present. What I am doing now indeed seems insignificant, since it advances him so little toward what must be his principal, his only object of happiness. His marriage must still remain a distant good; at least, I am afraid it cannot take place very soon."

Such was the sentence which, when misunderstood, justifiably offended the delicate feelings of Mrs. Jennings. Yet, after recounting what truly passed between Colonel Brandon and Elinor while they stood at the window, the gratitude expressed by the latter upon their parting may perhaps appear, in general, no less reasonably excited, nor less properly worded, than if it had arisen from an offer of marriage.
