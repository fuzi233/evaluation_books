“It was among the ways of good Queen Bess,
Who ruled as well as ever mortal can, sir,
When she was stogg'd, and the country in a mess,
She was wont to send for a Devon man, sir.”

—West Country Song.

The morning dawned bright and clear, yet Amyas Leigh was nowhere to be found. Not that he had fled into despair or wandered off to weep by the Torridge’s banks—he had simply ridden away, Frank discovered, to visit Sir Richard Grenville at Stow. His mother, quick to sense his intent, understood at once: Amyas had gone seeking a commission in the Irish army. Without hesitation, she sent Frank after him, charged with bringing him back and compelling him to reconsider.

Frank mounted his horse and rode ten miles through winding lanes and open moorland. With no inns along the way—fewer even today—he turned down the hill toward Clovelly Court, hoping to find shelter and refreshment under the hospitable roof of Mr. Cary, the local squire, in the manner so common among gentlemen of that time.

Stepping boldly into the long, dim hall of the court—like Menelaus shouting into the gathering—he was met immediately by the towering figure of Amyas, seated at the great oak table, half-buried in a pasty. His face alternated between being lost in the pastry and buried within it, as if sorrow had only sharpened his appetite. Opposite him, young Will Cary knelt on the bench, elbows resting on the table, speaking earnestly and fiercely in a low voice.

“Hillo! lad,” Amyas called out, lifting his head. “Come here and rescue me from this fire-eater! I swear he’ll kill me unless I let him kill someone else first.”

“Ah, Mr. Frank!” said Will, who, like most young men of the region, held Frank in high regard—seeing him as a paragon of fashion, learning, and chivalry. “Welcome! I’ve been longing for you. I wanted your counsel on half a dozen matters. Sit down and eat. There’s ale.”

“None so early, thank you,” replied Frank.

“Ah, no!” Amyas muttered, lowering his head into his tankard before mimicking Frank with exaggerated solemnity. “Avoid strong ale in the morning. It heats the blood, thickens the animal spirits, and clouds the cerebrum with frenzied, lymphatic idols that obscure the pure light of reason. Eh? Young Plato, young Daniel, come hither to judgment! And yet—though I can’t see through the bottom of this tankard already—I can still see plainly enough that Will shall not fight.”

“Shall I not?” Will shot back, eyes flashing. “Who says so? Mr. Frank, I appeal to you—only hear!”

“We are in the judgment-seat,” Frank declared, settling himself beside the pasty. “Proceed, appellant.”

“Well,” Will began, “I told Amyas that Tom Coffin of Portledge—I will not endure him any longer.”

“Let him be,” Amyas said, chewing thoughtfully. “He stood very well on his own feet when I last saw him.”

“Plague on you, keep quiet!” Will snapped. “Does he have the right to look at me the way he does whenever I pass?”

“That depends on how he looks,” Amyas replied. “A cat may look at a king—provided she doesnno take him for a mouse.”

“Oh, I know how he looks—and what he means. He shall stop, or I will stop him. The other day, when I mentioned Rose Salterne—”

“Ate’s apple again!” Frank groaned.

“Never mind what I said,” Will continued. “He burst out laughing in my face. Is that not cause enough for a quarrel? Worse than that—I know he wrote a sonnet and sent it to her by a market woman. What right has he to write poetry when I cannot? It’s not fair play, Mr. Frank—or I am a Jew, a Spaniard, and a Papist all at once—it’s not!” And with that, he slammed his fist on the table, sending plates clattering.

“My dear knight of the burning pestle,” Frank said, smiling, “I have a plan—a device, a resolution, according to the noblest rules of chivalry. Let us fix a day and summon by drumbeat all young gentlemen under thirty living within fifteen miles of the dwelling of that peerless Oriana.”

“And all apprentices too,” Amyas called out from behind his pasty.

“And all apprentices,” Frank agreed. “The bold lads shall fight first with quarterstaves in Bideford Market until every head is broken; then, whoever remains unharmed must pay the penalty for the cowardice of their neck. After that grand tournament—greater than Tottenham’s flea-bite and batrachomyomachy—”

“Confound you and your long words!” Will cried. “I know you’re mocking me.”

“Pazienza, Signor Cavaliere,” Frank said gently. “What follows is no mockery—but bloody, warlike truth. Then, all young gentlemen shall gather in a field—on sand, or better yet, in a bog, where no one can flee if knee-deep in peat. Stripped to our shirts, armed with rapiers of equal length and temper, each shall slay his man, catch who can, and the victors shall fight again—like valiant gamecocks—until all are dead and freed from their sorrows. Then, the last survivor, lamenting before heaven and earth the cruelty of our Fair Oriana and the slaughter her basilisk eyes have caused, shall fall gracefully upon his sword and end the woes of our love-lorn generation. *Placetne Domini?* as they used to say in Oxford’s Senate.”

“Really,” Will said, “this is too much.”

“So is,” Frank added, “your fighting Mr. Coffin with anything longer than a bodkin.”

“Bodkins are too short for such fierce Bobadils,” Amyas interjected. “They’d close in too quickly—we’d end up wrestling after the first blow.”

“Then let them fight with squirts across the market-place,” Frank declared. “For by heaven and the queen’s laws, they shall fight with nothing else.”

“My dear Mr. Cary,” Frank said, suddenly shifting tone to one of gentle sincerity, “do not think I cannot feel your pain, nor that I, too, have not known the sting of love and the sharper sting of jealousy. But oh, Mr. Cary—does it not seem awful to waste the divine wrath—so rightly called by Plato the root of all virtue—upon your own petty quarrel? That wrath, given to you like all else, is meant to be spent in service to her whom all wicked souls fear and all noble ones adore—our peerless queen. Who dares, while she reigns over England, claim sword or courage as his own? Are there no Spaniards to conquer, no wild Irish to free from tyranny, that two gentlemen of Devon must waste their blades in each other’s hearts?”

“By heaven!” Amyas exclaimed. “Frank speaks like a book. And for my part, I believe Christian gentlemen should leave love quarrels to bulls and rams.”

“And the heir of Clovelly,” Frank smiled, “may find nobler examples to follow than the stags in his own deer park.”

“Well,” Will said, humbled, “you are a great scholar, Mr. Frank, and speak like one. But gentlemen must fight sometimes—or where would their honor be?”

“I speak,” Frank replied, a touch proud, “not merely as a scholar, but as a gentleman—one who has fought, and once killed a man (on whose soul may God have mercy). Yet I boast that I have never fought in my own quarrel, nor do I trust that I ever shall. For while there is nothing nobler than fighting for those we love, to fight for oneself is beneath a Christian man—unless refusal means certain death or dishonor. Even then, it may be (though I would not burden any conscience) better not to resist evil, but to overcome it with good.”

“And I can tell you, Will,” Amyas said, “I fear no ghosts. But when I cut off that Frenchman’s head, I said to myself, ‘If he had been slandering me instead of her Majesty, I’d expect to see that head lying on my pillow every night.’”

“God forbid!” Will shuddered. “But what shall I do? Tomorrow, I go to the market—even if it were choked with Coffins, and a ghost in each one.”

“Leave it to me,” Amyas said. “I have my own plan, as does scholar Frank here. If there’s a quarrel tomorrow, see if I don’t—”

“Well, you’re both good fellows,” Will said, rising. “Let’s have another tankard.”

“And drink to Mr. Coffin and all gallant lads of the North,” Frank said. “Now, to business. I must take this runaway youth home to his mother. If he won’t go quietly, I have orders to carry him across my saddle.”

“I hope your horse has a strong back,” Amyas laughed. “But I must go on to see Sir Richard, Frank. All jesting aside, my mind is made up.”

“Stop,” Cary said. “You must stay tonight. First, for fellowship; second, because I need the wisdom of our Phoenix—our oracle, our paragon.” He leaned close. “Mr. Frank, can you decipher this for me? Speak low—here comes my father. You’d best give me the letter again.”

“Eh? Company here?” the old squire called, entering. “Young men, always welcome—especially such as you. Would there were more of your sort in these dirty times! How is your good mother, Frank? Where have I been, Will? To the house-farm, checking the cattle. That sheeted heifer of Prowse’s is all wrong—her coat stares like a hedgehog’s. Tell Jewell to bring her in before night. Then up the forty acres—two coveys sprang, and I took a leash. The Irish hawk flies wild as any haggard and won’t make a bird. Had to hand her to Tom and take the little peregrine. Give me a Clovelly hawk against the world. And—heigh ho—I’m starving! Half-past twelve and dinner not served? What, Master Amyas, spoiling your appetite with strong ale? Better have tried sack, lad. Have some now with me.”

With that, the worthy old man settled onto a bench by the chimney, placed his hawk on a perch above his head, while his cockerels curled near the warm peat ashes. His son began removing his boots, careful not to disturb his father’s corns.

“Come, Master Amyas,” he said, “a pint of white wine and sugar, and a bit of shoeing-horn to start. Some pickled prawns, or a rasher off the coals to whet your appetite?”

“Thank you,” Amyas replied. “But I’ve drunk many foreign liquors in the past three years—better and worse—and none compare to good ale. It needs neither shoeing-horn before nor after. It takes care of itself—and of honest stomachs, I think.”

“You speak like a book, boy,” old Cary chuckled. “And what good comes of these newfangled hot wines and aqua vitae that’ve come since the wars? Only madness and fever in the blood.”

“I fear we haven’t seen the end of it,” Frank said. “My friends in the Netherlands write that our men are falling into a swinish habit of drinking like the Hollanders. Heaven grant they don’t bring the custom home.”

“A man must drink,” Amyas said, “or die of ague in those vile swamps. When they return here, they won’t need it.”

“Heaven grant it,” Frank said. “I’d hate to see Devonshire become a drunken county. Many of our men serve with Mr. Champernoun.”

“Ah,” Cary nodded. “There, as in Ireland, we prove her Majesty’s words true: Devonshire is her right hand, and its young men like arrows in the giant’s hand.”

“They may well be,” his son said, “when some of them are giants themselves—like my tall schoolmate opposite.”

“He’ll be up and doing again soon,” old Cary said.

“And so I shall,” Amyas declared. “I’ve been devising brave deeds—enchanters to bind, dragons to choke, empires to conquer—though not in Holland.”

“You do?” Will asked sharply, sensing more than he heard.

“Yes,” Amyas said, brushing it off. “I go to what Raleigh calls the Land of the Nymphs. Another month, and I hope to be abroad in Ireland.”

“Abroad?” old Cary said. “Call it rather at home. Ireland is full of Devon men from end to end. You’ll find friends everywhere. George Bourchier commands in Munster, Warham St. Leger is marshal, George Carew serves Lord Grey of Wilton—poor Peter Carew fell at Glendalough. After last year’s defeat, when that villain Desmond cut off Herbert and Price, six hundred Devon men filled the ranks, led by Arthur Fortescue. So Devon holds her head high in Ireland, as in the Low Countries and the Spanish Main.”

“And where,” Amyas asked, “is Davils of Marsland? He taught me trout fishing when I stayed at Stow. Is he in Ireland too?”

“Ah, my lad,” Cary said, “that’s a sad tale. I thought all England knew it.”

“You forget, sir,” Amyas said, “I’m a stranger. Surely he’s not dead?”

“Murdered foully, lad! Like a dog—by the man he treated as his son, who pretended to call him father.”

“His blood avenged?” Amyas asked, eyes blazing.

“No, by heaven, not yet! Wait—don’t shout. I’m growing old. Let me tell it my way. Last July—was it not, Will?—Saunders arrived in Ireland, one of those Jesuit foxes, as the Pope’s legate, with money, bulls, and a banner blessed by the Pope. With him, James Fitzmaurice—the same man who swore on his knees to Perrott at Kilmallock to be loyal to Queen Elizabeth, swearing by saints and all his Irish howling, until Perrott told me he had to cover his ears. Well, Fitzmaurice had tried the King of France, got nothing but laughter, so he went to the Most Catholic King, promising to unite Ireland to Spain and restore Popery. And the King, thinking Ireland better in his hands than the Pope’s bastard, outfitted him and sent him off—on an errand like Stukely’s. Though I say, for Devon’s honor, if Stukely lived like a fool, he died like an honest man.”

“Sir Thomas Stukely dead too?” Amyas asked.

“Wait, lad. You’ll hear that tragedy later. Where was I? Ah—Fitzmaurice and the Jesuits landed at Smerwick, three ships, chose a site for a fort, blessed it with holy water and ritual washings to cleanse it of heretic rule. Meanwhile, a Courtenay—of Haccombe, was it? Or Boconnock? Silence, Will—I’ll recall. Yes, Haccombe—was anchored nearby in a warship, seized the three vessels, cutting off the Dons from the sea. John and James Desmond, with a rabble, joined the Spaniards. Earl Desmond refused to join but wouldn’t fight, waiting to side with the winner. Then came poor Davils, sent by the Lord Deputy to command the Desmonds, in the queen’s name, to attack the Spaniards. Some say it was rash—but I say, what better could be done? Everyone knows Davils was the stoutest, shrewdest soldier alive. The young Desmonds looked to him as their father. But he learned what it meant to trust Englishmen turned Irish.

The Desmonds suddenly realized the Spaniards were desperate Paladins—madness to meddle, even at five to one. Poor Davils, seeing no fight in them, returned for help and slept that night at Tralee. Arthur Carter of Bideford, St. Leger’s lieutenant, a soldier as stout as Davils himself, shared his bed. Their lacquey-boy—now serving Sir Richard at Stow—slept at their feet. But in the dead of night, who should enter but James Desmond, sword drawn, with a dozen ruffians behind him, glibs over their faces, skenes in hand. Davils leapt up, asking only, ‘What is the matter, my son?’ The traitor, without letting him pray, struck him naked, crying, ‘Thou shalt be my father no longer, nor I thy son! Thou shalt die!’ Then all attacked. The boy jumped to shield his master, received several skene thrusts, and fell—dead or dying. Captain Carter and Davils were truly dead. God reward them! Then the ruffians slaughtered every Englishman in the house—save the boy, who crawled out wounded through a window. Desmond, covered in blood, returned to the Spaniards, boasting, ‘There! Take that as proof of my faithfulness to you.’ That, my lad, was the end of Henry Davils—and the fate of all who trust the faith of wild savages.”

“I’d ride a hundred miles to see that Desmond hanged!” Amyas said, tears streaming. “Poor Mr. Davils! And now—what of Sir Thomas?”

“Your brother must tell you that, lad. I’m somewhat breathless.”

“And I have a right,” Frank said, smiling, “to tell it. Do you know I nearly became Earl of the Bog of Allen and a peer of the realm to King Buoncompagna, son and heir to Pope Gregory XIII?”

“No, surely!”

“As I am a gentleman. When I was in Rome, I often saw poor Stukely. He offered me this and more, on behalf of the Pope, if I’d only reconcile to the Catholic Church and join the invasion of Ireland.”

“Poor deluded heretic!” Will said. “To lose an earldom for such silly loyalty!”

“It’s no jest,” Frank said. “I saw him often. I couldn’t believe he was in his right mind—so mad with vanity and ambition. Yet in private, he was as honorable a gentleman as any. Still, he sailed for Ireland with eight hundred Spaniards and Italians. And yes—I know the King of Spain paid their expenses. Marquis Vinola—James Buoncompagna—remained in Rome, preferring Stukely conquer Ireland for him while he guarded the family treasures. I saw him off at Civita Vecchia. Though younger, I felt compelled, as a gentleman and a Devon man, to warn him about his duty to the queen and the honor of his country. We argued fiercely. God forgive me if I spoke too harshly—I never saw him again.”

“Too harshly to an open traitor, Frank? Why not run him through?”

“Nay,” Frank said. “I had no clean life for Sundays, so I couldn’t waste my weekday one. As for England’s welfare, I knew he’d do little harm—and told him so. At that, he went mad with pride, swearing he’d have fought me then, if the wind hadn’t favored sailing. I said I was ready whenever he wished. He left, saying, ‘It’s a pity, sir, I cannot fight you now. When next we meet, it will be beneath my dignity to measure swords with you.’”

“I suppose he expected to return a prince,” Amyas said. “Heaven knows. I bear him no ill will. He’s paid all debts now.”

“How did he die?”

“On his voyage, he stopped in Portugal. King Sebastian was sailing for Africa with Mohammed, Prince of Fez, to aid King Abdallah. He persuaded Stukely to join him. Some thought both he and the Spaniards didn’t want the Pope’s son ruling Ireland. Others said he thought an island too small for his ambition—must conquer a continent. I know not why, but he went. They faced heavy seas. Upon landing, many soldiers were seasick. Stukely wisely advised waiting two or three days to recover. But Don Sebastian was mad for battle—wanted his veni, vidi, vici. Instead, he got veni, vidi, perii. Sebastian, Abdallah, and his son Mohammed all died in the first battle at Alcasar. Stukely, surrounded and overwhelmed, fought until he could fight no more, then died heroically—wounded only in front. May God have mercy on his soul!”

“Ah,” Amyas said. “We heard of the battle near Lima, but not of poor Stukely.”

“That last,” old Cary said, “was a Popish prayer, Master Frank.”

“Most worshipful sir,” Frank replied, “surely you wouldn’t wish God not to have mercy on his soul?”

“No—eh? Of course not. But it’s settled now—he’s dead, poor fellow.”

“Certainly, my dear sir. And you can’t help feeling fond of him still.”

“Eh? Why, I should be a brute if I weren’t. He and I were schoolfellows—though he was younger. I thrashed him many times. One cannot help having tenderness for a man after that. Besides, we hunted together in Exmoor, and drank royal nights in Ilfracombe—mad young blades. Fond of him? I’d sooner lose my forefinger than see him go to the dogs.”

“Then, my dear sir,” Frank said, “if you still feel for him despite his faults, how can you doubt God might feel for him too? For my part,” he added, “without believing in Popish Purgatory, I cannot help holding with Plato that heroic souls—those lacking but little of greatness—are disciplined afterward into better minds. Perhaps, as some ancients believed, transmigrated into animals they resembled in spirit. If Sir Thomas Stukely’s soul now animates a lion, I’d say he’d be a valiant, royal lion—and likely grow ashamed and penitent for being nothing more than a lion.”

“What now, Master Frank? I don’t trouble my head with such things. I say Stukely was a good-hearted fellow at heart. If you plague me with your dialectics and college quips, you shall have no more sack, sir. But here come the servants—and I hear the cook knocking for dinner.”

After a few madrigals and an Italian song by Master Frank—both sung sweetly—the ladies rose and withdrew. Then Will Cary drew his chair close to Frank’s and slipped a crumpled letter into his hand.

“This was left for me,” he whispered, “by a countryman this morning. Read it and tell me what to do.”

Frank opened it and read:

“Mister Cary, be you wary
By deer park end to-night.
Yf Irish ffoxe com out of rocks
Grip and hold hym tight.”

“I would have shown it to my father,” Will said, “but—”
