When Miss Dashwood recounted the details of her conversation to her sister, the impact was not entirely what she had anticipated. Marianne did not question the truth of a single word; she listened with steady, submissive attention, offering no objections, making no remarks, and attempting no defense of Willoughby. Instead, her tears revealed that she accepted the reality of his guilt as an impossibility to deny. While this behavior confirmed to Elinor that Marianne's conscience was finally burdened—with evidence of this shift appearing in her willingness to speak to Colonel Brandon with a newfound, albeit sorrowful, respect, and in her reduced agitation—Elinor also saw that her sister remained profoundly wretched.

Marianne's mind had settled, but only into a deep, gloomy depression. She weighed the loss of Willoughby's character far heavier than the loss of his affection. The memory of his seduction and abandonment of Miss Williams, the suffering of that innocent girl, and the haunting suspicion of what his intentions toward her once truly were, preyed relentlessly on her spirit. So consumed was she by these thoughts that she could not bring herself to speak of them even to Elinor. By brooding silently over her grief, she inflicted more pain on her sister than any open confession could have caused.

To describe Mrs. Dashwood's reaction to Elinor's letter would merely repeat the sentiments already shared by her daughters: a disappointment nearly as acute as Marianne's, and an indignation surpassing even Elinor's. Through a series of rapid, lengthy letters, Mrs. Dashwood poured out her anguish, expressing her anxious concern for Marianne and imploring her daughter to endure the misfortune with fortitude. How terrible must Marianne's affliction be, her mother reasoned, that she could speak of "fortitude"? How humiliating must the cause of such regret be, that her mother wished her not to indulge it?

Against her own desire for comfort, Mrs. Dashwood decided it was better for Marianne to be anywhere else than at Barton. There, every sight would resurrect the past in its most agonizing form, constantly presenting Willoughby as she had always known him. Therefore, she urged her daughters to extend their stay in London, a visit originally expected to last five or six weeks. The city offered a variety of occupations, sights, and company unavailable in the countryside, which might, she hoped, distract Marianne enough to find fleeting interests beyond her own sorrow, however repugnant those distractions might seem to her now. Furthermore, Mrs. Dashwood believed London safer than the country regarding Willoughby. In the crowded capital, his acquaintances would naturally sever ties with her family, making a meeting unlikely through design or accident. Chance held less sway in the throng of London than in the quiet retreat of Barton, where fate might have forced an encounter during his upcoming wedding at Allenham—a union Mrs. Dashwood had resigned herself to expecting.

Finally, a letter from her son-in-law informed her that he and his wife would arrive in town by mid-February, and she deemed it proper for them to occasionally see their brother. Though Marianne internally protested, believing her mother's plan entirely misguided and depriving her of the only true solace—her mother's personal sympathy—she submitted without opposition. Yet, Elinor found consolation in the fact that while this arrangement brought hardship to Marianne, it offered protection. Elinor, anticipating she could not avoid Edward Ferrars, comforted herself by reasoning that staying in London was preferable to an immediate return to Devonshire.

Elinor's vigilance in shielding her sister from the name of Willoughby proved effective. Neither Mrs. Jennings, Sir John, nor Mrs. Palmer ever mentioned him before Marianne. Elinor, however, could not enjoy this same privacy and was forced to endure the daily indignation of her circle. Sir John, unable to comprehend the situation, raged against the man he had once admired. "A man of whom I had such high regard! Such a good-natured fellow! I don't believe there is a bolder rider in England!" he exclaimed. "It is an unaccountable business. I wish him to the devil with all my heart. I would not speak another word to him, meet him where I might, for all the world! No, not if it were beside Barton Covert, and we were kept watching for two hours together. Such a scoundrel! Such a deceitful dog! It was only the last time we met that he offered me one of Folly's puppies, and this is the end of it!"

Mrs. Palmer echoed this sentiment in her own way. "She is determined to drop his acquaintance immediately," she declared, relieved she had never been close to him. "I wish Combe Magna wasn't so near Cleveland, but it doesn't matter; it's too far to visit anyway. I hate him so much I'm resolved never to mention his name again. I'll tell everyone I meet how useless he is." Mrs. Palmer's sympathy further manifested in her eagerness to share gossip about the impending marriage, detailing the coachmaker building the new carriage, the artist painting Willoughby's portrait, and the shop selling Miss Grey's dresses.

In stark contrast, Lady Middleton's calm and polite indifference provided a welcome relief to Elinor, whose spirits were often crushed by the clamorous kindness of the others. It was a profound comfort to Elinor to know there was at least one person in her circle who showed no curiosity about the particulars and no anxiety for her sister's health. Every social qualification seems elevated by circumstance; sometimes, one is driven to value good breeding more highly than good nature. Lady Middleton voiced her disapproval daily, simply stating, "It is very shocking, indeed!" This gentle, continuous vent allowed her to greet the Miss Dashwoods without emotion, soon forgetting the matter entirely. Having upheld the dignity of her sex and condemned the error, she felt free to focus on her own social engagements, deciding (much to Sir John's dismay) to call on Mrs. Willoughby once she was married, recognizing her as a woman of elegance and fortune.

Colonel Brandon's delicate, unobtrusive inquiries were always a comfort to Elinor. He had earned the right to discuss Marianne's despair through his zealous efforts to soothe it, and they always spoke with complete confidence. His greatest reward was the pitying glance Marianne occasionally cast his way and the softness of her voice when she was compelled to address him. These signs assured the Colonel that his efforts had fostered goodwill, giving Elinor hope for future reconciliation. Meanwhile, Mrs. Jennings, unaware of this silent progress, noticed only that the Colonel remained as grave as ever. Unable to persuade him to propose or to ask her to do so, she began to fear that the marriage would be delayed until Michaelmas, and eventually concluded it would not happen at all. The apparent harmony between the Colonel and Miss Dashwood suggested that the garden's attractions—the mulberry tree, the canal, and the yew arbor—would belong to her alone, leading Mrs. Jennings to cease thinking of Mrs. Ferrars entirely.

Early in February, barely two weeks after receiving Willoughby's letter, Elinor performed the painful duty of informing her sister of his marriage. She ensured the news reached Marianne privately, sparing her the shock of reading it in the public papers, which she eagerly scanned each morning. Marianne received the tidings with resolute composure, shedding no initial tears. However, shortly thereafter, they burst forth, leaving her for the remainder of the day in a state scarcely less miserable than when she first feared the event.

The newlyweds departed London immediately after the ceremony. Elinor now hoped to gradually encourage her sister to venture outdoors again, as she had previously done. Around this time, the two Miss Steeles returned to their relatives' homes in Conduit and Berkeley Streets, greeted with great warmth. Only Elinor felt sorrow at their presence; their arrival always pained her, and she struggled to respond graciously to Lucy's overwhelming delight at finding her still in town.

"I should have been quite disappointed if I hadn't found you here *still*," Lucy remarked, emphasizing the word. "But I always thought I *should*. I was almost sure you wouldn't leave London for a while, though you *told* me at Barton you wouldn't stay more than a month. I thought, at the time, you'd likely change your mind when it came to the point. It would have been such a pity to have gone before your brother and sister arrived. And now, surely, you aren't in a hurry to leave. I am amazingly glad you didn't stick to your word."

Elinor understood the sting behind these words and used all her self-control to mask her displeasure. "Well, my dear," Mrs. Jennings interjected, "how did you travel?"

"Not in the stage, I assure you," Miss Steele replied with quick exultation. "We came post all the way and had a very smart beau to attend us. Dr. Davies was coming to town, so we joined him in a post-chaise. He behaved very genteelly and paid ten or twelve shillings more than we did."

"Oh, oh!" cried Mrs. Jennings. "Very pretty, indeed! And the Doctor is a single man, I warrant you."

"There now," said Miss Steele, feigning a simper, "everyone laughs at me about the Doctor, and I can't understand why.
