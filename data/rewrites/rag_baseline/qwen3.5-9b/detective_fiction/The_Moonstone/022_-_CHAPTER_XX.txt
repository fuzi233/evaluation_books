The rumors had preceded us, spreading like wildfire through the corridors before we even reached the threshold. Upon entering, we found the household servants convulsing in a collective panic, a chaotic tableau of fear. As we approached my lady's chamber, the door flew open with violent urgency from within. My mistress emerged amidst the throng, trailed closely by Mr. Franklin, who strove in vain to compose her fraying composure. She was utterly undone by the sheer horror of the revelation.

"You are answerable for this!" she screamed, brandishing her hand in a wild, accusing gesture toward the Sergeant. "Gabriel! Give that wretch his money—and release me from the sight of him!"

Among the trembling group, the Sergeant alone possessed the fortitude to stand firm, being the sole individual capable of meeting her fury without flinching. "I am no more answerable for this distressing calamity, my lady, than you are," he replied. His tone was wrapped in profound respect yet cut with unyielding steel. "Should you still insist on my departure from this house within half an hour, I shall accept your ladyship's dismissal, but I most certainly shall not accept your ladyship's money."

The words landed with the weight of a gavel strike, affecting both my mistress and myself. She relented, allowing Mr. Franklin to guide her back into the sanctuary of the room. As the door sealed shut behind them, the Sergeant turned his observant gaze upon the cluster of female servants. While the others trembled in mere fright, he noted that Penelope was weeping openly.

"When your father has changed his wet clothes," he instructed her gently, "come and speak to us, in your father's room."

Before the allotted half-hour elapsed, I had donned my dry garments and provided the Sergeant with the fresh attire he required. Penelope entered then, summoned to hear the purpose of his summons. Never before had I felt the depth of my filial devotion so acutely as in that instant. I drew her close, seating her upon my knee, and implored God to bless her. She buried her face in my bosom, wrapping her arms tightly around my neck, and together we waited in a heavy, shared silence. In my heart, I believed the poor dead girl, Rosanna, lay at the root of this tragedy, alongside my daughter and myself.

The Sergeant moved to the window, standing sentinel as he gazed outward. I deemed it fitting to express my gratitude for his consideration of our plight, and I did so. There exists a peculiar luxury reserved for those of high station—the privilege of indulging raw emotion. For those of lower birth, necessity dictates otherwise; compassion is withheld from us. We are taught to suppress our feelings, to internalize our grief, and to march onward with our duties as patiently as possible. I make no complaint of this harsh reality; I merely observe it.

Soon, both Penelope and the Sergeant were prepared. When questioned regarding what motive might have driven her fellow servant to such a fatal act, my daughter answered—as one might predict—that it stemmed from her love for Mr. Franklin Blake. Next, asked if she had confided this suspicion to anyone else, Penelope replied, "I have not mentioned it, for Rosanna's sake."

I felt compelled to interject. "And for Mr. Franklin's sake, my dear, as well," I added softly. "If Rosanna *did* perish for love of him, it was neither with his knowledge nor by his fault. Should he depart this house today, let him do so without the useless agony of bearing the truth."

Sergeant Cuff nodded in agreement, "Quite right," before falling silent again, seemingly weighing Penelope's confession against some private hypothesis of his own.

At the stroke of the half-hour, my mistress's bell rang. En route to answer it, I encountered Mr. Franklin emerging from his aunt's sitting-room. He informed me that her ladyship was ready to receive the Sergeant, in my presence as previously arranged, and added that he desired a brief word with the officer first. Returning to my quarters, he paused to examine the railway timetable hanging in the hall.

"Are you truly intending to leave us, sir?" I asked. "Miss Rachel will surely recover if you simply grant her time?"

"She will come right again," Mr. Franklin answered, his voice devoid of hope, "once she realizes I have departed, and that she will never see me again."

I initially interpreted his words as a reaction to the young lady's recent treatment of him, but my assessment proved mistaken. From the moment the police arrived, my mistress had observed that the mere mention of his name ignited Miss Rachel's temper into a blaze. Too devoted to his cousin to admit this to himself until the truth forced its way upon him, he had retreated to his aunt's residence. Now, with eyes opened to a cruel clarity—a transformation familiar to those who know him—Mr. Franklin had made his resolve. It was the singular decision a man of spirit could make: to withdraw.

What he intended to say to the Sergeant was delivered in my presence. He described her ladyship as acknowledging that she had spoken too hastily. He then asked if the Sergeant would consent, under these circumstances, to accept his fee and leave the matter of the Diamond exactly as it stood.

"No, sir," the Sergeant replied flatly. "My fee is earned for performing my duty. I decline to accept payment until that duty is fully discharged."

"I do not understand you," Mr. Franklin said, perplexed.

"I shall explain, sir," the Sergeant continued. "Upon arriving here, I undertook to shed the necessary light upon the disappearance of the Diamond. I am now ready, and waiting, to fulfill my pledge. Once I have presented the current state of the case to Lady Verinder and clearly outlined the course of action required to recover the Moonstone, the burden of responsibility shall fall entirely upon her shoulders. Only then, after she decides whether to permit me to continue or cease, shall I consider my undertaking complete—and only then shall I claim my fee."

In those precise words, Sergeant Cuff reminded us that even within the ranks of the Detective Police, a man possesses a reputation to safeguard. His stance was undeniably correct, leaving no room for further debate. Rising to escort him to my lady's room, he inquired if Mr. Franklin wished to accompany us.

"Not unless Lady Verinder desires it," Mr. Franklin answered. Then, lowering his voice to a whisper as I followed the Sergeant out, he confessed, "I know what that man is about to say concerning Rachel; and I am too fond of her to listen, lest I lose my temper. Leave me to myself."

I departed, finding him miserably leaning against the window sill, his face concealed in his hands, while Penelope peered through the doorway, yearning to offer solace. Had I been in his position, I would have summoned her immediately. When one is mistreated by a woman, there is immense comfort in voicing the grievance to another, for nine times out of ten, the listener will take your side. Perhaps, while my back was turned, he did call her in? If so, it is only fair to declare that Penelope would stop at nothing to comfort Mr. Franklin Blake.

Meanwhile, Sergeant Cuff and I proceeded to my lady's room. During our previous conferences, we had found her reluctant to raise her eyes from the book resting on the table. This time, however, a marked improvement was evident. She met the Sergeant's gaze with a steadiness that mirrored his own. A fierce family spirit illuminated every line of her face, and I knew that Sergeant Cuff would find his equal when a woman of my mistress's caliber was primed to endure the harshest truths.
