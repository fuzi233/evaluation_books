Romola returned from the hospital later than she had intended, her steps lingering as she took a longer route toward the river, veering away from the Ponte Vecchio and reaching it at a distance. She turned toward the bridge, determined to hurry to San Stefano in search of Baldassarre. Though she dreaded what she might learn, a deep sense of obligation held her—forsaking him would feel like abandoning a bond too close to ignore. But as she neared the crossroads where the Por’ Santa Maria lay to her right and the Ponte Vecchio to her left, she found herself swept into a crowd that suddenly dropped to their knees. Instinctively, Romola knelt with them. The Great Cross of the Duomo was passing—the head of the procession. She had arrived later than expected and now must wait until the solemn train passed by.

When she rose again, the stillness and standing posture made her fatigue more acute. Her limbs ached, and the silence pressed upon her. A shopkeeper beside her spoke gently: “Madonna Romola, you must be weary standing. Gian Fantoni will gladly offer you a seat in his home. His door is just here—let me open it for you. He loves God and the Frate as we do. His house is yours.”

Romola had grown accustomed to such warm familiarity from ordinary citizens whose faces she knew from the Duomo. For her, home no longer meant only the quiet house in the Via de’ Bardi, where loneliness often settled like dust. It was now the city itself—the towers, the winding streets, the people who greeted her not with cold recognition but with eyes full of trust or silent appeal. She welcomed the invitation and stepped through the open doorway, guided by the kind hose-vendor to an upstairs window. There, a stout woman with three children, all dressed plainly in the fashion of the Piagnoni, made room for her with reverence, drawing aside bright draperies to give her a clear view.

From this vantage, Romola could see the procession moving slowly across the Ponte Vecchio, its solemn rhythm echoing between the houses. Beyond, the Arno flowed quietly toward the bridge of Santa Trinita. The air was heavy with silence. No chant broke the hush—only the steady tramp of feet and the soft whisper of woolen robes.

At first, young men passed by—Florentine youths bearing aloft a white image of the youthful Jesus, crowned with golden light, standing beside a tall cross where thorns and nails waited. Then came the Companies of Discipline—men bound by secret vows of penance and devotion. Their garb concealed their heads and faces entirely, leaving only their eyes visible. These were Florentine citizens—merchants, artisans, scholars—now stripped of identity, walking as symbols of shared faith. Each company wore its own color and emblem, yet all were wrapped in shrouds that erased individuality, leaving only unity in purpose.

In contrast, the monks stood out as distinct figures, despite their common tonsures and habits. First came the reformed Benedictines in white, then the long line of Franciscans in grey, their cords knotted at the waist, some barefoot in wooden sandals. They were the most numerous order in Florence, known for their compassion and opposition to the Dominicans. After them followed the Augustinians of San Spirito, their dark robes topped with thoughtful, cultured faces—men who had inherited Boccaccio’s library and formed the most learned community in the city. Then came the Carmelites, their white over dark garments marking their spiritual lineage; and finally, the Servites, black-clad, founded by seven merchants who abandoned wealth to worship the Divine Mother.

Then came the moment that stirred hearts—black mantles over white scapularies, the unmistakable sign of the Dominicans. Those from Fiesole led the way, one after another, their coarse cloaks worn thin in places, their humility evident. The Prior of San Marco had enforced strict poverty among his followers, and their garments bore the mark of that discipline.

Yet amid the plainness, one figure stood apart—a mantle slightly less ragged than the rest, a tonsured head beneath it that, though unremarkable at first glance, carried a weight of presence. To those who knew him, it was Savonarola. His face had been seen on bronze medals bearing the sword of God, or flanked by armed guards on his way to the Duomo, or illuminated with fire when he preached to rapt crowds. Now, as he approached, no one dared break the silence with a cry or shout. Yet the air thrummed—there was a low hiss, half curse, half breath of awe, mingled with murmurs that yearned to be prayers. A few of his disciples fell to their knees as if something sacred passed. That gesture spread like a ripple along the street—some remained still, others bowed deeply. But hatred, too, burned fiercely. As Savonarola walked up the Por’ Santa Maria, Romola saw someone at a high window spit toward him.

Monks followed—Frati Umiliati from Ognissanti, proud of their ancient role in the wool trade; then Vallombrosan and other Benedictines, their habits subtly different, reminders of past reforms that had reshaped the spirit of the Church. Finally, the secular priests, untonsured, walked in quiet dignity.

Next came the twenty-one Arts of Florence, their banners waving proudly—bakers, tailors, judges, notaries—all asserting their place in the city’s life. Then the lesser officers of State, rising in rank, until the line broke with the Canons of the Duomo carrying a sacred relic: the head of San Zenobio, bishop of Florence, encased in silver. He was said to have saved the city centuries before, and now his presence anchored the procession.

Behind the relic came the archbishop, robed in splendor, under a canopy. And then—the hidden Image. It was veiled first by brocade curtains, then by an older, inner tabernacle never opened in living memory. Within it rested the unseen Madonna—found long ago in the soil of L’Impruneta, crying out as the spade struck her. This image had rarely left its shrine without rich gifts preceding it—veils of silk, mantles of gold, offerings from noble families and monasteries. The most famous gift, legend said, had come from a poor abbess and her nuns, who wove a golden brocade mantle with their prayers, embroidered it with devotion, and watched it carried to the Virgin by two winged youths who vanished into the sky.

But today, no gifts preceded the tabernacle. Only the poor were to receive offerings. That was the decree of Fra Girolamo, who preached not for the glory of relics but for the relief of suffering. Altars had been set up outside churches, where alms were collected for the hungry. Not even a torch lit the path. The unseen Mother, it seemed, cared less for finery than for the cries of the starving.

Florence was at her end. She had given all she could. Only divine help remained. Savonarola had promised it would come—and many clung to his words more than to the miracle of the image. Others, hardened by doubt, hoped he would be proven wrong.

Slowly, the tabernacle moved forward. Knees bent. Silence deepened. The procession drew to a close—the Priors and Gonfaloniere awaited their turn. The last of the symbolic trains faded from sight. A faint hope flickered, struggling against despair.

Romola, her heart swelling with both dread and the familiar warmth of communal faith that had become part of her soul, let out a deep sigh. She remained on her knees, drained. Then—suddenly—a flash of bright color burst from between the buildings onto the distant bridge.

She leapt up, arms outstretched, leaning from the window. Her black veil slipped back, revealing the golden gleam of her hair and the flush of sudden emotion on her face. A shout rose instantly. The final ranks of the procession paused. All eyes turned toward the bridge.

But the rider was already past it—galloping swiftly along the Arno. His bay horse, streaked with foam, seemed almost white from speed. His cap flew loose beside his red becchetto, and in his hand he waved an olive branch.

A messenger—bringing good news!

The blessed olive branch spoke from afar. But the people could not wait. They surged forward, grabbing the horse’s reins, pushing and trampling.

Romola saw then that the rider was Tito—her husband—sent to Pisa days earlier on a private mission. Recognition brought no new joy to her eyes. She had already checked her impulse to reach out. Her only thought was the news—what relief had come for Florence?

“Good news!” “Best news!” “News to be paid with hose!” (_novelle da calze_)—Tito answered the crowd’s clamor, barely able to speak until he reached the meeting point where the Gonfaloniere and Priors waited.

He paused, bowed low, and said: “Magnificent Signori! I bring the joyful tidings that the French galleys, laden with corn and men, have arrived safely at Leghorn, thanks to a strong wind that kept the enemy fleet at bay.”

His words echoed down the streets. A great roar erupted, rolling along the river, then returning in waves from the procession toward the Duomo. Shouts multiplied, spreading like ripples across a lake stirred by one force. For minutes, no one spoke. The Signoria lifted their caps, standing bare-headed before a rescue that came not from their own power, but from beyond—from the realm of faith and divine grace.

At last, as the signal to move forward was given, Tito smiled and added: “I should say, any reward of hose offered by the Magnificent Signoria should go not to me, but to another man—Meo di Sasso—who rode hard to bring these tidings. His horse broke down just before Signa. He’ll arrive within the hour, and rightly deserves the honor, having borne the greatest labor and missed the greatest joy.”

It was a graceful acknowledgment. After a brief reply from the _Proposto_, the procession continued. Tito turned his horse and followed. The great bell of the Palazzo Vecchio began to toll, amplifying the people’s joy.

Romola, instead of seeking recognition, pulled her veil back over her head and stayed still. Yet she felt certain Tito had seen her. He had that look—seeing everything without seeming to see anything at all.
