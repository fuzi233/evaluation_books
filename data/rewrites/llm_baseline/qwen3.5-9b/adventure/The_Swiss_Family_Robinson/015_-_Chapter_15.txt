Finally, the day arrived for Fritz to test his new vessel, the *cajack*. Dressed in a complete swimming outfit—trousers, jacket, and cap—it was undeniably comical to behold him cowering inside the narrow canoe, puffing and blowing until he resembled nothing so much as the frog in the ancient fable. All traces of his dignified stature vanished instantly, replaced by a shapeless blob of fabric and limbs. Shouts of laughter erupted from the family, though even his mother, who had invented the costume herself, could not suppress a smile at his ridiculous appearance.

I retrieved the larger boat to ensure my wife and I were ready to launch to his aid should disaster strike. The *cajack* was lowered from a convenient slope onto the emerald mirror of the ocean. Fritz immediately began practicing maneuvers: darting forward with arrow-like speed, wheeling sharply left and right, and finally lying flat on his side. His mother screamed in mock terror, but the tiny craft proved stable, neither capsizing nor sinking. Recovering his balance, he paddled confidently onward.

Encouraged by our cheers, Fritz boldly ventured into the swift current of Jackal River, which carried him rapidly out toward the open sea. This exceeded my expectations. Without hesitation, I gave chase in the larger boat, accompanied by Ernest and Jack. My wife urged us to greater speed, declaring that "that horrid soap bubble" was certain to meet with an accident.

Soon we reached the outer bay, scanning the waters near the site of the former shipwreck for any sign of the runaway. After a moment, we spotted a faint puff of smoke, followed by the report of a pistol. We fired a signal shot, which was promptly answered. Steering toward the sound, we soon heard the boy's cheerful hail. The *cajack* darted from behind a headland, and we quickly reunited.

"Come to this rocky beach!" cried Fritz. "I have something to show you."

With expressions of blank amazement, we beheld a magnificent, well-grown young walrus, harpooned and lifeless.

"Did you kill this creature, my dear Fritz?" I exclaimed, glancing anxiously around, half-expecting a naked savage to emerge and claim the prize.

"To be sure, Father! Do you not see my harpoon? Why do you doubt it?"

"Well, I scarcely know," I replied, laughing. "Success so speedy, so unexpected, and so fitting for an amateur Greenlandic hunter took me by surprise. I congratulate you, my boy! But I must warn you that you have alarmed us by venturing so far. You should not have left the bay. I left your mother in grave distress."

"Indeed, Father, I had no intention of leaving sight of the island," Fritz explained. "Once caught in the current, I was carried along and could not stop. Then I encountered a herd of walruses. I longed so desperately to secure a prize that I forgot everything else. I chased them beyond the current's pull until I was close enough to harpoon this fine fellow. He swam more slowly, so I struck him a second time. He retreated among these rocks and expired. I landed and approached him, but I took care to fire my pistol at him first, remembering the terrible final strike of the giant serpent against you, Jack."

"You ran a very great risk," I said. "Walruses are generally harmless, but when wounded and cornered, they become furious. With their long tusks, they can easily destroy a sturdy whaleboat. However, thank God for your safety! I value that above a thousand such creatures. Now, what shall we do with him? He must be nearly fourteen feet long, though not yet fully grown."

"I am very glad you followed me, Father," Fritz said. "But our combined strength will not move this prodigious weight from among the rocks. Please, let me carry away the head with those magnificent snow-white tusks! I would love to mount it on the prow of the *cajack* and name it the 'Sea-Horse'."

"We must certainly remove the beautiful ivory tusks," I agreed. "But hurry! The air feels oppressively hot and sultry; I fear a storm is brewing."

"But the head! The head! We must have the whole head!" cried Jack. "Think how splendid it will look on the *cajack*!"

"And how splendid it will smell when it begins to rot," added Ernest grimly. "What a treat for the helmsman!"

"Oh, we shall prepare for that," Fritz assured him. "It will be soaked, cleaned, and dried until it is as hard as a wooden model. It shall not offend your delicate nose in the least, Ernest!"

"I had supposed the walrus to be an animal peculiar to the Arctic regions," remarked Ernest.

"And so it is," I replied. "Though they may occasionally appear elsewhere; these likely wandered from the Antarctic seas. I know that on the eastern coast of Africa exists a smaller species called the dugong. It possesses long incisor teeth but lacks true tusks, resembling a seal more than a walrus."

While discussing this, we busied ourselves with decapitating the walrus and cutting long strips of its skin. The task took considerable time due to our lack of proper tools. Fritz noted that in the future, the *cajack* required a hunting knife and a hatchet. Furthermore, he requested a small compass housed in a glass-topped box, mounted in front of the helmsman's seat. Seeing the necessity, I promised to install it.

Once finished, I proposed loading Fritz and the canoe aboard our boat so we could all return together. However, I relented to his earnest wish to return alone as he had come. He longed to serve as our vanguard, announcing our approach to his mother. Thus, he skimmed away over the water while we trailed behind at a slower pace.

Dark clouds gathered thick and fast overhead, and a tremendous storm descended. Fritz was already out of sight and beyond our reach. We strapped on our swimming belts and lashed ourselves securely to the boat to avoid being washed overboard by the towering seas crashing against us.

The horizon was swallowed by darkness. Violent gusts whipped the ocean into white foam, rain poured in torrents, and livid lightning flashed across the gloom. Both my boys faced the danger with noble courage. My alarm was tempered by pride as I observed how well the boat handled the chaos.

The tempest raged on, yet the sky cleared as suddenly as it had darkened. Nevertheless, the stormy waves continued to threaten our fragile vessel for a long time, despite its buoyancy and steadiness.

I never lost hope for ourselves; all my fears were concentrated on Fritz. In truth, I had given him up for lost, and my agonized heart rose in prayer for the strength to accept whatever fate awaited: "Thy will be done!"

Eventually, we rounded the point and entered Safety Bay once more, drawing near the little harbor.

What was our surprise—and our overwhelming delight—to find Mother, Fritz, and his little boy kneeling in prayer so earnestly for our deliverance that they did not perceive our approach until our joyful cries attracted their attention.

Then ensued a truly happy reunion, and we gave thanks together for the mercy that had spared our lives.

Returning joyfully to Rockburg, we exchanged our drenched garments for warm, dry clothes. Seated at a comfortable meal, we recounted and described the perils of the storm at our leisure.

Later, the walrus head was transported to our workshop. There, it underwent a skilled and thorough process of cleaning, embalming, and drying. Soon, it was fixed upon the prow of the *cajack*, presenting a most imposing appearance! The strips of hide, once well-tanned and prepared, yielded valuable leather.

The recent storm had caused significant damage. Heavy rains had flooded the streams and damaged crops that should have been harvested before the regular rainy season. The bridge over Jackal River was partially collapsed, and the water tanks and pipes required urgent repair. Our time was largely consumed restoring order.

One day, working near the cascade, we discovered a scattering of dark-red berries on the ground. About the size of hazelnuts, they featured small leafy crowns at the tips. The boys, finding them inviting, tasted them immediately. Angry exclamations, spitting, and spluttering followed the experiment—even Knips rejected them. They would have been discarded in contempt had not the scent prompted me to examine them. I identified them as the fruit of the clove plant.

Plants were immediately planted in the nursery garden. My wife was delighted to possess this excellent spice to flavor her boiled rice and other dishes, serving as a welcome substitute for pepper.

Possessing a good supply of clay excavated from the bed near Falconhurst, I proposed using it to construct aqueducts. Observing how the recent rains had stimulated the growth of our young corn, I decided to irrigate the fields using drainage water from our crushing mill.

The fishing season proved successful once again. Large catches of salmon, sturgeon, and herring rewarded our annual efforts, and our storerooms were replenished. Although I wished for a constant fresh supply, I was obliged to reject Jack's naive suggestion to tether a shoal of salmon by the gills to the bottom of the bay, as we had previously done with turtles.
