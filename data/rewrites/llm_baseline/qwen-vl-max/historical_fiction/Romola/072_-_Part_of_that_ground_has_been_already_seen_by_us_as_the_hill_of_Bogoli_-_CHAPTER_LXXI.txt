When Romola returned home with Tessa and the children, April was drawing to its close, and another great anxiety had reached its peak—the publication of *Fra Girolamo’s Trial*, or rather, the confessions extracted from him by sixteen Florentine citizens appointed to interrogate him. Issued under the authority of the Signoria, this document stirred such widespread suspicion and unrest that it was swiftly ordered withdrawn. Yet copies had already slipped into circulation, and soon a second edition—this one unauthorized—found eager readers across the city.

Romola, who had begun to despair of ever speaking with Fra Girolamo again, read the confession over and over, seeking clarity amid the chaos of conflicting claims. She wanted not merely to believe, but to understand—to pierce through the noise of partisan outrage and cynical dismissal and find truth where it might lie.

Among Savonarola’s most devoted followers, his failure to hold firm under torture, and his retraction of prophetic claims, sparked a deep, almost unbearable grief. It seemed impossible that the man they had known as a prophet could have broken so completely. But then came the growing belief—soon confirmed—that many of the statements attributed to him were not his own words at all, but fabrications penned by Ser Ceccone, the notary of ill repute who compiled the record. If the Frate had denied divine inspiration, perhaps it was only because the agony of torture had forced the denial from him—not because he truly renounced it.

And indeed, there were troubling facts about the examination itself. Half the panel of sixteen examiners were known enemies of Savonarola. Among them was Dolfo Spini, a member of the newly elected Eight—a body chosen in haste, clearly designed to ensure a predetermined outcome. This was no trial; it was a political execution masked in legal form. The Old Palace had become a stage for slow, ceremonial murder.

The Signoria, eager to secure favorable terms with the Pope and the Duke of Milan, saw Savonarula as an obstacle—too dangerous to leave alive. He threatened corrupt clergy, greedy princes, and self-serving citizens alike. His influence undermined their power. So the decision was made: either hand him over to Rome, or execute him publicly in Florence, thereby asserting the Republic’s dignity. Which path would be chosen? That was the only question left.

Under such circumstances, how could anyone trust the confession? If he had denied prophecy, it was not by choice, but by pain—by the crushing weight of torment inflicted on a soul too sensitive, too finely tuned, to endure long without breaking. And yet, the official account claimed he had suffered only three sessions of the rope and pulley, all on a single day, and that his admissions were made freely, without coercion. Who could believe that?

Others knew better. They remembered the anguish of those who loved him—the way his confessions had caused such sorrow among his followers. They knew he had been tortured far more severely, repeatedly, until his spirit cracked under pressure. Still, some less fervent allies believed the core of the confession was genuine, even if shaped and distorted by the notary’s pen. But they insisted: no crime had been proven. No act of treason, no corruption, no betrayal of faith. Nothing that warranted death.

His fellow monks—men who had lived beside him, watched his conduct, shared his silence—spoke of a life unblemished. One, once his secretary for years and naturally inclined to critique, still affirmed his integrity. Even after the shock of the retraction, he could not deny the purity of Savonarola’s character, the consistency of his virtue. The Pope himself had failed to charge him with heresy beyond disobedience to papal mandates and defiance of excommunication. That breach of discipline was hard to justify, yet many men of conscience felt a moral resistance toward Rome’s authority. They began to see the Church not as a monolithic institution, but as a flawed human enterprise. Disobedience, in this case, did not seem so monstrous.

Men like Soderini and Piero Guicciardini, sharp observers of politics, may have smiled bitterly at the spectacle. Here was a man condemned not for lawlessness, but for being too honest, too zealous—too inconvenient. Had Savonarola been less pure, more cunning, his ambitions might have been welcomed as useful tools by powerful men. But because he spoke truth with fire, he was deemed a threat.

Still, these shrewd minds admitted: though the Republic's actions were clumsy and unjust, the Pope’s response was not entirely without reason. To allow a man who called for a General Council to depose him to remain free was to invite chaos. Savonarola was not just a rebel—he was a dangerous one. Florence had heard him say plainly: *I will not obey the devil.* There could be no peace between such a man and the Papacy.

Yet why must Florence be the instrument of his destruction? Why must the city become the executioner of a man whose vision had once lifted it?

Romola listened to these voices—the faithful, the skeptical, the worldly—and found herself torn. She could not accept either side fully. Her own heart had known both devotion and disillusionment. Over the years, she had observed things—small betrayals, subtle shifts, moments of pride beneath humility—that whispered a deeper truth: perhaps Savonarola’s retraction was not merely a reflex of torture, but something more complex, more tragic.

And yet, she refused to let go of the man she had once revered. She clung to the idea that, despite his flaws, his life had been driven by noble purpose. In the depths of her despair, a new kind of faith emerged—one born not of certainty, but of love. It was impossible, she now believed, that the light she had seen in him was false. That voice, which once stirred her soul, could not have been mere pretense. Any falsehood in him had been a fall, not a design—an entanglement, not a scheme.

She studied the printed confessions closely. Many passages struck her as crude fabrications—sentences marked by awkward repetition, heavy self-accusation, the kind used only by the most shallow hypocrites. But these very distortions made her more certain that the rest of the text reflected what had truly been spoken. For they contradicted not only Savonarola’s nature, but the overall tone of the confession. Only one aspect dishonored him: his claim to divine inspiration. Elsewhere, he remained steadfast in his intentions for Florence, for the Church, for the world.

He confessed nothing about using base means. He admitted no personal corruption. Even in this document, marred by the notary’s venom, Fra Girolamo appeared as a man who sought greatness—but not for himself alone. He desired fame, yes, but only as a means to achieve something greater: the moral renewal of humanity. He labored not with empty sermons, but with concrete efforts to transform belief into action.

One passage stood out:

> “Everything I have done, I have done with the intention of being famous in the present and future ages, of winning credit in Florence, and ensuring that nothing of great importance should be done without my sanction. Once established here, I intended to do great things in Italy and beyond, through the chief personages with whom I had formed friendships and discussed high matters—such as the calling of a General Council. As my efforts succeeded, I would have taken further steps. Above all, when the Council was convened, I meant to rouse the princes of Christendom, especially those outside Italy, to subdue the infidels. It was not much in my thoughts to become Cardinal or Pope. When I achieved my goal, I would, without being Pope, be the first man in the world in authority and reverence. If I had been made Pope, I would not have refused—but I believed that leading such a work was greater than being Pope, because a man without virtue can be Pope, but *a work like this demands a man of excellent virtues*.”

This mingling of ambition and moral idealism was familiar to Romola. She had heard it before in the Duomo, echoing through the pews. Savonarola believed in grand visions, and he believed he was the one to fulfill them. Evil must fall; justice, purity, love must prevail—and they would prevail through him, by his voice, by his blood.

In moments of ecstasy, his sense of self dissolved into the divine. Then, perhaps, he experienced true humility. But in the world, before others, preeminence was essential. It was not arrogance—it was necessity.

And perhaps the confession, even when it described a divided mind, revealed not deliberate deceit, but the natural wavering of a soul under immense pressure. How could one remain unchanged when the adoring crowd turned to scorn, when friends became judges, when torture tore at the nerves and the mind screamed, *“Yes, yes, I am guilty—let me go!”*?

Romola imagined the horror that followed—whether in solitary confinement, his conscience recoiled or reaffirmed the words he had spoken. That anguish pressed upon her own heart, forcing slow, bitter tears.

Every common man in Florence was quick to judge him, to condemn him. But *he* knew sorrow no one else could know—the sorrow of one who loved the highest good, who pursued it with every fiber, and who now saw himself fallen.

She had not yet seen—would not see until later—the evidence of his inner state after the confession. As days passed, reports of new examinations ceased. Savonarola was left alone in prison, granted pen and ink. He wrote—not to defend himself, not to protest—but to speak to God.

His writings were not pleas for vindication. They were a conversation with divine purity, a cry for spiritual rebirth. No trace of old defiance remained. No boastful assertion: *Look at my work!* Instead, a voice of profound sorrow:

> “God placed thee in the midst of the people as if thou hadst been one of the excellent. Thou hast taught others, but hast failed to learn thyself. Thou hast cured others, but thyself art still diseased. Thy heart was lifted up by the beauty of thy deeds, and through this thou hast lost thy wisdom and art become, and shalt be to all eternity, nothing... After so many benefits with which God has honored thee, thou art fallen into the depths of the sea; and after so many gifts bestowed on thee, thou, by thy pride and vainglory, hast scandalized all the world.”

And when Hope speaks, it does not promise glory. It says only:
> “Thou art not forsaken, else why is thy heart bowed in penitence? That too is a gift.”

There is no evidence that, from imprisonment to the final hour, Savonarola ever called himself a martyr. The idea of martyrdom had always been tied to triumph—to seeing his vision fulfilled. Now, that dream was gone. In its place was a quiet resignation, unnamed, unglorified.

Yet precisely because he endured not for honor, but for truth—because he faced power not for sin, but for greatness—because he sought not to deceive, but to elevate—*he may rightly be called a martyr by all who come after.*

For he suffered a double agony: not only the insults, the torture, the death—but the collapse of his own vision. From the heights of divine purpose, he fell into darkness. And in that darkness, he whispered:
> “I count as nothing. Darkness encompasses me. Yet the light I saw was the true light.”
