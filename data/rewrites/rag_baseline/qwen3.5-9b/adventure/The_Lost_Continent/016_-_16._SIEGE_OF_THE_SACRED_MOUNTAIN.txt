The crossing of the continent was a blur of danger and fatigue, a narrative I do not feel compelled to recount in full. There was only one stop of consequence, dictated by necessity rather than design. I had stalked a stag through the open savannah, a long pursuit that ended in silence. I checked the wind, confirming the absence of predators, and began the labor of butchery, bundling the meat for transport.

Then, the grass yielded something unexpected. A great-legged worm, a monstrous green entity, coiled beneath my foot. Its bite was venomous, swift as lightning. As I shifted, my heel brushed it; it struck, its tiny head driving fangs deep into my lower thigh. I drew my knife, severing the creature's neck. It thrashed, twisting its hundred legs in convulsions before dying. I cleaned the blade against the earth and drove it deep around the wound, forcing the blood to flush the toxin from the flesh. With the crimson fluid trickling down my leg, I hoisted the meat and continued, grateful to escape what could have been a gruesome end.

Pain followed. Within hours, a tightness seized my leg, escalating into a throbbing rhythm until the skin stretched taut like parchment. Fever racked my frame, rendering me vulnerable to any predator. As dusk fell, I hauled myself into the crook of a tree, enduring the night in wakefulness and agony.

At dawn, the nocturnal beasts retreated to their dens. Clambering down, I leaned heavily on my spear, limping through the shadowed forest. Moss on the north side of trees guided my path, but soon I noticed moss encircling every trunk. The pain and swelling clouded my mind, yet I pressed on, driven by the final command of the High Council: secure the enlargement of Nais.

My last conscious memory was of a figure in the black forest offering aid, after which my senses dissolved into oblivion.

Consciousness returned slowly. I lay on a bed of leaves in a rock cleft, reinforced with stakes against marauders. My wound was bound with herbal poultices. Across the small cavern, a woman cooked a stew of wood-grubs and honey over a stick-fire.

"How did I arrive here?" I asked.

"I brought you," she replied.

"And who are you?"

"A nymph," she said. "I gather herbs, heal the sick, read fortunes, and predict futures. Payment is whatever one can afford; if not, curses ensure withering. Wars and pestilence have left few poor souls, making this a lean existence."

"Do you travel to Atlantis?"

"Not I. Phorenice would boil me in brine if she caught me. Our Empress tolerates no magic save her own. Rumor says she intends to tear the Priests from their mountain."

"Does she still rule?"

"Vilely."

"As a maiden?"

"As the mother of twin sons. Tatho is her husband now, for three years."

"Tatho! The viceroy of Yucatan?"

"There is no Yucatan," she corrected. "A swarm of little hairy men from the West overran us. Armed only with stone-tipped clubs, their number was their weapon. They desired no plunder, no slaves, only the flesh of Atlanteans. They consumed each city in turn, leaving it to the forest beasts, moving on until they reached the coast. Tatho and the survivors fled by sea. They even ate Tatho's wife. They are persistent things. Perhaps the Gods prevented them from crossing to Atlantis, lest they bring a plague worse than Phorenice."

I recalled tales of these creatures, beings straddling the line between man and beast, held in check by the Gods, unleashed only as devastating armies. Yet, I said nothing to the nymph, a simple woman easily gulled by her own pretensions. Grief gnawed at me for the lost colony of Yucatan, where I had poured infinite effort into construction.

"It does not suit my purpose to reveal my name or station prematurely," I decided, steering the conversation elsewhere. "What of Egypt?"

"In its customary darkness," she said. "Who cares for Egypt now? Or anything, really, except oneself and one's estate? Once, villagers brought offerings out of piety. Now, they only approach seeking profit. Consequently, I eat lean meals of honey and grubs. It is a poor life for a nymph in these times. Mystery is out of fashion."

"What pestilence did you mention?"

"I have not seen it. Thank the Gods it has not reached us. It grows from the bodies Phorenice leaves unburied. She kills constantly, and the corpses pile higher than birds and beasts can consume them. For what sin did the Gods allow Phorenice to reign? I wish she and her twins were boiled in brine before they obstructed an honest nymph."

"She has placed images of herself in all the temples," she continued. "Prayers and sacrifices are demanded day and night. All other gods are declared inferior. Those insufficiently devout have their hamstrings slit by tormentors to force devotion. Will you eat? There is nothing else. Your back was bloody from carrying meat, but you lost your load. Eat now, or go hungry."

I stayed with the nymph for six days. Her charms healed my leg. Upon recovery, I hunted a fat horse on the plains as payment and resumed my journey.

Beyond this point, the land bore the marks of a sturdy population that had expanded outward, building homes in trees to avoid cave bears and tigers. Some were sturdier, constructed of massive, dovetailed logs impervious to beasts, allowing defenders to shoot through gaps. Yet, fewer than one in ten of these settlements remained. Fires were cold, hearths overgrown, vegetable patches reclaimed by scrub. Villages, once warded against beasts, lay silent; gates rotted in undergrowth, and only kitchen middens testified to past habitation. Phorenice's influence spread like a blight over a once-smiling land.

Men had fled or stood defiant but non-aggressive, sensing my lack of initial hostility. A few offered shelter. Near the city, however, paths grew muddy and crowded. Watchers in towers spotted me and shouted, "Masterless man!" People rushed forth to strip my weapons and conscript me as labor.

I felt no desire to slaughter these wretched people, pity filling me for their decline. Often, words sufficed to make them step aside, staring in awe at my ferocity before letting me pass. When words failed, I struck lightly, aiming only to move forward without leaving unnecessary dead. Finding the modern way of these villages intolerable, I adopted a custom: turning into the forest to circle wide whenever I smelled their refuse.

Approaching greater towns, I kept outside their walls, unwilling to risk delay from arbitrary laws. My progress was slow, slinking, but my pride did not reproach me. The fever of haste burned hotter within me, leaving little room for other emotions.

Finally, I saw the city of Atlantis, a half-day's journey away. The Sacred Mountain loomed high, crowned with a ring of fires. Caution became paramount. Evidence everywhere suggested the countryside had been drained of fighters. Women prayed for the priests' defeat or Phorenice's death, hoping for peace.

An army besieged the Sacred Mountain, guarding its single approach with narrow vigilance. After such a journey, I feared I would sit helpless, unable to fulfill the High Council's orders or claim the promised reward. Force was useless here. A single man against an army would end my utility. Guile was required.

I found cover in the outskirts of a wood, lying there, hammering my brain for a plan. Before me lay the grim walls of the city, with temples, palaces, and pyramids visible beyond. The step-sides of the royal pyramid caught my eye; Phorenice had overlaid their white stone with sheets of shining gold. But it was not the gold that moved me. I remembered the granite throne in the square below, carved with snakes and an outstretched hand. Beneath it slept Nais, my love, drugged and unconscious for eight years, yet alive, waiting for me. If I could reach the place where Zaemon defied the Empress, I could announce my arrival.

For a day and a night, I raged in the covert, desperate for a solution. Then, in the following morning, a man ran toward me.

"You need not threaten me with your weapons," he cried. "I mean no harm. You are Deucalion, though I should not recognize you in these rags and tangled hair. Give me your word; I have not loitered."

He was a lower priest I knew, held in low esteem. His name was Ro, a greedy soul unworthy of much trust.

"From whom do you come?" I asked.

"Zaemon commanded me. He came to my house, though I cannot explain how, for Phorenice's army blocks all passage. I told him I wished no part of his schemes. I am a peaceful man, Deucalion, with a wife requiring nourishment. I still serve the temple, though we have swept out the old gods and replaced them with the Empress's image. The remaining people are tidily pious, and life is easy. Offerings abound now. I had no wish to jeopardize my snug post or my head."

"I can believe all of that, Ro."

"But Zaemon could not be denied. He burst into a black fury. While he spoke, I felt as good as dead. Do you know his powers?"
