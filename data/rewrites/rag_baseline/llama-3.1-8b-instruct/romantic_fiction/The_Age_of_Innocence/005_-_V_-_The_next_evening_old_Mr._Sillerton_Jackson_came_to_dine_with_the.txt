The afternoon light filtering through the windows of the Archer residence cast a pale glow over the carefully arranged rooms. In the dining room, the scent of cooking wafted from the kitchen, mingling with the faint tang of dust and old books. Mrs. Archer sat poised at the head of the table, her slender fingers drumming a gentle rhythm on the polished wood. Beside her, her daughter Janey fidgeted with the delicate lace trim on her dress, her eyes fixed on the clock ticking away on the mantle.

Old Mr. Sillerton Jackson arrived punctually, his worn top hat tucked under his arm. As he settled into his chair, Mrs. Archer greeted him with a reserved smile, her eyes sparkling with a hint of curiosity. She valued Mr. Jackson's insights into the social world beyond her own narrow sphere, and tonight, she had invited him specifically to discuss the latest news from the Mingott household. His sister, Miss Sophy, remained at home, collecting fragments of gossip and anecdotes to share with her brother, who, in turn, would bring them to the Archer residence to be woven into the rich tapestry of their lives.

As the meal progressed, the conversation turned to the world of letters, and Mrs. Archer's opinion on the merits of various authors. She and her daughter were devoted readers, devouring novels about society and its intricacies, though they preferred the descriptions of landscapes and the more sentimental aspects of life. They spoke little of the likes of Dickens, whose characters struck them as coarse and unsophisticated, and dismissed Thackeray as less at home among the aristocracy than Bulwer, whose works were beginning to seem dated. The conversation flowed smoothly, a gentle stream of observations and opinions, each one carefully weighed and measured.

As the evening wore on, the topic shifted to their shared love of travel and scenery. Mrs. Archer and her daughter spoke of their trips abroad, where they marveled at the grandeur of architecture and the beauty of the natural world. They considered these subjects the domain of men and scholars, who pored over the writings of Ruskin and other learned authorities. The two women were content to appreciate the scenery from a distance, their own tastes and interests focused on the more refined pursuits of art and literature.
