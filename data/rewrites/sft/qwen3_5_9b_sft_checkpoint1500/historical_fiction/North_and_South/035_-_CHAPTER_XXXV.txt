"There is naught so finely spun
But it cometh to the sun."

Mr. Thornton sat on and on. He perceived that his company afforded genuine solace to Mr. Hale, and he was moved by the half-formed, persistent plea that he stay a little longer—the soft, earnest "Do not go yet" which his friend uttered repeatedly. He wondered why Margaret had not returned, yet he lingered not with any hope of seeing her. In the presence of a man who so acutely felt the vanity of earthly things, Mr. Thornton was reasonable and self-commanded. He listened with deep interest to everything his father-in-law said regarding death, the heavy slumber, and the dulled brain.

It was remarkable how Mr. Thornton's presence unlocked secrets within Mr. Hale that remained barred even from Margaret. Perhaps because she would sympathize so keenly, displaying emotions so vivid, Hale feared the reaction upon himself; perhaps because his speculative nature generated endless doubts at such a solemn time, pleading to be resolved into certainty, knowing she would shrink from expressing them—or from hearing him deemed capable of conceiving them. Whatever the cause, Mr. Thornton became a confessor to whom he could unburden the frozen thoughts and terrors of his mind better than anyone else. Mr. Thornton spoke little, yet every sentence he uttered fortified Mr. Hale's reliance and affection. Did Hale pause in expressing some remembered agony? Two or three words from Mr. Thornton completed the sentence, revealing how deeply its meaning resonated. Was it a doubt, a fear, a wandering uncertainty seeking rest but finding none, its eyes blinded by tears? Instead of shock, Mr. Thornton appeared to have traversed that very stage of thought himself, able to suggest precisely where the ray of light lay to illuminate the dark places. Though a man of action, busy in the world's great struggle, there bound Mr. Thornton to God a deeper religion in his heart, persisting through all his mistakes and strong wilfulness, than Mr. Hale had ever imagined. They never spoke of such matters again, as fate decreed, but this single conversation rendered them peculiar souls to one another, knitting them together in a bond that loose, indiscriminate discourse on sacred things could never forge.

Throughout this time, Margaret lay as still and white as death upon the study floor. She had succumbed under her burden. It had been heavy in weight and long carried; she had been meek and patient until, all at once, her faith faltered, and she groped in vain for aid. A pitiful contraction of suffering marred her beautiful brow, though no other sign of consciousness remained. Her mouth, which had recently projected sullenly in defiance, was now relaxed and livid.

*"As if from her lips moves a spirit gentle and full of love,
Who goes saying to the soul: 'Sigh'!"*

The first sign of returning life was a quivering around the lips—a silent, mute attempt at speech—but the eyes remained closed, and the quivering sank back into stillness. Then, leaning feebly on her arms for a moment to steady herself, Margaret gathered her strength and rose. Her comb had fallen from her hair. With an intuitive desire to erase the traces of weakness and restore order, she sought for it, occasionally sitting down to regain strength during the search. Her head drooped forward, her hands meekly laid one upon the other. She endeavored to recall the force of her temptation by trying to remember the details that had thrown her into such terror, but she could not. She grasped only two facts: that Frederick had been in danger of being pursued and detected in London, not merely for manslaughter, but as the unpardonable leader of the mutiny, and that she had lied to save him. Yet there was comfort: her lie had saved him, at least by gaining time. If the inspector returned tomorrow, after she received the letter she longed for assuring her of her brother's safety, she would brave shame and stand in bitter penance—"the lofty Margaret"—acknowledging before a crowded justice hall, if need be, that she had been "like a dog, and done this thing." But if he returned before she heard from Frederick, if he came back as he had half-threatened, within a few hours! She would tell the lie again. Though she did not know how the words would emerge, after such a terrible pause for reflection and self-reproach, without betraying her falsehood, she knew it would gain time for Frederick.

She was roused by Dixon's entrance into the room; she had just been letting Mr. Thornton out.

He had hardly taken ten steps into the street before a passing omnibus stopped close by, and a man alighted, approaching him and touching his hat. It was the police inspector.

Mr. Thornton had secured him his first position in the police and had heard from time to time of his protégé's progress, but they had seldom met, and initially, Mr. Thornton did not recognize him.

"My name is Watson—George Watson, sir, whom you employed..."

"Aye, yes! Why, you are getting on famously, I hear."

"Yes, sir. I ought to thank you, sir. But there is a little matter of business for which I made bold to approach you. I believe you were the magistrate who attended to take down the deposition of a poor man who died in the Infirmary last night."

"Yes," replied Mr. Thornton. "I went and heard some sort of a rambling statement, which the clerk said was of no great use. I'm afraid he was but a drunken fellow, though there is no doubt he came to his death by violence at last. One of my mother's servants was engaged to him, I believe, and she is in great distress today. What about him?"

"Why, sir, his death is oddly connected with someone in the house I saw you leaving just now; it was Mr. Hales', I believe."

"Yes!" said Mr. Thornton, turning sharply and looking into the inspector's face with sudden interest. "What about it?"

"Well, sir, it appears to me that I have acquired a fairly distinct chain of evidence incriminating a gentleman who walked with Miss Hale that night at Outwood Station as the man who struck or pushed Leonard off the platform, thereby causing his death. But the young lady denies that she was there at the time."

"Miss Hale denies she was there!" repeated Mr. Thornton in an altered voice. "Tell me, what evening was it? What time?"

"About six o'clock, on the evening of Thursday, the twenty-sixth."

They walked on, side by side, in silence for a minute or two. The inspector broke the quiet first.

"You see, sir, there is likely to be a coroner's inquest; and I have a young man who is pretty positive—at least, he was at first; since he has heard of the young lady's denial, he says he should not like to swear; but still, he is pretty positive that he saw Miss Hale at the station, walking about with a gentleman, not five minutes before the time when one of the porters saw a scuffle, which he attributed to some of Leonard's impudence—but which led to the fall that caused his death. And seeing you emerge from the very house, sir, I thought I might make bold to ask if—you see, it is always awkward dealing with cases of disputed identity, and one does not like to doubt the word of a respectable young woman unless one has strong proof to the contrary."

"And she denied having been at the station that evening!" repeated Mr. Thornton, in a low, brooding tone.

"Yes, sir, twice over, as distinct as could be. I told her I should call again, but seeing you just as I was on my way back from questioning the young man who claimed it was her, I thought I would ask your advice, both as the magistrate who saw Leonard on his death-bed, and as the gentleman who got me my berth in the force."

"You were quite right," said Mr. Thornton. "Take no steps until you have seen me again."

"The young lady will expect me to call, from what I said."

"I only wish to delay you an hour. It is now three. Come to my warehouse at four."

"Very well, sir!"

And they parted. Mr. Thornton hurried to his warehouse, sternly forbidding his clerks to allow anyone to interrupt him, and went to his private room, locking the door. Then he subjected himself to the torture of thinking it all over, realizing every detail. How could he have lulled himself into the unsuspicious calm in which her tearful image had mirrored itself not two hours prior, until he had weakly pitied her and yearned toward her, forgetting the savage, distrustful jealousy with which the sight of her—and unknown to him—at such an hour, in such a place, had inspired him! How could one so pure stoop from her decorous and noble bearing! But was it decorous? Was it? He hated himself for the idea that forced itself upon him, just for an instant—no more—and yet, while it was present, thrilled him with its old potency of attraction toward her image. And then this falsehood—how terrible must be some dread of shame to be revealed? For, after all, the provocation given by a man like Leonard, when excited by drink, might, in all probability, be more than enough to justify anyone who came forward to state the circumstances openly and without reserve! How creeping and deadly that fear which could bow down the truthful Margaret to falsehood! He could almost pity her.
