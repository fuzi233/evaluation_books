Section 22. How I Then Tried to Diffuse the Theory of Three Dimensions by Other Means, and of the Result

My failure with my grandson left me disheartened but not defeated. I realized that relying solely on the phrase "Upward, not Northward" was insufficient—too abstract, too easily dismissed as nonsense. To truly spread the truth, I needed something more tangible: a demonstration, a clear exposition that could be understood even by those bound by the rigid laws of Flatland. And so, in solitude and silence, I devoted months to writing a treatise—a work I titled *Through Flatland to Thoughtland*—in which I sought to reveal the mysteries of the Third Dimension without directly defying the Law.

To avoid arrest or immediate condemnation, I did not speak of physical space or real dimensions. Instead, I constructed a metaphorical realm I called *Thoughtland*, a place of pure imagination where figures might look down upon our world and see through its surfaces, perceiving interiors at once. There, I suggested, one might imagine a being bounded by six squares and crowned with eight points—something beyond our experience, yet not entirely impossible to grasp. It was a delicate dance: conveying the essence of three-dimensional reality while cloaking it in philosophical disguise.

But the limitations of Flatland made my task nearly impossible. In our world, diagrams are drawn only in lines—straight, unbroken, confined to a single dimension. We cannot represent depth; we cannot show perspective. My treatise, though carefully reasoned, was littered with descriptions that could not be visualized. Words alone were not enough. Without the aid of true diagrams, I feared few would comprehend what I meant, or worse, they would dismiss it as madness.

And so, despite my efforts, I remained isolated. Life lost its color. Every sight now carried a secret burden—the knowledge of what lay beneath the surface, unseen by others. The flatness of buildings, the edges of men, the empty spaces between objects—all reminded me of the deeper truths I could not share. I neglected my duties, withdrew from society, and spent hours trying to reconstruct the vision of the Cube in my mind’s eye. Once, after eleven months of exile from Spaceland, I closed my eyes and tried to summon it again. But it slipped away—faint, distorted, uncertain. Though I eventually regained the image, I never felt certain it was the same as before. That doubt became a weight heavier than any prison wall.

Still, I could not remain silent. My spirit burned with conviction. Even when I knew danger surrounded me, I found myself slipping into forbidden speech. At gatherings of the highest circles, I would quote ancient sages who said prophets are always deemed mad. I spoke of “the eye that sees within,” of “the all-seeing land.” Once, twice, I let slip the words “Third Dimension” and “Fourth Dimension”—terms that, in Flatland, are not just heretical but treasonous.

Then came the final mistake. At a meeting of the Local Speculative Society—held in the very palace of the Prefect—some fool read a paper arguing that God had ordained two dimensions for good reason, and that omniscience belongs only to the Supreme Being. In response, I could no longer contain myself. I stood and recounted everything: my journey with the Sphere, our descent into Space, the Council Chamber transformed, the revelation of the Cube, the return home. At first, I claimed it was fiction—a tale of an imagined traveler. But my passion betrayed me. I dropped the pretense. I described the sights, the sounds, the logic behind it all. And then, in a fervent cry, I urged them to shed their prejudices and believe in the Third Dimension.

Needless to say, I was arrested.

The next morning, I stood before the Council, in the same chamber where the Sphere had once appeared beside me. I began my defense, uninterrupted—though I knew from the start what would follow. The President ordered the guards replaced: those of high angularity, trusted and respected, were removed and replaced with lower-class officers—men whose angles barely exceeded thirty degrees. I understood the meaning instantly. They were to be sacrificed. Their memories erased. My story would vanish with them. The state would ensure no witness remained.

After my plea, the President asked two questions:

1. Could I point to the direction meant by “Upward, not Northward”?
2. Could I describe or diagram the Cube, beyond mere numbers?

I answered neither. I said I could offer no further proof, only my faith in Truth. The President nodded. He agreed with my sentiment. “You do well,” he said. “Truth will prevail in time.”

And so I was sentenced to perpetual imprisonment. No torture, no cruelty—only silence, solitude, and the slow erosion of hope. I am allowed occasional visits from my brother, a man of integrity, intelligence, and kindness. He was there when the Sphere appeared. He saw the changing forms, heard the explanations. Yet after seven years of listening to my accounts—repeated week after week—he still does not understand. He refuses to believe in a Sphere. He calls it fantasy.

No convert has come from my suffering. No revolution has begun. I am the Prometheus of Flatland—bound not for stealing fire, but for bringing down nothing at all. For my people have no use for what I bring.

Yet sometimes, in moments of clarity, I hold onto a fragile hope: that these words, written in secret, may one day reach beyond this world. That somewhere—in a higher dimension, or in the minds of future rebels—they may awaken curiosity, spark rebellion, ignite a new way of seeing. That is my prayer.

But often, despair returns. I wonder if I remember the Cube correctly. Does it truly exist? Or is it merely a dream? In the dark, the phrase “Upward, not Northward” echoes like a riddle without answer. Sometimes, even the walls of my cell seem unreal. The tablets I write on, the air I breathe, the very ground beneath me—do they matter? Or are they all illusions, like the One-Dimensional Line or the Zero-Dimensional Point?

I endure. Not because I know the truth will win. But because I must believe it can.
