Flambeau’s holiday began as all his adventures did—with a whim. He had chosen a small sailing boat, so modest it often became a rowing boat, and set his course along the narrow, winding rivers of the Eastern counties. The vessel was snug, built for two, stocked with what Flambeau considered essentials: tinned salmon, loaded revolvers, a bottle of brandy, and—most peculiar of all—a priest.

Years ago, when Flambeau had been the most notorious thief in Paris, he had received countless letters—fanfare, threats, even love notes. But one had lingered in his memory: a visiting card, posted from England, scrawled in green ink. *"If you ever retire and become respectable, come and see me. I want to meet the man who outwitted an entire police force."* On the front, engraved formally: *Prince Saradine, Reed House, Reed Island, Norfolk.*

At the time, Flambeau had dismissed it. The prince was a figure of faded European glamour—a scandalous elopement, a rumored suicide, a life of restless exile. But now, retired and settled in England, curiosity tugged at him. He had no real expectation of finding the place; Reed Island sounded like the stuff of half-forgotten legends.

Yet legend had a way of meeting reality.

One dawn, moored beneath a bank of high grass and gnarled trees, Flambeau and Father Brown awoke to a world still bathed in moonlight. The riverbanks loomed like the walls of some forgotten nursery, daisies towering like ancient oaks.

"By Jove," Flambeau murmured, "it’s like stepping into a fairy tale."

Father Brown crossed himself abruptly. "Fairy tales are seldom kind," he said.

The river led them to a village of stooping roofs and silent streets, where a round-faced man with fiery whiskers pointed them onward without a word. Around the next bend, the reeds parted to reveal an island—long, low, and crowned with a house of bamboo, its ribs humming in the wind like a monstrous pan flute.

Reed House stood before them, eerie in its stillness.

The butler, a gaunt and ghostly figure, informed them that Prince Saradine was expected—his Highness traveled often, but the house was always kept ready. The visitors were ushered inside, where mirrors multiplied every movement, giving the illusion of a crowd where there was none. A cold luncheon was laid out; the housekeeper, a woman of severe beauty, watched them with dark, knowing eyes.

Flambeau, ever the charmer, coaxed fragments of gossip from the staff. The prince, it seemed, had been drained by a ne'er-do-well brother—Captain Stephen, whose name soured the butler’s expression. The housekeeper’s whispers were sharper: *"There isn’t a good one."*

Then the prince arrived—white-haired, flamboyant, trailing the scent of orchids and old scandal. He was a man who had lived too much in too many places, his stories peppered with opium dens and bandit hideouts. Yet beneath the charm, Father Brown sensed something frayed, something *unstable*.

Evening fell like a shroud. The river murmured against the reeds; a bittern’s call echoed like a warning.

Then the boat came.

Six swarthy men rowed it, oars held like weapons. At the stern sat a young man in antiquated black, his face solemn, his posture rigid. He introduced himself as Antonelli.

And then he struck the prince across the face.

Saradine lunged, but the young man stepped back with odd courtesy, gasping in broken English: *"I have insulted. I will give satisfaction."*

A case was opened. Inside lay a pair of dueling pistols.

Father Brown, watching the reflections in the endless mirrors, felt the weight of a truth just out of reach. Somewhere beyond this glass maze, retribution was coming—not for the prince, perhaps, but for the *right* man.

And the wrong person still stood in the way.
