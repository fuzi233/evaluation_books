*"When some beloved voice that was to you
Both sound and sweetness, faileth suddenly,
And silence, against which you dare not cry,
Aches round you like a strong disease and new—
What hope? what help? what music will undo
That silence to your sense?"*
—**Elizabeth Barrett Browning**

The blow had fallen heavily. Margaret lay stricken, not with loud weeping, but with a stillness more terrible—a silence that spoke of devastation within. She scarcely moved upon the sofa, her eyes closed, her lips pale, answering only in whispers when addressed.

Mr. Bell, helpless in the face of such grief, dared not leave her side. The journey back to Oxford, once a thought in his mind, was now impossible—her exhaustion was too profound. He sat by the fire, turning over plans in his weary mind, while Margaret lay like a figure carved in marble beside him.

Dixon, her own tears flowing freely, attempted to coax him downstairs to a meal prepared with trembling hands, but he refused. A plate was brought to him instead, though the food might as well have been ashes for all he tasted of it. When he tried to persuade Margaret to eat, she turned her head away, her throat surely too tight even for a morsel.

With a heavy sigh, Mr. Bell rose and followed Dixon from the room.

"I cannot leave her," he muttered. "Oxford must be written to—let them prepare things in my absence. But she cannot be left like this. Where is Mrs. Lennox? She must come."

Dixon, wiping her eyes, explained that Mrs. Lennox was too near her confinement to travel.

"Then Mrs. Shaw must come," Mr. Bell declared impatiently. "She is in England, is she not?"

Dixon, reluctant at the thought of another overseeing her domain, admitted that Mrs. Shaw had indeed returned—but would she leave her daughter at such a time?

Mr. Bell nearly swore. "What does her daughter’s ‘interesting time’ matter compared to this? Margaret lies there like a effigy on a tomb, and you speak of confinements and convenience? No—Mrs. Shaw *shall* come. See that a room is ready for her by tomorrow night."

His letter, sharp and commanding, had its effect. Mrs. Shaw, weeping over its resemblance to her late husband’s gout-induced dictates, allowed herself to be packed off to Milton, though not without Edith’s tearful exhortations from the staircase:

"Bring Margaret back with you, Mamma! She must come live with us—tell Mr. Bell to arrange it!"

Edith, in the drawing-room afterward, prattled to Henry Lennox about new chintz for Margaret’s room, how it must be cheerful, how it would lift her spirits from dreary Milton. Henry, feigning interest in a review, murmured vague agreement.

Mrs. Shaw, meanwhile, steeled herself for the meeting, dreading the sorrow she must witness, yet already planning Margaret’s swift removal to the comforts of Harley Street.

"Oh, those horrid chimneys!" she lamented to her maid. "To think my sister lived here! I should have fetched her away sooner."

Margaret, when told of her aunt’s coming, had shown no reaction—no relief, no resistance. But when the cab at last arrived, her eyelids fluttered, her lips trembled, and she rose unsteadily. At the sight of Mrs. Shaw, she stumbled forward into her aunt’s embrace, and there—at last—the tears came, a flood of anguish for all she had lost.

Mr. Bell, giving them privacy, retreated to the study, where he distractedly examined Hale’s books, each one a reminder of his lost friend.

A voice at the door interrupted his brooding—Mr. Thornton, come to inquire after Margaret. Dixon, newly self-important with the arrival of Mrs. Shaw’s maid, dismissed him curtly—until Mr. Bell called him in.

"I don’t know what I wished to say," Mr. Bell admitted, once they were alone. "Only that it’s grim, sitting where everything speaks of the dead. And now Margaret’s aunt means to take her away at once—as if she’s fit to travel!"

Mr. Thornton’s face was unreadable. "Is her aunt here, then?"

"Yes, maid and all. As if the woman couldn’t travel alone at such a time!"

They spoke briefly of arrangements—Mr. Bell would stay with the Thorntons rather than the Clarendon—before the conversation turned to Frederick, whose existence came as a shock to Mr. Thornton.

"He’s her brother," Mr. Bell said, puzzled. "I thought you knew—the one involved in the mutiny. He lives in Spain, unable to return."

Mr. Thornton’s mind raced. "Was he here—near the time of Mrs. Hale’s death?"

"No. Why do you ask?"

"I saw a man with Miss Hale once—I thought perhaps..."

"That would have been young Lennox, the lawyer."

Mr. Bell, studying Thornton’s face, suddenly asked, "Did you ever care for Margaret?"

No answer.

"I once thought you did. So did Hale, after I put the notion in his head."

Mr. Thornton’s jaw tightened. "I admired her. Anyone would. She is a beautiful creature."

Mr. Bell scoffed. "Is that all? ‘A beautiful creature’—as if she were a painting or a horse! I thought you had more heart."

Thornton’s eyes burned, but he would not be drawn. "Some men are not free to speak as you are. Let us talk of something else."

And so they did—of business, of the new dining-room Thornton was building for his workers, of the shared meals that had bridged the gap between master and men.

Mr. Bell, amused, offered funds for a feast, but Thornton refused. "I want no charity in it. Let it stand on its own."

"People will talk regardless."

"Let them. But you, as a friend, will oblige me by keeping silent."

Mr. Bell agreed, and so the matter rested—while upstairs, Margaret wept in her aunt’s arms, and the weight of her losses pressed ever heavier upon her.
