The path wound deeper into the pine-forest, a corridor of shadows where the air grew thick with a sacred, suffocating gloom. We walked in silence, the only sounds the crunch of our boots and the rustle of needles overhead. The trees before us swelled in size, ancient sentinels twisted by centuries into grotesque, gnarled forms that seemed to lean inward, guarding secrets too old for mortal tongues. As we pressed on, the canopy thinned, allowing slivers of pale light to pierce the darkness until we emerged onto an open heath.

"You see that hawthorn?" my guide asked, his voice rasping like dry leaves. He extended a long, black beak, pointing toward the edge of the clearing.

I squinted against the glare. "I see a gnarled old man, with a great white head," I replied, my eyes tracing the twisted silhouette.

"Look again," he corrected gently. "It is a hawthorn."

"It certainly looks ancient," I conceded, "but this is not the season for blossoms."

"The season for the hawthorn to blossom," the raven intoned, "is simply when the hawthorn blossoms. That specific tree stands in the ruins of the church on your home-farm. Recall the morning of the thunder; were you not intending to give the bailiff directions regarding its churchyard?"

"I was planning to tell him I wished to transform it into a wilderness of rose-trees, forbidding the plough from coming within three yards of the site."

"Listen!" the raven commanded, holding his breath as if straining to catch a whisper from the void.

I strained my ears. Was it the sighing of a distant wind, or the ghost of music that had once known joy? The sound was faint, almost imperceptible, yet it vibrated through the marrow of my bones.

"They go there still," the raven murmured.

"Who goes there? And where do they go?" I asked, turning to face him.

"Some of the people who once prayed there," he replied, "still make the journey to the ruins. But I fear they will not go much longer."

"What compels them now?"

"They require assistance from one another to complete their thinking and hatch their feelings," the raven explained. "They speak and sing together, and in doing so, the great thought floats out of their hearts like a massive ship leaving a river at high tide."

"Do they pray as well as sing?"

"No," the raven shook his head. "They have discovered that each prays best in their own silent heart. Some are always at prayer. Look! There goes one!"

He pointed upward. A snow-white pigeon mounted the sky, its wings beating with frantic, accelerating speed, spiraling up an invisible staircase of light. Sunlight flashed and quivered off its feathers.

"I see a pigeon!" I exclaimed.

"Of course you see a pigeon," the raven agreed, "for there is the pigeon! But I see a prayer ascending. I wonder now what heart serves as that dove's mother. Perhaps someone has awakened in my cemetery."

"How can a pigeon be a prayer?" I argued, confused. "I understand how it could be a symbol, a likeness, but for a living bird to emerge directly from a heart?"

"It *must* puzzle you! It cannot fail to do so!"

"A prayer is a thought, a purely spiritual entity!" I insisted.

"True enough! But if you understood any world besides your own, you would understand your own much better. When a heart is truly alive, it is capable of thinking living things. There exists one heart whose thoughts are strong, happy creatures, and whose dreams are lives themselves. Some lift heavy thoughts from the ground only to drop them back down; others send their prayers up in living shapes, the nearest likeness they can forge. All living things were thoughts at the beginning, and thus are fit to be used by those who think. When one speaks to the Great Thinker—'Here is one of thy thoughts: I am thinking it now!'—that is a prayer. A word from one of its own little hearts to the big heart. Look, there is another!"

This time, the raven pointed his beak downward, toward the base of a granite block. I looked and saw a small flower. It was unlike anything I had ever witnessed, evoking a feeling of grace and trust, a scent that smelled of a new world that was simultaneously the old. It resembled an anemone, pale rose in hue, with a golden center.

"That is a prayer-flower," the raven stated.

"I have never seen such a flower before!" I rejoined.

"There is no other such. Not a single prayer-flower is ever quite like another," he returned.

"How do you know it is a prayer-flower?" I asked.

"By the expression upon it," he answered. "Beyond that, I cannot tell you. If you know it, you know it; if you do not, you do not."

"Could you not teach me to recognize a prayer-flower when I see it?"

"I could not. But if I could, what greater purpose would serve you? You would not know it of YOURSELF and ITSELF! Why learn the name of a thing when you do not know the thing itself? Whose work is it but your own to open your eyes? Indeed, the business of the universe is to make such a fool of you that you will finally know yourself for one, and so begin to be wise!"

Yet, I saw that the flower was undeniably different from any bloom I had known. Therefore, I knew I was witnessing a shadow of the prayer contained within it. A profound awe washed over me, not just at the sight, but at the thought of a heart listening to the flower.
