I have not yet found Lona, though Mara remains close beside me, instructing me in endless layers of understanding. Is it possible that even this final awakening occurred within a dream? Am I still trapped in the chamber of death, suspended between slumber and reality, unripe for true waking? Or did I fail to surrender fully to sleep, rising prematurely before the hour was right? If this awakening was merely a dream, then it must have been a prophecy of a greater dawn to come—a sign that I was not deceived by a false vision. Even a shadow holds the promise of a brighter light at its core.

In moments of uncertainty, I cry out, "Could God Himself weave such exquisite realities?" And Hope replies, "From where then sprang your dream?" "From the depths of my darkness, emerging into the light of my conscious mind," I answer. "But how did you first descend into that darkness?" Hope asks. "My brain gave birth to it, and the fever coursing through my veins acted as its father."

"Speak rather," Hope suggests gently, "that your brain was the violin from which the music poured forth, and the fever the bow drawing the strings. Yet, who crafted the violin? Who guided the bow across those strings? Consider instead: who placed every songbird upon its branch in the Tree of Life, startling them one by one from their perches? Where did the fantasia originate? What life danced to its rhythm? Was it you, in the obscurity of your own unconsciousness, declaring, 'Let beauty exist; let truth appear,' and instantly bringing beauty into being while truth remained mere semblance?"

Man dreams and yearns; God broods, wills, and brings to life. When a man dreams his own dream, he is merely the plaything of his imagination; but when Another grants him the dream, that Other possesses the power to fulfill it. Since that day, I have never looked into a mirror again. The hand that returned me refused entry through that door once more. "For all the days of my allotted time, I shall wait until my transformation arrives."

Occasionally, gazing upon my books, they seem to tremble as if caught in a breeze, their solidity wavering as another world threatens to breach our reality. Sometimes, walking outdoors, the heavens and earth, the trees and grass, appear to shudder as though ready to dissolve; yet lo, they settle once more into their familiar forms. At other times, I hear faint whispers surrounding me, as if beloved ones speak of me, but whenever I strive to catch their words, silence falls heavy and absolute. I cannot tell if these sensations arise within my mind or invade it from without. I do not chase them; they arrive, and I release them.

Vague, indistinct memories—refusing any clear definition—often gaze upon me through the misted panes of the past, visible even in broad daylight. Yet I no longer dream. Perhaps, however, when I feel most awake, I am merely dreaming more deeply than ever. But when I finally awaken into that existence which, like a mother cradling her child, holds this life within its embrace, I shall truly know that I am awake, and doubt shall vanish forever. I wait. Whether sleeping or wide-eyed, I wait.
