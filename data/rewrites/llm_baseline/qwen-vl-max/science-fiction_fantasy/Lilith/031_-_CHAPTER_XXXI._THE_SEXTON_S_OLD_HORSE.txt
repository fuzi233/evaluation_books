I stood motionless as the final trace of the white leopardess dissolved into the night, a ghostly shimmer fading beyond the edge of sight. Then I turned—reluctantly—to follow my guide. Sleep? What did I have to do with sleep? Reason was constant across all worlds, and reason told me that no living man should lie down among the dead when dawn called him to action. Who would wake me? And how could I trust myself to rise at all? In that house, the sleepers drifted through morning into noon, noon into dusk, untouched by time. I murmured my doubts aloud, but still I walked. There was no other path.

The librarian moved ahead in silence, and I followed, equally quiet. Time stretched thin between us. The sun dipped below the hills, and darkness began to seep into the world like ink poured into water. A chill crept into the air—a cold born of tombs and stillness—and my spirits sank with it. The figure before me grew thinner, more indistinct, until I could no longer see his long coat or hear the whisper of his stride through the heather.

Then came the sound: slow, deliberate flaps of wings—the raven’s voice in the wind. Now and then, fireflies flickered into being, and glowing moths rose like sparks from an unseen flame. At last, the moon emerged, creeping slowly over the distant horizon.

“You are weary, Mr. Vane?” the raven said, landing on a stone. “You must meet the horse who will carry you tomorrow.”

He gave a sharp whistle through his beak, long and hollow. A dark spot appeared on the face of the half-risen moon. Moments later, the thunder of hooves rolled through the silence—soft yet swift, like distant drums beneath the earth. From the very disc of the moon, the horse burst forth.

His mane streamed behind him like a wave torn apart by the sea, frothing silver-white in the lunar glow. His tail whipped the air, blinding the moon’s eye with each sweep. He stood nineteen hands high—massive of bone, taut of hide, rigid with muscle. He looked not like a beast of flesh, but a creature forged in death’s own forge. The holy Death himself might ride such a mount.

In the pale light, he seemed a skeleton stitched together with shadow. Yet he moved with the grace of a winged thing, silent and swift. As he neared, his pace slowed. His mane and tail settled around him like smoke calming after a storm.

I had always loved horses—not just one, but every horse I’d ever seen. I had spent every coin upon them, never sold one. This creature, though terrifying, stirred in me a hunger so deep it burned. Not desire for possession—but something darker, sharper: covetousness. It was evil, I knew, in any world. I wouldn’t steal him. But if I could buy him, I would, regardless of law or fate.

I reached out and touched him. My fingers brushed the ridges of bone beneath skin as smooth and tight as satin, reflecting the moon’s shape like a mirror. I stroked his pointed ears, whispered secrets into them, breathed into his red nostrils—life into life. In return, he breathed into mine, and we were bound.

His eyes—blue-filmed, like those of the dead—held within them glowing coals. The raven watched, wings half-spread, pleased.

“That is well,” he croaked. “Be friends with him. He will carry you better tomorrow. Now—we must go home.”

My longing to ride him flared into fever. “May I not mount him now?” I asked.

“By all means,” the raven answered. “Mount and ride.”

The horse lowered his head, gentle as a lover. I gripped his mane, pulled myself up, aided by the jutting bones beneath. “He could outrun any leopard in creation!” I exclaimed.

“Not tonight,” the raven replied. “The road is treacherous. But wait now—gain comes later. To run is easier than to stay. Go on, my son. Straight to the cottage. I shall arrive before you. Your mother will rejoice to see her son on such a steed.”

I sat silent. The horse stood still, immovable.

“Why delay?” the raven asked.

“I cannot resist the urge to chase the leopardess,” I confessed. “I can barely hold myself back.”

“You have promised.”

“The debt to the Little Ones feels heavier than my word to you.”

“Then yield to temptation, and you bring harm to them—and to yourself.”

“What does it matter to me? I love them. Love harms no one. I will return.”

But truthfully, I had forgotten the children. I was drunk on the horse, on power.

Eyes gleamed in the dark. Adam stood beside me, in his true form. I heard the strain in his voice—the anger held back by will.

“Mr. Vane,” he said, “do you not know why you’ve done nothing worth doing?”

“Because I’ve been a fool,” I answered.

“In what?”

“In everything.”

“And which is your greatest folly?”

“Bringing the princess back to life. I should have let her die.”

“Nonsense,” Adam said. “You could not have known she was evil. And you never brought anyone back to life—how could you? You are dead.”

“Dead?” I cried.

“Yes. And you will remain so until you choose to die.”

“Back to your riddles again,” I scoffed.

“Listen,” he said softly. “The only foolish thing you ever did was to flee from our dead.”

I pressed the horse’s ribs. He surged forward like a gust of wind. I patted his neck, and he spun in a tight arc—low to the ground, like a cat circling prey, his mane brushing the heather.

Through the dark, I heard the raven’s wings—five quick beats—and he landed on the horse’s head.

The horse stopped instantly, digging his hooves into the earth.

“Mr. Vane,” the raven croaked, “think! Twice already evil has struck you—once from fear, once from carelessness. Breaking your word is worse. It is a crime.”

“The Little Ones are in danger because of me!” I shouted. “But I will not break my promise. I’ll return. I’ll stay in your house as many nights, days, years as you wish.”

“I tell you again—you will do them harm if you go now.”

But the horse’s strength had infected me. A false power pulsed through me, unearned, unreal. I could not hear.

“Would you take away my last chance to make amends?” I roared. “This time there will be no running. It is my duty. I will go—even if I die for it!”

“Go then, foolish boy,” the raven croaked, laced with fury. “Take the horse. Ride to ruin. May it lead you to humility.”

He spread his wings and vanished into the sky.

I pressed the horse’s ribs again. “After the leopardess,” I whispered.

He turned his head, sniffed the air, then stepped forward—slow at first, then faster. He broke into a trot, then a gallop. In moments, he flew.

He saw in the dark. Never stumbled. Never faltered. I sat as though atop a wave, feeling every muscle ripple beneath me. His joints were springs; his movements flowed without jar. He ran not with feet, but with wind. The air rushed past like a storm.

We crossed the cursed hollow like a bolt from a crossbow. No beast dared stir. They knew the hooves that thundered above.

We climbed the hills, plunged down their slopes. Over rocky chasms, he leapt without hesitation, his gallop unbroken.

The moon, halfway across the sky, watched with solemn sorrow.

I rode in triumph—proud, powerful, free. We neared the river’s many channels. The horse cleared them one by one—sometimes two in a single stride—then gathered himself for a great leap.

As the moon reached the peak of her arc, something terrible happened.

She began to roll—like the wheel of Fortune cast by gods. Faster and faster she tumbled. Her face—human, wide-eyed—twisted upside-down, then righted, then flipped again. One moment her forehead faced the sky, the next her chin. I stared, paralyzed.

From the ravines came the howl of wolves.

A cold dread crept into my chest. My confidence cracked.

The horse kept racing—ears forward, nostrils flaring, drinking the wind.

But the moon… the moon rolled like a broken chariot-wheel down the slope of heaven. With every turn, the world dimmed.

Then—she vanished over the edge of the world. Light died.

We were mid-leap when darkness swallowed us whole.

The horse’s head dropped. Momentum carried him across the channel, but he collapsed on the bank—limp, still, lifeless.

I dismounted, knelt beside him, searched his body. No broken bones. But he was gone. Not dead. Unmade.

I sat upon his body, burying my face in my hands.
