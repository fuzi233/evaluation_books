This was no army marching to war, nor a band seeking enemies; yet the music they carried was martial, and the eyes of some—Miss Keeldar among them—betrayed a spirit stirred by the sound, though not by the thought of blood. Old Helstone glanced at her, and they exchanged a laugh.

"There is no battle in prospect," he said gently. "Our country does not ask us to fight. There is no tyrant threatening our liberty. We are merely taking a walk. Keep your hand on the reins, Captain, and dampen that fire. It is not needed."

"Take your own advice, Doctor," Shirley replied. To Caroline, she murmured, "I shall borrow from imagination what reality refuses. We are not soldiers; bloodshed is not my desire. If we are soldiers, we are soldiers of the Cross. Time seems to have rolled back centuries, binding us on a pilgrimage to Palestine. But no, that is too visionary. I crave a sterner dream. We are Lowlanders of Scotland, following a Covenanting captain into the hills to meet out of reach of persecuting troopers. We know battle may follow prayer, and that in the worst outcome, heaven must be our reward. We are ready to redden the peat-moss with our blood. That music stirs my soul; it wakes all my life, making my heart beat not with its usual pulse, but with a thrilling, new vigour. I almost long for danger—for a faith, a land, or at least a lover to defend."

"Look, Shirley!" Caroline interrupted. "What is that red speck above Stilbro' Brow? You have keener sight than I. Turn your eagle eye to it."

Miss Keeldar looked. "I see," she said, then added quickly, "there is a line of red. They are cavalry soldiers. They ride fast. There are six of them. They will pass us. No; they have turned right. They saw our procession and are avoiding it. Where are they going?"

"Perhaps they are only exercising their horses."

"Perhaps so. We see them no more."

Mr. Helstone spoke up. "We shall pass through Royd Lane to reach Nunnely Common by a short cut," he said.

Into the straits of Royd Lane they defiled. It was so narrow that only two could walk abreast without falling into the ditches lining the sides. Halfway through, excitement rippled through the clerical commanders. Boultby's spectacles trembled; Helstone's cane shook; the curates nudged one another nervously, while Mr. Hall turned to the ladies with a smile.

"What is the matter?" someone demanded.

He pointed his staff toward the end of the lane. There, emerging from the shadows, was another procession, led by men in black, accompanied by music.

"Is it our double?" Shirley asked, her voice tight. "Our manifold wraith? Here is a card turned up."

"If you wanted a battle, you are likely to get one—at least of looks," Caroline whispered, laughing.

"They shall not pass us!" the curates cried in unison. "We will not give way!"

"Give way!" Helstone retorted sternly, turning around. "Who speaks of giving way? You, boys, mind your business. The ladies, I know, will be firm. I can trust them. There is not a churchwoman here who will not stand her ground against these folks for the honour of the Establishment. What does Miss Keeldar say?"

"She asks what it is."

"The Dissenting and Methodist schools, the Baptists, Independents, and Wesleyans," Helstone declared, "have joined in an unholy alliance, turning purposely into this lane to obstruct our march and drive us back."

"Bad manners!" Shirley exclaimed. "And I hate bad manners. Of course, they must have a lesson."

"A lesson in politeness," Mr. Hall suggested, ever the peacemaker. "Not an example of rudeness."

Old Helstone quickened his pace, marching yards ahead of his company. When he neared the opposing leaders—a large, greasy man with black hair combed flat on his forehead—the hostile commander called a halt. The procession paused. He drew a hymn book, gave out a verse, set a tune, and they launched into the most dolorous of canticles.

Helstone signaled his bands. They clashed out with all the power of brass. He ordered "Rule, Britannia!" and commanded the children to join vocally, which they did with enthusiastic spirit. The enemy was sung and stormed down; his psalm was quelled. By noise alone, he was conquered.

"Now, follow me!" Helstone exclaimed. "Not at a run, but at a firm, smart pace. Be steady, every child and woman. Keep together. Hold on by each other's skirts if necessary."

He strode on with a determined, deliberate gait, perfectly seconded by his scholars and teachers. Neither ran nor faltered; they marched with cool, solid impetus. The curates, caught between Helstone and Miss Keeldar—who watched any deviation with lynx-eyed vigilance, ready to rebuke the slightest breach of order with cane or parasol—were compelled to match their step. First amazed, then alarmed, the body of Dissenters was borne down, pressed back, and finally forced to turn tail, leaving the outlet from Royd Lane free. Boultby suffered in the onslaught, but Helstone and Malone held him up, bringing him through the business whole in limb, though sorely tried in wind.

The fat Dissenter who had sung the hymn was left sitting in the ditch. A spirit merchant by trade and a leader of the Nonconformists, it was said he had drunk more water in that single afternoon than he had swallowed for a year prior. Mr. Hall had ensured Caroline's safety, and Caroline had cared for him. Later, he and Miss Ainley made quiet comments on the incident. When the party had fairly cleared the lane, Miss Keeldar and Mr. Helstone shook hands heartily. The curates began to exult, but Helstone soon put a curb on their innocent spirits, reminding them that the business was none of their managing and that they had better hold their tongues.

About half-past three, the procession turned back, regaining the starting place by four. Long lines of benches were arranged in the close-shorn fields surrounding the school. Children were seated, and huge baskets covered with white cloths, along with great smoking tin vessels, were brought out. Before the distribution of good things began, a brief grace was pronounced by Mr. Hall and sung by the children. Their young voices sounded melodious, even touching, in the open air. Large currant buns and hot, well-sweetened tea were administered in the proper spirit of liberality. No stinting was permitted; the rule was that each child should receive about twice as much as they could possibly eat, leaving a reserve for those prevented from coming by age, sickness, or other impediments. Buns and beer circulated among the musicians and singers. Afterwards, the benches were removed, and they were left to unbend their spirits in licensed play.

A bell summoned the teachers, patrons, and patronesses to the schoolroom. Miss Keeldar, Miss Helstone, and many other ladies were already there, glancing over the arrangement of their separate trays and tables. Most of the female servants of the neighbourhood, along with the clerks', singers', and musicians' wives, had been pressed into service as waiters. Each vied with the other in smartness and daintiness of dress, and many handsome forms were seen among the younger ones. About half a dozen were cutting bread and butter, another half-dozen supplying hot water from the rector's kitchen coppers. The profusion of flowers and evergreens decorating the white walls, the show of silver teapots and bright porcelain on the tables, and the active figures in blithe faces and gay dresses flitting about everywhere formed a refreshing and lively spectacle. Everybody talked, not very loudly, but merrily, while canary birds sang shrilly in their high-hung cages.

As the rector's niece, Caroline took her place at one of the three first tables; Mrs. Boultby and Margaret Hall officiated at the others. At these tables, the *élite* of the company were to be entertained, strict rules of equality not being more in fashion at Briarfield than elsewhere. Miss Helstone removed her bonnet and scarf to escape the oppressive heat. Her long curls, falling on her neck, served almost in place of a veil, and her muslin dress was fashioned modestly as a nun's robe, enabling her to dispense with the encumbrance of a shawl.

The room was filling. Mr. Hall had taken his post beside Caroline, who, as she rearranged cups and spoons, whispered remarks on the day's events. He looked grave about what had transpired in Royd Lane, and she tried to smile him out of his seriousness. Miss Keeldar sat near—unusually still, neither laughing nor talking. She seemed afraid lest some intruder should take a seat she apparently wished to reserve next to her own. Ever and anon she spread her satin dress over an undue portion of the bench, or laid her gloves or embroidered handkerchief upon it. Caroline noticed this *manège* and asked what friend she expected. Shirley bent toward her, almost touching her ear with rosy lips, and whispered with a musical softness that often characterized her tones when stirring some sweet secret source of feeling, "I expect Mr. Moore. I saw him last night and made him promise to come with his sister, and to sit at our table. He won't fail me, I feel certain; but I apprehend his coming too late, and being separated from us. Here is a fresh batch arriving; every place will be taken. Provoking!"

In fact, Mr. Wynne the magistrate, his wife, his son, and his two daughters now entered in high state. They were Briarfield gentry. Of course, their place was at the first table, and being conducted thither, they filled up the whole remaining space. For Miss Keeldar's comfort, Mr. Sam Wynne inducted himself into the very vacancy she had kept for Moore, planting himself solidly on her gown, her gloves, and her handkerchief. Mr. Sam was one of the objects of her aversion, and the more so because he showed serious symptoms of an aim at her hand. The old gentleman had also publicly declared that the Fieldfield and De Walden estates were delightfully *contagious*—a malapropism which rumour had not failed to repeat to Shirley.

Caroline's ears still rang with that thrilling whisper, "I expect Mr. Moore"; her heart still beat and her cheek still glowed with it, when a note from the organ pealed above the confused hum of the place. Dr. Boultby, Mr. Helstone, and Mr. Hall rose, as did all present, and grace was sung to the accompaniment of the music; then tea began. She was kept too busy with her office for a while to have leisure for looking round, but when the last cup was filled, she threw a restless glance over the room. There were some ladies and several gentlemen standing about, unaccommodated with seats. Amidst a group, she recognized her spinster friend, Miss Mann, whom the fine weather had tempted, or some urgent friend had persuaded, to leave her drear solitude for one hour of social enjoyment. Miss Mann looked tired of standing; a lady in a yellow bonnet brought her a chair. Caroline knew well that *chapeau en satin jaune*; she knew the black hair, and the kindly though rather opinionated and froward-looking face under it; she knew that *robe de soie noire*, she knew even that *schall gris de lin*; she knew, in short, Hortense Moore, and wanted to jump up and run to her—to give her one embrace for her own sake and two for her brother's. She half rose, indeed, with a smothered exclamation, and perhaps, for the impulse was very strong, she would have run across the room and actually saluted her; but a hand replaced her in her seat, and a voice behind her whispered, "Wait till after tea, Lina, and then I'll bring her to you."

And when she could look up, there was Robert himself close behind, smiling at her eagerness, looking better than she had ever seen him look—looking, indeed, to her partial eyes, so very handsome that she dared not trust herself to hazard a second glance; for his image struck on her vision with painful brightness, and pictured itself on her memory as vividly as if daguerreotyped by a pencil of keen lightning.

He moved on and spoke to Miss Keeldar. Shirley, irritated by some unwelcome attentions from Sam Wynne and by the fact of that gentleman being still seated on her gloves and handkerchief—and probably also by Moore's want of punctuality—was by no means in good humour. She first shrugged her shoulders at him, and then said a bitter word or two about his "insupportable tardiness." Moore neither apologized nor retorted. He stood near her quietly, as if waiting to see whether she would recover her temper; which she did in little more than three minutes, indicating the change by offering him her hand. Moore took it with a smile, half-corrective, half-grateful. The slightest possible shake of the head delicately marked the former quality; it is probable a gentle pressure indicated the latter.

"You may sit where you can now, Mr. Moore," said Shirley, also smiling. "You see there is not an inch of room for you here; but I discern plenty of space at Mrs. Boultby's table, between Miss Armitage and Miss Birtwhistle. Go! John Sykes will be your *vis-à-vis*, and you will sit with your back towards us."

Moore, however, preferred lingering about where he was. He now and then took a turn down the long room, pausing to interchange greetings with other gentlemen in his own placeless predicament; but still he came back to the magnet, Shirley, bringing with him, each time he returned, observations it was necessary to whisper in her ear.
