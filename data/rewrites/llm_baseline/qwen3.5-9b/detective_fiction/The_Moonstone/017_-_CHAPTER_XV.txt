The sergeant remained silent, deep in his own contemplations, until we crossed the plantation of firs leading toward the quicksand. Only then did he rouse himself, appearing as a man whose resolve had finally hardened, and addressed me once more.

"Mr. Betteredge," he said, "since you have honored me by rowing in my boat, and since I believe you may still be of some assistance before evening falls, I see no point in continuing to obscure the truth between us. I propose setting an example of plain speaking on my part. You are determined to withhold any information prejudicial to Rosanna Spearman solely because she has been a good girl to *you*, and because you pity her heartily. These humane sentiments earn you high credit, but in this specific instance, they are utterly misplaced. Rosanna Spearman faces absolutely no danger of trouble—not if I pin her to the charge of being involved in the diamond's disappearance on evidence as clear as the nose on your face!"

"Do you mean that my lady will not prosecute?" I asked.

"I mean that your lady *cannot* prosecute," replied the sergeant. "Rosanna is merely an instrument in the hands of another person, and she will be held harmless for that individual's sake."

He spoke with grave earnestness; there was no denying it. Yet, a restless feeling stirred within me against his certainty. "Can't you give that other person a name?" I pressed.

"Can't *you*, Mr. Betteredge?"

"No."

Sergeant Cuff stood stock-still, surveying me with a look of melancholy interest.

"It is always a pleasure to me to be tender toward human frailty," he remarked. "I feel particularly tender at this moment, Mr. Betteredge, toward you. And you, sharing that excellent motive, feel particularly tender toward Rosanna Spearman, do you not? Do you happen to know if she has acquired a new outfit of linen recently?"

I was completely baffled by this abrupt insertion of such an extraordinary query. Seeing no harm in admitting the truth regarding Rosanna, I confessed that the girl had arrived with scant provisions, noting that my lady, in reward for her exemplary conduct—which I emphasized heavily—had gifted her a fresh wardrobe just two weeks prior.

"This is a miserable world," sighed the sergeant. "Human life, Mr. Betteredge, resembles a target; misfortune is perpetually firing at it and invariably striking the bullseye. Had it not been for that recent outfit, we would undoubtedly have discovered a discarded nightgown or petticoat among her belongings and pinned her down immediately. You cannot fail to follow my reasoning, can you? You have inspected the servants yourself and recall the discoveries two of them made outside Rosanna's door. Surely you know what the girl was up to yesterday after falling ill? You cannot guess? Oh, dear me, it is as plain as that strip of light visible at the tree line. At eleven o'clock on Thursday morning, Superintendent Seagrave—a veritable mass of human infirmity—pointed out the smear on the door to all the female staff. Rosanna, suspecting foul play regarding her own things, seized the first opportunity to retreat to her room. Finding the paint stain on her nightgown, petticoat, or whatever else bore the mark, she feigned illness and slipped away to town. There, on Thursday night, she procured materials to construct a replacement petticoat or nightgown, sewing it entirely in secret within her room. She lit a fire—not to destroy the evidence, for two fellow-servants were prying outside her door, and she knew better than to create smoke or scatter tinder—but to dry and iron the substitute garment after wringing it out. She kept the stained original hidden, perhaps wearing it beneath her clothes, and is currently engaged in disposing of it in some convenient location on that lonely stretch of beach ahead of us.

I have traced her this evening to your fishing village and to a specific cottage, which we may need to visit before returning. She lingered there for some time and emerged carrying, as I believe, something concealed beneath her cloak. A cloak draped over a woman's back serves as an emblem of charity, covering a multitude of sins. I observed her depart northward along the coast after leaving the cottage. Does your local seashore offer a fine specimen of marine landscape, Mr. Betteredge?"

I answered briefly, "Yes."

"Tastes differ," noted Sergeant Cuff. "From my perspective, however, I have rarely seen a seascape I admire less. If you are pursuing someone along a coastline and that person happens to glance around, there is not a single scrap of cover to conceal you. I faced a choice: take Rosanna into custody on suspicion, or leave her, for the time being, to complete her little game in her own hands. For reasons I shall not trouble you with, I decided to make any sacrifice rather than raise the alarm tonight to a certain individual whom I shall keep nameless between us. I returned to the house to request that you escort me to the northern end of the beach via an alternate route. Sand, regarding its ability to record footsteps, is one of the finest detective officers I know. Should we fail to intercept Rosanna Spearman by circling her in this manner, the sand itself may reveal her activities, provided the light holds out. Here is the sand. If you will permit my suggestion—please remain silent and allow me to lead."

A condition akin to "detective fever" had clearly taken hold of my humble servant. Sergeant Cuff proceeded between the sand dunes down to the beach. I followed closely, my heart pounding in my throat, waiting anxiously for the outcome.

As fate would have it, I found myself standing nearly where Rosanna Spearman and I had conversed previously when Mr. Franklin unexpectedly appeared before us upon our return from London. While my eyes tracked the sergeant, my mind drifted involuntarily to that past encounter between Rosanna and me. I declared I almost felt the poor girl slip her hand once more into mine, giving it a grateful squeeze to thank me for my kindness. I almost heard her voice repeating that the Shivering Sand seemed to draw her irresistibly whenever she ventured out—I almost saw her face brighten anew as it had when she first spotted Mr. Franklin emerging briskly from among the dunes. My spirits sank lower with each recollection, and the view of the desolate little bay, when I forced myself to look about, only intensified my unease.

The last vestiges of evening light were fading, casting a still and terrible calm over the desolation. The swell of the open ocean against the great sandbank in the bay rose silently. The inner sea lay lost and dim, undisturbed by a breath of wind. Patches of foul ooze floated, yellow-white, upon the dead surface of the water. Scum and slime shimmered faintly in spots where the dying light caught them on the two great rocky spits jutting north and south into the sea. Now was the time of the turning tide; and even as I stood waiting, the broad brown face of the quicksand began to dimple and quiver—the sole movement in that horrific scene.

I saw the sergeant start as the tremor of the sand caught his eye. After gazing at it for a minute, he turned and returned to me.

"A treacherous place, Mr. Betteredge," he said. "And there are no signs of Rosanna Spearman anywhere on the beach, search where you may."

He guided me lower onto the shore, and I confirmed with my own eyes that his footsteps and mine were the only impressions left in the sand.

"How fares the fishing village from our current position?" asked Sergeant Cuff.

"Cobb's Hole," I replied (that being the name of the place), "lies approximately due south."

"I saw the girl this evening walking northward along the shore from Cobb's Hole," stated the sergeant. "Therefore, she must have been heading toward this location. Is Cobb's Hole situated on the other side of that point of land there? And can we reach it now, during low tide, via the beach?"

"Yes," I answered to both queries.

"If you'll excuse my suggestion, we shall move briskly," said the sergeant. "I wish to locate the spot where she abandoned the shore before darkness fully claims it."

We had walked roughly two hundred yards toward Cobb's Hole when Sergeant Cuff suddenly knelt upon the beach, appearing seized by a sudden fervor for prayer.

"There is merit in your marine landscape after all," remarked the sergeant. "Here are a woman's footsteps, Mr. Betteredge! Let us designate them Rosanna's footsteps until evidence proves otherwise. They are remarkably confused, you will observe—deliberately confused, I should say. Ah, poor soul, she understands the detective virtues of sand as well as I do! But has she not been in such a haste that she failed to tread the marks thoroughly? I believe so. Here is one footprint proceeding *from* Cobb's Hole; and here is another returning to it.
