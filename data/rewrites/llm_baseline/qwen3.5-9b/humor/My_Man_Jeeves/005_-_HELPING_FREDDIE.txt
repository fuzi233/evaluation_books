I shan't bore you with the usual preamble about "don't you know" and "all that sort of rot," but I simply must tell you the tale of dear old Freddie Meadowes. I'm not exactly a master of literary flourish, but fear not—I've got a chap who writes plays to polish it up later, so it'll be perfect in the final draft.

Dearest Freddie has been my pal for years. One morning, I walked into the club and found him slumped in a dark corner, staring blankly at nothing, looking as desolate as the last rose of summer. Naturally, I was alarmed. Usually, the old rogue is the life and soul of the set, a tiny bundle of mischief.

Jimmy Pinkerton was with me. Jimmy's a playwright—a brilliant fellow—and between us, we interrogated the poor, glassy-eyed man until the truth came out. As suspected, it was a girl. He'd had a row with Angela West, the woman he was engaged to, and she'd broken it off. He didn't explain the details, but she was thoroughly fed up: no contact, no phone calls, and his letters returned unopened.

I felt for Freddie. I knew the pain. Once, I was hopelessly in love with a girl named Elizabeth Shoolbred, and the fact that she couldn't tolerate me at any cost will be recorded in my autobiography. I understood his agony.

"Change of scene is what you need, old scout," I said. "Come to Marvis Bay with me. I've rented a cottage. Jimmy's arriving on the twenty-fourth. We'll be a cozy trio."

"He's absolutely right," Jimmy chimed in. "A change is vital. I knew a bloke whose girlfriend rejected him. He went abroad. Two months later, she wired, 'Come back, Muriel.' He started writing a reply, then forgot her surname, and never sent it."

But Freddie wouldn't be consoled. He continued to look as though he'd swallowed his last sixpence. Reluctantly, he agreed to come to Marvis Bay, claiming he might as well be there as anywhere else.

Do you know Marvis Bay? It's in Dorsetshire. Not terribly exciting, but it has its charms. You bathe during the day, sit on the sands, and in the evening, you stroll the shore with the gnats. At nine, you apply ointment to your bites and retire.

It suited Freddie perfectly. Once the moon rose and the breeze sighed through the trees, you couldn't drag him from that beach with a rope. He became a favorite among the gnats, who would hover around him, saving their best attacks specifically for him.

Yes, it was peaceful. But by the end of the first week, I wished Jimmy had arrived sooner. As company, poor Freddie was hardly worth writing home about. When he wasn't chewing a pipe and scowling at the carpet, he was at the piano playing "The Rosary" with one finger. He couldn't play anything else, and he couldn't play much of that either. Around the third bar, a fuse would blow, and he'd have to restart.

One morning, as I returned from bathing, he was playing it again.

"Reggie," he said, his voice hollow, looking up. "I've seen her."

"Seen her?" I asked. "Miss West?"

"I was at the post office collecting mail, and we met in the doorway. She cut me!"

He restarted "The Rosary" and slipped on the second bar.

"Reggie," he insisted, "you shouldn't have brought me here. I must leave."

"Leave?" I protested. "Don't talk nonsense. This is exactly where you'll turn things around."

"She cut me."

"Never mind. Be a sport. Try again."

"She looked right through me!"

"Of course she did. But ignore that. Give me the reins. I'll handle it. What you need," I said, "is to place her under an obligation. Get her to timidly thank you. You want—"

"But what should she thank me for?"

I paused for thought.

"Save her from drowning," I suggested.

"I can't swim," Freddie admitted.

That was Freddie all over: a dear chap in a thousand ways, but useless when push comes to shove.

He cranked the piano up again, and I sprinted outside.

Strolling onto the sands, I realized the intellectual labor had to fall to me. Freddie had strengths—he was ace at polo and could mimic cats fighting in a yard—but he lacked initiative.

While my brain whirred like a dynamo near some rocks, I spotted a blue dress. By Jove, it was her. Though I'd never met her, Freddie had sixteen photos of her scattered across his room, so I knew instantly. She sat on the sand, helping a small, fat child build a castle. Nearby, an elderly lady read a novel. Hearing the girl call her "aunt," I deduced the child was her cousin. I figured if Freddie were there, he'd try to craft some sentimental angle regarding the kid. Personally, I couldn't muster it; the child was one of those round, bulging types who inspired zero sentimentality.

Once the castle was done, the child grew bored and began to whimper. The girl took him to a sweet stall. I walked on.

Fellows, if you ask them, I'm a chump. I admit it. All the Peppers are chumps. But occasionally, when you least expect it, I get a blazing brain-wave. That's what happened now. An idea so brilliant it likely wouldn't occur to a dozen geniuses.

On my return walk, I saw the fat child meditatively smashing a jellyfish with a spade. The girl wasn't there; no one was in sight. Just as I passed, the spark ignited. I analyzed everything in a flash. The girl loved this kid (her cousin), so if I kidnapped him temporarily, causing her great anxiety, and then had Freddie appear leading the infant by the hand, claiming he'd found him wandering and nearly lost his life... her gratitude would force a truce.

I grabbed the child and fled. En route home, I visualized the reunion so vividly I choked with emotion.

Freddie, however, was slow to grasp the nuance. When I arrived, dumping the screaming child in the sitting-room, he didn't effervesce with joy.

"Stop it!" he yelled. "Does anyone else have troubles besides you? What's all this, Reggie?"

The child retaliated with a yell that rattled the windows. I rushed to the kitchen for a jar of honey. It worked immediately; the child smeared his face and quieted down.

"Well?" Freddie asked when silence returned.

I explained the plan. Slowly, it dawned on him.

"You're not such a fool as you look, sometimes, Reggie," he conceded gracefully. "Seems pretty good."

He freed the child from the honey jar and marched out to scour the beach for Angela.

I felt happier than ever. Knowing Freddie would soon regain his brightness made me feel as if someone had left me a million pounds. Leaning on the veranda, smoking peacefully, I saw Freddie returning, still dragging the kid. He looked miserable.

"Hello!" I called. "Couldn't find her?"

"Yes, I found her," he replied with a bitter laugh.

"Well, then—?"

Freddie collapsed into a chair, groaning.

"This isn't her cousin, you idiot!" he shrieked. "No relation at all. Just a kid she met on the beach. She'd never seen him before."

"What! Who is he?"

"I don't know! Oh, Lord, what a time I've had! Thank goodness you'll probably spend the next few years in Dartmoor for kidnapping. That's my only comfort. I'll come and jeer at you through the bars."

"Tell me everything," I urged.

It took forever; he broke off every sentence to insult me, but eventually, the story emerged. She listened like an iceberg, then implied that if he ever met Dr. Cook, it would be the greatest duel in history. He retreated with the child, humiliated.

"And mind, this is your mess," he concluded. "I'm not involved. If you want to avoid prison, find the parents and return him."

By Jove, until I started tramping the area with this infernal child, I never realized restoring a kid to anxious parents was so devilishly hard. How do kidnappers ever get caught? I searched Marvis Bay like a bloodhound, but no one claimed the infant. It seemed he lived alone in a cottage. Only when I asked the sweet-man did I learn his name was Medwin and his parents lived at Ocean Rest, Beach Road.

I dashed there like an arrow. No answer. Movements inside, but no one opened. Just as I prepared to batter down the door, a voice from upstairs shouted, "Hi!"

I looked up to see a round, pink face with grey whiskers staring down.

"Hi!" it shouted again.

"What the deuce do you mean by 'Hi'?" I demanded.

"You can't come in," the face said. "Hello, is that Tootles?"

"My name isn't Tootles, and I don't want to come in," I retorted. "Are you Mr. Medwin? I've brought back your son."
