"Oubliez les Professeurs." Those were Madame Beck's words, delivered with a gravity that belied her wisdom. It was a fatal mistake. That night, she failed to leave me calm; instead, she ignited a fever within me. I was no longer indifferent, isolated, or content to exist alone in my own estimation. No, I was suddenly, violently connected to a second person whom I was meant to forget.

*Forget him?* The irony was bitter. The "wiseheads"—Père Silas and Madame Beck—had concocted a plan so sagacious it bordered on the absurd. They had unveiled the inner sanctum of his soul, transforming the boy I knew into a stainless hero. They revealed a love so profound it had defied death itself, clung to the spirit against the ravages of matter, and kept vigil beside a tomb for twenty years. They showed me a man who had traded vengeance for a cross, dedicating his best energies to an unselfish purpose.

Before this revelation, I had known only the shadows of his nature: jealousy, suspicion, tendernesses that arrived like warm air and vanished like early dew, leaving traces of irritation. Now, however, I saw the grand design beneath the surface. His fidelity was proven not by empty sentiment, but by sacrifice.

As for Justine Marie, I saw her clearly now. She was merely phlegmatic, pale, and slow—an inert vessel of kindness, indistinguishable from the ordinary. If she wore angel wings, it was a poet's fantasy; if her brow shimmered with a halo, it was the reflection of someone else's fire. Was I to be paralyzed by the image of a dead nun? Was the barrier of her vows an eternal wall? Why should the charity he lavished on others or his vow of virginity stand between me and the truth of his heart?

These questions, posed by Madame Beck, became the deepest puzzles, the strongest obstructions, and the keenest stimuli of my life. For a week, I slept and dreamed in their wake. There was no answer in the world but a single, dark figure: the man who stood under the brim of a *bonnet-grec*, wrapped in a tattered *paletôt*, his clothes stained with ink, his spirit aflame.

After my visit to the Rue des Mages, I craved to see him again. Knowing what I now knew, I felt his countenance would reveal pages of lucidity I had missed before. I longed to trace the imprint of that primitive devotion, that half-knightly, half-saintly chivalry the priest had attributed to him. He had become my Christian hero, and I needed to view him through that lens.

Opportunity did not wait. The following day, my new impressions underwent the ultimate test. About three o'clock, the peaceful order of the *première classe*, maintained by Madame Beck's serene sway, shattered with the wild intrusion of a *paletôt*.

I sat quietly, safe under her gaze, edified by her lesson. I was tracing an elaborate line engraving, finding pleasure in the tedious labor of copying it. Suddenly, chaos erupted. My drawing, my pencils, my precious copy were snatched into a crushing handful and scattered. I was lifted from my chair, dragged across the floor, and deposited in the center of the *grande salle*, a vast hall usually reserved for dance and song.

There, standing amidst the wreckage, were two men. One dark, one light; one military in a braided *surtout*, the other careless in the garb of an artist. Both boasted magnificent moustaches and whiskers. M. Emanuel stood apart, his eyes burning with choler, gesturing with the authority of a tribune.

"Mademoiselle," he declared, "your business is to prove to these gentlemen that I am no liar. You will answer questions to the best of your ability and write a theme of their choosing. In their eyes, I am an unprincipled impostor who forges the works of my pupils. Disprove this charge."

Great heavens! The trial I had so long evaded had descended upon me like thunder. These were the dandy professors of the college, Messieurs Boissec and Rochemorte, a pair of cold-blooded fops and skeptics. It appeared that M. Paul had rashly exhibited an essay I had written—something he had never praised, perhaps because I had deemed it forgotten. The essay was not remarkable; it only *seemed* so compared to the average drivel of foreign schoolgirls. But these gentlemen suspected forgery, and I was now to bear witness to the truth under the torture of their interrogation.

The scene was memorable. They began with classics, then French history. I knew nothing of Mérovée or Pharamond. They tested me in various sciences, receiving only a shake of the head and the unvarying reply, *"Je n'en sais rien."*

Then, after a pause, they moved to general information. M. Emanuel, who had stood silent like the winter solstice, brightened slightly, expecting me to show some competence. He was mistaken. Answers surged in my mind, ideas blooming like a rising well, but words refused to follow. Either I could not speak, or I would not. My nerves were frayed, my humor crossed.

One examiner, the man in the braided coat, whispered to his colleague, *"Est-elle donc idiote?"*

*"Yes,"* I thought, *"she is an idiot, and always will be, for creatures like you."*

Yet I suffered cruelly. I saw damp sweat gather on M. Paul's brow, and his eye spoke a passionate, sad reproach. He could not believe in my total lack of cleverness; he thought I *could* be prompt if I *would*.

Finally, to relieve him, to spare us both further humiliation, I stammered: "Gentlemen, you had better let me go; you will get no good of me; as you say, I am an idiot."

My tongue, a traitor, faltered. Seeing the judges cast a hard, triumphant look at M. Emanuel, and hearing the choked tremor of my own voice, I burst into a fit of tears. The emotion was far more anger than grief. Had I been a man and strong, I could have challenged them on the spot—but it was emotion, raw and unbridled, and I would rather be scourged than betray it.

*The incapables!* Could they not see the crude hand of a novice? The subject was classical. When M. Paul dictated the theme, it was new to me; I had no materials. I had gone to the library, read up the facts, constructed a skeleton from dry bones, and tried to breathe life into them. It had been a difficult, anxious struggle, but I had pleasure in the final aim. Whatever knowledge I lacked, I had gathered fresh like wild herbs, shredding them green into the pot. Messieurs Boissec and Rochemorte did not perceive this. They mistook my frantic labor for the work of a ripe scholar.

They would not let me go. I had to sit and write before them. As I dipped my pen into the ink with a shaking hand, surveying the white paper with eyes half-blinded, one judge began to mince apologies. *"Nous agissons dans l'intérêt de la vérité. Nous ne voulons pas vous blesser."*

Scorn gave me nerve. *"Dictate, Monsieur,"* I replied.

Rochemorte chose the theme: *"Human Justice."*

*Human Justice!* What was I to make of it? A blank, cold abstraction, unsuggestive of any inspiring idea. Standing there, sad as Saul and stern as Joab, was M. Emanuel, surrounded by his accusers. At these two men, I looked. I was gathering the courage to tell them I would write no more, that their theme suited me not, and that anyone casting a shadow on M. Emanuel's honor outraged truth itself. I meant to utter all this when, suddenly, a light darted across my memory.

Those two faces, framed by forests of hair, moustache, and whisker—cold, bold, trustless, yet presumptuous—were the same faces that had projected from behind the pillars of a portico, half-frightening me to death on the night of my desolate arrival in Villette. These, I felt morally certain, were the heroes who had driven a friendless foreigner beyond her reckoning, chasing her breathless over the town.

*"Pious mentors!"* I thought. *"Pure guides for youth! If 'Human Justice' were what it ought to be, you two would scarce hold your posts or enjoy your credits."*

Once seized, the idea took flight. *"Human Justice"* rushed before me in a novel guise: a red, random beldame with arms akimbo. I saw her in her house, a den of confusion. Servants called for orders she ignored; beggars waited starving at her door; a swarm of sick, quarrelsome children crawled around her feet, yelling for cure and redress. The honest woman cared for none of these. She had a warm seat by the fire, a short black pipe, and a bottle of Mrs. Sweeny's soothing syrup. She smoked, she sipped, she enjoyed her paradise. Whenever a cry of suffering pierced her ears too keenly, she seized the poker or the hearth-brush.
