Many of my gentle readers will no doubt know the famous menagerie in Regent’s Park, where our finned, feathered, and four-footed fellow creatures are boarded and lodged, and show themselves off for our amusement and instruction. There I wandered one day, my private thoughts clinging to me even as I stopped before the aquarium glass, and to my surprise, found my mind still turning to our old friends the Warrington brothers of Virginia. The most graceful creature gliding through the green water was none other than our homely, familiar friend the flounder—every curve of his body smooth and harmonious, now turning his glossy black back to the light, now baring his soft pale underbelly in a motion so quick and elegant one would never guess he was the same fish we devour unthinkingly in water souchy at Greenwich, never suspecting he could be so beautiful.

As the race of man goes, so too does the race of flounders. See the creature in his proper element, and he is agile, strong, comely. Drag him out onto dry land, and all that grace vanishes: he flaps absurdly against the unforgiving ground, gasping out his weak life before your very eyes. Tenderly lift him before it is too late, and cast him back into the Thames, and he is himself once more—though I need not belabor the old proverb of a fish out of water, I daresay other naturalists have noted the same truth long before me.

Harry Warrington, for many months past, had been floundering far outside his natural element. The moment he stepped back into a life that suited him, health, spirits, strength, and all his old energy flooded back. The tap of an epaulet on his shoulder transformed him entirely. He fell utterly in love with his new army profession, devoured every detail of drill and protocol, and mastered them with the eager quickness of a boy who has found his calling. Had I half the wit of my friend Lorrequer, I would follow Harry into camp, watch him on the march, at the regimental mess, striding the parade ground, share a dozen roistering evenings with his brother officers, camp with him under canvas, and explain every nuance of war’s maneuvers and the life of a soldier. As it is, the reader must fill in the bright colors of the picture themselves, drawing on their own imagination and experience, and picture Mr. Harry Warrington in his smart new red coat trimmed with yellow facings, happier than he has ever been in his life, proud to bear the King’s colors and eager to learn and perform every duty of his post.

George Warrington, we may be sure, was bursting with pride at his brother’s success. The two young men admired each other unreservedly, and recognized one another’s finest qualities without a hint of jealousy. George penned a warm, affectionate letter home to their mother in Virginia, recounting all the praise he had heard of Harry’s gallantry—praise Harry’s own modesty would never have repeated aloud. He described how Harry had won his first commission by his own courage, and asked Madam Esmond’s permission to split the cost of purchasing him a higher rank, as a small recompense for the fact that George’s unexpected return from the dead had stripped Harry of the entire inheritance he had long considered rightfully his. George noted that Harry had run up far more debts than he ever would have dared as a mere younger son, on the false assumption he stood to inherit the whole Esmond estate, and he counted it a joy, a thank-offering for his own narrow escape from captivity, to contribute generously to secure his brother’s future.

Having finished his account of Harry’s affairs, George turned to his own, and broached a subject close to his heart. He reminded his mother that the kindest, truest friends the boys had found in England were Mr. and Mrs. Lambert, the latter an old schoolfellow of Madam Esmond’s youth. Where their own blood relations had been cold and calculating, the Lamberts had opened their hearts and their home to the pair, unreserved and generous. General Lambert was respected by the entire army and beloved by everyone who knew him, and Mrs. Lambert had doted on both boys as if they were her own sons. Now, George wrote, he had formed an attachment to the General’s eldest daughter Theo, on which he believed the whole of his future happiness depended, and he begged his mother’s leave and approval for the match. He had not yet made a formal proposal to the young lady or her parents, but he had made no secret of his feelings, and he was certain both Theo and her family looked on his suit favorably. Theo, he wrote, was the most dutiful, gentle, pious creature alive, and he had no doubt she would prove a loving, devoted daughter to his mother as well. In page after page, he painted her as a paragon of perfection, and swore that his entire future joy hung on winning her hand.

The sentimental reader may ask why I do not reproduce this letter here, given that the full Warrington correspondence sits in my hands. The answer is simple: it would be cruel to lay bare the secrets of a young man’s first love, to record his incoherent vows, his wild raptures, the soft foolish fancies of his passion in cold black and white. Shall we play eavesdropper at twilight window embrasures, count every sigh and hand-shake, bottle hot tears, press a stethoscope to the delicate young breast of a girl in love to track its every throb? I protest, love is a sacred thing. Wherever you see it in the world—flaring sudden between two pairs of eyes, softening a mother’s gaze as she looks down at the baby in her lap, lighting a father’s face as his daughter whirls across the dance floor with her captain, glinting in the tired old eyes of a couple who have walked life’s path together for half a century—do not pry. It is a sin not just to kiss and tell, but to tell of kisses. Anyone admitted to that sweet mystery should hush about it. Woe to the man who would vulgarize the sacred secrets of love: you cannot dine with him without him printing every word of your private conversation, cannot sail with him without him throwing you over to the public eye.

While Harry has been off smelling gunpowder, charging enemy lines, and carrying wounded comrades out of the line of fire, George has been far more peacefully occupied: perhaps penning sonnets to the curve of Theo’s eyebrow, pacing the dark street under her window late at night to watch the small lamp glimmer through her chamber curtains, inventing a hundred trivial excuses to send tiny notes that never quite need a reply, and somehow always get one, culling lines from his favorite poets and fresh Covent Garden flowers for her delight. He walks to St. James’s Church with her, sits side by side in the same pew sharing the same Prayer-book, and never hears a single word of the sermon, so entirely is his mind full of her. He is unfailingly affectionate to every member of Theo’s family: dotes on her little schoolboy brother and sister, teases her sharp-witted younger sister Hetty, and is so warm and attentive to Mrs. Lambert that General Lambert jokes that the matron is half in love with George herself. It is a known truth, after all, that while fathers sometimes glower at the arrival of their future son-in-law, mothers turn sentimental, and live their own young courtship all over again through their daughter’s.

Gumbo and Sady, the Warrington boys’ enslaved servants, are forever trotting back and forth between Southampton Row, where the Lamberts live, and Dean Street, where George has his lodgings. All summer long, the group plans endless outings: trips to Ranelagh, strolls on Hampstead Heath, evenings at Vauxhall, concerts at Marylebone Gardens, and a hundred other small pleasures. George insists the famous tragedy he has been writing needs to be copied out fair for the stage, and who has a more delicate, beautiful Italian hand than Miss Theo? The sheets pass back and forth between them, every bundle wrapped in tiny notes of thanks, teasing questions, and soft, unspoken admiration. I hold the very packet of her letters in my hands, marked in George’s neat, precise script: “T’s letters, 1758-9.” Shall we pry them open and lay their tender secrets before the public eye? Those gentle, blushing lines were written for one pair of eyes alone. Years later, no doubt, George read them over with sweet pangs of remembrance, long after they were man and wife. It would be a sacrilege to expose them to strangers. Take my word for it: the young lady’s letters are modest, pure, and full of quiet warmth, the gentleman’s full of respect and soft devotion.

Theo is not, perhaps, the most beautiful woman in London. A hundred other girls, from Coelia to Ardelia to their own cousin Flora, are far more striking to look at. But her sweet face pleases George better than any other in the whole world. She is not the wittiest, or the most learned, but her voice is the dearest sound he knows, and in her company he finds himself sharper, more eloquent, more generous and clever than he ever is anywhere else—so of course he delights in being with her. And as thank Heaven I have never thought so poorly of women as to believe them entirely fair, I need not tell you that Theo endows her George with every virtue, wit, and grace under the sun.

When George’s letter arrived at Castlewood in Virginia, it sparked no small commotion around Madam Esmond’s fireside. The boy was in love, and wished to marry! It was only natural, and would keep him out of mischief. So long as he chose a well-bred, Christian young woman, Madam Esmond saw no harm in it.

“I knew every unmarried woman in England would be setting their caps at him,” sniffed Mrs. Mountain, her lifelong companion. “They all fancy his wealth is as vast as his estate. He never says if the girl has a penny of her own. I fear she has nothing at all.”

“I have no doubt plenty of people here at home would set their caps at him too,” Madam Esmond replied, fixing her dependent with a sharp grim look. “They would all try to catch Mr. Esmond Warrington for their own daughters, girls no richer than this Miss Lambert may be.”

“I suppose you are pointing at me!” Mrs. Mountain cried, stung. “My Fanny is poor, as you say, and it is very kind of you to remind me of her poverty!”

“I said people would set their caps at him. If the cap fits you, tant pis, as my old father used to say.”

“You think I am scheming to marry Fanny to George? After all these years we have lived together, you have a very low opinion of us!”

“My dear Mountain, I know you far too well to imagine you could ever fancy your daughter would be a suitable match for a gentleman of Mr. Esmond’s rank and station,” Madam replied, drawing herself up with great dignity.

“Fanny Parker was every bit as good as Molly Benson at school! My Mountain’s daughter is as good as any Lambert’s daughter in England!” Mrs. Mountain exclaimed.

“Then you did indeed plan to marry her to my son! I shall write to George at once and tell him how sorry I am to have disappointed you.” We may well suspect Mrs. Mountain had long harbored ambitious hopes for her daughter, for nothing else would explain the rage she flew into at the news of George’s intended match.

In her reply to her son, Madam Esmond wrote that she was delighted by the fraternal affection George showed, and entirely agreed that some compensation was due to Harry for the debts he had run up under the false impression he would inherit the whole estate. She would gladly split the cost of purchasing Harry’s higher commission, she said, and thanked Heaven that Harry’s own gallantry had won him his first step. This generosity to his brother, she added pointedly, was far more satisfactory than other foolish extravagances she would not name. The extravagance she alluded to, of course, was the ransom George had paid to the French captain’s poor family after his release from captivity—a sum Madam Esmond had flatly refused to reimburse. Her will was as stubborn as her son’s own, and George’s persistence in paying the sum she dismissed as foolish pride and obstinacy.

After paying his own ransom, settling all Harry’s gambling debts, and putting aside half the sum for his brother’s new commission, George found a large chunk of his personal patrimony was already spent. But he made the sacrifice cheerfully. His mother never ceased reminding him in every letter that he was descended from the noble old Esmond line, and must uphold the dignity of his family at all costs. She sent him endless commissions to buy goods in England, and though she had sent him barely a penny in remittances so far, she constantly exhorted him to move in the highest circles of London society, attend court as his ancestors had done, and live in a style befitting his rank. George took her at her word, assuming his mother would send the funds as soon as his own ran short, and obeyed her injunctions to the letter. The surviving Esmond papers from that period are full of bills for elegant dinner parties, tailor’s receipts for court suits, payments for fine liveries for his servants and chairmen, and horse-dealer’s bills for his fine team of carriage horses. George was no wild spendthrift like his brother: he gambled never, kept no racehorses, but his household was generous, his equipages handsome, and his purse always open. Everyone knew he was the heir to a vast Virginia estate, and no one doubted he was rolling in wealth.

The honest Lamberts were not mercenary people by any means, but they could not help but be pleased to see their eldest daughter growing attached to a young man of such sterling character, fine wit, noble family, and boundless expectations. George was a true gentleman: a little cold and reserved towards strangers, especially the lords and ladies of high society, but never supercilious, unfailingly courteous to all women, and unfailingly tender, lively, and warm to the small circle of people he loved. No wonder Theo had come to think him the best man in the whole wide world—even better than her dear papa. Great love transforms a man, making him kinder, sharper, sweeter, in the eyes of the woman who holds his heart, than every other man alive. We will not linger over their soft private whispers any longer. Those words were never meant to be written down for strangers. Lay down the book for a moment, if you are of a sentimental turn, and think back on your own first love. It may be decades dead and buried, but for a moment it rises from the grave, smiles, whispers, just as it did when it clung to your arm, and dropped hot fresh tears on your heart. It is long gone now, of course. The vase still sits on your shelf, but the roses within are dust. The shore is still there, but the ship that once moored there raised its anchors long ago, and sailed over the horizon forever.

But that is idle sentiment, and has nothing to do with George and Theo. The whole Lambert family was perfectly satisfied with the state of affairs between the two, and though George had not yet popped the formal decisive question, everyone in the house already knew exactly what Theo’s answer would be. Mrs. Lambert thought the question was taking far too long to come.

“Psha, my dear,” General Lambert laughed at her. “There is time enough. Theo is barely seventeen. George is not yet forty, for goodness sake, and he has to wait to get his mother’s approval from Virginia first. What is the rush?”

“But what if Madam Esmond refuses?” his wife fretted.

“That will be a sad day for all of us,” the General replied. “But let us imagine she will consent. I cannot fancy anyone in the whole world refusing Theo anything she has set her heart on—and I am certain her heart is entirely set on this match.”

So the whole household waited with bated breath for Madam Esmond’s reply, trembling daily for fear that French privateers patrolling the Atlantic might seize the packet ship carrying the precious, long-awaited letter.
