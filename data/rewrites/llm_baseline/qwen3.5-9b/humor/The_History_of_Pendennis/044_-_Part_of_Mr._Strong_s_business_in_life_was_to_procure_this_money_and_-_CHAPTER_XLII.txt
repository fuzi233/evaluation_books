Mr. Pen's life had recently taken a turn toward literary ambition. Following his defeat at Oxford and his return home, he had spent his days composing various works, including a substantial draft of a novel. Written while he was drowning in youthful embarrassments—both romantic and financial—the book was a tempest of Byronic despair, Wertherian melancholy, and Mephistophelian bitterness. Having just begun studying German, he had naturally mimicked his favorite poets, pouring his anguished soul onto the page. Tears had once stained the manuscript, and his pencil had slashed furiously across the paper. Yet, years later, he lacked the energy or the tears to recreate that "early dew." Reading his old work now, he found the passages pompous and the emotions weak. He recognized the borrowed phrases he had once proudly claimed as original, and the specific memories attached to certain lines made him blush at the faint echoes of his past self. Just as one finds camel tracks and withered grass in a desert, signaling a vanished oasis, Pen realized his own inner landscape was no longer green; the *fons lacrymarum* was dry.

One morning, he recounted this metaphor to Warrington, who sat smoking his pipe. Excited, Pen slammed his manuscript onto the table, rattling the tea service and causing the milk to slosh violently. He explained that the book, titled *Leaves from the Life-book of Walter Lorraine*, was born of his infatuation with Miss Amory, to whom he had secretly dedicated verses hidden in trees.

"That was a clever pun," Warrington observed, puffing his pipe. "'Amory' becoming 'Amori.' Show some of the rubbish." Using his fire-tongs, he snatched the manuscript and began reading aloud: *"False as thou art beautiful! Heartless as thou art fair! Mockery of Passion!"*

"Stop!" Pen cried, lunging for the book. "Don't read that. That's about my second flame, Lady Mirabel. I saw her last night at Lady Whiston's. She invited me to a party, seemingly only because she saw me talking to the famous Monsieur Dubois. Imagine meeting both my flames at the same table!"

"Two flames?" Warrington scoffed. "More like two piles of cold ash."

"They're both in the book," Pen insisted. "Leonora, who marries the Duke, is based on Magnus Charters from Oxford; Miss Amory is Neaera. Despite its flaws, there's good stuff here. If Bungay won't publish it, maybe Bacon will."

"All poets are humbugs," Warrington declared philosophically. "We suffer, we cry, we write it down, and we sell it. Once a man starts selling his feelings for coin, he's a fraud. Even Homer and Shakespeare are just bigger versions of you, pigmies trying to cash in on their misery."

"But surely," Pen argued, defending his craft, "poets possess a unique sensibility. They see and feel more keenly. Why shouldn't a man sell his sentimental thoughts as easily as you sell your political opinions or Paley sells his legal knowledge? A poet paints his experiences on paper just as a painter paints a landscape. If I have the talent to write an epic, I'll try. If I can just crack a joke, I'll do that."

"Not a bad speech," Warrington conceded, though he remained skeptical. "But that doesn't stop you from being a humbug."

"What about Shakespeare?"

"Their names aren't spoken in the same breath as yours," Warrington retorted. "There are men, and then there are... amateurs."

Pen countered that Shakespeare wrote for money just like anyone else, leaving Warrington momentarily speechless. It became clear that *Walter Lorraine* was a direct product of Pen's private griefs and follies. Although he had stored the manuscript in a chest with his old academic regalia for several years—not to let it mature, but simply because he didn't know where else to put it—he now felt ready to judge it. He wasn't convinced it was a masterpiece, but compared to the current flood of fashionable novels, he thought it held genuine passion and vigor.

After reviewing the text, Warrington admitted, "There's some good stuff in it, Pen. It has a fresh greenness. The bloom fades from poetry once you start shaving and wearing curling irons. Springtime was the best."

"Do you think we can sell it?" Pen asked hopefully.

"I'd rather burn it," Warrington joked, picking up the manuscript with his tongs. "But since you value it so much, let's not destroy it. Let's take it to Egypt and sell it for silver, gold, and liquor. We'll give it a modern touch, trim the overly green parts, add some satire, and sell it to Bacon or Bungay for a hundred piasters."

Delighted by this high praise, Pen agreed. Warrington playfully slapped him on the cheek, turning his face bright red, and Pen retired to his room to revise the manuscript accordingly. Meanwhile, Warrington worked his charm on the literary agents for Messrs. Bacon and Bungay, exaggerating Pen's status as a fashionable society figure. When the ambitious publisher Mr. Bacon arrived to inspect the ramshackle rooms shared by Pen and Warrington, he expected poverty-stricken impostors. Instead, he found invitations to balls hosted by the Marchioness of Steyne and other high society figures.

"You see?" Warrington explained smoothly. "Pendennis dines with the elite, yet he's perfectly happy eating mutton chops with us. He's a true gentleman."

Impressed by the social connections hinted at by the cards, Bacon pressed Pen for details about the novel. Pen feigned indifference, claiming he was already negotiating with Bungay, which sparked a bidding war between the two rivals. Ultimately, thanks to Warrington's manipulation and Pen's own luck, the novel was sold for a handsome sum.

Rather than splurging on a carriage or moving to a luxury flat—as his Uncle Major Pendennis angrily suggested, dismissing the genre as mere "rubbish"—Pen chose a different path. Remembering his debts, he wrote a tender letter to Laura, enclosing an installment that nearly cleared his obligation. The gesture moved everyone around him: the Widow Pendennis wept, and Dr. Portman, overcome with emotion, kissed his wife and quoted poetry about the joy of prosperity. For Pen, the real victory wasn't just the money, but the opportunity to repay those who had supported him.
