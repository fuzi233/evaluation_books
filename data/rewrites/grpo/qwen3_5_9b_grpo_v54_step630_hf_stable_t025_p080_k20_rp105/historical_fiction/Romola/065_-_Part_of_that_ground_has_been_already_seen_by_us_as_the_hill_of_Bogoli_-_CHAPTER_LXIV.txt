The Prophet in His Cell.

The announcement of Tito's arrival had preceded him, and Fra Niccolò, Savonarola's secretary, ushered him immediately up the spiraling stairs into the monastery's long, echoing corridors. These halls, lined with cells, bore witness to the delicate artistry of Fra Angelico; his frescoes, soft as rainbows on melting clouds, broke the austerity of the stone, casting sudden, ethereal reflections of the Madonna in radiant glory and the Divine Infant with perpetual promise upon the unaccustomed eye.

It was a hour of quietude within the monastery, and most cells stood empty. Light filtered through narrow windows to illuminate nothing but bare walls, hard pallets, and crucifixes. Even at the far end of a long corridor, in the inner cell accessible only through an antechamber where the Prior typically sat at his desk, a single shaft of light fell upon a solitary figure. It was a man in the long white Dominican tunic and scapulary, kneeling with bowed head before a crucifix. To the casual observer, he might have been any ordinary Fra Girolamo, confessing only trivial sins—thoughts of impropriety during choir service or a fleeting spite when a fellow monk dropped ink upon his illuminated breviary—a soul content merely to climb the narrow ladder of prayer, fasting, and obedience toward Paradise. Yet beneath this plain white garment beat a heart pulsing with a consciousness alien to the average monk, perhaps incomprehensible to any man who has not traversed a tumultuous inner life. Here, irrevocable errors and lapses from truth were inextricably entwined with noble purposes and sincere beliefs; self-justifying expediency was woven into the very fabric of a great work that the whole being seemed unable to abandon, just as the body cannot cease trembling before the objects of hope and fear. Consequently, no matter the course chosen, perfect repose for the conscience remained elusive.

Savonarola was not merely in the posture of prayer; Latin supplications flowed from his lips, yet he was not truly praying. He had collapsed to his knees seeking a calmness that would serve as a warrant against the resolutions urged upon him by crowding thoughts and passions, fearing he was being wrested from Divine support. But the previsions and impulses working within him for the last hour were too imperious. While his hands pressed against his face and his lips uttered *"Cor mundum crea in me,"* his mind remained cluttered with images of the snares his enemies had prepared, busy constructing arguments to justify himself against their taunts.

His defense was required not only against his opponents. That morning, he had received fresh proof that his own friends and followers were as eager to press for the Trial by Fire as his adversaries. They desired and tacitly expected that he would finally accept the challenge, evoking the long-awaited miracle to dissipate doubt and triumph over malignity. Had he not declared that God would reveal Himself at the fitting time? To the plain Florentines, eager to settle party questions, no time seemed more fitting than the present. Certainly, if Fra Domenico walked through the flames unhurt, *that* would be a miracle, and the fervor of that good brother served as a cheering augury. Yet Savonarola was acutely conscious that his reasons for refusal had failed to quell the secret longing of his followers to see him accept the challenge.

It was impossible for him to satisfy them. With bitter distress, he realized he could no longer resist the prosecution of the trial in Fra Domenico's case. He had not spoken a falsehood when declaring his belief in a future supernatural attestation; however, his constitution was such that while it was easy for him to believe in a distant, undefined miracle screened by strong reasons, and even easier to believe in inward miracles like his own prophetic inspiration, it was insurmountably difficult for him to believe in the probability of a miracle pressing all its details upon his imagination—a miracle demanding not just belief but exceptional action.

Savonarola's nature held opposing tendencies in almost equal strength: a passionate sensibility, impatient of definite thought and flooding every idea with emotion, alternating with a keen perception of outward facts and a vigorous practical judgment. In this case, the latter characteristics were stimulated by an acute physical sensitiveness that gave overpowering force to the conception of pain and destruction as a necessary sequence of past experiences. The promptitude with which men consent to touch red-hot iron with a wet finger is not measured by their theoretical acceptance of the impossibility of burning; practical belief depends on what is most strongly represented in the mind at a given moment. With the Frate's constitution, when the Trial by Fire was urged upon his imagination as an immediate demand, it was impossible for him to believe that he or any other man could walk through the flames unhurt—impossible for him to believe that even if he resolved to offer himself, he would not shrink at the last moment.

But the Florentines were unlikely to make such fine distinctions. To the common run of mankind, finding moral questions easy and judging conduct by concise alternatives is a proof of mental vigor. Nothing seemed plainer than that a man who declared God would not leave him without a miraculous guarantee, yet drew back when the test was proposed, had said what he did not believe. Were not Fra Domenico and Fra Mariano, along with scores of Piagnoni, ready to enter the fire? What caused their superior courage if not superior faith? Savonarola could not explain his conduct satisfactorily to his friends, nor thoroughly to himself. Our naked feelings hasten to clothe themselves in propositions lying at hand among our store of opinions; to give a true account of what passes within us requires something besides sincerity, even when that sincerity is unmixed. In these very moments, as Savonarola knelt in audible prayer, he had ceased to hear the words on his lips. They were drowned by argumentative voices within him shaping reasons for an outward audience.

"To appeal to heaven for a miracle by a rash acceptance of a challenge, which is a mere snare prepared by ignoble foes, would be a tempting of God, and the appeal would not be responded to. Let the Pope's legate come, let the ambassadors of all great Powers come and promise that the calling of a General Council and the reform of the Church shall hang on the miracle, and I will enter the flames, trusting that God will not withhold His seal from that great work. Until then, I reserve myself for higher duties directly laid upon me: it is not permitted to me to leap from the chariot for the sake of wrestling with every loud vaunter. But Fra Domenico's invincible zeal to enter the trial may be the sign of a Divine vocation, may be a pledge that the miracle—"

No! When Savonarola brought his mind close to the threatened scene in the Piazza, imagining a human body entering the fire, his belief recoiled. It was not an event his imagination could simply see; he felt it with shuddering vibrations traveling to the extremities of his sensitive fingers. The miracle could not be. Nay, the trial itself was not to happen; he was warranted in doing all in his power to hinder it. The fuel might be ready in the Piazza, the people assembled, the formalities gone through—all this was perhaps inevitable now, and he could no longer resist it without bringing dishonor upon himself, yes, and therefore upon the cause of God. But it was not really intended that the Franciscan should enter the fire; while *he* hung back, there would be means to prevent Fra Domenico's entrance. At the very worst, if Fra Domenico were compelled to enter, he should carry the consecrated Host with him, and with that Mystery in his hand, there might be a warrant for expecting that the ordinary effects of fire would be stayed; or, more probably, this demand would be resisted, becoming a final obstacle to the trial.

These intentions could not be avowed; he must appear frankly to await the trial and trust in its issue. This dissidence between inward reality and outward seeming was not the Christian simplicity after which he had striven through years of youth and prime, which he had preached as a chief fruit of the Divine life. In the stress and heat of the day, with cheeks burning and shouts ringing in the ears, who is so blest as to remember the yearnings of the cool, silent morning and know that he has not belied them?

"O God, it is for the sake of the people—because they are blind—because their faith depends on me. If I put on sackcloth and cast myself among the ashes, who will take up the standard and head the battle? Have I not been led by a way I knew not to the work that lies before me?"

The conflict could not end. In the effort at prayerful pleading, the uneasy mind laved its smart continually in thoughts of the greatness of that task which there was no other man to fulfill if he forsook it. It was not a thing of everyday that a man should be inspired with the vision and daring that made a sacred rebel.

Even the words of prayer had died away. He continued to kneel, but his mind was filled with images of results to be felt through all Europe; the sense of immediate difficulties was being lost in the glow of that vision, when the knocking at the door announced the expected visit.

Savonarola drew on his mantle before leaving his cell, as was his custom when receiving visitors. With that immediate response to any appeal from without which belongs to a power-loving nature accustomed to make its power felt by speech, he met Tito with a glance as self-possessed and strong as if he had risen from resolution instead of conflict.

Tito did not kneel but simply offered a greeting of profound deference, which Savonarola received quietly without sacerdotal words. Desiring him to be seated, he asked at once: "Your business is something of weight, my son, that could not be conveyed through others?"

"Assuredly, father, else I should not have presumed to ask it. I will not trespass on your time by any proem. I gathered from a remark of Messer Domenico Mazzinghi that you might be glad to make use of the next special courier sent to France with despatches from the Ten. I must entreat you to pardon me if I have been too officious; but inasmuch as Messer Domenico is at this moment away at his villa, I wished to apprise you that a courier carrying important letters is about to depart for Lyons at daybreak tomorrow."

The muscles of Fra Girolamo's face were eminently under command, as must be the case with all men whose personality is powerful, and in deliberate speech he was habitually cautious, confiding his intentions to none without necessity. But under any strong mental stimulus, his eyes were liable to a dilatation and added brilliancy that no strength of will could control. He looked steadily at Tito and did not answer immediately, as if considering whether the information he had just heard met any purpose of his.

Tito, whose glance never seemed observant but rarely let anything escape it, had expected precisely that dilatation and flash of Savonarola's eyes which he had noted on other occasions. He saw it, and then immediately busied himself in adjusting his gold fibula, which had become misaligned, seemingly implying that he awaited an answer patiently.

The fact was that Savonarola had expected to receive this intimation from Domenico Mazzinghi, one of the Ten, an ardent disciple whom he had already employed to write a private letter to the Florentine ambassador in France, preparing the way for a letter to the French king himself in Savonarola's handwriting, which now lay ready in the desk at his side. It was a letter calling on the king to assist in summoning a General Council that might reform the abuses of the Church and begin by deposing Pope Alexander, who was not rightfully Pope, being a vicious unbeliever elected by corruption and governing by simony.

This fact was not what Tito knew, but what his constructive talent, guided by subtle indications, had led him to guess and hope.

"It is true, my son," said Savonarola quietly, "it is true I have letters which I would gladly send by safe conveyance under cover to our ambassador. Our community of San Marco, as you know, has affairs in France, being, amongst other things, responsible for a debt to that singularly wise and experienced Frenchman, Signor Philippe de Comines, on the library of the Medici, which we purchased; but I apprehend that Domenico Mazzinghi himself may return to the city before evening, and I should gain more time for preparation of the letters if I waited to deposit them in his hands."

"Assuredly, reverend father, that might be better on all grounds, except one, namely, that if anything occurred to hinder Messer Domenico's return, the despatch of the letters would require either that I should come to San Marco again at a late hour, or that you should send them to me by your secretary; and I am aware that you wish to guard against the false inferences which might be drawn from a too frequent communication between yourself and any officer of the government." In throwing out this difficulty, Tito felt that the more unwillingness the Frate showed to trust him, the more certain he would be of his conjecture.

Savonarola was silent; but while he kept his mouth firm, a slight glow rose in his face with the suppressed excitement growing within him. It would be a critical moment—that in which he delivered the letter out of his own hands.

"It is most probable that Messer Domenico will return in time," said Tito, affecting to consider the Frate's determination settled, and rising from his chair as he spoke. "With your permission, I will take my leave, father, not to trespass on your time when my errand is done; but as I may not be favoured with another interview, I venture to confide to you—what is not yet known to others, except to the magnificent Ten—that I contemplate resigning my secretaryship and leaving Florence shortly. Am I presuming too much on your interest in stating what relates chiefly to myself?"

"Speak on, my son," said the Frate; "I desire to know your prospects."
