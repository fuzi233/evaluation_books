Back in the early days of the Regency of George the Magnificent—you know, the portly fellow who spent more on waistcoats than most aristocrats spent on their entire estates—there lived in the tiny west-of-England town of Clavering a gentleman by the name of Pendennis. Old locals still swore they could remember seeing his name painted on a board hung beneath a gilded pestle and mortar, over the door of a dinky little Bath shop where Mr. Pendennis worked as an apothecary and surgeon. He’d attend to sick gentlemen in their bedrooms, help ladies through those “most interesting periods” no one dared name aloud, and still stoop across the counter to sell a brown-paper plaster to a farmer’s wife, or flog toothbrushes, hair powder, and fancy London perfume to anyone who could spare the pennies. The good folk of Clavering had memories far stickier than their metropolis counterparts, so none of these very humble details ever quite got swept under the genteel carpet later.

The kicker? That same apothecary who doled out penny salts and overpriced Windsor soap to stray customers was a university-educated gentleman, with a Somersetshire family tree so old it stretched back to the Druids—who knows, maybe even further, to the blokes who first invented mud? His lot only deigned to marry Normans *very* late in their family history, thank you very much, and they were distantly related to every half-decent aristocratic clan in Wales and Brittany. He’d nearly finished his degree at Cambridge too, and would have gone on to great things, if his father hadn’t dropped dead insolvent halfway through his second year, forcing poor Pen to strap on an apron and pick up the pestle. He loathed the trade with every fiber of his being, and only took the job at the urging of his mother’s brother, a coarse, low-born London apothecary whose class his father had apparently demeaned himself to marry.

No sooner was his apprenticeship up than he fled his crass relative and set up that little Bath shop on his own. Poverty nipped at his heels for years; he could barely afford to keep the gilt pestle from tarnishing and his bedridden mother fed and comfortable. Then fate intervened in the silliest way possible: Lady Ribstone was being carried to the Bath Rooms by a very drunk Irish chairman, who stumbled straight into Pendennis’s doorpost, rammed the pole of his chair right through the shiniest pink glass bottle in the shop window. Her ladyship screamed loud enough to rouse every stray cat on Milsom Street, Pendennis hauled her inside, plied her with cinnamon and sal-volatile, and so the tide turned.

His manners were so impossibly soft and gentlemanly that Sir Pepin Ribstone, of Codlingbury, Somerset, instantly named him the personal apothecary to his entire sprawling household. When young Master Ribstone came home from Eton for Christmas, overate himself into a fever, and Pendennis nursed him back to health with endless patience, he was made for life. Bath’s high society adopted him wholesale, the ladies adored him, and step by step he climbed the social ladder: first his scruffy little shop got a fancy remodel, then he banned all sales of toothbrushes and perfume as unworthy of a man of his ancient Druid lineage, then he shut the shop entirely and kept only a tiny surgery run by a genteel young assistant, then he bought a gig with a driver, and finally he splashed out on a one-horse closed carriage, with the full Pendennis family crest blazoned all over the panels. His poor old bedridden mom got wheeled to her bedroom window to see him step into it, and blubbered “What would Arthur say now? That boy never once came to see my dearest Johnny through all his years of starving and scraping!”
“Captain Pendennis is with his regiment in India, mother,” John would mutter, mortified, darting a glance at his young assistant Mr. Parkins. “And I’d much rather you didn’t call me Johnny in front of the lad, if you please.”

It wasn’t long before his mother stopped calling him Johnny, or any other pet name at all, and the house felt awfully quiet without her grumbly but loving voice. He moved his night bell straight into her old bedroom, dragged the great big four-poster bed in there, and slept there himself. He was over forty by then, before the Napoleonic Wars ended, before George the Magnificent took the throne, long before our story even starts. And what’s a newly minted gentleman without his pedigree? He dragged his own long-forgotten family tree out of a trunk, just like Sterne’s old soldier fetching his sword, had it framed and glazed, and hung it in his drawing-room between prints of Codlingbury House and his old Cambridge college.

Around the time his mother died, one of his regular patients, the virtuous old Lady Pontypool, kicked the bucket too. That lady was the daughter of the 12th Earl of Bareacres, great-great aunt to the current earl, widow of both the 2nd Lord Pontypool and the Reverend Jonas Wales of Clifton’s Armageddon Chapel. For five years before her death, she’d been waited on hand and foot by Miss Helen Thistlewood, a distant poor relation of the Bareacres clan, whose father Lieutenant Thistlewood had died fighting at the Battle of Copenhagen. Helen got free board and lodging under Lady Pontypool’s roof, sure—but she also got five full years of the kind of petty, vicious tyranny only old noblewomen can inflict on a helpless orphan. Pendennis, who popped in to see his patient twice a day, couldn’t help but notice how sweet and gentle the girl was, even when the old bat screamed at her for trivial nonsense. It was while they were all trundling to Bath Abbey in the fourth mourning coach to drop Lady Pontypool’s remains off that he looked at her pale, sweet face and decided he had to propose—so much so that his pulse spiked to at least 90 beats a minute.

He was over twenty years older than her, and never the most fiery of men. Maybe he’d had a young love he’d had to snuff out, the way you drown a litter of unwanted blind kittens, all the best romances of the poor having to be disposed of sooner or later. At 43, he was a quiet, neat little gentleman in black stockings, with a bald spot on his head. A few days after the funeral he called on her, pretended to take her pulse, held onto her hand, and asked her where she’d go now that the Pontypools were boxing up every plate, chair, and teacup in the house, locking everything in green baize chests under three keys, and carting it all away right under her nose.
She teared up and said she had no clue. The old lady had left her a thousand pounds, she said, but she’d probably have to move into a boarding house or work at a school. She had no other plans.
Still holding her cold little hand, Pendennis told her she could come live with him. He was old, he admitted, way too old for such a blooming young lady, but he was well-born, honourable, even-tempered, his fortune was growing every year, and he was all alone in the world—he’d spend the rest of his days making her happy, if she’d have him. The whole speech he’d composed in bed that morning, and rehearsed word for word in his carriage on the ride over.
Helen probably could have dreamed up a more romantic match, if she’d had a choice: a stuffy little man who rapped his teeth when he was nervous, who bowed extra hard to the butler and simpered at the lady’s maid the same way he used to back when he was a mere apothecary running errands for rich patrons. But she also knew how good he’d been to his mother, how honourable and prudent he was, so she blushed bright red, curtsied as low as her knees would let her, and asked if she could please, please think the very kind proposal over.

They got married in the dead, dull Bath season, when all the fancy people were in London. Pendennis, through a friend who was a Member of the Royal College of Surgeons, booked them lodgings in Holles Street, Cavendish Square, brought her up in a chaise and pair, took her to the theatres, the parks, and the Chapel Royal, showed her the crowds arriving for a royal drawing-room, and gave her every fancy London pleasure she could possibly imagine. He left calling cards on Lord Pontypool, the Earl of Bareacres, and his oldest patrons Sir Pepin and Lady Ribstone. Bareacres ignored the card entirely. Pontypool called on them, complimented Helen, and said Lady Pontypool would come visit soon—though all that ever came was her footman, who dropped off her mistress’s card and an invitation to a concert five weeks later. The Ribstones, though, actually invited them to a grand dinner, a story Pendennis bragged about right up till the day he died.

His entire life’s secret ambition was to be seen as a proper gentleman, no strings attached. It took him years of careful saving, but fortune smiled on him: he dumped a pile of cash into buying a little house and estate right next to the village of Clavering, and he was so proud the day he walked his own acres that he could barely speak, never mind admit to anyone how giddy he was. He also bought shares in a wildly successful copper mine that doubled his fortune, sold his Bath apothecary business to Mr. Parkins for a fat pile of cash and a lifetime annuity, and retired forever from pestles, pills, and plasters.

His son Arthur was eight years old when he moved to Fairoaks Park, which explains why little Pen barely remembered the Bath shop existing at all—his dad never once mentioned it. He called in the local Clavering doctor to treat his own family, retired his old black medical breeches and stockings forever, started wearing bottle-green coats with brass buttons and drab gaiters to market and quarter sessions, and acted like he’d been a landed gentleman his whole entire life. He stood at his lodge gate waving gravely at the passing coachmen and guards, founded the Clavering Book Club, set up the Samaritan Soup and Blanket Society, and even successfully lobbied to move the local mail route away from the nearby dumpy village of Cacklefield and through Clavering instead. At church he was an active vestryman, every Thursday he trundled round the market stalls feeling oats, punching geese in the breast, chatting with the farmers like he’d been doing it his whole life, and if anyone dared call him “Doctor” he got huffy. The only title he’d answer to was Squire.

Before anyone could blink, half a dozen “ancestor portraits” turned up hung round his oak dining room. He swore blind every last one of them was a genuine Lely or Van Dyck, and if anyone asked who the figures were, he’d vaguely say they were old Pendennises, don’t you know? Roger Pendennis who fought at Agincourt, Arthur Pendennis who charged at Crecy, General Pendennis who won glory at Blenheim and Oudenarde. His wife Helen clearly thought these stories were utter rubbish, and always tried to change the subject whenever he started going on about them—but little Arthur believed every single word. Those long-dead Pendennis warriors were as real to him as Robinson Crusoe, Peter Wilkins, or the Seven Champions of Christendom he read about in his nursery books.

His total fortune never topped 800 pounds a year, so he couldn’t afford to hang out with the very top tier of county nobility, but he had a perfectly comfortable little set of B-list gentry friends, the sort of people who lived close enough to the roses to smell the posh scent, even if they weren’t blooming themselves. They dined each other twice a year, travelling a dozen miles across the shire to show off their silver and eat overcooked roast beef till midnight. The only downside was the hordes of Clavering townsfolk who kept poking their noses into the house: nosy Mrs. Pybus was always snooping in Helen’s conservatories, messing up her soup ticket distributions, and swaggering Captain Glanders of the 50th Dragoon Guards was forever hanging round the stables trying to rope Pendennis into his endless petty feuds with the vicar, the postmaster, and the grammar school headmaster for beating his son too hard. The Pendennises thanked their lucky stars Fairoaks House was a full mile outside the village, or they’d never have had five minutes of peace.

The Fairoaks lawn sloped straight down to the tiny little river Brawl, and across the water were the overgrown woods of Clavering Park, Sir Francis Clavering’s estate. The big Clavering house was all shuttered up, a grand freestone palace with huge staircases, marble statues, and a grand portico that got a full page spread in *The Beauties of England and Wales*. Sir Francis’s grandfather had gone broke building the ridiculous palace, his son had finished the family ruin by living in it, and the current Sir Francis was off gallivanting abroad somewhere, no one rich enough to rent the massive mouldy pile. Little Arthur spent hours wandering its empty, creaking halls as a boy, half-terrified he’d run into a ghost. At sunset, the view from Fairoaks lawn was stunning: the grass and the trees turned bright gold, the upper windows of the great empty house flamed so bright you had to squint, the river slid quietly west into the dark woods, and the purple towers of old Clavering Abbey rose up behind the treeline. Little Arthur and his mother would stand there, their long blue shadows stretching across the grass, reciting Milton’s lines about “These are thy glorious works, Parent of Good” in soft voices, to Helen’s utter delight. Their walks usually ended in a whole heap of hugs, for loving and praying were Helen’s favourite pastimes. Little Arthur even grew up claiming he was guaranteed a spot in heaven, because his mother would never agree to go there without him.

As for John Pendennis the Squire, everyone respected him like he was the Medes and the Persians rolled into one. His hat was always the best brushed hat in the whole county, every meal was served exactly at the same minute, woe betide little Pen if he turned up late. Prayers, letter reading, business, stable inspections, hen house and pigsty checks, all happened on a rigid schedule. After his exactly 6pm dinner, he always napped in his armchair with the *Globe* newspaper on his knee and his yellow bandanna handkerchief spread over his face—those handkerchiefs were a gift from his brother the Major, who’d helped John buy his army majority years back, so they were great friends now. By the time that pretty sunset view rolled round at half past seven, he was still snoring away inside, completely oblivious to all the poetry and mother-son affection happening out on the lawn.

As soon as he walked back into the drawing-room, newspaper under his arm, Helen and Pen instantly hushed, no more giggling or gushing. While Pen curled up in a great big armchair gobbling up every book he could get his hands on, the Squire wrote his little gardening articles for the *Gardener’s Gazette*, or played a very serious game of picquet with Helen, or the occasional friend from the village.

Pendennis always scheduled his grandest dinner parties for the biennial visits of his brother Major Pendennis, who’d sold out of the army after his regiment came back from India and New South Wales, and lived comfortably on half-pay. “My brother the Major” was Pendennis’s favourite conversation opener at the reading rooms, at quarter sessions, all across the county. The Major was their personal hotline to fancy London high society: he always turned up with the latest gossip about lords and dukes, dined with nobility every other week, and talked about them all with perfect soldierly decorum. “Lord Bareacres was good enough to invite me up to Bareacres for the pheasant shoot,” he’d say, or “Lord Steyne was kind enough to ask me down to Stillbrook for Easter.” His fame as a fashionable man about town was so widespread that gentry drove ten miles out of their way to call on him when he stayed at Fairoaks. Everyone was shocked when he turned down a marriage proposal from Miss Hunkle, the attorney’s daughter, who had 1500 pounds a year of her own. “As a bachelor,” he explained, “no one cares how poor I am. I move in circles where a few thousand pounds a year doesn’t make a difference in how people see you. Miss Hunkle is perfectly respectable, but she doesn’t have the breeding or the manners to fit into that world. I’ll die an old bachelor, and she’ll find a far better husband than a worn out half-pay soldier.” He turned out to be right: Miss Hunkle married a wild young French nobleman a year later, got titled Baroness de Carambole, and dumped her useless husband barely six months after the wedding.

The Major was everyone’s favourite at Fairoaks: he was as good-natured as he was well-bred, and adored Helen, whom he swore was one of the finest ladies in all of England. Helen’s quiet beauty, her natural sweetness, and that simple dignity that only perfect purity and innocence can give a woman made the praise entirely deserved. Arthur thought of her as less of a human and more of a literal angel when he was a boy—all wisdom, kindness, and grace. When John drove her to the county assize balls and concerts, he’d walk in with her on his arm, and stare down all the rich lords like he was daring any of them to show him a wife half as good as his. Of course, that made half the local county ladies, who had three times her fortune, seethe with jealousy: Miss Pybus said she was cold and haughty, Miss Pierce said she was far too proud for the wife of a former doctor, and the parson’s wife Mrs. Wapshot insisted she got to go first through every door, since Helen was only a “medical man’s wife.” Helen didn’t notice any of the drama at all. She just went about her days saying her prayers, loving her family, helping her neighbours, and doing her duty, completely unbothered.
