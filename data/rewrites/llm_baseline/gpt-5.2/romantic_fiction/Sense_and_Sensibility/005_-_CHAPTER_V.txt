Mrs. Dashwood had scarcely folded her answer—had scarcely let the ink dry in her mind—before she yielded to the pleasure of telling her son-in-law and his wife that a house was secured, and that she would trouble them no longer than the short time needed to make it fit for their living in.

The announcement fell among them like a sudden change of weather. Mrs. John Dashwood, whose civility was ever a carefully measured portion, said nothing at all. Mr. John Dashwood, with a propriety that came easily when it cost him nothing, hoped his mother would not settle too far from Norland.

Mrs. Dashwood tasted the triumph of her reply as one tastes a well-kept preserve—sweet, bright, and a little sharp.

“Devonshire,” she said.

At the name, Edward, who had been quiet, turned on the instant. Surprise and concern were both in his voice, and there was no need for Mrs. Dashwood to ask what lay beneath them.

“Devonshire! Are you, indeed, going there? So far from hence! And to what part of it?”

She told him. It was within four miles northward of Exeter.

“It is but a cottage,” she added, with that airy courage which made a small thing seem spacious, “but I hope to see many of my friends in it. A room or two can easily be added; and if my friends find no difficulty in travelling so far to see me, I am sure I shall find none in accommodating them.”

The words were hospitality; the intention was also war. She ended with a kind invitation to Mr. and Mrs. John Dashwood to visit her at Barton, and to Edward she gave one with a warmth that, like the fragrance of bread in an oven, spreads itself whether one will or no.

Her late conversation with her daughter-in-law had fixed her resolution not to remain at Norland a day longer than necessity required; but it had not produced the effect which that conversation had chiefly aimed at. To separate Edward and Elinor was not her object; it was as far from it as ever. On the contrary, she chose this moment, by so pointed an invitation to her brother, to let Mrs. John Dashwood understand—without a single open quarrel—how little she regarded her disapprobation of the match, and how much she would insist on affection being spoken in her own household dialect, not translated into Fanny’s colder terms.

Mr. John Dashwood began at once to repeat, with great earnestness and with a conscientious vexation that almost deserved credit, how exceedingly sorry he was that she had taken a house at so great a distance as to prevent his being of any service to her in removing her furniture. For the one exertion to which he had bounded the performance of his promise to his father was, by this arrangement, rendered impossible; and he felt himself aggrieved by the impossibility, as men often feel most deeply injured by the loss of a virtue they never meant to practise.

The furniture, in truth, was all sent round by water—packed, corded, sealed: household linen folded into pale, clean strata; plate wrapped and muffled against the knocking of the waves; china nested like careful eggs; books stacked into heavy, mute bricks of memory; and Marianne’s handsome pianoforte, swathed and crated as if it were some great joint to be preserved for a feast at the other end of England. The yard and passage were full of the sounds of departure—the rasp of rope through a sailor’s hand, the dull thud of a box set down, the quick calls of men to one another that made the very air feel busier than it ought to be in a house that had lately known mourning.

Mrs. John Dashwood watched the packages go with a sigh. It was not grief; it was that sort of complaint which rises when one sees a good thing leave one’s reach. She could not help thinking it hard that, as Mrs. Dashwood’s income was to be so trifling compared with their own, she should yet carry away any handsome article of furniture. The clink of plate and the imagined sheen of polished wood seemed to offend her sense of proportion, as if elegance were a language reserved for the rich—and the poor ought to speak in plainer syllables.

Mrs. Dashwood took the house for a twelvemonth. It was ready furnished, and she might have immediate possession. No difficulty arose on either side in the agreement; and she waited only for the disposal of her effects at Norland, and to determine her future household, before she set off for the west. And because she was exceedingly rapid in the performance of everything that interested her, this, too, was soon done.

The horses left her by her husband had been sold soon after his death; and now an opportunity offered for disposing of her carriage. For her own pleasure—yes, and for the comfort of her children—she would willingly have kept it; there is a particular ease in being carried, sheltered from weather, the wheels making their steady music on the road. But Elinor, who heard always the longer consequence behind the short desire, advised its sale, and her discretion prevailed.

It was Elinor’s wisdom, likewise, that limited their servants to three—two maids and a man—chosen quickly from among those who had formed their establishment at Norland. The man and one of the maids were sent off immediately into Devonshire to prepare the cottage for their mistress’s arrival; for, as Lady Middleton was entirely unknown to Mrs. Dashwood, she preferred going directly to the house meant to be her home rather than arriving first as a visitor at Barton Park. She relied so undoubtingly on Sir John’s description of the cottage as to feel no curiosity to examine it herself until she entered it as her own—trusting, as some women do, to another’s cheerful report in place of her own cautious inspection, because her imagination was already seasoning the bare rooms with future warmth.

Her eagerness to be gone from Norland was not diminished by anything she saw around her. Indeed, it was preserved—fed, even—by the evident satisfaction of her daughter-in-law at the prospect of her removal; a satisfaction but feebly covered by a cold invitation to defer her departure, offered as one offers a dish one hopes will not be accepted.

Now, if ever, was the time when her son-in-law’s promise to his father might have been fulfilled with propriety. Since he had neglected to do it on first coming to the estate, their quitting his house might naturally have been considered the most suitable period for its accomplishment. But Mrs. Dashwood soon gave over every hope of the kind. From the general drift of his discourse, she became convinced that his assistance extended no farther than their maintenance for six months at Norland. He spoke so often of the increasing expenses of housekeeping, and of the perpetual demands upon his purse—demands, he said, to which a man of any consequence in the world was exposed beyond calculation—that he seemed rather to stand in need of more money himself than to have any intention of giving money away. His talk had its own tiresome rhythm, like a clock one cannot stop: cost, cost, cost—each word striking the ear, dulling the heart.

In a very few weeks from the day which brought Sir John Middleton’s first letter to Norland, everything was so far settled in their future abode as to enable Mrs. Dashwood and her daughters to begin their journey. Many were the tears shed in their last adieus to a place so much beloved. Even the house itself seemed to listen—to hold its breath between the familiar, gentle sounds of their former life and the new hush that would follow. The footfalls in corridors, the soft opening and shutting of drawers, the rustle of gowns as rooms were searched one final time: all of it made a kind of domestic music, and then, as each task ended, another note dropped away.

“Dear, dear Norland!” cried Marianne, wandering alone before the house on the last evening of their being there. Her voice trembled as if the air itself were cold against her throat. “When shall I cease to regret you!—When learn to feel a home elsewhere!—Oh! happy house, could you know what I suffer in now viewing you from this spot, from whence perhaps I may view you no more!—And you, ye well-known trees!—but you will continue the same.—No leaf will decay because we are removed, nor any branch become motionless although we can observe you no longer!—No; you will continue the same; unconscious of the pleasure or the regret you occasion, and insensible of any change in those who walk under your shade!—But who will remain to enjoy you?”
