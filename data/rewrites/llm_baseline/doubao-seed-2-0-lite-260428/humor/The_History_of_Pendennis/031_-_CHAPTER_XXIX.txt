C’mon, reader, tear yourself away from the Clavering village gossip, Fairoaks’ soggy garden paths, and the nice boring humdrum of west country life, and hop the “Alacrity” stagecoach alongside Arthur Pendennis: he’s off to London for good, ready to wrestle the world into submission and make his fortune. As the wheels spin him further and further away from his home’s familiar creaky gates under the dark night sky, Pen’s brain buzzes with a hundred half-baked life plans, all full of prudence, hard work, and obviously imminent fame and success. He’s dead certain he’s sharper than half the guys who’ve zoomed past him in the life race so far: that first disastrous romantic misadventure left him with a healthy dose of remorse and a lot of time to think, but didn’t kill his nerve, or — let’s be honest — his very high opinion of himself.

A hundred giddy fancies and bright hopes keep him wide awake. He feels a decade older than the callow kid who trundled down this same road to and from Oxbridge only 12 months prior. His thoughts drift soft and tender to his mum, who knelt to bless him goodbye at the gate, who loves and trusts him completely even after all his stupid past follies. He stares up at the stars through the coach window and prays he’s got the grit to work, to stick it out, to stay honest, dodge dumb temptation, and be worthy of that woman who loves him so unreservedly. He’d bet good money she’s awake right now too, sending up far purer, better prayers for his safety to the same God. That mother’s love is his lucky charm, the one thing he’s counting on to keep him on the straight and narrow.
And Laura? He’d love to say he carried her affection with him, but she turned him down flat, and he knows he doesn’t deserve it. He squirms a little with shame admitting her heart and character are far better than his own — and yet, deep down, he’s low-key relieved to be free. “I’m not good enough for someone that perfect,” he tells himself, shrinking back from her spotless goodness the way a guy who snuck off to get drunk every Sunday for a year avoids the church he used to go to as a kid: he’s not mad at it, just knows he’s got no right to walk back in that pure space yet.

With all that rattling around his head, Pen doesn’t doze off until the sharp nipping October dawn creeps over the horizon, and wakes up all refreshed when the coach pulls into that old familiar breakfast stop, the same one he’s eaten a dozen merry meals at on his way to and from school and college since he was a little kid. By the time they pull out, the sun’s blazing bright, the coach is flying, the guard’s horn is blaring, and the milestones are zipping by so fast they’re almost a blur. Pen smokes, jokes with the guard, his fellow passengers, and every random person hanging around the roadside stops, and the whole road gets busier, louder, more exciting by the minute. The last team of dappled greys swap in at the next stop, and the Alacrity rolls straight into London.
What young lad from the country hasn’t felt that electric little zing when he first lays eyes on the sprawling, roaring big city? Hundreds of other carriages full of thousands of strangers are all hustling towards the same chaos. “This is my spot!” Pen thinks, practically bouncing on the coach roof. “This is where my fight starts. I’ve got to win, or face plant trying. I’ve been a lazy dawdling boy long enough. I want to prove I can be a proper man.” He leans over the edge of the roof, gawking down at the endless streets, with that same feral giddy excitement a new 19-year-old private gets the night before his first battle.

On the ride in, Pen struck up a chat with a cheery fellow passenger wrapped in a shabby old cloak, who would not stop gossiping about all the famous literary blokes he swore were his closest personal pals. Turns out he was Doolan, a London newspaper reporter, heading home after covering a big professional wrestling match out west. This guy apparently knew every single well-known writer of the era, chatting about Tom Campbell, Tom Hood, Sydney Smith and all the rest like they drank pints with him every Tuesday. When they passed Brompton, he points out Mr. Hurtle, the legendary vicious book reviewer, ambling along with his umbrella. Pen cranes so far over the coach railing to stare at the great man he nearly topples right off. “He went to Boniface College, same as me!” Pen crows. “Faith, he did, and I know him like the back of my hand,” Doolan replies. Pen is over the moon just to have gotten a glimpse of the reviewer whose work he’s obsessed with. At this point in his life, he still believes every author, reviewer, and newspaper editor is a magical genius, even that hack Wagg whose books he doesn’t even think are that good — he still secretly reveres him as a successful writing legend. When he mentions he ran into Wagg out in the countryside, Doolan drops the bombshell that Wagg makes three hundred pounds *per volume* for his novels. Pen’s brain immediately does the maths — wait, that means I could make five grand a year? No problem!

The very first person Pen recognises the second the coach pulls up at the Gloster Coffee House is his old pal Harry Foker, prancing down Arlington Street behind an absurdly enormous cab horse, wearing crisp white kid gloves and holding white reins, his chin now sporting a very proud tuft of new facial hair. A tiny knock-kneed cabby boy in skin-tight leather breeches swings off the back of Foker’s custom ride. Foker spots the dusty old Alacrity he used to ride as a kid, and blinks. “Pen! My boy!” he yells, waving his whip, and Pen whoops back, thrilled to see his weird old mate’s friendly face. Mr. Doolan is suddenly extremely impressed that Pen has a friend who owns such a fancy private cab, and Pen is bouncing off the walls with excitement that he’s actually, finally free in London. He invites Doolan to eat dinner with him that night at the Covent Garden Coffee House, where he’s booked a room, hops in a random hack cab, and rattles off there in the highest of spirits. He’s so glad to see the bustling overworked waiters and bowing landlord he remembers from years prior, he asks after the landlady, misses the old boots man, and would have happily shaken every single employee’s hand if he could. He’s got a hundred whole pounds tucked in his pocket! He pulls on his very best suit, eats a modest dinner with one single pint of sherry (he’s determined to be extremely economical, thank you very much), and trots off to the theater right next door.

The bright lights, blaring music, massive crowd and giddy vibe completely charm him, the way those things always charm young lads fresh out of college and the country who haven’t seen a proper big city show yet. He cackles at every bad joke, cheers loud enough to make his throat sore at every song, to the complete bewilderment of all the grumpy old regulars in the boxes who haven’t felt a single spark of excitement at the theater in 20 years, who are delighted to see someone young and dumb and happy to be there. After the first act ends, he struts up and down the theater lobbies like he owns the place, like he’s at the fanciest royal reception in town. Every single tired grown-up who’s lived in London for 10 years must remember that exact silly first delusion, and wish they could get that giddy naivety back, right?
And sure enough, there’s Harry Foker again, the dedicated professional party boy, hanging around with Grandy Tiptoff of the Household Brigade, the brother of Lord Tiptoff, and Lord Colchicum, Tiptoff’s ancient uncle — a very old peer who’s been partying nonstop ever since the first French Revolution kicked off. Foker spots Pen, barrels over to drag him straight into his private box, where a lady with the longest ringlets and fairest shoulders you’ve ever seen is sitting. That’s Miss Blenkinsop, the famous star of high comedy, and dozing in the back of the box in a very obvious ill-fitting wig is old Blenkinsop, her dad. The theater papers call him “the veteran Blenkinsop,” “the reliable Blenkinsop,” “the nation’s favourite old trouper Blenkinsop” — he spends his whole career playing the grumpy, stuffy “heavy father” roles in every drama, and acts exactly the same grumpy stuffy heavy father in real life, too.

Meanwhile back home in Fairoaks, Mrs. Pendennis has just gone to bed, wondering if her dearest Arthur is fast asleep after his long journey. Laura’s still awake too, wide-eyed. 24 hours prior, Pen was rumbling over quiet west country commons dotted with glowing cottage windows and under dark starlit woods, swearing he’d reform, resist all temptation, and keep his heart tied to home. Right now, the farce on stage is going down a storm, and Mrs. Leary, decked out in a hussar jacket and braided pantaloons, is enchanting every single person in the audience with her cheeky grin, great figure, and catchy ballads.

Pen, new in town, would have loved to sit and listen to her sing, but the rest of the people in the box couldn’t care less about Mrs. Leary or her songs, chattering nonstop the whole time. Tiptoff knows exactly where she got her stage tights from. Colchicum saw her perform back in 1814. Miss Blenkinsop declares her singing is completely off key, which shocks Pen — who thinks Mrs. Leary is as beautiful as an angel and sings better than a nightingale. When Hoppus walks on stage as Sir Harcourt Featherby, the young hero of the farce, the guys in the box all complain he’s way too old for the part, and Tiptoff threatens to throw Miss Blenkinsop’s bouquet at his head.
“Absolutely not!” Miss Blenkinsop squawks. “Lord Colchicum gave that to me!”
Pen perks up, blushes, and bows. He says he owes Lord Colchicum a favour — he proposed Pen for membership at the Megatherium Club, at the request of Pen’s uncle Major Pendennis.
“What, you old Wigsby’s nephew, are you?” the old peer says. “Pardon the familiarity, but we all call your uncle Wigsby behind his back. We voted you in last week, didn’t we? Last Wednesday. Your uncle didn’t even show up.”
Pen is over the moon, gushes a whole fancy speech thanking the old lord, who just stares at him through his double opera glass. Pen can’t believe he’s officially a member of the fancy posh club.
“Stop gawking at that other box, you naughty thing!” Miss Blenkinsop yells at Tiptoff.
“Mirabel’s one hell of a fine looking woman, isn’t she?” Tiptoff says. “Though Mirabel was a total fool to marry her.”
“Silly old softie,” the old peer agrees.
“Mirabel?” Pen echoes, confused.
Harry Foker snorts laughing. “Oh we’ve definitely heard of her before, haven’t we, Pen?”
It was Pen’s first ever great love. Miss Emily Fotheringay. A year prior, she’d walked down the aisle with Sir Charles Mirabel, Knight Grand Cross of the Bath, former envoy to the Court of Pumpernickel, who’d had a very important role in all the peace negotiations before the Congress of Swammerdam, and signed the Peace of Pultusk on behalf of the British crown.
“Emily was always as thick as an owl,” Miss Blenkinsop sniffs.
“Eh, eh — not that stupid,” the old peer rumbles.
“Oh for shame!” the actress cries, having no idea what he just said.
Pen stares out at her across the theater, and sees his first love again — and suddenly he can’t for the life of him work out what he ever saw in her in the first place.

That’s how, on his very first night in London, Pen gets himself introduced to a fancy gentleman’s club, a famous leading lady, a grumpy veteran stage dad, and a whole crew of jolly party lads both young and old. Lord Colchicum is ancient, balding, and wobbly on his feet, but he still parties harder than blokes half his age, and brags he can drink more claret than any 20-something in his social circle. He hangs around with young “men about town” all the time, throws endless huge dinners at Richmond and Greenwich, is a massive patron of every kind of stage performance from opera to ballet, and invites every type of performer — English, Italian, French, you name it — to his banquets. His villa on the Thames hosts sumptuous parties for hordes of posh young guys, who are all very happy to hang out with all the greenroom actors and actresses (mostly the actresses, because Lord Colchicum swears they’re way more fun and witty than the blokes).

The next day, Pen trots over to the club and pays his membership entrance fee, which eats exactly a third of his 100-pound travel fund immediately. He wanders around the fancy building, eats lunch there grinning like an idiot, flops into an armchair in the library and tries to read every magazine on the shelf, wondering if all the other members are staring at him and thinking how weird it is that everyone is allowed to keep their hats on indoors. He sits down at a desk and writes a long letter to his mum in Fairoaks on the fancy club letterhead, gushing about how amazing this place will be to relax in after a long day of work.
Then he heads over to his uncle Major Pendennis’s lodgings on Bury Street, a little nervous, just like his mum begged him to, and is very relieved to find the Major hasn’t come back to London yet. The apartment is completely empty, covered in dust sheets, bills and unopened letters piled up on the mantelpiece waiting for the owner to return. The landlady tells him the Major’s off in Badnbadn on the continent, vacationing with the Marquess of Steyne. Pen leaves his card on the mantelshelf with all the rest, still marked “Fairoaks” under his name.

When the Major does get back to London right as November’s thick yellow fog rolls in, planning to spend Christmas at a country friend’s house after he’s suffered through a few weeks of London fog, he finds another card of Arthur’s, this one engraved with “Lamb Court, Temple,” plus a note from Pen and his mum saying he’s moved to London, joined the Upper Temple law school, and is studying hard to become a barrister.
Lamb Court, Temple? Where the hell is that? The Major vaguely remembers some posh society ladies talking once about dining with Mr. Ayliffe, the famous barrister who “moves in high society” and lived there, and he swears Lamb Court is probably a part of the old debtors’ prison near the King’s Bench. He sends his valet Morgan off to find out exactly where Lamb Court is, and report back on what kind of digs Pen has landed himself in. Morgan is the kind of discreet guy who could track down a runaway in hiding three counties over, so he finds Pen’s apartment in 10 minutes flat.
“What sort of place is it, Morgan?” the Major asks from behind his bed curtains the next morning, as his valet helps him dress in the thick, murky yellow London fog.
“Bit of a quiet, run-down little spot, sir,” Morgan says. “All lawyers live there, paint their names on their doors. Mr. Arthur’s three flights up, sir. A Mr. Warrington lives there too.”
“Warrington from the Suffolk Warringtons! Good family, that,” the Major thinks. “Loads of well-connected younger sons go into the law for a living. Comfy rooms, I suppose?”
“Only saw the outside of the door, sir, with their two names painted on it, and a scrap of paper stuck up saying ‘Back at 6.’ No sign of a servant anywhere, sir.”
“At least he’s being economical,” the Major says.
“Extremely, sir. Three flights up, sir. Nasty black moldy staircase the likes of which I’ve never seen. Can’t imagine how a gentleman would choose to live there.”
“And since when are you the expert on where gentlemen should and shouldn’t live, Morgan?” the Major huffs, very dignified. “Arthur’s studying for the bar, you know.” He closes the conversation, and gets dressed in the thick yellow fog.
“Boys will be boys,” the soft-hearted uncle thinks to himself. “He wrote me a damn good letter. Colchicum says he met him at dinner, thinks he’s a proper gentlemanly kid. His mum’s one of the nicest souls alive. If he’s sown all his wild oats and decides to stick to his work, he could turn out very well. Can you believe old fool Charley Mirabel married that girl Pen had a crush on, the Fotheringay? And Pen’s being polite, waiting for me to reach out before he shows up at my place, instead of waltzing in uninvited. He put it very nicely, very manly. I was furious with him after all his silly Oxbridge escapades, made it very clear when he was here before — but gad, I’ll go visit him, damn it all.”

Once Morgan confirms he can get to the Temple easily via city omnibus, the Major heads over one morning after breakfast at his own club (not the new-fangled Megatherium that Pen just joined — the Major is far too smart to have his nephew hanging around the same club he frequents, after all). He hops on the omnibus, tells the conductor to drop him right at the Upper Temple gate.
It’s about noon when he steps through the Temple’s dingy old entrance. A friendly porter in a white apron shows him through a bunch of dark alleys and sad little archways, every court more dreary than the last, until he finally finds Lamb Court. If it was dark and foggy over in Pall Mall, it’s pitch black in Lamb Court.
