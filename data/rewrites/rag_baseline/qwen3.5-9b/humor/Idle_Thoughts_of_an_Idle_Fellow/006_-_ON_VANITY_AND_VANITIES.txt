All is vanity, and everyone is vain. Women are terribly vain; men are equally so, perhaps even more so if such a comparison is possible; and children, particularly children, embody the trait with terrifying intensity. At this very moment, one of them is hammering upon my legs, demanding to know my verdict on her new footwear. Candidly, I hold little regard for them. They lack symmetry and curve, possessing an indescribable lumpiness—I suspect, moreover, that they have been placed upon the wrong feet. Yet, I offer no critique. What she desires is not honesty, but flattery, and so I gush over them with a degrading effusiveness that feels entirely inappropriate. Nothing less would satisfy this self-opinionated cherub.

On one occasion, I attempted the strategy of the conscientious friend. She had solicited my judgment on her general conduct, specifically asking, "Wot oo tink of me? Oo peased wi' me?" Seizing the opportunity for a salutary correction regarding her recent moral trajectory, I replied bluntly: "No, I am not pleased with you." I recounted the events of that very morning: how she had roused the entire house at five AM, overturned a water-jug at seven, attempted to place the cat in the bath at eight, and sat upon her father's hat at nine-thirty-five. What was her response? Was she grateful for my candor? Did she reflect upon my words and resolve to lead a better, nobler life from that hour forward? Not a bit of it. She immediately erupted into a howl of rage, declaring me a "naughty, bad unkie" and a "bad man," before rushing to inform her mother. Since that incident, whenever my opinion is sought, I have concealed my true sentiments behind a veil of unbounded admiration for her actions, regardless of their merit. She nods approvingly and trots off to broadcast my praise to the household, employing it as a testimonial for mercenary gain; shortly thereafter, I hear distant cries of, "Unkie says me dood dirl--me dot to have two bikkies." There she goes now, gazing rapturously at her own toes and murmuring "pittie"—a towering monument of conceit and vanity, alongside countless other wickednesses.

They are all alike. I recall sitting in a garden one sunny afternoon in the suburbs of London when a shrill treble voice suddenly rang out from a top-story window, addressing some unseen entity in a neighboring garden: "Gamma, me dood boy, me wery good boy, gamma; me dot on Bob's knickiebockies." Indeed, even animals are vain. I witnessed a magnificent Newfoundland dog recently sitting before a mirror at the entrance to a shop in Regent's Circus, examining himself with a smug satisfaction unparalleled anywhere save perhaps within a vestry meeting. I was once at a farmhouse during a high holiday, likely a May Day or Quarter Day, when they draped a garland of flowers around the head of one of the cows. That absurd quadruped wandered about all day as perky as a schoolgirl in a new frock; when they removed the wreath, she became instantly sulky, refusing to stand still until it was replaced. This is not a fable told by Percy; it is plain, sober truth. As for cats, they rival humans in their vanity. I have known a cat rise and walk out of a room upon a derogatory remark concerning her species, while a neatly turned compliment will set her purring for an hour. I do like cats; they are unconsciously amusing, possessing a comic dignity, a distinct "How dare you!" air. Dogs, conversely, are "Hail, fellow, well met" to every Tom, Dick, and Harry. When I meet a familiar dog, I slap his head, hurl opprobrious epithets, and roll him onto his back; he lies there gaping, utterly unmindful. Imagine attempting such familiarity with a cat! She would never speak to you again. To win a cat's approval, one must proceed with caution. Begin by saying, "Poor pussy," adding "did 'ums" in a tone of soothing sympathy. You may not know what you mean, nor the cat either, but the sentiment implies a proper spirit, touching her feelings sufficiently that, provided you are of good manners and passable appearance, she will stick her tail up and rub her nose against you. Once matters reach this stage, you may venture to chuck her under the chin and tickle the side of her head; the intelligent creature will then dig her claws into your legs, and all is friendship and affection, as beautifully expressed in the lines: "I love little pussy, her coat is so warm, / And if I don't tease her she'll do me no harm; / So I'll stroke her, and pat her, and feed her with food, / And pussy will love me because I am good." The final two lines reveal a remarkably accurate insight into a cat's notion of human goodness. In her estimation, virtue consists solely of stroking, patting, and feeding.

I fear this narrow-minded view of merit is not confined to felines. We are all inclined to adopt a similar standard in judging others: a good man is one who is good to us, and a bad man is one who fails to do what we want. The truth is, each of us harbors an inborn conviction that the entire world, with every person and thing in it, was created as a necessary appendage to ourselves. Our fellow men and women were fashioned to admire us and minister to our various requirements. You and I, dear reader, are each the center of the universe in our respective opinions. You, as I understand it, were brought into existence by a considerate Providence solely to read and pay for what I write; while I, in your opinion, am an article dispatched into the world to produce something for you to consume. The stars—those myriad other worlds rushing down beside us through the eternal silence—were placed in the heavens merely to make the night sky interesting for us; and the moon, with its dark mysteries and ever-hidden face, exists as an arrangement for us to flirt beneath. I fear we are mostly like Mrs. Poyser's bantam cock, who fancied the sun rose every morning solely to hear him crow. "'Tis vanity that makes the world go round."

I do not believe any man ever existed without vanity; if he did, he would be an extremely uncomfortable companion. He would, of course, be a very good man, worthy of our deepest respect. He would be an admirable specimen, a man to be placed under a glass case for display, mounted on a pedestal to be copied like a school exercise, revered but not loved—not a human brother whose hand we would care to grip. Angels may be excellent in their own way, but we, poor mortals in our current state, would likely find them tedious company. Even mere good people are rather depressing. It is in our faults and failings, not our virtues, that we touch one another and find sympathy. We differ widely in our nobler qualities, but we unite in our follies. Some are pious, some generous; a few are honest, and fewer still are truly truthful. But in vanity and kindred weaknesses, we can all join hands. Vanity is one of those touches of nature that make the whole world kin. From the Indian hunter, proud of his belt of scalps, to the European general swelling beneath his row of stars and medals; from the Chinese, gleeful at the length of his pigtail, to the "professional beauty" enduring tortures to reduce her waist to the size of a peg-top; from draggle-tailed little Polly Stiggins strutting through Seven Dials with a tattered parasol, to the princess sweeping through a drawing-room with a train four yards long; from 'Arry, winning vulgar laughter with his chaff, to the statesman whose ears are tickled by cheers for his high-sounding periods; from the dark-skinned African bartering rare oils and ivory for glass beads, to the Christian maiden selling her white body for a score of tiny stones and an empty title—all march, fight, bleed, and die beneath its tawdry flag. Yes, vanity is truly the motive power that drives humanity, and flattery is the grease that keeps the wheels turning.

If you wish to win affection and respect in this world, you must flatter people. Flatter the high and low, the rich and poor, the silly and the wise. You will fare famously. Praise this man's virtues and that man's vices. Compliment everyone upon everything, and especially upon what they lack. Admire men for their beauty, fools for their wit, and boors for their breeding. Your discernment and intelligence will be extolled to the skies. Everyone can be overcome by flattery. The "belted earl"—a phrase I believe to be correct, though I cannot define it unless it refers to an earl wearing a belt instead of braces—is certainly get-overable by flattery, just as every other human being is, from a duchess to a cat's-meat man, from a plow boy to a poet—and the poet is far easier to manage than the plowboy, for butter sinks better into wheaten bread than into oaten cakes. As for love, flattery is its very lifeblood.
