If you had asked Ginger Kemp to list his finest qualities, he’d have scratched his head, chewed his pencil, and maybe—after a long pause—muttered something about *meaning well*. Tact and sensitivity? Not exactly his strong suits. Yet, against all odds, he displayed a surprising amount of both by giving Sally space in the days following her heartbreak.

Ginger was no stranger to life’s *nasty jars* (as he called them), and his own coping strategy had always been to sulk in solitude until the storm passed. So, assuming Sally wanted the same, he kept his distance. By Saturday, however, he decided it was time to re-enter her orbit—preferably in style.

The weather was practically *showing off*—one of those golden Indian Summer days that made even New York’s grubby rooftops look poetic. Standing atop Mrs. Meecher’s boarding house, dodging laundry lines like an obstacle course, Ginger squinted at the distant smokestacks of the *Mauretania* gliding down the Hudson. A sentimental sight, that ship—it had brought him over from England.

Inspiration struck. A drive! A *dinner*! He’d blow his week’s salary (what remained after Mrs. Meecher’s *strategic deductions*) on an open car, whisk Sally up the Boston Post Road, and cheer her up with bad jokes and good food.

Optimism carried him all the way to Sally’s apartment building—where it promptly crashed into the immovable object of Bruce Carmyle’s back.

Ginger’s mood curdled. His cousin—*here*? In *New York*? In *this hallway*? It was like spotting a cockroach in your soup. Worse, Carmyle was deep in conversation with the janitor, and the shiny hired car idling outside suggested he’d had the *same* idea about cheering up Sally.

Carmyle turned, his expression suggesting he’d just bitten into a lemon. "Hullo."
"Hullo," Ginger echoed.

The silence that followed was thick enough to carve.

"Here to see Miss Nicholas?" Carmyle finally said.
"Yeah."
"She’s not here."

Ginger blinked. "Not here?"

"Gone to England," Carmyle announced, as if personally offended by Sally’s audacity. "Sailed this morning on the *Mauretania*."

Ginger’s stomach performed a backflip. The *Mauretania*—the very ship he’d just been admiring like some kind of sentimental idiot. He’d *watched* her leave.

Carmyle, meanwhile, was busy dismissing his hired car with the air of a man who’d just been charged for a banquet he didn’t eat. The chauffeur, a philosopher in overalls, spat thoughtfully and declared, *"You’ll have to pay just the same."*

Ginger didn’t stick around for the financial negotiations. He wandered the streets in a daze, the city suddenly drained of color. Central Park? Boring. Sidewalks? Boring. Existence? *Deeply* boring.

Then—salvation. A letter.

Not just any letter. A *Mauretania*-crested, Sally-scented lifeline. Ginger carried it to his room like a sacred relic, set the stage (pipe lit, shoes off, stolen photograph of Sally propped against the washstand), and began to read.

*I’ve run away. I’m a coward, but I couldn’t stay. New York’s full of ghosts, and I’m not brave enough to face them. Mr. Faucitt’s in England, floundering in dresses and despair (his brother left him a fashion house—long story), so I’m fleeing to help him. Distraction, Ginger. That’s the plan.*

*Elsa Doland wrote me a gushing letter. Sweet of her, considering I financed the play that made her a star. Gerald wrote too. I burned his unopened. What’s the point? Explanations don’t un-break things.*

*Here’s the ugly truth: I’m not heartbroken because I lost him. I’m heartbroken because my *pride* got trampled. I was *cocky*, Ginger. Thought I had life figured out. Now I’m just another girl who didn’t see the punch coming.*

*Don’t you dare mess up your life while I’m gone. You’re my project. Stay employed. Stay out of trouble. And if you *do* make a hash of it, picture me glaring at you from your mantelpiece.*

Ginger set the letter down, exhaled a sound that was half growl, half sigh, and briefly fantasized about throttling Gerald Foster. Then, because there was nothing else to do, he picked up the letter and read it again.
