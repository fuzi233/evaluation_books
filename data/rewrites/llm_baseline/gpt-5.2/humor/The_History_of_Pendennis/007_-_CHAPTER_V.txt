Rebecca the mare, being of a temper which considered all roads as mere suggestions, carried Arthur Pendennis into Baymouth with the insolent ease of an animal born to make young gentlemen feel heroic. Pen put her up at the inn stables, where a stable-boy—who smelt pleasantly of oats and the honest heat of horseflesh—took the reins and, with them, any remaining dignity Pen might have had.

Pen, still tasting the salt in the wind from his ride, ran straight to Mr. Foker’s lodgings, which perched over a chemist’s shop. Down below, commerce did briskly in cigars and sodawater, those two great medicaments of youthful dissipation; above, the young gentlemen were busy ruining their constitutions with the very articles intended to preserve them.

He found not Foker, but Mr. Spavin—Foker’s friend and part-proprietor in the tandem—smoking in the fashion of a man who had made a pact with the air, and teaching a little dog tricks with a bit of biscuit. The dog, at least, had a clean motive.

Pen’s face—fresh, wind-scrubbed, and ruddy with country health—looked absurdly wholesome beside Spavin’s waxy, jaded little countenance, which seemed to have been kept overnight in a drawer with dice, bad brandy, and a moral.

“Who’s that man?” Spavin thought, watching Pen as if he were a rare vegetable. “He looks as fresh as a bean. His hand don’t shake of a morning—I’d bet five to one.”

Mr. Foker had not come home at all. This was not merely disappointing; it was insulting to Pen’s feverish expectation. Spavin could not say when his friend would return—sometimes a day, sometimes a week—according to whatever tide and moon governed Mr. Foker’s pleasures. Would Pen take anything? There was, he hinted, a very fair tap of ale. Pen, who wanted something much hotter than ale—information—laid down his card (for he had a card now, and the possession of a card is a little ceremony by which a young man informs the world he exists), and presently took his leave.

He went down by the rock and walked the sand, biting his nails, while the sea made a great noise at him. The bay was bright, immeasurable; the waves came rolling in with their old hoarse cookery of foam and brine, as if they were perpetually basting the shore. But Pen stared with blank eyes, scarcely tasting the scene. There was a tide in his head more violent than the tide under his feet; and he had no more power to stop it than to stop the Channel by throwing pebbles.

He did throw stones, however—one after another—just to see something strike and vanish; but the sea continued to arrive in perfect good humour, as if it had not noticed his rebellion.

He must see Foker. He would see Foker. And if Foker was not here, perhaps he was—elsewhere. “Suppose I go on—on the Chatteris road—just to see if I can meet him,” Pen thought, as if he were proposing a casual stroll rather than a campaign.

Within half an hour Rebecca was saddled again. She cantered on the grass by the Chatteris road; and about four miles from Baymouth, where the Clavering road branches off (as everybody knows, and as horses remember), the mare naturally made for that turning, as if home were a warm mash and not a mere direction. Pen, with the cruelty of youth and love, cut her over the shoulder and forced her past. He reached the turnpike without seeing any sign of black tandem or red wheels.

But now he was at the turnpike, and that fact, like many facts, demanded further folly. So he rode on to the George. The yard had its orchestra: hoofs striking cobble, a bucket clanged, a stable-door banged, and somewhere a boy whistled tunelessly—those small rural sounds that keep the mind from entirely falling into itself. The hostler, who had the satirical intelligence of his tribe, told Pen that Mr. Foker was there sure enough, and that he’d been “a makin’ a tremendous row the night afore, a drinkin’ and a singin’, and wantin’ to fight Tom the postboy: which I’m thinkin’ he’d have had the worst of it,” the man added with a grin that tasted of stable-wit.

“Have you carried up your master’s ’ot water to shave with?” he called, very jeeringly, to Mr. Foker’s domestic, who appeared bearing his master’s clothes, brushed and arranged as reverently as if they were church vestments.

“Show Mr. Pendennis up to ’un,” said the hostler.

And Pen, at last, was admitted to the chamber where, in the midst of an immense bed, Mr. Harry Foker lay reposing, as a small idol might repose in a temple made of feathers. The bed and bolsters swelled all round him so that his little sallow face and red silk nightcap seemed like an afterthought in upholstery.

“Hullo!” said Pen.

“Who goes there? brother, quickly tell!” sang out a voice from the bed—flippant, musical, and perfectly satisfied with itself. “What! Pendennis again? Is your Mamma acquainted with your absence? Did you sup with us last night? No stop—who supped with us last night, Stoopid?”

“There was the three officers, sir, and Mr. Bingley, sir, and Mr. Costigan, sir,” answered Stoopid, with the gravity of a man describing a funeral.

“Ah yes: the cup and merry jest went round. We chanted—and I remember I wanted to fight a postboy. Did I thrash him, Stoopid?”

“No, sir. Fight didn’t come off, sir,” said Stoopid, still solemn as a ledger.

He was arranging Mr. Foker’s dressing-case, a travelling altar presented by a fond mother. It contained plate enough to feed a bishop: a silver dish, a silver mug, silver boxes and bottles for every imaginable essence, and a choice of razors waiting against the distant day when Mr. Foker’s beard should arrive and demand negotiation.

“Do it some other day,” said the young fellow, yawning and flinging his thin arms up. “No, there was no fight; but there was chanting. Bingley chanted, I chanted, the General chanted—Costigan I mean.—Did you ever hear him sing ‘The Little Pig under the Bed,’ Pen?”

“The man we met yesterday,” Pen said, all in a tremor that had nothing to do with sea-air. “The father of—”

“Of the Fotheringay,—the very man. Ain’t she a Venus, Pen?”

Before Pen could gather his mind out of his mouth, Stoopid announced, “Please sir, Mr. Costigan’s in the sittin-room, sir, and says, sir, you asked him to breakfast, sir. Called five times, sir; but wouldn’t wake you on no account; and has been here since eleven o’clock, sir—”

“How much is it now?”

“One, sir.”

“What would the best of mothers say,” cried Foker, “if she saw me in bed at this hour? She sent me down here with a grinder. She wants me to cultivate my neglected genus—He, be! I say, Pen, this isn’t quite like seven o’clock school,—is it, old boy?” And he burst into a boyish laugh, pleased with his own rebellion. “Go in and talk to the General whilst I dress. And I say, Pendennis, ask him to sing you ‘The Little Pig under the Bed;’ it’s capital.”

Pen went off in a whirl—toward Mr. Costigan, toward the source of that name which had been burning in him since last night.

Of Mr. Foker’s two grandfathers, one, from whom he had his fortune, brewed; the other was an earl, from whom he inherited a mother who worshipped him. The family had marched, generation after generation, through the Cistercian school; and our friend’s name might be read over the playground wall on a public-house sign, where “Foker’s Entire” was painted with cheerful candour. There he had been bullied for his trade, his face, his dirt, his stupidity, his appetite—indeed for everything that he could not help. Those who know what tyranny does to a sensitive boy will understand how, once free, he sprang into a new sort of creature: humorous, sarcastic, showy—still a dunce, but now a splendid one. Learning cannot be acquired by simply leaving school; but scent and linen can, and were. When he joined his guests he was already perfumed and arrayed, his very collar seeming to declare independence.

Captain Costigan—he preferred Captain to General, as men prefer the title that sounds most like action—sat in the window with the newspaper held at arm’s length. His eyes were dim, and he spelled the words with his lips as well as those bloodshot orbs, like a man chewing letters because he cannot swallow them whole. His hat cocked on one ear; one foot lay up in the window-seat; and the boot on that foot, large and shabby, declared that poverty had begun where it generally begins—with a man’s extremities.

Poverty, you will observe, is a polite enemy: it does not seize a gentleman by the throat at first; it nibbles at his gloves, his hat, his boots, and only afterward considers his heart. All these parts of the Captain were rakish and worn. He rose and saluted Pen: first two fingers, half-hidden in a broken black glove, to the hat; then the hat removed altogether in a flourish. He was going bald, but drew lank iron-grey hair across his pate, with two wisps falling down on either side of his face, as if determined not to desert it entirely. Whisky had ruined what complexion he once had; his handsome face now shone with a copper tinge, like a kettle too long on a fire. His stock was very high, scarred and stained; his dress-coat buttoned tightly wherever the buttons had remained loyal.

“The young gentleman to whom I had the honour to be introjuiced yesterday in the Cathadral Yard,” said the Captain with a bow that belonged, by rights, to a drawing-room. “I hope I see you well, sir. I marked ye in the thayatre last night during me daughter’s perfawrumance; and missed ye on my return. I did but conduct her home, sir, for Jack Costigan, though poor, is a gentleman; and when I reintered the house to pay me respects to me joyous young friend, Mr. Foker—ye were gone. We had a jolly night of ut, sir—Mr. Foker, the three gallant young dragoons, and your ’umble servant. Gad, sir, it put me in mind of one of our old nights when I bore His Majesty’s commission in the Foighting Hundtherd and Third.”

He produced an old snuff-box and presented it with a solemn air, as if snuff were a sacrament.

Arthur was too flurried to say much. This shabby, theatrical old buck—this was her father. The Captain’s breath was perfumed with last night’s cigars, and he twisted the tuft on his chin with the jaunty affectation of a man who had once been admired and could not bear to forget it.

“I hope—Miss—Miss Costigan is well, sir,” Pen stammered, crimsoning. “She—she gave me greater pleasure than—than I—I ever enjoyed at a play. I think, sir—I think she’s the finest actress in the world.”

“Your hand, young man! for ye speak from your heart,” cried the Captain, catching it. “Thank ye, sir; an old soldier and a fond father thanks ye. She is the finest actress in the world. I’ve seen the Siddons, sir, and the O’Nale—they were great, but what were they compared to Miss Fotheringay? I do not wish she should ashume her own name while on the stage. Me family, sir, are proud people; and the Costigans of Costiganstown think that an honest man, who has borne Her Majesty’s colours in the Hundred and Third, would demean himself, by permitting his daughter to earn her old father’s bread.”

“There cannot be a more honourable duty, surely,” Pen said, eager to be noble.

“Honourable! Bedad, sir, I’d like to see the man who said Jack Costigan would consent to anything dishonourable. I have a heart, sir, though I am poor; I like a man who has a heart. You have: I read it in your honest face and steady eye. And would you believe it”—and here the Captain’s voice sank into a confidential whisper—“that that Bingley who has made his fortune by me child, gives her but two guineas a week: out of which she finds herself in dresses, and which, added to me own small means, makes our all?”

Now, the Captain’s means were so small they might be said to be invisible; yet like certain herbs that thrive in cracks of pavement, he contrived to exist in places no sensible person would call nourishing. If he had told his history it might have made a moral tale; but he neither would have told it if he could, nor could if he would. He was not merely unaccustomed to tell the truth—he had long ceased to keep it separate in his own mind. Fact and fiction reeled together in his muzzy, whiskified brain like dancers after too much punch.

He began life brightly: a pair of colours, a fine person and legs, and one of the most beautiful voices in the world. To the last he sang Irish ballads with admirable humour and pathos—and cried at his own singing with the generosity of an audience of one. Poor Cos! brave and maudlin, humorous and an idiot; always good-natured, sometimes almost trustworthy. He would drink with any man and back any man’s bill; and his end would be in a spunging-house, where the sheriff’s officer who took him was fond of him.

Before half an hour had passed he extracted two sovereigns from Pen for tickets for his daughter’s benefit. Pen had but two pounds in his purse; he handed them over with a trembling sort of pride, and would have been afraid to offer more lest he should offend the Captain’s delicacy—an organ which, like certain extinct dialects, survived chiefly in ceremonial phrases. Costigan scrawled him an order for a box, slipped the coins into his waistcoat, and slapped his hand over them. They warmed his old sides like a poultice.

“Faith, sir,” said he, “the bullion’s scarcer with me than it used to be, as is the case with many a good fellow. I won six hundthred of ’em in a single night, sir, when me kind friend, His Royal Highness the Duke of Kent, was in Gibralther.”

And he poured out at once a flood of stories—claret drunk, bets made, races ridden by the garrison there—till their host appeared with breakfast.

The breakfast came in with a smell that might have redeemed a worse company: devilled turkey, sharp and peppery; mutton chops hissing with their own fat; toast browned to a proper bitterness; and coffee which, even at an inn, managed to smell like a promise. The crackle of the fire, the clink of plates, the knife sawing through crisped skin—these homely sounds set a kindlier music under the Captain’s talk than any theatre orchestra.

It was good to see Costigan before food, as some old hounds look handsome once they scent meat. His stories gushed without intermission; his spirits rose with every mouthful. When sunshine fell on him—be it the sunshine of a young man’s company or the warm gravy of a devilled dish—the old lazzarone basked like a creature grateful for heat. He prated of his affairs and past splendours, and all the lords, generals, and Lord-Lieutenants he had ever known. He described the death of his darling Bessie, the late Mrs. Costigan, and the challenge he had sent to Captain Shanty Clancy of the Slashers for looking rude at Miss Fotheringay as she sat on her kyar in the Phaynix; and then how Captain Clancy apologised and gave a dinner in Kildare Street, where six of them drank twinty-one bottles of claret, etc. He announced that to sit with two such noble and generous young fellows was the pride and happiness of an old soldier’s existence; and after a second glass of Curacoa grew so happy he began to cry.

Altogether, the Captain was not a man of much strength of mind, nor an eligible companion for youth; but there are worse men in better places—more calculating, more cold—who have never committed half so many rogueries as he. When they left the George he held an arm of each of his dear young friends and walked between them in a maudlin state of contentment. He winked at one or two tradesmen’s shops where, possibly, he owed money, as if to say, “See the company I’m in—sure I’ll pay you, my boy,” and they finally parted with Mr. Foker at a billiard-room, where that young gentleman had an engagement with some officers of Colonel Swallowtail’s regiment.

Pen and the Captain continued together. Costigan, in his sly way, began sounding Pen about Foker’s fortune and station. Pen, candid as bread-and-milk, told him Foker’s father was a celebrated brewer, and his mother Lady Agnes Milton, Lord Rosherville’s daughter. At this the Captain burst into extravagant compliment and panegyric about Mr. Foker’s “native aristocracie,” which could be seen, he said, “with the twinkling of an oi—and only served to adawrun other qualities which he possessed, a foin intellect and a generous heart”; not one word of which the Captain accurately believed, though he pronounced it with the rich confidence of a man who had long since eaten through his own scruples.

Pen walked on, listening, wondering, amused, puzzled. It had not yet entered into his head to disbelieve any statement made to him; being truthful himself, he took what was told him as truth—like a hungry man taking what is put before him, without asking whether it came from an honest kitchen. Costigan had never had a better listener and was flattered by Pen’s attentive modesty.

So pleased was he with this artless young gentleman that he offered an invitation he seldom accorded: would Mr. Pendennis do him the favour to enter his humble abode, near at hand, where the Captain would have the honour of inthrojuicing his young friend to his daughther, Miss Fotheringay?

Pen was so shocked—so exquisitely, deliciously shocked—that he nearly fell from the Captain’s arm. Happiness, when it comes suddenly, has a rude physicality; it makes the knees untrustworthy. He gasped out incoherent words about the high gratification he should have in being presented to the lady for whose talents he had conceived such admiration—such extreme admiration—and followed, scarcely knowing where.

He was going to see her. He was going to see her. Yesterday, before he knew her, seemed a century and a language ago—as if the very words he had used then belonged to another boy.

The Captain led him into Prior’s Lane, a quiet little street in Chatteris, in the ecclesiastical quarter near Dean’s Green and the canons’ houses, overlooked by the enormous towers of the cathedral. The stones seemed to keep the cathedral’s coolness; the air had that faint, ancient smell of damp masonry and old hymn-books. Even the street sounded different: footsteps were softened, voices lowered, as if the place had trained the very sparrows to respect it.

Costigan’s lodging was on the first floor of a low gabled house, on the door of which was a brass plate: “Creed, Tailor and Robe-maker.” Creed was dead, however. His widow was a pew-opener at the cathedral; his eldest son was a little scamp of a choir-boy who played toss-halfpenny, led his brothers into mischief, and had a voice sweet as an angel’s—so that the cathedral got the music and the lane got the trouble.

Two of the smaller boys sat on the door-step that dropped down into the passage. They sprang up eagerly at the Captain and plunged at the swallow-tails of his dress-coat, to Pen’s surprise. The truth was, when the good-natured gentleman was in cash, he often brought them an apple or a bit of gingerbread—an economy of kindness which, as he remarked later with a wink and a finger to his nose, ensured “the widdy never pressed me for rint when not convanient.”

Pen tumbled down the step; and as he followed his companion up the creaking stair his knees trembled so that he feared the whole building would hear. The stairs complained under them with every plank—an honest domestic music, not like the shriek and hammer of modern streets where every sound must be a cruelty. Here even the house spoke in an old dialect: groan, creak, hush—sounds that said, We have held many footsteps; yours are not the first to be foolish.

He entered—her room. He saw something black before him, moving as if making a curtsey; he heard Costigan’s magniloquent voice, indistinct at first through the pounding in his ears, presenting him to “me child,” and describing Mr. Awther Pindinnis as “a young gentleman of property in the neighbourhood, a person of refoined moind and enviable manners, a sincare lover of poethry, and a man possest of a feeling and affectionate heart.”

“It is very fine weather,” Miss Fotheringay said in an Irish accent, with a deep rich melancholy voice which seemed to carry theatre-music even into a modest room.

“Very,” said Mr. Pendennis.

In this romantic way their conversation began. He found himself seated, and at last had leisure to look.

She looked handsomer off the stage than under the lamps. All her attitudes were naturally grand and majestical, as if she had been trained, not by Bows, but by some ancestral queen. If she stood against the mantelpiece, her robe draped itself classically; her chin settled on her hand; the other lines of her form arranged themselves in harmonious undulations—she looked like a Muse waiting for a poet worth her patience. If she sat on a cane-bottomed chair, her arm rounded over the back, her hand seemed to require a sceptre; the folds of her dress fell into order as if they had been taught discipline. In the morning light her hair was blue-black; her complexion dazzling fair, with the faintest blush flickering like a quick flame in her cheek. Her eyes were grey with prodigiously long lashes; and her mouth, Pen would afterward insist, was of a staring red, outshining geraniums, sealing-wax, and guardsmen’s coats.

“And very warm,” continued this empress, as if meteorology were a royal proclamation.

Pen assented again, helplessly, and the conversation rolled on with that solemn triviality by which strangers first approach one another. She asked her father whether he had had a pleasant evening at the George; and he recounted supper and tumblers of punch as lovingly as if he could still taste them. Then he asked her how she had been employing the morning.

“Bows came,” she said, “at ten, and we studied Ophalia. It’s for the twenty-fourth, when I hope, sir, we shall have the honour of seeing ye.”

“Indeed, indeed, you will,” Pen cried; marvelling that she said “Ophalia” and spoke Irishly now, who had not the least Hibernian accent on the stage. It struck him—though he could not have phrased it—that a voice changes its country according to who is listening; and that there are selves which can only be spoken in certain sounds.

“I’ve secured ’um for your benefit, dear,” said the Captain, tapping his waistcoat pocket—wherein lay Pen’s sovereigns—and winking at Pen, who blushed at being both generous and used.

“Mr—-the gentleman’s very obleging,” said Mrs. Haller.

“My name is Pendennis,” said Pen, blushing harder. “I—I—hope you’ll—you’ll remember it.” His heart thumped so at this audacity that he nearly choked.

“Pendennis,” she answered slowly, looking him full in the eyes with a glance so straight, so clear, so bright, so killing, and in a voice so sweet and low and round, that the single word and look seemed to shoot through him and fix him in a blissful pain.

“I never knew the name was so pretty before,” Pen said.

“’Tis a very pretty name,” Ophelia said. “Pentweazle’s not a pretty name. Remember, papa, when we were on the Norwich Circuit, Young Pentweazle, who used to play second old men, and married Miss Rancy, the Columbine; they’re both engaged in London now, at the Queen’s, and get five pounds a week. Pentweazle wasn’t his real name. ’Twas Judkin gave it him, I don’t know why. His name was Harrington; that is, his real name was Potts; fawther a clergyman, very respectable. Harrington was in London, and got in debt. Ye remember; he came out in Falkland, to Mrs. Bunce’s Julia.”

“And a pretty Julia she was,” the Captain interposed; “a woman of fifty, and a mother of ten children. ’Tis you ought to have been Julia, or my name’s not Jack Costigan.”

“I didn’t take the leading business then,” Miss Fotheringay said modestly; “I wasn’t fit for’t till Bows taught me.”
