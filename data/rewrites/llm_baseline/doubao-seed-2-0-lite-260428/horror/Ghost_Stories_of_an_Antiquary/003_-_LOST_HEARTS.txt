As near as surviving parish records can confirm, it was September 1811 when a dust-caked post-chaise rattled to a halt before the oaken doors of Aswarby Hall, deep in Lincolnshire’s flat, wind-scoured fens. The small boy who was its only passenger—Stephen Elliott, newly orphaned after his parents’ fever took them six months prior in Warwickshire—leaped down before the footman could open his door, his bare cheeks stung sharp by the early autumn air, and stared up at the house that was to be his new home.

It was a tall, square red-brick pile built in Queen Anne’s reign, a stark classical portico of stone pillars tacked on in 1790, its dozens of tall narrow windows glowing amber in the low evening light like smouldering coals, not glass. A pediment pierced by a round bull’s-eye window crowned the front, and two wings strung to the main block by glassed colonnades held the stables and out offices, each capped by a gilded vane that glinted like a shard of flame. Beyond, the park stretched flat and endless, dotted with ancient oaks and fringed by dark stands of fir that clawed at the soft blue sky. Far off, hidden in the trees at the park edge, the parish church clock struck six, the sound drifting slow and soft on the wind, and its golden weathercock was the only other thing catching the light. For a breath, Stephen felt a fragile, homesick sort of hope: this place might be kind to him.

No one in the shire could quite fathom why the austere, reclusive Mr. Abney—his distant elderly cousin, whose offer of shelter had come out of nowhere—would take a small orphan boy into his quiet, book-locked household. Cambridge’s Greek professor had once said no man alive knew more of the dark lost rites of late paganism than the master of Aswarby. His library held every existing volume on the Orphic mysteries, Mithraic worship, and the wild doctrines of the Neo-Platonists. The marble entrance hall held a great imported marble statue of Mithras slaying the bull, written up at length in the *Gentleman’s Magazine*, and he’d published a whole series of obscure papers on the secret superstitions of the late Roman empire. He was a man wrapped tight in his manuscripts, and the whole neighbourhood gossiped nonstop about his bizarre decision to take on the orphaned Stephen.

When the front door swung open, Abney himself darted out of his study, thin bony hands rubbing together so fast the knuckles whitened, his long pale face stretched into what he clearly thought was a welcoming smile.
“How are you, my boy? How old are you?” he blurted, then paused without waiting for an answer. “You aren’t too tired from the journey to eat your supper, I hope?”
“Quite well, thank you, sir,” Stephen said, bewildered.
“Good lad. And how old are you, my boy?”

The second time he asked, Stephen’s skin prickled. “I turn twelve next birthday, sir.”
“Your birthday—eleventh of September, isn’t it? Splendid. Nearly a whole year away. I like to write these things down, you see, in my little book. Quite sure you’re twelve? No mistake?”
Stephen nodded, and Abney waved the staid butler Parkes off to take the boy down to the housekeeper’s quarters.

Mrs Bunch was the warmest thing in the whole of Aswarby, plump, flour-dusted, perpetually wiping her hands on her apron, and she and Stephen were thick as thieves within fifteen minutes. Born locally fifty-five years prior, she’d lived at the Hall for twenty, and knew every crevice, every forgotten old story the place held. She answered all his silly eager questions: who built the broken stone temple at the end of the laurel walk? Why did the portrait of the old man on the staircase have a skull tucked under his hand, half hidden from view? But there were two stories she hesitated to tell, until one cold November evening Stephen sat cross-legged by her fire and asked, all serious, “Is Mr Abney a good man? Will he go to heaven?”

Mrs Bunch huffed, but her smile faded. “Bless the child, of course he’s kind. Don’t you know he took two little homeless waifs in, years back? One little gipsy girl, Phoebe, the one he found wandering the lanes, and then a little foreign Italian boy, named Jevanny, who played the hurdy-gurdy on the drive.” She spoke slow, picking at a loose thread on her apron. The girl had stayed three weeks, Mrs Bunch said, and then one morning she was gone. No tracks, no trace, no note. Abney had all the Hall ponds dragged for her, turned the park upside down, swore he was frantic with worry. But there’d been gipsy singing drifting around the house all the night she vanished, Parkes swore he heard voices calling for her in the woods, and everyone assumed her people had snuck her away. The boy, Jevanny? He’d disappeared the exact same way, one winter morning, left his hurdy-gurdy sitting on the highest pantry shelf, still covered in rosin dust. No one ever saw him again.

That night, Stephen had the dream. His bedroom was at the far end of the top floor passage, beside an old disused bathroom kept locked, its upper door half glass, the old muslin curtain long gone. He found himself staring through that glass, the moon shining bright through the bathroom window, and there on the lead-lined bath laid a figure: thin, dust-coloured, wrapped in a yellowed shroud, lips pulled tight in a faint, terrible smile, both hands pressed so hard to its chest Stephen thought he could see the bones digging through the skin. A thin, gurgling moan seeped out of its mouth, and its arms began to twitch.

Stephen woke screaming, standing barefoot on the cold passage boards, the moon full on his face. Against every instinct of a twelve-year-old boy, he tiptoed to the bathroom door and peered in. The bath was empty. He ran back to his bed and pulled the covers over his head.

Mrs Bunch sewed a new muslin curtain over that glass the next morning, no questions asked. Stephen told Abney about the dream over breakfast, and his cousin’s eyes lit up, scribbling pages of notes in his little leather notebook, muttering that this was exactly as the old texts had said. Abney began reminding him, almost daily, that the spring equinox was coming—ancient peoples believed it was a critical, dangerous time for young boys, he said, he ought to lock his bedroom window tight every night, Censorinus wrote whole chapters on the dangers that lurked for the young then.

Two weeks later, Mrs Bunch was mending Stephen’s nightgown when she yelped, holding it up to the fire. The left side of the chest was scored full of long, parallel slits, six inches long, some half-cut through the linen, as if someone had dragged long sharp nails slow, deliberate, right over where his heart lay. “They’re the same scratches that are on my bedroom door,” Stephen said, bewildered. “Too high for a cat to reach. I never made them.”

Mrs Bunch’s face went white as flour. She snatched a candle and ran upstairs, came back ten minutes later shaking. “Not a word to Mr Abney, you hear? They’re marks like my old uncle used to talk about, the ones the Chinese sailors say their ghosts leave. Lock your door every night before you say your prayers, Master Stephen. Lock it tight.”

The next evening, Parkes the butler stumbled into Mrs Bunch’s parlour, so flustered he’d forgotten to wipe the wine stains off his apron. “I’m not going down to the far end of the wine cellar after dark, not for no man,” he said. “Heard voices down there, two of them, whispering in a foreign tongue, talking about stolen hearts. Like two children, down there in the dark bin. You can go listen if you don’t believe me. I’m not going back.” He clammed up the second he noticed Stephen in the corner, pretended he was joking, and wouldn’t say another word.

March 24, 1812, was a day of screaming, tearing wind, the kind that made it sound like hundreds of unseen people were rushing past the house, screaming for something they’d lost. After luncheon, Abney found Stephen by the library shelf and touched his shoulder, his fingers cold as ice. “Come to my study at eleven tonight, Stephen. I’ve got something to show you, something that will shape your whole future. Don’t tell Mrs Bunch, don’t tell Parkes. Go to your room at your usual bedtime, no one needs to know.”

Stephen peeked through the library door on his way upstairs that evening. The old brazier he’d always seen in the corner was dragged out before the fire, smoking thick with stinking incense. A gilded silver cup full of dark red port sat on the oak table, beside a stack of yellowed handwritten pages. Abney was sprinkling more frankincense into the brazier, and didn’t notice the boy watching.

The wind died after ten, leaving the night dead still, the moon hanging full and white as a polished bone. Stephen stood at his open bedroom window, looking out over the mere, and heard strange cries drifting across the water: not owls, not waterfowl, high thin despairing wails, drifting closer, until they seemed to circle the shrubberies at the edge of the lawn. Then they stopped.

Two figures were standing on the gravelled terrace below his window. A girl, thin, in a ragged gipsy dress, hands clasped tight over her chest, that same faint terrible smile from his dream. Beside her stood a thin ragged Italian boy, black hair matted, his skin so translucent Stephen could see the moon shine right through his outstretched, claw-nailed hands. And on the left side of his chest, there was nothing but a wide black gaping hole, empty where his heart had been. A wordless, hungry scream flooded straight into Stephen’s head, no sound needed, so sharp it made his teeth ache. The two of them glided across the gravel, silent as shadows, and vanished around the corner of the Hall.

Stephen grabbed his candle and ran for Abney’s study, his heart hammering so hard he thought it would burst out of his own chest. The door was not locked, but his knocks got no answer. He heard a choked, gurgling cry from inside, then silence. He shoved the door open so hard it slammed against the wall.

The scattered pages on Abney’s desk told the whole story, for when Stephen was old enough to read them:
The ancients wrote that you could gain total power over the spirits that rule the elements, defy death itself, steal immortality, by absorbing the hearts of three living people under the age of twenty-one. Simon Magus did it, killing a young boy to steal his heart so he could fly and turn invisible. Hermes Trismegistus laid out the ritual plainly, step by step. For twenty years, I have worked at this. My first subject: Phoebe Stanley, gipsy girl, taken March 24 1792. My second: Giovanni Paoli, the hurdy-gurdy boy, taken March 23 1805. My third, final, perfect sacrifice: my cousin Stephen Elliott. His heart will be cut from his living body tonight, burnt to ash, stirred into the red wine, and the power will be mine. The disused bathroom and the far wine cellar make perfect hiding places for their remains. Their feeble ghosts can do no harm to a man of philosophic mind.

Abney was slumped back in his leather chair, eyes bulging, his face twisted with unnameable terror. A great ragged gaping wound tore through his left side, right over his heart. The clean sharp ceremonial knife he’d laid out on the table was still spotless, no blood on it. The study window stood wide open to the moon. The coroner ruled he’d been mauled to death by a stray wild cat that had wandered in from the fens. But Stephen knew better. He caught one last flash of two small figures vanishing into the dark of the fir wood that night, their hands pressed gentle to their chests, their long-lost hearts beating warm and whole at last, free of Aswarby Hall, free of the man who had stolen them.
