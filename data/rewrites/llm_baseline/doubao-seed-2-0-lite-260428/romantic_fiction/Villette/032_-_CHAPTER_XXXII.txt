It is high time, now, to ask: where was Paulina Mary, and what had become of my quiet, intermittent visits to the grand, gilded Hôtel Crécy? Our little pattern of meetings had been broken for weeks by absence: M. de Bassompierre and his daughter had been travelling, splitting their time between sunlit French provincial towns and the glitter of the Parisian season, and I had not known the exact day of their return until chance brought me face to face with them.

I was wandering a quiet, tree-lined boulevard one mild April afternoon, slow under the soft honeyed sun, the air sharp with the faint green scent of new linden leaves and the first bloom of lilacs tucked in garden borders, when I spotted a small group of riders halted mid-path, as if they had just stumbled on one another, laughing as they exchanged greetings. On one side was a middle-aged gentleman and a young lady, on the other, a tall, striking young man who sat his horse as lightly as a bird. The lady held herself with a grace so rare, her riding habit stitched with such quiet, costly care, that even from a distance I knew I did not gaze on a stranger. Drawing a little closer, I recognised them all at once: the Count Home de Bassompierre, his daughter Paulina, and Dr. Graham Bretton.

Graham’s face was lit so bright it might have caught and held the very April sun. The joy on it was warm, true, and yet edged with a soft shyness, the kind of reverence only a man who has finally found the exact treasure he has spent years unknowingly searching for can feel. This was the exact state of things to bind Dr. John heart and soul: the pearl he worshipped was pure, rare, priceless in itself, but he was never a man who could admire a fine gem blind to the beauty of its setting. Had he met Paulina as a pretty, unguarded working girl, on foot in a simple cotton gown, he would have thought her a lovely, charming creature, admired the lightness of her step and the softness of her smile, but he would never have fallen as utterly, as completely vanquished as he was now. Graham was a man of the world through and through: his own private pleasure was never enough. The world he moved in must approve, society must admire the choice he made, or he would count his own actions foolish and unworthy. In the woman who would rule his heart, he required all that stood before him now: the mark of gentle, careful upbringing, the quiet protection of a father who would never let a harsh thing touch her, the little graces that fashion decrees, that wealth buys and good taste refines. Every single one of his unspoken conditions was met here, perfectly, and now he stood proud, passionate, and half afraid, doing open, soft homage to Paulina as his queen. As for her, the smile on her lips was not the smile of someone who knew she held power, but the soft, secret smile of a girl who felt joy bloom deep in her bones.

They parted not long after. Graham spurred his horse and passed me at a gallop, skimming over the paving stones so lightly he hardly seemed to touch the earth, his eyes fixed on nothing ahead but the fading shape of Paulina on her horse, so wrapped up in his own happiness he did not even see me standing at the path’s edge. He looked magnificent: all his old quiet purpose was roused, his whole frame thrumming with bright, eager life.

“Papa, there is Lucy!” called a clear, musical voice I would have known anywhere. “Lucy, dear Lucy, do come over here!”

I hurried to her side. She pushed back her riding veil, leaned down from her saddle to press a warm, soft kiss to my cheek. “I was coming to call on you tomorrow,” she said, laughing, “but now you shall come to me instead. Come at seven o’clock, won’t you? I will have no other guests.” I promised I would be there.

The next evening found me shut away with her in her private bedchamber, the fire burning low and ruddy in the hearth, the curtains drawn against the soft spring dusk. I had not seen her since that evening at the pensionnat, when her quiet, gentle charm had outshone the bright, shallow Ginevra Fanshawe so completely, and she had no end of stories to tell me of her travels. She spoke fast and vividly, painting little pictures of village festivals and Parisian galleries, and yet her voice was so soft, her diction so artless, that she never seemed to speak too quickly or say too much. Even my attention, long used to being easily wearied by idle chatter, did not flag. But after a little while, she seemed eager to wrap up her tales quickly. Silence fell then: a restless, dreamy silence, full of little signs that her thoughts were far away.

“Lucy,” she said, turning to me, her voice half timid, half pleading.

“I am right here,” I told her.

“Is my cousin Ginevra still staying at Madame Beck’s?”

“Still there. I thought you would be longing to see her.”

“No, not much,” she said, twisting the edge of her silk gown between her fingers. “I suppose she still talks about being married?”

“Not to anyone you care for,” I assured her.

“But she still thinks of Dr. Bretton, of course? She couldn’t have changed her mind, not when she was so set on it two months ago.”

“It does not matter, truly,” I said. “You saw for yourself what little there ever was between them. She is not unhappy, rest assured.”

She paused, then, a little flush rising in her cheeks. “During our absence, did you ever hear or see anything of Graham?”

“Papa got letters from him now and then, about business,” she said. “He offered to manage some legal matter for us that needed tending while we were out of the country. Dr. Bretton seems to respect papa very much, and goes out of his way to do him favours.”

“I saw you two meet on the boulevard yesterday,” I said. “You could tell from his face that his friends never need worry over his health, could you not?”

“Papa said the same thing, when he thought no one was listening,” she smiled. “Papa is never very observant, he is always lost in his own thoughts, but as Graham rode away he muttered ‘It does a man good to see that boy’s spirit and energy.’ He still calls him a boy, you see, just as he still thinks I am his little Polly, not a grown woman at all.” She left her armchair then, and came to sink onto the low little stool at my feet. I stroked her soft hair back from her forehead. It is not often I say I truly like someone I meet, but I liked her deeply. Long acquaintance and close insight had never revealed anything in Paulina but delicacy, intelligence, and perfect sincerity. My regard for her was quiet, but rooted deep.

“What is it you want to ask me, little one?” I said. “Be brave, speak out.”

But her eyes dropped, and the flush on her cheek burned deeper, a warm, inward excitement that had nothing to do with a passing surface blush. “Lucy, I want to know what you truly think of Dr. Bretton. Tell me your real, honest opinion of his character, his disposition. You know him well.”

“His character is sterling, and everyone who knows him respects it, and they are right to.”

“And his disposition? Tell me what he is like as a son, you have seen him with his mother.”

“He is the kindest, most devoted son in the world. He is his mother’s whole comfort, her pride, her greatest joy.”

She held my hand between both of hers, and stroked my fingers gently, one soft caress for every favourable word I spoke. “What else is he good at? Tell me more.”

“Dr. Bretton is gentle, benevolent to every living soul. He would show kindness even to the most savage stranger, or the worst criminal in the world.”

“I heard some of papa’s friends saying that exact same thing not long ago,” she said, her eyes shining. “They say the poorest patients at the hospital, who tremble before all the other cold, selfish surgeons, run to meet him when he walks the wards.”

“They are right,” I said. “He once took me on a tour of that very hospital, and I saw how they welcomed him.”

She lifted her eyes for a second, bright with soft, quiet gratitude. She had more to say, but she hesitated, as if waiting for the room to grow darker, the hour later. The dusk was pooling soft in the corners now, the firelight gilding only the edges of the furniture. “It is so quiet and secluded here,” I said, to reassure her.

“Yes. No one will call us down for tea. Papa is dining out this evening.” She kept playing with my fingers unconsciously, slipping her rings one by one onto my digits, then winding a strand of her own beautiful golden hair around my wrist, before she pressed my palm to her warm cheek. When she spoke at last, her voice was as clear and soft as a lark’s song, only a little unsteady. “You must think it very strange that I talk so much about Dr. Bretton, ask all these questions, care this much. But—”

“Not strange at all,” I said. “Perfectly natural. You like him.”

“And if I did?” she said, with a little flash of quick petulance, just like the little girl Polly I had known at Bretton all those years ago. “Is that any reason I should gush about it? You must think I am silly and weak, just like my cousin Ginevra.”

“If I thought you were one bit like Ginevra, I would not be sitting here waiting to hear what you have to say. I would stand up, walk straight across the room, and lecture you until you were tired.”

“I am going to tell you anyway,” she retorted, the old sensitive, stubborn spark back. “If I loved Dr. John so much I felt fit to die of it, that would never give me leave to be noisy about it, to whine and weep about a crush that was only one sided, would it? You would despise me, Lucy Snowe, if I lost all self-control that way.”

“It is true I have no patience for girls who brag about their little romantic triumphs, or wail about their silly little heartbreaks. But you, Paulina? Speak as much as you like. I want to hear every word. Whatever you tell me will be a relief to you, and a pleasure to me.”

“Do you truly care for me, Lucy?”

“Truly I do, child.”

“And I love you. Even when I was a little naughty, stubborn girl at Bretton, I always felt most at peace when I was with you. Now I trust you more than anyone. Listen, then.” She leaned her weight gently against my arm, no greedy, heavy weight like Ginevra’s, light as a bird. “A few minutes ago you asked if we had heard from Graham during our travels, and I said there were two business letters for papa. That was true, but I did not tell you all of it. I evaded.” She smiled, a little guilty. “Now I will tell you the whole truth. It is dark enough now, no one will see my face. Papa always lets me sort through the post bag and hand him his letters. One morning three weeks ago, what do you think I found? Amidst a dozen letters all addressed to M. de Bassompierre, there was a note addressed to Miss de Bassompierre. I spotted the handwriting instantly. It was Graham’s. I was just about to say ‘Papa, here is another letter from Dr. Bretton’ when the ‘Miss’ on the envelope stopped my tongue. I had never received a letter from a gentleman in my whole life. Should I have handed it straight to papa, let him open it and read it first? I could not, Lucy. I know how papa sees me: he forgets I am grown. He still thinks I am a little schoolgirl. So I handed him all twelve of his letters, his whole little pile of post, and kept back my one, my very own letter. It lay in my lap all through breakfast, like a secret alive, looking up at me. I felt like I was two people at once: still a little girl to my dear papa, but not a child to myself any more. After breakfast I carried it straight up to my room, turned the key in the lock behind me, and sat there staring at the outside of my treasure for ten whole minutes. I could not bring myself to break the seal right away. It was like storming a fortress, you do not rush right in. Graham’s handwriting is just like him, you know, clear, firm, rounded, no messy slants, no careless ink blots. His seal was just as perfect: a full solid drop of wax, a clean, sharp impress, no harsh point pricking at your eye. It is exactly like his face, the same clean, chiselled lines. I cut the paper around the seal with my embroidery scissors, so I did not break the wax at all. Then I pulled back again, one more time. I did not want to drink that sweet draught all at once, the sparkle in the cup was too beautiful, I wanted to look at it a little longer. Then I realised I had not said my prayers that morning. I had woken late, seen papa go down to breakfast earlier than usual, and run down straight after dressing, thinking I could put prayers off later. But a little voice in my head said I ought to pray first, before I dared read what I ached to read. I knelt down, and I prayed that no matter what happened, I would never hurt papa, never make him sad, never put anyone above him. The very thought of making him unhappy made me cry. But I knew, too, that one day he would have to be told, that he would have to understand his little girl was grown.

“I read the letter then. Lucy, people say life is full of nothing but disappointments. But I was not disappointed. My heart did not just throb, it trembled fast, every beat like a thirsty creature that has just found a well, drinking deep. The well was full, the water clear as glass, no mote, no moss, no tiny insect floating in it, the sun shining right through the golden ripples. Life is supposed to be full of pain, people say. I have read biographies where people wander from one sorrow to the next, where hope flies just out of their reach, where they sow in tears and their harvest gets blighted before it can ripen, and they end the winter with empty shelves, starving in the cold dark. I am not a particularly good person. But God let me grow up safe, warm, sheltered by my dear father, and now… now another person has come. Graham loves me.”

We sat quiet for a long minute, the fire popping soft in the hearth. “Does your father know?” I asked, at last, in a low voice.

“Graham wrote of papa with such deep respect, but said he dared not speak to him yet, not until he had proved he was worthy, not until he knew what my feelings were. I replied to him, I did not turn him away. But I wrote the letter three times over, Lucy! Every time I rewrote it, I made it a little calmer, a little more restrained, until it was like a block of cold ice with just the faintest hint of sugar and fruit flavour in it, so he would not think me too eager. Then I sent it.”

“That was perfect,” I said. “Your instinct is so fine. You understand Graham perfectly.”

“But what will I do about papa? I am so afraid to tell him.”

“Do nothing at all, for now. Wait. Only promise me you will not write another letter to him, not until your father knows everything and gives you his full permission.”

“Do you think he will ever agree?”

“Wait, and time will tell. You already did the right thing, didn’t you?”

“I wrote him one letter back, telling him that while my feelings stayed the same, I could not write to him any more without papa’s knowledge. He wrote once more after that, so grateful for my calm little note.”

“Dr. Bretton will respect that more than any gushing, eager note you could have sent. It will make him prouder of you, fonder of you, if it is even possible for him to be fonder.”

“Lucy, I know how delicate he is,” she said. “I know no amount of care is too much for how I treat him.”

“And you will be just as truthful, just as open, just as gentle with your father, when the time comes.”

“Oh it will hurt so much to wake him up from his dream, to tell him I am not his little girl any more!” she whispered.

“Do not rush it,” I told her, smoothing her hair. “Leave that to time, to fate. She has been so gentle to you all your life, she will arrange the perfect hour. I knew from the very first day I saw you two together at La Terrasse that you two were meant for one another. Everything about you and Graham fits perfectly. Your youth will not end in storm and strife. You will get to live the quiet happy life so few mortals ever get, the kind of life that feels like a leftover trace of Eden. Not everyone gets that, I know. Some people walk through nothing but storm and gale, get lost in the early winter dark. But that path is not yours. You are safe, and you are loved.”

Outside, the last of the April sun had faded entirely, and the soft spring dark wrapped around the room like a blanket. Somewhere far off, a church bell rang soft, for evening prayers, and we sat on in that golden hush, holding hands, no more words needed.
