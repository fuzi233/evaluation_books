On the appointed day, our two heroes Pen and Warrington turned up at Mr. Bungay’s Paternoster Row doorstep—*not* the scuffed public entrance where a never-ending parade of dewy-eyed, nervous scribblers loitered clutching crumpled virgin manuscripts, praying Sultan Bungay would toss them a few shillings for their unwritten masterpieces. No, this was the fancy private side door, from which Mrs. Bungay would glide out to her chaise, fluffing her satin skirts on the cushions and sending unmistakeable death glares straight across the street at Mrs. Bacon’s drawing room windows. Because at that point, Emma Bacon had no chaise at all.

Whenever Mrs. Bacon was particularly seething at her sister-in-law’s new carriage, she’d fling her sash all the way up, plant herself behind the glass with her four cherubic little kids lined up beside her, and practically hold up a giant neon sign that read “*Look at these four darlings, Flora Bungay!* You’d give a whole coach and four to have a brood of your own instead of sitting alone in that pretty chariot, childless and seething.” Those four little Bacon kids were her sharpest weapons, and she was not afraid to fire them straight across the cobblestones.

Just as Pen and Warrington trotted up, a grand old carriage and a rickety cab pulled up directly opposite, at the Bacons’ door. Portly old Dr. Slocum descended from the carriage like a boulder rolling downhill—his whole ponderous vibe, from his creaky boots to his booming lecture voice, made all the booksellers in the Row pause and listen, just out of habit. Two impossibly bright white waistcoats then tumbled out of the cab.

Warrington snickered. “See? Bacon’s throwing his own dinner party too. That’s Slocum, who wrote *Memoirs of the Poisoners*. You’d never spot our friend Hoolan under that fancy waistcoat, would you? And there’s Doolan, one of Bungay’s lads, making his way over.” Sure enough, Hoolan and Doolan had ridden over from the Strand in the same cab, bickering the whole trip over who got stuck paying the shilling fare. Doolan was clad in sober black, but his hands were engulfed in a pair of enormous brand-new white gloves he kept staring at like they were a pair of priceless pet swans.

Inside Bungay’s hallway, the porter was squeezed into a too-small evening coat, and a small army of men wearing gloves nearly as big as Doolan’s (but of fancy Berlin web) were lined up to snatch hats and coats and yell guests’ names up the stairs as loud as they could. A few folks had already arrived, and the only lady in the drawing room was Mrs. Bungay, decked out in scarlet satin and a turban that looked like it had been decorated for a royal parade. She curtsied to every new arrival, but it was obvious her mind was elsewhere: she was fully obsessed with spying on the Bacons’ dinner party. The second she’d greeted the last guest, she zoomed back to the window nook to gawk at every single one of Emma Bacon’s guests pulling up in their carriages. The sight of Dr. Slocum’s giant, horse-drawn vehicle made her physically wince—so far, every single guest who’d knocked on *her* door had showed up in a shabby hack cab.

All the guests were literary types, most of whom Pen had never met. There was Mr. Bole, the actual hardworking editor of Bungay’s magazine, whose entire job was done for the glory of the nominal chief, Mr. Wagg. There was Mr. Trotter, who’d burst onto the scene a decade prior as a tragic suicidal poet, and had since mellowed out to spend all his days reading slush piles in Bungay’s back office. Then there was Captain Sumph, a washed-up old dandy who claimed he was besties with Lord Byron and vaguely related to half the peerage, who had never once opened his mouth without mentioning some long-dead Regency poet. “I remember poor Shelley at school, getting sent up for good for a poem I wrote every single line of, by Jove!” he’d announce, or “I was right there with Byron at Missolonghi, offering to bet Gamba fifty pounds that the wind would turn…” and everyone would nod along like he was telling the truth. Mrs. Bungay ate every single one of his aristocratic anecdotes up, and he was almost as big a celebrity in her eyes as the great Mr. Wagg himself. If he’d turned up in his own carriage? Bungay would have bought every book the man ever scribbled, sight unseen.

Mr. Bungay bounced around his guests doing the honours, brimming with over-the-top cordiality. “How do, sir? Fine day, innit? Glad to see you, sir! Flora love, let me introduce Mr. Warrington to you. Mr. Warrington, Mrs. Bungay; Mr. Pendennis, Mrs. Bungay. Hope you lads brought huge appetites, eh? You, Doolan, I *know* you have, you’ve always had a deuce of a hollow leg.”

“Lor, Bungay!” Mrs. Bungay huffed, pretending to be scandalized.

“Faith, a man’d have to be a proper fussy eater not to polish off a good dinner in this house,” Doolan simpered, winking and stroking his lean cheeks with his giant fluffy gloves, trying to flatter Mrs. Bungay so hard she nearly rolled her eyes out of her head. She later told her friends privately that she couldn’t stand that simpering little man, no matter how hard he tried.

As they chattered, Mrs. Bungay’s eyes went wide: a magnificent dapple-grey cab horse came trotting fast down the Row, with a pair of tiny white-gloved hands holding the reins, a pale face sporting a very dramatic chin tuft peeking out the window, and a tiny little groom bobbing over the top of the cab. “The Honourable Percy Popjoy is *perfectly* on time!” she yelped, and zoomed to the front door to wait for him.

“That’s Percy Popjoy,” Pen said, leaning out the window to see the man himself clamber down the cab steps in boots so lacquered you could see your face in them. It was none other than the eldest son of Lord Falconet, who was Bungay’s very posh star author.

“He was my fag at Eton,” Warrington said, snorting. “I definitely should have whupped him a few more times.” Pen had also run into him at the Oxbridge Union debates, where he’d absolutely wiped the floor with poor Percy, who soon stumbled into the drawing room, hat under his arm, face round and dimpled and grinning with that particular dumb good humor that comes from being born into a title.

The porter yelled “THE HONOURABLE PERCY POPJOY” at the top of his lungs, and Percy jumped so hard he nearly dropped his hat. “Why’d that bloke go and take my hat, Bungay?” he complained. “Can’t bow to Mrs. Bungay without it, mate. You look absolutely stunning today, Mrs B, by the way. Haven’t seen your carriage in the Park lately—where’ve you been? I missed you, honest I did.”

“P’raps you’re just teasing,” Mrs. Bungay simpered, already half in love.

“Me? I never make jokes—hullo! Pendennis? Warrington? How the devil did you two end up here?” he said, spinning around on his shiny lacquered heels, forgetting all about Mrs. Bungay for a second.

“Those two know him?” Mrs. Bungay hissed at Bungay, eyes wide.

“Top fellers, I tell you,” Bungay whispered back. “The young one’s chums with half the nobility.” The pair of them immediately started beaming and bowing even harder, just as the next two great celebrities were announced: Mr. Wenham and Mr. Wagg.

Wenham drifted in with his usual smug little smile, staring down at the tips of his own shiny little boots like they were the most interesting thing in the room, barely bothering to make eye contact with anyone. Wagg, meanwhile, burst in with a laugh loud enough to rattle the teacups, his enormous bright white waistcoat glowing, his red chubby face shining with excitement at the thought of jokes and a full dinner. Nothing bad could get Wagg down for long—no debt, no bad review, no domestic squabble, could ruin his mood when he knew a good dinner was waiting. And if he spotted a lord? He’d fire off a pun before he even said hello.

Wenham crept over to Mrs. Bungay, whispering soft little compliments and peeking at her from under his eyebrows, while Wagg told her she looked ravishing, then bounded straight over to Percy Popjoy, called him “Pop”, and immediately launched into a raunchy funny story seasoned with just the right amount of coarse salt. He was over the moon to see Pen too, slapping him on the back so hard Pen nearly spilled his drink, yelling about how they’d last met down in Baymouth, asking after all their fancy posh friends at Clavering Park, and making sure everyone in the room knew he dined at country manors and was chums with baronets and duchesses. Wenham also shook Pen’s hand, and Mrs. Bungay took careful notes, already mentally jacking up the fee for any article Pen might write for her husband’s magazine later.

Pen had read and even admired some of the work of the last guest, Miss Bunion, the poet who wrote *Passion-Flowers*. He’d expected her to match her own self-description: a shy shrinking violet, a timid little fawn hiding in the oak woods, delicate and soft. So he was more than a little surprised to see a bony, six-foot woman in crumpled satin come creaking into the room with a step heavy enough to match a grenadier’s march. Wagg spotted a piece of straw stuck to the hem of her dress and was about to leap up and pick it up, but Miss Bunion just planted one big boot on the straw to separate it from her skirt, picked it up herself, and told Mrs. Bungay she was sorry she was late, but the omnibus was very slow, and wasn’t it lovely that you could ride all the way from Brompton for sixpence? No one laughed—she said it so plainly, no trace of embarrassment at all, like being poor was no more shameful than having a bit of dust on your coat.

“Is that the Passion-Flowers poet?” Pen whispered to Wenham. “Her portrait in the book makes her look like a sweet young thing.”

“Passion flowers go to seed same as every other flower,” Wenham murmured, smirking. “That portrait was painted nigh on twelve years ago, I’d wager.”

“Well, I like her for not being ashamed of it,” Pen said.
“Oh, I quite agree,” Wenham said, who would have starved in a garret before he ever turned up to a formal dinner on an omnibus. “But does she really have to wave that straw around like a trophy? My dear Miss Bunion, how do you do? I was in a duchess’s drawing room this very morning, and everyone was raving about your new volume. Those lines you wrote about Lady Fanny Fantail’s christening? Brought the Duchess herself to tears. I told her I’d likely meet you today, and she begged me to pass on her thanks for such a masterpiece.”

That story, delivered with such a smug, soft smile, completely one-upped Wagg’s whole name-dropping routine, and Wenham spent the next ten minutes reeling off endless anecdotes about dukes and lords, trying to drag Percy Popjoy into the conversation. But Percy couldn’t care less—he’d snuck back to the window nook with Mrs. Bungay, gawking at the Bacons’ guests pulling up across the street, and Mrs. Bungay was glowing, because she was 100% sure Emma Bacon could see her with the Honourable Percy Popjoy hanging out in her drawing room.

By the time St Paul’s bells tolled half past the dinner hour, the last two guests finally turned up, and Pen’s face lit up: it was Captain and Mrs Shandon. After greeting the Bungays and nodding awkwardly at the rest of the room, Pen and Warrington bounded over to shake Mrs Shandon’s hand warmly, and she got a little teary, remembering where they’d last met just a few days prior. Shandon was all spruced up for the occasion, in a red velvet waistcoat and a starched frill with his wife’s very best brooch pinned right to the front. But poor Mrs Shandon was trembling like a leaf near the formidable Mrs Bungay in her scarlet satin and bird-of-paradise feather, and only relaxed a little when Mrs Bungay boomed out asking after her “dear little gurl.”

“Nice-looking woman,” Percy Popjoy whispered to Warrington. “Introduce me to Captain Shandon, won’t you? Everyone says he’s dead clever, and dammit I adore intellect, by Jove I do!” It was true: Heaven hadn’t blessed Percy with much of a brain of his own, but it had given him an overabundant urge to hang around people who did. “And introduce me to Miss Bunion too! She’s clever, right? She looks a bit odd, but who cares. Dammit I’m a literary man myself, I want to meet all the clever folks.”

Just as the two of them finished getting acquainted, the dining room doors were flung open, and everyone trooped in to sit down. Pen ended up wedged between Miss Bunion on one side and Mr. Wagg on the other—Wagg had made a dramatic show of fleeing the empty seat next to the poetess, leaving Pen high and dry.

Miss Bunion barely talked the whole dinner, but Pen noticed she had a fantastic appetite, and never turned down a glass of wine the butler offered her. After staring Pen over for a minute, in his fancy dandy getup with all his gold chains and fancy shirt studs, she pegged him immediately as a stuck-up Mayfair prig, and decided it was far more sensible to focus on her food than make small talk with him. She told him as much later, straight out: “I thought you were one of those silly little dandies, looking as solemn as a tiny undertaker, and since I couldn’t stand the odious bloke on the other side of me, I decided to eat my dinner and keep my mouth shut.”

“And you did both perfectly, my dear Miss Bunion,” Pen laughed.

“Well, I did, but next time I’m going to talk your ear off. You’re not half as stupid or prissy as you look.”
“Ah, Miss Bunion, I’m already counting the days till that next dinner,” Pen said, grinning. But back to the feast at Paternoster Row.

The dinner was unbelievably over-the-top, what Wagg whispered to Pen as “florid Gothic style, all gaudy garnish and no actual flavour.” The waiters, all wearing creaky shoes and formal black coats, zipped back and forth behind the guests chattering quietly to each other, and Doolan accidentally yelled “WAITHER!” at one of them before blushing so hard his ears turned bright red. Mrs. Bungay’s tiny footboy was completely lost in the crowd of giant waiters, half of whom Pen realized were moonlighting undertakers from Amen Corner across the road, the kind who worked both funerals and fancy dinner parties.

“See that bloke over there with the huge bow window waistcoat?” Wagg murmured. “He buries people for a living. Cold meat for funerals, hot meat for dinners—simple. And mark my words, wherever there’s a fake undertaker butler, there’s fake wine. This sherry’s absolute rubbish. Hey Bungay, old boy, where’d you get this *delicious* brown sherry? Best I’ve tasted all year!”

“Glad you like it, Wagg! Glass with you!” Bungay boomed. “Got it from Alderman Benning’s store, paid a fortune for the stuff, I’ll tell you that. Mr Pendennis, join us! Your health, lads!”
“Old rogue bought it from the pub down the street,” Wagg snickered to Pen a second later. “This stuff’s so strong you need two blokes to carry a single bottle. I wish I had a case of my old friend Lord Steyne’s wine here—your uncle and I drank dozens of bottles of the stuff together. He sends his chef and his butler around with ice buckets full of champagne to all his friends’ dinner parties, you know. I remember once at poor Rawdon Crawley’s—Sir Pitt’s brother, the governor of Coventry Island—Steyne’s whole kitchen staff showed up at the door before the guests even arrived.”

“This dinner’s absolutely perfect,” Percy Popjoy gushed. “You must have a proper cordon bleu French chef in your kitchen, Mrs Bungay!”
“Oh yes, your lordship,” Mrs Bungay said, having not the faintest clue what a cordon bleu was, and assuming it was some kind of fancy new jack chain for the stove.
“I mean a French cook,” Percy clarified politely.
“Oh yes, your lordship, of course we do!”
“Is your chef actually claiming he’s French, Mrs B?” Wagg yelled across the table.
“Well I’m sure I wouldn’t know,” she said, blushing.
“Because if he is, he’s *quizzin’ yer*!” Wagg cackled, but no one got the pun, and the poor joke fell completely flat, leaving him a little deflated. “The whole dinner’s from Griggs, the caterer in St Paul’s Churchyard, same as Bacon’s,” he whispered to Pen. “Bungay pays half a crown a head more than Bacon to make his fancier, Bacon pays half a crown more than Bungay to make *his* better. They’d poison each other’s ices if they could sneak into the other’s kitchen. This dish here? *Brimborion à la Sévigné*? Absolutely delicious, Mrs B!” he added, helping himself to whatever weird creamy mess the undertaker waiter was holding.

Mrs Bungay nodded, flustered, having never heard of that dish name before, and instantly decided she hated Wagg with every fibre of her being. She would have fired him from running Bungay’s magazine
