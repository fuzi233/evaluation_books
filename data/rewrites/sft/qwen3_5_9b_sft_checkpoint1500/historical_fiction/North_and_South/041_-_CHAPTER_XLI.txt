The journey was ending, yet the season offered no corresponding warmth. Winter was slowly yielding to lengthening days, but the February sun brought no brightness, no spark of hope to the heavy, grey atmosphere. Mrs. Thornton had completely withdrawn from the household, her presence a memory rather than a reality. Mr. Thornton visited occasionally, but his stops were brief, confined strictly to the study where he conferred with her father. To Mr. Hale, these rare encounters seemed to hold a heightened value, a precious currency of trust. From what Margaret could discern, Mr. Thornton's silence stemmed not from wounded pride or lingering resentment regarding the strike, but from the sheer complexity of his business affairs, which demanded a focus far greater than he had given last winter. Yet, amidst his serious discourse, Margaret noticed a thread of consistency: whenever his name arose, or when he spoke of her, it was always with a calm, friendly neutrality. Neither avoidance nor active pursuit marked his words; they simply existed, unburdened by the storm clouds of the past.

Margaret herself lacked the resilience to lift her father's spirits. The long period of anxiety and turmoil that had preceded this dreary, peaceful stillness had stretched her mind thin, robbing it of its elasticity. She sought solace in teaching the younger Boucher children, working diligently at "goodness" with a fierce, almost painful intensity. Her heart, however, remained numb, dead to the fruits of her labor. Though she performed her duties punctually, a shadow of bleakness clung to her existence, rendering her life devoid of cheer. Her greatest triumph, ironically, was not in her teaching but in her silent, unconscious piety toward her father. She was a mirror for his moods, anticipating his unspoken wishes with a meek obedience that bordered on the divine. These were quiet desires, often hesitated over, yet she fulfilled them with a completeness that softened the edges of their shared solitude.

Spring arrived with news from abroad: Frederick's marriage. Letters flowed between him and Dolores; hers, a blend of Spanish and English, and his, peppered with linguistic inversions that revealed how deeply his bride's culture was taking root. Upon receiving a letter from Henry Lennox detailing the slim chances of a court-martial clearing Frederick's name, Frederick responded with a vehement renunciation of England. He spoke of wishing to "unnature" himself, declaring he would reject a pardon and refuse to return to the country if permitted. At first, these words tore at Margaret, seeming unnatural and harsh. But reflection softened her blow; she saw the raw poignancy of a hope so thoroughly crushed. Patience became her only shield. Soon, Frederick's tone shifted to joyful anticipation of the future, and Margaret found within herself the capacity for the patience he craved. Dolores's letters, though penned by a young Spaniard, possessed a charming timidity, filled with erasures and feminine care designed to impress her lover's English relations. Accompanying these missives was a magnificent black lace mantilla, chosen by Dolores herself for her unseen sister-in-law—a paragon of beauty, wisdom, and virtue whom Frederick described. This union elevated Frederick's worldly station significantly, placing him as a junior partner in Barbour & Co., one of Spain's most extensive trading houses. Margaret smiled faintly, then sighed, recalling her old tirades against commerce. Here was her *preux chevalier*, her brother, transformed into a merchant trader. Yet, rebellion stirred within her, protesting the false equivalence she felt between a Spanish merchant and a Milton mill-owner. Regardless of trade or profession, Frederick was undeniably happy, and the mantilla was exquisite. Thus, she returned to the weight of the present.

Her father had begun to suffer from occasional difficulties in breathing, distressing him greatly. While Margaret was less alarmed, noting the condition vanished in intervals, her heart urged him to accept Mr. Bell's invitation to visit Oxford in April. Mr. Bell's note specifically commanded Margaret's attendance, yet she felt a profound relief in remaining quietly at home, free from any external responsibility. For over two years, she had carried the burden of caring for an invalid, planning his days and soothing his ailments. Now, with her father gone, a strange, staggering liberty descended upon her. No one depended on her for cheer; no illness required her vigilance. She could be idle, silent, forgetful—and, perhaps most liberating of all, unhappy if she chose to be. For months, her personal sorrows had been stuffed into a dark cupboard, hidden from view. Now, with leisure, she could unpack them, mourn over them, and dissect their nature. Sitting motionless in the drawing-room for hours, she reviewed the bitterness of every remembrance with an unwincing resolve. Once, a cry escaped her lips at the sting of the faithlessness that had birthed her abasing falsehood.

Yet, she refused to acknowledge the force of the temptation that had led her astray. Her plans for Frederick had failed, leaving the temptation a dead mockery, a lie so despicably foolish in retrospect that faith in truth seemed infinitely wiser. In her nervous agitation, she opened a book belonging to her father. The words that caught her eye seemed tailored to her state of acute self-abasement: *"Meurs de honte... reveilons-nous, et quittons-la pour jamais..."* ("Die of shame... let us wake up and leave it forever"). "The way of humility," Margaret thought. "Ah, that is what I have missed!" She resolved to turn back, trusting in God's help to find the lost path.

Rising, she summoned Martha, whose grave, mechanical obedience masked a depth of individual character. Through gentle probing, Margaret learned a surprising history: Martha's father, once a warehouse clerk, had been in early life connected to Mrs. Thornton's husband, even showing him kindness. Circumstances had separated the families until Martha grew up poor and destitute, facing the prospect of being "lost." Then, Mrs. Thornton and Mr. Thornton intervened. They had nursed sick Martha in their own home, sending her to the sea despite medical warnings about contagious fever. "Miss Fanny," Martha noted, referring to Mrs. Thornton's daughter, had feared the contagion but ultimately allowed the care to proceed. "And she's going to be married!" Martha exclaimed. To a wealthy gentleman named Watson, older than she, with mills beyond Hayleigh. "A very good marriage," Martha concluded, despite Watson's grey hair. Margaret listened in silence, the news settling over her like a heavy cloak, before Martha swept up the hearth and departed with her usual wooden composure. Margaret realized she must stop the habit of imagining how every event concerning Mr. Thornton would affect him, weighing his potential joy or displeasure.

The next day, after lessons with the Boucher children and a long walk, Margaret visited Mary Higgins. To her surprise, Nicholas was already home. The lengthening daylight had deceived her regarding the hour. Seemingly entering a new phase of humility, Nicholas was quieter, less self-asserting. He remarked on the old gentleman's departure, attributing the wanderlust to the weather, and mentioned that Master Thornton was also "spinning about the world." When Margaret innocently asked if this explained his early arrival, Nicholas dismissed it with contemptuous confidence. He was not a man of two faces; he owed his position to Margaret, and Thornton's mill, while decent, was merely a tool for honest labor. "Stand down, lad, and say yo'r pretty hymn to Miss Marget," he instructed, leading her to teach the little boy a Methodist hymn. Its swinging rhythm captivated him, and he recited it with the cadence of a parliamentarian, eventually requesting another, and yet another, drawn unconsciously into an interest in sacred matters he had once scorned.

Returning home late, Margaret found the house empty, a comfort that allowed her to think freely. Resolving to sort through a large packet of letters, she selected those destined for destruction. Among them were several from Henry Lennox regarding Frederick's affairs. Initially, she intended only to assess the legal merits, but as she read, the stiff, formal wording betrayed a deeper current. Lennox had never forgotten his relationship to her. The letters were clever, lacking the hearty, genial atmosphere she expected, yet they were invaluable. She preserved them. As the sorting ended, a reverie seized her. Thoughts of her absent father haunted her mind; she almost blamed herself for having felt his solitude as a relief. But these two days had restored her, filling her with new strength and brighter hope. Tasks that had previously seemed burdensome now appeared as pleasures. The morbid scales had fallen from her eyes, revealing her position and work more clearly. If Mr. Thornton would restore their lost friendship, or merely visit to cheer her father, she felt a sense of clarity returning to her future, even if it were not brilliant. A sigh escaped her as she prepared for bed. Despite the single, plain duty of devotion to her father, an anxiety and a pang of sorrow lingered at her heart.

Meanwhile, across town, Mr. Hale thought of Margaret with the same persistent strangeness. Fatigued by revisiting old friends and familiar places, he had harbored exaggerated fears of how his altered opinions might be received. Yet, as he anticipated, shock and grief quickly melted into a tender gravity. Those who had once loved him penetrated the silence beneath his reserve, taking him to their hearts with a protecting kindness reminiscent of how they would treat a beloved woman. This renewal of affection, after so many years and changes, overwhelmed any roughness or disapproval.

"I'm afraid we've done too much," Mr. Bell warned, concerned by Hale's apparent frailty. "You're suffering from living so long in that Milton air."
