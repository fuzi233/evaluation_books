From the well-loved fairytales we read as children, we all know that no christening or wedding ever passes without at least one guest nursing a grudge, swearing vengeance for the slight they have suffered. So no one need be surprised to learn that cousin Will Esmond raged and cursed with unbridled fury the second he learned his older brother had secured a formal engagement to the wealthy colonial heiress. In the first flush of his wrath, Will stormed out of the house swearing he would never set foot in Castlewood again. But no one, not even Will himself, put much stock in his dramatic oaths. The unrepentant prodigal drifted back home after only two days: the promise of the lavish wedding feasts spread out across Castlewood’s tables was too great a temptation for him to resist. He returned to eat and drink his fill, framing every mouthful as a petty act of revenge. He raised a brimming bumper to the newly married couple, muttering a curse of perdition on the bride under his breath. As the earl spoke his vows to love, honour, and cherish his new wife in the castle chapel, Will’s responses to the service were all smothered maledictions.

He was far from the only disgruntled party to the match, as George Warrington soon discovered. In the months that followed, he heard an endless litany of abuse directed at the new countess and her grandfather. The old merchant’s City friends, his legal advisor, the Dissenting minister whose chapel they had attended on their first arrival in England, and even gentle Jack Lambert, the earnest young orthodox divine who had cherished hopes of winning her hand, all seethed at what they called the young American’s treachery. Every one of them had a tale to tell of being charmed, led on, then cast aside by the heiress. The lawyer, who had once compiled a meticulous inventory of all her properties, estates, ships, enslaved people, and future fortunes, now swore the entire list was a fabrication: he claimed New York and Virginia were hardly real places, that the Guinea trade was a myth, and that the old planter Van den Bosch had invented every last “black falsehood” about his Virginian holdings. The Dissenting pastor moaned over his stray lamb – if such a wily, mischievous creature could ever be called a lamb at all. Poor Jack Lambert wrote to his mother to confess he still kept a lock of the heiress’s black hair, which he soaked with tears and addressed in language far too unclerical for a man of his calling.

As for Will Esmond, he howled and cursed so loudly the walls almost shook – even within earshot of his new sister-in-law – calling on Acheron itself to bear witness that no such artful young devil as Miss Lydia existed anywhere above ground. He swore she was an infernal female Cerberus, and rained down every curse he could conjure on his swindling rascal of a brother, who he claimed had cajoled him with fair words only to steal his fortune right out from under his nose.

“Why,” George said dryly, when Will ranted to him at length on the subject, “if the girl is half the she-devil you describe her to be, you should count yourself lucky to have missed her. If you’re right and she means to deceive her husband and slip him a dose of poison, you escaped a terrible fate! You ought to thank the gods, Will, for robbing you of that fury. The best revenge you could possibly get on my lord is letting him keep her all to himself.”

“That’s all very well,” Will snarled back, “but that doesn’t make my brother any less of a scoundrel, cheating me out of the fortune I risked my very life to win.”

George blinked, hardly able to fathom how Will had ever risked his precious life (which, George privately thought, a whipping at the end of a rope would have suited perfectly). Will, never one for false modesty, spilled the whole story: how the little Cerberus herself had arranged that duel between the two of them, the one broken up by Sir John Fielding’s men; how she had constantly taunted George for sneering at Will, and taunted Will in turn for being too much of a poltroon to retaliate to George’s jibes. She’d even spread tales that George had run screaming from Braddock’s battlefield, nearly dead of fright. “Deuce take me,” Will said, “I never was more surprised in my life than when you stood your ground that coolly in Tottenham Court Fields. My second and I had already wagered half a guinea you’d never even turn up to the meeting!”

George laughed out loud, and thanked Will for that flattering estimation of his courage. “Though it’s lucky for me the constables turned up when they did,” he added. “Another minute and you’d have run your sword right through my waistcoat. Didn’t you see how clumsy I was standing there before you? You were so angry you turned white all over and shook like a leaf!”

“Curse me, that’s just my way of showing rage,” Will grumbled, flushing bright red this time. “I was confoundedly angry with you, cousin! But now the only man I hate is my brother, and that little devil of a countess – a countess, indeed! A pretty countess she makes!” He punctuated the remark with another thunderous volley of oaths.

“Well, cousin,” George said, looking him dead in the eye, “you let me off remarkably easy. I dare say I owe you my life, or at the very least an undamaged waistcoat. I admire your forbearance and your spirit. What a waste it is for a man with your courage to be stuck as a mere court usher! The king’s army is missing out on a fine officer there, you truly are.”

“I never can tell if you’re joking or being serious, Mr. Warrington,” Will growled.

“Very few gentlemen would dare joke with you, cousin, if they valued their own ears or their hides,” George laughed. He loved teasing his grand kinsman in that deadpan way, watching with droll amusement as Will choked back curses, ground his teeth too cowardly to lash out, and smothered his seething, petty anger. “And you really ought to moderate your language when you speak of the dear countess and my lord your brother,” he went on. “They both speak of you with nothing but the greatest tenderness. Her ladyship has told me every last detail.”

“Every last detail?” Will yelped, taken aback.

“As much detail as any woman ever tells, cousin. She confessed she thought you were more than a little in love with her. What woman could possibly resist liking a man who adores her?”

“Why, she hates you,” Will blurted out, “says you were wild about her, Mr. Warrington!”

*Spretae injuria formae*, cousin!” George quoted.

“For me? What’s for me?” Will asked, confused.

“I never cared for her at all, which is probably why she dislikes me. Don’t you remember that old story of the captain of the guard’s wife?”

“Which guard?” Will frowned. “Lord Falmouth is Captain of the Yeomen of the Guard, Lord Berkeley of the Pensioners. Before that it was Lord Hobart. I suppose you haven’t been in England long enough to know who’s who, cousin.”

George explained he was talking about the captain of the guard for the King of Egypt, whose wife hounded the young man Joseph for rejecting her advances. Will waved the story off, saying Egypt must be a confoundedly long way away, and if that lord’s wife was telling lies about people, that was just the way of women the whole world over.

The truth was, when George paid his first post-wedding visit to Castlewood, the new countess had told him her own version of the falling out between herself and Will. It differed wildly from Will’s account – though George would not say for certain which of them was telling the full truth. The little countess grieved to think how sadly she had been deceived in her brother-in-law. She feared his years hanging around court and the town had eroded the noble, high principles all Esmonds were born with. She claimed Will’s word could never be trusted, that his loose lifestyle and endless money troubles had made him mercenary, blunted his honour, and even eaten away at the chivalrous courage “that we Esmonds,” she tossed her pretty little head as she spoke, “that we Esmonds must all possess – at least, you and me, my lord, and my cousin Harry have it, I know! Oh, cousin George! I must confess I was even led to doubt your courage for a moment, and no man of our ancient and noble family is worthy of the name if he lacks that! As a Christian lady, and the head of one of the very first families in all the kingdom and the whole world, I shall try to forgive my brother William for speaking ill of a member of our family, even if you are a younger branch through the female line, and for making me doubt you, however briefly. That’s exactly what he did. I daresay he told you a hundred wicked lies about the things you supposedly said about me.”

“I, my dear lady?” George cried, astonished.

“Things he claimed you said about me, cousin. I hope you never did, and I pray heartily that you didn’t. I can afford to despise any such stories. It is true he paid me court, of course – so have plenty of others, I’m quite used to it. He might even have fared better than he did, back before I met my dear lord and realised how great and eminent his qualities are, out of the fullness of my grateful heart now! But oh! I soon found William was completely lacking in courage. No man without a spark of it can ever earn the esteem of Lydia, Countess of Castlewood, never! He claimed it was you who lacked spirit, cousin, and made me so angry by telling me you were always abusing me. But I forgive you, George, truly I do! And when I tell you that he was the coward – the mean skunk! – who actually sent for those constables to break up that duel between you and him, you won’t wonder I don’t value a fellow like that even a single pinch of snuff.” She snapped her little fingers for emphasis. “Noblesse oblige, and an Esmond without courage is worth nothing to me. Look at our ancestors on these walls! The Esmonds have always fought for their king and their country. Not a single one of us would shrink back when the moment comes to prove he is an Esmond and a nobleman! If my eldest son ever showed the white feather, I would say to him ‘My Lord Esmond – that’s our family’s second title, you know – I disown you right now!’” As she spoke, she glanced around the drawing room at the stately portraits of her new forebears, painted by Lely and Kneller, lining every wall.

Very quickly, the new Countess had total dominion over the whole apartment, the entire house, the surrounding estate, and the neighbouring village. It was astonishing how fast she learned to command. She did not follow the usual unspoken rules of precedence that great English ladies abided by, but invented her own regulations, issued them as law, and made everyone around her submit. She had been raised a Dissenter, and was not overly familiar with the services of the Established Church – George noticed she made a few blunders in her first few attendances at chapel, for instance, forgetting when to turn to face east, a custom unknown in the other Reformed churches. But by George’s second visit to Castlewood after the wedding, she had mastered every part of her duties to perfection. She sailed into the chapel on George’s arm, her two footmen trailing behind her carrying her massive prayer book, as demure as the pious old devotee accompanied by her lackey in Hogarth’s famous *Morning* print, as if she had kept a personal chaplain her entire life. She patronised not just the new chaplain, but the whole service and the church itself, as if she had never once in her life sat through a ranting sermon in a barn back in the colonies.

She made all the most established county families – grave baronets and their wives, worthy squires who could trace their line back twenty generations – who rode over to Castlewood to pay their respects to the newlyweds, know their proper place, and give her precedence. She hunted down an old heraldry book, and tracked down a sharp old maiden lady from Winchester who was a walking encyclopedia of court etiquette and genealogy, who had learned the rules back in the days of Queen Anne. Before long she was chattering volubly about gules and sables, bends and saltires, not always correctly, but with unshakable confidence. She made grand excursions to the neighbouring towns in her gilded coach and six, or through the village in her sedan chair, claiming a near-royal right to homage from every tenant and labourer on the estate. She lectured the parson on his theology, the bailiff on best farming practices, instructed the astonished housekeeper on how to preserve fruit and pickle vegetables, and even tried to teach the fancy London footmen how to leap up behind the carriage, though it was too high for her little ladyship to climb up and demonstrate. She gave the village wives detailed instructions on how to nurse their children long before she had any of her own, and when it came to dosing the household with physic, she was every bit as stubborn and dictatorial as Madam Esmond was back in Virginia. It was hard not to think of the Fisherman and the Genie from the Arabian Nights: how had such a prodigious, forceful spirit ever been corked up inside such a tiny little bottle of a body?

When George headed back to London after that first post-wedding visit, she pressed him to carry small gifts back for his young friends in Dean Street, the Lamothes: a trinket for one, a book for the other. George swore to himself that Theo’s present was a fine wax doll, and Hetty’s a nursery primer full of single-syllable words. As for Will, her younger brother-in-law, she treated him with a kind of maternal gravity and tender protectiveness that struck George as endlessly diverting, even if it made Will’s usual cursing and blasphemy twice as loud as before.

Lydia had very little respect for old age as a general rule. As soon as the marriage settlements were signed and settled in her favour, she treated old Mr. Van den Bosch and his enormous periwig with no more ceremony than she treated Dinah, her personal Black attendant. She would pinch Dinah’s great ears and yank at her woolly hair without a second thought when the woman annoyed her – or so Dinah told Gumbo, who passed the tale on to his master. Every last soul in the household trembled before the new countess: the old housekeeper, who even the late dowager and the previous earl had treated with deference; the pampered London footmen, who used to grumble if they were disturbed mid-card game while they swilled endless tankards of beer, now scurried to obey the second they heard her ladyship’s voice. Even old Lockwood, the gate porter who had held his post for more than half a century, tried to rally his foggy old wits when she marched into his lodge to throw open his window, inspect his wood store, and chase his mangy old hounds out into the cold. Lockwood bared his bald old head, cast a pleading look at his niece, and trembled before the tiny new mistress’s authority.

Gumbo, dressing his master for dinner that evening, babbled about the prophet Elisha that the chaplain had read of that very morning, “with his bald head, and the bad boys who called him names, and the bears that ate ’em all up – served them right, too,” Gumbo said. But when George brought up old Lockwood to the countess later, she waved the old man off dismissively. “Pooh, pooh! Stupid old man! Past all his work, and his dirty old dogs! They’re as old and ugly as those mouldy old carp in the castle pond!” She pointed to the two legendary monster carp that local tradition claimed had lived in the Castlewood garden ponds for centuries, their backs crusted over with thick grey slime. “Lockwood has to go. The workhouse is the place for him. I’ll put a smart, tall, good-looking fellow in that lodge, who’ll do our livery credit.”

“He was my grandfather’s own man, and served him through all the Queen Anne wars,” George protested.

“O Lord, Queen Anne’s dead, isn’t she?” the countess snapped back petulantly. “We aren’t going to go into mourning for her, are we?”

The matter of Lockwood was brought up at the family dinner that night, when the countess formally announced her intention of dismissing the old porter.

“I’ve heard,” old Mr. Van den Bosch said demurely, “that the laws here in England take excellent care of poor old servants and all kinds of elderly poor folks. I do wish we had such asylums back home in the colonies – it would save us all the trouble and expense of keeping our old labourers on when they can’t work no more.”

“If a man can’t work, he ought to go!” the countess cried.

“Yes, indeed, that’s a fact!” her grandfather agreed.

“What? An old servant who’s served the family for fifty years?” the earl asked, frowning.

“Mr. Van den Bosch must have been very independent of servants back in his youth,” George remarked.

“Greased my own boots, opened my own shutters, sanded and watered my own – ” the old man began.

“Sugar, sir?” the earl teased.

“Not sugar, my son-in-law, the floor!” the old man laughed. “Though there’s plenty of that sort of trickery in the grocery stores, begging your ladyship’s pardon.”

“La, pa, what do I know about grocery stores?” Lydia cried, flustered.

“Hey, don’t you remember stealing the sugar as a boy, and the trouble it got you in, my dear ladyship?” her grandfather chortled.

“At any rate, a fine tall man in our livery will look far better than that shrivelled old porter creature!” the countess insisted.

“No livery is half as becoming as a head of silver hairs, madam,” George said, “and no lace as fine. What will the rest of the county say if you banish old Lockwood?”
