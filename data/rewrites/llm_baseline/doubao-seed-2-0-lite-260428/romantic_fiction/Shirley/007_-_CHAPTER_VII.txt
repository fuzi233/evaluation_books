Caroline Helstone was just eighteen, and at that age, the true story of your life has barely even turned its first page. Before you hit that milestone, you spend all your time listening to a tale half stitched from fantasy: sometimes delightfully soft, sometimes sad, but almost never real. Your world at seventeen is all heroic grandeur: the people in it are half angels, half demons, the woods darker and more enchanted than any real copse, the skies brighter, the streams more glittering and dangerous, the flowers sweeter than any you’ll find in a garden tended by mortal hands. The moon you gaze up at then hangs heavy with unnameable beauty, the silver light trembling in your bones like a secret the whole universe is sharing only with you. Your sun is a burning hearth stoked by the gods themselves.

At eighteen, you draw right up to the edge of that misty, dream-kissed fairyland you’ve lived in your whole life. Elfland recedes behind you, and the blue, soft-looking shores of Real Life rise up on the horizon, still far enough away that all you can see is the promise of spring meadows stretching beneath the haze, silver ribbons of running water glinting through the fog. You’re sure once you step off this dream-shore, you’ll never hunger or ache for anything again—never guessing that you’ll have to cross miles of barren wilderness, wade through rivers as cold and black as death itself, before you ever taste a single drop of true joy. Every happiness life has to offer has to be fought for, won with scraped palms and stinging breath, and only those who’ve wrestled for the greatest prizes know how hard that struggle cuts.

At eighteen, you don’t know any of that. When Hope leans in and promises you perfect happiness tomorrow, you believe her wholly. When Love wanders to your door looking like a lost, bruised angel, you fling the door open and pull him inside, never glancing at the quiver slung over his shoulder. When his arrows sink into your skin, you think the thrum in your veins is new life, not a wound. You never fear the poison in the tips, never imagine the barb will sit under your flesh forever, no healer able to pull it free. That wild, perilous passion that is agony at least half the time, for many agony from start to finish? You think it’s nothing but unqualified, glowing good. At eighteen, you’re just about to step through the doors of the school of Experience—whose lessons are humbling, bruising, grinding, but that leave you purified, strong, finally rooted in the real world.

Alas, Experience! No other mentor has a face so lined and frozen with weariness, no other wears a robe so deep and unrelieved black, no other carries a rod so heavy, no other grabs you by the wrist and drags you to your task so sternly, forcing you to learn no matter how you squirm. It’s only her guidance that can lead you safely through life’s wild thickets—without her, you stumble, you stray, you step into forbidden ground and go tumbling down cliffs you never saw coming.

That evening, after Robert Moore had walked her home, Caroline had no wish to linger in the same room as her uncle. His study was sacred, almost unapproachable space to her, and she stayed far away until the bell rang to call the household to prayers. Their small worship followed the standard church service, Mr. Helstone reading the words in his usual loud, nasal, perfectly monotonous tone. The moment the rite was done, Caroline stepped up to his side as she always did.
“Good-night, uncle.”
“Hey! You’ve been gadding all day—visiting, dining out, what not?”
“Only over at the cottage.”
“And did you learn your lessons?”
“Yes.”
“And finish that shirt you were mending?”
“Only half of one.”
“Well, that’ll do. Stick to your needle, learn to make shirts, gowns, and proper pie crust, and you’ll turn into a clever woman some day. Off to bed now. I’m busy with this pamphlet.”

A few minutes later, Caroline was locked in her small bedroom, the door bolted, her soft white dressing gown slipping over her shoulders, her long hair falling thick and wavy down to pool at her waist. She paused in the middle of brushing it, leaned her cheek on her hand, and stared down at the carpet, and all the gilded, tender visions that only come to girls of eighteen swirled up around her.
Her thoughts were speaking to her, warm and gentle, and she smiled listening to them. She looked lovely, lost in those daydreams—but brighter than any pretty face was the glowing spirit of Young Hope standing right at her shoulder. This sweet prophet told her she’d never know cold disappointment again: she’d stepped into the dawn of a perfect summer day, no false light, but the real, soft sunrise, and her sun would climb fast and bright. She had no idea she was being fooled. Her hopes felt solid, rooted in something unshakable.
“When two people love,” she reasoned to herself, “the next step is that they marry. I love Robert, and I know deep down Robert loves me. I’ve thought it a hundred times before, but today I *felt* it. When I looked up at him after reciting that Chénier poem, his eyes—what handsome eyes he has—sent the truth straight through my heart. Sometimes I’m too scared to talk to him, worried I’ll be too frank, too forward: I’ve said too much before, blurted out feelings I shouldn’t, and feared he’d think me silly and indiscreet. But tonight, I could have told him every single thought in my head, he was so kind, so gentle. How he walked with me down that lane! He doesn’t flatter, doesn’t say the foolish silly lines the heroes in books say. His affection—his friendship, I should say; of course I don’t call him my lover yet, but I hope he will be one day—is far better than anything in novels. It’s quiet, manly, sincere. I do love him. I’d be such a good wife to him. I’d tell him gently of his small faults, tend to every little comfort, cherish him, do everything I could to make him happy. I’m sure he won’t be cold tomorrow. I’m almost certain he’ll come here tomorrow evening, or ask me to come over to the cottage.”

She went back to brushing her hair, as long and flowing as a mermaid’s. When she turned her head to arrange it, she caught sight of herself in the mirror. Plain girls find their reflection sobering enough: they look at themselves and know no one will see any magic there. But a pretty girl draws different conclusions: the face gazing back is charming, so it must charm everyone else. Caroline saw her own soft cheeks, loose hair, the pink glow of hope in her eyes, and all her sweet daydreams felt even more real, more certain. She climbed into bed that night with a heart light as dandelion fluff.

She woke up the next morning still glowing, and walked into the breakfast room to wish her uncle a soft, cheerful good morning. Even that hard, gruff little man of bronze, Mr. Helstone, paused for a second and thought his niece was growing into quite the fine young woman. Usually she was quiet, timid, docile but never talkative around him, but that morning she chattered away about small, safe things she knew he wouldn’t mind: the new spring crocuses pushing up in the garden, when the gardener would come to trim the borders, the starlings starting to build nests in the church tower right next to the rectory, how she wondered the constant tolling of the bells didn’t scare them off.
Mr. Helstone huffed that they were just like every other silly newly paired fool, too wrapped up in each other to notice inconvenience. Caroline, giddy and brave from her good mood, dared to say something she never would have before.
“Uncle, you always talk about marriage like it’s something to scorn. Do you really think no one should marry?”
“Staying single is far and away the wisest plan, especially for women.”
“Are all marriages unhappy?”
“Millions are. If everyone told the full truth, I expect almost all of them are unhappy to some degree.”
“You always grumble when you have to officiate a wedding. Why?”
“Because I don’t care to be an accomplice to pure, unadulterated folly.”
He spoke easily, almost glad for the chance to air his harsh views on the subject. Emboldened that he hadn’t snapped at her yet, Caroline pushed further.
“But why is it pure folly? If two people like each other, why shouldn’t they choose to live together?”
“They tire of each other in a month. A spouse isn’t a companion—they’re just a fellow sufferer yoked to you for life.”
Caroline’s next remark wasn’t sweet naivete: it was sharp dislike for his cold views, quiet anger that he could dismiss all love like that.
“Anyone would think you’d never been married yourself, uncle. Anyone would take you for an old bachelor.”
“Practically, I am one.”
“But you were married. Why were you so inconsistent as to tie the knot?”
“Every man’s a fool once or twice in his life.”
“So you grew tired of my aunt, and she grew tired of you, and you were miserable together?”
Mr. Helstone pushed his lip out in that familiar cynical scowl, wrinkled his brow, and grunted something unreadable.
“Didn’t she suit you? Wasn’t she good tempered? Didn’t you grow used to her? Weren’t you sorry when she died?”
“Caroline,” he said, slowly lifting his hand an inch above the table before slamming it hard on the mahogany, “understand this. It’s vulgar and childish to mix up general rules with specific exceptions. Your questions are stupid and babyish. Ring the bell if you’ve finished your breakfast.”

The meal was cleared away, and customarily uncle and niece would part ways, not seeing each other again until dinner. But today, Caroline didn’t leave. She walked over to the window seat and sat down. Mr. Helstone glanced over at her uneasily a few times, clearly wishing she’d go, but she was staring out the window, seemingly paying him no mind, so he went back to his paper—full of exciting new dispatches from Lord Wellington’s campaigns in the Peninsula, that day. He had no idea what was swirling in her head, thoughts his harsh words had stirred up, but didn’t create: old, tangled memories that buzzed in her brain like disturbed bees in a hive, thoughts she’d carried with her for years.
She was replaying his views on marriage, and on the other side of that wide, uncrossable chasm between his cold outlook and her hopeful one, she saw a dim, sinister, half-forgotten figure standing next to him: her own father, James Helstone, Matthewson Helstone’s brother.
She’d heard rumors of him from old servants, dropped hints here and there: he wasn’t a good man, he’d never been kind to her. She remembered dark, sharp fragments of a few weeks spent with him in a big, smoky town somewhere: no maid to dress her or look after her, locked day and night in a bare garret with no carpet, a bed with no curtains, no other furniture at all. He left early every morning, often forgot to come back and feed her all day, and when he stumbled home drunk at night he was raving and furious, or worse—senseless, blank, impossible to reach. She knew she’d fallen ill there, and one night, burning up with fever, he’d burst into the room screaming he’d kill her, because she was nothing but a burden. Her screams had brought the neighbors running to break down the door. She’d never seen him again, except lying dead in his coffin.
That was her father. She had a mother, too, though Mr. Helstone never spoke of her, though she couldn’t remember ever seeing her face. She knew her mother was alive, though. That woman had been the drunkard’s wife. What could their marriage have been like? Caroline turned away from the window, where she’d been staring at the starlings without actually seeing them, and spoke in a soft, sad, bitter little voice that cut through the silence.
“You think all marriage is miserable, I suppose, because you saw what my parents went through. If my mother suffered half of what I suffered locked in that garret with Papa, she must have had a dreadful life.”
Mr. Helstone wheeled around in his chair, staring at her over the top of his spectacles, completely taken aback. Her father and mother? He’d never spoken a single word about them in the twelve years she’d lived under his roof. He never imagined she had any memories, any questions of her own about them.
“Your father and mother? Who’s been talking to you about them?”
“Nobody. But I remember bits of what Papa was like. And I pity Mama. Where is she?”
That question had sat on the tip of her tongue hundreds of times before, but she’d never dared say it out loud.
“I barely know her,” Mr. Helstone said, flustered. “I haven’t heard from her in years. Wherever she is, she doesn’t think of you. She never asks after you. I have good reason to believe she never wants to see you. Now come on, it’s school time. You go over to your cousin’s at ten, don’t you? The clock just struck.”
Caroline might have said more, but Fanny walked in to tell her master the churchwardens were waiting for him in the vestry. He hurried off, and Caroline set out for the cottage.
The road from the rectory down to Hollow’s Mill sloped gently downhill, so she ran most of the way. Fresh air, the exercise, the thought that she’d soon see Robert, or at least be near him, lifted her fallen spirits fast. When the white house came into view, and she could hear the mill’s great thundering roar and the rush of the mill race, the first thing she saw was Moore standing at his garden gate. He was wearing his loose, belted Holland blouse, a soft light cap over his hair, the casual suit fitting him perfectly. He was looking down the lane, not the direction she was coming from. She stopped, stepped behind a willow tree, and just studied him for a minute.
“He has no equal,” she thought. “He’s as clever as he is handsome. What sharp, bright eyes he has! What clear, spirited features—thin and serious, but so graceful. I do love his face, I do love the way he carries himself. I love him more than any of those shuffling little curates, more than anyone. My kind, bonny Robert.”
She stepped out to greet him, and when he saw her, she was half convinced he’d vanish like a phantom if he could. But he was solid, real, so he had to stand and greet her. His greeting was short, cool. Cousinly. Brotherly. Friendly. Anything but lover-like. All the nameless soft magic of the night before was gone from his manner. He was a different man, as if the warm heart she’d seen beating under his blouse last night was nowhere to be found. It was a rude, sharp shock of disappointment. At first the eager girl refused to believe the change, even though she could see it, feel it cold in her bones. It was hard to pull her hand away from his without getting at least a kind, gentle squeeze back. It was hard to drop her eyes from his without catching one soft, fond look, something warmer than that cool welcome.
A man disappointed in love can speak up, demand an explanation. A woman? She can say nothing. To speak would bring nothing but burning shame, bitter remorse for betraying her own heart out loud. She would feel such self-contempt afterward it would break her. The wisest thing to do is take what you’re given without questions, without complaint. You expected bread, and he handed you a stone? Grin and bear the scrape of it against your teeth, don’t scream. You held out your palm for an egg, and fate set a scorpion in it? Don’t flinch. Close your fingers tight around the gift, let it sting. In time, the scorpion will die, and you will have learned the greatest lesson of all: how to endure sorrow without a single sob.
Robert had done her no wrong, told her no lie. If anyone was to blame for the pain pricking at her chest, it was her own silly, hopeful heart. She’d loved him before he’d ever asked her to. It was a natural, inevitable thing sometimes, but it always brings misery. Robert had seemed fond of her, yes—but he’d fought that feeling, hard. He didn’t want to get tangled in an attachment that would force him into a marriage he knew was imprudent, bad for both of them. So he was pulling back, deliberately.
Caroline had more sense than to chase him. She left his side quietly, no complaints, no questions, not a single tear, no change in her calm, soft expression. She went to Hortense’s lessons as usual, and walked home for dinner without lingering.
Once she’d finished eating, and her uncle was lingering over his small, temperate glass of port wine, she was alone in the rectory drawing room, and one thought tangled in her head: how on earth was she going to get through the rest of this day?
Last night she’d hoped it would be just like the day before, full of soft hours and Robert’s company. She knew now that hope had been foolish—but she couldn’t quite shake the tiny, whispering thought that he might still stop by. He’d wandered up to the rectory after tea more than once before, even when he’d been distant with her, just to sit with her uncle and chat. He’d never talk much to her in her uncle’s presence, but he’d glance over at her, soft and relenting, while she sewed at her little work table, the few words he said to her gentle and kind. False Hope whispered that he’d come tonight. She almost knew it was a lie, but she still listened.
She tried to read, but her thoughts wandered far from the page. She tried to sew, but every stitch felt like a tedious chore, unbearable. She pulled out her desk to write a French composition, but all she could scrawl down was mistakes.
