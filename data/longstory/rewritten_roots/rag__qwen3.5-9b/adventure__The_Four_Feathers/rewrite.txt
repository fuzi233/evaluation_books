Slavery was woven into the fabric of daily life; hardly a street in that long, brown, treeless squalor was free from the clink of chains, a constant auditory reminder of the caste system's permanence.

But for the European, escape was a different calculus entirely. Each white prisoner was a marked man, isolated by the color of his skin and the weight of imperial expectation. Evasion required more than just the procurement of camels and the stationing of drivers; it demanded immense wealth, long preparations, and, crucially, devoted natives willing to risk their lives against the machinery of the state. The camels could be secured, but their drivers might vanish; the preparations could be made, only to be undone by the lash of the gaoler, who might flog a captive within an inch of death on a mere suspicion of hidden funds. The loyal servant might shrink at the critical moment. Colonel Trench began to lose all hope. He knew his friends worked for him—the boy bringing food would sometimes bid him be ready; a passerby might jostle his camel during a parade of the Khalifa's troops and whisper encouragement. But these gestures dissolved into the ether. Nothing ever came of them. He watched the sun rise daily beyond the bend of the river, burning across a sky that offered no solace, while the months dragged on, one agonizing iteration after another.

On an evening towards the end of August, in that year when Durrance returned blind from the Sudan, I sat in a corner of the enclosure, watching the sun drop westward toward the plain with an agony of anticipation. There was no window; a few small apertures near the ceiling made a pretense of ventilation, yet into this foul, pestilent hovel the prisoners were packed, screaming and fighting. Colonel Trench fought like the rest. There was a corner near the door which he coveted with a fierce desperation he had never felt in the days of his freedom. Occupying that spot meant shelter from the blows, the stamping feet, and the bruising pressure of neighboring shackles; it provided a support against which to lean his back during the ten interminable hours of suffocation.

"Oh!" General Feversham sighed. He requested no further elucidations. As he always had, he took the questions that baffled him and set them aside, banishing them from his thoughts entirely. Yet, he did not proceed to open his remaining letters. Instead, he continued smoking his cigar, gazing out over the summer landscape, listening intently to the distinct sounds rising from the fields below.

Sutch had already perused his entire correspondence before Feversham spoke again.

"I have been reflecting," the general said. "Have you noticed the date of the month, Sutch?"

Sutch looked up sharply. "Yes," he replied. "Next week, on this same day, marks the anniversary of our assault upon the Redan, and Harry's birthday."

"Precisely," Feversham affirmed. "Why should we not revive the Crimean Nights?"

Sutch sprang from his chair. "Splendid!" he cried. "Do you suppose we can muster a tableful?"

"Let us ascertain," Feversham said, ringing a handbell on the table to summon the servant for the Army List.

Bending over the list, the two veterans might have been left to their planning, but the narrative demands a final word concerning another figure in this tale.

That night, after the invitations had been dispatched from Broad Place and the last light extinguished within the house windows, a solitary figure leaned over the railing of a steamer anchored at Port Said. He listened to the rhythmic chanting of Arab coolies as they shuffled up and down the gangplanks, balancing coal baskets between the barges and the ship's hull. The chaotic clamor of the town drifted across the water to his ears. In his mind's eye, he visualized the flare of braziers upon the quays, the glowing port-holes, and the dark funnels looming ahead and behind in the procession of anchored vessels.

Attended by a servant, he had returned to the East.

Early the next morning, the steamer pushed through the canal, and as sunset approached, emerged into the chilling waters of the Gulf of Suez. Kassassin, Tel-el-Kebir, Tamai, Tamanieb, the siege of McNeil's zareeba—Durrance relived these moments, re-immersing himself in the golden years of his vigor, the era of abundance. Within that western land, the long preparations were advancing steadily, destined one day to roll up the Dervish Empire and crush it into dust. Upon the glacis of the ruined fort of Sinkat, Durrance had pledged his hand to this grand endeavor, yet the desert he loved had struck him down and cast him forth. Nevertheless, the boat steamed southward into the Red Sea.

Three nights more passed, and though he would not witness it, the Southern Cross would rise obliquely into the twilight sky.
