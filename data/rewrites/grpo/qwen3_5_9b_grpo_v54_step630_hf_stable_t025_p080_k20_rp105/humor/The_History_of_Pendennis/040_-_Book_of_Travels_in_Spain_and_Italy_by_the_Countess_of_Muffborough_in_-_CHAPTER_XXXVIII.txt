Better minds than Morgan, the valet, possessed a clearer understanding of Lady Clavering's actual wealth, yet the London rumor mill spun a tale of staggering opulence. Upon her arrival in the capital, the gossip claimed her fortune was infinite: indigo factories, opium clippers, banks swollen with rupees, and diamonds from native princes. The story went that these princes owed her father vast sums, paying interest that filled her London account with so many zeros it seemed to multiply the letter 'O' in every admirer's gasp. Even an envoy from an Indian Prince, the extraordinary Colonel Altamont—a man rumored to have embraced Islam after surviving a thousand perils—was currently in town, attempting to sell the Nawab of Lucknow's famous nose-ring, "the Light of the Dewan," to the Begum Clavering.

Before Lady Clavering even descended upon the city, her fame, branded as the Begum, had already swept through polite society. It was the boast of Delolme and Blackstone that the British Constitution admits merit of every kind, allowing the lowliest-born to wear the robes of a peer if they deserve it. So, too, should good society boast: though haughty and jealous of its privileges, it instantly removes all barriers for the wealthy. If an individual possesses enough coin, the gates fly open; they eat his dinners, dance at his balls, marry his daughters, or gift their own lovely girls to his sons with the affability of a commoner. This, they argued, proved British independence and honesty; the higher orders were not the haughty aristocrats the ignorant claimed, but rather, if a man had money, they held out their hands, eager to serve.

Chevalier Strong, having overseen the country mansion, now applied his taste to the fashionable London upholsterers preparing the town house for the Claverings. In decorating this elegant abode, honest Strong's soul rejoiced as if he were the proprietor himself. He hung and re-hung pictures, studied sofa placements, and interviewed wine merchants. Simultaneously, the Baronet's factotum furnished his own chambers and stocked his snug cellar. Friends complimented the neatness of Strong's quarters, while select guests sharing his cutlets now enjoyed excellent claret. The Chevalier was "in clover," residing in comfortable rooms at Shepherd's Inn, served by a former Spanish Legionary comrade he had rescued from a fort breach in Spain and elevated to body-servant. This loyal man was none other than the Nawab's favorite, the valiant Colonel Altamont.

Ned Strong, however, was a man of few curiosities and great discretion. He avoided inquiring into the mysterious connection established shortly after their meeting at Baymouth between Sir Francis Clavering and the Nawab's envoy. The Colonel clearly held some secret leverage over Clavering, a hold Strong suspected stemmed from the Baronet's irregular early life and lackluster career in India. Strong knew Clavering was a man weak in purpose, principle, and intellect—a moral and physical poltroon.

After their initial meeting, Strong witnessed several private interviews between Clavering and His Excellency, the nature of which Clavering never disclosed. Yet, Clavering sent letters via Altamont, who acted as his ambassador in all affairs. On one occasion, the envoy was in a foul mood, crushing the letter in his hand and declaring, "A hundred, be hanged. I'll have no more letters nor no more shilly-shally. Tell Clavering I'll have a thousand, or by Jove I'll split, and burst him all to atoms. Let him give me a thousand and I'll go abroad, and I give you my honour as a gentleman, I'll not ask him for no more for a year. Give him that message from me, Strong, my boy; and tell him if the money ain't here next Friday at twelve o'clock, as sure as my name's what it is, I'll have a paragraph in the newspaper on Saturday, and next week I'll blow up the whole concern."

Strong relayed this threat to his principal. The effect was immediate; on the appointed day and hour, the Chevalier appeared at Altamont's hotel at Baymouth with the required sum. Altamont behaved like a gentleman, paid his bill, and departed on a foreign tour, embarking at Dover. Strong concluded it must be forgery at the very least, noting that the Colonel had secured the bill.

However, within the year, the Colonel returned. A "confounded run on the red" had ruined him at Baden Baden; no gentleman could withstand a color rising fourteen times. Forced to draw upon Sir Francis Clavering for his return, Clavering, though pressed for funds due to election expenses and furnishing costs, reluctantly accepted the bill. He cursed the Colonel internally, wishing he were locked in a German debtor's goal for life, yet he paid. These sums were raised without Lady Clavering's knowledge. Though she was sumptuous in her own spending and inherited a knack for business from her father, Snell, she gave her husband only a handsome allowance, demanding accurate accounts for any extra payments. Regarding the subsidies to the Colonel, Clavering frankly told Strong he could not speak to his wife.

Strong's business involved procuring these funds. In his Shepherd's Inn apartments, negotiations flourished between moneyed gentlemen and Sir Francis Clavering, with valuable banknotes and stamped paper changing hands. A man accustomed to debt from youth rarely finds permanent fortune; the money-lender soon returns. Clavering found it more convenient to meet these creditors at Strong's lodgings. The Chevalier, though possessing not a shilling of his own, drew almost all the bills of exchange Clavering accepted. He discounted them in the City, parleyed with holders when due, and exchanged instalments for fresh acceptances. Like the troops at Comorn, who danced and played despite the threat of assault and the gallows, hundreds of gallant spirits in this town walked in good spirits, dining in gaiety and sleeping comfortably, with a bailiff nearby and a rope of debt around their necks. Ned Strong, the old soldier, bore these trifling inconveniences with ease.

Meanwhile, Lady Clavering and her friends were kept waiting at the doorsteps of Grosvenor Place. They entered a gorgeous dining room, fitted up in a middle-aged style, which Lady Clavering laughed off, saying, "Unless, said her good-natured ladyship, 'because me and Clavering are middle-aged people.'" They were offered the copious remains of the luncheon they had just shared. Our little Sylphide, who ate scarcely more than six grains of rice at dinner, was most active with her knife and fork, consuming a substantial portion of mutton cutlets—a hypocrisy she shared with other young ladies of fashion. Pen and his uncle declined the food but admired the room, pronouncing it "very chaste," the proper phrase. Indeed, there were high-backed Dutch chairs of the seventeenth century, a sculptured carved buffet of the sixteenth, a sideboard robbed from a Low Country church, and a large brass cathedral lamp over the round oak table. Old family portraits from Wardour Street, French tapestry, bits of armor, double-handed swords and battle-axes of carton-pierre, looking-glasses, saint statuettes, and Dresden china filled the space. Nothing could be chaster.

Behind lay the library, fitted with busts and books of uniform size, wonderful easy-chairs, and solemn bronzes in the severe classic style. Here, guarded by double doors, Sir Francis smoked cigars, read *Bell's Life in London*, and slept after dinner, unless he was smoking over the billiard table at his clubs or punting at Saint James's gambling-houses.

What could equal the chaste splendour of the drawing-rooms? The carpets were so magnificently fluffy that one's foot made no more noise than a shadow. On their white grounds bloomed roses and tulips as large as warming-pans. High and low chairs, bandy-legged seats so attenuated only a sylph could sit upon them, marquetterie-tables covered with gimcracks, china ornaments of all ages, bronzes, gilt daggers, *Books of Beauty*, yataghans, Turkish papooshes, and boxes of Parisian bonbons adorned the room. Everywhere sat Dresden shepherds and shepherdesses; light blue porcelain poodles, ducks, cocks, and hens; nymphs by Boucher and shepherdesses by Greuze, very chaste indeed; muslin and brocade curtains; gilt cages with parroquets and love-birds, two squealing cockatoos out-chattering each other; a clock singing tunes on a console-table, and another booming the hours like Great Tom on the mantelpiece. In a word, everything comfort could desire and elegant taste could devise. A London drawing-room fitted up without regard to expense is surely one of the noblest sights of the day. The Romans of the Lower Empire and the Marchionesses of Louis XV could scarcely have exhibited finer taste than our modern folks. Everyone who saw Lady Clavering's reception rooms confessed them most elegant; even Lady Harley Quin's, Lady Hanway Wardour's, or Mrs. Hodge-Podgson's rooms lacked such consummate "chastity."

Poor Lady Clavering, meanwhile, knew little of these things and showed little respect for the splendours around her. "I only know they cost a precious deal of money, Major," she said to her guest, "and that I don't advise you to try one of them gossamer gilt chairs: I came down on one the night we gave our second dinner-party. Why didn't you come and see us before? We'd have asked you to it."

"You would have liked to see Mamma break a chair, wouldn't you, Mr. Pendennis?" dear Blanche said with a sneer, angry because Pen was laughing with Mamma, who had made blunders describing the house.

"I should like to have been by to give Lady Clavering my arm if she had need of it," Pen answered, bowing and blushing.

"Quel preux Chevalier!" cried the Sylphide, tossing up her little head.

"I have a fellow-feeling with those who fall, remember," Pen said. "I suffered myself very much from doing so once."

"And you went home to Laura to console you," said Miss Amory. Pen winced. He disliked the memory of Laura's consolation and was displeased that his rebuff was known; so, turning his attention to the furniture, he praised Lady Clavering's taste with all his might.

"No, don't praise me," said honest Lady Clavering. "It's all the upholsterer's doings and Captain Strong's, they did it all while we was at the Park—and—and—Lady Rockminster has been here and says the salongs are very well," she added with deference.

"My cousin Laura has been staying with her," Pen said.

"It's not the dowager: it is the Lady Rockminster."

"Indeed!" cried Major Pendennis, hearing the great name of fashion. "If you have her ladyship's approval, Lady Clavering, you cannot be far wrong. No, no, you cannot be far wrong. Lady Rockminster, I should say, Arthur, is the very centre of the circle of fashion and taste. The rooms are beautiful indeed!" The Major's voice hushed as he spoke, surveying the apartments awfully and respectfully, as if at church.

"Yes, Lady Rockminster has took us up," said Lady Clavering.

"Taken us up, Mamma," cried Blanche, in a shrill voice.

"Well, taken us up, then," said my lady. "It's very kind of her, and I dare say we shall like it when we git used to it, only at first one don't fancy being took—well, taken up, at all. She is going to give our balls for us; and wants to invite all our dinners. But I won't stand that. I will have my old friends and I won't let her send all the cards out, and sit mum at the head of my own table. You must come to me, Arthur and Major—come, let me see, on the 14th.—It ain't one of our grand dinners, Blanche," she said, looking round at her daughter, who bit her lips and frowned savagely.

The Major, with a smile and a bow, said he would much rather come to a quiet meeting than a grand dinner. He had had enough of large entertainments and preferred the simplicity of the home circle.

"I always think a dinner's the best the second day," said Lady Clavering, trying to mend her speech. "On the 14th we'll be quite a snug little party;" at which blunder, Miss Blanche clasped her hands in despair, saying, "O, mamma, vous etes incorrigible." Major Pendennis vowed he liked snug dinners of all things and confounded her ladyship's impudence for daring to ask such a man as him to a second day's dinner. But he was economical, bethinking himself he could throw over these people if anything better offered, and accepted with the blandest air. As for Pen, not yet a diner-out of thirty years' standing, the idea of a fine feast in a fine house was perfectly welcome.

"What was that pretty little quarrel which engaged itself between your worship and Miss Amory?" the Major asked Pen as they walked away. "I thought you used to au mieux in that quarter."

"Used to be," answered Pen, with a dandified air, "is a vague phrase regarding a woman. Was and is are two very different terms, sir, as regards women's hearts especially."

"Egad, they change as we do," cried the elder.
