"Far, far from hence
The Adriatic breaks in a warm bay
Among the green Illyrian hills, and there
The sunshine in the happy glens is fair..."

These lines of Matthew Arnold spoke of a distant, sun-drenched Italy, yet they found a haunting echo in the rugged, shadowed valleys of Devon and Cornwall. Here, the landscape was not a gentle embrace but a jagged confrontation with the Atlantic. The "combs" of the west were deep, narrow gorges where the land rose abruptly from the sea, creating a world of stark contrasts. Inland, the earth was rich with oak woodlands and soft, fertile meadows; seaward, it dissolved into a terrifying wilderness of dark green furze, smooth turf, and towering cliffs of ironstone that stretched like the ribs of a leviathan into the deep.

Each cove possessed its own unique character: a crystal trout stream winding through gray stone mills, black rock pools teeming with salmon, and beaches strewn with golden trefoil and crimson lady's finger. But the most striking feature was the "mouth" of the cove itself—a black field of shark's-tooth rock paved with shell sand and laced with white foam. These formations, tilted by ancient earthquakes, formed a natural jaw, a barrier so formidable that a single rasp of its teeth could grind the timbers of the stoutest ship to splinters. To the landward side lay peace and richness; to the sea, a howling waste of rock and roller, barren to the fisherman and hopeless to the shipwrecked.

Among these mouths, one stood apart: Marsland. Protected by a long sea-wall of rock, it was the sanctuary of Lucy Passmore, the region's infamous "White Witch." It was here, under the pale light of a bright October moon, that two figures stood on the lonely beach just before midnight: Rose Salterne and Lucy herself. Rose, driven by a feverish mix of curiosity and superstition, had kept her appointment, drawn by the very danger of the spell.

"You be safe enough here to-night, miss," Lucy said, her voice cutting through the silence. "My old man is snoring soundly, and no soul sets foot here save the mermaids now and then. Goodness, Father, where's our boat? It ought to be up on the pebbles."

Rose pointed to a strip of sand forty yards nearer the water, where the small vessel lay abandoned.

"Oh, the lazy old villain!" Lucy exclaimed, bustling toward the boat with Rose trailing behind. "He's been chasing pollock around the rocks and forgot to haul her up. I'll trounce him when I get home. I only hope he made her fast, that's all! He's more plague to me than my money ever will be."

The goodwife checked the mooring line, relieved to find the boat secure, and the oars aboard. "Well, I never! Oh, the lazy thief, to leave them here to be stolen!" She sat down in the boat, coiling herself comfortably. "I'll just watch, dear, while you go down to the sea. You must be entirely alone, or you'll see nothing. There's the looking-glass; now go, dip your head three times, and mind you don't look to land or sea until you've spoken the words. Be quick, it's just upon midnight."

Rose hesitated, then slipped off her clothes, shivering in the cool night air. She stepped into the water, wading cautiously between two walls of rock. On her left, a towering cliff hid her in deepest shade; on her right, a lower rock face caught the full blaze of the moonlight, draped in festoons of live and purple sea-weed that shaded dark, goblin-filled crevices. Above, the peaks frowned black; above that, the downs slept bright and cold.

The breeze had died away. Not even a wave broke the perfect stillness of the cove. The gulls were asleep on the ledges. An autumn silence hung heavy, a silence so profound it seemed audible. Rose stood awed, listening for any sign of life beside her own.

A faint bleat, like a newborn lamb, drifted from above. She started, looking up. Then a wail from the cliffs, like a child in pain, answered by another from the opposite rocks. They were merely passing snipe and an otter calling to her brood, but to Rose, they sounded like supernatural goblins answering her call. Nevertheless, they only quickened her expectation. The witch had told her not to fear them. If she performed the rite duly, nothing could harm her.

Yet, as she stepped forward, mirror in hand, she felt the hammering of her own heart. She waded hastily as far as she dared, then stopped, aghast.

A ring of flame encircled her waist. Every limb was bathed in lambent light. The myriad life of the autumn sea, stirred by her approach, flashed suddenly into glory. Around her, the lamps of the sea nymphs swam—heavy, panting, fiery globes. Rainbows of crimson, azure, and emerald shattered into star-showers, lighting the wine-dark depths of the crystal. She saw every shell crawling on the white sand, every rock-fish playing in the crannies with broad, bright eyes staring at her. The great palmate oarweeds waving along the chasm seemed to beckon her down with long brown hands to a grave among their chilly bowers.

She turned to flee, but she had gone too far. Hastily dipping her head three times, she hurried to the sea's edge and, looking through her dripping locks at the magic mirror, pronounced the incantation:

"A maiden pure, here I stand,
Neither on sea, nor yet on land;
Angels watch me on either hand.
If you be landsman, come down the strand;
If you be sailor, come up the sand;
If you be angel, come from the sky,
Look in my glass, and pass me by;
Look in my glass, and go from the shore;
Leave me, but love me for evermore."

The words were hardly finished when her eyes strained into the mirror, seeing only the sparkle of water droplets from her hair, when the sound of rattling pebbles echoed down the beach—the hasty feet of men and horses.

Rose darted into a cavern of the high rock and hastily dressed. Peering out, half-dead with terror, she saw four men. Two had just leaped from their horses, leaving them to drift, and were helping the others run the boat down.

From the stern sheets arose, like an angry ghost, the portly figure of Lucy Passmore. She shrieked in a shrillest treble: "Eh! ye villains, ye roogs, what do ye want staling poor folks' boats by night like this?"

The party recoiled in terror. One turned to run up the beach, shouting, "'Tis a marmaiden—a marmaiden asleep in Willy Passmore's boat!"

"I wish it were any such good luck," Rose heard Will say; "'tis my wife, oh dear!" He cowered, expecting the hearty cuff he received duly. The White Witch, leaping from the boat, dared any man to touch her and thundered to her husband to go home to bed.

The wily dame, as Rose guessed, was using this delay to buy time for her pupil. But she had more solid reasons for making the fight as hard as possible. She, like Rose, had already discerned in the ungainly figure of one of the party the suspicious Welsh gentleman whose calling she had divined long ago. As a loyal subject, she held in extreme horror her husband's meddling with such "Popish skulkers"—unless on consideration of a very handsome sum of money.

In vain Parsons thundered, Campian entreated, Mr. Leigh's groom swore, and her husband danced round in an agony of mingled fear and covetousness.

"No," she cried, "as I am an honest woman and loyal! This is why you left the boat down to the shoore, you old traitor, you, is it? To help off such noxious trade as this out of the hands of her majesty's quorum and rotulorum? Eh? Stand back, cowards! Will you strike a woman?"

This speech, as usual, was merely indicative of her intention to strike. Getting out one of the oars, she swung it round and round fiercely, finally catching Father Parsons such a crack across the shins that he retreated with a howl.

"Lucy, Lucy!" shrieked her husband in a shrillest Devon falsetto. "Be you mazed? Be you mazed, lass? They promised me two gold nobles before I'd lend them the boot!"

"Tu?" shrieked the matron with a tone of ineffable scorn. "And do you call yourself a man?"

"Tu nobles! tu nobles!" shrieked he again, hopping about at oar's length.

"Tu? And would you sell your soul under ten?"

"Oh, if that is it," cried poor Campian, "give her ten, give her ten, brother Pars—Morgans, I mean—and take care of your shins, Offa Cerbero, you know—Oh, virago! Furens quid faemina possit! Certainly she is some Lamia, some Gorgon, some—"

"Take that, for your Lamys and Gorgons to an honest woman!" In a moment, poor Campian's thin legs were cut from under him. The virago, mounting on his trunk astride like the famous one on Hudibras, cried, "Ten nobles, or I'll keep ye here till morning!" And the ten nobles were paid into her hand.

Now the boat, its dragon guardian pacified, was run down to the sea, passing close to the nook where poor little Rose was squeezing herself into the farthest, darkest corner among wet sea-weed and rough barnacles, holding her breath as they approached.

They passed her, and the boat's keel was already in the water. Lucy had followed them close, for reasons of her own. Perceiving a dark cavern near the water's edge, she cunningly surmised it contained Rose and planted her ample person right across its mouth. She grumbled at her husband, the strangers, and above all at Mr. Leigh's groom, to whom she prophesied pretty plainly Launceston gaol and the gallows. The wretched serving-man, who would as soon have dared to leap off Welcombe Cliff as to return railing for railing to the White Witch, vainly entreated her mercy, trying by all possible dodging to keep one of the party between himself and her, lest her redoubted eye should "overlook" him once more to his ruin.

But the night's adventures were not ended. Just as the boat was launched, a faint halloo was heard upon the beach. A minute later, a horseman plunged down the pebbles and along the sand, pulling his horse up on its haunches close to the terrified group before dropping, rather than leaping, from the saddle.

The serving-man, though he dared not tackle a witch, knew well enough how to deal with a swordsman. Drawing his weapon, he sprang upon the newcomer, then recoiled.

"God forgive me, it's Mr. Eustace! Oh, dear sir, I took you for one of Sir Richard's men! Oh, sir, you're hurt!"

"A scratch, a scratch!" almost moaned Eustace. "Help me into the boat, Jack. Gentlemen, I must with you."

"Not with us, surely, my dear son, vagabonds upon the face of the earth?" said kind-hearted Campian.

"With you, forever. All is over here. Whither God and the cause lead"—and he staggered toward the boat.

As he passed Rose, she saw his ghastly, bleeding face, half bound up with a handkerchief that could not conceal the convulsions of rage, shame, and despair twisting it from all its usual beauty. His eyes glared wildly round—and once, right into the cavern. They met hers, so full, keen, and dreadful, that forgetting she was utterly invisible, the terrified girl was on the point of shrieking aloud.

"He has overlooked me!" she said, shuddering to herself, recalling his threat of yesterday.

"Who has wounded you?" asked Campian.

"My cousin—Amyas—and taken the letter!"

"The devil take him, then!" cried Parsons, stamping up and down upon the sand in fury.

"Ay, curse him—you may! I dare not! He saved me—sent me here!" With a groan, he made an effort to enter the boat.

"Oh, my dear young gentleman," cried Lucy Passmore, her woman's heart bursting out at the sight of pain. "You must not go forth with a grane wound like that. Do ye let me just bind mun up—do ye now!" She advanced.

Eustace thrust her back.

"No! Better bear it, I deserve it—devils! I deserve it! On board, or we shall all be lost—William Cary is close behind me!"

At that news, the boat was thrust into the sea faster than ever it had gone before, only in time. It was just rounding the rocks, out of sight, when the rattle of Cary's horsehoofs was heard above.

"That rascal of Mr. Leigh's will catch it now, the Popish villain!" said Lucy Passmore, aloud. "You lie still there, dear life, and settle your spirits; you're so safe as ever was a rabbit to a burrow. I'll see what happens, if I die for it!"
