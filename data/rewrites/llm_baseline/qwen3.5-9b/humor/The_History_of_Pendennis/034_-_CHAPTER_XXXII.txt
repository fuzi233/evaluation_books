In the middle of his modest celebrations, Pen suddenly felt a shadow loom over his fun. While his parties were humble and his spending moderate, his funds were running dangerously dry. His club subscriptions had already eaten up a third of his cash, and he'd just bought the essential furniture for his tiny bedroom. Now, staring at the final five-pound note in his wallet, he realized he couldn't afford to replace it. After all, he was raised like a royal heir—or perhaps like a crying infant fed by a devoted mother.

His friend Warrington, however, remained blissfully unaware of Pen's financial crisis. To Warrington, Pen looked like a man with a bottomless vault: an only child, a wealthy mother at her country estate, and a dandy uncle who dined daily with high society. Pen wore gold chains and carried a dressing case worthy of a duke. His lifestyle screamed aristocracy—not because he splurged wildly on specific luxuries, but because he refused to be penny-pinching. He wouldn't tip a waiter a penny, wouldn't walk in the rain unless absolutely necessary, and whenever he hailed a cab, he invariably tipped the driver double the fare. He despised clean gloves and frugal habits. If he'd been born to a ten-thousand-a-year inheritance, he likely would've been even more generous. Yet, despite his apparent wealth, he possessed a heart of gold; he couldn't resist helping beggars or pitiful children. It was a sumptuous nature, perhaps, unable to grasp the value of money, driven by natural kindness and a vanity that loved praise—even from waiters and cab drivers.

Warrington didn't know this, nor had Pen deemed it wise to share his true financial history. Everyone knew Pen had been wild at college, but no one suspected the depth of his debts or the severity of his mother's limited means.

The truth finally surfaced one evening as Pen grimly counted the change from his last five-pound note, lying on the pub tray beside Warrington's pot of ale.

"It is the last rose of summer," Pen sighed dramatically. "Its bloommates have long departed; behold, the final flower of the garland has shed its petals." He then confessed everything: his mother's dwindling resources, his own follies, and Laura's generous support. Warrington smoked his pipe, listening intently.

"Poverty will do you good," Warrington declared, tapping ash from his pipe. "For an honest man, nothing is more refreshing than a tight squeeze. For others, it's useless medicine. It acts as a tonic, keeping your moral spirit in a constant state of alertness. Think of a rider facing a fence or an opponent wielding a stick; necessity forces you to brace yourself. A little hardship reveals your grit and nerves you to fight fortune. You'll soon realize what you can live without: no need for new gloves, polished boots, cologne, or cabs. You've been spoiled by women, Pen. A healthy, brainy bachelor who can't find work doesn't belong in this world. Pay your last halfpenny, jump over Waterloo Bridge, steal a leg of mutton, get transported, and leave the country. *Dixi*—I have spoken. Pass the ale."

"But how does one survive?" Pen asked. "Beef and bread are plentiful in England, but they require coin or labor. Who will hire me, and what work can I do?"

Warrington burst into laughter. "Advertise in the *Times*!" he suggested. "Seek an usher's position at a classical academy: 'Gentleman, B.A. of St. Boniface College, plucked for his degree.' Offer lessons in classics, math, and basic French. Mention you can cut hair, supervise younger students, and play second chair piano for the principal's daughters. Address: A.P., Lamb Court, Temple."

"Stop it," Pen groaned.

"Men adapt to any profession," Warrington continued. "Your friend Bloundell is a professional rogue traveling Europe, fleeceing young noblemen. Bob O'Toole, my schoolmate, now drives the Ballynafad mail, carrying honest Jack Finucane's letters. I know a doctor's son who argued with his boss over finances, ran out of money, grew his mustache, moved to a provincial town, claimed to be 'Professor Spineto, Chiropodist to the Russian Emperor,' cured the local newspaper editor, and practiced happily for three years. He's back home now, inheriting his father's medical supplies."

"Hang medical supplies!" Pen cried. "I can't drive coaches, trim feet, or cheat at cards. What else do you suggest?"

"There's always writing," Warrington said. "Before you told me your troubles, I assumed you were rich based on your airs. Since you can't drain your mother further, you must settle with Laura—Laura, here's to you!—and carry bricks instead of asking for shillings."

"How do you earn a living?" Pen asked.

"On my brother's allowance," Warrington admitted, his face falling slightly. "I squandered that five years ago. If I hadn't killed myself sooner, maybe it would have been better. I've been playing my own game ever since. I don't need much. When my purse empties, I work to refill it, then lounge like a serpent until I'm satisfied. Look," he held up a thin leather pouch containing a few coins. "I feel empty again."

"I write," Warrington whispered, blushing. "Not publicly, of course. I don't want people saying George Warrington writes for bread. But I contribute to law reviews and newspapers. Here," he handed Pen a file of the *Dawn*. "Read these."

Pen scanned the pages, delighted. He recognized the sharp wit, concise sentences, satire, and scholarship immediately.

"I'm no match for that," Pen admitted admiringly. "I know little of politics or history, and my Latin is shaky. I can't soar on wings like yours."

"But you have your own flight, lighter and perhaps higher," Warrington encouraged gently. "Those little verses I've seen show a rare talent. Don't blush, you conceited young jackanape. You've had the poetic spark for ten years. Our oil lamps are dim compared to your inner fire. You're a poet, Pen."

Touched, tears welled in Pen's eyes. "How kind you are, Warrington!"

"I like you, old chap," Warrington replied warmly. "Chambers were lonely; your honest face cheered me. Remember how you laughed at Lowton? Anyway, I needed someone to keep me company."

So absorbed in his own joy, Pen missed the melancholy glint in Warrington's eyes. "Thank you for your friendship and your words," Pen said. "I've always suspected I was a poet. Will I be one? Do you mean the *Ariadne in Naxos* I wrote at eighteen, or the Prize Poem?"

Warrington roared with laughter. "Young goose! Of all my failures, *Ariadne* is the most sickening nonsense. The Prize Poem is so pompous and weak I'm amazed it didn't win. You don't imagine you're a serious poet trying to rival Milton, do you? Setting yourself up as a Pindar? No, my boy, you can write magazine pieces and charming verses. That's all."

"By Jove!" Pen stamped his foot. "I'll prove you wrong!"

Warrington just chuckled and blew twenty-four rapid puffs from his pipe.

Soon, an opportunity arose. Mr. Bacon, the eminent publisher of Paternoster Row, released annually a lavish volume called the *Spring Annual*, edited by Lady Violet Lebas. It featured poems from the elite—young lords like Lord Dodo and Percy Popjoy, alongside illustrations of beauties. Because the engravings were created months in advance, poets had to write specifically to match the images.

One day, while visiting Mr. Hack, Bacon's manager, Warrington discovered a disaster. Percy Popjoy had written dreadful verses for a picture titled *The Church Porch*: a Spanish maiden rushing to church while a cloaked youth watched her from a niche. The image was lovely, but the poem was atrocious.

"It won't do," Mr. Hack lamented. "Bungay is releasing a superior collection. We can't lose the plate; the Governor paid sixty pounds for it. Lady Violet admits it's terrible. What's the solution?"

"I know someone," Warrington said. "Let me take the plate home. Send for the verses tomorrow. You'll pay generously, of course."

Agreed, Warrington returned home with the plate. "Here's your chance, boy. Write some verses for this."

"What is it?" Pen asked, confused. "A church porch? A lady entering, a lecher looking from a wine shop? What am I supposed to do?"

"Try," Warrington urged. "Earn your living, you who dream of doing so."

"All right," Pen agreed.

Warrington left for dinner. When he returned late, Pen was ready. "Done," he said proudly. "Squeezed them out. I think they'll work."

After reading, Warrington approved. The poem, *The Church Porch*, depicted the scene with elegant restraint:

> Although I enter not, Yet round about the spot
> Sometimes I hover, And at the sacred gate,
> With longing eyes
