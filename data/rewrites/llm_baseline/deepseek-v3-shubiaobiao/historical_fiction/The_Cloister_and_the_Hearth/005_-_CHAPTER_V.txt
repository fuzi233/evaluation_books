Ghysbrecht van Swieten was a man of cunning. He began his conversation with Gerard obliquely, circling the true matter that gnawed at him.

"The town records," he said, his voice smooth as aged parchment, "are written in a cramped hand, the ink faded with time." He offered Gerard the task of transcribing them anew, presenting it as an honor. Gerard, ever practical, asked what payment he might expect. Ghysbrecht named a pittance—barely enough to cover the cost of ink and parchment.

"My labor must also be accounted for," Gerard protested. "This is a year's work."

"Labor?" Ghysbrecht scoffed. "Scratching words onto parchment is no labor. Where is the sweat in that?"

"It is labor indeed, and skilled work at that—better paid than brute toil, sweat or none. And my time, burgomaster—what of that?"

"Your time?" Ghysbrecht's lip curled. "What is time to a man of two-and-twenty?" Then, sharp as a knife, he struck closer to the truth. "Or is it idleness that grips you? Love, perhaps? Your body kneels with these chanting monks, but your heart—ah, your heart lingers with Peter Brandt and his red-haired daughter."

"I know no Peter Brandt."

The denial only sharpened Ghysbrecht’s suspicion. He leaned forward, eyes glinting. "You lie! Did I not see you at her side on the road to Rotterdam?"

Gerard stiffened. "Ah."

"Ah, indeed! And you were seen in Sevenbergen but days ago."

"Was I?"

"At Peter’s very house!"

"At Sevenbergen?"

"Ay, at Sevenbergen."

It was a gambit—a guess flung boldly, wrapped as fact, to pry loose the truth. But the reaction was not what Ghysbrecht expected. Gerard stood abruptly, his composure fracturing like thin ice.

"Burgomaster," he said, voice unsteady, "I have not set foot in Sevenbergen these three years. I know neither the names nor the dwelling of those you claim to have seen me with. And since my time, though worthless to you, is precious to me—good day."

With that, he was gone, his eyes alight with something between fury and fear.

Ghysbrecht surged to his feet, rage boiling—but then he sank back into his chair. The boy had not flinched. That meant defiance—or knowledge.

He snapped his fingers, summoning his most trusted servant. "See that man?" he hissed, pointing through the window. "Follow him. But mark me—do not be seen. He is young, but his cunning runs deep. Watch where he goes, what he does. Do not return until you can tell me all."

The servant nodded and slipped away.

Night had fallen when he returned. Ghysbrecht seized him by the arm. "Well?"

"Master," the servant murmured, "the young man went from you straight to Sevenbergen."

Ghysbrecht's breath caught.

"To the house of Peter the Magician."
