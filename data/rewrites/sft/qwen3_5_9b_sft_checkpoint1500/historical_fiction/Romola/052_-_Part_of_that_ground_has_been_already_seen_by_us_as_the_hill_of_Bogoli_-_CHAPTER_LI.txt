As Romola spoke of someone expecting her, she referred to her cousin, Brigida. Yet, the depth of her relative's need remained entirely unsuspected by her. Together, returning toward the Piazza, they had spotted a gathering of lads halting before Tessa. Upon approaching close enough to witness the simple country girl's distress, Romola paused, telling Brigida, "Wait for me a moment, cousin." To this, Monna Brigida replied hastily, "No, I cannot proceed. Come with me to Boni's shop; I shall return there."

In truth, Monna Brigida harbored a dual torment. On one side lay a keen consciousness of the "vanities" adorning her person—the rouge, the false hair, the pearl embroidery. On the other surged a profound alarm that the *Piagnoni* (Weepers) might be correct in their conviction that these adornments damaged the soul. Their grave perspective hung in the air like a pervasive odor, distorting reality until nothing tasted as it once had. Standing next to her youthful and beautiful cousin, who lived a life suggesting rigorous moral demands, the fifty-five-year-old widow found her usual sources of satisfaction threatened. Her joy had long been derived from how she viewed herself and how she believed others perceived her appearance. Maintaining buoyant spirits under such shifting tides required a vast reservoir of imagination.

Recently, Brigida had fought frequent battles at her toilette. Was it truly worthwhile to apply rouge and braid her hair if her soul could prosper better without them? However, when she raised her hand-mirror and beheld a sallow visage with sagging cheeks and crow's-feet impossible to disguise through lip-smiles, her resolve crumbled. She parted her grey hair, arranging it simply around her face in the manner of the *Piagnoni*, only to feel her courage evaporate. Monna Berta, though only fifty-two, possessed a superior vitality that would surely erupt into laughter at Brigida, calling her an ancient hag. Such an insult, delivered by a younger woman, would cut deeply. Furthermore, every non-*Piagnona* would likely shrink from her, and men would address her with the deference reserved for grandmothers. At fifty-five, she reasoned, a woman was not yet truly old; merely a touch of makeup sufficed to mask age. Thus, the rouge, the braids, and the embroidered *berretta* returned. Satisfied with this familiar transformation, she felt secure. Today, however, as she prepared for the *Piagnone* Carnival, the internal struggle between fear and longing proved too great. Seeking refuge in the druggist's shop to avoid the collectors of the *Anathema*, she hoped to bypass the ordeal while Romola remained absent. Yet, her retreat lacked sufficient speed. Even before she turned away, she had been sighted by the white-robed boys trailing behind the crowd that had wheeled toward Tessa. Perhaps their eagerness to seize her stemmed from the knowledge that another member of their troop had recently confronted a figure bearing the *Anathema* more visibly.

Among these youths, several belonged to the youngest cohort, undergoing special training. A small boy of ten, adorned with an olive wreath above cherubic cheeks and wide brown eyes, embodied a specific kind of childhood solemnity. His imagination was seized by a hovering awe of existence, sensing that great consequences hinged on every act of goodness or wickedness. Despite this reverence, his longings leaned toward mischief and mastery. He was the first to intercept Monna Brigida, placing himself squarely in her path. Anger flared within Brigida, and she scanned the surroundings for an exit, but none existed nearby. A flight now would only exacerbate her plight.

It was not the cherubic toddler who first addressed her, but a youth of fifteen gripping the handles of a wide basket.

"Venerable mother!" he began, his voice earnest. "The Blessed Jesus commands you to surrender the *Anathema* you bear. That cap embroidered with pearls, those jewels securing your false hair—they must be given up and sold for the poor. Cast the hair itself aside as a lie worthy only of the fire. Surely, beneath your silk mantle, you wear other treasures?"

"Yes, lady," chimed in the youth at the opposite handle, who had memorized many of Fra Girolamo's sermons, "these burdens are too heavy for you. They weigh heavier than a millstone, dragging you toward damnation. Will you deck yourself in the hunger of the poor and boast of carrying God's curse upon your brow?"

"In truth, you are old, *buona madre*," intoned the little boy in a sweet soprano. "You appear frightfully ugly with the red on your cheeks and that glistening black hair, along with those fine trinkets. It is only Satan who takes pleasure in seeing you. Your Angel grieves; He wishes you to wipe away the red."

The small fellow snatched a soft silk scarf from the basket and extended it toward Monna Brigida, offering it as her guardian angel desired. Her anger and humiliation rapidly surrendered to a spiritual terror. Monna Berta and the usual cloud of high-society observers were nowhere to be seen. Instead, she was encircled by young monitors clad in white robes, wreaths, and red crosses. Their uncanny candor and the sheer awesomeness of their unusual garb struck her with dread. With Fra Cristoforo of Santa Croce absent to bolster her skepticism of Dominican teachings, she was helplessly gripped by a vague, overwhelming sensation that a supreme warning had arrived. Unconsciously considering any alternative action, she accepted the offered scarf and, with trembling submission, wiped her cheeks.

"It is well, madonna," declared the second youth. "It is a holy beginning. Once you have stripped your head of these vanities, the dew of heavenly grace shall descend upon it." As his words grew infused with mischief, he reached for one of the jeweled pins fastening her braids to the *berretta*. He plucked it free. The heavy black plait cascaded down over Monna Brigida's face, pulling the rest of her headgear forward. This served as a final catalyst for hesitation; she raised her hands frantically, undone the remaining fastenings, and cast her beloved crimson-velvet *berretta*, with its unparalleled embroidery of seed-pearls, into the basket of doom. She stood revealed as an unrouged woman, her grey hair pushed back from a countenance where the deep lines of age had decisively conquered *embonpoint*.

Yet, the *berretta* was not permitted to rest in the basket. With impish fervor, the youngsters lifted it, holding it up pitilessly, allowing the false hair to dangle.

"Behold, venerable mother," proclaimed the taller lad, "how ugly falsehoods you have liberated yourselves from! And now you resemble the Blessed Saint Anna, the Mother of the Holy Virgin."

Thoughts of immediate entry into a convent, vanishing from the world forever, raced through Monna Brigida's mind. Nothing remained possible for her save the guarding of her soul. Of course, spectators laughed, needing no glance around to confirm their amusement. Perhaps, she mused, it would prove better to be forced to contemplate Paradise. But the notion that the cherished, accustomed world was no longer within her grasp summoned a cluster of hard tears, just enough to moisten her elderly eyes. Through a mist, she discerned a large, rough hand clutching a red cross, suddenly thrust before her over the shoulders of the boys, accompanied by a strong, guttural voice.

"Only four *quattrini*, madonna, blessing included! Purchase it. You will find comfort in it now that your wig is gone. *Deh!* What do we sinners accomplish all our lives? Making soup in a basket and receiving but the scum for our stomachs. Better buy a blessing, madonna! Only four *quattrini*; the profit amounts to no more than the scent of a coin, and it goes to the poor."

Confused and vision blurred, Monna Brigida moved toward the further submission of extracting money from her embroidered pouch, currently concealed by her silk mantle, when the circle surrounding her—which she had not yet conceived of escaping—suddenly parted. Into the gap stepped a figure as welcome as an angel releasing bolts from a prison cell.

"Romola, look at me!" cried Monna Brigida in a pitiful tone, extending both hands.

The white-robed troop was already drifting away, possessing a faint awareness that their zeal regarding the headgear had been excessive, thereby affording a dispensation from any further demands for penitential offerings.

"My dear cousin, do not be distressed," said Romola, overcome with pity yet barely restraining a smile at the sudden apparition of her kinswoman in a genuine, natural guise, so strikingly contrasted with all former memories. She removed the black drapery from her own head and draped it over Monna Brigida's shoulders. "There," she continued soothingly, "no one will notice you now. Let us turn down the Via del Palagio and go directly home."

Hastening away, Monna Brigida clutched Romola's hand tightly, as if seeking a firmer assurance of her actual presence.

"Oh, my Romola, my dear child!" exclaimed the short, stout woman, hurrying with frequent steps to match the majestic stride of her companion beside her. "What an old scarecrow I am! I must be good—I mean to be good!"
