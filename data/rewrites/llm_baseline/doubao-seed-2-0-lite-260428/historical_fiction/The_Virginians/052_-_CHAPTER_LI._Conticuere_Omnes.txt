Come step across the street with me, if you will, gracious reader, and you will find our young guests gathered at Lord Wrotham’s London house—one he has lent outright to his old friend General Lambert, that tight little household we first came to know at Oakhurst and later at Tunbridge Wells. James Wolfe promised to join them for dinner this evening, but the young lieutenant-colonel is loitering at Miss Lowther’s side, counting a single glance from her dear eyes far sweeter than the finest spiced kickshaw Wrotham’s celebrated French cook could turn out, or the lavish dessert laid out for tonight’s feast. You shall take his place at the table; rest assured he will not be coming. As for me, I shall linger at the sideboard, pen in hand, to jot down every word that passes.

Look first at the women’s faces, glowing bright as warm candlelight! When Harry Warrington was dragged away by bailiffs not long prior, I had half a mind to tell you how the good Mrs. Lambert flew straight to her husband the second she heard of the boy’s misfortune, begging, imploring, insisting that Martin do everything in his power to pull Harry out of the scrape. “Never mind his stubborn huff the other day,” she pleaded, “never mind that he was angry when we sent his presents back—of course a high-spirited lad like Harry would be cross! Never mind that our pockets are stretched thin, and every spare penny is earmarked for our own boys’ university fees. There must be some way to free him. Did you not pull Charles Watkins out of ruin two years ago? And did he not pay you back every last halfpenny? You made that whole family happy, blessed be God, and Mrs. Watkins prays for you to this very day. I swear every good thing that has happened to us since then has come of it—there is no doubt in my mind that your kindness is the very reason you made Major-General,” the fond wife finished, patting his arm.

Martin Lambert required barely any persuasion to do a good turn. He agreed at once to seek out his friend James Wolfe to stand bail for Harry, grabbed his hat and walking stick, squeezed Theo’s hand so gently she smiled and knew exactly what errand he was bound on (or perhaps her silly mamma had blabbed the whole plan already), kissed little Hetty’s flushed, excited cheek, and stepped out of the parlour—his wife following fast on his heels.

Alone with him in the hallway, the enthusiastic matron could not hold back her joy a moment longer. She threw her arms around his neck, kissed him a hundred times over in an instant, called down God’s blessing on his head, and wept freely on his shoulder. It was in this very sentimental embrace that old Mrs. Quiggett, Lord Wrotham’s bustling, long-tenured housekeeper, found them. The good woman stared a moment, utterly flabbergasted by the conjugal display.

“Had a little tiff, have you, and are making it up?” the General said, grinning, as he broke away. “Don’t go spreading tales out of school, Mrs. Quiggett!”

“La, I never!” Mrs. Quiggett cackled, her shrill laugh echoing through the hall, as sharp and lively as an ancient white cockatoo—an impression she bore no small resemblance to, with her hooked nose, snow-white head of hair, and decades of busy clattering around the great house. She laughed so hard her ring of jangling house keys bounced at her hip, and her old sides shook. “Well I never did!”

“Oh Quiggett!” Mrs. Lambert sobbed. “What a man that is!”

“Making up after a quarrel, are you, mum? That’s the right way of it,” the old housekeeper nodded.

“Quarrel with him? He never told a greater fib. My General is an angel, Quiggett. I could worship him, I could fall down and kiss his boots, I declare I could! There never was a man half so good as mine. What did I ever do to deserve such a husband? How dare I be so lucky?”

“My dear, I’d say the pair of you are matched perfectly,” the old cockatoo said. “Now what would you like for your supper later?”

When Lambert trundled in very late that night for that meal, and told them the whole tale: that Harry was free, and that his brother George, whom they had all mourned for months as dead, was alive and well and had rescued him himself—you can imagine the uproar of joy that shook that little parlour. If Mrs. Lambert had thought her General an angel before, he was a saint now. If she had wanted to kiss his boots that morning, what grander act of adoration might she have tried that evening? Little Hetty crept silent to her father’s side, nestled against his arm, and sipped a tiny sip of wine from his glass. Theo’s and her mother’s faces beamed like twin full moons, bright with uncomplicated happiness.

After supper, at a quiet little signal, all four of them sank to their knees, giving glad, reverent thanks—pure joy such as the angels are said to feel, for a lost sinner returned home. Just then there came a heavy, echoing knock at the front door. Who could it be? Lord Wrotham was miles away in the country, and it was past midnight, so long had they sat talking. Mrs. Lambert smiled, soft and knowing, before anyone else could speak.

A young man pushed through the door, leading his companion in. “This is George,” Harry said, breathless. “We’ve just come from Aunt Bernstein’s. We couldn’t go to bed, Aunt Lambert, without coming to thank you too. You dear, dear, good—” No more words came out audible. Mrs. Lambert was smothering Harry in kisses, Theo snatched up Hetty who had gone deathly pale with shock, and hugged her back to laughing, crying life. George Warrington stood with his hat off, then stepped forward once Harry had been released, and knelt a little to kiss Mrs. Lambert’s hand. The General passed his palm over his eyes. All of them looked soft and tender, bright with that special happy warmth generous hearts know so well: when a wrong is forgiven, when peace is restored, when a loved one everyone thought lost comes home at last.

“We saw your lights still on as we walked past from Aunt Bernstein’s,” Harry explained, grinning through his tears. “We simply could not sleep without saying good night. Could we, George?”

“‘Tis the finest nightcap any man could ask for, lads,” the General laughed. “When will you come and dine with us? Tomorrow?” No, they had to spend the next day at Madame Bernstein’s. The day after, then? Yes, they promised, they would come. And that day is this very day, this very dinner the reader has just been invited to take a seat at, in Lieutenant-Colonel Wolfe’s empty place.

It is no mere make-believe feast, let me assure you. We are all gathered here, not in Lord Wrotham’s grand formal dining hall, but in the snug little front parlour study. The tablecloth has been drawn away, the General has toasted the King, the servants have withdrawn to the kitchens, every soul around the table sits quiet, conticuere omnes—all silent, their full attention on their guest. George Esmond Warrington clears his throat, blushes a little, and begins.

“I remember, back at the General’s camp table in Virginia, that sharp little Philadelphia agent Benjamin Franklin made exactly the objections to our campaign that the disastrous end of Braddock’s march would prove to be perfectly right. ‘Of course, your Excellency,’ he said, ‘once your troops stand before Fort Duquesne, that tiny ramshackle place will never hold out against such a fine general, such a grand army, such fine artillery as you are bringing to bear. But have you calculated the difficulty of ever reaching the fort at all? Your march will cut through untrodden woods, over roads you will have to build yourselves, strung out across four miles of wilderness. That thin line, forcing its way through the forest, will be open to attack from front, rear, and both flanks by enemies you will never see, whose entire art of war is the laying of clever ambushes.’ ‘Pshaw, sir,’ Braddock scoffed. ‘Your raw American militia may be frightened of savages, but the Indians will never make any impression on His Majesty’s regular troops.’ ‘I heartily hope not, sir,’ Franklin sighed. The officers gathered around the general’s table all sneered at the postmaster, that pert civilian who had no business daring to give an opinion on matters far beyond his understanding.

“We despised the Indians on our own side, and our commander made light of their skill as fighters. Our officers drove away almost all the Indigenous scouts who had joined our expedition with their rude, insulting behaviour to the chiefs and their women. Only seven or eight of them remained with our force by the time we neared the fort. Had we had two hundred of those skilled forest fighters out in front of us on that fatal 9th of July, the whole course of the day would have turned out very different. They would have scattered the French and their raiding allies before they could spring their trap, and prevented the total panic that broke our lines. We know now that the French had already made plans to abandon Fort Duquesne, certain they could never hold it against our overwhelming numbers, and that their own Indigenous allies had argued against the audacity of attacking an army so much larger than theirs.

“I was with General Braddock and the main body of the troops when the first shots rang out ahead. One aide-de-camp after another galloped forward to carry orders. At first, our advance guard answered the enemy fire briskly, and our men cheered and shouted, full of fight. But soon our fire grew slack, while from behind every tree and every bush ringing us, single rifle shots cracked, laying man after man low. We had been marching in neat orderly line, skirmishers ahead, colours and two small field guns at the centre, baggage wagons guarded at the rear, moving across an open stretch of clear ground a mile long and half a mile wide—thick, tangled brushwood and forest hemming us in on both sides. Before long, the firing burst from both sides of the trees surrounding our whole column. Men dropped like flies, officers even faster than the rank and file. At first, as I said, we cheered and fired back, our guns blasting at the treeline and seeming to silence the ambushed French for a moment. But the hidden rifle fire picked up again. Our men halted, huddling together in a terrified mass, ignoring the shouts and orders of officers screaming for them to advance, firing wildly into the brush where they could see no enemy at all. No good that did, of course. Soldiers from the advance line came running back to the main body, pale and wounded, screaming that there were five thousand Frenchmen and a horde of howling Indian devils in front, scalping everyone who fell. We could hear their wild war cries ringing out from the woods, every time one of our own men crumpled to the ground. No force on earth could make our men step forward now. Aide-de-camp after aide-de-camp was sent out, and never came back. At last my turn came. I was sent forward with a message to Captain Fraser of Halkett’s regiment, a message he would never receive, and I would never deliver.

“I had not run thirty paces ahead when a rifle ball tore through my leg. I crumpled straight to the ground. I remember a rush of Indians and French soldiers surging past me, the first yelling their fiendish war cries, the latter just as wild and ferocious as their savage allies. I was stunned and mortified to see how few of the white-coated Frenchmen there were. Barely a score ran past me; in all, there were fewer than fifty French soldiers in that accursed battle that routed two of the finest regiments in the British army.

“One of them, half French, half Indian, dressed in moccasins, a tattered white uniform coat and a cockade, saw me lying wounded on the ground, turned back, and ran straight for me, his musket clubbed over his head to dash my brains out and rob me as I lay. My little fusil, the one Harry gave me before I left for the campaign, had fallen on the ground right by my side. I seized it, and the Frenchman dropped dead six yards before me. I was spared for that moment, but bleeding heavily from my leg wound, and growing very faint. I nearly fainted trying to reload my gun. It slipped from my hand, and my arm sank limp, useless, to the grass.

“I was barely conscious, the gunshots and yells ringing dim and far away in my ears, when I saw an Indian standing over me. He was busy rifling the pockets of the Frenchman I had just shot, but he glanced over at me, lying there bleeding. He stripped the dead man first, tearing open his coat and rooting through every pocket, then scalped him, his bloody knife glinting between his teeth as he turned to advance on me. I watched him coming as if through a film, as in a dream. I had no strength left to move, no strength to fight him off.

“He put his knee on my chest, his bloody hand seizing my long hair to yank my head up off the ground. As he lifted me, I saw a young French officer running fast towards us right behind him. Good God! It was Florac, the young man who had been my second in the duel at Quebec a year prior. ‘A moi, Florac!’ I cried out. ‘C’est Georges! Aide moi!’

“He started, ran straight to me at the sound of my voice, and clapped a hand on the Indian’s shoulder, yelling at him to stop. But the savage did not understand French, or chose not to. He gripped my hair even tighter, waving his dripping knife over my scalp, motioning for the young Frenchman to leave him to his prey. I could only cry out again, piteous, ‘A moi!’

“‘Ah, you brute, you want blood? Take this instead!’ Florac cursed, and the next moment the Indian fell dead across my chest, Florac’s sword run clean through his body.

“My old friend glanced around quickly. ‘Eh, what a pretty mess!’ he said. ‘Where are you wounded? The leg?’ He bound my thigh tight with his military sash to stop the bleeding. ‘The rest will kill you if they find you here. Here, take this white coat, this hat with the white cockade. If anyone passes by, yell out in French. They will take you for one of us. You are Brunet of the Quebec Volunteers. God keep you, Brunet! I have to go forward. The whole of your redcoats are running for their lives, my poor boy.’ It was an utter rout, a day of unmitigated disgrace for England.

“Florac’s rough bandage slowed the bleeding in my leg, and the kind creature helped me prop myself up against a thick oak tree, loaded my fusil for me and set it within easy reach to protect me from any other looters who might stumble by. He also gave me the gourd of the very French soldier I had shot earlier, the man who had meant to kill me. The water in that gourd revived me more than I can say. He marked the thick tree I was leaning against, noted every landmark around to make sure he could find me again, and hurried off to the fighting. ‘You see how much I love you, George,’ he said, ‘that I stopped behind for you at a moment like this.’ I do not think I ever told you, Harry, that Florac owed me a small kindness. I won money from him at cards in Quebec, only when he begged me to play, and when he could not pay the debt, I tore up his note of hand and lit my pipe with it. You see, sir, you are not the only reckless gambler in the Warrington family.

“When dusk fell, and the last of the brutal pursuit was over, that faithful lad came back for me, accompanied by two Indians with still-reeking scalps hanging from their belts. He told them I was his brother, a French soldier wounded early in the fight, who must be carried safely back to the fort. They laid me in a soft wool blanket and carried me, groaning in agony, all the way to Fort Duquesne, Florac walking right beside me the whole time to make sure they did not slip off the path to rob and murder me. He promised them a cask of brandy when we reached the fort if they brought me there unharmed. I barely remember the journey, the pain in my leg so fierce I fainted more than once. At last we arrived. I was carried straight to the officer’s log hut, and laid on Florac’s own bed.

“Thank God for my delirium. I had been smuggled into the fort as a wounded French soldier of their garrison. I learned later, during the worst of my fever, that the few British prisoners taken that day had been dragged under Fort Duquesne’s walls by their savage captors, and burned, tortured, and butchered to death, right in full view of the entire French garrison.”

A murmur of horror ripples through the little hush of listeners. Theo squeezes Hetty’s hand, and stares at George, eyes wide with alarm. Harry slams his fist down on the table, hard. “The bloody, murderous, red-skinned villains! There will never be peace in the colonies until every last one of them is hunted down!”

“They were offering a hundred and thirty dollars apiece for Indian men’s scalps in Pennsylvania when I left home,” George says, quiet and mild, “and fifty for the scalps of women.”

“Fifty for women, my love! Do you hear that, Mrs. Lambert?” the General laughs, lifting a handful of his wife’s soft curls.

“Murderous villains!” Harry snarls again. “Hunt them down, sir! Hunt every last one of them down!”

“I do not know how long I lay feverish and raving,” George resumes. “When I woke up clear headed, my dear Florac was gone. He and his company had been sent off on an expedition to attack an English fort in Pennsylvania that the French claimed as their own. When I was well enough to ask questions, I learned there were barely thirty European soldiers left in Fort Duquesne. The place could have been stormed a dozen times over, had any of our own men had the courage to come back after their terrible defeat.
