Margaret severed a massive chunk of roasted venison and dashed to the window, hurling it outward toward the emerald-eyed beasts below. With a savage snarl, the creatures lunged at the meat; the sound of tearing flesh and crunching bone echoed sharply. Suddenly, a hound bayed so loudly and close to the house that it vibrated through the timbers, causing the three figures at the window to recoil in alarm. The leopard, fearing the loss of her meal, slipped away swiftly and silently into the shadows of the woods. Before she had vanished completely, a chaotic throng of horses, men, and dogs thundered past the window, pursuing her on a full cry.

Martin and his companions exhaled, relieved. The leopard was too fast to be cornered within a league of their dwelling. They clasped hands in relief. Seizing the brief respite, Margaret broke down sobbing softly. Gerard leaned forward and kissed the tears from her cheeks.

They returned to the table, and Gerard raised a toast to the power of female intellect. "Woman's wit is stronger than man's strength," he declared.

"Ay," Margaret replied, "but only when those she loves are in peril; otherwise, it is merely cleverness."

That night, Gerard lingered with her longer than usual, returning home feeling prouder than ever and happier than a king. A short distance from his home, beneath the canopy of ancient trees, he encountered two figures blocking his path. It was his father and mother.

"Out so late!" he thought, a chill seizing him. "What could possibly bring them here?"

He halted, staring at them. They stood grim and silent. He stammered a question.

"Why ask?" his father retorted sharply. "You know why we are here."

"Oh, Gerard!" his mother interjected, her voice thick with reproach yet undeniably tender.

Gerard's heart hammered against his ribs; he fell silent. Seeing his son's confusion, his father softened slightly. "Do not hang your head, lad. You are not the first young fool ensnared by a rosy cheek and a pair of azure eyes."

"Nay, nay!" Catherine added. "It was witchcraft. Peter the Magician is famous for such tricks."

"Come, Father," the elder man resumed, addressing his son directly. "You know you must not meddle with womenfolk. But give us your promise to cease visiting Sevenbergen, and here matters end. We shall not be harsh for one fault."

"I cannot promise that, Father."

"Not promise it, you young hypocrite!"

"Nay, Father, do not misname me," Gerard insisted, his voice trembling. "I lacked the courage to tell you what I knew would grieve you. I am deeply grateful to that good friend—whomever he be—who allowed you to know. It is a weight lifted from my soul. Yes, Father, I love Margaret. Do not call me a priest, for I shall never be one. I would sooner die."

"That we shall see, young man," his father warned sternly. "Come, deny me no more; you will learn the price of disrespecting a father."

Gerard held his tongue, and the trio walked home in a somber silence, broken only by the occasional heavy sigh of Catherine. From that hour, the small house at Tergou ceased to be a sanctuary of peace.

The following day, Gerard was summoned before the entire family. Every voice rose up against him, save for little Kate and the dwarf, who instinctively sided with him without understanding why. Cornelis and Sybrandt were more bitter than their father. Gerard was dismayed to find so many enemies and cast a longing glance at his little sister. Her eyes were filled with tears as harsh words rained down on the boy who had been everyone's favorite just yesterday. Yet, she offered no comfort. Turning her head away, she whispered, "Dear, dear Gerard, pray to Heaven to cure you of this folly!"

"What, are you against me too?" Gerard asked, his heart breaking.

With a deep sigh, he rose and fled the house, heading straight for Sevenbergen.

A quarrel between parties bound by affection yet divided by opposing interests begins innocently enough. Often, both sides hold truth in their initial arguments. It is only as the conflict drags on that a calm, judicious friend—one capable of seeing both perspectives—becomes a divine necessity. For the longer the discord persists, the wider and deeper it grows, fueled by human fallibility and irritability. These flaws exist on both sides until inevitably, both arrive at the same conclusion: both are wrong.

In this struggle, the combatants were unevenly matched. Elias was furious; Cornelis and Sybrandt were spiteful. But Gerard, possessing a broader and more cultivated mind, perceived the nuances they missed. He suffered from fits of indecision and felt not wrath, but profound unhappiness. He was utterly alone in this fight, unable to confide in anyone.

Margaret Van Eyck was a spirited girl, but he dared not burden her with his domestic torment. She might sympathize, yet she was also capable of sacrificing him to his relatives to preserve her own happiness. Though she had been a great comfort to him on other occasions, he now feared making her his confidante. Her own history was well-known: in her youth, she had rejected numerous marriage proposals to dedicate herself to art, a pursuit incompatible with the duties of wife and mother. Consequently, she remained single, painting alongside her brothers. How could he confess that he had declined the clerical position she had secured for him, doing so precisely because of the very calling she had once despised and sacrificed lightly?

At this juncture, Gerard seemed poised to surrender. However, the opposition possessed a formidable ally in Catherine, the senior. This good-hearted but uneducated woman lacked the quiet firmness of her daughter and the ability to formulate plans. Sometimes she irritated Gerard, inadvertently bolstering his resolve, for anger often sustains courage. Other moments, she would suddenly turn on her own side, undermining their efforts.

Consider one instance among many: one day, with the whole household assembled, Cornelis declared, "Shall our Gerard wed Margaret Brandt? Why, it is hunger marrying thirst!"

"And what will happen when *you* marry?" Catherine shrieked.

"Gerard can paint, Gerard can write, but what can *you* do to keep a woman, you lazy fool? Nothing but wait for your father's shoes." She laughed derisively. "We see why you and Sybrandt refuse to let the poor boy marry. You fear he will come to us for a share of our wealth. If he does, and we give it to him, it is not yours we part with, nor will it likely be yours anyway."

On such occasions, Gerard would smile slyly, regain his composure, and temporary bewilderment would fall upon Catherine's unfortunate accomplices.

But after more than six months of relentless irritation, the situation reached its climax. The father announced to the entire family that he had ordered the burgomaster to imprison Gerard in the Stadthouse rather than allow him to marry Margaret.

Gerard turned pale with rage but managed to remain silent through sheer effort. His father continued, "And before the year is out, you shall be a priest, whether you will or no."

"Is that so?" Gerard cried out.

"Then listen to me, all of you. By God and Saint Bavon, I swear I will never be a priest while Margaret lives. Since force is to decide this matter—not love and duty—try your force, Father. But force shall fail you. The day the burgomaster comes for me, I leave Tergou forever, and Holland too, and my father's house, where it seems I have been valued these many years not for myself, but for what can be extracted from me."

With that, he stormed out of the room, white-faced with anger and despair.

"There!" Catherine exclaimed. "That is the result of driving young people too hard. Men are crueler than tigers, even to their own flesh and blood. Now, Heaven forbid he should ever leave us, married or single."

As Gerard emerged from the house, his cheeks pale and his heart pounding, he met Reicht Heynes carrying a message: Margaret Van Eyck wished to see him.

He found the elderly lady seated as grimly as a judge. She wasted no time on pleasantries, asking coldly why he had not visited recently. Before he could respond, she spoke in a sarcastic tone, "I thought we had been friends, young sir."

At this, Gerard appeared the picture of doubt and consternation.

"It is because you never told her you were in love," Reicht Heynes said, pitying his confusion.

"Silence, wench! Why should he tell us his affairs? We are not his friends; we have not earned his confidence."

"Alas, my second mother," Gerard murmured, "I did not dare to tell you my folly."

"What folly? Is it folly to love?"

"I am told so every day of my life."

"You need not have been afraid to tell my mistress; she is always kind to true lovers."

"Madam—Reicht—I was afraid because I was told..."

"Well, you were told...?"

"That in your youth you scorned love, preferring art."
