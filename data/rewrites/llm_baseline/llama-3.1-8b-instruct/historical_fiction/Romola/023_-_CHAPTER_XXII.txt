The grey morning light cast a melancholic hue over the Piazza del Duomo, but the vibrant colors of the festive decorations seemed to defy the somber atmosphere. The blue drapery and golden yellow lilies that adorned the piazza were a testament to the joyous celebrations that filled the air. As the Advent sermons approached, the streets buzzed with anticipation, and the crowds that streamed towards the Duomo were a mix of the devout and the curious.

Among the throngs of people hurrying to and fro, a group of laborers stood out near the Via de' Cerretani. Their worn, earth-stained clothing and the tools they carried – hammers, axes, and sickles – spoke to the hard work that filled their days. Yet, despite their rugged appearance, they seemed absorbed in the words of Ser Cioni, the notary, who had paused on his way to the Duomo. His voice cut through the din of the crowd, his tone sharp as a knife.

"Do not speak to me of foreign armies," he declared, his words dripping with conviction. "Let us not forget the bravery of our own people. Peasants have proven time and again that they can hold their own against even the finest armies of our condottieri. We Florentines need not fear any enemy within our walls."

A burly man with arms stained with crimson dye, his face a map of creases and lines, nodded in agreement. A small hatchet hung at his belt, a symbol of his craft as a butcher. He had a tale to share, one that would make the listeners' blood run cold.

"I recall the day the French cavalry rode through our streets," he began, his voice low and gravelly. "They strutted about, their fine doublets and hose making them look like peacocks in the sun. But I, a humble cloth merchant, knew better. I'd rather take on those gallant knights with nothing but my meat-axe. And that's exactly what I did when I saw them eyeing our city with disdain. 'Come, fine cavaliers,' I said, 'stick out your bellies, I'll show you what a real Florentine looks like.'"
