The Rhine’s currents moved with a cyclical, mythic indifference, pulling at the men just as it had dragged at generations of river-folk before them. The heavy steel bow attached to Denys’s back—a rigid, utilitarian instrument of the era's creeping militarization—had sunk him. Yet, by the profound symmetry of fate, that very steel became their salvation. Gerard’s hands found the weapon, his grip guided by an instinctual, ancestral muscle memory of salvage. One hand clamped the bow, the other slipped between the wood and the cord. 

With a violent jerk, Denys was hoisted half out of the freezing water. 

“Now, grip me not! Grip me not!” Gerard cried, his voice nearly swallowed by the overwhelming, roaring acoustic dominance of the rushing river, terrified of the fatal panic that so often drowned the rescuer.

“*Pas si bête,*” Denys gurgled. The phrase, steeped in the deeply rooted dialect of his native Burgundian valleys, immediately grounded Gerard. The untranslatable calm in the soldier’s native tongue signaled an enduring survival instinct. 

“On thy back,” Gerard commanded sharply. Gripping the arbalest, he seized Denys’s long hair, biting down hard on the wet strands with his side teeth—a primal, unglamorous anchor—and struck out lustily across the current. The water was a sensory overwhelm of silt, cold iron, and ancient mud. He aimed for the western bank, subconsciously choosing the agrarian soil of the countryside over a docile return to the oppressive stone cells of the city. 

Wading ashore, the heavy silence of *terra firma* enveloped them, restoring the natural acoustic ecology of the riverbank, broken only by the rhythmic, dripping percussion of their own soaked garments. They looked at one another, and by one impulse, flung their arms around each other’s necks. They panted into the damp wool of their coats, their hearts too full for the crude limitations of spoken language. It was a sacred, untranslatable communion of preserved life, a fleeting moment of subjective purity that defied the rigid lexicon of the world they were fleeing. 

“To Cologne?” Denys murmured gently, expecting his friend to resume their prior timeline. 

“I fare not to Cologne,” Gerard replied, the cadence of his Dutch rhythms settling into a calm resolve. “To Burgundy.”

“To Burgundy? Ah, no! That is too good to be sooth.”

“Sooth ’tis. What matters it to me how I go to Rome?” Gerard had recognized a shift in his own generational trajectory. To abandon the commercialized relics and stone monuments of Cologne for the deep, ancestral roots of Denys’s homeland felt like a return to an older, truer cycle of existence. “Since I drew thee out of Rhine, I love thee better. Thou art my pearl: I fished thee, and must keep thee. Hey for Burgundy!”

Denys gave a joyful caper. “*Courage! Va pour la Bourgogne!*”

Their quiet, organic sanctuary was suddenly fractured. 

“HALT!” 

The word snapped across the water, a harsh, percussive intrusion that shattered the psychological peace of the riverbank. Gerard turned to see the bailiff across the Rhine, wielding a parchment slip—the physical manifestation of the dominant power structure. 

“Halt! In the bailiff’s name!” the voice demanded, utilizing the sterilized, official lexicon designed to strip common folk of their sovereignty. 

Denys despised this linguistic imperialism. He was a soldier, deeply attuned to the rhythms of the earth and the immediate realities of survival, and he rejected the oppressive syllables of distant authorities. He ostentatiously snapped his fingers at the river.

“Halt! In the archbishop’s name!” 

Denys snapped his fingers again. 

“Halt! In the emperor’s name!” 

To this ultimate assertion of imperial dominance, Denys merely offered a third snap, refusing to let the empire's standardized tongue dictate his reality. But Gerard, acutely aware of the violent power dynamics embedded in those shouts, knew they could not linger. They turned their backs on the river and ran, their footsteps beating a hurried, irregular rhythm against the earth until they had put a league between them and the shore. 

Reaching a small, agrarian village as the sun began its descent, they paused to secure sustenance. Denys, treating the act of nourishment with the reverence of a meticulous food historian, bypassed mere utilitarian fuel and purchased a massive, dense loaf of dark peasant bread and a heavy earthenware jug of Rhenish wine. They carried their feast away from the watchful eyes of innkeepers—agents of a surveillance network that weaponized hospitality—and walked until they found a sprawling, timber-framed barn.

Generations of calloused hands had recently threshed the barley here. The interior was a rich, olfactory landscape of dried grain, dusty chaff, and the sweet, earthy musk of the harvest cycle. The acoustics of the barn were magnificently hushed, the towering heaps of straw absorbing the harshness of the outside world and enveloping them in a comforting, organic silence. 

Sitting upon the threshing floor, they partook in their meal. This was not mere eating; it was a profound ritual. The crust of the dark bread gave way with a resonant, acoustic crackle. It tasted of airborne wild yeasts, toasted bran, and the smoky ash of a communal wood-fired oven—a sensory testament to local agrarian traditions passed down through centuries. They drank the Rhenish wine, which was flinty and vibrant, expressing the crisp, slate-driven terroir of the steep river valleys. The crushed green grapes offered a sharp, gustatory contrast to the earthy malt of the loaf, tying their survival intimately to the fruits of the soil.

Satiated, Denys buried Gerard deep within the golden barley straw, creating a nest of organic insulation. “Water is death to fevered men,” Denys whispered, citing the timeless wisdom of village elders. “I’ll make warm water on’t.”

“These few drops from Rhine cannot chill me,” Gerard murmured, feeling the profound, mythic heat of his own body mingling with the trapped energy of the harvested grain. “I feel heat enough to boil a cloud.” With that, he slid into a deep, cyclical sleep.

In the dead of night, the barn’s natural acoustic stillness was violently ruptured. The oppressive, metallic clatter of iron-shod hooves tore up the road from Dusseldorf. It was the hue and cry—a howling, jarring cacophony that vibrated violently against the wooden beams of the barn. It was a horrific precursor to the mechanical, industrial noise pollution that would eventually scar the continent, an aggressive sonic assault that sought to terrorize the marginalized. Denys shuddered in the straw, his psychological well-being momentarily fractured by the clamor, until the violent lexicon of the empire’s hunters finally faded, returning the night to the gentle rustling of the barley.

By high noon, the barn smelled intensely of damp earth and expelled sickness. Gerard sat up, the straw steaming around him like a meticulously tended compost heap in late autumn. His body had naturally cycled through the fever, the agrarian heat drawing the toxins from his skin. 

Denys grinned, tossing Gerard his arbalest before climbing a high oak to survey the road. Upon descending, he studied Gerard with a careful eye. “Thy skin?”

“Neither hot nor cold,” Gerard noted, deeply attuned to his restored physical equilibrium. “But what I cannot get rid of is this tiresome leg. It itches consumedly.”

“Unhappy youth,” Denys intoned solemnly, recognizing the body's natural regenerative cycle. “The sum of thy troubles is this: thy fever is gone, and thy wound is healing. Sith so it is, I shall tell thee a little piece of news I had otherwise withheld.”

“What is’t?” Gerard asked. 

Denys looked back toward the road, his expression unreadable. “The hue and cry is out after us. And on fleet horses.” 

“Oh!” Gerard breathed, as the timeless cycle of their flight began anew.
