It was essential to Martin’s plan that he remain at home that day. So, when breakfast was served, he barely touched his plate, and just as the school bell would have summoned Mark away, he clutched his chest with a sudden, dramatic pain—so severe, he declared, that it made it unwise for him to venture out. Instead of walking to grammar school with his brother, he took over his father’s armchair by the fireside, along with the morning paper, which had previously been Matthew’s domain. The arrangement was settled quickly: Mark was gone to Mr. Summer’s class; Matthew and Mr. Yorke were absorbed in business at the counting-house. Now only three—no, four—tasks remained before him.

The first was to finally eat the breakfast he’d skipped, an act of hunger that no fifteen-year-old could afford to ignore. The second, third, and fourth: to ensure that his mother, Miss Moore, and Mrs. Horsfall were all safely occupied—and out of the way—by four o’clock that afternoon.

For now, though, food was paramount. His stomach was empty, and the energy required for what lay ahead demanded fuel. Martin knew exactly where the larder was, and without hesitation, he slipped into it. The servants were still eating their breakfast in the kitchen, doors tightly shut, while his mother and Miss Moore strolled on the lawn, whispering about the closed doors and suspecting mischief. But Martin was undisturbed. Inside the larder, he surveyed the provisions with the eye of a connoisseur.

His usual breakfast—bread and milk—was dull. Today, he would indulge in something more refined. He selected three rosy apples from a shelf lined with straw. From a dish, he chose an apricot puff and a damson tart. He passed over the plain bread, but paused at the currant tea-cakes—choosing one with quiet approval. With his clasp-knife, he cut a wing from a fowl and a slice of ham. A small portion of cold custard pudding seemed like the perfect finish. Satisfied, he gathered his feast and stepped out into the hall.

He was halfway across when the front door opened—and there stood Matthew.

If it had been the Old Gentleman, horned and hooved, Martin might have felt less exposed. But Matthew? A skeptic, a mocker—he had already questioned Martin’s heartache, muttering under his breath, “Shamming Abraham,” and casting disapproving glances at the armchair and newspaper. Seeing Martin now—arms full of stolen delicacies—Matthew’s suspicion flared into certainty.

Martin froze—just for a moment. Then, with the sharp clarity of youth, he saw not failure, but opportunity. This confrontation could serve his purpose perfectly. He knew that any clash between him and Matthew inevitably sent his mother into a fit of hysterics. And after such a storm, she always retreated to her room for the rest of the day—exactly where Martin wanted her.

So, the collision began. A dry laugh from Matthew. A sneering remark. A cutting retort from Martin—cool, calm, yet devastating. They lunged at each other. Martin, who usually kept his voice low during these spats, now raised it loudly. Servants rushed in. Mrs. Yorke and Miss Moore arrived too. No woman could separate them. Mr. Yorke was called.

“Sons,” he said sternly, “one of you must leave this house if this happens again. I will not have Cain and Abel quarreling under my roof.”

Martin allowed himself to be pulled away. He was hurt—but not angry. He was cool, even smiling. The hardest part was done.

Later, as he lounged on the sofa—still claiming illness—his thoughts drifted. “It’s not worth bothering myself for Caroline,” he murmured. But within minutes, he was back in the dining room, staring at the portrait of the man with wild hair and eyes clouded by despair. “Yes,” he whispered, “I made her cry. She shuddered. Almost fainted. I’ll see her smile before I’m done. And I want to outwit all these women.”

After dinner, Mrs. Yorke retired to her room—just as Martin had hoped.

Now came Hortense. She was comfortably seated in the back parlour, mending stockings, when Martin—pretending to read lazily on the sofa—began a casual conversation about Sarah, the maid at the Hollow. With careful drifts of talk, he hinted that Sarah had three suitors—Frederic Murgatroyd, Jeremiah Pighills, and John-of-Mally’s-of-Hannah’s-of-Deb’s—and that Miss Mann had claimed the girl often hosted them at meals, feeding them the best the cottage had to offer.

No more was needed. Hortense could not resist. She rose immediately, determined to investigate this scandal firsthand.

Only Mrs. Horsfall remained.

Martin, now in full command, rummaged through his mother’s workbasket and found a set of keys. He unlocked the sideboard, took out a black bottle and a small glass, placed them on the table, then hurried upstairs. He knocked gently on Mr. Moore’s door. The nurse answered.

“If you please, ma'am,” Martin said politely, “you’re invited to the back parlour for refreshment. You won’t be disturbed—the family are all out.”

He watched her descend, watched her enter. Then he shut the door behind her. She was safe. The hard work was complete.

Now, the pleasure.

He grabbed his cap and ran toward the wood. It was half-past three. The sky had darkened. Snow began to fall. The wind howled. The trees looked grim, the old oak spectral. Yet Martin welcomed the shadows. There was something thrilling in the gloom.

He waited. Back and forth he walked, watching the snow grow heavier. The wind moaned, then shrieked.

“She’s late,” he muttered, scanning the path. “I wonder why I want her so much? She’s not coming for me. But I hold power over her. And I need her to come… so I can use it.”

Time passed. Then, a sound—light, swift—barely audible beneath the rustle of leaves. A figure emerged, stepping bravely through the storm.

“Well, Martin,” she said, breathless, “how is he?”

He stared. “How strange,” he thought. “She thinks only of *him*. The snow, the cold—they mean nothing to her. And yet she’s just a ‘chitty-faced creature,’ as my mother would say. I’d give anything to wrap her in a cloak.”

But he said nothing.

“You’ve seen him?” she asked.

“No.”

“Oh! You promised you would.”

“I meant to do better than that. Didn’t I say I don’t care to see him?”

“But now it’s been so long. I can’t wait any longer. Martin, *please*—go see him. Tell him Caroline Helstone sends her regards. Ask if there’s anything that can help him.”

“I won’t.”

“You’ve changed. You were so kind last night.”

“We must go. It’s too cold to stand here.”

“But promise me—you’ll come back tomorrow with news.”

“No. I’m far too delicate for winter appointments. If you knew how much pain I had this morning, how I skipped breakfast, how I got knocked down—I’d feel you shouldn’t bring me out in this snow.”

“Are you really ill, Martin?”

“Don’t I look it?”

“You have rosy cheeks.”

“That’s hectic. Will you come—or won’t you?”

“Where?”

“With me. I was a fool not to bring a cloak. I’d have kept you warm.”

“You’re going home. My way is the opposite.”

“Put your arm through mine. I’ll take care of you.”

“But the wall—the hedge—it’s hard to climb. And you’re so young. You might hurt yourself.”

“You’ll go through the gate.”

“But—”

“But, but—will you trust me or not?”

She looked into his face.

“I think I will. Anything rather than return as anxious as I came.”

“I can’t guarantee that. But this I promise: follow me, and you’ll see Moore yourself.”

“See him—myself?”

“Yes.”

“But, dear Martin—does he know?”

“Ah! I’m dear now. No, he doesn’t.”

“And your mother? The others?”

“All is arranged.”

Caroline fell into silence, but walked on beside him.

They neared Briarmains.

“Have you decided?” he asked.

She didn’t answer.

“Decide. We’re almost there. I won’t see him—unless I announce your arrival.”

“Martin, you’re a strange boy. This is a strange step. But everything I’ve felt… has been strange. I will see him.”

“Then you won’t hesitate?”

“No.”

“There. We’re here. Don’t fear passing the parlour window—no one will see you. My father and Matthew are at the mill. Mark is at school. The servants are in the back kitchen. Miss Moore is at the cottage. My mother is in bed. Mrs. Horsfall is in paradise. Observe—no need to ring. I open the door. The hall is empty. The stairs are quiet. The gallery too. The whole house is under a spell. I won’t break it until you’re gone.”

“Martin, I trust you.”

“You never said a truer word. Let me take your shawl. I’ll shake off the snow and dry it. You’re cold. But there’s a fire upstairs. Are you ready?”

“Yes.”

“Follow me.”

He left his shoes on the mat and climbed the stairs barefoot. Caroline followed, silent and swift.

There was a gallery, then a passage. At the end, Martin paused before a door and tapped. Twice. Thrice.

A voice—familiar to one listener—finally replied, “Come in.”

Martin entered briskly.

“Mr. Moore,” he announced, “a lady called to inquire after you. None of the women were about. It’s washing-day. The maids are buried in soap-suds in the back kitchen. So I brought her up.”

“Up here, sir?” Moore asked.

“Up here, sir. But if you object, she can go down.”

“Is this a place—or am I a person—to bring a lady to, you absurd lad?”

“No. So I’ll take her off.”

“Martin, you stay. Who is she?”

“Your grandmother from that château on the Scheldt Miss Moore talks about.”

“Martin,” a soft voice whispered at the door, “don’t be foolish.”

“Is she there?” Moore asked urgently.

“She’s there—fit to faint. Standing on the mat, shocked by your lack of filial affection.”

“Martin, you’re an evil cross between an imp and a page. What does she look like?”

“More like me than you—for she’s young and beautiful.”

“You are to show her forward. Do you hear?”

“Come, Miss Caroline.”

“Miss Caroline!” Moore repeated.

And when Miss Caroline entered, she was met in the middle of the room by a tall, thin, wasted figure who took both her hands.

“I give you a quarter of an hour,” Martin said, stepping back. “No more. Say what you must in that time. I’ll wait in the gallery. Nothing shall approach. I’ll see you safely away. Should you stay longer, I leave you to your fate.”

He shut the door.

In the gallery, he felt like a king. No adventure had ever made him feel so powerful, so vital. This was the finest thing he’d ever done.

“You’ve come at last,” Moore said, gazing at her with hollow eyes.

“Did you expect me sooner?”

“For nearly two months we’ve been close—very close. I’ve suffered pain, danger, misery, Cary.”

“I couldn’t come.”

“Couldn’t you? The rectory and Briarmains are only two miles apart.”

Her face held both pain and relief. To defend herself was sweet and bitter.

“When I say I couldn’t come, I mean I couldn’t see you. I came with mamma the very day we heard the news. Mr. MacTurk told us no strangers could be admitted.”

“But afterward—every fine afternoon, I’ve waited. Something here”—he touched his chest—“told me you’d think of me. Not that I deserve it. But we are cousins. We are old friends.”

“I came again, Robert. Mamma and I came again.”

“Did you? That’s worth hearing. Sit. Let’s talk.”

They sat. Caroline drew her chair close. The snow thickened outside. An icy blast drove it wildly.

They heard nothing. Saw nothing. Only each other.

“So you came again?”

“And Mrs. Yorke treated us strangely. We asked to see you. ‘No,’ she said. ‘Not in my house. I’m responsible for his life. It shall not be risked for idle gossip.’ But I won’t tell you all she said—it was unpleasant. Still, we came again—mamma, Miss Keeldar, and I. We thought three against one, with Shirley on our side, would win. But Mrs. Yorke unleashed a storm.”

Moore smiled.

“What did she say?”

“Things that astonished us. Shirley laughed. I cried. Mamma was deeply annoyed. We were driven away. Since then, I’ve only walked past the house once a day—just to see your window, drawn with curtains. I dared not enter.”

“I’ve wished for you, Caroline.”

“I didn’t know that. I never dreamed you thought of me. If I’d imagined it…”

“Mrs. Yorke would still have stopped you.”

“She wouldn’t. I would have tried stratagem. I would have come to the kitchen door. The servants would have let me in. I would have walked straight upstairs. It was more fear of intrusion—of you—than of her that kept me away.”

“Only last night I despaired of seeing you again. Weakness has crushed me—terribly.”

“You sit alone?”

“Worse than alone.”

“But you’re getting better, since you can leave your bed?”

“I doubt I’ll live. After such exhaustion, decline seems certain.”

“You—must go home to the Hollow.”

“Dreariness would follow. Nothing cheerful would come near me.”

“I will change this. I will make it change—though there were ten Mrs. Yorkes to oppose me.”

“Cary, you make me smile.”

“Do smile. Smile again. Shall I tell you what I’d like?”

“Tell me anything—just keep talking. I am Saul. Without music, I perish.”

“I’d like you to be brought to the rectory. Given to me and mamma.”

“A precious gift! I haven’t laughed since they shot me—until now.”

“Do you suffer pain, Robert?”

“Not so much now. But I’m hopelessly weak. My mind—dark, barren, powerless. Can you see it in my face? I look like a ghost.”

“Altered. Yet I’d recognize you anywhere. But I understand. I’ve felt something similar. Since we met, I’ve been very ill.”

“Very ill?”

“I thought I’d die. My life seemed finished. Every night at midnight, I woke from terrible dreams. The book lay open—‘Finis.’ I had strange feelings.”

“You speak my experience.”

“I believed I’d never see you again. I grew so thin—as thin as you are now. I couldn’t rise or lie down. I couldn’t eat. Yet here I am—better.”

“Comforter—sad as sweet. I’m too weak to express what I feel. But while you speak, I *do* feel.”

“Here I am, at your side—where I thought I’d never be again. I speak to you. You listen. You look at me kindly. Did I count on that? I despaired.”

Moore sighed—a deep, almost groaning sigh. He covered his eyes.

“May I be spared to make some atonement,” he prayed.

“For what?”

“We’ll not speak of it now. Unarmed as I am, I cannot face it. Was Mrs. Pryor with you during your illness?”

“Yes.” Caroline smiled. “You know she’s mamma?”

“I’ve heard—Hortense told me. But I’ll hear it from you. Does she add to your happiness?”

“What! Mamma? She is _dear_ to me. How dear I cannot say. I was weary. She held me up.”

“I deserve to hear that—when I can scarcely lift my hand.”

“It is no reproach.”

“It is a coal of fire on my head. And every word you say—every glance you give—is a flame. Come closer, Lina. Give me your hand—if my thin fingers scare you.”

She took them—both hands cradling his. She bent her head and kissed them—*et les effleura de ses lèvres*. (I write it in French because *effleurer* is a perfect word.)

Moore trembled. Tears traced paths down his pale cheeks.

“I’ll keep these things in my heart, Cary. That kiss—I’ll treasure it. One day, you’ll hear of it again.”

“Come out!” Martin called, bursting the door. “Come away! You’ve had twenty minutes instead of fifteen.”

“She won’t move yet, you hempseed.”

“I dare not stay longer, Robert.”

“Can you promise to return?”

“No,” Martin said firmly. “This mustn’t become a habit. I can’t be bothered. Once is enough. I won’t allow it again.”

“You won’t allow it again.”

“Hush! Don’t upset him. We couldn’t meet today without him. But I *will* come again—if it’s your wish.”

“It *is* my wish—my *only* wish—almost the only thing I feel.”

“Come now. My mother coughed. She’s up. She’s put her feet on the floor. Let her catch you on the stairs, Miss Caroline. You’re not to bid him farewell. You’re to march.”

“My shawl, Martin.”

“I have it. I’ll put it on when you’re in the hall.”

He parted them. He would allow no farewell but one expressed in looks. He half-carried Caroline down the stairs.

In the hall, he wrapped her shawl around her. And if his mother hadn’t creaked on the gallery above, and if a quiet sense of modesty—true, natural, noble—hadn’t held him back, he might have said: “Now, Miss Caroline, for all this—give me one kiss.”

But before the words formed, she was across the snowy road—skimming through the drifts, not wading.

“She owes me,” Martin whispered. “And I *will* be paid.”

He convinced himself it was chance, not courage, that failed him. But he misjudged himself. He underestimated the depth of his own nature.
