Spring arrived, painting England's surface in greens and blooms, yet the nation's heart remained heavy. The war dragged on, draining wealth and shedding blood for ends that seemed increasingly hollow. While sporadic victories echoed from the Peninsula, they were drowned out by the arrogant triumphalism of Bonaparte. For those suffering under the conflict, the struggle felt endless and desperate; merchants like Yorke and Moore, trembling on the brink of ruin, demanded peace with the frantic energy of those fighting for survival.

Meetings were held, petitions drafted, and speeches made, driven by a singular desire to escape the financial paralysis threatening the trading classes. Yet, in their collective selfishness, these men cared little for the terms of peace, only for the preservation of their own commerce. The narrative of the era painted a stark picture of a society ruled by Mammon, where chivalry had died and honor was secondary to profit. The British merchant, it was argued, would endure any insult from the French until the very last coin was threatened; only then would they transform into "British bulldogs," biting at the thief's throat until the treasure was restored. When speaking against war, they claimed moral superiority, yet their hearts were often cold, viewing anyone outside their trade as a parasite consuming the bread of idleness.

Robert Moore, though no self-sacrificing patriot, was acutely aware of his own precarious position. Urged toward ruin, he fought with vigor, stirring agitation in the north and leveraging his connections to influence others. Yet, amidst the chaos, flashes of reason pierced his despair. He saw Europe arming against Bonaparte, Russia rising to defend her frozen soil and serfs from foreign tyranny, and realized that a free England could not submit to such injustice. News of Lord Wellington's steady, unostentatious progress in the Peninsula offered a glimmer of hope—a power that was vigilant and genuine, destined eventually to win. But for Moore, that victory felt distant. In the meantime, he feared he would be crushed, his hopes ground to dust. His focus narrowed to his own survival and destiny.

This fierce determination led to a decisive rupture with his old Tory friend, the rector, Mr. Helstone. Their disagreement erupted at a public meeting and escalated into pungent newspaper exchanges. Mr. Helstone denounced Moore as a Jacobin, severed all ties, and explicitly ordered his niece, Caroline, to cease her visits to Hollow's Cottage and abandon her French lessons. He dismissed the language as frivolous and harmful to female minds, comparing it to feeding a rickety child on chalk and water. He viewed Moore's presence at the rectory with suspicion, fearing clandestine meetings.

Mr. Helstone was a man of sharp observation. He had noticed that while the boisterous Malone entertained the household with his antics, causing Caroline to vanish upstairs, Robert Moore's quiet, gentle demeanor drew her into a state of wondrous contentment. He had witnessed them exchange glances—once, only once—eyes that were clear and gentle in Moore, manly and searching in Caroline. Each acknowledged a charm in the other, a silent understanding that annoyed the rector, who viewed such flirtation as a folly to be chastised. Combining these private suspicions with his political objections, Helstone resolved to separate the cousins.

One evening, as Caroline worked near the window, Helstone delivered his command in a dry, significant tone. He noted her paleness and the absence of Robert's name from her lips for weeks, suspecting secret rendezvous. Caroline acquiesced immediately, her face devoid of the expected flush or tears. The shadow of thoughtfulness that had settled over her remained undisturbed. She obeyed because the mandate aligned with her own judgment; going to Hollow's Cottage had become a source of pain. Hope and love had departed that little tenement, replaced by the reality that Robert seemed to have deserted its precincts.

At church, the only remaining chance to see him, Caroline rarely looked. To do so was both too much pain and too much pleasure, an emotion she knew was wasted. On one dark, wet Sunday, however, she caught a glimpse of him alone in his pew. He looked sad and abstracted, his face a tintless olive, his thoughts wrapped in mysteries of business and responsibility that excluded her entirely. She meditated on his condition, realizing the vast difference between their lives. Her heart was consumed by love, a predominant emotion for two years, while his mind was occupied by forces she could not comprehend. He would not turn his head; he gave her no glance. The grief of being unseen choked her.

That evening, alone in her chamber with a cheerless fire, Caroline's mind wandered through images of shared winters and summers, of nuts and berries shared like a bird feeding its fledgling. But these visions faded, leaving the cold reality of her narrow room. She faced the terrifying prospect of her future: a life of seventy years without marriage or children. She mused on the role of the old maid, rejecting the notion that virtue lay solely in self-abnegation. She questioned the hollowness of an existence given away to others without something of one's own to bestow. Reflecting on mortality and the soul's journey, she found comfort only in the biblical promise of a refuge in eternity.

The following day, with her uncle away, Caroline spent her time in silent contemplation. Fanny, her maid, observed her mistress's stillness and urged her to visit Miss Mann or Miss Ainley, noting that old maids were often unhappy. Caroline, however, dismissed the idea of Miss Mann as a dismal prospect, fearing the "unhappy race" of old maids. Yet, Fanny's insistence sparked a resolve. Caroline decided to visit Miss Ainley, wondering what life had been to make her unamiable.

Upon arriving at Miss Mann's, Caroline found a scene of perfect, almost austere neatness. Miss Mann sat primly in a rocking chair, her gaze fixed with a formidable intensity that Robert Moore had once described as comparable to Medusa's. This stare, combined with a dry, monotonous voice, felt like a graven image of a bad spirit. However, as Caroline spoke with a sympathetic tone, the old maid's demeanor softened. Caroline saw the bloodless pallor and deeply worn lines of Miss Mann's face, recognizing the loneliness behind the grim exterior. Miss Mann, usually reserved, opened up, revealing a history of cruel, slow-wasting sufferings and devoted care for a wretched relative. Caroline realized that Miss Mann's moroseness was a shield against a world that had forgotten her, and she resolved to offer her affection and respect, visiting her weekly.

Next, Caroline visited Miss Ainley, a woman even plainer and poorer than Miss Mann. Despite her unattractive appearance and starched manners, Miss Ainley possessed a serene, unselfish mind. She spoke only of others, their wants and sufferings, displaying a religious devotion that commanded reverence. Her goodness was evident in her service to the poor, nursing the sick and working tirelessly despite her own privations. Though she received little reward in this life, her sincerity was undeniable. Caroline, who had previously dismissed such figures as objects of ridicule, now bowed her mind in reverence before Miss Ainley's practical excellence.

Caroline returned home with a new resolve. She planned to divide her time between study, charitable work directed by Miss Ainley, and exercise, aiming to stem the anguish that had poisoned her spirit. She determined to be wise if she could not be good, refusing to waste her youth in inert languor. Yet, despite her conscientious efforts, the change was not immediate. The work helped to keep her occupied and prevented brooding, offering fleeting moments of satisfaction when she did good. However, the deep ache of lost love persisted. Her memory kept harping on Robert Moore's name; a funereal cry haunted her, and the heaviness of a broken spirit settled slowly on her buoyant youth. Winter seemed to be conquering her spring, her mind's soil freezing into barren stagnation, even as she strove to build a life of purpose.
