The evening had drawn dark across Florence, heavy with the threat of rain, when Romola made her way home from the hospital of San Matteo. She had stopped there after vespers, and walked beside the old servant Maso, whose small, shuffling steps slowed their progress, his guttering lantern casting wavering gold over the glistening cobblestones. Just as they turned onto the Via de’ Bardi, she came face to face with her husband, Tito, who was just stepping out of the cool, shaded archway of the San Marco monastery. He had returned home briefly earlier that day, then slipped out again, and had barely crossed paths with her at all in the hours since. He dismissed Maso at once, complaining that the old man’s lagging pace would have them caught in the coming storm, and offered to escort her the rest of the way home.

Such small, careful acts of attention were rare from Tito these days, only ever offered when he sensed they were expected of him. The two of them never argued, never raised their voices, never remonstrated with each other. The chasm between their inner lives had grown so wide that they had long abandoned the effort of fighting towards any kind of shared understanding. They spoke at length of all public and private matters, adhering rigidly to a carefully constructed, polite routine: if Tito asked for a supper to be laid out in the old library, now converted to a bright, pleasant banqueting room, Romola would assent at once and arrange every detail. In turn, Tito never interfered with her daily habits, accepted her help transcribing texts and compiling digests for his work, and quietly supplied the funds she needed for her charities. All the while, he avoided meeting her eyes, pretended he thought she was out of the house to avoid knocking at her rooms, and laughed off her absences as a preference for solitude over his company.

When she had first returned to Florence, abandoning her plan to flee the city for good, Romola had made shy, tentative efforts to rebuild the frank, easy intimacy they had once shared. But that intimacy could only ever be restored by open conversation about the rifts between them, by trying to reach a shared moral understanding. Tito, meanwhile, could only ever have felt close to her again if she had returned to the soft, effusive fondness of their early marriage, a state that would have required her to forget every betrayal she had witnessed. He wanted no explanations, knew full well a full accounting between them was impossible, and never guessed how utterly she could not feign the tenderness he craved. She could be gentle, submissive, and repress every flicker of repulsion, but she could not summon love that did not exist. The gap between them felt uncrossable, a fact Tito used to justify keeping her far removed from the political secrets he guarded so closely.

He tried to convince her that he believed the Medici cause was hopeless, and that he served the popular Florentine government, which she now supported wholeheartedly, both out of practicality and sincere conviction. But faint, unplaceable impressions, subtle as the scent of incense clinging to a monk’s robes, left her deeply uneasy about his ties to the circle at San Marco. She was torn constantly: dreading that she might find concrete proof of his treachery, but driven by a desperate urge to watch for harm before it could unfold.

As they walked that evening, Tito said, “My work for the day is not yet done. I will see you safely to our door, my Romola, and then I have one more commission to fulfil that may take an hour, before I can rest as I well deserve to.” He went on to chat lightly, amusing her with stories of what he had seen on his recent trip to Pisa, until they drew near a small open loggia where a guttering oil lamp hung before a painted image of the Virgin. The street was quiet, and they had passed barely a handful of people all evening — but now they heard the heavy sound of many approaching footsteps, and the hum of confused, loud voices.

“We will get soaked through if we do not take shelter under this loggia,” Tito said hastily, hurrying her up its steps, his movement sharp and startled.

“Surely this fine drizzle is nothing to wait out,” Romola said, surprised by his urgency.

“No — I can feel it growing heavier. Let us pause here a little.” His mind, perpetually tensed for the faintest sign of danger, had caught a glint in the lamplight: the leader of the oncoming group wore a bright red feather in his cap, and a glittering, polished sword hilt. It was Dolfo Spini, the last person on earth he would have chosen to meet with Romola at his side at this hour. He had already held one critical, secret meeting with Spini that day, and the “errand” he had mentioned to Romola was a second, follow-up meeting, arranged just after his visit to San Marco.

Tito had carried carefully forged letters to Savonarola that very afternoon. One of them bore the exact signature and seal of the Cardinal of Naples, the man who had fought harder than any other member of the Sacred College in Rome to advocate for the Frate. The letters claimed the Cardinal was travelling north from Pisa, and for a number of strong, private reasons could not enter Florence itself. He wanted to meet Savonarola at this fraught political juncture, the letters said, and had arranged to stop at San Casciano, ten miles outside the city. At sunrise the next morning, he would ride out disguised as an ordinary priest, and meet Savonarola as if by chance, at a lonely spot five miles outside Florence on the Pisa road.

The full plot, for which these forged letters were the first step, was this: Dolfo Spini and his band of Compagnacci street fighters would lie in ambush on that road at dawn, seize Savonarola and the single Dominican brother he would bring with him for company, and hand them over to a small detachment of Milanese cavalry waiting nearby. They would carry the Frate straight across the border into Papal territory, far out of Florentine reach. There was a strong chance that the sharp, wary Savonarola would suspect a trap, and refuse to leave the city walls — for even when he preached publicly, his friends insisted he be surrounded by an armed guard. But Tito had left him barely any time to consider the request, and gambled that Savonarola’s burning eagerness to rally Church support for a general council, and to win the Cardinal’s help in his ongoing fight against the Pope, would override his usual caution. He had refused to hand the letters to any messenger, insisting he must deliver them to the Frate personally, and had even pretended to worry aloud that the meeting was risky, urging Savonarola to request an armed guard from the Signoria, offering to carry that request for him in total secrecy. Savonarola had only replied that an armed guard would destroy the required privacy, his eyes flashing with resolve, and Tito had left the interview fully convinced he meant to go through with the meeting.

Tito cared very little how the plot turned out. He had arranged his affairs so cleverly that every possible outcome worked to his advantage. If Savonarola fell into the trap, he would be richly rewarded with papal gold and favour. If he did not, his careful, apparently loyal behaviour would strengthen his standing with Savonarola’s followers, his position with Spini and his faction, and keep his secret Medici patrons happy, who valued him all the more for the confidence both sides placed in him.

Spini, though, was a dangerous, clumsy accomplice. He loved the thrill of plots, but had none of the self-restraint or intelligence to keep a secret. He drank often, joining the bands of tavern topers that even staid Florence counted among its citizens, and despite their agreement to pretend they were barely acquaintances in public, there was every chance he would get drunk and loud, and blurt out their whole plot at any moment. Tito had hoped Spini had not spotted them in the dark — but Spini, standing in shadow, had seen Tito lit up full in the glow of the Virgin’s lamp for a split second, and recognised him at once. Romola, wrapped in her dark cloak, had gone unnoticed, and she now stood behind Tito, tucked into the far corner of the loggia.

“Ha! My little carrier pigeon!” Spini’s harsh, grating voice hissed, loud enough to carry, as he clamped a hand down hard on Tito’s shoulder. “What were you hiding for? You did not know it was comrades coming. It is lucky I spotted you — saves us so much time. What of the chase tomorrow morning? Will that bald old game come out of his den? Do we need to get the falcons ready?”

If Tito had been capable of unthinking, unplanned rage, he would have felt it then, towards this bull-headed fool unfit to be either a leader or a tool. His lips went white, his mind racing for a way out. If he tried to shush Spini, Romola’s suspicions would only deepen — he knew well enough that once Romola’s alarm was roused, she could not be silenced or tricked. If he pushed Spini away angrily, the wine-flushed Compagnaccio would turn violent, quick to take offence and slow to see any subtlety of motive. He chose a third path, one that revealed how much power Romola still held over him: the power to make him fear the consequences of his own actions. He pressed her hand lightly, as if to signal her to be quiet, and spoke in a warm, easy tone of equal camaraderie.

“Yes, my Dolfo, you can prepare fully. But do not bring trumpets with you.”

“Never fear,” Spini said, a little stung. “You do not need to act all high and mighty with me. I know exactly where the devil hides his tail, same as you. Wait — the old fool swallowed the bait whole? His famous prophetic nose never scented the hook?”

“Swallowed it as easily as you swallow a cup of Trebbiano wine,” Tito said aloud, while he thought to himself, The brute will not rest until he has ruined every last careful plan. “Look, there are torches coming. I hear there has been a fresh outbreak of the pestilence — that must be a bier coming back from the hospital.”

“Santiddio! I cannot stand the sight of those plague biers. Good night!” Spini said, stumbling away fast, his band of men trailing after him.

The torches were real enough, but they preceded a church vicar returning home, not a dead body. Tito had invented the bier story to get rid of Spini without ordering him to leave. The moment Spini was out of earshot, he turned to Romola, and said calmly, “Do not be alarmed by anything that brute said, my Romola. The rain has not gotten heavier — we can go home now.”

Romola was shaking, every inch of her thrumming with indignant, determined resolve. Tito’s casual, unconcerned tone made her trust him even less. “I will not take another step,” she said. “I will not move nearer home until I have your guarantee that this treachery against the Frate will never happen.”

“At least wait until these torches pass,” Tito said, perfectly self-possessed, but a cold new dislike rising in him for a wife who now, he saw clearly, had the power to thwart him, no matter how he claimed authority over their household.

The procession passed, Tito tipping his hat in respect to the cleric, but Romola saw nothing of it. If it had been necessary to hurl herself and her husband over a precipice right then to stop the planned betrayal, she felt she would have done it. All the two years of careful, self-imposed discipline she had practised since her return felt to her like it had vanished in an instant. The gulf between her and Tito felt wider than ever, unbridgeable. They stood almost entirely in shadow, able only to see the dim outline of each other’s faces.

“Tell me the truth, Tito,” Romola said, her voice low and trembling. “For once, tell me the truth. It will be far safer for you.”

“Why would I tell you anything else, my angry little saint?” Tito said, a faint flicker of contempt slipping into his voice as an outlet for his annoyance. “The truth is exactly what you will be most glad to hear: I know all about Spini’s plot, and that knowledge means I can stop the Frate from falling victim to it.”

“What is the plot?” Romola demanded.

“I will not tell you that,” Tito replied. “It is enough that the Frate will be kept safe.”

“It is a trap to lure him outside the gates so Spini can murder him!”

“There was never any plan to murder him. It is simply a scheme to force him to obey the Pope’s summons to Rome. But I serve the popular government, and I know the Frate’s presence in Florence is needed to keep it stable right now — so I choose to stop him from being taken away. You can sleep entirely easy tonight.”

Romola was silent for a long moment. Then she spoke, her voice thick with anguish. “Tito, it is no use. I do not believe a single word you say.”

She could just make out him shrug his shoulders, and spread his palms in a dismissive, silent gesture. The cold, unpassionate anger of a man who feels no real rage, only annoyed dislike, was hardening in his chest. “If the Frate leaves the city — if any harm comes to him,” Romola said, after a pause, her tone sharp with unshakable resolve, “I will go to the Signoria and declare everything I have heard. You will be disgraced. I am your wife, but that will not stop me. I will be disgraced right alongside you. If we are bound together, then I am the part of you that will force you to stop this crime. No one else will be betrayed because of your secrets.”

“I am perfectly aware of what you would do, anima mia,” Tito said, his voice smooth and unhurried. “So use a little reason now, if you can: if you cannot believe anything else I say, believe this — I will take every possible step to protect myself, and I will never give you the chance to ruin me.”

“Then you promise me the Frate has been warned? He will not go outside the city gates tomorrow?”

“He shall not go outside the gates.”

Romola did not relax. “I will go back to San Marco right now to confirm it,” she said, stepping forward.

“You will not,” Tito hissed in a bitter whisper, seizing her wrists hard with all his masculine strength. “I am your husband. I am master here. You will not defy me.” He had heard the approaching footsteps of passersby, which was why he had kept his voice so low. Romola did not struggle. Her sense of dignity, sharp as always, made her yield at once as Tito turned to lead her out of the loggia, not wanting to draw a crowd and humiliate them both in the street.

They walked on in silence through the fine, misty drizzle. Romola’s first rush of hot, unthinking indignation faded, making space for more complicated feelings that made speech and action feel almost impossible. For a bright, heated moment, defying her husband openly had felt like the easiest path — but now, the habits of years of self-restraint, the memories of all the impulses she had tamed after her failed flight, returned to her. Tito’s harsh grip on her wrists, rather than stoking her anger, filled her with a shuddering horror of this open, physical conflict between the two of them, the first time they had ever been at declared war since she had come back to Florence. She began to reason that Tito would never risk her exposing him. No matter what his original plan had been, the fact that she knew all his secrets now would force him to act to keep her from revealing them. She did not need to do anything drastic, she told herself, as long as she made it perfectly clear she would follow through on her threat if he betrayed her. It was still possible, she clung to the hope, that he had never meant to help betray the Frate at all.

Tito, meanwhile, was turning the situation over in his mind, calculating every possible outcome, and did not speak again until they were almost at their door. “Well, Romola,” he said then, “have you had time to calm down now? You do not need to trust my words — you can use simple logic. You can see, surely, that if I had ever intended to help Spini carry out his plot, I would never have let my wife, a known Piagnone loyal to Savonarola, learn its secret. That would be the height of foolishness.”

Romola spoke in a low, tired voice. “Yes, Tito. You are right. You know I will defend the Republic from treachery. You are correct to fear that — if the Frate is harmed, I will denounce you. But perhaps I spoke too hastily. Perhaps you never meant to go through with it. I only wish you did not need to be seen as that man’s friend.”

“Practical men must make these kinds of connections, my Romola,” Tito said, pleased to see her struggling with her own doubt. “You fair, noble creatures live far above the messy realities of the world. Go inside and rest easy,” he added, holding the door open for her.

Romola stepped across the threshold, the weight of unspoken, unresolvable distrust hanging thick in the air between them.
