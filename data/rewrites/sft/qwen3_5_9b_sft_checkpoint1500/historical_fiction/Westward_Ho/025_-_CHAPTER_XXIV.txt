"The Pleasure of the Devil"

*"Let us alone. What pleasure can we have
To war with evil? Is there any peace
In always climbing up the climbing wave?
All things have rest, and ripen toward the grave
In silence; ripen, fall, and cease:
Give us long rest or death, dark death, or dreamful ease."*

Alexander von Humboldt, in his travels, once observed a wretched band of Indians sitting listlessly around their fires, smeared with grease and paint, subsisting on ants and clay. He naively remarked that were it not for the teachings of science—which posit that this was the primitive state from which humanity evolved—he might have been tempted to view these unfortunate souls as the final, degraded remnants of a dying race. One wishes Humboldt had succumbed to that temptation, allowing his common sense to override the dogmas of modern anthropology, which lack factual basis for such wild notions. His instinctive judgment aligned with the Biblical doctrine that man, in a state of nature, is a fallen being doomed to death—a somber truth, perhaps, but far more honorable to humanity than the theory that we descended from apes. It is more comforting to believe these indigenous peoples were imperfect versions of what they ought to be, rather than unintelligent creatures dragging on for millennia without discovering better sustenance.

Yet, our voyagers, like their contemporaries, dismissed such philosophical inquiries. Accepting the Bible narrative as absolute truth, they sided with Humboldt's personal intuition rather than his scientific theories—or, more accurately, with Humboldt's conscience rather than the shallow anthropological fashions of the age. Consequently, they viewed their new hosts not as biological ancestors, but as immortal souls "captivated by the devil at his will," lost in the pathless forests and destined for spiritual ruin. Indeed, facts seemed to validate their antiquated views, though these Indians had not sunk quite as low as the Guahibas encountered on the lower reaches of the river.

Upon landing, the explorers found a scattered village of palm-leaf sheds, hammocks strung between trees, with patches of cassava and indigo visible in forest clearings. The settlement possessed a surprising degree of neatness and comfort. Yet, ominous signs of the evil spirit immediately presented themselves. Music, it seemed, was not inspired by a benevolent force. As Cary noted, if Apollo or Mercury ever visited America, they had played a cruel trick on the forefathers, providing them with inferior instruments and abysmal taste. On either side of the landing, four or five sturdy men stood with tall drums and long earthen trumpets. These instruments, hollowed into multiple spheres, erupted into a deafening cacophony of howls, groans, and thuds the moment the strangers stepped ashore, fully justifying Yeo's comment, "They are calling upon their devil, sir." Cary, displaying a modicum of logic, countered that these noises ensured they would not be disappointed, noting that "none but Sir Urian would ever come to listen to such a noise."

"And you mark, sirs," Yeo observed, "there's some feast or sacrifice toward. I'm not overconfident of them yet."

"Nonsense!" Amyas retorted confidently. "We could kill every soul of them in half an hour, and they know it as well as we."

Nevertheless, a grand demonstration was clearly underway. The forest children stood in two lines flanking the open space, men in front, women behind, all adorned to their utmost ability with annatto, indigo, and feathers. Suddenly, with a hideous yell, a figure leaped into the center. This individual, undeniably suited for the role of the devil, wore a jaguar-skin with a long tail, grinning teeth, horns, a plume of black and yellow feathers, and a massive rattle.

"Here's the Piache, the rascal," Amyas identified.

"Aye," Yeo agreed, "in Satan's livery, and I've no doubt his works are according. Trust him for it."

"Don't be frightened, Jack," Cary reassured, stepping back alongside Brimblecombe. "It's your business to tackle him, you know. At him boldly, and he'll run."

The men laughed, and the Piache, intending to project solemnity, hesitated briefly. Accustomed to earning his livelihood through impudence, he quickly regained his composure, advanced, struck one of the musicians over the head with his rattle to enforce silence, and began a harangue. Amyas listened patiently, a cigar hanging from his lips.

"What's it all about, boy?"

"He asks if you have seen Amalivaca on the other shore of the great water?" Amyas responded, as was customary, by praising Queen Elizabeth.

The Piache insisted she must be one of Amalivaca's seven daughters; some he had brought back, while he had broken the legs of the others to prevent escape, leaving them to populate the forests. Amyas corrected him, stating that her legs were certainly intact, for she was a model of grace and activity, the finest dancer in her dominions. However, his priority shifted to practical matters: would the tribe provide cassava bread and allow them to remain peacefully on the island until they could cross the mountains to fight the "clothed men"—the Spaniards?

Enchanted by Amyas's candor and the evident lack of weapons among the Indians, who seemed in high spirits, the party followed the Piache to a carefully closed hut. Crawling up to the entrance on all fours in an abject manner, the Piache began whining inside.

"Ask what he is about, boy."

The accompanying lad queried the old cacique, who revealed the Piache was consulting the Daughter of the Sun.

"Here is our mare's nest at last," Cary muttered as the Piache transitioned from whines to screams, gesticulating wildly, foaming at the mouth, and rolling his eyes until he collapsed exhausted, appearing dead.

"As good as a stage play."

"The devil has played his part," Jack observed. "And now, by the rules of all plays, Vice should come on."

"And a very fair Vice it will be, I suspect; a right sweet Iniquity, my Jack! Listen."

From within the hut emerged a low, sweet song. The simple Indians bowed their heads in reverence, while the English stood hushed in astonishment. The voice was not shrill or guttural like an Indian's, but round, clear, and rich, possessing the resonance of a European. As it swelled, revealing a compass and power extraordinary for any throat, the listeners, many of whom were skilled in glee, catches, and psalms, were captivated. The melody leaped, ran, and sank, rising again to fall, inarticulate yet perfect in its haunting beauty, akin to a bird on a bough. The wild wanderers were rapt in new delight, welcoming the notes as messengers from a higher realm. Finally, a triumphant burst shattered the silence, followed by profound quiet. The Piache, suddenly revived, jumped upright and resumed his preaching to Amyas.

"Tell the howling villain to make short work of it, lad! His tune won't do after that last one."

The lad grinned, informing Amyas that the Piache signified the acceptance of the English as friends by the Daughter of the Sun; her friends were theirs, her foes theirs. An ecstatic scream rose from the Indians. Amyas, rolling another tobacco leaf, simply requested cassava.

The hut doors opened, and the Indians prostrated themselves as a fair apparition emerged—the same maiden they had met on the island, now decked in feather robes and plumes of every hue. Moving slowly and stately, accustomed to command, she approached Amyas, glancing proudly at her prostrate subjects. Pointing gracefully to the trees, gardens, and huts, she conveyed through expressive gestures that everything was at his service. Taking his hand, she lifted it gently to her forehead.

At this sign of submission, a shout of rapture erupted from the crowd. As the mysterious maiden retreated, the English were swarmed by adoring natives, who marveled at their swords, bows, and beast trophies. Women hurried to offer fruit, flowers, cassava, and calabashes of intoxicating drink. Soon, the English sat beneath the trees, feasting merrily amidst the hideous music of drums and trumpets, while lithe young girls and boys performed uncouth dances. The spectacle scandalized Brimblecombe and Yeo so thoroughly that they urged Amyas to withdraw. Willing to return to the island fortress while the men were still sober, the party exchanged leave-taking promises and paddled back, puzzling over the identity of the enigmatic maiden.

Amyas, however, had settled on the belief that she belonged to the lost Inca race, perhaps a descendant of the fair girl, wife of the Inca Manco, whom Pizarro had tortured and killed decades prior to extract information. That evening, after services and singing, twenty mellow voices rang above the roar of the cataract. Once or twice, they thought they heard an echo, but ignored it until Cary returned from a brief excursion and whispered Amyas aside.

"The sweet Iniquity is mimicking us, lad."
