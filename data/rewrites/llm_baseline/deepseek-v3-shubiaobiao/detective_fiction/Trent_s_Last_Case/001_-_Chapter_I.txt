The world seldom judges wisely between what truly matters and what merely appears to. When a bullet from an unknown hand shattered the relentless mind of Sigsbee Manderson, the loss was not one to inspire mourning. He had amassed a fortune without earning a single friend, built an empire without leaving behind a single act worthy of honor. Yet when news of his death spread, the financial world convulsed as if the earth itself had been struck.

Manderson was no ordinary titan of industry. Others had wielded capital with ruthless efficiency, reaping millions as their reward, but he alone carried the aura of a rogue—a pirate who had somehow become the guardian of stability. His grandfather had been one of the old raiders of Wall Street, a chieftain of the lesser sort, and that legacy of daring had never quite left him. Though he was raised in the traditions of European wealth—taught to value quiet grandeur over vulgar display—there was always something of the gambler in his blood.

In his youth, he had been a prodigy of speculation, a genius of the Stock Exchange, where every trade was a duel and every victory a conquest. But when his father died, something shifted. The reckless speculator vanished, replaced by the calculating steward of an empire. He took control of his father’s banking firm, transforming it into an unshakable fortress of finance. The Street, once wary of his past, now revered him as its pillar.

Yet those closest to him knew the truth: the old Manderson still lurked beneath the surface. There were moments when the thrill of the game called to him, when plans for daring raids on the markets were drafted in secret—only to be locked away, unexecuted. It was as if a retired pirate, now a respectable merchant, still dreamed of the sea.

His death came at the worst possible moment. The markets were already uneasy, strained by scandal and inflated prices. For days, his associates had fought to maintain the illusion of control, whispering that Manderson himself was steering them through the storm. But when the news broke—when the Colossus was found dead—panic erupted like a wildfire. Stocks plummeted. Men who had staked everything on his myth saw their fortunes dissolve in hours. In New York, London, Paris, and Frankfurt, despair drove men to desperate ends.

Yet outside the fevered world of finance, life continued undisturbed. The fields still yielded their harvest, the machines still turned, the ordinary rhythms of existence carried on. Manderson’s death, for all its seismic shock to the markets, meant nothing to the vast majority of mankind. Within days, the frenzy subsided. The world moved on.

And as for Manderson himself? He was buried far from the country he had dominated, in a quiet English churchyard. No pilgrims would visit his grave, no mourners would weep for him. The man who had once shaken continents was forgotten before the earth had settled over his coffin.

The Colossus had fallen—and the world barely noticed.
