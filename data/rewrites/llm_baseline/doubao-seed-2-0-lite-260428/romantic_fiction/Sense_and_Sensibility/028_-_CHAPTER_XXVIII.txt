For three full, empty days that stretched longer than any Elinor could remember, nothing happened to soften her quiet regret that she’d written to their mother in the country, laying out the tangled mess of Marianne’s heartbreak. Willoughby never called, never sent a note, never even loitered on the street outside their Berkeley Street lodgings. By the fourth evening, they were due to accompany Lady Middleton to a grand town party, while Mrs. Jennings stayed home to tend to her youngest ailing daughter.

Marianne prepared for the event as if she were only marking time, not even pausing to smooth the ribbons on her favorite gown or run a comb through her half-tousled curls. She sat hunched by the drawing-room fire long after tea, completely lost to her own brooding thoughts, so disconnected from the world around her that she did not stir or shift her position even when Elinor passed right in front of her. When the maid finally announced Lady Middleton was waiting for them at the front door, Marianne jolted upright as if woken from a deep trance, as if she had forgotten they were expected anywhere at all.

They arrived at the party right on time, riding in the Middleton carriage through a long line of other waiting vehicles, before alighting, climbing the candle-lit stairs, and hearing their names called out loud from landing to landing for the crowd to hear. The room they stepped into was dazzling with chandeliers, packed shoulder to shoulder with guests, and sweltering enough to make the silk of their gowns cling damp to their backs. After curtsying to their hostess to perform their required polite duty, they were swallowed up in the throng, adding their own share of body heat to the already stifling space.
When Lady Middleton settled in for a long game of Cassino, Elinor spotted two empty chairs a short distance from the card table, and tugged Marianne over to sit, knowing her sister had no energy left to drift through the small talk of strangers.

They had not been seated five minutes before Elinor caught sight of Willoughby, standing barely three yards off, deep in earnest conversation with a fashionably dressed young woman whose hat was piled high with ostrich plumes. Their eyes met first, and he gave her a stiff, distant bow, not making the smallest move to approach, not even sparing a glance for Marianne who he must surely have seen the second he looked up. Elinor turned to check her sister, and that exact moment Marianne spotted him. Her whole face flooded with sudden, bright, disbelieving joy, and she would have sprung up and run to him immediately if Elinor had not clamped a firm hand on her arm to hold her back.
“Good heavens!” Marianne gasped, barely loud enough for Elinor to hear. “He’s there—he’s right there! Why won’t he look at me? Why can’t I go speak to him?”
“Be calm, I beg of you,” Elinor whispered back, heart thrumming. “Do not let every stranger in this room see how you feel. Perhaps he has not noticed you yet.”
Elinor did not even believe that lie herself, and Marianne was far too raw for composure, her face tight with an agony of impatience that twisted every line of her features.

At last Willoughby turned fully toward them. Marianne shot to her feet, spoke his name in a soft, trembling tone full of old affection, and held her hand out to him. He walked over, but his gaze stayed fixed almost entirely on Elinor, as if he was actively forcing himself not to meet Marianne’s eyes, and asked in a hurried, hollow tone after Mrs. Dashwood, and how long they had been in town. Elinor was so stunned by the cold formality of it that she could not get a single word out in reply.
Marianne’s face burned crimson. “Good God, Willoughby!” she cried, voice breaking with emotion. “What is the meaning of this? Have you not received all my letters? Will you not even shake my hand?”
He could not avoid the gesture then, but his touch was light and fleeting, as if holding her hand burned him. Elinor watched him fight to steady his expression, forcing it into a flat, impassive calm after a long pause.
“I did myself the honor of calling at Berkeley Street last Tuesday,” he said evenly, “and was very sorry not to find you all at home. I hope my card was not mislaid.”
“But my notes?” Marianne pressed, frantic now. “This is some terrible mistake, I know it is—what can possibly be the matter? Tell me, Willoughby, for heaven’s sake tell me what has gone wrong.”
He made no reply, his face flushing bright red and his old embarrassment flooding back, until he caught the eye of the fashionable young woman he had been speaking to earlier. The small jolt of it made him snap back to self-control. “Yes, I did receive word that you had arrived in town, the note you were kind enough to send,” he said, then gave a shallow, distant little bow, turned on his heel, and walked straight back to rejoin his friend.

Marianne went deathly white, her legs buckling under her, and she sank back hard into her chair. Elinor was certain she would faint at any second, so she leaned over to shield her sister from the curious stares of nearby guests, dabbing lavender water to her temples to bring her around.
As soon as Marianne could draw breath again, she gripped Elinor’s wrist. “Go to him,” she begged. “Force him to come back to me. Tell him I must see him—must speak to him this instant. I will not rest, I will not have a single moment’s peace until this misunderstanding is sorted out. Go to him now.”
“It cannot be done here,” Elinor soothed. “This is no place for such conversations. Wait, only until tomorrow.”
It was all she could do to hold Marianne back from chasing after Willoughby herself. There was no chance of persuading her to feign even a small degree of composure, and she kept breaking into quiet, muffled exclamations of wretchedness under her breath. A few minutes later, Elinor saw Willoughby slip out the door toward the staircase, and told Marianne he had left the party entirely. Marianne immediately begged her to ask Lady Middleton to take them home, so miserable she could not bear to stay another minute.
Lady Middleton was halfway through a rubber of cards, but far too polite to refuse a guest who claimed to feel unwell. She handed her cards off to a friend, and their carriage was fetched as quickly as the staff could manage. No words were exchanged on the ride back to Berkeley Street. Marianne was trapped in a silent, heavy agony too deep for tears, and the luck that Mrs. Jennings was still out when they arrived let them slip straight up to their private rooms without pausing for questions. A whiff of hartshorn revived Marianne a little, and she soon undressed and climbed into bed, asking to be left alone.

Elinor stepped out to wait for Mrs. Jennings to return, with plenty of quiet time to turn every second of the night over in her head. She had no remaining doubt that Willoughby and Marianne had been fully, secretly engaged. It was equally clear he had tired of that bond. No matter how desperately Marianne clung to the hope of some innocent misapprehension, Elinor could not explain his behavior away as anything but a total, cruel shift in his feelings. The only thing that softened her rage at him was that flicker of guilt and embarrassment she had spotted on his face, proof he had not set out to toy with Marianne’s heart from the very start with no honest affection at all. Absence had worn down his regard, Elinor reasoned, and some new, more convenient match had made him choose to cast Marianne off—but the love he had shown her at Barton Cottage had once been real.
For Marianne, though, the pain of that night’s meeting was only the start. Elinor thought of her own lot by comparison: she still held all her deep respect and affection for Edward, no matter what barriers or distance might come between them, and her heart had that solid, steady ground to lean on. But every bright romantic dream Marianne had ever tied to Willoughby was shattering before her eyes, and it looked more and more likely that this separation would be final, irreconcilable, and sharper than any pain she had ever known.
