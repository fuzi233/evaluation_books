To understand the architecture of Miss Keeldar's resistance, one must first dismantle the absolute tyranny of her guardian's worldview. Mr. Sympson and his niece were two souls vibrating on frequencies that could never harmonize; he was a rigid, irritable autocrat, while she was a spirited entity craving the winds of liberty. He was steeped in the dust of the world, whereas she harbored a romance that defied his pragmatic grasp.

His descent into Yorkshire was not accidental; it was a calculated campaign with a singular objective: to secure a suitable match for his ward, to bind her to a proper husband, and ultimately, to wash his hands of her forever. Yet, the tragedy of their coexistence lay in the semantic war waged since her infancy regarding the definitions of "suitable" and "proper." Shirley had never accepted his lexicon, and it remained uncertain whether, at the precipice of her destiny, she would capitulate to his interpretation.

The trial arrived swiftly. Mr. Wynne formally proposed for his son, Samuel Fawthrop Wynne.

"Decidedly suitable! Most proper!" Mr. Sympson declared, his voice ringing with the certainty of a judge delivering a verdict. "A fine, unencumbered estate; real substance; impeccable connections. It must be done!"

Summoning his niece to the oak-paneled parlour, he sealed the room against the outside world, leaving them alone to confront the reality of the offer. He delivered the proposal, voiced his approval, and demanded her consent.

It was withheld.

"No," she stated, her voice steady despite the storm brewing within her. "I shall not marry Samuel Fawthrop Wynne."

"I demand a reason. I must have justification. In every conceivable respect, he is more than worthy of you."

She stood upon the hearth, a figure of stark contrast against the white marble slab and cornice behind her. Her skin was pale as the stone itself, her eyes flashing wide, dilated, and devoid of a smile.

"And I," she countered, her tone sharp, "ask in what specific sense that young man is worthy of *me*?"

"He possesses twice your fortune, twice your common sense, equal connections, and equal respectability."

"Even if he held my money counted fifty times over, I would take no vow to love him."

"Please, state your objections clearly."

"He has run a course of despicable, commonplace profligacy. Accept that as the primary reason for my rejection."

"Miss Keeldar, you shock me!"

"That conduct alone sinks him into a gulf of immeasurable inferiority. Furthermore, his intellect fails to reach any standard I can esteem; there is a second stumbling block. His views are narrow, his feelings blunt, his tastes coarse, his manners vulgar."

"The man is a respectable, wealthy man! To refuse him is sheer presumption on your part."

"I refuse point-blank! Cease to annoy me with this subject; I forbid it!"

"Is it your intention ever to marry, or do you prefer the solitude of celibacy?"

"I deny your right to claim an answer to that question."

"May I inquire if you await some man of title—a peer of the realm—to demand your hand?"

"I doubt if the peer breathes on whom I would confer it."

"Were there insanity running in your family, I should believe you mad. Your eccentricity and conceit touch the very verge of frenzy."

"Perhaps, ere I have finished, you will witness me leaping over it entirely."

"I anticipate no less. Frantic and impracticable girl! Take warning! I dare you to sully our name with a *mésalliance*!"

"Our name! Am *I* called Sympson?"

"God be thanked that you are not! But be on your guard; I will not be trifled with!"

"What, in the name of common law and common sense, would you—or could you—do if my pleasure led me to a choice you disapprove?"

"Take care! Take care!" he warned, his voice and trembling hand betraying a fear he dared not admit.

"Why? What shadow of power do you wield over me? Why should I fear you?"

"Take care, madam!"

"Scrupulous care I will take, Mr. Sympson. Before I marry, I am resolved to esteem—to admire—to *love*."

"Preposterous stuff! Indecorous, unwomanly!"

"To love with my whole heart. I know I speak in an unknown tongue; but I feel indifferent whether I am comprehended or not."

"And if this love of yours falls upon a beggar?"

"On a beggar it will never fall. Mendicancy is not estimable."

"On a low clerk, a play-actor, a playwright, or..."

"Take courage, Mr. Sympson! Or what?"

"Any literary scrub, or a shabby, whining artist."

"For the scrubby, shabby, whining, I have no taste; for literature and the arts, I have plenty. And therein lies my wonder: how does your Fawthrop Wynne suit me? He cannot write a note without orthographical errors; he reads nothing but sporting papers; he was the booby of Stilbro' Grammar School!"

"Unladylike language! Great God! To what depths will she descend?" He lifted his hands and eyes to the ceiling in despair.

"Never to the altar of Hymen with Sam Wynne."

"To what will she come? Why are the laws not more stringent, allowing me to compel her to hear reason?"

"Console yourself, uncle. Were Britain a serfdom and you the Czar, you could not *compel* me to this step. *I* will write to Mr. Wynne. Give yourself no further trouble on the subject."

Fortune is proverbially called changeful, yet her caprice often manifests as a repetitive stroke of luck striking the same quarter. It appeared that Miss Keeldar—or rather, her fortune—had by this time created a sensation throughout the district, producing impressions in quarters she had never envisioned. No fewer than three offers followed Mr. Wynne's proposal, all varying degrees of eligibility. Each was successively pressed upon her by her uncle, and each was successively rejected. Yet, amidst these rejections, there were several gentlemen of unexceptionable character and ample wealth. Many, besides her uncle, asked what she meant, whom she expected to entrap, given her insolent fastidiousness.

Finally, the gossips believed they had unlocked the key to her conduct, and her uncle was certain of it. More importantly, this discovery revealed his niece to him in a completely new light, prompting a total transformation in his demeanor toward her.

Fieldhead had recently grown too hot to contain them both. The suave aunt could not reconcile their differences; the daughters froze at the sight of their quarrels. Gertrude and Isabella whispered by the hour in their dressing room, becoming chilled with decorous dread if they happened to be left alone with their audacious cousin. But, as fate would have it, a change supervened. Mr. Sympson was appeased, and his family tranquility restored.

The village of Nunnely had long been alluded to—its ancient church, its forest, its monastic ruins. It also boasted a hall called the Priory—an older, larger, more lordly abode than any Briarfield or Whinbury owned—and, crucially, a man of title: its Baronet. Neither Briarfield nor Whinbury could boast such a possession, their proudest and most prized asset having been merely nominal for years. The current Baronet, a young man hitherto resident in a distant province, was unknown on his Yorkshire estate.

During Miss Keeldar's stay at the fashionable watering-place of Cliffbridge, she and her friends met and were introduced to Sir Philip Nunnely. They encountered him repeatedly on the sands, the cliffs, and in various walks, sometimes at the public balls of the place. He seemed solitary. His manner was remarkably unpretentious—too simple to be termed affable, rather timid than proud. He did not condescend to their society; he seemed genuinely glad of it.

With any unaffected individual, Shirley could easily and quickly cement an acquaintance. She walked and talked with Sir Philip; she, her aunt, and cousins occasionally took a sail in his yacht. She liked him because she found him kind and modest, and was charmed to feel she possessed the power to amuse him.

There was, however, one slight drawback—where is friendship without it?—Sir Philip had a literary turn. He wrote poetry: sonnets, stanzas, ballads. Perhaps Miss Keeldar thought him a little too fond of reading and reciting these compositions; perhaps she wished the rhyme possessed more accuracy, the measure more music, the tropes more freshness, the inspiration more fire. At any rate, she always winced when he returned to the subject of his poems and usually did her best to divert the conversation into another channel.

He would beguile her to take moonlight walks with him across the bridge, seemingly solely for the purpose of pouring into her ear the longest of his ballads. He would lead her away to secluded rustic seats, whence the rush of the surf to the sands sounded soft and soothing.
