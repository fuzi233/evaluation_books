My godmother’s house stood in Bretton, a town as clean and old as a well-scoured brass pan hung in an ancestral kitchen—bright with long use, and quiet with a quiet that seemed bred into the stones. Her husband’s people had lived there for generations and carried the place in their very name—Bretton of Bretton—so that when one spoke it aloud, it sounded like the closing of a circle: the family returning, again and again, to the same threshold, the same street, the same air.

As a girl, I went there perhaps twice a year. I liked it with a liking that was almost hunger; for some houses feed you. The large rooms—peaceful as lard in a cool jar—were ordered without show. The furniture had its appointed stations like well-trained servants; the wide windows admitted a clear, temperate light; and outside, the balcony looked down on an antique street whose Sundays and holidays never seemed to depart. Even on common days the place held a Sabbath hush: the paving-stones lay clean, and the air had that rinsed quality towns sometimes carry after rain, only here it seemed perpetual.

In a household of grown people, one child is generally made much of; and though my godmother was not given to demonstrative fondness, she noticed me quietly, steadily, as one keeps a small flame alive in a room not meant to be cold. Mrs. Bretton had been left a widow before I knew her. Her husband—a physician—had died while she was still young and handsome. When I remember her, she was no longer young; but she remained handsome: tall, well-made, dark for an Englishwoman, with the clear bloom of health in her brunette cheek and a pair of black eyes that looked as if they had never yet consented to be dulled. She had, too, a voice that suited the house—firm, audible without harshness—like a door that shuts soundly and makes no complaint.

Her son, John Graham Bretton, had not inherited her colouring. People called it a pity. His eyes were blue—piercing even in boyhood—and his long hair was of a shade friends would not name except when the sun struck it, and then they permitted themselves “golden.” Yet he had her lines, her good teeth, her stature—or the promise of it, for he was not full grown—and, better than all, her unblemished health and her steady spirits. He moved through the rooms as through his own element; and when he laughed, it was like clean water running.

In the autumn of the year ——, I was staying at Bretton longer than usual; my godmother had come in person to claim me from the kinsfolk with whom my permanent residence was then fixed. I think she saw, plainly, certain events approaching—events whose shadow I scarcely guessed; yet even a faint suspicion was enough to settle an unquiet sadness upon me, and make me glad to exchange scene and company. At her side time always flowed smoothly—never in haste, never in shock—like a full river gliding across a level plain. There was no glitter of variety, no stir of incident: the days did not sparkle; they nourished. Peace, to me, was not dullness but diet; I sought stimulus so little, that when it came I almost felt it as an intrusion, as if a loud, ill-timed instrument had been carried suddenly into a room where one had been listening to the low, right sounds of life—curtains stirring, a distant cart, the measured step of a servant, a kettle settling on the hob.

One day a letter arrived which surprised Mrs. Bretton and touched her with some concern. At first I thought it might be from home; I felt the old tremor that waits on bad tidings before it has even learned their nature. But the letter made no mention of me, and whatever cloud it brought drifted, outwardly at least, away from my side.

The next day, returning from a long walk, I found, on entering my chamber, a change as startling as if another person’s breath had already altered the air. In addition to my own French bed—set in its recess with its shade and drapery—there was, in a corner, a small crib hung with white. And where my mahogany chest of drawers stood, a tiny rosewood chest now appeared, neat as a casket.

I stood still and looked, and the question formed itself without effort: what are these signs and tokens? The answer was plain. A second guest was expected; the house was preparing its place for another life.

At dinner explanations came. A little girl, Mrs. Bretton told me, would shortly be my companion—the daughter of a friend and distant relation of the late Dr. Bretton. The child had recently lost her mother; though, my godmother added after a moment, the loss was not quite what one might first suppose. Mrs. Home—Home being the name—had been very pretty, but giddy and careless; she had neglected her child and disappointed and disheartened her husband. Their union had proved so uncongenial that, at last, they separated—by mutual consent, without legal process. Soon after, the lady, having over-exerted herself at a ball, caught cold, took fever, and died after a brief illness.

The husband—Mr. Home—was, it seemed, a man of very sensitive feeling; the sudden news shocked him beyond what words could measure. He persuaded himself—would scarcely be persuaded otherwise—that some over-severity on his part, some want of patience and indulgence, had helped to hasten her end. He brooded on that thought until his spirits were seriously affected. Physicians urged travel as a remedy; and meanwhile Mrs. Bretton offered to take charge of his little girl.

“And I hope,” my godmother concluded, setting her knife down with an emphasis that was not anger but decision, “the child will not be like her mamma; as silly and frivolous a little flirt as ever sensible man was weak enough to marry. For Mr. Home is a sensible man—in his way—though not very practical. He’s fond of science; he spends half his life in a laboratory trying experiments—things his butterfly wife could neither comprehend nor endure; and indeed,” she admitted with honest brevity, “I should not have liked it myself.”

At my question she added that Dr. Bretton used to say Mr. Home had derived this scientific bent from a maternal uncle—a French savant; for he came, it seemed, of mixed French and Scottish origin, and had connections still living in France, where more than one wrote de before his name and called himself noble. Names and particles—little dominations of ink—passing down generations as if a syllable could confer rank as bread confers strength.

That evening at nine o’clock a servant was sent to meet the coach by which our small visitor was expected. Mrs. Bretton and I sat alone in the drawing-room to await her. John Graham was absent, visiting one of his schoolfellows in the country. My godmother read the evening paper; I sewed. The night was wet. Rain drove against the panes, and the wind, never content with one note, went restless along the street—now a low growl, now a sudden sharper gust, as if some unseen hand struck and released a cord. The house answered with its own domestic sounds—the tick of a clock, the faint settling of coal in the grate—softly resisting the outside uproar.

“Poor child!” said Mrs. Bretton more than once. “What weather for her journey! I wish she were safe here.”

A little before ten the bell announced Warren’s return. The instant the door opened I ran down to the hall. There lay a trunk and several band-boxes. Beside them stood a person like a nurse-girl. Warren was at the foot of the stairs holding a shawled bundle in his arms.

“Is that the child?” I asked.

“Yes, miss.”

I would have lifted the shawl for a look, but the bundle was turned from me quickly to Warren’s shoulder.

“Put me down, please,” said a small voice as Warren opened the drawing-room door. “And take off this shawl.” The speaker’s minute hand found the pin, and with a fastidious haste she doffed the clumsy wrapping, as if it offended her sense of order.

The creature revealed was exceedingly tiny—yet neat, completely fashioned: a light, straight little figure, like a small stalk that has grown slender through careful tending. She made a deft attempt to fold the shawl, but the cloth was far too heavy for those thin arms.

“Give it to Harriet, please,” she directed, “and she can put it away.”

Then she turned and fixed her eyes on Mrs. Bretton.

“Come here, little dear,” said my godmother. “Come and let me see if you are cold and damp: come and let me warm you at the fire.”

The child advanced promptly. Lifted onto Mrs. Bretton’s ample lap, she looked a doll there—her delicate neck like wax, her head a soft mass of silky curls that seemed to hold the lamplight as cream holds a faint gold. My godmother talked to her in quiet fond phrases while she rubbed the small hands and warmed the feet. At first the child met her with a wistful gaze; soon a smile answered.

Mrs. Bretton was not generally caressing. Even with her son, whom she deeply cherished, her manner was rarely sentimental—often the reverse; but that small smile drew a kiss from her.

“What is my little one’s name?” she asked.

“Missy.”

“But besides Missy?”

“Polly, papa calls her.”

“Will Polly be content to live with me?”

“Not always; but till papa comes home. Papa is gone away.” She shook her head with an expressiveness beyond her years. “He will return to Polly, or send for her.”

“Will he, ma’am? Do you know he will?”

“I think so.”

“But Harriet thinks not—at least not for a long while. He is ill.”

Her eyes filled. She drew her hand from Mrs. Bretton’s and made a movement to leave her lap. Mrs. Bretton held her at first, but the child said, with a restrained steadiness—

“Please, I wish to go. I can sit on a stool.”

She slipped down. Taking a footstool, she carried it to a corner where the shade was deep, and there seated herself, as if she had chosen a refuge. My godmother—commanding in grave matters, peremptory if need required—was often passive in trifles; she allowed the child her own way. She said to me in a low voice, “Take no notice at present.”

But I did take notice. I watched the small elbow rest on the small knee, the head lean on the hand. I saw her draw, from the doll-pocket of her doll-skirt, a square inch or two of pocket-handkerchief. And then I heard her weep.

Other children cry aloud, unashamed; this child was wept. Only the tiniest occasional sniff betrayed emotion; the sorrow was held in as carefully as one holds a hot liquid in a thin cup. Mrs. Bretton did not hear—and perhaps it was well.

Presently the small voice issued from the corner, precise and imperious through its grief.

“May the bell be rung for Harriet?”

I rang. The nurse came.

“Harriet, I must be put to bed,” said her little mistress. “You must ask where my bed is.”

Harriet said she had already asked.

“Ask if you sleep with me, Harriet.”

“No, Missy,” the nurse answered. “You are to share this young lady’s room,” indicating me.

The child did not leave her seat, but her eyes sought me. She considered me for some minutes in silence; then she rose and came out of the shadow.

“I wish you, ma’am, good night,” she said to Mrs. Bretton. To me she said nothing as she passed.

“Good-night, Polly,” I said.

“No need to say good-night, since we sleep in the same chamber,” she replied, and vanished from the drawing-room.

We heard Harriet offer to carry her up-stairs.

“No need,” came again the answer—“no need, no need;” and her small step toiled up the staircase, weary but resolute.

When, an hour later, I went to bed, I found her still wide awake. She had arranged her pillows to prop her into a sitting posture. Her hands lay one within the other on the sheet, quiet and composed with an old-fashioned calm that did not belong to childhood. For some time I did not speak to her; but before extinguishing the light, I told her she should lie down.

“By and by,” she answered.

“But you will take cold, Missy.”

She took some tiny article of clothing from the chair beside her crib and drew it over her shoulders, as if she had long ago learned that comfort must sometimes be made, not waited for. I let her do as she pleased.

In the darkness I listened. She still wept—under restraint, cautiously, as if afraid of the sound of her own sorrow in a strange house. Outside, the rain had lessened; the wind went on, but at a distance, like a world that could not be shut out entirely, only muffled.

At daylight a trickling of water caught my ear. She was risen and mounted on a stool near the washstand, inclining the ewer with pains and difficulty, for she could not lift it, and letting the water pour into the basin. It was curious to watch her: so small, so busy, so noiseless—like some careful household sprite set to work before the rest of us had stirred. She was plainly unused to managing her own toilet; buttons and strings, hooks and eyes offered difficulties. Yet she met them with a perseverance good to witness—patient as a cook with a stubborn fire that must be coaxed into flame, not struck into it.

She folded her night-dress; she smoothed the bedclothes neatly. Then, withdrawing into a corner where the sweep of the white curtain concealed her, she became still. I half rose, craning my head to see. On her knees, forehead bent on her hands, she was praying.

A tap came at the door: her nurse.

Polly started up at once.

“I am dressed, Harriet,” she said. “I have dressed myself, but I do not feel neat. Make me neat!”

“Why did you dress yourself, Missy?”

“Hush—speak low, Harriet, for fear of waking the girl,” meaning me, though I lay with my eyes shut. “I dressed myself to learn, against the time you leave me.”

“Do you want me to go?”

“When you are cross, I have many a time wanted you to go; but not now. Tie my sash straight; make my hair smooth, please.”

“Your sash is straight enough. What a particular little body you are!”

“It must be tied again. Please to tie it.”

“There, then. When I am gone you must get that young lady to dress you.”

“On no account.”

“Why? She is a very nice young lady. I hope you mean to behave prettily to her, Missy, and not show your airs.”

“She shall dress me on no account.”

“Comical little thing!”

“You are not passing the comb straight through my hair, Harriet; the line will be crooked.”

“Ay, you are ill to please. Does that suit?”

“Pretty well. Where should I go now that I am dressed?”

“I will take you into the breakfast-room.”

“Come, then.”

They moved towards the door. Polly stopped.

“Oh, Harriet, I wish this was papa’s house! I don’t know these people.”

“Be a good child, Missy.”

“I am good, but I ache here.” She put her hand to her heart, moaning as she repeated, “Papa! papa!”

I could not let that scene swell unchecked. I roused myself and started up, the bedclothes falling back, my voice and movement cutting into their private grief like a sudden new sound in a fragile morning.

“Say good-morning to the young lady,” Harriet prompted.

“Good-morning,” said Polly; and then she followed her nurse from the room.

That same day Harriet left for a time to visit friends in the neighbourhood. When I went down, I found Paulina—she called herself Polly, but her full name was Paulina Mary—seated at the breakfast-table by Mrs. Bretton’s side. A mug of milk stood before her; a morsel of bread was in her hand, lying passive on the cloth, as if she held it by habit rather than hunger. She was not eating.

“How we shall conciliate this little creature,” said Mrs. Bretton to me, watching the untouched milk, “I don’t know: she tastes nothing, and by her looks she has not slept.”

I said I felt confidence in time and kindness.

“If she were to take a fancy to anybody in the house,” replied my godmother, “she would soon settle; but not till then.”
