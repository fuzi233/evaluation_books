The first of October dawned bright and wind-still. Soft grey autumn clouds streaked the wide Devonshire sky from east to west, as if all heaven and earth had paused to rest after the brutal, storm-torn summer of sea battles and near-mutinies off the Spanish Main. The battered galleon *Vengeance* slid silent over Appledore Bar, as if she herself was hushed with grief, glided past the drowsy sand hills, and dropped her anchor with her flag flying half-mast high. Stowed in her hold lay the body of Salvation Yeo.

A small boat pulled for the western edge of the strand, where crowds of villagers had gathered, and Cary and Brimblecombe stepped out first to steady Amyas Leigh, newly blinded, leading him slow up the hill toward his childhood home at Burrough. The onlookers clustered close, their cheers softening into murmurs of blessing and quiet sobs from kind-hearted local women: every soul in Bideford and Appledore knew full well the torments Amyas had endured at the hands of the Spanish, and the sight of that great, broad-shouldered sailor moving unseeing through familiar sand brought tears to even the hardest old fisherman’s eyes.

“Spare me, good friends,” Amyas said gently, holding up a hand. “I landed here to come home quiet, no parading through the town to be gawked at. Think not of me, go not on about me. But follow behind me decently, as Christian men, and see to the grave a far better man than I will ever be.”

As he spoke, the second boat pulled for the shore, and there was Yeo’s coffin, draped in the cross of St. George. The people took him at his word, sending a runner ahead to Burrough House to warn Mrs. Leigh her son was home at last. When the coffin was heaved to its bier, Amyas and his two oldest friends took their place as chief mourners behind it, the rest of the *Vengeance*’s crew falling in line, and the crowd swelling behind them until by the time they reached Northam churchyard, the funeral train numbered close to five hundred souls. They had sent word via fishing skiff the day before to the sexton to dig the grave, and the parson waited for them at the lychgate.

Mrs. Leigh had stayed home, too soft-hearted to face the crowd, and content (as she always was) to let her son honor the faithful old servant who had sailed half the world over to watch over him. She sat in the old bay window with Ayacanora at her side, and when the church bell stopped tolling, she opened her prayer book and began to read the burial service aloud.

“Ayacanora, child,” she said, pausing mid-line, “they are burying old Master Yeo, who loved you, who hunted for you across the wide southern seas, who pulled you from the jaws of a crocodile when you were little. Why do you look so bright and happy today, are you not sorry for him?”

Ayacanora blushed bright red and hung her head. She had not been thinking of Yeo at all, the poor wild thing: every thought in her head was fixed on Amyas, home at last.

When the service ended and the blessing was spoken, the crowd lingered, clustering round the fresh grave to stare from the dirt to the blind giant standing at its head, as if they sensed there was more to come. And more did come. Amyas drew himself up to his full six and a half feet of height, waved one broad hand to call for quiet, and the eyes of every man there locked on his sightless face. Twice he tried to speak, twice his voice choked in his throat, until at last the words came out, deep and ringing:

“Good people all, you seamen among whom I was raised, to whom I come home blind today to dwell among you until my last breath—here lies the very pattern of all bold English mariners. The truest of friends, the most terrible of foes, unshakable in his purpose, sharp in council, quick to strike. Modest in triumph, as patient through failure as no other mortal man I ever knew. He was the first of all Britons to humble Spanish pride at Rio de la Hacha and Nombre, the first to sail the South Seas that by God’s grace will soon be as open to our English keels as the bay outside this bar. Purged of his young sins by unspeakable torments at the hands of the papist enemy, he learned to fear God and naught else on this earth. He served as a righteous scourge to the Spaniard, and a faithful soldier of Christ, and now he has gone to his reward, as Elijah did of old, carried up to heaven in a chariot of fire. I pray his mantle of courage and godliness falls on every one of you left behind, that these shores may never want for brave, pious mariners who count their lives worth nothing if they are spent for their country, their Bible, and their Queen. Amen.”

He reached out to find his friends’ hands, and walked slow out of the churchyard, across the village street and up the lane to Burrough gates, the crowd stepping back to make way for him in solemn silence, as if he was some sacred figure wrapped in the dark of his own strange, heavy fate. He knew the place by muscle memory, unlocked the gate himself, and strode up the gravel path without stumbling, while Cary and Brimblecombe followed trembling, half afraid of a burst of wild grief from him or his mother. He stepped across the threshold, and called soft: “Mother!”

In an instant she was pressed to his chest. Neither spoke for long minutes: she sobbed inwardly, tears never spilling down her face, while he stood firm and steady, his great arms wrapped tight around her.

“Mother,” he said at last, “I’m home, you see. Could not stay away. Will you take me in, look after this useless old carcase? I won’t be very troublesome, I promise. Won’t I?” He smiled down at her, and kissed her forehead.

She said not a single word, but slipped her arm gentle around his waist and led him inside. “Mind your head, dear child. The doorways are low.”

When Amyas turned to call Cary and Brimblecombe inside, the two good men had already slipped away down the path.

“Thank God we’re gone,” Cary said, wiping his eyes on his sleeve. “I’d have blubbered like a babe in another minute, watching that angel of a woman. The way her face worked and she never let a tear fall!”

“Ah, well,” Brimblecombe sighed. “There’s a brave servant of the Queen, his work not a quarter done. I’d best go see my old father now, and then—”

“Then you’re coming home with me,” Cary laughed, clapping him on the back. “We’ve pulled the same oar in the same boat for years, Jack. We’re not parting now you’re ashore. I won’t let you blow all your prize money buying ale for half Bideford for a week running. I’ve got to look after you.”

“Oh, Mr. Cary, what a thing to say!” Brimblecombe sputtered, scandalized. “Come home with me instead, we’ll beg my father the parson to give you the best rectory in the shire.” So the two set off for Clovelly that very day.

Left alone, Amyas settled into the old bay window where he’d sat as a tiny boy, poring over tattered copies of *King Arthur*, Fox’s Book of Martyrs, and the old pamphlet detailing Spanish cruelties across the Atlantic. He reached out, and found the three books sitting exactly where they had lain twenty years before. The window was open, and the cool autumn air carried the same scents he remembered: four-season roses, rosemary, and sweet gillyflower. On the table beside him sat a dish of apples, their sharp sweet scent instantly recognizable—the very same cider apples he used to climb the orchard trees to gather as a boy.

He picked them up one by one, fingering their smooth skins, and all his life flashed through his mind, the way visions are said to flash before a drowning man. He saw every port he’d ever docked in, heard every word ever spoken to him, until he was thinking of that fairy island on the Meta river, the roar of the cataract, the palm fronds glowing green over the spray, the wild girl Ayacanora he’d rescued from the jungle. How he’d wronged her, he thought. Well, he’d been punished, hadn’t he? Who would want to tend a great blind oaf like him, who’d need to be fed and led about like a toddler for the rest of his days?

He fumbled with the apples, and one slipped clean through his fingers, clattering onto the floor. He stooped to feel for it, could not find it, turned too fast, and cracked his head hard against the table leg. That small, silly, trivial frustration snapped every last taut nerve he’d held in check for months. He stamped his foot once in petty irritation, then burst out crying, great heaving sobs wracking his broad frame.

A soft rustle passed his side. The apple was pressed right back into his palm, and Ayacanora’s voice sobbed right next to his ear. “There! There it is! Do not weep, I beg you! I cannot bear it! I will fetch everything you ever want, tend you, feed you, lead you, I will be your slave, your dog! Only let me stay with you!” She fell to her knees at his feet, seizing both his hands and covering them over and over with kisses. “I have you safe now! You cannot sail off to sea, you cannot turn your back on me any more! You cannot escape!” She paused, and her voice broke. “Oh, what a wretch I am, to rejoice that you are blind! I am just a poor wild Indian savage, I know—but I love you, Amyas. I love you more than the sea loves the shore.”

A great, shuddering breath shook Amyas from head to toe. He sat silent for a long minute, and then spoke soft and solemn: “Is this grace still possible? Then God have mercy on me, a sinner.”

He bent down, lifted her as easily as a man lifts a lamb, crushed her to his chest, and kissed her tear-streaked face over and over.

The door creaked open. Ayacanora sprang away from him with a little cry, half trembling and half defiant, as if to say no one on earth could take him from her now.

“Who is there?” Amyas asked.

“Your mother,” Mrs. Leigh said, stepping forward.

“You see I am bringing forth fruits meet for repentance, mother,” Amyas said, smiling. He heard her step closer, felt her soft hand brush his cheek.

“Amyas, my son,” she said, her voice soft and clear as distant angel chimes, “do not fear to hold her to your heart. It was I, your mother, who laid her there for you to find.”

Amyas breathed deep, and pressed Ayacanora tighter to his chest. “It is true, then,” he murmured to himself. “What God has joined together, no man can put asunder.”

From that hour, Ayacanora’s lost gift of song returned to her. Day after day, year after year, her voice rose bright and sweet through the quiet rooms of Burrough House, soaring upward like a lark’s wings, carrying Amyas’s peaceful, happy thoughts back to the green sunlit paradises of the west they had seen together. And all around them, generation after generation of brave English mariners set sail to build a new, greater England across the uncharted ocean, crying the old proud cry that had carried them so far: Westward Ho!
