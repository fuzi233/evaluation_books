Major Pendennis, having indulged in the fine fare of Grosvenor Place and the maritime delights of Greenwich, found his affection for the Clavering family growing warmer by the day—or perhaps it was the generous portions of Lady Clavering’s dinners that stoked the flames. His visits became as frequent as a debtor at a bailiff’s door, and his attentions to the lady of the house so persistent that one might suspect him of either deep admiration or a calculated scheme. Being a seasoned navigator of London’s drawing rooms, he was well-equipped to guide her ladyship through the perilous waters of high society.

*"Would you care to attend the grand spectacle at Gaunt House?"* he inquired with the air of a man offering salvation. *"Or perhaps the breakfast ball at Viscount Marrowfat’s? Everyone who is anyone—and several who are decidedly not—will be there. Even royalty might deign to nod in your direction!"*

Sir Francis Clavering, meanwhile, remained as popular as a tax collector at a pauper’s banquet. The world, ever unkind, had taken to viewing him with the enthusiasm one reserves for spoiled fish. Blackballed at two clubs in swift succession, he consorted chiefly with the House of Commons’ most disreputable members—men who made even hardened politicians shudder. Captain Raff, the honourable member for Epsom (recently departed for the Levant under mysterious circumstances), Hustingson of Islington (now conveniently governing Coventry Island), and Bob Freeny, the dead shot of Booterstown, were among his chosen companions.

*"If you need Clavering for a vote,"* sneered Mr. Hotspur, the party whip, *"you’ll likely find him in a gambling den or a debtor’s prison. Mark my words, he’ll squander the Begum’s fortune on thimblerig and end his days on a prison hulk."*

Yet if Hotspur, despite his disdain, could endure Clavering for political expediency, why shouldn’t Major Pendennis cultivate the family for reasons of his own?

*"The man has an excellent cellar and a tolerable cook,"* the Major mused to his nephew Arthur. *"As long as he keeps his mouth shut, he’s harmless. And if he insists on losing his money to rogues, who am I to interfere? Every man has a skeleton in his cupboard—why rattle the bones when the rest of the house is so hospitable?"*

He then turned his avuncular charm upon Pen, nudging him toward the ever-charming Miss Amory. *"Come now, my boy, the girl is clearly fond of you. Or are you too grand now for anything less than a duke’s daughter?"*

Pen, inflated by recent successes and his uncle’s flattery, waved off the suggestion with the weariness of a man who has seen too much of the world at twenty-five. *"Marriage? Not for me. I’ve outgrown such fancies. My first love was a fool, and I’d have been a greater one to marry her."*

The Major, ever the tactician, shifted targets—next attempting to mold young Master Clavering into a suitable protégé. The boy, a terror in short trousers, had already mastered vice with the proficiency of a seasoned rake. By ten, he was filching his father’s cigars and drinking champagne with the gusto of a cavalry officer. The Major, undeterred, urged Pen to take the lad under his wing—or at least under his wallet, for young Clavering had a habit of departing with more sovereigns than he arrived with.

Meanwhile, the Major played confidant to Lady Clavering, extracting her sorrows over tea and sympathy. *"Ah, Major,"* she sighed, *"I may have diamonds and carriages, but I’m no happier than my scullery maid."*

Blanche, ever the dramatic, lamented her fate with the flair of a tragic heroine. *"The men of today are boobies, Major! Guardsmen without wit, clerks without prospects. I might as well enter a convent!"*

*"Nonsense,"* the Major soothed. *"The world is more democratic now. A clever woman with a clever husband can conquer society—provided she has the means and the nerve."*

And so the game continued, with the Major maneuvering his pieces with the precision of a chess master—though whether his endgame was fortune, influence, or mere amusement remained to be seen.
