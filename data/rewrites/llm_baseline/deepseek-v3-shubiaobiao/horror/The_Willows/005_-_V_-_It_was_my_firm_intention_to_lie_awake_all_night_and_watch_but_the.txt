I swore to myself I would stay awake—to watch, to listen, to stand guard against whatever lurked beyond the rustling willows. But exhaustion is a patient hunter, and it dragged me under despite my resolve. My companion’s uneasy sleep hastened my own surrender. At first, he twisted on his mattress, jerking upright to whisper, *“Did you hear that?”* or *“The river—it’s rising!”* Each time, I forced myself outside, squinting into the dark, only to return with hollow reassurances. Eventually, his breathing steadied. The snoring that followed was an obscene comfort—proof that, for now, we were still human, still unclaimed.

Then something pressed down on me.

Not just the blanket. A weight, like a body slumped across mine. I called out, shoving at the dark—but the tent was *surrounded*. That sound, that terrible *pattering*, filled the night like rain if rain had purpose, if it whispered. The Swede’s mattress was empty. The tent flap hung loose—an invitation.

I lunged outside.

The air itself was alive, vibrating with a hum that wasn’t a hum at all but a *voice*, layered, endless, pouring from the sky and the earth and the river all at once. It thickened in my lungs. The horizon bled pale light, just enough to see the willows choking the island, their branches clawing at nothing.

Then I saw him.

The Swede stood at the river’s edge, one foot already in the black water. He didn’t resist when I seized him. He fought like a man drowning in air, spitting words that weren’t words—*“The way of the wind—inside—inside—”*—until I dragged him back to the tent. The moment the flap closed, the humming stopped.

As abruptly as if it had never been.

He blinked up at me in the gray dawn, his face slack with childlike relief. *“They’ve found a victim in our place,”* he murmured, and then he slept—deep, untroubled, as if the night had been nothing but a dream.

By morning, he remembered none of it.

He built the fire, boiled coffee, remarked on the cold river. When I mentioned the silence—no hum, no pattering—he nodded as if it were the most natural thing in the world. *“Everything has stopped,”* he said, then hesitated. *“Because they’ve found another victim.”*

He said it so simply. As if it were fact.

We found the body downstream, wedged in the roots. A peasant, half-submerged, skin puckered with small, perfect hollows—*exactly like the sand-funnels*. The Swede reached for him, muttering about burial, but the current twisted the corpse face-up.

The marks were everywhere.

Then the humming returned—not from the air, but from the *body itself*, a swarm of unseen things taking flight. The dead man spun into the current, limbs loose, his ruined face staring at the sky as the river swallowed him whole.

The Swede fell to his knees. *“Their mark,”* he whispered.

I said nothing. The water had already erased the evidence. But the willows swayed, though there was no wind.
