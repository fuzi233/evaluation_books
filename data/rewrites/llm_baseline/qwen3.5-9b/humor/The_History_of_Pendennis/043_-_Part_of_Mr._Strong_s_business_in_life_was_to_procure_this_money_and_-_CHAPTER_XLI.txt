Mr. Strong's business in life was largely to procure funds, but for Mr. Foker, the business of the evening was to endure a social disaster. The dinner at Richmond proved to be the most dreary entertainment on which any mortal man had ever squandered a guinea.

"I wonder how the deuce I could ever have liked these people," Foker muttered to himself, staring blankly at the table. "Why, I can clearly see the crow's-feet crinkling under Rougemont's eyes, and the paint on her cheeks is applied as thick as a clown's in a pantomime! And that Calverley—his slang is absolutely disgusting. I hate chaff in a woman. And look at old Colchicum! That ancient peer arriving in his brougham, wearing his coronet, and squeezing himself bodily between Mademoiselle Coralie and her mother! It's too bad. An English peer and a horse-rider of Franconi's! It simply won't do. By Jove, it won't do. I'm not proud, but it *won't* do!"

"Twopence-halfpenny for your thoughts, Fokey!" cried Miss Rougemont, extracting a cigar from her truly vermilion lips as she spotted the young man lost in contemplation at the head of the table. Around him lay melting ices, sliced pineapples, half-empty bottles, and cigar ashes scattered over the wreckage of a dessert that held no pleasure for him.

"Does Foker ever think?" drawled Mr. Poyntz. "Here is a considerable sum offered by a fair capitalist at this end of the table for the present emanations of your valuable and acute intellect, old boy!"

"What the deuce is Poyntz talking about?" Miss Calverley asked her neighbor, wrinkling her nose. "I hate him. He's a drawling, sneering beast."

"What a droll little man is that little Fokare, my lord," Mademoiselle Coralie remarked in her native tongue, accentuating the words with the rich twang of sunny Gascony that matched her swarthy cheeks and bright black eyes. "What a droll man! He doesn't look to have twenty years."

"I wish I were of his age," sighed the venerable Lord Colchicum, inclining his purple face toward a large goblet of claret.

"C'est la jeunesse. Peuh! je m'en fiche," said Madame Brack, Coralie's mother, taking a generous pinch from Lord Colchicum's delicate gold snuffbox. "Je m'aime que les hommes faits, moi. Like Milord. Coralie! N'est-ce pas que tu n'aimes que les hommes faits, ma bichette?"

My lord grinned. "You flatter me, Madame Brack."

"Taisez-vous, Maman, vous n'êtes qu'une bête," Coralie retorted with a shrug of her robust shoulders. Upon hearing this, my lord conceded that she wasn't flattering him anyway, promptly pocketing his snuffbox before Madame Brack's dubious fingers could plunge too frequently into his Mackabaw tobacco.

There is no need to detail the animated conversation that followed during the remainder of the banquet; suffice it to say, it would not much edify the reader. Nor is it necessary to assert that all ladies of the *corps de danse* are like Miss Calverley, any more than all peers resemble the late, lamented Viscount Colchicum. Yet, history remembers instances where members of high society preferred the company of riotous youth to their own age group, offering them the precious benefits of experience and example. Even respectable men often cared less for the nature of the entertainment than for its lack of privacy.

Our friend Major Pendennis, for instance, would have made no objection to joining such a party, provided it were held *en petit comité*, excluding men like Lord Steyne and Lord Colchicum.

"Give the young men their pleasures," the worthy guardian had told Pen more than once. "I'm not one of your strait-laced moralists, but an old man of the world, begad. I know that as long as it lasts, young men will be young men." He even granted that seventy years was the proper period for sowing wild oats—for men of fashion.

That night, Mr. Foker drove his lovely guests home to Brompton in the drag. However, he remained thoroughly thoughtful and gloomy throughout the short journey from Richmond. He ignored the jokes of the friends behind him and failed to lighten the mood with his usual facetious sallies. When the ladies alighted at their doors and asked their accomplished coachman if he wouldn't step in for a drink, he declined with such a melancholy air that they suspected a falling-out with the Governor or some personal calamity. He kept his reasons hidden, leaving Mesdames Rougemont and Calverley to call out from their balconies like Jezebels, begging him to throw another party soon.

Foker sent the drag back under the care of a groom and continued on foot, hands in his pockets, plunged in deep thought. The stars and moon shone tranquilly overhead as he sentimentally regarded them, gazing upward at the house in Grosvenor Place and the windows he imagined belonged to his beloved object. He moaned and sighed with a pitiful intensity that drew the attention of Policeman X. The officer later informed Sir Francis Clavering's household, while enjoying a beer on the coach-box at a nearby pub, that there had been another chap loitering about the premises that evening—a little fellow dressed like a swell.

With the perspicacity and enterprise born of pure passion, Mr. Foker began to dodge Miss Amory through London, appearing wherever he could intercept her. If Lady Clavering attended the French play, Mr. Foker, whose linguistic skills were otherwise not conspicuous, managed to secure a stall. He discovered her engagements—perhaps thanks to Anatole, his servant, who knew a contact within Sir Francis's circle—and attended many of these parties to the surprise of everyone, including his mother. He instructed his mother to apply for cards to events he previously despised, claiming he attended to "see the world." He lied to the unsuspecting Lady Agnes that he danced to perfect his language, asserting he had learned in Paris. In reality, Anatole knew his young master slipped away privately to an academy in Brewer Street to practice for hours in the mornings.

Meanwhile, at Saint James's Church, Old Pendennis fulfilled his duty as a gentleman by patronizing public worship. One Sunday, he and Arthur walked from the park to attend a charity sermon, though the Major noted he usually skipped services due to thriftiness and other plans.

"We'll go to church across the Park," the Major suggested. "Then, begad, we'll go to the Claverings' and ask for lunch in a friendly way. Lady Clavering likes to be asked; she's uncommonly kind and monstrously hospitable."

"I met them at dinner last week at Lady Agnes Foker's, sir," Pen replied. "The Begum was very kind. She's in the country, so she's everywhere. But I share your opinion about Miss Amory—one of yours, that is, Uncle, since you were changing your tune last time we spoke."

"And what do you think of her now?" the elder asked.

"I think her the most confounded little flirt in London," Pen laughed. "She made a tremendous assault on Harry Foker, who sat next to her, giving him all the talk, though I tried to bring her down."

"Bah! Henry Foker is engaged to his cousin; the whole world knows it. Not a bad coup of Lady Rosherville's, that. Young Foker stands to inherit fourteen thousand a year from the brewery, plus properties in Logwood and Norfolk. I've no pride, Pen. I like birth certainly, but damn it, I like a brewery bringing in fourteen thousand a year. Hey, Pen? Ha, ha! That's the sort of man for me. Stick to fellows with a stake in the country, begad."

"Foker sticks to me, sir," Arthur interjected. "He's been at our chambers recently, asked me to dinner. We're almost as close as we were in our youth, and his talk is nothing but Blanche Amory from morning till night. I'm sure he's sweet on her."

"I'm sure he's engaged to his cousin, and they'll keep the bargain," the Major insisted. "Marriages in these families are matters of state. Lady Agnes was made to marry old Foker by the late Lord, despite her notorious partiality for her cousin who was killed at Albuera. Remember, sir, Lady Agnes was an exceedingly fine woman. But why didn't she marry her father's man? Well, Mr. Foker sat for Drummington until the Reform Bill and paid dearly for his seat. Depend on it, sir, that Foker Senior, a parvenu who loves a great man, has ambitious views for his son as well as himself. Your friend Harry must do as his father bids. Lord bless you! I've known hundreds of cases of love in young men and women. They kick, they resist, they make a deuce of a riot, but they end by listening to reason, begad."

"Blanche is a dangerous girl, sir," Pen admitted. "I was smitten with her myself once, very far gone, too... but that was years ago."
