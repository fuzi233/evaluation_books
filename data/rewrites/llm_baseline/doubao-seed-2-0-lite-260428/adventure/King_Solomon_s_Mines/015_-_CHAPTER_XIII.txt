No rush, no shouts, no fanfare: three enemy columns crept forward across the valley floor with the slow, inexorable weight of a landslide. When they pulled to a halt 500 yards from our horseshoe-shaped hill redoubt—our two forward points facing the royal town of Loo—the centre column anchored itself at the root of a narrow, grassy tongue of plain that jutted up towards our position, waiting for the two flanking columns to circle all the way around our rear. Their plan was simple: hit every side of our tiny defence at the exact same second.

“Oh for a Gatling gun,” groaned Good, staring out at the packed, bristling phalanxes of Kukuana warriors below. “I’d clear that whole plain in twenty minutes flat.”
“Since we haven’t got one, daydreaming won’t help,” Sir Henry replied, clapping me on the shoulder. “C’mon, Quatermain, take a shot. Bet two to one you miss that tall fellow out front who looks to be their commander. Even a whole sovereign, paid out the second we get out of this alive, that you can’t land your bullet within five yards of him.”

That stung my pride— a hunter of my reputation doesn’t get bested by a long shot wager. I loaded my express rifle with a solid lead ball, and waited. Sure enough, the commander stepped ten paces out from his ranks, only one orderly at his heels, to get a clearer look at our defences. I laid the rifle across a flat rock for stability, lined up the sight. The gun was only calibrated to hit 350 yards, so I aimed halfway down the officer’s neck to compensate for the bullet’s downward drift at 500 yards—should have landed him square in the chest. I squeezed the trigger.
When the smoke cleared, my target was completely unharmed. Three paces to his left, his orderly lay flat on his back, stone dead.
The commander yelped in alarm and sprinted back for his lines. “Bravo, Quatermain!” Good cackled. “You scared the wits out of him.”

I was furious. A man who makes his living with a rifle hates to miss in public, hates to look like a fool in front of the others. Without thinking, I snapped the rifle up, tracked the running commander, and fired the second barrel.
He threw his hands straight up in the air and pitched forward face-first into the grass. No mistake that time. I’m not proud to say it— but for a second, all I felt was stupid, gleeful relief my marksmanship reputation was intact.
Our allied Kukuana troops cheered so loud the hills echoed. They thought the two shots were white man’s magic, a good omen for the fight. The dead commander’s whole regiment reeled back in chaos, shocked their leader had dropped dead from half a mile away. Sir Henry and Good opened fire with their Winchesters, mowing down the packed ranks, and I got off another two shots before the enemy advance got close enough we could no longer see individual targets through the dust. We counted at least six or eight men knocked out of the fight before they vanished into the cloud of advancing spears.

Then a deep, thunderous roar burst from our far right. An identical roar answered it from our left. The flanking columns had engaged.
The mass of warriors in front of us spread out, broke into a slow trot up the grass slope, and sang a low, guttural war song as they came. We fired steady, picking them off as they climbed, Ignosi taking potshots alongside us when he could spare the time. But we might as well have been throwing pebbles at an incoming tidal wave. The sheer weight of thousands of armed men kept coming, shouting, spears clashing. They overran the small picket line we’d hidden in the rocks at the foot of the hill, and slowed a little after that, climbing steep to save their breath. Our first defence line was halfway down the slope, our second 50 yards further back, our third strung tight along the plateau edge.
They stormed up screaming their war cry: *“Twala! Twala! Chiele! Chiele!”* — Twala! Smite! Smite!
Our men yelled right back: *“Ignosi! Ignosi! Chiele! Chiele!”*
Throwing knives flashed back and forth across no man’s land, then the lines crashed together. Warriors fell fast, like leaves in a gale. Within minutes, the sheer press of numbers pushed our first line back until it merged with the second. The second line held fiercely for ten minutes more, but then they too were forced uphill, until 20 minutes after the fight began, our third and final line of defence was fully engaged.
The attackers were winded now, though. They’d lost hundreds of men dead and wounded on the climb, and they couldn’t break that wall of sharp spear points strung along the plateau edge. The battle swayed back and forth, ebbing and flowing, the outcome anyone’s guess. Sir Henry watched for a second, eyes blazing, then said not a single word. He hefted his battle axe and charged straight into the thickest part of the fight, Good hot on his heels.
The allied warriors spotted his giant frame cutting a swathe through the enemy, and a roar went up: *“Nanzia Incubu! Nanzia Unkungunklovo!”* — The Elephant! The White Giant! Smite!
From that second the fight was no longer in doubt. Inch by inch, the attacking force was cut down and pushed back down the slope, until they broke and retreated in disarray to their reserves.
A messenger came sprinting up moments later, gasping that the left enemy column had been completely repulsed. I was just starting to let myself relax, think the fighting was done for the day, when my stomach dropped. The small unit holding our far right flank was being chased straight towards us across the plain, thousands of Twala’s warriors pouring after them—they’d broken through there completely.

Ignosi saw it all in a glance, and barked a sharp order. The elite Greys reserve regiment spread out in a line in front of us. Another order snapped out, repeated down the line by every captain, and before I knew what was happening, I was swept up in a full-tilt charge straight at the oncoming enemy. I huddled as far behind Ignosi’s broad, muscled frame as I could, hobbling along behind him, fully expecting to get skewered at any second. We plowed straight through our own retreating men, who immediately snapped back into formation behind us, all I could hear was the deafening, rolling thunder of thousands of shields clashing together. Then a huge, wild warrior with eyes bulging out of his skull came sprinting straight at me, his spear dripping fresh blood.
I didn’t freeze. I threw myself flat on the ground right at his feet. He couldn’t stop his momentum, tripped right over me and went headfirst into the dirt. Before he could scramble back up, I was on my feet, my revolver pressed to his back, and the fight was over.
Then something heavy whacked me across the side of the head, and I blacked out.

I woke up back on the plateau koppie, Good leaning over me, dribbling water from a gourd onto my face. “You alright, old man?” he asked, pale with relief.
I shook myself off and stood up. “Fine. Just a crack on the head knocked me silly. How’s it all ended?”
“Every attack repulsed,” he said, pointing down the slope. “But the casualties are horrific. We’ve got two thousand of our men dead or wounded, and they’ve lost closer to three. Look at that.”
Long lines of stretcher bearers filed past, four men to a flat hide tray the Kukuana army carried for exactly this purpose. On every tray lay a wounded man. The ten regimental medicine men hurried between them, checking every wound. Any man they could patch up was carried off to get whatever care they could manage. But any man with a fatal gut tear from those massive, broad Kukuana spears? The medics slit an artery fast, clean, no pain, and the man was dead in less than a minute. It was a brutal, merciful thing, but ghastly to watch. Worse than the day I saw Swazi troops bury their hopelessly wounded alive after a battle, years before.

We hurried around the far side of the koppie, away from the gruesome triage site, and found Sir Henry—still clutching his dented battle axe—huddled in urgent consultation with Ignosi, Infadoos, and the rest of the chiefs.
“Thank God you’re up, Quatermain,” Sir Henry said. “Twala’s got fresh troops pouring in from the town right now. He’s not going to risk another head-on attack. He’s going to besiege us, starve us out.”
“Worse,” Infadoos put in, grave. “The spring on this hill is drying up. By midnight every one of us will be dying of thirst. We have three choices. Sit here and waste away like a lion trapped in a den. Fight our way north through their lines. Or”—he pointed at the massive enemy camp spread across the plain—“charge straight at Twala’s throat. Incubu says charge, and he’s right—he fought today like a buffalo in a net, cutting men down like hail through corn, I saw it with my own eyes. What do you say, Macumazahn, the wily old fox who never misses a thing? What’s our move?”
Ignosi, standing beside him, was clad head to toe in full war regalia, looking every inch the king he was. “You speak, my father,” he said to me. “I am still a child in wisdom beside you.”
After a quick huddle with Good and Sir Henry, I told them my call: wait was death, retreat was suicide. We attack Twala right now, before our men’s wounds stiffen, before the sight of Twala’s thousands of fresh troops makes our captains lose heart and defect.

Ignosi nodded, and the full plan came out, brilliant in its boldness.
The sun was already dipping west. He would send Infadoos and Sir Henry, with the Greys regiment, down that narrow grassy tongue of plain sticking out towards the enemy. Twala would send every regiment he had to crush that small, exposed force. But the tongue was so narrow, only one enemy regiment could attack at a time. The Greys would hold them off, chew through them one by one, and every last man in Twala’s army would stare at that fight, no eyes on the flanks.
While they were all distracted, 6000 of our men would creep along the right curve of the horseshoe hill to hit Twala’s left flank. Another 6000 would creep along the left curve to hit his right flank. Good would lead the right horn, his sharp “transparent eye” the Kukuanas admired there to steady the men. When both flanks were in position, Ignosi would lead the final reserve charge straight at Twala’s command tent. We would crush him before sundown, and sit in the palace at Loo before the moon rose.
No one hesitated. The Kukuana military machine moved faster than any force I’d ever seen. In a little over an hour, every man had eaten his rations, the regiments were in their positions, only a small guard left to watch over the wounded.
Good came over to shake our hands. “Off to the right wing,” he said, grinning a little tight. “Figured I’d say goodbye, just in case we don’t meet again.”
We shook hands in silence, no overblown speeches, all of us too English to gush about our feelings. “I don’t expect to see tomorrow’s sun,” Sir Henry rumbled, his deep voice shaking just a little. “The Greys are almost certainly going to get wiped out to buy the flanks time. But it’s a man’s death. Good luck, Quatermain. If you make it out, go get those diamonds. But take my advice: never go chasing any more pretenders, or lost mines, or fool’s errands ever again.”
Good squeezed our hands, and vanished towards the right flank. Infadoos clapped Sir Henry on the back and led him down the slope to the front ranks of the Greys. And I, with a heavy, knotty feeling in my stomach, followed Ignosi to my post in the second attacking regiment, waiting for the signal that would decide if any of us survived the day.
