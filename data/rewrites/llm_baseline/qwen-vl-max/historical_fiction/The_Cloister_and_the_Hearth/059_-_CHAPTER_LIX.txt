Happy the man who has two chain-cables: Merit, and Women. Oh, that I, like Gerard, had a 'chaine des dames' to pull up by. I would be prose laureate, or professor of the spasmodic, or something, in no time. En attendant, I will sketch the Fra Colonna.

The true revivers of ancient learning and philosophy were not scholars in the strict sense, but writers of fiction—Petrarch and Boccaccio. Their efforts did not bring them immediate fame or public acclaim; yet they planted seeds that took root in quiet soil, waiting for the sun to rise. From their time onward, Italy never lacked at least one native scholar fluent in Greek, and every learned Greek who arrived was welcomed as a brother. By the end of the fourteenth century, figures such as Poggio, Valla, and the elder Guarino had emerged. Early in the fifteenth, Florence under Cosmo de’ Medici became a haven for Platonists. Led by Gemistus Pletho, a man born in Greece, these thinkers began around 1440 to challenge Aristotle’s dominance. For few minds are broad enough to honor one great thinker without slighting another. Theodore Gaza defended Aristotle with moderation; George of Trebizond attacked him fiercely, turning his criticism toward Plato. Then Cardinal Bessarion, also a native Greek, rose in defense of Plato in his treatise *Adversus calumniatorem Platonis*. Controversy, whether wise or foolish, is a sign of life.

Born without a taste for debate in an age of fervent argument, Francesco Colonna—a young nobleman of Florence—devoted himself to the arts. At twenty, he entered the Dominican order, seeking solitude and study. He withdrew from idle chatter and political strife, from the buzz and sting of human society, to find peace with St. Dominic and the Nine Muses. An insatiable student of languages, art, sculpture, chronology, coins, and inscriptions, he grew skeptical of popular histories. He traveled extensively in the East, returning enriched with rare manuscripts and mastery of Greek, Latin, Hebrew, and Syriac.

He found his homeland transformed. Other enlightened princes had risen beyond Cosmo—Alfonso, King of Naples; Nicolas d’Este; Lionel d’Este—and above all, his old friend Thomas of Sarzana, now Pope, had given new vigor to scholarship. The Vatican library swelled to five thousand manuscripts under his patronage. He commissioned Poggio to translate Diodorus Siculus and Xenophon’s *Cyropaedia*, Valla to render Herodotus and Thucydides, Gaza to tackle Theophrastus, George of Trebizond to work on Eusebius and Plato’s dialogues. Thus, when Colonna returned, he found Plato and Aristotle in uneasy truce, while Poggio and Valla clashed over grammar and syntax, burning with philological fury.

For Colonna, this was paradise. He settled into a life of tranquil delight. Few are so fortunate as those whose talents span many fields—provided they do not need to earn their bread through them. And Fra Colonna was such a man. He spoke seven or eight languages, knew some mathematics, could write, paint, sculpt, sing, and play the lute—each skill modestly, but with genuine appreciation. His greatest gift was his ability to admire excellence in others. Such a mind deserves happiness—and he had it.

Yet wisdom often shuts out at one door. Among your acquaintances, how many have intellects deaf, blind, or dead to some great art or science? And those who are both foolish and proud parade their ignorance instead of hiding it. A passionate lover of art, Colonna remained a skeptic in faith. In every age, there are men who carry the beliefs of another—past or future. Simple-minded, his doubt was as innocent as his enthusiasm. He viewed religious rituals not with reverence, but as the ancients once saw pagan rites—mummers’ plays for the masses. Yet his mind drew a curious line: any Christian ceremony he traced back to pagan roots, he respected for its antiquity. Had he lived among the pagans, he might have mocked the same rites from his philosophical perch.

Fra Colonna admired Gerard deeply. With access to half the palaces in Rome, he praised the young artist so widely that soon nobles sought to hire him away. He told Gerard of princely offers. “But I am so happy with you, father,” Gerard protested. “Fiddlestick about being happy with me,” replied the friar. “You must not be happy—you must be a man of the world. That’s the lesson I teach the young. These Montesini can pay you three times what I can—and they shall, by Jupiter.” The friar set a high price on Gerard’s talent, which was accepted without hesitation. In those days, copying manuscripts fetched far more than authorship ever would under the printing press. Gerard earned three hundred crowns for transcribing Aristotle’s *Rhetoric*.

The powerful showered affection on their favorites—while the passion lasted. Amid the frenzy for Greek manuscripts, Gerard became a cherished pet. Nobles of both sexes fawned over him like a lapdog. It might have turned a vain man’s head, but the cautious Dutchman saw the steel beneath the velvet glove and kept his distance. Still, it was a proud day when he sat beside Fra Colonna at the table of Cardinal Bessarion.

They were near the bottom of the long table, but it mattered little—they were there. Gerard marveled at the roast pheasants served whole, feathers intact, as if freshly plucked from the woods. Chickens cooked in bottles, tender as peaches. But the true wonder was the napkins—exquisitely fine, folded into cocked hats, birds’ wings, fans—instead of lying flat. This astonished Gerard, though modern readers may have seen such things without falling backward in their chairs.

After dinner, the tables were removed, revealing another beneath, laden with sweets. The ladies rushed forward and devoured them; the men watched with wary eyes. “But, dear father,” Gerard said, “I see no bifurcal daggers—the ones they say his excellency arms the left hand with.” “Nay,” replied Colonna, “that is the invention of Cardinal Orsini—a silly tool for guests to fumble with. One man, in haste, skewered his tongue to his palate with it, I hear. O tempora, O mores! The ancients, reclining like gods at their feasts, would have scorned such absurdities.”

Once the women had finished their sugar-plums, the tables vanished again. Guests sat in a row against the wall. Two ecclesiastics entered, bowing low, each carrying a lute. They knelt before the cardinal and sang the day’s service. Then, with deep reverence, they bowed and retreated. The cardinal touched his skullcap—just as our late Iron Duke touched his hat. The company dispersed. Gerard had dined with a cardinal and one who had thrice come close to becoming pope.

But greater honor awaited. One day, the cardinal summoned him, praised his work, and took him in his coach to the Vatican. Up a private stairway led to a luxurious chamber with a grand oriel window. Inkstands, sloping writing frames, and tools of art adorned the room. The cardinal whispered to a courtier, and soon the Pope’s private secretary arrived bearing a grimy, ancient manuscript of Plutarch’s *Lives*. Soon, Gerard was alone, copying it, awestruck—yet half-joyful at the thought that the Holy Father himself would handle and read his work.

The papal inkstands gleamed outside, but within, the ink was foul. Gerard always carried his own—homemade, in a dirty little horn. He knelt and prayed for steady hands, then began.

One side of the room was dominated by a heavy curtain, split down the middle but overlapping at the edges. After a while, Gerard felt drawn to peek through it. He resisted. Then the impulse returned. Overcome, he left Plutarch, crept across the matted floor, gathered the folds with his fingers, and pressed his nose through the gap—only to meet the cold steel of a halberd. Two soldiers, fully armed, stood guard, their weapons crossed in a triangle.

Gerard pulled back swiftly—but in that moment, he heard soft voices and saw a group of people bowing before a hidden figure. He never tried again to look through the guarded curtain, though he often glanced at it. Every hour or so, an ecclesiastic would enter, glance at him, make him feel chilled, and leave.

It was gloomy, mechanical. But the next day, a richly armored gentleman burst in, glaring. “What is going on here?” he demanded. Gerard explained he was copying Plutarch, aided by the saints. The nobleman said he didn’t know the man in question. Gerard gently clarified the circumstances—time and space having deprived the ancient Greek of conversation with the modern lord. “Oh!” said the noble, “one of those dead Greeks they fuss so much over.” “Yes, signor,” replied Gerard, “one of them—who, though dead, still lives.” “I don’t understand,” said the noble, with all the dignity of ignorance. “What did he write? Love stories?” His eyes sparkled. “Merry tales, like Boccaccio?” “No, signor,” answered Gerard, “lives of heroes and sages.” “Soldiers and popes?” “Soldiers and princes.” “Will you read me one some day?” “With pleasure, signor. But what would my employers say if I stopped work?” “Never mind them,” said the noble. “Do you not know who I am? I am Jacques Bonaventura, nephew to His Holiness the Pope, and captain of his guards. I came to check on my men—I suspect they’ve been moved out for you.”

Bonaventura hurried off. But he soon returned, drawn to the quiet artist. Finding Gerard good company, he began visiting regularly, chattering idly while Gerard wrote the immortal lives.

One day, he arrived changed—moody, restless. He threw himself into a chair and muttered, “Ah, traitress! traitress!” Gerard asked what ailed him. “Traitress! traitress!” was the only reply. So Gerard resumed his work.

Then Bonaventura said, “I am melancholy. For Our Lady’s sake, read me a story from Ser Plutarcho—something to soothe my bile. Is there nothing in that Greek about lovers betrayed?”

Gerard read him the life of Alexander. The nobleman grew excited, pacing the room, embracing the reader, and swearing to shun “soft delights”—that bed of nettles—and follow glory.

Who could be happier than Gerard? His art was honored, his fees fabulous. In a year or two, he would sail home to Holland, wealthy, and settle with Margaret in Bruges, Antwerp, or beloved Augsburg—living in peace, love, and honest labor. His heart never strayed from her. In prosperity, he did not forget poor Pietro. He brought Fra Colonna to see the painting. The friar examined it closely, then embraced the artist and carried the picture to a member of the Colonna family, who paid a generous sum. Pietro descended to the first floor and lived like a gentleman. But Gerard stayed in his garret. To increase his expenses would have delayed his return to Margaret. Luxury held no appeal for the single-hearted man when love stood before him.

Jacques Bonaventura introduced Gerard to other young nobles. They liked him and tried to lure him into vice and extravagance. But Gerard humbly begged to be excused. He escaped that temptation. But a greater one lay ahead.
