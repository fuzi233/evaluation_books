Let those who possess a genuine, heartfelt love for London society and the exclusive privileges of its highest circles admit that Major Pendennis was a man of extraordinary generosity and deep affection in the sacrifice he was now making. In May, he abandoned the capital—its newspapers, its morning routines, its endless circuit of clubs, his private calls upon "my Ladies," his gallops along Rotten Row, his dinners, his box at the Opera, his frantic weekend dashes to Fulham and Richmond, his bows to the Duke and the Marquis at grand entertainments, and even his name in the *Morning Post* the following day. He surrendered his quiet, select, and delightful festivals to lock himself inside a lonely country house. Here, he would reside with a simple widow, a greenhorn of a son, a sentimental curate, and a little girl of ten.

He made this magnificent sacrifice, and it was all the more impressive because few realized its magnitude. His letters arrived franked from town, and he presented the invitations to Helen with a visible sigh. It was a scene of beautiful tragedy to watch him decline party after party—at least for those who understood, as Helen did not, the melancholy grandeur of his self-denial. Helen merely smiled at the awful pathos with which the Major spoke of the *Court Guide*; young Pen, however, regarded the great names on the envelopes with profound respect. He listened with constant interest and sympathy to his uncle's stories of the fashionable world.

The elder Pendennis's memory was a treasure trove of thousands of such delightful tales, which he poured into Pen's eager ears with unfailing eloquence. He knew the name and pedigree of everyone in the Peerage and their intricate relationships.

"My dear boy," he would say, with mournful earnestness and absolute truth, "you cannot begin your genealogical studies too early. I wish to Heaven you would read *Debrett* every single day. Do not focus on the historical parts (for, between ourselves, many pedigrees are purely fabulous, and few families can boast such a clear descent as our own); instead, study the accounts of family alliances and connections. I have known a man's entire career blasted by ignorance on this vital subject. Just last month, at dinner at Lord Hobanob's, a newly admitted young man, Mr. Suckling (author of a certain work), began to joke lightly about Admiral Bowser's treachery to Ministers in the most audacious manner imaginable. But who do you think sat next to him? Why, it was Lady Grampound Bowser's daughter, and opposite him sat Lord Grampound Bowser's son-in-law. The infatuated youth went on cutting jokes at the Admiral's expense, fancying the whole world laughed with him. Imagine Lady Hobanob's feelings—Hobanob's!—and the sentiments of every well-bred man as this wretch exposed himself. He will never dine in South Street again. I promise you that."

With such discourses, the Major entertained his nephew while pacing the terrace during his two-hour constitutional or sitting together after dinner over wine. He grieved that Sir Francis Clavering had not moved to the park to create a local society, and he mourned that Lord Eyrie was absent, preventing an introduction.

"He has daughters," the Major noted. "Who knows? You might have married Lady Emily or Lady Barbara Trehawk. But alas, those dreams are over; my poor fellow, you must lie on the bed you have made for yourself."

These words inclined young Pendennis seriously, though they lacked the charm of oral delivery. Yet, the Major's anecdotes of Great George, the Royal Dukes, and the statesmen and beauties of the day filled Pen's soul with longing and wonder. While these conversations sadly bored and perplexed poor Mrs. Pendennis, they were anything but tedious for Pen.

One cannot claim that Mr. Pen's new guide, philosopher, and friend discoursed on the most elevated subjects, nor did he treat them with high-minded gravity. However, his morality was consistent. It may not have aided a man's progress in the next world, but it was excellently calculated to advance his interests in this one. Furthermore, the Major never doubted for an instant that his views were the only practical ones and that his conduct was perfectly virtuous and respectable. He was, in a word, a man of honor with his eyes wide open. Taking pity on this young greenhorn nephew, he sought to open his eyes as well.

No man attended church more regularly in the country than the old bachelor.

"It doesn't matter so much in town, Pen," he explained. "There, the women go, and the men aren't missed. But when a gentleman is *sur ses terres*, he must set an example for the country folk. If I could turn a tune, I'd even consider singing. The Duke of Saint David's, whom I have the honor of knowing, always sings in the country, and let me tell you, it has a damned fine effect from the family pew. And you are someone down here. As long as the Claverings are away, you are the first man in the parish, and as good as any. You might even represent the town if you played your cards right. Your poor dear father would have done so had he lived; so might you. Though, not if you marry an amiable lady whom the country people wouldn't recognize. Well, well... it's a painful subject. Let us change it, my boy."

Yet, if the Major changed the subject once, he returned to it a dozen times that day. The moral of his discourse was invariably that Pen was throwing himself away.

Now, it requires little coaxing to make a simple boy believe he is a fine fellow. Pen eagerly accepted his uncle's counsel. The conversation of Captain Costigan had become unpleasant to him, and the image of that tipsy, unshaven, punch-reeked father-in-law haunted him with terror. He could not bring that man to associate with his mother. Even regarding Emily, he faltered when the pitiless guardian questioned him.

"Was she accomplished?"
He was forced to admit, no.
"Was she clever?"
"Well, she possessed a very good average intellect," he stammered, unable to affirm she was truly clever.
"Come, let us see some of her letters."

Pen confessed he possessed only three—the trivial invitations and replies we have already mentioned.
"She is cautious enough," the Major said dryly. "She is older than you, my poor boy." Then, with utmost frankness and humility, he apologized, flinging himself upon Pen's good feelings and begging the lad to forgive a fond old uncle who acted solely for his family's honor. For Arthur was ready to flare with indignation whenever Miss Costigan's honesty was doubted, swearing he would never have her name mentioned lightly and would never, under any circumstances, part from her.

Arthur repeated this defense to his uncle and friends at home, and, it must be confessed, to Miss Fotheringay and the amiable family at Chatteris, where he still spent some time. When Miss Emily heard news of the guardian's arrival, she correctly assumed he came with hostile intentions toward herself.

"I suppose you intend to leave me now that your grand relation has come down from town. He'll carry you off, and you'll forget your poor Emily, Mr. Arthur!"

Forget her? In the presence of Miss Rouncy, the Columbine and Milly's confidential friend, and even the Captain himself, Pen swore he could never think of any woman but his beloved Miss Fotheringay. The Captain, looking up at the foils hanging as trophies on the wall of the room where they used to fence, grimly stated he would advise no man to rashly meddle with the affections of his darling child. He vowed he would never believe his gallant young Arthur—who he treated as a son and called his son—would ever commit conduct so revolting to every idea of honor and humanity. He rose to embrace Pen, crying and wiping his eye with one large, dirty hand while clasping the boy with the other. Arthur shuddered in that grip, thinking of his uncle at home. His father-in-law looked unusually dirty and shabby; the odor of whisky-and-water was even more pronounced than usual. How was he to reconcile that man with his mother? He trembled recalling that he had written to Costigan, enclosing a sovereign (a loan the worthy gentleman needed), stating that one day he hoped to sign himself "your affectionate son, Arthur Pendennis."

Gladdened to escape Chatteris that day—from Miss Rouncy the confidante, the old toping father-in-law, and the divine Emily herself—Arthur cried inwardly as he rattled homeward on Rebecca: "Oh, Emily, Emily, you little know what sacrifices I am making for you!—for you who are always so cold, so cautious, so mistrustful!" He thought of a character in Pope to whom he had often involuntarily compared her.

Pen never rode over to Chatteris for a specific errand without the Major discovering it. Faithful to his plan, the Major offered no direct hindrance; yet, the constant feeling that the senior's eye was upon him, accompanied by an uneasy shame anticipating the inevitable confession of the evening's conversation, caused Pen to visit his charmer less frequently than before. There was no use deceiving him; no pretext of dining with Smirke or reading Greek plays with Foker sufficed. Upon returning from a flying visit, Pen felt that everyone knew whence he came, appearing quite guilty before his mother and guardian while they studied books or played picquet.
