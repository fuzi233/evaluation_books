After my mistress had left the room, I found myself with time to reflect on Sergeant Cuff. There he sat, tucked away in a quiet corner of the hall, his face twisted into a grimace as he stared at his notebook. “Still taking notes on the case?” I asked.

“No,” he replied, not looking up. “Checking what my next assignment is.”

“Oh!” I said, surprised. “So you think it’s all over here?”

“I think,” he answered calmly, “that Lady Verinder is one of the most intelligent women in England. And I also think a rose is far more worth looking at than a diamond. Where’s the gardener, Mr. Betteredge?”

That was all he would say about the Moonstone. He’d lost interest in the whole affair—had turned his attention entirely to the dog-rose dispute. An hour later, I heard them arguing fiercely in the conservatory, the very same flower once again at the heart of their quarrel.

Meanwhile, my duty was to find out whether Mr. Franklin still meant to leave by the afternoon train. After learning of the conversation in my lady’s room and how it ended, he decided to wait for news from Frizinghall. This change—entirely natural, and likely to mean nothing to most people—had an unfortunate effect on him. It left him restless, with idle hours on his hands, and caused every hidden side of his character to emerge, one after another, like rats from a hole.

Now he was an Italian-Englishman, now a German-Englishman, now a French-Englishman—drifting from room to room, talking only about Miss Rachel’s treatment of him, and addressing only me.

I found him, for instance, in the library, sitting beneath the map of Modern Italy, utterly absorbed in his own troubles. “I have many noble aspirations, Betteredge,” he said, “but what can I do with them now? I’m full of good qualities waiting to be awakened—if only Rachel had helped me bring them out!”

He spoke so passionately about his unappreciated virtues and lamented so deeply that I didn’t know how to comfort him—until it struck me: this was a moment for *Robinson Crusoe*. I hobbled to my room, returned with the book, and found no one in the library. The map of Italy stared back at me, and I stared back at it.

I checked the drawing-room. His handkerchief lay on the floor—proof he’d been there. But the room was empty—proof he’d moved on. In the dining-room, Samuel sat quietly with a biscuit and a glass of sherry, staring into space. A minute earlier, Mr. Franklin had rung furiously for refreshment. By the time Samuel brought it, Franklin had vanished before the bell downstairs had even finished ringing.

I tried the morning-room—and finally found him. He stood at the window, drawing strange symbols in the damp on the glass with his finger.

“Your sherry is waiting, sir,” I said.

He didn’t respond. He was lost in thought, unreachable. “How do *you* explain Rachel’s conduct, Betteredge?” he muttered, without turning.

Not having a ready answer, I held up *Robinson Crusoe*, convinced some wisdom could be found in its pages if we searched long enough.

Mr. Franklin closed the book sharply and launched into his German-English rant. “Why not look into it?” he demanded, as if I’d personally objected. “Why lose your patience when patience is all that’s needed to uncover the truth? Don’t interrupt me. Rachel’s behavior is perfectly clear—if you’ll just give her the justice of first seeing the Objective view, then the Subjective, and finally the Objective-Subjective perspective. What do we know? We know she was thrown into a state of nervous agitation when the Moonstone was lost on Thursday morning—a condition she hasn’t recovered from yet. Do you deny that? Very well. Now, being in such a state, how could we expect her to act normally toward anyone around her? That’s the Subjective view. I defy you to argue against it. Then what follows? Naturally, the Objective-Subjective explanation: Rachel is not truly Rachel—she’s Someone Else. Do I mind being treated cruelly by Someone Else? You’re unreasonable, Betteredge—but you can’t accuse me of that. So how does it end? It ends, despite your stubborn English narrowness, with me being perfectly content. Where’s the sherry?”

By now, my head felt so foggy I couldn’t tell whose thoughts were whose. In that dazed state, I managed three sensible things: I fetched him his sherry, retreated to my room, and smoked the most soothing pipe of tobacco I’d ever known.

But I wasn’t free of him yet. Drifting from the morning-room to the hall, he made his way to the offices, smelled my pipe, and remembered—too late—that he’d given up smoking for Miss Rachel’s sake. In an instant, he burst in on me with his cigar-case, launching into his usual argument, but now in his sharp, witty, French manner.

“Give me a light, Betteredge. Can you imagine a man who’s smoked as long as I have not realizing there’s a complete philosophy of women hidden in his cigar-case? Listen closely—I’ll prove it in two words. You pick a cigar, try it, and it fails. What do you do? You throw it away and try another. Now apply it! You choose a woman, try her, and she breaks your heart. Fool! Learn from your cigar-case. Throw her away and try another!”

I shook my head. Clever, yes—but experience told me otherwise. “In the days of the late Mrs. Betteredge,” I said, “I often felt tempted to follow your logic, Mr. Franklin. But the law says you must finish what you’ve started. Once you’ve chosen a cigar, you smoke it.” I added a wink for emphasis.

Mr. Franklin laughed uproariously, and we were both in high spirits—until another facet of his personality surfaced.

And so it went between us—while Sergeant Cuff and the gardener continued their endless battle over roses.

The pony-chaise returned half an hour earlier than expected. My lady had decided to stay with her sister for the time being. The groom delivered two letters—one for Mr. Franklin, one for me.

I sent Franklin’s letter to him in the library, where he’d drifted back. I read mine in private. A cheque slipped out as I opened it, revealing before I’d even read the contents that Sergeant Cuff had been officially dismissed from the investigation.

I sent word to the conservatory that I wished to speak with him immediately.

He arrived, still furious over the gardener and the dog-rose, declaring that no man alive—past or future—could match Mr. Begbie for stubbornness.

“Please,” I said, “set aside this trivial matter. I need your full attention for something serious.”

He glanced at the letter in my hand. “Ah,” he sighed, “you’ve heard from her ladyship. Have I anything to do with it, Mr. Betteredge?”

“You shall judge for yourself,” I replied.

I read aloud the letter, carefully and clearly:

“My dear Gabriel,

I request that you be informed that I have fulfilled my promise to you. As regards Rosanna Spearman, Miss Verinder solemnly declares she has spoken to her privately since the woman entered my household. They never met, even by accident, on the night the Diamond was stolen. No communication of any kind occurred between them from the moment the alarm was raised on Thursday until this Saturday afternoon, when Miss Verinder departed.

After informing my daughter abruptly and plainly of Rosanna’s suicide—this is the result.

Having approached Miss Verinder as the officer deemed most effective, I then addressed her in the way I believed would move her most. On two separate occasions before she left, I warned her privately that she was placing herself under suspicion of the most unbearable and degrading kind. I have now told her plainly that my fears have come true.

Her reply, on her solemn oath, is as clear as words can be. First, she owes no money to any living person. Second, the Diamond has never been in her possession since she placed it in her cabinet on Wednesday night.

The trust she places in me ends there. When I ask if she can explain the disappearance, she remains silent. When I appeal to her to speak for my sake, she refuses, weeping. ‘The day will come when you will understand why I care so little about being suspected, and why I remain silent even to you. I have done much to make my mother pity me—nothing to make her blush.’

After all that has passed between you and me, I believe—though a stranger—you should know what Miss Verinder has said. Read my letter to him, and hand him the enclosed cheque. In resigning my claim on his services, I only wish to say that I am convinced of his honesty and intelligence. Yet I am more certain than ever that the circumstances have fatally misled him.”

I paused. “What do you make of that, Sergeant?”

“I should only offend you if I voiced my opinion,” he said. “Continue, Mr. Betteredge.”

At that moment, I nearly responded with something sharper than polite—especially after hearing him complain about the gardener’s obstinacy. But I held back. I resumed reading.

“That’s all,” I said, handing him the cheque.

“Do you believe that part of her ladyship’s letter?” I asked, unable to hide my irritation.

He looked at the cheque, raised his eyebrows in mock admiration. “Such a generous estimate of my time,” he said, “that I feel obliged to remember it. I’ll keep this amount in mind when the occasion arises.”

“What do you mean?”

“Her ladyship has handled matters neatly,” he said. “But this family scandal is the kind that resurfaces when least expected. We’ll have more detective work on our hands before the Moonstone is many months old.”

If those words meant anything, and if his tone meant anything—it meant this: to him, Miss Rachel had hardened herself against reason, lied to her own mother, and concealed her guilt under a veil of false innocence.

I could not let that stand. “Sergeant Cuff,” I said firmly, “I consider that last remark an insult to my lady and her daughter!”

“Mr. Betteredge,” he replied coolly, “consider it a warning to yourself—and you’ll be closer to the truth.”

Even in my anger, I was silenced by the sheer confidence in his voice. I walked to the window to regain my composure. The rain had stopped. And there, in the courtyard, stood Mr. Begbie, waiting to resume the dog-rose debate.

“My compliments to the Sairgent,” he called. “If he’s minded to walk to the station, I’m agreeable to go with him.”

“What!” exclaimed the Sergeant behind me. “Are you not convinced yet?”

“The de’il a bit I’m convinced!” Mr. Begbie shot back.

“Then I’ll walk to the station!” declared the Sergeant.

“Then I’ll meet you at the gate!” countered Mr. Begbie.

My anger flared—but what could I do against such relentless interruption?

Sergeant Cuff noticed my change in mood and offered a timely suggestion. “Come now,” he said, “why not treat my view of the case as her ladyship treats it? Why not say, the circumstances have fatally misled me?”

To accept her ladyship’s judgment was a privilege—even if offered by the Sergeant himself. I calmed down slowly, adopting a lofty contempt for any opinion of Miss Rachel other than my own or my mistress’s.

Yet I could not stop thinking about the Moonstone. Common sense told me to let it rest—but alas, the virtues of modern times weren’t invented in my era. Sergeant Cuff had struck a nerve, and though I looked down on him, the wound still throbbed.

In the end, I could not resist. I drew him back to the subject.

“I’m satisfied myself,” I said. “But never mind that. Go on—as if I were still open to persuasion. You think Miss Rachel cannot be trusted. You say we’ll hear of the Moonstone again. Back your opinion, Sergeant,” I concluded lightly. “Back your opinion.”

Instead of taking offense, he seized my hand and shook it so hard my fingers ached. “I swear to heaven,” he said solemnly, “if I had the chance to serve alongside you, I’d take domestic work tomorrow. To say you’re as transparent as a child is to flatter children who don’t deserve it. There—no more arguing. Let me tell you, for your sake, three things that will happen—and which, I believe, you’ll be forced to notice, whether you like it or not.”

“Go on,” I said, unabashed.

“First,” he began, “you will hear from the Yollands—when the postman delivers Rosanna’s letter to Cobb’s Hole on Monday.”

A bucket of cold water couldn’t have shocked me more. Miss Rachel’s claim of innocence left Rosanna’s actions—the new nightgown, the hidden smeared one—completely unexplained. And I hadn only just realized it, thanks to him.

“Second,” he continued, “you will hear of the three Indians again. If Miss Rachel stays in the area, you’ll hear of them nearby. If she goes to London, you’ll hear of them there.”

I shrugged. I’d already dismissed the jugglers and believed Rachel innocent—so I took this prediction easily.

“So much for two,” I said. “Now for the third.”

“Third,” he said, “you will, sooner or later, hear of that money-lender in London whom I’ve mentioned twice before. Give me your pocket-book. I’ll write down his name and address—so there’s no mistake.”

He scribbled: *Mr. Septimus Luker, Middlesex-place, Lambeth, London.*

“There,” he said, pointing. “Those are the last words I’ll say on the Moonstone—for now. Time will show whether I’m right or wrong. Meanwhile, I carry away a sincere liking for you—one I believe honors both of us. If we don’t meet again before my retirement, I hope you’ll visit me in a small house near London I’ve set my eye on. There will be grass walks, Mr. Betteredge, I promise you—in *my* garden. And as for the white moss rose—”

“The de’il a bit ye’ll get the white moss rose to grow unless ye bud him on the dogue-rose first!” shouted a voice at the window.

We turned. Mr. Begbie stood there, too eager to wait.

The Sergeant grabbed my hand and dashed outside, fire in his eyes.

“Ask him about the moss rose when he returns,” he called through the window. “See if I’ve left him a leg to stand on!”

“Gentlemen, both!” I called after them, trying once more to mediate. “There’s much to be said on both sides of the moss rose!”

But it was no use. They were off, battling over roses with no quarter asked or given. The last I saw, Begbie was shaking his head stubbornly, and the Sergeant had him by the arm like a prisoner.

Ah, well. I confess—I couldn’t help liking the Sergeant, even as I hated him. Try to understand that contradiction, if you can.

You’ll soon be rid of me and my contradictions. Once I report Mr. Franklin’s departure, the events of Saturday will be complete. And when I describe the strange occurrences of the coming week, I’ll have done my part. Then I’ll pass the pen to the next narrator.

If you’re as tired of reading this as I am of writing it—Lord, how we shall enjoy ourselves a few pages further on!
