*It was among the ways of good Queen Bess, Who ruled as well as ever mortal can, sir, When she was stogg'd, and the country in a mess, She was wont to send for a Devon man, sir.*
—West Country Ballad

The sun spilled thin gold over the silver curve of the Torridge at dawn, but Amyas Leigh was nowhere to be found. He had not fled to drown his sorrows in the river’s eddies, nor hidden in the woods to bewail some lost hope: when Frank Leigh tracked his horse’s hoof prints down the lane, it was plain the giant young man had ridden hard for Sir Richard Grenville’s estate at Stow, determined to beg a fighting commission in the queen’s Irish army. His mother had seen his purpose at a glance, and sent Frank spurring after him to bring the runaway home, or at least make him pause and weigh his choice.

Frank rode ten miles over the heather-cloaked hills, and found the sun dipping low enough that he faced another ten miles of rutted, steep track before reaching Stow, with no roadside inns to rest himself or his mount. As was the universal, unwritten hospitality of the Elizabethan West Country, he turned his horse down the winding lane that led to Clovelly Court, the honey-stone manor perched high above the Atlantic cliffs, to beg a meal and a stall for his horse from the squire, Mr. Cary.

He walked unannounced, as welcome as the tide, through the great oak door into the long, dark wainscoted hall, redolent of peat smoke and spiced cider, and the first sight that met his eyes was Amyas himself, settled at the far end of the heavy oak table, alternating between shoving great chunks of venison pasty into his mouth and tipping ale down his throat, his sorrows apparently only sharpening his ravenous appetite. Young Will Cary knelt on the bench opposite him, elbows propped on the table, leaning in to argue fiercely in a low, heated tone.

“Hullo, lad!” Amyas roared when he spotted him, pasty crumbs flying from his beard. “Come over and deliver me out of the hands of this fire-eater. I swear he’ll run me through before the sun sets if I don’t help him pick a fight with some poor soul first.”

“Ah, Mr. Frank!” Will said, scrambling to his feet, for every young gentleman across north Devon held Frank Leigh as a paragon of wit, learning, and chivalry. “You could not have turned up at a better time. I’ve been half mad wanting your advice on a dozen matters. Sit, eat, the ale’s still frothing.”

“None so early, thank you,” Frank laughed, hanging his riding cloak by the hearth.

“Ah, of course!” Amyas bellowed, tilting a full tankard to his lips and mimicking Frank’s precise, scholarly tone. “Avoid strong ale before noon, you know. It heats the blood, thickens the animal spirits, and obfuscates the cerebrum with frenetical and lymphatic idols that cloud the quintessential light of pure reason. Eh? Young Plato, young Daniel, come to sit in judgment on us sinners! Well, even if my wits are half fuddled already, I can see plain enough that you, Will Cary, are not going to fight Tom Coffin.”

“Am I not?” Will snapped, stabbing a finger at the table. “Who says that? Mr. Frank, you be the judge, just hear me out.”

“Court is in session,” Frank said, settling comfortably into a bench and helping himself to a slice of pasty. “Proceed, appellant.”

“I was telling Amyas,” Will huffed, “that Tom Coffin of Portledge has gone too far. I won’t abide it a day longer.”

“Let him be, then,” Amyas mumbled around a mouthful of meat. “Last I saw him he could stand on his own two feet well enough.”

“Plague take you, hold your tongue! What right has he to glower at me every time we pass each other on the road?”

“Depends how he glowers,” Amyas shrugged. “A cat may look at a king, long as he doesn’t mistake him for a mouse and pounce.”

“Oh I know exactly how he looks, and what he means, too! He’ll stop staring, or I’ll stop him. The other day, when I made a casual remark about Rose Salterne—”
“Ah!” Frank groaned. “The apple of discord again.”
“Never mind what I said! He burst out laughing straight in my face. Is that not provocation enough for a fair quarrel? And what’s more, I’ve learned he wrote a sonnet to her, and sent it all the way to Stow by a market woman. What right has he to scribble love verses when I have not dared to send one? That’s not fair play, Mr. Frank. I’d be a Jew, and a Spaniard, and a Papist myself if that was fair!” He slammed his fist on the table so hard the pewter plates danced.

“My good knight of the burning ire,” Frank said, grinning, “I have a proper chivalric solution, approved by every rule of knightly order. We shall send summons by tuck of drum to every unmarried gentleman under thirty, living within fifteen miles of our peerless Oriana Rose Salterne, to attend a grand tournament. We’ll let all the prentice lads fight first, with good quarterstaves, in Bideford market square, till every last head is well thwacked. Any man whose head is unbroken after that will be deemed a coward, and soundly thrashed on the back for his dishonor. After that grand melee, which will make the old Battle of Tottenham look like a trivial brawl between frogs and mice, all the young gentlemen shall adjourn to a soft, boggy field—best a bog, because no man can run away when he’s sunk up to his knees in peat—strip to their shirts, and fight with rapiers of equal length. Each man shall slay his opponent, the survivors shall fight on like gamecocks till all are dead of their love wounds, and the very last man left alive shall fall gracefully on his sword, wailing to heaven of the slaughter Rose’s bright eyes have caused. That shall end all our lovelorn squabbles. Placetne Domini? As they used to ask in the old Oxford senate.”

“Confound you and your long words, sir!” Will groaned, red-faced. “You’re mocking me.”

“Patience, Signor Cavaliere. This is no mockery, it’s deadly earnest. For why fight a single petty duel when we can settle the whole rivalry once and for all? Though,” he added, his tone softening to a quiet, serious warmth, “to tell you the truth, I would not have you fight Tom Coffin with anything longer than a bodkin. Do not mistake me, I know well what the sting of love feels like, and the far sharper sting of jealousy. But does it not seem a waste to squander that divine, fiery wrath— which Plato says is the very root of all virtue, that God gave you not to spend on your own petty quarrels, but to devote to the service of our queen, whom all wicked men fear and all good souls adore? What Devon gentleman worth the name dares call his sword his own, while Elizabeth sits on the throne? Are there no Spaniards to fight, no oppressed Irishfolk to free, that two brave lads of Devon must needs flesh their blades in each other’s hearts?”

“By heaven, you speak truth, Frank!” Amyas boomed. “I’ve long thought Christian gentlemen ought to leave love quarrels to bulls and rams.”

“And the heir of Clovelly,” Frank added, smiling, “surely can find a nobler example to follow than the stags in his own deer park.”

“You’re a great scholar, Mr. Frank,” Will said, looking properly penitent. “But gentlemen must fight sometimes, or where is their honor?”

“I speak not merely as a scholar,” Frank said, a little proud, “but as a gentleman who has drawn his blade before, and has killed his man—may God have mercy on his soul. And I count it my greatest pride that I have never fought for my own private cause, and I pray I never shall. There is no work more noble than fighting for those you love, but a Christian man ought never to raise his sword for his own petty grievance, unless refusing to do so would cost him his life or his utter honor. Even then, the good book bids us overcome evil with good, not return blow for blow.”

“And I can back that, Will,” Amyas put in. “I’m no man afraid of ghosts, but once, when I cut off the head of a French privateer who slandered the queen, I thought to myself: if that man had been slandering me instead, I’d see his grinning head on my pillow every night before I slept.”

“God forbid!” Will shuddered. “But what am I to do? I’m going to Bideford market tomorrow if it’s full of Coffins, each with a ghost in his own coffin to back him.”

“Leave it to me,” Amyas said. “I’ve got my own plan, just as clever as Frank’s. If there’s a quarrel brewing in the market tomorrow, you’ll see I step in before any blades are drawn.”

“You’re both good fellows,” Will laughed. “Let’s call for another tankard.”

“And we’ll drink to the health of Tom Coffin, and every gallant lad in north Devon,” Frank agreed. “But I’ve got business, remember: I’m here to drag this runaway giant here back home to his mother. If he won’t come quietly, she bade me tie him across my saddle.”

“Your nag had better have a very strong back, then,” Amyas grinned. “But I mean to ride on to Stow and see Sir Richard today. Jests are all well and good, but my mind is made up to go to Ireland.”

“Stop here tonight, the both of you,” Will begged. “First for the sake of good company, and then because I need your advice, our oracle, our paragon. Speak low, though—here comes my father. Hand me that letter I gave you a minute ago.”

The old squire, Cary of Clovelly, trundled in a moment later, his peregrine falcon perched on his gloved wrist, a pair of fluffy spaniels trotting at his heels. “Eh, lads? Company! Welcome, the lot of you. I wish there were more of your sort in these degenerate days. How is your good mother, Frank? Will, I’ve been round the home farm all morning. That sheeted heifer of Prowse’s is ailing, her coat standing up like a hedgehog’s. Send Jewell to bring her in before nightfall. Then I went up to the forty-acre field, flushed two coveys of partridge, took a brace out of them. That new Irish hawk is still wild as any stray haggard, never going to make a proper bird. I handed her off to Tom, took the little Clovelly peregrine—nothing beats a Clovelly hawk, anywhere! Heigh ho, I’m starving. Half past twelve, dinner not served? Master Amyas, spoiling your appetite with ale? You ought to try sack, lad, have a cup with me.”

He settled himself on the broad stone bench inside the chimney breast, set his falcon on a perch above his head, and his spaniels curled up in the warm peat ash at his feet while Will knelt to pull his boots off, fending off his father’s repeated warnings not to jostle his sore corns.

“Come, Master Amyas, a pint of white wine and sugar, with a pickled prawn or a rasher off the coals to whet your appetite before dinner.”

“Thank you, sir,” Amyas said, grinning. “I’ve drunk a hundred different odd liquors, good and bad, over the last three years at sea, and I’ve never found aught that beats plain good Devon ale. It needs no chaser, no fancy spice, it keeps honest men happy all on its own.”

“You speak sense, lad,” old Cary nodded. “All these newfangled hot wines and aqua vitaes that’ve come in since the wars do nothing but muddle men’s wits and fire their blood up to fever pitch.”

“I fear that trouble is not over,” Frank said. “My friends write from the Netherlands that our men there have taken to swilling ale as heavily as the Dutch. Heaven grant they do not bring that drunken fashion home with them.”

“They say you have to drink heavily or die of the ague in those vile swamp lands,” Amyas pointed out. “Once they’re back home in Devon, they’ll have no need for it.”

“Heaven grant it,” Frank said. “I’d hate to see Devon turn into a drunken shire, and hundreds of our lads are out there with Mr. Champernoun right now.”

“Ah, well,” old Cary said, “that proves the queen’s saying true: Devonshire is her right hand, and her young men are like arrows in the hand of a giant.”

“Arrows that are full-grown giants themselves, if you ask me,” Will laughed, nodding at Amyas. “He’ll be off doing great deeds soon, I’ll warrant.”

“That I shall!” Amyas said. “I’ve got brave deeds planned—enchanters to bind, dragons to slay, whole empires to win, though not in Holland.”

“You do?” Will asked, sharp, having half suspected Amyas was hiding a grander purpose.

“Aye,” Amyas said, dropping his jest. “I’m off to what Walter Raleigh calls the Land of the Nymphs. I hope to sail for Ireland within the month.”

“Call that home, not abroad,” old Cary said. “Ireland is full of Devon men from one end to the other, you’ll not meet a stranger anywhere. George Bourchier of Tawstock commands the army in Munster, Warham St. Leger is marshal, George Carew rides with Lord Grey of Wilton. Poor Peter Carew was killed at Glendalough last year, after that villain Desmond ambushed and butchered Herbert and Price’s men. They reinforced the companies with six hundred Devon lads under Arthur Fortescue, so our county holds her head high in Ireland just as she does in the Low Countries and the Spanish Main.”

“Is Davils of Marsland there, too?” Amyas asked. “The old man who taught me to catch trout when I stayed at Stow? He was a rare good soldier.”

“Ah, lad,” old Cary said, his face falling. “That is a sad tale. I thought all England had heard it.”

“Surely he is not dead?” Amyas said, shocked.

“Murdered foully, like a dog! By the man he treated as his own son, the false knave who used to call him father. Last July, that Jesuit fox Saunders landed there, posing as the Pope’s legate, with bags of gold, papal bulls, and a blessed banner, stirring up rebellion. With him was James Fitzmaurice, the rascal who knelt in the church at Kilmallock and swore on every saint in the calendar to be loyal to Queen Elizabeth, so loud that Perrott said he had to stop his own ears. He went to the King of France first, got nothing but mockery, then went to the King of Spain, promising to hand all of Ireland over to him and restore Popery. The King of Spain fitted him out with ships and men, just like that other rogue Stukely—though I will say, for the honor of Devon, if Stukely lived like a fool, he died like an honest man.”

“Sir Thomas Stukely is dead too?” Amyas gasped.

“Wait, lad, I’ll tell you that tale after. Fitzmaurice and the Jesuits landed at Smerwick with three ships, built a little fort, blessed it with their holy water to purge it of heretic taint. But a Courtenay of Haccombe, anchored nearby with his king’s warship, took their three ships in a midnight raid, cut them off from the sea. John and James Desmond brought their ragged rabble over to join the Spaniards. Earl Desmond would not fight for them, but he would not fight against them, waiting to side with whichever side won. So poor Davils was sent by the Lord Deputy to order the Desmond brothers, in the queen’s name, to attack the small Spanish force. Folks said it was rash, but Davils was the boldest, shrewdest soldier alive. The young Desmond boys used to call him father, he was so kind to them. But he learned the danger of trusting Englishmen who’d gone native Irish. The Desmonds suddenly decided the Spaniards were far too strong to attack, though they outnumbered them five to one. Davils went back to Tralee that night to gather reinforcements, sleeping in the same bed as Arthur Carter, St. Leger’s lieutenant, as brave an old soldier as himself. Their young lacquey boy, now in Sir Richard’s service at Stow, slept on the floor at their feet. In the dead of night, in came James Desmond, sword in hand, with a dozen of his ruffians, each with their Irish fringe over their faces and a skene dagger hidden in their palms. Davils woke up, naked, and asked only ‘What is the matter, my son?’ The villain struck him down before he could even say a prayer, screaming ‘Thou shalt be my father no longer, nor I thy son!’ All the rest fell on him and Captain Carter. The poor lacquey boy threw himself over his master to shield him, got three stab wounds, and was left for dead. The ruffians murdered every last Englishman in the house, except the boy, who crawled out a window, bleeding, to escape. Desmond went back to the Spaniards, his elbows dripping with blood, and showed them his bloody blades, saying ‘Will you take this as proof I am loyal to you?’ That was the end of Henry Davils. That’s what becomes of any man who trusts the word of those wild savages.”

“I would ride a hundred miles to see that Desmond hang!” Amyas roared, great tears rolling down his sunburnt cheeks. “Poor Mr. Davils! Now tell me the tale of Sir Thomas Stukely.”

“Your brother can tell you that, lad. I’m winded.”
