As we ventured forth from our makeshift campsite, the warm tropical sun cast a golden glow upon the landscape, illuminating the dense foliage of the island's vegetation. Our party consisted of myself, along with my sons, Ernest, Fritz, and Jack, each of whom had developed a profound appreciation for the intricacies of botany. Our objective for the day was to tend to the nascent fruit trees that had been carefully selected from the surrounding woods and replanted in the fertile soil of our newly established orchard. The boys and I worked in tandem, methodically inspecting each tree to assess its potential for cultivation. Many of the saplings had grown stronger and taller, but others lay prostrate on the ground, their trunks snapped by the recent tempest.

We set about righting the fallen trees, employing sturdy bamboo props to support their weakened limbs. As we labored, Ernest inquired about the distinction between wild and domesticated trees. Jack, ever the wit, quipped that we were taming the wild trees, much like we had the buffalo, and that soon they would produce fruit wherever we desired. Franz, however, remained skeptical, positing that certain species, such as crab-apples, grew naturally in the woods, whereas others, like pears and peaches, were cultivated in more controlled environments. I smiled, recognizing an opportunity to educate the young minds around me.

"Grafting," I began, "is a horticultural technique where a section of a mature tree is inserted into a 'eye' – a natural opening or knot in the branch of another tree. This process allows the new growth to thrive, producing fruit characteristic of the donor tree, rather than the host. For instance, if we were to take a slip from a sour crab tree and graft it onto a pear tree, the resulting branch would yield delicious pears, not crab apples." Ernest and Fritz listened intently, their faces reflecting a mix of curiosity and wonder.

As we walked through the orchard, Fritz inquired about the origin of certain fruits, particularly cherries. I explained that cherries were actually native to Cerasus, a region in ancient Pontus, in Asia Minor. They were later introduced to Europe by the Roman general Lucullus, who brought them back from his conquests. Similarly, hazelnuts originated in Pontus, while walnuts hailed from Persia.
