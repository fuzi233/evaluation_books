The road stretched out before him, a ribbon of mud and dust leading nowhere certain, until Gerard encountered a tableau of human folly. Two men in sober, dark suits leaned with the languid indifference of predators resting between kills, flanking a horse that stood as the centerpiece of their amusement. Between them lay the Count, a figure of grotesque excess dressed in a silk doublet and a bright green jerkin of English cloth, glossy as a mole's hide. He sprawled flat upon his stomach in the afternoon sun, looking less like a nobleman and more like an enormous, discarded lizard. A velvet cloak, flaming yellow, was carefully draped over the horse's loins, a ridiculous attempt at warmth for a beast that required none.

"Is aught amiss?" Gerard inquired, his voice cutting through the lazy atmosphere.

"Not that I wot of," one of the servants replied, his tone dripping with feigned concern.

"But your master," Gerard pressed, unable to suppress his indignation, "lies like a corpse. Are ye not ashamed to let him grovel on the ground?"

The servant's response was immediate and dismissive. "Go to; the bare ground is the best cure for his disorder. If you get sober in bed, it gives you a headache; but you leap up from the hard ground like a lark in spring. Eh, Ulric?"

"He speaks sooth, young man," Ulric agreed warmly, his eyes gleaming with a shared malice.

"What, is the gentleman drunk?" Gerard asked, the question hanging in the air with naive simplicity.

The servants erupted into a hoarse, raucous laughter, mocking the very concept of Gerard's inquiry. Suddenly, however, Ulric halted, his expression shifting from joviality to grave scrutiny as he eyed Gerard from head to toe. "Who are you, and where born, that know not the Count is ever drunk at this hour?" The question transformed Gerard instantly from a concerned traveler into a suspect character, an outsider whose ignorance marked him as dangerous.

"I am a stranger," Gerard declared, straightening his spine, "but a true man, and one that loves knowledge; therefore ask I questions, and not for love of prying."

"If you be a true man," Ulric countered, his voice sharpening with shrewdness, "then give us trinkgeld for the knowledge we have given you."

Gerard blinked, confused, but quickly masked his bewilderment with a polite facade. "Trinkgeld you shall have, such as my lean purse can spare, an if you will tell me why ye have ta'en his cloak from the man and laid it on the beast."

Inspired by the prospect of payment, the two men immediately offered Gerard two competing theories, each more absurd than the last. The first suggested that since the Count was a seasoned toper prone to rapid sobriety, finding his horse sweating in the cold while a cloak lay idle nearby would inevitably provoke a fit of cursing and violence. The second, more pretentious explanation posited that a horse is a poor milksop, requiring constant warming, whereas a master, filled with the internal heat of good beer, rendered a cloak a mere shred of idle vanity. Each speaker fell in love with their own logic, having clearly taken a bite from the dog that had bitten their master to the brain. Their voices rose in a heated debate until the green sot began to growl instead of snore, oblivious to the fact that his audience was arguing over his comfort.

Before long, the intellectual discourse devolved into physical combat. Hans, gripping the bridle with his right hand, delivered a solid cuff to Ulric's jaw with his left. Ulric returned the favor with equal enthusiasm, his right hand free. They engaged in a chaotic dance of blows, *ding dong*, over the horse's mane, pummeling one another and jostling the poor beast until it backed away, stumbling over a promontory of the green lord's body. Like a toad stung by Ithuriel's spear, the Count started up howling, clutching his smarting side with one hand and tugging at his hilt with the other. The servants, seized by terror, released the reins. The horse galloped off, whinnying in panic, while the men chased after him, crying out in fear. The green noble followed, volleying curses, his naked sword drawn, his body rebounding wildly from hedge to hedge in a headlong, zigzag career down the narrow lane.

"In which hurtling," Gerard muttered, turning his back on the chaos, he walked calmly south. He was glad to have saved the four tin farthings he had prepared for trinkgeld, yet his heart remained heavy, burdened by the sight of their drunken extravagance.

The sun was nearing its setting, painting the sky in hues of bruised purple. Gerard, who had hoped in vain for an inn along the route, felt a profound unease settle in his bones. Black clouds gathered overhead, swallowing the light. He quickened his pace, almost running, but it was futile. Down came the rain in torrential sheets, drenching the bewildered traveler and seemingly extinguishing the sun itself; its fading rays could not compete with this new assailant.

Gerard trudged on, dark, wet, and lost in an unknown region. "Fool! to leave Margaret," he chided himself, the words tasting bitter on his parched lips.

Soon, the darkness thickened into an impenetrable veil. He was entering a great wood. Huge branches shot across the narrow road like skeletal fingers, and the benighted stranger groped his way through what seemed an interminable, inky cave with a rugged floor. He stumbled constantly, his shivering limbs and empty stomach betraying him, his heart fainting with every step. Then, the wolves rose from their lairs, bayed all around the perimeter of the forest, their cries echoing like death knells.

Gerard's hair bristled, but he gripped his cudgel tightly, preparing to sell his life dearly if necessary. There was no wind, yet his excited ears caught the patter of light feet over the newly fallen leaves and the rustle of low branches as creatures glided swiftly past. Suddenly, in the sea of ink, a great fiery star appeared close to the ground. He hailed it as he would his patron saint. "CANDLE! a CANDLE!" he shouted, attempting to run toward it. But the dark, rugged path soon thwarted his efforts; the light proved more distant than he had dared hope. Finally, in the very heart of the forest, he found a house, illuminated by candles and filled with loud voices. He looked up for a signboard, but there was none. "Not an inn after all!" he said sadly. Yet, reasoning that no Christian would turn a dog out into this wood tonight, he resolved to seek shelter. He approached the door that led to the voices, opening it slowly and peering in timidly. He withdrew abruptly, as if slapped in the face, and recoiled into the rain and darkness.

He had glimpsed a large but low room, the center dominated by a huge round stove or clay oven reaching to the ceiling. Around it, wet clothes hung to dry—some on lines, others more compactly on rustic pegs. These garments, soaked with the day's rain and stained with the dirt of travel, lined with what locals called "rammish clowns," emitted rank vapors and compound odors inexpressible, rising in steaming clouds. In one corner sat a traveling family, large and numerous, releasing a peculiar, sickly smell of neglected brats into the common air. Garlic filled the interstices of the space. With windows closed and the intense heat of the central furnace battling the breath of at least forty people, the atmosphere was suffocating.

They had just finished supper.

Like many artists, Gerard possessed sensitive organs, and the potent effluvia struck dismay into him. Yet, the relentless rain lashing him outside, combined with the temptation of light and fire, drew him back. He could not force his way through the palpable perfumes all at once, returning to the doorway repeatedly like a singed moth. Eventually, he discerned that the various smells did not entirely mix, no fiend stirring them together. The odor of family predominated in two corners; stewed rustic reigned supreme in the center; and garlic in the noisy group by the window. By hasty analysis, he noted that the garlic described the smallest aerial orbit, while the scent of reeking rustic darted farthest—a flavor as if ancient goats, or the fathers of all foxes, had been dragged through a river and dried by Nebuchadnezzar.

So Gerard crept into a corner close to the door. Though the solidity of the main fetors isolated him somewhat, the heat and reeking vapors circulated, making the walls drip. The home-nurtured novice felt something like a cold snake wind about his legs, his head turning to a great lump of lead. Next, he felt like choking, sweetly slumbering, and dying, all in one.

He was within an ace of swooning, but recovered to a deep sense of disgust and discouragement. Settling on a resolution to return to Holland at peep of day, he plucked up a little heart. Being faint with hunger, he asked one of the men of garlic whether this was indeed an inn.

"Whence come you, who know not 'The Star of the Forest'?" was the reply.
