Lieutenant Sutch, having been discharged from the half-pay list, found himself traversing a high, white road in Hampshire, cutting through commons of heather and gorse. Beside him, the landscape stretched flat and green, but inside his head, the terrain was jagged and steep. He was plagued by the same anxiety that had consumed him when Calder failed to locate Harry Feversham in Assouan. Sutch, accustomed to solitude, often voiced his internal monologue, muttering to the empty air as his pony trotted along: "I foresaw trouble. From the very beginning, I foresaw trouble."

The road dipped abruptly into a hollow, plunging downward past dense forests of pine toward a solitary railway station. As Sutch watched the gleaming rails stretching into the distance, converging like telegraph poles until they vanished into the haze, his composure shattered. He pulled up his reins, staring with a furrowed brow at the red-tiled roof of the station.

"I promised Harry to keep silent," he whispered, finding a fragile anchor in the words. "I promised faithfully in the Criterion grill-room."

A distant, shrill whistle pierced the afternoon silence, heralding an approaching train. Its white smoke drifted across the fields like a ribbon. Sutch watched it approach, his doubts momentarily replaced by a grim resolve. "I wonder what brings him," he murmured, steeling himself. "Well, there is no use in shirking." With a sharp crack of his whip, he urged the pony down the hill.

As the train clattered to a halt, two figures emerged onto the platform. It was Major Durrance and his servant. Before the pair could descend fully, Sutch called out, halting them at the side of his trap.

"You received my telegram in time, then?" Durrance asked, stepping forward.

"Luckily it found me at home," Sutch replied, though his tone lacked the warmth his words suggested. To Durrance, the hesitation rang clear. Yet, Durrance had anticipated a reluctant welcome; he had sent his telegram at the eleventh hour precisely to avoid a direct refusal. The note itself had mentioned nothing of purpose, only arrival, yet it served as a grim warning to Sutch that affairs between Ethne Eustace and Durrance had soured into something grave.

Before Durrance could mount, Sutch noticed the man's reliance on feeling for the step of the trap—the unmistakable sign of blindness. A wave of relief washed over Sutch, mingled with a sudden, sharp pang of pity. Reaching out, he guided Durrance's arm, helping him into the vehicle.

"I was very sorry, Durrance, to hear of your bad luck," Sutch said as they began the ascent back uphill. "I know the sting of it. My leg wound was nothing compared to yours, though. I don't speak of compensations or patience—that is the gabble of the comfortable. We know that for a young man whose career is suddenly severed, not by his fault, there is no consolation."

"Through no fault of his," Durrance echoed softly. "I agree. It is only the man whose career ends through his own error who receives compensation."

Sutch glanced sharply at his companion. Was this a cryptic reference to Harry Feversham? Did Durrance possess knowledge Sutch did not?

"Compensations of what kind?" Sutch asked, his voice tight.

"For one thing, the chance of self-knowledge," Durrance said. "He is stopped in his tracks, perhaps disgraced. Yes, perhaps disgraced." The word hung heavy in the air. "But consider: the shock of disgrace is his opportunity. Until that moment, his life has been a sham, an illusion. He thought he was one thing, but he was not. Now, at last, he sees the truth. Once he knows, he can begin to repair his ruin. There are compensations for such a man. You and I know a case in point."

Sutch's heart hammered. He realized with certainty that Durrance was speaking of Harry. Though Durrance's understanding of Harry's situation was imperfect, Sutch could not allow his friend's reputation to be slighted further. His devotion to Harry, fierce and concentrated like a lover's, burned brighter than ever.

"The case you and I know is not quite in point," Sutch argued, trying to steady his voice. "You are speaking of Harry Feversham."

"Who believed himself a coward and was not," Durrance corrected. "He committed the fault that halted his career, discovered his mistake, and set himself to retrieve his honor. Is that not a case in point?"

"Yes, I see," Sutch admitted, though he sensed a dangerous shift in perspective. "But there is another view—a wrong view, as I know it to be. You imply Harry thought he was brave and stumbled upon his cowardice. How did you learn this? No one knew the whole truth except myself."

"I am engaged to Miss Eustace," Durrance said simply.

"She knew of the disgrace, but not of the determination to fix it," Sutch noted.

"She knows now," Durrance stated sharply. "And you are glad, I suspect—very glad."

Sutch was stunned. He had not intended to betray his feelings, yet his face had given him away. Puzzled but unable to deny the accusation, he steeled himself.

"It is true," Sutch said stoutly. "I am very glad she knows. From your perspective, perhaps it is better if she does not. But I cannot help it. I am glad."

Durrance laughed, a sound devoid of malice. "I like you the better for being glad."

"How does Miss Eustace know?" Sutch pressed. "I told her nothing, and no one else could possibly have learned the truth."

"You are mistaken," Durrance replied. "Captain Willoughby arrived in Devonshire six weeks ago. He delivered a white feather to Miss Eustace as proof that he had withdrawn his charge of cowardice."

Sutch slammed the reins against the pony's flank, stopping the horse mid-road. The joy flooding his chest was so profound it rendered him mute. For a moment, he forgot the blind man beside him, sitting still with a glow of happiness that defied his age. He was sixty years old, a man of sober pleasures, yet a youthful buoyancy surged within him. Five years prior, standing on the pier at Dover watching a ship vanish into the rain, he had prayed for this exact moment. Now, it had arrived. His heart swelled with gratitude. Since that fateful night outside the War Office when Harry Feversham had confessed his despair, Sutch had been crushed by guilt. Harry was Muriel Feversham's son, and Sutch, failing in his duty to watch over him since the boy's parents died, had felt Muriel's reproachful gaze from heaven. *Just for my sake,* she seemed to say, *you might have helped.* Now, the weight lifted from his shoulders, borne away by Harry's own action.

"I knew!" Sutch cried, his voice trembling. "I knew he wouldn't fail! Oh, I am glad you came today, Colonel Durrance. Partly it was my fault that Harry incurred that charge. There was a chance at Broad Place during the Crimean war, a single word could have saved him, and I held my tongue. I have never ceased blaming myself. I am grateful for your news. Do you have the particulars? Captain Willoughby was in danger, and Harry came to his aid?"

"Not exactly," Durrance replied.

"Tell me! Tell me!"

Durrance recounted the story of the Gordon letters and their recovery. The narrative was brief, yet it filled Sutch with a boundless relief.

"At least you understand," Durrance said, "that I have not come to ask the questions I posed in the club courtyard. On the contrary, I am here to give you information."

Sutch urged the pony onward. He had carefully avoided revealing his fear that Durrance sought to renew those old inquiries, yet he was perplexed by the colonel's uncanny intuition. However, the crushing weight of the good news silenced his doubts.

"So Miss Eustace told you the story?" Sutch asked. "Did she show you the feather?"

"No," Durrance corrected gently. "She said not a word. She forbade Captain Willoughby to hint at it, sending him away before I even knew he had arrived. You are disappointed, I see."

Startled, Sutch felt a spike of jealousy. He loved Harry deeply, but he also cherished the memory of Ethne, wishing she remembered him as he did her. He had hoped her news would sever her engagement to Durrance immediately.

"Then how did you learn the truth?" Sutch demanded.

"Somebody else told me," Durrance said with a laugh. "I heard rumors of Willoughby's visit and the white feather. I lay in wait for him in London. He is not very clever; he tried to obey Ethne's command of silence, but I extracted what I needed. I pieced together the rest myself. Ethne slips up occasionally. Am I not clever enough to find the truth with my own wits?"

Sutch jumped in his seat. Chance played a large part, yet the blind man's insights unnerved him.
