Without quite knowing how he arrived there, Oleron found himself striding over the loose board he had hastily laid upon the broken step near Miss Bengough's apartment. He was hatless, moving downward into the gloom of the stairwell. It was only later that a hazy recollection surfaced: he remembered leaving the candle burning on the table, having opened the door just wide enough for his own passage, and slipping out while keeping the latch soft against the frame.

At the foot of the stairs, another shock awaited him. Something darted upward from the disused cellars with a frantic flurry and vanished through the open doorway. It was merely a cat, yet Oleron let out a childish sob. He pushed through the gate and paused beneath the "To Let" signs, nervously picking at his lip while staring up at the faint glow filtering through one of the red blinds. Still casting anxious glances over his shoulder, he stumbled up the square.

A small public house rounded the corner—a place Oleron had never previously entered—but tonight he crossed its threshold and dropped a shilling onto the counter, missing the edge by mere inches. "Br—brand—brandy," he stammered, then bent to retrieve his coin. He possessed the small, sawdust-covered bar entirely to himself; the few patrons present—carters, laborers, and local tradesmen—were clustered in the far compartment, separated from the main area where the white-haired landlady bustled among her taps and bottles.

Oleron sank onto a hard wooden settee with a perforated seat, drained half his brandy, and, deciding it was better to finish it than waste it, consumed the remainder. Immediately, his thoughts drifted to which of the men he heard arguing across the room would handle the removal of his belongings tomorrow. In the interim, he ordered another measure. He had no intention of returning to that room where the candle burned alone. No, indeed! He could not face even the entrance, let alone the staircase with its treacherous break—certainly not that pith-white, mesmerizing chamber. For the moment, he resolved to revert to his former arrangement: a workroom and separate sleeping quarters. He would visit his old landlady shortly, once he had finished his drink, to ask if she could accommodate him for the night.

His glass was empty now. He signaled for a refill, sat down again, and considered the question: If anyone asked why he was moving again? Oh, he had ample justification! Nails that magically reseated themselves and sliced fingers, steps that shattered underfoot, and women who invaded a man's private space to groom their hair in the darkness—all were sufficient causes. He felt querulous and aggrieved by it all. He had secured the place for himself, not for invisible women to preen in the dark; the lawyer from Lincoln's Inn ought to be informed of this outrage within the hour. Agreements such as these were preposterous!

A cut-glass partition divided the section where Oleron sat from the area occupied by the landlady, though it ended several inches below the counter level. There was no barrier separating the far bar. Soon, Oleron raised his eyes and perceived faces watching him through the gap. When he looked directly at them, the watchers vanished. He shifted to a corner where he could not be seen from the opposite bar, but this maneuver aligned him squarely with the white-haired landlady. She recognized him; she had likely observed him passing repeatedly. Eventually, she remarked on the weather. Oleron did not recall his reply, but it prompted her to note that winter had been terrible for influenza, though spring seemed imminent.

Even this trivial connection to the mundane steadied Oleron somewhat. An idle, budding curiosity arose regarding whether the landlady combed her hair nightly and, if so, whether it emitted those tiny electric crackles—but this thought was silenced instantly. Oleron felt better. With his next glass of brandy, he decided firmly to return to his flat. Return? Indeed, he would! They would soon discover whether he intended to be evicted like that! He wondered why he was engaging in such an unusual behavior for himself—sitting hatless, consuming brandy in a pub. What if he confessed everything to the landlady? Told her that a visitor had scratched her hand on a nail, that misfortune struck when she stepped through a rotting stair, and that he, trapped in an ancient house filled with squeaks, creaks, and whispers, had fled in terror? She would surely deem him mad. Pshaw! The true reality was simply that he had neglected his work, wasting days in dreams and filling his mind with moonshine about a new *Romilly* (as if the old one wasn't adequate). Now he was startled that the devil should inhabit an empty head. Yes, he would go back. He would take a walk in the fresh air—he hadn't walked enough recently—and then seize control, resolve the mess of that sixteenth chapter of *Romilly* (it was astonishing he had foolishly considered destroying fifteen!), and henceforth remember his obligations to mankind and the work awaiting him. That was the essence of the matter.

He finished his brandy and exited. He walked for some time before any perspective other than his own personal plight occurred to him. Initially, the crisp air intensified the heady effects of the alcohol, but eventually, his mind cleared more sharply than it had since morning. As clarity returned, his boastful assurances lost their finality, replaced by a firm conviction that, once all explanations were exhausted, something inexplicable remained. The hysteria of the previous hour faded; he grew steadily calmer, yet a disturbing certainty persisted. A profound fear seized him—not for himself, but for Elsie. Something within his residence was hostile to her safety. Her two accidents alone might not have convinced him, but she had spoken. *"I'm not wanted here..."* She had declared the place was wrong, seeing it even before he had. Very well. One fact stood clear: if this were true, she must be kept away for reasons far deeper than those that had so confused and humiliated Oleron. Fortunately, she had expressed an intent to stay away; he must ensure she adhered to it. Moreover, he realized his initial impulse—to never set foot in the place again—was absurd. People did not act thus. With Elsie secured, he could not, with any regard for his own dignity, allow himself to be driven out by a shadow or a mere danger, especially since he needed to live somewhere. He would remain. He mastered the faint chill of fear accompanying this decision and abruptly turned his walk around. Should fear resurface, he might consider one more glass of brandy... but by the time he reached the short street leading to the square, it was too late. The pub was still lit but closed, with a couple of men chatting on the curb. Oleron noted a sudden silence fall over them as he passed, and observed that the long-nosed Barrett, whom he encountered slightly lower down, did not offer a greeting.

He turned into the broken gate, hesitated for a fleeting second in the alley, and ascended the stairs once more. Only an inch of wax remained in the Sheffield stick, and Oleron refused to light another candle. Deliberately, he forced himself to pick it up and inspect all five rooms before retiring. It was as he returned from the kitchen across his small hall that he spotted a letter lying on the floor. Carrying it into the sitting room, he glanced at the envelope before opening it. It was unstamped and had been slipped through the door by hand. The handwriting was clumsy, running continuously without commas or periods. Oleron read the first line, checked the signature, and finished the missive. It was from Barrett, informing Oleron that he would be obliged if Mr. Oleron would arrange alternative provisions for his breakfasts and the cleaning of his premises. The sting lay in the postscript, a biblical text containing an allusion that could only refer to Elsie Bengough.

A rare, deep frown carved itself into Oleron's brow. So! That was it! Very well; they would see about that tomorrow. For the rest, this appeared to be yet another reason for Elsie to remain absent. Then his suppressed rage erupted. The foul-minded lot! The devil himself could not have sneered more bitterly at anything that had transpired between Paul Oleron and Elsie Bengough, yet this nosing rascal dared pry and gossip! Oleron crumpled the paper, held it to the candle flame, and ground the ashes beneath his heel. However, the letter served one useful purpose: it ignited a wrathful blaze within Oleron that effectively banished the pale shadows.

Nevertheless, one final puzzle awaited him. As he undressed, he happened to glance at his bed. The coverlets bore an impression as if someone had lain upon them. Oleron could not recall doing so himself during the day—in fact, he was certain he had not; yet he could not be absolutely sure. His indignation toward Elsie, perhaps amplified by the lingering effects of the brandy, excluded all other considerations. He extinguished the candle, lay down, and immediately plunged into a deep, dreamless sleep that, lacking Mrs. Barrett's morning summons, lasted nearly twenty-four hours.
