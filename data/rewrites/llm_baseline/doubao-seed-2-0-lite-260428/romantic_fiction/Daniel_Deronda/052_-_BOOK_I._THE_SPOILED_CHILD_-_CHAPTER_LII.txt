“The same firmness that serves to resist love also serves to make it violent and long-lasting; weak souls, who are forever tossed about by passing passions, are almost never truly filled by any one of them.” — LA ROCHEFOUCAULD

When Deronda sorted through his post the next morning, one letter from Hans Meyrick stood out immediately, its four quarto pages covered in the tiny, elegant cursive that ran in the Meyrick bloodline.

*In exchange for your sharp breakdown of Italian political shifts and your level-headed take on global affairs, I’ll have you know the wisest consensus at home right now about the fallout of everything going on is simply that “time will tell.” As for what caused all last year’s messes? Everyone’s decided those late fraudulent telegrams are the real culprit behind the cattle plague — which handily debunks all that fake philosophy nonsense and justifies every penny the farmers got in compensation. My own theory, that a murrain of sorts is about to sweep through the commercial class, and the cause will turn out to be them paying top dollar for all the paintings real judges turned down? They called that a misuse of analogy, but there are cruel minds out there who won’t even let a neglected painter keep his silly little solace. I think it’s quite beautiful, the idea that some idiot critic could overpay wildly for my Berenice series, and all those greedy city men will get their just deserts for funding my utterly unearned good luck.*

*Meanwhile, I’m making the most of your absence instead of moping — shining bright as Hesperus now that Hyperion’s gone off to do his grand hero stuff. I’ve been sitting with our Hebrew prophet Mordecai for hours you used to spend with him, sketching his head, and he’s taken to me as a learned young Gentile who’d convert to Judaism tomorrow if he could work out the logistics. We’re in full agreement that everything that is good and great is Jewish by nature. I’m no master of strict logic, obviously, but even I can see the math: if all that is best is A, and B happens to be best, then B must be A, no matter how surprising that feels. That same logic once fueled a whole pamphlet I found proving all good art is Protestant, so you see how flexible it is.*

*Anyway, Mordecai is a far more fascinating sitter than Rembrandt ever had for his famous Rabbi portraits. I walk away from every session with some new revelation. For one thing, for all the blazing passion he carries for his people and their traditions, he’s no rigid, narrow-minded Orthodox Jew who spits after he says the word “Christian” and daydreams about us all drooling uselessly for a bite of Leviathan’s roasted flesh while Israel passes their plates around for seconds. You know I used to write off all your stories about him as over-generous, you always have that soft spot for making even the most archaic views sound reasonable instead of silly. But sitting with him in the flesh? He’s this wild, brilliant mix of philosopher, allegorist, and mystic, and he’s got a sharp enough dialectic wit to prick any windbag arguing with him silent in ten seconds flat. It must be a Jewish superpower.*

*He’s so broad-minded that all my little carefully held correct opinions fit into his worldview perfectly comfortably, and if they don’t line up with all the rest of his grand ideas? That’s his problem to solve, not mine. I long ago gave up on trying to find a “solid basis” for my beliefs — every last one of them is just a world-bearing elephant, expensive to feed and hard to keep upright, and I don’t make enough as a painter to afford a private elephant. I stick to mystery instead, it’s cheaper, longer lasting, the kind of gas that keeps bubbling up as those elephants slowly decompose. If an opinion looks nice, I treat it politely, no grubby suspicious digging. I’m quite fond of his idea that every whole Christian is three-fourths a Jew, and every great, broad thinker from the Alexandrian era onward has been essentially Jewish — because it gives me a perfect excuse to point out to Mirah that Arabic and all that life stuff aside, there’s barely any difference between me and Maimonides.*

*I’ve realized lately only a shallow lover rushes to blurt out a grand declaration. If Mirah’s little ways weren’t so endlessly distracting, if just being in her presence and watching her wasn’t the closest thing to heaven I’ve ever known, I’d have flung myself at her feet ages ago, begged her to tell me point-blank if she wanted me to go blow my brains out. But I’ve got this knack for hoping, you see, which is as good as a reversion estate if you just don’t rush to seize certainty and ruin the whole magic. My Hope wanders the orchard blossoms, feels the warm snow fall through the sunshine on its face, doubts nothing — until it spots Certainty far off, that ugly Janus-faced monster with a leering, dubious grin, and it turns tail and runs fast. You, with your overdeveloped reasonableness, your habit of erasing yourself to prepare for the worst? You’ve no idea what Hope is like — that immortal, delicious maiden forever courted, forever kind, whom fools call deceitful, as if Hope was the one holding the cup of disappointment, when it’s her enemy Certainty that carries that, whom she only escapes by shape-shifting. (See my new allegorical streak? Impressive, right?)*

*But seriously. Truth wins out. Prejudice melts. Merit makes even the oddest differences feel like fascination. No virtuous, earnest wish ever gets frustrated. All those are tenets of the schools, aren’t they? Which means the Jewess I’m head over heels for will eventually prefer me. Any blockhead can quote generalities. The real master of the mind sees exactly the specific case those generalities were written for.*

*I don’t flatter myself that my company makes up for Deronda being gone, but we do get another visitor sometimes — Jacob Cohen, that wild little six-year-old boy from the Jewish neighborhood. It’s a sight to see Mordecai with him perched on his knee, pouring out that Semitic wisdom with that look of sublime melancholy, patience, and devoutness. Sometimes little Jacob decides Hebrew sounds far more fun if he presses his palms over his ears and mimics the muffled noises Mordecai makes. When Mordecai gently pulls his little fists down and holds them tight, Jacob’s face lights up with a hundred little tics, like he’s walking through a zoo and trying to imitate every animal at once — he’s got the owl and peccary down pat, anyway. He treats me like a second-hand Christian commodity that’s bound to drop in price, comments on all my flaws with such frank cheer you’d swear he’s already planning to buy me cheap later. But the second Mirah walks in? He turns soft as a lamb, the kind of dog that was raised entirely by women and won’t listen to anyone else. He’s still fond of Mordecai, though, brings him sugarplums to share, shoveling half his own mouth full so he can watch Mordecai eat the smaller portion and look suitably pleased. Judging by this little modern Jacob, I’m astonished his whole race hasn’t bought all of us up ages ago, pocketed us all as stock and slave property. Though I can think of one Jewess I’d be perfectly happy to be a slave to.*

*Though lately I’ve noticed something that doesn’t make me happy: Mirah’s been getting a little sadder, and she’s trying so hard to hide it. It makes sense, of course — she’s watching her brother slowly waste away, and she looks at him with such loving, devout reverence that I catch myself half-wishing I was the one lying sick, getting all that soft, adoring attention.*

*Other than that, we’ve been merrier than usual. You remember Rex Gascoigne, the law student with the sharp, good upper lip whose sketch you once admired? He’s got rooms in town not far from us now, and his very neat, equally good-lipped sister has been staying with him for a fortnight. I introduced them to my mother and the girls, and they found out Miss Gascoigne is cousin to that Vandyke duchess you’re always going on about — your Lady Mallinger, that is! Those exclamation marks are for how shocked I was at first, before I realized there’s zero reason I should have been surprised, unless I genuinely thought no one could be anyone’s cousin without my prior knowledge.*

*Anyway, that relation came out when Mirah was over at their place talking about the Mallingers. Speaking of which, I’ve got rival invitations now. Gascoigne wants me to come down to his father’s rectory in August and see the countryside there, but I think enlightened self-interest is going to drag me to Topping Abbey. Sir Hugo invited me, and bless his rash, kind heart — he wants me to paint a portrait of his three daughters sitting on a bank, Gainsborough style. He stopped by my studio the other day and told me I ought to devote myself to portraiture. Of course I know what that means: he’s trying to say my attempts at historic, poetic art are pitiable, that my brush is perfect for portraiture — just enough truth, a whole lot of facility for pretty falsehood — that my idealism will never make gods and heroes look right, but it’ll make a fortune flattering rich people. But he says it all so cheerfully, so kindly, that I don’t mind at all. His kindness to me tastes extra sweet, because it’s all coming from how much he loves you, old boy. He told me your duchess — that fair Supralapsarian Gwendolen, whose weird religious tenets you once argued about — has gone yachting to the Mediterranean with her husband. I suppose it’s easy enough to land from a yacht, or get picked up by one from shore. Any chance you’ll run into her and pick up that old theological debate? Is the Duke Alphonso theological too? Maybe an Arian who objects to the whole trinity thing. (Stage direction: As Deronda reads this, profound scorn builds on his face, until at that last stupid joke he flings the letter down, grasps his coat collar like a marble statue, and stands there looking thunderous for a full minute while he mentally rants about how absolutely ridiculous his friend is.)*

*Sorry for the brevity, you’re used to me only spouting bare facts, no digressions or commentary, of course. One fact I left out: the Klesmers, right before they left town, were magnificently generous, the kind of grand gesture you expect when two stars of genius and fortune align. Mirah is loaded down with their beautiful oriental gifts, she’s practically a princess now.*

*What luck it’ll be if you come back and show up at the Abbey while I’m there! I’ll be the soul of discretion, win everyone’s golden opinions. Though I’ll run up to town every now and then, just for a peep into our little Garden of Eden. Look how far my Hebrew has gotten — I’m right up there with Lord Bolingbroke, who knew no Hebrew at all but “understood that sort of learning and what is writ about it.” If Mirah commanded me, I’d dive deep enough to get past all the triliteral roots. I don’t even care if the vowel points are there or not now. But while her brother lives? I suspect she won’t listen to a lover, even one whose “hair is like a flock of goats on Mount Gilead” — and I flatter myself very few heads could pull that comparison off better than mine. So I’ll stay here, with my hope wandering the orchard blossoms. — Your devoted, HANS MEYRICK.*

Only a few months prior, this letter would have gnawed at Deronda, annoyed him no end. Hans’ silly, starry-eyed infatuation with Mirah would have stung, sharp and unkind, with no trace of sympathy for the heartbreak his friend would almost certainly face. But everything had shifted since March. Mirah was no longer in the desperate, vulnerable position she’d been in when the Meyricks first took her in, and Deronda’s own life had been upended, the secret of his birth and his true Jewish heritage just laid bare to him. The new future stretching out before him, even though he dared not wrap it in definite fantasies, cast a soft new light over every old memory and present moment. So what Hans called his “hope” looked to Deronda now not like a foolish, harmful unreasonableness that made him indignant, but just an extravagant, silly little dance of his friend’s overactive fancy — and he felt fully capable of pitying whatever pain Hans might face, if he could even imagine Hans ever caring enough to hurt deeply. A whole day stretched ahead of him, no new summons from his mother, and his thoughts turned over and over the quiet argument that Hans was not the kind of man whose love could sink those deep roots that turn disappointment into lifelong grief. He was too restless, too easily charmed by any new novelty, too quick to turn every hurt into material for his imagination, to wear sorrow like some dramatic, fancy new costume.

“Already he’s playing at love,” Deronda murmured to himself. “He’s treating the whole thing like a comedy. He knows full well there’s no chance for him. So typical of him — never stopping to wonder if I might have any feelings about his constant gushing over Mirah. Poor old Hans! If we were stuck out in a hailstorm of fire together, he’d howl like a Greek, and if I didn’t howl right along with him, it’d never cross his mind I was in just as much pain. But he’s good-hearted, affectionate, even if he does always twist everyone else’s inner life to fit his own daydreams.”

That little touch of dry, fond annoyance was enough to burn off the faint heat Hans’ naive openness had stirred in him. The stupid joke about Gwendolen, confirming she was gone yachting with her husband, only brought a soft, lingering restlessness, a memory of that strange, charged parting they’d had. But one line in the letter sparked a far sharper, more immediate anxiety. Hans had noticed Mirah’s hidden sadness, unprompted by any of his own wishes for what he’d find, so Deronda couldn’t dismiss that observation out of hand. He immediately began to wonder what could be hurting her. Was it something that had happened while he was away? Some new fear she was hiding? Was there something wrong with the new life they’d built for her, that could be fixed? Or — had Mordecai, against his usual rock-solid resolve, told her all those precious, secret hopes he had about Deronda? Had Mirah’s quick, sensitive nature been hurt, realizing her brother’s fierce, single-minded conviction had been pressing him and Deronda together, that maybe Deronda’s kindness to them was more pity and self-suppression than equal, genuine regard? Mirah had spent her whole life being dismissed, patronized, until she’d met him — her soul thirsted so badly for equal respect, that it would cut her deep if she realized the reverence she’d thought he held for her brother was tinged with condescension.

He was right about that part of Mirah’s nature, of course. The long, quiet protest against the way the world had treated her all her life was the exact reason her gratitude to Deronda was so fierce, the sharp contrast between how he treated her and how everyone else had seen her. But he was wrong to think Mordecai had broken his silence. Mordecai had breathed no word of their shared connection, no hint of the secret of Deronda’s birth, to any living soul but Deronda himself. Those things were too sacred, too precious, to share without the heaviest, most urgent reason — and he’d seen Deronda flinch every time anyone brought up his parentage. The same strict reserve that had kept him from prying into the Cohen family’s private affairs made him hold this secret tighter than ever.

One day, Mirah had turned to him gently and said, “Ezra, how strange it is. I keep almost speaking to Mr. Deronda as if he were a Jew.”

He smiled at her, soft and calm. “That’s only because he treats us like we are his brother. But he does not like to have the difference of his birth dwelt upon.”

“Mr. Hans says he never lived with his parents,” Mirah went on, curious, as she was about every person she cared for.

“Do not seek to learn such things from Mr. Hans,” Mordecai said gravely, laying his hand lightly on her curls, the way he always did. “What Daniel Deronda wishes us to know of himself, he will tell us himself.”

Mirah felt rebuked, the same quiet way Deronda had once felt, but being corrected by Ezra only made her feel proud of him.

“I don’t think there’s anyone in the world as great as my brother,” she told Mrs. Meyrick a few days later, stopping by the Chelsea cottage on her way home, and finding the little widowed mother alone as she’d hoped. “It’s hard to believe he’s ever been part of the same world as the people I used to live with. I told you once how they made life feel like a madhouse. But being with Ezra, he makes me feel that life is a great, beautiful good, even after all the suffering he’s borne. Unlike me — who once wanted to die after I’d only suffered a little, for a little while. His soul is so full, he could never want death the way I did. I got that same feeling yesterday, when I was tired, and walked home through the park after the sweet rain had fallen, and the sunshine lay all over the grass and the flowers. Everything in the sky and under it looked so pure and lovely, that all the weariness and trouble and stupid hurt felt like just a tiny, tiny part of everything that is, and I felt a little more patient, a little more hopeful.”

There was a soft, dovelike note of melancholy in her voice that made Mrs. Meyrick set down her needlework, turning her full attention to the girl. Mirah took off her hat, smoothed down her curls with a little gesture of fatigue, and settled into her usual chair across from her friend, feet and hands gently crossed. From a distance, she could have been a serene colored statue. But Mrs. Meyrick spotted a new, suppressed look of suffering in her face, exactly matching her quiet hint that being patient and hopeful took far more effort than it used to.

“Is there any new trouble weighing on you, my dear?” she asked.

Mirah hesitated, then shook her head softly. “I think I’m too quick to talk about my troubles. It feels unkind to put painful things into other people’s heads, unless I’m sure it will stop something worse from happening. Maybe I’m just too hasty, too fearful.”
