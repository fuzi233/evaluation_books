Christmas afternoon fell early, as it always did upon that far western edge of England where daylight seemed a thing carried in the hands, and set down gently before it was snatched away again by the sea. Even-song had ended in Bideford, and the folk poured out as if the church door were the mouth of some warm oven: men with their cheeks reddened by hymns and ale, children already sticky-fingered with spice-cakes, lovers walking close in the lane as if the very air were too cold to waste between them. From open windows came the busy clatter of platters and trenchers; the scent of baking, of fried apples, of beer foaming up in brown jugs; and underneath all, the small domestic music of Christmas—knives on boards, laughter, the thud of a stool set down, the scrape of a fiddler’s bow beginning to try its strings.

One woman only went apart from that cheerful tide.

She was wrapped close in a black muffler, her maid behind her, and she walked swift as duty, sad as hunger, toward the long causeway and bridge that led to Northam. Sir Richard Grenville and his wife, meeting her, turned and overtook her with gentle haste.

“You will come home with us, Mrs. Leigh,” Lady Grenville said, laying a hand upon her sleeve as one might upon a dear, trembling bird. “And spend a pleasant Christmas night?”

Mrs. Leigh’s smile came and went like the thin light over the river. She touched Lady Grenville’s arm—grateful, and yet as if she could not bear to lean—and pointed westward.

“I cannot well spend a merry Christmas night,” she said, “while that sound is in my ears.”

They looked where she pointed.

The sky above them was fading from soft blue into a ash-gray, and a star or two blinked out, shy as children peeping through a door-crack. In the west the downlands and Raleigh woods met Abbotsham’s, and the last of the sun had left there a strange, delicate glory—white flakes as if the blue had been webbed and turf’d with frost-light, iridescent stains like the shifting colors on a dying dolphin, and, low on the rim of the world, a long band of grassy green.

It was dead calm. Not a breath stirred the river reeds. And yet—listen—there was a sound, a vast one, not sharp but weighty: a deep, steady roar that did not belong to the town at all, but came from beyond it, as if the earth itself had turned in sleep and groaned. It hovered over down and wood, over salt-marsh and river, like the roll of a thousand wheels, the tramp of endless armies, or, truer than both, the thunder of a mighty surge upon the boulders of the pebble ridge.

“The ridge is noisy to-night,” Sir Richard said, turning his head as a man turns when he hears distant carts upon a road. “There has been wind somewhere.”

“There is wind now,” Mrs. Leigh answered, and her voice tightened as cord tightens, “where my boy is—God help him!”

They all knew what she meant. The Atlantic storm had sent its herald inland: that smooth ground-swell whose voice you may hear two miles from shore on a calm day, telling you, before the first breath touches your cheek, that the west has begun to heave. To-morrow, perhaps, the pebbles would be rattling like shot, leaping ridge-high, and the advancing wave would fling them, round as cannon-balls, far up on the marsh in its rage.

“God help my boy,” she said again, like a prayer that would not be finished.

“God is as near him by sea as by land,” said Sir Richard, who had long practised speaking comfort as he practised command—plainly, and with faith in it.

“True,” she answered; “but I am a lone mother; and one that has no heart just now but to go home and pray.”

So she went on up the lane, the muffler drawn close, the maid’s steps small behind her. And all that night, between her prayers, she listened—listened as women listen who have fed children and watched fevers and stood with candle in a doorway for the sound of late feet—until the low thunder of the surge was swallowed, long before sunrise, in the nearer thunder of the storm itself.

And where was Amyas, while his mother counted the sea’s voice as if it were beads?

Amyas was sitting bareheaded in the stern of a boat in Smerwick Bay, the spray whistling through his curls, his teeth shining as he laughed into the gale.

“Pull, and with a will, my merry men all!” he shouted. “And never mind shipping a sea. Cannon-balls are a cargo that don’t spoil by taking salt-water.”

Christmas Eve had been one of those black, still nights which feel too warm for winter, as if the year were holding its breath. But now the western gale had been roaring for twelve hours upon the Irish coast. The short light was failing fast. Behind him a line of billows leapt and broke into flying mist; beside him green foam-fringed pillars rushed up the black rock and fell again in cataracts of white, as if the cliffs themselves were being washed and scoured for judgment. Before him the bay lay deep and sheltered—yet even that shelter was only a pause, a bowl held under a pouring roof.

For out at sea, four miles off and more, began a slope of thick gray cloud, like a low ceiling laid upon the world. It stretched over their heads, inland and away, cutting the cliffs at mid-height and hiding all the Kerry mountains. Under that awful roof the storm howled without stopping, sweeping before it the gray salt spray and great sponges of foam until land and sea alike were hazy, dim, dun—no horizon, no distance, only a roaring nearness.

Let it howl. There was more than weather at work. Along the sand-hills, in the evening gloom, red sparks flashed that never fell from heaven. The fort which the invaders, with their golden dreams, had christened Fort del Oro, held San Josepho and eight hundred of the foe; and over it flaunted the hated golden flag of Spain. Three nights ago Amyas and Yeo and the rest of Winter’s shrewdest hands had slung four culverins out of the Admiral’s main deck, floated them ashore, and dragged them up to the battery among the sand-hills. Now the question was plain: could Spanish and Italian condottieri hold their own on British ground against men of Devon?

Small blame to Amyas if, for that hour, he thought less of Burrough Court than of those quick bright flashes—sand-hill answering fort, fort answering sand-hill—where Salvation Yeo laid his eighteen-pound shot with deadly patience. He watched, with that cool bitter smile of triumph that sits strangely upon an honest face, the sand fly from the gabions as if the earth itself were being peppered, and the wickerwork crack and spill. The battery was out of ball. For four hours they had been firing stones, as hungry men chew crusts when meat is gone.

Now Amyas’s boat ran in through the surf at a cove where landing could be dared. They drove her up as if they cared not whether she staved, and scrambled over the sand-hills with each man carrying a brace of shot slung across his shoulder, the cold iron wet and heavy, chafing the straps, each step sucking and sliding in sand that would not hold. Into the trenches Amyas leapt, and called out to Yeo with a cheer that tasted of Devon ale and Christmas mirth even there, among salt and powder.

“More food for the bull-dogs, Gunner, and plums for the Spaniards’ Christmas pudding!”

“Don’t speak to a man at his business, Master Amyas,” Yeo growled, without lifting his eye from his work. “Five mortal times have I missed; but I will have that accursed Popish rag down, as I’m a sinner.”

“Down with it, then; nobody wants you to shoot crooked. Take good iron to it, and not footy paving-stones.”

“I believe, sir,” said Yeo, and his voice had that obstinate superstition sailors carry like a knife in their pocket, “that the foul fiend is there, a-turning of my shot aside, I do. I thought I saw him once: but, thank Heaven, here’s ball again. Ah, sir, if one could but cast a silver one! Now, stand by, men!”

The great gun spoke—one blunt, belly-deep roar, and then the hiss and rush of the shot. Amyas’s heart leapt in him with it.

And glory! The great yellow flag of Spain, streaming in the gale, lifted clean into the air, flagstaff and all, as if the very wind had taken it by the throat; then it pitched wildly down headforemost, far to leeward.

A hurrah broke from the sailors, and the soldiers in the other camp answered it; the sound rolled up under the cloud-roof like a wave striking stone. But before the echoes died, a tall officer sprang upon the fort’s parapet with the fallen flag in his hand. He reared it upon his lance-point and held it there against the gale while, within, they raised a new staff.

A dozen long-bows were bent in an instant—cords creaking, arrows nocked and drawn.

“Shame, lads!” Amyas shouted from behind. “Stop, and let the gallant gentleman have due courtesy!”

So they stopped, and Amyas, springing up on the battery’s rampart, took off his hat and bowed. The Spaniard—or Italian—held his post until relieved; then, free of his charge, he returned the bow with the same grave politeness, and went down.

By this time darkness had thickened. Firing slackened on all sides. Salvation and his brother gunners covered up their slaughtering tackle with tarpaulins and retired, and the men, after their scant supper of biscuit—provisions running so short that each mouthful seemed to accuse the next—lay down under arms among the sand-hills and grumbled themselves to sleep. The wind tore at their cloaks; the sand crept into their collars and teeth; and now and then, as a gust shifted, you could hear the fort’s life too—distant shouts muffled by storm, a hammer’s tap, the squeal of a pulley.

Amyas had volunteered the watch till midnight. He paced up and down in the gusty darkness, exchanging a word now and then with the sentinel, while the world seemed made only of three things: the low boom of surf beyond the spit, the nearer moan of wind through sand-grass, and the faint clink of arms as men slept with hands upon their weapons.

After an hour or more two men came into the battery, talking busily together. One wore complete armor, that made a dull music of metal when he moved; the other was wrapped in the plain short cloak of a man of pens and peace. Yet their talk was neither of culverin nor sally, but of English hexameters—of metres, of syllables, of the very shape of speech, as if words too were weapons to be set upon a parapet.

Amyas listened, puzzled, half amused, half vexed that any man could find sport in such matters while the storm and the Spaniard were so near. But the two talked on: of learned fashions at court and college; of men who would force the queen’s English to march in foreign shoes; of lines that hopped and stumbled upon the tongue like a lame dog over stones.

The armored man’s voice was quick, bold, impatient—Raleigh’s voice, with laughter in it like a knife’s flash. The cloaked man’s speech had a softer, grave cadence—Spenser’s, the secretary’s, full of thought that seemed to weigh on him like wet wool.

Raleigh was railing, half in jest and half in earnest, at those who would “reform” English poetry by making it ape the ancients, as if the old land had not its own tune. Spenser defended, then faltered, then defended again; and Raleigh tossed him a new taunt as a cook tosses a pancake—careless, dexterous, sure it will land.

“Tut, tut, Colin Clout,” Raleigh said at last, “much learning has made thee mad. A good old fishwives’ ballad jingle is worth all your sapphics and trimeters—hey? do you mind that precious verse?”

“But, dear Wat, Homer and Virgil—”

“But, dear Ned, Petrarch and Ovid—”

“But, Wat, what have we that we do not owe to the ancients?”

“Ancients, quotha? Why, the legend of King Arthur, and Chevy Chase too, which even your fellow-sinner Sidney cannot deny stirs his heart like a trumpet-blast though he hear it from a blind fiddler. Speak well of the bridge that carries you over, man! Did you find your Redcross Knight in Virgil, or such a dame as Una in old Ovid? No more than you did your Pater and Credo, you renegado baptized heathen, you!”

Spenser answered with something about “divine antiquity,” and “imitate afar,” and Raleigh laughed him down, declaring that if Homer was blind, then by that rule Spenser ought to poke out his own eye; and that hexameters on English roads went twitching and stumbling “like a three-legged terrier over a pebble-bank.”

Amyas, as he paced, began, half to warm his own courage and half because Christmas makes the tongue remember old sweetness, to sing under his breath. The wind snatched at the tune, carrying bits of it away and bringing others back. Yet the words held, simple and stubborn, like a hearth-song that will not be blown out:

“As Joseph was a-walking
He heard an angel sing—
‘This night shall be the birth-night
Of Christ, our heavenly King.

His birth-bed shall be neither
In housen nor in hall,
Nor in the place of paradise,
But in the oxen’s stall.

He neither shall be rocked
In silver nor in gold,
But in the wooden manger
That lieth on the mould.

He neither shall be washen
With white wine nor with red,
But with the fair spring water
That on you shall be shed.

He neither shall be clothed
In purple nor in pall,
But in the fair white linen
That usen babies all.’

As Joseph was a-walking
Thus did the angel sing,
And Mary’s Son at midnight
Was born to be our King.

Then be you glad, good people,
At this time of the year;
And light you up your candles,
For His star it shineth clear.”

Raleigh stopped short, as if struck by the plainness of it, and called out in the darkness with a harsh tenderness he would not confess.

“There, Edmunde Classicaster! Does not that simple strain go nearer to the heart of him who wrote The Shepherd’s Calendar than all your outlandish ‘Wote ye why his mother with a veil hath covered his face?’ Why dost not answer, man?”

There was a pause. Even the wind seemed for an instant to listen, as it does when it changes its note among the grass.

Then Spenser spoke, quietly, with shame in it.

“Because I was thinking rather of the rhymer than the rhyme. Good heaven! how that brave lad shames me—singing here the hymns which his mother taught him, before the very muzzles of Spanish guns; instead of bewailing, as I have done, the love I held, I doubt not, as dear as he holds his. This is his welcome to the winter’s storm; while I, who dream of heavenly inspiration, can but see therein an image of mine own cowardly despair. Pah! away with frosts, icicles, and tears, and sighs—”

“And with hexameters and trimeters too, I hope,” Raleigh cut in. “And all the trickeries of self-pleasing sorrow.”

“I will set my heart to higher work,” Spenser said, as if making a vow to himself, “than barking at the hand which chastens me.”

“Wilt put the lad into the Faerie Queene, then, by my side?” Raleigh cried. “He deserves as good a place there as ever a Guyon, or even Lord Grey your Arthegall. Let us hail him. Hallo! young chanticleer of Devon! Art not afraid of a chance shot, that thou crowest so lustily upon thine own mixen?”

“Cocks crow all night long at Christmas, Captain Raleigh, and so do I,” Amyas called back cheerfully. “But who’s there with you?”

“A penitent pupil of yours—Mr. Secretary Spenser.”

“Pupil of mine?” Amyas said, laughing. “I wish he’d teach me a little of his art; I could fill up my time here with making verses.”

“And who would be your theme, fair sir?” Spenser asked.

“No ‘who’ at all,” Amyas answered, honest as bread. “I don’t want to make sonnets to blue eyes, nor black either: but if I could put down some of the things I saw in the Spice Islands—”

“Ah,” Raleigh said, and Amyas could hear him grin in the dark, “he would beat you out of Parnassus, Mr. Secretary. Remember, you may write about Fairyland, but he has seen it.”

“And so have others,” Spenser replied. “It is not so far off from any one of us. Wherever is love and loyalty, great purposes, and lofty souls, even though in a hovel or a mine, there is Fairyland.”

“Then Fairyland should be here, friend,” Raleigh said. “For you represent love, and Leigh loyalty; while, as for great purposes and lofty souls, who so fit to stand for them as I, being—as my enemies and my conscience swear—ambitious and proud as Lucifer’s own self?”

“Ah, Walter, Walter,” Spenser murmured, “why wilt always slander thyself thus?”

“Slander? Tut.—I but give the world fair warning. Ask Leigh here—he has but known me a fortnight—whether I am not vain as a peacock, selfish as a fox, imperious as a bona roba, and ready to make a cat’s paw of any man if there be a chestnut in the fire; and yet the poor fool cannot help loving me, running my errands, taking my schemes and dreams for gospel, and believing that I shall be the man in the moon some day, and he my big dog.”

“Well,” Amyas said, half apologetic, for he could not bear a friend’s self-mocking, “if you are the cleverest man in the world what harm in my thinking so?”

Raleigh’s laugh snapped.

“Hearken to him, Edmund! He will know better when he has outgrown this callow trick of honesty, and learnt of the great goddess Detraction how to show himself wiser than the wise by pointing out the fool’s motley peeping through the philosopher’s cloak. Go to, lad! slander thy equals, envy thy betters; pray for an eye that sees spots in every sun, and for a vulture’s nose to scent carrion in every rose-bed. If thy friend win a battle, say he threw away men; if he lose, hint he sold it; if he rise, argue favor; if he fall, argue justice. Believe nothing, hope nothing, but endure all things—even to kicking—if aught may be got thereby; so shalt thou be clothed in purple and fine linen, and sit in kings’ palaces, and fare sumptuously every day.”

“And wake with Dives in the torment,” Amyas answered. “Thank you for nothing, captain.”

“Go to, Misanthropos,” Spenser said, reproving Raleigh with a quiet firmness. “Thou hast not yet tasted the sweets of this world’s comfits, and thou railest at them?”

“The grapes are sour, lad,” Raleigh muttered.

“And will be to the end,” Amyas said, “if they come off such a devil’s tree as that. I really think you are out of your mind, Captain Raleigh, at times.”

“I wish I were,” Raleigh returned; “for it is a troublesome, hungry, windy mind as man ever was cursed withal. But come in, lad. We were sent from the lord deputy to bid thee to supper. There is a dainty lump of dead horse waiting for thee.”

“Send me some out, then,” Amyas said, matter-of-fact as if the talk had been of nets and tides. “And tell his lordship that, with his good leave, I don’t stir from here till morning, if I can keep awake. There is a stir in the fort, and I expect them out on us.”

“Tut, man! their hearts are broken. We know it by their deserters.”

“Seeing’s believing. I never trust runaway rogues. If they are false to their masters, they’ll be false to us.”

“Well, go thy ways, old honesty,” Raleigh called, half admiring and half vexed. “And Mr. Secretary shall give you a book to yourself in the Faerie Queene—‘Sir Monoculus, or the Legend of Common Sense,’ eh, Edmund?”

“Monoculus?” Spenser echoed, amused despite himself.

“Ay, Single-eye, my prince of word-coiners—won’t that fit? Give him the Cyclops head for a device. Heigh-ho! They may laugh that win. I am sick of this Irish work; were it not for the chance of advancement I’d sooner be driving a team of red Devons on Dartside; and now I am angry with the dear lad because he is not sick of it too. What plague business has he to be paddling up and down, contentedly doing his duty, like any city watchman? It is an insult to the mighty aspirations of our nobler hearts—eh, my would-be Ariosto?”

“Ah, Raleigh!” Spenser’s voice softened. “You can afford to confess yourself less than some, for you are greater than all. Go on and conquer, noble heart! But as for me, I sow the wind, and I suppose I shall reap the whirlwind.”

“Your harvest seems come already; what a blast that was!” Raleigh said, as a gust struck them. “Hold on by me, Colin Clout, and I’ll hold on by thee. So! Don’t tread on that pikeman’s stomach, lest he take thee for a marauding Don, and with sudden dagger slit Colin’s pipe and Colin’s weasand too.”

Their footsteps and their voices stumbled away into the dark, leaving Amyas to his pacing, with the wind for company and the Spanish fort for neighbor.

He turned Raleigh’s wild words over and over, as a housewife turns a cake on the griddle to see if it is burning. There was, he began to think, some strange savor in cleverness that sat ill upon the stomach; some sourness hidden in the sweetest comfits. He thanked God he was neither scholar, courtier, nor poet—only a plain man who could hear a carol and feel it warm him.

The promised “dainty” came at last: a lump of horseflesh, boiled or stewed till it had given up what little richness it owned. The smell rose blunt and rank in the wet air; yet to hunger it was as if venison had been set down. Amyas said grace over it, ate it cleanly and with gratitude, and went back to his post. This time he kept his mouth shut, for the night was drawing on, and there was no need to tell the Spaniards that any man was awake and watching.

The hours went by, slow as a pot that will not boil. All lights were out in battery and shipping. The only sounds of living things were the monotonous tramp of the sentinels and, now and then, a grunt from the men sleeping under arms some twenty yards to the rear. The wind’s note changed continually, as if it were trying different pipes; it whistled through the sand-grass, moaned along the gun-barrels, and now and again dropped low enough that Amyas could hear, far off, the sea’s deeper breathing.

He began to think of his mother, and how she might be spending her Christmas—whether she had tasted even a mouthful, or only sat with folded hands and listened to the ridge’s thunder. He thought of Frank, and wondered what brave court festival his brother might be attending, amid bright lights and sweet music and gay ladies; how he was dressed; whether he remembered, even for a moment, the dark Atlantic shore. Amyas said his prayers and his creed. Then he tried not to think of Rose Salterne, and of course thought of her the more, as a man forbidden to taste will taste with his mind.

He paced to and fro, peering often over the strip of sand-hill that lay between him and the fort. All was blank and black. And then the rain began, furious and slanting, as if flung from buckets; it beat upon helmet and cloak, it hissed on the sand, it ran cold down Amyas’s neck and wrists, and the whole world smelled of wet salt and trampled grass.

Suddenly, amid the wind’s loud whispering, he caught another sound: a rustle in the harsh sand-grass—not altogether like the wind’s hand. Then a soft sliding noise, as of something that had slipped down a bank and drawn sand after it.

Amyas stopped. He crouched beside a gun, and laid his ear to the rampart. Through the rain and the storm he heard it clearly now: the cautious, broken rhythm of approaching feet—whether rabbits or Christians he could not yet tell; but his heart, trained by danger, guessed the latter.
