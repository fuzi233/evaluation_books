Once the slaughter ceased, Sir Henry and Good were borne into Twala's hut, where I followed close behind. We were a sorry sight; both men were utterly drained by the exertion and the loss of blood, and my own state was hardly better. Though I am naturally wiry and accustomed to enduring fatigue thanks to my lean frame and years of training, that night I was completely spent. Worse still, exhaustion triggered the old scar from the lion's claw, sending fresh waves of pain through me, while the blow to my head earlier that morning continued to throb violently. In truth, it would have been hard to find a more miserable trio than we were that evening. Our only solace was the grim realization that we were lucky enough to be alive to feel misery, rather than lying dead on the plain alongside the thousands of brave souls who had fallen that night, rising strong in the morning only to perish by sunset.

With the help of Foulata—who, grateful for having been spared by us, had become our devoted handmaiden, particularly attending to Good—we managed to remove the chain mail shirts that had undoubtedly saved our lives. As I feared, the flesh beneath was a mess of terrible contusions. While the steel links had stopped weapons from penetrating, they had failed to prevent severe bruising. Both Sir Henry and Good were covered in them, and I was far from clean either. Foulata brought us some pounded green leaves with a strong aroma; applied as a poultice, they offered significant relief. However, these bruises paled in comparison to the anxiety caused by our deeper wounds. Good had a gaping hole through the fleshy part of his "beautiful white leg," from which he had bled profusely. Sir Henry, besides other injuries, bore a deep slash across his jaw inflicted by Twala's battle-axe.

Fortunately, Good proved to be a capable surgeon. As soon as his small medical kit was produced, he meticulously cleaned the wounds and stitched them up with surprising competence, despite the dim light cast by the primitive Kukuana lamps. He then generously coated the injuries with an antiseptic ointment from his supply and bandaged them with the remnants of our pocket-handkerchiefs. Meanwhile, Foulata prepared a rich broth, for we were too weary to eat solid food. We drank it greedily and collapsed onto piles of magnificent *karrosses* (fur rugs) scattered across the dead king's great hut.

By a cruel twist of fate, it was on Twala's own couch, wrapped in his personal rug, that Sir Henry—the man who had slain him—spent that night. I say "slept," but after such a day, sleep was elusive. The air was thick with farewells to the dying and mournings for the dead. From every direction came the wailing of women whose husbands, sons, and brothers had perished. No wonder they cried; over twelve thousand men, nearly a fifth of the Kukuana army, had been destroyed in that horrific struggle. Listening to their grief was heart-rending, forcing me to grasp the full horror of the carnage wrought in the name of human ambition.

Towards midnight, the ceaseless crying subsided slightly, until the silence was broken only occasionally by a long, piercing howl from a hut directly behind ours. Later, I learned this was Gagool "keening" over the dead king. After that, I managed a fitful rest, waking repeatedly with a jolt, reliving the terrifying events of the last twenty-four hours. Sometimes I saw the warrior I had felled charging down the mountain; other times, I found myself back in the glorious ring of Greys making their immortal stand on the little mound; and again, I saw Twala's plumed, gory head rolling past my feet, mouth agape and eyes blazing.

When dawn finally broke, I found my companions had slept no better than I. Good was running a high fever and quickly became light-headed. To my alarm, he began spitting blood—a sure sign of internal injury sustained when a Kukuana warrior had tried to drive his massive spear through his chain armor. Sir Henry, however, seemed relatively fresh despite his facial wound, which made eating difficult and laughing impossible due to the pain and stiffness.

Around eight o'clock, Infadoos visited us. The tough old warrior looked surprisingly unharmed by the battle, though he admitted to staying awake all night. He was delighted to see us but deeply concerned about Good's condition, shaking our hands warmly. I noted that he addressed Sir Henry with a peculiar reverence, treating him almost as a deity. Indeed, as we later discovered, the great Englishman was viewed throughout Kukuanaland as a supernatural being. Soldiers claimed no man could have fought as he did, or slain Twala—the strongest warrior in the country—in single combat, shearing through the king's bull neck in one stroke. That strike became proverbial; any extraordinary feat of strength was thereafter known as "Incubu's blow."

Infadoos also reported that all of Twala's regiments had submitted to Ignosi, and similar surrenders were trickling in from outlying chiefs. Twala's death at Sir Henry's hands had ended all prospects of rebellion, for Scragga, his only legitimate son, was already dead, leaving no rival claimant. I remarked that Ignosi had risen to power through blood. The old chief shrugged. "Yes," he replied. "But the Kukuana people can only remain calm by letting their blood flow sometimes. Many are killed, yes, but the women survive, and others will soon grow up to replace the fallen. After this, the land will be quiet for a while."

Later that morning, Ignosi himself paid us a visit, wearing the royal diadem. As he advanced with kingly dignity, flanked by an obsequious guard, I couldn't help but recall the tall Zulu who had approached us at Durban months prior, seeking employment, and reflect on the strange turns of fortune.

"Hail, O king!" I said, rising.

"Yes, Macumazahn. King at last, by the might of your three right hands," he answered readily.

All seemed to be going well. He hoped to host a great feast within two weeks to display himself to the people. When I asked about Gagool, he declared her the evil genius of the land, promising to kill her and all the witch-doctors with her. She had lived so long that no one remembered her youth, and she had trained the witch-hunters, making the land wicked in the eyes of heaven.

"Yet she knows much," I countered. "It is easier to destroy knowledge, Ignosi, than to gather it."

"That is true," he said thoughtfully. "Only she knows the secret of the 'Three Witches' yonder, where the great road leads, where the kings are buried and the Silent Ones sit. Yes, and the diamonds are there. Do not forget your promise, Ignosi; you must lead us to the mines, even if you must spare Gagool alive to guide the way."

"I will not forget, Macumazahn, and I will consider your words."

After Ignosi departed, I checked on Good, finding him in a delirium. The fever from his wound had taken hold, compounded by his internal injury. His condition remained critical for several days. I am convinced that without Foulata's tireless nursing, he would have died. Women are women, regardless of color, yet it felt strangely poignant to watch this dusky beauty tend to the fevered man day and night, performing the merciful duties of a sickroom with the instinct of a trained nurse.

For the first couple of nights, I and Sir Henry attempted to assist, but Foulata resented our interference. She insisted we leave him alone, claiming our movements disturbed him, which I suspected was true. She watched him constantly, feeding him a native cooling drink made of milk infused with tulip bulb juice, and shielding him from flies. Night after night, by the flickering light of our lamp, I saw the scene: Good tossing restlessly, his features gaunt, eyes wide and luminous, babbling nonsense; and beside him, seated on the floor, the soft-eyed Kukuana beauty. Her face, worn from vigil, was animated by infinite compassion—or perhaps something deeper?

We feared he would die for two days, moving about with heavy hearts. Only Foulata refused to accept it. "He will live," she insisted.

A circle of silence surrounded the hut for over three hundred yards, as the king had ordered everyone nearby removed to ensure no noise reached the sick man. On the fifth day of his illness, I went to check on him before retiring. Entering carefully, the lamp revealed Good lying perfectly still, tossing no more. Had it come at last? Bitterness choked me, and I gave a sob-like gasp.

"Hush—h—h!" came from the dark shadow behind Good's head.

Creeping closer, I saw he wasn't dead, but sleeping soundly, with Foulata's slender fingers clasped tightly in his pale hand. The crisis had passed; he would live. He slept like that for eighteen hours. I hesitate to mention it, for fear of disbelief, but during the entire time, this devoted girl sat by his side, terrified that if she moved or withdrew her hand, he would wake. The suffering she endured from cramps, exhaustion, and hunger is unknowable, but when he finally awoke, she had to be carried away; her limbs were so stiff she could not move them.
