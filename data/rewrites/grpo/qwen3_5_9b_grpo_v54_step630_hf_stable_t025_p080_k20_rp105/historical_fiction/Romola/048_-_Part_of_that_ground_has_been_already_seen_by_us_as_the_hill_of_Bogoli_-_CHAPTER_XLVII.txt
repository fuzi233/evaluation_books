Tito's intricate schemes had been thwarted not by grand designs, but by the petty, unpredictable accidents that no mastermind can foresee. It was rare for him to stroll with Romola in the evening, yet fate had conspired to place them together precisely when her presence was a liability. Life proved to be a labyrinthine game where calculated moves were constantly undone by chance, as fragile as falling dandelion seeds.

Tito did not resent the collapse of Spini's plot; rather, he grappled with the delicate art of deception required to navigate his warnings. To alert Savonarola without arousing suspicion among the populace was one challenge; to manage Dolfo Spini without triggering the fury of a cornered beast was another. If he rushed to the monastery to warn the friar immediately after delivering the forged letters, the proximity of the acts would invite dangerous speculation. Conversely, warning Spini instantly would require confessing the truth—that Savonarola had altered his course—and Tito knew Spini well enough to understand that such honesty would only confirm Tito's treachery, painting him as a man who had "turned round" and betrayed his allies. Yet, delaying the warning to Savonarola until morning risked losing the window to stop Spini's enraged band of Compagnacci from returning in defeat. Weighing these dangers, Tito chose the lesser evil: he would soothe Spini with lies, attributing the failure solely to the friar's caution, while accepting the risk of missing the critical moment to save Savonarola.

Annoyed and irritable, Tito found himself unable to maintain his usual composure. Determined to avoid further confrontation with Romola, he abandoned his home that night and vanished into the city's shadows.

Inside, Romola remained awake, her clothes untouched, as the rain intensified outside. She found solace in the storm, viewing the tempestuous heavens as a divine barrier against human malice, forcing men to stand still. Yet, her own mind was a battleground. Doubt gnawed at her regarding Tito's fidelity, compounded by a terrifying uncertainty about her own actions. What falsehoods might he have whispered to her? What hidden agendas might she remain ignorant of? Every person who trusted Tito now stood in peril, and her attempts to convince herself otherwise felt futile. Was she not acting out of pride when she hesitated to warn others against him? "If my husband is a criminal, my place is beside him in prison"—this thought offered a grim comfort, yet it left a burning question unanswered: Could a wife allow her spouse to commit atrocities when she possessed the power to intervene? Prayer felt impossible; her mind, agitated by frantic speculation, refused the stillness required for faith.

As the night wore on, the storm's violence waned, the clouds dispersed, and a clear morning broke over Florence. Paradoxically, the clarity only deepened her torment. Wrapping her mantle tightly, she rushed to the loggia, scanning the vast landscape as if some external sign could dictate her next move, though she saw only rooftops concealing the streets where Savonarola might walk toward ruin.

Could she summon her godfather to block the gates without revealing specific details? It seemed too late. Distress tightened her chest; she realized she had gleaned no crucial information from Tito, and the hour had long passed seven. There was no alternative but to proceed to San Marco.

She descended the stairs with urgency, ignoring the sick who awaited her care, and hurried along the Via de' Bardi toward the Ponte Vecchio. She chose the path through the city's heart, the most direct route, hoping that in the great Piazza she might intercept her husband. Perhaps, by some miracle, he could reassure her of the friar's safety, rendering her journey to San Marco unnecessary. Upon reaching the courtyard of the Palazzo Vecchio, she peered eagerly into the pillared space and scanned the square, but the figure she had once loved and now feared was absent. Undeterred, she pressed on toward the Piazza del Duomo.

The square buzzed with life: worshippers ascending the marble steps, men pausing for conversation, vendors laden with goods. Amidst this throng, Romola spotted her husband. He had diverted from San Marco to Nello's shop and now leaned casually against the doorpost. As she drew near, she observed him with a relaxed demeanor, holding his cap, shaking back his freshly combed hair. The stark contrast between his effortless ease and the agony he had inflicted upon her convulsed her with indignation; the vision of his cold hardness intensified her dread. She recognized Cronaca and two other regulars of San Marco standing nearby. A sudden resolve flashed through her mind: "I will force him to speak before these witnesses."

Her light step carried her swiftly toward him before he could retreat, just as Cronaca announced, "Here comes Madonna Romola."

A jolt ran through Tito as he faced his wife. She appeared haggard from sleepless vigilance, yet her eyes burned with a intensity that went beyond mere worry.

"Has the Frate passed beyond the gates?" she demanded.

"No," Tito replied, feeling utterly powerless before her gaze, straining every ounce of his self-control to mask his fear with feigned surprise.

"And are you certain he is not departing?" she pressed.

"I am certain he is not going."

"That is sufficient," Romola declared, turning up the steps to seek refuge within the Duomo, needing the sanctuary of the church to steady her trembling hands.

Tito watched her retreat, a sensation of hatred rising within him that bordered on the abyss. Among the crowd gathered in the square were not only devoted followers of the friar but also Ser Ceccone, the notary. Like Tito, Ceccone was secretly an agent of the Mediceans.

Ser Francesco di Ser Barone, known infamously as Ser Ceccone, was neither learned nor handsome, nor successful, and possessed no generosity. He was a traitor devoid of charm, a fact that ensured he held no affection for Tito Melema.
