Messer Bernardo del Nero held firm, just as Romola had predicted: his advice that the marriage between Tito and Romola ought to be deferred until Easter was unshakable, and on this point the blind scholar Bardo submitted entirely to the sway of his sagacious, practical old friend.

Yet Bernardo, who had never once felt the soft pull of Tito’s personal charm that had wrapped so many others around the young Greek’s finger, could not quite resist the undeniable argument of worldly success that carries such weight with seasoned men of society. Tito was advancing at a remarkable pace through Florence’s highest circles. He had won particular favour with the young Cardinal Giovanni de’ Medici, who had even mentioned naming Tito to his learned retinue for a forthcoming journey to Rome. That bright, quick-witted young Greek, whose tongue was always ready but never quarrelsome, grew more welcome by the day at lavish late-night suppers in the Medici’s Via Larga palace, and at Florentine games where he never pretended to outshine the regular masters, but could praise Piero de’ Medici’s incomparable skill with such effortless grace that every onlooker warmed to him. By that unbroken rule of elite social life, Tito’s reputation as a delightful companion in “magnificent” high society made his existing scholarly talents shine all the brighter; he was accomplished enough that no overinflated praise of his gifts ever exposed him to public embarrassment.

Messer Bernardo’s old loyalties and long-held attachments soon began to wear down that faint, lingering new prejudice against the too-supple young stranger from abroad. For a lifelong Florentine of his standing, it was impossible to dismiss the credential of being fully accepted by the city’s most respected families, the small circle whose shared judgment was the undisputed standard of social worth. It began to feel irrational to pretend there was any flaw in the prospect of such a man as a son-in-law for Bardo, and a husband for Romola. No one could deny that Tito’s arrival had dawned a whole new life for the blind scholar and his daughter, and the reality had outstripped even the brightest early hopes.

Bardo, whose unflinching pride and devotion to truth would never let him take part in the petty game of absurd, performative admiration and equally absurd vicious backbiting that made up almost the whole of learned social intercourse in Florence, had long ago fallen into neglect even among his fellow citizens. For years, common gossip had held that while his blindness and the loss of his son Dino were pitiable misfortunes, he was tedious for harping on the value of his own life’s work, and his discontent was inconsistent for a man who had openly disregarded religious rites, and turned down multiple patronage offers back in the day that asked only that he take holy orders — a small condition, then, since there was scarcely any other easy way for patrons to support a scholar’s work.

But since Tito’s arrival, the narrative tied to Bardo’s name was no longer one of unbroken quiet gloom. Word spread that the old man had stopped his endless plaints, and his fair daughter would no longer be trapped in lonely, dowerless pride waiting for a noble arranged marriage match. Tito’s winning manners and rising favour made that new public interest in the old household warm and genuine, so that visitors to the quiet Via de’ Bardi library were no longer a rare novelty. Elderly acquaintances drifted back, prompted by that vague urge to reconnect with an old friend everyone was suddenly talking about again. Young men Tito had once invited to visit found it easy to drop by unannounced later, when they caught him on his way to the Bardi house, slinging an arm over his shoulder to fall into easy, meandering chat. They came to gaze on Romola’s famed beauty: to see her live up to the old writer Firenzuola’s ideal of womanly majesty, “sitting with a certain grandeur, speaking with gravity, smiling with modesty, and casting around, as it were, an odour of queenliness.” She unfolded there like a strong white lily under the genial breath of admiration and homage, and all this new public warmth felt no different to her than the bright, glowing new life Tito’s love had already given her.

Tito had even stoked the dearest, long-buried hope in Bardo’s breast: that before he died, he might secure his beloved library against being absorbed into some other man’s private collection, against being handed over to a monastery and renamed for the order that housed it, so it would stand forever as the Bardi Library, open for the use of all Florentines. The old generation’s instinct to trust the Medici, whose influence was still the most powerful lever in the Florentine state, ran too deep to fade, and Tito, who already had the ear of Cardinal Giovanni de’ Medici, could do far more even than Bernardo to win that desired patronage: he could stand before a crowd of learned elites and lay out the unique, irreplaceable value of Bardo’s collection for all to see. Tito spoke of this future with bright, sanguine certainty, happy to cheer the old man on, and he knew full well Romola repaid those gentle kindnesses to her father with a kind of adoration no direct compliment to herself could ever win.

The question of the library was the subject of many quiet, private discussions between Bernardo and Tito after Christmas, when the prospect of the fast-approaching marriage drew closer — always well out of Bardo’s hearing. For the old man clung to a vague, unchallenged belief that all his property apart from the library was more than enough to meet every demand on his household. He would never, except in a moment of sharp, angry despair, admit to himself that the will he had written disinheriting Dino had left Romola the heir to nothing but debts, and that all he asked from patronage, in exchange for gifting his library to the Florentine Republic, was a guarantee it would be assigned its own permanent, separate home.

“My judgment is this,” Bernardo said to Romola, during a quiet consultation they held under the household loggia. “Since the marriage is settled, and Messer Tito has a solid, competent income of his own, we ought to begin winding up all your father’s old affairs now, to calculate exactly how much sum is needed to save the library entirely from being touched by creditors, rather than letting the old debts accumulate any longer. Your father needs nothing more than his daily small shred of mutton and his plate of macaroni to live comfortably, and I think Messer Tito has agreed to supply that for whatever years he has left, in place of the traditional morgen-cap wedding gift.”

“Tito has always known my life is bound up with my father’s,” Romola said softly. “And he is better to my father than I am: he delights in making him happy.”

“Ah, he is not made of the same common clay as other men, is he?” Bernardo said, smiling dryly. “Thy father thought to shut all woman’s folly out of thee by cramming thee full of Greek and Latin, but thou wert as ready to believe in the first pair of bright eyes and the first soft words that crossed thy path, as if thou couldst recite nothing but Paternosters like every other ordinary Christian girl.”

“Now, godfather,” Romola said, shaking her head playfully, “as if it were only bright eyes and soft words that made me love Tito! You know better. You know I love my father and you because you are both good, and I love Tito too because he is good. I see it, I feel it, in everything he says and does. And if he is handsome, too, why should I not love him the better for that? It seems to me beauty is part of the finished language by which goodness speaks. You know you must have been a very handsome youth, godfather” — she looked up with one of her happy, loving smiles at the stately old man — “you were about as tall as Tito, and you had very fine eyes; only you looked a little sterner and prouder, and —”

“And Romola likes to keep all the pride to herself?” Bernardo cut in, not immune to the pretty coaxing. “Still, it is well that in one way Tito’s demands are far more modest than any proper Florentine husband of fitting rank we should ever have found for you: he wants no dowry at all.”

That was the agreement settled between Messer Bernardo del Nero, Romola, and Tito. Bardo assented with a dismissive wave of the hand when Bernardo told him he thought it would be wise to start selling off small bits of property to clear the old debts; he had always thought of property and debts as a kind of thick tangled wood his blind imagination never once penetrated, let alone navigated. And Tito set about winning Messer Bernardo’s full respect, throwing his quick, ready faculties into mastering all the fine points of Florentine money matters: the inner workings of the public funds known as the Monti, the market value of real estate, the ins and outs of banking profit.

“You will soon forget that Tito is not a native Florentine, godfather,” Romola said brightly. “See how he is learning every last thing about Florence.”

“It seems to me he is one of the demoni, the spirits of no particular country at all, child,” Bernardo said, smiling. “His mind is a little too nimble to be weighted down with all the heavy, messy old loyalties and memories we ordinary men carry tucked in our hearts.” Romola smiled back, wrapped in unshakable, happy confidence.
