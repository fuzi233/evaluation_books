All loyal Britons, when they read what Mr. George Warrington did—or did not do—towards a gallant and gracious Prince, felt that special kind of hot, respectful indignation which rises like strong mustard to the nose. Here was the beloved son of the best of monarchs, Captain-General of the British army, graciously offering favour—preferment—one of those gilded steps by which a young man climbs, not merely to fortune, but to the public gaze; and here was a colonial gentleman, with Virginia acres in his pocket and book-dust on his cuffs, quietly putting the honour aside as though it were a dish over-salted.

Will Esmond, whose tongue carried oaths as a kitchen carries knives—ready to hand, keen, and used without ceremony—lavished his fiercest upon his cousin’s conduct. It pleased him mightily that the confounded young Mohock was cutting his own throat with his own obstinacy, and he said so with a relish, as if he had bitten into something sharp and triumphant.

My Lord Castlewood, tasting the quarrel as a man tastes a sauce—coolly, to find what it is made of—observed that a savage gentleman had a right to scalp himself if he liked; or perhaps, he added with a charitable shrug, Mr. Warrington had heard enough of the war-whoop at Braddock’s affair and had no more stomach for fighting.

Mr. Will rejoiced that the younger brother had gone to the deuce, and rejoiced to think the elder was following him. The first time he met the fellow, he vowed, he would take care Mr. George should know what he thought of him.

“If you intend to insult George,” cried Lady Maria, “at least you had best take care that his brother Harry is out of hearing!”

We may imagine what fresh maledictions leapt then from Mr. Will regarding his twin kinsfolk.

“Ta, ta, ta!” says my lord, tapping the air, as if calling the household to order like a maître d’hôtel silencing a too-noisy table. “No more of this squabbling! We can’t be all warriors in the family!”

“I never heard your lordship laid claim to be one!” says Maria.

“Never, my dear; quite the contrary!” returns his lordship. “Will is our champion, and one is quite enough in the house. So I dare say with the two Mohocks—George is the student, and Harry is the fighting man. When you intended to quarrel, Will, what a pity it was you had not George, instead of t’other, to your hand!”

“Your lordship’s hand is famous—at piquet,” says Will’s mother, who could prick as neatly with a phrase as with a pin.

“It is a pretty one,” says my lord, turning his fingers as though they were something to be appraised at market, with a simper. “My Lord Hervey’s glove and mine were of a size. Yes, my hand, as you say, is more fitted for cards than for war. Yours, my Lady Castlewood, is pretty dexterous too. How I bless the day when you bestowed it on my lamented father!”

In this play of sarcasm—as in certain games of skill—his lordship was never sorry to engage, having a cool head, and being able to beat his family all round, leaving them as nettled as if they had been stung by smoke.

Madame de Bernstein, when she heard of Mr. Warrington’s bevue, was exceedingly angry. She stormed and scolded her immediate household—one could almost hear her wrath before one saw her, like an ill-tuned drum in a narrow street. She would have scolded George too, but she was growing old, and had not the courage of her early days; and, what was more, she was a little afraid of her nephew, and respectful to him, as people sometimes are to the very person who has disobliged them—because that person is self-contained, and does not beg to be forgiven.

“You will never make your fortune at court, nephew!” she groaned, taking snuff as if it were the only seasoning left to her patience.

“It was never my wish, madam,” said Mr. George, very stately, as though he were laying a napkin smooth across his conscience.

“Your wish was to help Harry? You might hereafter have been of service to your brother, had you accepted the Duke’s offer. Princes do not love to have their favours refused, and I don’t wonder his Royal Highness was offended.”

“General Lambert said the same thing,” George confessed, colouring a little, as if heat had risen to his face from the hearth of memory. “And I see now that I was wrong. But you must please remember I had never seen a court before, and I suppose I am scarce likely to shine in one.”

“I think possibly not, my good nephew,” says the aunt, a dry verdict dropped as neatly as a pinch of salt.

“And what then?” asked George. “I never had ambition for that kind of glory, and can make myself quite easy without it. When his Royal Highness spoke to me—most kindly, as I own—my thought was, I shall make a very bad soldier, and my brother would be a very good one. He has a hundred good qualities for the profession, in which I am deficient; and would have served a commanding officer far better than I ever could. Say the Duke is in battle, and his horse is shot, as my poor chief’s was at home—would he not be better for a beast that had courage and strength to bear him anywhere, than with one that could not carry his weight?”

“Au fait. His Royal Highness’s charger must be a strong one, my dear!” says the old lady, with a lift of eyebrow.

“Expende Hannibalem,” mutters George, with a shrug. “Our Hannibal weighs no trifle.”

“I don’t quite follow you, sir, and your Hannibal,” the Baroness remarks, for Latin was to her what a foreign spice is to an English palate—known by name, suspected of virtue, but not understood.

“When Mr. Wolfe and Mr. Lambert remonstrated with me as you have done, madam,” George rejoined, laughing a little, “I made this same defence. I said I offered to the Prince the best soldier in the family, and the two gentlemen allowed that my blunder at least had some excuse. Who knows but that they may set me right with his Royal Highness? The taste I have had of battles has shown me how little my genius inclines that way. We saw the Scotch play which everybody is talking about t’other night; and when the hero, young Norval, said how he longed to follow to the field some warlike lord, I thought to myself, ‘how like my Harry is to him, except that he doth not brag.’ Harry is pining now for a red coat, and if we don’t mind will take the shilling. He has the map of Germany for ever under his eyes, and follows the King of Prussia everywhere, as a hungry boy follows the smell of a bakery. He is not afraid of men or gods. As for me, I love my books and quiet best, and to read about battles in Homer or Lucan.”

“Then what made a soldier of you at all, my dear? And why did you not send Harry with Mr. Braddock, instead of going yourself?” asked Madame de Bernstein.

“My mother loved her younger son the best,” said George, darkly—an old bitterness, like old coffee left too long on the grounds. “Besides, with the enemy invading our country, it was my duty, as the head of our family, to go on the campaign. Had I been a Scotchman twelve years ago, I should have been a—”

“Hush, sir! or I shall be more angry than ever!” said the old lady, with a perfectly pleased face; for there are women who rebuke treason as a duty, and enjoy the flavour of it all the same.

George’s explanation might appease Madame de Bernstein, whose principles, we fear, were not so tight-laced as her cap; but to the loyal heart of Sir Miles Warrington and his lady, the young man’s conduct gave a severe blow indeed—like a dish sent back untouched, not because it is ill-cooked, but because it is offered by the wrong hand.

“I should have thought,” her ladyship said, “from my sister Esmond Warrington’s letter, that my brother’s widow was a woman of good sense and judgment, and that she had educated her sons in a becoming manner. But what, Sir Miles—what, my dear Thomas Claypool—can we think of an education which has resulted so lamentably for both these young men?”

“The elder seems to know a power of Latin, though, and speaks the French and the German too. I heard him with the Hanover Envoy at the Baroness’s rout,” says Mr. Claypool. “The French he jabbered quite easy; and when he was at a loss for the High Dutch, he and the Envoy began in Latin, and talked away till all the room stared.”

“It is not language, but principles, Thomas Claypool!” exclaims the virtuous matron. “What must Mr. Warrington’s principles be, when he could reject an offer made him by his Prince? Can he speak the High Dutch? So much the more ought he to have accepted his Royal Highness’s condescension, and made himself useful in the campaign! Look at our son—look at Miles!”

“Hold up thy head, Miley, my boy!” says papa, with that hearty household voice which, when it sounds, sets the servants moving and the very spoons seeming to listen. “I trust, Sir Miles, that, as a member of the House of Commons, as an English gentleman, you will attend his Royal Highness’s levee to-morrow, and say, if such an offer had been made to us for that child, we would have taken it, though our boy is but ten years of age.”

“Faith, Miley, thou wouldst make a good little drummer or fifer!” says papa. “Shouldst like to be a little soldier, Miley?”

“Anything, sir, anything! a Warrington ought to be ready at any moment to have himself cut in pieces for his sovereign!” cries the matron, pointing to the boy, as if he were a joint offered up upon the altar of duty.

But the child, as soon as he comprehended his mother’s proposal, protested against it by a loud roar—one of those honest domestic noises, more true than speeches at a levee—midway through which he was carried off by Screwby, the house absorbing him again like a pantry door shutting on a squall.

In obedience to conjugal orders, Sir Miles went to his Royal Highness’s levee the next day, and made a protest of love and duty which the Prince deigned to accept, saying, “Nobody ever supposed that Sir Miles Warrington would ever refuse any place offered to him.”

A compliment gracious indeed—and repeated everywhere by Lady Warrington, as showing how implicitly the august family on the throne could rely on the loyalty of the Warringtons; she served it up to every visitor, warm again and again, till it was known in all her circles, as certain family dishes are, by repetition rather than novelty.

Accordingly, when this worthy couple saw George, they received him with a ghastly commiseration—such as dear relatives will sometimes extend when we have done something fatal or clumsy; when we have come badly out of a lawsuit; when we enter just as the company has been abusing us; when our banker has broke; or we have had to figure in the commercial columns of the London Gazette. Who does not know that face of pity—pity doled out like thin broth, with a look that says you have brought hunger on yourself?

It was not, indeed, just on Sir Miles’s part to turn upon George and be angry with his nephew for refusing the offer of promotion; for Sir Miles himself had agreed in George’s view of pursuing quite other than a military career, and it was in respect to this plan that Madam Esmond had written from Virginia. George had announced his intention of entering at the Temple, and qualifying himself for those magisterial and civil duties which, in the course of nature, he would be called to fulfil; nor could any one applaud his resolution more cordially than his uncle, who introduced him to a lawyer of reputation, under whose guidance we may fancy the young gentleman reading leisurely, with that patient appetite which belongs to studious minds—taking in clause and precedent as others take in a well-stewed dish, slowly, tasting the structure, noting what gives strength and what gives savour.

Madam Esmond from home signified approval, fully agreeing with Sir Miles (to whom and his lady she sent grateful remembrances) that the British Constitution was the envy of the world and the proper object of every English gentleman’s admiring study. The chief point to which George’s mother objected was the notion that Mr. Warrington should have to sit down in the Temple dinner-hall and cut at a shoulder of mutton, and drink small-beer out of tin pannikins, by the side of rough students who wore gowns like the parish-clerk. It was not the mutton—Virginia had made her no stranger to plain meat—but the company and the vessels: pewter and tin, like coarse words, seemed to her to rub away gentility by contact.

George’s loyal younger brother shared the repugnance. Anything was good enough for him, Harry said; he was a younger son, prepared to rough it. But George, in a gown, dining in a mess with three nobody’s sons off dirty pewter platters! Harry never could relish this condescension on his brother’s part, nor fancy George in his proper place at any table except the high one; and he was sorry that a plan Madam Esmond hinted at in her letters was not feasible—namely, that an application should be made to the Master of the Temple, to be informed that Mr. George Warrington was a gentleman of most noble birth, and of great property in America, and ought only to sit with the very best company in the hall.

Rather to Harry’s discomfiture, when he communicated his own and his mother’s ideas to the gentlemen’s new coffee-house friend, Mr. Spencer, Mr. Spencer received the proposal with roars of laughter, loud as the rattle of cups and saucers in a crowded room; and I cannot learn, from the Warrington papers, that any application was made to the Master of the Temple upon this subject.

Besides his literary and historical pursuits—which he loved best, and followed as others follow hunting or gaming—Mr. Warrington studied the laws of his country, attended the courts at Westminster, where he heard a Henley, a Pratt, a Murray, and those other famous schools of eloquence and patriotism, the two houses of parliament: rooms in which voices—trained, various, and powerful—made their own weather, and where a word, like a note struck true, could set a whole bench murmuring.

Gradually Mr. Warrington made acquaintance with some of the members of the House and the Bar; who, when they came to know him, spoke of him as a young gentleman of good parts and good breeding in terms so generally complimentary, that his good uncle’s heart relented towards him, and Dora and Flora began once more to smile upon him.

This reconciliation dated from the time when his Royal Highness the Duke, after having been defeated by the French in the affair of Hastenbeck, concluded the famous capitulation with the French, which his Majesty George II refused to ratify. His Royal Highness, as ’tis well known, flung up his commissions after this disgrace, laid down his commander’s baton—which, it must be confessed, he had not wielded with much luck or dexterity—and never again appeared at the head of armies or in public life.

The stout warrior would not allow a word of complaint against his father and sovereign to escape his lips; but as he retired with wounded honour, and as he would have no interest or authority more, nor any places to give, it may be supposed that Sir Miles Warrington’s anger against his nephew diminished as his respect for his Royal Highness diminished—resentment cooling when there was no longer any heat of patronage to keep it simmering.

As our two gentlemen were walking in St. James’s Park one day, with their friend Mr. Lambert, they met his Royal Highness in plain clothes and without a star, and made profound bows to the Prince, who was pleased to stop and speak to them. He asked Mr. Lambert how he liked my Lord Ligonier, his new chief at the Horse Guards, and the new duties in which he was engaged; and recognising the young men with that fidelity of memory for which his Royal race hath ever been remarkable, he said to Mr. Warrington, “You did well, sir, not to come with me when I asked you in the spring.”

“I was sorry, then, sir,” Mr. Warrington said, making a very low reverence, “but I am more sorry now.”

Upon which the Prince said, “Thank you, sir,” touched his hat, and walked away—the sound of his steps falling back into the Park’s general hush, mingling with the far-off wheel-rumble and the bird-notes, as if even royal displeasure, once passed, became only another tone in London’s great sound.

The circumstances of this interview, and the discourse which passed at it, being related to Mrs. Esmond Warrington in a letter from her younger son, created so deep an impression in that lady’s mind, that she narrated the anecdote many hundreds of times, until all her friends and acquaintances knew it and, perhaps, were tired of it; for memory, like a household recipe, is repeated not only because it delights, but because it proves something about the teller’s house and name.

Our gentlemen went through the Park and so towards the Strand, where they had business. And Mr. Lambert, pointing to the lion on the top of the Earl of Northumberland’s house at Charing Cross, says, “Harry Warrington! your brother is like yonder lion.”

“Because he is as brave as one,” says Harry.

“Because I respect virgins!” says George, laughing.

“Because you are a stupid lion. Because you turn your back on the East, and absolutely salute the setting sun. Why, child, what earthly good can you get by being civil to a man in hopeless dudgeon and disgrace? Your uncle will be more angry with you than ever—and so am I, sir.”

But Mr. Lambert was always laughing in his waggish way, and indeed he did not look the least angry.
