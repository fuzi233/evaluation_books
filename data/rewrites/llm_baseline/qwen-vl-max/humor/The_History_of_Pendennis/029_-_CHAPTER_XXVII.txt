In the dim, calico-draped nook of a window at Clavering Park, Arthur Pendennis adopted the solemn expression of a man who’d just discovered he’d lost his last shilling—and perhaps his soul. There he stood, like a disgruntled statue, watching Miss Laura Bell waltz through her first quadrille with Mr. Pynsent as partner. Now, Mr. Pynsent was not merely serious—he was *gravely* serious. He danced as if attending a funeral in church, each step measured, each glance directed to the heavens above, as though awaiting divine approval for his footwork. Not a smile marred his face; not a twitch betrayed his inner life. He was a man whose dance moves were likely rehearsed in silence, by candlelight, while reading Milton.

But poor Laura! She was all joy, all light, all unspoiled country charm. Her white dress flared out like a daisy in bloom, her brown curls tumbled over her shoulders like a waterfall of chocolate, and her rosy cheeks glowed as if she’d been kissed by the sun itself. The music, the lights, the sheer madness of it all—she devoured it. And so did the room. More than one gentleman, from the most discreet to the most absurdly bearded, found themselves doing double takes at her. Lady Fogey, who had a house in London and thought that gave her the right to judge anything (especially other people’s faces), leaned over to Lady Rockminster and whispered, “Who is that young person?” Then, after a moment’s study, declared: “She resembles the reigning beauty of the season—though not quite as tall—but she’ll do.”

Lady Rockminster nearly choked on her fan. “Do? My dear, I’ve never seen a girl who wouldn’t ‘do’!” she snapped. “And what gives you the audacity to assess such matters at all?”

She then examined Laura with the intensity of a royal inspector surveying a new breed of spaniel. “Her manner is excellent,” she decided. “Her arms are rather red, but that’s youth, my dear. And her tone—oh, far superior to that little minx Miss Amory, dancing opposite.”

Indeed, Miss Blanche Amory was currently twirling across the floor like a miniature tornado in silk. She was, of course, Laura’s vis-à-vis, which meant they passed each other every few seconds like two rival planets in a solar system of social ambition. Blanche smiled at Laura with a sweetness that could only be described as patronizing. She nodded, she murmured, she winked—always making sure Laura knew she was being observed. Her shoulders—white as alabaster—never stopped shifting, as if they feared being caught still. Her eyes darted about like nervous sparrows. Her whole body seemed to shout: “Look at me! Look at my tiny foot! Look at my exquisite shoe! This is Parisian elegance! Not that rustic, bouncing country lass over there—Miss Bell, who couldn’t even step in time until *I* taught her!”

Laura danced on, blissfully unaware of the war being waged around her. But she *did* keep an eye on Pen behind the curtain. He didn’t move during the first quadrille. Not a muscle. Not a blink. He remained frozen like a particularly bitter statue—until Lady Clavering, bless her good-natured soul, beckoned him up to the dais where the dowagers sat like ancient queens judging the world. Pen blushed, tripped slightly, and made his way forward with the grace of a man trying to walk on ice while wearing boots full of peas.

He bowed stiffly to Lady Rockminster, who barely glanced at him. Then he turned to the widow of the late Amory—a woman who looked like she’d been dressed by a jeweler with a grudge against modesty—decked in diamonds, lace, feathers, and enough gold to buy a small nation. He offered his respects, she accepted them with a nod, and he retreated to the shadows of conversation.

Young Mr. Fogey, then in the fifth form at Eton and already dreaming of spurs and dragoon commissions, stepped up next to dance with Miss Bell. He was smitten. Utterly, completely, hopelessly. “You’re much more charming than the French girl,” he said honestly, referring to Miss Amory, whom he’d just danced with.

Laura laughed—brightly, warmly, like a bell rung in spring. And as she laughed, she caught sight of Pen. And Pen, in turn, stared back with the intensity of a man who’d just realized he’d left the front door open.

The next dance was a waltz. Young Fogey sighed dramatically. “I don’t know how to waltz,” he confessed. “I shall have a master next holidays.”

Mr. Pynsent claimed Laura again. Pen watched, furious. How dare she be twirled round by *him*? It was unthinkable! Back in the summer evenings at Clavering Park, when the tables were cleared and the governess played piano, Pendennis and the Chevalier Strong—whose dance repertoire included British hornpipes, German waltzes, and Spanish fandangos—had often improvised little balls with the girls. Laura had always been the spark of those dances. Even Mr. Pynsent, usually as stiff as a poker, had once or twice loosened up under her infectious energy.

But now, Blanche had a problem. Her partner, Captain Broadfoot of the Dragoons, was a disaster. He tried hard—very hard—but lacked rhythm, musicality, and any sense of timing. His movements were slow, ponderous, like a rhinoceros learning to tap-dance. So, naturally, Blanche saw Laura stealing the spotlight—and wasn’t pleased.

After a few turns with the heavy-handed captain, she feigned fatigue and asked to be led back to her mother. “Why didn’t you ask me to waltz?” she demanded of Pen. “Why leave me to the mercy of that odious man in spurs and a red coat?”

“I thought spurs and scarlet were the most fascinating things in the world to young ladies,” Pen replied, smirking. “I never would have dared to put my black coat in competition with that splendid red jacket.”

“You’re cruel and sulky and naughty,” Blanche declared, shrugging. “You’d better go away. Your cousin is watching us over Mr. Pynsent’s shoulder.”

“Will you waltz with me?” Pen asked.

“No,” she said firmly. “Not this waltz. I just sent away Captain Broadfoot. Look at Mr. Pynsent—have you ever seen such a creature? But I’ll dance the next waltz with you. And the quadrille too. I’m promised, but I’ll tell Mr. Poole I forgot.”

“Women forget very easily,” Pendennis said.

“But they always come back,” Blanche countered, “repentant and sorry.”

Just then, Laura arrived, leaning on Mr. Pynsent like a bookshelf holding up a pile of novels. “I’m so glad to have come, dear Pen,” she said, offering her hand. “How is mamma? The three dances are over. I’m engaged to you for the next one.”

“I’ve just promised to dance with Miss Amory,” Pen said.

Blanche nodded curtly. “I don’t intend to give him up, dearest Laura.”

“Well, then,” Laura said sweetly, “he’ll waltz with me, dear Blanche. Won’t you, Pen?”

“I promised Miss Amory,” Pen repeated.

“Provoking!” Laura exclaimed, curtsied, and moved off to take refuge under Lady Rockminster’s wing.

Pen, meanwhile, felt triumphant. The two prettiest girls in the room were fighting over him. He leaned against the wall, elbow propped, and began to mock the entire gathering—dragoons, dandies, and ladies with hair so elaborate it looked like someone had thrown a fruit basket into their coiffure.

“One has a bird’s nest on her head,” he quipped. “Another has six pounds of grapes—plus false pearls. It’s a coiffure of almonds and raisins,” he added. “Could be served for dessert.”

He was sharp, witty, and utterly merciless. As the quadrille progressed, he kept Blanche laughing—not just at his jokes, but at the fact that Laura could see them, hear them, and feel excluded. “Arthur is charming tonight,” Blanche whispered to Laura.

“Who?” Laura asked.

“Arthur,” Blanche replied, switching to French. “Oh, what a pretty name!”

Then the young ladies joined Pen’s side, and Cornet Perch performed a pas seul. He had no waistcoat pockets, so his hands hung awkwardly—large, swollen, like two sausages tied to his sleeves.

Between dances, Pen ignored Laura—except to ask if Cornet Perch was amusing, and whether she liked him better than Mr. Pynsent. Two subtle daggers to the heart. Then he returned to Blanche, joking loudly, proudly, like a man who knew he was winning.

Laura, confused and hurt, couldn’t understand why Pen was so cold. But she wasn’t angry—she was too kind-hearted for that. And let’s face it: a man’s jealousy, even when poorly executed, can be oddly flattering.

So when Pen refused to dance with her, she took the Chevalier Strong instead. And since she loved dancing—like any sensible, lively young woman should—she threw herself into it with gusto.

Captain Broadfoot, meanwhile, was paired with Miss Roundle, a lady of proportions so vast that she might have been sculpted by a baker. She wore a strawberry-ice-colored crape dress, and together they moved like a pair of slow-moving glaciers.

Now, Pen—armed with Blanche and a sense of superiority—set off on his waltzing triumph. He spun, he whirled, he felt brilliant. He may have glanced toward Laura to see if she approved. But she was busy with Strong, oblivious.

But glory is fleeting. Just as Pen and Blanche reached their peak of elegance, they collided—full force—with Captain Broadfoot and Miss Roundle. The result? A catastrophic chain reaction. All four went down. Pen landed flat on his back. Blanche bounced onto a bench. Miss Roundle collapsed like a deflated balloon. Captain Broadfoot laughed—good-naturedly, as if it were all part of the fun.

But Blanche was furious. She was humiliated. And when the crowd giggled, Pen turned crimson. He hated laughter—at least, laughter aimed at him. He stormed over to apologize, blaming the pink-clad woman, asking if she was hurt, offering another dance.

She refused. She was *very* hurt. A cavalier with a blue ribbon and a three-pointed star rushed forward with a glass of water. She drank it gracefully, smiled at him, then turned her white shoulder toward Pen with the hauteur of a queen dismissing a servant. She held out her hand.

The man trembled. He bowed. He pressed her hand to his chest. He was ecstatic.

It was Mirobolant—the cook.

Pen, stunned, cried out: “By Jove, it’s the cook!”

The words slipped out before he could stop them. He instantly regretted it. Because Blanche had invited Mirobolant to escort her. And Mirobolant, hearing the insult, shot Pen a look that could have melted steel.

The artist, poor fellow, had never considered himself less than anyone else. In his native land, he’d seen noblewomen dance with peasants. He would have danced with Blanche himself—if not for Pen’s cruel remark. But Strong, knowing the truth, stepped in. He muttered something in rapid Spanish, and urged Blanche to take an ice.

Mirobolant, crestfallen, bowed deeply and withdrew.

“Don’t you know who he is?” Strong asked.

“The chef Mirobolant.”

“How should I know?” Blanche replied. “He has a croix. He’s distinguished. He has beautiful eyes.”

“The poor fellow is mad for your beaux yeux,” Strong said. “He’s a good cook, but not quite right in the head.”

“What did you say to him?” Blanche asked.

“He’s a Gascon, from the borders of Spain. I told him he’d lose his job if he walked with you.”

“Poor Monsieur Mirobolant!” Blanche sighed. “Did you see the look he gave Pendennis? I think he’d like to run him through with one of his spits.”

“Broadfoot looked like he’d like to kill him too,” Strong said. “So did Pynsent.”

“What ice will you have?” he asked. “Water ice or cream ice?”

“Water ice. Who is that odd man staring at me? He’s decore too.”

“That’s Colonel Altamont,” Strong said. “A very queer character. Serves the Nawaub of Lucknow.”

“Hallo! What’s that noise?” Strong suddenly shouted, and dashed out to investigate.

The refreshment room—reserved for the elite, guarded by a waiter who barred entry to commoners—was now occupied by the Colonel, who had been drinking champagne steadily all night. He was flushed, slightly unsteady, and had taken a peculiar interest in everyone who entered.

When Strong left, the Colonel rose, eyes glowing like embers, and approached Blanche, who was sipping her ice.

“Who was he?” she asked later. “It was quite exciting.”

“And so you’re Betsy Amory,” he said, gazing at her.

“Betsy Amory, by Jove!”

“Who—who speaks to me?” she stammered.

But the noise in the ballroom was growing louder. We must rush back—before the chaos reaches its climax.
