As the languid summer drew to a close, the once-thriving social scene of London began to dissipate like the morning mist that clung to the Serpentine. The gentlemen of the law, having fulfilled their obligations, had departed for the provinces, leaving behind a trail of empty townhouses and vacant clubs. The fashionable set, too, had bid farewell to the city, seeking the vibrant hues of the Continent or the rugged beauty of the Scottish moors. Even the usually bustling streets of St. James's seemed deserted, the red-coated guards now a rare sight before the palace gates. The tradespeople, freed from their duties, strolled along the embankment, their faces aglow with the promise of leisure.

In this desolate landscape, Major Pendennis found himself increasingly isolated. His recent exertions at Gaunt House had left him drained, and he retreated to the soothing waters of Buxton in search of solace. The town, too, had emptied, its residents either on holiday or absent on business. As Parliament recessed, the city's rhythms slowed, and the only sounds to be heard were the distant chimes of Big Ben and the soft lapping of the Thames against its banks.

Pen, ever the social butterfly, found himself confined to his chambers, his newspaper duties and the temporary absence of Captain Shandon keeping him tethered to the city. Though he professed to be jaded, his constitution remained robust, and his appetite for life, if not always satisfied, was certainly unbroken. He would dine on humble fare with equanimity, content in the company of anyone who would share a meal with him – whether in a tavern or on a steamer plying the Thames. His tastes ran broad and eclectic, and he reveled in the simple pleasures of life: a stroll through Hampstead with Mr. Finucane, a visit to the summer theatres, or a sojourn in the Royal Gardens of Vauxhall, where he knew the great Simpson and could indulge in witty repartee with the principal comedian.

As he watched the crowds at Vauxhall, Pen's gaze wandered over the disparate throngs: the rowdy youths, the beaming families, and the outcasts, each with their own stories and struggles. His heart went out to them all, his sympathies a blend of irony and kindness.
