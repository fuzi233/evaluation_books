I laid the yellowed manuscript down, breath catching at the quiet, unlooked-for comfort of it: my father, too, had peered behind the veil of that uncanny, unnameable world, had crossed paths with the uncanny Mr. Raven I’d fled only the night before. Then a memory pricked sharp, a gap I’d never once thought to question: no one had ever told me the cause, the smallest circumstance of my father’s death. The realization bloomed cold in my chest—he must have followed Mr. Raven, finally, and never come back.

Shame burned hot in my throat immediately. What unthinkable wonders of life, of death, of the vast, unseeable realms that stretched far past the thin edge of ordinary perception could I have already gathered, if I’d not run like a frightened child? The Ravens were nothing but good, I told myself fiercely. A single night under their roof would never have harmed me! They were strange, yes—but the man’s oddities were stitched from otherworldly faculty, the woman’s strangeness wrapped in a beauty so luminous it made mortal faces look dull as unglazed pottery. And I’d doubted them! Treated them as if they meant me harm, as if they hid some dark design behind their quiet smiles.

The longer I turned over every rude, fearful word and gesture I’d shown them, the more I loathed myself. What was there to fear from the dead who rested in that holy house? To share that soft, unbroken stillness was an honor I’d proven myself far too small and suspicious to deserve. What harm could that sleeping king on his bier, that gentle lady with the faint wound in her palm, ever have done me? I found myself aching for the quiet, stately sweetness of their faces, and broke into weeping.

I fell onto the couch mid-sob, and dropped off to sleep so fast it was as if the dark of the room had swallowed my consciousness whole. I woke just as abruptly, certain someone had spoken my name. The house was so still it might have been an empty church, hollow with echoes of old prayers. A blackbird trilled bright from the lawn outside, clear as a bell.
“I’ll go back,” I said to the quiet air. “I’ll tell them I’m ashamed. I’ll do anything they ask of me.”

I rose and climbed the stairs straight to the garret, no hesitation in my steps. The wooden chamber was exactly as I’d left it, the tall mirror on the far wall dim, blurring the edges of every object it caught. It was nearly noon now, the sun a little higher in the sky than it had been on my first visit. I’d just have to shift the hood over the skylight a fraction, adjust the mirrors to catch the light right! If only I’d paid attention when Mr. Raven had done it the first time.
I hauled on the chains, tilting the first mirror to spill its beam of sunlight across the surface of the second. When I turned to look, the shapes I’d seen in my first crossing were there, but wavering—shapes of heather and far peaks, trembling like a landscape reflected in a pool ruffled by the first soft breath of wind. I pressed a finger to the glass, and it wouldn’t give an inch. I remembered then that polarized light was likely the trick, so I shifted the mirrors, twisted them, reworked their angles again and again, until by some happy chance the light slotted perfectly between them.

There were the blue mountains, sharp and steady, no tremor to them at all. I stepped forward, and my boots sank straight into soft, springy heather.
All I remembered of the path to the Ravens’ cottage was that we’d passed through a thick pine forest. I tramped past thickets and small stands of fir, over and over convincing myself I recognized some rock or fern, but no great forest stretched before me. By the time the sun hung low, bleeding red across the horizon, the air was sharp with the first bite of coming winter. And then I saw it, a small dark shape lurching toward me across the moor: the raven himself.

I ran to meet him. “Sir,” I said, bowing my head, “I beg your pardon for my rudeness last night. Take me with you now, I’m sorry, I know I don’t deserve it.”
He looked up at me, dark eyes glinting. “Ah,” he said, after a long pause. “My wife did not expect you tonight. She regrets that we ever encouraged you to stay with us last week.”
“Take me to her, please,” I begged, humbled. “Let me tell her how sorry I am.”
“It is no use,” he said. “Your time to rest was not ripe then, or you would never have fled us. It is not ripe now. I cannot show you the way. The dead rest soft under their daisies, all of them curled in the roots of heaven’s flowers—they were so happy at the thought of how you would delight when winter passed, and the soft bird-loud morning came. The moment you turned your back to run, they shivered in their beds. When the great spring of the universe comes—though that is ages off, how many I do not know, and do not care to know—then you might be welcome.”

“Tell me one thing, Mr. Raven,” I said, my throat tight. “Is my father with you? Did you see him after he left our world?”
“Yes. He is with us, fast asleep. That was him you saw the night you fled, his arm slung over the edge of his coverlet, his hand half curled.”
“Why didn’t you tell me? I was that close to him, and I never knew!”
“And so close you turned your back on him,” the raven corrected. “If you had been ready to lie down beside him, you would have known him of your own accord. Old Sir Up’ard, and your great-great-grandfather, both rose and left us long ago. Your great-grandfather has rested with us for many a year—he will soon begin to stir. You saw him last night, too, though you had no way of knowing.”
“Why of course no way?”
“Because he is far closer to waking than you are. No soul that will not first rest can ever wake.”
“I don’t understand a word of that!”
“You turned away, and would not let yourself understand.”

I fell silent, panicking that he would walk away and leave me there. “My grandfather,” I pressed. “Is he with you too?”
“No. He is still in the Evil Wood, fighting the dead.”
“Where is the Evil Wood? I will go find him.”
“You will not find him, but you will not miss the wood. It is the place where those who refuse to sleep wake at night, to hunt their own dead, kill them, and bury them again.”
“I still do not understand.”
“Naturally not. Neither do I understand you. I cannot read your heart, or read your face. When my wife and I cannot understand our children, it is because there is not enough of them yet to be understood. Only God can make sense of foolishness.”

I felt naked, hollow, worthless, as if the moor wind had blown all the substance out of me. “Then please,” I said, “show me the shortest way home. There are more ways than one, I know, I have come by two already.”
“There are many more than two,” he said.
“Tell me how to tell which is the nearest.”
“I cannot,” the raven answered. “You and I use the same words, but they hold different meanings for us. We often cannot tell people what they need to know, because they are busy chasing something else, something they want to know instead, and they would only twist and misunderstand whatever truth we gave them. Home is held soft in the palm of your own hand, there is no use telling you how to reach it. But you will get there. You must get there. You have no choice. Every soul that is not at home is on their way there. You thought the house you fled was your home, the one you ran back to after you left us: if that had truly been your home, you would never have been able to leave it. No one can leave home. And no one ever reaches home without walking the path there themselves.”

“Riddles piled on riddles!” I burst out. “I did not come here to be fed puzzles.”
“No, but you showed up, and the riddles were waiting for you. You are the only real riddle there is. What you call riddles are plain truths, they only seem confusing because you yourself are not yet whole and true.”
“Worse and worse!” I cried.
“And you must answer the riddles,” he went on. “They will keep asking themselves of you, over and over, until you finally understand who you are. The whole universe is a riddle trying to unfold itself, and you are barring your own door tight against it.”
“Have pity,” I said. “Tell me what to do, where I must go.”
“How am I to tell you your own purpose, or the path that leads to it?”
“If I cannot go home, at least point me to other souls like me.”
“I know of none nearby. The beings most like you are that way.” He pointed his beak toward the horizon. All I could see was the setting sun, so bright it blinded me.

“Fine,” I said, bitter. “I feel hardly used. Dragged from what I thought was home, abandoned in a strange world, and no one will tell me where I am or what I am meant to do!”
“You forget,” the raven said, “that the first time I brought you here, and you refused my hospitality, you made it back to what you called home perfectly safe. This time you came here of your own accord. Good night.”
He turned, and began to walk slowly away, his beak tilted toward the heather.

I stood dazed. It was true I’d come of my own will, but hadn’t I come to make amends? My heart ached, my brain was empty of all quest, all purpose, all hope, all desire. I stared after him, half inclined to follow, but knowing it was useless.
Then he pounced, all his weight slamming down onto his beak, and dug furiously at a spot in the heather for several long moments. He fluttered his wings, tossed his head back, and something shot out of his bill, flying high into the air. The sun set the very same instant, the world dipping into dusk fast, but that small something unfurled into soft golden radiance, pulsing toward me like a firefly, but far larger, its glow warm and yellow. It flew over my head. I turned, and followed.

Here I pause my tale, to note that every step of this account is a struggle to put words to things that refuse to be said, to describe events and beings so far outside the bounds of our ordinary mortal world that I can only render how they felt to me, not the things themselves. Even that small task I do with a constant, sharp awareness of failure. I can only ever show one small facet of a reality that has a thousand layered, overlapping meanings, one thin sphere of a truth that unfolds in endless concentric layers. A single object here could be a dozen different things at once, with a quiet, shifting core of identity that changed its shape the moment you thought you’d pinned it down. The language and perceptions of our mortal world are not made to carry this strangeness clearly, to make it make sense even to someone who has walked these moors far longer than I have. I might be fully, unshakably certain I was watching a scene of living, moving activity, and at the very same second, be aware in my bones that I was reading a metaphysical argument written across the sky.
