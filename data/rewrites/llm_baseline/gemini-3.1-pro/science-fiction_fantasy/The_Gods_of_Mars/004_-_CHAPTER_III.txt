When the mechanized cruelty of that laugh finally ceased tearing at the cave’s acoustic harmony, Tars Tarkas and I stood bathed in a heavy, expectant silence. The harsh, unnatural frequency had violently disrupted the cavern's natural reverberations, a jarring intrusion of sonic pollution that set my teeth on edge. No further sound broke the stillness, and within the dim periphery of our vision, nothing moved. 

At length, Tars Tarkas laughed softly. It was a dry, percussive rhythm, a deeply resonant acoustic marker unique to his strange kind when confronted with the horrific. It possessed an inherent untranslatability—a Tharkian dialect of mirth that found profound, absurd humor in the very things that moved Earth men to loathing or tears. I had seen them roll upon the ground in the same uncontrollable fits while witnessing the grim spectacles of the Great Games, their cultural memory steeped in a lexicon where agony and amusement were inextricably braided.

I looked up at the great green warrior, letting a smile touch my own lips; a steady countenance was a far better anchor in this disharmonious space than a trembling chin.

“What do you make of it all?” I asked. “Where in the deuce are we?”

He looked at me in genuine surprise. “Where are we?” he repeated. “Do you tell me, John Carter, that you know not where you be?”

“That I am upon Barsoom is all I can taste in the air,” I replied. “But for your presence and the heavy musk of those great white apes, I should not even guess that. The sights I have seen this day are as alien to my beloved Barsoom—whose spiced winds I knew ten long years ago—as they are to the world of my birth. No, Tars Tarkas, I know not where we be.”

“Where have you been since you opened the mighty portals of the atmosphere plant years ago? After the keeper died, when all Barsoom was suffocating, gasping for the very breath that flavors our communal hearths? Your body was never found. The Jeddak of Helium and his granddaughter, your princess, offered fabulous bounties. Princes of royal blood scoured the red plains. When all physical traces evaporated, there was but one linguistic refuge, one mythic conclusion left to the elders: that you had taken the long, last pilgrimage down the mysterious River Iss. They believed you awaited Dejah Thoris in the Valley Dor, upon the shores of the Lost Sea of Korus, expecting to share the eternal feasts of the ancestors.”

“Why you had gone, none could guess,” he continued, “for your princess still lived—”

“Thank God,” I interrupted, the tension releasing from my chest in a long, ragged exhale. “I did not dare to ask. I feared I might have been too late to preserve her. She was fading so quickly when I left her in the royal gardens of Tardos Mors on that long-gone night. And she lives yet?”

“She lives, John Carter.”

“You still have not told me where we are,” I gently reminded him.

“We are where I expected to find you—and another,” he said, his voice dropping to a timbre rich with the sorrow of lost histories. “Many years ago, you heard the story of the woman who taught me the thing green Martians are reared to excise from their vocabulary: the woman who taught me to love. You know the cruel tortures, the butchery her devotion earned her at the hands of Tal Hajus. I believed she awaited me by the Lost Sea. And you, the Earth man who taught this cruel Thark the dialect of friendship, I thought you too roamed the carefree Valley Dor. For a month, I have traveled the long pilgrimage, drawn by the profound yearning to reunite in that mythic domesticity. Do you understand now where you be, John Carter?”

“And that was the River Iss, emptying into the Lost Sea of Korus in the Valley Dor?” I asked, trying to reconcile the circular pacing of my own life—returning to Mars only to be thrust into the culmination of its oldest myth.

“This is the valley of love and peace,” he replied, his tone chillingly cold, heavily ironical. “The destination to which every Barsoomian since time immemorial has longed to journey, seeking the restorative calm of the eternal hearth after a life of bloodshed. This, John Carter, is Heaven.”

His bitterness was a stark reflection of a shattered worldview. To have one’s multi-generational faith—the very foundation of an agrarian and warrior culture’s hope for a final, peaceful harvest—uprooted and revealed as a slaughterhouse might have excused a vastly greater demonstration. 

I laid my hand upon his shoulder. “I am sorry,” I said, knowing the inadequacy of my native Earth syllables to bridge the chasm of his disillusionment. 

“Think of the countless billions of Barsoomians,” he murmured. “Pilgrims who drifted down this cruel river, expecting the fragrance of slow-roasted meats and the warm embrace of ancestors, only to fall into the ferocious clutches of the terrors that assaulted us today. There is an ancient legend—a suppressed oral history—of a red man who returned from the River Iss. He spoke what the dominant lexicon deemed a fearful blasphemy: that horrific brutes inhabited this wondrous valley, devouring pilgrims upon the very shores where they sought sustenance and peace. The ancients executed the blasphemer, enforcing the linguistic death of his truth. But now we know the legend was absolute fact. We are trapped between the wild thoat of certainty and the mad zitidar of fact.”

“As Earth men say, we are between the devil and the deep sea,” I replied, a grim smile touching my face at the trans-planetary symmetry of our idioms. “We can only take things as they come. Whoever slays us will pay an exorbitant price in blood. White ape or plant man, they will learn the terrible cost of attempting to consume John Carter, Prince of the House of Tardos Mors, and Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark.”

He joined me in a rare, genuine laugh, a shared acoustic resonance that solidified our bond. 

“But where have you been?” he asked. 

“I have been back to Earth,” I said. “For ten Earth years, I have consumed meals that were mere utilitarian fuel, utterly devoid of the complex olfactory landscapes of Barsoom. I existed in a sensory famine, praying to return to this grim planet, whose ancient, spiced winds I love more than the world of my birth. And now, having miraculously returned to the very heartbeat of my past, I am hurled into the one tiny spot of all Barsoom from which there is apparently no escape. A half-hour before I saw you battling the plant men, I was standing in the moonlight on Earth. Do you believe me?”

“I believe,” replied Tars Tarkas, “though I cannot understand.”

As we spoke, I studied the chamber. It was two hundred feet in length, hewn from the living cliff. In the dim light of a single minute radium illuminator, the walls glowed with the dull, buttery warmth of ancient gold, patched with polished ruby, emerald, and diamond. The floor was worn to a glassy smoothness by the friction of countless generations. 

As I extended my hand toward the locked doorway to search for a controlling mechanism, that mocking, artificial laugh ruptured the quiet once more. It was so close I involuntarily shrank back, my hand tightening on the hilt of my great-sword. 

From the far corner, an artificially amplified, hollow voice chanted: “There is no hope, there is no hope; the dead return not, the dead return not; nor is there any resurrection. Hope not, for there is no hope.”

The voice was a profound violation of acoustic ecology—a sterile, disembodied projection designed entirely to erode psychological well-being. My spine chilled, the short hairs at the base of my neck rising like a hound sensing the unseen. I walked toward the mournful sound, but it evaporated, only to be replaced by a shrill, piercing shriek from the opposite wall.

“Fools! Fools!” the new voice weaponized its lexicon. “Thinkest thou to defeat the eternal laws of life and death? Wouldst cheat the mysterious Issus, Goddess of Death, of her just dues? Did not her mighty messenger bear you upon her leaden bosom to the Valley Dor? Go back the way thou camest, to the merciful maws of the children of the Tree of Life or the gleaming fangs of the great white apes. But insist in your rash purpose to thread the mazes of the Golden Cliffs, and Death in its most frightful form will overtake you—a death so horrible that the Holy Therns themselves avert their eyes.”

Then the awful laugh broke out again. 

“Most uncanny,” I remarked, refusing to let their sonic terrorism take root. 

“What shall we do?” Tars Tarkas asked. “We cannot fight empty air. I would sooner face foes into whose flesh I may feel my blade bite, to know I am selling my carcass dearly.”

“Those voices come from beings as real as you or I,” I called out loudly, deliberately projecting my voice to reclaim the cavern's acoustic space. “In their veins flows lifeblood that may be let. Their invisibility is the best proof of their mortal cowardice. Think you that John Carter will fly at the first shriek of a foe who dare not step into the open?”

I suspected this theatrical noise pollution was a calculated maneuver to herd us back to the valley’s hungry beasts. We waited in silence. 

Suddenly, a stealthy, rhythmic whisper of footfalls sounded behind me. I wheeled to behold a great, many-legged banth creeping sinuously across the golden floor. The Barsoomian lion is a fierce beast, practically hairless save for a bristly mane, its long, lithe body supported by ten powerful legs. As it lashed its tail, the heavy, musky odor of its carnivory—rancid marrow and the metallic tang of old blood—flooded my senses. It emitted a terrifying roar, a percussive blast meant to induce paralysis, and launched its bulk toward me. 

But my nerves were immune to its acoustic assault. My sword met its charge, sliding cleanly into its vitals rather than the tender flesh its needle-like fangs gaped to engulf. The beast’s hot, copper-scented blood spilled over my hands as I drew my blade from its heart. Turning, I saw Tars Tarkas dispatching a similar monster. 

For the better part of an hour, one hideous creature after another was loosed upon us. Tars Tarkas reveled in the tangible combat, the honest clashing of steel against bone offering a profound relief from the manipulative, disembodied voices. The beasts howled in very real pain, bleeding very real blood. 

Yet, I noticed they only appeared when our backs were turned. They did not materialize from thin air. 

Among the ornaments on Tars Tarkas’ leather harness was a small mirror, resting flat against his broad back. As he stood over a fallen antagonist, I caught sight of the mirror’s reflection. 

“Move not, Tars Tarkas! Move not a muscle!” I whispered. 

He froze. In the mirror, I watched a section of the wall behind me execute a flawless mechanical rotation. It pivoted perfectly, taking a bisected half of the floor with it, much like a card spun upon a silver coin. On the rotating floor sat a fresh beast, newly delivered to our side of the partition. 

But it was the glimpse through the rotating gap that seized my attention. Beyond lay a brilliantly lit, sterile room. Within it, chained to the wall, were several red Martians—men and women whose ancestors had cultivated the great agrarian plains, now stripped of their dignity and kept like livestock. Directing the secret doorway was a wicked-faced man with flowing yellow hair and skin as white as my own. He possessed the soft, uncalloused hands of one who has never tilled soil or respected the profound rituals of sustenance, treating life only as fuel for his cruel machinery. 

“Watch the wall at your end, Tars Tarkas,” I murmured. “It is through secret doorways they loose the brutes upon us.”

Our tormentors were observing us through hidden apertures. I outlined a plan in a low whisper. Tars Tarkas grunted his assent, the deep vibration in his chest a comforting acoustic anchor. Slowly, he backed toward the wall I faced, while I advanced ahead of him. 

When we were ten feet from the secret doorway, I halted him. I turned my back to the door, feeling the baleful gaze of the pale executioner upon my spine. I glued my eyes to the mirror on the Thark’s back. 

The golden surface commenced its rapid pivot. I gave the signal. Simultaneously, we both sprang for the receding halves of the door. 

A single bound carried me into the adjacent room, bringing me face to face with the yellow-haired man. He was muscled like an Earth man, armed with a long-sword, a short-sword, a dagger, and a radium revolver. Ignoring the honorable ethics of swordsmanship, he immediately drew his firearm. I closed the distance instantly, an uppercut from my blade sending the weapon flying before he could discharge it. 

He drew his long-sword, his face twisting in rage, and we engaged. He was a master of the blade, his movements precise and deadly, but it did not take long for my muscle memory to awaken. I found my rhythm, the familiar, ringing song of clashing steel returning to my arm. My guard proved impregnable, and soon his pristine skin was marred by a dozen minor, bleeding wounds. 

“Who are you, white man?” he hissed, breathing heavily. “That you are no Barsoomian from the outer world is evident from your color. And you are not of us.”

The question revealed the rigid hierarchy of his worldview. I decided to weaponize his own oppressive lexicon against him. 

“What if I were from the Temple of Issus?” I hazarded. 

“Fate forfend!” he exclaimed, his face draining of color beneath the smeared blood. The sheer power dynamics embedded in that single name paralyzed him, exposing his deep-seated terror of his own religious masters. In that momentary lapse of his guard, I drove my sword between his ribs.

The chained prisoners had watched the duel in tense, absolute silence. Not a sound had disturbed the room save the ringing steel and the soft shuffling of our feet. But as the yellow-haired man collapsed, a shrill, desperate cry tore from one of the female prisoners—a native dialect raw with the instinct to preserve life.

“Turn! Turn! Behind you!” she shrieked. 

I wheeled to find a second yellow-haired man lunging from a dark corridor, his sword already raised. Tars Tarkas was trapped on the other side of the closed secret panel. 

I had fought continuously for hours. I was profoundly depleted. It had been twenty-four hours since I had last eaten or slept, and my exhaustion was not merely physical—it was an agonizing severance from the vital, grounding rituals of sustenance. Without the restorative alchemy of a shared meal to anchor my spirit, my body was running on fumes, rendering objects blurry and my movements sluggish. 

I tried to rush him, hoping to overwhelm him through sheer impetuosity, but he was an adroit, calculated fighter. He parried and sidestepped, letting my starved muscles burn themselves out. He led me a desperate chase, backing me toward the corpse of his fallen comrade. 

With a sudden, aggressive rush, he forced me backward. My heel struck the dead man. The physical obstacle disrupted my stance entirely, and I tumbled backward. 

My head struck the hard pavement with a resounding, violent *crack*. The sharp pain exploded behind my eyes, yet the sheer intensity of the shock acted as a brutal acoustic reset. It cleared the muddy exhaustion from my brain, igniting a primal temper that temporarily eclipsed my physical hunger. I was ready to tear the man apart with my bare hands. And I verily believe I would have attempted it, had not my right hand, bracing against the floor to lift my body, closed around the cold, reassuring grip of a discarded metal hilt.
