Suddenly I found myself awake, staring out through the half-loosened door of the tent as though some hand had drawn my eyelids apart. The canvas, powdered with sand, breathed in and out with the gusts; and my watch—pinned where I could find it by habit even in darkness—shone coldly in the moonlight. It was past twelve: the old day had been kneaded away and a new one begun, clean and ominous as a white board before the first cut.

The Swede slept on, heavy and trustful, his mouth a little open, the rhythm of his breathing stubbornly human against the long, furious voice of the wind. That wind had not slackened since we lay down; it worried the willows without mercy, set their thin wands clattering, and drove the river’s breath across the sand with a taste like wet stones. Something plucked at me—no thought exactly, rather a tug in the nerves, as if the place had changed its seasoning while we slept and my body detected it first. The immediate neighborhood felt disturbed, as though an unseen hearth had been upset and its ash blown about.

I sat up, listening. In such places you learn the land not by sight but by its acoustics: the pitch of the river, the way a gust finds a hollow and booms like a drum, the fine rattling of leaves that ought to soothe and yet—here—worked on the mind like a repeated syllable in a language you almost understand but cannot translate. There was no machinery, no iron din of towns, no vulgar commercial music blaring meaning at you; and precisely because the soundscape was so pure, every alteration carried authority. The river suddenly seemed louder, nearer; the wind’s howl had an edge to it, as if a different mouth were making it.

I crawled quietly toward the opening, careful not to wake my companion. A curious excitement ran through me, sharp as the smell when you first grate bitter peel into warm sugar—bright, unsettling, impossible to ignore. I was half out, kneeling on all fours, when my eye caught the tops of the bushes opposite us. The willows, etched against the sky, made moving tracery in the moon, and the tracery—this was the thing—kept gathering itself into shapes that were not the shapes of branches.

I sat back on my haunches and stared until my eyes smarted with the effort of refusing to blink. There, close—some fifty feet in front of me—were outlines among the willows that held together while everything else swayed and tore. They were monstrous in suggestion, shifting rapidly under the moon, and yet not dissolving as any trick of leaf and light ought to dissolve when you stare it down.

My first instinct was to shake the Swede awake, to force another pair of eyes into the matter, to make it communal and therefore safer—like tasting a doubtful broth and calling someone else to confirm the bitterness. But something in me hesitated. I did not welcome corroboration. If another man saw them too, then the world would truly have changed its rules; and I crouched there, alone with my sight, whispering fiercely to myself that I was not dreaming.

Then the figures became properly visible—huge, bronze-coloured, moving—wholly independent of the willow-tops that seemed to fringe them like weed around a rising fish. They were immensely larger than human, and something in the very proportion and poise of them declared them not human at all. They did not merely flicker with the boughs. They shifted of their own accord, and they rose—unceasingly—upwards from earth to sky, vanishing utterly as soon as they reached the darker vault above the moon’s glare.

As my mind steadied enough to observe, I saw that they were interlaced one with another, making a great column. Limbs and enormous bodies melted in and out of each other as though the substance of them could not be held to one outline, forming a serpentine line that bent and swayed and twisted spirally with the contortions of the wind-tossed trees, yet was not governed by those contortions. Nude, fluid shapes passed up through the bushes—within the leaves almost—rising in a living column into the heavens. Their faces I could never see. The column poured upwards in great bending curves, dull bronze on their skins like the colour of old cookware darkened by years of fire and handling.

I stared as though I could force more vision out of my eyes by sheer hunger. For a long time I expected them to vanish each moment, to resolve into branch and moonlight and prove themselves a mistake—the way a scent in a kitchen might seem for an instant like smoke until you find the scorched corner and understand. I searched everywhere for the proof that would restore the old standard of reality. Yet all the while I understood that the standard had shifted, that I was measuring with instruments that no longer applied.

The longer I looked, the more certain I became that these figures were real and living—though perhaps not according to the tidy demands of camera or biologist. Far from fear, I was possessed with a sense of awe so clean and consuming it made my throat ache. It was as if I gazed at the personified elemental forces of this haunted, primeval region, the way old people in field-country speak of weather as though it had intention and kinship and could be offended. We had intruded; our presence had stirred whatever lay under the willows, and the place answered.

Stories and legends—those slow-cooked broths of generations—filled my head to bursting: spirits of river and sandbank, deities of grove and wind, acknowledged and worshipped in every age by men who knew that certain experiences cannot be carried into another tongue without losing their essential taste. Here, in this moonlit island, I felt the helplessness of modern words. “Wind,” “river,” “willows”—the dominant lexicon made them small, domesticated, safe. Yet what I saw and heard refused that domestication. The very language in which I tried to think seemed a poor vessel for the thing pressing in upon us.

Before I could shape even a private explanation, something impelled me farther out. I crept forward on the sand and stood upright. The ground under my bare feet was still warm, holding the day’s heat the way baked clay holds it; grains pressed into my skin with intimate precision. The wind tore at my hair and face, bringing with it the raw, green scent of willow-sap and the colder, mineral breath off the river. And then the sound of the river burst upon my ears with a sudden roar, as if a door had opened. It was not merely loud; it was patterned—rush and pull, rush and pull—an immense throat speaking in a dialect older than ours, and I knew by that clarity of sound that my senses were not failing me.

The figures still rose from earth to heaven, silent and majestic, in a great spiral of grace and strength that overwhelmed me with a deep emotion that was, unmistakably, worship. I felt that I must fall down and worship—absolutely worship—as naturally as one kneels when the ground itself seems a shrine. Another minute, and I might have done it.

A gust struck me then with such force that it blew me sideways. I nearly stumbled and fell, my feet sliding on the sand as on sifted flour. The shock jarred something loose. It shook the spell—if spell it was—violently out of me, or at least gave me a different angle upon it. The figures remained; they still ascended from the heart of the night. But my reason, bruised into activity, began to assert itself.

It must be subjective, I argued—none the less vivid for that, but still subjective. Moonlight and branch-work, the incessant music of wind and river, and the exhausted nerves of two men sleeping on sand: these could combine to paint pictures upon the mirror of imagination, and for some reason I might project them outward and mistake them for objective things. I clung to that explanation as to a familiar recipe when the meal has gone strangely wrong.

I took courage from the thought and began to move forward across the open patches of sand. Yet even as I forced my legs to obey, doubt gnawed at the argument. By Jove, was it hallucination? Was it merely subjective? Did not my reason argue in the old futile way from the little standard of the known—making a cage out of vocabulary and calling the cage “truth”? The column of figures ascended darkly into the sky for what seemed a very long time, with a completeness of reality such as most men are accustomed to call reality.

Then, suddenly, they were gone.

They did not fade. They did not unravel back into willow and moon. They were simply—absent, as if some sluice had been shut. And the instant their great presence withdrew, fear came down upon me with a cold rush, as though all warmth had been ladled out of the air. The lonely and haunted meaning of this region flamed up within me; the willows that had seemed mere trees became a watchful multitude. I began to tremble dreadfully.

I took a quick look round—horror close to panic—calculating vainly ways of escape: the water, the sand, the low tent, the interminable islands. The wind and the river had the place; we were only guests who had not been invited and could not now pretend ignorance of the household’s deeper life. Realizing how helpless I was to achieve anything effective, I crept back silently into the tent.

I lowered the door-curtain to shut out the moonlit willows, as one shuts a cupboard on something one cannot bear to see. Then I lay down again upon my sandy mattress, burying my head as deeply as possible beneath the blankets to deaden the sound of the terrifying wind, trying—futilely—to muffle the country’s vast, articulate voice until it became once more the ordinary noise of weather and not the utterance of powers we had wakened.
