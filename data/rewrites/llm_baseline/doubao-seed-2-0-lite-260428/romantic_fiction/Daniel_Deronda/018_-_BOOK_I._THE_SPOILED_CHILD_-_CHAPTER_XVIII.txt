Life is a mother of endless faces: one day she drapes herself in plumes and gemstones, climbs marble staircases with her head held high, never glancing back at the footmen trailing in her wake; the next, she tramps grim and shabby down dark alleyways, breathes the fumes of hot gin, screams in the raw chaos of a pauper’s riot. But for the Meyrick women, she arrived as a frugal, brisk, cheerful matron, sharp-eyed at making joy stretch thin, who found whole worlds of warmth in the smallest, shabbiest slice of foggy London.

Their narrow, unassuming house hugged the river on one side, and sun-dappled back gardens on the other, so on that mild autumn evening, all the windows stood open to freshen the air of the double front parlor lit by a single lamp and two beeswax candles. One candle sat at a separate small table for Kate, hunched over pen and ink, drawing book illustrations for a local publisher. The lamp lit Mrs. Meyrick as she read aloud from a tattered French novel, and cast soft golden light over Amy and Mab, who stitched satin cushions embroidered with wild poppies and roses for wealthy clients in “the great world.” From the street outside, the glow through their holland blind showed off the heavy, old-fashioned window frames, making the place look like just one more grim, cramped home carved out of London’s overcrowded rows. But few outsiders would guess how much gentle, unvulgar richness lived inside its walls: poverty had stripped away any urge to perform grandeur, left the Meyricks free to love beauty for its own sake, no petty rivalry for fine displays to taint it.

Every inch of their parlor held beloved, familiar things: the old engravings Mr. Meyrick had collected before his death, which the children had memorized the faces and stories of before they could read full sentences, the scuffed piano in the corner, the frayed wool rug stitched by Mrs. Meyrick as a new bride. Their furniture was worn, no secondhand broker would have wasted time haggling over it save for the prints and the piano, but it held more space for joy in music, poetry, and painting than many a grand townhouse stuffed with gaudy new possessions. Even in their hardest years, before Kate’s illustration work brought in steady pay, when they had no servant to light their fires or sweep the floors, they remained fastidious and kind, refusing to believe the high society tales they read in novels were anything but cruel exaggeration of how ladies ought to behave. They were a little eccentric, these four Meyricks, their minds like medieval houses full of hidden nooks, sudden staircases, and unexpected bright outlooks—bound tight by three unbreakable ties: fierce family love, a shared devotion to the finest art and kindest deeds, and a habit of gentle, uncomplaining industry. When their son Hans, the painter, had tried to spend his first big paycheck buying them fancy new furniture and a full staff, they’d all turned him down flat, happy instead to save up for a single grand treat: a trip to the opera, sitting in the cheap gallery seats, whenever he came home to visit.

Looking at them that evening, no one could have wished their life to be any different. They were all small in stature, perfectly suited to their miniature rooms: Mrs. Meyrick, barely five feet tall, half French half Scotch, lively and quick-witted, with rippling silver hair tucked under a plain Quaker cap, bright brown eyes, and a black buttoned dress cut almost like a priest’s cassock that suited her neat frame perfectly. Her three daughters matched her size: Amy soft and round, Kate sharp and focused behind her drawing, and Mab with Hans’ pale sun-streaked hair and faint, quirky little facial tics that reminded the whole family of him. All of them wore plain grey skirts, cut unapologetically against the current fashion that would have required four layers of crinoline to swallow every spare inch of their small parlor, their dark hair coiled tight at the nape of their necks in a simple Chinese knot. Even the largest thing in the room was perfectly mild: Hafiz, their fluffy Persian cat, balanced on the back of the oldest leather chair, opening his great golden eyes every few minutes to make sure no one forgot to give him a scratch.

Mrs. Meyrick had just finished reading the last page of Erckmann-Chatrian’s *Histoire d’un Conscrit* when Mab dropped her embroidery on the floor, leaning forward so far she nearly tipped off her chair.
“That is the finest story in the whole world,” she declared, breathless.
“Of course it is,” Amy teased, grinning. “It’s the last one you heard. Everything that makes you giddy is automatically the best thing that’s ever been written.”
“It’s barely even a story,” Kate put in, leaning back to squint at her half-finished illustration. “It’s a piece of history pulled right up to our eyes with a telescope. We can see the soldiers’ faces, hear their boots squelching through mud—we can almost feel their hearts beating.”
“I don’t care what you call it!” Mab swatted her thimble across the table, giddy. “Call it a chapter out of Revelation. It makes me want to do something grand, something kind. I feel like Schiller—like I could wrap the whole world in my arms and kiss it. I’ll kiss you instead, little mother!” She threw herself across the room to tuck her arms around Mrs. Meyrick’s neck.
“Whenever you get this heroic, your work goes straight to the floor,” Amy laughed. “It would be a very good deed to finish that cushion you’re stitching without getting ink all over it.”
“Oh no,” Mab groaned, bending to grab her discarded hoop. “I wish I had three wounded conscripts right here to nurse back to health, instead of satin poppies.”
“You’d spill all their beef tea while you were telling them how brave they are,” Amy said.
“Poor thing, don’t tease her,” Mrs. Meyrick said, holding out a hand for the embroidery hoop. “You keep your grand, heroic thoughts, Mab. I’ll stitch the pink poppies for you.”
“Mama, I think you’re more teasing than Amy,” Kate said, still squinting at her drawing.
Mab hopped to her feet, stretching her arms over her head. “I wish something wonderful would happen right now. I feel like the flood—all the windows of heaven are open! I’m going to play scales on the piano.”

She was halfway to the piano keys when a cab pulled up sharp outside the narrow house, followed by a quick, urgent rap on the knocker.
“Dear me,” Mrs. Meyrick said, jumping to her feet. “It’s past ten, and Phoebe the maid already went up to bed.” She hurried out of the parlor, leaving the door propped open.
The three girls heard her gasp, clear as day from the hall: “Mr. Deronda!”
Mab clasped her hands to her mouth, whispering so loud it was almost a shout: “There! Something wonderful *did* happen!” Kate and Amy dropped their work in surprise, but Deronda’s voice was so low, gentle and harried, that they couldn’t make out a single word he said, and a moment later Mrs. Meyrick closed the parlor door tight behind her.
“I know I’m asking for an extraordinary favor, trusting you like this,” Deronda was saying, the faint shake in his voice obvious even to her. “You can’t imagine how helpless I felt when I pulled her out of the river, that poor young thing, just minutes from drowning. I couldn’t drag her off to a house full of strangers, not in that fragile, terrified state. I trusted you would be the only ones who wouldn’t turn her away. I hope you don’t think my act is unwarrantable.”
“On the contrary—you honor me by trusting us,” Mrs. Meyrick said at once. “I see exactly how hard this must be for you. Bring her right in. I’ll go prepare the girls.”

While Deronda stepped back out to the waiting cab, Mrs. Meyrick slipped back into the parlor to face her three wide-eyed daughters.
“Mab, you got your wish for someone to take care of instead of wounded conscripts,” she said softly. “A poor girl who was about to drown herself in despair. Mr. Deronda pulled her out of the river just in time, brought her here in his boat, and knew no one else but us would be gentle enough to receive her. She’s a Jewess, he says, refined—speaks Italian, plays music.” The three girls crowded close, their faces soft with sudden compassion, Mab looking half awe-struck, as if the wild wish she’d breathed out two minutes before had been answered by magic.

Outside, Deronda leaned down to the cab window, where a pale, dark-haired face was peeking out, bright with confused, new hope.
“I’ve brought you to some of the kindest people in the world,” he told her, holding out his hand. “They have daughters just about your age. This is a happy, safe home. Will you let me take you to meet them?”
She stepped out of the cab without hesitation, her small cold hand slipping into his, her hat slipping forgotten off the back of her head. When he led her into the full warm light of the parlor, where the four small Meyrick women waited for her, the sight of her would have stirred even the coldest heart. Her dark curls were still damp at the edges from the river, her fine pale gown spattered with dark water stains, her great dark eyes dazed from the shock of the bright parlor lamps. Deronda felt a quiet rush of relief that all the Meyricks were so small, none of them towering over her, so she would never feel overwhelmed.
“You must be so weary, poor child,” Mrs. Meyrick said softly, taking her other hand.
“We will take care of you! We will comfort you! We will love you!” Mab burst out, unable to hold herself back, wrapping both her hands around the girl’s small one.
That gentle warmth seeped into the strange girl’s bewildered, tight chest. She hung back a little, looking each of the four Meyricks in the face in turn, the tense, terrified line of her shoulders softening, no smile coming to her lips but a quiet, peaceful relief settling over her features. For a second she looked up at Deronda, as if all this unexpected kindness was a gift directly from him, then turned back to Mrs. Meyrick, her sweet soft voice steady for the first time since he’d met her on the riverbank.
“I am a stranger. I am a Jewess. You might have thought I was wicked.”
“No! We know you are good!” Mab burst out.
“We think no evil of you at all, poor little one,” Mrs. Meyrick soothed. “You are safe here now. Come sit down. We’ll get you a cup of hot tea, and then you can rest.”
The girl looked back up at Deronda. “I would not fear here. I could rest. You are all ministering angels, I think.”
“Tell us your name, dear,” Kate said, pulling a soft armchair forward for her.
Hafiz, who had been watching the scene restlessly, padded over to rub his fluffy side against her ankles.
“My name is Mirah Lapidoth,” she said, as Amy hurried out to bring the tea tray. “I traveled all the way from Prague alone. I escaped from terrible people who kept me trapped. I came to London to find my mother and my little brother—they took me from her when I was small, and I thought I could find her again. But all the old neighborhoods are gone. I lost all trace of them. I ran out of money, and I could not see any other way.”
“Our mama will look after you just like your own,” Mab said.
Deronda stirred at last, knowing it was time to leave. “Will you let me come back tomorrow, around five o’clock, to check on her?” he asked Mrs. Meyrick.
“Of course. We’ll all know each other a little better by then.”
He turned to Mirah, holding out his hand to say goodbye. She stood to take it, and the moment sent a sharp, thrumming jolt through both of them, bringing back the second she had first reached up to take his outstretched hand on the cold riverbank. She lifted her great dark eyes to his, her voice thick with reverence.
“The God of our fathers bless you, and deliver you from all evil, as you have delivered me. I never thought there was a man so good. No one before ever thought I was worthy of the best. You found me poor and miserable, yet you gave me kindness no one else ever dreamed of offering.”
Deronda could not find words to reply. He gave the Meyricks a quiet, wordless nod of farewell, and hurried out the door, his chest tight with a new, unnameable feeling that he would never be able to shake.
