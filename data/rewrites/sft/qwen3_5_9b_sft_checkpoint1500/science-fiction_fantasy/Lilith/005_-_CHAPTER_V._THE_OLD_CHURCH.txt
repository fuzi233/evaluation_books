My guide led me down a path that wound deeper into the pine woods, until the canopy above us thickened into a sacred gloom that seemed to wrap around our shoulders like a heavy cloak. We walked in silence, save for the soft crunch of our boots on the earth, as the trees around us grew older and stranger. Their trunks twisted into grotesque shapes, bearing the weight of centuries, until finally, the forest began to thin, giving way to the open expanse of a heath.

"You see that hawthorn?" my companion asked suddenly, extending a long, curved beak to point at the edge of the clearing where the trees gave way to grass.

I squinted through the dappled light where the wood faded away. "I see a gnarled old man," I replied, "with a great, white head bowed low under the burden of age."

"Look again," he corrected gently. "It is merely a hawthorn."

"It certainly appears to be an ancient specimen," I admitted, though I frowned. "Yet, it is not the season for hawthorns to bloom!"

"The season for a hawthorn to blossom," he answered with a calm, melodic voice, "is simply when it chooses to blossom. And that specific tree stands in the ruins of the church upon your home farm. Did you not intend, the morning of the thunder, to give directions to the bailiff regarding the state of its churchyard?"

"I was planning to instruct him," I said, recalling my earlier resolve, "that I wished it transformed into a wilderness of rose-trees, ensuring that the ploughshare would never fall within three yards of those stones."

Suddenly, the bird stilled. He seemed to hold his breath, tilting his head as if listening to a frequency invisible to human ears. "Listen!" he whispered.

I strained my senses. Was it the sighing of a distant, musical wind? Or perhaps the phantom echo of joyous melodies from a forgotten past? For a moment, doubt clouded me, but then the sound cut through the silence.

"They go there still," the raven murmured.

"Who goes there?" I asked, my curiosity piqued. "And whither do they go?"

"Some of those who once knelt within those walls return to the ruins," he explained. "But I fear they will not remain there for much longer."

"What draws them back?"

"They require aid from one another to complete their thinking and hatch their feelings," he said. "Together, they speak and sing. And then, they claim, a great thought lifts out of their hearts like a massive vessel sailing down a river swollen by high waters."

"Do they offer prayers alongside their songs?"

"No," he shook his head. "They have discovered that each soul prays most effectively in the solitude of their own heart. Observe—there comes one now!"

His beak shot upward, tracking a figure against the sky. A snow-white pigeon mounted the air, beating its wings with a rapid, spiraling rhythm, ascending an invisible staircase of ether. Sunlight flashed off its feathers, making them seem to vibrate.

"A pigeon!" I exclaimed.

"Naturally, you perceive a pigeon," the raven agreed, "for it *is* a pigeon. Yet, I perceive a prayer taking flight. I wonder, however, what heart birthed such a dove. Perhaps someone has awakened within my cemetery today?"

"But how can a living pigeon constitute a prayer?" I pressed, struggling with the concept. "I understand how it might serve as a symbol, a likeness, but for a living bird to emerge directly from a heart?"

"It *must* confuse you!" the raven chuckled, a sound like dry leaves skittering on stone. "It is impossible not to!"

"A prayer is a thought, a purely spiritual entity!" I insisted.

"Precisely! Yet, if you were acquainted with worlds beyond your own, you would comprehend your reality far more deeply. When a heart truly beats with life, it generates thoughts that are themselves living creatures. Consider the heart whose every thought is a strong, happy being, whose dreams are vivid existences. Those who pray often carry heavy burdens, lifting them from the soil only to set them down again. Others, however, launch their petitions in living forms—this shape, that shade—the closest possible mirror to the truth. All living things began as thoughts; consequently, they are perfectly suited for use by those who think. When one speaks to the Great Thinker, saying, 'Behold, here is one of Your thoughts; I am giving it life,' that act *is* a prayer—a message sent from a small heart to the vast heart of creation. Watch closely, there is another!"

This time, the raven directed his gaze downward, pointing toward the base of a block of granite. I bent forward and saw it: a tiny flower. It was unlike anything I had ever encountered, possessing a grace and trustfulness that struck me with immediate force. Its hue was a pale rose, and its center glowed with a golden warmth, smelling faintly of a new world that somehow felt intimately familiar. It resembled an anemone, yet it was distinct.

"That is a prayer-flower," the raven stated flatly.

"I have never seen such a bloom before!" I gasped.

"There exists no other like it," he assured me. "Not a single prayer-flower is ever exactly alike."

"How do you know it is a prayer-flower?" I asked, leaning closer.

"By the expression it wears," he replied. "Beyond that, I cannot explain. If you recognize it, you recognize it; if you do not, you do not."

"Could you not teach me to identify a prayer-flower when I encounter it?"

"I could not," he said, shaking his feathers. "But even if I could, what greater good would you achieve? You would not know it through *yourself* and *Itself*. Why label a thing when you do not truly know the thing itself? What business is it but yours alone to open your eyes? Indeed, the grand design of the universe is to make you a fool of yourself just enough that you realize you are one, and thereby begin the journey toward wisdom!"

Nevertheless, I saw clearly that this flower was unlike any other I had known. Therefore, I understood that I must be gazing at a shadow of a prayer contained within it. A profound awe settled over me as I considered the magnitude of the heart that listened so intently to this fragile, blooming testament.
