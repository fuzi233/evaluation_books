The White Horse Tavern had set its pleasures in an English way: not merely within doors where plates were cleared and glasses replenished, but out upon the bowling-green itself, which came up close under the eating-room windows as if the lawn were another chamber of the house. A table or two stood there for those who liked their tea cooled by open air, or their punch sweetened with sunlight. The dinner of the three gentlemen ended much about the same time; and Mr. Morris, who found conclusions intolerable unless they led to fresh beginnings, proposed that they should adjourn to the Green and drink a cool bottle.

“Jack Morris would adjourn to the Dust Hole, if it served as pretext for a fresh drink,” said my lord, laughing.

To which Jack, with the candour of a man who could make a jest of his own vice while keeping the vice safe, replied that each gentleman had his own favourite road to the deuce; his weakness, he owned, was a bottle.

“My Lord Chesterfield’s deuce is deuce-ace,” says my Lord March. “His lordship can’t keep away from the cards or dice.”

“My Lord March has not one devil, but several devils,” said Morris. “He loves gambling, he loves horse-racing, he loves betting, he loves drinking, he loves eating, he loves money, he loves women; and you have fallen into bad company, Mr. Warrington, when you lighted upon his lordship. He will play you for every acre you have in Virginia.”

“With the greatest pleasure in life, Mr. Warrington!” my lord broke in, as quick as a knife finding the seam of a roast.

“And for all your tobacco, and for all your spices, and for all your slaves, and for all your oxen and asses, and for everything that is yours.”

“Shall we begin now? Jack, you are never without a dice-box or a bottle-screw. I will set Mr. Warrington for what he likes.”

“Unfortunately, my lord,” said Harry, whose plain honesty always sounded a little like stubbornness in such company, “the tobacco, and the slaves, and the asses, and the oxen, are not mine, as yet. I am just of age, and my mother, scarce twenty years older, has quite as good chance of long life as I have.”

The air on the Green carried the crisp clack of bowls from another party; from within the tavern came the muffled scrape of chair-legs on boards, and the thin treble of a spoon against porcelain. Those small sounds—the ecology of idleness—seemed to quicken the appetite of the two men of fashion for wagers, as the smell of cork and wine does.

“I will bet you that you survive her,” said my lord with the cool ease of one who could name death as if it were a point in a game. “I will pay you a sum now against four times the sum to be paid at her death. I will set you a fair sum over this table against the reversion of your estate in Virginia at the old lady’s departure. What do you call your place?”

“Castlewood.”

“A principality, I hear it is. I will bet that its value has been exaggerated ten times at least amongst the quidnuncs here. How came you by the name of Castlewood?—you are related to my lord? Oh, stay: I know,—my lady, your mother, descends from the real head of the house. He took the losing side in ’15. I have had the story a dozen times from my old Duchess. She knew your grandfather. He was friend of Addison and Steele, and Pope and Milton, I dare say, and the bigwigs. It is a pity he did not stay at home, and transport the other branch of the family to the plantations.”

“I have just been staying at Castlewood with my cousin there,” said Harry.

“Hm! Did you play with him? He’s fond of pasteboard and bones.”

“Never, but for sixpences and a pool of commerce with the ladies.”

“So much the better for both of you. But you played with Will Esmond if he was at home? I will lay ten to one you played with Will Esmond.”

Harry’s face answered before his tongue: the flush of a truth he had no mind to dress up in language.

He owned that, of an evening, his cousin and he had had a few games at cards.

“And Tom Sampson, the chaplain,” cried Jack Morris—who had that way of shouting a name as if it were a toast—“was he of the party? I wager Tom made a third, and the Lord deliver you from Tom and Will Esmond together!”

“Nay; the truth is, I won of both of them,” said Harry, smiling over his wine.

“And they paid you? Well, miracles will never cease!”

“I did not say anything about miracles,” said Harry, who liked credit but disliked cant.

“And you don’t tell tales out of school—the volto sciolto—hey, Mr. Warrington?” my lord said, tossing in foreign words as a cook tosses salt—carelessly, to show there is plenty.

“I beg your pardon,” said Harry, downright as ever; “French is the only language besides my own of which I know a little.”

“My Lord March has learned Italian at the Opera,” Morris remarked, “and a pretty penny his lessons have cost him. We must show him the Opera—mustn’t we, March?”

“Must we, Morris?” my lord answered, and there was a shade in his voice as if he half resented the familiarity that had been purchased with too many shared bottles.

Both of those gentlemen were dressed alike in small scratch-wigs without powder, in blue frocks with plate buttons, in buckskins and riding-boots, in little hats with narrow lace, and no outward mark of fashion; yet the very plainness had a studied air, like a dish served in earthenware to prove that silver is unnecessary.

“I don’t care about the Opera much, my lord,” said Harry, warming with his Burgundy; the wine had come to him with a full, ripe smell—berries crushed, a faint tang as of wet wood, and that velvet heat that rises into the cheeks and loosens the tongue. “But I should like to go to Newmarket, and long to see a good English hunting-field.”

“We will show you Newmarket and the hunting-field, sir. Can you ride pretty well?”

“I think I can,” Harry said; “and I can shoot pretty well, and jump some.”

“What’s your weight? I bet you we weigh even, or I weigh most. I bet you Jack Morris beats you at birds or a mark, at five-and-twenty paces. I bet you I jump farther than you on flat ground, here on this green.”

“I don’t know Mr. Morris’s shooting—I never saw either gentleman before—but I take your bets, my lord, at what you please,” cried Harry, who by this time was more than warm; the world had grown loud and bright, and the tavern’s laughter seemed a kind of music after all.

“Ponies on each!” cried my lord.

“Done and done!” cried my lord and Harry together. The young man thought it for the honour of his country not to be ashamed of any wager made to him.

“We can try the last bet now, if your feet are pretty steady,” said my lord, springing up and stretching, looking at the crisp dry grass as a man looks at a clean table he means to make a mess upon. He drew his boots off, then his coat and waistcoat, buckling his belt round his waist, and flinging his clothes down upon the ground.

Harry had more respect for his garments; it was his best suit, and he folded the velvet coat and waistcoat with a kind of carefulness that belonged to home habits—like wrapping yesterday’s bread to keep it from drying. Seeing the tables outside slopped with drink, he stepped to the eating-room window and laid his clothes within, where the air smelled of roasted fowl and peppered gravy gone cold, and of lemon peel from emptied punch-bowls.

A new guest sat there: Mr. Wolfe, soberly eating a chicken and salad, with a modest pint of wine. The chicken had been cut with soldierly exactness; the salad—sharp with vinegar, green with fresh herbs—stood as if it had not been allowed to wilt beneath the tavern’s noise.

Harry, in high spirits, told the Colonel he had a bet with my Lord March—would Colonel Wolfe stand him halves? Wolfe said he was too poor to bet. Would he come out and see fair play? That he would with all his heart. He set down his glass and stalked through the open window after his young friend.

“Who is that tallow-faced Put with the carroty hair?” said Morris, whose Burgundy made him bold and stupid.

Harry explained it was Lieutenant-Colonel Wolfe, of the 20th Regiment.

“Your humble servant, gentlemen!” said the Colonel, making the company a rigid military bow.

“Never saw such a figure in my life!” cried Morris. “Did you—March?”

“I beg your pardon, I think you said March?” the Colonel asked, eyes widening with a look half surprise, half scrutiny.

“I am the Earl of March, sir, at Colonel Wolfe’s service,” said the nobleman, bowing. “My friend, Mr. Morris, is so intimate with me, that, after dinner, we are quite like brothers.”

Why is not all Tunbridge Wells by to hear this? thought Morris, and in his delight shouted, “Two to one on my lord!”

“Done!” called out Harry; and the enthusiastic Jack, having trapped himself, was obliged to cry “Done!” too.

“Take him, Colonel,” Harry whispered.

But Wolfe said he could not afford to lose, and therefore could not hope to win.

“I see you have won one of our bets already, Mr. Warrington,” my Lord March remarked. “I am taller than you by an inch or two, but you are broader round the shoulders.”

“Pooh, my dear Will! I bet you you weigh twice as much as he does!” cried Morris.

“Done, Jack!” said my lord, laughing. “The bets are all ponies. Will you take him, Mr. Warrington?”

“No, my dear fellow—one’s enough,” said Jack, suddenly prudent when his purse was threatened.

“Very good, my dear fellow,” said my lord. “And now we will settle the other wager.”

Harry had already put himself into his best silk stockings, black satin-net breeches, and neatest pumps; he would not take off his shoes as his antagonist had done. My lord’s heavy riding-boots and spurs were little calculated for leaping, and he had shed them with a frankness that made the wager seem more sport than pride; but Harry’s pumps were as much a part of his dignity as the ribbon on a dish at table makes it fit for company.

They had before them thirty yards of even turf—enough for a run, enough for a jump. A gravel walk ran round the green; beyond it stood the wall and gate-sign with the Hanoverian White Horse rampant between two skittles, and for motto the landlord’s name and the animal’s—both equally advertised. The sign creaked a little in the afternoon air, and that small complaint of wood and iron seemed a thin prophecy of harsher noises that England would one day learn to call improvement.

Morris laid a handkerchief on the ground for the mark. Mr. Wolfe stood at the other end to note where each came down, as gravely as if he were marking ground for artillery.

“My lord went first,” Mr. Warrington later wrote to Mrs. Mountain at Castlewood, in a letter still extant, “He was for having me take the lead; but, remembering the story about the Battel of Fontanoy which my dearest George used to tell, I says, ‘Monseigneur le Comte, tirez le premier, s’il vous play.’ So he took his run in his stocken feet, and for the honour of Old Virginia, I had the gratafacation of beating his lordship by more than two feet—viz., two feet nine inches—me jumping twenty-one feet three inches, by the drawer’s measured tape, and his lordship only eighteen six. I had won from him about my weight before (which I knew the moment I set my eye upon him). So he and Mr. Jack paid me these two betts.”

The tape, produced with tavern importance, was stretched and called over; boots thudded back on, coats were resumed, and bank-notes—dry, crackling, smelling faintly of leather and pocket—passed hands. Harry’s purse, lately thinned by fees to Castlewood servants and by the purchase of a horse at Oakhurst, felt suddenly fat again, as if restored to its proper diet.

In the same letter Harry added, with dutiful anxiety, that his mother would not be displeased with him, for he had bet for the honour of the Old Dominion, and his opponent was a nobleman of the first quality, holding two earldoms and heir to a duke. “Betting is all the rage here,” he wrote, “and the bloods and young fellows of fashion are betting away from morning till night.”

And then—here the domestic, the remembered, slipped into the very centre of this public frolic—he told how he had spoken of a gentleman at home who could beat him by a good foot; and when asked who, he said Colonel G. Washington, of Mount Vernon. At that name Wolfe had pricked up as if at a trumpet, and questioned him closely; spoke of last year’s unhappy campaign as if he knew every inch of ground, naming rivers and roads with an exactness that made Harry laugh when Wolfe called the Potowmac the Pottamac. In that mispronunciation lay the whole tragedy of empire: the land known in a stranger’s mouth, the sound altered, the very syllables conquered before the soil.

These feats being over, the four gentlemen strolled from the tavern garden into the public walk, where a great deal of company was assembled. Here Mr. Morris, loud and free by nature and now made louder by drink, chose to scatter remarks as a cook scatters pepper—without much care whom it might make sneeze. If my Lord March made a joke, of which he was not sparing, Morris roared, “Oh, ho, ho! Oh, good Gad! Oh, my dear earl! Oh, my dear lord, you’ll be the death of me!” It seemed, as Harry sagaciously wrote, as if he wished everybody to know his companion was an earl.

A variety of characters passed, and the Walk had its own orchestra: the rustle of gowns, the click of buckles on gravel, the call of chairmen, the distant tap of bowls, and, over all, the soft busy murmur of polite society—less honest than the wind in trees, yet soothing in its way. M. Poellnitz, no finer dressed than at dinner, grinned and saluted with his great laced hat and tarnished feathers. Then came my Lord Chesterfield, in a pearl-coloured suit with blue ribbon and star, and saluted the young men in his turn.

“I will back the old boy for taking his hat off against the whole kingdom, and France either,” said my Lord March. “He has never changed the shape of that hat of his for twenty years. Look at it. There it goes again! Do you see that great, big, awkward, pock-marked, snuff-coloured man, who hardly touches his clumsy beaver in reply. D— his confounded impudence—do you know who that is?”

“No, curse him! Who is it, March?” asked Morris, with an oath.

“It’s one Johnson, a Dictionary-maker, about whom my Lord Chesterfield wrote some most capital papers, when his dixonary was coming out, to patronise the fellow. I know they were capital. I’ve heard Horry Walpole say so, and he knows all about that kind of thing. Confound the impudent schoolmaster!”

“Hang him, he ought to stand in the pillory!” roared Morris.

“That fat man he’s walking with is another of your writing fellows,—a printer,—his name is Richardson; he wrote Clarissa, you know.”

At that name, Colonel Wolfe and Mr. Warrington spoke together, their voices breaking through the general hum as if two birds had startled at once.

“Great heavens! my lord, is that the great Richardson? Is that the man who wrote Clarissa?”

Harry ran forward to look. The old gentleman toddled along with a train of admiring ladies about him, as if he were some kindly household saint come out into the sunshine to be worshipped. One lady—Miss Mulso—was saying, “Indeed, my very dear sir, you are too great and good to live in such a world; but sure you were sent to teach it virtue!”

“Ah, my Miss Mulso! Who shall teach the teacher?” said the good fat old man, raising a kind round face skywards. “Even he has his faults and errors! Even his age and experience does not prevent him from stumbl—. Heaven bless my soul, Mr. Johnson! I ask your pardon if I have trodden on your corn.”

“You have done both, sir. You have trodden on the corn, and received the pardon,” said Mr. Johnson; and he went on, swaying to and fro, mumbling verses to himself, eyes turned towards the ground, hands behind him, now and then endangering with his great stick the meek honest eyes of his companion-author.

“They do not see very well, my dear Mulso,” Richardson said to the young lady, with gentle alarm, “but such as they are, I would keep my lash from Mr. Johnson’s cudgel. Your servant, sir.” Here he made a low bow and took off his hat to Mr. Warrington, who shrank back with many blushes after saluting the great author.

Richardson was accustomed to be adored. A gentler wind never puffed mortal vanity. Enraptured spinsters flung tea-leaves round him and incensed him with the coffee-pot. Matrons kissed the slippers they had worked for him. There was a halo of virtue round his nightcap. All Europe had thrilled, panted, admired, trembled, wept over the pages of the immortal little kind honest man with the round paunch.

Harry came back glowing and proud at having received a bow. “Ah!” he said. “My lord, I am glad to have seen him!”

“Seen him! why, dammy, you may see him any day in his shop, I suppose?” said Morris, with a laugh that had more beer than brains in it.

“My brother declared that he, and Mr. Fielding, I think, was the name, were the greatest geniuses in England; and often used to say, that when we came to Europe, his first pilgrimage would be to Mr. Richardson,” cried Harry, impetuous and tender whenever he spoke of the dearest friend.

“Your brother spoke like a man,” cried Mr. Wolfe too, his pale face flushing up. “I would rather be a man of genius, than a peer of the realm.”

“Every man to his taste, Colonel,” said my lord, amused. “Your enthusiasm—I don’t mean anything personal—refreshes me, on my honour it does.”

“So it does me—by gad—perfectly refreshes me,” cried Morris.

“So it does Jack—you see—it actually refreshes Jack! I say, Jack, which would you rather be?—a fat old printer, who has written a story about a confounded girl and a fellow that ruins her,—or a peer of Parliament with ten thousand a year?”

“March—my Lord March, do you take me for a fool?” said Jack, and his voice, for all its bluster, turned suddenly tearful with wounded worship. “Have I done anything to deserve this language from you?”

“I would rather win honour than honours: I would rather have genius than wealth. I would rather make my name than inherit it, though my father’s, thank God, is an honest one,” said the young Colonel. And then, as if ashamed of having shown his heart among gamblers, he made them a hasty salutation and ran across the parade toward a young and elderly lady and a gentleman advancing.

“It is the beautiful Miss Lowther. I remember now,” said my lord. “See! he takes her arm! The report is, he is engaged to her.”

“You don’t mean to say such a fellow is engaged to any of the Lowthers of the North?” cried Morris. “Curse me, what is the world come to, with your printers, and your half-pay ensigns, and your schoolmasters, and your infernal nonsense?”

As if to answer that question with another kind of nonsense—solemn, ceremonious, and richly rewarded—Mr. Johnson, who had shown so little desire to bow to my Lord Chesterfield, was seen now to take off his beaver and bow almost to the ground before a florid personage in a large round hat with bands and a gown: my Lord Bishop of Salisbury, wearing complacently the blue riband and badge of the Garter, of which noble order his lordship was prelate.

Mr. Johnson stood hat in hand during the whole of his conversation with Dr. Gilbert, who made many flattering and benedictory remarks to Mr. Richardson, declaring that he was the supporter of virtue, the preacher of sound morals, the mainstay of religion—of all which points the honest printer himself was perfectly convinced.

And indeed, hearing these praises, one might fancy a book could be made a sort of sacrament; yet tastes alter, and with them the very permission of language. The old tears shed over Clarissa seemed to belong to a dialect of feeling now half lost; the words that once were household bread grew strange on the tongue, as if the nation’s palate had changed. A century may make a masterpiece into contraband, and the same shelves that once displayed it proudly may hide it away behind other titles, as families hide an old recipe no longer fashionable, though it once saved them from hunger.

“Who is yonder queer person in the high headdress of my grandmother’s time, who stops and speaks to Mr. Richardson?” asked Harry, as a fantastically dressed lady came up and performed a curtsey and compliment to the bowing printer.

Morris nervously struck Harry a blow in the side with the butt end of his whip. Lord March laughed.

“Yonder queer person is my gracious kinswoman, Katharine, Duchess of Dover and Queensberry, at your service, Mr. Warrington. She was a beauty once! She is changed now, isn’t she? What an old Gorgon it is! She is a great patroness of your book-men; and when that old frump was young, they actually made verses about her.”

The Earl quitted his friends for a moment to make his bow to the old Duchess, Morris explaining, with relish, how at the Duke’s death my Lord of March and Ruglen would succeed to his cousin’s dukedoms.

“I suppose,” said Harry simply, “his lordship is here in attendance upon the old lady?”

Morris burst into a loud laugh.

“Oh yes! very much! exactly!” he cried. “Why, my dear fellow, you don’t mean to say you haven’t heard about the little Opera-dancer?”

“I am but lately arrived in England, Mr. Morris,” Harry answered, smiling; “and in Virginia, I own, we have not heard much about the little Opera-dancer.”

Luckily for us, the secret about the little Opera-dancer never was revealed; for their talk was interrupted by a lady in a cardinal cape, with a hat not unlike those headpieces fashion loves to forget and then, generations later, to proclaim new. She made a profound curtsey to the two gentlemen and received their salutation. Then she stopped opposite Harry and held out her hand rather to his wonderment.

“Have you so soon forgotten me, Mr. Warrington?” she said.

Off went Harry’s hat in an instant. He started, blushed, stammered, and cried out Good Heavens! as though any celestial wonder lay in meeting a lady on a public walk. It was Lady Maria come out for air. The truth—cruel to his vanity and crueler still to hers—was that he had not been thinking of her at all. She had been, for a moment, so utterly out of his mind that her re-entry there, and her appearance in the body, startled his faculties and brought those guilty blushes crowding to his cheeks.

A week ago—a year, a hundred years ago it seemed—he would not have been surprised to meet her anywhere: gliding from dark shrubbery, loitering on stairs, hovering even in dreams, she had been his constant apparition. A week ago his heart used to beat at her very name. And it was only last Tuesday that his love had been stabbed and slain; now he had not only left off mourning but had forgotten.

“You will come and walk with me a little?” she said. “Or would you like the music best? I dare say you will like the music best.”

“You know,” Harry said, “I don’t care about any music much, except—” He was thinking of the evening hymn—“except of your playing.” He turned very red again; he felt he was perjuring himself horribly.

The poor lady was agitated too by the flutter she saw in him. Could that tremor mean she had been mistaken, and that the lad was still faithful? “Give me your arm, and let us take a little walk,” she said, waving a curtsey toward the other two gentlemen. “My aunt is asleep after her dinner.”

Harry could not but offer his arm, and press the hand that lay against his heart. Lady Maria made another fine curtsey to Harry’s bowing companions and walked off with her prize.

Meanwhile Morris, leaning on his noble friend’s arm, went away perfectly happy; he had dropped a light airy remark about a particular run of cards at Lady Flushington’s the night before; Lady Maria had replied graciously; and in his ears the Walk’s polite murmur had turned into applause. The tragedy passed beside him unheard—swallowed by the agreeable noise of the world.
