Oleron found himself striding over the loose board Miss Bengough had hastily laid upon the broken step, descending the stairs into the gloom. Hatless and breathless, he did not realize until the cold air of the hallway bit at his neck that he had left the candle burning on the table, the door ajar just enough for his escape, and the latch clicking softly shut behind him. At the foot of the stairs, a frantic shape darted upward from the disused cellars and vanished through the open door. It was only a cat, yet Oleron let out a childish sob, the sound swallowed instantly by the heavy silence of the house.

He pushed through the gate and paused beneath the "To Let" boards, nervously picking at his lip while staring up at the faint, sickly glow filtering through one of the red blinds. Still glancing over his shoulder as if expecting pursuit, he stumbled up the square. A small public-house rounded the corner—a place he had never dared enter—yet now he threw open its door and slammed it shut behind him. He dropped a shilling onto the counter, missing the edge by mere inches.

"B--b--bran--brandy," he stammered, then bent to retrieve his coin.

The bar was empty save for him; the usual clientele of carters, laborers, and local tradesmen huddled in the far compartment beyond the reach of the white-haired landlady's domain. Oleron collapsed onto a hardwood settee with a perforated seat, drained half his brandy, and then, unable to bear the waste, finished the glass.

His mind, fogged by alcohol and terror, drifted to the practicalities of survival. Which of the men in the shadows across the room would agree to move his effects tomorrow?

He ordered another glass.

He could not return to that room where the candle burned alone. No, he could not face the entry, the treacherous staircase, or that pith-white, fascinating chamber that seemed to breathe with malevolent intent. For now, he would revert to his old arrangement: a workroom and separate sleeping quarters. He would visit his old landlady shortly, once he had consumed enough spirits to steady his nerves, and beg for a night's lodging.

His glass was empty. He signaled for a refill and sank back into the chair.

If anyone questioned his sudden departure, he had ample justification. Nails that retracted into the wood to gash a man's hand, steps that shattered underfoot, and women who invaded a man's sanctuary to brush their hair in the darkness—these were reasons enough! He felt querulous, deeply wounded. He had taken the flat for himself, not for invisible specters to perform their rituals. The lawyer in Lincoln's Inn would learn of this outrage within the hour; it was scandalous to bind a man to such a cursed agreement!

A cut-glass partition separated Oleron's corner from the landlady's workspace, though it ended mere inches above the counter. There was no barrier at the far bar. Raising his eyes, Oleron saw faces watching him through the gap, but they dissolved into the shadows the moment he focused on them. He shifted to a deeper corner, hidden from the other patrons, only to find himself directly in the line of sight of the white-haired woman.

She recognized him, perhaps having seen him pass a dozen times already, and soon remarked on the weather. Oleron offered a vague reply, which prompted her to note that the winter had been terrible for influenza, but spring seemed imminent. This trivial exchange with the mundane world steadied him; the idle, nascent wonder whether the landlady brushed her hair nightly and emitted those electric crackles was snuffed out with a snap. He felt better.

With his next glass, the urge to return to his flat became overwhelming. Return? Indeed, he would return! They would soon discover if he was to be evicted like a rat! He wondered why he was behaving so uncharacteristically—sitting hatless, drowning his sorrows in brandy. What if he confessed to the landlady? What if he told her of the caller who scratched her hand on a nail, the misfortune of stepping through a rotten stair, and his own flight from an old house filled with squeaks, creaks, and whispers? She would surely deem him mad. Pshaw! The truth was simpler: he had done too little work, filling his days with moonshine about a new *Romilly* (as if the old one wasn't sufficient), and now he was surprised that the devil would inhabit an empty head.

Yes, he would go back. First, a walk in the fresh air—he had neglected that lately—then he would take himself in hand, settle the mess of the sixteenth chapter of *Romilly* (fancy that he had considered destroying fifteen!), and finally remember his obligations to his fellow men. That was the matter in a nutshell.

He finished the brandy and stepped out into the night.

He walked for some time before the nature of his situation clarified itself. Initially, the fresh air amplified the brandy's heady effect, but soon his mind cleared, sharper than it had been since morning. As clarity returned, his boastful self-assurances wavered, replaced by a firm conviction that, once all explanations were exhausted, something remained utterly inexplicable. The hysteria of the hour faded, replaced by a growing calm, yet a deep, disquieting fear took root. It was a fear for Elsie.

Something in his flat was inimical to her safety. Her two accidents alone might not have convinced him, but she had spoken: "_I'm not wanted here_..." She had declared the place wrong, seeing it before he had. Clearly, if this was true, she must be kept away for reasons far more profound than the humiliation Oleron had suffered. Fortunately, she had expressed an intention to stay away; he must ensure she adhered to it.

Moreover, he realized his first impulse—to never set foot in the place again—was absurd. People did not simply abandon their homes to shadows. With Elsie secured, he could not, with any respect for himself, allow a shadow or a mere danger to expel him. He had to live somewhere, and he would live there. He had to return.

He mastered the faint chill of fear that accompanied this decision, turning abruptly in his walk. If the dread returned, he might seek one more glass of brandy...

But by the time he reached the short street leading to the square, it was too late for more spirits. The public-house was still lit but closed, with a few men standing on the curb. A sudden silence fell over them as Oleron passed. Lower down, the long-nosed Barrett did not return his greeting. Oleron turned into the broken gate, hesitated for an instant in the alley, and then climbed his stairs again.

Only an inch of wax remained in the Sheffield stick, and Oleron refused to light another candle. Deliberately, he forced himself to carry the dying flame and inspect all five rooms before retiring. It was as he returned from the kitchen across his small hall that he noticed a letter lying on the floor. He carried it into the sitting-room and glanced at the envelope before opening it.

It was unstamped, inserted by hand. The handwriting was clumsy, running from start to finish without a single comma or period. Oleron read the first line, checked the signature, and finished the letter.

It was from Barrett, informing Oleron that he would be obliged if Mr. Oleron made other arrangements for preparing breakfasts and cleaning the premises. The sting lay in the postscript, a text of Scripture containing an allusion that could only refer to Elsie Bengough.

A seldom-seen frown carved deeply into Oleron's brow. So! That was it! Very well; they would see about that tomorrow. For the rest, this seemed merely another reason why Elsie must remain away.

Then his suppressed rage erupted.

The foul-minded lot! The devil himself could not have leered more contemptuously at anything that had passed between Paul Oleron and Elsie Bengough, yet this nosing rascal must pry and talk!

Oleron crumpled the paper, held it in the candle flame until it curled and blackened, and then ground the ashes under his heel.

One useful purpose the letter had served: it had ignited a wrathful blaze in Oleron that effectively banished the pale shadows. Nevertheless, one other puzzling circumstance closed the day. As he undressed, he glanced at his bed. The coverlets bore an impress as if someone had lain upon them. Oleron could not recall lying down during the day—offhand, he would say certainly not—but he could not be positive. His indignation for Elsie, acting perhaps with the residue of the brandy, excluded all other considerations. He blew out the candle, lay down, and immediately sank into a deep, dreamless sleep that, in the absence of Mrs. Barrett's morning call, lasted almost a full twenty-four hours.
