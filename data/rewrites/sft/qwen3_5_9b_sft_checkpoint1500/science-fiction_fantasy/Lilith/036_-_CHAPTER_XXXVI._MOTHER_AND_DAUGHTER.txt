A profound disgust gnawed at Lona, a hatred for the people, and specifically for the women among them, so intense that she desired to flee the place immediately. Yet I stood firm against this impulse. To retreat now would be a willful failure where success was within reach, and far worse, it would shatter the resolve of the Little Ones, casting them into a peril far greater than any immediate threat. If we fled, the Princess would certainly assault us without mercy; if we engaged her, the hope anchored in the prophecy would perish with us. Mother and daughter must meet. Perhaps Lona's radiant loveliness would seize the Princess's heart like a storm! If violence erupted, I would stand between them. And if I found myself powerless to stop the blow, I was ready, for Lona's sake, to strike the Princess's closed hand with pitiless force. I knew the girl was doomed; it was likely decreed that her fate should unfold through us.

Without revealing the nature of my bond to the Princess, I laid out this grim scenario to Lona. Instantly, she agreed to accompany me to the palace.

High atop one of the great towers, the Princess had seen the approaching army of the Little Ones just before dawn, while the city still slept. The sight had awakened an overwhelming terror within her: she had failed in her endeavor to destroy them, and they were upon her! The prophecy was about to be fulfilled.

When she returned to reality, she descended to the Black Hall and seated herself in the northern focus of the ellipse, directly beneath the roof's central opening. To facilitate her "thinking"—a process requiring a consciousness of herself not as she truly was, but as she chose to believe—she had suspended a mirror slightly above and beyond her. Invisible in the hall's darkness, it awaited the full sunlight reflected from her person. For the vision of her own splendor, she waited for the meridian sun.

Shadows moved about her in the gloom, but whenever her inner eye caught sight of one, she refused to acknowledge it. Close beneath the mirror stood the Shadow that attended her walks, yet, consumed with its own existence, she did not see it.

By midday, the city had fallen. The inhabitants cowered in terror, and the Little Ones with their strange cavalry encamped in the square. The sun shone upon the Princess, and for a fleeting moment she beheld herself glorious. But the vision passed. Night fell, cloaking the glass and filling the hall with darkness, yet she did not stir. A gloom teeming with shadows swam in the palace; the servants shivered and shook, daring not to leave because of the beasts of the Little Ones. All night long, the Princess sat motionless. She *must* see her beauty again! She *must* try again to think! But courage and will had grown weary of her spirit and departed, leaving her hollow.

At daybreak, we selected twelve of the tallest and bravest boys to join us. We rode our great horses alongside their smaller mounts and elephants.

The Princess sat waiting for the sun to grant her the joy of her own presence. The tide of light crept up the horizon, but until the sun stood overhead, not a single ray could penetrate the Black Hall. Then it rose, climbing swiftly to our eyes and ascending the dome of the great hall. It looked in through the eye of the dome—and with sudden radiance, the Princess flashed upon her own reflection. But she sprang to her feet with a cry of despair. Alas, her whiteness! A spot covered half her side, black as the surrounding marble. She clutched her robe and collapsed back into her chair. The Shadow glided out, and she finally saw him depart.

We found the gate open as usual, passed through the paved grove to the palace door, and entered the vestibule. There, in her cage, lay the spotted leopardess, seemingly asleep or lifeless. The Little Ones paused to observe. Suddenly, she leaped up rampant against the bars. The horses reared and plunged; the elephants stepped back. In the next instant, she fell supine, writhed in quivering spasms, and lay motionless. We rode into the Great Hall.

The Princess still leaned back in her chair, bathed in the shaft of sunlight, when the clatter of hooves reached her ears from the courtyard stones. She started, listening, shaking. Never had such a sound been heard in her palace! She pressed her hand to her side and gasped. The trampling drew nearer, entering the hall itself. Moving figures that were not shadows approached her through the darkness!

To us, it appeared as a glory, a magnificent woman centering the dark. Lona sprang from her horse and bounded forward. I dismounted and followed close behind.

"Mother! Mother!" she cried, her clear, lovely voice echoing through the dome.

The Princess shivered. Her face grew nearly black with hate, her eyebrows meeting on her forehead. She rose to her feet and stood tall.

"Mother! Mother!" Lona cried again, leaping onto the dais and flinging her arms around the Princess.

In another instant, I would have reached them—but in that split second, I saw Lona lifted high and dashed onto the marble floor. Oh, the horrible sound of her fall! At my feet, she lay still. The Princess sat down, wearing the smile of a demoness.

I dropped to my knees beside Lona, raised her from the stones, and pressed her to my bosom. With indignant hate, I glanced at the Princess; she answered me with her sweetest smile. I would have sprung upon her, seized her by the throat, and strangled her, but the love for the child was stronger than the hate for the mother, and I clasped my precious burden tighter. Her arms hung helpless; her blood trickled over my hands, falling to the floor with soft, slow little plashes.

The horses scented it—mine first, then the small ones. Mine reared, shivering and wild-eyed, circling blindly before thundering down the dark hall, with the little horses following. Lona's steed stood frozen, gazing down at his mistress, trembling all over. The boys flung themselves from their saddles, and failing to see the black wall ahead, they dashed themselves, along with mine, to pieces against it. The elephants advanced to the foot of the dais, stopping and trumpeting wildly; the Little Ones sprang upon them, standing in horror. The Princess lay back in her seat, her face that of a corpse, her eyes alone alive, wickedly flaming. She was again withered and wasted to what I had found in the woods, her side scarred as if a great branding hand had been laid upon it. But Lona saw nothing, and I saw only Lona.

"Mother! Mother!" she sighed, and her breathing ceased.

I carried her into the court. The sun shone upon her pale face, illuminating the pitiful shadow of a ghostly smile. Her head hung back. She was "dead as earth."

I forgot the Little Ones, forgot the murderous Princess, forgot the body in my arms, and wandered away, searching for my Lona. The doors and windows were crowded with brute-faced onlookers who jeered at me but dared not speak, for they saw the white leopardess trailing close at my heel. I spurned her with my foot. She hesitated a moment, then followed me again.

I reached the square: the little army was gone! Its emptiness roused me. Where were the Little Ones, *her* Little Ones? I had lost her children! I stared helplessly about me, staggered to a pillar, and sank upon its base.

As I sat gazing at the still countenance, it seemed to offer a live, momentary smile. I never doubted it was an illusion, yet I believed what it said: I would yet see her alive! It was not she who was lost, but I who was lost, and she would find me!

I rose to pursue the Little Ones, instinctively seeking the gate by which we had entered. I looked around, but saw no trace of the leopardess.

The street was rapidly filling with a fierce crowd. They saw me encumbered with my dead, but for a time dared not assail me. Before I reached the gate, however, they had gathered courage. The women began to hustle me; I held on heedless. A man pushed against my sacred burden; I kicked him away, sending him howling. But the crowd pressed upon me, and fearing for the dead who could take no further hurt, I clasped my treasure closer and freed my right arm. That instant, a commotion arose in the street behind me. The crowd broke, and through it came the Little Ones I had left in the palace. Ten of them rode four of the elephants; on the other two elephants lay the Princess, bound hand and foot, quite still, save that her eyes rolled in their ghastly sockets. Two other Little Ones rode behind her on Lona's horse. Every now and then, the wise creatures bearing her threw their trunks backward to feel the tightness of her cords.

I walked on in front, leading the way out of the city. What an end to the hopes with which I entered this evil place! We had captured the bad Princess, and lost our all-beloved Queen! My life was bare; my heart was empty!
