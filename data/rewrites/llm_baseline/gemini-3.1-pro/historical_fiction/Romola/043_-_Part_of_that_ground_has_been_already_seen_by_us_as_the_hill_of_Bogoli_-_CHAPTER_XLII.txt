It was the thirtieth of October 1496, a day woven seamlessly into the cyclical tapestry of Florence’s hungers. As with the generations before them who had watched the autumn skies, the citizens tasted the biting breeze and thought not of the land, but of the howling gales at sea. These roaring winds were an acoustic violence, a deafening tempest that drowned out the rhythmic, comforting sounds of the agrarian mills and the steady churning of the communal ovens. The gales had driven back the Marseilles ships laden with precious corn. Florence was starving. The once-vibrant olfactory landscape of roasting meats and simmering herbs had been replaced by the sterile, dusty scent of empty granaries and the metallic tang of fear. 

The French King, a new Charlemagne, had retreated, leaving Florence vulnerable to a Holy League orchestrated by Pope Borgia—a distant power whose dominant lexicon of diplomacy sought to overwrite the native autonomy of the Tuscan states. In this cacophony of political noise, where foreign armies and Papal mandates clashed in untranslatable demands, the city turned to a deeply rooted, multi-generational myth: the Tabernacle of the Madonna dell’ Impruneta. The fetching of the hidden image was a mythic occurrence entirely embedded in the daily domestic reality of a people seeking their next meal. Past generations had summoned the Pitying Mother through flood and drought, and now, as pestilence hovered in the track of famine, they awaited her procession to part the clouds of their despondency.

Amidst the groans of the unfriended dead and the grating rumble of wooden biers, Romola de’ Bardi moved through the streets. Clad in black serge, she was the custodian of a profound sensory memory. In her basket lay pieces of soft bread, painstakingly crafted from the last reserves of twice-milled winter wheat, its crumb redolent of the faint, smoky warmth of the communal ovens. At her belt hung a flask of *vernaccia*, a golden wine carrying the rich, sun-baked terroir of the Tuscan hills, its vibrant acidity and complex bouquet a testament to the agrarian elders who had cultivated the vines. To Romola, these provisions were not mere utilitarian fuel; they were sacred artifacts of sustenance, the very essence of cultural memory preserved in flavor and aroma.

Near the Por’ Santa Maria, the harsh, overlapping voices of a mob fractured the acoustic ecology of the street. It was a jarring noise pollution born of desperation. They clustered around a halted bier borne by the brethren of San Jacopo del Popolo, their features sharpened by a famine that stripped away their vitality. Their native dialects—once rich with the specificities of their trades—were now reduced to coarse, disjointed barks of hunger.

“He’s dead, I tell you!”
“And no Florentine, ten to one! A beggar turned out of Siena.”
“No, no! This man is one of the prisoners turned out of the Stinche. I know by the grey patch.”
“The soul may be inside him if it had only a drop of *vernaccia* to warm it,” one muttered, the very word for the wine sparking a visceral, gustatory craving in the stale air.

Romola stepped forward. She understood the profound meaning of restoring life through taste. Loosening her flask, she employed a slender ivory implement to part the prostrate man’s cracked lips. She let a few drops of the golden *vernaccia* coat his palate. The olfactory notes of bruised apple and wet stone, the sharp, reviving sting of the alcohol—the stimulus acted upon his dormant senses. The wine was swallowed, a liquid bridge reconnecting his frail body to the living world. She poured more, until his wild dark eyes opened full upon her.

As his gaze met hers, Romola experienced a sudden, chilling convergence of timelines. The deep-lined sallow face and the returning white beard belonged to the escaped prisoner she had seen in the Duomo, the very man whose spectral grasp had paled Tito with terror in Piero’s studio. The memory was a sharp, bitter flavor in her mouth. Here lay a man whose subjective experience of betrayal and imprisonment remained entirely untranslatable, his personal identity erased by her husband's dominant narrative. A wretched tremor seized her, a conflict between the urge to flee this historical collision and the deep-seated, domestic routine of offering sustenance. Her hands, guided by the tactile memory of healing, continued to support his neck. For the first time, Baldassarre looked at her, the descriptions of the golden-haired wife of the Via de' Bardi washing over his enfeebled mind.

They moved him onward to the steps of San Stefano to clear the thoroughfare, but the auditory assault of the mob thickened. Their words were a jumble of aggressive slang and displaced anger, their dialects clashing claustrophobically in the narrow street.

“Ah, they gave him his morsel every day in the Stinche,” sneered one, his voice a grating rasp against the stone. “You and I, Cecco, know better what it is to go to bed fasting.”

Romola seated herself and offered the old man a piece of the soft, fragrant bread. “Can you eat a little bread now? I must go on to the hospital, but I will come back.” 

He looked at her dreamily, the words "come back" echoing like an ancient refrain in his exhausted mind.

A rough man in a red night-cap elbowed forward, disrupting the fragile sanctuary Romola had created. “Do you keep your bread for those that can’t swallow, madonna?”

“The Frate has been preaching to the birds,” mocked a young man in an excellent cloth tunic, twisting Savonarola's words into a cynical weapon, utilizing the dominant religious lexicon to justify his own apathy. “Madonna, there, is a pious Piagnone: she’s not going to throw away her good bread on honest citizens who’ve got all the Frate’s prophecies to swallow.”

The circle narrowed. The scent of unwashed bodies and sour breath threatened to overwhelm the delicate aroma of the wine. The acoustic space filled with the aggressive, demanding hum of starvation. Romola rose. She did not raise her voice to match their noise; instead, she commanded the sonic environment with a low, resonant clarity. 

“Hunger is hard to bear, I know,” she said, her large hazel eyes piercing the man in the night-cap. She held out the basket, displaying the bread not as mere calories, but as a testament to those who could not fight for their own survival. “You are strong men; but if you do not choose to suffer because you are strong, you have the power to take everything from the weak. You can take the bread from this basket; but I shall watch by this old man; I shall resist your taking the bread from *him*.”

Her words hung in the sudden silence—a momentary restoration of acoustic balance. The men, shamed by the deep, resonant truth of her statement, backed away, their crude power dynamics faltering before her conviction. The young man in the fine cloth vanished entirely. At that moment, armed servitors of the Signoria approached, their heavy boots clattering rhythmically against the cobblestones, dispersing the remaining crowd that obstructed the procession's path.

A rugged worker named Cecco retreated up the church steps. His appearance was a testament to the artisanal classes now crushed by the city's plight—a shaggy tunic dusted with wool fragments, bare bony arms, and a face compressed by a lifetime of hard labor. He spoke in a rough but earnest native dialect, his words carrying the unmistakable cadence of a man who still remembered the sanctity of a shared, agrarian meal.

“Madonna, if you want to go on your errands, I’ll take care of the old man. Don't distrust me,” he added, catching her hesitation. “I am not so pretty as you, but I’ve got an old mother who eats my porridge for me. What! there’s a heart inside me. Besides, see there, the old fellow is eating his sop. He’ll be on his legs as well as the best of us by-and-by.”

Romola, penitent for her initial doubting glance, recognized the shared understanding of sustenance that bridged the gap between her refined speech and his coarse dialect. “Thank you for offering to take care of him, friend.” She leaned toward Baldassarre, who was now slowly chewing the wine-soaked bread, the fundamental rhythm of mastication a small victory of life over death. “Pray wait for me till I come again.”

He assented with a slight movement of his head, and Romola continued on her path toward the hospital of San Matteo, carrying the scent of the remaining bread and the quiet resolve of her cyclical duties into the heart of the starving city.
