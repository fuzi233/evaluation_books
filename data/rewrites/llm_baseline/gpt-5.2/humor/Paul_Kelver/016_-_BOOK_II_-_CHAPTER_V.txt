Dan and I made a compact over supper, the sort of compact a man makes most sincerely when there is gravy in it. The beef had been coaxed into tenderness by Dan’s patient spooning, and the onions—sliced thin as theatre excuses—had gone down in that slow, golden sigh they make when they surrender to butter. With the kettle murmuring on the hob like an elderly aunt who has opinions but does not insist on them, we exchanged our histories between mouthfuls, as men do when the food is good and the future still feels edible.

Dan’s past, it turned out, had been as various as the contents of a boarding-house teapot.

Leaving school earlier than I, he had gone to Cambridge; then—two years later—his father had died of an old wound from the Indian Mutiny that had never forgiven him.

“You mightn’t imagine that would upset me,” Dan said, laying down his fork with the care of a man who respects cutlery, “but it hit me hard. At Cambridge I discovered I was, by temperament, a scholar. At school they made learning into a sort of compulsory march-past—like being herded through a picture gallery by Cook’s, where the goal is not to look at anything, only to be able to say you have been there. At college I chose my own subjects, and studied them in my own way. I fed on knowledge, instead of being crammed with it like a Strassburg goose.”

He had returned to London and taken a position in a bank through the chairmanship of an old friend of his father’s. The advantage, he said, was time for study; the disadvantage was the City’s peculiar way of grinding sound into the mind—pens scratching like a thousand trapped beetles, coins clinking with the cheerfulness of chains, the constant clatter of urgency. After his mother’s death, he shook the dust of it off and joined a small fit-up theatrical company.

For eighteen months he lived among grease-paint and draughty halls; played everything from Romeo to Paul Pry; helped paint scenery; helped bill-post—an art, he assured me, in which the paste-laden poster possesses a natural malice and will, if treated without reverence, rebound upon your own head and remain there, sticking to your hair like a public opinion you do not deserve.

Wearying of the stage proper, he joined a circus; became “Signor Ricardo, the daring bare-back rider,” and also one of the “Brothers Roscius” in a trapeze act. Then, inclined again toward respectability—perhaps because he had begun to value his bones—he waited three months at Ostend, and from that descended (or ascended; social geography is uncertain) to footman.

“One never knows,” Dan observed. “I may end up a society novelist. Inside knowledge of the aristocracy will give me an advantage over competitors who only know them from the outside of their gates.”

Other callings followed like courses at a dinner too generous to be wise: he tramped through Ireland with a fiddle; through Scotland with a lecture on Palestine aided by dissolving views; he was a billiard-marker; then a schoolmaster. For the last three months he had been a journalist—dramatic and musical critic to a Sunday paper. Often I had dreamed of such a thing for myself, imagining it to be a life composed chiefly of free stalls and superior adjectives.

“How did you get it?” I asked.

Dan laughed, and poured the coffee, which came out dark and aromatic, with that slight bitterness that tastes like determination.

“The idea occurred to me one afternoon,” he said, “sauntering down the Strand and wondering what I should do next. I was on my beam ends with only a few shillings. Luck, however, has always seemed to take an interest in my education. I went into the first newspaper office I saw, went upstairs to the first floor, and opened the first door without knocking. I passed through a small empty room into a larger one, littered with books and papers. It was getting dark. A gentleman of extremely youthful figure was running round and round cursing, because he had upset the ink, couldn’t find the matches, and had broken the bell-pull. In the gloom, assuming him to be the office boy, I thought it would be amusing to mistake him for the editor. As it happened, he was the editor.

“I lit the gas for him and found him another ink-pot. He was slim and had the voice and manner of a schoolboy, though I dare say he was five or six-and-twenty. He had the job because his aunt owned the paper. He asked if he knew me. Before I could explain he did not, he went on talking, oppressed by a general sense of injury.

“‘People come into this office,’ he said, ‘as if it’s a shelter from the rain—people I don’t know from Adam. And that damned fool downstairs lets them march straight up—anybody: men with articles on safety valves, people who’ve only come to raise a row. Half my work I do on the stairs.’

“I suggested he should insist on strangers writing their business on a slip of paper. He thought it genius.

“‘For the last three-quarters of an hour,’ he said, ‘I’ve been trying to finish one column, and four times I’ve been interrupted.’

“At that moment someone knocked.

“‘I won’t see him!’ he cried. ‘I don’t care who it is. Send him away! Send everybody away!’

“I went to the door. An elderly gentleman tried to sweep past. I barred the way, closed the editorial door behind me, told him the editor was not to be seen, and handed him paper to write his business. For half an hour I stayed in the ante-room, turning away ten or a dozen people. Their affairs cannot have been important, or I should have heard of them later.

“The last was a tired-looking man smoking a cigarette. I asked his name.

“He looked at me, then said, ‘Idiot!’

“I remained firm, refused to let him pass.

“‘It’s awkward,’ he said. ‘Couldn’t you make an exception for the sub-editor on press night?’

“I told him it was against instructions.

“‘Oh, all right,’ he said. ‘I only wanted to know who’s going to the Royalty to-night. It’s seven already.’

“Then the thought came. If a sub-editor doesn’t know who’s being sent to the theatre, it means the dramatic critic’s post is vacant for some reason.

“‘That’ll be all right,’ I told him. ‘I shall be in time.’

“He asked if I’d arranged with the Guv’nor.

“‘Just waiting to see him again for a moment,’ I replied. ‘Have you got the ticket?’

“‘Haven’t seen it.’

“‘About a column?’ I suggested.

“‘Three-quarters,’ he corrected, and went.

“The instant he was gone, I slipped downstairs, met a printer’s boy coming up, and asked the name of the sub—tall fellow, black moustache, looks as if sleep is a rumour.

“‘Penton,’ said the boy.

“I went back in. The editor was still furious at the universe.

“‘What is it now?’ he snapped.

“‘I only want the ticket for the Royalty Theatre,’ I said. ‘Penton says you’ve got it.’

“‘Don’t know where it is,’ he growled.

“I found it on his desk after a little hunting.

“‘Who’s going?’ he asked.

“‘I am,’ I said. And I went.

“They’ve never discovered to this day that I appointed myself. Penton thinks I’m some relation of the proprietress, and everyone treats me with respect. Mrs. Wallace thinks I’m Penton’s discovery—she trusts his judgment; and with her I get on. The paper isn’t thriving; the salary’s small but enough. Journalism suits me; I shall likely stick to it.”

“You’ve been something of a rolling stone,” I remarked.

“From the stone’s view,” Dan answered, “I never understood the advantage of moss. I’d rather stay visible, movable. But now—what are your plans? Your opera—thanks to those gentlemen, the gods have christened ‘Goggles’—will run through the winter. Are you getting any salary?”

“Thirty shillings a week,” I told him, “and full salary for matinees.”

“Say two pounds,” said Dan. “With my three, we could set up our own establishment. I have an idea—original. Shall we work it out together?”

I told him with fervour that nothing could please me more—partly because I meant it, and partly because he had just produced a dish of devilled kidneys whose peppery steam rose like a hymn.

“There are four delightful rooms in Queen’s Square,” he said. “Charming furniture. A fine sitting-room in front, two bedrooms and a kitchen behind. The last tenant was a Polish Revolutionary. Three months ago he was foolish enough to go back to Russia, and he’s now living rent free. The landlord—Deleglise, an engraver—is an original. He occupies the rest of the house. He says I can have the rooms for whatever I like to offer. I suggest thirty shillings a week. Under ordinary circumstances they’re worth three or four pounds. But he’ll only let them on condition we do for ourselves. He hates petticoats—especially elderly petticoats. He has one servant, an old Frenchwoman—she was his mother’s housekeeper—and he and she do the housework together, mostly quarrelling over it. No other domestic female will he permit inside. Not even an occasional charwoman for us. But it’s a beautiful old Georgian house—Adams mantelpieces, stone staircase, oak-panelled rooms—and our portion would be the entire second floor: no pianos, no landlady. He’s a widower with one child—a girl of about fourteen, perhaps older. What do you say? I’m a fair cook. Will you be house-and-parlour-maid?”

A week later we were installed.

For nearly two years we lived there, and we did not perish; which, considering the prejudice that men left to themselves can do little more than put themselves to bed—roughly—should be regarded as a triumph of civilisation. The house itself had a handsome hush: old wood that swallowed footfalls, a staircase that gave out a dignified creak like a cello clearing its throat, and rooms panelled so thickly they seemed to remember every conversation that had ever been held within them. Outside, London’s iron rattle and cab-grind came and went like weather; inside, the old place took the edge off it. Deleglise’s hatred of “petticoats” may have been eccentric, but he had, by accident, protected us from a good deal of shrill domestic warfare. Modern life, even then, seemed bent on making a loud advertisement of itself; Queen’s Square, for all its proximity to the city’s clangour, still held pockets of older sound—sparrows, the knock of a milkman’s can, the human voice unamplified and therefore worth listening to.

Dan had not boasted idly. His plain cooking was excellent; his coffee—properly ground, properly timed, boiled only as much as it ought, and never bullied—was a solace. His omelettes were as neat as folded letters, and his devilled kidneys had that honest sting that makes you feel grateful to still possess a tongue. Had he remained within the boundaries of what he could do without passion, our domestic chronicle would have been as monotonously successful as a recipe that never varies.

But there is a temptation in cookery, as in art: applause. An occasional tea or supper party would kindle Dan’s ambition; old Deleglise, who for twenty years had made cooking his hobby, would lure him toward perilous heights. Dan would “experiment.”

Pastry, it became plain, was his enemy. His hand was heavy, his temperament too earnest; he attacked flour as if it were a moral problem. There was a thing called lemon sponge which demanded the beating of eggs until they held air as a net holds fish. In the cookery-book—a fat volume, lubricious with pictures of brightly coloured dishes—it rose airy and dazzling, an edible cloud. In Dan’s hands it came to table suggesting, in both colour and architecture, a miniature earthquake. It was spongy, certainly; you could force it apart with spoon and fork and it yielded with a gentle tearing sound, like linen giving up.

He had another favourite: manna-cake. Of this I will only say that if it resembled in any degree the substance that sustained the Children of Israel, then their fretfulness becomes comprehensible and their nostalgic yearnings for Egyptian fleshpots acquire a certain dignity. One slice—solid, crusty outside, marshy toward the centre—filled most people with a sense not only of repletion but of having done their duty.

For supper parties he would attempt trifles—none open to the criticism of being light as air—soufflés that guests, despite my admonishing kicks, persisted in referring to as pudding; and in winter, pancakes. Eventually he became competent with these, but in the beginning he insisted on tossing them, because Thorough was his motto. Turning a pancake by knife and fork is less showy but more certain; it avoids the danger of catching the half-baked thing upon one’s head instead of in the pan, of dropping it into the fire, or among the cinders. Dan, however, regarded these accidents as the price of artistry. And after all, small particles of coal—or a few hairs—can always be detected by the conscientious feeder and removed, thereby adding a mild athleticism to the meal.

For twenty-three hours out of every twenty-four Dan was the most even-tempered man alive. For the remaining hour he became another creature entirely—uncertain, irritable, even ungrateful. At first, in pure good nature, I offered counsel: why the custard was pimply, why the mayonnaise suggested hair-oil. My reward was sneers, insults, abuse, followed—if I did not retreat quickly enough—by spoilt tomatoes, cold coffee-grounds, anything to hand. Wounded, I would withdraw; he would kick the door after me.

His greatest enemy, in his own view, was the oven. The oven thwarted his finest schemes. It was the oven’s fault that the snowy bun emerged like red sandstone, that macaroni cheese assumed the aspect of Cambrian clay. One could have sympathised more, had his language been restrained. As it was, the virulence of his reproaches almost inclined one to take the oven’s side. The poor iron box had no voice in its own defence; it endured insult with the patience of an object, while Dan, the artist, shouted at it as if it were a rival critic.

As for our housekeeping, I can speak in terms of unqualified praise—of myself. There are, alas, fussy house-maids—who has not suffered them?—who overdo the thing, lacking repose, lacking instinct. Perpetual stirring-up of dust is, I have always maintained, a scientific error. Left alone, dust is harmless; it may even be regarded as a delicate domestic bloom, giving homeliness to objects that would otherwise gleam coldly. Nature, in her wisdom, has ordained that dust, on table, floor, picture-frame, shall sink and settle. There it remains, quiet and inoffensive—until some woman with a cloth goes summoning it into the air, where it can be inhaled by innocent people.

So also with crumbs, bits of string, eggshell, match-stumps, cigarette ends: what fitter place than under the nearest mat? To sweep them up is labour and moral peril. They cling, you grow cross, you curse, you deteriorate. Under the mat they go willingly. Compromise is statesmanship. There will come a day when you will be grateful for an excuse not to do something else you ought to be doing; then you may take up the mats and feel virtuous at the quantity of work that must be done—some time or other.

To distinguish between the essential and the non-essential—there, I have observed, woman fails. What is the use of washing a cup that will be dirty again in half an hour? If the cat is willing and able to polish a plate until not a speck of grease remains, why deprive her of pleasure to inflict toil on yourself? If a bed looks made and feels made, then for all practical purposes it is made; why upset it to put it straight again? It would surprise most women how much labour may be avoided by a judicious respect for inertia.

In needlework I confess I never excelled. Dan could handle a thimble; my own second finger was a coward. When called upon it had to be found, other fingers removed, and then it would push feebly, slip, prick itself badly against the needle-head, and retire in terror, incapable of further service. More practical, I found, to press the needle through by means of door or table, employing furniture as an ally against textiles.

The opera, as Dan had predicted, ran far into the following year. When it ended, another took its place—one in which “Goggles” appeared as a principal—and that was even more successful. After Nelson Square, my salary of thirty-five shillings—occasionally forty—seemed princely. Before my eyes floated the possibility of becoming a great opera singer. On six hundred pounds a week, I felt I could be content.

Then the O’Kelly set about puncturing my bubble with the tenderness of a man who dislikes seeing others drift toward rocks.

“Ye’d be makin’ a mistake, me boy,” explained the O’Kelly. “Ye’d be wastin’ ye’re time. I wouldn’t tell ye so if I weren’t convinced of it.”

“I know it is not powerful,” I admitted.

“Ye might almost call it thin,” added the O’Kelly.

“It might be good enough for comic opera,” I argued. “People seem to succeed in comic opera without much voice.”

“Sure, there ye’re right,” he sighed. “An’ of course if ye had an exceptionally fine presence and were strikingly handsome—”

“One can do a good deal with make-up,” I suggested.

He shook his head. “It’s never quite the same thing. It would depend on your acting.”

Immediately I began to dream of becoming a second Kean, of taking Macready’s place. Why should I not combine it with literature? Fill Drury Lane in the evening, write epoch-making novels in the morning, and author my own plays in between meals.

Every day I studied in the reading-room of the British Museum. Wearying of success in Art, I might eventually enter Parliament. Why not a Prime Minister with a thorough knowledge of history? With Ollendorf for guide, I continued French and German. It might be the diplomatic service that would attract me in my old age. An ambassadorship! A pleasant winding-down of a brilliant career. Even the Museum had its own peculiar music—pages turning in waves, pencils rasping, the hush that is not silence but discipline; and outside, the city’s clang pressed at the windows like a salesman. I began to suspect that modern life’s chief assault on the soul is not poverty but noise—noise that flattens thought into slogans, until even art becomes something to be hawked in the street.

There was excuse for my optimism. Everything was going well. A story of mine had been accepted—by a journal whose name escapes me now, and which is dead. Most papers that printed my earliest efforts are dead. My contributions might be considered their swan songs. Proofs had been sent, corrected, returned. But proofs are not facts; once before I had been lifted sky-high only to tumble because the paper collapsed before my story appeared. (Ah, why did they delay? It might have saved them.)

This time I kept my counsel. Each publication day I slipped out early, bought the paper, and scanned it eagerly. For weeks hope teased me and went away laughing. Then at last—one bright winter day in January—walking down the Harrow Road, I stopped dead before a bill outside a small news-vendor’s shop. There, in blunt print, was my name as I had never seen it: “The Witch of Moel Sarbod: a legend of Mona, by Paul Kelver.” For that, I had even risked discovery by the Lady ’Ortensia.

My legs trembled as if I had been asked to sing without rehearsal. I went into the shop. A ruffianly man in dirty shirt-sleeves looked astonished that anyone should want a copy, but at length found one on the floor under the counter—as if my future had been swept down there with the day’s litter. With it in my pocket, I retraced my steps as in a dream. On a seat in Paddington Green I sat and read it.

The hundred best books! I have waded through them all since; none ever charmed me as that one short story in that now-forgotten journal. Need I add it was sad and sentimental? Once upon a time there lived a mighty King—one—(but the names were Welsh and unpronounceable, and their choice had cost me hours among lexicons in the Museum, with gazetteers and translations from early Celtic poets, complete with footnotes). He loved and was loved by a beautiful Princess, whose name, translated, was Purity.

One day the King, hunting, lost his way; and being weary, lay down and slept. By chance the spot lay near a place which, by infinite pains and a magnifying glass, I had discovered upon the map—a name meaning in English the Cave of the Waters—where dwelt a wicked Sorceress. While he slept she cast her spell so that he woke forgetting honour and people, desiring only the Witch of Moel Sarbod.

In that kingdom by the sea lived a great Magician; and Purity, loving the King more than herself, went and begged his aid. There was but one way: with bare feet Purity must climb the rocky path to the Witch’s dwelling, go boldly up, fearing neither sharp claws nor strong teeth, and kiss her upon the mouth. Thus the spirit of Purity would pass into the Witch’s soul and make her a woman; but the Witch’s form and spirit would pass into Purity, transforming her, and she must abide forever in the Cave of the Waters.

So Purity gave herself that the King might live. With bleeding feet she climbed, clasped the Witch, kissed her upon the mouth. The Witch became a woman, and reigned with the King wisely and helpfully. Purity became a hideous witch, and to this day abides on Moel Sarbod, where the Cave of the Waters is. They who climb the mountain still hear, above the cataract’s roar, the sobbing of Purity, the King’s betrothed—though many say it is rather a joyous song of love triumphant.

No writer worth his salt is satisfied with what he writes; so I am told, and so I try to believe. Evidently I am not worth my salt. Candid friends—and others—to whom I had shown my work in my salad days, asking for “a frank opinion,” meaning, of course, unadulterated praise, assured me for my good that this first-born was a feeble, ill-shaped child: its attempted early-English a cross between The Pilgrim’s Progress and Old Moore’s Almanac; its scenery (which had cost me weeks of research) an apparent attempt to sum up, in the manner of a local guide-book, the Garden of Eden combined with Dante’s Inferno; its pathos of the penny-plain and two-penny-coloured order.

Perhaps they were right. Much have I written since that seemed good at the time and later made me blush and frown. But of this—my first to whom the gods gave life—I am no judge. Touching those yellowing, badly printed pages, I felt again the deep thrill that ran through me when first I unfolded and read them. Again I was young, and life opened out immeasurable, no goal too high. This child of my brain—my work—would spread my name throughout the world. It would be a household word in lands I should never see. Friends whose voices I should never hear would speak of me. I would die, but it would live—yield fresh seed, bear fruit I could not imagine. Generations unborn would read it and remember me. My thoughts, my words, my spirit: in it I should live again; it would keep my memory green.
