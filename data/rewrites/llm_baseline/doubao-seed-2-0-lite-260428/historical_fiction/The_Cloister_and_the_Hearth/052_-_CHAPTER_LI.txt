When Margaret Brandt descended the stairs that afternoon, every line of her had softened. Her eyes glistened with recent tears, but they were steady, calm, all the hot rage and bitter hurt that had possessed her for days washed clean away.

“Denys,” she murmured softly, stepping to where the old soldier whittled by the hearth, “I have my orders now, from Gerard’s own hand. I am to read his letter aloud to his folk.”

The soldier gaped at her. “Nay, you will never do that—after all they have done to part you two?”
“Ay, will I.” A faint, tender smile touched her lips. “I see not a jot of kindness in that letter toward them, Denys, not a jot. But if I hated them as poison itself, I would not disobey my love. To obey him is so sweet—sweetest of all, to obey one who is a hundred leagues away across wild roads, who cannot force my duty with a harsh word or a hand, who only trusts my love to make me keep his command. Ah, my Gerard, my darling, if you stood before me now, I might have pouted and slighted your orders, still smarting from the ill will his folk have shown me. But now? If you bade me wade straight into the raging North Sea and read your sweet letter to the sharks, I would go. So go to his mother, Denys. Tell her I hold a letter from Gerard, and if she and hers would hear it, I am their servant. Let them name their hour, and I will seat them as best my small lodgings allow, and welcome them as warmly as I may.”

Denys wasted no time trundling off to Catherine’s cottage, where the whole family sat at their midday dinner. The news that a long, sure letter had arrived from their long-lost Gerard sent a buzz of joy round the table so loud the pewter mugs rattled. Before they could even cheer aloud, Denys added, “And her heart is softened. She will read every word of it to you herself, with her own pretty mouth. You have but to choose the time that suits you best.”

Catherine frowned, setting down her trencher hard. “What, does she think none of us can read but her? Let her send the letter over here, and we will read it ourselves.”
“Nay, mother,” little Kate put in gently, “mayhap she cannot bear to let it leave her hand. She loves him far too dearly.”
Cornelis snickered from the far end of the table. “Doubtless she thinks we will steal it. She means to wedge herself into this family for good, using that scrap of parchment as her key.”

Denys fixed the young man with a look of withering scorn. “There spoke a rotten heart. Let me tell you true: that girl hates all of you like wormwood. Do not mistake me, I do not defend her wild temper—but that is the plain truth. And yet, maugre all that spleen, at a single word from Gerard she proffers to read you his letter, and her voice is sweet as honey. That is a fair offer, and none of you should snarl at it.”

“Honest soldier speaks sense,” rumbled Eli, the patriarch of the house, thumping one calloused hand on the table. “It deserves a civil reply. Hold your tongues, all you squabbling women, and I will answer her. Go tell her that I, Gerard’s father, set aside every last old grudge for this grace of hers, and thank her heartily. If she would earn double that thanks, let her send my son’s letter back with your faithful hand. I will read it aloud to every one of his own flesh and blood, and I will return it to her as surely as my name is Eli, son of Dierich, son of William, son of Luke—free burgher of Tergou, same as all my forebears, and a man who keeps his word.”
“Ay, and a man who keeps it far better than most who swear to it,” Catherine put in fondly. “The only such man I ever met.”
“Hold your peace, wife,” Eli chided, not unkindly.

“Art a man of sense, Eli, a proper thing-em-bob,” Denys cheered, snapping his fingers. “That lass will be right glad to obey Gerard and not have to face the lot of you she hates, mark my words. She is all submission today, says obedience tastes like honey. And small wonder—she gets so little of it, what with her pretty face and soft tongue, and the way she flies into little fits that scare the wits out of us soldiers if we cross her, now chiding us sharp as a thorn, now petting us like lost lambs. She has turned two stout, swashbuckling lads into her most loyal slaves: I am the ironing ruffian these days, and the other fool does all the washing. Before long, when the little one comes into the world, I shall be rocking the cradle, and that knave will be scrubbing all the bibs and tuckers. So I will go fetch the letter from her right now. You will let me bide and hear it read, will you not?”
“Else our hearts would be black as Newcastle coal,” Catherine said warmly.

Denys left grinning, but he returned not long later, crestfallen, dragging his boots across the threshold. “She will not let that letter leave her hand. Not to me, not to any one of you, not to any man or woman alive.”
“I knew it,” Cornelis snickered.
“Whisht, man, whisht,” Eli waved him quiet. “Let Denys tell the whole of it.”

“I argued with her, to be sure,” Denys said. “Said, ‘Be ruled by me, lass.’ ‘Not I,’ says she. ‘Come now,’ says I, ‘what of that sweet honey obedience you spoke of?’ ‘You are a fool,’ she says, and laughs right in my face. ‘Obedience to Gerard is sweet. But who ever said obeying any other soul on God’s green earth was sweet?’ At the last, she softened a little, and gave me this scrap of written paper, to give to you folks.”

Eli took it, scanned the neat, looping handwriting, and read it out almost in a whisper, for the words were Gerard’s own:
“And sweetheart, if these lines travel safe to you, make trial of my people’s hearts. Maybe they are turned toward me, now that I am far away. If that is so, they will show it to you, for they cannot show it to me now. Read this letter aloud to them, then! But I strictly forbid you to let it leave your own hand. And if they still hold aloof from you, why, say nought of me, let them think me dead. Obey me in this, for if you slight my will here, you do not love me.”

A hush fell over the cottage. The little scrap of parchment was passed from hand to hand, every one leaning in to trace the ink, as if touching Gerard’s own words could bring him closer.
“Well,” Catherine said at last, huffing a little, “that changes matters. But still, methinks it should be them coming to us, not us trundling over to her lodgings.”
“Alas, mother, what difference does that make?” little Kate pleaded.
“Nay, it makes enough,” Eli said. “Tell her we are too many of us to traipse all the way to her small rooms. Bid her come here, the sooner the better.”

When Denys had left, Eli sighed, and admitted it rankled him. “When that lass crosses my threshold, all the mischief and misery she has sown in this house these past years will come pouring in after her, all at once. But what else could I do, wife? We have to hear news of our Gerard. I saw it in your eyes, I felt it in my own bones. She is backed by our undutiful but still well-loved son, and so she is stronger than us. She has brought our noses right down to the grindstone, the sly, sharp little jade. But never heed. We will hear that letter, and then she can go back out the door as unblessed as she came unwelcome.”
“Set your mind at rest,” Catherine said, though there was a faint, unmistakeable tone of regret in her voice. “She will not come at all.”

Not long after, Richart arrived, the family’s long-absent son, grave and dignified in his burgher’s velvet robe, gold chain glinting at his throat, starched ruff soft at his neck, furred cap set square on his head. They welcomed him not just with affection, but with deep respect—for he had climbed a rung higher on the social ladder than his parents, and such rises in medieval Holland were marked as clearly as the steps on a household’s staircase. When he was admitted to the family council, he made no secret, though he was far from rude, that his burgher pride was deeply wounded that they had even deigned to treat with Margaret Brandt. “I see the temptation,” he said, stiff. “But who among us has not, at times, had to wish one way and do quite another?” His words cast a cold chill over the old couple.
“Vex not yourself, dear Richart,” little Kate soothed him. “Mother says Margaret will not come at all.”
“All the better, then,” Richart said. “I fear that if she does cross this threshold, I will turn right around and hie me back to Amsterdam that very hour.”

Just then, Denys popped his head in through the doorway, grinning. “She will be here at three, by the great dial of the town clock.”

Not a soul spoke. They all stared at one another, the heavy, charged silence hanging thick as smoke in the low, raftered room.
