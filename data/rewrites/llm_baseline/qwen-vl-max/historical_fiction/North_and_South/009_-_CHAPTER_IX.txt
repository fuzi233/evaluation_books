“Let China’s earth, enriched with coloured stains,
Pencil’d with gold, and streaked with azure veins,
The grateful flavour of the Indian leaf,
Or Mocha’s sunburnt berry glad receive.”

The morning after her encounter with Higgins and his daughter, Mr. Hale ascended to the small drawing-room at an unseasonable hour. He moved slowly from one object to another—examining a framed print, adjusting a book on the shelf, lingering over a vase—as if in quiet contemplation. Yet Margaret, watching him from her seat near the window, knew this was no idle inspection. It was a nervous habit, a way of delaying the moment when he would speak what he both longed and dreaded to say.

At last, he turned to her, his voice gentle but firm: “My dear, I’ve invited Mr. Thornton to tea this evening.”

Mrs. Hale sat back in her armchair, eyes closed, her face drawn with the familiar ache that had settled upon her lately like a shadow. But at her husband’s words, she stirred abruptly, her voice rising in irritation. “Mr. Thornton? And tonight? What possible reason could he have for coming here? And Dixon is washing my finest muslins and laces—there’s no soft water, not with these dreadful east winds that seem determined to blow all winter through Milton.”

“The wind is turning,” said Mr. Hale, glancing out the window where smoke drifted steadily from the east. Though he still struggled with the compass points, he made them fit his mood as best he could.

“Don’t tell me!” Mrs. Hale shuddered, pulling her shawl tighter around her shoulders. “But whether it’s east or west, I suppose this man comes.”

“Oh, Mama,” Margaret replied, stepping forward, “you’ve never seen Mr. Thornton. He’s the kind of man who thrives on opposition—on storms, on enemies, on hardship. The worse the weather, the more certain we are to see him. But don’t worry—I’ll help Dixon. I’m becoming quite skilled at starching. And he won’t need any grand entertainment beyond talking with Papa. Papa, I confess I’m eager to meet your Pythias—your Damon, as you once called him. I’ve only seen him once before, and we were so awkward, so unsure of what to say, that our conversation didn’t go far.”

“I’m not sure you’d ever find him agreeable, Margaret,” Mr. Hale said thoughtfully. “He’s no man of fashion. Not a lady’s man.”

Margaret smiled, lifting her chin slightly. “I’ve never cared much for ladies’ men, Papa. But Mr. Thornton isn’t here as a suitor—he’s here as your friend, someone who respects you deeply.”

“The only person in Milton,” Mrs. Hale added dryly, “who does.”

“So we shall welcome him warmly,” said Margaret. “And we’ll serve cocoa-nut cakes—Dixon will be flattered to make them. And I’ll iron your caps, Mama, if you’ll let me.”

All morning, Margaret found herself wishing Mr. Thornton were miles away. She had planned other things: writing to Edith, reading Dante, visiting the Higginses. Instead, she stood at the ironing board, listening to Dixon’s endless complaints about the hardness of the water, the stiffness of the linen, the injustice of life. She forced herself to listen, to nod, to respond with sympathy—anything to keep the woman from venturing into deeper sorrows, lest they stir Mrs. Hale’s pain anew.

She had to remind herself again and again of her father’s regard for Mr. Thornton, to hold back the growing weariness that pressed against her temples, threatening one of the headaches that had begun to plague her. When at last she sat down, she told her mother, half-jokingly, “I’m no longer Peggy the laundry-maid. I’m Margaret Hale—the lady.”

She meant it as a jest—but the words struck too close to home. Her mother looked up sharply, her expression serious. “Yes,” she said, “if anyone had told me, when I was Miss Beresford, one of the finest young women in the countryside, that my child would spend half a day bent over a kitchen stove, scrubbing clothes like a servant, just to prepare for receiving a tradesman—and that this tradesman would be the only one in Milton who truly appreciated your father…”

“Oh, Mama!” Margaret rose quickly, her voice tight with emotion. “Don’t punish me for such careless talk. I don’t mind ironing—or any work—for you and Papa. I am still a lady, born and bred, even if I must scour floors or wash dishes. I’m tired now—just for a little while—but in half an hour, I’ll be ready to do it all again. And as for Mr. Thornton being in trade—well, he can’t help that. Poor fellow. I doubt his education prepared him for anything else.”

She turned and walked slowly to her room, needing silence, needing space. For now, she could endure no more.

Meanwhile, in Mr. Thornton’s house, a scene unfolded that mirrored yet contrasted with the Hales’. In a large, handsomely furnished dining-room, a woman of strong build and sharp features sat mending a fine tablecloth. She was past middle age, her presence commanding, her movements deliberate. Her face, though not soft, carried a dignity that drew attention—passersby often paused to watch her pass, rigid and purposeful, on her way to some fixed destination.

She wore stout black silk, every thread intact, every seam perfect. She held the cloth aloft, examining it under the light, searching for thin spots that required careful stitching. No books littered the room—only six volumes of Matthew Henry’s Bible commentaries, arranged neatly on the sideboard between a tea-urn and a lamp. From another room came the sound of piano practice—a rapid, uneven performance, each third note missing or blurred, the final chords often false, yet played with unwavering conviction.

A step echoed in the hall—decisive, heavy, unmistakably masculine.

“John! Is that you?” she called.

Her son entered, closing the door behind him. “What brings you home so early? I thought you were going to tea with Mr. Bell’s friend—Mr. Hale?”

“That’s right,” John said. “But I came home to dress.”

“Dress?” she scoffed. “When I was a girl, young men dressed once a day. Why should you dress to take tea with an old parson?”

“Mr. Hale is a gentleman,” John replied calmly. “His wife and daughter are ladies.”

“Wife and daughter?” she frowned. “Do they teach? What do they do? You’ve never mentioned them.”

“No, Mother. I’ve never met Mrs. Hale. I’ve only seen Miss Hale for a brief moment.”

“Then take care you don’t fall for a penniless girl, John.”

“I’m not easily caught,” he said, “as you well know. But I won’t have Miss Hale spoken of in that tone—it offends me. I’ve never known any young woman try to catch me, nor do I believe any has ever wasted the effort.”

Mrs. Thornton refused to yield. Pride, perhaps, kept her from conceding. “Well, I only warn you. Perhaps our Milton girls are too proud to fish for husbands—but this Miss Hale comes from the aristocratic counties, where rich husbands are counted prizes.”

John’s brow furrowed. “Mother,” he said, a short laugh escaping him, “you’ll make me confess. The only time I saw Miss Hale, she treated me with a haughty politeness that tasted of contempt. She held herself apart from me as if I were a lowly vassal, unwashed and unclean. Be at ease—she doesn’t interest me.”

“No?” she retorted. “I am not at ease. What right has she—a clergyman’s daughter, no less—to look down on you? If I were you, I’d dress for none of them—such a saucy set!”

As he turned to leave, he said, “Mr. Hale is good, gentle, learned. He is not saucy. As for Mrs. Hale—I’ll tell you what she’s like tonight, if you care to hear.”

He shut the door behind him.

“Despise my son? Treat him as a vassal?” Mrs. Thornton muttered, her voice low but fierce. “Humph! I’d like to know where she could find another like him. Boy and man, he’s the noblest, strongest heart I’ve ever known. I may be his mother—but I can see clearly. I know what Fanny was. I know what John is. Despise him? I hate her.”
