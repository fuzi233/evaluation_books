The die was well and truly cast. Sir Philip Nunnely carried the weight of his unspoken question thrumming under his skin; Shirley Keeldar felt its faint, strange echo at the back of her consciousness; and Mr. Sympson preened, utterly convinced the glittering, advantageous match he’d schemed for was as good as signed, sealed, and settled. That evening, when every last member of the Fieldhead household dined at Nunnely Priory beneath the family’s crested tapestries, had tipped every scale.

A handful of small, perfect turns had conspired to push the soft, poetic baronet to act. He’d watched Shirley all night, quiet, a faint rose flush high on her cheek, a faraway pensive air that tugged hard at his most romantic, unguarded heart. A sonnet was half-written in his head before he knew it, lines tangled like wild ivy, when one of his sisters coaxed Shirley over to the piano, begging her to sing a ballad Philip had penned himself: the least flowery, most unforced, and by far the finest of all his verses, an ode to faithful love that refused to break through disaster, that clung tighter through poverty and storm.

Just minutes before, Shirley had been staring out the tall sash window, watching that wild silver storm-light spill over the park—the same gale-tousled moonlight Louis Moore was no doubt contemplating from their oak parlour lattice back at Fieldhead. She’d seen the gale wrench at the gnarled ancient oaks, send tall, proud beeches bowing like knights before their queen, heard the low thunder of the forest roaring far below, watched clouds tear past the moon so fast the whole sky seemed to race. When she sang, she poured all that roused, wild feeling into the notes. Her voice curled soft around the tender verses, then swelled with unyielding passion, dramatic, bright, so alive that every person in the long shadowy saloon sat still, holding their breath.

Afterwards, she slipped to sit on a low half-stool, half-cushion by the hearth, and all the ladies clustered around her, staring like plain barnyard fowl gawking at a wandering egret they had never seen the like of. The Misses Sympson and Misses Nunnely could not fathom how she sang so: so much fire, so much individuality, nothing like the meek, decorous schoolgirl performance propriety dictated. Old Lady Nunnely, ensconced in her great carved armchair, stared at her stonily, her gaze spelling plain as day: This woman is not for my son. She is not one of us. Philip caught that look, his heart jolting hard in his chest—he was about to lose the prize he’d dreamed of, if he did not act fast.

The old saloon had once been Sir Monckton Nunnely’s picture gallery, long and shadowy, stretching far back, with a deep window recess tucked at one end that held nothing but a couch, a small table, and a little carved fairy cabinet: so cut off from the rest of the room that two people could speak in low tones and no one would overhear a single word. Philip sent two of his sisters off to pluck at a tuneless duet at the far end of the room, occupied the Misses Sympson with idle small talk, left the elder ladies gossiping by the fire, then drifted over to Shirley where she lingered, gazing at the portraits on the wall. He told her a soft tale of one of his ancestresses, a dark southern beauty whose portrait hung there, and when she leaned down to peer at the old missal and rosary laid out on the inlaid shelf of the cabinet in the recess, he leaned down too, and whispered his proposal, fast and breathless. Shirley froze stock still for a heartbeat, as if that soft whisper had turned her to marble, then she lifted her head, and answered him. A few moments later they parted, Shirley back to her seat by the fire, Philip lingering behind his screeching, unmusical sisters, and only Mr. Sympson, hovering by the door, had spotted the whole quiet exchange.

He raced home that night giddy, cock-a-hoop, certain the engagement was sealed. He could barely keep the secret, and the next morning, he puffed himself up before Louis Moore, his son’s tutor, announcing in grand, pompous tones that he would soon pack the household back for the south, because his long, hard work out here in Yorkshire had finally borne fruit: they were about to welcome a most eligible new relation into the family.
“Sir Philip Nunnely?” Louis had conjectured, quiet as ever, and Sympson chortled so hard he nearly choked on his snuff, waving the man back to his papers.

For days after that, Sympson fidgeted, hovering by every window, listening for the crunch of chariot wheels on the drive, waiting for the formal follow-up letter, the lawyers, the whole grand worldly ritual of arranging settlements for the marriage. At last, the letter came—he pulled it straight out of the post bag, recognising the baronet’s handwriting and crest at a glance. Shirley took it straight to her room, read it, locked herself away for nearly the whole day writing her reply, and when she came back down, simply said it was done. Sympson did not dare press her, not with that sharp, unreadable look on her face, as inscrutable to him as the writing on Belshazzar’s wall. He half wanted to drag Louis Moore in to decode it for him, but his pride would not let him, and Louis himself looked like a scholar staring at a page of blank grammar, no clue what was going on behind Shirley’s bright, steady eyes.

Then one afternoon, Sympson came back early from a visit to De Walden Hall, his face flushed, his boots stamping hard across the oak parlour floor, and called Shirley aside. He wanted a strictly private interview, he said, and Shirley rose without a single word of protest, laying down her sewing and thimble on the windowsill, following him to the drawing room with the same calm resignation someone might show following the dentist to pull a long aching tooth they’d been dreading for weeks.

They sat opposite each other in armchairs, a few yards apart, the pretty white and green carpet stretching between them. Sympson said he’d been to De Walden, and learned the most shocking news: Nunnely Priory was shut up. The whole family, Lady Nunnely, the daughters, Sir Philip himself, had left for their estate in another shire, without a word of warning.
Shirley only said “Indeed.”
Sympson fumed, demanded to know if this was news to her, and she said yes, it was. He lost his temper then, all pomp falling away into fidgety, irritable wordiness, ranting that he would not be trifled with, that he insisted on a full explanation.
“Calm down, uncle,” Shirley said, steady as a rock. “Ask what you want to know. I’ll give you truthful answers.”
He demanded to know if Sir Philip had made her an offer.
“He has,” she said, unflinching.
Sympson gaped, stammered out that he’d seen it all, the proposal in the picture gallery recess by the cabinet, that he knew she’d got the letter after. He demanded to see the letter, and Shirley told him flatly he could not. He sputtered that he was her guardian, and Shirley pointed out she was no longer a ward—she had no guardian.
Then he shouted the question he’d been dying to ask: had she accepted him?
“I refused him,” Shirley said, plain as day.
Sympson bounced right out of his chair, trotting back and forth across the rug, nearly apoplectic. “Refused him! Shirley Keeldar, you refused Sir Philip Nunnely, one of the most eligible baronets in the shire?” He raved that she was ungrateful, that her mind was poisoned by French novels and wild French ideas, that this would end in infamy. Shirley cut through his rant, calm still, and laid out her reasons: Philip was too young, little more than a boy to her. His mother and sisters would never accept her, and she would not marry a man she did not respect enough to obey, a man who could not match her fire, who would let her lead him around by the nose. She wanted a master, she told him, a man of thirty, with the wisdom of forty, whose approval was a prize, whose displeasure could sting, someone she could both love and fear. Not a soft poetic boy who wrote sonnets.

Sympson jeered, asked her if she’d pictured that husband from real life, and Shirley’s cheeks flushed bright rose. She teased him that she’d loved Socrates, Leonidas, Washington, all sorts of heroes, before she dragged the joke out long enough to exclaim that her present idol was none other than the great Arthur Wellesley, Lord Wellington. Sympson was so furious he bounced out of the room then bounced right back again, slamming the door, and then he dropped the ugly rumour he’d heard all over the neighbourhood: everyone was whispering that she was carrying on an improper entanglement with her bankrupt foreign tenant, Louis Moore’s brother Robert, the mill-owner.

Shirley’s eyes flashed bright, her temper snapping at last. She defended the Moores fiercely, saying they were all honour and courage, far worthier than any petty baronet with a handful of verses to his name. She told Sympson plainly she was done with his meddling, his petty small-town prejudices, his narrow rules that she would never bow to. “Your god is not my god,” she told him, bright and unyielding. “I walk by a different creed, a different light, a different hope than yours.”
Sympson gasped, scandalised to his very bones.
“An infidel! You’re an infidel!” he shrieked.
Shirley smiled, sharp and bright. “An infidel to your religion. An atheist to your god.”
The word hung in the air, ringing through the drawing room, as Mr. Sympson stared at her, aghast, and realised for the first time that he had not the slightest power over his fierce, brilliant, headstrong niece.
