For Daniel Deronda, returning from the Abbey, the discovery waiting at Brompton Lodgings was a bitter shock. His erstwhile sanctuary had been invaded by the "undesirable father" who now occupied the premises. Mirah, driven by necessity, had confessed to her father, Lapidoth, though she carefully curated the narrative. She omitted the depth of Deronda's aid, specifically the terrifying rescue from drowning, and minimized her shelter with the Meyricks. Her aim was to lead Lapidoth to believe that Deronda had merely met her through the Meyricks' circle. She could not endure the scrutiny of her father's gaze upon the true nature of her bond with Deronda. Consequently, Lapidoth, for his own reasons, refrained from pressing questions regarding her flight or arrival, yet his interest peaked at the revelation that his child possessed a benefactor seemingly elevated in the world.

It was Ezra who broke the news to Deronda of this new intruder. "I have grown calmer in witnessing him," Ezra remarked, his voice heavy with resignation. "I cling to the hope that Mirah's tenderness, coupled with the quiet rhythm of a peaceful life, may eventually draw him away from temptation. I have strictly forbidden her to entrust him with any funds, convincing her that his acceptance of money would seal his own ruin."

Deronda first encountered Lapidoth three days after his arrival. The tailor's measurements for new suits had not yet yielded garments, and Desperate to avoid presenting himself in his shabby attire, Deronda hoped to make a favorable impression. As he approached the door, he peered through the window. He was startled by the visitor's apparent youth, a detail Mirah had conveniently glossed over. To Deronda, this youthful visage seemed incongruous with the somber studies shared with the brooding Ezra. Yet, Lapidoth's imagination quickly filled the gap; he surmised that Deronda's primary motivation must be a romantic attachment to Mirah. To Lapidoth, this was a blessing. A suitor bound by love offered his daughter a shield against harsh judgment, whereas a tie solely to Ezra invited severity. Lapidoth, buoyed by this hope, adopted an air of amiability, striving to domesticate himself within their sphere. He engaged with Mirah's music, submitted to her rules regarding smoking (which his hostess, Mrs. Adam, detested), and strolled the square with his German pipe, fueled by tobacco Mirah graciously supplied. He wisely held his tongue regarding Mirah's steadfast refusal to give him money, accepting it as a solemn duty owed to her brother, content to wait.

On the following visit, Lapidoth, freshly dressed and preening before his mirror, sat in the room with Ezra. Ezra, engaging in a grim penance, had resolved to tolerate his father's presence whenever imposed. Deronda felt a chill distance toward the man who had blighted the lives of his wife and children; the sight of Lapidoth provoked a visceral repulsion. Undeterred, Lapidoth requested permission to remain and listen to Ezra read from the old chest. He proved unexpectedly useful, assisting in deciphering a complex German manuscript. Seizing the moment, Lapidoth suggested transcribing the document and offering his services to copy papers written in Roman characters. Although Ezra's vision was failing, Lapidoth's eyes remained sharp. Deronda, interpreting this eagerness as a sign of moral grace, accepted the offer. However, Ezra, wary of accidents involving fire or loss, insisted that all work be done within his immediate sight, effectively treating his father like a convict on furlough.

This arrangement cast a heavy shadow over Ezra's existence. Confining his attention to appease Deronda was a constant torment, akin to wearing an ill-fitting garment. He sought relief constantly—stepping out to smoke, pacing the floor, collapsing into silence marked by a frantic, dumb pantomime of facial expressions and gestures. When Mirah was present, he fell back into his old habits, gossipping about past adventures and theatrical plots, deluding himself into believing he could summon the vitality of his younger days. This behavior was a mortal affliction to Ezra. Even when Mirah intervened to remove her father to the parlor, the burden persisted. What kind of heroism is forged by a single resolve? It is the daily, unflinching endurance of consequences that stain the dawn with irritation and shame. These burdens were carried by Ezra and Mirah, along with countless other children of unworthy fathers, hoping that their sacrifices would stretch into a solid future.

Yet, Lapidoth's presence erected an invisible barrier between Deronda and Mirah. Both feared the intrusive inferences of the father's mind, leading to a mutual misunderstanding of each other's growing reserve. Soon, however, light began to pierce Deronda's confusion.

Following his return from the Abbey, Deronda visited Hans Meyrick, believing it a debt of friendship for Hans to know the reasons behind his recent journey. Hans was absent, supposedly in the country, but a week later, Deronda returned to find him in the studio. Hans appeared disheveled, his hair wet from a bath, his face worn and wizened. Before Deronda could speak, Hans stood before his easel, palette in hand, an act that seemed performed on short notice.

"You don't look as if you'd been in the country, old fellow," Deronda noted, observing Hans's gaunt appearance. "Is it Cambridge?"

"No," Hans replied curtly, dropping his palette with a sudden, clumsy motion. "I've been to 'I-don't-know-where.' No Man's Land. A mortally unpleasant country it is."

"You don't mean you've been drinking, Hans?" Deronda asked, sitting anxiously opposite.

"Not so good. I smoked opium. I intended to try it sometime to measure its bliss, and finding myself out of other joys, I thought it prudent to seize the chance. But I pledge you my word, I shall never touch that bliss again. It disagrees with my constitution."

"What troubled you? You wrote in high spirits."

"Oh, nothing specific. The world began to look seedy, like a cabbage garden with all the heads cut down. A malady of genius, I'm sure. And frankly, I was tired of being virtuous without reward, especially in this stifling London heat."

"Nothing else? No real vexation?"

Hans shook his head. "I came to discuss my affairs, but I cannot do so gracefully if you intend to hide yours."

"I have no affairs in the world," Hans declared lightly, "save a quarrel with a trinket dealer. Besides, since this is the first time you've spoken of your matters, you are only beginning to pay a long debt."

Deronda sensed artificiality in Hans's demeanor but trusted that frankness would return. "You laughed at the mystery of my journey to Italy," Deronda began. "It touched the roots of my happiness. I knew nothing of my parents, and I went to Genoa to meet my mother. My father died when I was an infant; my mother was the daughter of an eminent Jew, and my father was her cousin. Long before I left, probability pointed to this origin. I was prepared for the result, and glad to find myself a Jew."

"You need not expect me to feign surprise, Deronda," Hans said, shifting his posture to examine the heel of his slipper. "My mother told me. She went to the house the morning after you visited—the brother and sister informed her. You may imagine we cannot rejoice as they do. But whatever brings you joy, I shall eventually join in, *when* exactly that hour arrives, I cannot predict." Hans lowered his voice, a tone usually reserved for bad moods, yet devoid of fuss.

"I understand you cannot share my feelings," Deronda conceded, "yet I could not let silence grow between us on a matter casting new light on my future. I have adopted some of Mordecai's ideas and intend to strive for them, as far as one man's effort permits. I suspect I shall soon travel to the East and be gone for years."

Hans said nothing, rising to mix paint on his palette, his back to Deronda. The air grew heavy, charged with an irritating stillness. Presently, Hans spoke softly, without turning. "Excuse the question, but does Mrs. Grandcourt know of all this?"

"No!" Deronda snapped, anger flaring. "I beg you, Hans, cease jesting on that subject. Your notions are wide of the truth; they are the exact reverse."

"I am no more inclined to jest than I shall be at my own funeral," Hans retorted. "But I am not entirely sure you realize what those notions are."

"Perhaps not," Deronda admitted. "But let me say, once for all, that in relation to Mrs. Grandcourt, I have never held, and never shall hold, the position of a lover. If you have interpreted anything as proof of affection, you are supremely mistaken."

Silence lingered, thick and uncomfortable. Then Hans resumed. "Perhaps I have been mistaken in another interpretation, as well."

"What is that?"

"That you had no desire to occupy the position of a lover toward another woman, who is neither wife nor widow."

Deronda felt a sting. "I cannot pretend not to understand you, Meyrick. It is painful that our wishes should clash. I hope you will tell me if you have grounds to believe you might succeed."
