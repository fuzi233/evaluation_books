"I have found that holy place of rest / Still changeless." —Mrs. Hemans.

When Mr. Thornton left the house that morning, he was nearly blinded by a baffled fury. He felt as dizzy as if Margaret, rather than appearing as a tender, graceful woman, had been a rough fishwife who had struck him across the face with clenched fists. He suffered actual physical pain—a splitting headache and a pulse that throbbed intermittently. The noise of the streets, the glaring sunlight, and the constant rumble of traffic were unbearable to him. He chided himself for being so weak, yet he could not immediately recall the specific cause of his anguish, nor judge if the trigger warranted the severity of his reaction.

It would have been a relief to sit on a doorstep and weep openly, perhaps crying alongside a small child who was raging against some perceived injustice. "I hate Margaret," he told himself, but as soon as the words formed, a wild, sharp surge of love tore through his dull, thunderous anger like lightning. His greatest solace was in embracing his torment, clinging to the certainty he had voiced to her: that even if she despised him, scorned him, or treated him with proud indifference, nothing would change within him. She could not alter his nature. He loved her, and he would love her; he would defy her and this miserable physical pain. He paused for a moment to cement this resolve.

An omnibus passed, heading toward the countryside. The conductor assumed he wanted a seat and pulled up near the curb. Apologizing seemed too much trouble, so he boarded and was carried away—past endless rows of houses, then past detached villas with neat gardens, until they reached true country hedgerows and eventually a small rural town. Everyone alighted, and Mr. Thornton followed. Walking briskly into the open fields, he sought the sharp motion to soothe his agitated mind.

Now, the details of the morning flooded back. He remembered the pathetic figure he must have cut, the absurdity of doing precisely what he had repeatedly convinced himself was the most foolish act imaginable, only to meet the exact consequences he had always predicted. Had he been bewitched by those beautiful eyes, that soft, half-open, sighing mouth pressed against his shoulder just yesterday? He could not shake the memory of her presence, of her arms around him—even if never again. He only caught fleeting glimpses of her; he did not fully comprehend her. One moment she was brave, the next timid; now tender, then haughtily regal. He replayed every encounter in an attempt to forget her, seeing her in every dress and mood, unable to decide which version was best. This morning, she had looked magnificent, her eyes flashing with the notion that sharing his danger yesterday proved her affection for him!

If Mr. Thornton considered himself a fool in the morning, he certainly did not become wiser by afternoon. The sixpence fare of the omnibus bought him only a sharper conviction: that no one in the world was like Margaret; that she did not love him and never would; yet that neither she nor the entire universe should prevent him from loving her.

He returned to the market square and rode back to Milton. Late in the afternoon, he was set down near his warehouse. The familiar surroundings triggered his habitual routines. He knew his workload had increased due to the previous day's commotion. He needed to meet his fellow magistrates, finalize arrangements for the safety of his newly imported Irish workers, and ensure they remained isolated from the discontented locals of Milton. Finally, he had to return home and face his mother.

Mrs. Thornton had spent the entire day in the dining room, waiting for news of her son's acceptance by Miss Hale. She had braced herself against every sudden noise, snatching up half-finished embroidery to stitch diligently despite her dimmed spectacles and trembling hands. Time after time, the door opened for trivial errands, causing her rigid face to soften from its frozen expression into a look of rare despondency. She wrested herself away from contemplating the dreary changes her son's marriage would bring, forcing her thoughts back into the safe grooves of household management.

She counted out stacks of linen, distinguishing between items marked *G.H.T.* (George and Hannah Thornton) and those purchased by her son with his own money. Some of the older Dutch damasks marked with her son's initials were exquisite treasures, unmatched today. Standing before them, Mrs. Thornton recalled her own pride in them during her youth. She knitted her brow, pinched her lips tight, and carefully unpicked the initial 'G' to replace it with her son's letter. She searched for the red thread needed for the new markings but found it exhausted; she lacked the heart to order more just yet.

Staring into the empty space, a series of visions played before her, all centered on her son—her pride, her property. He still had not arrived. Undoubtedly, he was with Miss Hale. The new love was already displacing her position as the first in his heart. A terrible, physical pang of jealous despair shot through her, forcing her to sit down. Moments later, she rose again, as upright as ever, wearing a grim smile for the first time that day, ready to greet the triumphant visitor who would never know the deep regret his mother felt regarding his union.

In all this, Mrs. Thornton gave little thought to the future daughter-in-law as an individual. She was merely "John's wife." Taking Mrs. Thornton's place as mistress of the house was just one of the glorious rewards accompanying the supreme honor. Household abundance, fine linens, honor, love, obedience, and loyal friends would flow naturally like jewels onto a king's robe, valued only for their collective splendor. Being chosen by John elevated a kitchen maid above the rest of the world. As for Miss Hale, she wasn't entirely bad. If she were a native of Milton, Mrs. Thornton would have liked her instantly. She possessed pungency, taste, spirit, and flavor. True, she was prejudiced and ignorant, but that was expected of someone raised in the South.

A strange, mortifying comparison between Fanny and Miss Hale churned in Mrs. Thornton's mind. For once, she spoke harshly to her daughter, abusing her roundly, then performed a penance by picking up Henry's *Commentaries* to force her attention away from the linen.

Then, he appeared! She heard his footsteps even as she believed she was finishing a sentence. While her eye scanned the page and her memory could mechanically repeat the words, her heightened senses interpreted every sound: now he was at the hat stand, now approaching the room door. Why did he hesitate? Did he want to tell her the truth? Keeping her head bowed over the book, she waited.

He approached the table and stood still, waiting for her to finish the paragraph that seemed to absorb her completely. With an effort, she looked up.

"Well, John?"

He understood the weight of those few words. Though bitterness surged within him, longing to answer with a jest, he held his tongue; his mother deserved better. He moved behind her so she could not see his expression, bent forward to lift her gray, stony face, and kissed it, murmuring, "No one loves me, no one cares for me, but you, mother."

Turning away, he leaned his head against the mantelpiece, tears pricking his manly eyes. She stood up, then tottered. For the first time in her life, the strong woman faltered. Placing her hands on his shoulders, she looked deeply into his face, compelling him to look at her.

"Mother's love is given by God, John. It holds fast forever and ever. A girl's love is like a puff of smoke—it changes with every wind. And she would not have you, my own lad, would she?" She gritted her teeth, showing them like a dog along the full length of her mouth.

He shook his head. "I am not fit for her, mother. I knew I was not."

She ground out words between her closed teeth. He could not hear them, but the look in her eyes conveyed a curse—not necessarily coarse in wording, but cruel in intent as anything ever spoken. Yet, her heart leaped lightly to know he was hers again.

"Mother!" he said hurriedly. "I cannot hear a word against her. Spare me—I am very weak in my sore heart. I love her yet; I love her more than ever."

"And I hate her," Mrs. Thornton replied in a low, fierce voice. "I tried not to hate her when she stood between you and me. I told myself she would make you happy, and I would give my heart's blood to ensure that. But now, I hate her for your misery's sake. Yes, John, there is no use hiding your aching heart from me. I am the mother who bore you, and your sorrow is my agony. If you do not hate her, I do."

"Then, mother, you make me love her more. She is unjustly treated by you, and I must restore the balance. But why discuss love or hatred? She does not care for me, and that is enough, too much. Let us never mention the subject again. That is the only thing you can do for me. Let us never name her."

"With all my heart. I only wish that she, and everything connected to her, were swept back to where they came from."

He stood still, gazing into the fire for a minute or two longer. Dry, dim eyes filled with unexpected tears as she watched him, yet she remained as grim and quiet as usual
