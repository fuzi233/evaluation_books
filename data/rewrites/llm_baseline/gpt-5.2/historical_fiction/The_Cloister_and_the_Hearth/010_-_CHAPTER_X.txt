The banns had to be called thrice, as folk do still, yet here the old rule bent easily in a little country church. The curate at Sevenbergen was new in his cure, and youth pleading with eyes is a language no priest is proof against. So on Monday, at matins and again at vespers, he cried Gerard and Margaret—swift, hushed, and with no gaping Tergou faces to carry the news home like chaff on the wind. They went out into the sharp, clean air with hearts that beat as if each rib were a drum.

At dawn next day they stole in again, warm from walking fast, smelling of dew and lane-dust, their hands cold but their hopes hot. The church had that homely scent of damp stone, old wood, and yesterday’s incense clinging like smoke in a cookhouse beam. The curate began—

—and a stranger rose.

“ I forbid the banns.”

The word fell like a cleaver on a board. He spoke of age, of parents, of consent. Margaret’s breath stopped; Gerard went white as flour.

Outside, by the church-door where lichens mottled the stone, they clung together and argued in broken whispers, as if speech itself might be overheard and twisted into evidence. They had scarce decided anything when the very man who had done the mischief came sidling up with the air of a tradesman offering wares.

He was sorry—so sorry. In truth, he loved to further young happiness; only his living lay in forbidding banns. Yet there was a remedy, simple as salting a pot: “The young people give me a crown, and I undo my work handsomely. I tell the cure I was misinformed, and all goes smoothly.”

“A crown?” Gerard burst out. “I will give you a golden angel.”

The fellow’s eyes brightened as if a lamp had been lit behind them. He took Gerard by the sleeve, went in, and with oily humility confessed his “ridiculous mistake,” swearing that one look at the parties had corrected him. The curate, relieved to have the knot cut, promised to marry them next day at ten.

Thus the knave walked off with an angel in his purse and mischief in his throat. Like many a sharp rogue, he was a fool in drink. He went straight to a hostelry at Tergou by the green where men practised archery and played at their rude sports, and there he drank his angel away as if it were ale, bragging between gulps of how he had barred and unbarred a marriage with the same easy hand.

Sybrandt was there—Sybrandt who haunted such places as flies haunt a larder. He drank in every word, not missing a syllable, for inheritance had made his ears keen. He ran home, found his father gone to Rotterdam to buy cloth, caught Cornelis’s eye, drew him out, and spilled the tale.

In most great households some sheep go black. These two were Gerard’s. Idleness had soured them; waiting on a death they ought to fear as sin soured them more. Their mother’s thrift, which in her and her husband was joined with labour and meant provision for children, had turned in these two into a dry, hungry avarice. It made them cruel in fancy first, then in counsel, and counsel is often the first kneading of a crime.

They agreed to keep their mother in ignorance—her heart blew hot and cold—then went to the burgomaster. They had sense enough to know Ghysbrecht Van Swieten disliked this match, though they could not tell why.

The burgomaster saw through them as one sees through thin beer. Yet he did not let them see through him. He put on magistrate’s coldness as a cook puts a lid on a pot, hiding the simmer beneath.

“Since the father of the family is not here,” said he, “his duty falleth on me, who am the father of the town. I know your father’s mind; leave all to me; and, above all, tell not a woman a word of this—least of all the women that are in your own house: for chattering tongues mar wisest counsels.”

He dismissed them with a touch of contempt; he was ashamed of the company he must use.

They went home, and there sat Gerard, low on a stool at his mother’s knee. She was smoothing his hair with the same hand that had kneaded bread for him when he was little; her voice had the softened sound of milk poured into a pot.

She promised to stand by him with his father, to thwart him no more.

Her change was born, not of argument, but of that quick remorse which sometimes follows a woman’s quick wrath. In a moment of sharp irritation she had cut Margaret’s picture to pieces. She had watched Gerard gather the fragments with hands that trembled as if handling sacred wafer; she had seen him turn as pale as death and sit motionless, staring till tears came and blinded him. Terror at what she had done struck her; then grief; then a wordless resolve—she would never confess, but she would make it up. So now her tenderness flowed back to her son like warm broth into a chilled belly.

Gerard, knowing nothing of the cause, felt shame for his own dark suspicion. In his inexperience of women’s winding ways, her kindness seemed proof. He kissed her again and again, and went to bed with a heart lifted, thinking his mother was his mother at the very hinge of his fate.

At ten next morning, Gerard and Margaret stood in Sevenbergen church. He was bright with joy; she was all blush and trembling courage. Peter was there, and Martin Wittenhaagen—no more. Secrecy was their only shield.

Margaret had refused Italy. She could not leave her father—so learned, so helpless in the world’s plain dealings, as a scholar may be helpless before a boiling pot. It was settled they should slip into Flanders for a few weeks till the storm in Tergou spent itself.

The curate came quickly. It seemed an age. He stood at the altar and called them forward. They went hand in hand, the happiest two in Holland. The book opened.

But ere one word of the rite could pass his lips, a harsh voice struck the air.

“Forbear!”

Constables from Tergou came up the aisle and laid hands on Gerard in the name of the law. Martin’s long knife flashed out, quick as a cook’s blade at a table.

“Forbear, man!” cried the priest. “What! draw your weapon in a church? And ye who interrupt this holy sacrament—what means this impiety?”

“There is no impiety, father,” said the burgomaster’s servant, respectful as a man who carries authority. “This young man would marry against his father’s will, and his father hath prayed our burgomaster to deal with him according to the law. Let him deny it if he can.”

“Is this so, young man?”

Gerard hung his head. He could not lie in God’s house.

“We take him to Rotterdam to abide the sentence of the Duke.”

Margaret’s cry burst out as if torn from her throat. The two, who a heartbeat ago had been poised on heaven’s edge, fell to sobbing in each other’s arms so bitterly that even the men who seized him drew back, ashamed. One of them, good-natured, came close under colour of parting them and whispered to Margaret, “Rotterdam? It is a lie. We but take him to our Stadthouse.”

They dragged Gerard out, set him on horseback, and took the road toward Rotterdam—then halted, turned, made sly detours as if stirring a pot to hide what lies at the bottom, and by evening carried him to Tergou. Outside the town a rude covered cart waited, canvas drawn. They thrust him in, and about five o’clock smuggled him into the Stadthouse prison.

Up flights of stairs they drove him, then into a small room with only a slit of window crossed by a single vertical iron bar. The place smelled of old damp and human fear. The only furniture was a huge oak chest, black with age and rubbed smooth at the edges like a kneading-trough that has served generations.

Imprisonment then was a road toward death. Cold, solitude, hunger, torture—often poison. Gerard felt himself in an enemy’s hands.

“Oh, the look that man gave me on the road,” he thought. “There is more here than my father’s wrath. I doubt I shall see no more the light of day.”

He knelt and commended his soul to God.

Then he sprang up and flung himself at the iron bar, clutched it, braced his knees against the wall, and peered out. For a bare minute he saw what only a captive values beyond measure: Martin Wittenhaagen’s back.

Martin sat below by the brook near the Stadthouse, fishing—quiet as if nothing in the world mattered but the bite of a fish, though his stillness was watchfulness. Gerard whistled. Martin’s head snapped round; he saw Gerard’s face in the slit, made a signal, gathered his line and bow, and went swiftly away.

Gerard held on a moment longer, hungry for that comfort of a friendly sight. Falling back heavily, he wrenched the rusty bar loose from the stonework—it gave with a groan like a nail in rotten plank—just as Ghysbrecht Van Swieten slipped in behind him.

The burgomaster’s eye flicked to the iron, then to the window. He said nothing. The slit was a hundred feet above the ground; if Gerard chose to jump, let him.

He set down a brown loaf and a pitcher of water on the chest in solemn silence. The loaf’s smell rose—coarse rye, honest and sour—yet Gerard’s stomach, that had been gnawing him, turned wary. Poison was a cook’s fear too: a little bitterness hidden in sweetness, a death folded into food.

His first impulse was to brain Ghysbrecht with the iron and rush out; but the burgomaster coughed softly, and three stout fellows showed at the door, armed.

“My orders are to keep you thus,” said Ghysbrecht, “until you shall bind yourself by an oath to leave Margaret Brandt, and return to the Church, to which you have belonged from your cradle.”

“Death sooner.”

“With all my heart.”

The burgomaster withdrew.

Martin sped back to Sevenbergen. There he found Margaret pale, shaken, yet like a woman whose hands, once set to kneading, will not stop till the bread is made. She was finishing a letter to the Countess Charolois, appealing against Ghysbrecht’s violence and treachery.

“Courage!” Martin cried. “I have found him. He is in the haunted tower, right at the top of it. Ay, I know the place: many a poor fellow hath gone up there straight, and come down feet foremost.”

He told how he had looked up and seen Gerard’s face at a window like a slit in a wall.

“Oh, Martin—how did he look?”

“He looked like Gerard Eliassoen.”

“But was he pale?”

“A little.”

“Looked he anxious? Like one doomed?”

“Nay, nay; as bright as a pewter pot.”

“You mock me. Stay—then that must have been at sight of you. He counts on us. Oh, what shall we do? Martin, good friend, take this at once to Rotterdam.”

Martin held out his hand for the letter. Peter had sat silent, strangely attentive, his scholar’s brow folded as if he were reading a page none else could see.

“Put not your trust in princes,” said he.

“Alas!” Margaret answered. “What else have we to trust in?”

“Knowledge.”

“Your learning will not serve us here,” she cried, the words sharp with despair.

“How know you that?” Peter returned, kindling. “Wit hath been too strong for iron bars ere to-day.”

“Ay,” said Martin, “but nature is stronger than wit, and she is against us. Think of the height! No ladder in Holland might reach him.”

“I need no ladder,” said Peter. “What I need is a gold crown.”

“I have money,” said Margaret quickly. “I have nine angels. Gerard gave them me to keep; but what do they avail? The burgomaster will not be bribed to let Gerard free.”

“What do they avail?” Peter leaned forward, the old man’s eyes bright. “Give me but one crown, and the young man shall sup with us this night.”

His certainty startled them. For an instant hope rose in Margaret—then she caught Martin’s look: benevolent contempt, as for a learned man speaking of fieldwork he has never done.

“It passes the powers of man’s invention,” she sighed.

“Invention!” cried Peter. “A fig for invention. What need we invention at this time of day? Everything hath been said that is to be said, and done that ever will be done. I shall tell you how a Florentine knight was shut up in a tower higher than Gerard’s; yet did his faithful squire stand at the tower foot and get him out, with no other engine than that in your hand, Martin, and certain kickshaws I shall buy for a crown.”

Martin turned his bow over in his hands, as if interrogating the wood and horn. His doubt stayed. Then Peter told his tale—how the squire at Brescia sent up fine thread, drew up stronger cord by it, then a rope, simple as drawing a thick line through a needle’s eye by first passing silk. Like the best craft—like good cookery, too—the science was plain when once shown. Their wonder changed: how had they called impossible what was not even difficult?

The letter never went to Rotterdam. They trusted to Peter’s learning and their own hands.

It was nine o’clock, clear moon, the kind of night where every sound carries—the brook’s whisper, a late footstep, the dry clack of a shutter in wind. Gerard senior was still away; the rest of the household had long been abed.

In the Eliassoen house, a white figure stood by the dwarf’s bed, moonlight glancing off it. With an unearthly noise between yell and snarl, Giles rolled off his bed and under it in one unbroken movement, as quick and smooth as a cat.

A soft voice followed him.

“Why, Giles, are you afeard of me?”

His head rose cautiously. It was only his sister Kate. She put her finger to her lips.

“Hush! lest the wicked Cornelis or the wicked Sybrandt hear us.”

Giles’s hooked hands seized the bed; with one gymnastic motion he was back atop it.

Kate whispered what she had learned. All day Gerard had not come home; fear drove her to listen at Cornelis and Sybrandt’s door. She had heard Gerard’s name and a discovery that chilled her: Gerard was in prison, in the haunted tower of the Stadthouse—there by their father’s authority, they said. Yet their father was in Rotterdam. Treachery lay in it, plain as mould in a loaf.

She begged Giles to go with her to the tower foot, to give poor Gerard a word of comfort and tell him their father was absent and would surely release him when he returned.

“Dear Giles, I would go alone, but I am afeard of the spirits men say do haunt the tower; but with you I shall not be afeard.”

“Nor I with you,” said Giles stoutly. “I don’t believe there are any spirits in Tergou. I never saw one. This last was the likest one ever I saw; and it was but you, Kate, after all.”

Within half an hour they slipped out, Kate making Giles carry a lantern though the night was bright.

“The lantern gives me more courage against evil spirits,” she whispered.

Up in the tower, the first day of solitude broke Gerard as hunger breaks the stubborn. As the sun fell, his heart sank with it. He was faint; he had feared the loaf and water and had scarcely tasted them. The cell grew colder; the damp seemed to crawl into his bones. He sat on the chest, head bowed, arms hanging, his whole body a picture of despondency.

Then something struck the wall beyond him with a sharp tap and rattled to the floor at his feet.

An arrow.

He saw the white feather and shrank, a chill sliding over him—assassination, then, from outside. He crouched and waited for the next. None came.

He crept forward and took up the arrow. It had no head. Hope leapt in him, sudden and fierce. He felt along it with trembling fingers and found something soft bound to it. One of his odd habits—keeping tinder close—served him now like a blessing. He struck a light.

By the little flame he saw a skein of silk tied to the shaft, and words written on the wood.

Well beloved, make fast the silk to thy knife and lower to us: but hold thine end fast: then count an hundred and draw up.

His eyes devoured the words. He seized the oak chest and dragged it to the window with strength that would not have come to him a moment before. He climbed atop, peered down, and saw figures at the tower foot—so dim they seemed one great shape.

He waved his bonnet. Then he unwound the silk carefully and tied it to his knife, lowered the knife till the thread slackened, held fast, and counted a hundred with lips moving soundlessly.

He drew up. The silk came heavier. At a knot, stout whipcord replaced it. He hauled again, and another knot: now a thicker cord. He began to pull, and the weight grew heavy.

Suddenly he understood. He pulled with all his might until sweat ran down him, and below in the moonlight a great shape rose out of the tower’s shadow like a snake: rope, rope, rope, coming up as bread dough rises under a cloth. With a last heave the rope’s end reached his hand.

He dragged it into the cell, passed it through both handles of the chest, and knotted it fast. Then he sat a moment, chest heaving, to gather breath and courage.

He must be sure the chest would bear him. He jumped on it. Once—twice—at the third jump the side burst open and a host of parchments scuttled out like pale rats. After the first fear, he saw it had opened, not broken—some secret spring loosed. Yet it shook his trust in the chest’s strength. He made it safer: he took the iron bar he had torn from the window and lashed it across the rope and the window with the small cord, to help bear strain.

Then he climbed up again, put a foot through the slit, and sat half in, half out, one hand on the rope within. The night was very still; he could hear his own heart as if it were a drum in a hollow room. Free air touched his face, and that touch gave him courage to risk what all must lose one day, but not yet—for liberty.

The worst was the first move onto the rope outside. He set himself like a swimmer: body to the waist still in the cell, legs out. Holding the inside rope with both hands, he felt with his feet for the rope outside, pinched it between the soles, made it fast, murmured a short prayer, then wriggled out.

For one dreadful moment he hung outside from the iron bar by one hand while the other searched for the rope; it lay tight against the wall, hard to grasp. At last his fingers closed on it. He let go the bar, caught the rope with both hands—but his body dropped a yard.

A stifled cry rose from below.

He clenched his teeth, nipped the rope with his feet, and began to descend, hand over hand. Rough stones slid past his cheek. One was green with moss. He glanced up—his window already far—and once down: the figures below seemed terribly small. Dizziness swam in him. He fixed his eyes on the wall close by and went down, down, down, past a rusty slimy streak ten feet long. The rope burned his hands as if he were pulling hot pots from a fire.

He looked up again. The slit was distant now. He dared look down: thirty feet below, Margaret and Martin stood with arms outstretched, their eyes and teeth shining in the moon as they breathed hard.

“Take care, Gerard—oh, take care! Look not down,” Margaret whispered.

“Fear me not,” he cried, and came faster, joy lending speed.

In a minute his feet reached their hands. They seized him before he touched ground, and all three clung together, silent, trembling with relief.

“Hush,” breathed Margaret. “Away in silence, dear one.”

They stole along the wall’s shadow. They had not gone many yards when a stream of light shot from an angle and lay across their path like a barrier of fire, and whispers and footsteps sounded close.

“Back!” hissed Martin. “Keep in the shade.”

They hurried back, passed the dangling rope, and made for a little square projecting tower. They had barely rounded it when the light went trembling past them and flickered away.

“A lantern,” groaned Martin. “They are after us.”

“Give me my knife,” whispered Gerard. “I’ll never be taken alive.”

“No, no,” murmured Margaret. “Is there no way out where we are?”

“None,” Martin whispered. “None. But I carry six lives at my shoulder.” He strung his bow, set an arrow. “In war never wait to be struck. I will kill one or two ere they shall know whence their death comes.”

He motioned them still and glided to the corner, bow half drawn, ready to loose the moment a man offered his chest to the moon.

Gerard and Margaret held their breath, sick with the thought. They had never seen a man die. And Gerard, in that tight silence, felt a wild hope—half shameful—that the pursuer might be Ghysbrecht himself. A soldier would send an arrow through a burgher as through a boar.

But the future, though a footstep away, stays unknown.

Martin’s bow did not steady. It wavered—shook violently. The stout man staggered back, knees knocking, cheeks blanched. The arrow fell from his fingers. He clutched Gerard’s shoulder as if to prove he touched life.

“Let me feel flesh and blood,” he gasped. “The haunted tower! the haunted tower!”

His terror leaped to them like fire to dry straw. They could scarce speak for fear.

“Hush!” he panted. “It will hear you up the wall! It is going up the wall! Its head is on fire. Up the wall, as mortal creatures walk upon green sward. If you know a prayer, say it—for hell is loose to-night.”

“I have power to exorcise spirits,” Gerard whispered, trembling. “I will venture forth.”

“Go alone then,” said Martin hoarsely. “I have looked on’t once, and live.”
