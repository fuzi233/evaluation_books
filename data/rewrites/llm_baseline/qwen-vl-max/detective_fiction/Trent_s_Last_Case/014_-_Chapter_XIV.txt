“If you insist,” Trent said, “I suppose I’ll do as you ask. But I’d far rather write this letter when I’m alone. Still, if it must be done here—bring me paper whiter than starlight, or the hand of a singing angel. I mean plain note-paper, nothing stamped with your address. Don’t think I’m not making a sacrifice. Honestly, I’ve never felt less inclined to write in my life.”

She smiled at him, amused and patient. “What shall I say?” he asked, pen poised above the blank sheet. “Shall I compare him to a summer’s day? What *shall* I say?”

“Say whatever comes to mind,” she suggested gently.

He shook his head. “What I really want to say—what I’ve wanted to shout to every person I’ve met for the past twenty-four hours—is ‘Mabel and I are engaged, and all is well.’ But that wouldn’t make much sense as the opening line of a letter meant to sound formal, even ominous. I’ve managed ‘Dear Mr. Marlowe’ so far. What now?”

“I’m sending you a manuscript,” she prompted, “one I thought you might find interesting.”

“Do you realize,” he said, “that in that sentence only two words have more than one syllable? This letter is supposed to impress—not comfort. We need long words.”

“I don’t see why,” she replied. “It’s customary, I know, but why? I’ve received countless letters from lawyers and businessmen, always beginning with ‘with reference to our communication’ or something equally ponderous. They go on like that for pages. Yet when I meet them in person, they speak plainly enough. It seems absurd.”

“It’s not absurd to them,” Trent said, putting down his pen as if relieved, then rising. “Let me explain. Our people, generally speaking, aren’t fond of using their minds. We get by with a limited, simple vocabulary. Long words are unusual—and like anything unusual, they’re either hilarious or deeply solemn. Take ‘intelligent anticipation,’ for example. If someone used that phrase in France or Germany, no one would blink. But here? It’s become a national joke. Everyone laughs when they hear it in speeches or newspapers. Why? Because it’s made of two long words. The idea behind it is as dull as cold mutton. Then there’s ‘terminological inexactitude’—how we all roared at that! And the whole humor lies in the length of the words. Same thing when we want to sound serious—we reach for big words. A solicitor who starts a sentence with ‘pursuant to the instructions communicated to our representative’ feels he’s earning his fee. Don’t laugh—it’s true. Continental Europeans don’t feel that way. They care about ideas, and so even shopkeepers and peasants use a vocabulary that would baffle most Britons. I once dined with a Paris cabman friend. We were at a grim little restaurant near the central post office—full of drivers and porters. Conversation was lively, and I noticed how effortlessly words like ‘functionary,’ ‘unforgettable,’ ‘exterminate,’ and ‘independence’ flew across the table. These weren’t intellectuals—just ordinary, cheerful, red-faced men. Mind you,” he added quickly as Mrs. Manderson stepped forward and picked up his pen, “I mention this only to illustrate my point. I’m not suggesting cabmen should be philosophers. I agree with Keats—happy is England, sweet her artless cabmen, enough their simple loveliness for me. But when we talk about the people who shape the country’s industrial mind… well, do you know—”

“Oh no, no, no!” Mrs. Manderson interrupted, stepping away from the desk. “I don’t know anything right now, except that your talking must stop if we’re ever going to finish this letter to Mr. Marlowe. You’re not getting out of it. Come!”

She placed the pen firmly back in his hand. Trent looked at it with distaste. “Don’t discourage me from talking,” he said, sounding defeated. “Believe me, men who don’t talk are worse company than those who do. Beware of silent natures. I confess—I’m avoiding writing this. It feels almost indecent. It’s mixing two moods: writing a letter of grave intent while sitting in the same room with you.”

She guided him back to his chair before the escritoire and gently pushed him down. “Just try,” she said. “I want to see what you write—and I want it sent immediately. I could be content to let things rest, but you say you must uncover the truth. And if you must, I want it settled soon. Do it now—you know you can—and I’ll send it off the moment it’s ready. Don’t you ever feel that urge—the desperate need to seal the envelope, drop it in the post, and be free of it forever? So you can’t recall it, so you can stop worrying?”

“I’ll do as you wish,” he said, turning to the paper and dating it from his hotel.

Mrs. Manderson watched him, her eyes soft with affection. She reached out as if to smooth his unruly hair, but stopped herself. Instead, she walked silently to the piano and began to play softly.

Ten minutes passed before Trent spoke. “If he refuses to reply—says he won’t speak?”

She glanced over her shoulder. “Of course he won’t take that stance. He’ll answer—to keep you from exposing him.”

“But I won’t expose him anyway,” Trent said. “You wouldn’t allow it—you said so. Besides, I won’t, even if you did. The case is too uncertain now.”

“But poor Mr. Marlowe doesn’t know that,” she laughed.

Trent sighed. “What strange codes of honor we have!” he murmured. “There are things I’d do without hesitation—like giving a man who insulted me a black eye, or swearing violently after stubbing my toe in the dark—that would shame you if you did them. And now you’re calmly asking me to bluff Marlowe with a threat I don’t intend—a tactic even the devil himself wouldn’t use in the depths of guilt. No, I won’t do it.”

He returned to writing. The lady, smiling indulgently, continued her quiet music.

A few minutes later, Trent paused. “At last—I’m finished. Want to read it?”

She hurried across the dimly lit room and turned on the reading lamp beside the desk. Leaning over his shoulder, she read:

_You will perhaps remember that we met, under unfortunate circumstances, in June of last year at Marlstone._

_On that occasion it was my duty, as a journalist, to investigate independently the circumstances surrounding the death of Sigsbee Manderson. I conducted my inquiry and reached certain conclusions. You may learn what those conclusions were from the enclosed manuscript—originally written as a dispatch for my newspaper. For reasons I need not explain, I decided at the last moment not to publish them nor inform you directly. Only two people besides myself know the contents._

At this point, Mrs. Manderson lifted her eyes from the page. Her brows furrowed slightly. “Two persons?” she asked, voice low.

“My uncle is the other,” Trent said. “I told him everything last night. Have you any objection? I’ve always felt uneasy keeping it from him—he expected to hear all I discovered, and my silence seemed like secrecy. Now it’s time to clear it up. There’s no question of protecting you. I wanted him to know the full truth. He’s shrewd in his own way, and I’d like him with me when I meet Marlowe. I have a feeling two heads will be better than one on my side.”

She sighed. “Yes, of course—uncle should know. I hope no one else does.” She squeezed his hand. “I just want that horror buried—deep. I’m happy now, dear, but I’ll be happier still when you’ve satisfied your curious mind, uncovered everything, and sealed it beneath the earth.”

She resumed reading.

_Quite recently, however, new facts have come to light which have changed my decision. I do not intend to publish what I found, but I have resolved to approach you directly and request a private statement. If you have anything to say that might alter the understanding of events, I can see no reason why you should withhold it._

_I expect, therefore, to hear from you when and where I may call upon you—unless you prefer the meeting to take place at my hotel. In either case, I insist that Mr. Cupples, whom you will recall and who has read the enclosed document, be present.—Faithfully yours,_

_Philip Trent._

“What a stiff letter!” she remarked. “I’m sure you couldn’t have made it stiffer in your own rooms.”

Trent slipped the letter and manuscript into a long envelope. “Yes,” he said. “I think it’ll make him sit up sharply. And this mustn’t fail. Best to send a special messenger with strict orders to deliver it personally. If he’s not home, it shouldn’t be left.”

She nodded. “I’ll arrange it. Wait here a moment.”

When she returned, Trent was rummaging through the music cabinet. She sank onto the carpet beside him, her dark dress spreading around her like a shadow.

“Tell me something, Philip,” she said softly. “If it’s among the few things I know.”

“When you saw your uncle last night,” she continued, “did you tell him about—about us?”

“No,” he answered. “I remembered you said nothing should be told. It’s for you to decide—when, if ever, we reveal it to the world.”

“Then will you tell him?” she asked, looking down at her clasped hands.

“I wish you to tell him,” she said. “Perhaps you’ll understand why if you think about it... There! That’s settled.”

She raised her eyes to his. For a long moment, they sat in silence.

Eventually, Trent leaned back in the deep chair. “What a world,” he murmured. “Mabel, will you play something on the piano—something that expresses pure joy? Not feverish, not thorny, but real joy—joy that has chosen the universe? It won’t last long, so let’s take all we can from it.”

She rose and went to the piano. After a pause, she struck a few chords, then launched into the final movement of Beethoven’s Ninth Symphony—the theme that sounds like the gates of heaven opening.
