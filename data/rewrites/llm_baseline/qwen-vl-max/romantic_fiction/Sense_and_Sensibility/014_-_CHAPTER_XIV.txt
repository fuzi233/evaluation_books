Colonel Brandon’s abrupt departure from the park, and his quiet refusal to explain why, stirred Mrs. Jennings into a state of constant speculation that lasted for days. She was naturally inclined to wonder—indeed, she thrived on it—especially when it came to the comings and goings of her acquaintances. Now, with Colonel Brandon gone so suddenly, her mind raced through every possible explanation. He had looked so grave, so deeply troubled, that she was certain something sorrowful must have happened.

“Something very melancholy must be the matter,” she declared more than once. “I could see it in his face. Poor man! I’m afraid he’s in financial trouble. Delaford isn’t worth much more than two thousand a year, and his brother left everything in debt. I do think he must have been summoned about money matters. What else could it be? I wonder if it’s true. I’d give anything to know what’s really going on.”

Her thoughts turned next to Miss Williams. “Perhaps it’s about her,” she mused. “He did look rather guilty when I mentioned her name. Maybe she’s ill in town—nothing would surprise me more, since I’ve always thought she’s rather delicate. I’d wager my last farthing it’s about her.” Then, reconsidering, she added, “But then again, it might not be. He’s such a sensible man—he must have cleared up the estate by now. So perhaps it’s not money after all. Could it be his sister in Avignon? She’s been unwell, hasn’t she? And he left so quickly—it looks just like a family emergency. Well, I truly hope he finds peace soon. And maybe, just maybe, he’ll come back with a wife too!”

Mrs. Jennings continued to shift her theories as often as the wind changed direction, each one seeming equally plausible at the moment it arose.

Elinor, though genuinely concerned for Colonel Brandon’s well-being, found herself less absorbed by his sudden departure than Mrs. Jennings did. Her thoughts were drawn elsewhere—by the silence of her sister Marianne and Willoughby. Their refusal to speak of their growing intimacy puzzled her deeply. It was clear, from their every glance and gesture, that something significant had occurred between them. Yet they said nothing—not even to their own family. Why this secrecy? Elinor couldn’t understand it.

She knew marriage might not be immediate—Willoughby, though independent, wasn’t wealthy. Sir John had estimated his income at only six or seven hundred pounds a year, yet he lived extravagantly. He himself had often lamented his lack of funds. But still, Elinor found it hard to believe that mere financial caution warranted such careful concealment. Their behavior was so open, so tender, that the silence seemed almost contradictory. Was there no engagement at all? That question sometimes crept into her mind, and it kept her from pressing Marianne for answers.

Yet Willoughby’s affection was impossible to miss. To Marianne, he showed every sign of deep, devoted love—gentle, passionate, and utterly sincere. To the rest of the Dashwood family, he was warm, attentive, and full of kindness. The cottage at Barton had become, in his eyes, a home. He spent more time there than at Allenham, and whenever he rode out in the morning, he invariably returned to it, spending the day beside Marianne, his favorite dog resting quietly at her feet.

One evening, about a week after Colonel Brandon had left, Willoughby seemed especially moved by the place. When Mrs. Dashwood casually mentioned plans to improve the cottage in spring, he reacted with genuine distress.

“What?” he exclaimed. “Improve this dear cottage? Never. Not while I live. No stone shall be added to its walls, no inch increased in size—if I am to have any say in it.”

“Don’t worry,” said Elin ornament, smiling gently. “No such thing will happen. Mother doesn’t have the money for it.”

“I’m truly glad to hear it,” he replied warmly. “May she stay poor forever, if she can’t use her wealth better than this.”

“Thank you, Willoughby,” she said, amused. “But I promise you, I wouldn’t sacrifice even one sentiment of your attachment—for anyone I love—to make changes like these. Whatever little sum remains after our accounts are settled in spring, I’d rather leave it untouched than spend it in a way that pains you. But are you *really* so fond of this cottage that you see no flaws?”

“I am,” he said firmly. “To me, it is perfect. In fact, I believe it’s the only kind of house where true happiness can exist. If I were rich enough, I’d tear down Combe and rebuild it exactly as this cottage stands.”

“And with dark stairs and a smoky kitchen, I suppose?” Elinor teased.

“Yes!” he answered, almost laughing. “Every detail—the good and the bad—must remain unchanged. Only then might I find the same joy at Combe that I’ve known here.”

“I daresay,” Elinor replied, “that even with larger rooms and wider stairs, you’ll one day find your own home as perfect as this one seems to you now.”

“There are circumstances,” Willoughby said softly, “that could make it dearer still. But this cottage will always hold a claim on my heart no other place can share.”

Mrs. Dashwood watched Marianne closely—her eyes fixed on Willoughby with an expression that spoke volumes. She smiled, knowing how deeply her daughter understood him.

“How often,” Willoughby continued, “did I wish, this time last year, when I was at Allenham, that someone lived in Barton cottage! I never passed it without admiring its beauty—and grieving that it stood empty. How little I imagined then that the first news I’d hear from Mrs. Smith when I returned to the country would be that the cottage was taken. And yet, I felt such joy—such interest—in that news. It must have been because I already sensed the happiness I’d find here. Mustn’t it have been so, Marianne?” he whispered, turning to her.

Then, returning to his usual tone, he added, “And yet you’d change it, Mrs. Dashwood? You’d ruin its charm with false improvements? This parlour—where our friendship began, where so many happy hours have passed—would you turn it into a mere hallway? Everyone would rush through it, unaware that it holds more comfort and warmth than any grand room in England.”

Mrs. Dashwood assured him again that no changes would be made.

“You’re a kind woman,” he said, sincerely. “Your word puts my mind at ease. But go a step further—promise me that not only will the house stay the same, but that you and yours will remain unchanged too. That you’ll always treat me with the kindness that has made everything about you so precious to me.”

The promise was given without hesitation.

That evening, Willoughby’s presence radiated contentment and affection. As he prepared to leave, Mrs. Dashwood asked, “Shall we see you tomorrow for dinner?”

“I don’t ask you to come in the morning,” she added, “since we must walk to the park to visit Lady Middleton.”

“I’ll be there by four o’clock,” he promised, and with a smile, he took his leave.
