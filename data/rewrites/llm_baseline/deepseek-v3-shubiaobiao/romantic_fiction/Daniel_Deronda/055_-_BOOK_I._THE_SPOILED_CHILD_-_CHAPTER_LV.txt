*"Return to your wisdom,
Which holds that the more perfect the thing,
The more it feels joy, and thus sorrow."*

As Deronda crossed paths with Gwendolen and Grandcourt on the staircase, his thoughts were heavy with anticipation. Only moments before, he had received word of a second meeting with his mother. Within hours of their parting, the Princess Halm-Eberstein had vanished from the hotel, leaving him free to continue his journey to Mainz—to fulfill his duty to Joseph Kalonymos and claim the family chest that awaited him. Yet something unspoken, an intangible weight of emotion, held him back.

The farewell lingered in his mind, not as an end but as a beginning—a surge of memories now sharpened by reflection. In solitude, he allowed himself to grieve, not with the detachment of an observer but with a depth of feeling that surpassed even a woman’s sorrow. He mourned for a life so entwined with his own, yet forever distant. The world had shifted around him, reshaped by the certainty of bonds that altered his hopes and fears, leaving him with the eerie sensation of having strayed into the wrong company, only to wake at dawn and find his true kin far beyond reach.

His grandfather’s spirit seemed to stir within him—those same fervent passions and cherished ideals now awakening in his own blood. And through it all, the silent presences of Mordecai and Mirah remained, their hands clasped with his in unspoken understanding. Beneath his composed exterior, Deronda was a man of quick and tender sensibilities, shaped by the quiet strength of early solitude.

The insistent chime of a bell reminded him of the hour. He thought of consulting *Bradshaw*, of arranging his departure—yet he did not move. His desires pulled him in two directions: toward Mainz and the relics of his heritage, toward London and the dearest attachments of his life. But another, quieter longing anchored him to Genoa—a reluctance to leave without one last encounter, a dread of finality.

He did not consciously decide to stay. He did not tell himself, *I will remain because it is Friday, because I wish to attend the synagogue where they must have gone, because I may see Gwendolen again.* Instead, he lingered, motionless, his thoughts drifting between the ancient Spanish-Hebrew prayers and the troubled face of Gwendolen Harleth. The liturgy, carried through centuries of exile, moved him with its endurance, its whispered kinship to lost homelands. And Gwendolen—her distress clung to him, an unshaken plea not to leave without some word, some gesture of reassurance, no matter Grandcourt’s displeasure.

So he delayed. He ate without tasting, then rose abruptly, seeking the synagogue. On his way, he paused to ask after the Grandcourts. The porter gave their room number but added that they had gone boating. The news struck him with unexpected force, splitting his attention between the solemn worship before him and the image of Gwendolen upon the water.

The synagogue’s sparse congregation, their dark eyes alight with devotion, their voices weaving reverence with casual familiarity, made him ponder his grandfather’s legacy. Had he, too, been an anomaly among his people—a man of fervor and vision, like Mordecai? Were such men not rare in every age, the ones who carried the dreams that shaped the world, while others lived confined to the narrowness of instinct?

A restless sorrow mingled with his concern for Gwendolen, sharpening his impatience. He left the synagogue, drawn toward the quay—a place he had always favored, now magnetized by the chance of seeing the Grandcourts return. If he found them, he resolved to approach without hesitation, to offer his presence regardless of Grandcourt’s pride.

The sun had sunk behind clouds, leaving only a faint gold glow upon the restless waves. Deronda moved slowly along the shore, his gaze fixed on the commotion ahead. A boat rowed swiftly toward land, surrounded by a clamoring crowd. Telescopes were raised; voices rose in speculation—some claimed a drowned *milord*, others insisted it was *miladi*. A Frenchman, lacking a glass, jested darkly that the English husband had taken his wife out to drown her, "as was their custom." An English sailor scoffed at the notion, declaring the figure in the boat unmistakably female.

Deronda’s pulse quickened. Fear twisted through him as he watched the oars cut through the water, his mind racing through possibilities—had some horror befallen Gwendolen? Was this the shattered aftermath of some unseen catastrophe?

Then, no doubt remained. The boat reached the shore, and there—pale as death, drenched and trembling beneath layers of tarpaulin and coats—was Gwendolen. Her hair streamed with seawater, her eyes wide with stunned horror, as if she had awakened into a nightmare. She struggled to rise, her limbs unsteady, her gaze darting wildly until it locked onto Deronda.

At once, she strained toward him, arms outstretched, though the men supporting her held her back. Her voice, choked and desperate, broke through:

"Quiet yourself," Deronda commanded, firm but gentle. Then, to her rescuers: "I am a connection of her husband’s. Take her to the *Italia* at once—I will see to everything else."

He stayed long enough to hear the grim truth from the boatman: Grandcourt had drowned. The empty boat still drifted. They had heard a cry, seen Gwendolen leap in after him, and pulled her from the water just in time.

With that, Deronda hurried to the hotel, ensuring medical aid would be sent. Then, with swift precision, he dispatched telegrams—to Sir Hugo, urging his immediate arrival, and to Mr. Gascoigne, the nearest link to Gwendolen’s mother.

Words she had once spoken echoed in his mind, clear as prophecy: *If only my mother were here.* Now, he would make certain she came.
