From the Cape to Durban is four or five days’ steaming, if the weather holds and the sea does not make a mock of your schedules. Sometimes a vessel hangs about off East London for a whole day because the surf will not let the cargo boats creep out—there is always talk of a grand harbour to be built there, and always money vanishing into it like mealie-meal into a hungry pot, but the sea has not yet been argued into obedience. This time, though, we were lucky. The Bar was quiet, the tugs came fussing out at once, and behind them dragged a string of ugly, flat-bottomed boats that looked as if they had been hammered together in a hurry and had never forgiven anybody for it.

The unloading began in the usual fashion: not with care, but with confidence. Cases were pitched into the boats as though they were sacks of sand. Over they went, slap-bang—china, cloth, tools, whatever the labels begged you to respect, all treated alike. I saw a case of champagne—four dozen bottles—burst open as it landed. The wine frothed up in the grime of the boat’s bilge, white bubbles boiling like porridge that has been left too long on the fire. It made me cross to see such waste. The Kafirs handling the boat thought so too, only their remedy for waste was immediate and practical: they fished out two bottles that had survived, knocked the necks off against the gunwale, and drank as if they were bailing out a leak.

They had not reckoned on the way champagne grows inside you. In a minute their bellies began to swell, and they rolled about on the planks, coughing and shouting that the stuff was tagati—bewitched. The sound of their panic carried up to our deck—high, startled cries against the steady breathing of the sea. I called down and told them it was the strongest medicine of the white man, and that they were as good as dead men. They stared at the froth in horror, like children who have stolen a pot from the fire and find it full of spirits. They went ashore trembling, and I don’t think those two will ever put their mouths to champagne again, bewitched or not.

All the while we steamed northward for Natal I was turning Sir Henry Curtis’s proposal over in my mind like a cook turning a strip of biltong above coals—testing it, watching what renders out, judging how much it will cost a man before it is fit to swallow. For a day or two we said little about it. I told them hunting yarns instead—true ones. There is no need to lie about hunting; the bush provides enough oddity without a man adding his own. Yet even as I spoke, part of me kept listening for the decision inside my own head, as one listens for a pot to come to the boil.

At last, on a January evening—our hottest month—we ran along the Natal coast, expecting to make Durban Point by sunset. It is a handsome shore from East London onwards: red sandhills, broad sweeps of green, kraals scattered like dark seeds, and a ribbon of surf that keeps throwing up white pillars where it hits the rocks. But near Durban there is an extra richness, as though the land has been fed and watered with more affection. The hills are cut into steep kloofs by centuries of rushing rain; the rivers wink and sparkle as they race down them. There is bush as God planted it—thick, deep green—and then the softer greens of mealie gardens and sugar patches, laid out like careful beds. Now and again a white house smiles down at the placid sea and makes the whole thing feel inhabited, as if beauty has been given a hearth and a table. I have lived too much in wilderness not to value that homely note, though civilisation has a way of driving off the game. Eden must have been fair before man, but I’ve always thought it must have been fairer when Eve was there to put it in order.

We misjudged our time. The sun was already low when we dropped anchor off the Point, and the signal gun boomed out to tell Durban the Mail had arrived. It was too late to think of crossing the Bar that night, so we went down comfortably to dinner after watching the Mail-bags taken off in the life-boat. When we came up again the moon was out, laying a bright, clean path over sea and shore, so strong that the lighthouse flashes seemed almost impatient against it. From land drifted those sweet, spicy smells—hot earth, leaf, flower, and the faint tang of cooking fires—that always put me in mind of hymns and missionaries and the way they try to make strange places smell like home. On the Berea the windows winked with a hundred lights. From a brig lying near us came the sailors’ singing as they worked their anchor, the voices rising and falling with the creak of timber and the slap of small waves against her side. It was one of those Southern African nights that make men softer without their knowing—silvered, musical, and peaceful. Even a great bulldog aboard, that had been yearning all voyage to come to grips with a baboon in a cage on the foc’sle, gave in at last and snored contentedly by a cabin door, no doubt finishing his enemy magnificently in his dream.

We three—Sir Henry, Captain Good, and I—sat by the wheel and said nothing for a while, listening. The ship had its own small orchestra: cordage tapping, a block knocking now and then, the low talk of men on watch. Sound does more to a man than he admits; a quiet night can steady a mind the way warm broth steadies a stomach. There is health in honest natural noises, and poison in the ceaseless grind and clatter that men build for themselves in towns. Here there was only sea, wind, voices, and the far boom of surf—no iron screaming, no commercial din dressing itself up as “progress.”

Sir Henry broke the silence first. “Well, Mr. Quatermain,” he said, “have you been thinking about my proposals?”

“Ay,” echoed Captain Good. “What do you think of them, Mr. Quatermain? I hope you are going to give us the pleasure of your company as far as Solomon’s Mines, or wherever the gentleman you knew as Neville may have got to.”

I got up and knocked the ashes out of my pipe. I had not quite made up my mind, and I wanted a breath of time—just the little pause it takes for a man to swallow. Before the glowing tobacco hit the water I knew. It is often like that: you worry a matter for days, and then one extra second settles it like a stone dropping through clear water.

“Yes, gentlemen,” I said, sitting down again. “I will go—and, by your leave, I will tell you why, and on what conditions.

“First, the terms I ask.

“1. You pay all expenses; and any ivory or other valuables we get to be divided between Captain Good and myself.

“2. You give me five hundred pounds for my services on the trip before we start, I undertaking to serve you faithfully till you choose to abandon the enterprise, or till we succeed, or disaster overtakes us.

“3. That before we trek you execute a deed agreeing that, in the event of my death or disablement, you will pay my boy Harry—who is studying medicine in London at Guy’s—two hundred a year for five years. By then he ought to be earning his own living, if he’s worth his salt.

“That’s all, I think, and I daresay you will say it is quite enough too.”

“No,” said Sir Henry at once. “I accept gladly. I am bent on this project, and would pay more than that for your help, considering the peculiar and exclusive knowledge you possess.”

“Pity I didn’t ask it, then,” I answered; “but I won’t go back on my word. Now that my terms are settled I’ll tell you my reasons. First, gentlemen, I have been watching you both these last few days, and if you won’t think me impertinent, I’ll say plainly I like you. I believe we shall come up well to the yoke together. That is something, with a long journey ahead.

“And now as to the journey itself. I tell you flatly I do not think it probable we can come out of it alive—if we attempt the Suliman Mountains. What was the fate of old Dom da Silvestra three hundred years ago? What was the fate of his descendant twenty years back? What has been your brother’s fate? I tell you frankly, gentlemen, that as their fates were, so I believe ours will be.”

I watched their faces. Captain Good shifted a little; he did not like to have the taste of death put into his mouth before he had even eaten. Sir Henry’s expression did not change.

“We must take our chance,” he said.

“You may wonder,” I went on, “why, if I think this—and I told you I am a timid man—I should go. Two reasons. First, I am a fatalist. I believe my time is appointed without reference to my movements; if I am to go to Suliman’s Mountains to be killed, I shall go and I shall be killed. God Almighty knows His mind about me, so I need not fret Him or myself on that point.

“Second, I am a poor man. For nearly forty years I have hunted and traded and never made more than a living. And perhaps you do not know that the average life of an elephant hunter from the day he takes to the trade is between four and five years. I have lived through about seven generations of my class, so my time cannot be far off in any case. If something happened to me in the ordinary course, by the time my debts were paid there would be nothing to keep my son Harry while he learned to earn. But now he will be set up for five years. That is the whole matter in a nutshell.”

Sir Henry listened as seriously as a man taking oath. “Mr. Quatermain,” he said, “your motives reflect credit on you. Whether you are right or wrong time will show. But right or wrong I am going through with it to the end—sweet or bitter. If we are to be knocked on the head, all I have to say is that I hope we get a little shooting first, eh, Good?”

“Yes, yes,” said the Captain quickly. “We have all three been used to danger in our different ways. No good turning back now. And now I vote we go down to the saloon and take an observation—just for luck, you know.”

And we did—through the bottom of a tumbler.

Next day we went ashore, and I put them up at my little shanty on the Berea, which I call my home. It has three rooms and a kitchen, built of green brick with a galvanised iron roof, but it has a good garden, and the best loquot trees I know. There are young mangoes too, gifts from the curator of the botanical gardens—trees that smell, when the sap runs, like pepper and honey together, and make a man imagine future seasons of sticky fingers and bowls set out on tables. The place is cared for by an old hunter of mine, Jack, a Griqua by birth, whose thigh a buffalo cow broke so badly in Sikukuni’s country that he will never hunt again. He can potter about, though, and he gardens with the steady patience of a man who has had noise enough in his life and now prefers the small sounds: a spade biting soil, water poured into a trench, leaves rubbing together in a mild wind. You will never get a Zulu to take much interest in gardening; it is a peaceful art, and peaceful arts are not much in his line.

There was no room in the house for Sir Henry and Good, so they slept in a tent pitched among my orange trees at the end of the garden. Durban oranges carry fruit, green and gold, and blossoms all at once—life, promise, and fulfilment together on the same branch. The bloom scented their canvas, and with the few mosquitos we get on the Berea—unless the rains have been unusually heavy—I daresay they found it pleasant enough.

Now, to get on—for if I don’t, Harry, you will be tired of my story before we ever fetch up at Suliman’s Mountains—once my mind was made up I began preparations.

First I secured the deed from Sir Henry providing for you in case of accident. There was difficulty about legal execution, as he was a stranger here and the property to be charged lay over the water; but with a lawyer’s help it was got over at last. The lawyer charged twenty pounds for the job, which I thought outrageous—like paying a man a sovereign for salting your pot when you have brought the meat yourself. Then I pocketed my cheque for five hundred.

Having paid this tribute to my bump of caution, I bought a wagon and a span of oxen on Sir Henry’s behalf, and beauties they were. The wagon was twenty-two feet long with iron axles, very strong and light, built throughout of stinkwood. It had been to the Diamond Fields and back—not quite new, but, in my opinion, all the better. If there is green wood or a weakness in a wagon it shows itself on the first hard trip, just as bad flour shows itself the first time you try to bake. This one had been tested by distance and had not complained. It was a “half-tented” wagon—covered only over the after twelve feet, leaving the front open for the rough necessities of travel. In the tented part was a hide cartle, a bed where two could sleep, racks for rifles, and a number of small conveniences that matter greatly once you are away from shops and carpenters. I gave one hundred and twenty-five pounds for it and thought it cheap.

Then I bought a beautiful team of twenty Zulu oxen, which I had been watching for a year or two. Sixteen is usual, but I took four extra for casualties. Zulu cattle are smaller than Africanders—half the size—but they will live where the big oxen would starve, and with a moderate load they can make five miles a day better going. They are quicker, and less liable to footsoreness. What is more, this lot were thoroughly “salted”—they had worked across South Africa and were comparatively proof against red water, which so often destroys whole teams when they reach strange veldt. As for lung-sick—dreadful pneumonia, common here—they had been inoculated.

The inoculation is a cruel-looking business. You cut a slit in the tail and bind in a piece of diseased lung from an ox that has died. The animal takes the sickness mildly, and then, as a rule, the tail drops off about a foot from the root, and the ox becomes proof against future attacks. It seems hard to rob a beast of its tail in a land full of flies, but better a tail sacrificed than an ox lost. A tail without an ox is not much use, except for dusting. Still, it looks strange to trek behind twenty stumps where there ought to be tails, as though Nature had made a small mistake and stuck bulldogs’ sterns onto oxen’s rumps.

Next came provisioning and medicines—matter that needs more judgement than most people think, for one must not overload the wagon and yet must not go without what cannot be replaced. Fortunately Captain Good is something of a doctor. At some point in his life he managed to pass through medical and surgical instruction, and he has kept it up after a fashion. He is not qualified, but he knows more than many a man who writes M.D. after his name, as we were to discover. He had a splendid travelling medicine chest and a set of instruments kept as tidy as a cook’s knives. While we were at Durban he cut off a Kafir’s big toe in a way that was a pleasure to watch—clean, quick, not wasting movement, as if he were jointing meat properly. The Kafir sat stolidly through it, then calmly asked Good to put on another, saying that a “white one” would do at a pinch. That nonplussed the Captain completely.

When those matters were settled there remained two important points: arms and servants.

As to arms, I cannot do better than set down the list we chose from Sir Henry’s ample store and my own. I copied it at the time into my pocket-book:

Three heavy breech-loading double-eight elephant guns, about fifteen pounds each, carrying eleven drachms of black powder.

Two were by a well-known London firm, beautifully made. Mine was not so finely finished and I cannot tell who made it, but I have used it on several trips, shot many elephants with it, and found it always reliable—like an old pot that is blackened outside but boils true.

Three double .500 Expresses, built to stand six drachms—sweet weapons for medium game like eland or sable, and for men too, especially in open country with the semi-hollow bullet.

One double No. 12 central-fire Keeper’s shot-gun, full choke both barrels. That gun proved invaluable later for shooting meat for the pot—the difference between eating well and chewing misery.

Three Winchester repeating rifles (not carbines), spare.

Three single-action Colt’s revolvers with the heavier American cartridge.

That was our total armament. You will note the weapons of each class were of the same make and calibre so cartridges were interchangeable—a vital point, as any experienced hunter knows. A fine gun without proper ammunition is like a stew without salt: it may look right, but it won’t carry you.

Now as to the men.

After much talk we decided to limit our number to five: a driver, a leader, and three servants. The driver and leader I found easily enough—two Zulus named Goza and Tom. But the servants were harder. We needed men we could trust, men with steady hearts, because on a journey like this our lives might hang on a servant’s courage or his fear.

At last I secured two: a Hottentot named Ventvögel—“windbird”—and a little Zulu named Khiva, who spoke English perfectly. Ventvögel I had known before. He was one of the best spoorers I ever worked with, tough as whipcord, never seeming to tire. But he had a failing common enough with his people: drink. Put gin within reach and you could not trust him. Still, we were going beyond grog-shops, so the weakness mattered less.

For a third servant I looked in vain. In the end we decided to start without one, trusting to luck to pick up a suitable man on the road.

But the evening before our fixed departure Khiva told me a Kafir was waiting to see me. We were at table. Dinner had been simple—bread, meat, and such fresh things as my garden and Durban could offer, with the clean scent of citrus drifting in whenever someone moved near the door. After we had done I told Khiva to bring the man in.

Presently a tall, handsome fellow came in—about thirty, and very light-coloured for a Zulu. He lifted his knob-stick in salute, then squatted in the corner on his haunches and sat silent. I did not notice him for a while. It is a mistake to rush at a Zulu with questions; he takes quick talk for lack of dignity. While we waited I studied him. He was a keshla—a ringed man—with the black head-ring of polished gum and fat worked into the hair that Zulus assume when they reach a certain age or standing. And his face tugged at my memory.

“Well,” I said at last, “what is your name?”

“Umbopa,” he answered in a slow, deep voice.

“I have seen your face before.”

“Yes,” he said. “The Inkoosi, the chief, my father, saw my face at the place of the Little Hand—Isandhlwana—on the day before the battle.”

Then I remembered. In the Zulu War I had been one of Lord Chelmsford’s guides, and by good fortune I had left the camp in charge of some wagons the day before the slaughter. While I waited for cattle to be inspanned I had spoken with this man, who had some small command among the native auxiliaries, and he had expressed doubts about the camp’s safety. I had told him to hold his tongue and leave such matters to wiser heads. Afterwards, his words came back to me with a bitter taste.

“I remember,” I said. “What is it you want?”

“It is this, Macumazahn,” he answered—my Kafir name, meaning the man who gets up in the middle of the night, he who keeps his eyes open. “I hear you go on a great expedition far into the North with the white chiefs from over the water. Is it a true word?”

“It is.”

“I hear that you go even to the Lukanga River, a moon’s journey beyond the Manica country. Is this so also, Macumazahn?”

“Why do you ask where we go?” I said sharply. “What is it to you?” For our business had been kept dead secret, and secrets, like good food, spoil when too many hands are in the pot.

He did not flinch. “It is this, O white men, that if indeed you travel so far, I would travel with you.”

There was something in the way he spoke—especially his use of “O white men” instead of “O Inkosis”—that carried an assumption of dignity. It was as though he were measuring us as much as we measured him.

“You forget yourself a little,” I said. “Your words run out unawares. That is not the way to speak. What is your name, and where is your kraal? Tell us, that we may know with whom we have to deal.”

“My name is Umbopa. I am of the Zulu people, yet not of them. The house of my tribe is in the far North; it was left behind when the Zulus came down here a thousand years ago, long before Chaka reigned. I have no kraal. I have wandered many years.

“I came from the North as a child to Zululand. I was Cetewayo’s man in the Nkomabakosi regiment, serving under the great captain Umslopogaasi of the Axe, who taught my hands to fight. Afterwards I ran away from Zululand and came to Natal because I wanted to see the white man’s ways. Next I fought against Cetewayo in the war. Since then I have worked in Natal. Now I am tired, and would go North again. Here is not my place.

“I want no money. But I am a brave man, and worth my place and meat. I have spoken.”

I was puzzled. His story, in the main, rang true, yet something in him stood aside from ordinary Zulus—an air of holding something back, as if his real name lay under his tongue like a word in a dead dialect that cannot be translated into the speech of the present. And I mistrusted any man who offered to come without pay; even generosity can be a kind of chain.

Being doubtful, I translated his words to Sir Henry and Good and asked what they thought.

Sir Henry nodded once. “Ask him to stand up.”

Umbopa rose. As he did so he slipped off a long military great coat and stood naked except for the moocha round his middle and a necklace of lions’ claws. In the lamplight his skin looked scarcely more than dark, and here and there deep black scars—old assegai wounds—ran across him like the writing of history on a living page. He was a magnificent man—six foot three, broad, straight, and shapely as a spear.

Sir Henry walked up and looked into his face. The two stood near each other—both big men, both with that quiet weight of presence. Captain Good, watching, said softly, “They make a good pair, don’t they? One as big as the other.”

“I like your looks, Mr. Umbopa,” said Sir Henry in English, “and I will take you as my servant.”

Umbopa understood; I saw it in the way his eyes took in the words. He answered in Zulu, “It is well,” and then, glancing at Sir Henry’s great stature and breadth, added with grave simplicity, “We are men, thou and I.”
