Catherine was a model of domestic industry, a woman who rarely ventured beyond her walls; yet whenever she did, chaos invariably ensued. Acutely aware of this pattern, she had postponed her trip to Sevenbergen for weeks, waiting for a break in her household duties. Finally, one afternoon, when Peter Buyskens' mule happened to be away, she resolved to go.

Before departing, however, Eli summoned the whole family. "Tell me truthfully," he asked, "have you considered visiting Margaret Brandt?"
"Ay, my man," Catherine admitted.
"Then I forbid you."
"Oh, do you?" she challenged, flushing.
"I do forbid it," Eli repeated sternly.
"There is nothing more to say, then," she murmured, her defiance curdling into shame.

Once alone with her daughter, Kate, Catherine berated herself furiously, not Eli. "I acted like a cat cornered by a robin," she lamented, pacing. "I am a silly hen that cannot lay an egg without cackling and gathering the whole flock to watch me. Next time either of us desires something, let us do it immediately, without thought or speech. If questioned later, we can claim ignorance, say *'Alack, we knew naught'*—who would suspect us? For two pins, I would have gone to Sevenbergen despite their teeth!"

Such wild defiance staggered Kate. "Nay, Mother, Father will come around with patience."
"And so will Michaelmas," Catherine retorted, her tone softening slightly. "But when? I was so set on you seeing the girl. Perhaps then we might consult. Say what they will, one judges a beast or human only by the eye. If I had fifty more sons, I would never thwart one's fancy until I had seen the object and verified it. Life is a school, and the lessons never end; we discard one error only to commit another, stumbling blindly until we reach our graves."

Kate stepped forward timidly. "Mother?"
"Well, what brings this look? No good tidings, I fear."
Kate faltered. "An avowal she hath to make."
"Now, there is a noble word," Catherine declared proudly. "Gerard taught you that, I swear. Speak it."
"Well, Mother, sooth to say, I have seen her."
"And?"
"And spoken with her."
"And you never told me? After this, miracles are dust."
"Mother, you were so angry against her. I waited until I could speak without further provoking you."
"Ay," Catherine sighed, half sadly, half bitterly. "Like mother, like daughter; cowardice is our ruin. I buffeted the others, or how would the house fare? Did you, Kate, ever receive a harsh word or look from your poor mother? Nay, I will not have you weep, girl. Ten to one you used sound judgment. Rise, brave heart, and tell me all. Better late than never. First and foremost, how did you manage to visit Sevenbergen with your crutches, and I not knowing?"

"I never was there in my life," Kate stammered. "And, Mammy dear, to say that I never wished to see her... but I never sought her out."
"There now," Catherine disputed, "did I not say 'twas unlike you to seek her secretly? Come, I am all agog."
"Thus it befell. Some manner of ear caught wind, and prithee, good mother, on my knees, ask me not how, that Gerard was a prisoner in the Stadthouse tower."
"Catechism," Catherine murmured, her sigh turning to a moan. "Blacker than I thought."
"Giles and I went out at night to encourage him. There, at the foot of the tower, stood a brave maiden, utterly strange to me. At first, we frightened one another through the gloom of his bad reputation and our fearful imaginations, paling in the moonlight. But instantly, she cried, 'You are Margaret,' and I, 'And you are Kate.' Think on it!"
"Treason!" Catherine exclaimed. "He would have talked back and forth of you to her, and her to you."
In return, Kate bestowed upon Catherine a gift of supreme value—the composite kiss. She pressed a single kiss to her cheek, conveying four distinct messages simultaneously:
1. Quite correct.
2. Good, clever mother, for guessing so quickly.
3. How sweet it is to be of one mind again after discord.
4. And many others.

"Now, speak thy mind, child," Catherine urged gently. "Gerard is not here. Alas, what am I saying? Would to Heaven he were."
"Well, Mother, she is comely and resembles her portrait but little."
"Eh, dear! Hark to the youth! I care for good deeds, not mere looks. Does she love my boy as he deserves?"
"Sevenbergen lies farther from the Stadthouse than does our home," Kate reflected. "Yet she was there before I arrived."
Catherine nodded knowingly.
"Nay, more," Kate continued. "She had freed him before I came. Yes, down from the captive's tower."
Catherine shook her head in disbelief. "The highest tower for miles! It is not feasible."
"'Tis sooth," Kate insisted. "She and an old man brought found means and wit to send him up a rope. There he dangled from his prison, and our Giles ascended it. When I first saw the rope hanging, I thought, 'This is glamour.' But when the frank lass's arms enfolded me, and her bosom beat against mine, and her cheeks grew wet, I realized, 'Tis not glamour: 'tis love.' She is not like me, but sturdy and capable. Even I, poor frail creature, feel sometimes that I could move the world for those I love. I love you, Mother. And she loves Gerard."
"God bless her for it! God bless her!" Catherine cried.
"But..."
"But what, lamb?"
"Is her love truly honest? 'Tis strange. Yet that same quality which warms your heart has somewhat cooled mine towards her. Poor soul. She is no wife, you know, Mother, when all is done."
"Humph! They stood at the altar together."
"Ay, but they departed as they entered, maid and bachelor."
"The parson says so?"
"Nay, for that I know not."
"Then I will take no man's word but his own in such a tangled skein." After a pause, she added, "Nevertheless, you are right, girl. I shall go to Sevenbergen alone. A wife I am, but not a slave. All are in darkness here. She holds the key. I must question her, and no one beside me—not you, lest you interfere. I will take no lily to a house with a stain, no, not to a palace of gold and silver."

As Catherine pondered these words, her resolve hardened. Curiosity, a potent and nearly omnipotent passion, began to drive her. One fine afternoon, after dinner, she whispered to Kate, "Keep the house from falling to pieces, if you can." Donning her finest kirtle and hood, her scarlet clocked hose, and brand-new shoes, she marched briskly toward Sevenbergen, troubling no man's mule.

Upon arrival, she inquired for Margaret Brandt. The first person she asked shook his head. "The name is strange to me." Moving slightly, she approached a girl of fifteen standing at a door. "Father," the girl called inside, "another seeker comes after the magician's daughter." The man emerged, informing Catherine that Peter Brandt's cottage lay just outside town on the east side. "You may see the chimney from hence," he pointed out. "But you will find neither father nor daughter; they fled this week, bless you."
"Do not deceive me, good man," Catherine protested, noting the distance from Tergou. "From Tergou? Then you must have met the soldier."
"What soldier?"
"Ay, I did meet a soldier."
"Well, then, that soldier was here seeking that very Margaret."
"Ay," the girl interjected, "and he was frantic with us because she was gone. His long beard and her cheek are no strangers, I warrant."
"Says no more than you know," Catherine snapped sharply. "You are too young to slander your elders. Stay! Tell us more about this soldier, good man."
"Nay, I know no more than that he sought Margaret Brandt. I told him she and her father had vanished in a moonlight flight this past fortnight. Some thought the devil had whisked them away, being magicians. 'And,' says he, 'the devil fly away with thee for thy ill news!' That was my thanks. 'But I doubt 'tis a lie,' said he. 'If you think so,' I replied, 'go and see.' 'I will,' he said, and burst forth with a handful of gibberish. My wife thought 'twas curses; he hurried to the cottage. Soon he returned, singing another tune. 'You were right, and I was wrong,' he said, pushing a silver coin into my hand. Show it to your wife, some of you, and she will believe me; I have been called a liar twice today."
"It needs not," Catherine said, examining the coin, "though I inspected it anyway."
