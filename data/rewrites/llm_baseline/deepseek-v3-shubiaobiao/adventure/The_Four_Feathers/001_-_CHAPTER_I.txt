Lieutenant Sutch was the first to arrive at Broad Place that afternoon. The June sun hung low over the Surrey hills, painting the old Feversham estate in hues of amber and deep green. The house, nestled among pines, stood like a sentinel overlooking the rolling plains below.

Sutch limped across the hall, his gaze drifting over the generations of Fevershams staring sternly from their portraits. He found General Feversham on the terrace, his posture rigid, eyes fixed on the distant Sussex Downs.

“How’s the leg?” the general asked without turning.

“Troublesome in winter,” Sutch replied. “But no matter.”

Feversham gave a curt nod. Silence settled between them, broken only by the murmur of laborers in the fields below. A train snaked along the horizon, its smoke a fleeting scar against the sky.

“I thought I’d find you here,” Sutch remarked.

“My wife’s favorite spot,” Feversham said flatly. “She liked empty spaces.”

“She had the mind to fill them,” Sutch murmured.

The general shot him a blank look but let the comment pass.

“Glad you came early,” he said instead. “I wanted your thoughts. Today isn’t just the anniversary of the Redan. At the very hour we stood ready in the dark—”

“West of the quarries,” Sutch interrupted, his voice tight. “I remember.”

“—my son was born. I thought, if you’ve no objection, he might join us tonight. He’s home. He’ll enter the service soon enough—best he hears what’s ahead.”

“By all means,” Sutch agreed, curiosity flickering. He had never met Harry Feversham, though he’d long wondered which parent the boy took after—the iron-willed general or his late wife, whose keen intellect had always been a mystery in her choice of husband.

That evening, Harry sat quietly at the table, listening as the men traded stories of the Crimea—tales of frozen trenches, reckless charges, and the slow, grinding horror of war. The boy’s dark eyes never wavered from the speakers, his face mirroring every grim detail as if the battles unfolded before him. Once, when a major spoke of the agonizing wait before an advance, Harry’s shoulders tensed as though he, too, braced for the order.

Then his gaze darted backward—just once—and Sutch stiffened. He knew that look. He’d seen it on green recruits mid-battle, the moment before panic took them.

No one else seemed to notice. The stories rolled on, each more brutal than the last. A broken officer’s disgrace. A surgeon who’d slit his own throat at the whistle of a bullet.

Harry sat motionless, his fingers digging into his knees, his face bone-white.

“Strange, isn’t it?” the general mused. “Some men simply break. No explaining it.”

Sutch leaned toward the boy. “You understand any of this?”

Harry’s eyes met his, dark and unreadable.

Before he could answer, the general scoffed. “Harry? Of course he does. He’s a Feversham.”

But Sutch wasn’t so sure.

When the clock struck midnight, Harry rose obediently, pausing at the door as if the darkened hall beyond held unseen terrors. Then he stepped through, candle in hand.

Sutch followed moments later.

The boy stood in the hall, the flame trembling as he stared up at the portraits—generations of soldiers, stern and unyielding. Their eyes seemed to bore into him, judging, condemning.

For a long moment, Harry didn’t move. Then, slowly, he bowed his head.

As if he’d been found wanting.

Sutch cleared his throat. “Harry.”

The boy turned, calm as stone.

“If you ever need to talk—” Sutch began, offering a card.

“Thank you, sir,” Harry said, his voice flat. He took it without another word and climbed the stairs, the candlelight fading into the dark.

Back in the dining room, glasses clinked as the men toasted.

“To Harry Feversham!” they roared. “May he honor the name!”

Upstairs, the boy lay rigid in bed, the echoes of their cheer twisting like a knife in his gut.

In his mind, he saw a disgraced officer slinking through London’s shadows.

A surgeon’s lifeless hand, a blade glinting in lamplight.

And both faces were his own.
