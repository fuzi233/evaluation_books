Mr. Yorke was, in every conceivable sense, the quintessential Yorkshire gentleman. At fifty-five, he appeared even older at first glance, his hair a stark, silvery white. His forehead was broad rather than high, his complexion fresh and robust. The harshness of the North was etched into his features and echoed in his voice. Every trait was thoroughly English—devoid of any Norman lineage. It was an inelegant, unclassical, and decidedly non-aristocratic countenance. Those accustomed to fine things might have labeled it vulgar; sensible individuals would have called it characteristic; while the shrewd among us would have delighted in it, recognizing the pith, sagacity, and raw, original intelligence stamped upon every line and furrow. Yet, it remained an indocile, scornful, and sarcastic face—the visage of a man difficult to lead and impossible to drive. He was tall, well-made, wiry, and possessed a stately integrity of posture; there was absolutely no hint of clownishness about him.

To sketch Mr. Yorke's physical appearance is relatively easy; to define his mind is far more arduous. Do not expect to meet a perfection here, nor a benevolent, philanthropic elder. While he has spoken with sense and good feeling to Mr. Moore, do not assume he always thinks or speaks justly and kindly.

First, Mr. Yorke lacked the organ of veneration—a significant deficit that misaligns a man wherever reverence is due. Second, he lacked the faculty of comparison, a deficiency that strips one of sympathy. Third, he possessed too little of the organs of benevolence and ideality, robbing his nature of glory and softness, diminishing those divine qualities across the universe.

This lack of veneration rendered him intolerant toward those above him—kings, nobles, priests, dynasties, parliaments, and all their institutions. To him, their doings, enactments, forms, rights, and claims were abominations, mere rubbish. He found no pleasure in them and believed that razing their high places and crushing their occupants would bring clear gain to the world with no damage whatsoever. Furthermore, this absence of veneration deadened his heart to the electric delight of admiring what is admirable, drying up a thousand pure sources of enjoyment and withering a thousand vivid pleasures. He was not irreligious, belonging to no sect, but his faith was that of a man lacking awe, imagination, and tenderness. He believed in God and Heaven, but his conception of them was stripped of wonder.

His inability to compare made him inconsistent. While professing excellent general doctrines of mutual toleration, he harbored a bigoted antipathy toward specific classes. He spoke of "parsons" and their ilk, of "lords" and their retinue, with a harshness and insolence that was both unjust and insufferable. He could not place himself in the shoes of those he vilified; he could not weigh their errors against their temptations, or their defects against their disadvantages. Unable to realize how circumstances might affect him similarly, he frequently voiced ferocious and tyrannical wishes regarding those he deemed to have acted tyrannically. By his threats alone, one might suspect he would employ arbitrary and cruel means to advance freedom and equality. Yet, "equality!" was a word he spoke, but at heart, he was a proud man. Very friendly to his workpeople, generous to those beneath him, and content to submit quietly to being beneath them himself, he was haughty as Beelzebub toward anyone the world considered his superior. Revolt ran in his blood; he could not bear control. Neither his father, his grandfather, nor his children could endure it.

His lack of general benevolence made him impatient of imbecility and any fault that grated on his strong, shrewd nature, leaving no check to his cutting sarcasm. Being unmerciful, he would sometimes wound repeatedly, oblivious to the pain inflicted or caring how deeply he thrust.

Regarding the scarcity of ideality in his mind, this is scarcely a fault. Possessing a fine ear for music and a correct eye for color and form, he retained the quality of taste. And who cares for imagination? Who does not view it as a dangerous, senseless attribute, akin to weakness, perhaps bordering on frenzy—a disease rather than a gift?

Most likely everyone thinks so, except those who possess it or fancy they do. Listen to them speak, and you would believe their hearts grow cold without that elixir, their eyes dim without that refining flame, and they become lonely without that strange companion. You would suppose it imparts glad hope to spring, charm to summer, tranquility to autumn, and consolation to winter, feelings you yourself do not experience. It is, of course, an illusion; yet the fanatics cling to their dream, refusing to exchange it for gold.

Since Mr. Yorke did not possess poetic imagination himself, he considered it a superfluous quality in others. He could tolerate, even encourage, painters and musicians because he could appreciate the results of their art; he could enjoy a fine picture and good music. But a quiet poet—regardless of the struggle or fire burning within his breast—if he could not function as a man in a counting-house or a tradesman in the Piece Hall, might live despised and die scorned under the gaze of Hiram Yorke.

Given that there are many Hiram Yorkes in the world, it is fortunate that the true poet, though outwardly quiet, often possesses a truculent spirit beneath his placidity. Full of shrewdness in his meekness, he can measure the stature of those who look down on him and accurately assess the value of the pursuits they disdain. It is fitting that he maintains his own bliss and society with Nature, independent of those who find little pleasure in him and in whom he finds none. Just as the world often turns a dark, cold side to him—and rightly so, for he first turns a dark, cold, careless side to them—he should be able to sustain a festal brightness in his bosom, making all bright and genial for him, while strangers might deem his existence a Polar winter never gladdened by the sun. The true poet is not to be pitied; indeed, he is apt to laugh in his sleeve when a misguided sympathizer whines over his wrongs. Even when utilitarians sit in judgment, pronouncing him and his art useless, he receives their sentence with hard derision and a broad, deep, merciless contempt for the unhappy Pharisees who utter it. These, however, are not Mr. Yorke's reflections; we are currently dealing with Mr. Yorke.

I have outlined some of his faults, reader. Regarding his virtues, he was one of the most honorable and capable men in Yorkshire; even those who disliked him were forced to respect him. He was deeply beloved by the poor, thoroughly kind and fatherly to them. Toward his workmen, he was considerate and cordial. When dismissing someone from employment, he would strive to guide them toward new opportunities or, failing that, assist them and their families in moving to districts where work might be found. Moreover, if any individual among his "hands" showed signs of insubordination, Mr. Yorke—who, like many who abhor being controlled, knew how to exercise control with vigor—possessed the secret of crushing rebellion in the germ, eradicating it like a bad weed so it never spread within his sphere of authority. With his own affairs in such a happy state, he felt free to speak with utmost severity of those in different situations, attributing all unpleasantness in their positions entirely to their own fault. He severed ties with the masters and advocated freely for the cause of the operatives.

The Yorke family was the first and oldest in the district. Though not the wealthiest, Mr. Yorke was one of the most influential men. His education had been excellent. Before the French Revolution, he traveled extensively on the Continent. He was adept in French and Italian. During a two-year sojourn in Italy, he collected numerous fine paintings and tasteful rarities, adorning his residence with them. When inclined, his manners were those of a finished gentleman of the old school; his conversation, when he chose to please, was singularly interesting and original. Usually expressing himself in the Yorkshire dialect, he preferred his native Doric to a refined vocabulary, asserting that "a Yorkshire burr" was as much superior to a Cockney's lisp as a bull's bellow to a rat's squeak.

Mr. Yorke knew everyone and was known by everyone for miles around, yet his intimate acquaintances were few. Thoroughly original himself, he had no taste for the ordinary. A racy, rough character, high or low, always found acceptance; a refined, insipid personage, however exalted, was his aversion. He would spend an hour talking freely with a shrewd workman or a queer, sagacious old woman among his cottagers, grudging a moment to a commonplace fine gentleman or the most fashionable, elegant, yet frivolous lady. He carried these preferences to an extreme, forgetting that amiable and admirable characters exist among those who cannot be original. Yet, he made exceptions to his own rule. There existed a certain order of mind: plain, ingenuous, neglecting refinement, almost devoid of intellectuality, and quite incapable of appreciating what was intellectual in him. Yet, this group never felt disgust at his rudeness, was not easily wounded by his sarcasm, and did not closely analyze his words, deeds, or opinions.
