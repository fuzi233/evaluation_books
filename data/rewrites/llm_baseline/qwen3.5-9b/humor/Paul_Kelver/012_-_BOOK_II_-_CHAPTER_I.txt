"Rooms to let for a single gentleman."

Sometimes, driven by sheer foolishness during an idle hour, I find myself knocking on the door. After a pause—long or short—the "slavey" answers. In the morning, she is a slattern, hiding her arms deep within her apron; in the afternoon, she is sharp-dressed in a dirty cap and apron. How well I know her! Unchanged, not an inch taller since I last saw her: round, bewildered eyes; an open mouth; touzled hair; and hands scored red with scrubbing. I make a conscious effort *not* to mutter, "Sorry, forgot my key," as I push past her and thunder up the narrow stairs. The carpet ends at the first floor; beyond that, it is bare stone. Instead, I ask politely, "What rooms have you to let?" Scuttling up the kitchen stairs, she calls over the banisters, "A gentleman to see the rooms!"

Up comes a harassed-looking, elderly woman, genteel in black, panting slightly. She crushes past the little slavey and approaches me, sizing me up critically.

"I have a very nice room on the first floor," she informs me, "and one behind on the third."

I agree to inspect both, claiming I am doing a favor for a young friend. We squeeze past the hat and umbrella stand—just enough room if you hug the wall.

The first-floor apartment is imposing. It boasts a marble-topped sideboard, three feet by two, whose doors can be kept shut simply by wedging a wad of paper between them. A green tablecloth matches the curtains. The lamp is safe only if placed dead center; shift it, and disaster strikes. A paper fire-stove ornament somehow imbues the room with an air of chastity. Above the mantelpiece hangs a fly-blown mirror, its once-gilt frame and glass serving as a canvas for invitation cards. Indeed, one or two remain stuck there, proving that even bed-sitters partake in social delights. Opposite, an oleograph of a Swiss landscape—sold by Cheap Jacks on Saturday nights in Pimlico Road and warranted "hand-made"—adorns the wall. Generally, a Swiss landscape possesses more "body" than local scenery. A dilapidated mill, a foaming torrent, a mountain, a maiden, and a cow are reliable fixtures.

There is an easy chair (I disclaim all responsibility for calling it "easy"). It is stuffed with coils of steel wire, each sporting a functional "business end," and covered in highly glazed horsehair. It awaits the uninitiated. There is only one way to sit: plant your feet firmly on the floor and cling to the extreme edge. Attempt to lean back, and you will inevitably slide out. The chair seems to say, *"Excuse me, you are very heavy. You would be much more comfortable on the floor. Thank you so much."* Behind the door lies the bed; behind the bed sits the washstand. If you face the window, you can forget the bed exists. Conversely, if multiple friends visit, you will be grateful for the lack of seating options, as experienced guests invariably march straight for the bed, refusing to trade it for the wire-filled armchair.

"And this room is?"

"Eight shillings a week, sir—with attendance, of course."

"Any extras?"

"The lamp is eighteenpence a week. Kitchen fire, if you wish to dine in, is two shillings."

"And fire?"

"Sixpence a scuttle, sir. I charge for coals."

"It's rather a small scuttle."

The landlady bristles. "The usual size, I think, sir." One presumes there is a special size manufactured exclusively for lodging-house keepers.

Agreeing to check the third-floor-back room, the landlady opens the door but stays on the landing, unwilling to dwarf the apartment by comparison. Here, you cannot ignore the bed. It is the life and soul of the room, refusing to efface itself. Its only rival is the straw-colored washstand, glaring defiantly from the corner with a staring white basin and jug. *"I know I'm small,"* it seems to declare, *"but I'm very useful; and I won't be ignored."* The rest of the furniture includes two honest chairs (they are neither easy nor pretending to be), a small chest of light-painted drawers with white china handles holding a tiny mirror, and an attenuated, four-legged table occupying the remaining space—roughly three square feet for the tenant. The sole ornament is a funeral card, framed in beer corks, hanging above the fireplace. Like the corpses the ancient Egyptians brought to their banquets, it serves as a reminder that life's luxuries have an end—or perhaps a consolation that things could be worse.

Rent is three-and-sixpence a week, plus attendance. Lamp is eighteenpence; kitchen fire is a shilling.

"But why is the first-floor fire two shillings and this only one?"

"Well, as a rule, sir, the first floor requires more cooking."

"You are quite right," I concede. "The gentleman on the third floor eats 'plain.' His lunch is taken out. You can see him pacing the quiet square, up and down the narrow street leading nowhere, between twelve and two. He carries a paper bag. Into this, when he is certain no one is watching, his mouth disappears. Studying the neighborhood reveals the contents: Saveloys (twopence), meat pies (twopence), fruit pies (a penny). The lady behind the counter uses a broad knife to lift the pastry cleanly—a marvel given its thinness. Roley-poley pudding is a penny a slice, wonderful when cold. Peas pudding is comforting on chilly days. For tea, he takes two eggs or a fourpenny haddock; occasionally a chop or steak, which you fry for him despite his protests that grilling is preferable. Fried food in your single pan loses flavor, but he shuts his eyes, relying on smell and taste to imagine variety: starting with herrings, moving to liver and bacon, pausing to realize he's eating a joint, and finishing with a hint of toasted cheese. Dinner is eaten out. Perhaps he isn't hungry, or perhaps, calculating beneath a lamppost, appetite strikes. Then there are cheerful spots selling fried plaice (three halfpence), sliced potatoes (a penny), stewed eels (fourpence), vinegar ad libitum, or, on red-letter evenings, a half sheep's head for sevenpence—a supper fit for any hungry king."

I explain I will discuss the matter with my young friend upon his arrival. The landlady agrees, accustomed to what she terms the "wandering Christian." Easing my conscience by slipping a shilling into the slavey's astonished, lukewarm hand, I step out into the long, dreary street, now echoing with the sad cry of "Muffins!"

Sometimes, in the evening, when lamps are lit and the remnants of meat tea clear away, I go visiting. No need to ring the bell or climb stairs. Through thin, transparent walls, I see you clearly, old friends. Fashions have changed slightly. We wore bell-shaped trousers (eight-and-six to measure); tied our neckties with horseshoe pins; and boasted gayer waistcoats. Our cuffs and collars were of paper: sixpence-halfpenny the dozen. On Sundays, they were white and glistening; by Tuesday morning, decidedly dappled. But on Tuesday evening, promenading down Oxford Street between nine and ten, we shot our arms and cocked our chins with the best. Your rubberized linen has advantages—it withstands storms and heat. A quick wipe with a sponge restores your "Dicky" collar. We sacrificed the glaze with bread crumbs, yet our paper outfits were more dazzling in the first few hours.

For the rest, I see no change. I wave greetings from the misty street. *God rest you, gallant gentlemen,* lonely and friendless, making the best of joyless lives on twelve, fifteen, or eighteen shillings a week. Saving scraps to send to mothers in the country, proud of their "gentleman" sons with book-learning and City prospects. Bullied from nine to six, you become rorty young dogs afterward. The half-guinea topper, warmed before the fire, shines resplendent. Second-hand trousers, drawn from beneath the bed, pass for new in the gaslight. May your cheap necktie impress your soul! May your penny cigar be mistaken for Havana! May the barmaid call you "Baby"! May sweet-shop girls invite you to walk, snigger at your humor, and envy your companions!

You dreamers of dreams! While others sleep, you toil upward in the night. You have read Mr. Smiles' *Self-Help*, Longfellow's *Psalm of Life*, and strengthened your resolve with *French Without a Master*. Turning from the alluring streets into the stuffy air of Birkbeck and the Polytechnic, you await success. May the partner's chair you envisioned become yours! May you live one day in Clapham in a twelve-roomed house!

After all, we have our moments. Saturday night at the play. The wait is short. We converse with kindred souls of the British Drama, dreaming of Florences and Kates. Her photo transforms the dismal room into a shrine. We commence poems we intend to post to her. The great doors groan and quiver—that wild thrill! Push
