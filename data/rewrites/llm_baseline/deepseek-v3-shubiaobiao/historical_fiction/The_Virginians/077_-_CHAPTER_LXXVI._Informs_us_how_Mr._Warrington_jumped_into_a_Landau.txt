The first shock of their meeting past, Hetty Lambert wasted no time in laying bare her grievances.

"So, you’ve finally come to inquire after Theo?" she began, her voice sharp with accusation. "And now you’re sorry your neglect has made her ill? Six weeks she’s been unwell, and not a word from you! Charming attention, Mr. George, truly!"

"Neglect?" Warrington gasped, stung.

"Oh, don’t pretend! A year at her side, day after day, and then—poof!—gone without so much as a farewell! Is that what you call devotion?"

"I gave your father my word—"

"Your *word*!" Hetty scoffed, tossing her head. "A fine thing, a man’s word! Enough to break a girl’s heart and then vanish on a whim. If I were a man, I’d know better than to play such cruel games. But then, I suppose gentlemen make promises just for the pleasure of breaking them."

Warrington, exasperated, flung out his hands. "Good God, Hetty! Was it I who withdrew? Was it not your father who forbade me the house, who bound me by my honor never to see her again?"

"Honor!" she cried, her eyes flashing. "You keep your oath to him and break faith with her! A pretty notion of honor indeed! If I were a man—" Here her voice faltered, the fire dimming to anguish. "Oh, George, I scarcely know what I say. I am half-mad with grief. You mustn’t leave her. They don’t see it at home—but I know her best. She will *die* if you forsake her. Have pity, I beg you!"

From fury to tears she swung, her small frame trembling. Was she right? Was Theo’s life truly hanging by so frail a thread? Love, Warrington knew, could wound as deeply as any blade—yet surely hearts mended in time? Had not Hetty herself borne such a blow and survived? But no—this was different. Their love was no common passion, and the thought of Theo fading away unmanned him utterly.

The physician, when consulted, would hear none of it. "I’ll have no part in matchmaking," he declared, eyes twinkling despite his stern tone. "If two patients of mine chance to meet and converse, so be it—but meddle in affairs of the heart? Not I! Unless you wish the General to call me out at Montagu House?" And with that, he took his leave, leaving Warrington to his torment.

Yet fate, ever capricious, soon intervened. Days later, as Warrington wandered the Tottenham Road, a familiar landau rolled into view—and there, beside the driver, sat young Charley Lambert, waving madly. Within, propped against cushions, pale as mist but with eyes alight, was Theo.

"The doctor prescribed air," Hetty said primly. "Hampstead should do her good."

"I have been ill," Theo murmured, her voice a fragile thread. "But I am better now."

At that moment, a hymn swelled from the chapel nearby—a soaring, triumphant psalm. Warrington seized her hand, and in her gaze, he found the same unspoken truth that had always bound them. No past, no parting, existed in that look.

Charley, grinning down from his perch, was swiftly dispatched with Hetty to hear the sermon ("I’ve a great curiosity about Mr. Whitfield!" Hetty announced, dragging her brother away), leaving Warrington alone with Theo.

What passed between them in that landau? The old Squire, recounting the tale years later, teased his listeners mercilessly.

"Shall I divulge your mother’s secrets?" he asked, eyes dancing as his children clamored.

"Tell us!" cried the girls, while Mademoiselle Eleonore hid a smile behind her hand.

"Very well," said the Squire, unfolding his manuscript with mock solemnity. "There we were—a young scoundrel, breaking every promise, and a lady too good for him..."

But the heart of the matter was this: Warrington, honor be damned, leapt into that carriage. And Theo, though she chided him gently, did not let go of his hand.
