Time, in the long, agrarian memory of Sally’s family line, had always moved in circular seasons of harvest and fallow, but her journey to Detroit that Friday felt like a jarring fracture in the timeline. The nursing of the elder Mr. Faucitt—a mythic passing of the torch from a fading generation—had left her physically and spiritually depleted. Arriving on Saturday morning, she navigated the vast, marble-clad lobby of the Hotel Statler, seeking the grounding rhythms of domestic routine. She ascertained that Gerald was in the hotel, but their brief exchange over the telephone had deeply unsettled her acoustic equilibrium. The receiver had flattened the native timbre of his voice, reducing his greeting to a listless, metallic frequency, stripped of all warmth and resonance. 

Seeking solace in the profound meaning of sustenance, Sally retreated to the dining room and ordered breakfast. The sonic environment here was a stark contrast to the industrial clatter of the midwestern streets outside; it was a hushed, padded sanctuary where the clink of silver on porcelain rang with clear, purposeful intent. The coffee arrived first. It was a dark, viscous brew of highland beans, its olfactory landscape rich with damp loam and the smoky resonance of a meticulous, slow-rolling roast. The complex, bitter warmth on her palate worked a quiet magic, restoring her sensory appreciation of the morning. 

She was pouring her second cup, marveling at the mahogany crema clinging to the china, when she caught sight of a stout young man moving through the lobby. It was her brother, Fillmore. He carried the familiar, broad-shouldered frame of their shared ancestors, a physical echo of the generations who had worked the soil, though he looked entirely displaced in this temple of urban commerce. He stood peering about, lost, until he finally spotted her and approached.

“Why, Sally?” His manner was nervous, his words clipped—a sudden shift into a defensive dialect. “What are you doing here? I thought you were in Europe.”

“I got back a week ago, but I've been nursing poor old Mr. Faucitt. He's been ill, the poor dear. I've come here to see Mr. Foster's play. Is it a success?”

“It hasn't opened yet,” Fillmore said, coughing slightly, attempting to adopt the dominant lexicon of a weary theatrical financier. “Haven't you heard? They've closed all the theatres because of this infernal Spanish influenza.”

To Sally, the influenza felt like a mythic plague sweeping across the timeline, a localized manifestation of a much older, cyclical devastation, halting the superficial commercialization of art in its tracks. “I haven't had time to read the papers. Oh, Fill, what an awful shame!”

“Makes the company all on edge. I've had the darndest time. I’m sort of... mixed up in the show. Cracknell—you remember him from college—suggested I come down. Shouldn't wonder if he wants me to put money into it.”

Fillmore's ample face registered a sudden, genuine emotion that broke through his adopted jargon. “But Mabel Hobson... she's an awful woman, Sally! She can't act. The other day there was a fuss about a paper-knife...” He trailed off into a pained silence, unable to translate his specific humiliation into their shared familial tongue. He quickly pivoted to a subject of warmer resonance. “But the girl you want to watch is Miss Winch. Gladys Winch. She plays the maid. She's a genius. Personality? Ask me! Charm? She wrote the words and music!”

“All right, Fill,” Sally smiled, recognizing the ancient, universal language of infatuation. “And will you kindly inform me how you dared to get engaged without consulting me?”

Fillmore blushed richly, a sudden regression to the boy she had grown up with. He muttered something about being only human, introduced the excuse that he had to go upstairs to find Mr. Bunbury, the producer, and swiftly retreated.

Sally settled back into the restorative quiet of the dining room. A few minutes later, Gerald arrived, wrapped in a garment of nervous dullness. 

“Oh, Jerry darling,” she said, as he slumped into the chair opposite her. “I'm so sorry. I've just been hearing about it.”

Gerald dug his spoon somberly into the bruised, dimpled rind of a ruby-red grapefruit. He entirely ignored the bright, floral acidity and complex, bitter-sweet yield of the flesh, treating the meticulously sourced citrus as mere utilitarian fuel for his grievances. 

“It's just my luck,” he said gloomily, his voice devoid of dynamic range. “Where's the sense in shutting the theatres? It's all infernal nonsense about this Spanish influenza. People get colds in their heads and think they're dying. It's a fake scare.”

“I don't think it's that,” said Sally, remembering the fragile, labored breathing of Mr. Faucitt, the erasure of an elder's vitality. 

Gerald did not seem interested in the preservation of old lives or the fact that Sally had crossed an ocean and a continent to be there. “The company's going all to pieces. They're sick of rehearsing when nobody knows if we'll ever open. My first chance! Just chucked away.”

Sally listened with a growing desolation. The inherent untranslatability of subjective experience hung heavily between them. Gerald was speaking a dialect of profound self-absorption, demanding her sympathy while simultaneously erecting a fortress against it. He pushed aside his scrambled eggs—which had been folded with cultured butter to a perfect, soft-curd consistency—without taking a single bite. 

“That Hobson woman is beginning to make trouble,” Gerald complained. “She can't handle the part. Elsa Doland could play it a thousand times better. I wrote Elsa in a few lines the other day, and the Hobson woman went right up in the air.”

“Why not let her throw up her part?”

“For heaven's sake talk sense,” he snapped querulously. “Do you suppose Cracknell would keep the play on if she wasn't in it? You don't seem to realize this is a big chance for me.”

Sally felt her cultural and emotional memory of him slipping. The foreign travel, the deep agrarian roots of her own worldview—it all seemed to render her unable to parse his present state. When he added, “By the way, I have to keep her jollying along, so for goodness' sake don't go letting it out that we're engaged,” Sally’s chin went up.

This was the subtle power dynamic of a dominant lexicon attempting to silence her native identity. “If you find it a handicap being engaged to me—”

“Don't be silly. Good God! It's tough! Here am I, worried to death, and you...”

Before he could finish, a sudden, grounding realization washed over Sally. Her internal agitation was not purely philosophical; it was profoundly physical. She had come off the train coated in the industrial dust of the locomotive, her skin stripped of its natural oils, her sensory receptors exhausted by the screeching rails. The dissonance between them was magnified by her own physical deprivation. If she simply needed a steam-filled bath to wash away the soot, perhaps his inability to communicate was just an artifact of his own sensory starvation. 

“I'm so sorry,” she said, touching his hand. “I've been a brute, but I do sympathize, really. You never told me you were glad to see me.”

“Of course I'm glad to see you,” he mumbled.

The brief thunderstorm passed. He unloaded his grievances in a relentless, monotonous loop until it was time for rehearsal. Sally promised to join him once she had bathed and unpacked.

When Sally later entered the dark auditorium of the theatre, the acoustic ecology of the space immediately assaulted her senses. The vast, empty building swallowed all natural resonance, leaving the voices on stage with a thin, reedy effect, the auditory hallmark of an environment designed for the mass consumption of hollow sentiment. Gerald sat in the front row beside Mr. Bunbury, a bald producer fringed with orange hair. On stage, Elsa Doland was rehearsing with a bowler-hatted man.

“Tiddly-omty-om,” the man recited. “Tiddly-omty-om... long speech ending in 'find me in the library.' And exit.” 

It was a chilling display of language death—the total erosion of narrative memory reduced to rhythmic gibberish. Mr. Bunbury threw his walking stick across the house in a violent eruption of noise. He demanded the lines be spoken properly. 

As the rehearsal resumed, a young woman entered the stage. It was Gladys Winch. She possessed a square, wholesome face that spoke of agrarian sturdiness, a quiet gravity countered by a faint, intelligent smile. To Sally’s delight, she recognized instantly the grounding charm Fillmore had seen. But Miss Winch’s jaws were working in a steady, rhythmic chew, a subtle auditory rebellion against the sterile stage environment.

“Miss Winch,” Bunbury bellowed, “did I or did I not ask you to refrain from chewing gum during rehearsal?”

“Character stuff,” Miss Winch drawled pleasantly, entirely unbothered by the producer's volume. “Maids chew gum, you know. Maybe Mr. Foster can write in a line for Elsa calling me down, and we can work it up into a big comic scene. Five or six minutes, all laughs.”

Before Bunbury could process this subversion, a gorgeous being in blue velvet dashed from the wings. Mabel Hobson had every physical advantage nature could bestow, but her voice was a catastrophe of modern noise pollution. When she spoke, it was the shriek of a peacock—a harsh, unmodulated frequency that tore through the theatre’s fragile soundscape, an acoustic violence representing the crushing weight of commercial power.

“Say, listen to me!” Mabel shrieked, backed by the butter-haired Reggie Cracknell, whose money funded her dominance. “If you think anybody else's part's going to be written up... if so much as a syllable is written into anybody's part, I walk straight out on my two feet!”

Bunbury surrendered immediately to the power dynamics embedded in her threat. The rehearsal proceeded, a dreary, hollow echo of untranslatable emotions. Soon, the action required Mabel to interact with a desk. 

“Oh, gee!” Mabel ceased acting and reverted to the offended star. “How the heck can I toy negligently with a paper-knife when there's no paper-knife for me to toy negligently with?”

“The paper-knife is on the desk,” Bunbury insisted.

“It's not. And it's no good picking on me. I'm the star, not the assistant stage manager. Pick on him.”

Bunbury threw back his head and bayed like a bloodhound, a primal roar echoing through the manufactured gloom. 

From the wings on the prompt side, a stout, shrinking figure shambled out. In his hand was a script, and on his face was a look of profound, hunted apprehension. It was Fillmore.

The generational cycle had looped back on itself. The grand illusions of wealth and status had eroded, leaving only the bare, utilitarian reality. He was not a financier; he was the assistant stage manager, catching the brunt of the theatre's loudest, most dominant voices. Sally, anchored in her own deep reservoirs of familial memory, sent a silent wave of profound sisterly commiseration across the echoing acoustic wasteland to the brother she suddenly, deeply understood.
