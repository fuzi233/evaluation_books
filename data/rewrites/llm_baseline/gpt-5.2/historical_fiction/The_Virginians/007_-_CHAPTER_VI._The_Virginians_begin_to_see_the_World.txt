After the unlucky divine had ridden away from Castlewood—his books shut, his arguments left like cold ashes on the hearth—Madam Esmond and her eldest boy moved about one another with a careful peace, such as follows thunder. They did not speak again of the quarrel. Yet silence, like salt on a raw place, bit the more.

George bore it in his body. A fever came upon him with the first hard weather, and in the night, when the house lay muffled under snow and the pines outside stood listening, the child’s mind broke its tether. He would start up, eyes wide in the candle’s wavering light, and cry out in a voice that was not his own: “Broken! Broken! It never, never can be mended!” The words struck Madam Esmond with a terror more keen than any bodily danger, because they sounded like prophecy—like some old family hurt returning in a new tongue.

She watched him hour after hour, the bedcurtains breathing with his tossing, the chamber filled with the small, cruel noises of sickness: the rasp of breath, the clink of spoon against cup, the shifting of linen. Her closet of remedies—root and leaf and cordial, powders wrapped in paper, bottles that smelled of camphor, bitter bark, and steeped mint—failed her. She had always healed her people with those nostrums; she could set a servant’s ague right with a draught, or bring down a child’s heat with a cloth and a tea, and had long trusted the knowledge that women hand to women without writing it down. Now her simples were like words in a dead language: remembered, repeated, and powerless.

At last she took the only medicine left, which was humiliation. One day, while the snow lay crisp and bright and the air rang with the distant crack of trees in frost, little Mr. Dempster saw her at his own poor door, sitting her pony, cloak drawn tight, cheeks stung red. She had ridden through the weather to beg him.

“I shall bury my resentment, madam,” said he—small, stiff, and very pale with pride under his civility—“as your ladyship buried your pride. Please God, I may be time enough to help my dear young pupil!”

He packed his lancet and his little store of medicaments as carefully as if they were silver; called his one negro-boy; shut his lonely hut; and returned to Castlewood like a man going back into a household which had wounded him and still commanded him.

That night, and for some days following, it seemed likely that Harry would soon be heir of Castlewood. The fever mounted; the boy’s limbs burned; his speech wandered. Madam Esmond’s mind ran in circles, each turn bringing her again to the bedside, to the smell of vinegar and sweat, to the wick’s faint smoke, to the hush of the house that listens for a change in breathing. But Mr. Dempster, with the harsh mercy of his art, bled and dosed and watched with an attention that was almost tenderness. The heat broke. The intermittent attacks lessened. George returned by degrees, as one might hear a lost tune find its way back into the ear.

When the peril had passed, Dempster urged a change of air—“a voyage even to England,” he said—but Madam Esmond had quarreled with her children’s kin there, and now, with the contrition that comes when one has been forced to ask help, owned she had been too hasty. It was resolved instead that the lads should go north and east. So the two young gentlemen set out with Mr. Dempster for tutor, and two servants to attend them; and, leaving the quiet music of Castlewood—the mill’s steady groan, the frogs in the marsh, the kitchen’s morning stir—they took ship to New York and then journeyed up the Hudson to Albany.

They were received there by the first gentry of the province, and from thence passed into the French settlements, where their recommendations opened doors as readily as if gold had been laid upon the threshold. If the English colonies spoke one sort of sound—axes and carts, the long crack of whips, the hammering of new boards—these places had another music: fiddles in parlours, soft French voices that slid like cream, church bells that seemed to sweeten the air, and kitchens where the very pans spoke a language of their own. At tables where stews were tended with patience, where butter was beaten pale as winter sunlight, where herbs were lifted between fingers and breathed upon before they went into the pot, George listened and learned as if nourishment itself were a kind of history.

Harry, who was made for the open and noisy world, went where the sound was loudest. He camped with the Indians, took furs, and shot bears; slept under trees that creaked and sighed like old men; and came home from such excursions smelling of smoke, wet leather, and blood. George never cared for field-sports. His health remained delicate; his pleasure lay not in the crack of a gun, but in the minute attentions of a well-appointed room, the exact note of a violin, the way a sauce is thickened without haste, the name by which a Frenchwoman calls a particular bread.

He became a favourite with the French ladies, who were accustomed to see very few young English gentlemen speak their language with ease. George did more than speak: he wore it. He perfected his accent until, if he chose, he might pass for a Frenchman; he had the bel air, every person allowed. He danced the minuet with an elegance learned not only in the feet but in the ear, for a minuet is as much a pattern of sound as of motion. He learned the latest imported catches and songs, and played them beautifully on his violin; he would have sung them too, but his voice broke at that time and changed from treble to bass—an alteration which vexed him more than he pretended, for it made his own throat a stranger for a season.

And, to the envy of poor Harry—who was absent on a bear-hunt and could only hear the tale later—George had even an affair of honour with a young ensign of the regiment of Auvergne, the Chevalier de la Jabotière, whom he pinked in the shoulder. They afterwards swore an eternal friendship, as young men will, when a little blood has been paid for pride. Madame de Mouchy, the superintendent’s lady, said the mother was blest who had such a son, and wrote a complimentary letter to Madam Esmond upon Mr. George’s behaviour. Had Mr. Whitfield been there to read it, he would scarcely have been pleased with the widow’s elation at hearing of her son’s prowess.

When the lads returned at the end of ten delightful months, their mother was surprised at their growth and improvement. George especially had grown so as to come up to his younger-born brother. When their hair was powdered they might hardly be told apart; but powder was too cumbrous for country life, and commonly each wore his own: George his raven black, Harry his light locks tied with a ribbon.

The reader who has followed the first pages of the lads’ simple biography will have observed that Mr. George Esmond was of a jealous and suspicious disposition—most generous and gentle, incapable of an untruth; too magnanimous to revenge, and almost incapable of forgiving an injury. He had left home with no goodwill toward an honourable gentleman, whose name afterwards became one of the most famous in the world; and he returned not in the least altered in his opinion of his mother’s and grandfather’s friend.

Mr. Washington, though then but just of age, looked and felt much older. He always exhibited an extraordinary simplicity and gravity. He had managed his mother’s and his family’s affairs from a very early age, and was trusted by all his friends and the gentry of his county more than persons twice his senior.

Mrs. Mountain—Madam Esmond’s friend and companion, who dearly loved the two boys and her patroness in spite of many quarrels with the latter and daily threats of parting—was a droll letter-writer, and had written to the lads on their travels. She was of a jealous turn likewise; especially she had a great turn for match-making, and fancied everybody had a design to marry everybody else. There scarce came an unmarried man to Castlewood but Mountain imagined he had an eye towards the mistress of the mansion.

She was positive that odious Mr. Ward intended to make love to the widow, and pretty sure the latter liked him. She knew that Mr. Washington wanted to be married; was certain that such a shrewd young gentleman would look out for a rich wife; and as for differences of age, what matter that the Major—major was his rank in the militia—was fifteen years younger than Madam Esmond? Were they not used to such marriages in the family? Was not my lady her mother older than the Colonel when she married him—when she married him and was so jealous she would never let the poor Colonel out of her sight? The poor Colonel! after his wife, he had been henpecked by his little daughter. And she would take after her mother and marry again, be sure of that.

Madam, Mountain would say, was a little chit of a woman, not five feet in her highest headdress and shoes, and Mr. Washington a great tall man of six feet two. Great tall men always married little chits of women: therefore Mr. W. must be looking after the widow. What could be clearer?

She communicated these sage opinions to her boy—George—who begged her, for Heaven’s sake, to hold her tongue.

“This I can do,” Mountain said. “But I cannot keep my eyes always shut.” And she narrated a hundred circumstances from the young gentleman’s absence, which, to her mind, proved all.

Had Mountain imparted these suspicions to his brother? George asked sternly.

No. George was her boy; Harry was his mother’s boy. “She likes him best, and I like you best, George,” cries Mountain. “Besides, if I were to speak to him, he would tell your mother in a minute. Poor Harry can keep nothing quiet, and then there would be a pretty quarrel between Madam and me!”

“I beg you to keep this quiet, Mountain,” said Mr. George with great dignity; “or you and I shall quarrel too. Neither to me nor to any one else in the world must you mention such an absurd suspicion.”

Absurd! Why absurd? Mr. Washington was constantly with the widow. His name was forever in her mouth. She was never tired of pointing out his virtues and example to her sons. She consulted him on every question respecting her estate and its management. She never bought a horse or sold a barrel of tobacco without his opinion. There was a room at Castlewood regularly called Mr. Washington’s room.

“He actually leaves his clothes here and his portmanteau when he goes away. Ah, George, George! One day will come when he won’t go away,” groaned Mountain—who, of course, always returned to the subject of which she was forbidden to speak.

Meanwhile Mr. George adopted towards his mother’s favourite a frigid courtesy, at which the honest gentleman chafed but did not care to remonstrate; or a stinging sarcasm, which Washington would break through as he would burst through brambles on those hunting excursions where he and Harry Warrington rode so constantly together—hoofs drumming, dogs yelping, men shouting through the woods, a racket that shook the quiet. George, retreating to his tents, read mathematics and French and Latin, and sulked in his book-room more and more lonely, as if language itself were safer when it lay dead upon the page.

Harry was away from home with some sporting friends—his acquaintances, it is to be feared, not all as eligible as Mr. Washington—when the latter came to pay a visit at Castlewood. He was so peculiarly tender and kind to the mistress there, and received by her with such special cordiality, that George’s jealousy had well-nigh broken out in open rupture.

But the visit, it appeared, was one of adieu. Major Washington was going on a long and dangerous journey, quite to the western Virginia frontier and beyond it. The French had for some time been making inroads upon our territory. The government at home, as well as those of Virginia and Pennsylvania, were alarmed at this aggressive spirit of the Lords of Canada and Louisiana. Some settlers had already been driven from their holdings by Frenchmen in arms, and the governors of the British provinces were desirous to stop the incursions, or at least to protest against the invasion.

We chose to hold our colonies by a law convenient to its framers: that whoever possessed the coast had a right to all the territory inland as far as the Pacific; the charters laying down limits from north to south and leaving them free from east to west. The French, meanwhile, had their colonies to the north and south, and aimed at connecting them by the Mississippi and the St. Lawrence and the great intermediate lakes and waters westward of the British possessions. In 1748, though peace was signed in Europe, the colonial question remained unsettled—like a pot set off the fire too soon, the contents still seething where you cannot see them—until either party should be strong enough to urge it. In 1753 it came to an issue on the Ohio, where British and French settlers met.

There existed, to be sure, other people besides French and British who thought they had title to the soil—namely the native Indians. But the logicians of St. James’s and Versailles wisely chose to consider it a European question, eliminating the Red-man from argument, while employing his tomahawk as it served either litigant. The Ohio Company, having grants from Virginia of lands along that river, found themselves invaded by French military detachments, who roughly ejected the Britons. These applied to Mr. Dinwiddie, Lieutenant-Governor of Virginia, who determined upon sending an ambassador to the French commanding officer on the Ohio, demanding that the French should desist from their inroads upon his Majesty King George’s territories.

Young Mr. Washington jumped eagerly at the chance of distinction. He volunteered to leave home and his rural pursuits to carry the governor’s message. With a guide, an interpreter, and a few attendants, and following Indian tracks, in the fall of 1753 he made his way from Williamsburg almost to the shores of Lake Erie and found the French commander at Fort le Boeuf. The reply was brief: his orders were to hold the place and drive all the English from it. The French avowed their intention of taking possession of the Ohio. With this rough answer the messenger had to return through danger and difficulty—across lonely forest and frozen river, shaping his course by compass, camping at night in the snow by forest fires whose crackle was the only friendly speech.

Harry Warrington cursed his ill-fortune that he had been absent from home at a cock-fight when he might have had sport so much nobler; and on Major Washington’s return—after an expedition conducted with heroic energy and simplicity—the Major was a greater favourite than ever with the lady of Castlewood. She pointed him out as a model to both her sons.

“Ah, Harry!” she would say, “think of you with your cock-fighting and your racing-matches, and the Major away there in the wilderness, watching the French, and battling with the frozen rivers! Ah, George! learning may be a very good thing, but I wish my eldest son were doing something in the service of his country!”

“I desire no better than to go home and seek for employment, ma’am,” says George. “You surely will not have me serve under Mr. Washington, in his new regiment, or ask a commission from Mr. Dinwiddie?”

“An Esmond can only serve with the king’s commission,” says Madam. “And as for asking a favour from Mr. Lieutenant-Governor Dinwiddie, I would rather beg my bread.”

Mr. Washington at this time was raising such a regiment as, with the scanty pay and patronage of the Virginian government, he could gather, and proposed with these men-of-war—so the provincials called themselves in brave irony—to lay a more peremptory veto on French invaders than the solitary ambassador could. A small force under another officer, Colonel Trent, had already been despatched westward with orders to fortify themselves. The French troops, greatly outnumbering ours, came up with the English outposts at a place on the confines of Pennsylvania where the great city of Pittsburg now stands. A Virginian officer with forty men could not resist twenty times that number. He was suffered to draw back without molestation; and the French, taking possession of his fort, strengthened it and christened it by the name of the Canadian governor, Du Quesne.

Up to this time no actual blow of war had been struck. The troops were in presence—the guns loaded, but no one had yet cried “Fire.” It was strange that in a savage forest of Pennsylvania a young Virginian officer should fire a shot and wake a war to last for sixty years; to cover his own country and pass into Europe; to cost France her American colonies; to sever ours from us and create the great Western republic; to rage over the Old World when extinguished in the New; and of all the myriads engaged, to leave the prize of the greatest fame with him who struck the first blow. He little knew of the fate in store for him. A simple gentleman, anxious to serve his king and do his duty, he volunteered for the first service and executed it with admirable fidelity.

In the ensuing year he took command of the small body of provincial troops with which he marched to repel the French. He came up with their advanced guard and fired upon them, killing their leader. After this he had himself to fall back, and was compelled to capitulate to the superior French force. On the 4th of July, 1754, the Colonel marched out with his troops from the little fort where he had hastily entrenched himself—Fort Necessity—gave up the place to the conqueror, and took his way home. His command was over; his regiment disbanded after the fruitless, inglorious march and defeat.

Saddened and humbled, the young officer presented himself after a while to his old friends at Castlewood. There were no trumpets now in his step—only the quiet sound of boots on boards, the murmur of servants carrying dishes in and out, the house continuing in its domestic rites as if history were but another guest to be fed.

He was very young, and before he set forth on his first campaign he may have indulged in exaggerated hopes and spoken them. “I was angry when I parted from you,” he said to George Warrington, holding out his hand, which the other eagerly took. “You seemed to scorn me and my regiment, George. I thought you laughed at us, and your ridicule made me angry. I boasted too much of what we would do.”

“Nay, you have done your best, George,” said the other, who quite forgot his previous jealousy in his old comrade’s misfortune. “Everybody knows that a hundred and fifty starving men, with scarce a round of ammunition left, could not face five times their number perfectly armed; and everybody who knows Mr. Washington knows that he would do his duty. Harry and I saw the French in Canada last year. They obey but one will: in our provinces each governor has his own. They were royal troops the French sent against you—”

“Oh, but that some of ours were here!” cries Madam Esmond, tossing her head. “I promise you a few good English regiments would make the white-coats run.”

“You think nothing of the provincials; and I must say nothing now we have been so unlucky,” said the Colonel gloomily. “You made much of me when I was here before. Don’t you remember what victories you prophesied for me—how much I boasted myself very likely over your good wine? All those fine dreams are over now. ’Tis kind of your ladyship to receive a poor beaten fellow as you do.” And the young soldier hung down his head.

George Warrington, with his acute sensibility, was touched at the other’s emotion—at sorrow spoken plainly, without ornament. He was about to answer with something more friendly still, had not his mother, to whom the Colonel had been speaking, replied herself.

“Kind of us to receive you, Colonel Washington!” said the widow. “I never heard that when men were unhappy, our sex were less their friends.”

And she made the Colonel a very fine curtsey—so fine, so full of that old-fashioned grace which could seem like generosity and command in one motion—that straightway her son, who had been softened for a moment, felt the old jealousy leap up again, bitter as burned sugar on the tongue.
