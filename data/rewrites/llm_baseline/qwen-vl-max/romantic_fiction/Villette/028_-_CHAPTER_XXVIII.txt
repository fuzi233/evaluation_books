M. Paul Emanuel had a sharp sensitivity to interruptions during his lessons—any disturbance, no matter how small, was met with a fury that made the very air tremble. To enter his classroom under such circumstances was considered by both teachers and students alike as perilous as stepping into a storm unprepared. Even Madame Beck, who usually moved with calm authority, would rush through the room like a startled bird, her skirt pulled tight, skirting the edge of the raised platform as if avoiding jagged rocks. As for Rosine, the gatekeeper, whose duty it was every half-hour to fetch pupils from their studies for music lessons in the oratory or saloon, she often became so flustered on her second or third attempt that she could barely speak—her face pale, her breath caught in her throat, all from the terrifying glare behind M. Paul’s spectacles.

One morning, I sat in the carré, stitching on an embroidery begun by one of the girls but left unfinished. My fingers worked steadily, while my ears listened to the rising cadence of a voice in the neighboring classroom—harsh, insistent, growing more agitated by the moment. A solid wall separated us, and just beyond me stood a glass door leading to the courtyard—a quick escape route, should the storm break this way. But instead of fear, I found myself amused by the mounting tension.

Poor Rosine was not spared. That morning alone, she had already crossed the forbidden threshold four times. Now, for the fifth time, she faced the daunting task of retrieving a pupil directly from under M. Paul’s watchful eye.

“Mon Dieu! Mon Dieu!” she gasped. “What shall I do? Monsieur will kill me—I’m certain of it! He’s furious!”

With trembling courage, she opened the door.

“Mademoiselle La Malle au piano!” she called out.

Before she could retreat or fully close the door, a voice thundered:

“From this moment—this class is forbidden. The first person who opens this door or enters this room will be hanged—be it Madame Beck herself!”

Ten minutes later, Rosine’s soft slippers scuffed again along the corridor.

“Mademoiselle,” she whispered, “I wouldn longer go back in there for five francs. Monsieur’s glasses—they’re truly dreadful. And here’s a messenger with a message from the Athénée. I told Madame Beck I couldn’t deliver it, and she said you must take it.”

“Me?” I replied. “That’s rather unfair! It’s not my job. Come on, Rosine—be brave. Take it yourself.”

“I? Impossible! I’ve crossed him five times today. Madame must hire a gendarme. Ouf! I can’t bear it anymore!”

“Bah! You’re just afraid. What does the message say?”

“Exactly the kind he hates most—an urgent summons to go immediately to the Athénée. There’s an official visitor—an inspector—someone important—and Monsieur _must_ go. You know how he feels about ‘must.’”

Yes, I knew well. The little man detested constraints, despised obligation. Anything forced upon him provoked rebellion. Still, I accepted the task—not without dread, but with a mix of fear and curiosity. I opened the door, stepped inside, and shut it quickly behind me, my hand unsteady. To move slowly, to rattle a latch, or leave a door ajar—these were sins worse than the act itself.

There I stood. He sat at his desk, bent over his work. His mood was clearly foul—perhaps at its worst. He’d been teaching arithmetic, a subject he disliked, and which always soured his temper. Not a student dared breathe too loudly when he mentioned numbers. He didn’t lift his head at my entrance. To look up at a breach of his rule was an effort he couldn’t bring himself to make—thankfully, because it gave me time to walk down the long room. I preferred facing his anger close up rather than feeling it from afar.

At the front of the class, I paused. Naturally, I wasn’t worthy of immediate attention. He continued his lesson. But silence wouldn’t suffice. He had to hear me. He had to answer.

Too short to see over the elevated desk, I peeked around its edge, hoping only for a better view of his face. When I entered, it had struck me as oddly resembling a sallow, dark tiger. Twice I glimpsed him—unnoticed. The third time, my eye barely cleared the desk’s shadow when it was seized—pierced—by his spectacles. Rosine was right: those lenses held a cold, unyielding terror, deeper than the fire in his own eyes.

Now, proximity worked in my favor. His nearsightedness rendered the glasses useless for spotting someone right before him. So he removed them. Suddenly, we stood on equal ground.

I was glad I wasn’t truly afraid—because standing there, I felt nothing but calm. When he demanded rope and gallows for the sentence he’d just declared, I offered him a needle full of embroidery thread with such quiet grace that it must have softened some of his rage. I didn’t show off the gesture. I simply passed the thread around the desk and tied it neatly to the back of his chair, ready for hanging.

“What do you want?” he growled, his voice low and grating, teeth clenched, as if swearing silently that no force on earth would draw a smile from him.

I began without hesitation: “Monsieur,” I said, “I ask for the impossible, for unheard-of things.” Then, deciding honesty was best, I delivered the message in a swift, quiet voice, exaggerating its urgency.

He refused to listen. “I won’t go. I won’t leave this class, not even if every official in Villette summoned me. I won’t move an inch for king, cabinet, or chamber.”

I knew he *had* to go. Duty and reason both demanded it. So I waited in silence, as if he hadn’t spoken. He asked what else I wanted.

“Only your reply to give the messenger.”

He waved a dismissive hand.

Then I reached toward the bonnet-grec resting on the windowsill. He followed my movement with narrowed eyes—no doubt amazed and pitying my boldness.

“Ah!” he muttered. “If it came to that—if Miss Lucy touched my bonnet-grec—she might as well wear it herself, become a boy for the night, and kindly go to the Athénée in my place.”

With respect, I placed the bonnet on his desk. Its tassel seemed to nod at me ominously.

“I’ll write a note of apology—that will suffice,” he insisted, still resisting.

Knowing it wouldn’t, I gently pushed the bonnet toward his hand. It slid across the polished surface, carrying with it the delicate steel-framed spectacles. And then—fearfully—the glasses fell to the platform below.

I’d seen them fall before—always unharmed. This time, however, they shattered. Each lens became a broken star.

Dismay gripped me—dismay and regret. I knew how much those glasses meant. M. Paul’s vision was rare, hard to correct; these were perfect for him. I’d heard him call them his treasures. As I picked them up, cracked and useless, my hands shook. I feared what I’d done—but I was even more sorry than afraid.

For a few moments, I couldn’t meet his gaze. He spoke first.

“There!” he said. “I am now widowed of my spectacles! I suppose Mademoiselle Lucy will admit the rope and gallows are fully earned. You tremble at your fate. Ah, traitress! traitress! You mean to leave me blind and helpless in your hands!”

I lifted my eyes. His face wasn’t angry. It wasn’t even sad. Instead, it bloomed with the same gentle smile I’d seen that evening at the Hotel Crécy—warm, alive, forgiving. He wasn’t furious. He wasn’t even hurt. In fact, he showed mercy where others would have punished. This disaster, which I thought had ruined my chance to persuade him, became my greatest ally. Difficult to manage when unharmed, he became tenderly pliant once I stood before him as a guilty, remorseful offender.

Still teasing me—calling me “a strong woman,” “a terrible Englishwoman,” “a little destroyer”—he admitted he could not refuse one who had shown such dangerous power. It was like the great Emperor smashing a vase to strike awe. Finally, he crowned himself with the bonnet-grec, took the broken spectacles from my hand with a warm, forgiving clasp, bowed, and left for the Athénée—cheerful, spirited, and in high spirits.

Yet despite all this kindness, I found myself quarreling with M. Paul again by nightfall. And though I wished it otherwise, I couldn’t help it.

It was his custom—pleasant and welcome—to arrive unexpectedly in the evenings, bursting into our quiet study hour. Without warning, he’d impose his presence, commandeer our activities, send books aside, pull out workbags, and with a single volume or handful of pamphlets, replace the dull religious readings with something grand—some passionate tragedy read with fire and soul. Or he’d illuminate our dim world with tales of Parisian wit, reading passages from enchanting novels or witty feuilletons, always censoring anything unsuitable for young girls. Where deletion left gaps, he would improvise—filling in dialogue, description—often creating something stronger than the original.

That evening, we sat in silence, the pupils studying, the teachers working. I was embroidering—something light, yet meaningful. It was meant as a gift, and the occasion was near, so I worked quickly, focused.

We heard the sharp bell ring—the familiar sound. Then his footsteps, swift and sure. “Voilà Monsieur!” everyone murmured at once. The double doors flew open as they always did for him, and he stood among us.

Two long tables, each lit by a central lamp, flanked by benches. Teachers sat beneath the lamps, students arranged by age and diligence—serious ones near the light, the idle ones toward the edges. M. Paul’s habit was to offer a chair to Zélie St. Pierre, the senior mistress, then take her seat, using the bright light needed by his nearsightedness.

As usual, Zélie rose with a wide, toothy smile—one that stretched from ear to ear but never warmed the eyes. But M. Paul ignored her. Perhaps he saw her, perhaps not—he was capricious, like women are said to be. Or maybe his new spectacles excused his oversight. Whatever the reason, he passed her by, came to the other side of the table, and before I could rise, whispered, “Don’t move,” and settled between me and Miss Fanshawe—my ever-present neighbor, whose elbow always pressed into mine, no matter how many times I said, “Ginevra, I wish you were at Jericho.”

Easy enough to say “Don’t move,” but how could I? I had to clear space for him, ask the girls to shift so I could retreat. Ginevra could cling to me, “keeping warm” on winter nights, poking and fidgeting until I pinned a needle to my belt for protection—but M. Paul was not to endure such treatment. So I swept away my materials, made room for his book, and withdrew—leaving just a yard of bench, a reasonable distance, I thought.

But M. Paul was never reasonable. Like flint and tinder, he sparked instantly.

“You don’t want me for a neighbor,” he growled. “You act like a noblewoman. You treat me like a pariah.” He scowled. “Very well—I’ll fix this.” And he began.

“Rise, all of you, Mesdemoiselles!” he commanded.

They rose. He sent them all to the other table. Then he placed me at one end of the long bench, carefully bringing my basket, silk, scissors, everything I needed. He seated himself at the opposite end.

The absurdity of it stunned us all. Not a soul dared laugh. To giggle would have been fatal. I took it calmly. I sat isolated, cut off from conversation, and returned to my work, quiet and content.

“Is this far enough?” he demanded.

“You are the judge, Monsieur.”

“You well know it isn’t. You created this vast emptiness. I did nothing.”

And with that, he began to read.

He chose a French translation of what he called “a drama by Williams Shackspire—the false god,” he added, “of those foolish pagans, the English.” How differently he might have described him, had his temper been calmer, I need not say.

The translation was poor, and I couldn’t hide my disdain for its clumsy phrases. Not that I spoke—only looked. But his spectacles caught every glance. He noticed them all. Soon, he discarded the lenses entirely, letting his eyes burn freely. And at the northern pole of the room, where he’d exiled himself, he grew hotter than any southern sun.

When the reading ended, we waited—uncertain whether he’d leave in silence or vent his anger. Suppression wasn’t in his nature, but what had I done? I hadn’t spoken. Could I be blamed for moving my eyes?

Supper arrived—bread, milk diluted with tepid water. Out of respect, the rolls and glasses remained untouched.

“Take your supper, ladies,” he said, busy writing notes in the margin of his “Williams Shackspire.”

They took theirs. I took mine—roll and glass—but stayed seated, continuing my work as I ate, calm and composed, almost serene. It felt as if his restless, fiery presence drew all agitation from me like a magnet, leaving only peace.

He rose. Would he leave without another word? He turned toward the door.

No—he turned back. Perhaps to retrieve his pencil case, left on the table.

He took it, snapped the pencil against the wood, broke the point, re-sharpened it, pocketed it, and walked straight to me.

The others chattered around the other table—loudly, as usual. They never lowered their voices at meals.

M. Paul stood behind me. He asked what I was making.

“A watchguard,” I said.

“For whom?”

“For a gentleman—one of my friends.”

He leaned down and, in a whisper—true to form—his voice hissed into my ear.

Of all the women he knew, he said, I was the one who could make herself most unpleasant. With me, friendship was impossible. I had an “unbearable character,” perverse beyond belief. How I managed it, he didn’t know. But whatever goodwill anyone brought to me—crac!—it turned to discord, kindness to hostility. He, M. Paul, meant me no harm. He claimed the right to be a neutral acquaintance, free of malice. Yet how I treated him! With biting sarcasm, rebellious energy, unjust fury!

I couldn’t help widening my eyes slightly. “Sarcasm? Energy? Fury? I didn’t know…”

“Shh! Right now! There! There I go—alive as gunpowder!” He was sorry—for my sake. This impetuosity, this passion—generous, perhaps, but excessive—would ruin me one day. It was a pity. I wasn’t, he believed, wholly without good qualities. If only I’d listen, be calmer, less flighty, less vain, less obsessed with appearances, less captivated by men praised for their height, “doll-like colors,” “moderately good noses,” and immense stupidity—I might yet become useful, even admirable. But as it was…

His voice faltered.

I wanted to look up, to reach out, to soothe him. But I feared I’d either laugh or cry—such a strange blend of tenderness and absurdity.

I thought he was finished. But no. He sat down to continue.

While he, M. Paul, spoke of such painful truths, he would dare my anger for my own good. He’d mention a change in my dress. He confessed that when he first saw me—or rather, caught glimpses of me—he found my appearance satisfactory. The gravity, the plain simplicity, inspired hope. But what cursed influence had led me to add flowers to my bonnet, wear “embroidered collars,” and even appear in a _scarlet gown_? He might guess, but he wouldn’t say.

I interrupted—this time with indignation and horror.

“Scarlet, Monsieur Paul? It wasn’t scarlet! It was pink—pale pink—softened with black lace!”

“Pink or scarlet, yellow or crimson, green or blue—it makes no difference. All are flashy, reckless colors. And that lace? That’s just another ‘trinket.’” He sighed over my decline. “I cannot, I’m sorry to say, be precise on this matter. I lack the names of these trinkets—I might make small errors, inviting your mockery and provoking your sudden, passionate temper. But generally speaking—and I know I’m right—your costume has lately taken on ‘worldly airs,’ which wound me deeply.”

What “worldly airs” he saw in my plain winter merino and white collar, I couldn’t imagine. When I asked, he said it was too concerned with effect. And then—“Do you not have a ribbon bow at your neck?”

“And if you disapprove of a ribbon bow for a lady, Monsieur, would you also reject something like this for a gentleman?”—holding up my bright silk-and-gold chainlet.

His only answer was a groan—over my levity, I suppose.

After a few silent minutes, watching me stitch more diligently than ever, he asked: “Will what I’ve just said make you hate me completely?”

And I, after a pause, answered quietly:
“Perhaps not. But it certainly makes me understand you better.”
