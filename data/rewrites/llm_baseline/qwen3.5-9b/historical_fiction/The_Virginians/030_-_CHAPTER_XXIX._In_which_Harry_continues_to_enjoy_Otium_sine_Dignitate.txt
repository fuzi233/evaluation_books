**Chapter XXIX: In Which Harry Continues to Enjoy *Otium Sine Dignitate***

While there were plenty of card players eager to meet her at her lodgings and the assembly rooms, Madame de Bernstein chose to remain comfortably at the Wells, busy scolding her niece and engaging in rounds of rubber bridge. At Harry's age, nearly every location offers delight if it provides lively company, fresh air, and ample diversion. Indeed, all pleasures seem exquisite at twenty. One rushes forth to encounter them with alacrity, speculates eagerly on their arrival, and counts down the days until the rendezvous. Yet, how gently and coolly we view these joys toward the twilight of life's long season! Do you not recall your first ball, Madam? Does your memory not occasionally drift to that golden past while you sit adorning the walls as your daughters dance? As for myself, I can still remember thinking it delightful to walk three miles and return through the countryside merely to dine with old Captain Jones. Can you imagine enjoying such a trek today just to share a meal with Jones and sip his half-pay port? Undoubtedly, it was purchased from a local village merchant for a modest price; however, it was served with a warm welcome, and youth imbued it with a flavor that neither aged wine nor matured men can replicate now. *Viximus nuper*—we have recently existed.

I am not inclined to judge young Harry's conduct and idleness as harshly as his friend, the stern Colonel of the Twentieth Regiment. Oh, blessed idleness! Divine lazy nymph! Fetch me a novel while I lounge in my dressing gown at three in the afternoon; mix a sherry cobbler for me, and bring a cigar! You, dear slatternly, smiling enchantress! They may assail you with vile names, swear away your reputation, and label you the Mother of Evil; yet, despite this, you remain the finest company in the world!

Lord March departed for the North, and Lord Chesterfield, discovering that the Tunbridge waters failed to cure his deafness, returned to his solitude at Blackheath. However, other gentlemen remained to sport and indulge, and Mr. Warrington found sufficient companionship at his regular gathering at the White Horse. Soon, he mastered the art of ordering a French dinner as well as any fashionable gentleman from St. James's. He conversed with Monsieur Barbeau in his native tongue far more fluently than most others, cultivated an elegant and discerning palate for wines, and could distinguish between Clos Vougeot and Romande with remarkable expertise. He became the young king of the Wells, where the frequenters were easygoing men of the world who were hardly shocked by Harry's growing reputation for gallantry and extravagance—a reputation that had terrified Mr. Wolfe.

Though our Virginian lived amidst the revellers, swimming and sporting in the same waters as the "loose fish," the boy possessed a natural shrewdness and honesty that kept him clear of the snares and baits typically set for the unwary. He made very few foolish bets with the jolly idle fellows around him, and the oldest hands found it difficult to deceive him. He engaged in games indoors and out due to his innate skill and aptitude, proving capable of holding his own against almost any fair competitor. He was scrupulous, playing only with gentlemen he knew and always settling his debts immediately. While he might have fared poorly in a college examination, he possessed prudence, fidelity, keen perception, great generosity, and dauntless personal courage. There were indeed occasions to demonstrate the quality of his character.

For instance, when the unfortunate little Cattarina, who had caused him so much trouble, pushed her demands beyond the limit Harry believed his generosity should reach, he withdrew from the advances of the Opera-House siren with perfect coolness and skill, leaving her to attempt her blandishments on a more susceptible victim. In vain did the mermaid's hysterical mother wait upon Harry, vowing that a cruel bailiff had seized all her daughter's goods for debt and that her venerable father was currently languishing in a London gaol. Harry declared that between himself and the bailiff there could be no dealings. Since he had the good fortune to know Mademoiselle Cattarina and had gratified her caprices by presenting her with various trinkets and knick-knacks she fancied, he was not bound to pay her family's past debts. Furthermore, he refused to act as bail for her father in London or settle her outstanding accounts at Tunbridge.

First, the Cattarina's mother called him a monster and an ingrate; then, with a veteran smirk, she asked why he did not accept payment for the services he had rendered to the young person. Initially, Mr. Warrington could not comprehend the nature of such payment. But once the old woman clarified the matter, the honest lad rose in horror at the thought of a woman trafficking in her child's dishonor. He told her he hailed from a country where even the savages would recoil from such a bargain. Having bowed the old lady ceremoniously to the door, he ordered Gumbo to note her well and never admit her to his lodgings again. No doubt she retired breathing vengeance against the Iroquois; she declared that no Turk or Persian would treat a lady thus. She and her daughter retreated to London as soon as their anxious landlord permitted them.

Then came Harry's perils of gaming, alongside his perils of gallantry. A man who plays at bowls, as the phrase goes, must expect to encounter rubbers. After dinner at the ordinary, having declined to play piquet further with Captain Batts, and being roughly questioned for his refusal, Harry frankly told the Captain that he only played with gentlemen who paid, like himself. Yet, he expressed such readiness to settle Mr. Batts' outstanding account that the Captain declared himself satisfied *d'avance*, straightway leaving the Wells without paying Harry or any other creditor.

He also had an occasion to display his spirit by beating a chairman who had been rude to old Miss Whiffler one evening as she headed to the assembly. Moreover, finding that the calumny regarding himself and that unlucky opera dancer was repeated by Mr. Hector Buckler—one of the fiercest frequenters of the Wells—Mr. Warrington approached Buckler in the pump room. There, the latter was regaling a group of water drinkers with the very slander. Mr. Warrington publicly informed Buckler that the story was false and warned him that he would hold anyone accountable who uttered it again. Thus, though our friend, being at Rome, certainly did as Rome did, he proved himself a valorous and worthy Roman. By hurling with the wolves, he was acknowledged by Mr. Wolfe himself to be as brave as the best of them.

If that officer had revealed the stories that had caused Colonel Lambert so much pain, we may be certain that when Mr. Wolfe discovered his young friend's innocence, he took the first opportunity to withdraw the odious charges. Joy rippled through the Lamberts upon the lad's acquittal—something akin to the pleasure higher natures feel at the recovery of sinners. Never had the little family been happier—not even when they received news of Brother Tom winning his scholarship—as when Colonel Wolfe rode over with the account of his conversation with Harry Warrington.

"Hadst thou brought me a regiment, James, I think I should not have been better pleased," said Mr. Lambert. Mrs. Lambert called to her daughters in the garden, kissed them both upon their entry, and shouted the good news to them. Hetty leaped for joy, and Theo performed some unusually brilliant operations on the harpsichord that night. When Dr. Boyle arrived for his game of backgammon, he could not initially account for the illumination on all their faces until the three ladies, in a happy chorus, told him how right he had been in his sermon and how dreadfully they had wronged that poor, dear, good young Mr. Warrington.

"What shall we do, my dear?" asked the Colonel of his wife. "The hay is in, the corn won't be cut for a fortnight—the horses have nothing to do. Suppose we..." Here, he leaned over the table and whispered in her ear.

"My dearest Martin! The very thing!" cried Mrs. Lambert, taking her husband's hand and pressing it.

"What's the very thing, mother?" cried young Charley, who was home for his Bartholomew tide holidays.

"The very thing is to go to supper. Come, Doctor! We will have a bottle of wine tonight and drink repentance to all who think evil."

"Amen," said the Doctor, "with all my heart!" And with this, the worthy family proceeded to their supper.
