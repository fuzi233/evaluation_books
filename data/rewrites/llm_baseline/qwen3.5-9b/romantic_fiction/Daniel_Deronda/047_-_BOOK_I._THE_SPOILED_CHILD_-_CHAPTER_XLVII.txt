One might almost envy Daniel Deronda the simple delight of procuring new clothes for Mordecai. As he imagined the effect of fine gray flannel shirts and a dressing gown resembling a Franciscan's brown frock upon Mordecai's lean frame, he felt a surge of creative pleasure, as if he were sketching a portrait. Much of this satisfaction stemmed from seeing his friend's brother through Mirah's eyes, shielding her from any jarring impressions by presenting a dignified image. Yet, even after preparing everything, a shadow of doubt crept in. Was he perhaps misreading her nature? Did he mistake the superficial for the profound? Could Mirah not appreciate the striking contrast between her brother's newfound appearance and his history of poverty, just as Daniel himself had once admired such distinctions?

However, practical concerns intervened. There were the Meyrick family to consider, specifically their potential prejudice against this "too Judaic" brother. Daniel realized he was driven to hide anything that might trigger immediate dislike in minds untouched by the sacred vision of true kinship—the ability to bathe every object in the solemn glow of loving reverence. This impulse would have been stronger had he listened to the conversation around Mrs. Meyrick's hearth that evening, after Mirah retired.

Hans, now settled in his Chelsea lodgings, had stayed late. Mrs. Meyrick, stoking the fire into a blaze, called out, "Now, Kate, extinguish your candle. Let us all gather cozy by the fire. Hans, dearest, cease laughing at those poems for the ninety-ninth time and join us. I have something wonderful to announce."

"As if I didn't know that, ma," Kate retorted, watching the girls step forward to warm their feet on the fender. Hans pushed his chair close, straddling it with his fists and chin resting on the backrest. "I've noticed it in the corner of your eye for ages, along with your feigned errands."

"Well, since you're so perceptive," Mrs. Meyrick declared clearly, "perhaps you know that Mirah's brother has been found!"

"Oh, confound it!" Hans exclaimed instantly.

"Hans, that is wicked," Mab protested. "What if we had lost *you*?"

"I cannot help feeling rather sorry," Kate admitted. "And where is her mother?"

"She is dead," Hans replied flatly.

"I hope the brother isn't a bad man," Amy ventured timidly.

"Nor a fellow covered in smiles and jewelry—a 'Crystal Palace Assyrian' with a hat on," Hans sneered, in the worst possible mood. "Are there such unfeeling children?" Mrs. Meyrick challenged, emboldened by the need for opposition. "Do you suppose Mirah cares the slightest bit about her brother's joy in this reunion?"

"You know, ma, Mirah hardly remembers her brother," Kate explained.

"People lost for twelve years should never return," Hans insisted. "They are always in the way."

"Hans!" Mrs. Meyrick reproached sharply. "If you had been lost for *twenty* years, I would have thought—"

"I said twelve years," Hans interrupted. "Anywhere near twelve years is the exact duration lost relatives should keep out of sight."

"But it is nice to find people; there is something to tell," Mab said, clasping her knees. "Did Prince Camaralzaman find him?"

Mrs. Meyrick proceeded to recount everything she knew in her neat, narrative style, uninterrupted. "Mr. Deronda holds the highest admiration for him," she concluded. "He seems to look right up to him. And he insists that Mirah is precisely the sister needed to understand this brother."

"Deronda is becoming perfectly preposterous about those Jews," Hans spat with disgust, rising and slamming his chair back. "He wants to do everything possible to encourage Mirah in her prejudices."

"Oh, for shame, Hans! To speak of Mr. Deronda that way," Mab chided. A subtle, unreadable tension flickered across Mrs. Meyrick's face.

"And now we shall never be all together," Hans continued, pacing with his hands deep in the pockets of his brown velveteen coat. "But we must invite this Prophet Elijah to tea, and Mirah will spend her days sitting amidst the ruins of Jerusalem. She'll be spoiled like an artist—mind that—and grow as narrow-minded as a nun. Everything will be ruined—our home and everything. I shall take to drinking."

"Oh, really, Hans," Kate snapped impatiently. "I truly think men are the most contemptible creatures in existence. Every single one demands everything go their way, or they become unbearable."

"Oh, oh, oh, it's dreadful!" cried Mab. "I feel as though ancient Nineveh has returned."

"I'd like to know what good comes of attending university and knowing everything if you remain so childish, Hans," Amy said. "You ought to accept the kindness Providence sends you. *We* shall have to endure him."

"I hope you will all enjoy the new *Lamentations of Jeremiah*—'to be continued in our next'—that's all," Hans said, grabbing his wide-awake hat. "There is no point in being one thing more than another if you must suffer the company of men with fixed ideas, staring blankly at you and expecting all your comments to serve merely as footnotes to their texts. If you're trapped under a petrifying wall, you might as well be an old boot. I don't feel like an old boot."

Abruptly, he leaned down to kiss his mother's brow in a hurried, desperate gesture, then offered a condescending nod to the girls on his way to the door. "Good night, little mother."

"Suppose Mirah knew how you are behaving," Kate whispered.

His reply was the sharp slam of the front door. Turning back to his mother, he added, "I *would* like to see Mirah when Mr. Deronda tells her. I know she will look so beautiful."

On second thought, however, Daniel Deronda had chosen to write a letter. Mrs. Meyrick received it the following morning, requesting that she reveal the news rather than waiting for Daniel's arrival. He omitted his true reason—that he wished to avoid reliving a narrative where he appeared central and benevolent—and instead suggested that he stay with Mordecai while Mrs. Meyrick brought Mirah for a visit. This arrangement would create a brief interval before the move Daniel anticipated Mirah would soon propose.

Secretly, Daniel felt a mixture of wonder and anxiety. How far would Mordecai, after years of solitary immersion in abstract ideas exacerbated by declining physical strength, allow himself to feel tenderness for his sister beyond mere religious duty? His affection for the Cohen family, particularly little Jacob, remained active and persistent; these had been part of his daily life for years. Yet, Deronda noted that Mordecai asked no new questions about Mirah, maintaining an unusual silence regarding all subjects and seemingly submitting passively to the impending changes in his personal life.

Mordecai obeyed willingly when dressed in the new clothes, but afterward, offering a faint smile to Daniel, he said, "I must keep my old garments nearby as a reminder."

When they sat waiting for Mirah, Mordecai spoke no word. He kept his eyelids lowered, yet his face and hands betrayed a restless agitation. Indeed, Mordecai was experiencing a peculiar nervous disturbance familiar only to those whose minds, long flowing strongly in one direction, are suddenly forced into a new or reopened channel. People susceptible to deep currents, whose strength has been absorbed by dormant biases, often dread interviews that imperiously revive the past with the same terror they feel facing a threatening illness. Joy may be present, but joy, too, can be terrifying.

Daniel caught the infection of this excitement. Upon hearing the doorbell, he rose to greet Mirah beforehand, unsure of exactly why he felt the urge. He was startled to find her wearing the hat and cloak in which he had first encountered her—the memorable cloak that had once been soaked and used as a winding sheet. She had descended stairs clad in this attire. When Mrs. Meyrick asked in a questioning tone, "You wish to go in that dress, dear?" Mirah replied, "My brother is poor, and I want to look as much like him as I can, else he may feel distant from me"—presuming she would meet him in his workman's garb.

Daniel could offer no comment, feeling a secret shame for his own fastidious preparations. They shook hands silently; Mirah looked pale and awe-struck. As Daniel held the door open for her, Mordecai had risen, his eyes turning eagerly toward the entrance. Mirah took only two or three steps before stopping. They stared at one another, motionless. It was less their physical presence that registered than a spiritual connection; they were meeting first in memory, a bond deeper than mere touch.

Mirah broke the silence first, standing where she was. "Ezra," she said, using the exact same tone she employed when describing her mother's call to him.

With a sudden movement, Mordecai stepped forward and placed his hand on her shoulders. He was a head taller, looking down at her tenderly. "That was our mother's voice," he said softly. "Do you remember her calling me?"

"Yes," Mirah answered, "and how you responded—'Mother!'—and I knew you loved her."
