One day—after many such days—he lay upon his rude pallet and let the groans go out of him as if they were his only breath. No prayer rose with them, no psalm kept time, no hope leaned on them. The cave was a mouth of black stone that seemed to swallow all sound and give back only his own misery, thickened and made dull.

Then, sudden as a spark struck from flint, a thought came clean through the smoke of his mind: what he had done for the poor and the wayfarer, he would do for himself. He had fed other men’s bodies to keep their souls from fainting; he would feed his own soul to keep it from dying.

He reached, not for a sword—he had none—but for the humbler iron that honest labour wields: his chisel. His hand closed on it as if on a handle of life. He set its edge against the soft stone of his wall and began to work with a fury that was half craft and half combat. He drew no guiding lines; he did not pause to measure; he went straight to memory, to the storehouse of words that had once been the common bread of his days, and he drove them into the stone as if engraving them could nail them inside his heart.

He begged from any who came near—shepherds, women with baskets, children shy as hares—for candle-ends and rancid oil. “Anything is good enough for me,” he would say, with the plain humility of a man who has ceased to bargain with comfort, “if ’twill but burn.” By night the little cell shone through its window and the gaping seams of the door like a smithy at work; and the rustics, hearing the steady chip and seeing that unnatural glow, crossed themselves and whispered of devilry. Yet within, the only magic was the old Church’s spell of syllables, cut into rock with aching wrists while the body starved and the mind wrestled.

Kyrie Eleison. Christe Eleison. Sursum Corda. Deus Refugium nostrum et virtus. Agnus Dei, qui tollis peccata mundi, miserere mihi. Sancta Trinitas unus Deus, miserere nobis. Ab infestationibus Daemonum, a ventura ira, a damnatione perpetua. Libera nos Domine. And the long collect—Deus, qui miro ordine Angelorum ministeria—spilled from his recollection as naturally as water from a jar once filled.

Beneath the great crucifix, fixed against the stone like a judgment and a refuge, he graved Augustine’s counsel, word by word, as one sets loaves in an oven, trusting the heat will do what hands cannot: O anima Christiana, respice vulnera patientis… Look, Christian soul, on the wounds, on the blood, on the price; weigh it in the balance of the mind until He is nailed to the heart who for thee was nailed to the cross—so that, remembering Christ’s Passion, nothing on earth remains too hard to bear with a level spirit.

The sweet sentences, set up about him like a fence against darkness, soothed him—little, very little—but the labour and watching ground him down. One morning, his limbs gave way as if the bones had been unpegged; he fell full length and sleep poured over him, deep and heavy, for hours.

When he woke, it was quietly; the first thing that met him was not fear, but a small sound, a thin cheep, like a needle of music pricking through wool. He opened his eyes.

Upon his breviary, laid on a low stool near his feet, perched a robin redbreast. The bird fluffed its feathers with one brisk pull, smoothed them with the next, cocked its head this way and that in an elaborate pretence of slyness, and looked as if it had invented caution and was proud of it.

Clement held his breath. He narrowed his eyes lest even the shine of them should startle this bright scrap of life away. The robin dropped to the floor and performed its little comedy of craft, then—pim, pim, pim—hopped in three stiff jerks like a ball of worsted dancing on wires, and landed upon the hermit’s bare foot.

He shut his eyes altogether then; he was content to feel, scarcely feel, the minute claw—feel it only because he knew it was there. After a moment the busybody flicked its wings and was back upon the breviary.

Clement, who had fed many mouths in better days and knew how gentleness must be measured, resolved to offer this guest a crumb without frightening it. A piece of bread lay within reach. To reach it was a whole task: he crept his hand towards it inch by inch until his fingers touched the crust, and then he dared not move his arm. He pinched a morsel and shot it with thumb and finger as boys shoot marbles, keeping his hand as still as a stone.

The robin saw it fall near, did its pantomime of sagacity, and would not move. Another crumb followed, then another. At last the bird darted down, snatched one, and, not trusting the mystery, fled with it to a safer height—namely, the hermit’s knee. So the game went on, a quiet traffic between hunger and trust, until a larger fragment than usual rolled out and lay like a prize.

The robin pounced, lifted it, and fled so swiftly into the world that the cave rang with the beat of buffeted air.

“Now bless thee, sweet bird,” sighed the stricken solitary. “Thy wings are music, and thou, a feathered ray, camedst to light my darkened soul.”

The sound of that flight—so small, so certain—tuned him like a string. He turned back to his orisons and then to his tools with a morsel of courage, and this was the day’s carving:

Veni, Creator Spiritus,
Mentes tuorum visita,
Imple superna gratia
Quae tu creasti pectora…
and so onward, the prayer shaped like a chant, the chant like a hearth-fire’s steady breathing.

Thus the days rolled. The weather sharpened; the cold came with clear teeth. Yet, strangely, Clement’s heart warmed. Despondency, that had come upon him like a cloud that blots out all landmarks, began to thin. It had not been argued away; it was outlived. One morning he perceived it was gone, as a man notices the fever is broken because the room looks itself again.

Then all was reversed. The cell that had been a den seemed lit from within. His work pleased him; his prayers had unction; his psalms became praise instead of pleading. Birds—hosts of them—followed their crimson leader and, fleeing the snow and a parish full of Cains, made friends one by one with Abel, and then fast friends. The cave’s soundscape, once mere echo and groan, filled with the scratch of claws on stone, with quick chirrs and feather-rustlings—small domestic noises that were as homely as a pot simmering in a poor cottage, and as wholesome.

One keen frosty night, as he sang to his psaltery, the hollow cave flung back the holy psalmody upon the dark as if the stone itself were a great instrument’s shell. Then he heard, outside, a long clear whine—not unmelodious. It rose and fell, grew louder and less in tune. He peeped through the chinks of his rude door and saw a great red wolf sitting and moaning with its nose high in the air, as though the sky were a bowl and it drank the cold.

Clement’s heart leaped. “My sins are going,” he cried, “and the creatures of God are owning me, one after another.” In a burst of fervour he struck up: “Praise Him, all ye creatures of His! Let everything that hath breath praise the Lord.” And at intervals, between his lines, the wolf bayed—answering, it seemed, in the only liturgy it knew.

After this, he believed himself drawing nearer to that celestial intercourse which saints had promised and hermits sought. His dreams grew vivid, not like common dreams, but like visions. Bright figures, clad in woven snow, leaned over him; eyes lovelier than any antelope’s he had once seen at Rome looked down; broad wings hued like a rainbow fanned him; gentle voices bade him speed and endure. Sleep, which had been an abyss, became a chapel.

But he had not long enjoyed this felicity when the dreams changed, and their taste went strange.

He wandered again with Fra Colonna among the relics of antique nations. The friar was lame now and leaned upon a staff; and when he waved that staff over ruins—Egyptian, Greek, or Roman—temples and palaces rose at once from their wrecks like breath from warmed glass, complete and crowded with the famous dead. The air filled with music: songsters who seemed to eclipse Apollo himself poured out their lays; women, godlike and draped like Minerva, moved in voluptuous yet effortless dances through marble courts. Sculptors carved while pupils watched; and forms of supernatural beauty sprang from Parian marble in the time it takes to knead a loaf and set it near the coals. Philosophers, grave and subtle, conversed while youth listened reverently. It was like a whole world restored—and still, underneath that splendour, an unease lay coiled in Clement’s mind.

This is a prologue, he thought. There is something behind. Something that means me no good.

One night, when the wizard Colonna surpassed himself, he pointed with his stick, and many great ancient cities were swallowed up. They stood upon a vast sandy plain beneath a huge crimson sun sinking toward rest. Palm-trees stood in black shapes; and everywhere on the sand were low bulrush hives, scarcely a man’s height, dotted to the horizon.

“These are the anchorites of the Theban desert,” said Colonna calmly—“followers not of Christ and His apostles and the great fathers, but of the Greek pupils of the Egyptian pupils of the Brachmans and Gymnosophists.”

Clement burned to run and embrace those lonely men, to tell them his trouble and ask their counsel. Yet he found his feet held fast, tied by something he could not see. The crimson sun sank; dusk thickened; the hives faded. Colonna’s figure grew shadowy and shapeless, but his eyes shone tenfold brighter; and that thing—all eyes—spoke: “Nay, let them be. A pack of fools; I see how dismal it all is.”

Then, with sudden sprightliness: “But I hear one of them has a manuscript of Petronius, on papyrus. I go to buy it. Farewell for ever—for ever—for ever.”

It became pitch dark; and behind Clement there came a light, gentle as a hand laid warm upon the shoulder—a glorious roseate light that both brightened and heated. It loosened his feet. He turned.

There stood Margaret Brandt, her face irradiated with sunshine, her hair glittering like a saint’s gloriola. She blushed and smiled, and her look was tenderness itself, unbearable in its truth.

“Gerard,” she murmured, “be whose thou wilt by day, but at night be mine.”

The very shock of her presence awakened him. He lay trembling from head to foot, as if the dream had struck a tuning-fork in his nerves and left it humming.

Awake, he could pray, praise, and worship; he could marshal his thoughts and drive them where he willed. But let him close his eyes, and Margaret—or Satan wearing her likeness—came to beset him, a seeming angel of light. He might dream a thousand things, as wide apart as poles, yet before waking her image would blaze and extinguish all else in a moment, as the sun makes lesser stars vanish. She came with two beauties never joined in heaven: an angel’s radiance and a woman’s blush. Angels cannot blush; therefore he knew it was a fiend.

The discovery alarmed him; yet it did not astonish him as much as the artifice’s fineness. From Anthony to Nicholas of the Rock, what hermit had not been thus assaulted? Sometimes by gross voluptuousness, sometimes by tender phantoms warm and womanly without and demoniac within. The records of such wars were part of his meat and drink. With those records came also the victories, and the weapons.

Domandum est Corpus. The body must be tamed. For twelve hundred years that cry had rung through the caves and deserts. They had called even the heart’s earthly affections “body,” and crushed it through mortified flesh. Clement told himself the enemy had only withdrawn to strike more surely; had given him days of purity and joy to lull him, and now poured soft blandishments over the soul that had stood the buffeting of black wings.

So he set himself to tame the body. He shortened sleep, lengthened prayer, and pressed temperance into abstinence. Hitherto he had eaten only at sunset; now he ate but once in forty-eight hours, and drank a little water daily.

The visions grew more distinct.

Then he flew to the anchorites’ famous antidote, their grand febrifuge: cold water. A stream ran by his cell, rising not far away at a holy well. He found its deepest reach and cleared the bottom of stones, making a hole where he could stand with water to his chin. Fortified by the examples he had read till they seemed like family lore, he sprang from his rude bed upon the next diabolical assault and plunged in.

The icy water stole his breath. It made him gasp, almost shriek; it froze his marrow. “I shall die,” he cried through chattering teeth, “I shall die; but better this than fire eternal.”

Next day he was so stiff he could scarcely move. Every joint seemed a hinge ground with sand; he was one ache from crown to heel. Even in sleep he felt his bones like so many raging teeth—until the phantom he dreaded came, gave one pitying smile, and all pain was gone, as if her presence were a balm.

Then, fearing that to go again into the icy stream—enfeebled by fasting as he was—might carry the guilt of suicide, he scourged himself till blood ran; and he lay down smarting. When exhaustion dulled the sting to a throbbing, the present slipped away and the past came smiling back.

He sat with Margaret at the duke’s feast; minstrels played so divinely that their notes seemed to gild the air; purple fountains gushed. Youth and love reigned in every heart and perfumed the very breath. Then the scene shifted, and they stood at the altar together, man and wife. No interruption came this time. They wandered hand in hand, and told each other their horrible dreams. He confessed he had dreamed she was dead and he a monk—and the dream had been so vivid, so crowded with particulars, that only his waking sight could now persuade him it had been false, and that they were truly one.

Once that keynote was struck, every tune ran upon it. Awake he was Clement the hermit, risen from visions as dangerous as they were sweet; asleep he was Gerard Eliassoen, happy husband of the loveliest, best, and truest girl in Holland—happier still for having lately been tormented by hideous dreams in which he had lost her.

Austerity had reduced him nearly to a skeleton; yet still, on those aching bones, flesh seemed unsubdued, quivering with earthly passion—or why should ill spirits have such power? His fear was confirmed when one day he caught himself sinking toward sleep with a shameful complacency, because now Margaret would come and he should feel no more pain; the unreal would be real, and the real unreal, for an hour.

He sprang up with a cry of dismay. Stripping to the skin, he climbed to the brambles above his cave and flung himself upon them, rolling and writhing. The thorns bit like hooked knives. He came back a mass of gore and lay moaning for hours, until sheer exhaustion dropped him into deep, dreamless sleep.

He woke to bodily pain and mental exultation. He had broken the fatal spell. Another day passed, then another; her image molested him no more.

Yet he caught himself sighing at his victory.

The birds grew tamer and tamer. They perched upon his hand. Two of them even let him gild their little claws, holding still with the solemn patience of creatures that live by light and seed and do not invent torment. Eating but once in two days, he had more crumbs to give them; and the soft scratch of their feet on his palm was like a small domestic comfort, the way a familiar ladle’s tap against a pot can steady a weary mind.

His tranquillity did not last.

A woman’s voice came from outside and, in a very few words, told him his own story—told it as if it were already a tale traded in the market—and asked him where Gerard was to be found. The sound struck him harder than a blow, because it carried the world’s language into his hermitage and named him in the tongue of men.

So astonished was he that instinct, not reason, answered. “Pray for the soul of Gerard the son of Eli,” he said—meaning that Gerard was dead to the world. Then he sat, staring at stone, while his heart thumped like a mallet on wood.

When the woman was gone, he fought an inward battle and determined to risk being seen; he peeped out after her to discover who she could be. But he took so many precautions, and she ran so quickly back to her friend, that when at last he dared look, the road was empty and harmless as if no human voice had ever travelled it.

“Satan!” said he directly.

That night the visions returned with a vengeance. Earthly love and happiness flooded him so vividly he could count every auburn hair on Margaret’s head, see the very pupils of her eyes as though she were before him in daylight. Despair rose again, and with it a new fear of his own cell.

“I must leave this country,” he whispered. “Here I am bound fast in memory’s chain.” And then, with sudden loathing: “A breath from hell hath infected it, and robbed even these holy words of their virtue.”

Unconsciously imitating Jerome in his own warfare with hallucinations, he took his warmest covering out into the wood hard by and flung himself under a tree—torn, wrinkled, a leather bag of bones that not long ago might have served a sculptor for Apollo. Whether his imagination’s fever intermitted—since all things, they say, intermit—or whether the change of place broke some subtle link, he did not know; but his sleep was dreamless.

He woke nearly frozen, yet warm with joy within. “I shall yet be a true hermit, Dei gratia,” he said.

Next day some good soul left on his little platform a new lamb’s-wool pelisse and cape, warm, soft, ample. For a moment he misgave at its delicious softness—so like an invitation to the body. Then the scruple passed. It was the right skin, a token that Heaven approved his present course. When he came in from his short rest, it put warmth back into his bones as broth restores a fainting man: not luxurious, but sustaining.

Now he would see victory before him if he could but live to it; then again he would tell himself, trembling, “’Tis but another lull. Be on thy guard, Clement.” The thought kept his nerves in continual awe. He lived like a soldier within the enemy’s lines, listening to every sound as if sound itself might betray him.

One night—a beautiful, clear, frosty night—he returned to his cell after a short rest in the wood. The stars were wonderful. Heaven seemed a thousand times larger, and brighter too, than earth; and to look with a thousand eyes instead of one.

“Oh, wonderful,” he cried, “that there should be men who do crimes by night; and others scarce less mad, who live for this little world, and not for that great and glorious one, which nightly—if custom have not blinded—reveals its glowing glories. Thank God I am a hermit.”

In this mood he came to the door of his cave and paused. It was closed.

“Why, methought I left it open,” said he. “The wind? There is not a breath of wind. What means this?”

He laid his hand on the rugged door. Through one great chink—since the door in places was far smaller than the hole it pretended to stop—he saw his little oil wick burning exactly where he had left it.

“How is it with me,” he sighed, “when I start and tremble at nothing? Either I did shut it, or the fiend hath shut it after me to disturb my happy soul. Retro Sathanas!”

He entered rapidly and set about, with nervous haste, to light one of his largest tapers. As he kindled it, a soft sigh sounded within the cave.

He started and dropped the candle at the very instant it caught; it went out. He stooped, seized it, and lighted it again, listening so hard he seemed to listen with his skin. When it burned, he shaded it with his hand and threw the small light about the cell.

In the farthest corner the outline of the wall was broken.

He took a step toward it, his heart beating. Another step—his knees knocking together.

It was the figure of a woman.

IT WAS MARGARET BRANDT.
