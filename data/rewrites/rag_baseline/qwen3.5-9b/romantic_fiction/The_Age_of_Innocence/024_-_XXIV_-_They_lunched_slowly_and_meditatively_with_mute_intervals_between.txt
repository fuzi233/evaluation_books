They lunched slowly, the rhythm dictated by the heavy, meditative pauses between bursts of speech. Once the initial spell was broken, conversation flowed easily, yet it was often merely the acoustic backdrop to long dialogues of silence. Archer guarded his own affairs not by design, but because he feared missing a single syllable of her history. Leaning forward, chin resting on clasped hands, she recounted the year and a half since their meeting. She spoke of growing weary of "society"—New York had been kind, almost oppressively hospitable, yet after the novelty faded, she found herself too "different" to care for its concerns. Thus, she had chosen Washington, a place supposedly populated by greater varieties of people and opinions. On balance, she intended to settle there, establishing a home for Medora, who had exhausted the patience of every relative precisely when protection from matrimonial perils was most urgent.

"But Dr. Carver—are you not afraid of him? I hear he has been staying with you at the Blenkers'," she asked, smiling. "Oh, the Carver danger is past. Dr. Carver is a brilliant man seeking a wealthy wife to fund his schemes; Medora serves simply as a good advertisement for his conversion." "Conversion to what?" "To various new and radical social experiments. Yet, strangely, they intrigue me more than the blind conformity to tradition—their tradition—that permeates our circle. It seems foolish to discover America only to replicate another country." She smiled across the table. "Do you suppose Christopher Columbus undertook such arduous voyages merely to attend the opera with the Selfridge Merrys?" Archer flushed. "And Beaufort—do you utter such sentiments to Beaufort?" he demanded abruptly. "I have not seen him in ages, though I once did; and he understands." "Ah, as I have always suspected: you dislike us. You favor Beaufort because he stands in such stark opposition to us." He surveyed the sparse room, the barren beach, and the row of stark white village houses strung along the shore. "We are dreadfully dull. Devoid of character, color, variety. I wonder," he erupted, "why you do not return?" Her eyes darkened; he braced for indignation. Instead, she sat silent, contemplating his words, and fear seized him lest she admit she wondered as well. Finally, she spoke: "I believe it is because of you." It was impossible to confess more dispassionately, or in a tone less flattering to the vanity of the listener. Archer blushed to the temples, paralyzed, as if her words were a rare butterfly whose flight depended on absolute stillness. "At least," she continued, "it was you who revealed that beneath the dullness lie things so fine, sensitive, and delicate that even those I cherished in my former life appear cheap by comparison. I cannot fully articulate it," she furrowed her brow, "but it seems I never understood until now how much hardness, shabbiness, and baseness exquisite pleasures demand." "Exquisite pleasures—it is something to have experienced them!" he felt compelled to retort, but the plea in her eyes silenced him. "I wish," she persisted, "to be perfectly honest with you—and with myself. For a long time I have awaited this opportunity: to tell you how you have aided me, what you have made of me—" Archer stared beneath frowning brows, interrupting with a laugh. "And what do you conclude you have made of me?" She paled slightly. "Of you?" "Yes; for I am of your making far more than you ever were of mine. I am the man who married one woman because another instructed him to." Her pallor shifted to a fleeting flush. "I thought—you promised—you would not say such things today." "Ah—how typical of a woman! None of you will ever see a bad transaction through!" She lowered her voice. "_Is_ it a bad business—for May?" He stood by the window, drumming fingers against the raised sash, feeling in every fiber the wistful tenderness with which she had invoked her cousin's name. "That is the burden we must always carry—is it not, by your own logic?" she pressed. "My own logic?" he echoed, his vacant eyes fixed on the sea. "Or if not," she continued, applying herself to the thought with painful intensity, "if it is not worthwhile to sacrifice, to miss opportunities, so that others may be spared disillusionment and misery—then everything I returned home for, everything that rendered my previous life so barren and poor by contrast, because no one there acknowledged it—all these things are a sham or a dream—" He turned without shifting from his spot. "And in that case, there is no earthly reason why you should not return?" he concluded for her. Her eyes clung to him desperately. "Oh, _is_ there no reason?" "Not if you wagered your entire existence on the success of my marriage. My marriage," he said savagely, "will not be a spectacle to detain you here." She offered no reply, and he proceeded: "What is the point? You granted me my first glimpse of a real life, and simultaneously commanded me to continue a sham one. It exceeds human endurance—that is all." "Oh, do not say that; when I am enduring it!" she exclaimed, her eyes flooding with tears. Her arms dropped along the table; she sat with her face abandoned to his gaze, reckless in the desperation of peril. That exposure revealed her as completely as if it were her entire person, with the soul visible behind it: Archer stood dumbstruck, overwhelmed by what it suddenly disclosed. "You too—oh, all this time, you too?" In response, she allowed tears to spill over her lashes, running slowly downward. Half the width of the room separated them, yet neither made any pretense of closing the distance. Archer felt a strange indifference to her physical presence; he would scarcely have noticed it had not one of the hands she had thrown onto the table captured his gaze, as it had in the small Twenty-third Street house, where he had watched it deliberately to avoid looking at her face. Now his imagination swirled around the hand as around the edge of a vortex; yet he made no effort to approach. He knew the love sustained by caresses and feeding them; but this passion, closer than his bones, refused superficial satisfaction. His sole terror was doing anything that might erase the sound and impact of her words; his singular thought was that he should never again feel entirely alone. But soon, the sense of waste and ruin overtook him. There they were, close together, safe, enclosed; yet so chained to their separate destinies that they might as well be worlds apart. "What is the use—when you will return?" he broke out, a great, hopeless _How on earth can I keep you?_ screaming beneath his words. She sat motionless, lids lowered. "Oh—I shall not go yet!" "Not yet? Sometime, then? A time you already foresee?" At that, she raised her clearest eyes. "I promise you: not as long as you hold out. Not as long as we can look straight at each other like this." He sank into his chair. What her answer truly conveyed was: "If you raise a finger, you will drive me back—to all the abominations you know, and all the temptations you half suspect." He understood it as clearly as if she had spoken, and the realization anchored him to his side of the table in a kind of moved, sacred submission. "What a life for you!--" he groaned. "Oh—as long as it is a part of yours." "And mine a part of yours?" She nodded. "And that is to be all—for either of us?" "Well; it _is_ all, isn't it?" At that, he sprang up, forgetting everything save the sweetness of her face. She rose too, not to meet him or flee, but quietly, as though the hardest labor was finished and she need only wait; so quietly that, as he approached, her outstretched hands acted not as a barrier but as a guide. They fell into his, while her arms, extended but not rigid, maintained enough distance for her surrendered face to convey the remainder. They may have stood thus for a long time, or perhaps only moments; but it sufficed for her silence to communicate all she possessed, and for him to realize that only one thing mattered. He must do nothing to make this meeting their final encounter; he must leave their future in her care, asking only that she maintain a firm grip on it. "Don't—don't be unhappy," she said, her voice breaking as she withdrew her hands; and he replied: "You won't go back—you won't go back?" as if it were the sole impossibility he could not endure. "I won't go back," she said; and turning away, she opened the door and led the way into the public dining-room. Strident schoolteachers were gathering their belongings for a straggling flight to the wharf; across the beach lay the white steam-boat at the pier; and over the sunlit waters, Boston loomed in a line of haze.
