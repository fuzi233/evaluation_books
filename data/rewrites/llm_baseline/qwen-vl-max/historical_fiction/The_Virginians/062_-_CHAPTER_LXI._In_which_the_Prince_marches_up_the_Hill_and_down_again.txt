The news of Mr. George Warrington’s refusal to join the Duke of Cumberland’s army stirred a storm of indignation among loyal Britons. To decline an invitation from the beloved son of King George II, Captain-General of the British forces, was seen as nothing short of sacrilege. What a chance for advancement had been cast aside! What a rare favour spurned! Esmond, ever prone to fiery rhetoric, could scarcely contain his wrath. “That cursed young Mohock,” he raged, “is cutting his own throat!” He took grim satisfaction in imagining the consequences of such ingratitude.

Cousin Castlewood offered a more charitable interpretation: “A savage gentleman may scalp himself if he chooses.” Or perhaps, he added with quiet sympathy, “our cousin heard enough war-whoops at Braddock’s defeat and has lost his taste for battle.”

Mr. Will, meanwhile, rejoiced that the younger brother had gone to ruin—and hoped the elder would follow. “The next time I see him,” he declared, “I’ll make sure he knows exactly what I think.”

“Then you’d best be careful,” Lady Maria interjected sharply, “that Harry isn’t within earshot!”

Will muttered darkly—likely something about twins and their shared disgrace.

“My Lord Hervey,” said the Duke, stepping into the fray with a smile, “we can’t all be warriors in one family.”

“And your lordship never claimed to be one?” Maria retorted.

“Never, my dear. Quite the contrary. Will is our champion. One warrior is quite enough for any household. And so, I suppose, with the two Mohocks—George is the scholar, Harry the soldier. When you chose to quarrel, Will, how unfortunate it was you didn’t have George on your side instead of the other!”

“Your lordship’s hand is famous—at piquet,” Mrs. Hervey observed dryly.

“It is a fine hand,” the Duke replied, examining his fingers with a faint smirk. “My Lord Hervey’s glove and mine were of a size. Yes, my hand is better suited to cards than to swords. Yours, my Lady Castlewood, is remarkably dexterous too. How I bless the day you gave it to my lamented father!”

Such banter delighted the Duke—he possessed both wit and coolness, and often outmanoeuvred his family in verbal duels.

Madame de Bernstein, upon hearing of George’s refusal, flew into a rage. She scolded her servants, nearly lashed out at George himself—but age and caution held her back. She feared her nephew, and though she disapproved, she could not bring herself to confront him directly.

“You will never rise at court, nephew,” she sighed when George came to call shortly after his rejection.

“It was never my wish, madam,” George replied with calm dignity.

“But you might have helped Harry. You could have served your brother by accepting the Duke’s offer. Princes do not take kindly to refusal. It’s no wonder his Royal Highness was offended.”

George turned slightly red. “General Lambert said much the same. I now see I was wrong. But please remember—I’ve never known court life. I don’t expect to shine there.”

“I fear you won’t, my good nephew,” the Baroness replied, taking snuff.

“And what then?” George asked.

“I’ve never sought that kind of glory. I’m content without it. When His Royal Highness spoke to me—kindly, I must say—my thought was this: I am no soldier, but my brother Harry is. He has every quality the profession demands; I lack them all. If the Duke were wounded and fell from his horse, as poor Braddock did, would he not be better off with a mount that could carry him through fire and storm than one that couldn’t bear his weight?”

“Au fait,” the old lady murmured. “His Royal Highness’s charger must be strong indeed, my dear.”

“Expende Hannibalem,” George muttered, shrugging.

“Your Hannibal weighs no trifle,” the Baroness noted.

“I don’t quite follow you,” she said.

“When Wolfe and Lambert remonstrated with me, just as you have, madam,” George replied with a laugh, “I gave this very argument. I told them I offered the Prince the best soldier in the family. They admitted my mistake had some excuse. Perhaps they’ll still speak well of me to His Royal Highness. My experience of war has taught me my true inclinations lie elsewhere. The other night, we saw the Scottish play everyone’s talking about. When the hero, young Norval, said he longed to follow a warlike lord to battle, I thought, ‘How like Harry!’—except that he doesn’t brag. Harry dreams of red coats. He follows the King of Prussia everywhere. He fears neither man nor god. As for me, I prefer books and peace. I read battles in Homer or Lucan—not fight them.”

“Then why become a soldier at all?” Madame de Bernstein asked. “And why not send Harry with Braddock instead of going yourself?”

“My mother loved her youngest son best,” George answered, voice low and dark. “And when the enemy invaded our land, it was my duty—as head of the family—to go. Had I been a Scot twelve years ago, I should have been a——”

“Hush, sir!” the Baroness snapped, though her face betrayed amusement. “Or I shall be more angry than ever!”

George’s explanation may have satisfied the Baroness—a woman whose principles were, we suspect, not rigid. But to Sir Miles Warrington and his wife, the news struck like a blow.

“I thought,” Lady Warrington said, “from my sister Esmond’s letters, that her widowhood had preserved her sense and judgment, and that she had raised her sons with proper decorum. Yet what are we to make of an education that produced such failure in both boys?”

“The elder speaks Latin fluently,” Mr. Claypool remarked, “and French and German too. I heard him converse with the Hanover Envoy at the Baroness’s gathering. He jabbered French easily. When he lacked High Dutch, he and the envoy switched to Latin—talked away till the whole room stared.”

“It’s not language, Thomas Claypool,” Lady Warrington insisted, “but principle. What must George’s principles be, if he could refuse an offer from his Prince? The more he speaks, the more he should have accepted it! Look at our son, look at Miles!”

“Hold up thy head, Miley, my boy!” Sir Miles declared.

“I trust, Sir Miles,” the matron continued, “that as a Member of Parliament and an English gentleman, you’ll attend His Royal Highness’s levee tomorrow and declare—if such an offer had been made for our child—we would have accepted it, even though he’s only ten.”

“Faith, Miley,” said Sir Miles, “thou’d make a fine little drummer or fifer!”

“Shouldst like to be a little soldier, Miley?”

“Anything, sir, anything!” the boy cried. “A Warrington ought to be ready at any moment to be cut in pieces for his sovereign!”

He roared loudly—then was promptly removed by Screwby.

Next day, obedient to his wife’s command, Sir Miles attended the levee. There he paid solemn homage to the Prince, who graciously accepted his declaration, saying: “Nobody ever supposed Sir Miles Warrington would refuse any office offered to him.”

A compliment echoed throughout London, repeated by Lady Warrington as proof of the royal family’s full trust in the Warringtons’ loyalty.

Thus, when George returned home, he was met not with anger, but with a sorrowful pity—the kind reserved for those who have failed spectacularly: the bankrupt, the disgraced, the one who walks into a room where they’ve just been condemned. Who does not recognize that face? Whose kin hasn’t mourned them, not dead, but living?

Not you? Then, sir, if you’ve never stumbled, never sown wild oats, never spoken imprudently, never sinned and repented, never been foolish and regretted it—then you’re a wise man, and this tale is not for you.

Yet it was unjust of Sir Miles to turn on George. For Sir Miles himself had once agreed with George’s decision: to pursue a civilian life, not a military one. This was precisely the plan Madam Esmond had outlined in her letters from Virginia. George had announced his intention to enter the Temple, to train for the civil and judicial duties he would one day assume. And no one had praised this choice more heartily than Sir Miles himself, who introduced George to a respected lawyer under whose tutelage we imagine the young man studying diligently.

Madam Esmond approved fully. She wrote to Sir Miles and his wife, expressing gratitude and affirming that the British Constitution was the envy of the world—a subject every English gentleman should study with admiration.

Her only objection? That George would dine in the Temple mess hall—cutting mutton, drinking small beer from tin pannikins beside rough students in clerical gowns. Harry, too, shared this disdain. “Anything is good enough for me,” he said. “I’m the younger son. I can endure hardship.” But George, in a gown, eating off pewter platters beside nobodies? Harry could not stomach it. He wished fervently that an application had been made to the Master of the Temple, declaring that Mr. George Warrington was of noble birth and great American wealth, and therefore deserved a seat among the elite.

But when Harry shared this idea with their friend Mr. Spencer at the coffee-house, Spencer burst into laughter. And history, as recorded in the Warrington papers, shows no such petition was ever sent.

Beyond law, George pursued literature and history—his true passions. He attended Westminster courts, listening to Henley, Pratt, Murray—men famed for eloquence and patriotism. He followed debates in both Houses of Parliament. Gradually, he gained notice among members of the Bar and Commons, who spoke of him as a young man of intelligence and refinement. Their regard softened Sir Miles’s heart, and Dora and Flora began to smile upon him again.

This reconciliation came at a turning point: after the Duke of Cumberland’s defeat at Hastenbeck, he signed a capitulation the King refused to ratify. Humiliated, the Duke resigned his commissions, laid down his baton—though truth be told, he had wielded it poorly—and withdrew from public life forever.

Though he never blamed his father, the loss of power and prestige diminished his standing. With it, Sir Miles’s anger toward George faded.

One day, walking in St. James’s Park with Mr. Lambert, the brothers encountered the Duke—dressed plainly, without star or staff. They bowed deeply. The Prince stopped, smiled, and asked Mr. Lambert how he liked Lord Ligonier, the new Commander-in-Chief, and his duties at Horse Guards.

Then, recognizing the young men, he turned to George: “You did well, sir, not to come with me when I asked you in the spring.”

“I was sorry then, sir,” George replied, bowing low, “but I am more sorry now.”

“Thank you, sir,” the Prince said, touching his hat, and walked on.

When Harry wrote this account to his mother, she treasured it. She recounted it hundreds of times until all her friends knew it by heart—and likely grew weary of it.

As the three men walked toward the Strand, Mr. Lambert pointed to the lion atop the Earl of Northumberland’s house at Charing Cross.

“Harry Warrington!” he said. “Your brother is like yonder lion.”

“Because he is as brave as one,” Harry replied.

“Because I respect virgins!” George laughed.

“Because you’re a stupid lion,” Harry shot back. “Because you face west, saluting the setting sun. Why, child, what good comes from being civil to a man in disgrace? Your uncle will be angrier than ever—and so am I, sir.”

But Mr. Lambert only chuckled, unbothered. He was always laughing—never truly angry.
