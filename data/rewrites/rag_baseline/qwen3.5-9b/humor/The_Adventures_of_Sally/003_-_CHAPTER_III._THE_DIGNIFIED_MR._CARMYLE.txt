The air inside the Casino Municipale tasted of stale tobacco and desperate ambition. By six o'clock, the probability of Ginger surviving the evening without intervention had plummeted to zero. Sally knew the mathematics of the situation: the seven-fifteen express to Paris waited in the shadows, a gateway to Cherbourg and the transatlantic liner bound for New York. If she did not depart soon, the momentum of her escape would fracture. Yet, driven by a compulsion to interfere in the private geometries of others—a trait her brother Fillmore dismissed as meddling—she lingered. She needed to ensure Ginger Kemp had not been abandoned to his fate.

She pushed through the crowd, seeking the source of the commotion. In the center of the room, illuminated by the harsh glare of the chandeliers, a head of flame burned with frantic intensity. This was Ginger. He was not merely playing; he was conducting a siege. Behind a fortress of square counters, he stood entrenched, a wooden-faced croupier sliding chips across the green felt with the mechanical precision of a clockwork toy.

"Epatant!" a man whispered beside Sally, his elbow digging into her ribs to emphasize the gesture. The sentiment rippled through the room. There exists a peculiar altruism among spectators at continental roulette tables; they derive a spiritual sustenance from witnessing another's triumph. The crowd leaned in, starving for validation.

The croupier twisted his moustache with his left hand and the wheel with his right. Silence descended, thick and expectant. Sally adjusted her position, pressing closer to the edge of the chaos. She saw Ginger's face clearly now. He resembled a terrier caught in the act of sniffing a rat-hole, his hair bristling with a nervous energy that made his ears seem permanently pricked forward.

As the tension peaked, Sally laughed. It was an involuntary eruption, clear and sharp, cutting through the sacred hush like a bell in a cathedral. The effect was immediate and catastrophic. The worshippers of luck recoiled, their faces masks of shock and pain. The ball clattered into a red compartment, confirming that the great winner had chosen the wrong color. A collective groan rolled through the assembly, akin to the convulsions of penitents at a revival meeting. Glances of reproach stung Sally; her presence had allegedly broken the spell.

Ginger, however, remained oblivious to the theological crisis he had inadvertently precipitated. He gathered his winnings, shoved them into his pocket, and navigated the crowd to reach Sally, who now stood branded as a pariah. Regimental regret swept the room, as if a star had prematurely exited the stage mid-performance. Even a violent dispute between two gamblers over a five-franc chip failed to restore the atmosphere.

"This is topping," Ginger said, extracting Sally from the hostile throng. "Meeting you like this. I've been searching everywhere."

"It is funny you did not find me," Sally replied, matching his tone. "I was searching for you."

"No, really?" Ginger feigned surprise, leading her to the quiet ante-room beyond the gambling hall. They settled in a corner, the only sanctuary available save for the gorgeously uniformed attendant standing guard by the door. "That was awfully good of you."

"I felt compelled to speak with you before the train departs."

Ginger started violently. "Your train? What do you mean?"

"The puff-puff," Sally explained, using the local vernacular. "I am leaving tonight."

"Leaving?" Horror washed over Ginger's features, mirroring the devastation Sally had witnessed earlier. "You aren't going away from Roville?"

"I am afraid so."

"But why? Where are you going?"

"Back to America. My boat sails from Cherbourg tomorrow."

"Oh, my aunt!"

"I am sorry," Sally said, touched by the genuineness of his distress. She was a warm-hearted creature, accustomed to being appreciated. "But..."

"I say..." Ginger Kemp turned a shade of scarlet that rivaled the worst sins of the gambling floor. He fixed his gaze on the uniformed official, who watched their tête-à-tête with the weary indulgence of a veteran. "Look here, will you marry me?"

Sally stared at his vermilion profile, her mind reeling. Ginger was, she admitted, a surprising young man, but this exceeded the bounds of surprise.

"Marry you!"

"You know what I mean."

"Well, yes, I suppose I do. You allude to the holy state. Yes, I know what you mean."

"Then how about it?"

Sally began to regain her composure. Her sense of humor, usually a shield, was momentarily tickled. She regarded Ginger gravely. He avoided her eyes, instead drinking in the official, who had begun to hum a love ballad under his breath, completely lost in the romantic delusion. The official could not hear them, nor would he have understood the syntax, yet he was an expert in the language of eyes.

"But isn't this—" Sally hesitated, choosing her words carefully. "Don't think I am trying to make difficulties—isn't this a little sudden?"

"It has to be sudden," Ginger complained, his voice rising. "I thought you were staying for weeks."

"But, my infant, my babe, has it occurred to you that we are practically strangers?" She patted his hand tolerantly, eliciting a tender sigh from the sentinel. "I see what has happened. You are mistaking me for some other girl, some girl you know intimately and were properly introduced to. Take a good look at me, and you will see."

"If I take a good look at you," Ginger said feverishly, "I'm dashed if I'll answer for the consequences."

"And this is the man I was going to lecture on 'Enterprise.'"

"You're the most wonderful girl I've ever met, dash it!" Ginger declared, his gaze still riveted on the official. "I dare say it is sudden. I can't help that. I fell in love with you the moment I saw you, and there you are!"

"But..."

"Now, look here, I know I'm not much of a chap and all that, but... well, I've just won the deuce of a lot of money in there..."

"Would you buy me with your gold?"

"I mean to say, we should have enough to start on, and... of course I've made an infernal hash of everything I've tried up till now, but there must be something I can do. With you to buck me up and so forth, don't you know. Well, I mean..."

"Has it struck you that I may already be engaged to someone else?"

"Oh, golly! Are you?"

For the first time, Ginger turned fully to face her. The look in his eyes stripped away the ludicrous veneer, revealing a raw seriousness that touched Sally profoundly. Absurd as the situation was, this man was genuinely serious.

"Well, yes, as a matter of fact I am," she said soberly.

Ginger Kemp bit his lip, falling silent for a moment.

"Oh, well, that's torn it!" he finally conceded.

Sally felt an emotion too complex to categorize instantly. It contained pity, but also amusement. It was a maternal instinct, the wavering between tears and laughter mothers feel when their children plead with engaging absurdity for things they cannot bestow. She wanted to lift Ginger and kiss him, yet she could not view him, despite her sorrow, as a reasonable adult.

"You don't really mean it, you know."

"Don't I!" Ginger said hollowly. "Oh, don't I!"

"You can't! There isn't such a thing in real life as love at first sight. Love comes when you know a person well and..." She paused. The irony struck her: she was hardly the girl to lecture on this subject. Her own love for Gerald Foster had been instantaneous, their engagement sealed within two weeks of meeting. What did she truly know of Gerald? Only that she loved him. The memory dampened her eloquence, and she concluded tamely, "It's ridiculous."

Ginger had simmered down to a mood of melancholy resignation.

"I couldn't have expected you to care for me, I suppose, anyway," he said sombrely. "I'm not much of a chap."

This diversion was precisely what Sally had sought. She seized the opportunity to shift the conversation from the intimate and sentimental to the practical.

"That's exactly what I wanted to talk to you about," she said, adopting the tone of an aunt—or perhaps a sister. "Really, the best plan would be for you to adopt me as an honorary sister. What do you think?"

Ginger did not appear notably elated by the proposal.

"Because I really do take a tremendous interest in you."

Ginger brightened. "That's awfully good of you."

"I'm going to speak words of wisdom. Ginger, why don't you brace up?"

"Brace up?"
