So Ayacanora took up formal residence in Lucy’s tiny cabin, recognized by every soul on the ship as a full, accredited member of the crew.

But she proved a most troublesome addition, for that perilous, all-consuming crisis had fallen upon her: the same peril that erodes the bodies and souls of native peoples the moment they first mix with white European society, the same force that would within a few short years hasten the near-extermination of the North American tribes. Were it not for Amyas Leigh’s steady good sense and unshakable constancy, Ayacanora might have met an even more wretched end than the famous Pocahontas, Virginian king’s daughter: who was once received at King James I’s court with honors equal to a sovereign sister, became the fabled ancestress of a dozen old Virginia families, and died in squalor in a cramped Wapping garret.

A savage’s mind, crushed by the overwhelming sight of white men’s superior skill, wealth, and knowledge, loses its self-respect at first. His body, meanwhile, fed on easy luxuries instead of forced to toil for every crust of sustenance, loses its self-reliance. Strip away those two things — self-respect and self-reliance — and all the sparse, fragile virtues of indigenous life vanish too, leaving a wide, easy road that leads straight to begging, theft, drunkenness, and idleness.

Down that very road poor Ayacanora seemed to be speeding at first. The warrior-prophetess of the Omaguas swiftly devolved into what looked for all the world like a very naughty, willful child. The Diana of the Meta, after she’d satisfied her wide-eyed wonder at the great floating house by wandering from deck to deck peeping into every cupboard and crack, developed a terrible habit of stealing and hiding (too proud or too shy to ever ask permission) every trinket that caught her fancy. When Amyas forbade her from taking anything without leave, she threatened to drown herself, and hid in her cabin sulking for the whole rest of the day. Still, she obeyed him — except when it came to sweets. Perhaps she craved the familiar vegetable fare of her Amazon forests; at any rate, the bishop’s stores of dried fruit and sweetmeats dwindled at a shocking rate. Worse still, the small stock of fine Spanish wine Amyas had set aside as a daily cordial for sick Lucy vanished too. Amyas gave her a sharp lecture, telling her how cruel it was to rob a poor invalid, and Ayacanora flew into a rage, threatened to throw herself overboard there and then, and ran for the deck to do it. Amyas caught her before she could climb the rail, and forgave her. She burst into a storm of tears, swore penitence, and made all kinds of good promises. A week later, Amyas found out she’d cheated both her own conscience and the rules: she’d badgered the Portuguese steward into giving her a different supply of wine behind his back. But luck was on his side: the first time she drank too much, she behaved so foolishly through the evening, and woke up with such a splitting headache the next morning, that Amyas’s warnings about wine making men mad came true for her. The whole secret spilled out, and Amyas called for the steward to give the man a sound flogging — but Ayacanora, to her credit, not only begged him off, but offered to take the lash in his place, confessing that the poor wretch had only given her wine because she’d threatened to kill him if he refused, and he’d acted in fear for his life.

That headache of hers, plus Amyas’s quiet, disappointed look, was lesson enough. After one more half-hearted attempt to drown herself, the wilful beauty settled down for a good long stretch — and better yet, she would never, for the rest of her life, willingly touch fermented drink.

Still, Amyas wore himself ragged trying to figure out how to care for a lady who threatened suicide at the slightest provocation. He dared not punish her, even if he’d had the heart to. And as for putting her ashore, he had a deep, un-superstitious instinct that her strange, fierce affection for the English was no accident of Heaven, that God had placed her in his charge, and that he would have to answer for the fate of that lost fair lamb when the time came.

At his wits’ end, he prayed earnestly, many times, for guidance on what to do with her before she killed either herself, or one of the crew — which seemed just as likely. It struck him that the best path forward was to have Parson Jack Brimblecombe teach her the rudiments of the Christian faith, so she could be properly baptized when they sailed home to England.

A new trouble arose instantly: she flatly refused to learn from Jack, or anyone at all, but Amyas himself. And Amyas had a dozen excellent reasons to decline being her schoolmaster. For two more weeks, Ayacanora remained untaught in scripture — though she picked up spoken English with a marvellous, almost unnatural speed.

Worse followed. She developed a violent dislike not only for Parson Jack, whose gait and voice she would openly mimic to make the rest of the crew snort with laughter, but for Will Cary, whom she would not allow to speak to her or come within arm’s length. Perhaps she was jealous of his close friendship with Amyas; or perhaps, with a woman’s quiet instinct, she guessed he was the only other man on board who might dare to court her (though Will, to do him justice, had no such intention whatsoever). When remonstrated with, her only reply was that Cary was a cacique — chief — just like Amyas, and no ship could have two caciques. One day she even outright proposed that Amyas kill his supposed rival, and keep the whole ship for himself. She sulked for three days straight when Amyas, to the crew’s howls of laughter, repeated her precious advice straight to Will’s face.

She also showed unutterable, unprovoked repugnance for the Black members of the crew, at first hiding from them whenever she could, then throwing every loose object within reach at their heads, until the poor men, fearing for their lives, complained to Amyas, who finally got her to leave them alone.

Over the rest of the sailors, she ruled like a born princess, calling them away from their work to run her errands and carve her little toys, and enforcing her commands with a sharp, well-placed box on the ear when they dragged their feet. The good fellows, especially old Salvation Yeo, doted on her, obeyed her, and jested with her — much as one might treat a tamed leopard, whose claws could spring out to catch you unawares at any moment. She amused them all, and she amused Amyas too. Sailors always needed a ship’s pet, and no pet they could have found was half so pretty. For Amyas, her constant presence, even the constant worry of her wilfulness, kept his mind occupied, and chased away the heavy, brooding melancholy that had haunted him ever since he set sail: the dread of facing his mother, of telling her the terrible tragedy that had befallen his brother, the weight that would have turned all his worldly success and miraculous deliverance to worthless ash in his mouth if he’d been left to dwell on it unbroken.

At last, matters came to a head — as most lucky things do.

The ship slipped steadily day after day before a fair, favorable breeze. They’d passed the ring of the West India islands, and were now crawling safely out of sight of any pursuit, through the vast tangled weed beds of the Sargasso Sea. There, for the first time, the captains decided it was safe to relax the strict discipline they’d enforced all voyage, and “rummage” their captured Spanish prize ship. The gold, silver, jewels, and merchandise they found on board would interest no reader here. Suffice it to say the hoard was large enough, after paying out all agreed shares to the crew, Will Cary’s full cut, and Mr. Salterne’s third as the ship’s owner, to make Amyas wealthy for the rest of his life. Tucked away in the dead captain’s private cabin, they found two large chests: one full of gorgeous Mexican featherwork robes, the other stuffed full of fine Spanish and East Indian ladies’ finery, bound for a noblewoman in Caracas after stopping in Havana and Cartagena. At Will Cary’s suggestion, the crew voted by acclamation to give both chests to Ayacanora, as her fair share of the plunder, to reward her Amazonian courage and her invaluable help in the fight.

The poor girl seized the treasure with greedy delight, had the chests carried straight to Lucy’s cabin, and knelt poring over the contents for hours at a time. She scorned the Mexican featherwork as “savage” fit only for the Omaguas; but the Spanish dresses were a treasure beyond her wildest dreams. For three days straight she paraded the quarterdeck like a peacock, decked out in Seville mantillas, oversize Madrid hats, and stiff Indian brocade farthingales, all worn in the most absurd, mismatched ways possible.

The crew snickered behind their hands. Amyas felt far more like crying. Nothing is more heartbreaking than a child’s vanity, save a grown person aping a child’s vanity, and nothing more pitiable than the moment they realize everyone is laughing at them, not admiring them. Amyas wanted to speak up, but he was afraid to break her heart. The farce went on, as far as Ayacanora was concerned, perfectly successfully, until the end of the third day. Unable to contain her pride any longer, she turned to Amyas and asked, “Ayacanora is quite English girl now, is she not?”

She heard a snicker behind her, spun around, and saw a dozen honest tars grinning broadly. She understood it all in an instant. She darted down the companion ladder and vanished.

Amyas, fully convinced she would throw herself overboard, ran after her as fast as he could. She’d locked herself in the cabin with Lucy, and he could hear her heaving, violent sobs, and Lucy’s faint, weak voice begging her to say what had gone wrong.

He knocked in vain. She refused to come out all day, and by evening they had no choice but to break the door down, lest poor Lucy starve to death trapped inside. Ayacanora was there, half her finery torn off and scattered across the floor in a fit of rage, crying as if her heart would break; poor Lucy was crying too, half from fear and hunger, half from sympathy for the girl.

Amyas tried to soothe her, swore the men would never laugh at her again. “But then,” he added, “you must not be so — so —” He could not find the right word.

“So what?” she asked, crying even more bitterly.

“So like a wild girl, Ayacanora.”

Her hands dropped to her knees. A sharp, painful spasm ran through her throat and chest, and she fell on her knees before him, looking up at his face with imploring eyes.

“Yes — wild girl. Poor bad wild girl. But I will be English girl now.”

“Fine clothes will never make you English, my child,” Amyas said gently.

“No! Not English clothes — English heart! Good heart, like yours! Yes, I will be good, and Sir John shall teach me!”

“There’s my good maid,” Amyas said. “Sir John shall start teaching you tomorrow.”

“No! Now! Now! Ayacanora cannot wait. She will drown herself if she is bad another day! Come, now!”

She made him fetch Brimblecombe at once, sat patiently listening to the good man for more than an hour, and told Lucy every word he’d said that very night. From that day on, whenever Jack went in to read and pray with the sick girl Lucy, Ayacanora no longer slipped away to the deck. She stood by patiently, trying to make sense of every word, knelt when he knelt, and tried to pray too — that she might have a good English heart. And her prayers, dumb as they were, were surely not unheard.

Things went on smoothly, full of hope, for several days, until one morning just after they’d sailed clear of the Sargasso weed beds. The ship sailed on under steady breeze, the men lounged on deck under the canvas awning mending nets, patching clothes, and chatting. Brimblecombe was catechising his new pupil in the cabin. Amyas and Will Cary, cigars in their mouths, were chatting about every subject under the sun — and above all, planning their next adventure against the Spaniards as soon as they returned to England, while Amyas poured out the dark, seething hatred he held for the whole Spanish nation, the grim purpose of avenging his brother and Rose Salterne that had settled like murky fog into every cranny of his heart.

Suddenly there was a scuffle and a shout from below that made both men leap to their feet. Jack Brimblecombe came rushing up on deck, holding his head on with both hands.

“Save me! Save me from that she-fiend! She is possessed of a whole legion of demons! She has broken my nose, torn out half my hair — and I had none to spare to begin with! Here she comes! Stand by me, gentlemen both! Satanas, I defy thee!” He ducked behind Amyas and Will, as Ayacanora whirled onto the deck like a raging Maenad, and skidded to a stop the moment she saw Amyas.

“If you’d defied Satan down in the cabin,” Will said, laughing, “I suspect he would never have burst out on you so boldly, Master Jack.”

“I am innocent! Innocent as the unborn babe!” Jack sputtered indignantly, while Amyas asked what on earth had happened.

“He looked at me,” Ayacanora said sturdily.

“Well, a cat may look at a king,” Amyas said, bewildered.

“But he shall not look at Ayacanora. Nobody shall but you, or I’ll kill him!”

Jack protested his innocence, swore he had not even glanced at her, but the fancy had seized her and would not shift. She refused to go below for any more lessons that day. Jack wandered off grumbling, his scalp bleeding, and walked around with a black eye for the whole next week.

“At all events,” Will said, re-lighting his cigar, “that’s a fault on the right side.”

“God give me grace,” Amyas said quietly, “or it may turn out a fault on the very wrong side for me.”

“He will, old heart-of-oak!” Will said, throwing his arm around Amyas’s neck. The gesture visibly disgusted Ayacanora, who stomped off to the ship’s rail, fetched a fishing line, and amused herself dangling it over the side. The ship slipped on quietly and silently, only broken when Ayacanora laughed and clapped her hands watching flying fish dart away from pursuing bonitos. “Tis Sunderland the song’s about, not Bideford, John Squire,” his mate teased.

Salvation Yeo interrupted then. “Aren’t you ashamed at your age, John Squire, singing such carnal vanities, after all the providences the Lord has shown us? Sing the songs of Zion, man, if you’re determined to make a caterwauling noise all day long.”

“Well then, you sing ’em yourself, gunner,” John Squire challenged.

“And why shouldn’t I?” Yeo pulled out his worn psalm book, and struck up a rousing verse of the old hymn:
> “Such as in ships and brittle barks Into the seas descend,
> Their merchandise through fearful floods To compass and to end;
> There men are forced to behold The Lord's works what they be;
> And in the dreadful deep the same, Most marvellous they see.”

“Humph,” John Squire grumbled. “Very good and godly, but still I do love a merry catch now and then. Wouldn’t you let a body sing ‘Rumbelow’ even when heaving up the anchor?”

“I don’t know,” Yeo said. “But the Lord’s people ought to praise Him then too, and pray for a good voyage, instead of howling out —
> “A randy, dandy, dandy O,
> A whet of ale and brandy O,
> With a rumbelow and a Westward-ho!
> And heave, my mariners all, O!”

“Is that fit talk for immortal souls? How does that child’s trumpery sound next to the Psalms, John Squire?”

As it happened, Salvation Yeo had sung that old shanty in full, rich nasal tone, just to mock the silly thing — the exact same way he’d sung it a dozen years prior, heaving up John Oxenham’s anchor in Plymouth Sound. And Ayacanora, standing at Amyas’s side trying to follow the men’s chatter, heard it, started, and half to herself, picked up the strain, singing every word perfectly, in the exact same tune and tone.

Salvation Yeo jumped, and turned deathly pale.

“Who sung that?” he demanded sharply.

“The little maid here,” Amyas said. “She’s coming on wonderfully well with her English.”
