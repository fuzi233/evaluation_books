Anyone who has watched sedate, clerical rooks wing home at dusk, straggling in staid companies, will have seen the odd pair break away from the flock, double back, and hover mid-air as if pulling a clever little ruse to pretend they have no part to play in the rest of the colony’s affairs.

The same trick plays out in the old cathedral’s echoing Close, right after evensong. The choir shuffles out, a crowd of venerable, rook-faced vergers and canons drift off to their evening meals, and two of them turn back, strolling slow through the flagstoned courtyard.

It is not just the day that is waning, but the whole year. The low sun glows fiery but sharp-cold behind the ruins of the old monastery, and half the deep red Virginia creeper leaves clinging to the cathedral walls have showered down onto the pavement. There was rain that afternoon, and a wintry gust shivers through the puddles pooled in the cracked flagstones, and ripples through the giant elms as they shed their leaves like cold tears. A handful of those leaves dart for sanctuary under the low arched cathedral door, only to be kicked back out by two men stepping outside. One twists a heavy iron key in the lock to secure the door, the other slips away fast, clutching a thick folio of sheet music to his chest.

“Was that Mr. Jasper, Tope?” the Dean asks.
“Yes, Mr. Dean.”
“He stayed very late.”
“Aye, your Reverence. I waited up for him. He was taken a bit poorly.”
“Say *taken*, Tope—to the Dean,” the younger of the two, the pink-cheeked, boyish Minor Canon Septimus Crisparkle, cuts in quietly, a gentle touch of correction. The Chief Verger Tope, who is used to lording it over gaping excursion parties, pretends not to notice the slight at his grammar.

“And when and how has Mr. Jasper been taken, as Mr. Crisparkle rightly notes—” the Dean picks up, “—poorly, Tope?”
“His breathing was so short when he came in,” Tope picks his words carefully, steering clear of any more grammar missteps, “he could barely get his notes out. That brought on a fit, like. His memory went dazed, a dimness and giddiness came over him I’ve never seen the like of, though he didn’t seem half as bothered by it as I was. A bit of rest and a glass of water pulled him right out of that daze, though.” Tope repeats the word with obvious pride, as if daring anyone to find a better one.

“Has he gone home quite himself?” the Dean asks.
“Quite himself, your Reverence. I saw his fire lit ten minutes back, and it’s chilly enough after the rain that he’ll need it. The cathedral was damp as a tomb all afternoon, he was shivering so hard his teeth chattered.”

All three men glance across the Close at the old stone gatehouse, the one with the arched passage cutting under it. A fire glows through its latticed window, gilding the falling dark and leaving the ivy and creeper draped over its front wall in shadow. The deep cathedral bell booms the hour, and a ripple of wind runs through the hanging vines, the sound echoing like a faint hum that drifts through every tomb, broken niche, and eroded statue in the great church at their backs.

“Is his nephew with him?” the Dean asks.
“Not yet, sir, but he’s expected. There’s his own shadow, betwixt his two windows—the one facing this way, the other looking down the High Street—drawing his curtains just now.”

“Well, well,” the Dean says, briskly wrapping up their little conference, “I do hope Mr. Jasper does not set his heart too firmly on that boy. Our affections, however noble, ought never to master us in this transitory world. We guide them, never the other way around. And it seems my dinner bell is reminding me it is time to go do a far less pious sort of guiding. Mr. Crisparkle, would you pop in on Jasper on your way home, to check he’s well?”
“Of course, sir. And pass on your kind regards?”
“Exactly so. Wished to know he was alright, by all means.” The Dean tips his quaint clerical hat, and trundles off towards his snug red brick residence, his polished gaiters clicking on the stone path.

Crisparkle, the minor canon who swims every cold deep river within ten miles of Cloisterham, who coaches the cathedral choir, who gave up tutoring wealthy pagan schoolboys for his quiet Christian post, turns his steps towards the gatehouse.

“Sorry to hear from Tope you were unwell, Jasper,” he says, stepping through the door.
Jasper looks up from his chair by the fire. He is dark, twenty-six, with thick lustrous black hair and sideburns that make him look older than his years, his voice deep and rich, his manner permanently a little somber. His rooms are perpetually dim: even on the brightest summer day the sun never touches the grand piano tucked in the recess, the stacked folios of sheet music, or the clumsy, unskilled painting hanging over the mantelpiece. It is a portrait of a blooming schoolgirl, her brown hair tied back with a blue ribbon, her face twisted in that perfectly childish, half-saucy look of petty discontent that is so uniquely hers. Jasper painted it, and the likeness is sharp, almost vengefully accurate.

“It was nothing at all,” Jasper waves the concern off. “Tope makes a drama out of everything that happens inside the cathedral walls, it’s his job.”
“I can tell the Dean you are fully recovered, then? I came expressly on his behalf.”
“Of course. My respects and thanks to him.”
“I’m glad to hear you’re expecting young Edwin Drood. He’ll do you more good than a whole chemist’s shop of medicine, you know.”
“More good than a dozen doctors. I’d rather swallow arsenic than the tonics they peddle.”

The two men chat a little longer, Crisparkle humming snatches of a glee tune under his breath as he takes his leave. He calls out a greeting to someone at the bottom of the stairs, and Jasper is on his feet in an instant, yanking the door open to catch the young man who has just stepped inside in a crushing hug.

“My dear Edwin!”
“My dear Jack! It’s so good to see you!” The boy—he is barely more than a boy, bright, scruffy, full of restless energy—shrugs off his coat, boots and gloves, chattering nonstop. Jasper’s face softens into a look no one else ever sees: intent, hungry, all his focus fixed entirely on Edwin, not a scrap of attention straying anywhere else. It is a look he will wear, unbroken, every time he turns to look at his nephew, for the rest of their days.

They head through to the small adjoining dining room, where the verger’s wife Mrs. Tope has laid a full feast. Edwin claps his hands, grinning. “What a fancy spread! Whose birthday is it, Jack?”
“Not yours, I know.”
“Not mine, no! It’s Pussy’s! Rosa’s birthday!” Jasper’s eyes flick for half a second to the silly portrait over the mantel, as if he has included the girl in the room with them.

Edwin shoves a hand on Jasper’s shoulder, and Jasper laughs, returning the gesture, and the two march into dinner arm in arm like they are leading a revolutionary parade. The meal passes in cheerful, trivial chatter, Edwin flirting outrageously with blushing Mrs. Tope, until the cloth is cleared away and a dish of walnuts and a decanter of deep rich sherry is set between them.

“Tell me, Jack,” Edwin says, cracking his first nut, “do you ever feel like calling us uncle and nephew puts some weird distance between us? I don’t. You’re only six years older than me, for God’s sake. We’re more like mates than family.”
“Uncles are almost always far older than their nephews,” Jasper says evenly, “the difference sticks in my head.”
“Then wouldn’t it be better if I was older than you? I could take the lead, keep you from getting bored down here stuck in the cathedral.” Edwin laughs, then takes a sip of sherry. “Oh, wait—we forgot to toast her! Pussy, and many happy returns! Hooray!” Jasper drinks the toast in silence, his dark eyes never leaving the boy’s face.

“Now tell me how she is, Jack. How’s my betrothed?”
“Her music lessons are going well.”
“Ah, but you know her. She’s as stubborn as a mule. She’ll never put the work in if she’s not in the mood.”
“Very like that portrait up there, isn’t she?” Jasper nods at the daub over the mantel.
Edwin glares playfully at the painting, waggling his nutcrackers at it. “That’s the exact pout she gives me every time I turn up to visit and we bicker over the stupid prearranged marriage. Our dads signed us off to marry each other before we could even talk, for Christ’s sake. It’s not fair. I don’t get to choose her, she doesn’t get to choose me. We’re both being forced into something neither of us asked for.”

Jasper’s face goes suddenly deathly pale. A thin white film slips over his eyes, his fingers go rigid around his nutcracker.
“Jack? Good God, you look terrible!”
“It’s nothing,” Jasper says, his voice faint. “I take opium for a agonizing, crippling pain that hits me sometimes. The drug creeps over me like a blight, it’ll pass in a minute. Don’t look at me. It’ll go faster if you look away.”

Edwin stares down at the ashes in the hearth, scared, while Jasper sits rigid in his chair, beads of cold sweat rolling down his forehead, his breath catching sharp in his throat. A minute later, the haze clears. He blinks, and he is his usual self again.

“They say every house has a hidden skeleton,” he says, with a faint, forced laugh, “you never guessed mine was opium, did you, Ned?”
“I never thought you had any secrets at all, Jack.”
“All of you think my life is perfect. The quiet cathedral, the choir, no stress, no change, I’m perfectly suited for it. But the monotony grinds me down, grain by grain. The sound of my own voice echoing off the arches every day sounds like it’s mocking me. None of the old monks who droned their lives away in those stalls could have been more sick of this place than I am. They carved demons into the woodwork to pass the time. What am I supposed to do? Carve them out of my own heart?”

Edwin stares at him, horrified, leaning forward to put a sympathetic hand on his knee. “I thought you loved this life.”
“No one else sees how much it’s eating me alive. That’s why I’m telling you this, as a warning. Don’t let a stale, unchosen routine grind you down the same way. Don’t let your life get mapped out for you, line by line, before you even get a say.”

But Edwin is too bright, too young, too unburdened to take the warning in. He just smiles, patting Jasper’s hand. “Don’t worry about me. In less than a year I’ll graduate, I’ll carry Pussy off from that school, and we’ll head out East where I’ve got my engineering post. We might bicker now, but once the marriage is done and we don’t have that awkward forced engagement hanging over us, we’ll be happy. I’ll sing, she’ll dance, we’ll make the most of it.” He nods at the portrait, grinning. “I’ll paint her a far better likeness than that daub you made, when we’re out there.”

Jasper sits quiet, watching him, his face soft with that same hungry, consuming affection.
“You still won’t take the warning, then?” he asks.
“Not a chance, Jack. I’m fine. Come on—let’s go for a walk in the churchyard. I’ve got a parcel for Rosa, one pair of gloves for every year of her age, I’m going to drop it off at the Nuns’ House. It’s poetic, isn’t it?”

Jasper smiles, the shadow of the opium haze still lingering at the edge of his dark eyes. He murmurs a line from an old love song under his breath: “Nothing half so sweet in life.”

Edwin shoves his coat on, and Jasper follows him out the door. The autumn dark wraps around them as the cathedral bell booms again, and their two shadows stretch long across the flagstones, vanishing into the shadow of the gatehouse arch.
