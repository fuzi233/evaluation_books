I will waste no breath describing my own sensations in that first moment after I laid hands on the nightgown.

The shock of what I saw short-circuited every last scrap of thought and feeling in my body. I have no memory at all of how I acted when Betteredge found me on the beach—all I have is his word for it: that I cackled like a fool when he demanded to know what ailed me, pushed the paint-stained linen into his hands, and bade him read the riddle for himself. The stretch of shore between us is a total blank to me now. The next clear memory I can pull back into focus is the path winding through the fir plantation, pine needles crunching soft under our boots, and Betteredge’s gruff, steady voice rumbling that we’ll both be able to face whatever this mess throws at us, the second we’ve downed a glass of good strong grog.

The scene shifts next to Betteredge’s tiny, sun-dappled sitting room, tucked away at the back of the servant’s wing. I’d sworn never to set foot inside Rachel Verinder’s house ever again, and I don’t even remember making the choice to cross the threshold. All I feel is the blessed cool, quiet shade of the small room, and the ice-cold well water he stirs into my spirits, a luxury I’d never have touched at ten o’clock in the morning on any ordinary day. Under other circumstances, that drink would have knocked me flat. Right then, it tightened every frayed nerve in my body, sharpened my wits, just as Betteredge promised it would. We were both ready to face the truth, at last.

I suspect anyone reading this will think my behavior absurd, at best. Stuck in a situation with no parallel I can name, what do I do first? Do I lock myself away from every other living soul? Sit down to pick apart this impossible, undeniable fact staring me straight in the face? Catch the very first train back to London to bring in top detectives and launch a full, urgent inquiry? No. I take shelter under the very roof I swore I’d never disgrace myself by entering again, and I sit tippling spirits and water at the kitchen hour, side by side with an old family steward. What rational person would act that way, caught in my position? I can only plead that the sight of Betteredge’s weathered, familiar face was the only comfort I had left in the world, that his well-mixed grog pulled me back from total collapse when nothing else could. I offer that as my only excuse, and tip my hat to all the perfectly dignified, logically consistent souls who never once stumble in an emergency, from the cradle to the grave.

“Now, Mr. Franklin, there’s one thing we can count on for certain,” Betteredge said, flinging the nightgown down hard on the table between us, pointing at it like it was a living, breathing enemy that could hear every word. “The whole damn thing’s a lie, to start with.”

That comforting take didn’t match the thought that was ringing in my head. “I am as innocent of stealing the Moonstone as you are,” I told him. “But the evidence is staring us right in the face. The paint on the nightgown, my name stitched to the cuff—those are facts.”

Betteredge lifted my glass and pushed it back into my hand, firm but gentle. “Facts?” he repeated. “Take another sip of that grog, sir, and you’ll get over this silly habit of believing in facts. Foul play, that’s what this is,” he went on, dropping his voice to a confidential murmur. “Somebody’s been playing a dirty trick, and you and I are going to find out who. Was there anything else in that tin case, besides the nightgown, when you reached in?”

The question jolted my memory, and I fished the thick, closely written letter out of my coat pocket. I flipped straight to the signature at the end. Rosanna Spearman.

The name lit a spark in my fogged brain, and a sharp new suspicion flared to life. “Wait a second!” I exclaimed. “Rosanna Spearman came to my aunt’s house straight out of a reformatory, didn’t she? She was a convicted thief once, right?”

“No denying that, Mr. Franklin. What of it, now?”

“What of it? How do we know she didn’t steal the Moonstone herself? How do we know she didn’t deliberately smear my nightgown with that paint to frame me for the whole crime?”

Betteredge laid a heavy hand on my arm, stopping me before I could say another word. “You will be cleared of this mess, beyond all shadow of doubt, sir. But I pray you aren’t cleared that way. Read what she wrote. You owe it to the poor girl’s memory to read every line.”

I felt the quiet rebuke in his earnest tone, and it stung. I knew I’d slandered her thoughtlessly, before I even knew what had driven her to hide my nightgown. “You can make up your own mind about her,” I said. “I’ll read the letter out loud.”

I started in, and the very first line of her confession made the letter slip right out of my fingers. “Sir—I have something to own to you. A confession which means much misery, may sometimes be made in very few words. This confession can be made in three words. I love you.”

I stared up at Betteredge, stunned. “What in God’s name does this mean?”

He shifted in his chair, avoiding my eye. “You were alone with Limping Lucy this morning, sir, weren’t you? Didn’t she say a single word about Rosanna Spearman?”

“Not a word. She never even named her.”

“Go back to the letter, then, Mr. Franklin. Plain and simple, I can’t find it in my heart to distress you more than you’ve already been distressed. Let her speak for herself. And for your own sake, man, finish that grog.”

I picked the letter up off the floor and started reading again.

“It would be very disgraceful to me to tell you this, if I was a living woman when you read it. I shall be dead and gone, sir, when you find my letter. That’s what makes me bold. Not even my grave will be left to mark where I was. I can tell you the whole truth, with the quicksand waiting to swallow me the second I set down the last word.

“You’ll find your nightgown in my hiding place, all smeared with that wet paint, and you’ll wonder why I hid it, why I never breathed a word about it while I was alive. I only have one reason for all those strange things. I did them because I loved you.

“I won’t bore you with the whole sad story of my life before you came to Lady Verinder’s house. Lady Verinder pulled me straight out of the reformatory. I got sent to the reformatory after a stretch in prison. I went to prison because I was a thief. I was a thief because my mother had to turn to the streets when I was a tiny little girl, and she had to do that because my father, the gentleman, abandoned her without a penny. It’s a common, tired old story, you read it every other day in the newspapers.

“Lady Verinder was kind to me, and Mr. Betteredge was kind to me. Those two, and the matron at the reformatory, are the only good people I ever met in my whole life. I might have scraped by at that house, not happy, but surviving, if you hadn’t come visiting. I don’t blame you, sir. This is all my fault, every bit of it.

“Do you remember that morning you stumbled out from behind the sandhills, looking for Mr. Betteredge? You looked like a prince right out of a fairy tale. Like the lover every girl dreams of, that no real man ever lives up to. The second I saw you, something that felt like the happy life I’d never once gotten to live jumped right up inside me. Don’t laugh at this, if you can help it. Oh, if you could only understand how deadly serious this is to me!

“I went back to the house, and wrote your name and mine in my workbox, and drew a true lovers’ knot right under them. Then a little voice whispered to me, ‘Go look in the mirror.’ I did, and the sight of myself broke my heart—but I was too stupid to take the warning. I kept falling harder and harder for you, like I was a lady of your own rank, the prettiest creature you’d ever laid eyes on. I tried so hard, every single day, to get you to even look at me. If you knew how I cried myself to sleep every night, miserable and humiliated that you never so much as noticed me, you might have pitied me, and given me a tiny little look I could live on, just to get me through the day.

“I wouldn’t have been half so hurt, maybe, if you knew how much I hated Miss Rachel. I think I figured out you were in love with her before you did, yourself. She used to tuck roses in your buttonhole for you. Ah, Mr. Franklin, you wore my roses in that buttonhole more times than either you or her ever knew! The only little comfort I had back then was slipping my own rose secretly into your glass of water, swapping it for hers, and throwing hers straight in the bin.

“I might have borne it better if she was half as pretty as you all thought she was. No, I expect I’d have hated her worse, still. Imagine stripping off all her fine clothes, putting her in a servant’s dress, and taking all her trinkets away? No one’d call her pretty then. She was too thin, her figure was all wrong. But who knows what mad things men like? And fine young ladies can act as rude and lazy as they please, and never get sent packing, while a servant would get sacked on the spot for half the same behavior. It’s none of my business, I know. I shouldn’t be writing this way, or you’ll never finish reading my letter. But it makes my blood boil to hear everyone call Miss Rachel beautiful, when you know full well it’s all just her fancy gowns and her brash confidence doing all the work.

“Try not to lose patience with me, sir. I’ll get to the part you care about, the part where the Diamond went missing, as fast as I can. But there’s one thing I have to tell you first.

“My life wasn’t half so hard to bear back when I was a thief. It was only after the reformatory matrons taught me to feel my own shame, to try and be a respectable person, that the days got long and heavy and empty. Thoughts of the future started creeping up on me, and I felt every honest person around me judging me, even the kindest ones. That terrible, heart-breaking loneliness stuck to me no matter where I went, who I was with. I knew I should make friends with the other servants, but I couldn’t do it. They all looked at me like they knew what I’d been, like they were watching me to steal something. I don’t regret them making me try to be a better woman—but it was such a tired, lonely life. You came into it like a flash of sunshine at first, and then you forgot all about me, just like everyone else. I was fool enough to fall in love with you, and I couldn’t even get you to notice I existed. That hurt worse than anything.

“Now I’ll tell you the part that matters. Back in those bitter, lonely days, I snuck off two or three times on my afternoons off to my favourite spot—the beach right above the Shivering Sand. I used to say to myself, ‘I think this is where it ends. When I can’t bear it any more, this is where I’ll go.’ That place had a spell over me even before you arrived. I always had a weird feeling something big would happen to me there. But I never looked at that quicksand like it was the place I could go to end all my troubles, hide me forever, until the days I just told you about.

“That’s everything leading up to the morning everyone in the house started yelling that the Moonstone was gone.

“I was so sick of all the silly gossip among the other servants, all of them whispering about who to suspect first, and so mad at you for working so hard to hunt down the Diamond and send for the police, that I stuck to myself as much as I could, until the Frizinghall superintendent, Mr. Seegrave, turned up at the house.

“You remember how he started? He posted a guard on all the women’s bedrooms, and every last one of the maids stormed upstairs after him in a rage, demanding to know what he thought he was doing, insulting them like that. I went with the rest of them, because if I’d stayed back, he’d have marked me as suspicious immediately. We found him in Miss Rachel’s bedroom. He yelled that he didn’t want a crowd of women cluttering the room, pointed at that smudge of wet paint on the door, and said one of our long petticoats must have brushed against it, then sent all of us back downstairs.

“After we left Miss Rachel’s room, I stopped on the landing for a minute by myself, checking to see if any of that paint had gotten on my own gown. Penelope Betteredge was the only servant I was even halfway friendly with, and she walked past and saw me staring down at my skirt.

“‘You don’t need to waste time worrying about that, Rosanna,’ she said. ‘That paint on Miss Rachel’s door has been dry for hours. If Mr. Seegrave wasn’t such an idiot, I’d have told him that already. I’ve never been so insulted in my whole life.’

“Penelope had a hot temper. I calmed her down, and asked her how she could be so sure the paint was dry. ‘I was in that room with Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin all yesterday morning, mixing their colours while they finished painting the door,’ she said. ‘I heard Miss Rachel ask if the door would be dry that evening, for the birthday party guests to see. Mr. Franklin shook his head and said it wouldn’t be fully dry for twelve whole hours. They didn’t finish until after luncheon, three o’clock in the afternoon. Do the math, Rosanna. That door was totally dry by three o’clock this morning.’

“‘Did any of the ladies go upstairs to look at it last night?’ I asked. ‘I thought I heard Miss Rachel warning everyone not to go near the door.’

“‘None of them made that paint smudge,’ Penelope said. ‘I helped Miss Rachel into bed at twelve o’clock last night, and I glanced at the door then. There wasn’t a mark on it.’

“‘Shouldn’t you tell Mr. Seegrave that?’ I said.

“‘I wouldn’t say a single word to help that fool Seegrave for all the money in the world.’

“She went back to her work, and I went to mine. My job that morning was to make your bed and tidy your room. That was the happiest hour of my whole day. I used to kiss the pillow your head rested on all night. No one’s ever folded your clothes half as neatly as I did, or polished every little knick-knack in your dressing case until there wasn’t a single speck of dust on any of them. You never noticed it, same as you never noticed me. Forgive me, I’m rambling. I’ll get on with it.

“So I went into your room that morning to tidy up, and there was your nightgown tossed across the bed, exactly the way you’d thrown it off when you woke up. I picked it up to fold it—and there it was, the smudge of that very same wet paint on the fabric.

“I was so shocked I ran straight out of the room, nightgown clutched in my hand, locked myself in my own bedroom, and shut the door so no one could interrupt me.

“As soon as I caught my breath, I thought back to what Penelope had just told me. This is proof, I thought, that you were in Miss Rachel’s sitting-room between twelve last night and three o’clock this morning!

“I won’t spell out what the first terrible thought was that popped into my head right then. You’d only get angry, and tear my letter up before you could finish it. Let me just say this: I thought it through as best I could, and I decided it couldn’t be true, because if you’d been in that room at that hour with Miss Rachel’s full knowledge, she’d never have let you walk away with that nightgown, carrying the evidence right off with you. But I still wasn’t 100% sure I was right. You know I hated Miss Rachel a little—there’s no use lying about that now. But I decided to lock that nightgown up in my drawer, wait, watch, and see what I could do with it. Back then, sir, I never even dreamed that you were the one who stole the Moonstone.”

I broke off reading right there, and pushed the letter across the table to Betteredge. I’d read all those earlier parts of her confession about how she’d loved me in total shock, and true, unfeigned distress. I already regretted the cruel, careless accusation I’d flung at her memory before I knew what she’d been through. But now that I’d gotten that far, the bitter resentment was creeping over me, hot and sharp. “You read the rest for yourself,” I told him. “If there’s anything I absolutely have to know, you can fill me in as you go.”

“I understand, Mr. Franklin,” he said, nodding slowly. “It’s only natural for you to feel that way. And Lord help us all, it was just as natural for her to feel the way she did.”
