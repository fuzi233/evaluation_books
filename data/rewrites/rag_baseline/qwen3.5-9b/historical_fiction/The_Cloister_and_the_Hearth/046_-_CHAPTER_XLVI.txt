To understand the architecture of Catherine's domestic universe, one must first grasp the precarious balance she maintained between duty and desire. She was a woman of impeccable housekeeping, yet her very virtue rendered her perpetually anxious; she feared that even a single day's absence would unravel the fragile tapestry of her home. Thus, her planned journey to Sevenbergen was deferred day by day, postponed until the domestic tide receded sufficiently to allow passage. Finally, fortune aligned: Peter Buyskens' mule was occupied elsewhere, clearing the path for departure. Yet, fate intervenes where human planning falters. One morning, Eli summoned the entire household to witness a confrontation.

"Eli asked her publicly, before the gathered kin, if it were true she harbored thoughts of visiting Margaret Brandt."

"Ay, my man," she admitted softly.

"Then I forbid it," Eli declared, his voice cutting through the room like a blade.

"Oh, do you?" she challenged, her color rising.

"I do."

"There is nothing further to discuss, then," she murmured, flushing deeper.

"Not a word," Eli replied, his sternness absolute.

Once the family dispersed, Catherine retreated to the privacy of her chamber, where she turned her severity inward, directing her wrath not at Eli, but at her own capitulation. She berated herself for behaving like a cat cornered by a robin—a creature driven to panic by mere proximity—or a hen that cannot lay an egg without announcing its intent to the whole flock. "Next time," she vowed to herself, "if we must pursue something amiss, let us act immediately, in the heat of the moment, rather than deliberating and speaking aloud. If questioned later, we might claim ignorance, feign innocence, and escape scrutiny. For two pence, I would have gone to Sevenbergen openly, amidst their teeth and judgment."

Such defiance, wild and picturesque, staggered Kate. "Nay, mother," she pleaded gently, "with patience, father will relent."

"And so will Michaelmas," Catherine retorted, her spirit hardening. "But when? I was so eager for you to see the girl. Only then could we have united our counsel regarding her. Let them speak what they will; no judge or beast can be truly known except by sight. Were I to bear fifty more sons, I would never thwart one's affection until I had laid eyes upon the object of their desire and verified the truth. Perhaps I should have considered this before condemning Gerard's fancy. But life is a relentless school, the lesson never finished. We discard one fault only to embrace another, stumbling blindly here and there until we stumble into our graves, and there ends our tale."

"Mother," Kate interjected timidly.

"Well, what brings this shadow to your face? No good tidings, surely. What terrorizes this poor wretch?"

"An avowal she intends to make," Kate faltered, her voice barely audible.

"Now, there is a noble sentiment for you," Catherine exclaimed, pride swelling in her chest. "Gerard taught you that phrase, I give you my oath. Come forth with your confession."

"Well then, truthfully, I have seen her."

"And?"

"And I spoke with her as well."

"And you withheld this from me? Now wonders are merely dust."

"Mother, you burned with such hatred against her. I waited until I could speak without provoking greater anger."

"Ay," Catherine sighed, a mixture of sadness and bitterness coloring her tone. "Like mother, like daughter; cowardice is our bane. I occasionally buffet the others, or how would the house endure? But did you, Kate, ever receive a harsh word or a cruel glance from your poor mother, that you..." She paused, seeing her daughter's distress. "Nay, I will not have you weep, girl. Ten to one you acted rightly. Rise, brave heart, and tell me everything. Better late than never. First and foremost, when and how did you travel to Sevenbergen with your crutches, and I remain unaware?"

"I never set foot there in my life," Kate confessed. "And, mammy dear, to say that I never wished to see her—that I refused—is false. But I never went, nor did I seek her out."

"There now," Catherine disputed, shaking her head. "Did I not say it was entirely unlike my girl to seek someone unknown to me? Come, I am all agog."

"Thus it happened. News reached my ears, by whatever means, and pray, good mother, on your knees, never ask me how. Gerard was a prisoner in the Stadthouse tower."

"Ah."

"Under the pretext of Father's command."

Catherine exhaled a sigh that bordered on a moan. "Blackest than I imagined," she whispered faintly.

"Giles and I ventured out at night to encourage him. And there, at the foot of the tower, stood a brave maiden, utterly strange to me, I swear, on the same mission."

"Lookee there now, Kate."

"At first, we frightened one another profoundly, haunted by his notorious reputation and our minds filled with demons. In the moonlight, we paled with fear. But in the next instant, I cried, 'You are Margaret.' And she replied, 'And you are Kate.' Think on it!"

"Did anyone ever? 'Twas Gerard! He must have spoken endlessly of you to her, and her to you."

In return for this revelation, Kate bestowed upon Catherine one of nature's finest gifts—the composite kiss. She pressed a single kiss to her mother's cheek, conveying four distinct meanings simultaneously: first, complete correctness; second, acknowledgment of her mother's sharp intuition; third, the sweetness of their minds aligning again after discord; fourth, and so on.

"Now then, speak freely, child. Gerard is not here. Alas, what am I saying? Would to Heaven he were."

"Well then, mother, she is comely, and her likeness bears her little resemblance to the truth."

"Eh, dear; listen to the youth! I value good deeds, not good looks. Does she love my boy as he deserves to be loved?"

"Sevenbergen lies farther from the Stadthouse than we do," Kate reflected. "Yet she arrived there before me."

Catherine nodded, understanding dawning.

"Nay, more," Kate continued, her voice gaining strength. "She had already secured his release before I arrived. Yes, she lowered him from the captive's tower."

Catherine shook her head in disbelief. "The highest tower for miles! It is not feasible."

"'Tis true nonetheless. She and an old man she accompanied devised means and wit to lower him via a rope. There he hung, suspended from his prison, and our Giles ascended it. When I first beheld the rope hanging there, I remarked, 'This is magic.' But when the frank lass's arms embraced me, and her bosom beat against mine, and her cheeks grew wet, then I realized, 'Tis not magic: 'tis love.' For she is not like me; she is robust and capable. And, dear heart, even I, poor frail creature, sometimes feel I could move the world for those I cherish: I love you, mother. And she loves Gerard."

"God bless her for it! God bless her!" Catherine cried out.

"But..."

"But what, lamb?"

"Is her love founded on certainty and honesty? 'Tis most strange; yet that very quality which warms your heart has somewhat cooled mine toward her. Poor soul. She is no wife, you know, mother, when all is said and done."

"Humph! They stood at the altar together."

"Ay, but they departed as they arrived: maid and bachelor."

"The parson claims so?"

"Nay, for that I know not."

"Then I will accept no man's word but his own in such a tangled skein." After a moment of reflection, she added, "Nevertheless, you are right, girl. I shall go to Sevenbergen alone. A wife I am, but not a slave. We are all in darkness here. She holds the key. I must question her, and no one else—not even you. I will not carry a lily to a house marked with a stain, no, not to a palace of gold and silver."

As Catherine pondered this resolve, she felt increasingly drawn to Margaret. Moreover, she was "all agog" with curiosity, a potent passion shared by all, and nearly omnipotent for those like Catherine who do not slake it with books. Finally, one bright day after dinner, she whispered to Kate, "Keep the house from falling apart, if you can," donned her finest kirtle and hood, paired with scarlet clocked hose and new shoes, and marched briskly toward Sevenbergen, troubling no man's mule.

Upon arrival, she inquired where Margaret Brandt resided. The first person she approached shook his head. "The name is strange to me." She walked slightly farther and addressed a girl of about fifteen standing at a doorway. "Father," the girl called out into the house, "here is another after that magician's daughter." The man emerged and informed Catherine that Peter Brandt's cottage lay just outside the town on the eastern side. "You may see the chimney from here," he indicated, pointing upward. "But you will not find them there, neither father nor daughter; they have left the town this week, bless you."

"Say not so, good man, and me walking all the way from Tergou."
