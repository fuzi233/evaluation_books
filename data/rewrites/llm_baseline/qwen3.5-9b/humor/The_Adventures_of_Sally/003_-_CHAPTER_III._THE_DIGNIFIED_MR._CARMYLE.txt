By six o'clock the next evening, Sally had reached the grim conclusion that Ginger would have to navigate life's rough seas without the benefit of her intended commentary. Having spotted absolutely no sign of him all day, she knew that in another hour she would be boarding the seven-fifteen express to Paris, en route for Cherbourg and the transatlantic liner bound for New York. Driven by a lingering hope that he might still be lurking somewhere, she abandoned her luggage to an obliging porter at the station and made one final pilgrimage to the *Casino Municipale*.

Sally possessed a trait common to many alert, active-minded girls: an irresistible urge to interfere in—or, as her brother Fillmore termed it, "mess about with"—the private affairs of others. She had judged Ginger Kemp to be a man worthy of a friendly shove toward the right path. Consequently, she felt a surge of gratification upon entering the casino and spotting a flaming head cutting through the crowd at a roulette table.

There were two casinos in Roville-sur-Mer. The one on the promenade catered to sea breezes and a gentle game called *boule*. However, the grand *Casino Municipale*, nestled in the Palais Masséna near the railway station, was the sanctuary of the serious gambler. It was immediately obvious to Sally that Ginger wasn't just playing; he was doing business, and he was winning big.

Ginger was playing with ferocious intensity. He was entrenched behind a towering mound of square counters, and even as Sally watched, a stoic croupier slid another stack of chips across the green felt to him at the end of his long rake.

"Epatant!" murmured a wistful gentleman beside Sally, removing his elbow from her ribs to gesture wildly. Though Sally wasn't a French scholar, she understood the sentiment: he was startled and delighted. Indeed, the entire room seemed seized by a collective shock and joy. There is a peculiar altruism among spectators at a Continental roulette table; they derive a spiritual thrill from witnessing another's victory.

The croupier twisted his moustache with his left hand and the wheel with his right, and silence descended once more. Sally, having maneuvered to a spot where the crush of bodies was slightly less acute, finally caught a glimpse of Ginger's face. Upon seeing it, she burst into an involuntary laugh. He looked precisely like a dog sniffing a rat-hole. His hair bristled with excitement, and one could almost imagine his ears perked up.

As the crowd fell silent again for the restart of the wheel, Sally's laugh rang out with an embarrassingly clear volume. It had a profound effect on everyone nearby. There is something akin to religious ecstasy in the demeanor of roulette spectators when someone is on a hot streak; had she guffawed inside a cathedral, she likely wouldn't have caused more pained consternation. The earnest worshippers gazed at her with shocked expressions, and Ginger, turning with a start, saw her and leaped to his feet.

At that exact moment, the ball clattered into a red compartment. As the wheel slowed and it became apparent that the big winner had chosen the wrong color, a shuddering groan rippled through the congregation, reminiscent of the convulsions on a penitent's bench during a revival meeting. More glares of reproach were directed at Sally. The general consensus was that her indiscreet behavior had cursed Ginger's luck.

The only person seemingly unaffected was Ginger himself. He gathered his winnings, stuffed them into his pocket, and pushed his way through the crowd, which now regarded Sally as a social pariah. There was a universal sense of regret that he had decided to call it a day. To the spectators, it was as though a star had abruptly exited the stage mid-performance. Not even a loud, violent argument erupting between two excited gamblers over a disputed five-franc chip could fully soothe their disappointment.

"I say," Ginger said, skillfully extracting Sally from the throng, "this is topping, meeting you like this. I've been looking for you everywhere."

"It's funny you didn't find me, then, for that's exactly where I've been. I was looking for you."

"No, really?" Ginger seemed genuinely pleased. He guided her to the quiet ante-room outside the gambling hall, and they settled into a corner. It was pleasant here, devoid of noise save for the gorgeously uniformed attendant standing guard by the door.

"That was awfully good of you," Ginger said.

"I felt I must have a talk with you before my train left."

Ginger started violently. "Your train? What do you mean?"

"The puff-puff," Sally explained. "I'm leaving tonight, you know."

"Leaving?" Ginger looked as horrified as the devout congregation Sally had just alienated. "You don't mean leaving? You're not going away from Roville?"

"I'm afraid so."

"But why? Where are you going?"

"Back to America. My boat sails from Cherbourg tomorrow."

"Oh, my aunt!"

"I'm sorry," Sally said, touched by his concern. She was a warm-hearted girl who enjoyed being appreciated. "But..."

"I say..." Ginger Kemp turned a brilliant shade of scarlet and glared ahead at the uniformed official, who was watching their tête-à-tête with the indulgent eye of someone experienced in such matters. "I say, look here, will you marry me?"

Sally stared at his vermilion profile in frank amazement. Ginger, she realized, was a surprising young man, but she hadn't anticipated him being *this* surprising.

"Marry you!"

"You know what I mean."

"Well, yes, I suppose I do. You allude to the holy state. Yes, I know what you mean."

"Then how about it?"

Sally began to regain her composure. Her sense of humor was tickled. She looked at Ginger gravely. He refused to meet her eye, instead continuing to stare at the uniformed official, who was now so swept away by the romance of the situation that he had begun humming a love ballad under his breath. The official couldn't hear them, nor would he have understood them even if he could; but he was an expert in the language of the eyes.

"But isn't this—don't think I'm making difficulties—isn't this a little sudden?"

"It's got to be sudden," Ginger complained. "I thought you were going to be here for weeks."

"But, my infant, my babe, hasn't it occurred to you that we are practically strangers?" She patted his hand tolerantly, causing the official to heave a tender sigh.

"I see what's happening," she said. "You're mistaking me for some other girl, someone you actually know and were properly introduced to. Take a good look at me, and you'll see."

"If I take a good look at you," Ginger said feverishly, "I'm dashed if I'll answer for the consequences."

"And this is the man I was going to lecture on 'Enterprise.'"

"You're the most wonderful girl I've ever met, dash it!" Ginger exclaimed, his gaze still riveted on the official by the door. "I dare say it is sudden. I can't help that. I fell in love with you the moment I saw you, and there you are!"

"But..."

"Now, look here, I know I'm not much of a chap and all that, but... well, I've just won the deuce of a lot of money in there..."

"Would you buy me with your gold?"

"I mean to say, we'd have enough to start on, and... of course, I've made an infernal hash of everything I've tried up till now, but there must be something I can do. With you to buck me up and so forth, don't you know. Well, I mean..."

"Has it struck you that I may already be engaged to someone else?"

"Oh, golly! Are you?" For the first time, he turned to face her, and there was a look in his eyes that touched Sally and banished all sense of the ludicrous. Absurd as it was, this man was truly serious.

"Well, yes, as a matter of fact, I am," she said soberly.

Ginger Kemp bit his lip and remained silent for a moment. "Oh, well, that's torn it!" he said finally.

Sally felt an emotion too complex to analyze. There was pity in it, but also amusement. The feeling, though unrecognized, was maternal. Mothers, listening to their children plead with engaging absurdity for something entirely out of their power to grant, feel that same wavering between tears and laughter. Sally wanted to pick Ginger up and kiss him. The one thing she couldn't do was look at him, sorry as she was for him, as a reasonable, grown-up man.

"You don't really mean it, you know."

"Don't I!" said Ginger, hollowly. "Oh, don't I!"

"You can't! There isn't such a thing in real life as love at first sight. Love comes when you know a person well and..." She paused. It had just occurred to her that she was hardly the girl to deliver such a lecture. Her own love for Gerald Foster had been sufficiently sudden, even instantaneous. What did she know of Gerald except that she loved him? They had become engaged within two weeks of their first meeting. This recollection dampened her eloquence, and she ended tamely: "It's ridiculous."
