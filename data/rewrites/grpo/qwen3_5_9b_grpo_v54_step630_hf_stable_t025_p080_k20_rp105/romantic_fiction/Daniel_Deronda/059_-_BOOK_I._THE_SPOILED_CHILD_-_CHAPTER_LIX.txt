"I count myself in nothing else so happy
As in a soul remembering my good friends."

Sir Hugo Mallinger did not depart for Genoa with the haste that had characterized Mr. Gascoigne's movements, and Daniel Deronda resolved to delay his own exit until he had secured a private audience with the baronet. The shadow of Grandcourt's death, compounded by the recent upheaval in his own life, demanded a face-to-face communion that letters could not convey; specifically, Deronda felt unable to detail the fleeting, spectral presence of his mother in written correspondence. It was not until the fifth evening, guided by a telegram, that Deronda awaited Sir Hugo at the station, expecting his arrival between eight and nine. As he gazed toward the platform, anticipating the sight of a familiar face rooted in his earliest memories, a faint smile touched his lips despite the tragedy that had recently marred his spirit. He imagined the baronet's delight in finally mastering his estates, perhaps even the joy of securing a legacy through a grandson, or at least, through the adoption of a makeshift feminine line. One cannot be a miser with another's joy, unless that joy contradicts one's own rigid ideals of righteousness; to do so would only sour the soul, freezing its gaze while one's own desires remained stubbornly misaligned. Yet, there is a necessary comradeship with imperfection. It is possible to feel gratitude even when witnessing a mistake born of affectionate intent, executed over a lifetime of kindness.

Deronda's conscience recoiled from Sir Hugo's role in perpetuating a falsehood—the very word he used to describe the concealment of his origins. Still, the baronet likely lacked clear knowledge of his mother's breach of trust. With his characteristic lightness of spirit, Sir Hugo had deemed it reasonable to raise his son as an English gentleman, especially given his mother's eccentric refusal to sever ties with her child. Daniel's deep affection for Sir Hugo drove him to seek excuses rather than blame. It is as easy to be rigid in principle yet tender in censure as it is to be disturbed by disorder while remaining patient with the one who perceives it wrongly. Had Sir Hugo, in his bachelor days, viewed children merely as accessories to adult comfort, he had shared a common, unspoken assumption of that era. Deronda, recalling his own painful internal struggles, remembered that such experiences had been entirely absent from Sir Hugo's worldview. Ignorant kindness can inflict cruelty, but to condemn it as direct malice is itself a form of ignorance, alien to Deronda's broad capacity for leniency. Perhaps, after the harrowing revelations of the last ten days, which had lifted the veil on lives unlike his own, Daniel was simply less inclined toward the rash indignation that often masks a desire for punishment. When Sir Hugo's familiar figure emerged from the train, the lifelong bond of affection washed over him, drowning out any fresh grounds for blame.

"Well, Dan," Sir Hugo said, seizing Daniel's hand with earnest gravity. No further greeting was needed; the air between them hummed with mutual understanding. They directed the courier and then proposed a slow walk under the mild evening sky, unhurried by the need to reach the hotel.

"I traveled easily and am in fine condition," Sir Hugo remarked as they stepped out beneath the stars, still faint with the day's lingering glow. "I took my time to investigate matters, which allowed me to read your letter to Lady Mallinger before I left. But now, how fares the widow?"

"Growing calmer," Daniel replied. "She seems to have escaped the physical illness one might have feared following her plunge and the terrible excitement. Her uncle and mother arrived two days ago, and she is being cared for well."

"Is there any chance of an heir?"

"Based on what Mr. Gascoigne told me, I doubt it," Daniel said. "He spoke as if the question was merely whether the widow would retain the estates for her lifetime."

"Will the loss of her husband be a great wrench to her affections?" Sir Hugo asked, glancing around.

"The suddenness of his death was a severe blow," Daniel answered, gently sidestepping the deeper implications.

"Do you know the terms of Grandcourt's will?"

"Yes," Sir Hugo replied promptly. "If there is no legitimate heir, he has left everything to a boy he had with a Mrs. Glasher. You know nothing of this, I presume? She was a companion to him for many years, and they have three older daughters. The boy is to take his father's name; he is already known as Henleigh, and he will become Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt. The Mallinger fortune will be of no use to him, thank heavens, but the young rascal will have more than enough to sustain him during his fourteen-year minority—no need to fill the gaps with my fifty thousand pounds meant for Diplow, which he had no right to touch. Meanwhile, my beauty, the young widow, is to settle for a mere two thousand a year and the house at Gadsmere. A pleasant exile, if she chooses to lock herself away there, though I doubt she will. His mother has lived there for years. I am thoroughly disgusted with Grandcourt. I cannot say I hold him in higher regard because he drowned, though regarding my own affairs, nothing in his life suited him better than ending it."

"In my view, he erred in marrying this woman—not necessarily in leaving his estates to the son," Daniel said, his tone dry.

"I take no issue with him bequeathing the land to the lad," Sir Hugo countered. "But since he married her, he should have provided a substantial allowance, sufficient for her to maintain the rank he had elevated her to. Four or five thousand a year and the London house for her life—that is what I would have done. Given she was penniless, her friends surely could not demand a formal settlement, otherwise, one cannot trust a man's will made after marriage. Even the wisest men often let some folly leak into their wills; my father did, I know. If a man harbors spite or tyranny, he is likely to bottle it up in such documents. It is clear Grandcourt intended his death to extinguish his wife's prospects if she failed to bear him an heir."

"And in the other scenario, I suppose everything would have been reversed—illegitimacy receiving the extinguisher?" Daniel asked, with a note of scorn.

"Precisely—Gadsmere and the two thousand. It is peculiar. One annoyance is that Grandcourt has named me executor; but as he was the son of my only brother, I cannot refuse. I shall mind it less if I can assist the widow. Lush suggests she was not ignorant of the family situation or the will's intent, hinting at a lack of harmony between the couple. But I fancy you are the one who knew best what Mrs. Grandcourt felt or did not feel, eh, Dan?" Sir Hugo dropped his usual jocularity, speaking with a lowered, genuinely interested tone. Daniel felt that evasion would be misconstrued.

"She was certainly not happy. They were ill-suited. But regarding the property, judging by what I have observed of her, I predict she will be quite content with it."

"Then she is unlike most women," Sir Hugo shrugged. "Still, she must be extraordinary, for there must be an entanglement between your horoscope and hers—eh? When that tremendous telegram arrived, the first thing Lady Mallinger said was, 'How very strange that it should be Daniel who sends it!' But I have experienced something similar in my own life. Once, at a foreign hotel, I found a lady abandoned by her husband with no money. When I stepped forward to help, who should it be but an early flame of mine, foolish enough to marry an Austrian baron with a long mustache and short affection? But it was an affair of my own that brought me there—nothing resembling knight-errantry, any more than your journey to Genoa had to do with the Grandcourts."

A silence fell between them. Sir Hugo had shifted to the safer topic of the Grandcourts, yet both men felt a reluctance to achieve total frankness regarding the events binding them. Daniel sensed that his letter, following the first meeting with his mother, had acted more as a thickening of the ice than a breaker. He waited for Sir Hugo to open the door. Just as they were about to lose sight of the port, the baronet paused, turning for a final look, his voice taking on a more serious hue. "And regarding the main purpose of your coming to Genoa, Dan? Have you not been deeply pained by what you have learned? Is there nothing that makes you wish to alter your position? Remember, whatever happens to you will always matter to me."

"I wish to meet your goodness with perfect confidence, sir," Daniel replied. "But I cannot answer those questions with a simple yes or no. Much of what I have heard about the past has pained me. It was painful to meet and part from my mother in her suffering state, as I was compelled to do. But knowing my parentage clears up doubts for which I am grateful. As for my position, my gratitude to you for your fatherly care remains unchanged. However, learning that I was born a Jew may have a momentous influence on my life, one I am hardly able to articulate at present."

Daniel spoke the final sentence with a resolve that overcame his diffidence. He felt that the differences between Sir Hugo's nature and his own would soon reveal themselves more sharply than ever before. The baronet cast him a quick glance and turned to walk on. After a moment of silence, during which he weighed the meaning of Daniel's words, he spoke again.

"I have long expected something remarkable from you, Dan; but, for God's sake, do not descend into eccentricities! I can tolerate any difference of opinion, provided a man states it without dressing himself up as a lunatic. In this age, if a man wishes to be taken seriously, he must avoid melodrama. Do not misunderstand me. I do not suspect you of inventing madness on your own. I only fear you might be led arm-in-arm with a lunatic, especially if he sought a defender. You have a passion for those who are pelted, Dan. I pity them too; but as a basis for companionship, it is poor ground. Nevertheless, I do not ask you to anticipate your inclinations. When you decide on a course requiring funds, I have some sixteen thousand pounds accumulating for you, beyond the interest you have received. And now that I am here, do you wish to return to England as soon as possible?"

"I must first go to Mainz to retrieve a chest belonging to my grandfather, and perhaps visit a friend of his," Daniel said. "Though the chest has lain there for twenty years, I feel an unreasonable nervous eagerness to secure it under my care, as if it were more likely now that something might happen to it. And perhaps I am more uneasy because I lingered after my mother left, instead of departing immediately. Yet, I cannot regret staying—otherwise, Mrs. Grandcourt would have had only servants to act for her."

"Yes, yes," Sir Hugo replied, with a flippancy that masked a hidden vexation; "I hope you are not going to set a dead Jew above a living Christian."

Daniel flushed and suppressed a retort as they turned toward the *Italia*.
