The evening hung heavy with the promise of rain as Romola made her way back from the hospital of San Matteo, her lantern casting a frail glow against the gathering dark. Maso trudged beside her, his short steps measured and slow. At the monastery of San Marco, a familiar figure emerged—Tito, stepping briskly into the damp air.

Their encounters had become a careful dance of civility, each movement calculated to avoid the jagged edges of their estrangement. He offered to escort her home, dismissing Maso with a wave. It was a perfunctory kindness, one of the few he extended when propriety demanded it.

They spoke of trivial things—his recent travels, the affairs of the city—each word a polished stone laid to bridge the chasm between them. Romola had long since abandoned the hope of true reconciliation. Their marriage was a fresco of pleasant surfaces, its cracks artfully concealed beneath layers of courtesy.

As they neared a loggia, its arched shelter lit by a flickering lamp before a painted Madonna, Tito tensed. A murmur of approaching voices reached them.

"Come," he said abruptly, guiding her beneath the loggia’s cover.

Romola frowned. "The rain is barely more than mist."

"It will worsen," he insisted, his eyes scanning the street.

Then she saw them—a band of men led by a figure whose swagger was unmistakable even in shadow: Dolfo Spini, captain of the Compagnacci, his sword hilt glinting.

Tito’s grip tightened on her arm.

Spini’s grating voice cut through the damp air. "Ha! My little messenger! Hiding from your friends?"

Romola felt Tito’s stillness beside her, a predator poised between flight and fight.

"What of tomorrow’s hunt?" Spini pressed, too loud, too eager. "Will the quarry take the bait?"

Tito’s voice was smooth, amused. "Prepare as you like, Dolfo. But leave the trumpets behind."

Spini scoffed. "No need to lecture me. The beast swallowed it whole, then?"

A procession of torches approached—a church dignitary’s retinue—and Tito seized the moment. "The plague spreads, they say. A grim sight for revelers."

With a muttered curse, Spini melted into the dark.

Romola turned on Tito, her voice low and shaking. "What treachery is this?"

He met her gaze, unreadable. "A plot to lure Savonarola beyond the gates. But fear not—I mean to thwart it."

"Tell me the truth."

"The truth is, I serve Florence. The Frate’s safety is secured."

Her breath came sharp. "I don’t believe you."

A shrug. "Then don’t."

"If he is harmed, I will denounce you before the Signoria."

Tito’s smile was cold. "And disgrace yourself with me? Bold words, wife."

"I am your conscience, whether you will it or not."

For a heartbeat, his mask slipped—a flicker of irritation, of fear. Then it was gone. "Enough. You will not race to San Marco like a frantic novice. I forbid it."

The clatter of passersby forced civility. He took her arm, steering her homeward through the drizzle.

When they walked again in silence, his voice was lighter, as if smoothing ruffled feathers. "You see? Spini’s drunken bluster means nothing. Go to bed, Romola. Sleep without fear."

But she would not sleep. Not while the night whispered of knives in the dark, and the man beside her wore lies like a second skin.
