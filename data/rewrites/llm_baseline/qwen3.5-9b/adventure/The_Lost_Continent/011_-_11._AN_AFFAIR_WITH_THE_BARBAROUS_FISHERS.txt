"Does this mighty Empress choose to be jealous of a mere woman prisoner?"

My mind, honed by years of military discipline to operate with soldierly swiftness during moments of crisis, seized upon the correct course the instant those words left her lips. I was bound by the High Council of Priests to sacrifice myself for Atlantis, and while necessity demanded that Nais be sacrificed alongside me, a scheme to save her struck me in the same fleeting moment.

I bowed deeply before the Empress and declared, "In this, and in all other matters where a human hand holds sway, I shall execute your wishes, Phorenice." She patted my arm, and fresh currents of emotion surged from the depths of her wondrous eyes. Truly, the gods had granted her half her schemes and half her victories simply by granting her form, her voice, and the magnetic allure etched into her features.

By now, the merchants and neighbors dwelling near the royal quay had descended upon the scene, offering changes of clothing and safe lodging. Phorenice radiated grace. Though I cared little for the dampness soaking my coat, the section of the harbor into which the mammoth had tossed us held no savory appeal, and I gladly followed her lead. I spoke no further word to Nais, refraining even from a final glance of farewell. Yet, this small concession was insufficient nourishment for Phorenice's ego; she issued strict orders to the port captain to guard his prisoner before departing.

Inside the merchant's house, I was ushered into a bath and relieved of my plainest garments, leaving the host pleased to have parted with them so cheaply. However, I spent an hour standing on the pavement, listening to the distant roar of bombardment, before Phorenice emerged again. I could not suppress a grim amusement at the face of the merchant trailing behind her; he was clearly ruined. His store of jewels and gauds—costly items he had purchased in partnership with others—had been stripped entirely by Phorenice's eye.

"Are they not pretty?" she asked, displaying them to me. "See how they flash under the sun. I am quite glad, Deucalion, that the mammoth gave us that furious ride and spill, for it brought me such a bonny present. Tell this fellow that when he earns more, I shall visit as his guest. Ah! Litters have arrived. Send one away; you shall share mine with me, sir. We must pretend to be lovers today, even if true love awaits us both with greater certainty tomorrow. Do not order more bearers; my own slaves will suffice. I am glad you are not one of your gross, overfed men, Deucalion. I am small and slim, and I dislike being 'husbanded' by a man who might overshadow me."

"Back to the royal pyramid?" I asked.

"No, nor to the walls. I neither wish to fight nor sit as Empress today, sir. As I have told you, it is my whim to be Phorenice, the maiden, for a few hours. If someone I know would woo me now, as other maidens are wooed, I should esteem it a luxury. Bid the slaves carry us around the harbor's rim and warn these starers that if they follow, I will call down fire upon them as I did upon the sacrifice."

Having witnessed the street unrest firsthand and gathered hints from the pyramid gatekeeper regarding the Empress's welcoming banquet, I deemed it unnecessary to remind her that such behavior was already common knowledge. I dismissed the guard and drove away the throng of gaping sightseers with sharp words, silencing the silly creatures eager to stare at their betters. Seated in the litter, I watched as the bearers lifted the poles.

We swung away along the wide pavement separating the merchant houses from the mariners and the dimpling waters of the harbor. I felt a pang of sadness looking at the few ships floating in the splendid basin and the scant evidence of business on the quays. In former times, vessels were berthed so closely that many waited in the estuary outside the walls, prompting memorials to the King to double the port's capacity. Even then, in the age of oars and sails, when solar-powered machines were rare aids, a voyage to Egypt and back took a year. Of three ships setting out, one usually perished to the dangers of the deep or the terrible beasts haunting the waters. Yet, trade once roared with lusty life, expanding endlessly. Merchants were portly men who gave generously to the gods.

Now, however, the world seemed locked in arms, and trade had become vulgar. A wealthy merchant, if he existed, forgot his merchandise, swearing that haggling was more indecent than blasphemy, curling his beard in the new fashion, and becoming a courtier instead. While his father spent anxious days managing cargo tallies and shipmasters, the son wasted hours directing seamstresses and nights vaporing at banquets.

Smaller merchants, lacking reserves, were ground into starvation by taxes and the constant bickering of war. With the country in perpetual uproar, markets vanished, and nothing was produced that could be exported. If a weaver was pressed into making fire-tubes, he ceased weaving cloth; if a farmer played rebel, he stopped buying slaves to till his fields. Indeed, they told me that a month prior to my return, a fine cargo of slaves entered the harbor—the finest from Europe—and there was nowhere to sell them. They were cast ashore across the estuary to starve or live wild, for no man in all Atlantis would accept another slave as a gift.

Though grieved by this decline, remedies belonged to a later time. Speaking of it while riding in the litter would only worsen the mood. I was learning Phorenice's moods well enough. Still, I suspected she had studied mine, doing her best to engage me between her bursts of triviality.

We traveled until the westernmost harbor wall joined the land, where the panting bearers set us down. She led me into a solitary stone house built on a tidal promontory. After much unlocking, she revealed her new gold collectors.

In the dry knowledge taught in the colleges of the Sacred Mountain, it was a known fact that gold existed in a dissolved state within all seawater. Plans for extracting it into solid yellow bars, however, had never been discussed. Here, this field-raised upstart Empress had stumbled upon the trick as if it were written in a book.

She patted my arm laughingly as I stared around. "I tell all of Atlantis that only the gods possess this secret," she said. "That they gave it to me as one of themselves. But I am no goddess to you, am I, Deucalion? By my face! I have no other explanation for how this plan was invented. Let us suppose I dreamed it. Look! Seawater sluices in through that culvert, passes over these rough metal plates set in the floor, and flows out again. See the yellow metal caught in the ridges? That is gold. My fellows melt it with fire into bars and take it to my smith in the city. Tides vary constantly, as you priests know, drawn by the quiet moon. It requires little calculation to determine how much sea passes through these culverts monthly and how much gold is caught. My workers initially tried to cheat me, but I towed two of them underwater behind a galley until cannibal fish devoured them. Since then, the others have given me credit for... what do you think?"

"More divinity."

"I suppose it is that. But I am showing you the mechanics. Have the wit to work out a sum and see the effect. You will be a god among these silly Atlanteans if you will only use your cleverness."

Was she mocking me? Or was she sincere? I could not tell. Sometimes she attributed her successes to pure thought and brilliance; the next moment, she offered impossible explanations, leaving me to deduce her supernatural nature. Truthfully, this little woman, with her supple mind and body, mystified me increasingly. More and more, I grew despairing that Atlantis could ever be restored to peace and the ancient gods through my agency, even after fulfilling the High Council's commands and marrying her.

One plan seemed humanly possible: to curb her mischief with death, allowing the wretched country to recover naturally. It was a simple dagger stroke, executed instantly. Yet, the very idea revolted me. When the desperate thought arose, I clung to the answer that if such an act were fitting, the High Gods, in their infinite wisdom, would have issued explicit commands.

Nevertheless, the fascination of Phorenice proved overwhelming. When we left the gold collectors and stumbled into peril, a slight hesitation in holding my hand would have delivered her to the nether gods. Instead, I found myself fighting when she called upon me—a rarity for me. While some blame lies with the primal lust for battle that thrills even the coldest hearts when war cries ring, there is no doubt that the joy of protecting Phorenice, coupled with the distaste of seeing her torn down by those rude, uncouth fishers, infused my blows with special nerve and vehemence.

The root cause was the prevailing street violence and desperate poverty driving common folk to take any risk for a meal. We had remounted the litter, borne onward by the bearers' swinging trot. Phorenice recounted her new gold supplies and fresh sumptuary laws.
