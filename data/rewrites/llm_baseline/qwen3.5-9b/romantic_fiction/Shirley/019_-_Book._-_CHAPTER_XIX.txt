The air was crisp and clear, favoring the rapid kindling of stars as dusk settled over the countryside.

"There will be just light enough to show me the way home," Miss Keeldar remarked, preparing to bid farewell to Caroline at the rectory garden door.

"You must not go alone, Shirley; Fanny shall accompany you."

"That she shall not," Shirley replied firmly. "Of what need should I be afraid in my own parish? I would walk from Fieldhead to the church on any fine midsummer night, three hours past this hour, simply for the pleasure of seeing the stars and the chance of encountering a fairy."

"Just wait until the crowd disperses," Caroline suggested gently.

"Agreed," Shirley conceded. "There are the five Misses Armitage streaming by, Mrs. Sykes's phaeton, Mr. Wynne's close carriage, and Mrs. Birtwhistle's car. I have no desire to perform the tedious ceremony of bidding them all good-bye. Let us slip into the garden and take shelter among the laburnums for a moment."

Indeed, the rectors, their curates, and the churchwardens were emerging from the porch, engaging in animated conversation, shaking hands, offering congratulations on recent sermons, and advising caution regarding the night air. Gradually, the throng dissolved, and the carriages rolled away. As Shirley stepped out from her floral refuge, Mr. Helstone entered the garden, intercepting her.

"Oh, I wanted you!" he exclaimed, relief evident in his voice. "I feared you had already departed. Caroline, come here."

Caroline approached, anticipating—as Shirley did—a lecture on her absence during the service. However, the rector's mind was occupied with other matters.

"I shall not be sleeping at home tonight," Mr. Helstone announced. "I have encountered an old friend and promised to accompany him. I expect to return, perhaps, around noon tomorrow. Thomas, the clerk, is unavailable, and I cannot arrange for him to sleep in the house as I typically do when I am away overnight. Now..."

"Now," Shirley interrupted, "do you require me, the premier gentleman of Briarfield, to fill your place? To act as master of the rectory and guardian of your niece and maids while you are absent?"

"Exactly, Captain. I believe the position suits you. Will you grant Caroline the courtesy of becoming your guest for one night? Will you remain here rather than returning to Fieldhead?"

"And what of Mrs. Pryor? She expects me home."

"I shall send her word. Come, decide to stay. It is growing late, and the dew is falling heavily. You and Caroline will undoubtedly enjoy each other's company."

"I promise, then, to stay with Caroline," Shirley replied. "As you say, we shall not be separated tonight. Rejoin your old friend, Doctor, and fear nothing for us."

"If there should be any disturbance tonight, Captain—should you hear a lock being picked, a pane of glass cut out, or the stealthy tread of footsteps about the house (and I need not hesitate to tell *you*, who possess a well-tempered, mettlesome heart beneath your girl's ribbon sash, that such incidents are entirely possible in these times)—what would you do?"

"I do not know; I might faint, perhaps, fall down, and require someone to pick me up. But, Doctor, if you assign me this post of honor, you must provide me with arms. What weapons are available in your stronghold?"

"You could not wield a sword?"

"No; I could manage a carving-knife better."

"You will find a suitable one in the dining-room sideboard—a lady's knife, light to handle, and as sharp-pointed as a poniard."

"It will suit Caroline. But you must give me a brace of pistols. I know you have pistols."

"I have two pairs. I can place one pair at your disposal. You will find them suspended over the mantelpiece in my study, inside cloth cases."

"Are they loaded?"

"Yes, but not cocked. Cock them before you retire. It is a significant compliment, Captain, to lend you these. Were you part of the 'awkward squad,' you certainly would not deserve them."

"I will take care. You need delay no longer, Mr. Helstone. You may depart now." As the rector exited through the garden gate, Shirley remarked, "He is gracious to lend me his pistols." Turning to Caroline, she added, "Come, Lina, let us go inside and have supper. I was too distressed by the proximity of Mr. Sam Wynne at tea to eat anything, and now I am truly hungry."

Entering the house, they proceeded to the darkened dining-room. Through the open windows, the cool evening air drifted in, carrying the scent of garden flowers, the distant echo of retreating footsteps from the road, and a soft, vague murmur. Caroline explained the latter sound as she listened at the casement: "Shirley, I hear the beck in the Hollow."

She rang the bell, requesting a candle, bread, and milk—Shirley's customary supper, which happened to be her own as well. When Fanny brought the tray, she attempted to close the windows and shutters, but was asked to refrain. The twilight was too calm, its breath too balmy, to be excluded just yet. They ate in silence. Caroline rose once to move a vase of flowers from the sideboard to the window-sill, the fragrance being somewhat overpowering for the sultry room. Returning, she half-opened a drawer and withdrew something that glinted clear and keen in her hand.

"You assigned this to me, then, Shirley, did you?" Caroline asked, holding the blade. "It is bright, keen-edged, finely tapered; it looks dangerous. I have never felt the impulse to direct this against a fellow creature. It is difficult to imagine circumstances hard enough to nerve my arm to strike home with such a long knife."

"I should hate to do it," Shirley replied, "but I think I could, if driven by certain exigencies I can imagine." She quietly sipped her glass of fresh milk, appearing somewhat thoughtful and slightly pale—a complexion she rarely lacked, for she was never naturally florid.

Having finished their milk and bread, Fanny was summoned again. She and Eliza were sent to bed, eager to escape the fatigue of the day's exertions, including the baking of currant buns and the constant refilling of urns and teapots. Soon, the maid's chamber door clicked shut. Caroline took a candle and quietly inspected the entire house, ensuring every window was fastened and every door barred. She did not even spare a glance for the haunted back kitchen or the vault-like cellars. Satisfied, she returned.

"There is neither spirit nor flesh in the house that should not be there," she stated. "It is nearly eleven, fully bedtime. Yet, if you do not object, Shirley, I would prefer to sit up a little longer."

"Here," she continued, placing the brace of pistols retrieved from her uncle's study on the table before her friend. "You may examine them at your leisure."

"Why would you rather stay up?" Shirley asked, picking up the firearms, inspecting them, and setting them down again.

"Because I have a strange, excited feeling in my heart."

"So do I."

"Could this state of sleeplessness and restlessness be caused by something electrical in the air?"

"No; the sky is clear, the stars countless. It is a fine night."

"But very still. I hear the water fretting over its stony bed in Hollow's Copse as distinctly as if it flowed beneath the churchyard wall."

"I am glad it is so still. A moaning wind or rushing rain would vex me to fever just now."

"Why, Shirley?"

"Because it would baffle my efforts to listen."

"Do you listen toward the Hollow?"

"Yes; it is the only quarter from which we can hear a sound just now."

"The only one, Shirley."

Both women sat near the window, leaning their arms on the sill and inclining their heads toward the open lattice. By the starlight and the dim June twilight—which does not fully fade from the west until dawn breaks in the east—they could see each other's youthful faces.

"Mr. Helstone believes we have no idea which way he has gone," Miss Keeldar murmured, "nor on what errand, nor with what expectations, nor how prepared. But I suspect much; do you not?"

"I suspect something."

"All those gentlemen—including your cousin Moore—believe that you and I are currently asleep in our beds, unconscious."

"Caring nothing about them—hoping and fearing nothing for them," Caroline added.

Silence reigned for half an hour. The night was quiet, measured only by the church clock striking the quarters. Words were exchanged regarding the chill in the air. They wrapped their scarves tighter, replaced their removed bonnets, and resumed watching.

Just past midnight, the persistent, monotonous bark of the house-dog broke the tranquility of their vigil. Caroline rose silently, navigating the dark passages to the kitchen to appease him with a piece of bread. Upon returning to the dining-room, she found it completely dark; Shirley had extinguished the candle. The outline of her shape was visible near the still-open window, leaning outward.
