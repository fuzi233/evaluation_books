CHAPTER X. _1st Gent._ What woman should be? Sir, consult the taste of marriageable men. This planet’s store—iron, cotton, wool, or chemicals—all matter shaped by our skill—takes form only in response to demand. The market’s pulse sets high or low, by rule sublime. Our daughters must become wives, and wives must embody what men desire. Men’s taste is woman’s test. You mark the phrase? ’Tis good, I think—the sense well-winged and poised with t’s and s’s. _2nd Gent._ Nay, but turn it round: give us the test of taste. A fine _menu_—is it today what Roman epicures insisted a gentleman must eat to earn the dignity of dining well?

Brackenshaw Park, where the archery meeting was held, rose gently from its heights, overlooking the neighboring valley, the distant eastern downs, and the slow swell of cultivated land stretching westward like a vast curtain. The castle perched atop the highest plateau of the clustered hills, built of rough-hewn limestone streaked with shadows and dust from lichens, washed by rain. Beech and fir trees sheltered it on the north, spreading down the green slopes like flocks drawn to water glinting below.

The archery ground lay at the far end of the park—a carefully tended enclosure on a flat stretch of land, shielded from the southwest by tall elms and a thick screen of hollies. These cast a pleasant afternoon shade over the gravel walk and the freshly mown turf where the targets stood. To the north, the Archery Hall, with its arcade front, gleamed white against the greenery, like a temple among trees. It made a perfect backdrop for the flower-like groups of ladies who moved, bowed, and turned their necks with languid grace—just as lilies might if they could walk.

Even when the military band from Wanchester fell silent, the air remained alive with sound: musical laughter in every tone, friendly chatter rising now to gentle excitement, then sinking into soft murmurs. No outdoor event could feel more free from the crush and noise that ruin most modern pleasures. This archery gathering was select—only invited guests accompanying members, tickets strictly limited so that the evening dinner and ball within the castle would not overflow. Outside the enclosure, no common spectators were allowed except Lord Brackenshaw’s tenants and their families. Of these, it was mostly the women who came, bringing children, little brothers and sisters. The men amused themselves with fanciful bets—"anything you like"—on their favorite archers. But the young girls had a different measure: they dreamed of becoming like the elegantly dressed ladies before them. Perhaps the ideal form they’d longed for most was not Gwendolen’s—someone with rosy cheeks and golden hair, fashionably bright—but among the male eyes near her, there was rare agreement: she was the finest girl present.

No wonder she thrived that July day. Pre-eminence is sweet to those who love it—even under ordinary circumstances. It may not be mythical that a slave once took pride in being bought first; nor impossible that a barnyard fowl, though unaware it was "the best of a bad lot," might still strut with quiet self-knowledge. But true joy requires harmony between outward and inward. And for Gwendolen, that harmony was complete.

Who can deny that bows and arrows are among the most graceful weapons for feminine hands? They invite stances of elegance and strength, where the focus of marksmanship is freed from bloodshed. The old British habit of “killing something” is no longer practiced with bow and quiver; armies defend their passes under different skies than clouds of arrows. Poisoned darts survive only in rhetoric or faraway lands. Archery has no foul scent of brimstone; it harms no shins, breeds no grotesque athletes. Its only risk is failure—and for generous spirits, that is enough to shape mastery.

At Brackenshaw, prizes were noble and symbolic—not parcels of gain, but gold and silver arrows, stars worn proudly for months, then passed on to the next worthy. These tokens honored achievement without the burden of vanity. They were like wreaths without the sweat, avoiding the melancholy of overheated ballrooms. Altogether, the Brackenshaw Archery Club was an institution of refined taste—free by design from absurdity.

And today, fortune favored every detail. The sun warmed gently; no wind stirred hair, dress, or arrow. Skill had fair play. When the crowd marched together to retrieve arrows, it became a joyful procession—light-hearted, laughing, moving in unison toward a shared purpose. A sight worth watching. Here, Gwendolen seemed a Calypso among nymphs. Her movements, her posture—everyone acknowledged her charm.

“That girl is like a high-mettled racer,” Lord Brackenshaw remarked to young Clintock, one of the guests. “First chop! Tremendously pretty too,” said the elegant Grecian, who had been attentive to her. “I never saw her look better.”

Perhaps she had never looked so radiant. Her face glowed with youthful delight, untouched by envy or discontent. Satisfied with her own place, she felt kindness toward all and contentment with the world. Not to hold the highest rank, not to be marked as an heiress like Miss Arrowpoint—this gave her triumph a sharper edge. For personal affection, she wouldn’t trade her family group for any other: her mother looked fit for an amiable duchess; her uncle and aunt Gascoigne, along with Anna, formed equally pleasing figures. Gwendolen, full of belief in herself, felt no jealousy—even though Miss Arrowpoint was a skilled archer.

Even the unexpected return of Herr Klesmer, which startled others, seemed to suit Gwendolen’s mood perfectly. Short of Apollo himself, what great musician could make such a figure at an archery meet? She watched the Arrowpoint party enter with a wry smile, the contrast between Klesmer and the typical English country folk at its peak—especially near his hosts—or patrons, as Mrs. Arrowpoint preferred to call them, so she could claim she no longer patronized genius, whose royalty was universally acknowledged.

The contrast might have amused a more solemn observer. We English are a mixed people—fifty random faces show many forms of structure and expression. Yet our general look lacks the liveliness of a passionate race, preoccupied with ideals and treating reality as mere weight. The English gentleman’s strength lies in ease—his figure, his clothes. He resists sharp contrasts in costume, and he avoids appearing inspired. Imagine a gathering of such men, seeing Herr Klesmer arrive: his mane of hair flying backward, defying the stiff chimney-pot hat perched above his strong, well-shaped features and powerful jaw. His tall, lean frame wore clothing that, while not strictly English, drew attention precisely because it seemed so deliberately foreign. Draped in a loose garment with a Florentine _berretta_, he might have stood beside Leonardo da Vinci. But in trousers ill-suited to English taste—knees awkwardly emphasized—and with fire in his glance and movement, reduced to comedy by a hat demanding cropped hair and calm demeanor—like Mr. Arrowsmith’s, whose blank face and flawless tailoring passed unnoticed everywhere—Klesmer became a spectacle.

One understands why greatness often feels better dead, stripped of its physical form. Many knew Klesmer, or had heard of him, but only seen him in candlelit concerts, where he was merely a musician. He hadn’t yet reached the global fame that makes an artist revered even by the uninitiated through sheer cost and reputation. Now, unexpectedly, here he was—on a July afternoon, in exclusive society. Some laughed. Others frowned at the poor judgment of the Arrowpoints in inviting him.

“What extreme guys those artistic types usually are!” young Clintock whispered to Gwendolen. “Look at the figure he cuts—bowing with his hand over his heart to Lady Brackenshaw, and Mrs. Arrowpoint’s feather just brushing his shoulder.”

“You’re one of the profane,” Gwendolen replied. “Blind to the majesty of genius. Herr Klesmer strikes me with awe. I feel crushed in his presence. My courage leaks away.”

“Oh, you understand his music?”

“No indeed,” she laughed lightly. “It’s he who understands mine—and thinks it pitiable.” Klesmer’s critique of her singing had become easier to bear since he’d admired her _plastik_. “It’s not meant for future ears, I suppose. I’m glad. It suits mine.”

“Oh, you’re very kind. But how remarkably well Miss Arrowpoint looks today! She’d make quite a fine painting in that gold dress.”

“Too splendid, don’t you think?”

“Well, perhaps a little too symbolic—too much like Wealth in an allegory.”

Gwendolen’s remark carried a hint of mischief, but it wasn’t malice. She didn’t wish anyone out of the way. She believed in her own luck more than in her skill. As the shooting continued, that belief grew stronger. She promised a top score—astonishing everyone as a newcomer. For Gwendolen, one success led to another. She walked on air. Everything seemed possible. The moment was enough. She didn’t need to plan what came next.

“How does the scoring stand?” asked Lady Brackenshaw, seated gracefully with two daughters and a sturdy son, presiding as lady paramount. Her husband approached during a break.

“It seems Miss Harleth may win the gold arrow,” he replied.

“Gad, I think she will—if she keeps this up! She’s running Juliet Fenn close. Remarkable for a first-year archer. Catherine isn’t at her usual level,” he added, turning to Mrs. Arrowpoint nearby. “But she won last time. And there’s luck even in games of skill. That’s better—it gives the rest a chance.”

“Catherine will be delighted for others to win,” Mrs. Arrowpoint said. “She’s so generous. It was entirely her thought to bring Herr Klesmer instead of Canon Stopley, who wanted to come. For her own pleasure, I’m sure she’d have chosen the canon. But she always thinks of others. I told her it wasn’t quite _en règle_ to invite someone so outside our circle. She said, ‘Genius itself is not _en règle_; it comes to make new rules.’ And one must admit it.”

“Ay, to be sure,” Lord Brackenshaw replied, dismissively. “For my part, I’m not magnanimous—I’d like to win. But confound it! I never get the chance now. I’m growing old and idle. The young ones beat me. As old Nestor says—the gods don’t give us everything at once. I was young once. Now I’m getting wise. Old, at least—which is a gift everyone gets if they live long enough. So it raises no jealousy.”

He smiled comfortably at his wife.

“Oh, my lord,” Mrs. Arrowpoint said, “neighbors for twenty years shouldn’t talk about age.” She paused. “Years, as the Tuscans say, are made for renting houses. But where is our new neighbor? I thought Mr. Grandcourt was coming today.”

“Ah, yes—he was supposed to. Time’s slipping,” Lord Brackenshaw glanced at his watch. “But he only reached Diplow the other day. Came to us Tuesday and said he’d been delayed. Might have been pulled elsewhere. Why, Gascoigne!”—the rector was walking past with Gwendolen on his arm, turning at the call—“you’ve not only beaten us yourself, but brought your niece to beat all the archeresses!”

“It _is_ rather scandalous,” Mr. Gascoigne said, inwardly pleased, curling his upper lip. “But it’s not my doing, my lord. I only meant her to make a decent figure—nothing more.”

“It’s not my fault either,” Gwendolen replied, with playful defiance. “If I aim, I can’t help hitting.”

“Ay, ay,” Lord Brackenshaw chuckled. “That may be fatal for some.” Then, checking his watch again, he turned to Mrs. Arrowpoint. “Time’s slipping, as you say. But Grandcourt’s always late. In town, he’s always late. And he’s no bowman—doesn’t understand a thing. But I told him he must come. He’d see the cream of the neighborhood. He asked about you—had seen Arrowpoint’s card. I think you hadn’t met him in town. He’s been abroad a lot. People don’t know him well.”

“No,” Mrs. Arrowpoint said. “We’re strangers. But that’s not what one might expect. His uncle, Sir Hugo Mallinger, and I are great friends when we meet.”

“I don’t know,” Lord Brackenshaw said, smiling toward the rector. “Uncles and nephews aren’t as likely to be seen together as uncles and nieces.” Then, turning to Gascoigne: “Just come with me a moment, will you? I want to speak about the clout-shooting.”

Gwendolen chose to go too, joining her mother and aunt until her next turn. The possibility that Mr. Grandcourt might not appear had begun to dim her joy. Beneath her sharp wit—sparked by her sense that others saw him as a match for her—lay a deeper feeling. She cared deeply about the impression she made on him. True, he had no power over her (though Gwendolen hadn’t grasped that desire to conquer is a kind of surrender). She’d decided he’d be another of those admiring, complimentary men—like several she’d already met, with different beards and styles. The knowledge that her friends expected her to find him delightful made her resist thinking him charming. But that didn’t mean she could afford to miss his presence. Even a fleeting thought of rejecting him caused no wish for him to avoid her altogether. The image of Mr. Grandcourt ignoring her and soon marrying Miss Arrowpoint was not one her imagination welcomed.

So Gwendolen listened closely to Lord Brackenshaw’s explanation of Grandcourt’s delay. And when he finally arrived, no consciousness—Mrs. Arrowpoint’s, Mr. Gascoigne’s, or even her own—was more alert than hers. Though she refused to look in his direction, she avoided shifting her gaze, determined not to betray that his presence mattered. She returned to the shooting, resisting the urge to scan the crowd. Even if he stood prominently, she would pretend not to notice. Yet all the while, the certainty of his being there threaded through her awareness.

Perhaps her aim improved for it. At any rate, her shots grew sharper. She ended with three consecutive hits in the gold—an achievement that sent waves of applause through the field. Among Brackenshaw’s archers, this earned not a shilling, but a special gold star to wear on the breast.

That moment was not only her own triumph—it was exactly what her mother and uncle would have wished. The crowd parted to let her advance, and her graceful form moved with perfect poise in the clear afternoon light, shadows long and still. She was the center of the scene—every eye upon her. Enough. She refused to fix her gaze on anyone, focusing only on Lady Brackenshaw. Yet her thoughts wandered elsewhere.

A small part of her pleasure came from knowing Herr Klesmer watched her—when music was irrelevant, when his superiority faded into the background. Vanity is uneasy under indifference, as tenderness is under unrequited love. Even unconquered, Klesmer cast a shadow across her awareness: Mr. Grandcourt was seeing her at her best, likely admiring her without criticism. She didn’t expect to admire him—but that wasn’t necessary for peace.

Gwendolen met Lady Brackenshaw’s smile without blushing (a trait reserved for surprise), but with radiant warmth. She bent gracefully as the star was pinned near her shoulder.

The ceremony over, she exchanged light words and received congratulations as she moved among the groups discussing scores. Then, standing apart, examining the tip of an arrow with a distracted air, Lord Brackenshaw approached.

“Miss Harleth,” he said, “here is a gentleman who refuses to wait any longer for an introduction. He’s had Mrs. Davilow send me to escort him. Will you allow me to introduce Mr. Mallinger Grandcourt?”
