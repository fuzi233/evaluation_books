It was nearly four in the afternoon, the light slanting low and heavy across the cobblestones. Inside the workshop, Eli labored with the rhythm of a man bound to his trade, while his eldest and youngest sons wandered the streets beyond. Outside, the air hummed with a tension born of absence; Catherine and her diminutive, crippled daughter had already departed, driven by an anxious hope to scour the distant road for any sign of Gerard. Left alone in the sitting room was Giles, a figure of grotesque disproportion who served as both the room's guardian and its most unsettling ornament.

To understand the Hollanders, one must first discard the notion that they merely traded cloth or dyes. They claimed invention as their birthright—the press, the pigment, the very concept of liberty itself—but above all, centuries before the tales of this era began, they invented cleanliness. While the English gentry, clad in velvet jerkins and chicken-toed shoes, trampled upon floors of stale rushes—a foul receptacle for bones, rotting food, spittle, and the refuse of dogs and eggs—the hosier's chamber at Tergou gleamed with a defiant purity. Its floor was paved with Dutch tiles, glazed to such a luster and scrubbed with such constancy that they invited the touch of a diner's plate.

Dominating one wall was a single large window, its center framed by massive cross-stone work that rose in stark relief, mimicking a crucifix for the devout souls within. The panes were tiny, lozenge-shaped shards soldered together with strips of lead, a testament to medieval craftsmanship that remains visible even today in rural cottages. Within this sanctified space sat chairs of rude, primitive construction, save for one armchair of solid oak. Its back, rising at a rigid right angle to the seat, towered so high that a seated man's head hovered two feet below the apex, crowned with intricate carvings. Beside it stood a copper pail, waist-deep, filled with holy water, accompanied by a small besom to scatter the blessing. And looming over the long, narrow, massive oak table was Giles, clinging to the rim by his teeth, his eyes blazing with a predatory intensity, his claws raised like those of a pouncing vampire.

Nature, it seemed, had fashioned Giles not out of malice, but through a distracted hand. She had sculpted his head and torso with her usual diligence, yet her focus wavered, leaving the remainder to chaotic chance. The result was a human wedge, an inverted cone of flesh and bone. As the Roman poet Horace might have lamented regarding an amphora that failed to stand upright, Giles's center of mass was entirely absent. Bisected vertically, the upper half of his body would have outweighed the lower three times over. Yet, this very imbalance granted him powers that would have baffled the legendary Milo. His brawny arms, unburdened by the weight of his own torso, allowed him to scale vertical poles with the agility of a squirrel or hang suspended from a tree branch by a single hand, dangling like a fruit from its stem. Had he possessed the suction of a lizard's foot, he could have walked upon ceilings.

But this pocket-athlete found his greatest ecstasy in defying gravity atop the dinner table. He would grip the surface with both hands, swing wildly, and then, in a climax of pure delight, clamp his jaws shut on the wood. Releasing his hands, he would cling to the table edge with his teeth alone, holding on with the tenacity of a grim death. All joy, however elevating, is subject to interruption. Little Kate, spotting Sampsonet in this acrobatic tableau, froze in horror. Like her mother, her heart belonged to the stability of the furniture, not the twelve-mo gymnast performing upon it.

"Oh, Giles! How can you?" she cried. "Mother is here. Look at the dent you've made in the table."

"Go and tell her, little tale-bearer," snarled Giles, his voice thick with irritation. "You are the one destined for mischief."

"Am I?" Kate replied, her tone unnervingly calm. "That is news to me."

"The biggest troublemaker in Tergou," Giles grumbled, tightening his grip on the table.

"Oh, indeed!" Kate said dryly.

Having launched this unexpected barb without consequence, Giles settled back into his post. Kate sat quietly and began to weep. At that precise moment, her mother entered, and Giles hurled himself beneath the table, emerging only to glare from the shadows.

"What is to be done now?" the woman demanded sharply. Turning her experienced gaze from Kate to the dwarf, noting his contorted position and the sheepish flush on his face, she signaled a threat of corporal punishment.

"Nay, mother," the girl interjected softly. "It was but a foolish word Giles spoke. I had not noticed it at another time; but I was weary and consumed with worry for Gerard, you know."

Then, a faint voice echoed from the doorway. "Let no one be in care for me."

Gerard appeared, tottering into the room. Pale, dust-covered, and utterly drained, he stumbled amidst a chorus of uplifted hands, cries of delight, and mingled currents of curiosity and anxiety, collapsing exhausted into the nearest chair. The journey from Rotterdam, searching like a shadow for Margaret, and the arduous trek thereafter, had completely broken him. Yet, the elasticity of youth proved resilient; quickly, he became the gravitational center of an eager circle.

First, they demanded the details of the prizes. Gerard recounted how he had been granted access to view the competitors' works, displayed in an immense hall awaiting the judges' verdict.

"Oh, mother! Oh, Kate!" he exclaimed, his voice trembling with awe. "When I beheld the goldsmiths' creations, I felt as though I ought to have fallen to my knees. I scarcely believed that all the goldsmiths upon earth possessed such abundance of gold, silver, jewels, and such divine craft of design and facture. In truth, all the arts are sacred."

Seeking to delight the women, he proceeded to describe the reliquaries, feretories, chalices, crosiers, crosses, pyxes, monstrances, and other ecclesiastical wonders, alongside goblets, hanaps, watches, clocks, chains, and brooches, until their mouths watered with desire.

"But, Kate," he continued, his tone shifting to melancholy, "when I turned to the illuminated manuscripts from Ghent and Bruges, my heart sank. My own work was dirt by comparison. For the first minute, I could almost have wept; but I prayed for a purer spirit, and soon I was able to admire them, thanking God for those lovely works and for those skilled, patient craftsmen, whom I acknowledge as my masters. Well, the colored work was so breathtaking I forgot all about the black and white. But the following day, when the other prizes had been distributed, they came to the writing competition. Whose name do you suppose was called first?"

"Yours," Kate answered confidently.

The others scoffed at her presumption.

"You may well laugh," Gerard said gently, "but for all that, 'Gerard Eliassoen of Tergou' was the name the herald shouted. I stood stupefied; they pushed me forward. Everything swam before my eyes. I found myself kneeling on a cushion at the feet of the Duke. He spoke to me, but I was so fluttered I could not reply. Then, he placed his hand to his side—not drawing a glaive to sever my dull head, but granting me a gold medal, and here it is."

A roar erupted, followed by a scramble for possession.

"And then he gave me fifteen great, bright golden angels," Gerard declared triumphantly. "I had seen one before, but never handled one. Behold them."

"Oh, Gerard! Oh, Gerard!" the family cried out.

"There is one for you, our eldest; one for you, Sybrandt; one for you, Little Mischief; and two for thee, Little Lily, because God has afflicted thee; and one for myself, to purchase colors and vellum; and nine for her who nursed us all and risked the two crowns upon poor Gerard's hand."

The gold revealed their true natures. Cornelis and Sybrandt clutched their coins with alternating glares of greed and envy directed at Kate, who had secured two pieces. Giles seized his medal, rolled it across the pristine tiled floor, and gamboled after it. Kate set aside her crutches, sat down, and extended her small arms toward Gerard in a gesture of heavenly love and tenderness. The mother, initially stunned numbness by the shower of wealth falling upon her apron, suddenly broke into tears.

"Leave kissing him, Kate; he is my son, not yours," she cried, though her voice trembled. "Ah, Gerard! My boy! I have not loved you as you deserve."

Gerard threw himself to his knees beside her, and she flung her arms around him, weeping with overwhelming joy and pride upon his neck.

"Good lad! Good lad!" cried the hosier, moved to emotion. "I must go and tell the neighbors. Lend me the medal, Gerard; I will show it to my good friend Peter Buyskens. He is ever regaling me with tales of how his son Jorian won a tin mug for shooting at the butts."
