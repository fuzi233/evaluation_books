Margaret hacked off a great heaving slab of venison, flew to the casement, and tossed it out to the pair of fire-green eyes glinting in the dark. The leopard sprang upon the meat with a ravening snarl, and the quiet of the night was rent by the sound of tearing fur and crunching bone. At that very instant, a hound bayed so near, so loud, the reverberations shook the timbers of the little cottage. The three huddled at the window shrank back as one.
The leopard, fearing its supper would be stolen, grabbed the half-eaten venison in its jaws and slipped away swift and silent for the dark line of the woods. Barely a heartbeat later, the whole hunt thundered past the window: horses’ hooves drumming the rutted mud, halloos ringing out, the whole pack of hounds baying full cry on the leopard’s trail. Martin and his companions let out a long, shared breath. The beast was fleet of foot; no hound alive would run it to ground within a league of their house. They clasped hands tight in relief. Margaret, the rush of adrenaline draining away all at once, burst into quiet tears, and Gerard kissed the salt from her cheeks.
They returned to the table, and Gerard raised his cup to toast Margaret. “A woman’s wit is stronger than any man’s brute force,” he said.
“Ay,” she replied, smiling through damp eyelashes, “only when those she loves are in danger. Never otherwise.”
That night Gerard stayed far later than he usually dared, and walked home prouder of Margaret than he had ever been, light of heart as any crowned prince. A short stretch from his own lane, under the shadow of the gnarled oak trees that lined the road, two figures stepped out and all but barred his path. It was his father and mother. Out so late? A cold dread settled in his bones. He stopped, and stared at them. They stood grim, silent, no light of welcome on their faces.
He stammered out a question of what brought them there.
“Why ask?” his father said gruffly. “You know full well why we are here.”
“Oh, Gerard,” his mother wept, her voice thick with reproach and brimming over with old affection.
Gerard’s heart quailed. He could not speak. Then his father, seeing his utter confusion, softened a little. “Nay, you need not hang your head. You are not the first young fool to be led astray by a red cheek and a pair of bright blue eyes.”
“Nay, nay!” Catherine cut in quickly. “It was witchcraft. Everyone knows Peter the Magician of Sevenbergen casts such spells.”
“Come, you would-be priest,” his father went on, “you know you have no business meddling with women. Swear to us now you will never go to Sevenbergen again, and all is forgiven. We will not be hard on you for this one foolish mistake.”
“I cannot promise that, father.”
“Not promise, you young hypocrite!”
“Nay, father, do not call me such names,” Gerard said. “I lacked the courage to tell you something I knew would grieve you, and I am grateful to the friend who has let you hear the truth at last – it is a weight off my mind. I love Margaret. Call me no priest, for a priest I will never be. I would die first.”
“That we shall see,” his father snapped. “Do not defy me again. You will learn what it means to disrespect your own father.”
Gerard held his peace. The three of them walked home in heavy, gloomy silence, broken only by Catherine’s soft, catching sighs.
From that hour, the little house in Tergou was no longer a home of peace. The very next day, Gerard was called to account before the whole family, and every voice was raised against him – save little Kate’s, and the dwarf’s, who copied her lead without even understanding why he did. Cornelis and Sybrandt, his two older brothers, were far more bitter than their own father had ever been. Gerard, dismayed to find so many turned against him, looked wistfully at his little sister’s face. Her eyes were brimming with tears at the harsh words they hurled at the boy who had once been the family’s universal pet. But she gave him no comfort. She turned her head away, and said, “Dear, dear Gerard, pray Heaven to cure you of this folly.”
“Even you are against me?” Gerard said, his voice heavy with sadness. He rose with a deep sigh, left the house, and walked all the way to Sevenbergen.
When quarrels break out between people bound by love even as they are divided by opposing desires, the first sparks are almost innocent. Both sides might even start out half right. At such a time, a calm, wise friend who can see both perspectives is a gift from Heaven. But the longer the rift drags on, the wider and deeper it grows, stoked by human fallibility and hot tempers on both sides. There is no side that does no wrong, until at last both are in the wrong equally.
The fight was unfairly matched: Elias was furious, Cornelis and Sybrandt were spiteful and petty, but Gerard, with his wider, more cultivated mind, saw both sides where they saw only one, and was wracked with fits of irresolution. He was not angry, only unhappy. And he was utterly alone in his struggle. He could not unburden his heart to anyone. Margaret was high-spirited, and he dared not tell her the abuse he endured at home, for she would no doubt choose to renounce him to spare him pain, even if it broke her own heart. Margaret Van Eyck had been a great comfort to him once before, but he dared not confide in her now. Everyone knew her story: in her youth she had refused every marriage offer to dedicate herself to art, a calling that left no time for the duties of a wife and mother. How could he tell her he was turning down the fine benefice she had worked to secure for him, and rejecting the very life of art she had sacrificed all love for, for the sake of the love she had thrown away so lightly?
Gerard was on the verge of breaking. But his mother, kind-hearted but uneducated, rash and inconsistent, was an unexpected, terrible ally on his side. She could not act with quiet, steady purpose like her daughter, nor hold to a single plan, and she irritated Gerard so often that anger gave him the very courage he was losing. At other times, she turned on her own family mid-argument without warning. One day, when the whole household was gathered, Cornelis sneered, “Our Gerard wed Margaret Brandt? Why, that’s hunger marrying thirst.”
“And what will it be when you marry?” Catherine roared back at him. “Gerard can paint, Gerard can write – what can you do to keep a wife, you lazy loon? Nothing but wait for your father to die so you can wear his boots. We all see why you and Sybrandt do not want the poor boy to marry. You are afraid he will ask for his share of the family property. And even if he did, it is not yours to give away – it may never be yours at all!”
On days like that, Gerard would smile to himself, pick up his courage, and leave his father and brothers floundering in sudden confusion.
But after six months of this constant, grinding irritation, the climax came. Elias stood before the whole family and told his son he had already ordered the burgomaster to lock Gerard in the Stadthouse prison before he would ever let him marry Margaret Brandt. Gerard paled with hot anger, but with a great effort held his peace.
“And you will be a priest before the year is out, willing or not,” his father finished.
“Is that so?” Gerard cried. “Then hear me, all of you. By God and Saint Bavon, I swear I will never be a priest while Margaret lives. If force is to decide this, not love and duty, then try force, father. But force will not serve you. The very day the burgomaster comes to drag me away, I will leave Tergou forever, leave Holland too, and this house where I have lived for years valued not for myself, but for what profit others could wring out of me.”
He flung out of the room, white with anger and desperation.
“There!” Catherine cried. “That is what comes of driving young folk too hard. Men are crueller than tigers, even to their own flesh and blood. Heaven forbid he should ever leave us, married or unmarried.”
As Gerard stepped out of the house, his cheeks pale and his heart pounding, he met Reicht Heynes. She had a message for him: Margaret Van Eyck wished to see him. He found the old painter lady seated as grim as a judge. She wasted no time on pleasantries, and asked coldly why he had not visited her for so long. Before he could answer, she spoke in a sharp, sarcastic tone: “I thought we were friends, young sir.”
Gerard looked utterly lost and confused.
“He never dared tell her he was in love,” Reicht said, taking pity on his stricken face.
“Silence, wench!” the old lady snapped. “Why should he tell us his affairs? We are not his friends, we have not earned his confidence.”
“Alas, my second mother,” Gerard said, “I did not dare tell you of my folly.”
“What folly? Is it folly to love?”
“I am told so every day of my life.”
“You had no cause to fear telling me,” Margaret Van Eyck said, softening. “I have always been kind to true lovers.”
“Madam – Reicht – I was told that in your youth you scorned love, and chose art instead.”
“I did, boy. And look what came of it. Here I am, a barren stock, while all the girls I grew up with have children at their sides and grandchildren at their knees. I gave up the sweet joys of wifehood and motherhood for what? For my dear brothers, who left this world long ago. For my art – which has almost left me too. I still have all the knowledge, but what use is it when my hands shake so I cannot hold a brush steady? No, Gerard. I look on you as my own son. You are good, you are handsome, you are a painter – though not half as good as the masters I learned from. I will not let you throw your youth away as I did mine. You shall marry this Margaret. I have made inquiries, and she is a good, honest daughter. Reicht here is a gossip, she has told me every last detail. But that is no reason you should not tell me your side of the story.”
Overjoyed, Gerard poured out his heart, and praised Margaret aloud to someone who could understand exactly what he loved in her. Soon there were two pairs of wet eyes listening to his story, and when Gerard saw that, his own eyes overflowed too.
Women are brimful of a peculiar courage of their own: it is vicarious, the kind of courage that makes them sit unflinching through a bullfight while the men beside them tremble for the fighters, that makes them send their men off to battle with far more resolution than any man could muster on his own. And Gerard profited from that trait that day. Margaret and Reicht were both of one mind: the only sensible thing to do was to marry Margaret Brandt at once, offhand. Once the deed was done, the old people would come around in time, and all the bickering that dragged on would only grow worse the longer they waited.
“See how pale and thin they have made him,” Reicht said. “It makes my heart ache to see a young man so worn down. When I met him on the street today, I almost burst out crying, he has changed so.”
“And I will wager Cornelis and Sybrandt still keep their coarse, ruddy colour, no doubt,” the old painter said. “We painters are no match for boors. We are glass, they are stone. We cannot stand the endless, petty nagging of small minds – and it is no good to anyone that we should be exposed to it. It is hard enough to design and paint a masterpiece, without being stung to death by gnats and flies all day long.”
Gerard was still reeling from his father’s threat to throw him in prison, and he listened eagerly to these friendly voices telling him that rebellion was the wisest course. But he was not yet fully convinced. “I do not fear my father’s prison,” he said. “But I fear his curse. He will not jail me, he will disown me. I would take Margaret away from her father, and give her a poor husband who will never thrive, weighed down by his own parent’s hatred. Madam, I have thought sometimes that if I could marry her in secret, then take her away to some country where my craft is better paid than it is here. After a year or two, when the storm has blown over, I could come back with a purse full of money, and say ‘My dear parents, we do not ask for your property, we only ask that you love us once more as you used, for we have never ceased to love you.’ But I suppose these are only the foolish dreams of an inexperienced young man.”
The old lady’s eyes sparkled. “It is no dream, it is the most sensible thing any boy has ever said to me. The only question is whether you have the courage to carry it out. There is a land waiting for you, Gerard, where certain fortune is yours if you are bold enough. Here the arts freeze, but there they flourish as no art has ever flourished in any age.”
“It is Italy!” Gerard cried. “It must be Italy!”
“Ay, Italy! There painters are honoured like princes, and scribes are paid three hundred crowns for copying a single manuscript. Do you not know the Holy Father the Pope has sent letters to every land, seeking skilled scribes to copy the hundreds of precious manuscripts pouring into Rome from Constantinople, where barbarian Turks are driving out all learning and all learned men?”
“I never knew that,” Gerard said, “but visiting Italy, the queen of all the arts, has been the dream of my whole life. But the journey… we are all so poor.”
“If you have the heart to go, I will find the means. I have ten golden angels set aside, enough to take you both to Rome, if the girl loves you as she ought.”
They sat talking long past midnight. After that day, Gerard’s spirits came back, and he carried a secret talisman against all the jeers and harsh words his brothers hurled at him at home. Beyond the money for the journey, Margaret Van Eyck gave him something far more precious. “I will tell you secrets the old masters taught me, secrets no living soul knows now. Even the Italians have never heard them, and what I tell you here in Tergou you will be able to sell for a fortune in Florence. Look at my brother Jan’s paintings. Time, which fades every other painter’s colours, leaves his as bright as the day they left the easel. He never trusted a hireling to grind his colours, he did it himself, or stood over the work to make sure it was done right. He prepared his panels, again and again, a whole year before he ever laid a single brushstroke. No lazy painter bothers to do that. Above all, Gerard, never use much oil, and never boil it. Boiling oil melts the vegetable dross deep into its heart, when your whole aim should be to wash that dross away. Pour your oil into a bottle of clear water. In two days the water will turn muddy – that is all the muck leaching out of the oil. Pour the dirty water away, add fresh water. You will think the oil is clear then, but you are wrong.” She nodded to Reicht, who fetched a glass trough with a tight-fitting glass lid. “Pour your washed oil into this trough with fresh water, set it in the sun all day. The water will turn turbid again. Do not go too far, or the sun will turn your oil to varnish. When it is as clear as crystal, drain it carefully, cork it up tight. Grind your finest colours by hand, lay them on with this pure oil, and they will last forever. Jan Van Eyck was never in a hurry. That is why the world will never forget him in a hurry.”
With these secrets of the Van Eyck at his fingertips, and the ten golden angels tucked safe away, Gerard decided he would marry Margaret, and flee with her to Italy. All that was left was to tell Margaret Brandt his plan, and arrange to publish the banns as quietly as possible. He went to Sevenbergen earlier than usual, and told her all that Margaret Van Eyck had done for him, and his new plan to run away.
Margaret refused outright. “No, Gerard. You and I have never spoken of your family, but when it comes to marriage… I do not believe your father bears me any special ill will. He told Peter Buyskens as much, and Peter told me. But so long as he is determined you should be a priest – you ought to have told me this, not I you – I cannot marry you, dearly as I love you.”
Gerard argued with her in vain. It was easy to make her cry, but impossible to make her yield. At last, hot with impatience, he cried, “Very well! You are on their side. You will drive me to be a priest, for this must end one way or another. My parents hate me in earnest, but my lover only loves me in jest.” He stormed off, leaving Margaret weeping.
But when a man a girl truly loves misbehaves out of misery, she does not grow angry – she pities him. Less than an hour and a half after Gerard left, he came running back to her, the broken fragments of his half-finished portrait of her in his hands, panting with rage and grief.
“There, Margaret! Look! The wretches! They have cut your portrait to pieces.”
Margaret stared. Some spiteful hand had hacked the portrait into five jagged pieces, cutting straight through the face he had spent six months painting. She flushed bright red to the very top of her forehead. “Who did this?”
“I do not know,” Gerard said. “I dared not ask, for I would have hated the hand that did it till my dying day. Six months of my work, gone for nothing. They have cut right through your sweet face, that everyone who sees it loves. Oh, heartless, cruel vipers!”
