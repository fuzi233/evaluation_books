When the household doorman Maso pulled open the heavy oak door and ushered in their two unexpected visitors, Nello the barber first sank into a deep, exaggerated reverence before Romola, then gave the young man beside him a gentle nudge forward, steering Tito Melema across the shadowed, book-lined room to stand before her blind father.

“Messer Bardo,” he said, dropping his usual chattering, streetwise tone for something measured and respectful, “I have the honour to bring you the Greek scholar you have long heard me speak of. He has burned to meet you no less for my accounts of your unrivalled learning and your irreplaceable collection of ancient texts, than out of hope you might patronize him through the hard straits that shipwreck has laid him low in. His name is Tito Melema, and he is at your full service.”

Romola’s astonishment could scarcely have been greater if the stranger had stepped through the door clad in a panther skin and carrying the ivy-wreathed thyrsus of a Greek bacchanal. The crafty barber had never once mentioned the Greek’s age or looks, and the scholarly visitors who came to call on her father were almost all middle-aged, their heads frosted with grey. Only one young, beautiful male face had ever stuck fast in her memory: that of her little brother, who years before had sat her on his knee, kissed her forehead, and vanished from Florence never to return, fair-haired and sun-kissed, just like her own. Her usual quiet, proud self-reliance, hardened by years of listening to her father’s bitter laments over the world’s injustice, was like a thick snowbank holding back a rush of unlooked-for admiration. Tito’s bright face glowed with rich, warm colour above his plain black knee-length tunic, no other ornament to compete with it: he looked like a wreath of spring wildflowers that had dropped straight into the cold, book-bound winter of Romola’s young life, where all she had ever inherited was memories—of a dead mother, a lost brother, the happy days before her father went blind, faint glimmers of old light and love and beauty buried deep in the dark mines of leather-bound tomes, that could never wake to brightness again until some new joy kindled them for her. Still, she returned Tito’s polite bow at the door with her usual pale, proud composure. But as he drew nearer, the snowbank softened, and when he dared glance her way again while Nello spoke, a soft pink flush spread over her cheeks, only to vanish a heartbeat later, as if her own stern will had called it back. Tito’s gaze, though, held a gentle, almost beseeching warmth of admiration that was the kindest possible appeal to a proud, shy woman—almost the only apology a handsome man can ever make for being too handsome. The easy charm of his air came entirely from the fact that he made no demands, no assumptions: he moved like a fleet, soft-coated dark-eyed creature that does not bound away from you in indifference, but unexpectedly rests its chin on your palm and looks up asking to be petted, as if it already loves you.

“Messere, you are welcome,” Bardo said, a faint gracious condescension in his tone. “Misfortune wedded to learning, and most of all to Greek learning, is a letter of credit that ought to win the ear of every educated Florentine. You know well, I am sure, that since your countryman Manuelo Crisolora spread the light of his teaching across the great cities of Italy nearly a hundred years ago, no man can call himself a true scholar if he only knows the second-hand, derivative literature of the Latins. Those lazy students we rightly dismiss as *opici*—barbarians, as the old Romans called those who drew water not from the original fountainhead, but from other men’s urns. And as you can see for yourself, and Nello has surely warned you, I am completely blind: a calamity we Florentines are said to be peculiarly prone to, whether from the sharp spring winds that rush down on us through the Apennine passes, or the sudden jolt from the cool dark of our palazzo interiors to the blazing glare of our summer sun, the same shift they say made so many of the ancient Romans go short-sighted—or some hidden cause we cannot easily guess. But sit, I beg you. Nello, my friend, sit too.”

Bardo waited until his sharp, practiced ear confirmed both visitors had settled into chairs, and Romola had slipped into her usual place at his right hand, then spoke again.

“From what part of Greece do you hail, Messere? I had thought your unhappy country had almost run out of sons who still held any memory of its old glory, though lately the barbarian Sultans have shown they are not unwilling to graft the precious old vine of Greek learning onto the wild stock of their own realm, the very same vine their own raiders once cut down and trampled underfoot. Where in Greece are you from?”

“I sailed last from Nauplia,” Tito answered, “but I have lived in Constantinople and Thessalonica both, and travelled stretches of the mainland almost no Western Christians have visited since the Turks took control. I ought to tell you, though, Messere, I was not born in Greece at all. I was born in Bari, and spent the first sixteen years of my life in Southern Italy and Sicily.”

As Tito spoke, a soft emotion rippled across Bardo’s delicate, lined features like a breeze skimming over still water. He leaned forward, stretched his right hand out toward Romola, and turned his head as if to speak to her, before correcting himself and turning back, his voice soft and shaken.

“Forgive me… but you are young, are you not?”

“I am three and twenty,” Tito said.

“Ah,” Bardo breathed, still quiet, his tone thrumming with subdued excitement. “And you had a father, I am sure, who tended to your earliest lessons—who was a scholar himself, perhaps?”

There was a small pause before Tito answered, and both Romola and Nello saw a faint tremor run through his frame, his lips twitching for a fleeting moment, as if the question had stirred a sharp, unhealed memory.

“Yes,” he said at last, “a father by adoption. He was a Neapolitan, and a man of great accomplishment in both Latin and Greek. But…” he paused again, “he is lost to me now. He died on a voyage he took too rashly to the island of Delos.”

Bardo sank back in his chair at once, too sensitive to press for more details of a grief he could see was still fresh. Romola, who knew every deep old wound her father carried, felt this new acquaintance had somehow slipped past the high walls that had long separated their quiet, book-bound household from the rest of the world, in the space of a few short minutes.

Nello, seeing the conversation had hit a gentle lull that gave him a perfect graceful out, spoke up at once.

“By your leave, it is as clear as the finest Venetian glass that this fine young man has had the very best training! The two Cennini brothers have already set him to work at their Greek printing sheets, and those men are not the sort to start cutting paper before they test the edge of their new tools. They vetted him thoroughly first, I can swear to it. They say there are two things you can never hide: love, and a cough. I say there is a third, and that is ignorance, the moment a man has to do something more useful than just nod his head in agreement. The half-educated barber cannot hide his flaws the second he picks up the shears, is that not right, Messer Bardo? I speak as a man of my trade, but as Luigi Pulci wrote—
‘Pardon me if I err: let he who hears
Translate my common Tuscan to his Latin ears.’”

“Nay, my good Nello,” Bardo said, his tone half friendly half severe, “you are not entirely unlettered, and you might have made far better progress in scholarship if you spent less time lingering at street corners swapping gossip, which all Florentines are far too fond of doing—and more importantly, if you had not stuffed your memory full of that frivolous trash Luigi Pulci writes, the very worst example of all the absurd, incoherent rhymes that have nothing to do with the pure great models of classical antiquity. They are more like those silly *grylli* conceits of old, where men thought hiding a mystic meaning in a work excused making it an ugly, misshapen mess—except Pulci keeps the ugliness, and leaves out all the actual meaning. It is pitiful next to the great epic of Virgil, who as I long argued before Landino ever claimed the idea, wove all the deepest philosophical lessons into a graceful, tightly constructed fable. And I cannot help but think the spread of these babbling, lawless rhymes—even patronized and half enjoyed by Lorenzo himself, who is otherwise a true friend to learning—is a sign that the great bright hopes of this century are going to be snuffed out. That all this is just the misleading prologue to an age worse than the iron age: an age of tinsel and gossamer, where no thought has enough weight to be shaped into something consistent, lasting, and true.”

“Again, I beg your pardon,” Nello said, holding his palms out and shrugging his shoulders. “I find so many good Tuscan phrases popping out of my mouth before I have time to think up their Latin equivalents! Messer Luigi’s rhymes are always slipping off the lips of my customers, that is what corrupts me. And speaking of customers, I have left my shop and my reputation far too long in the hands of my slow apprentice Sandro, who is not even good enough to be called a half-skilled barber—he is just a plain bungler. So by your leave, Messer Bardo, I will take my leave, on the understanding I am at your service any time Maso calls for me. It feels like a thousand years until I get to dress and perfume the damigella’s hair, which deserves to shine in the heavens as a constellation—though it would be a terrible pity to send it so far out of reach.”

Three voices called out friendly farewells to Nello, who retreated with a final bow to Romola and a little beckoning nod to Tito. The sharp-witted barber could see his young favourite had already won Bardo’s full goodwill, and satisfied his introduction had gone off without a hitch, he knew it was time to leave them to talk.

The small burst of irritation Nello’s unlucky quotation had stirred up had cleared Bardo’s mind of the soft, tangled feelings that had paused their conversation earlier, and he spoke to Tito again in his usual calm, steady tone.

“Ah, young man, you are fortunate to have combined all the advantages of travel with those of study. We welcome you here as a man who can bring us fresh news from a land that has become shockingly strange to us, known only through the accounts of restricted trade agents and hasty pilgrims. The days I remember so well, when men like Aurispa and Guarino travelled to Greece as if it were a vast storehouse of treasure, and came back loaded down with manuscripts every scholar was desperate to borrow—though, I am ashamed to say, not always willing to return—those days are long gone. Even the days when learned Greeks fled to our shores for refuge seem farther away than the day I first lost my sight. But surely, young man, your travels were not for nothing, you went searching for the old treasures of antiquity along the way?”

“Most assuredly not,” Tito answered. “On the contrary, my companion—my father—was willing to risk his very life out of sheer zeal for discovering ancient inscriptions and other traces of long lost civilizations.”

“And I trust there is a full record of his research, and all he found,” Bardo said, leaning forward eagerly. “They must be even more precious than the notes of Ciriaco, which I have pored over diligently, even if they are not always as well informed as they might be.”

“There was such a record,” Tito said, “but it was lost, along with every other thing I owned, in the shipwreck I suffered off the coast of Ancona. The only record left is what remains in—my own memory.”

“You must waste no time writing every last bit of it down, young man,” Bardo said, his interest growing by the moment. “I am sure you remember a great deal, if you helped him transcribe it. When I was your age, words etched themselves into my mind as if they were carved there with a graver’s tool. It still astonishes me how capricious my own daughter’s memory is, clinging tight to some things and letting fall all the tiny little details that are the very soul of accuracy, the soul of all true scholarship. But I have no such fears for you, young man, if your training was backed up by the will to learn.”

When Bardo mentioned his daughter’s memory, Tito dared glance over at her, and at the gentle joke of her supposed flaw, his face broke into its brightest, warmest smile—one that Romola’s own face reflected back as surely as sunlight reflects off still water. That soft, shared smile was the most soothing, delightful thing Romola had ever experienced. She had never once dreamed there existed a scholar anywhere in the world who would smile at the flaw she was constantly scolded for, that made her feel like a disappointment to her father. It was like the dawn of a new sense in her: the sense of comradeship. Neither of them looked away at once, as if the smile was something stolen. They held each other’s gaze for a moment, smiling with unguarded, open enjoyment.

She is not nearly as cold and proud as she pretends, Tito thought to himself.

Does he forget little details too, sometimes? Romola wondered. But I hope not, or he will frustrate my father.

But Tito had to turn back to Bardo to answer his question.

“I have had a great deal of practice transcribing texts,” he said, “but in the case of inscriptions copied on those memorable, dangerous journeys, my memory of the exact written characters might have slipped a little. On the plains of the Eurotas, or among the giant stones of Mycenae and Tiryns—especially when the fear of Turkish patrols hangs over you like a hovering vulture—your mind does wander, even if your hand writes down exactly what your eyes see. But I do remember a great deal,” he added, with a modesty that was not entirely false, even if he knew it was perfectly politic, “things that might be of some use, if they were illustrated and corrected by a wider, deeper learning than my own.”

“That is very well spoken, young man,” Bardo said, delighted. “And I will not hold back any of the help I can offer you, if you will share your memories with me. I can see now a work taking shape that will be a perfect, useful supplement to Christoforo Buondelmonte’s *Isolario*, that will stand alongside the *Itineraria* of Ciriaco and the great Ambrogio Traversari. But we must prepare ourselves for slander, young man,” Bardo went on with sudden energy, as if the book was already half written and the enemies were already at the gate. “If your book contains new findings, people will accuse you of forgery. If my explanations clash with some other scholar’s pet interpretation, they will attack our very characters, spread lies about terrible things we did. They will say your mother was a fishmonger, your father was a renegade priest or a hanged criminal. I myself, for making a single mistake in one tiny preposition, once had a whole vicious invective written against me, where I was accused of treachery, fraud, indecency, and even the most hideous crimes. That, my young friend, is what the path of scholarship is strewn with: those are the flowers of learning. Now tell me, I learned much about Byzantium and Thessalonica long ago from Demetrio Calcondila, who only left Florence recently. But you visited far less familiar places, did you not?”

“Yes,” Tito said, eager to steer the conversation away from the personal questions about his adoptive father. “We went on what I can only call a pilgrimage full of danger, to visit places that the West has almost completely forgotten, so far off the usual path of pilgrims that most scholars think they exist only in books. My father always said a new, glorious era for learning would begin when people started looking for commentaries on the ancient writers not in other men’s books, but in the ruins of old cities and temples, in the paths of rivers, on the faces of valleys and mountains.”

“Ah!” Bardo breathed, fervid with excitement. “Your father was no ordinary man. Was he happy? Did he have many friends?” The last words were heavy with unspoken meaning, the pain of Bardo’s own long years of petty quarrels with other scholars.

“No, he made enemies, mostly, I think, because he was impetuously honest. They blocked his advancement, so he lived his whole life in obscurity. And he would never stoop to flatter anyone to get ahead. He could never forgive an injury.”

“Ah,” Bardo said again, the sound a long, deep, resonating note.

“On one of our most dangerous expeditions,” Tito continued, “I remember a hasty, snatched visit to Athens more vividly than any other. We were in such a rush, caught between the double risk of being seized as spies by the Turks, and our galley weighing anchor and sailing off without us before we could get back, that the whole place felt like a fevered dream. The wide plain, the ring of distant mountains, the ruined porticos and columns standing far off as if they were receding from our hurrying footsteps, jammed all tangled together between the tiny homes of Christians reduced to serfdom, and the forts and towers of their Moslem conquerors, who have their stronghold right on the Acropolis.”

“You astonish me,” Bardo said. “I had imagined Athens had been completely destroyed, swept off the face of the earth.”

“No wonder you think that,” Tito said. “Even most Greeks who live outside the borders of Attica know nothing of what Athens looks like now, or *Setine* as the sailors call it.
