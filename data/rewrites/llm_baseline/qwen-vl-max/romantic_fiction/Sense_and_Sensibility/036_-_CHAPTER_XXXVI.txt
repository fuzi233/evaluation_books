Within days of the gathering at Mrs. Jennings’s, the newspapers announced that Mrs. Thomas Palmer had safely delivered a son and heir—a piece of news that brought joy to all who already knew it, though none more so than Mrs. Jennings herself. This event, though modest in its own right, reshaped the daily rhythms of those around her. With Charlotte now confined to bed and recovering, Mrs. Jennings devoted her mornings and evenings entirely to her care, rising early and returning only late, her presence a constant fixture at the Palmer home. As a result, Elinor and Marianne found themselves obliged—by the kind but insistent wishes of the Middletons—to spend each full day in Conduit Street, under Lady Middleton’s roof.

Though they would have preferred the quiet comfort of Mrs. Jennings’s house, especially during the morning hours, they yielded without protest. But their presence there was neither welcomed nor appreciated as sincerely as it seemed to be. Lady Middleton, despite her outward politeness, harbored no real affection for them. She found their silence unflattering, their preference for books suspiciously critical, and their lack of admiration for her children deeply offensive. To her, their reserve spoke not of dignity, but of coldness; their love of reading, of hidden satire. They were, in her eyes, an awkward intrusion—dampening her idle moments and disrupting Lucy Steele’s carefully cultivated flattery.

Lucy, too, viewed the Dashwoods with unease. Their mere presence threatened her place in the household, cutting into the attention she craved. While she could not help but admire their grace and refinement, she resented them for sharing the warmth of Lady Middleton’s regard. She longed for just one moment of confessional intimacy—a full account of Marianne’s heartbreak with Mr. Willoughby—and believed such a revelation would make her forgiveness complete. Yet Elinor remained silent, indifferent to her pleas, while Marianne responded with scornful detachment. Even a lighter gesture—such as a shared laugh about the Doctor—might have softened Lucy’s resentment, but the Dashwoods offered nothing. When Sir John dined away from home, Lucy might go an entire day without hearing a single teasing word on the subject, except those she gave herself.

None of this troubled Mrs. Jennings in the least. In her cheerful ignorance, she saw only the benefit of the girls being together. Each evening, she would return from Charlotte’s side, radiant with pride, and greet Elinor and Marianne with joyful exclamation: “How delightful to escape that stupid old woman for another day!” She moved between Sir John’s house and her own, always bubbling with news—minute by minute updates on the baby’s health, his weight, his cries, and even the exact shade of his eyes. Only one thing disturbed her: Mr. Palmer’s stubborn refusal to see any distinction in his newborn son. Though she could plainly detect likenesses to both sides of the family, he insisted all babies looked alike. He refused to believe the child was anything but ordinary—let alone the finest infant in England. Her complaints echoed daily, yet he remained unmoved.

Meanwhile, Mrs. John Dashwood faced a different sort of misfortune. On the very day her sisters and Mrs. Jennings called upon her in Harley Street, another acquaintance dropped in unexpectedly. It was a chance encounter, seemingly harmless. But the visitor, carried away by imagination, mistook the Miss Dashwoods for permanent guests in the house. The error spread quickly, and within days, invitations arrived—not only for John and Fanny, but for Elinor and Marianne as well—to a small musical soirée at her home.

The consequence was immediate and unwelcome. Mrs. Dashwood was forced not only to send her carriage to collect the sisters, but also to endure the discomfort of appearing attentive to them. Worse still, she feared they might expect to be invited again. While she retained the power to refuse future invitations, the very idea of being judged for her actions stung her pride. When people choose a course they know is wrong, they resent being held to a higher standard.

Marianne, meanwhile, had grown so accustomed to daily engagements that going out had become automatic. She dressed without thought or enthusiasm, her appearance as unconcerned as if she were merely stepping into a room. Miss Steele, however, watched every detail with rapt attention. She catalogued every stitch, asked the price of each item, guessed the number of gowns Marianne owned, and hoped to discover how much her laundry cost weekly. Her scrutiny ended not with silence, but with a compliment: “Upon my word, you look vastly smart! I dare say you’ll make a great many conquests.” To Marianne, this was the ultimate impertinence—after being dissected like a specimen, she was told she’d charm men. Such words sent her quietly toward the carriage, which waited five minutes after arriving—an arrival that frustrated Mrs. Dashwood, who had hoped for a delay.

The evening itself passed uneventfully. The musical party was typical: some guests truly enjoyed the music, others did not; the performers considered themselves the finest private musicians in England. Elinor, uninterested in music and unashamed of it, allowed her gaze to wander freely. Among a group of young men, she spotted the same man who had once lectured them on toothpick cases at Gray’s. He soon turned his attention to her, speaking easily with her brother. Just as Elinor resolved to ask John for his name, he approached her himself. John introduced him as Mr. Robert Ferrars.

He greeted her with polished civility, bowing with a flourish that confirmed everything Lucy had said: he was exactly the kind of man who prided himself on appearances. Elinor felt a pang of regret—for if her affection for Edward had depended less on his character and more on the merit of his family, Robert’s arrogance might have shattered her hopes entirely. But instead of letting it harden her heart, she observed the contrast between the brothers. Robert explained it himself, speaking candidly of Edward’s shyness. “It’s not that he lacks sense,” he said, “but he was educated privately. I, on the other hand, went to Westminster. That makes all the difference. My mother often mourns it, but I tell her, ‘You must accept it. You chose to send Edward to Mr. Pratt’s instead of to school. If you’d done otherwise, he’d be quite different.’” He said this with genuine conviction, and Elinor refrained from arguing—though she could not forget the loneliness of Edward’s childhood.

Robert then remarked, “I believe you live in Devonshire—in a cottage near Dawlish?” Elinor corrected him gently, and he expressed surprise that anyone could live in Devonshire without living near Dawlish. But when she described their home, he warmed instantly. “Ah, a cottage! I adore cottages. There’s such comfort, such elegance. If I had money, I’d buy land near London and build one myself. I’d drive down whenever I pleased, gather friends, and be happy.” He recalled advising Lord Courtland, who had shown him three architectural plans. “‘My dear Courtland,’ I said, throwing them into the fire, ‘don’t build a mansion. Build a cottage.’ And I think he’ll do it.”

Elinor nodded politely. She didn’t feel the need to argue. His opinions were not worth challenging.

John Dashwood, equally unimpressed by music, found himself thinking during the evening. The mistaken belief that his sisters were staying with Mrs. Dashwood had sparked a new idea. Why not invite them properly? While Mrs. Jennings was occupied, it would be a simple kindness—one that might fulfill his father’s dying wish. The expense was negligible, the inconvenience slight. It was a duty, he decided, to ease his conscience.

Fanny, however, recoiled at the suggestion. “But they’re Lady Middleton’s visitors,” she protested. “They spend every day with her. How can we take them away?”
“Lady Middleton has no right to claim them forever,” John replied gently. “They’ve already spent a week with her. A few more days with us would be perfectly reasonable.”
Fanny hesitated. Then, with sudden resolve, she said, “I’d gladly invite them—if I could. But I’ve already promised the Miss Steeles. They’re good girls. Their uncle helped Edward. We should show our gratitude. Besides, they may not be in town next year. I’m sure you’ll like them—you already do. And Harry adores them.”

John, convinced, accepted. He would invite his sisters another year. But privately, he suspected that year would never come—because Elinor would likely be married to Colonel Brandon, and Marianne would be visiting them as a guest.

Fanny, relieved and triumphant, wrote immediately to Lucy, inviting her and her sister to stay in Harley Street as soon as Lady Middleton could spare them. The note was a triumph for Lucy. It felt as though Mrs. Dashwood herself was working on her behalf—nurturing her hopes, advancing her cause. To be near Edward, to be welcomed into his family, was her greatest desire. The invitation was a dream fulfilled. She declared the visit to Lady Middleton would end in two days’ time—though it had previously had no set limit.

When Elinor saw the letter, her own feelings shifted. Such favor, so swiftly granted, suggested Lucy’s goodwill stemmed from something deeper than mere spite. Perhaps, with time and effort, it could grow into something powerful. Lucy had already won over Lady Middleton with her flattery and touched Mrs. John Dashwood with her charm. If she could win them, perhaps she could win more.

The Miss Steeles moved into Harley Street. Sir John, visiting frequently, returned with glowing reports. Mrs. Dashwood, he said, had never been so pleased with any young women in her life. She had given each a needlebook made by an emigrant, called Lucy by her first name, and lamented that she might never part with them.

And so, the second volume came to a close.
