"The most exquisite transgression I ever orchestrated," Flambeau would later confess in his pious twilight years, "coincidentally marked my final descent into villainy. It occurred during the Christmas season. As an artist of crime, I sought to tailor offenses to the specific geography and atmosphere of my stage, treating each location as a canvas for a living tableau. Thus, a squire should be fleeced within the oak-panelled grandeur of a long gallery; conversely, a Jew should discover sudden destitution amidst the gilded glare of the Café Riche. In England, if I desired to strip a dean of his wealth—a feat far more difficult than it appears—I required the verdant lawns and grey stone spires of a cathedral city. Similarly, in France, extracting coin from a wealthy peasant demanded the backdrop of solemn Gaulish plains, where the spirit of Millet brooded over fields of clipped poplars.

"My final crime was a Christmas offense, a cheerful, cozy, quintessentially English bourgeois tragedy in the vein of Charles Dickens. I executed it in a respectable middle-class residence near Putney, featuring a crescent driveway, an adjacent stable, a house bearing its name upon twin gates, and a distinctive monkey tree. The setting was archetypal. I must admit, my mimicry of Dickensian style was dexterous, though I regretted adopting that persona the very same evening."

Flambeau would then recount the tale from the perspective of the perpetrator, yet even this internal view retained an uncanny strangeness. Observed from without, the drama appeared utterly inexplicable; therefore, the stranger must analyze it from the vantage point of the observer. From this external standpoint, the tragedy commenced when the front doors of the house with the stable swung open onto the garden containing the monkey tree, and a young girl emerged carrying bread to feed the birds on the afternoon of Boxing Day. She possessed a lovely countenance with courageous brown eyes, though her silhouette remained enigmatic, obscured by layers of brown fur until one could barely distinguish hair from fabric. Without her striking face, she might have resembled a small, toddling bear.

The winter afternoon was deepening toward dusk, casting a ruby hue over the flowerbeds, which now held only the ghostly remnants of dead roses. To the left stood the stable; to the right, an alley of laurels led to the expansive rear gardens. After scattering crumbs for the avians—the fourth or fifth time that day, as the canine had previously consumed the offering—the young woman moved unobtrusively down the laurel path and into a shimmering grove of evergreens behind. Here, she emitted an exclamation of genuine or ritualized wonder. Gazing upward at the high garden wall above her, she beheld a fantastical figure astride the boundary like an aerial rider.

"Oh, do not leap, Mr. Crook!" she cried, her voice tinged with alarm. "The height is prohibitive!"

The individual perched atop the party wall, mimicking an equestrian, was a tall, angular youth with dark hair standing erect like bristles on a brush. His features were intelligent and distinguished, yet his complexion was sallow and distinctly alien. This pallor was accentuated by an aggressive red tie, the sole element of his attire he seemed to treat with care. It may have served as a symbol. Ignoring the girl's frantic warning, he leaped from the wall to the ground beside her, landing with the grace of a grasshopper, though the impact threatened to fracture his limbs.

"I believe I was destined to be a burglar," he stated placidly. "Had I not been born in this pleasant house next door, I undoubtedly would have succeeded. I perceive no inherent malice in the attempt."

"How can you utter such sentiments!" she remonstrated.

"Well," the young man replied, "if one is born on the wrong side of the wall, I fail to see why crossing it constitutes wrongdoing."

"I cannot predict your words or actions," she noted.

"Nor can I often predict my own," Crook responded. "However, I am currently positioned on the 'right' side of the wall."

"And which side constitutes the right?" the young lady asked, smiling.

"Whichever side you occupy," the man named Crook declared.

As they traversed the laurels toward the front garden, the triple blast of a motor horn announced an approaching vehicle. A car of magnificent speed, elegance, and pale green livery swept up to the front doors like a bird and idled, its engine throbbing.

"Hullo, hullo!" exclaimed the youth in the red tie. "Here comes someone born on the right side, at least. I did not realize, Miss Adams, that your Santa Claus had evolved into such modern machinery."

"Oh, that is my godfather, Sir Leopold Fischer. He invariably arrives on Boxing Day."

Following a brief, innocuous pause that inadvertently betrayed a lack of fervor, Ruby Adams added:

"He is exceedingly kind."

John Crook, a journalist, was acquainted with this eminent City magnate; conversely, it was not Crook's fault if the magnate lacked knowledge of him, particularly regarding the austere treatments Sir Leopold received in articles within *The Clarion* or *The New Age*. Yet, Crook remained silent, observing grimly as the motor-car underwent a lengthy unloading procedure. A large, immaculate chauffeur in green exited the front, while a smaller, equally neat manservant in grey descended from the rear. Together, they deposited Sir Leopold upon the doorstep and proceeded to unwrap him as if he were a fragile artifact. Baskets of rugs sufficient to stock a bazaar, furs representing every beast of the forest, and scarves displaying the entire spectrum of colors were removed one by one, eventually revealing a form resembling humanity: a friendly yet foreign-looking elderly gentleman with a grey, goat-like beard and a beaming smile, rubbing his massive fur gloves together.

Before this revelation concluded, the double doors of the porch swung open, and Colonel Adams—the father of the fur-clad young lady—emerged personally to escort his distinguished guest indoors. He was a towering, sunburnt, and profoundly taciturn figure, wearing a red smoking-cap reminiscent of a fez, giving him the appearance of an English Sirdar or Pasha in Egypt. Accompanying him was his brother-in-law, recently arrived from Canada, a burly and boisterous young gentleman-farmer known as James Blount, sporting a yellow beard. Also present was the less conspicuous figure of the priest from the neighboring Roman Church; for the Colonel's late wife had been Catholic, and consequently, the children had been raised in her faith. Everything about the priest seemed nondescript, extending even to his name, Brown, yet the Colonel consistently found him companionable, frequently inviting him to family gatherings.

Within the spacious entrance hall, there was ample room for Sir Leopold and the removal of his garments. Indeed, the porch and vestibule were disproportionately large relative to the house, forming a vast antechamber with the front door at one extremity and the base of the staircase at the other. Before the roaring hearth, beneath which hung the Colonel's sword, the process culminated, presenting the assembled company—including the saturnine Crook—to Sir Leopold Fischer. The venerable financier, however, continued to struggle with the layers of his well-lined attire. Eventually, he produced from a deep interior pocket of his tailcoat a black oval case, which he radiantly identified as his Christmas gift for his goddaughter. With an unaffected vanity that possessed a disarmingly charming quality, he extended the case before them all. Upon a mere touch, it sprang open, momentarily dazzling them. It was as if a crystal fountain erupted directly into their eyes. Nestled in orange velvet lay three white, vivid diamonds, burning with an intensity that seemed to ignite the surrounding air. Fischer stood beaming benevolently, savoring the astonishment and ecstasy of the girl, the grim admiration and gruff gratitude of the Colonel, and the collective wonder of the group.

"I shall return them now, my dear," said Fischer, sliding the case back into his coat tails. "I exercised extreme caution transporting them. They are the three great African diamonds known as 'The Flying Stars,' owing to their frequent theft. Every major criminal is tracking them; yet, even the rough elements roaming the streets and hotels could scarcely resist them. I might easily have lost them along the road here. It was entirely plausible."

"Quite natural, I should surmise," growled the man in the red tie. "I would not blame them if they had taken them. When they beg for bread and receive not even a stone, I posit they might seize the stone for themselves."

"I forbid you to speak thus," cried the girl, glowing with curiosity. "You have adopted such language only since becoming a detestable... what's-his-name. Do you understand? What term describes a man who wishes to embrace the chimney-sweep?"

"A saint," replied Father Brown.

"I believe," interjected Sir Leopold with a superior smile, "that Ruby intends to refer to a Socialist."

"A radical does not denote a man subsisting on radishes," Crook remarked with impatience. "Nor does a Conservative imply a man preserving jam. Furthermore, I assure you, a Socialist does not signify a man desiring a social evening with the chimney-sweep. A Socialist advocates for all chimneys being swept and all chimney-sweeps compensated accordingly."

"But who refuses to permit you," the priest whispered, "to own your own soot."
