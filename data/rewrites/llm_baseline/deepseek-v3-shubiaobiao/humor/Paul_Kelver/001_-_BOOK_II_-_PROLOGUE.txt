At the far end of a London street so monotonous it could make a brick weep—a street where the windows and doorsteps line up with military precision, as if auditioning for a geometry textbook—there’s a house. Not just any house, mind you. This one has *character*, which is more than can be said for its neighbors, whose idea of architectural flair is "let’s all look exactly the same forever."

This house, you see, was built back when the area still had delusions of being countryside, before the marshland gave up and accepted its fate as a soot-stained urban wasteland. Now it sulks behind a patch of dirt that might’ve been a garden once, if optimism were a fertilizer. Clothes hang there like spectral flags of surrender, perpetually drying in the smog. The porch, stripped of its stucco vanity, stands naked and unashamed—much like my dignity after three pints.

Occasionally, I wander by in daylight, when the street is alive with the melodious cries of coal vendors (who’ve long since given up hope) and the clatter of gossip at every gate. But the house, embarrassed by its shabbiness under the sun’s harsh glare, pretends not to know me. Only at night, when the shadows are kind enough to hide its flaws, does it welcome me like an old nanny with too many stories and not enough filters.

Then the street falls silent, the drunks at the "King of Prussia" across the way pass out, and the house and I get to chatting. It’s like flipping through a scrapbook filled with faces you half-remember—some fondly, some with a shudder.

First comes my father, hobbling along with his fancy cane, pausing every fifty yards like a wind-up toy running low on steam.

*"Handsome chap, wasn’t he?"* I muse.

*"Oh, sure,"* snorts the house. *"Before life chewed him up and spat him out. Though honestly, what did he expect, setting up shop as a solicitor in Poplar? ‘A rising neighborhood,’ he said. Rising straight into the Thames, more like."*

I bristle. *"It was a calculated risk!"*

*"Calculated by a toddler, maybe,"* retorts the house. *"The man starved himself on five shillings a week just to keep his name clean, and you think he’d have thrived among dockyard hustlers? Please."*

Then my mother’s face flickers by, soft and sad. *"And her! Bless her heart, she couldn’t spot a viper in a nursery. Trusted the wrong people, loved the wrong ones, cried over spilt milk and then cried harder when there was no milk left to spill."*

*"Children,"* I mutter.

*"Exactly!"* crows the house. *"The pair of them, bumbling through life like kittens in a dog park."*

A tiny ghost of a girl darts past the railings—her feet pattering like raindrops. *"Whatever happened to her?"* I ask.

*"Oh, she’s around,"* says the house, grinning. *"Fourteen stone and nine kids later. Runs a shop. You’d walk right past her. Probably for the best."*

Other faces swirl in the gloom: a jolly hypocrite I never quite trusted, a rogue with a mouth like sin and eyes like salvation, a sweet old lady who’d skin you alive if you looked at her sideways. And at the center of it all, a quiet boy with curls too pretty for his own good, watching the world like it’s a puzzle he’ll never solve.

*"Would he even know me now?"* I wonder.

*"Doubt it,"* says the house. *"You’re not the ghost he’d expect."*

Then, as always, it needles me: *"You’re a writer. Why not tell his story?"*

*"Because nothing happened!"* I protest. *"No dragons, no princesses, just… life."*

*"And what’s more interesting than that?"* snaps the house. *"You think people only want tales of heroes and villains? Half of ’em are just trying to get through the day without setting their trousers on fire."*

So here it is—a story without much glory, where the good guys fumble, the bad guys aren’t all bad, and the magic thread leads straight into a ditch. If you’re the sort who likes your endings neat and your morals clearer than gin, move along. But if you’ve ever looked in the mirror and thought, *"Well, that could’ve gone better,"* then pull up a chair.

Just don’t blame me if it’s a bit of a mess. Blame the house. It started this.
