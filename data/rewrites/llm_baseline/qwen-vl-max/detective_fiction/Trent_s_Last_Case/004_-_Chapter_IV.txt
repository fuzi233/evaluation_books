Philip Trent, painter and son of a painter, had by his early thirties earned a name for himself in the world of English art. His work sold—solidly, steadily—and not just because of family connections or inherited wealth, though both helped. What truly set him apart was a rare combination: an original, forceful talent, matched with a disciplined yet passionate approach to creation. He painted not in bursts of frenzy, but in long, quiet stretches of concentration, punctuated by sudden surges of inspiration. More than that, he had a gift—unconscious, perhaps—for making people like him. He was good-humored, quick-witted, and genuinely interested in others. People felt at ease around him, never self-conscious, never under scrutiny. Whether laughing through absurdity or deep in thought, his face retained a spark of alertness, as if life itself were always a fascinating puzzle.

His education extended beyond painting. He read widely, though without pretension, and poetry was his true passion. At thirty-two, he still lived with the energy of youth, unburdened by routine, unafraid of adventure.

It was only by chance that he stepped into another world—one where logic and imagination became tools of detection.

One day, flipping through a newspaper, he came across a report of a murder committed on a train—a rare event in England. The case was baffling; two men were held under suspicion. Intrigued, Trent began reading every account he could find. He found himself reconstructing the facts in his mind, rearranging them, testing possibilities. A strange excitement took hold—something akin to the thrill of artistic creation, or of a daring escapade. That evening, he wrote a long letter to the editor of *The Record*, chosen simply because it had offered the most thorough coverage.

In it, he did what Edgar Allan Poe once did in the Mary Rogers case: he pointed out overlooked details, reorganized the evidence, and cast doubt on a man who had presented himself as a witness. Sir James Molloy, the paper’s editor, printed the letter in bold type. Later that same night, the *Sun* announced the arrest and confession of the man Trent had named.

Sir James, well-connected in London society, quickly sought out Trent. The two hit it off immediately—Trent possessed a natural charm that erased differences in age and status. He even painted a scene of the *Record*'s printing presses, which Sir James bought sight unseen, calling it a “machinery-scape” in the style of Heinrich Kley.

Then came the Ilkley mystery. Sir James invited Trent to dinner, then offered him a staggering sum to serve as the paper’s special correspondent. “You can write,” he said. “You know how to talk to people. And you’ve got a mind for puzzles. Think how it would feel if you cracked it.”

Trent hesitated. It was unfamiliar territory. But fear had long been something he fought against—not with courage, but with habit. He accepted.

He cracked it. Again, he gave the authorities a surprise, and again, his name spread across the country. Afterward, he returned to painting. He had no desire for journalism. Sir James, respectful of art and artists, left him alone. Yet over the years, he’d called upon Trent nearly thirty times—sometimes to solve mysteries abroad, sometimes in England. Sometimes Trent declined; sometimes the truth was already uncovered. But each time, his involvement added to his growing fame.

And now, walking briskly up the hill toward White Gables, Trent mused that the Manderson case might be simpler than it seemed. Cupples, the hotel manager, was wise—but likely biased when it came to his niece. Still, the man had spoken passionately of her beauty, but even more so of her character. “There isn’t a child here who doesn’t brighten at the sound of her voice,” he’d said. “Not a grown-up either. Everyone looked forward to her coming in summer. She’s not just kind—she’s got backbone. Pluck. Any amount of go.” And then, quietly: “Nobody in Marlstone isn’t sorry for her—though some may think she’s lucky, after all.”

Trent wanted to meet her.

Ahead, through a wide lawn and shrubbery, stood the house: a modern two-story structure of dull-red brick, its twin gables giving it its name. Ten years old, perhaps. Well-maintained, peaceful, serene. Behind it stretched woodlands and valleys; before it, meadowland sloped down to cliffs. It was hard to imagine violence here—so orderly, so rich in quiet dignity. Yet there, near the garden hedge and the gardener’s toolshed, the body had been found—tumbled against the wooden wall.

Trent walked past the gate, along the road until he reached the shed. Forty yards farther, the road turned sharply into thick plantations. At the corner, a small white gate marked the boundary. He approached it, opened it gently, and stepped onto a path between a high hedge and a wall of rhododendrons. Through a gap, he saw the wooden shed nestled among trees, facing a corner of the house.

He examined the area carefully—searching the ground, crouching low, fingers probing, eyes scanning. But nothing remained except the flattened grass where the body had lain. The search yielded nothing.

Then came a sound—the first from the house: the click of a front door closing.

Trent straightened, stepping to the edge of the drive. A man emerged from the house, walking quickly toward the main gate.

At the crunch of gravel, the man turned sharply, eyes wide. His face was pale, drawn—yet young. No wrinkles marred the hollows beneath his blue eyes, though they spoke of exhaustion, of sleepless nights. He was broad-shouldered, lithe, strong—his carriage stiff with fatigue, but his bearing unmistakable. His short, smooth blond hair, regular features, and calm voice betrayed a certain discipline. Trent smiled inwardly. “Oxford was your playground, I think,” he murmured.

“If you’re Mr. Trent,” the young man said, “you’re expected. Mr. Cupples called from the hotel. My name is Marlowe.”

“You were secretary to Mr. Manderson?” Trent asked.

“Yes,” Marlowe replied. “I’ve been driving all night and most of yesterday. Didn’t sleep after hearing the news. But I have an appointment now—down at the doctor’s. The inquest will probably be tomorrow. If you’ll go up to the house and ask for Mr. Bunner, he’ll expect you. He’s the other secretary—an American, a fine fellow. He’ll look after you. There’s a detective here too—Inspector Murch, from Scotland Yard. Came yesterday.”

“Murch!” Trent exclaimed. “But we’re old friends. How on earth did he get here so fast?”

Marlowe shrugged. “No idea. But he was here last night, before I returned from Southampton. Interviewing everyone. He’s been here since eight this morning. He’s in the library—by the open French window at the end of the house. You might want to speak with him.”

“I think I will,” Trent said. Marlowe nodded and continued on his way.

Trent moved silently across the thick turf, his footsteps as quiet as a cat’s. In moments, he was peering through the open leaves of the south-facing window. Inside, a large man bent over papers on a table—back broad, head bent, grizzled hair cut short.

“’Twas ever thus,” Trent said softly. “From childhood’s hour I’ve seen my fondest hopes decay. I thought I’d beat Scotland Yard this time—only to find the biggest officer in the entire Metropolitan force already in position.”

The man turned abruptly.

The inspector smiled grimly and stepped to the window. “I was expecting you, Mr. Trent. This is the sort of case you enjoy.”

“Since my tastes are being considered,” Trent replied, stepping inside, “I wish they’d kept my hated rival out of it. You’ve got a head start—I know all about it.” His eyes scanned the room. “How did you manage? You’re fast, I know—but how did you get here in time to be working last night? Did Scotland Yard start an aviation corps? Or are you in league with the devil? Either way, the Home Secretary should make a statement.”

“It’s simpler,” Murch said, matter-of-fact. “I was on leave with my wife at Havley—twelve miles down the coast. As soon as word came, I wired the Chief and was put in charge. I bicycled over last night. Been at it ever since.”

“Ah,” Trent said, distracted. “And how is Mrs. Inspector Murch?”

“Never better,” Murch replied. “She often speaks of you and the games you used to play with our kids. But I must say, Mr. Trent, you don’t need to talk nonsense while using your eyes. I know your ways by now. I understand you’ve secured permission to investigate. You’re going to cut me out again, aren’t you? I owe you one for beating me on the Abinger case. But if you’re not in the mood for pleasantries, let’s skip the compliments and get to business.”

Trent moved to the table, glanced over the papers, then opened the roll-top desk. He checked the drawers swiftly.

“Cleared out,” he noted. “Well then, inspector—let’s play the game as usual.”

Trent had met Inspector Murch many times before. He was a quiet, shrewd man—highly respected in the Criminal Investigation Department. Courageous, methodical, with a history of dealing with dangerous criminals. Broad in frame and broader in humanity. Their relationship was unusual: a mutual respect born of rivalry and shared curiosity. They had developed a kind of unspoken code—what Trent called “detective sportsmanship.” Neither used information gained from the other for journalistic purposes. Each reserved the right to withhold key insights. Yet they discussed cases freely, often learning from one another. Victory alternated between them—sometimes Murch’s experience, sometimes Trent’s intuition.

Now, leaning beside the French window, they reviewed the case.

Trent pulled out a thin notebook and began sketching a rough plan of the room—a habit he cultivated. It was usually idle, but occasionally useful.

This was a large, light room at the corner of the house. Two walls had generous windows. A broad table stood in the center. To the left, near the entrance, was the roll-top desk. The inner door was at the far end of the left wall, opposite a wide casement window. A carved corner cupboard rose high against the wall beyond the door. Another cupboard filled a recess beside the fireplace. Colored prints by Harunobu hung on the remaining walls. Books lined the shelves—bound in sober elegance, arranged like soldiers in formation. Most hadn’t been touched. Furniture was sparse: carved oak chairs, a modern armchair, a swivel office chair. The room was expensive but cold. The only movable items: a porcelain bowl of vivid blue on the table, a clock and cigar boxes on the mantel, and a telephone stand on the desk.

“Seen the body?” Murch asked.

Trent nodded. “And the spot where it lay.”

“The case puzzles me,” Murch said. “At first, I thought it might be a tramp—common robbery, though rare here. But no signs of burglary. No theft. The body wasn’t robbed. If it weren’t for a few facts, it’d look like suicide.”

“And why isn’t it suicide?” Trent asked, sitting on the window threshold, knees clasped.

“For one thing—no weapon. We’ve searched everywhere. Nothing within a stone’s throw. Second—the wrists. Fresh scratches, bruises—clear signs of a struggle. Third—who shoots themselves in the eye? And then there’s this: Manderson dressed fully before going out. But he forgot his false teeth. How does a man planning suicide, dressing neatly for his own corpse, forget such a thing?”

“That hadn’t occurred to me,” Murch admitted. “But it has weight. Still, I’m not considering suicide. I’ve been looking around the house. I expect you’re doing the same.”

“I am,” Trent said. “This is a case for ideas. Let’s begin with suspicion. Let’s suspect everyone. I suspect Mrs. Manderson, of course. Both secretaries—there are two, and I can’t decide which is more suspicious. The butler, the lady’s maid, the other servants—especially the boot-boy. By the way—what domestics do you have?”

“All very well to laugh,” Murch replied. “But at this stage, suspicion is the only safe principle. As for the staff—there’s the butler, lady’s maid, cook, three maids (one young), one chauffeur—away with a broken wrist. No boy.”

“What about the gardener?” Trent asked. “You’re keeping him hidden. Play the game—out with him—or I’ll report you to the Rules Committee.”

“The garden is cared for by a man from the village—he comes twice a week. I spoke to him. Last visit was Friday.”

“Then I suspect him all the more,” Trent said. “Now—about the house. I’ll start with Manderson’s room and bedroom. But since we’re here—what have you found?”

Murch nodded. “I’ve been through both bedrooms. Nothing significant. Manderson’s room is bare—simple, almost like a cell. No valet. Clothes and shoes—nothing unusual. Exactly as he left it, they say. Opens into Mrs. Manderson’s room—which is anything but a cell. She likes pretty things. But she cleared out after the discovery—said she couldn’t sleep in a room opening into her husband’s. Understandable. She’s staying in a spare bedroom now.”

Trent scribbled notes. “Have you got your eye on her?” he muttered. “That tone—you’re either hiding something, or you’re sure she’s innocent. Either way, it’s part of the game. And the game is getting interesting.”

To Murch, he said aloud: “I’ll draw the bedroom later. What about this?”
