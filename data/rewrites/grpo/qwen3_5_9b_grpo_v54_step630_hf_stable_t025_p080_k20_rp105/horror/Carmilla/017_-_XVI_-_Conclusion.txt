XVI. Conclusion

I write these words with a facade of composure, though the truth is, my hands tremble as I hold the pen. There is no calm in this recounting; only your repeated, earnest pleas could have dragged me from my sanctuary to undertake a task that has left my nerves frayed for months. It forces me to revisit the unspeakable horror that, even years after my deliverance, casts a shadow over my days and makes solitude a terror I cannot endure.

Before I close this account, I must speak of the quaint Baron Vordenburg. It was his curious, obsessive lore that led us to the grave of Countess Mircalla. He had retreated to Graz, surviving on a meager pittance from the shattered princely estates of his family in Upper Styria, dedicating his life to the minute investigation of vampirism. He knew every great and obscure work on the subject: *Magia Posthuma*, *Phlegon de Mirabilibus*, *Augustinus de cura pro Mortuis*, and John Christofer Herenberg's *Philosophicae et Christianae Cogitationes de Vampiris*. From a voluminous digest of judicial cases, he had extracted a system of principles governing the vampire's condition.

He taught me that the deadly pallor attributed to these revenants is a melodramatic fiction. In the grave, or when they emerge into human society, they possess the appearance of healthy life. Only when exposed to light within their coffins do they reveal the true signs of their undead existence. How they escape their tombs and return within specific hours, leaving not a single trace of disturbance in the clay or the shrouds, remains inexplicable. Their amphibious life is sustained by daily slumber in the earth, fueled by a terrible hunger for living blood that grants them vigor while awake.

They are prone to a fascination resembling the passion of love, driven by an engrossing vehemence toward specific individuals. To reach such a victim, they exercise inexhaustible patience and stratagem, overcoming a hundred obstacles. They will never cease until they have satiated their passion, draining the very life from their chosen prey. Yet, in these cases, they refine their murder with the taste of an epicure, heightening the torment through the gradual approaches of artful courtship, seemingly yearning for a semblance of sympathy or consent. In ordinary instances, however, they strike directly, overpowering with violence and exhausting the victim at a single feast.

The vampire is also subject to peculiar conditions. As you know, Mircalla was limited to names that were anagrams of her true identity—Carmilla, Millarca.

My father recounted to the Baron the story of the Moravian nobleman and the vampire at the Karnstein churchyard. When asked how the Baron had located the concealed tomb, his grotesque features puckered into a mysterious smile. He looked down at his worn spectacles, fumbling with the case, before speaking.

"I possess many journals," he said, "written by that remarkable man. The most curious treats of the visit you speak of. Tradition distorts the tale slightly. He might have been called a Moravian nobleman, having moved there, but he was truly a native of Upper Styria. In his youth, he was a passionate, favored lover of the beautiful Mircalla, Countess Karnstein. Her early death plunged him into inconsolable grief. Vampires multiply according to a ghostly law: a person who ends their own life, or is haunted by such demons, may become a vampire. This happened to Mircalla. My ancestor, Vordenburg, soon discerned this. He feared that suspicion would eventually fall upon her remains, leading to a posthumous execution that would profane her. He believed that expelling a vampire from its amphibious existence projected it into a far more horrible life. Driven by a horror for her desecration, he devised a stratagem: a pretended removal of her remains and the real obliteration of her monument.

"When age stole upon him, looking back from the vale of years, he filled with remorse. He traced the deception he had practiced, making notes that would guide a descendant to the spot. Death prevented him from acting further, but my hand, too late for many, has followed his clues to the beast's lair."

We spoke further, and he revealed another sign: the power of the hand. The slender fingers of Mircalla had closed like a vice of steel upon the General's wrist when he raised the hatchet. That grasp left a numbness in the limb, a paralysis that is rarely, if ever, fully recovered.

That spring, my father took me on a tour through Italy. We remained away for more than a year. Long before the terror of recent events subsided, the image of Carmilla returned to my memory with ambiguous alternations. Sometimes she was the playful, languid, beautiful girl; sometimes the writhing fiend I saw in the ruined church. Often, waking from a reverie, I start in fear, fancying I hear the light step of Carmilla pausing just outside the drawing-room door.
