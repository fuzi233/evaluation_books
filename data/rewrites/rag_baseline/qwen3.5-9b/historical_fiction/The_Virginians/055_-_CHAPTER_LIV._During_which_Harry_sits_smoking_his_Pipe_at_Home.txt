The air in the dining room was thick with the scent of old paper and the lingering smoke of a pipe that Harry had not yet extinguished. Outside, the world moved in a rhythm of decay and renewal, but inside, time had settled into a static, dusty suspension. George Warrington watched his aunt, Madame de Bernstein, move with the deliberate, heavy grace of a woman carrying the weight of a lifetime behind her eyes. She was a ruin of a former splendor, a palace reduced to rubble, yet she still wore her diamonds as if they were the final, defiant jewels of a crown.

"Beauty," George thought, watching her adjust her rouge. "Once a firework, now a skeleton wheel." He recalled the explosion at Williamsburg, the dazzling Roman candles that had blinded everyone before leaving only sockets and ash. His aunt's early career had been that same brilliant, terrifying flash, burning bright enough to blind the crowd, only to leave them staring at the empty sky where the light had vanished. Now, she was the aftermath. The buds of prime had fallen; the roses were withered; the naked branches shivered in the winter wind of her own making.

He wondered if it was sad or pleasant for a woman to recall her bloom when the heart was already withered. Did she love remembering how it once beat? Or did she simply endure the silence of the present, waiting for the memory to fade completely?

"And that was a beauty once!" George murmured, the thought forming involuntarily as she entered from the rout. "And that ruin was a splendid palace."

She stopped, her cane tapping a sharp, rhythmic note against the floor. She did not turn immediately. Instead, she pointed with the tip of the cane toward her escritoire, a gesture of command that belied her frailty. "Bring the letter," she said to her attendant. "The one under the inkstand."

George recognized the handwriting instantly. It was from home. From Richmond.

"It appears," the old lady said, her voice dropping to a register that sounded like dry leaves skittering on stone, "that since your return, there have been differences between you and my sister."

"Differences?" George asked, feigning surprise. "I did not know Madam Esmond had alluded to them."

The Baroness pulled a pair of spectacles onto her nose. Through the lenses, her eyes shot sparks, kindling passions that had long since cooled in the damp Virginia air. She glanced at the letter, then handed it to George. He unfolded the paper.

The words were formal, stiff, trying to impose order on chaos. The letter spoke of Harry returning from the Valley of the Shadow, of the anguish of captivity, of the false colors under which he had appeared in England. It detailed the tragedy of George's death, the injustice of Harry inheriting the title while his brother lay buried. But beneath the formalities, the raw emotion seeped through.

"My Harry's is the tenderest of any man's now alive," the letter read. "In the joy of seeing Mr. Esmond Warrington returned... he will forget the worldly misfortune which befalls him. He will return to comparative poverty without a pang."

George listened as his aunt read aloud, her voice rising and falling with the cadence of a recitation she had heard a thousand times. She paused at the mention of the lost opportunity—the land, the negroes, the noble estate that had slipped away because of legal forms and her elder son's scruples.

"I had a fancy to pay that debt myself," she interrupted, her tone shifting from reading to argument. "Why not have kept it to buy a pair of colours for him? Or to help towards another estate?"

"Nay, sir," George replied softly. "I ask leave to be my brother's banker in this matter. I have borrowed so much from my mother; it must be paid back to Harry."

"You are an extravagant fellow!" she laughed, a brittle sound. "Read on. Your mother thinks so too. Drink to your health, nephew George! 'Tis good Burgundy. Your grandfather never loved Burgundy. He loved claret."

George smiled faintly, taking the glass. He continued reading, letting the words wash over him. The letter spoke of Harry's obedience, of the danger of youth, of the unsuitability of Lady Maria Esmond. It praised Harry's polish gained in Europe, his refinement, his ability to be beloved by everyone. It was a portrait of perfection, painted by a grieving mother who saw only the virtues of her favorite son.

"There is a scratch with a penknife," George noted, pointing to a smudge near the bottom. "As if water had fallen on it. Your mother writes well, Aunt. I suppose you and she had a difference?"

"Yes, ma'am, many," George answered, his face falling into a shadow of sadness. "The last was about money. About ransom. I promised an old lieutenant who helped me escape. He had a mistress—a poor Indian woman—who helped me. She came to Richmond six weeks later, bringing the token from Museau. But at home... they received her sternly. They hardly gave her a welcome. She fell to drinking. She became the jest of our negroes, the subject of scandal. Even the Governor, usually sensible, could not stop the gossip. A fellow preached against me from the pulpit; I had to treat another with a cane. And I had a violent dispute with Madam Esmond because I insisted on paying the heirs Museau pointed out."

"I have suffered from it myself, my dear!" Madame de Bernstein said, her demeanor softening instantly. She poured a little cherry-brandy into his glass. "Fill thy glass, child! 'Twill do thee all the good in the world."

"As for my poor Harry's marriage," the letter continued, detailing the rejection of Lady Maria and the decision to let Harry return to his mother rather than face a lonely future. It ended with a postscript: *No need to tell him or your ladyship that his mother hopes to increase his small fortune. The match is altogether unsuitable.*

George laid down the paper. "My mother's letter conveys no news to me," he said quietly. "I always knew that Harry was the favorite son. He has a hundred good qualities which I have not the good fortune to possess. Better looks..."

"Nay, that is not your fault," the old lady said, looking at him slyly. "And, but that he is fair and you are brown, one might almost pass for the other."

George bowed, a faint blush tinging his pale cheek. "His disposition is bright, and mine is dark. Harry is cheerful, and I am otherwise. He knows how to make himself beloved by everyone. It has been my lot to find but few friends."

"My sister and you have pretty little quarrels," the Baroness mused, leaning forward. "There were such in old days in our family. If Madam Esmond takes after our mother—"

"My mother has always described hers as an angel upon earth," George interposed quickly.

"Eh! That is a common character for people when they are dead!" the Baroness cried, waving her fan. "Rachel Castlewood was an angel, if you like—at least your grandfather thought so. But let me tell you, sir, that angels are sometimes not very commodes à vivre. They are too good to live with us sinners. The air down below doesn't agree with them. My poor mother was so perfect she never could forgive me for being otherwise. Ah, mon Dieu! How she used to oppress me with those angelical airs!"

George cast down his eyes. He thought of his own melancholy youth, the isolation that had defined him since childhood. He did not care to submit more of his family secrets to the cynical inquisition of this old worldling, who seemed, paradoxically, to understand him perfectly despite her reticence.

"I quite comprehend you, sir, though you hold your tongue," the Baroness continued, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "A sermon in the morning: a sermon at night: and two or three of a Sunday. That is what people call being good. Every pleasure cried *fie* upon; all us worldly people excommunicated; a ball an abomination of desolation; a play a forbidden pastime; and a game of cards perdition! What a life! Mon Dieu, what a life!"

"We played at cards every night, if we were so inclined," George said, smiling despite himself. "And my grandfather loved Shakespeare so much that my mother had not a word to say against her father's favorite author."

"I remember. He could say whole pages by heart. Though, for my part, I like Mr. Congreve a great deal better. And then, there was that dreadful, dreary Milton, whom he and Mr. Addison pretended to admire!" The old lady tapped her fan sharply against the table.

"If your ladyship does not like Shakespeare, you will not quarrel with my mother for being indifferent to him, too," George said gently. "And indeed I think—and I am sure—you don't do her justice. Wherever there are any poor, she relieves them; wherever there are any sick, she—"

"She doses them with her horrible purges and boluses!" the Baroness snapped. "Of course, just as my mother did!"

"She does her best to cure them! She acts for the best, and performs her duty as far as she knows it."
