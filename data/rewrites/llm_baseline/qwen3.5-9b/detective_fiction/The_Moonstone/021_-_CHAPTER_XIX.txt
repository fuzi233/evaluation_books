News of Rosanna's disappearance had quickly rippled through the ranks of the outdoor staff. Like everyone else, they had begun their frantic inquiries, and it was from these very people that a breakthrough emerged. They had cornered a small, energetic boy known locally as "Duffy," who spent his days weeding the gardens. Duffy claimed to have spotted Rosanna Spearman less than thirty minutes prior. He insisted she wasn't merely walking; she was sprinting toward the sea-shore, passing right through the fir plantation.

"Does this boy know the coastline?" Sergeant Cuff asked sharply.

"He was born and raised right here on the coast," I replied.

"Duffy!" the Sergeant barked, his voice cutting through the damp air. "Do you want to earn a shilling? If so, come with me immediately. Mr. Betteredge, keep the pony-chaise ready until I return."

Without another word, the Sergeant bolted toward the Shivering Sands, moving with a speed that defied my own aging limbs. Behind him, the spirited boy let out a wild cry and scrambled to match his pace.

Once the Sergeant was gone, a strange, paralyzing restlessness seized me. I wandered aimlessly inside and outside the house, performing a dozen futile tasks, none of which I could recall later. I remained in this daze until Duffy returned, breathless, carrying a message. The Sergeant had handed the boy a scrap of paper torn from his notebook, upon which was scrawled in pencil: *Send me one of Rosanna Spearman's boots, and be quick.*

I dispatched the nearest female servant to retrieve the boot from Rosanna's room and sent the boy back to inform me that I would accompany him. Admittedly, this was not the swiftest method of compliance, but I was determined to witness the scene firsthand before entrusting the crucial piece of evidence to the Sergeant. An old instinct to protect the girl, dormant until now, surged back with renewed force. Fueled by this mix of protective fervor and detective fever, I grabbed the boot and ran as hard as a man of seventy could manage.

As I approached the shore, the sky darkened ominously. Rain lashed down in heavy, white sheets driven by the gale, while the thunderous roar of the ocean echoed from the bay's mouth. Further along, I found the boy cowering beneath the lee of the sandhills. Beyond him lay the chaotic spectacle of the sea: towering rollers crashing onto the bank, the wind whipping the rain across the water like a frantic cloak, and the vast, yellow expanse of the beach dominated by a single, stark black figure. It was Sergeant Cuff.

He spotted me and waved urgently toward the north. "Stay on that side!" he shouted above the storm. "Come down here to me!"

I descended to him, gasping for air, my heart hammering against my ribs. Words failed me entirely; a hundred questions burned on my tongue, yet none escaped my lips. His expression terrified me—a look of sheer horror etched into his features. Snatching the boot from my hand, he placed it directly into a fresh footprint on the wet sand. That print bore south from our position, leading straight toward the rocky ledge known as the South Spit. The imprint was sharp, unblurred by the rain, and the fit of the boot was precise.

Cuff pointed silently at the boot within the track. Seizing his arm, I attempted to speak, but my voice remained trapped. He ignored me, following the trail downward until the sand merged with the jagged rocks. The South Spit was already submerged by the incoming tide, the waters churning over the hidden quicksand below. With an almost terrifying patience, Cuff tested the boot against the tracks repeatedly. Every time, the alignment pointed unequivocally *toward* the rocks. Yet, despite his exhaustive search, he found absolutely no signs of footsteps leading *away* from them.

Finally, he ceased his hunt. Turning his gaze from the tracks to the churning sea, he looked at me, then back out at the deepening waves. I mirrored his gaze and read the terrible realization in his eyes. A cold, numb tremor swept through me, and I collapsed to my knees on the beach.

"She has returned to the hiding place," Cuff murmured to himself. "A fatal accident must have occurred on those rocks."

Memories flooded my mind: Rosanna's changed demeanor, her hollow words, the deadened way she had accepted my presence just hours earlier. These warnings confirmed his suspicion, though perhaps not the full extent of the tragedy. I tried to explain that her death was self-inflicted, that she had sought it out, but the words refused to form. The paralysis held me fast. I felt neither the driving rain nor the rising tide. In a vivid, dreamlike vision, I saw her again as I had on the morning I brought her home. I remembered her confession that the Shivering Sand drew her irresistibly, her wonderment that her grave might wait for her *there*. The horror of it pierced me deeply, echoing through my own grief for my daughter, who was Rosanna's age. Even a life as miserable as hers could have ended in such a dreadful manner.

Gently, the Sergeant lifted me up and turned me away from the site of her demise. Only then did I begin to breathe again, forcing myself to view the world as it truly was. Looking toward the sandhills, I saw the outdoor servants and a fisherman named Yolland rushing down the slope, shouting inquiries about the body.

In brief, grim terms, Cuff explained the evidence of the footprints and declared that a fatality had undoubtedly occurred. He singled out the fisherman and posed a critical question, turning his attention back to the sea: "Could a boat have taken her off in weather like this, from those rocks where her tracks end?"

Yolland gestured wildly at the crashing rollers and the massive waves leaping against the headlands. "No boat ever built could reach her through that," he answered firmly.

Cuff studied the fading footprints, now being washed away by the rain. "Here," he stated, "is proof she cannot have left by land. And here," he added, nodding at the fisherman, "is proof she could not have fled by sea."

Pausing to consider, he addressed Yolland. "She was seen running toward this spot half an hour before I arrived. Let us assume an hour has passed since then. How high was the water on this side of the rocks at that time?" Pointing to the southern side, free of the quicksand, the fisherman replied that there wouldn't have been enough water to drown a kitten. On the northern side, covered by the shifting sand, the water level would have been negligible.

"Therefore," Cuff concluded, turning to me, "the accident must have happened on the side of the quicksand."

My tongue finally loosened. "Not an accident!" I cried out. "When she reached this place, she was weary of life and chose to end it here."

The Sergeant recoiled slightly. "How do you know?" he asked.

The group pressed closer, but Cuff swiftly pushed them back. "He is an old man," he said calmly. "The discovery has shaken him. Leave him be for a moment."

Turning to Yolland, he asked if there was any chance of recovery when the tide receded. "None," the fisherman replied sadly. "What the Sand takes, it keeps forever."

Stepping forward, Yolland addressed me directly. "Mr. Betteredge, regarding the young woman's death... four feet out, broadside along the Spit, lies a shelf of rock about half a fathom beneath the sand. My question is: why didn't she strike it? If she slipped accidentally from the Spit, she would have fallen into shallow water with footing at the bottom, barely covering her waist. Unless she deliberately waded or jumped into the Deep beyond, she would not be missing now. It was no accident, sir. The Deeps of the Quicksand claim her, and they took her by her own hand."

With this authoritative testimony, the Sergeant fell silent, and so did we all. Together, we turned back up the beach slope. Just as we reached the sandhills, the under-groom rushed to meet us from the house. He was a loyal lad with a natural respect for me, handing me a folded note with a solemn expression.

"Penelope sent this for you, Mr. Betteredge," he said softly. "She found it in Rosanna's room."

It was her final farewell to the old man who had strived tirelessly to care for her. *"You have often forgiven me, Mr. Betteredge, in times past. When you next see the Shivering Sand, try to forgive me once more. I have found my grave where my grave was waiting for me. I have lived, and died, sir, grateful for your kindness."*

Those were the only words, yet they broke me completely. Tears flow easily when one is young and starting out; they flow just as readily when one is old and leaving everything behind. I burst into uncontrollable sobbing.

Sergeant Cuff stepped closer, intending kindness. I flinched and backed away. "Don't touch me," I whispered. "It is the dread of you that drove her to this."

"You are mistaken, Mr. Betteredge," he replied quietly. "But there will be time to discuss this when we are indoors."

Supported by the groom's arm, I followed the others back through the driving rain, returning to the house to face the turmoil and terror that awaited us within.
