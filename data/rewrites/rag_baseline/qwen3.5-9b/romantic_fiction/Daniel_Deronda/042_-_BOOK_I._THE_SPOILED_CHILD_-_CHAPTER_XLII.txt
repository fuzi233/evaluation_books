"If there are rungs on the ladder of suffering, Israel stands at the summit; if endurance sanctifies pain, the Jews claim the nobility of every land; and if a literature is deemed rich because it possesses a handful of classical tragedies, what judgment awaits a National Tragedy sustained for fifteen hundred years, authored and enacted by the heroes themselves?" These were the words of Zunz, echoing in Daniel Deronda's mind as he approached the Cohen household. Yet, standing before the shopfront, the weight of those verses felt strangely distant. Ezra Cohen wore no mantle of martyrdom; instead, his eyes gleamed with the pragmatic triumph of a man who had mastered the alchemy of profit, a success that grated against the very essence of the Jewish dispersion. This pawnbroker, a Jeshurun of commerce, was no living symbol of the great national sorrow. And yet, a disquieting irony settled over Deronda: how could a fragile vessel of collective consciousness, like Mordecai's, breathe so tenuously within the self-congratulatory ignorance of the Cohens' prosperity?

When Deronda reappeared, the gloom of the street vanished, replaced by a radiant, almost blinding gladness on the faces of the family. Ezra Cohen seized the moment to explain that while a diamond ring—or indeed, a little more capital—might have yielded greater returns, he cared naught for the coin when weighed against the sheer delight of his women and children in hosting a young gentleman whose arrival had become the sole topic of their discourse. Young Mrs. Cohen expressed regret that the infant slept, only to pivot instantly to the joy that Adelaide remained awake, urging Deronda to abandon the shop and retreat to the parlour to meet "mother and the children." Accepting the invitation, Deronda carried his portable gifts: a set of paper figures for Adelaide and an ivory cup-and-ball game for Jacob.

Inside, the grandmother presided over a chaotic tableau of card-playing, tossing plates into the air with a dexterity that kept them miraculously intact. "Stop!" cried Jacob, darting toward Deronda as he crossed the threshold. "Do not tread on my plate. Watch me throw it up again." Deronda paused, sharing a knowing glance with the grandmother, and watched the porcelain arc through the air, shattering only after several attempts. Once the child was satisfied, Deronda stepped forward to take his seat. He noted that the door from which Mordecai had emerged on his previous visit was now firmly shut, a barrier he intended to breach only after establishing a rapport with the family.

Only when Adelaide rested on his lap, arranging her paper figures in a miniature ballet, and Jacob practiced his throws with the ivory ball, did Deronda finally inquire, "Is Mordecai in just now?"

Ezra Cohen, pausing his business to peer over his shoulder, asked, "Where is he, Addy?"

"In the workroom," his wife replied, nodding toward the sealed door.

"The truth is, sir," Cohen began, his tone shifting to a mixture of apology and pride, "we cannot fathom what has come over him these last days. He is... 'touched,' as one might say—not entirely rational in all things, unlike you and me—but he has been wonderfully regular and industrious, taking as much delight in the boy as anyone could. Lately, however, he moves like a sleepwalker or sits as still as a wax figure."

"It is the disease, poor soul," the grandmother whispered tenderly. "I fear he cannot withstand it much longer."

"No, no," corrected the younger Mrs. Cohen. "It is merely something in his head. He turns over writing constantly, and it takes him a long time to hear and respond when I speak to him."

"You may consider us weak ourselves," Cohen admitted apologetically. "But neither my wife nor my mother would part with him, even if he became a heavier burden. We understand the long and short of matters; it is simply our principle. Some fools conduct business at a loss without realizing it. I am not one of them."

"Oh, Mordecai carries a blessing within him," the grandmother insisted.

"He has something wrong inside him," Jacob interjected, stepping forward to amend his grandmother's error. "He said he couldn't talk to me, and he refused a single bun."

"Far from being surprised at your concern," Deronda said gently, "I share that sentiment myself. I recently spoke with him at Ram's bookshop—in fact, I promised to bring him here so we might walk together."

"That explains it!" Cohen slapped his knee, his demeanor brightening. "He has been waiting for you. It seems to have lifted him. He must be talking about his learning to you. How kind of you, sir; though I suspect there is little to extract from it, or he would not be where he is. But here comes the shop." Hurrying away, Jacob leaned close to Deronda's elbow with obliging familiarity. "I'll fetch Mordecai for you, if you wish."

"No, Jacob," his mother commanded softly. "Open the door for the gentleman and let him go in himself. Hush! Make no noise."

Jacob, playing his part with skillful discretion, turned the handle with barely a whisper. Deronda followed, standing on the threshold of the small room. Lit only by a dying fire and a single shaded candle, the space held a somber intimacy. Scattered across the board beneath the window lay various articles of jewelry, while books were heaped in the corner. Mordecai sat on a high chair with his back to the door, his hands clasped on the board, a watch propped on a stand before him. He sat in a state of agonizing anticipation, akin to a prisoner awaiting a delayed release. When he heard Deronda's voice—"I am come for you. Are you ready?"—he turned instantly, seizing his fur-lined cap and rising to join him.

Within moments, they stood in the sitting-room. Jacob, noting the shift in his friend's demeanor, grabbed Mordecai's arm. "Look at my cup and ball!" he exclaimed, launching the ball high near Mordecai's face, hoping to cheer a recovering spirit. The gesture worked; the tension in Mordecai's mind visibly relaxed, allowing a smile to break through. "Fine, fine!" he breathed.

"You have forgotten your greatcoat and comforter," young Mrs. Cohen reminded him, and he retreated briefly to retrieve them.

"He has returned to life, do you see?" Cohen whispered, re-entering the room with a triumphant undertone. "I told you so; I am mostly right." Then, in his normal voice, he added, "Well, sir, we need not detain you further, I trust. But I hope this is not the final time we shall see you."

"Will you come again?" Jacob asked, stepping forward eagerly. "Watch, I can catch the ball! I'll wager I can catch it without stopping if you return."

"He has remarkably skilled hands," Deronda remarked, turning to the grandmother. "From which side of the family does he inherit them?"

The grandmother merely nodded toward her son, who responded promptly, "My side. My wife's family possess no such aptitude. But bless your soul! Ours is a cleverness as durable as gutta-percha; you can twist it whichever way you please. There is nothing some elderly gentlemen will not accomplish if you set them to it." Cohen winked down at Jacob's retreating back, though the effect was dubious; the child emitted a nasal whinnying laugh and stamped his feet, singing, "Old gentlemen, old gentlemen," in a chiding cadence.

Deronda found himself thinking, *I shall never grasp the full measure of these people until I ask Cohen directly whether he lost a sister named Mirah when she was six.* The moment of inquiry did not yet feel accessible to him. Nevertheless, his initial repulsion at their commonness began to soften, tempered by a kinder observation. Despite their unrefined mannerisms and speech, he was compelled to acknowledge a genuine moral refinement in their care for the consumptive workman, whose intellectual distinction they perceived primarily as a harmless, silent rambling.

"The Cohens seem to harbor a special affection for you," Deronda observed as he and Mordecai stepped onto the doorstep.

"And I for them," came the immediate reply. "They possess the heart of the Israelite within them, though they remain like horses and mules, lacking understanding beyond the narrow path they tread."

"I fear I have caused you unease," Deronda said, "by my delay in keeping my promise. I intended to come yesterday, but found it impossible."

"Yes—yes, I trusted you. Yet I was truly uneasy, for the spirit of my youth has been stirred within me, and this body is too frail to bear the beating of its wings. I am as a man bound and imprisoned for many years: behold him brought to speech with his fellow, his limbs set free. He weeps, he totters; the joy within him threatens to break and overturn the tabernacle of flesh."

"You must not speak too freely in this evening air," Deronda advised, feeling the weight of Mordecai's reliance like cords binding him painfully. "Wrap your mouth in the woolen scarf. We are heading to the *Hand and Banner*, I presume, and shall find privacy there?"

Tonight is the evening of the club I mentioned, and we might not secure any minutes alone until late, when everyone else has departed. Perhaps we should seek another venue.
