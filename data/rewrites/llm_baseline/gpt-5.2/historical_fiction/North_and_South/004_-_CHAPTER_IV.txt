He was gone; the click of the garden-gate had died away into the thickening evening, and the house gathered itself up, shutters and curtains and all, as if to keep the day’s sweetness from escaping. The last of the deep blue and the crimson edge of the sunset were smothered by the familiar indoor dimness. Margaret went up to dress for early tea, and found Dixon in one of those tempers that made every pin seem an insult, and every passing footstep an interruption contrived on purpose.

Dixon’s brush tugged through Margaret’s hair with a business-like cruelty, as if haste were a virtue that would excuse impatience. All the while she talked—half to herself, half to the absent mistress—of the day’s disturbances: of the ironing-blanket, burnt again, as if fire were forever lying in wait for household linen; and of how a certain Susan Lightfoot had been seen with artificial flowers in her bonnet, those stiff, everlasting blossoms that smelt of nothing and yet proclaimed vanity louder than any scent. In Dixon’s mouth even a bonnet became a moral history.

Yet, for all Dixon’s hurry, Margaret had to wait a long while alone in the drawing-room before her mother came down. The fire had been newly fed; the unlighted candles stood in readiness behind her like pale sentinels. She sat close to the hearth, letting its heat creep into her chilled hands, and tried to put the day into order within her mind—as one might lay out a supper-table, each dish in its place, so the meal will not reproach you with confusion.

There had been the happy walk, the pleasant sketching, the cheerful dinner—how the roast had smelt when it was set down, how the gravy had shone, how the bread had been torn with that quiet domestic sound that always seemed to make a home—and then the uncomfortable, miserable walk in the garden, where every leaf had looked the same and yet everything had altered. How different men were to women. She was shaken and unhappy because her very instinct had made anything but a refusal impossible; while he—scarcely minutes after meeting what should have been the deepest, holiest rejection of his life—could speak as if briefs and success, and the superficial conveniences of a good house and clever society, were the only avowed objects of desire.

If he had but been different—different in something that went low, deep down—how she could have loved him. And then, as if her heart were determined to try every argument against itself, she wondered whether his lightness had been assumed, a bright crust over bitterness, such as might have stamped itself on her own heart had she loved and been rejected. The thought was like tasting an unknown herb—sharp, unsettling, and yet compelling you to taste again.

Her mother came in before Margaret could settle the whirl into any shape that resembled peace. Margaret had to shake off the day as one shakes flour from one’s hands before touching clean linen, and become the sympathising listener to Dixon’s reports: the burnt blanket, the vain bonnet, the little village judgments that were, for Mrs. Hale, as necessary as tea itself.

Tea was brought in—always at Helstone with a quiet certainty, as if the very kettle knew its hour. Mr. Hale sipped in abstracted silence. Margaret, answering her mother, found herself making all the responses as if her father were absent, though he sat there; and she could not understand how both her parents could be so forgetful, so regardless of the companion through the day, as never once to speak his name. She forgot—how could she not?—that he had not made them an offer.

After tea Mr. Hale rose and stood with his elbow on the chimney-piece, leaning his head on his hand. From time to time he sighed—deeply, as if each breath were a burden lifted and set down again. Mrs. Hale went out to consult with Dixon about winter clothing for the poor—flannel, shawls, the small provisions by which comfort is doled out in parcels. Margaret drew her mother’s worsted work towards her, arranging the skeins and needles with hands that wanted employment, and shrank from the thought of the long evening. She wished for bed-time, that she might go over the events of the day again in solitude, as one returns to taste in private what one could not swallow in company.

“Margaret!” said Mr. Hale at last, in a sudden, desperate way that made her start.

“Yes, papa?”

“Is that tapestry thing of immediate consequence? I mean—can you leave it and come into my study? I want to speak to you about something very serious—to us all.”

The words struck her like a cold draught through a warm room: very serious to us all. For one instant her mind flew, with shame and guilt, to what had happened—whether Mr. Lennox had contrived to speak to her father privately at last, and whether her refusal had been—was it for her to decide? But almost as soon as she entered the study and saw her father’s face, she knew it was not that. This sorrow was older; it had been feeding in him like a hidden fire.

He made her take a chair near him. He stirred the fire, though it did not need stirring; he snuffed the candles with needless care, as if light could be regulated into courage. He sighed once or twice before he could make up his mind. When the words came, they came with a jerk, as if torn free.

“Margaret! I am going to leave Helstone.”

“Leave Helstone, papa! But—why?” The very name tasted in her mouth like bread she had eaten all her life; to be told it would be taken away was to feel hunger at once.

Mr. Hale did not answer immediately. He played with some papers on the table, opening his lips to speak and closing them again, as though the truth had edges that cut. Margaret could not bear his suspense; it seemed to hurt him even more than her.

“But why, dear papa? Do tell me.”

He looked up suddenly, and then said with a slow, enforced calmness, “Because I must no longer be a minister in the Church of England.”

For a moment her mind refused to take it in. She had imagined anything else—some preferment at last, some change imposed by fortune that would still leave him what he had always been. Even the thought of leaving Helstone for some cathedral close, grand and silent, would have been pain enough. But this—this struck at the root of her idea of him. The study wavered before her eyes.

What could he mean? The mystery made it worse. On his face there was such piteous distress—almost an imploring of mercy from his own child—that a sudden sickening seized her. Could it be Frederick? Frederick, the outlaw—had her father connived at something, out of love? Her thoughts ran desperately, as if searching cupboards in the dark for some lost necessity.

“Oh! what is it? do speak, papa! tell me all! Why can you no longer be a clergyman? Surely, if the bishop were told all we know about Frederick, and the hard unjust—”

“It is nothing about Frederick,” he said, quickly. “The bishop would have nothing to do with that. It is all myself. Margaret, I will tell you about it. I will answer any questions this once; but after to-night let us never speak of it again. I can meet the consequences of my painful, miserable doubts; but it is an effort beyond me to speak of what has caused me so much suffering.”

“Doubts, papa! Doubts as to religion?” The words came from her with a kind of frightened reverence, like touching something sacred and fearing you have soiled it.

“No! not doubts as to religion; not the slightest injury to that.” He paused; Margaret felt as if she stood on the verge of some new horror. He began again, speaking rapidly, as if speed could carry him over anguish.

“You could not understand it all, if I told you—my anxiety, for years past, to know whether I had any right to hold my living—my efforts to quench my smouldering doubts by the authority of the Church. Oh! Margaret, how I love the holy Church from which I am to be shut out!”

He could not go on for a moment. The silence in the room thickened, and outside, far off, the evening birds were calling in their accustomed order. Margaret heard them with a strange clearness, as if the world’s sounds were taking note of their grief. She could not tell what to say; it seemed to her as terrible and inexplicable as if her father were about to turn to an alien faith.

“I have been reading to-day of the two thousand who were ejected from their churches,” he continued, with a faint, painful smile, “trying to steal some of their bravery; but it is of no use—no use—I cannot help feeling it acutely.”

“But, papa, have you well considered? Oh! it seems so terrible—so shocking.” The tears broke loose at last, hot and uncontrollable. The one staid foundation of home—the idea of her beloved father—seemed rocking under her.

The sight of her distress nerved him into trying to comfort her. He swallowed down the sobs that had been rising, and went to the bookcase. Taking down a volume he had often read of late, he came back and put one arm round her waist.

“Listen, dear Margaret.” She took his hand and grasped it hard, but she could not lift her head; she could hardly attend, for agitation thundered within her like a storm heard from inside a house.

“This is the soliloquy of one who was once a clergyman in a country parish, like me,” he said. “It was written by Mr. Oldfield, minister of Carsington, in Derbyshire, a hundred and sixty years ago, or more. His trials are over. He fought the good fight.” The last words fell low, as if he spoke to himself.

Then he read aloud, the old sentences shaped by a conscience that had lived and suffered before his. As he read, he gained resolution—felt, for a few moments, as if he too could be brave and firm in doing what he believed to be right. But when he ceased, Margaret’s low, convulsive sob came like an answer, and his courage sank under the keen sense of what he was bringing upon those he loved.

“Margaret, dear!” he said, drawing her closer. “Think of the early martyrs; think of the thousands who have suffered.”

“But, father,” she cried, lifting her flushed, tear-wet face, “the early martyrs suffered for the truth, while you—oh! dear, dear papa!”

“I suffer for conscience’ sake, my child,” he replied, with a dignity trembling only from the very sensitiveness of his nature. “I must do what my conscience bids. I have borne long with self-reproach that would have roused any mind less torpid and cowardly than mine.”

He shook his head as he went on, and his words came out as if they had been stored up painfully, like winter provisions hoarded against famine.

“Your poor mother’s fond wish—gratified at last in the mocking way in which over-fond wishes are too often fulfilled—Sodom apples as they are—has brought on this crisis, for which I ought to be, and I hope I am thankful. It is not a month since the bishop offered me another living; if I had accepted it, I should have had to make a fresh declaration of conformity to the Liturgy at my institution. Margaret, I tried to do it; I tried to content myself with simply refusing the additional preferment, and stopping quietly here,—strangling my conscience now, as I had strained it before. God forgive me!”

He rose and walked up and down, speaking low words of humiliation that Margaret was thankful to hear but brokenly. The very floor seemed to take the weight of his steps unwillingly. At last he stopped.

“Margaret, I return to the old sad burden: we must leave Helstone.”

“Yes—I see. But when?” The question felt like asking the date of an execution.

“I have written to the bishop—I dare say I have told you so, but I forget things just now—informing him of my intention to resign this vicarage. He has been most kind; he has used arguments and expostulations, all in vain—in vain. They are but what I have tried upon myself, without avail. I shall have to take my deed of resignation and wait upon the bishop myself, to bid him farewell. That will be a trial. But worse—far worse—will be the parting from my dear people. There is a curate appointed to read prayers—a Mr. Brown. He will come to stay with us to-morrow. Next Sunday I preach my farewell sermon.”

The arrangements were so far advanced—so near completion—before she had even been told, that Margaret felt stunned, as if she had been struck and could not yet feel pain. Perhaps it was better to be stunned than to have the wound pricked day after day. Yet one thought rose, heavy as lead.

“What does mamma say?” she asked.

Her father began to pace again before he answered, as if the room could not hold that truth still. At length he stopped.

“Margaret, I am a poor coward after all. I cannot bear to give pain. I know so well your mother’s married life has not been all she hoped—all she had a right to expect—and this will be such a blow to her, that I have never had the heart—the power—to tell her.” He looked at Margaret with a wistful helplessness. “She must be told though, now.”

The idea that her mother knew nothing, while the deed was written and the curate coming, almost overpowered Margaret.

“Yes—indeed she must,” she said, and heard in her own voice the breaking and the effort. “Perhaps, after all, she may not—Oh yes! she will—she must be shocked.”

“Where are we to go to?” she asked at last, as if naming a place might make the change more bearable, as if one could swallow a bitter draught better for knowing what cup it was in.

“To Milton-Northern,” he answered, in a dull indifference that frightened her; as if his feelings had burnt down to ash.

“Milton-Northern! The manufacturing town in Darkshire?”

“Yes.”

“Why there, papa?”

“Because there I can earn bread for my family. Because I know no one there, and no one knows Helstone, or can ever talk to me about it.”

Bread. The word, in his mouth, sounded stripped of all comfort—mere necessity, not the warm loaf cut at a home table. She began, “Bread for your family! I thought you and mamma had—” and stopped, checked by the gloom gathering on his brow.

But he, with quick intuitive sympathy, read her thought and turned it aside with an effort.

“You shall be told all, Margaret. Only help me to tell your mother. I think I could do anything but that. The idea of her distress turns me sick with dread. If I tell you all, perhaps you could break it to her to-morrow. I am going out for the day, to bid farmer Dobson and the poor people on Bracy Common good-bye. Would you dislike breaking it to her very much, Margaret?”

She did. She shrank from it more than from anything she had ever had to do. For a moment she could not speak. He said, gently, as if he already knew the answer, “You dislike it very much, don’t you, Margaret?”

Then she conquered herself. She lifted her head and looked bright and strong, not because she felt so, but because he needed it of her.

“It is a painful thing,” she said, “but it must be done, and I will do it as well as ever I can. You must have many painful things to do.”

He shook his head, despondent, and pressed her hand in gratitude. The gesture nearly undid her; she felt tears rise again, but forced her thoughts to the practical, as one forces a frightened mind to count spoons and plates in the midst of disaster.

“Now tell me, papa—what are our plans? You and mamma have some money, independent of the income from the living, have you not? Aunt Shaw has, I know.”

“Yes. I suppose we have about a hundred and twenty pounds a year of our own. Seventy of that has always gone to Frederick, since he has been abroad. I don’t know if he wants it all,” he added, hesitating. “He must have some pay for serving with the Spanish army.”

“Frederick must not suffer,” said Margaret, decidedly. “In a foreign country; so unjustly treated by his own. A hundred is left. Could not you, and I, and mamma live on a hundred a year in some very cheap—very quiet part of England? Oh! I think we could.” In her mind she saw small rooms, plain food, careful fires—yet still England, still familiar lanes, still the old soft speech round them.

“No!” he said. “That would not answer. I must do something! I must make myself busy, to keep off morbid thoughts. Besides, in a country parish I should be so painfully reminded of Helstone, and my duties here. I could not bear it, Margaret. And a hundred a year would go a very little way, after the necessary wants of housekeeping are met, towards providing your mother with all the comforts she has been accustomed to, and ought to have. No: we must go to Milton. That is settled.”

Then, as if he felt the need to excuse the many decisions already made in secret, he added, “I can always decide better by myself, and not influenced by those whom I love. I cannot stand objections. They make me so undecided.”

Margaret resolved to keep silence. After all, what did place signify compared to this terrible change? Yet even while she tried to submit, she felt already the ache of leaving—not only walls and trees, but the sounds that had made Helstone what it was: the lowing of cattle at dusk, the distant clink of a pail, the church-bell’s familiar voice over the fields. She did not know Milton, but the name itself sounded hard—like a hammer on iron.

Mr. Hale went on, speaking now more steadily, as if narrative helped him bear his own choice.

“A few months ago, when my misery of doubt became more than I could bear without speaking, I wrote to Mr. Bell—you remember Mr. Bell, Margaret?”

“No; I never saw him, I think. But I know who he is. Frederick’s godfather—your old tutor at Oxford, don’t you mean?”

“Yes. He is a Fellow of Plymouth College there. He is a native of Milton-Northern, I believe. At any rate he has property there, which has very much increased in value since Milton has become such a large manufacturing town. Well; I had reason to suspect—to imagine—I had better say nothing about it, however. But I felt sure of sympathy from Mr. Bell. I don’t know that he gave me much strength. He has lived an easy life in his college all his days. But he has been as kind as can be. And it is owing to him we are going to Milton.”

“How?” Margaret asked.

“Why, he has tenants, and houses, and mills there; so, though he dislikes the place—too bustling for one of his habits—he is obliged to keep up some sort of connection; and he tells me that he hears there is a good opening for a private tutor there.”

“A private tutor!” Margaret could not keep the scorn from her voice. “What in the world do manufacturers want with the classics, or literature, or the accomplishments of a gentleman?” Her mind filled with a vague image of men all noise and money, eating quickly and speaking roughly, as if meals were only fuel for work.

“Oh,” said her father, “some of them really seem to be fine fellows, conscious of their own deficiencies, which is more than many a man at Oxford is. Some want resolutely to learn, though they have come to man’s estate. Some want their children to be better instructed than they themselves have been. At any rate, there is an opening, as I have said, for a private tutor. Mr. Bell has recommended me to a Mr. Thornton, a tenant of his, and a very intelligent man, as far as I can judge from his letters. And in Milton, Margaret, I shall find a busy life, if not a happy one, and people and scenes so different that I shall never be reminded of Helstone.”

There was the secret motive, naked and human, and Margaret understood it, for it was in herself too. Different. However discordant—however bleak and hateful all she had ever heard of the North, the manufacturers, the people—there was this one dreadful comfort: it would not be Helstone. It would not whisper Helstone at every corner.

“When do we go?” she asked, after a short silence that seemed filled with the crackle of the fire and the measured ticking of the clock, each sound marking time as if time were an enemy.

“I do not know exactly. I wanted to talk it over with you. You see, your mother knows nothing about it yet: but I think, in a fortnight—after my deed of resignation is sent in, I shall have no right to remain.”

“In a fortnight!” The words left her mouth like breath knocked out by a blow.

“No—no, not exactly to a day. Nothing is fixed,” he said anxiously, noticing the sudden film of sorrow that came over her eyes, the change in her colour.

But she recovered herself at once. “Yes, papa, it had better be fixed soon and decidedly, as you say. Only mamma to know nothing about it! It is that that is the great perplexity.”

“Poor Maria!” he answered tenderly. “Poor, poor Maria! Oh, if I were not married—if I were but myself in the world, how easy it would be! As it is—Margaret, I dare not tell her!”

“No,” said Margaret, sadly. “I will do it. Give me till to-morrow evening to choose my time.” Then, as if her heart would not let her rest with mere resolve, she broke out with sudden passionate entreaty: “Oh, papa—say—tell me it is a nightmare—a horrid dream—not the real waking truth! You cannot mean that you are really going to leave the Church—to give up Helstone—to be for ever separate from me, from mamma—led away by some delusion—some temptation! You do not really mean it!”

Mr. Hale sat rigidly still while she spoke, as if motion would betray him. Then he looked her full in the face and answered slowly, hoarsely, with measured force, each word laid down like a stone.

“I do mean it, Margaret. You must not deceive yourself in doubting the reality of my words—my fixed intention and resolve.”

He held her with that steady, stony look for some moments after he had finished. Margaret gazed back with pleading eyes, as if her will could bend his. When she could not, she rose and went towards the door without another word.

Her fingers were on the handle when he called her back. He was standing by the fireplace, shrunk and stooping; but as she came near he drew himself up to his full height. Placing his hands on her head, he spoke with solemn gravity.

“The blessing of God be upon thee, my child!”

“And may He restore you to His Church,” she answered, straight from the fulness of her heart.

The next moment she feared the response might be irreverent—might wound him coming from his daughter—and she threw her arms round his neck. He held her to him for a minute or two. She heard him murmur, as if to keep himself from failing, “The martyrs and confessors had even more pain to bear—I will not shrink.”

They were startled by hearing Mrs. Hale inquiring for her daughter. The sound—a simple domestic call—fell into the study like a spoon dropped on a flagstone: ordinary, innocent, and suddenly ominous. They started apart in full consciousness of all that lay before them.

Mr. Hale spoke hurriedly. “Go, Margaret, go. I shall be out all to-morrow. Before night you will have told your mother.”

“Yes,” Margaret replied. And she returned to the drawing-room in a stunned and dizzy state, as if she had walked out of one world and into another without crossing any visible boundary.
