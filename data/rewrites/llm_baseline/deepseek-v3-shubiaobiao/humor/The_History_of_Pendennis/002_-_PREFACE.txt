Ladies, gentlemen, and unfortunate souls who’ve stumbled upon this volume—welcome! What you hold in your hands (or glare at on your screen) is the literary equivalent of a slightly tipsy dinner guest: occasionally eloquent, often rambling, and prone to confessing things better left unsaid.

Yes, this book is flawed. Yes, it meanders. But isn’t that the charm of a good conversation? If you wanted polished artifice, you’d be reading someone far more dignified—someone who *doesn’t* admit to abandoning a perfectly good plot full of murderous rogues and swooning ladies because, frankly, the author realized he knew nothing about criminals. (A shame, really. I had grand visions of rivaling Eugène Sue, but it turns out my expertise ends at mildly improper dinner parties.)

Let’s be clear: this is not a tale of thrilling villainy. Colonel Altamont was *this close* to being shoved out a window for your entertainment, but alas, mercy prevailed. Instead, you get something far more scandalous—*the truth*. Or at least, my version of it.

Some of you, dear readers, have already clutched your pearls over my audacity to suggest that young men might, on occasion, *gasp*, be tempted by things. Shocking! Unthinkable! And yet, here we are. If you demand fiction where every hero is a paragon of virtue, untouched by human frailty, might I suggest a children’s fable? This story operates under the radical notion that people—even educated ones—are complicated.

So, proceed with caution. You’ll find no claptrap, no cheap thrills—just an earnest (if occasionally bumbling) attempt to reflect life as it is, not as your drawing-room etiquette insists it ought to be. If that offends, well… my apologies. But not enough to change a word.

Farewell, and may your reading be at least as entertaining as my writing *wasn’t* at times.

—Yours, with only *mild* regrets,
Pendennis
KENSINGTON, 1850 (or thereabouts)
