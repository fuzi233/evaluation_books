We have all, no doubt, encountered the world with wide-open eyes and witnessed a vast parade of human types. Yet, there exists a particular breed of man—often a favorite subject for satire in novels and plays—that I confess I have rarely, if ever, met in my dealings with this sinful race. I speak of the mere religious hypocrite: the man who preaches endlessly while believing not a single word of his own sermons; the infidel disguised in a broad-brimmed hat and sable cloak, expounding scripture, offering exhortations, pronouncing curses, and bestowing blessings, all without a shred of faith in his own heaven or fear of his own hell.

Consider the candid throngs of "hobnail" shoes clumping toward church on a Sunday evening; consider the rustling maid-servants in their ribbons trailed by young apprentices; the little regiments of schoolboys; the trim young maidens and staid matrons marching with their glistening prayer-books as the chapel bell rings out (likely passing Ebenezer, where the congregation of umbrellas, great bonnets, and pattens is already gathered beneath the flaring gas-lamps). Look at them! How many are true hypocrites, you ask? Perhaps the maid-servant is thinking of her sweetheart; perhaps the grocer is calculating how to buy that parcel of sugar and worrying if the County Bank will accept his notes; perhaps the head-schoolboy is cramming Latin verses for Monday's exercise; perhaps the young rogue is remembering that after service comes his father's exposition at home, followed by pie for supper; perhaps the clerk calling out the psalm has a daughter in trouble and drones through his responses without understanding a syllable; perhaps the very moment the parson hides his face on his cushion, he is thinking of a bill due on Monday.

These people are not heavenly-minded; they are of the world, deeply worldly, and their feet remain firmly planted upon it. Yet, they are not hypocrites, mark you. They possess their religion in some convenient mental locker—a valuable medicine reserved for times of illness. A man administers his nostrum to his neighbor, recommending his private cure for another's ailment. "My dear madam, you suffer from spasms? These drops are infallible!" "You have been drinking too much wine, my good sir? This pill will defy any evil consequence, allowing you to enjoy your port daily." Who are more eager dispensers of spiritual and bodily physic than women? Indeed, a century ago, every lady in the countryside maintained a still-room and a medicine chest filled with pills, powders, and potions for the entire village. My Lady Warrington took charge of the consciences and digestions of her husband's tenants and family alike. She held the faith and health of the servants' hall in her keeping. Whether she knew how to doctor them rightly is for Heaven to judge; but whether it was pill or doctrine, she administered either with equal conviction in her own authority, and her disciples swallowed both obediently. She believed herself to be one of the most virtuous, self-denying, wise, and learned women in the world. By constantly dunning this opinion into the ears of everyone around her, she succeeded in convincing many to join her persuasion.

At Sir Miles's dinner, with its magnificent sideboard of plate and numerous men in livery, it required a considerable amount of mental fortitude to realize that the beer served in the splendid tankard was of the poorest quality, and that there was merely one joint of mutton on the grand silver dish. When Sir Miles toasted the King's health and smacked his jolly lips over his wine, he regarded the liquor and the company as if it were ambrosia. He asked Harry Warrington if they drank port like that in Virginia. He claimed it was nothing compared to the wine Harry should taste in Norfolk. He praised the vintage so highly that Harry almost believed it was good, winking into his own glass in an attempt to discern the merits his uncle perceived in the ruby nectar.

Just as we observe in many well-regulated families of the present day, the Warringtons possessed their two paragons. Among the two grown daughters, one was the greatest beauty, and the other the greatest genius and angel of any young lady alive, according to Lady Warrington's description to Harry. The eldest, the Beauty, was engaged to dear Tom Claypool, a fact the fond mother shared with her cousin Harry in confidence. However, the second daughter—the Genius and the Angel—was forever setting herself upon our young friend to improve his wits and morals. She sang to him at the harpsichord (though rather out of tune for an angel, Harry noted); she offered advice, instruction, and conversation—with almost too much instruction and advice, Harry felt, preferring instead the society of the little cousin who reminded him of Fanny Mountain at home. Yet, after dinner, the last-mentioned young maiden usually retired to her nursery. Beauty departed on her own avocations; the mother attended to her correspondence or wrote voluminous letters; the father dozed in his armchair; and the Genius remained to keep her young cousin company.

The calm of the house somehow pleased the young man, and he liked to take refuge there, away from the riot and dissipation in which he ordinarily lived. Certainly, no welcome could be kinder than that which he received. Doors were opened to him at all hours. If Flora was not at home, Dora was ready to receive him. Within a few days of acquaintance, he and his little cousin Miles had gone for a galloping match in the Park. Harry, kind and generous to every man who crossed his path, had even considered purchasing a small horse for his cousin, far superior to the one the boy currently rode, until circumstances arose that brought all our poor Harry's coaches and horses to a sudden halt.

Though Sir Miles Warrington had imagined Virginia to be an island, the ladies were much better instructed in geography and were eager to hear from Harry about his home and native country. On his part, he was not averse to talking about it. He described the length and breadth of his estate, the rivers that bordered it, and the produce it yielded. Having practiced surveying with a friend in his boyhood, he drew a map of his county, marking fine towns here and there—which, in truth, were but log-huts (though, for the honor of his country, he wished them to appear as handsome as possible). There was the Potomac; there was the James River; there were the wharves from which his mother's ships and tobacco were brought to the sea. In reality, the estate was as large as a county. He did not boast excessively about the place. Seeing the handsome young fellow, dressed in a fine suit of velvet and silver lace, making his draughts, pointing out hills, forests, and towns, one might have imagined him a traveling prince describing the realms of his mother, the Queen. He almost fancied himself to be so at times. He had miles where gentlemen in England had mere acres.

Not only did Dora listen, but the beauteous Flora also bowed her fair head and heard him with attention. Why, what was young Tom Claypool, the brother baronet's son in Norfolk, with his great boots, his booming voice, and his inheritance of a mere five thousand acres, compared to this young American prince and charming stranger? As an angel, Dora began to lose her temper and started twitting Flora for flirting. Claypool, in his red waistcoat, sat dumbfounded before the splendid Harry in his ruffles and laces, listening to discussions of March, Chesterfield, Selwyn, Bolingbroke, and the entire company of macaronis.

Mamma began to love Harry more and more as a son. She grew anxious about the spiritual welfare of the poor Indians and the unfortunate negroes in Virginia. What could she do to help dear Madam Esmond (a precious woman, she knew) in this good work? She had a serious butler and housekeeper; they were delighted with the spiritual behavior and sweet musical gifts of Gumbo. "Ah! Harry, Harry! You have been a sad wild boy! Why did you not come sooner to us, sir, and not waste your time among spendthrifts and the vain world? But 'tis not yet too late. We must reclaim thee, dear Harry! Mustn't we, Sir Miles? Mustn't we, Dora? Mustn't we, Flora?"

The three ladies all looked up to the ceiling. They would reclaim the dear prodigal. It remained to be seen which would succeed most. Dora sat by watching Flora. As for Mamma, when the girls were away, she spoke to him more and more seriously, more and more tenderly. She would be a mother to him in the absence of his own admirable parent. She gave him a hymn-book. She kissed him on the forehead. She was actuated by the purest love, tenderness, and religious regard toward her dear, wayward, wild, yet amiable nephew.

While these sentimentalities unfolded, it is presumed that Mr. Warrington kept his own counsel regarding his affairs outdoors, which we have seen were in the very worst condition. He, who had been favored by fortune for so many weeks, was suddenly deserted by her, and a few days sufficed to knock down his heap of winnings.
