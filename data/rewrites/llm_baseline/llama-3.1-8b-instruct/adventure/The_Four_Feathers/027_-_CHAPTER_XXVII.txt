The sun beat down relentlessly on the desolate expanse of the eastern border of Omdurman, casting a golden glow over the parched earth. The House of Stone loomed before me, its imposing structure a testament to the brutal architecture of the empire. Beyond the prison walls, the city sprawled in every direction, a labyrinthine tangle of hovels and makeshift shelters that seemed to writhe like a living thing. No verdant oasis broke the monotony of the scene – only a sea of dusty earth, cracked and barren, stretched out to the horizon.

As I navigated the narrow, winding path that led to the Nile, the air grew thick with the smells of sweat, dust, and decay. I passed beneath the watchful gaze of the prison guards, their faces impassive behind the masks of worn leather and cloth. At the riverbank, the dhows bobbed gently on the water, their wooden hulls creaking softly in the breeze. The sound of laughter and haggling filled the air as merchants and traders bartered for goods and supplies.

As the day waned, the sun dipping low in the sky, the shadows cast by the buildings grew long and sinister. I spotted a group of prisoners making their way to the river, their chains clanking against the stone flags as they moved. They were a sorry-looking lot, their skin tanned to a deep brown, their eyes sunken and weary. I watched as they drew water from the river, their hands moving with a slow, mechanical precision.

The native prisoners moved with ease, their movements fluid and unhindered. They spoke in hushed tones, their words carried away by the wind. But the Europeans, like myself, were a different story altogether. We were a rare sight in these parts, our presence a constant reminder of the empire's power and reach. Our escape was a distant dream, one that required resources, connections, and a healthy dose of luck. And yet, I held onto that dream, even as the reality of my situation seemed to mock me at every turn.

I thought back to the whispers of encouragement I'd received from the boy who brought my food, the fleeting touch of a hand on my shoulder during a parade of the Khalifa's troops. They were moments of brief respite, moments that hinted at a world beyond the confines of the prison. But as the months dragged on, those moments grew fewer and farther between.
