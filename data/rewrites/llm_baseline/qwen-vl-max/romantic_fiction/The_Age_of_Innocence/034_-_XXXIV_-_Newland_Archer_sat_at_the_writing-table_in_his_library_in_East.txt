Newland Archer sat at his writing desk in the library of his home on East Thirty-ninth Street, the room quiet and still after a long evening. He had just returned from a grand reception celebrating the opening of the new galleries at the Metropolitan Museum—a night of polished conversation, elegant dresses, and the soft glow of candlelight reflecting off ancient marble and gilded frames. Yet amid all that splendor, something stirred deep within him. A woman’s voice drifted past: “Why, this used to be one of the old Cesnola rooms.” And with those words, the present vanished.

He was suddenly back—alone on a hard leather divan near a radiator, watching a slight figure in a long sealskin cloak disappear down a narrow, sparsely furnished corridor. The memory came not as a ghost, but as a living moment, sharp and clear. It pulled him into a world long gone—the museum he once knew, the life he once dreamed of.

Now, looking around the familiar library, he saw it anew. For over thirty years, this room had held the rhythm of his life: quiet evenings, family talks, moments both ordinary and profound. Here, nearly twenty-six years ago, his wife May had told him she was expecting their first child, her cheeks flushed, her voice hesitant, as if confessing something too tender for words. Here, their son Dallas—too frail for winter church services—had been baptized by the beloved Bishop of New York, a man whose presence had once seemed like a sacred institution itself. Here, Dallas had taken his first wobbly steps across the floor, shouting “Dad!” while May and the nurse giggled behind the door. Here, Mary—so much like her mother—had announced her engagement to one of Reggie Chivers’s sons, a man so dull he could barely hold a conversation. And here, Archer had kissed her through her wedding veil before they left for Grace Church, where tradition stood firm even as everything else shifted beneath their feet.

This room had seen the shaping of their children’s lives: Dallas’s studies, Bill’s quiet ambitions, Mary’s passion for sports and charity, and her curious tilt toward art. The young men of today were no longer bound to law or business. They were drawn to reform, to architecture, to the study of early American buildings, to preserving history with care instead of mocking it. No one called a house “Colonial” anymore—except, perhaps, in the suburbs, where millionaires built them like monuments to tasteless nostalgia.

But more than anything, it was here that the Governor of New York had once turned to him during dinner, slamming his fist on the table and glaring through his spectacles: “Hang the professional politician! You’re the kind of man the country wants, Archer. If the stable’s ever to be cleaned out, men like you have to lend a hand.”

Archer had felt it then—a surge of purpose, of belonging. It echoed Ned Winsett’s old call to action, but now spoken by a leader who lived it. He had believed in that vision, thrown himself into public service, served a term in the State Assembly—then stepped back when he wasn’t re-elected. He returned to quieter work: city affairs, occasional articles for reform magazines trying to wake a nation from its slumber. Not much, perhaps—but enough. Compared to the narrow paths others in his class had followed—money, sport, society—his small contributions felt meaningful. Like bricks in a wall. He hadn’t changed the world, but he had tried. And he had known great things: beauty, friendship, duty.

He had become what people began calling “a good citizen.” In New York, every movement—philanthropy, art, civic improvement—wanted his name, his judgment. “Ask Archer,” people said, when planning schools for disabled children, reorganizing museums, founding literary clubs, or launching music societies. His days were full, measured, decent. He thought that was all any man should hope for.

Yet there was one thing he had never had: the flower of life. But he no longer mourned it. It had seemed impossible from the start—like winning a lottery with a hundred million tickets and only one prize. The odds were too great. And so, when he thought of Ellen Olenska, it was not with longing, but with calm remembrance. She had become the idealized image of what might have been—a dream woven from memory, poetry, and regret. That dream had kept him faithful to May. When May died, suddenly, of pneumonia while nursing their youngest child, he truly grieved. Their marriage had not been passionate, but it had been honorable. And honor mattered. Without it, marriage became nothing but conflict—ugly, petty, exhausting. Looking back, he respected the life they’d built. There was value in the old ways.

His gaze swept the room—now decorated by Dallas with English prints, Chippendale furniture, blue-and-white porcelain, and soft electric lights. It rested on the old Eastlake writing table, untouched through the years, and beside it, the photograph of May he had never moved.

There she was—tall, graceful, in her starched muslin and wide-brimmed hat, as he remembered her beneath the orange trees in the Mission garden. And just as she looked then, she had remained: generous, steady, tireless. But also unchanging. She had no imagination, no sense of growth. The world transformed around her, yet she stayed fixed in her own quiet orbit. Her blindness to change had created a shared illusion in the family—father and children alike pretending nothing had shifted, protecting her from the truth. She died believing the world was still as it had always been—full of loving homes, proper values, and children who would carry on the legacy. She trusted Dallas to raise little Bill with the same principles, and Mary—just like herself—to live a life of dignity and order. She had no idea how much had changed. And so she passed peacefully into St. Mark’s vault, where Mrs. Archer already slept, safe from the unseen currents sweeping through society.

Opposite May’s portrait hung one of her daughter, Mary Chivers. Tall and fair like her mother, but broader-shouldered, flat-chested, dressed in the modern style. Her athletic feats—swimming, hiking, rowing—would have been impossible in May’s delicate frame. The difference seemed symbolic: May’s life had been tightly wrapped, like her waist; Mary’s was looser, freer, though no less conventional. She was no more intelligent, yet she embraced more of the world. There was good in the new ways, too.

The telephone clicked. Archer reached for the receiver.

“Chicago wants you.”

Ah—Dallas. The boy had been sent to Chicago to discuss plans for a lakeside mansion for a wealthy client. The firm always sent him on such trips.

“Hallo, Dad—yes, it’s me. I say—how do you feel about sailing on Wednesday? Mauretania—next Wednesday, as ever is. Our client wants me to look at some Italian gardens before finalizing the design, so I’ve got to hop over on the next boat. I’ll be back June first—” his voice broke into laughter—“so we must look alive. Dad, I need your help: do come.”

Dallas sounded as if he were in the room. The voice was warm, natural, as if he leaned against the fireplace. Long-distance calls had become routine—no more astonishing than electric light or transatlantic voyages. But the laugh startled Archer. Across forests, rivers, mountains, cities, millions of strangers—Dallas’s voice carried not just words, but joy. And beneath it, a truth: *I must be back on June first because Fanny Beaufort and I are getting married on the fifth.*

The voice returned: “Think it over? No, sir—not a minute. You’ve got to say yes now. Why not? If you can give me one reason—No, I knew it. Then it’s a go, eh? Because I count on you to call the Cunard office tomorrow and book a return from Marseilles. Dad—this will be our last time together, like this—Oh, good! I knew you would.”

The line went dead. Archer rose and began pacing.

It *would* be their last time like this. They’d have many more moments after Dallas’s marriage—father and son were born companions. Fanny Beaufort, whatever one thought of her, didn’t seem likely to break their bond. On the contrary, Archer suspected she’d fit right in. Still, change was change. And though he liked her, he couldn’t deny the pull of this final chance to be alone with his son.

There was no real reason not to go—except the deep-rooted habit of staying put. May had disliked travel unless necessary—visits to the sea, mountains, or Newport. After Dallas graduated, she felt it her duty to tour Europe for six months. They did the classic route—England, Switzerland, Italy—skipping France for reasons no one explained. Dallas had raged at missing Rheims and Chartres, but Mary and Bill wanted mountains. May, ever fair, balanced their desires. She had even suggested Archer go to Paris alone for two weeks, join them later. But he declined. “We’ll stick together,” he said. May smiled—pleased he set such an example.

Since her death, nearly two years prior, Archer had stayed rooted. His children urged him to travel—Mary insisted it would do him good to “see the galleries.” The mystery of such advice made her confident it would help. But Archer found himself held by habit, by memory, by a sudden fear of the unfamiliar.

Now, reviewing his life, he saw how deeply he’d sunk into routine. Doing one’s duty, it seemed, prepared one poorly for anything else. That was the belief of his generation—clear lines between right and wrong, honest and dishonest. Little room for surprise. Yet sometimes, a man’s imagination—long subdued by daily life—rises above it all and sees the winding path of fate. Archer stood there, suspended, wondering…

What remained of the world he’d grown up in? He remembered Lawrence Lefferts’s sneering prophecy, spoken in this very room: “If things go on like this, our children will be marrying Beaufort’s bastards.”

And now—Dallas, his pride, was doing exactly that. No one blinked. Even Aunt Janey, unchanged since her youth, had taken her mother’s emeralds and pearls from their cotton-wool wrapping and handed them to Fanny Beaufort. And Fanny, instead of being disappointed, had praised their old-fashioned charm, saying she’d feel like a painting by Isabey when she wore them.

Fanny Beaufort had arrived in New York at eighteen, orphaned, after her parents’ deaths. She won the city’s heart much as Madame Olenska had done decades earlier—only this time, society welcomed her without suspicion. She was pretty, witty, accomplished. What more could anyone want? No one cared about her father’s past failures or her own uncertain origins. Only the elders remembered Beaufort’s bankruptcy, his secret marriage to Fanny Ring, his departure with a young daughter. He’d been heard of in Constantinople, then Russia, later in Buenos Aires, where he ran an insurance agency. He and his wife died there, rich and respected. Years later, their daughter appeared in New York under the care of Mrs. Jack Welland, May’s sister-in-law. This made her practically a cousin to the Archers—and no one was surprised when Dallas proposed.

It was a sign of how far the world had come. People were too busy—busy with reforms, fads, causes—to care about each other’s histories. In the swirling kaleidoscope of modern life, who mattered more than anyone else?

Newland Archer stood at his hotel window in Paris, the city’s stately elegance unfolding below. His heart beat fast—wild, eager, almost youthful. It had been so long since it had raced like this, leaving him breathless, temples burning. He wondered if his son felt this way around Fanny Beaufort—and decided no. “It functions as actively, no doubt,” he mused, recalling Dallas’s calm announcement of the engagement, his certainty that the family would approve.

“The difference is that these young people assume they’ll get what they want. We assumed we wouldn’t. But I wonder—can something you’re certain of truly make your heart race?”

It was the day after their arrival. Spring sun warmed the air, and Archer lingered at the open window, gazing at the Place Vendôme. One condition he’d insisted on—almost the only one—was that they avoid the new luxury hotels. “Oh, all right,” Dallas had agreed easily. “I’ll take you to some jolly old-fashioned place—the Bristol, say.” Archer had been stunned. The centuries-old home of kings and emperors—now just “old-fashioned,” a relic of charm and inconvenience.

He had imagined his return to Paris often in younger days—then the personal dream faded, replaced by visions of Madame Olenska’s life. Alone at night in his library, he’d conjured the spring blossoms along the avenues, the flowers in the gardens, the scent of lilacs from street carts, the river flowing beneath grand bridges, the pulse of art and pleasure in every avenue. Now it was real—glorious, overwhelming. And yet, he felt shy, outdated, insignificant—just a grey speck compared to the bold, magnificent man he once dreamed of becoming.

Dallas’s hand landed lightly on his shoulder. “Hullo, father—this is something, isn’t it?” They stood in silence, watching the city. Then Dallas spoke again: “By the way, I’ve got a message. The Countess Olenska expects us both at half-past five.”

He said it casually, as if announcing the train schedule. Archer turned to him, sensing something in his eyes—a flicker of mischief, like his great-grandmother Mingott’s.

“Oh, didn’t I tell you?” Dallas continued. “Fanny made me swear to three things while I’m in Paris: get her the score of Debussy’s latest songs, go to the Grand-Guignol, and see Madame Olenska. You know, she was kind to Fanny when Mr. Beaufort sent her to the Assomption. Fanny had no friends in Paris—Madame Olenska took her in, showed her around. I think she was close to the first Mrs. Beaufort. And she’s our cousin, of course. So I called her this morning and told her you and I were here for two days and wanted to visit.”

Archer stared. “You told her I was here?”

“Of course—why not?” Dallas raised an eyebrow, then slipped his arm through his father’s. “I say, father—what was she like?”

Archer felt heat rise in his face. “Come on—own up. You two were great pals, weren’t you? Wasn’t she awfully lovely?”

“Lovely? I don’t know. She was different.”

“Ah—there it is! That’s always the answer. When she comes, *she’s different*—and you can’t say why. It’s exactly how I feel about Fanny.”

Archer stepped back, pulling away. “About Fanny? But, my dear fellow—I should hope so! Only I don’t see—”

“Dash it, Dad, don’t be prehistoric! Wasn’t she—once—your Fanny?”

Dallas belonged entirely to the new age. First-born of Newland and May, yet never taught reserve. “What’s the point of secrets?” he’d always say. “They only make people want to dig them up.” But now, meeting his father’s gaze, Archer saw the tenderness beneath the jest.

“My Fanny?”

“Well, the woman you’d have given up everything for—if you’d chosen to.”

“I didn’t,” Archer said quietly.

“No—you date, you see, dear old boy. But mother said—”

“Your mother?”

“Yes. The day before she died. She sent for me alone—you remember? She said we were safe with you, always would be, because once, when she asked you to, you gave up the thing you most wanted.”

Archer listened in silence. His eyes fixed on the sunlit square below. At length, he whispered, “She never asked me.”

“No. I forgot. You never asked each other anything, did you? Never told each other anything. You just sat and watched each other, guessing what lay beneath. A deaf-and-dumb asylum, really. Well, I give your generation credit for knowing more about each other’s hearts than we ever manage to learn about our own.”

He paused. “I say, Dad—are you angry?”

Archer didn’t go to Versailles. He walked through Paris alone, carrying a lifetime of silenced regrets. Soon, he stopped regretting Dallas’s honesty. Knowing someone had guessed—someone who loved him—felt like a weight lifted. And that it was May… it moved him beyond words. Dallas, for all his insight, wouldn’t understand. To him, it was just a story of wasted potential. But was it really?

For hours, Archer sat on a bench in the Champs Élysées, watching the stream of life pass by.

A few streets away, a few hours ahead, Ellen Olenska waited. She had never returned to her husband. When he died, she didn’t change her life. Nothing now stood between her and Archer—except the years, and the memories.

That afternoon, he would see her.

He rose and walked across the Place de la Concorde, through the Tuileries, to the Louvre. She had once said she came here often. He wandered through gallery after gallery, bathed in golden afternoon light. One by one, the paintings reawakened—vibrant, powerful, beautiful. For the first time in decades, he felt the fullness of art, the depth of feeling he had starved himself of.

After all—his life had been too barren.
