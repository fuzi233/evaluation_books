The birth of our little Miles came but a few days after the arrival of another prince into this world—one greeted with cannon fire and grand illuminations, with crowds pressing to glimpse his gilded cradle under the watch of noble attendants. No such fanfare heralded the arrival of our son. No courtiers bowed, no loyal subjects paid homage—only dear Gumbo and faithful Molly, whose devotion to the heir of our humble fortunes was as true as any king could wish.

Why, you may ask, was he not christened George, like his father and that other illustrious babe? The name Miles belonged to a wayward scamp of our family, borne by generations of Warringtons before him. Yet it was my poor little cousin Miles, that solitary kind soul among my kin, who had shown me affection when others withheld it. And so Theo and I agreed—our boy would carry his name.

We dutifully announced his birth in the *Daily Advertiser*, noting the humble address in Church Street, Lambeth, where he first drew breath. Aunt Bernstein, in her reply, chided me for advertising our modest circumstances to the world. "My dear," she wrote, "must you inform all of London that you are buried in such a place?" Still, she sent her kisses to Theo and a gift for the child—a silk coverlet trimmed with lace fit for a prince. Practical it was not, for the lace alone might have fed us for weeks, but Theo and Molly admired it greatly, and so our boy’s cradle was as fine as any lord’s.

Dr. Heberden, ever kind, visited often to see that Theo fared well, and he recommended a capable surgeon nearby. Yet my wife needed little tending beyond what our good landlady and Molly could provide. Then came the gilded spectacle of Lady Castlewood’s carriage rolling up to our gate, bearing a pot of jelly—likely some remnant of her ladyship’s latest banquet. She spoke grandly of the royal celebrations while depositing her offering with gracious condescension. Mr. Johnson, who happened to call that day, observed the scene with keen interest. His bows were deep, if inelegant, as she addressed me as "cousin" and transferred the jelly from silver to crockery with regal benevolence.

"Ah," said the Doctor afterward, savoring a spoonful, "the great are fortunate indeed. Their tables host not only the finest delicacies but the brightest wits. This jelly, sir, may have graced the lips of celebrated beauties, shared the company of tarts and creams most exquisite." Yet Theo, after a taste, set it aside, and Molly declared it had turned.

Our boy had, at least, an earl’s daughter for a godmother—poor Lady Maria, whose own hopes of motherhood had been lost. She doted on the child with a tenderness near heartbreaking to witness. Captain Miles may now strut in the splendid regimentals of the Prince’s Hussars, but let it be remembered that his first swaddling clothes were those meant for a player’s child.

Sampson christened him in the same Southwark chapel where Theo and I had wed. Never were the Prayer Book’s words read more movingly—though the parson’s voice faltered at the end, and there was not a dry eye among us. "Mr. Garrick himself," Hagan declared, "could not have spoken those lines with greater feeling. No child was ever welcomed with purer love."

Now, how our Miles came by his name—that remains to be told. Days before the christening, when we had yet resolved to call him George, the clatter of small hooves sounded at our gate. There stood young Miles, our cousin, breathless from his ride. "Cousin Harry gave me this pony," he panted, "and I like you because you’re like him—and because they say unkind things of you at home, and it isn’t fair." He thrust forth a coral whistle and a gold moidore. "This was my godmamma’s gift, but your baby may have it. And if you’re poor, here’s my moidore—I’m not allowed to spend it anyway."

We led him upstairs—his little boots clomping proudly—to where Theo lay. She kissed him, and his coin never left her purse.

When my mother, in due time, learned of the name Miles—one unknown among the Esmonds—she expressed royal displeasure. I did not explain then. But years later, when I told her the story, a tear traced the lines of her cheek. And I heard afterward that she questioned Gumbo often about the boy who had given his name to our Miles—our *Miles Gloriosus* of Pall Mall, Valenciennes, and Brighton.
