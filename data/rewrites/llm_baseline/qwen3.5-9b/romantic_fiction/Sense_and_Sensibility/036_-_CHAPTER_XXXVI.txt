Within a few days of their reunion, the newspapers proclaimed to the world that Mrs. Thomas Palmer, Esquire, had safely delivered a son and heir. While this news was merely a standard social update, it held profound significance for those intimately connected to the family, particularly Mrs. Jennings. The birth became the central focus of her domestic life, prompting a significant shift in her schedule. Determined to be with her daughter Charlotte as much as possible, Mrs. Jennings began visiting Conduit Street every morning immediately after dressing, remaining until late evening. Consequently, the Dashwood sisters, at the specific request of the Middletons, relocated to spend their entire days in Conduit Street.

Although the Miss Dashwoods would have preferred to remain at Mrs. Jennings's home, especially during the mornings, they felt compelled to comply with the collective wishes of everyone involved. Thus, their time was effectively transferred to Lady Middleton and the two Miss Steeles. In reality, the Dashwoods' company was valued by these ladies no more than it was claimed to be desired. The sisters possessed too much sense to serve as desirable companions for Lady Middleton, whose intellect required constant flattery. Furthermore, the Miss Steeles viewed the Dashwoods with suspicion, regarding them as intruders encroaching on territory they intended to monopolize.

Lady Middleton's behavior toward Elinor and Marianne was outwardly impeccable, yet her internal sentiments were quite different. Unable to believe anyone capable of goodness who refused to flatter her or her children, she judged the Dashwoods lacking in nature. Additionally, their evident fondness for reading led her to assume they were satirical—a label she applied loosely to anyone she disliked, regardless of whether they truly understood satire. Their presence acted as a constraint on both Lady Middleton and Lucy Steele; it curbed the former's idleness and threatened the latter's ability to dispense the flattery she cherished. Lady Middleton feared the Dashwoods would despise her for offering such praise, while Lucy worried they would mock her efforts.

Miss Steele, however, was the least disturbed by their arrival. She would have gladly accepted a detailed account of the affair between Marianne and Mr. Willoughby in exchange for yielding her prime spot by the fire after dinner. Yet, such information was withheld. Occasional expressions of pity from Miss Steele toward Marianne, or remarks on the fickleness of lovers dropped before Marianne herself, elicited only indifference from Elinor or visible disgust from Marianne. Even light-hearted teasing about the doctor would have secured Miss Steele's friendship, but the Dashwoods showed no inclination to oblige her. Should Sir John dine elsewhere, Miss Steele might spend an entire day without hearing a single jest directed at herself, save for the ones she kindly offered.

Despite these underlying tensions and resentments, Mrs. Jennings remained blissfully unaware. She delighted in the prospect of her daughters being together, frequently congratulating the young women nightly on escaping the company of a "stupid old woman" for so long. Whether joining them at Sir John's residence or her own, she arrived in high spirits, brimming with self-importance. She attributed Charlotte's well-being entirely to her own care and recounted the infant's condition with exhaustive detail, a narrative style that only Miss Steele possessed the curiosity to appreciate.

One persistent annoyance, however, troubled Mrs. Jennings daily. Mr. Palmer adhered to the conventional, albeit unfatherly, belief shared by many men that all infants are identical. Despite observing striking resemblances between the baby and various relatives on both sides, he refused to acknowledge the child's unique features. He could not be persuaded that the baby was anything other than a generic specimen of infancy, nor could he be moved to admit it was the finest child in the world.

A separate misfortune soon befell Mrs. John Dashwood. While her two sisters visited her in Harley Street alongside Mrs. Jennings, another acquaintance unexpectedly dropped by. Although this encounter seemed innocuous, human imagination often leads to erroneous conclusions based on scant evidence. Upon hearing the name of the Miss Dashwoods and learning they were Mr. Dashwood's sisters, the visitor immediately assumed they were residing in Harley Street. This misconception resulted in invitations, issued within a day or two, for a small musical gathering at her home, extending to the Dashwoods, their brother, and their sister.

Mrs. John Dashwood was forced to accept this invitation, submitting to the considerable inconvenience of arranging carriage transport for the Miss Dashwoods. Worse still, she dreaded the obligation of appearing attentive to them, fearing they might expect further outings. While she retained the power to disappoint them, she recognized that those determined to act wrongly feel aggrieved by any suggestion of higher standards. By this time, Marianne had developed a routine of daily excursions; it mattered little to her whether she attended or not. She prepared mechanically for every evening's engagement, often unaware of the destination until the last moment, and displayed perfect indifference regarding her attire.

In contrast, Miss Steele exercised minute scrutiny over Marianne's appearance. During the first five minutes of their meeting, she assessed every aspect of Marianne's toilette, questioning the price of every garment item, estimating the total number of dresses owned, and speculating on weekly laundry costs and annual spending allowances. These intrusive inquiries invariably concluded with a complimentary remark, intended as a sweetener but perceived by Marianne as the height of impudence. After enduring examinations of her gown's fabric, shoe color, and hairstyle, Marianne was typically dismissed with assurances that she looked "vastly smart" and destined to make numerous conquests. On this particular evening, such encouragement proved futile as the sisters departed promptly for their brother's carriage, leaving Mrs. John Dashwood disappointed that they had not delayed their departure to inconvenience her or her coachman.

The musical party itself was unremarkable, comprising a mixture of genuine connoisseurs and casual attendees, with performers who considered themselves—and were praised by their circle—as premier private musicians in England. Elinor, neither musically inclined nor pretentious, freely diverted her gaze from the grand piano to other objects in the room. Among a group of young men, she spotted the individual who had previously lectured them on toothpick cases at Gray's Inn. Shortly thereafter, he approached her brother, engaging in familiar conversation. Just as Elinor resolved to inquire about his identity, Mr. Dashwood introduced him as Mr. Robert Ferrars.

Mr. Ferrars greeted Elinor with easy civility, executing a bow that clearly signaled his reputation as the coxcomb Lucy had described. Had Elinor's affection for Edward relied less on his personal merits and more on those of his kin, this interaction might have sealed her disapproval. However, observing the stark contrast between the two brothers, she found no reason to lose her charitable view of Edward's modesty and worth.

During a brief conversation, Robert explained the disparity between them. Lamenting his brother's awkwardness (*gaucherie*), which he believed hindered his social integration, he candidly attributed it not to natural deficiency but to the misfortune of a private education. He argued that his own success stemmed solely from attending a public school, despite possessing no superior natural talents. "Upon my soul," he remarked, "I believe it is nothing more. I often assure my mother of this. 'My dear Madam,' I tell her, 'the damage is now irreparable, and entirely your fault. Why did you yield to my uncle Sir Robert's persuasion against your own judgment to send Edward to Mr. Pratt's at such a critical juncture? Had you sent him to Westminster like myself, this would never have occurred.' This is my perspective, and my mother is fully convinced of her error."

Elinor refrained from contradicting him, although she could not bring herself to approve of Edward's upbringing in Mr. Pratt's household. Shifting the topic, Robert noted their residence in Devonshire, specifically a cottage near Dawlish. Elinor corrected his geographical assumption, noting that one need not live near Dawlish to reside in Devonshire. Nevertheless, he enthusiastically endorsed their choice of housing. "I am excessively fond of cottages," he declared. "There is always such comfort and elegance about them. If I had funds to spare, I would purchase land and build one near London, allowing me to invite friends and enjoy happiness. I advise everyone building to construct a cottage."

He recounted an anecdote involving his friend Lord Courtland, who had presented three architectural plans by Bonomi. Robert had immediately rejected all three, advising instead to build a cottage. Later, at his friend Elliott's estate near Dartford, he demonstrated that a cottage could comfortably host a large dance, utilizing the dining parlour for couples, the drawing-room for card tables, the library for refreshments, and the saloon for supper. Elinor agreed with his reasoning, feeling no need to offer rational counterarguments.

As John Dashwood shared little enthusiasm for music, his mind wandered freely. A thought occurred to him during the evening, which he proposed to his wife upon their return home. Reflecting on Mrs. Dennison's error in assuming the Dashwoods were her guests, he suggested formally inviting them to stay while Mrs. Jennings was occupied elsewhere. He reasoned the expense would be negligible and the inconvenience minimal, viewing it as a necessary gesture to clear his conscience regarding his promise to his father.

Fanny reacted with alarm. "I cannot see how this can be done without offending Lady Middleton," she protested. "Since they spend every day with her, asking them away seems improper. I am always eager to show hospitality, as tonight proves, but they are Lady Middleton's visitors." Her husband, humbly disagreeing, argued that since they had already spent a week in Conduit Street, Lady Middleton would not be displeased if they spent an equal duration with such close relations.
