"The pang, the curse with which they died, Had never passed away: I could not draw my eyes from theirs, Nor lift them up to pray." —COLERIDGE.

That night, Deronda did not remove his clothes. Gwendolen, after demanding to see him once more before agreeing to undress, had fallen into a profound silence. With a whispering, repressed urgency, she had only begged him to promise that he would come to her when summoned in the morning. Yet, a foreboding gnawed at Deronda's heart. The possibility that her state might shift, the danger of a sudden fever, and the suspicion that the recent catastrophe was manifesting in her psyche through erratic words weighed heavily upon him. He instructed her attendant that he remained on standby for any alarming change, ensuring everyone understood he was in constant contact with her family in England and felt bound to guard her interests—a role he assumed easily, being well-known to Grandcourt's sole remaining valet from the voyage.

When exhaustion from the day's tumultuous emotions finally claimed Deronda, he slept fitfully, disturbed only by morning dreams that wove yesterday's events into a tangled web. These dreams culminated in an image born of his pressing anxiety, waking him abruptly.

Yet, it was morning, and there had been no summons. This silence brought cheer as he dressed, leading him to believe it was too early to inquire further. Later, he learned she had endured a sleepless night but showed no violent signs of agitation, eventually succumbing to sleep. He marveled at the strength dwelling within this creature, so acutely sensitive to dread; he felt an irresistible conviction that even under the shock of severe trauma, she was mastering herself with a determined secrecy. Conversely, he believed his own sensibilities had been dulled by the ordeal of meeting his mother. To him, his current actions felt merely like fulfilling obligations, his passionate sympathy temporarily suspended. Having lived recently in experiences far removed from Gwendolen's fate, his present cares for her felt like a revisit to familiar scenes, lacking the full revival of inner response they once commanded.

In the meantime, Deronda sought a formal, legal statement from the fishermen who had rescued Gwendolen. Little detail emerged. The boat Grandcourt had departed in was found drifting with its sail loose and was towed ashore. The fishermen surmised he had been knocked overboard by the flapping sail while maneuvering, unable to swim. However, though nearby, their attention was first seized by a cry resembling a man in distress. As they rowed urgently, they heard a shriek from the lady and saw her plunge into the water.

Upon returning to the hotel, Deronda was informed that Gwendolen had risen and desired to see him. He was led to a room darkened by blinds and curtains. Seated within, she wore a white shawl wrapped around her, gazing toward the open door with uneasy anticipation. Her long hair was gathered and coiled meticulously, and the blue star-shaped earrings retained their place. As she rose impulsively to her full height, encased in the white shawl, her face and neck equally pale save for a purple line beneath her eyes, her lips slightly parted with the distinct expression of the accused and helpless, she appeared as the tragic ghost of the Gwendolen Harleth Deronda had previously witnessed turning away from her gambling losses with firm lips and proud composure. The sight pierced him with pity, reviving the echoes of their shared history.

"I beseech you to rest—not to stand," Deronda said as he approached. She obeyed, sinking back into her chair.

"Will you sit down near me?" she requested. "I wish to speak very softly."

She occupied a large armchair, and he drew a small one beside her. The gesture touched her profoundly. Turning her pale face fully toward his, positioned very close, she whispered in the lowest audible tone, "You know I am a guilty woman?"

Deronda himself grew pale as he replied, "I know nothing." He dared not speak further.

"He is dead," she stated with the same subdued certainty.

"Yes," Deronda murmured, his voice heavy with mournful suspension, reluctant to articulate the words.

"His face will not be seen above the water again," Gwendolen continued, her tone not louder but charged with suppressed eagerness, her hands clenched tightly.

"No."

"Not by anyone else—only by me. A dead face... I shall never be able to escape it."

With an inward voice of desperate self-repression, she spoke these final words, averting her gaze from Deronda toward something distant on the floor. Was she viewing the entire event—including her own deeds—through an exaggerating lens of excitement and horror? Was she in a state of delirium colored by a sense of concealment and the necessity of hiding? Such thoughts flashed through Deronda as a faint hope. Yet, imagine the conflict of feelings that kept him silent. She was bent on confession, and he dreaded hearing it. Against his better judgment, he shrank from the task imposed upon him; he wished, yet chided the wish as cowardly, that she could bury her secrets within her own breast. He was not a priest. He feared the weight of this woman's soul cast upon his own with imploring dependence.

Then, hurriedly, she looked at him again. "You will not say that I ought to tell the world? You will not say that I ought to be disgraced? I could not do it. I could not bear it. I cannot have my mother know. Not if I were dead. I could not have her know. I must tell you; but you will not say that anyone else should know."

"In my ignorance," Deronda said mournfully, "I can say nothing except that I desire to help you."

"I told you from the beginning—as soon as I could—that I was afraid of myself." There was a pitiful pleading in the low murmur to which Deronda leaned his ear. Her afflicted face tormented him. "I felt a hatred within me that worked constantly like an evil spirit, contriving things. Every means I could devise to free myself came to mind; and it worsened—all things deteriorated. That is why I asked you to come to me in town. I thought then I would tell you the worst about myself. I tried. But I could not tell everything. And *he* came in."

She paused, a shudder passing through her body, before continuing.

"I will tell you everything now. Do you think a woman who cried, prayed, and struggled to be saved from herself could be a murderer?"

"Great God!" Deronda exclaimed, his voice deep and shaken. "Do not torture me needlessly. You have not murdered him. You threw yourself into the water with the impulse to save him. Tell me the rest afterward. This death was an accident you could not have prevented."

"Don't be impatient with me." The tremor and the childlike beseeching in these words compelled Deronda to turn his head and look at her face. The poor, quivering lips continued. "You said—you used to say—you felt more for those who had done something wicked and were miserable; you said they might improve—they might be scourged into something better. If you had not spoken thus, everything would have been worse. I *did* remember all you said. It came to me always. It came to me at the very last—that was the reason why I—But now, if you cannot endure listening while I tell you everything, if you turn away and forsake me, what shall I do? Am I worse than when you found me and sought to make me better? All the wrong I committed existed then—and more—and more—if you had not come and been patient with me. And now—will you forsake me?"

Her hands, so tightly clenched moments prior, were now helplessly relaxed and trembling on the arm of her chair. Her quivering lips remained parted as she ceased speaking. Deronda could not answer; he was forced to look away. Taking one of her hands, he clasped it as if preparing to walk together like two children; it was the only manner in which he could respond, "I will not forsake you." Throughout, he felt as though signing his name to a blank page that might be filled with terror. Their posture, his averted face bearing an expression of suffering he was solemnly resolved to endure, might have revealed half the truth to a stranger who had suddenly entered.

That grasp was an entirely novel experience for Gwendolen. Never before had any man offered a sign of tenderness that her very being required, and she interpreted its powerful effect as a promise of inexhaustible patience and constancy. The surge of renewed strength enabled her to continue, resuming her fitful, wandering confession where the repetition of experience seemed to erase the sense of time or order. She began again in fragments:

"All sorts of schemes in my mind—but all so difficult. And I fought against them—I was terrified at them—I saw his dead face"—here her voice sank to a whisper close to Deronda's ear—"so long ago I saw it and wished him dead. And yet it terrified me. I was like two creatures. I could not speak—I wanted to kill—it was as strong as thirst—and then immediately—I felt beforehand I had done something dreadful, unalterable—that would make me like an evil spirit. And it came—it came."

Silent for a moment or two, as if her memory had become lost in a web where every thread pulled the others, she resumed.
