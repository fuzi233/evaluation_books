All great literary men are shy. I am myself—though, as I’m told, it’s barely noticeable. I’m glad it is not. It used to be glaringly obvious once upon a time, and caused me no end of suffering—and quite a bit of inconvenience to everyone within shouting distance, especially my female acquaintances, who complained about it with the kind of dramatic flair usually reserved for lost gloves or broken teacups.

To be shy is not a happy state. Men find you dull; women think you’re aloof; and you, poor soul, loathe yourself. Practice doesn’t help. Time is the only cure—though I once stumbled across a remedy so delightfully absurd that I’ve never forgotten it. It appeared in the “Answers to Correspondents” section of a minor weekly publication and read:

Ah, bless the man! I can picture him now, reading those words with a smile so wide it must have stretched from ear to ear like a grinning jack-o’-lantern. "Adopt an easy and pleasing manner!" For heaven’s sake, don’t even *try*, young shyer. Your attempt to pretend you’re anything but your true self will result in you sounding like a wet noodle trying to flirt with a storm. You’ll come off gushing like a leaky faucet and familiar as a street urchin asking for spare change. Be yourself—your natural, awkward, blushing self—and you’ll just be seen as surly and dim-witted.

Yet, the shy man does get his revenge. He spreads his misery like a cold draft through a room. The most cheerful gathering turns solemn in his presence. People feel uneasy. They fidget. They check their watches. And all because they misread him. They mistake his hesitation for arrogance, his silence for superiority, his trembling hands for hauteur. When he blushes violently at the first word spoken to him, they assume he’s been insulted by passion—when in fact he’s simply being crushed by the weight of existence.

Misunderstanding is the shy man’s lifelong companion. He tells a joke—it’s taken as a lie. He makes a sarcastic remark—it’s accepted as gospel truth, and he’s labeled an idiot. He tries to compliment someone—it’s interpreted as mockery, and he’s blackballed for life. All these little disasters are endlessly entertaining to others, providing comic material since the dawn of satire. But if we look past the laughter, there’s something deeper—something almost tragic.

A shy man is, by definition, a lonely man. He walks among people but does not belong. There’s an invisible wall between him and the world—a barrier he can’t scale, no matter how hard he tries. On the other side, he sees smiling faces, hears laughter, feels the warmth of human connection. But he cannot reach out. He wants to join, to speak, to belong. But the crowd moves on, chatting merrily, unaware. He tries to step forward—but the walls follow him, closing in tighter than ever.

In the crowded street, the packed party, the office meeting, the theater—wherever people gather, wherever voices rise and eyes meet—the shy man stands apart, like a leper in a festival. His heart is full of kindness, longing, even love—but the world sees only silence. His soul screams, but his voice fails. Words die on his lips like fish out of water. He wants to comfort the weary, condemn injustice, cheer the downtrodden—but his emotions turn inward, festering like unhealed wounds. They don’t flow outward; they poison him. That’s why shy men often become cynics—not because they hate the world, but because they can’t express their love for it.

Yes, shy men, like ugly women, have a rough ride in this world. To survive it requires the hide of a rhinoceros. Thick skin is our moral armor. Without it, we’re unfit for polite society. A trembling, blushing creature with twitching fingers and knees like jelly is a spectacle—painful to watch, unbearable to endure. If it can’t fix itself, well… let’s just say the sooner it retires to the quiet woods, the better.

But here’s the good news: shyness *can* be cured. I know this from personal experience. I won’t go on about myself—usually I avoid such things—but today, for the sake of humanity, I’ll make an exception. Once, I was, as the lad in the Bab Ballad put it, “the shyest of the shy.” “Whenever I was introduced to any pretty maid,” he said, “my knees they knocked together just as if I was afraid.” And so did mine.

Now, I would—nay, have—done something remarkable. Just yesterday, I stood alone (as the schoolboy said when translating Caesar) and confronted a railway refreshment-room attendant in her own domain. I rebuked her—in tones of sorrow and mild indignation—for her callousness and lack of deference to the traveling Briton. I insisted, courteously but firmly, that I deserved proper attention. And then—I looked her straight in the eye.

Need I say more? Well, yes—because immediately afterward, I left the room with what may have seemed like haste. But that wasn’t fear. No, no. I simply changed my mind. I didn’t need refreshment after all.

One consolation for the shy: shyness isn’t stupidity. It takes real nerve to sneer at nerves. The highest minds aren’t always the ones with the thickest skins. The deer isn’t inferior to the pig. The horse isn’t lesser than the cock-sparrow. Shyness means sensitivity. It has nothing to do with vanity or self-consciousness—though fools insist on linking the two.

Conceit, however, is the best cure. When you realize you’re vastly superior to everyone else, bashfulness vanishes. You glance around a room and think: “These people are mere children. Magpies with degrees.” Suddenly, you’re not afraid of them. You’re above them. And that’s freedom.

Real conceit isn’t the loud, strutting kind—the one with the raised nose and falsetto voice. That’s just play-acting. Real conceit is quiet, confident, unbothered. It doesn’t need to show off. It doesnches not care about praise or blame. It’s content with itself. Such a man is genial, kind, simple. He’s equally at home with a duke or a cabbie. He doesn’t flatter or feign—he just *is*. And that’s rare.

The shy man, meanwhile, is humble—too humble. He doubts himself, fears judgment. But that’s fine for youth. Character is still forming. With time, insight, and experience, diffidence fades. Most men shed shyness by the age of twenty-five. If not, the world polishes it off anyway. You rarely meet a truly shy man—except in novels or on stage, where he’s wildly popular, especially with the ladies.

There, in that magical land of fiction, he’s a golden-haired saint—fair hair and virtue always go together on the stage. One actor once lost his wig mid-performance and had to go on with his natural jet-black hair. The audience howled. They thought he was the villain. They couldn’t believe a noble hero would look so dark and menacing.

This idealized version loves the heroine deeply—but only in asides. He’s noble, unselfish, speaks softly, adores his mother. The villains mock him, but he bears it with grace. Then, miraculously, it turns out he’s actually brilliant. The heroine confesses her love. He’s stunned, overjoyed. Everyone forgives him. He gives a few wise, sarcastic words and blesses them all. He seems to have a grand old time. So naturally, every non-shy young man wishes he were shy.

But the real shy man knows better. In reality, he’s clumsier, less devoted, and his hair is darker—making the whole fantasy collapse under its own weight. Still, one thing he shares with his fictional counterpart: faithfulness. He *is* loyal in love. Why? Because it takes all his courage just to look one woman in the face. He’d never have the strength to try again. He’s terrified of the entire female sex. One is enough. He’s safe in his solitude.

Not so the bold young man. He’s surrounded by roguish eyes and laughing lips. Naturally, he gets confused. He forgets which pair belongs to his intended and ends up making eyes at the wrong woman. The shy man? He stares at his boots. He sees nothing. He’s immune. Happy shy man!

Well, not really. He longs to “go it” like the others. He curses himself daily for lacking the nerve. Occasionally, he gathers himself and dives into mischief. But it’s always a disaster. He flounders, stumbles, and retreats—limp, defeated, pitiable.

And yet—no one pities him. Some misfortunes cause agony but earn no sympathy. Losing an umbrella? Funny. Toothache? Hilarious. Black eyes? Classic. But shyness? That’s comedy gold.

He becomes the living punchline. “Look!” cries the audience. “He’s blushing!”
“Watch his legs,” says another.
“He’s sitting on the edge of the chair!”
“Pity he’s got so many hands,” murmurs a lady, folding hers calmly.
“A yard off his feet wouldn’t hurt,” adds the comic relief.
“And with that voice, he should’ve been a sea captain!”

They point out his cough, his limited conversation, his nervous grip on his hat. They exhaust both him and themselves. Then his family joins in—because relatives are allowed to be extra cruel.

They mimic him. One pretends to walk in like him—shuffling, stiff, trembling—then explains, “That’s how you do it.” Another demonstrates “how you shake hands”—grabbing each person’s hand like it’s red-hot, then dropping it like a dead fish. Then they ask: “Why do you blush?” “Why do you stutter?” “Why do you whisper?” As if he’s doing it on purpose.

Then someone struts about like a peacock and says, “You should walk like this.”
The old man slaps him on the back: “Be bold, my boy!”
The mother beams: “Never do anything you need be ashamed of, Algernon. Then you’ll never be ashamed.”
The boys: “You’re worse than a girl!”
The girls: “No girl would be half as bad!”
Which is true. No girl would. There’s no such thing as a shy woman—at least, I’ve never met one. Until I do, I won’t believe in them.

People say women are timid, gentle, blushing creatures—like startled deer. Men, meanwhile, are bold and fearless. Women admire us, but fear us. A sweet idea. But nonsense.

The twelve-year-old girl is cool as ice. Her brother, twenty, stammers beside her. A woman enters a concert late, interrupts the performance, and doesn’t bat an eyelid. Her husband follows, crumpled with apologies. Women have stronger nerves in matters of love—from the first glance to the last day of the honeymoon. It’s universally acknowledged.

And anyway, love is *their* business. We all shed our weaknesses when it comes to work. The shyest man I ever knew? A photographic tout. He sold pictures with the confidence of a lion tamer.
