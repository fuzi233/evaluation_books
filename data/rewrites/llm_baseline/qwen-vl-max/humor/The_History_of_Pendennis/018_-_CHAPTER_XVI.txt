Pen’s little scandal—what a storm it stirred up! The whole village of Clavering seemed to be buzzing like a hive of bees that had just discovered a particularly juicy bit of gossip. And poor Pen, who’d merely defended his honor (or so he claimed) by throwing a punch at Foker after some insult involving Miss Costigan, now found himself the center of a social hurricane.

Doctor Portman, ever the model of moral rectitude and stiff upper lip, was furious. “You’ve disgraced yourself!” he thundered, pacing back and forth like a lion in a cage. “Violence? Threatening duels? You’re not a knight of old, you’re a boy with a bad haircut and worse manners!”

Pen, meanwhile, took this all with the solemn gravity of a man who’d just been appointed Prime Minister. He sat upright in his chair, chin raised, eyes wide with the seriousness of being *wronged*. “I regret the affair,” he announced gravely, “but I cannot allow myself to be insulted without defending my honor. A gentleman must do what is required.”

He then turned to his uncle, Major Pendennis, and asked with great dignity: “Uncle, could I have acted otherwise?”

“Vous allez trop vite, my good sir,” replied the Major, scratching his head as if trying to untangle a knot in his own thoughts. “Between gentlemen, perhaps. But between two schoolboys? It’s absurd. Like a pair of sparrows squabbling over a crumb.”

Pen blinked. “But it was an outrage!”

“Yes, yes,” said the Major, “but you’re not in the army yet. No pistols. No bloodshed. Just go back to your Latin.”

Mrs. Pendennis, on the other hand, was practically fainting into her handkerchief. “Oh, Arthur!” she wailed. “You’ve done something terrible! You’ve brought shame upon us all! That horrid Foker—why did he have to be so wicked? Why couldn’t he just leave you alone?!”

Pen kissed her forehead with theatrical tenderness. “Women don’t understand these things, dear mother. I placed myself in Foker’s hands. It was the only course.”

The Major grinned. “Ah, yes. The noble code of honor. How very grown-up.” Then he added sotto voce, “Though I suspect it was more about pride than principle.”

And so the family was divided: the doctor scolding, the mother weeping, the uncle chuckling, and Pen pretending he was the hero of a melodrama.

Still, despite the drama, one thing was certain: Pen was going to college. October term, no ifs or buts. Foker had promised to introduce him to “the right set”—a phrase that sounded suspiciously like “the people who drink too much and get into fights.” The Major, however, saw it as a golden opportunity. “Mr. Foker knows the very best young men at the University,” he declared. “There’s the young Marquis of Plinlimmon, Lord Magnus Charters, and even Lady Agnes will invite him to Logwood!”

Helen sighed. “I suppose you know best, brother.”

She didn’t believe it for a second. She knew Mr. Foker was a charming rogue—a man who could charm birds from trees and ladies from their husbands. But she also knew he was trouble wrapped in a waistcoat and polished with a smile.

Still, the decision was made. Pen would go. And as for Smirke, well… let’s just say he wasn’t exactly thrilled.

Smirke, the poor curate, had become Pen’s emotional confidante. With no one else to talk to—his mother wouldn’t listen, his uncle thought love was a distraction, and Foker was far too busy plotting how to steal another man’s horse—Pen poured his heart out to Smirke like a river flooding its banks.

He wrote sonnets. Dozens of them. In English, in Latin, in Greek (well, mostly in English). He dedicated them to “Amaryllis,” which, of course, was just a fancy name for Miss Costigan. He recited them aloud, often in the shrubbery, where the squirrels probably nodded in approval.

Smirke, bless him, pretended to be deeply moved. He praised every line. He said they were “exquisite” and “profound.” He even told Pen that one particular Sapphic verse reminded him of the beauty of nature—though really, it just rhymed “moon” with “soon,” which is hardly Shakespearean.

Pen, naturally, was delighted. He gave Smirke his best books, his gold chain, and even tried to give him his double-barrelled gun—though the Major stopped that before it could end in disaster. Pen also bought a gold pencil-case on credit (he was broke, remember) and inscribed it: “To my dearest friend, whose wisdom and kindness are beyond measure. Always yours, Arthur.”

Smirke accepted it with trembling hands and a heart full of emotion—and possibly a tiny bit of guilt.

But now came the cruel blow: Pen was leaving. College loomed. And with it, the end of Smirke’s daily visits to Fairoaks. The house where he’d been welcomed, where he’d seen Mrs. Pendennis smile, where he’d once dared to hope…

He couldn’t bear it. What would he do? Where would he go? Who would he talk to about his feelings?

He began to wonder: should he confess his love? Should he kneel before the widow and beg for her hand? Or should he wait? Because if he confessed and she refused… well, that would be the end of everything. The gate of Fairoaks would shut forever. And inside that gate lay his entire world.

Meanwhile, Madame Fribsby—the milliner with the French accent and the heart of a romance novelist—had taken matters into her own hands. She believed, with absolute certainty, that Smirke was in love with Mrs. Pendennis. And since she herself was a hopeless romantic (and slightly delusional), she decided to play matchmaker.

Every time she saw Mrs. Pendennis, she would gush: “Oh, such a gentle soul! Such a lonely man! Why, no lady would be so kind as to take pity on him!”

Mrs. Pendennis, of course, laughed politely and changed the subject. But Madame Fribsby was relentless. She spoke in French, half-remembered phrases from novels, and occasionally whispered, “Mong cure a boco souffare,” which roughly translated to “My heart is breaking.”

Smirke, meanwhile, was becoming increasingly nervous. Every time Madame Fribsby praised him, he blushed. Every time she mentioned Mrs. Pendennis, he sighed. And when she said, “Je conny cela biang,” meaning “I know this well,” he nearly fainted.

It was all too much. And then came the moment of truth: Major Pendennis informed Smirke that his services were no longer needed. “We’re grateful, sir,” he said grandly, “but the young gentleman is off to Oxford. You may retire to your parsonage with our warmest thanks.”

Smirke stood there, stunned. His life was ending. His dreams were crumbling. And Madame Fribsby, sensing the crisis, immediately ran to tell Mrs. Pybus.

“Oh, my dear!” she cried. “I know everything! There’s a secret attachment! A marriage is imminent!”

And thus, the gossip machine roared to life again.

Mrs. Pybus, Mrs. Speers, Mrs. Wapshot—they all gathered in Madame Fribsby’s shop, sipping tea and whispering like conspirators. “It’s all settled,” said Mrs. Pybus. “The boy goes to college, and then the widow marries the curate.”

“She’s been seeing him every day,” said Mrs. Speers.

“Poor Mr. Pendennis would turn in his grave,” said Mrs. Wapshot.

“And she married him for his money,” added Mrs. Pybus, “just like a common harlot.”

“It’s rather too open,” said someone else, “to pretend it’s just a tutor.”

Then, as if summoned by fate, Mrs. Portman entered the shop—her face pale, her hat tilted just so, her voice dropping to a whisper: “I heard it all.”

She rushed home and told Doctor Portman, who exploded like a volcano.

“That milksop of a curate!” he roared. “Pretending to be worthy of Mrs. Pendennis? Why, he’s barely out of his teens! And she’s old enough to be his mother!”

“She might be,” said Mrs. Portman, “but she’s still a woman. And he’s a low-church fool who thinks prayer is just a formality.”

So the curate was cast aside, though not entirely. For while the Doctor wanted to fire him, he couldn’t bring himself to do it—because, deep down, he knew Smirke was harmless. And maybe even a little sweet.

Back at Fairoaks, Pen continued to brood. He’d lost his beloved Amaryllis, his tutor, and now, it seemed, his reputation. Rumors flew: that he’d hanged himself in the orchard; that Miss Costigan had jilted him; that he’d paid her off with a thousand pounds (though no one could agree on the exact amount).

When he heard these stories, Pen went wild. “Why isn’t Pybus a man?” he yelled. “I’d challenge her to a duel!”

He glared at Simcoe, the local clergyman, so fiercely that Simcoe went home and wrote a sermon titled “The Perils of Youth and the Dangers of Theatrical Entertainment,” clearly aimed at Pen.

And so, like Achilles in his tent, Pen sulked. He refused to speak. He ate nothing. He stared out the window, imagining the glory of college life—where he would finally escape the gossip, the scandals, and the unbearable weight of being loved by a milliner, adored by a curate, and hated by everyone else.

But even in his anger, he couldn’t help wondering: Was it worth it? To leave behind the only person who truly listened to him?

Perhaps, he thought, it was. Perhaps the world was ready for Arthur Pendennis.

Or perhaps it wasn’t.

Either way, the next chapter was already writing itself.
