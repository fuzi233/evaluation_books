Let’s be real: even the guy whose entire university career consisted of failing three exams, hiding from the bursar, and getting chased by campus security for climbing the chapel roof will get all misty-eyed thinking back on his old college chums. Back then, you’d just been cut loose from your mum’s apron strings: no bills, no boss breathing down your neck, no doctor telling you you can’t drink three pints for breakfast, and you were absolutely convinced you were the cleverest, most charming human being to ever walk a university quad. That first sip of freedom? Sparkling, bubbly, so sweet it’ll make your teeth hurt—none of that stale, bitter leftover swill you’re sipping now when you’re 40, complaining your knees hurt every time you climb a flight of stairs. Even old curmudgeons who can only eat a poached egg and a glass of water for dinner love watching kids with good appetites tuck in, right? It’s way more fun to watch a pantomime when you’re not the one falling asleep in the third act, after all.

Not long back, I popped over to visit Oxbridge, the old university where our mate Arthur Pendennis spent a few years misbehaving, and ended up sharing a train carriage with a current Saint Boniface student who’d conned his way into a day pass to London. That lad talked nonstop the entire journey—jokes, loud cackles, wild plans for how he was going to cram 12 different pleasures into his 24 hours off—so much so that the ride felt 20 minutes long, and I was gutted when we pulled into the terminus. He hopped straight into a hansom cab the second we pulled up, grinning like an idiot, ready to hurl himself headfirst into whatever mischief London had to offer. Me? I’d only brought a tiny carpet bag, so I hopped up on the top of the omnibus, squished between a Jewish peddler smoking a cigar that smelled like wet hay and a valet who was doting on a very snooty poodle, and we trundled off at a snail’s pace, zero hurry to get to that smoky, noisy Babylon of London town. Neither of us was champing at the bit to dance at the casino or show off at our club that night. Give that overexcited kid 10 years, and he’ll be right there with us, happy to take the slow bus home.

When Arthur Pendennis first headed off to Oxbridge, there weren’t any trains around yet, so he went by fancy stagecoach, crammed full of stuffy dons, current students, nervous new freshmen, and their very overprotective guardians. One fat old city bloke in grey stockings, sitting inside next to Major Pendennis, was white as a sheet when he heard that the bloke who’d driven the last two stages was Harry Foker, a Saint Boniface legend who was mates with every coachman in the county and could drive a four-in-hand better than most professional drivers. Pen was perched right up on the coach roof, staring at every tree, cottage, and passenger like it was the most fascinating thing he’d ever seen. When those spiky old university towers, tall elms, and shiny river hove into view? His little heart was doing somersaults.

Before they left London, Pen spent a few days crashing at his uncle Major Pendennis’s Bury Street lodgings. The Major decided Pen’s wardrobe was well overdue for a glow-up, and Arthur certainly wasn’t going to argue with new waistcoats and fancy coats. The self-sacrificing uncle spared no expense for the lad, too—though London was dead quiet that time of year: all the rich folks were out of town, the Pall Mall pavements were practically empty, you couldn’t spot a single red-coated club man loitering by the windows. The Major dragged Pen around a couple of these ghostly club buildings, added his name to the candidate list, and Pen was so chuffed he could barely contain himself. When he saw his name written down in the fancy parchment book: “Arthur Pendennis, Esquire, Fairoaks Lodge, [shire], Saint Boniface College, Oxbridge; proposed by Major Pendennis, seconded by Viscount Colchicum”, he felt like he’d already won the lottery. “You’ll get your membership in three years, right when you finish your degree,” the Major told him, and Pen was already mentally redecorating the club’s stucco halls and vast libraries like they were his personal property. The Major snickered to himself watching the lad strut out of the building like he owned the whole street.

One day, Pen and his mate Foker hopped in Foker’s fancy little cab and drove over to their old school Grey Friars, to say hi to their old school friends. The boys crowded around the cab gate, gawking at Foker’s chestnut horse, and his tiny footman Stoopid, who was dressed up in full fancy livery, looking very serious. Just as they were hanging around the playground chatting, the afternoon school bell rang, and the terrifying headmaster walked past carrying his Latin grammar. Foker immediately ducked behind a bush like a kid avoiding detention, but Pen marched right up, blushing, and shook the old dignitary’s hand, laughing at the memory of that very same grammar smacking him over the ears half a dozen times. He was generous, good-natured, and 100% convinced he was the coolest bloke alive.

Then they popped over to the Foker family brewery, a huge pile of brickwork not far from Grey Friars, whose name was gilded on every pub sign within 20 miles. The brewery’s manager gave the pair of them silver flagons of Foker’s famous extra stout, so strong that not just the two lads, but Foker’s horse seemed to get a buzz off it—he zoomed back to the West End so fast he nearly took out three pie stalls and a bunch of old ladies crossing the road, smacked the cab steps off a street corner post, and made poor Stoopid swing around on his little perch behind like he was on a rollercoaster.

The Major was dead chuffed that Pen was mates with Foker the brewery heir, listening to all his silly stories like they were state secrets, buying the two lads a massive dinner at a Covent Garden coffee house and taking them to the play after. But he was over the moon when Mr and Lady Agnes Foker, who were in London at the time, invited the Pendennis pair over for dinner in Grosvenor Street. “Now that we’ve got an invite into Lady Agnes’s house,” the Major told Pen, deadly serious like he was sharing top secret military intelligence, “you can never let that slip. Whenever you’re in London, you make sure you call by there. Go read up Debrett’s on the Rosherville family tree, alright? Drop a little clever, complimentary reference to them sometime—you’ve got that poetic fancy, you can pull it off easy. Old Foker is a good bloke, even if he’s not from posh stock or well-educated, and he’ll make sure you get offered the family special porter after dinner. Don’t you dare turn it down. I hate beer, it disagrees with me something terrible, but I’ll drink it too, just to keep in good graces.” And the old martyr did exactly that, sitting there grinning through old Foker’s 100-year-old joke about Foker’s Entire stout that he told every single dinner guest.

Lady Agnes, who was 100% wrapped up in her precious son Harry, was the friendliest but silliest woman alive, and she fussed over Pen like he was a long-lost nephew, rambling on for an hour straight about how hard Harry was studying, and how she was terrified it’d ruin his poor dear health. Old Foker cackled so loud at that line the wine glasses rattled, and Harry winked very obviously at Pen behind his mum’s back. She went on for ages: Harry’s near-death from measles, the time he almost drowned, that horrible mean headmaster at Grey Friars who she’d never, ever forgive, no matter what. By the time she finished her rant, she declared the two Pendennises the most charming men she’d ever met, and invited them both up to their country house at Christmas, or whenever Harry was home for vacation.

Later that night back at Bury Street, as they were lighting their candles to go to bed, the Major clapped Pen on the shoulder. “That little reference you made to the Rosherville bloke who fought at Agincourt? Perfectly done, even if Lady Agnes had no clue what you were on about. Very impressive for a first timer—though stop blushing like a schoolgirl, won’t you? Promise me, Arthur: if you’ve got the right introductions, it’s just as easy to hang around good company as bad, no extra work at all. Don’t go making rubbish friends at Oxbridge, alright? First steps in life are the most important. Did you write to your mum today? No? Go do it before we leave, and ask Foker for a free postmark—they love that sort of thing. Good night, God bless you.”

Pen wrote a hilarious, rambling letter back home to his mum Helen at Fairoaks, going on about the play, the old school visit, the brewery, the dinner at the Fokers’, and Helen and little Laura read that letter over and over a dozen times, cooing over every line, the way mums and their surrogate daughters do. It was Pen’s very first big step out into the world, and boy, was that path full of potholes. Fellow travelers, hope you’ve got someone to catch you when you trip, that mercy’s there for you when you mess up, and love’s lighting your way the whole time—you’d be completely blind without it.

Soon enough, the coach pulled up to the Trencher, that lovely old inn on Oxbridge’s main street, and Pen’s eyes went wide: he spotted students wandering around in their gowns, chapel bells clanging nonstop from dawn to evensong, old towers peeking over the gabled roofs of the busy little town. They’d already sorted Pen’s enrolment with Dr Portman back home, and Mr Buck, his Saint Boniface tutor. Once the Major had fussed over his cravat to make sure he looked impressive, the pair walked down Main Street past Saint George’s grand gate and belfry, until they reached Saint Boniface’s own ivy-covered old gate. It had a little half-buried dome on top, a statue of Saint Boniface, and carvings of all the old royal benefactors’ coats of arms.

The porter pointed out the wonky old tower where Mr Buck’s rooms were, and they crossed the quad—Pen’s eyes drinking in every detail: the little fountain splashing in the middle of the grass, the tall chapel windows, the great hall with its fancy lantern window, the master’s lodge where the head of the college would soon emerge swishing around in his silk robes. The Major, who’d spent 40 years staring at Pall Mall pavements, didn’t care about any of the pretty old architecture, of course.

Big, fancy Saint George’s is the Oxbridge college everyone knows, with four massive quads and grounds so grand all its students strut around like they’re better than every other person on the planet. Tiny little Saint Boniface is basically a cosy little hermitage next to it, but it’s got an excellent reputation: posh families from half the counties send their lads there, the jobs you get after graduation are amazing, the fellows barely have to do any work, the college boat comes third on the river, the choir is almost as good as Saint George’s, and the Boniface ale is the best in the whole of Oxbridge. The walls of the wainscoted great hall are covered in portraits of old famous alumni: martyred scholars, archbishops, chief justices, dukes, poets, old friends of Dr Johnson, and the college cook is one of the best chefs in the whole university, his son even took top honours at the rival Camford University. The wine in the fellows’ common room is legendary, too.

So Pen, hanging on his uncle’s arm, trotted into Mr Buck’s rooms, where the tutor was waiting for them. Dr Portman had already written to Buck gushing about Pen, saying he was from a super old landed family, clever, and bound to make the college proud, so Buck was as nice as pie to them. He invited the Major to eat dinner in hall with Pen, to see the lad wear his gown for the first time, then asked them both to come back to his rooms for wine after dinner, and even offered Pen the best set of student rooms in the whole college, which had just happened to become free. The pair left Buck’s study chuffed, the Major declaring the tutor the politest man alive—because when a college don decides to be nice to you, they lay on the charm so thick you could spread it on toast, all grand and stately in their fancy robes.

In Buck’s antechamber, they found a tailor waiting with a whole bag of gowns and caps for Pen to try on. Pen was practically bouncing with excitement as the tailor fitted the gown, then plonked his new cap on at a jaunty angle, just like his old Grey Friars favourite teacher Fiddicombe used to wear it. He stared at himself in the big gilt mirror, preening like a peacock, and the Major snickered—he wasn’t mad at how handsome the lad looked.

Then Davis, the college servant, led them across the quad to Pen’s new rooms, which had just been vacated by a bloke called Spicer. They were cosy as anything, with big old wooden beams, tall wainscoting, and little windows set deep in the walls. Spicer was selling all his furniture, so the Major bought most of the basics for Pen, though they both drew the line at his collection of six terrible sporting prints and four posters of half-nude opera dancers.

They went off to hall after that, where Pen sat with all the other new freshmen eating their dinner, and the Major sat up at the high table with the dons and all the other visiting guardians. After dinner they had wine back at Buck’s, then trundled off to chapel, where the Major sat very properly up in the back, watching the Master, the learned Dr Donne, sit on his fancy carved throne under the organ loft, looking like a very pious marble statue. All the new freshmen were on their best behaviour, but Pen was horrified to see that little terror Foker rock up late, with a group of his mates, all giggling and whispering like they were sat in the opera stalls instead of chapel. To be fair, this was back when William IV was king, young lads are far better behaved these days, and Saint Boniface was always a bit of a party college.

Pen couldn’t sleep a wink that night back at the Trencher inn, he was so excited to move into his new rooms. He tossed and turned thinking of a hundred things: his mum back home at Fairoaks, definitely. That old ridiculous infatuation he’d had with the actress Miss Fotheringay, too—he blushed bright red in the dark thinking about how besotted he’d been, how he’d almost ended up with old drunk Costigan as a father-in-law, and laughed out loud at how stupid that was. “Poor old Smirke,” he mumbled to himself, “I’ll write him a letter, cheer the old sod up. He’s not going to die of a broken heart, ha!”

The Major might have heard a dozen of those silly little mutters through the wall that night. Admittedly, it would have been nicer if the lad had spent his first night thinking nicer, more wholesome thoughts, but his old selfish uncle had already gotten him all wrapped up in the ways of the world. Pen was a good lad, generous and frank, but vain, impetuous, a little bit selfish, and very easily distracted by whatever fun was in front of him. That whole six months of ridiculous heartbreak had aged him way more than a few months, and he was already convinced he was far more experienced and clever than all the other 18-year-old freshmen around him.

The next morning, he and the Major went out and bought all the extra bits and bobs he needed for his rooms, since old Spicer’s china was all chipped, his lamps were smashed, and his bookshelves were way too small to fit all the boxes Helen had packed back at Fairoaks. When those boxes arrived a few days later, Pen’s heart softened seeing his mum’s familiar handwriting on the labels, and he carefully unpacked all his old books, the linen Helen had picked out of the family store, and even all the little jam pots that Laura had wrapped up in straw for him.

Pen had a whole new Alma Mater now. Not every student, though, gets on with their new university mum half as easily as he did.
