I journeyed under the watchful gaze of the moon. As always, she was full—her face unchanging, eternal in its brilliance—and tonight, as she sank beneath the edge of the world, I thought I saw a faint smile upon her silver countenance. Just as her lower rim dipped below the horizon, a cottage appeared in the very center of her disc, painted there as if by invisible hands. Through its open door and window, her light poured forth, warm and inviting. And with that vision came an undeniable certainty: I was expected.

No sooner had I registered it than the moon vanished, the cottage dissolved into nothingness, and darkness crept over the land like a living thing. The path ahead wound through a series of narrow ravines, each one more shadowed than the last. With no sign of dawn, I decided to wait. I settled into a shallow hollow of sand, ate the fruit the children had given me at parting, and fell asleep quickly, lulled by the silence.

I woke abruptly. Above me, stars I had never seen before shimmered across the sky—constellations unknown to my old world. I lay still, gazing upward, when I became aware of a figure seated not far from me, slightly elevated on the slope above. My breath caught. I had been alone—until now.

The shape stood between me and the heavens, outlined sharply against the night. From where I lay low in the hollow, it seemed immense, towering over me like a sentinel of the dark. Then it turned its head—still facing away—and spoke.

“Will you not come with me?” said a voice, soft and deep as a well, unmistakably feminine.

I hesitated, curious but cautious. “Thank you,” I replied, “but I am comfortable here. Where would you have me go? I prefer sleeping under the open sky.”

“There is no harm in the air,” she answered, “but the creatures that wander these lands at night are not ones a man would wish near him while he sleeps.”

“I’ve not been disturbed,” I said.

“No,” she replied. “I’ve sat beside you ever since you laid down.”

That startled me. “Then how did you know I was here? Why do you show me such kindness?”

“I saw you,” she said, still without turning, “in the moonlight just as she disappeared. I see poorly in daylight, but at night my sight is perfect. The shadow of my house would have hidden you—but both doors were open. I was out on the wastes when I saw you enter this hollow. You were already asleep by the time I reached you, and I didn’t wish to wake you. People grow afraid when I appear suddenly. They call me the Cat-woman. It is not my name.”

I recalled the children’s tale—the one about the ugly woman who scratched. But her voice held no menace, only a quiet sorrow, almost apologetic. She could not be truly monstrous.

“You shall not hear it from me,” I said. “Please, tell me what I may call you.”

“When you know me,” she replied, “call me by the name that fits. That will tell me what kind of person you are. Few give me the right one. It is well when they do.”

I smiled faintly. “I suppose, madam, you live in the cottage I saw within the moon?”

“I do,” she said. “I live there alone—except when guests come. It is humble, but I do what I can for those who stay. Sometimes their sleep is sweet.”

Her voice settled into me, calming my nerves, easing the edges of my fear. I rose. “I will go with you, madam.”

She stood at once, and without looking back, began walking ahead. I followed, able just barely to make out her form in the dimness. She was taller than I, though not as tall as I’d first imagined. Yet she never turned her face to me. That made me curious—not fearful. Her voice rang true, clear, and sincere. But how could I name her if I could not see her?

I tried to walk beside her, but she moved faster when I did. No matter how I paced myself, she remained just ahead, always out of reach. Slowly, unease began to creep in. Why did she avoid being seen? Was it because she was hideous? Was I following some terrible, unseen horror through the dark?

A chill ran through me. Almost, I regretted accepting her invitation. Neither of us spoke. The silence stretched, thick and unbearable.

I had to break it. “I’m trying to find a place,” I said, “one I’ve heard of but don’t yet know by name. Perhaps you can tell me?”

“Describe it,” she said. “The stupid Bags know nothing. The careless Lovers forget nearly everything.”

“Where do they live?”

“You’ve just come from them.”

“I’ve never heard those names before.”

“You wouldn’t. Neither people knows its own name.”

“Strange.”

“Perhaps. But hardly anyone anywhere knows their true name. Imagine how many fine gentlemen would stare if they were addressed by the one that actually belongs to them!”

I fell silent, wondering what mine might be.

“What do you think yours is?” she asked, as if reading my mind. “But forgive me—it matters little.”

I opened my mouth to answer, then froze. My name was gone. Not just forgotten—I couldn’t recall even the first letter. This was the second time someone had asked, and both times I’d failed.

“Never mind,” she said gently. “It’s not needed. Your real name is written upon your forehead—but at present it whirls about so wildly that no one can read it. I will help steady it. Soon it will slow… and perhaps settle.”

I was stunned. We had walked for hours, leaving the ravines behind, yet no sign of the cottage had appeared.

“The Little Ones told me,” I said at length, “of a smooth green country, pleasant to walk upon.”

“Yes?”

“They also spoke of a giantess queen. Is that her land?”

“There is a city in that grassy land,” she replied, “where a woman rules as princess. It is called Bulika. But the princess is no girl. She is older than this world, and came from yours—with a terrible history, still unfolding. She is evil, and holds great sway over the Prince of the Power of the Air. Once, the people of Bulika were simple folk—farmers and shepherds. She came among them, and they welcomed her. She taught them to dig for diamonds and opals, to trade with strangers, to abandon their fields and build a city. One day they killed a great serpent. That enraged her, and she declared herself their ruler—becoming a terror to them all.

“At that time, the land was known as THE LAND OF WATERS. The dry channels you crossed were once rivers, and the valley where the Bags and Lovers now grow fruit was a vast lake. But the wicked princess gathered up half the water, sealed it in an egg, and carried it away. Her lap could hold no more. When she left, the rest fled underground, leaving the land parched and barren—just as her heart. Without water, rain does account for nothing. Without rain, springs fail. Since then, the princess has ruled Bulika, keeping her people in constant fear, preventing them from multiplying. Still, they boast of prosperity—skilled in trade, sharp in deceit, loyal only when interests align, proud of their princess, and contemptuous of all others. They believe themselves noble, honest, superior. But the depth of their corruption and arrogance cannot be understood unless you’ve seen them—unless you’ve known the self-deceived, misgoverned wretches.”

“I thank you, madam,” I said. “Now, if you please, tell me of the Little Ones—the Lovers. I long to serve them. Who are they? How did they come to be here? Those children are the greatest wonder I’ve encountered in this world of wonders.”

“In Bulika,” she said, “you may learn something of them. There is an ancient poem in the palace library—no one there can read it, but it plainly states that after the Lovers endure great trials and discover their true name, they will fill the land and make the giants their slaves.”

“By then, they’ll have grown, won’t they?” I asked.

“Yes, they will grow. Yet I think they will not. It is possible to grow and not grow—to grow less and greater at once—even to grow by refusing to grow.”

“Your words are strange,” I said.

“But some words mean more because they seem to mean less.”

“That is true,” she said. “Such words must be understood. If only the princess of Bulika could hear what the silence of the land shouts in her ears every hour! But she is too clever to understand anything.”

“Then when the Lovers grow, will their land have water again?”

“Not exactly. When they are thirsty enough, they will have water. And when they have water, they will grow. To grow, they must have water. Beneath the earth, it flows still.”

“I’ve heard that water twice,” I said. “Once when I rested in the hollow, and again when I fell—nearly killed—by the bad giant. Both times, I heard the voices of the water, and they healed me.”

She never turned her head, but I could hear every word. Her voice echoed the woman in the house of death. Much of what she said slipped from memory, but I forgot my fear entirely.

We walked on, crossing wide stretches of sand, until finally the cottage loomed before us. Its foundation was buried in deep sand, but I saw it was built upon rock. It resembled the sexton’s cottage, but sturdier, with thicker walls. The heavy door opened directly into a bare room, two small windows opposite each other, no glass. My hostess entered, walked straight to the far corner, and wrapped a long white cloth around her head and face. Then she closed the other door—through which the moon had looked—and lit a small horn lantern on the hearth.

“You are very welcome, Mr. Vane,” she said, calling me by the name I had lost.

“Your entertainment will be plain, but since night is not spent and day not near, it is better you stay indoors. Here you will be safe. A little lack is not a great hardship.”

“I thank you,” I replied. “But since you know my name, may I now know yours?”

“My name is Mara.”

I remembered the sexton and his black cat.

“Some take me for Lot’s wife,” she continued, “lamenting Sodom. Others say I am Rachel, weeping for her children. But I am neither.”

“I thank you again, Mara,” I said. “May I lie here on your floor until morning?”

“At the top of that stair,” she said, “you will find a bed. Some have slept better than they expected. Others have lain awake all night and slept all the next day. It is not soft, but better than the sand—and no hyenas sniff about it.”

The stair was narrow and steep, leading to a garret with no ceiling or partitions, and a single low dormer window. Under the sloping roof stood a narrow bed, its white coverlet stark and chillingly familiar—like the couches in the chamber of death. On the table, a dry loaf and a cup of cold water. For one who had eaten only fruit for months, it was a feast.

“I must leave you in the dark,” she called from below. “This lantern is all the light I have. There are things to do tonight.”

“It is of no consequence,” I said. “To eat, drink, lie down, sleep—these can be done in darkness.”

“Rest in peace,” she murmured.

I ate the bread, drank every drop of water, and lay down. The bed was hard, the covering thin, the night cold. I dreamed I was in the chamber of death, between the warrior and the lady with the healing wound.

I woke in the middle of the night, thinking I heard animals prowling. “Desert creatures scenting after me,” I muttered, reassured by the safety of the cottage. But then—a rough purr rose into a fierce howl beneath my window. I leapt from the bed.

Outside, in the full glow of the moon, a tall woman stood, draped in white, her back to me. She bent over a large white animal—like a panther—patting it with one hand, pointing with the other toward the moon, then drawing a line down to the horizon. Instantly, the creature darted off with impossible speed in that direction.

I watched it vanish. Then I looked back—she was gone.

I searched the distance, but saw only a faint white speck—or perhaps only imagined it.

What did it mean? What mission sent that beast?

I shuddered and returned to bed.

Then I remembered: when I’d lain in the hollow, the moon was setting. Now, just hours later, she was high in the sky—full and bright.

“Everything is uncertain here,” I whispered. “Even the heavens.”

Later, I learned this world had multiple moons, each following different paths. But the laws governing them remained beyond my grasp.

I slept again, undisturbed.

In the morning, I found bread and water waiting—this time, a larger loaf. I ate half. Mara sat beside me, wrapped in silence, until I asked how to reach Bulika.

“Go up the riverbed until it vanishes,” she said. “Then turn right until you reach a forest. You may spend a night there, but leave at dawn, facing the rising moon. Keep that direction until you meet a running stream. Cross it at right angles, and go straight until you see the city on the horizon.”

I thanked her, then ventured, “Last night, I saw your messenger. She understood you perfectly—moved so fast, so sure. If I had such a guide…”

She interrupted. “She went to Bulika—the shortest way.”

“How wonderfully intelligent she looked!”

“Astarte knows her work well enough to be sent.”

“Do you have many such messengers?”

“As many as I need.”

“Are they hard to teach?”

“They need no teaching. They are all of a certain breed—but none are alike. Their origin is so natural, it would seem incredible.”

“May I know it?”

“A new one came to me last night—from your head while you slept.”

I laughed. “Everyone here loves mystery.”

“Some chance word of yours sparked an idea—and in this form, she embodies the truth.”

“Then the creature is mine!” I exclaimed.

“Not at all,” she said. “Only what exists through our will can truly be ours.”

“Ah,” I thought, “a metaphysician too.”

I paused. “May I take the leftover bread?”

“You will need no more today,” she said.

“To-morrow, I may.”

She rose and walked to the door. “It has nothing to do with tomorrow—but you may take it if you wish.”

She opened the door and stood holding it. I picked up the loaf—but hesitated, longing to see her face.

“Must I go?” I asked.

“No one sleeps in my house two nights in a row.”

“I thank you for your hospitality,” I said. “Farewell.”

As I turned to leave, she murmured, “The time will come when you must stay with me many days and many nights.”

“Willingly,” I replied.

“Nay—not willingly,” she said.

I thought, *She’s right—I would not choose to return.* But instantly, my heart reproached me. Before I crossed the threshold, I turned back.

She stood in the center of the room. Her white garments flowed like waves at her feet, and the wrappings of her face lay scattered among them. Her face—revealed at last—was beautiful as a starlit night. Her great gray eyes looked upward, tears tracing paths down her pale cheeks. She reminded me of the sexton’s wife—though one seemed untouched by sorrow for ages, the other as if weeping constantly beneath her veil. Yet in her eyes, a quiet hope flickered: *Weeping may endure for a night, but joy cometh in the morning.*

I bowed my head, ready to kneel and beg forgiveness—when I looked up and found myself outside a doorless house.

I circled it, searching for an entrance. Nothing. I stopped beneath a window, about to cry out my confession—when a sudden shriek tore through the air, a wailing howl that froze my blood.

Something leapt from the window above and landed beyond me. I turned—there, a large gray cat, fur bristling, racing toward the riverbed.

I fell face-first into the sand, and within the house, I seemed to hear the gentle sobbing of one who suffered—but did not repent.
