Jorian Ketel hurried directly to Margaret's chamber, expecting to find the fugitive hidden away. Instead, he stumbled upon a scene of profound stillness that left him breathless. In the center of the room lay Gerard, pale and rigid, his head resting in Margaret's lap. She knelt over him, utterly silent, her form frozen in a paralysis of grief. Her eyes were wide and glassy, reflecting no light, hearing no voice, caring for nothing in the world save the white, lifeless face cradled against her breast.

Jorian stood transfixed, the candle trembling violently in his grip. "Where has he been all this time?" he cried out, but Margaret did not answer. Ignoring her, Jorian approached the open chest and examined its contents. A sudden comprehension dawned upon him, mirroring the girl's dumb, icy despair. "This is a pitiful sight," he muttered, his voice heavy with pity. "A black night's labor, all for a handful of skins! It would have been far better to have gone with us. She is past responding to me, poor creature."

He paused, then reached for a small, round mirror hanging nearby—no larger than his palm—and pressed it gently to Gerard's mouth and nostrils. Holding it there for a moment, he watched the surface closely. When he pulled it away, the glass remained dull and clouded.

"There is life in him!" Jorian whispered to himself, the realization striking him like lightning.

The word struck Margaret instantly. Though spoken softly, it shattered her stupor. As if a statue suddenly infused with spirit and passion, she sprang to her feet. She threw her arms around Jorian's neck, clutching the burly man with an intensity that bordered on fierceness. "Oh, bless the tongue that speaks this truth!" she gasped, holding him tight again and again. "Yes, yes! Lay him warm," Jorian urged gently.

In a swift movement, he lifted Gerard and placed him upon the bedclothes. Drawing a flask from his belt, he poured a generous measure of Schiedamse brandy into his hand and splashed it sharply onto Gerard's face. The pungent liquor acted swiftly; Gerard drew a faint, ragged sigh. Never had such a sound brought more relief to the human ear. Margaret rushed toward him, but halted abruptly, trembling with the fear that her touch might harm him further. Having lost all confidence in her own strength, she hovered uncertainly.

"That is correct—leave him be," Jorian instructed firmly. "Do not crowd him as you did me, or you will drive his breath back. Leave him alone; he will surely recover. He is not an old man."

Gerard sighed deeply, and a faint flush of color began to creep up his lips. Seizing the opportunity, Jorian moved toward the door. Hardly had he reached the threshold when his legs were seized from behind. It was Margaret! She curled around his knees like a clinging vine, kissing his hand and fawning upon him with desperate gratitude.

"You will not speak?" she pleaded. "You have saved his life; you possess no heart to cast him back into the grave, to undo your own good deed?"

"No, no!" Jorian reassured her warmly. "It is not the first time I have aided both of you; remember, I warned you in the church where we intended to hide him. Besides, what does Dierich Brower matter to me? I would see him hanged before I ever revealed his location. However, I must ask: where are the parchments? A hundred crowns are offered for them. That would be a wonderful fortune for my Joan and the children, you know."

"Ah! Those hundred crowns shall be yours," Jorian promised eagerly.

"What! Are the documents still in the house?" Jorian asked, leaning forward.

"Not there, but I know exactly where they are," Margaret declared, her voice rising with renewed vigor. "By God and Saint Bavon, I swear you shall have them tomorrow. Come to me whenever you wish, but come alone."

"I could not be otherwise," Jorian replied, his mind racing. "How could I share the hundred crowns with Dirk Brower? May my bones rot in my skin if I allow another soul to know the poor boy is here."

With that, he bolted from the room, fearing that any delay might arouse suspicion and draw the authorities to them. Left alone, Margaret knelt beside Gerard, quivering from head to toe, praying fervently for his recovery.

Outside, Jorian met Dierich Brower's questioning gaze. "What is to be done?" Jorian asked casually. "Well, we have frightened the girl out of her wits. She was in some sort of fit."

"We ought to go and tend to her ourselves," Dierich suggested.

"Oh, yes! And scare her right into the churchyard," Jorian retorted smoothly. "Her father is a physician, and I have already summoned him to restore her senses. Shall we check the fire instead?"

His casual demeanor disarmed all suspicion. Soon, the group concurred that the kitchen of the "Three Kings" inn was far warmer than Peter's dwelling, and they departed, having first untied Martin. At the door, Dierich Brower remarked triumphantly, "Note well, friend, that I was right and the burgomaster wrong. I predicted we would arrive too late to catch him, and indeed, we were too late."

Thus, Gerard escaped the twin terrors of prison and death. How did he achieve freedom? Not through cleverly devised hiding places nor Margaret's quick wit alone, but through a sudden impulse of kindness in one of his captors—a fragment of humanity remaining in a reckless heart—aided by a desire for profit. Human motives are often so tangled and contradictory; even our shrewdest plans are frequently shortsighted. Those whose gentle natures or fortunate lives shield them from the extreme heights of joy and depths of anguish may be the happiest of mankind, though to explain the unspeakable bliss that now settled upon these persecuted lovers to such readers would be a futile task.

To watch a beloved face return from the brink of the grave to the world, regaining health and beauty through swift, miraculous gradations; to see the roses bloom once more on the cherished cheek, the spark of love return to the beloved eye, and speech flow freely from the loved mouth—this was Margaret's joy, a happiness sufficient to balance years of sorrow. For Gerard, it was awakening from a trance to find his head pillowed on Margaret's arm, to hear the woman he adored whisper new words of eloquent love, and to receive showers of tears, tender kisses, and caresses. Until this sacred moment, he had never known the depth of her affection. He thanked his enemies.

They entwined their arms sweetly, feeling that trouble and danger belonged to a distant age. They called each other husband and wife. Had they not been solemnly betrothed? Had they not stood before the altar together? Did not the blessing of the Holy Church rest upon their union, while its curse fell upon all who sought to separate them? Yet, no woman's nerves can endure such terrible strain without consequence. Presently, Margaret grew faint and sank onto Gerard's shoulder, smiling weakly but completely unstrung.

Anxious, Gerard wished to seek help, but she held him with a gentle grasp, imploring him not to leave her even for a moment. "As long as I can place my hand upon you, I feel you are safe; otherwise, I am lost," she murmured. "Foolish Gerard! Nothing ails me. I am merely weak, dearest, but oh, so happy!"

Then it was Gerard's turn to support that dear head, with its great waves of hair flowing loosely over him. He nursed her, soothing her quivering frame on his bosom with soft, encouraging words and murmurs of love. There is no charm sweeter to a manly heart than a woman's vulnerability in moments of crisis. Poor souls! They were truly happy. Tomorrow they must part, but that thought meant nothing to them in that hour. They had stared into Death's face, and all other troubles seemed as light as air. Where there is life, there is hope; where there is hope, there is joy. Was separation for a year or two significant to two such young people who had glimpsed the grave? The future appeared bright, the present was heaven. So passed those blissful hours.

Alas, their innocence faced dangers beyond prisons and graves. Now that visible threats had vanished, they were in peril from their own hearts and their inexperience.
