The moment had come. Sir Philip Nunnely knew it. Shirley knew it. Mr. Sympson knew it. That evening, when the entire Fieldhead household dined at Nunnely Priory, the decision was sealed.

Several things had drawn the baronet to his resolve. He had noticed Miss Keeldar looking thoughtful and pale—more delicate than usual. This change stirred something tender in him, a poetic vulnerability. A sonnet formed itself in his mind before he could stop it. Then, one of his sisters persuaded her to sit at the piano and sing—one of Sir Philip’s own ballads. It was the simplest, most unadorned of all his verses, yet in its quiet honesty, it was perhaps his finest.

At that very moment, Shirley had been standing by a window, watching the storm-lit park. She saw the moonlight slashing through the trees—the broad oaks, the towering beeches—battling the wind. The forest roared below; clouds raced across the sky, and the moon seemed to dart past like a silver comet. She turned from the scene, not enchanted, but deeply moved—awakened, if not inspired.

She sang as asked. The ballad spoke of love that endured hardship, love that grew stronger in sorrow, love that clung tighter in poverty. The melody was old and noble; the words were plain, sweet, and true. On paper, they might lack fire—but sung with feeling, they needed nothing more. Shirley sang them with such depth, such passion, that she stirred every heart in the room—and captivated one in particular.

Afterward, she walked to the hearth and sat on a low stool-cushion. The ladies gathered around her, silent. The Misses Sympson and the Misses Nunnely stared at her as though she were an exotic bird—an egret, or an ibis—something foreign and unsettling. Why did she sing like that? _They_ never sang so. Was it proper? So expressive? So original? Certainly not. Strange things were wrong. Unusual things were improper. Shirley was judged.

Old Lady Nunnely watched her from her chair by the fire. Her eyes said plainly: “This woman is not of our kind. I disapprove of her as my son’s wife.”

Her son saw the look. He understood. He feared losing what he desired most. He must act quickly.

The room once served as a gallery. Sir Philip’s father, Sir Monckton, had transformed it into a salon, but it still held a sense of mystery—long shadows, deep recesses. One corner contained a small couch, a table, and a delicate cabinet. It was a space within a space—private enough for two people to speak without being overheard.

Sir Philip arranged for two of his sisters to perform a duet. He occupied the Sympsons. The older women chatted. While the others were distracted, Shirley rose to examine the paintings. He joined her and began telling a story about an ancestor—a dark-haired beauty, radiant as a southern flower.

There were relics of that lady in the cabinet. As Shirley bent to study the missal and rosary on the inlaid shelf, while the Misses Nunnely screeched their conventional duet, Sir Philip leaned down too—and whispered a few urgent words.

At first, Shirley froze—so still it seemed as though his voice had turned her to stone. But then she looked up and replied. They parted. She returned to the fire. He watched her go, then stepped behind his sisters. Only Mr. Sympson saw the exchange.

That man drew his own conclusions. Had he been half as sharp as he was intrusive, he might have read the truth in Sir Philip’s face. But he was shallow, hasty, and certain of himself. He went home triumphantly.

He was not one to keep secrets. When excited, he couldn’t help talking. The next morning, needing his son’s tutor to assist with paperwork, he announced, in grand, exaggerated tones, that the tutor should prepare to return south soon. The important business that had kept him in Yorkshire was nearing completion. His efforts, long and anxious, were finally rewarded. A most suitable alliance was about to be made.

“In Sir Philip Nunnely?” Louis Moore guessed.

Mr. Sympson responded with a pinch of snuff and a chuckle—cut short by dignity—and ordered the tutor back to work.

For days, he remained smooth and oily, yet restless as a hen on a hot stove. He paced, peered out windows, listened for carriage wheels. He waited—not just for news, but for formality, consultation, lawyers, settlements—every pompous detail of a grand union.

Then came a letter. He handed it to Miss Keeldar himself, recognizing the handwriting and the seal. He didn’t watch her open it—she took it to her room. Nor did he see her reply—she wrote it in private, and took hours. He asked if it was answered. She said yes.

He waited—silent now, unable to speak, stunned by something in her face—something terrible, unreadable, like the writing on the wall to Belshazzar. He almost called Daniel—Louis Moore—to interpret it. But pride forbade it. And Daniel himself seemed baffled—his face like a scholar staring at blank pages.

* * * * *

Mr. Sympson had gone out, seeking distraction among friends at De Walden Hall. He returned earlier than expected. His family and Miss Keeldar were gathered in the oak parlour. He addressed her directly.

“Miss Keeldar,” he said, “please step into another room with me. I wish to have a strictly private conversation.”

She rose without question, without surprise.

“Very well, sir,” she said, calm as a person told the dentist has arrived to pull the tooth that’s tormented her all month. She left her sewing and thimble in the window seat and followed him.

In the drawing-room, they sat opposite each other—armchairs, a few yards apart.

“I’ve been to De Walden Hall,” Mr. Sympson began. He paused. Shirley stared at the green-and-white carpet. No response was needed. She gave none.

“I’ve learned,” he continued slowly, “a circumstance that surprises me.”

She rested her cheek on her finger, waiting.

“It seems Nunnely Priory is closed—the family has returned to their estate in ----shire. Sir Philip himself accompanied his mother and sisters.”

“Indeed,” said Shirley.

“May I ask if you share my astonishment?”

“No, sir.”

“Is it news to you?”

“Yes, sir.”

“I mean—I mean,” he stammered, fidgeting now, slipping back into his usual tangled speech, “I want a thorough explanation. I will not be put off. I shall insist on being heard. My questions must be answered. I demand clear, satisfactory replies. I am not to be trifled with.” (Silence.)

“It is strange—extraordinary—most odd! I thought everything was settled. And now—they’re gone!”

“I suppose they had a right to leave.”

“_Sir Philip is gone!_” (emphasizing it).

Shirley raised her brows. “Bon voyage.”

“This will not do. This must be changed, ma'am.”

He pushed his chair forward, then back. He looked furious and helpless.

“Come now, uncle,” Shirley said gently, “don’t fret. We’ll make no progress if you rage. Ask me what you want. I’m willing to explain. I promise truthful answers.”

“I demand to know,” he said, “whether Sir Philip made you an offer.”

“He did.”

“You admit it?”

“I admit it. Now proceed. Consider that point settled.”

“He proposed that night we dined at the priory?”

“It is enough to say he did. Go on.”

“In the recess—the old gallery converted into a saloon?”

No answer.

“You were both examining the cabinet. I saw it. My insight is never wrong. Later, you received a letter from him. What was it about?”

“None of your concern.”

“Ma'am, is this how you speak to me?”

Shirley tapped her foot sharply on the carpet.

“There you sit—silent, sullen—_you_ who promised truth.”

“Sir, I have answered thus far. Proceed.”

“I’d like to see that letter.”

“You cannot.”

“I must and shall. I am your guardian.”

“Having ceased to be a ward, I have no guardian.”

“Ungrateful creature! Raised as my own daughter—”

“Once more, uncle, please stay on topic. Let us remain calm. I do not wish to lose temper. But push me too far, and I care little what I say. Listen: You asked if Sir Philip offered. I’ve answered. What else do you wish to know?”

“I want to know whether you accepted or refused him. And know it I will.”

“Certainly, you ought to know. I refused him.”

“Refused him! _You_, Shirley Keeldar, refused Sir Philip Nunnely?”

“I did.”

The gentleman leapt up, pacing the room like a caged animal.

“There it is! There it is! There it is!”

“Sincerely, I regret your disappointment, uncle.”

Concession only hardens some men. Mr. Sympson was one such.

“_I_ disappointed? What does it matter to me? Do I have any interest?”

“You imply I have motives.”

“Most people do.”

“She accuses me! I, who raised her as my own child, accused of bad motives!”

“Bad motives I did not say.”

“And now you evade—no principles!”

“Uncle, you tire me. I want to go.”

“You shall not! I will have answers. What are your intentions, Miss Keeldar?”

“In what regard?”

“In marriage.”

“To be quiet—and do as I please.”

“As you please! Such words are indecorous.”

“Mr. Sympson, I advise you not to become insulting. You know I won’t endure it.”

“You read French. Your mind is poisoned by novels. You’ve adopted French ideas.”

“The ground beneath you echoes hollowly. Beware.”

“It will end in infamy. I’ve foreseen it.”

“Do you assert something involving me will end in infamy?”

“That it will. You said you’d do as you please. You acknowledge no rules.”

“Silly talk—vulgar nonsense!”

“Disregarding decorum, you plan to defy propriety.”

“You tire me, uncle.”

“What could be your reason for refusing Sir Philip?”

“At last—a sensible question. I shall answer. Sir Philip is too young. I see him as a boy. His family—especially his mother—would resent such a match. It would divide him from them. I am not his equal in society.”

“Is that all?”

“Our natures don’t align.”

“Why, he’s the most amiable man alive.”

“He is kind—excellent—truly estimable. But _not my master_. Not in one way. I could not trust myself with his happiness. I would not guard it for thousands. I will accept no hand that cannot hold me in check.”

“I thought you liked to do as you pleased. You’re inconsistent.”

“When I promise obedience, it must be with certainty I can keep it. I could not obey a youth like Sir Philip. Besides, he’d expect me to rule—to guide—and I have no taste for that role.”

“You—no taste for commanding?”

“Not my husband. Only my uncle.”

“What difference?”

“There is a slight difference—certainly. And I know any man who wishes to live comfortably with me must be able to control me.”

“I wish you had a real tyrant.”

“A tyrant wouldn’t last an hour. I’d rebel—break free—defy him.”

“Are you not confusing yourself?”

“I clearly bewilder you.”

“You mentioned Sir Philip’s youth. He’s twenty-two.”

“My husband must be thirty—with the wisdom of forty.”

“You’d choose an old man—white-haired, bald?”

“No, thank you.”

“You could dominate a fool—pin him to your apron.”

“I might do that with a boy. But it’s not my purpose. Did I not say I prefer a _master_—one whose presence makes me want to be good; one whose authority my temper must respect; a man whose approval rewards me, whose displeasure punishes; a man I cannot help but love—and may fear?”

“What prevents you from feeling this for Sir Philip? He’s a baronet—wealthy, connected, respected. If intellect matters, he writes poetry—while you, clever as you are, cannot.”

“His title, wealth, poetry—none give him the power I need. They’re lightweights. Solid sense would have served him better.”

“You and Henry both adore poetry! You used to ignite at the subject.”

“O uncle, nothing truly valuable—nothing glorious—is not poetry!”

“Marry a poet, then!”

“Show me one, and I will.”

“Sir Philip.”

“Not at all. You’re nearly as poetic as he.”

“Madam, you stray from the point.”

“Indeed, uncle, I wanted to. I’d be glad to lead you away. Let us not quarrel. It’s not worth it.”

“Out of temper, Miss Keeldar? I should like to know who is.”

“I am not—yet.”

“If you suggest I am, you’re impertinent.”

“You will be soon, if you continue.”

“There it is! With your tongue, you’d try Job’s patience.”

“I know I would.”

“No levity! This is serious. I’ll probe thoroughly. You described your ideal husband with shocking freedom. Did you paint from life?”

Shirley opened her mouth—then flushed crimson.

“I shall have an answer,” Mr. Sympson declared, bold now.

“It was a historical portrait, uncle—from several originals.”

“Several originals! Bless my soul!”

“I’ve loved many times.”

“Cynical.”

“Heroes of many nations.”

“What next—”

“And philosophers.”

“She’s mad—”

“Don’t ring the bell, uncle. You’ll alarm my aunt.”

“My poor dear aunt—what a niece!”

“Once I loved Socrates.”

“Pooh! No trifling.”

“I admired Themistocles, Leonidas, Epaminondas.”

“Miss Keeldar—”

“Skipping centuries, Washington was plain—but I liked him. But now—present realities—”

“Ah! The present.”

“To abandon schoolgirl dreams—face truth.”

“Truth! That’s your test, ma'am.”

“To reveal whom I now worship—my present idol—”

“You’ll hurry, if you please. Luncheon is near. Confess you shall.”

“Confess I must. My heart is full. I must speak. I only wish you were Mr. Helstone instead of Mr. Sympson—you’d understand better.”

“It’s common sense, not sentiment. Did you say Mr. Helstone?”

“Not exactly—but close. They resemble each other.”

“I’ll know the name. I’ll have details.”

“They are quite alike. Their faces—like human falcons—dry, direct, decisive. But my hero is greater. His mind has the clarity of deep water, the endurance of rocks, the force of waves.”

“Rant and nonsense!”

“I dare say he can be harsh as a saw, gruff as a raven.”

“Does he live in Briarfield? Answer me.”

“Uncle, I’m about to tell you. His name trembles on my lips.”

“Speak, girl!”

“Well said, uncle. ‘Speak, girl!’ It’s tragic. England has howled at this man—and one day she’ll roar in triumph. He’s unshaken by the howl, and unmoved by the shout.”

“I said she was mad. She is.”

“This country will shift again and again in her treatment of him. He will never change in his duty. Cease your agitation, uncle—I’ll tell you.”

“You shall—or—”

“Listen! Arthur Wellesley, Lord Wellington.”

Mr. Sympson rose, furious. He stormed out—then rushed back, slammed the door, and sat down.

“Ma'am, you _shall_ tell me this. Will your principles allow you to marry a man without money—below you?”

“Never a man below me.”

(In a high voice.) “Will you marry a poor man?”

“What right have you to ask?”

“I insist.”

“You don’t go the way to know.”

“My family’s reputation must not suffer.”

“A fine resolution—keep it.”

“Madam, _you_ shall keep it.”

“Impossible, sir. I am not part of your family.”

“Do you disown us?”

“I disdain your tyranny.”

“Whom _will_ you marry?”

“Not Mr. Sam Wynne—I scorn him. Not Sir Philip Nunnely—I only esteem him.”

“Who then?”

“Four rejected candidates.”

“Such stubbornness means you’re under influence.”

“What do you mean? Certain phrases make my blood boil. Improper influence? What old gossip is that?”

“Are you a young lady?”

“I am a thousand times better: an honest woman. Treat me as such.”

“Do you know”—he leaned forward, whispering with grim solemnity—“the whole neighbourhood buzzes with rumours about you and a bankrupt tenant—your foreigner Moore?”

“Does it?”

“It does. Your name is on every lip.”

“It honours them. May it purify them.”

“Is it _him_ who influences you?”

“More than any you’ve championed.”

“Is it he you’ll marry?”

“He is handsome, strong, commanding.”

“You declare it to my face! The Flemish rogue! The low trader!”

“He is talented, daring, resolute. Prince wears his brow, ruler stands in his bearing.”

“She glorifies him! She hides nothing! No shame, no fear!”

“When we speak Moore’s name, shame should vanish. Fear must be cast aside. The Moores know only honour and courage.”

“I say she’s mad.”

“You’ve provoked me until my blood boils. You’ve worn me thin.”

“That Moore is my son’s tutor’s brother. Would you let the usher call you sister?”

Shirley’s eyes flashed bright and clear.

“No. Not for a province. Not for a century.”

“You cannot separate husband from family.”

“What then?”

“Mr. Louis Moore’s sister—you will be.”

“Mr. Sympson, I am sick of this weak babble. I will bear no more. Your thoughts are not mine. Your aims, your gods—none of these are mine. We see the world differently. We speak different tongues. Let us part.”

“It is not,” she added, trembling now, “that I hate you. You are a decent man. Perhaps you mean well. But we do not suit. You irritate me with petty meddling, with small tyranny. You provoke my temper, and keep me angry. Your little rules, your prejudices, your dogmas—pack them away. Offer them to your god. I reject them all. I walk by another creed—another light, faith, hope. I wash my hands of yours.”

“Another creed!” he cried. “I believe she’s an infidel.”

“An infidel to _your_ religion—an atheist to _your_ god.”

“_An—atheist!!!_”
