The sign "Room to let for a single gentleman" fluttered like a desperate plea for charity, and sometimes, driven by a foolish idleness, I would knock on that door. It opened eventually, usually after an interval measured in minutes or hours, revealed by the "slavey"—a creature of unchanging, bewildered features whose round eyes and open mouth seemed frozen in time. In the mornings, she appeared slatternly, her arms hidden beneath a stained apron; in the afternoons, she was sharp-eyed and smart in a dirty cap, yet never did she grow an inch taller, her touzled hair and scored red hands remaining a constant, comic tragedy. With a Herculean effort, I refrained from muttering, "So sorry, forgot my key," and instead pushed past her, ascending the narrow, partially carpeted stairs to the first floor. "Oh, what rooms have you to let?" I would inquire, only to be met by a scuttle up the kitchen stairs and a call over the banisters: "A gentleman to see the rooms."

Up came a harassed-looking, elderly female, genteel in black, who crushed past the little slavey and eyed me critically. "I have a very nice room on the first floor," she informed me, "and one behind on the third."

Agreeing to inspect them for a "young friend," I squeezed past the hat stand, where space was non-existent unless one hugged the wall like a leech. The first-floor apartment was an imposing edifice of poverty. A marble-topped sideboard, massive enough to require a wad of paper to keep its doors shut, dominated the corner. A green tablecloth matched the curtains, covering a loo-table that demanded the lamp stand in absolute center-point precision; shift it, and disaster ensued. A paper fire-stove ornament bestowed an artificial air of chastity upon the room, while above the mantelpiece hung a fly-blown mirror, its cracked glass serving as a canvas for discarded invitation cards—a testament to the fact that even bed-sitters partake in social delusions. Opposite lay an oleograph of a Swiss landscape, sold by Cheap Jacks on Saturday nights, promising "hand-made" authenticity. It featured a dilapidated mill, a foaming torrent, a mountain, a maiden, and a cow—the reliable quartet of the cheap print trade. Beside it sat an easy chair, a monstrosity stuffed with coils of steel wire and horsehair, designed to say, "Excuse me, you are very heavy; please, lie on the floor." Behind the door lay the bed, and behind the bed, the washstand. If you sat facing the window, you could forget the existence of the sleeping arrangements.

"And this room is?"

"Eight shillings a week, sir—with attendance, of course."

"Any extras?"

"The lamp, sir, is eighteenpence a week; and the kitchen fire, if the gentleman wishes to dine at home, two shillings."

"And fire?"

"Sixpence a scuttle, sir."

"It's rather a small scuttle."

The landlady bridled. "The usual size, I think, sir."

I proceeded to the third floor back. Here, the bed refused to hide, glaring as the life and soul of the room. Its rival was a straw-coloured washstand with a staring white basin, glaring defiantly from the corner, shouting, "I know I'm small, but I'm useful!" The remaining furniture included two honest chairs, a small chest of drawers, and an attenuated four-legged table that occupied the entire remaining space after accounting for three square feet of tenant. Hanging above the fireplace was a funeral card framed in beer corks, a morbid reminder that luxuries have ends, or perhaps a consolation that things could be worse.

The rent was three-and-sixpence a week, plus the lamp and a cheaper kitchen fire. When I questioned the disparity in fire costs, the landlady explained that the first floor simply required more cooking. Indeed, the gentleman on the third floor ate a diet of Saveloys, meat pies, roley-poley puddings, peas pudding, and occasional fried fish or steak, all consumed with eyes shut tight to avoid the monotony of reality. He paid me a shilling to slip into the slavey's hand, and I departed into the long, dreary street, now echoing with the cries of "Muffins!"

Later, when the lamps lit the foggy squares and the fires cosified the dingy rooms, I would visit the ghosts of old friends. Their fashions had changed slightly—bell-shaped trousers, paper collars, and india-rubber linens—but their spirit remained. They were gallant gentlemen, lonely and despised, keeping themselves genteel on twelve or fifteen shillings a week, dreaming of Clapham and partner's chairs while eating bread and cheese. They dreamed of Florence, Kate, and unfinished poems posted to beloveds, finding fleeting joy in the wild thrill of the theater box office, charging through crowds like demons until they secured a seat in the front row.

My own journey to this point had been a study in isolation. After my mother's death, and despite Hal's gentle refusal to offer aid, insisting I learn "not to be afraid of life," I had wandered the reading rooms of Ludgate News, surrounded by a seedy army of unemployed clerks and shopmen. We were a whispering legion, dividing into the cheery newcomers, who flitted from newspaper to newspaper with the zest of diners sampling a rich menu, and the old stagers, who sat steadily writing letters of modest aspiration, their hopes fading daily like ink in rain.

I, Paul Kelver, was a shy giant, trapped by a temperament that made me appear awkward, stupid, and sulky. I wrote dozens of letters, careful with margins and pens, yet shyness, that old schoolyard enemy, had returned to haunt me. Every interview ended with the polite, fatal dismissal: "We will write and let you know." My few pounds dwindled, and the world seemed ready to trample me down unseen.

Then, one morning, a letter awaited me from "Lott and Co., Indian Commission Agents." I changed into my silk hat, donned my gloves, and marched to Aldersgate Street, a soldier in a futile war against Fate. Rain began to fall, soaking my hat, and I climbed the bare stairs to the second floor. Tapping on the second door, I heard a shrill, plaintive voice inside. "Oh, do come in."

I entered a room that smelled of jam, pickles, wine, biscuits, candles, soap, boots, and cigars—a desert island of clutter. Sitting under a window obscured by tissue paper was Sylvanus Minikin, a youth with a remarkably small body and a remarkably large head, so disproportionate that I suspected he could detach it with his hands. He had one glass eye, fixed and glassy, and one working eye, which he used to glare at me while writing.

"I mean," I explained, "are these the offices of Lott & Co.?"

"It's one of them," Minikin replied, polishing his missing eye. "The back one. If you're really anxious for a job, you can shut the door."

I closed the door, announced my name, and presented my appointment letter. Minikin read it, removed his glass eye again, and offered me blunt advice. "Hook it," he said. "Best employer in London. He'll keep you going for twenty-four hours a day, and then offer you overtime at half salary."

Before I could respond, the other door opened. A man entered, and the bell rang. Minikin vanished, leaving the connecting door ajar. I overheard a conversation that chilled me: a dispute over a bill, accusations of lying, and a mention of "Thorneycroft's man." Finally, the boss, Mr. Lott, summoned me in.

Mr. Lott was a small, dingy man with dirty hands and restless eyes. He plunged immediately into business, waving a bulky bundle of letters tied with red tape. "One hundred and seventeen answers to an advertisement, in one day!" he cried. "Poor devils, poor devils! Some of them will starve."

I explained my situation: alone, dependent on my own efforts, with a few pounds left. Lott, with a condescending pity, asked why I wanted the job. "Because it's the worst written of them all," he declared, pushing the letter back to me. "Awful, isn't it?"

Handwriting and spelling failed me. Lott noted my lack of business acumen. "I'm going to do you a good turn," he proclaimed, deciding to save me from destitution. "What salary do you want?"

I hesitated, fearing the Cheeryble Brothers were not real, and suggested twenty-five shillings. Lott screamed with horror. "Five-and-twenty shillings for writing like that! And can't spell commission! Twelve!"

"But I can't live on twelve," I protested.

"Don't know how to live!" Lott retorted. "Veal and ham pie, roley-poley, Dutch cheese... you overload your stomachs. Try vegetarianism. Oatmeal for breakfast, beans for dinner. You can live on sevenpence a day."

I countered, offering eighteen, then settling on fifteen as a compromise. Lott, indignant at the quibbling, agreed. "Splitting the difference," he said. "Come the following Monday."
