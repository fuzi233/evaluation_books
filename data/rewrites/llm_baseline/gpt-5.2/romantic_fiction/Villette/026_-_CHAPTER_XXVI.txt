After that day, my life asked no sharp changes. I went out often, and with Madame Beck’s full consent; she approved, as she approved all things that promised her house an added credit, of the particular circle that opened itself to me—the château, the great hotel, the Rue Crécy.

She was never fulsome. In all that was worldly she had a cool head and a close hand; her self-interest burned without smoke. She contrived to show satisfaction without stooping to flatter, and, by some neat economy of phrase and manner, made it understood that she preferred her teachers to be seen where manners were refined and tongues well taught, rather than where they might be dulled and lowered. Only once did she give me a little speech that had the air of kindness. She sat in winter sun in the garden, her cup of coffee breathing beside her—coffee, strong and bitter, with that dark perfume that warms even a stone bench; the Gazette lay open under her small, quick fingers. I came to ask leave for the evening.

“Oui, oui, ma bonne amie: je vous donne la permission de cœur et de gré. Votre travail dans ma maison a toujours été admirable, rempli de zèle et de discrétion: vous avez bien le droit de vous amuser. Sortez donc tant que vous voudrez. Quant à votre choix de connaissances, j’en suis contente; c’est sage, digne, laudable.”

She closed her lips again, and the Gazette rose between us like a screen.

It will not be thought too grave a matter—yet to me it struck like theft in a chapel—that about this time the triply enclosed packet of five letters vanished from my drawer. I felt blankly chilled when I found the place empty; then, as quickly, I forced warmth back into my heart.

“Patience,” I whispered. “Say nothing. Wait. They will return.”

They did return. They had only made a short visit to Madame’s chamber; having endured their examination, they came back—every sheet, every fold, every seal, outwardly safe—by the next day.

What did she make of my correspondence? What estimate did she form of Dr. John Bretton’s firm, fluent hand—so English in its plain strength; of his thoughts, pithy without pretence, genial without any studied gallantry; of that half-humorous current which, for me, carried a rare taste of home, like a mouthful of honest bread after foreign sweetness? And of the kind words—few, not scattered like jewels in a fable, but rarer still for their spareness—how did she read them? Oh, Madame Beck—what was this to you?

I think the five letters found some favour in her eyes. One day after she had—borrowed them (for with so smooth a little woman, one must use smooth terms)—I surprised her watching me with a steady, thoughtful look: puzzled, but not unkind. It was between lessons; the pupils were out in the court, shrilling and stamping their play into the cold air, a small human tumult that made the stillness indoors feel deeper by contrast. Madame Beck and I remained alone in the first class. When I met her gaze, her thought pressed out into speech.

“Il y a,” said she, “quelquechose de bien remarquable dans le caractère Anglais.”

“How, Madame?”

She gave a little laugh and repeated “how,” in English—mocking, but lightly.

“Je ne saurais vous dire ‘how;’ mais, enfin, les Anglais ont des idées à eux, en amitié, en amour, en tout. Mais au moins il n’est pas besoin de les surveiller,” she added; and she got up and trotted away, compact and brisk as a well-fed pony.

“Then I hope,” I murmured, “you will let my letters alone for the future.”

But even as I formed the wish, something rushed into my eyes and drowned the room—the school benches, the winter garden, the pale sun. Never again would letters like those come to me. I had seen the last. That goodly river, on whose banks I had sat so long in thirst, had bent away; it would water other fields. Right, just, natural—there was no word to be spoken against it. Yet I had loved my Rhine, my Nile; I had almost worshipped my Ganges; and I grieved that the tide should roll estranged and vanish, mirage-like, leaving my little hut and acre sand-dry.

I wept—one heavy, brief shower. Then I said to myself: “The hope I mourn made me suffer long. It died late. After a death so lingering, the end should be welcome.”

I strove to make it so. Long pain had made patience a habit. In the end I closed my dead hope’s eyes, covered its face, and laid its limbs straight with a calm that surprised me.

But the letters must be put away—out of sight. Those who have been bereaved hoard relics as if they were sharp instruments; they lock them up, because it is not bearable to be pierced at every hour by the mere glance of a familiar fold of paper.

One empty holiday afternoon—a Thursday—I went to my treasure, meaning to decide its final disposal, and perceived, this time with an impulse of real anger, that it had again been handled. The packet lay there; but the ribbon had been untied and retied, and the drawer itself wore the look of having been visited.

That was too much. That Madame Beck should know the contents of my casket was unpleasant, but, perhaps, endurable; she had discretion and sense, and could see a thing in a true light. But the fear that she had communicated what she learned; that she had amused herself with a companion over papers which, to me, were sacred—this shocked me cruelly. And I saw reason to dread it. I guessed her confidant.

Her kinsman, M. Paul Emanuel, had been with her the night before; she was in the habit of consulting him as she consulted no one else. That morning, in class, he had thrown me a look that seemed borrowed from Vashti—blue, yet lurid, like a flash in a storm cloud. I had not at the moment understood it; I read it now. He was not apt to judge what concerned me with tolerance. Severe, suspicious—so I had always found him. The thought that those letters—mere friendly letters, yet the last of their kind—had fallen once, and might fall again, into his hands, jarred my very soul.

Where, in this strange house, could secrecy be made secure? What corner existed where a key was not a mockery and a padlock a jest?

In the grenier? No. Boxes there mouldered; drawers did not lock; damp ate ink, and rats and mice made free with everything. I could see the vermin’s teeth on my dear papers, could smell the must of ruined cloth, could imagine the writing blurring into nothing under a slow wetness. The grenier would not do. But where, then?

While I pondered, I sat in the dormitory window-seat. The afternoon was frosty and fair; the winter sun, already sinking, made a pale gleam on the tops of shrubs in the allée défendue. At the end of that forbidden walk stood the old pear-tree—the nun’s pear-tree—gaunt, grey, and stripped, like some ancient dryad’s skeleton. A thought struck me—one of those odd, fantastic thoughts that visit solitary people, and seem for a moment as practical as a plan.

I put on bonnet, cloak, and furs, and went out into the city.

By instinct, in a melancholy mood, I bent my steps towards the oldest quarter, where streets lay hoar and over-shadowed, and sound itself seemed aged—the hollow knock of a distant shoe on stone, the faint clink of a shutter-chain, the murmur of voices softened by thick walls and narrow lanes. Crossing a half-deserted place, I stopped before a broker’s shop—ancient, crowded with ancient things: tarnished brass, dulled glass, rusty locks, and vessels whose former uses had died with the hands that made them.

What I wanted was a metal box that might be soldered, or a thick glass jar that could be sealed air-tight. Among heaps and corners I found the latter: a stout bottle, its glass heavy, its mouth wide enough to receive my rolled papers. I bought it.

At home I made a little roll of the letters, wrapped them in oiled silk, bound them with twine, and put them in the bottle. Then I returned to the old Jew broker and had him stopper it, seal it, make it hermetic. As he worked, he glanced at me now and then from under frost-white eyelashes—suspicious, as if he scented a hidden crime. Perhaps, to him, this business of sealing words away looked like the burial of evidence. To me it was not pleasure, but a bleak, lonely satisfaction. The mood that drove me had something of the same quality as that which had once driven me to the confessional: the need to hide, to shut, to be absolved by concealment.

I walked back quickly and reached the pensionnat at dark, in time for dinner. The smell of soup in the corridor, the clatter of plates, the small chatter of girls—those ordinary domestic sounds came to me with a strange double meaning: life, continuing, indifferent; and my own secret act, carried like a weight beneath my shawl.

At seven the moon rose. At half-past seven, while pupils and teachers studied, while Madame Beck sat with her mother and children in the salle-à-manger, while the half-boarders had gone, while Rosine had left the vestibule and all grew still, I wrapped myself in a shawl, took the sealed jar, and stole out through the first-class door into the berceau, and thence into the allée défendue.

Methusaleh, the pear-tree, stood at the far end near my seat, dim and grey above the lower shrubs. Old as he was, he was sound in timber; only at the root there was a hollow—deep, hidden by ivy and creepers. I knew it well. I meant to hide my treasure there. Yet I was not only hiding treasure; I was burying grief. The thing I had lately wept over—my hope, wrapped and laid out—must be interred.

I cleared away the ivy and found the hole—large enough. I thrust the jar deep in. Then, from the tool-shed at the bottom of the garden, where building materials lay left by masons, I fetched a slate and some mortar, set the slate over the hollow, secured it with cement, covered all with black mould, and replaced the ivy. The earth had a cold, clean smell as I pressed it down—a smell like winter itself, like the cellar where roots are stored for lean months. When it was done, I leaned against the tree and lingered, as mourners linger, beside a newly made grave.

The air was very still, yet dim with a peculiar mist that turned moonlight into luminous haze. There was something in it—electrical, perhaps—that acted strangely on me. I felt as I had once felt in England, a year before, on a night when the aurora borealis streamed and swept round heaven—when, belated in lonely fields, I had watched that mustering of an army with banners; that quivering of serried lances; that swift ascent of messengers from below the north star to the dark keystone of the sky. I was not happy—far from it—but I was strong, as if reinforced by some stern element.

If life be war, it seemed my destiny to conduct it alone. I pondered how to break up my winter-quarters—to leave an encampment where food and forage failed. Perhaps to effect the change another pitched battle must be fought with fortune. If so, I had mind for the encounter: too poor to lose, I might, by God’s will, be destined to gain. But what road was open? What plan possible?

While I paused on this, the moon, dim hitherto, seemed to shine clearer. A ray lay white before me; a shadow became distinct. I looked more narrowly; whiter and blacker it grew; it took shape with an instantaneous transformation. About three yards from me stood a tall woman, sable-robed and snowy-veiled.

Five minutes passed. I neither fled nor shrieked. She was there still. I spoke.

“Who are you? and why do you come to me?”

She stood mute. She had no face—no features; all below her brow was masked with a white cloth. But she had eyes, and they viewed me.

I was not brave—yet I was desperate, and desperation will often do the work of courage. I advanced one step and stretched out my hand; I meant to touch her. She seemed to recede. I drew nearer; her silent recession became swift. A mass of shrubs—laurel and dense yew—intervened. I passed the obstacle; I looked. There was nothing.

I waited. “If you have any errand to men,” I said, “come back and deliver it.” Nothing answered; nothing re-appeared.

This time there was no Dr. John to whom I could have recourse. There was no one to whom I dared whisper, “I have again seen the nun.”

Paulina Mary sought me often in the Rue Crécy. In the old Bretton days she had never professed fondness for me; yet my society became, to her, an unconscious necessity. If I withdrew to my room, she would soon come trotting after, open the door, peep in, and say with her little peremptory accent, “Come down. Why do you sit here by yourself? You must come into the parlour.”

In the same spirit she urged me now. “Leave the Rue Fossette,” she said, “and come and live with us. Papa would give you far more than Madame Beck gives you.”

Mr. Home himself offered me a handsome sum—thrice my present salary—if I would become companion to his daughter. I declined. I think I should have declined even had I been poorer, with fewer resources and a narrower prospect. I had not that vocation. I could teach; I could give lessons; but to be a private governess or a companion was unnatural to me. Rather than take the former post in any great house, I would deliberately have taken a housemaid’s place—bought strong gloves, swept bedrooms and staircases, cleaned stoves and locks—in peace and independence. Rather than be a companion, I would have made shirts and starved.

I was no bright lady’s shadow—not Miss de Bassompierre’s. Overcast I often was by nature; subdued I could be: but the dimness must be voluntary—such as kept me docile at my desk among my now familiar pupils in Madame Beck’s first class; or alone by my bed in her dormitory; or in the alley and seat called mine in her garden. My qualities were not convertible to ornament another’s splendour; they could not be made the foil of any gem, the appendage of any greatness in Christendom.

Madame Beck and I, without assimilating, understood each other well. I was not her companion, nor her children’s governess; she left me free. She tied me to nothing—not to herself, not even to her interests. Once, when she had been called away a fortnight by illness in her family, and returned anxious lest her establishment had suffered, she found matters proceeding much as usual. She made each teacher a present. To my bedside she came at midnight and told me she had no present for me.

“I must make fidelity advantageous to the St. Pierre,” said she; “if I attempt to make it advantageous to you, there will arise misunderstanding between us—perhaps separation. One thing, however, I can do to please you—leave you alone with your liberty: c’est-ce que je ferai.”

She kept her word. Every slight shackle she had ever laid on me she removed, quietly. Thus I took a pleasure in voluntarily respecting her rules; a gratification in devoting double time, taking double pains, with the pupils she gave to my charge.

As for Mary de Bassompierre, I visited her with pleasure, though I would not live with her. My visits soon taught me it was unlikely that even my occasional society would long remain indispensable to her. M. de Bassompierre, however, seemed blind to any such possibility—unconscious, like a child, of the fitful beginnings of what, at its end, he might not approve.

Whether he would approve, I used to speculate. He was much taken up with scientific pursuits—keen and combative when roused upon them, but trustful in ordinary affairs. He still regarded his “daughterling” as a child, spoke of what should be done when “Polly” was a woman, when she should be grown. “Polly,” standing by his chair, would sometimes smile, take his honoured head between her little hands, and kiss his iron-grey locks; at other times she would pout and toss her curls; but she never said, “Papa, I am grown.”

She wore different moods for different people. With her father she truly was child-like—affectionate, merry, playful. With me she was serious, womanly as thought and feeling could make her. With Mrs. Bretton she was docile and reliant, but not expansive. With Graham—shy; very shy. Sometimes she tried to be cold; sometimes to avoid him. His step made her start; his entrance hushed her; his questions tangled her answers. When he left, she remained vexed with herself, disconcerted.

Even her father noticed.

“My little Polly,” he said once, “you live too retired. If you grow to be a woman with these shy manners, you will hardly be fitted for society. You really make a stranger of Dr. Bretton. How is this? Don’t you remember that, as a little girl, you used to be rather partial to him?”

“Rather, papa,” she echoed, dryly yet gently.

“And you don’t like him now? What has he done?”

“Nothing. Y—e—s, I like him a little; but we are grown strange to each other.”

“Then rub it off, Polly; rub the rust and the strangeness off. Talk away when he is here. Have no fear of him.”

“He does not talk much. Is he afraid of me, do you think, papa?”

“Oh, to be sure. What man would not be afraid of such a little silent lady?”

“Then tell him some day not to mind my being silent. Say it is my way, and that I have no unfriendly intention.”

“Your way, you little chatter-box? So far from being your way, it is only your whim.”

“Well, I’ll improve, papa.”

And very pretty was the grace with which next day she tried to keep her word. I saw her make an effort to converse with Dr. John on general topics. The attention brought into his face a pleased glow; he met her with caution, answering in his softest tones, as if some gossamer happiness hung between them, and he feared to break it by breathing too boldly. In her timid, earnest advance to friendliness there was, indeed, an exquisite, fairy charm.

When the Doctor had gone, she came to her father’s chair.

“Did I keep my word, papa? Did I behave better?”

“My Polly behaved like a queen. I shall be proud of her if this continues. By-and-by we shall see her receiving my guests with quite a calm grand manner. Miss Lucy and I must polish our best airs, lest we be thrown into shade. Still, Polly—there is a little flutter, a little stammer now and then, and even—to lisp as you lisped when you were six.”

“No, papa,” she interrupted, indignant. “That can’t be true.”

“I appeal to Miss Lucy. Did she not, in answering Dr. Bretton’s question whether she had ever seen the palace of the Prince of Bois l’Etang, say ‘yeth’ she had been there ‘theveral’ times?”

“Papa, you are satirical—you are méchant! I can pronounce all the letters of the alphabet as clearly as you can. But tell me—since you are so particular that I must be civil to Dr. Bretton—do you like him yourself?”

“To be sure. For old acquaintance’ sake I like him; then he is a very good son to his mother; besides being a kind-hearted fellow and clever in his profession. Yes—the callant is well enough.”

“Callant! Ah, Scotchman! Papa, is it the Edinburgh or the Aberdeen accent you have?”

“Both, my pet, both. And doubtless the Glaswegian into the bargain. It is that enables me to speak French so well: a gude Scots tongue always succeeds well at the French.”

“The French! Scotch again—incorrigible papa. You, too, need schooling.”

“Well, Polly, you must persuade Miss Snowe to undertake both you and me—to make you steady and womanly, and me refined and classical.”

The light in which M. de Bassompierre regarded “Miss Snowe” gave me inward amusement. What contradictory attributes are ascribed to us, according to the eye that looks! Madame Beck esteemed me learned and blue; Miss Fanshawe—caustic, ironic, cynical; Mr. Home—a model teacher, sedate and discreet, perhaps too strict and scrupulous, the very pattern of governess-correctness; while another person—Professor Paul Emanuel—never lost a chance to hint that mine was rather a fiery and rash nature: adventurous, indocile, audacious. I smiled at them all. If any one knew me, it was little Paulina Mary.

Since I would not be her nominal paid companion, she persuaded me to join her in some study as a settled means of sustaining communication. She proposed German—a language that, like myself, she found hard to master. We agreed to take lessons in the Rue Crécy from the same mistress; thus we were thrown together some hours each week. M. de Bassompierre seemed pleased: it met his approbation that Madame Minerva Gravity should share a portion of her leisure with his fair dear child.
