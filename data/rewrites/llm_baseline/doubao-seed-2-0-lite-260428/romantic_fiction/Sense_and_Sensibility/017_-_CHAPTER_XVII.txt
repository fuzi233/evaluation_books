The moment Edward Ferrars stepped across the threshold of Barton Cottage, Mrs. Dashwood’s surprise lasted no more than a single breath. In her eyes, his coming to call on Elinor at their quiet home was the most natural thing in the world, and her face broke at once into a glow of unfeigned, delighted welcome that far outstripped any fleeting wonder. He had arrived weighed down by shyness, cold reserve, a stiff awkwardness that had clung to him all the ride from Exeter—but that thin armour could not stand against the Dashwoods’ warm, unforced affection. It had already begun to crack before he crossed the door, and Mrs. Dashwood’s soft, captivating manners swept the last of it away entirely. Any man half in love with one of her daughters could not help but grow fond of her too, and Elinor’s heart lifted to see him relax at last, looking more like the easy, gentle young man she knew so well. He leaned in to ask after their days of walking and sketching, praised the sunlit charm of their cottage, admired the rolling green view out the parlour window, was kind and attentive to every one of them—and yet, there was a heavy quiet over him. He was not in spirits. The whole family noticed it at once, and Mrs. Dashwood, at once convinced his mood came from his mother’s stubborn selfishness, sat down to dinner seething a little at all overbearing parents who tried to warp their children to fit their own ambitions.

When the plates were cleared and they all pulled their chairs close around the crackling parlour fire, she turned to him with a gentle tease. “What plans has Mrs. Ferrars laid out for you now, Edward? Are you still destined to be some great famous orator, no matter how little you want it?”

He huffed a quiet, tired laugh. “No. I hope my mother has finally accepted I have no more talent for public life than I have desire for it.”

“But then how will you ever earn the fame your whole family demands of you?” she pressed on. “You have no love of spending grand sums, no knack for charming strangers, no profession, none of that brash confidence that pushes men to the top—you will find it a very hard road to make a name for yourself that way.”

“I have no wish to try,” he said softly. “I do not care to be distinguished at all. Thank heaven, no one can force me to become a genius or a great speaker against my will.”

“You have no ambition, I know that well,” Mrs. Dashwood said. “Your wishes are all perfectly moderate.”

“I daresay they are no more moderate than anyone else’s,” he replied. “I want to be perfectly happy, same as every soul alive—but I have to find that happiness in my own way. All the greatness in the world will never give it to me.”

“How strange that anyone would think it could!” Marianne cried, leaning forward, her eyes bright. “What do wealth and grandeur have to do with true happiness, at all?”

“Grandeur very little, no,” Elinor said, “but wealth has quite a great deal to do with it.”

“Elinor, for shame!” Marianne shook her head. “Money only buys happiness when there is nothing else left to give you. Past the point of a comfortable competence, it cannot bring you any real satisfaction, not where your own peace of mind is concerned.”

“Ah, then I expect we actually agree,” Elinor said, smiling. “Your idea of a competence and my idea of what counts as necessary wealth are probably almost exactly the same. The way the world works now, we both know there are no nice little comforts to be had without that baseline, don’t we? Your ideas are just framed more nobly than mine. Come, tell me—what would that perfect competence of yours amount to, a year?”

“Eighteen hundred, or two thousand a year,” Marianne said, unhesitating. “No more than that.”

Elinor laughed out loud. “Two thousand a year? I would call one thousand a year perfect wealth! I knew exactly how that answer would go.”

“And two thousand a year is hardly extravagant,” Marianne protested. “You can hardly keep a household running on less. I am not being unreasonable at all. The proper number of servants, a carriage, maybe two, and a pair of hunters—you could not possibly afford all that on a smaller income.”

Elinor bit back another smile, recognizing that Marianne was already itemizing every comfort of the future home she imagined sharing with Willoughby at Combe Magna.

“Hunters?” Edward repeated, blinking a little. “But why must you have hunters? Not everyone hunts.”

Marianne flushed. “But most people do, around here,” she said.

“I wish,” Margaret cut in suddenly, struck by a bright new thought, “that someone would give each of us a huge fortune all our very own!”

“Oh, I wish that too!” Marianne cried, her cheeks glowing, her eyes sparkling at the mere thought of such unbridled joy.

“I think we are all agreed on that wish,” Elinor said, “no matter what we say about wealth not buying happiness.”

“Oh, dear!” Margaret sighed. “How happy I would be! I do not even know what I would spend all that money on!”

Marianne’s face lit up like she could see every purchase in her mind’s eye.

“I think I would be quite stuck trying to spend that much money, if all my girls were rich without ever needing a penny of mine to do it,” Mrs. Dashwood said.

“Easily solved,” Elinor told her. “You would immediately start all those little improvements you have been planning for the cottage for months. You will run out of money faster than you know it.”

“And what a flood of orders would go out from this house to London, if you all came into a fortune!” Edward laughed, the light coming back into his face for a moment. “What a glorious day for the booksellers, the music shops, the print sellers! You, Elinor, would commission every new fine print that came out to be sent straight to Barton. And Marianne? I know her soul well enough—there is not enough sheet music in all of London to satisfy her. And the books! Thomson, Cowper, Scott—she would buy every new edition, over and over, I swear she would buy up every last copy in the city just to keep them out of the hands of people who would not appreciate a single line. She would buy every book ever written about how to admire a fine old twisted tree on a country walk. Am I right, Marianne? Forgive me if I am being saucy, but I wanted to show you I never forgot all our old little debates.”

“I love to be reminded of the past, Edward,” Marianne told him warmly. “Happy or sad, I love to look back on it. You will never offend me by talking about old times. And you are quite right about how I would spend at least a little of that money—all my spare cash would go straight to new music and new books for my shelves.”

“And the rest of the fortune would go to buying annuities for your favourite poets and their families, I expect?” Edward teased.

“No, I would have far better uses for it than that,” she said.

“Then perhaps you would give it as a prize to whoever writes the best defence of your favourite maxim— that no one can ever fall in love more than once in their whole life? I assume you still hold to that opinion?”

“Of course I do,” Marianne said firmly. “At my age, my opinions are already quite settled. I do not expect to ever see or hear anything that could change my mind on that.”

“Marianne is as steadfast as ever, you see,” Elinor said. “She has not changed one bit.”

“Only grown a little more grave than she used to be,” Edward said.

“Now, Edward,” Marianne protested, “you have no right to tease me for that. You are hardly the giddiest, most lighthearted person in the room yourself.”

“Why would you think that?” he replied, and he sighed, soft and heavy. “But gaiety was never a part of my nature, I suppose.”

“Nor is it really part of Marianne’s, either,” Elinor put in. “I would not call her a frivolous, lively girl at all. She is earnest, full of fire for everything she cares about, talks a great deal sometimes, always with all her heart—but she is not often truly merry.”

“I believe you are right,” Edward said. “And yet, all this time I have always thought of her as that bright, bubbly lively girl.”

“I catch myself making that exact kind of mistake all the time,” Elinor said. “I misjudge people completely in some way or another— decide they are far more cheerful, or far more grave, far cleverer or far stupider than they actually are, and I can never work out why I got it so wrong. Sometimes I go by what people say about themselves, far more often I go by what everyone else says about them, and never stop to take the time to judge them for myself.”

“But surely it is right to let other people’s opinions guide you?” Marianne said. “I thought our judgment was given to us only to defer to the judgment of our neighbours. That has always been your rule, Elinor, has it not?”

“Never, Marianne,” Elinor said. “My rule was never meant to make you surrender your own good sense. All I ever tried to do was influence how you behave towards people. You must not twist my meaning. I will admit, I have often wished you would be more courteous to the people we know in general—but when have I ever told you to adopt their opinions, or fall in line with their judgments on things that matter deeply?”

“You have never managed to get your sister to go along with your plan of universal gentle civility, have you?” Edward asked Elinor. “Are you making no headway at all?”

“Quite the opposite,” Elinor said, glancing pointedly at Marianne, a little amused.

“My judgment is entirely on your side of the argument,” Edward told her. “But I am afraid my actions all fall on your sister’s side. I never wish to offend anyone, but I am so foolishly shy, that I often come off as cold and negligent, when I am only held back by my own natural awkwardness. I have thought more than once that nature must have meant me to prefer the company of ordinary, uneducated people, I am so ill at ease around genteel strangers.”

“Marianne never has shyness to excuse any inattention she shows,” Elinor said.

“She knows her own worth far too well to suffer from that false kind of shame,” Edward said. “Shyness is only the result of feeling you are inferior in some way or another. If I could convince myself my manners were perfectly easy and graceful, I would never be shy again.”

“But you would still be reserved,” Marianne said. “And that is far worse.”

Edward jolted like she had struck him, his face flushing bright crimson. “Reserved? Am I reserved, Marianne?”

“Yes, very much so.”

“I do not understand you,” he said, his voice tight. “Reserved? How? In what way? What am I not telling you? What do you imagine I am hiding?”

Elinor was surprised by how deeply he had taken the comment, and hurried to laugh the tension away. “Do you not know my sister well enough to tell what she means? She calls anyone reserved who does not talk as fast as she does, and gush over every pretty sight and poem with as much rapture as she feels herself.”

Edward did not answer. The heavy, quiet gravity that had hung over him earlier settled back over him, thicker than before. He sat silent, staring into the flames, dull and withdrawn, and no one could coax another light word out of him for the rest of the evening.
