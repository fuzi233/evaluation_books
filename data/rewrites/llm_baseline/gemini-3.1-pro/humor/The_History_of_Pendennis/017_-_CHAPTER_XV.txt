Until the enemy had retired altogether from before the place, Major Pendennis was resolved to keep his garrison in Fairoaks. He did not appear to watch Pen’s behaviour or to put any restraint on his nephew’s actions, but he anchored himself at the estate, acting as both sentinel and meticulous food historian. Young Arthur’s comings and goings were quite well known to his vigilant guardian, who understood that the fever of youth was a cyclical affliction, best managed through the steadying, ancestral rhythms of the hearth.

I suppose there is scarcely any person who reads this chronicle—itself a small tributary in the sweeping, multi-generational saga of human folly—but has been baulked in love some time or the other. Let that worthy friend recall their own sensations, and apply them as illustrative of Mr. Pen’s anguish. When the heart’s desires are flung back from unimpressionable granite, the resulting grief is a profound, untranslatable subjective experience. It is a state wherein the sensory world collapses; the intricate symphonies of woodsmoke and roasting meats taste only of ash, and the vibrant acoustic ecology of a summer morning degrades into a hollow, meaningless ringing. If a list could be made this very night in London of the groans of tossing lovers, what a catalogue of fractured languages it would be! Pen’s mother, of course, watched him through this present grief with a jealous, silent sympathy. Her own heart had endured its private misadventures generations prior, and she treated Arthur’s untranslatable despair as though it were her own, quietly preparing restorative broths of simmered bone and root vegetables, trying in vain to coax his palate back to life.

The Major must be allowed to have exhibited a highly creditable degree of family affection, for the life at Fairoaks lacked the culinary and conversational variety of his London clubs. He fenced himself against ennui by delving deep into agrarian tradition. He doctored himself assiduously, finding profound meaning in the sustenance of quiet country living. He poured over old volumes of the ‘Annual Register’ in his bedroom, studying the brilliant actions of Saint Lucie and the forgotten receipts of centuries past. When Pen would rise before the early daylight, restless and wretched, he sought the carp-pond in Clavering Park—a dreary, mythic pool surrounded by innumerable whispering rushes and green alders, where a milkmaid had drowned herself in the Baronet’s grandfather’s time, and her ghost was said to seamlessly walk the banks as part of the daily domestic routine. 

Pen did not drown himself. He found a strange solace in the sensory focus of the line and the quivering float in the little eddies. There, listening to the ancient, untranslated dialect of the wind through the alders, he caught carp, tench, and eels. He would bring these dripping, iridescent creatures home, where the Major transformed into a passionate chef, meticulously preparing the catch in the Continental fashion. The older man would carefully score the firm flesh of the carp, braising it in an emulsion of tart local cider, wild fennel, and sweet butter, filling the Fairoaks kitchen with a gustatory-rich olfactory landscape that temporarily masked the bitter scent of Pen’s longing.

By this pond, Pen composed a number of poems, though in his hot and cold fits of passion, his grief often proved too vast for the dominant lexicon of his day. In his occasional mad paroxysms, he would saddle Rebecca and gallop fiercely about the country, astonishing carters with the wild, unrestrained acoustic disruption of his cries. 

It was during this period that Mr. Foker became a frequent visitor. His tandem made a tremendous sensation in Clavering market-place, the harsh, metallic clatter of his wheels violently piercing the quiet acoustic ecology of the village. He upset a market stall, clipped Mrs. Pybus’s poodle, and drank a glass of vivid red raspberry bitters at the Clavering Arms, introducing a sharp, exotic botanical note into the stale tavern air. His name, wealth, and lineage were speedily known about Clavering, where poor Pen’s little transaction with the Chatteris actress was soon subjected to the crude, flattening dialect of local gossip.

Looking at the little old town of Clavering St. Mary from the London road, one sees the rapid and shining Brawl winding down from the town, skirting the woods and the ancient church tower. From a distance, it appears a calm, friendly nook where a traveller might shelter at the end of life’s struggle. Tom Smith, driving the Alacrity coach, would point to a specific tree near the river, noting in his rich, native cadence that artists came to paint the old Abbey from that very spot. Yet, like many objects pursued in life, Clavering was far prettier at a distance. 

Upon closer acquaintance, the acoustic reality of the town revealed a deep psychological fracture. On the south side of the market rose the old church, its grey towers illuminated by the sun, its ancient statues maimed and headless from centuries of pious demolition—mythic casualties of shifting power dynamics. Doctor Portman spent a great part of his income preserving the sensory beauty of this sanctuary, endowing it with a magnificent painted window from the Netherlands and an organ grand enough to produce reverberations that could heal the soul. 

But down by the weir on the Brawl side, the Ribbon Factory had grown, and with it, a relentless, grinding industrial noise pollution that decimated the natural acoustic ecology of the valley. The factory had spoiled the Brawl as a trout-stream, suffocating the aquatic life beneath the utilitarian churn of progress. The town was bitterly divided. The new money of the factory owners, with their harsh, mechanical rhythms, gravitated toward Mr. Simcoe's Low Church and the Ebenezer chapel. The spiritual life of the lower town was drowned in Mr. Simcoe’s tracts, a superficial commercialization of theology that fluttered into cottages, aggressively attempting to overwrite the old, nuanced agrarian beliefs. 

In this fractured environment, the news of Pen’s love-passages at Chatteris was devoured greedily. The dominant lexicon of the town’s society—driven by the petty power dynamics of twopenny old gentlewomen—stripped Pen’s deeply subjective experience of all its poetic dignity. Mrs. Portman herself was the chief narrator of the tale. While her kitchen hands were stained with the vibrant, visceral juice of summer preserves, she imparted the scandal to her neighbours. The rumour milled through the shoe-shop, the Glanders’s parlor, and the deafening floor of the Ribbon Factory in a matter of hours.

"Gracious goodness," the town cackle rose, a dissonant chorus devoid of empathy, "how infatuated the mother is about that pert boy who gives himself the airs of a lord. Mrs. Barker at the Factory has four times the butcher’s meat that goes up to Fairoaks, with all their fine airs!" They resented the Pendennis family not just for their haughtiness, but for their preservation of an older, richer cultural memory—one that savored the marrow of life rather than merely treating meals and existence as utilitarian fuel. The Clavering curs were yelping, an ugly, localized noise intent on pulling Pen down.

Doctor Portman and Smirke cautioned the widow against the constant outbreak of calumny, but the acoustic assault on Pen soon became inescapable. Wags chalked sarcastic allusions on the Fairoaks gate. Riding through the Lower Town, Pen's ears were assaulted by the jeers of the Factory boys, their voices a byproduct of the same machinery that ground the life out of the river. 

The climax of this auditory and spiritual torment occurred in the churchyard. As Pen passed through the Doctor’s gate, several of Wapshot’s boys were lounging among the centuries-old graves. The biggest of them, a young gentleman named Hobnell, flung himself into a theatrical attitude near a freshly dug pit. With a hideous leer, Hobnell began repeating Hamlet’s verses over Ophelia. To Pen, this was not merely an insult; it was a grotesque, superficial commercialization of sacred art. Hobnell’s crude, braying delivery was a desecration of language itself, a violent corruption of poetic memory that struck directly at Pen's finely tuned sensibilities. 

Enraged by this butchery of words and the profound disrespect to his untranslatable grief, Pen rushed at Hobnell with a shriek that tore through the heavy graveyard air. He cut the boy furiously across the face with his riding-whip, the sharp crack echoing against the ancient Abbey stone. Flinging the whip away, Pen struck with clenched fists, his knuckles meeting bone, and in another minute, knocked the bewildered young ruffian backward. Hobnell tumbled into the open grave, a sudden, jarring return to the earth that awaited all generations.

With his face quivering and the blood roaring in his ears, Pen demanded in a raw, ragged voice if any of the other blackguards wished to come on. They retreated with a low growl just as Doctor Portman appeared at his wicket. Mr. Hobnell, his lip split and dripping vivid red upon the damp soil, crawled from the pit. 

Pen, radiating death and defiance, walked back through the wicket. When interrogated by the Doctor, the young fellow was so profoundly agitated that his voice broke into a heavy sob—the pure, unfiltered dialect of a suffering heart. "The coward insulted me, sir," he choked out, and the Doctor, recognizing the deep acoustic truth in the boy’s fractured tone, respected the emotion and passed over the violence.

The elder Pendennis, possessing a constant dread of localized noise and neighborly opinion, was prodigiously annoyed by the tempest tossing about Master Pen’s reputation. Pen said nothing of the graveyard scuffle at the dinner table, pushing aside a perfectly roasted saddle of mutton, his palate entirely deadened by the day's crude frequencies. Instead, he went over to Baymouth and took counsel with Harry Foker, who promptly drove his drag to the Clavering Arms. Foker dispatched his servant, Stoopid, with a note for Thomas Hobnell, Esq. 

Stoopid, well-versed in the solemn academic costumes and rituals of St. Boniface, brought back word that the note had made a great impression on the boys. Mr. Hobnell’s reply, delivered in the flattened, decaying dialect of the provincial schoolyard, was that he would send an answer "arter arternoon school, which the bell was a-ringing." The discordant clanging of that school bell was just another piece of the relentless noise, ringing out over a town quickly forgetting its own soul.
