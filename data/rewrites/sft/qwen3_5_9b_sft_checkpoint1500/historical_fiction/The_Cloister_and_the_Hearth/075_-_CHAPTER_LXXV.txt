Brother Clement exhaled a long, weary breath, a sound that betrayed a flicker of doubt regarding the path he had chosen with a soul so fiercely impassioned. Yet, in the quiet corridors of ecclesiastical wisdom, youth had already begun its steep education. He recalled the ancient precept: when faced with tangled difficulties, silence was the vessel that allowed truth to settle before action could be taken. To speak or act rashly was to risk shipwreck. Thus, Clement chose stillness. He withdrew his foot gently from beneath the heavy embrace of the kneeling woman, offering instead a silent prayer for illumination.

The penitent, however, reacted with a convulsive intensity. Her arms flew around his withdrawn limb, clutching it with desperate force, and a piteous cry tore from her throat. She wept openly over the bare skin, her body trembling with the dual agony of profound shame and burning contrition. A flush of crimson, stark against her pale complexion, raced up her neck, marking the heat of her humiliation.

"My daughter," Clement spoke softly, his voice steady despite the storm before him. "Take courage. Stop tormenting yourself over Gerard, who, in the sight of God, does not exist." He paused, allowing the weight of his identity to anchor the moment. "I am Brother Clement, whom Heaven has sent to comfort thee and aid thee in saving thy soul. Since thou hast made me thy confessor, I claim thy obedience."

"Oh, yes," she sobbed, the waterworks of grief momentarily stalling under the authority of his gaze.

"Leave this pilgrimage immediately and return to Rome," Clement instructed, his tone shifting from gentle to firm. "Penitence sought abroad holds little value. Wherever we dwell lies the battlefield of temptation; we must defeat it there or perish, rather than fleeing to escape to other, perhaps more gilded, arenas. It is easy to wash the feet of strangers masked in anonymity; far harder to remain meek and charitable toward those who surround us."

"I promise, I will never touch her again!" she gasped.

"Nay," Clement corrected gently. "I speak not of servants alone, but of dependents, kin, and friends. This is thy penance: at the last hour of the night and the first after matins, recall thy sin and God's boundless goodness. Let humility and gentleness govern thy dealings with human frailty. The world courteth the rich; seek thou the poor—not the beggar, for he is often neither honest nor truly destitute—but those who blush to approach thee, yet stand in direst need. To give unto them is to lend unto Heaven. And further, thou shalt wed a good son of the Church."

"Me?" she whispered, horror dawning in her eyes. "I will never marry."

"Thou shalt marry within the year," Clement declared, his voice resonating with spiritual gravity. "I entreat and command thee to choose one who feareth God. For thou art but clay; mated ill, thou shalt become naught. But wedded to a worthy husband, by the grace of God, thou mayest live as a pious princess and die a saint."

"I?" she stammered, unable to comprehend the transformation.

"Thou."

With these final instructions laid down, Clement bid her rise to perform the good works he had assigned. She knelt, removed her mask, and cast upon him a look fraught with unspoken emotion, before lowering her eyes in submissive reverence.

"I will obey thee as I would an angel," she whispered, her voice thick with tears. "How happy I am, yet how wretched; for oh, my heart whispers that I shall never behold thy face again. I cannot depart until I have dried thy feet."

"It needs not," Clement replied, shaking his head. "I have absolved thee of this bootless penance."

"'Tis no penance to me," she insisted, her hands hovering near him. "Ah! If thou dost not grant me leave to dry thy poor feet, then thou forgivest me not."

"So be it then," Clement said, resigning himself with a soft sigh. *Femina leviora sunt,* he thought internally, *lighter things.* Yet, even these fragile vessels, drawn inevitably toward the smallest earthly anchors as celestial bodies turn to the massive spheres, possess flashes of angelic intelligence.

Having finally dried the friar's feet, the princess gazed up at him through tear-filled eyes, murmuring with a singular tenderness that seemed to transcend her station. "Shall I have masses said for her soul? May I?" she asked timidly.

A faint blush colored Clement's cheeks, and his cold blue eyes softened. Such devotion from one he had just judged so lowly struck him with unexpected force. "It is a gracious thought," he admitted. "Do as thou wilt; often such acts fall back upon the doer like blessed dew. Remember, I am thy confessor, not hers; it is thy soul I must now strive to save, lest woe befall mine own. My daughter, my dearest child, I see both good and ill angels battling for thy soul this day, right now. Fight for thy own side. Dost thou remember all I bade thee?"

"Remember!" she exclaimed, her voice ringing true. "Sweet Saint, every syllable of thine is carved into my heart."

"But one word more," Clement urged. "Pray much to Christ, and pray little to His saints."

"I will."

"And that is the finest counsel I have to offer thee. With that, we part. Go to the place where thou art most needed—thy father's house—and I return to my Holy Mother's work."

"Adieu," she faltered, her voice breaking. "Adieu, thou whom I have loved too deeply, hated too poorly, known and revered only too late. Forgiving Angel, adieu—for ever."

The monk caught the word, though it lingered only in a heavy sigh. "For ever?" he cried aloud, his voice suddenly ardent, glowing with a sacred fire. "Christians live 'for ever,' and love 'for ever,' but they never part 'for ever.' They separate, as earth and sun do, only to meet more brightly in a little while. You and I part here for life, but what is life? It is but a single line in the grand narrative of the Church, a handful in time's vast sands, a drop in the ocean of eternity. Adieu—for the fleeting moment called 'life.' We part in trouble, we shall meet in peace; we part as creatures of clay, we shall meet as immortal spirits; we part in a world of sin and sorrow, we shall meet where all is purity and divine love, where no ill passions exist, but only Christ and His saints arrayed in white. There, in the turning of an hourglass, in the shattering of a bubble, in the drifting of a cloud, she, thou, and I shall meet again. And we shall sit at the feet of angels, archangels, apostles, and saints, beaming with joy unspeakable in the shadow of God upon His throne, FOR EVER—AND EVER—AND EVER."

Thus they parted. The monk stood erect, his eyes turned heavenward, glowing with the fervor of zeal; the princess retreated slowly, turning repeatedly to cast lingering glances of awe and tender regret upon that inspired figure. She departed home subdued and purified. Clement, in due course, arrived in Basel and immersed himself in his duties, teaching in the university and preaching throughout the town and its environs. His life became a study in two words: deep scholarship and severe mortification. The brethren of his order soon began to perceive in him a descendant of the saints and martyrs, a man whose spirit burned with a light that illuminated the darkest corners of the cloister.
