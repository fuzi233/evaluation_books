On a bright August morning, Harry Feversham rode across the Lennon bridge into Ramelton. The suns of the Sudan had bronzed his skin, and the years of trial had etched their marks upon him. He moved through the narrow streets unremarked, a stranger in his own past. At the crest of the hill, he turned onto the broad highway that wound through valleys and over ridges toward Letterkenny. He rode swiftly, yet not alone—accompanied by ghosts: echoes of people he once knew, memories of laughter and silence, words spoken long ago but still echoing in his mind.

The years between then and now had slipped away during his journey across Egypt and Europe. They were no longer real, only shadows. He did not dwell on what might have been, nor on the weight of his failure. What mattered was what had been—this quiet Irish town where joy and sorrow had both lived in him. Here, he had known happiness; here, he had suffered his deepest pain. Here, his presence had brought comfort—and harm.

He paused briefly opposite the church, perched high above the road. A sudden question stirred within him: Was Ethne still here? Was old Dermod alive? What would she say to see him again?

But then he came back to himself—sitting on his horse, the morning still and golden. Larks sang overhead, a landrail called from the meadow, and a rooster crowed from the valley below. He looked around, took a breath, and urged his horse forward.

Again, he rode with phantoms—the faces of friends, lovers, enemies, all walking beside him in memory. He passed a grove of trees, a broken fence, an open gate. Without hesitation, he turned in, riding down a weed-choked drive that led to a ruined house. The crumbling walls and roofless rooms jolted him from his reverie.

He dismounted, tied his horse to a tree, and hurried inside. “Ethne!” he called. No answer. Room after room stood empty. He descended into the garden, but found no one. The overgrown lawn, the tangled flowerbeds—everything spoke of abandonment. He mounted again and rode back at a brisk pace.

In Ramelton, he stopped at the inn, handed his horse to the ostler, and ordered lunch. As the landlady served him, he asked quietly, “So Lennon House burned down?”

“Five years ago,” she replied. “This very summer.” And without being prompted, she launched into a detailed tale: the fire, the fall of the Eustace family, Bastable’s drunkenness, Dermod’s death at Glenalla. “But we hope it’ll be rebuilt,” she added. “They say Miss Eustace will marry soon—then the house will rise again.”

Feversham said nothing.

“And where does Miss Eustace live now?” he asked.

“At Glenalla,” she answered. “Halfway to Rathmullen, there’s a track leading up to your left. It’s a poor mountain village—not fit for her at all. You’d like to see her?”

“Yes,” he said. “Please have my horse brought round.”

As he waited, he glanced through the hotel register and saw, under late July, the name: *Colonel Trench*.

“You’ll come back tonight, sir?” the landlady asked as he mounted.

“No,” he replied. “I don’t think I’ll return to Ramelton.”

He rode down the hill, crossed the Lennon bridge once more, and continued four miles until he reached the track near a small bay of the lake. Turning in, he passed a few white cottages and climbed into the purple hills.

It was about five o’clock when he arrived at the scattered village of Glenalla. The place seemed quiet, unplanned—cottages huddled together, then fields, then a stand of larches and a lone house beyond. After that, another gap revealed the glimmering water of the lough, gulls wheeling above it. Then, ahead, a small grey church stood bare on its rocky plateau.

A path of white shell-dust led from the church door to a wooden gate. As he approached, a collie dog barked from behind it.

He stopped. The dog was old—its muzzle grey, its eyes sharp. He recognized its markings. He looked toward the church. The door stood open.

He dismounted, tied his horse, and stepped into the churchyard. The dog sniffed at his knee, then erupted into joyful chaos—barking, leaping, whirling, screaming with delight. It ran to the door, barked fiercely, then dashed back to him, jumping up with wagging tail.

Feversham caught it gently, patted it, and laughed.

Then he froze.

At the church doorway stood Ethne Eustace.

He set the dog down and walked slowly along the path. She stood motionless, silent, watching him. Not a word passed between them until he drew near.

“Harry,” she said.

Just one word. But it shattered every ghost that had haunted him all day. Six years had passed since he last stood beneath her window on a June dawn. Now they faced each other, seeing changes invisible to others.

Feversham was thinner, worn—his eyes sunken, his face aged beyond his years. The hardships of the House of Stone had left their mark. Yet Ethne saw more than just weariness. She saw strength. The timidity that once clung to him—the fear of judgment—was gone. In its place was calm self-assurance. He had endured a test, and he knew he had passed.

She felt a surge of sorrow for the suffering he had borne—but also something deeper. She saw the man he had become. And though she stood still, her heart stirred with pride.

“It is not all harm,” she said softly. “These years were not wasted.”

He thought of her solitude in this remote village—of how she had chosen this life, even if he didn’t know it. He looked into her face. Her eyes were still clear, her cheeks still flushed with color. But something had changed. There was depth now—character shaped by hardship, wisdom earned through loss.

“I am sorry,” he said. “I wronged you six years ago. I needn’t have.”

She held out her hand. “Will you give it to me, please?”

He hesitated. “What?”

“That fourth feather,” she said.

He reached into his coat, pulled out his letter-case, and shook two feathers into his palm. One was large—an ostrich feather. He offered it to her.

“Both,” she said.

He hesitated no longer. He gave her both feathers. She clasped them tightly, pressing them against her chest with a quiet smile.

“I have all four now,” she said.

“Yes,” he replied. “All four. What will you do with them?”

She laughed—a light, defiant sound. “Do with them? Nothing. I’ll keep them. I’m proud to hold them.”

She kept them—as she once kept his portrait. Perhaps Durrance was right: women remember, cherish, build their lives from what has passed. Men let go. Feversham would have thrown the feathers away, crushed them underfoot. They had served their purpose. They could no longer shame or inspire. They were dead.

But Ethne held them close. To her, they were alive.

“Colonel Trench was here two weeks ago,” she said. “He told me you were coming.”

“He didn’t know about the fourth feather,” Feversham said. “I never told anyone.”

“Yes,” she replied. “You told Colonel Trench on your first night in the House of Stone. He told me. And I no longer hate him.”

She said it plainly, seriously—as if it were a truth worth stating.

“I’m glad,” Feversham said. “He’s a great friend.”

She was silent for a moment. Then:

“I wonder if you’ve forgotten our ride from Ramelton to the house—when I came to fetch you from the quay? We were alone in the dog-cart. We talked—”

“Of friends whom one knows instantly,” he interrupted. “People one recognizes, even if never seen before.”

“Indeed,” she said. “And whom one never loses—whether absent or dead.”

“You said one could always trust such friends,” he continued.

“And you said one could make mistakes,” she finished.

“I did,” he admitted.

“But I disagreed,” she said. “One may seem to err, and believe so for a time. But in the end, the mistake is not real. I’ve thought of those words often. When Captain Willoughby brought me the first feather—I remembered them, full of remorse. And now… I remember them again. Though I have no room for regret. I was right. I should have held fast to my belief. But I didn’t.”

Her voice trembled slightly. “I was young. I knew little. I judged too quickly. But now I understand.”

She opened her hand and looked at the white feathers. Then she turned and walked into the church.

Feversham followed.
