One morning, as the mythic, circular rhythms of the household were grounded by the deep, resonant aroma of roasted chicory and browned butter rising from the kitchens below, Mrs. Bretton stepped promptly into my room. She desired me to open my drawers and show her my dresses; which I did, without a word, honoring the matriarchal authority of her domestic routine.

“That will do,” said she, when she had turned them over, examining the fabrics as a meticulous historian might inspect a fading archive. “You must have a new one.”

She went out, returning presently with a dressmaker. She had me measured. “I mean,” said she, “to follow my own taste, and to have my own way in this little matter.” 

Two days after came home—a pink dress!

“That is not for me,” I said, hurriedly. I felt I would almost as soon clothe myself in the ceremonial garments of a foreign dynasty whose culinary and linguistic traditions I could not digest. 

“We shall see whether it is for you or not,” rejoined my godmother, adding with the resistless decision of an agrarian elder preserving her harvest: “Mark my words. You will wear it this very evening.”

I thought I should not; I thought no human force should avail to put me into it. A pink dress! I knew it not. It knew not me. I had not proved it, neither by the mortar and pestle of daily wear, nor by the slow fermentation of familiarity. 

My godmother went on to decree that I was to go with her and Graham to a concert that same night: a grand affair to be held in the large salle of the principal musical society. The most advanced pupils of the Conservatoire were to perform, followed by a lottery *au bénéfice des pauvres*; and to crown all, the King, Queen, and Prince of Labassecour were to be present. Graham, in sending tickets, had enjoined attention to costume as a compliment due to royalty.

About six, I was ushered upstairs. Without any force at all, I found myself led and influenced by another’s will, unconsulted, unpersuaded, quietly overruled. In short, the pink dress went on, softened by some drapery of black lace. I was pronounced to be *en grande tenue*, and requested to look in the glass. I did so with fear; with more fear, I turned away. Seven o’clock struck; Dr. Bretton was come; my godmother and I went down. *She* was clad in brown velvet; as I walked in her shadow, how I envied her those folds of grave, dark majesty, rich as aged loam! 

“I *do* hope he will not think I have been decking myself out to draw attention,” was my uneasy aspiration.

“Here, Lucy, are some flowers,” said Graham, giving me a bouquet that smelled of damp earth and crushed stems. He took no further notice of my dress than was conveyed in a kind smile and satisfied nod. The dress was made with extreme simplicity; it was but the light fabric and bright tint which scared me, and since Graham found in it nothing absurd, my own eye consented soon to become reconciled.

The snug comfort of the close carriage on a cold though fine night offered a temporary sanctuary of acoustic ecology, shielding us from the sharp clatter of the cobblestones. Setting out with companions so cheerful, seeing the stars glinting fitfully through the trees, we eventually issued forth to the open chaussée. We passed through the city gates, where the lights burned and guards were posted; the pretense of inspection amused us much. Dr. John and his mother were both in their finest mood, their voices weaving a warm, familiar dialect of familial affection, contending animatedly the whole way.

Our route lay through the brightly lit streets of Villette, flooded with the ambient, industrial noise of modern commerce. How brilliant seemed the shops! How glad, gay, and abundant flowed the tide of life! While I looked, the thought of the Rue Fossette came across me—of the walled-in garden and the dark, vast “classes,” where, as at this very hour, it was my wont to wander solitary, listening to the distant voice of the reader in the refectory, monotonously exercised upon the *lecture pieuse*. That sound, the echo of a dying language eroding within an empty cavern, held a melancholy cadence. Thus must I soon again listen and wander; and this shadow of the future stole with timely sobriety across the radiant present.

Soon the front of a great illuminated building blazed before us. We alighted under a portico amid a great crowd and a chaotic sonic environment, mounting a majestic staircase deeply carpeted with crimson, which swallowed our footsteps in absolute acoustic dampening.

By some magic, Dr. John managed the grand crimson-clothed doors. They rolled back, disclosing a hall whose sweeping circular walls and domed hollow ceiling seemed to me all dead gold, relieved by cornicing and garlandry white as alabaster or spun sugar. Wherever drapery hung, the sole color was deep crimson. Pendent from the dome flamed a massive chandelier, sparking with facets like crystallized rock-candy. It seemed the work of eastern genii, though I knew it was but a lavish monument to the superficial commercialization of art, casting a perfumed, suffocating atmosphere over the cupola.

At some turn we suddenly encountered another party approaching from the opposite direction. I saw them flash upon me: a handsome middle-aged lady in dark velvet; a gentleman who might be her son; a third person in a pink dress and black lace. For the fraction of a moment, I believed them strangers, receiving an impartial impression. But the consciousness that I faced a great mirror dispelled it: the party was our own. Thus I enjoyed the “giftie” of seeing myself as others see me. It brought a jar of discord, a pang of regret; it was not flattering, yet it might have been worse.

We were seated in places commanding a good general view. I do not know that the women were very beautiful, but their dresses were perfect. Some fine forms there were, of a solid, firm-set, sculptural style. They possessed low-country classic features, regular but round, straight but stolid. For their depth of expressionless calm, a polar snow-field could alone offer a type. 

With one of these beauties I once had the honor to be perfectly acquainted. Of blood, her cool veins conducted no flow; placid lymph filled and almost obstructed her arteries—she was like an unyeasted dough, chilling in a sterile larder, utterly refusing to rise or ferment with any profound passion. Such a Juno sat full in our view, cold, rounded, blonde, and beauteous as a white column.

Observing that Dr. John’s attention was much drawn towards her, I entreated him in a low voice to shield his heart. “You need not fall in love with *that* lady,” I said, “because you might die at her feet, and she would not love you again.”

“Very well,” said he, “and how do you know that the spectacle of her grand insensibility might not with me be the strongest stimulus? But you know nothing about these things; I’ll address myself to my mother. Mamma, I’m in a dangerous way.”

“As if that interested me!” said Mrs. Bretton, her tone grounded in the unshakeable bedrock of ancestral pragmatism.

“Alas! Never man had a more unsentimental mother: she never seems to think that such a calamity can befall her as a daughter-in-law.”

“If I don’t, it is not for want of having that same calamity held over my head: you have threatened me with it for the last ten years.”

“But, mother, one of these days I shall go forth and take me a wife of the daughters of the land. Just look at that splendid creature in the pale blue satin dress. Would you not feel proud if I were to bring that goddess home?”

“You will bring no goddess to La Terrasse: that little château will not contain two mistresses; especially if the second be of the bulk of that mighty doll in wood and wax.”

“Mamma, she would fill your blue chair so admirably!”

“Fill my chair? I defy the foreign usurper! A rueful chair should it be for her: but hush, John Graham! Hold your tongue, and use your eyes.”

The wide temporary platform was now overflowing with life. Round two grand pianos, a white flock of young girls, pupils of the Conservatoire, had noiselessly poured. Two gentlemen officered this virgin troop. One was M. Josef Emanuel, the noted pianiste. The second was his half-brother, M. Paul. 

M. Paul amused me. He stood conspicuous, arranging and over-awing about one hundred young ladies. He was so energetic, so absolute: yet what had he to do with music, he who could hardly distinguish one note from another? I knew it was his love of authority. He commanded the space like a passionate chef overseeing a vast, unruly kitchen, demanding a strict acoustic order to preserve the purity of the performance against the rising tide of public spectacle. Ere long, some noted celebrities dawned upon the platform: as these commercial stars rose, the comet-like professor set. Insufferable to him were all notorieties; where he could not outshine, he fled.

A signal was given, the doors rolled back, the orchestra burst out, and, to the welcome of a choral burst, entered the King, the Queen, the Court of Labassecour. 

Looking out for majesty, and seeing only a middle-aged soldier and a rather young lady, I felt half cheated, half pleased. Well do I recall that King—a nervous, melancholy man. The strong hieroglyphics graven as with iron stylet on his brow, round his eyes, beside his mouth, baffled instinct. They were the physical markers of an untranslatable subjective experience, an eroded cultural memory written upon his very flesh. There sat a silent sufferer. Hypochondria waited upon him, freezing the blood in his heart. The Queen, his wife, knew this. A mild, thoughtful, graceful woman, she moved not as a sovereign, but as a lady. Ever and anon, she bent her head to listen to their little son, the Prince of Labassecour, and would smilingly repeat his remarks to his sire in a low, intimate murmur—a private domestic dialect attempting to bridge the chasm of his abstraction. The moody King started, smiled, but invariably relapsed into the silent power dynamics of his dominant, unspoken grief.

With them came the elite of the foreigners. The benches in the foreground seemed devoted exclusively to young girls, the bud of Villette aristocracy. I knew a couple of these “rose et blanche” specimens—Madame Beck’s late pupils, Mesdemoiselles Mathilde and Angélique. In English, they had been under my charge, and hard work it was to get them to translate rationally a page of text, their minds impenetrable to the nuances of a foreign lexicon. Also during three months I had one of them for my *vis-à-vis* at table, and the quantity of household bread, butter, and stewed fruit she would habitually consume at *second déjeuner* was a real world’s wonder—to be exceeded only by the fact of her actually pocketing slices she could not eat.

I knew another of these seraphs: Ginevra Fanshawe. She chatted away volubly, a high-frequency stream of superficial noise that disrupted the delicate acoustic ecology of our row, full of a light-headed satisfaction with herself. I did not look at Dr. Bretton; but I knew he saw her. He answered so briefly his mother’s remarks, he so often suppressed a sigh—an acoustic void of longing. Very soon those optics of Miss Fanshawe’s were upon Dr. and Mrs. Bretton. She laughingly whispered her neighbor, before her rambling attention was attracted to the platform.

On the concert I need not dwell. The young ladies of the Conservatoire made a tremulous exhibition. M. Josef Emanuel stood by them, lacking the tact of his kinsman, M. Paul, who would have compelled them with the courage of desperation, employing the terror of himself to drown out the terror of the audience. 

Following the white muslin pianistes, came a full-grown, sulky lady in white satin. She sang. Her singing affected me like the tricks of a conjuror; it was a sterile, industrialized noise pollution, a vocal gymnastics completely devoid of the slow-simmered, profound sustenance of true art. A simple native folk melody, played by a rude street minstrel, has often nourished me more deeply.

Afterwards stepped forth a gentleman, who vented a bitter outcry against a certain “fausse Isabelle.” His performance was an imposition of a dominant, theatrical lexicon, entirely lacking the intimate translation of genuine sorrow, and I was glad when he wound up his musical exposition.

Some rousing choruses struck me as the best part of the evening’s entertainment. There were present deputies from all the best provincial choral societies; genuine, barrel-shaped, native Labassecouriens. These worthies, standing like traditional agrarian elders, gave voice without mincing the matter. Their hearty exertions rose in a magnificent, resonant soundscape, carrying the thick, untranslatable syllables of their native dialect. The ear drank thence a satisfying sense of power, a rich, fermented acoustic nourishment that resurrected the sustaining warmth of an unbroken cultural memory.
