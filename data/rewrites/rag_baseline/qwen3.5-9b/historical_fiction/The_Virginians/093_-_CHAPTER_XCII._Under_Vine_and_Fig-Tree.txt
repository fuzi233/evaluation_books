The air inside the house held the weight of a resolved silence. We had returned, the brood complete, and the mother's face bore the inscription of a joy that required no words, only the acknowledgment of her knees. Yet, the household expanded beyond its original bounds. Aunt Hetty refused the intimacy of our larger home, citing a pang too sharp to swallow: the surrender of her eldest children, who had belonged to her for three years, demanded a separation. She withdrew to Bury St. Edmunds, a nearby village, where she cultivated a private paradise for her oldest nephew and niece.

This retreat coincided with the death of Mr. B, the Jamaican planter, who left Hetty half his fortune. Only then did the narrative reveal the depth of his affection—a devotion forged in Jamaica, rejected by her, yet proven by his final gift. Meanwhile, the erosion of Hetty's independence continued, consumed by Monsieur Miles. The cost of his commission, his uniforms, his debts in the Minories, and his frivolities at the Cocoa-Tree drained her resources. Five hundred pounds a year could not sustain the architecture of his vanity. Hetty, resigned to the role of spinster, declared a vow: she would remain unmarried until she found a man equal to her father. She had found none. Sir George, she noted with a shrug, was not half as good.

Yet, when the General finally died—calm, aged, and ready to depart—the grief that settled the room belonged to my wife. She wept openly, confessing a terror that she had not loved him sufficiently. Hetty, conversely, maintained a stoic exterior, though her internal landscape shifted. She spoke of him constantly, recalling his mirth and tenderness, her face brightening into the guise of a girl again. In church, she gazed at the slab recording his virtues, telling no lies, sitting quietly amidst the memories.

I had suspected that Hal, the object of Hetty's youthful passion, still held a tether to her heart. Ten years prior, when he visited, I hoped he might propose to replace Mrs. Fanny, a loss that haunted my wife, who viewed her own lack of regret as a sin. Hal arrived submerged in sorrow, declaring no woman existed like her. The General, still living then, welcomed him as he had in his youth. The women played the same tunes from Oakhurst; hearts softened by nostalgia. Hal poured out his griefs to Hetty, recounting his wife's virtues and praising his beloved Aunt Lambert, whose eulogies were music to the faithful old husband, whose thoughts of his wife seemed never to pause for three waking minutes.

Later, General Hal traveled to Paris as an American officer in the fashionable blue and yellow, receiving attention at Versailles despite the Marquis de Lafayette's inability to secure the favor of the King and Queen. A Marquise took a liking to the Virginian General, offering marriage, but he resisted, fleeing back to England and Warrington. There, among listeners patient with his tales of his late wife, he found refuge.

We, the survivors, treated him with civility but lacked the enthusiasm of strangers, often stumbling when answering our children's questions about Uncle Hal's angel wife. The circle of us—Generals, Captain Miles, and Parson Blake, who had been knocked down at Monmouth to change coats and reclaim his living—fought the battles of the Revolution over bottles. The parson would cry, "By Jupiter, General," noting the political divide: Hal as the loyalist to the leaders, Sir George as the critic. When the parson prayed for the King, Hal followed loudly.

"I wear his coat," Hal insisted.
"If Wolfe had lived twenty years longer!" Lambert countered.
"You should hear the General talk about him!" Hal cried.
"Which General?" I asked, seeking to vex him.
"My General," Hal replied, raising a bumper. "His Excellency, General George Washington!"

The toast was met with agreement, though the parson's expression suggested distaste for the claret or the sentiment. Hal spoke endlessly of his General, eventually revealing a moment of disgrace. The friction centered on the Marquis de Lafayette, a figure who had risen rapidly through the ranks. Hal, an old soldier accustomed to merit, had expressed contempt for the congressional appointments, particularly the elevation of the Marquis, a boy of twenty who could not speak English. Washington, recognizing the strategic value of the Marquis and the diplomatic necessity of his favor, had protected him. Hal, however, had served as a colonel, spending his pay on his men rather than rank, viewing the hierarchy as irrelevant compared to the comfort of the troops.

During the winter of '77, at Valley Forge, amidst the scarcity of fire, blankets, and food, facing an enemy three times his size, Washington faced not only the British but the cowardice of Congress and the conspiracies of his own officers. The "Conway Cabal" emerged: an Irish Chevalier of St. Louis, promoted to brigadier, demanded a major-generalcy. Friends at Congress promised him the rank. Washington remonstrated, highlighting the injustice of elevating the youngest brigadier. Instead, he forwarded a letter from Conway to Gates, suggesting that a weak general and bad counselors would ruin America. Conway resigned, only to be appointed Inspector-General by Congress.

Hal recounted this era with passion, admitting his own jealousy had clouded his judgment. He realized he should have held his tongue regarding the "frogs" and the influx of Marquises. The General's demeanor grew cold, creating a schism. Mrs. Washington, perceptive and skilled in uncovering secrets, summoned Hal to the parade ground. She revealed the offense: Hal's casual remark about importing Marquises from Paris. The General, proud and sensitive, felt betrayed by a former comrade joining the opposition.

That night, Hal waited on the General alone. The atmosphere was charged with the gravity of altered times.
"It used not always to be Sir and Colonel Warrington, between me and your Excellency," Hal said.
"The times are altered," the General replied calmly.
"Et nos mutamur in illis," Hal responded. "Times and people are both changed."

The confrontation escalated. Hal accused the General of showing little trust, of allowing impertinent remarks at his table. The General asked which charge he preferred: the jealousy regarding the Marquis or the breach of friendship.
"Jealous, sir!" Hal exclaimed. "An aide-de-camp of Wolfe is not jealous of a Jack-a-dandy who was being whipped five years ago!"
"But you declined higher rank," the General noted, flushing slightly.
"But I never bargained to have a macaroni Marquis to command me!" Hal cried. "Since Congress and your Excellency choose to take your generals out of the nursery, I shall humbly ask leave to resign, and retire to my plantation."

"Do, Harry; that is true friendship!" the General said gently, urging him to leave while he was in difficulty.
"Sir!" Hal protested.
"Do as so many of the rest are doing... Et tu, Brute," the General muttered, referencing the play. "Well, well, Harry! I did not think it of you; but, at least, you are in the fashion."

Hal pressed the point, asking which charge the General would address first. The General admitted recommending the Marquis's promotion, acknowledging the disapproval of officers like Hal.
"I have spoken for myself, sir," Hal stated firmly.
"If you take me in that tone, Colonel Warrington, I have nothing to answer!" the General rose, fierce and defensive. "And presume that I can recommend officers for promotion without asking your previous sanction."
"Being on that tone, sir," Hal replied, rising to match him, "let me respectfully offer my resignation to your Excellency, founding my desire to resign upon the fact, that Congress, at your Excellency's recommendation, offers its highest commands to boys of twenty, who are scarcely even acquainted with our language."

He bowed, completing the formal severance, leaving the room filled with the unspoken weight of a friendship fractured by the machinery of war and the absurdity of rank.
