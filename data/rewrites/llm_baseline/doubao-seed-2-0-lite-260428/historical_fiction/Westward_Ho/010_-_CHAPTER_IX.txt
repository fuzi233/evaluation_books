“Take aim, you noble musqueteers, And shoot you round about; Stand to it, valiant pikemen, And we shall keep them out. There's not a man of all of us A foot will backward flee; I'll be the foremost man in fight, Says brave Lord Willoughby!”

It was the blessed, soft Christmas afternoon. The golden light was fading fast, evensong had rung out and ended, and Bideford’s good townsfolk trooped home in merry chattering groups: fathers herding laughing children, sweethearts arm in arm, all bound for spiced cakes, frothing nut-brown ale, fiery flapdragon games, and mummers’ plays, for the full joy of Christmas night. Only one lady, wrapped tight in a somber black muffler and followed by her quiet maid, walked swiftly yet sadly toward the long stone causeway and bridge that led over the water to Northam town. Sir Richard Grenville and his wife cantered up beside her and stopped her with gentle courtesy.

“You will come home with us, Mrs. Leigh,” Lady Grenville said warmly, “and share a pleasant Christmas night by our fire?”

Mrs. Leigh smiled soft and sad, laid one hand on Lady Grenville’s arm, and pointed westward with the other. “I cannot bring myself to feast merrily while that sound hums in my ears.”

The little group around her turned to follow her gaze. Above their heads the soft blue dusk streaked to gray, and the first misty stars pricked through here and there. But westward, where the rolling downs and woods of Raleigh’s estate met those of Abbotsham, the blue sky was laced with wispy white sea-fret, streaked with iridescent afterglow that blushed all the shifting colors of a dying dolphin, and low on the far horizon stretched a long band of pale grassy green over the Atlantic. But the sound that troubled Mrs. Leigh? None of the merry townsfolk, their ears dulled by the day’s bustle, had noticed it till that moment. Listen now, though: not a breath of wind stirred a single blade of grass inland, and yet the air thrumming with a low, deep roar that hovered over down and wood, salt marsh and river, like the roll of a thousand cartwheels, the tramp of endless marching armies – the thunder of a massive storm surge pummeling the boulders of the far-off pebble ridge, two miles out to sea.

“The ridge is loud tonight,” Sir Richard remarked. “There’s a gale blowing somewhere far out in the Atlantic.”

“The gale is blowing now, where my boy is. God help him,” Mrs. Leigh whispered, and all around knew she spoke the plain truth. The Atlantic storm had sent its herald ahead: that smooth, roaring ground-swell that carried the sound of the coming tempest miles inland. By tomorrow, the pebbles that now rattled down the ridge with every retreating wave would be leaping to its very crest, hurled like cannon shot far ashore across the marsh by the force of the oncoming surf, fleeing before the wrath of a full western hurricane.

“God help my boy,” Mrs. Leigh said again.

“God is as near a man at sea as He is on land,” good Sir Richard reminded her gently.

“That is true enough. But I am a lone mother, and my heart has no stomach for feasting this night. I will go home and pray.”

And so she walked on up the lane, and spent all Christmas night pacing between her prayers, listening to the thunder of the distant surge, till long before sunrise that low rumble was swallowed whole by the full, howling roar of the unleashed storm.

Where was Amyas on that same Christmas afternoon?

Amyas sat bareheaded in the stern of a pitching small boat in Smerwick Bay, sharp spray whistling through his curly hair, shouting cheer over the gale: “Pull hard, my merry men, never mind if you ship a sea! Cannon shot don’t spoil for a little salt water!”

His mother’s premonition had come true. The still, dark, steamy lulls of early winter had ended the night before, and a brutal western gale had raged nonstop over the Irish coast for twelve full hours.

The short light of the winter day was fading fast. Behind the boat, lines of frothing billows were lashed to mist by the tempest. Beside them, green foam-fringed walls of surf hurled themselves against black jagged rocks, shattering into a thousand white cascades. Ahead lay the deep, sheltered bay, but they could see little of it: four miles out to sea, a sloping roof of thick gray whirling cloud stretched over their heads, all the way inland, cutting off the cliff tops halfway up, swallowing the Kerry mountains, and plunging the hollows of the distant sea lochs into pitch-black night. Under that awful swirling roof of mist, the storm howled inland, sweeping great clumps of foam and stinging salt spray before it, till all the land was hazy, dim, and dun.

But let the wind howl. There was more smoke than salt spray rising into that gale, more thunder than sea-surge echoing off the Smerwick cliffs. Red sparks flashed in the evening gloom from the sandhills that lined the bay, sparks that never fell from heaven. The Spanish fort the invaders had named Del Oro, flying the hated golden flag of Spain, held San Josepho and eight hundred of his Spanish and Italian mercenary troops. Three nights prior, Amyas, Salvation Yeo, and the rest of Captain Winter’s ablest seamen had slung four heavy culverins off the admiral’s main deck, floated them ashore through the surf, and dragged them up to the battery built among the sand-dunes. Now the hour had come to see if foreign condottieri could hold British ground against the rough men of Devon.

No one could blame Amyas, in the thick of the action, for forgetting his lonely mother back at Burrough Court. His mind was fixed on those quick, bright flashes of muzzle fire across the sandhills, where Salvation Yeo was hurling eighteen-pound shot with deadly aim, watching with a cool, bitter smile of triumph as sand flew and gabions crashed apart. Amyas and his crew had risked their lives running back to the ship for fresh ammunition, for the battery had run out of proper roundshot four hours earlier, and had been firing rough paving stones for want of better. They ran their boat ashore through the surf in the only little cove that made landing possible, not caring if the hull stove to splinters, and every man scrambled over the dunes with two heavy cannon balls slung across his shoulders. Amyas leaped straight into the trenches, shouting merrily to Yeo: “More food for the bull-dog guns, Gunner! Christmas plums for the Spaniards’ pudding!”

“Don’t speak to a man while he’s at his work, Master Amyas! I’ve missed five times running, and I swear the foul fiend himself is turning my shot aside. But I’ll bring that cursed Popish rag down, as I’m a living sinner.”

“Down with it then! No one’s asking you to shoot crooked. Use good iron, not footy paving stones.”

“Thank the Lord, here’s proper roundshot at last. Stand clear, men!”

Yeo’s eighteen-pounder roared once more. And glory be! The great yellow Spanish flag, streaming in the gale, lifted clean into the air, flagstaff and all, pitched wildly headfirst far away to leeward.

A roar of cheers burst from the English sailors, answered by the soldiers in the trenches, shaking the very low cloud above their heads. But before the echoes died, a tall officer leaped onto the parapet of the fort, grabbed the fallen flag, spiked it to the end of his lance, and held it firm against the gale while his men hauled a new staff up inside the walls.

A dozen English longbows drew on the daring foeman in an instant – but Amyas shouted from behind: “Shame on you, lads! Stand down! Give a brave gentleman his due courtesy!”

The archers lowered their bows. Amyas sprang onto the battery’s rampart, doffed his hat, and bowed deep to the man holding the flag. The Spanish officer, as soon as he had handed the flag off to his men, returned the bow courteously before descending back out of sight.

By now it was nearly dark, and firing slackened on all sides. The gunners covered their culverins with tarpaulins for the night, and Amyas volunteered to stand watch till midnight. The rest of the force ate their scanty supper of dry biscuit – provisions were running very thin now – then curled up under arms among the sandhills and grumbled themselves to sleep.

Amyas had paced up and down the gusty dark for an hour or more, exchanging a quiet word now and then with the sentries, when two men strolled into the battery deep in conversation. One was clad in full armor; the other wore the plain short cloak of a scholar and man of peace. Their talk had nothing to do with sieges, sallies, catapults, bombardments or culverins – it was all about English hexameters.

Do not think, gentle reader, that the pair were fiddling while Rome burned. In that critical year of 1580, the great commonwealth of English poetry and letters was in far greater danger from those very hexameters than the miserable Irish campaign, as Raleigh called it, ever was from the Spaniards.

Copying classical Greek and Roman metre, “versifying” as the learned called it in opposition to simple rhyming, was all the rage among Cambridge’s wits. Stonyhurst and others had churned out clumsy hexameter translations of epic poems that were little more than doggerel, and every young wit was trying his hand at iambics, sapphics, elegiacs, twisting the Queen’s English into unnatural shapes that offended the ear. Gabriel Harvey, the Cambridge pedant, had even persuaded Spenser, Dyer, Sidney, and the whole clique of courtly literary minds around them to follow his stilted model, and had sneered at the opening book of *The Faerie Queene* (still in manuscript) as a shameful decline from classical rigor into romantic “trash.” Spenser, still raw from being jilted by a fair maid of Kent, had been wasting his mighty genius on that awkward doggerel, when a publisher had just released a volume of letters between Harvey and Spenser pushing the “new reformed versifying” that had all the London wits buzzing.

The two men bantered back and forth. Raleigh, the unabashed romantic, teased Spenser mercilessly for stretching English words till their joints snapped like an inquisitor torturing a heretic, saying a rough old fishwife’s simple rhyming ballad was worth all the frilly classical metres in the world.

“Listen,” Raleigh laughed, “there’s our young giant over there comforting his soul with a proper old ballad. No tortured words, no splitting ‘blindfold’ into three syllables to fill a metre, just rhyme and reason together.”

And Amyas, who had been humming half to himself, burst out full-throated with an old, simple Christmas carol his mother had taught him as a boy:
> “As Joseph was a-walking He heard an angel sing –
> This night shall be the birth night Of Christ, our heavenly King.
> His birthbed shall be neither In housen nor in hall,
> Nor in the place of paradise, But in the oxen's stall.
> He neither shall be rocked In silver nor in gold,
> But in the wooden manger That lieth on the mould.
> He neither shall be washen With white wine nor with red,
> But with the fair spring water That on you shall be shed.
> He neither shall be clothed In purple nor in pall,
> But in the fair white linen That usen babies all.'
> As Joseph was a-walking Thus did the angel sing,
> And Mary's Son at midnight Was born to be our King.
> Then be you glad, good people, At this time of the year;
> And light you up your candles, For His star it shineth clear.”

Spenser fell silent for a long moment, then sighed. “That brave lad shames me. Singing the hymns his mother taught him, right under the muzzles of Spanish guns, while I have been moping unmanly over my own petty heartbreak, writing verses of cowardly despair. I will set my heart to higher work than whining over the hand that chastens me.”

Raleigh laughed and said they would carve out a place for Amyas in *The Faerie Queene*, as the Knight of Common Sense. They called over to him, and the three chatted for a time, Raleigh cynically explaining how slander and flattery got men ahead at court, while Amyas brushed it all off, saying he would rather be honest and wake to heaven than rich and damned. Then Raleigh said the Lord Deputy had sent them to fetch Amyas to supper, where a good hunk of boiled horse meat waited for him, but Amyas refused to leave his post. “I won’t budge till morning,” he said. “There’s a stir in the fort, and I don’t trust deserters’ reports that their spirits are broken. If a man is false to his own masters, he’ll be false to us.”

Raleigh teased him a little more, then he and Spenser stumbled back off into the dark, leaving Amyas to pace the battery alone. Amyas puzzled over Raleigh’s sharp, cynical words and Spenser’s soft melancholy, and decided that the cleverest, most learned and ambitious people were almost always the unhappiest. He thanked his lucky stars he was no scholar, no courtier, no poet – just a plain Devon man, with a clear duty to do. He ate his horse meat as if it were venison, said his prayers, thought of his lonely mother back home, of his brother Frank feasting at the Queen’s glittering court, then of Rose Salterne, whom he tried very hard not to think about, and inevitably thought of all the more.

The hours dragged on, past eleven o’clock. All lights were out in the battery and the ships anchored in the bay. No sound broke the dark but the steady tramp of the two sentries, and an occasional grunt from the men sleeping under arms twenty yards away. The rain started pouring down hard.

Suddenly Amyas heard a rustle in the harsh sand-grass. The wind whistled through it loud enough, but that sound was not the wind. Then a soft sliding noise: something had slipped down the face of a sand-dune, dislodging a small shower of sand behind it. Amyas stopped dead, crouched down beside his culverin, and pressed his ear to the rampart. He heard the distinct, crunching sound of approaching feet. Not rabbits, he thought, shrewd and alert. Men.
