The morning of Gwendolen Harleth's wedding to Mr. Grandcourt was crisp and clear, the low sun casting a frost that curled the edges of the leaves. The procession moving toward the church was a spectacle in itself, drawing the entire village of Pennicote to line the path. An old friend of the rector officiated, while the rector himself stepped forward to act as the bride's father—a role that added a certain gravity to the proceedings. Yet, amidst the general rejoicing, two faces stood out in their sorrow: Mrs. Davilow's and Anna's. The mother's eyes were rimmed with pink, betraying tears shed throughout the night, a natural grief for the daughter who was the pride of her life. Anna's distress, however, puzzled the onlookers; she was dressed as a bridesmaid in splendor, yet her spirit seemed heavy.

The villagers, mostly, reflected the brilliance of the occasion, especially the bride. Gwendolen possessed the figure and carriage of a true "lady o' title." Her face, perhaps, lacked the rosy bloom one might expect of a titled woman, but the groom, noted for his pale complexion—much like the miller's wife's husband—made the match seem perfectly balanced in the public eye. They whispered that he must love her dearly, hoping he would never hold against her her humble past as a governess or her mother's modest cottage life. Even the fourteen-year-old miller's daughter, raised on tales of gentry cruelty, couldn't believe such high-born men treated their wives poorly, until her mother corrected her with a grim anecdote about a squire who whipped his wife in her own room.

"That's unlucky talk for a wedding, Mrs. Girdle," the tailor interjected. "A quarrel may end with the whip, but it starts with the tongue, and it's the women who carry the weight of that."

"The Lord gave it to them to use," Mrs. Girdle replied. "He never intended for you to have it all your own way."

The conversation drifted to Mr. Grandcourt's reputation. The groomer from Offendene had mentioned that the gentleman spoke with "wonderful little tongue," expecting everything to be done silently and obediently.

"Then he's the more whip, I suspect," Mrs. Girdle murmured. "She's got tongue enough, I warrant. Look, there they come!"

Gwendolen, radiant in her trousseau, displayed an elasticity of bearing and a lustre in her brown eyes that suggested the thrill of strong excitement. She was not feeling pain, but a feverish exhilaration akin to standing at a gambling table with a full house watching. She was beginning to lose, yet she enjoyed the risk. Any nagging unease from her conscience was dismissed as a minor ailment amidst the gratification of her ambition and desire for luxury. She did not truly repent accepting Grandcourt; fears of the future were drowned out by the glowing reality of the moment, where she was the central object of admiration.

Beneath the surface, however, a storm raged. She knew she was doing wrong. She knew a punishment might be hanging over her. She knew the woman she had betrayed was suffering in bitterness, and that Daniel Deronda likely despised her now as he had despised her for gambling. She felt the cord that once bound her to her lover being flung around her neck. This mixture of dim facts and vivid, terrifying images had plagued her during the engagement. But this morning, that agitation was surmounted by a defiant exultation. She felt herself entering a game of life with many eyes upon her, daring everything to win, or if she lost, to lose with éclat. She believed she was gaining the official strength of marriage to manage her circumstances, intoxicated by a renewed egoism. She walked among illusions with an erect head and elastic step, unaware that she was already slightly drunk on the dream.

"Thank God you bear it so well, my darling!" Mrs. Davilow cried, helping her remove the bridal white for her traveling dress. The mother trembled, her agitation urging Gwendolen to view the morning as a triumph.

"Why, you might have said that if I were going to Mrs. Mompert's, you dear, sad, incorrigible mamma!" Gwendolen laughed, pressing her hands to her mother's cheeks before spreading her arms to display herself. "Here am I—Mrs. Grandcourt! What else would you have me?"

"Hush, my child!" Mrs. Davilow whispered. "How can I help feeling it? But I can bear anything gladly if you are happy."

"Not gladly, mamma, no!" Gwendolen shook her head, smiling brightly. "Willingly you would bear it, but always sorrowfully. Sorrowing is your sauce; you can take nothing without it." She kissed her mother's cheeks profusely. "And you shall sorrow over my having everything at my beck—splendid houses, horses, diamonds! I shall have diamonds! Going to court, being Lady Certainly, and grand everywhere, yet always loving you better than anyone else."

"My sweet child! But I shall not be jealous if you love your husband better; he will expect to be first."

Gwendolen made a pretty grimace. "Rather a ridiculous expectation. However, I don't mean to treat him ill, unless he deserves it."

They embraced, and Gwendolen could not stop a rising sob as she wished her mother were coming with her. The dew on her long eyelashes only enhanced her charm as she handed her hand to Grandcourt to lead her to the carriage.

The rector stopped to offer a final blessing, telling Mrs. Davilow that Gwendolen was in a position well suited to her, chosen entirely for her own sake, and that she should feel proud.

The journey to Ryelands took an hour by rail. By the time they reached the station, the sky had darkened to twilight. As they drove through the park, Gwendolen gazed out the window at the grand outlines of the estate—the winding drives, the vast grassy spaces, and finally, the white house framed by a hanging wood.

She had been lively during the train ride, chatting incessantly, ignoring the shift in their dynamic. Grandcourt sat quietly, almost ecstatically so, while she seized his hand with both of hers, her vivacity that of a kitten refusing to be petted. She was becoming febrile with excitement. The changing light and scenery made her heart palpitate. Was it the novelty, or the fulfillment of her girlish dreams of being "somebody"? Walking through corridors of her own design, under ceilings of impossible height, where her painted Spring shed flowers and her Zephyrs blew trumpets? Where her servants were mere lackeys in her presence? Was it the closeness of this dream realized, or a dim, insistent dread mixing with her triumph? Hers was a nature where exultation inevitably carried an infusion of dread ready to curdle.

As they approached the gates, she fell silent. When Grandcourt said, "Here we are at home!" and kissed her on the lips, she barely registered it. It was merely the passive acceptance of a greeting amidst an absorbing show. After the half-willful excitement of the day, a numbness had settled over her.

The hall was brilliant with light, warmth, matting, carpets, and statues. Servants moved assiduously, though there were few of them. Gwendolen was led by Grandcourt down a subtly scented corridor into an ante-room, where an open doorway sent out a rich glow.

"These are our dens," Grandcourt said. "You will like to be quiet here till dinner. We shall dine early."

He pressed her hand to his lips and moved away, more in love than he had ever expected to be.

Gwendolen yielded her hat and mantle, threw herself into a chair by the glowing hearth, and saw her reflection in the glass panels, surrounded by her faint-green satin. The housekeeper lingered, seemingly eager to admire the new mistress, but Gwendolen, impatient for solitude, dismissed her maid.

"Will you tell Hudson when she has put out my dress to leave everything? I shall not want her again, unless I ring."

The housekeeper stepped forward. "Here is a packet, madam, which I was ordered to give into nobody's hands but yours, when you were alone. The person who brought it said it was a present particularly ordered by Mr. Grandcourt; but he was not to know of its arrival till he saw you wear it. Excuse me, madam; I felt it right to obey orders."

Gwendolen took the packet, letting it rest on her lap until the doors closed behind the housekeeper. It occurred to her that the packet might contain the diamonds Grandcourt had promised. In this moment of confused feeling and creeping luxurious languor, she was glad of the diversion—glad to try on her own diamonds.

Inside the sealed paper was a box, and inside the box, a jewel-case. Now she felt no doubt she held the diamonds. But the instant she opened the case and saw them gleam, she saw a letter lying atop them. She recognized the handwriting immediately. It was as if an adder had coiled upon the jewels. Her heart gave a leap that seemed to drain her strength; her hands trembled as she unfolded the thin paper.

*These diamonds, which were once given with ardent love to Lydia Glasher, she passes on to you. You have broken your word to her, that you might possess what was hers. Perhaps you think of being happy, as she once was, and of having beautiful children such as hers, who will thrust hers aside. God is too just for that. The man you have married has a withered heart. His best young love was mine: you could not take that from me when you took the rest. It is dead: but I am the grave in which your chance of happiness is buried as well as mine. You had your warning. You have chosen to injure me and my children. He had meant to marry me. He would have married me at last, if you had not broken your word. You will have your punishment. I desire it with all my soul.*

*Will you give him this letter to set him against me and ruin us more—me and my children? Shall you like to stand before your husband with these diamonds on you, and these words of mine in his thoughts and yours? Will he think you have any right to complain when he has made you miserable? You took him with your eyes open. The willing wrong you have done me will be your curse.*

At first, Gwendolen's eyes seemed spellbound, reading the horrible words over and over as a doom of penance. Suddenly, a new spasm of terror made her lean forward, stretching the paper toward the fire, lest accusation and proof meet all eyes. The letter flew like a feather from her trembling fingers, caught instantly in a great draft of flame. In her movement, the casket fell to the floor, and the diamonds scattered. She paid no attention, collapsing back into her chair, helpless. She could not see her reflection anymore; the glass showed only women petrified white. Coming near, one might have seen the tremor in her lips and hands. She sat thus for a long while, knowing little more than that she felt ill, and that those written words kept repeating themselves in her mind.

Truly, here were poisoned gems, and the poison had entered this poor young creature.

Finally, there was a tap at the door. Grandcourt entered, dressed for dinner. The sight of him brought a fresh, violent shock. Gwendolen screamed again and again with hysterical violence. He had expected to find her dressed and smiling, ready to be led down. Instead, he found her pallid, shrieking as if with terror, the jewels scattered around her on the floor. Was it a fit of madness?

In some form or other, the furies had crossed his threshold.
