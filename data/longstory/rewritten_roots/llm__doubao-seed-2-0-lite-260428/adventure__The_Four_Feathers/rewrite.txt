Lieutenant Sutch was the first guest to trudge up the drive to Broad Place, General Feversham’s Surrey home, at five o’clock on a sun-drenched mid-June afternoon. His bad leg, crushed under a falling scaling ladder at the Redan fourteen years prior, ached with every step, but the sight of the old red-brick manor glowing like a polished ruby against the dark, thick pine woods at its back made the throb fade a little. He limped through the high, oak-paneled hall, where generations of Feversham portraits climbed one above the other all the way to the rafters, and stepped out onto the stone flagging of the rear terrace.

General Feversham was perched on the edge of a wicker chair, ramrod straight as a cadet, staring south over the rolling downs toward Sussex. A wiry, white-haired old warhorse with steel-blue, unreadable eyes and a face chiseled sharp as a bayonet, he stood briskly to greet his guest.
“Leg giving you gyp?” he asked.
Sutch laughed, shifting his weight to ease the damaged limb. “Nothing unexpected. We’re all on permanent half-pay shelf anyway, aren’t we? The obit page’s just the final gazette kicking us out of service for good.”

The two men fell quiet for a minute. Below the terrace the ground dropped steeply to a patchwork of emerald fields and ploughed brown earth, dotted with dark copses; far off toward Horsham, a train’s wisp of white smoke snaked through the trees, and the white chalk patches of the Sussex Downs blazed on the horizon.
“This was my wife’s favourite spot,” Feversham said, his voice flat and empty of all emotion. “She loved wide, empty spaces.”
Sutch nodded. He’d known Muriel Graham before she married Feversham, the sharpest, loveliest woman in the London season of her year, with more imagination in her little finger than the entire Feversham family tree put together. He’d never figured out what had drawn her to this hard, unyielding man, who only cared about courage and regimental tradition, and wrote off anything he didn’t understand as not worth knowing.
“Empty spaces don’t stay empty for the person who can imagine them,” Sutch said. Feversham just blinked, uncomprehending, and switched topics at once.
“Three of our old lot shuffled off this winter — Collins, Barberton, Vaughan. We’ll toast them tonight. Before the others turn up, I wanted your take on something. Today isn’t just the anniversary of the Redan assault. The exact second we were standing under arms in the dark west of the quarries, my boy Harry was born right here in this house. He’s home from school for the holidays. I thought he could sit in with us tonight. He’ll be joining the army soon enough, might learn a thing or two from old campaigners like us.”

Sutch jumped at the chance. He’d never met Harry, and years of being stuck on half-pay with a crippled leg had turned him into a quiet, eager student of human nature. He was burning to see if the boy had inherited his mother’s soft, vivid mind, or his father’s iron lack of feeling.

That night, the 14-year-old slid into a seat at the end of the long dinner table, and the old soldiers’ stories poured out thick as cigar smoke: blizzards in the Crimea trenches that froze eyelashes together, charges through bullet-swept no-man’s-land, men sharing the last of their biscuit while shot whined over their heads. They told them all in dry, clipped, matter-of-fact tones, like they were describing a bad day at the market, never lingering on the gore. But Harry leaned forward so far his elbows dug into the tablecloth, his dark mother’s eyes wide and fixed on every speaker, like he was standing right there in that frozen Crimean snow himself. His face twitched with every tale of a close call, his shoulders tensing when an artillery major described the endless, sickening wait between forming up for battle and the command to advance.

Then Sutch saw it. A quick, furtive, wavering glance over Harry’s shoulder, that old tell-tale look he’d seen hundreds of times on raw recruits in their first battle. He remembered a giant private at Inkermann, charging forward in a rush, then freezing dead still, that exact same glance over his shoulder for his mates, before a Cossack lanced him through the throat before he even lifted his bayonet to defend himself. Sutch’s chest went cold. No one else at the table noticed — every man there was too busy waiting for his turn to tell his own war story. Sutch reached over and tapped Harry’s elbow lightly.
“These stories make me feel like I’m back there freezing,” he said.
Harry blinked, roused from his trance. “It’s not the stories. It’s you, listening like you’re living them again.”

Before Sutch could reply, Feversham’s sharp bark cut across the table.
“Harry. Look at the clock.”

Midnight. No one had realized four whole hours had slipped by while the boy sat silent, soaking up every word. The whole table burst out protesting when Feversham ordered him off to bed. “It’s his birthday, for Christ’s sake!” the artillery major laughed. “You won’t find a 14-year-old sit still that long if he isn’t eating this up. Give the lad an hour’s furlough from bedtime!” Feversham grumbled, then relented.

Half that extra hour had ticked by when Feversham blurted out a story he’d clearly been chewing on for years.
“Poor Lord Wilmington. Best family in England, whole estate full of portraits of fighting ancestors, supposed to be the manliest of the lot. Turned out he was a coward. Got posted as galloper to his general at Sebastopol, and the general gave him a chance to redeem the whispers — 300 yards of flat, bullet-swept ground, carry a message to the front. He just froze up on his horse, shaking, refused to go. The general was so mad he just said, all polite, ‘Oh, I do apologize, I didn’t realize you had a previous engagement.’ Can you imagine? A previous engagement on a battlefield? Of course he got broken. Slunk back to London, every door slammed in his face, even the street girls spat at him if he walked past. Blew his own brains out in a garret off Haymarket a year later. Didn’t have the guts to face the bullets, but had the guts to shoot himself. Odd, isn’t it?”

The table rumbled with low mutters of disgust, until the old bearded surgeon-general across from Harry leaned in, and dropped an even colder story.
“I’ve got one worse. In a hill campaign in India, we had a few Pathans sniping at the camp from the hills one night, a stray bullet ripped through the hospital tent canvas. Our camp surgeon, never been under fire before, just snuck off to his quarters, sliced his own femoral artery open with a lancet in the dark, and bled out before his orderly found him. Pure panic. The bullet never even touched him.”

A heavy, queasy silence fell over the table. These were hard men, used to seeing men die in every horrible way possible, but the idea of a human being so broken by fear he’d choose suicide over facing a stray bullet made every one of them shift uncomfortably in their chairs.

Only Harry didn’t move. His knuckles were white where he clutched his knees, his cheeks as pale as blank paper, his eyes blazing like a trapped wild animal ready to fight for its life. Sutch thought he was about to lunge across the table at the surgeon, he was that feral with despair. Feversham’s gruff voice cut through the silence before he could.
“Queer, inexplicable stuff. You don’t understand it. You just thank God you never have to live it.”

Sutch leaned over to Harry, soft. “Do you understand it?”
Harry didn’t flinch, just stared back at him, inscrutable. Feversham hooted with laughter from the head of the table.
“Understand? Why the hell would he? He’s a Feversham!”

The clock struck one. Harry stood up, slow, said goodnight, and stepped out into the pitch-black hall. Sutch watched him hesitate on the threshold, like the dark itself was full of nameless, waiting peril, then slip out and shut the dining room door behind him.

A minute later, Sutch slipped out after him, quiet as he could. He found Harry standing dead in the centre of the dark hall, holding a candle high over his head, staring up at the wall of Feversham portraits. Every last one of them wore a uniform, every last one was that same hard, thin-lipped, steel-eyed breed of fighting man, stretching back three hundred years: cavalrymen, infantry officers, dragoons, all men of iron nerve, no imagination, no softness, no room for fear. The candle flame was wavering, but there was no draft in the hall — Harry’s hand was shaking so hard he could barely hold it. Then, slow and solemn, he bowed to the line of portraits, like a criminal accepting his death sentence from a panel of judges.

He spotted Sutch in the doorway, and didn’t even jump.
“I was a friend of your mother’s, Harry,” Sutch said, gentle, stepping forward. “You don’t have to talk to me about anything you don’t want to, but — if you ever need to, my address is on this card. Come stay with me any time, we can go hunting, no questions asked.”

Harry took the card, quiet, thanked him, and trundled up the stairs, the candlelight slowly vanishing up the landing. Sutch went back to the dining room, stood up, and banged his glass to get everyone’s attention.
“Gentlemen! Today’s the Redan anniversary, and it’s Harry Feversham’s 14th birthday. Our fighting days are done. Let’s raise a toast to the new Feversham. May he carry on the family’s traditions, and make the name prouder than it already is.”

Every man at the table roared to his feet, thumping mugs so hard the silver rattled, cheering Harry’s name at the top of their lungs, before breaking out into a raucous rendition of *For He’s A Jolly Good Fellow*.

Up in his cold bedroom, Harry heard the muffled song seep up through the floorboards, and thought they were singing it for his father. He curled up under the blankets, shivering, and in his mind he saw Lord Wilmington slinking through the dark London streets, and the surgeon lying dead in his tent with a lancet still in his hand. And both of their faces were his own.

Thirteen years to the very same June, the toast of Harry Feversham’s health was raised once more—this time far quieter, in a far smaller room, perched ten stories up in that jagged, fortress-like block of buildings glowering over Westminster. A stranger wandering south across St. James’s Park’s suspension bridge after dark might lift their gaze unthinkingly, and stop dead, struck by the tiers of glowing gas windows stacked so precipitously high overhead that they could almost believe they’d stumbled on a secret mountain peak hidden in the heart of London, lit by the work of hard-working gnomes.

Harry had taken the flat for the year’s furlough he’d just pulled from his regiment’s posting in India, and it was in its wood-panelled, fire-warmed dining room that the small gathering unfolded. The air was thick with cigar smoke, and a cheery log blazed on the hearth to fend off the unseasonable June chill that had no business lingering so late in the year. The unblinded bay window looked out over the whole sprawl of London, gas lamps strung out like scattered embers as far as the eye could see. Four men lounged around the dinner table.

Harry himself had barely changed, save for a neat fair moustache that contrasted sharply with his dark hair, and the lean, long-limbed build of a seasoned athlete. His features still held that quiet, thoughtful cast Lieutenant Sutch had studied so closely all those years before. Two of his guests were brother officers on leave from the Indian posting, same as him: Captain Trench, a sharp, compact little man going rapidly bald, with a quick, resourceful face and jet-black eyes that darted restlessly, never missing a detail. The third was Lieutenant Willoughby, a man cut from very different cloth: round forehead, thick snub nose, vacant bulging eyes that gave him an air of almost comical stupidity. He rarely spoke, and when he did it was never to the point, always circling back to some half-formed thought he’d been chewing over for hours, twisting the absurdly curled ends of his moustache that jutted up toward his eyes like tiny, aggressive horns. Most people wrote him off as harmless on first meeting, only to think twice later: he was as stubborn as he was slow, too pigheaded to admit he was wrong even when his mistakes blew up in everyone’s faces, impossible to argue out of the few fixed ideas he clung to.

The fourth man at the table was Durrance, a lieutenant of the East Surreys, Harry’s oldest and closest friend, who’d come straight over in answer to Harry’s last-minute telegram. It was 1882, a year of rising storm across North Africa: civilians fretted over the news from Egypt, while every soldier breathed thick with eagerness for the fight. Arabi Pasha was shoring up Alexandria’s fortifications against all British warnings, and far to the south, an even greater danger was swelling like a thunderhead over the Sudan. Just a year prior, a slight, tall Dongolawi preacher named Mohammed Ahmed had marched from village to village along the White Nile, preaching with the fire of a revivalist that the long-awaited Saviour was coming. The tax-broken villagers who’d suffered under Turkish rule heard his promise echoed in the wind rustling through dry grass, swore they found the holy names he spoke of printed even on the eggs they gathered from the riverbanks. In 1882, Mohammed Ahmed had declared himself that promised Saviour—the Mahdi—and already won his first crushing victories against Ottoman garrisons.

“There’s going to be trouble,” Trench said, and for an hour the three soldiers circled that subject, spinning out theories of deployments and desert marches. It was a rare lull before Harry spoke up, turning their attention somewhere entirely new.
“Glad all of you could make it tonight. I telegraphed Castleton too—he’s one of ours,” he explained to Durrance, “but he’s dining with a War Office bigwig, then heading straight for Scotland, so he couldn’t make it. I’ve got news.”

The three men leaned forward, still half stuck on the thought of impending war—but Harry’s news had nothing to do with deployments.
“I only got back to London this morning from Dublin,” he said, a faint flush of embarrassment touching his cheeks. “I’ve been there a few weeks. I’m engaged to be married.”

Durrance lifted his glass to his lips, his face unreadable. “Well, here’s luck to you, Harry,” he said, and the words were almost curt, but warm with a weight no flowery speech could have matched. Their friendship had never run on affectionate phrases. Neither man had ever needed to say it out loud: they both knew it was a solid, unbreakable thing, a lifelong tool for hard days ahead, something they both cherished like a rare, unearned gift—even if they both knew it might one day demand a sacrifice neither of them would ever speak of.

“Thanks, Jack,” Harry said. “I owe this to you, you know. You’re the one who introduced me to Ethne. I won’t forget that.”

Durrance set his glass down slowly, his gaze fixed on the tablecloth. “Aye,” he said, his voice perfectly level. “I did you a good turn then.” He looked half-ready to say more, searching for the right words, before Trench’s sharp, practical voice cut in.
“Will this change things for you? Are you planning to leave the service?”

Harry drew slowly on his cigar. He didn’t answer Trench—he spoke straight to Durrance, who had abruptly turned and crossed to the window, his back to the whole room.
“I don’t know. It’s a lot to think through. There’s my career, my father’s name on one side. On the other, there’s her father, Dermod Eustace. He’s old, his estates are falling to pieces, and… well, you know what he thinks of army life. And Ethne. He named her right, that man—she’s Donegal through and through. She’s got the hills and the peat smoke in her bones. I don’t think she’d ever be truly happy stuck in some Indian cantonment, a thousand miles from the streams and the heather she grew up with.”

Harry kept talking, his eyes fixed on Durrance’s back, but Durrance was barely listening. He was staring out over the black, quiet expanse of St. James’s Park, all the glitter and roar of London ringing around it, and he was face to face with that unspoken sacrifice he’d always feared. He was in love with Ethne Eustace, too. He’d never breathed a word of it to Harry, never would. All he wanted right now was to slip out the door and vanish into the dark of the park, unseen, before anyone could read the look on his face.

Trench, meanwhile, hung on every word Harry said, and by the time Harry finished laying out all his careful, considered reasons, the sharp little captain nodded satisfied. They all raised their glasses, toasting Harry and his future bride, and the second their empty glasses clinked down on the table, a knock rapped at the door.

Harry’s servant stepped in, holding out a pink telegram. Harry tore the envelope open carelessly, scanned the slip of paper once—and went perfectly still. His face went grave, almost tight, and he sat staring down at the telegram as if it had burned him. The whole room fell silent all at once. No one dared look at him directly: Durrance turned back to the window, Willoughby twisted his moustache so hard his knuckles whitened, Trench shifted his chair around to stare deep into the fire. Everyone could feel it: the good news had barely landed before calamity had knocked right on its heels.

“No answer,” Harry said, his voice quiet, and lapsed back into silence. He half lifted his head to look at Trench, like he was about to speak, then thought better of it. A second later, the quiet was broken from outside: the drums and fifes of Wellington Barracks blared the tattoo, shrill and clear through the open window, the music swelling as the band marched across the parade ground, then fading, sounding like a sharp, unignorable summons. Harry’s face didn’t shift, but he was listening, thinking, as the music softened from a command to something that almost beckoned. That exact moment, the firelight gilding Harry’s face, the telegram loose in his hand, the distant drums ringing over the lamps of London, would stick in every one of the three men’s memories for the rest of their lives.

Right before the last note of the tattoo died away, Harry stood up abruptly. He crumpled the telegram loosely in his fist, tossed it straight into the hearth, and leaned back against the chimney piece. “I don’t know,” he said, as if he was summing up the earlier conversation about leaving the service, shoving the message out of sight.

The fire caught the paper, twisting it like something alive and in pain, half unrolling for a split second before the flame caught the smooth unburnt section. For a heartbeat, two words blazed out bright against the fire’s yellow glare: *EMBARKATION ORDERS*. Then the flame ate it, shrivelling it all to black ash. Durrance turned back from the window, all three men letting out a breath they hadn’t known they were holding.

“You’re heading back to Dublin soon, then?” Durrance asked.
“Not Dublin. Donegal, in three weeks. There’s a dance they’re throwing, hoping you’ll all come.”
“Can’t promise,” Durrance said. “There’s a chance I’ll get a spot on the staff, if the trouble in the East breaks.”

They talked of war deployments for the next hour, until the deep boom of the Westminster clock struck eleven. All three men said their goodbyes to Harry, trooped down the ten flights of stairs, and crossed the dark park together. They split up at the edge of Pall Mall: Durrance turned up St. James’s Street, while Trench slipped his arm through Willoughby’s, much to the other man’s surprise—Trench was never an affectionate sort.
“Got Castleton’s address?” Trench asked.
“Albemarle Street, number 14,” Willoughby said, confused. “He’s catching the 12 o’clock train out of Euston.”
“Ten past eleven now. I’m curious,” Trench said, his black eyes glinting. “That telegram Harry got? It had to come from Castleton. He was dining with the War Office bigwig tonight, right? If we take a hansom to Albemarle Street, we’ll catch him before he leaves. We’ll find out what orders Harry just got.”

Willoughby understood barely half of what Trench was saying, but he nodded agreeably, and hailed a passing cab. A moment later, the two men were rattling off through the gas-lit London streets, the shadow of the coming desert war stretching long ahead of all of them.

Durrance tramped back to his lodgings alone, his thoughts drifting two years into the past. On a strange, impulsive whim, old Dermod Eustace had fetched him, against his will, out to the stone house hunkered on the banks of Donegal’s Lennon River, and there Durrance had met Dermod’s daughter Ethne for the first time. She defied every expectation set by her rough, warm-spirited father immediately. He’d only stayed one night, but all through that evening she’d played her violin with her back turned to her small audience, as was her custom, so no stray glance or ill-timed gesture could break the sharp, tender focus of her concentration.
Even now, her melodies hummed in his ears. The girl had the gift of making her violin sing to every question her bow posed. There was one piece in particular — the Melusine Overture — that held the very sob of Atlantic waves crashing against Donegal’s cliffs. That night, as he sat listening to her turned back, the music had spoken to parts of him he’d never dared name: long perilous treks across unmarked land, the silver wake of a ship cutting across moonlit southern seas, the soft, calling voices that drifted from the sun-scorched edges of the desert. It had sunk deeper still, singing of grand, unreachable joys and noble, endless griefs, all the unnameable longings too big for spoken words — never a single cheap note of complaint.
It was a lesson he’d clung to tightly: true music never complains. So the next morning, when he rode into Hyde Park’s Rotten Row, his sun-browned face still carried all its usual easy friendliness, no hint of the quiet heartache he carried for Ethne.
He waited at 9:30 sharp by the thick clump of lilacs and laburnums at the far end of the sand. Harry Feversham never showed. For three full weeks, their long-standing Oxford-era custom of meeting at that spot whenever they were both in town was broken, and Durrance was puzzled. It felt like he was losing his oldest friend, too.
By then, scattered rumours of war in the Sudan had hardened into confirmed deployments. When Harry finally turned up for their ride, Durrance had big news.
“Told you luck would swing my way eventually, right?” he said, grinning so wide his blue eyes crinkled. “I’m posted to General Graham’s staff, shipping out for Egypt. Word is we’ll sail down the Red Sea for Suakin soon after.”
The raw, unmistakeable flash of envy in Harry’s eyes struck Durrance as odd, even in the middle of his own excitement — odd, and not unpleasing, until he misattributed it to his friend’s own love of adventure.
“Rotten luck your regiment’s being held back,” he said sympathetically.
Harry said nothing for a long stretch, his horse matching Durrance’s stride side by side. When they drew level with the line of park benches under the trees, he spoke.
“That was expected. The night you dined at my rooms, I sent in my resignation.”
“That same night?” Durrance twisted in his saddle to stare. “After the rest of us left?”
“Yes.”
Durrance rode silent for a few paces, and Harry braced for the quiet reproach he was sure would come. But Durrance laughed, hearty and unoffended.
“ I remember! You laid out all your reasons that night. Still, I can’t help wishing we were heading out together. When are you off to Ireland, then?”
“Tonight.”
“ That soon?”
They wheeled their horses and trotted west down the tree-lined avenue. The morning was still cool, limes and chestnuts bright with the pale green of early summer, and that year the late-blooming May blossoms hung white as fresh snow from the branches, glowing soft against the deep crimson of the rhododendron beds. The park shimmered in thin golden haze, and the distant low roar of London’s streets sounded like the tumble of a fast-flowing river.
“Feels like a lifetime since we swam in Sandford Lasher,” Durrance said.
“Or nearly froze solid in that Easter break, stuck in the snow gully on Great End,” Harry shot back.
Both men knew, without speaking, that that morning they were closing the final page of the easy, unburdened chapter of their youth. They looked back over those old shared memories one last time before shutting the book for good.
“You have to come stay with us, Jack, when you get back,” Harry said.
Durrance had schooled himself not to flinch at that easy “us” that meant Harry and Ethne’s shared life. His left hand only tightened fractionally on his reins, a sign too small for Harry to catch.
“If I come back,” he said. “You know my creed. I’d never pity a man who died on active service. That’s the kind of end I’d pick for myself.” It was a simple, uncomplicated belief, no trace of melancholy or foreboding in his tone: dying decently, out in the wild world, was worth far more than decades of a safe, small life. He glanced quickly at Harry, worried his friend might misinterpret his words, but all he saw was that same confusing flash of raw envy in his eyes.
“Worse things can happen than dying, anyway,” he went on. “Disablement, for one. Clever folks might find a way to make peace with being stuck in a chair the rest of their lives, but what the hell would I do? Just thinking about it makes me shiver.” He shook his broad shoulders to shake off the shadow of that fear. “C’mon. This is our last ride. Let’s gallop.”
He dug his spurs into his horse’s flank and bolted down the sand, Harry right beside him, neck and neck, racing all the way to the far end of the Row. They skidded to a stop, shook hands hard, and parted with nothing more than a quick, gruff nod. Harry rode out of the park, Durrance turned his horse and walked it slowly back toward the benches under the trees.
Ever since he was a boy growing up in the Southpool creek on Devon’s Salcombe estuary, he’d felt that sharp restlessness, that urge to sail out past the familiar wooded banks and out onto the open sea, to chase the queer, far-off countries and strange peoples he’d only read about in books. Even when he inherited his family’s estate, Guessens, with all its farms and sprawling land, he’d always thought of it as a place to return to, not a life to trap himself in. Now, as he daydreamed, the trees of Hyde Park blurred into gnarled African mimosas, the brown sand under his horse’s hooves turned bright honey-gold desert, and the scattered park boulders became sun-baked black stones glinting like mirrors in the brutal Sudanese sun. He was so deep in his visions of Suakin that he barely heard his name called, soft, from the park rails.
He pulled his horse up short. It was Mrs. Adair, his neighbour back in Southpool, a woman a few years his senior, tall, striking with her layered thick brown hair and her usual pale complexion, now flushed with a faint bright colour.
“Got two bits of news for you,” Durrance said, grinning, leaning down to shake her hand across the rail. “First: Harry Feversham’s engaged. You know her — you hosted him the night he first met her in your Hill Street house, I introduced them. He’s been seeing her in Dublin, it’s Ethne Eustace.”
Mrs. Adair lit up, clearly delighted. “I’m so glad! They’ll marry soon?”
“Nothing stopping them. Second bit? I’m off to General Graham’s staff in the Sudan.”
Her smile died immediately. The colour drained from her cheeks, her eyes sharpened with unmistakeable anxiety. “You’re very glad about that, I suppose?”
“Couldn’t be happier,” he said, no trace of doubt in his voice. “And the sooner I leave the better. I’ll come round for dinner one night before I ship out, if you’ll have me.”
“My husband will be pleased to see you,” she said, a little coldly, but Durrance didn’t notice. He was too wrapped up in his own excitement, spilling out all his wanderlust-fuelled hopes for the coming campaign, not paying her much mind. He’d always found her a little vague, pretty in the dark, unplaceable coastal way common to Devon and Cornwall, well-dressed and friendly, and best of all, a friend to Ethne Eustace. He had no idea that the conversation he’d just had with Harry that morning had set the threads of a new, terrible bond weaving between the two old friends, one that would tie them to each other in ways neither could have imagined out in the desert.
He only had one week to sort his kit, settle the last of his Devonshire estate affairs. The days blurred in a whirlwind of preparation, his newspaper left unopened on his desk every evening. The General’s party was travelling overland to Brindisi to catch the Egypt-bound steamer, and on a wild, rain-lashed evening at the end of July, Durrance stepped off Dover pier onto the mail boat bound for Calais. A small crowd had turned up to see the General off, and as the mooring ropes were cast off, a thin, ragged cheer drifted over the water.
Leaning on the bulwark, Durrance idly wondered if this would be his last sight of England, half-wishing one of his old friends had turned up to wave him off. Then he spotted it: a man standing in the shadow of a gas lamp on the quay, tall, exactly the same build and bearing as Harry Feversham. He rubbed his eyes. The wind made the gas flame flicker wildly behind its glass, rain streaked his field of vision, and he told himself it was a trick of the light. Harry was surely in Donegal by now, sitting in some high hillside garden listening to Ethne play her violin under a clear dark sky.
Then the wind died, just for a second. The lamp burned bright and steady, and the man’s face leaped out of the shadow, perfectly clear. It was Harry. But this Harry was haggard, hollow-eyed, his face carved through with a raw, unmistakeable agony that made him look like an outcast, cut off from all the people he loved.
Durrance leaned so far over the bulwark he nearly tipped into the water, yelling Harry’s name at the top of his voice. But the figure under the lamp never stirred. The wind gusted hard again, the lamp flickered out of focus, the boat’s paddles churned the Channel into white foam, and the mail steamer pulled away from the pier.
Durrance told himself it was an illusion, just a stranger who looked remarkably like his friend. He’d been far too busy all week to even think about that fateful telegram Harry had received, the long, agonising hours he’d spent poring over it. But he was wrong.
Harry Feversham had come to see him off, after all.

Harry Feversham had ridden hard back from his outing with Durrance in the Row, caught the midnight mail train for Dublin, and sailed across Lough Swilly at dawn the next day aboard a rickety weekly cargo steamer that chugged all the way up the Lennon River to Ramelton. Waiting for him on the quay was Ethne, perched in her dog-cart, and she reached out a hand to greet him with the easy, warm smile of a trusted comrade.

“You look surprised to see me,” she said, reading his face.
“I’m always surprised,” he answered. “You never fail to outstrip every thought I have of you.” The comrade’s smile softened on her lips, turning into something far deeper.
“I’ll drive slow,” she promised once his luggage was stowed away under the cart seat. “I left the groom behind on purpose. The guests don’t roll in until tomorrow. We’ve got all today to ourselves.”

She turned the cart along the wide riverside causeway, then up the steep, cobbled main street. Feversham sat silent at her side, drinking in every sight: the dark, thick stand of tall trees climbing the far riverbank, the weathered grey stone arch of the old bridge, the soft singing of the water spilling over the shallows beneath it, the drowsy, unhurried quiet of the whole small town. A swell of near-ownership pride rose in him – this was Ethne’s home, every stone and tree and stretch of water stitched into the fabric of the life he loved.

At twenty-one, Ethne was tall, strong, and limber, with square, steady shoulders that did not hunch into the exaggerated sloped silhouette Victorian women were taught to mimic, yet no less graceful for it, light-footed as a wild deer when she walked. Her dark brown hair was coiled tight at the nape of her neck, a bright high colour burned in her cheeks, and her clear grey eyes met every person she spoke to with unflinching, unfeigned frankness. Her character matched her looks exactly: honest, straightforward, the quiet simplicity of a person built for strength, who carried no petty cruelty and bore no taste for drama. The locals of Ramelton still told the story of her most famous act of courage, the one that never failed to send a small thrill of wonder through Feversham whenever he recalled it: she had been driving that same dog-cart down the steep hill leading to the river once, when her horse spooked at the clatter of a falling pail and bolted. The reins slipped loose onto the ground before she could catch them, and the horse was tearing full tilt toward the sharp curve where the road dipped over the bridge. She did not scream or freeze. She climbed clean over the front of the lurching, racing cart, balanced her way along the length of the wooden shafts, and snagged the reins at the horse’s neck, bringing the runaway to a full stop ten yards from the fatal curve. She had flaws that came with that same steel backbone, though Feversham was not to learn of them for some hours yet.

For the first half of the drive, Ethne was nearly as quiet as he was, and when she spoke her voice was distant, like she was turning a heavy, important thought over in her head. It was only once they had left Ramelton behind and hit the long, rolling straight road out toward Letterkenny that she turned sharp to face him and spoke her piece.
“I saw the morning orders today,” she said. “Your whole regiment’s been posted out from India to Egypt. You could have shipped out with them, if I hadn’t stepped in your path. There would have been every chance to make a name for yourself, and I’ve held you back. I blame myself terribly. I know you had no way of guessing the regiment would be called for active duty, but I can imagine how galling it must feel to be left behind.”

Feversham stared straight ahead, and his voice went suddenly hoarse. “You have no reason to blame yourself.”
“How can I not?” she pressed on. “I’m not like most other women, you know. If you’d gone out to Egypt and the worst had happened, I’d be lonely for the rest of my days, but I’d know for absolute certain that once those days were done, we’d see each other again.” She spoke the words in a steady, level tone, as if she was stating the most obvious, unarguable fact in the world, and Feversham caught his breath like a man who had just taken a blow to the ribs. He did not dare look at her, did not dare shift a muscle in his face, but he could not trust himself to speak, so he kept his lips pressed tight.
“I can bear the absence of the people I love far better than most,” she went on, hunting for the right words. “I never feel like I’ve lost them. You know how it is: you drift along in a dull routine for ages, and then suddenly a face jumps out from the crowd of all your acquaintances, and you know in an instant you’ve found a friend you’ve been waiting for your whole life, even if you never met them before. Those few people – the only ones that matter, really – you never lose them, not if they’re halfway across the world, not even if they’re dead.”
“Unless,” Feversham said slowly, his throat tight, “you were wrong. What if the face you thought you recognised was just a mask? What if you made a mistake?”
Ethne shook her head, sure and unwavering. “That kind of mistake is impossible. You might think you’ve made it for years, but in the end, you’ll find out you were never wrong at all.”

That unshakable faith of hers wrapped around Feversham and twisted, and he was just about to blurt out every terrible secret he had carried for years – when Ethne hauled the horse to a stop, laughed, and pointed her whip at the left-hand stone wall. A scarlet Royal Mail postbox was set deep into the stone there.
“I wanted to show you that,” she said, cutting him off mid-breath. “That’s where I posted every single one of my letters to you back when you were still with the regiment, all those long anxious weeks.” The moment was gone, and Feversham’s chance to speak slipped away.
“The house is tucked behind the oak and beech wood on the right,” she said, driving on, before pulling up again where a stretch of the park wall had crumbled to rubble. “That’s where I used to climb over the wall to post them, too. There’s a tree growing right up on the other side to give me a step. I’d run the whole half-mile down the dark avenue at night, just to get my letter in the post that much faster.”
“There could have been thieves out there!” Feversham exclaimed.
“Only thorns,” she grinned, and steered the cart through the big iron gates, pulling up to the porch of the long, low, weathered grey Lennon House. “Well, we’ve still got a full day left before the dance.”
“I assume the entire county is coming?” Feversham said.
“They don’t dare not,” Ethne laughed. “My father would send the police out to drag them here if they tried to skip, same way he dragged your friend Mr. Durrance to visit once. Oh, speaking of Durrance – he sent me a present: a Guarnerius violin.”

The front door swung open, and a thin, lanky old man with a fierce, hawk-like peaked face stepped out onto the steps. His expression softened instantly when he saw Feversham, a lopsided smile tugging at his lips that a stranger might have mistaken for a wink – but his left eyelid drooped permanently, covering half his eye.
“Welcome,” he said. “Glad to have you. Make yourself completely at home. If you want a glass of whiskey, stamp twice on the floor. The servants know the signal.” With that, he turned right around and vanished back inside.

The life of old Dermod Eustace would need a very wary biographer indeed. Though he had not been dead twenty years, he was already a full-blown local legend, anecdotes clinging to his memory like barnacles, every local raconteur quick to pin any wild Irish tale on him to make it stick. The facts, though, were plain enough: he ran an old-fashioned, tyrannically hospitable household, keeping Lennon House open to every traveler on the Letterkenny road, forcing food and a bed on even total strangers, as Durrance had once learned to his surprise. He was a man from a bygone century, glowering at the topsy-turvy modern world, refusing to be reconciled to it unless he had a full glass of whiskey in his hand. A kind of drunken Coriolanus, who believed the populace ought to be shepherded with a stick, but never failed in his manners even to the poorest woman in the county. The people of Ramelton said with quiet pride that even on his worst days, galloping down the steep cobbled streets on his seventeen-hand white mare, swaying drunk but somehow still glued to his saddle, his faithful collie trotting at the horse’s heels, he had never once run over a woman – only men. Two other facts were undisputed: he was slightly afraid of his own daughter, who very carefully kept him guessing if she was cross with him or not, and he had conceived an immediate, deep fondness for Harry Feversham.

Feversham saw almost nothing of Dermod that day, though. The old man locked himself away in the room he grandly called his “office”, while Feversham and Ethne spent the whole afternoon salmon fishing on the Lennon River. It was a quiet, restful afternoon, the kind that feels suspended outside of time, even the birds still and quiet. The lawns sloped steeply down from the house, shaded by old trees dappled with shifting sunlight, all the way to a valley where the Lennon ran swift and black under overhanging boughs. A waterfall slid smooth as polished glass over the rocks, only breaking at one sharp rocky spur into an amber foam that glowed gold in the sun. They sat opposite that spur for hours, talking only rarely, mostly listening to the low roar of the falling water and watching the river flow on, unstopping, until the sunset painted the sky pink and long shadows stretched across the grass. They stood up, smiled at each other, and walked slowly back to the house – the last unbroken, perfect afternoon Feversham would ever remember.

That night was the night of the ball. The band struck up the opening bars of the fourth waltz, and Ethne slipped away from her post greeting guests at the drawing room door, looping her arm through Feversham’s and stepping out into the empty hall. The front door was propped open wide to the cool summer night, and the soft lilt of the waltz and the thud of dancers’ feet drifted out from the ballroom behind them. “The post just came,” she said. “There are three letters for you, and a tiny little box.” She held it out to him – a light white cardboard jeweller’s box – and frowned, surprised at how little it weighed. “It must be empty.”

He made no move to deny it, no attempt to evade the truth. All those years he had spent dreading this exact moment, picturing the word coward written in fire across the walls of his mind, had finally arrived. Everyone knew. She heard the noise of laughter and chatter rising from the ballroom, the music having stopped for an intermission. The guests would be coming out into the hall any second. She stood up fast, clenching the feathers tight in her fist, and wrenched open the door to her private sitting room.
“Come in,” she said.

Feversham followed her inside, and she shut the door behind them, cutting off all noise from the party. “Now tell me,” she said, steady and calm, pale-faced but showing no anger, only a cold, determined hunger for the full truth. “Why were these feathers sent?”
He did not soften the story, made no excuses. He told her how Castleton had sent a telegram to his lodgings while Trench and Willoughby were dining with him, with the news that the regiment was being shipped out to Egypt on active service. He had hidden the news, burned the telegram, and sent in his military resignation before any of them could catch on.
“Then Trench found out,” he finished, his voice hollow. “And Durrance was there that night, too. Good God, Durrance. He probably knows now, too.” The thought that his friend Durrance, who had always looked up to him, now counted him a coward, hit him like something new and shocking – but what did it matter if every man on Earth knew, if Ethne knew?
“And that’s all?” Ethne asked.
“Isn’t that enough?” he said.
“No,” she said, softening a little, pleading with him. “We promised we’d always be completely frank with each other, no silly secrets, no misunderstandings. You owe me the full truth, Harry. I’m asking you for it as I’ve never asked for anything in my life.”

Feversham told her the rest then, the secret fear he had carried his whole life: how he had known from childhood he was destined for the army, and spent every waking hour terrified that the moment he stood under enemy fire he would break, disgrace his father’s name, bring shame on every generations of Fevershams that had fought before him. He had never dared tell anyone, not his dead mother, not his stern, unforgiving soldier father. He had forced himself to ride straight to hounds, but spent every waiting minute before a hunt sick with terror, every hedge and pit and treacherous bank mapped out in his mind in advance. So when that telegram had arrived, he had grabbed the escape it offered, and resigned his commission.
Ethne stripped off her glove slow, her face going very still. “I think I’m a great deal like your father,” she said. “I don’t understand this at all.” In the silence that followed, Feversham heard a small clink on the table, and looked over to see her engagement ring sitting on the wood, the diamonds glinting sharp in the gaslight.
“All this,” she said, her voice hard now, “you would have hidden from me forever, wouldn’t you? You would have married me, kept this secret, and never told me a single thing – if these three feathers had never arrived.”
Feversham could not deny it. He stood silent, and Ethne knew his answer.
“Yesterday,” she said, “you were about to tell me this, weren’t you? I cut you off to point out that postbox on the road up. I stopped you before you could speak.”
“Yes,” Feversham said, wearily.

Decades of practiced social habit did the heavy lifting for them both that night. The easy, mindless chatter of the ballroom rolled off their tongues as if no rift had split their world open beneath their feet, the bright, unburdened grins never slipped for a second, so that not a single soul gathered at Lennon House guessed that anything had severed Harry Feversham and Ethne Eustace in the space of a few quiet, terrible minutes.

Harry watched Ethne laugh and banter as she wove through the throng, and he stared, bewildered, never stopping to realize he wore the exact same mask of forced gaiety. When she swung past him in a waltz, the light tap of her dancing shoes on the oak floors almost tricked him into believing her heart was still in the fun, that the pink high on her cheeks was from excitement, not sheer force of will. She had asked him to put a brave face on it, and they both did, even when they stepped out to dance together. But every second, Ethne felt a crushing weight of pain and humiliation building in her chest that she knew would knock her flat the second she let her guard drop. And Harry? The four white feathers tucked against the inside of his coat felt like they were smoldering, burning a hole straight through to his ribs. He could not fathom how every last person in that glittering room didn’t see them glowing through his jacket. Every time he turned to ask a new partner to dance, he half expected her to flinch back and spit the word coward at him for all the world to hear. It didn’t even scare him. He would not have cared, if the whole room found out. He had already lost Ethne, after all.

His eyes never left her as the night wore on, and he scanned every other woman in attendance, as he already knew he would, and found none to compare. They were pretty, graceful, some even strikingly beautiful—but Ethne stood apart, sharp and luminous, her broad clear forehead, perfectly arched brows, steady silver-grey eyes, full red lips that could curve soft with a joke or set firm as steel when she made up her mind, and that easy, unapologetic royal bearing that made her stand out in any crowd. Harry stared at her through the blur of dancers, sick with despair that he had ever gotten to call her his.

Only once did her mask crack, for less than a heartbeat. They were mid-dance, and she glanced past his shoulder to the tall windows, where pale, cold dawn light was seeping around the edges of the blinds. “Look,” she said, and the next second all her weight slumped hard against his arm. The colour drained from her face, leaving it drawn, grey and exhausted. Her eyes squeezed shut tight then fluttered open again. He was sure she was about to faint. “The morning’s finally here,” she said, her voice as worn out as her face. “I wonder if it’s right, for anyone to hurt this bad.”

“Hush,” Harry whispered, stepping in front of her to block the gaze of the other dancers until she could get her feet under her again. “Courage. Just a few more minutes. Only a very few.”

“Thank you,” she said, quiet and grateful, and the whirl of the dance swept them back up again. It was a bizarre, lopsided moment—him urging her to hold on, her thanking him for the help—but neither of them noticed the irony, not then. Ethne was too raw from hours of holding her grief at bay, and Harry had just seen for the first time how deep the wound he’d dealt her went, in the hollows under her eyes and the raw pain she’d let slip for a second. He stopped telling himself he’d lost her, stopped thinking about his own loss entirely. That line she’d said—*I wonder if it’s right, for anyone to hurt this bad*—echoed in his ears, ringing so loud he thought he’d hear it the day he died, above all the noise of everyone gathered around his deathbed. It was his sentence, he knew. It wasn’t right.

The ball broke up not long after that. The last carriage rumbled down the drive, and the overnight guests drifted off to the smoking room or trundled up the stairs to their bedrooms, according to custom. Harry lingered in the entry hall with Ethne, and she knew exactly why he was waiting.

“There’s no need,” she said, her back to him as she lit a taper for her bedroom candle. “I already told my father. Told him everything.”

Harry bowed his head, accepting that. “Even so, I have to wait to see him.”

Ethne didn’t argue, but she turned to look at him fast, her brows pulled tight in confused perplexity. Waiting for her father in the wake of what he’d done felt like the kind of act only someone with real nerve would do. She felt a flicker of sharp apprehension when the study door creaked open, and Dermod Eustace’s heavy footsteps thudded across the floor.

Dermod walked straight up to Harry, and for once, every line of his face showed how old he was. He stood staring right into Harry’s eyes, his expression muddled, bewildered, two times opening his mouth to speak and no sound coming out. Finally he turned to the hall table, lit his own candle, then lit Harry’s too. He turned back, and moved so fast Ethne stepped forward, ready to get between the two of them—only he didn’t do anything but stare at Harry again, for what felt like an eternity. At last he lifted his candle. “Well—” he started, then stopped. He snipped the wick with a pair of candle scissors, tried again. “Well—” and stopped again. Lighting the candles had given him no clue what to say. He stared down into the flame instead of at Harry’s face, for just as long, and found not a single sharp word, not a single angry rebuke, nothing at all that fit. At last he spoke, lamely, gruff with suppressed feeling:
“If you need any whiskey, stamp twice hard on the floor. The staff knows the signal.”

He trundled up the stairs, slow and heavy. That quiet, merciful refusal to yell or call him out was harsher punishment for Harry Feversham than any shouted insult could have ever been.

It was full daylight by the time Ethne finally locked the door of her bedroom behind her. She dragged the blinds all the way up, threw the windows wide open, and the sharp, cool dawn air hit her like a draught of cold spring water. She looked out over the grounds, over a world not yet stitched with colour, and saw exactly what the rest of her life was going to look like. The tall dark trees stood black against the pale sky; the winding paths were a dead, stark white; the lawns lay dull and grey, even with dew strung over the grass like fine frost. It was a far louder world than it looked, though—blackbirds called back and forth from branches and the undergrowth, and the Lennon River gurgled and sang soft as it curved below the overhanging boughs.

Ethne stepped back from the windows. She had work to do before she slept. She was determined, in that stubborn, thorough way of hers, to cut every last tie to Harry Feversham all at once, so that when she woke up later that day, she would not stumble on a single thing that could bring his memory back to her. She started gathering up every gift he had ever given her—until she stopped. Every trinket, every small present, had a story, and every memory of the happy days before that terrible night rose up one by one, slow and soft, as she held them. She decided to keep one tiny, worthless thing of his. At first she picked a penknife he’d lent her once, that she’d forgotten to give back—but she dropped it fast. She was Irish, after all, she didn’t go in for superstitions, but she was never one to tempt bad luck for no reason. She picked his small framed photograph instead, locked it deep in the back of a drawer where no one would ever find it, then packed all the rest of his gifts carefully in a box, sealed it shut, addressed it, and left it by the front door for the servants to post that day.

She went back to her room, took all his letters, stacked them neat on her hearth, and lit the edge of the top one. The fire ate through them slow, curling the paper, blacking out his handwriting one line at a time, until all that was left were soft flakes of ash, floating up like feathers, pale white as the ones Harry carried tucked in his jacket. The last embers had barely stopped glowing when she heard a soft, cautious step on the gravel under her window. It was full daylight, but her bedroom candle was still burning on the table beside her, and on instinct she reached out, snuffed it out, and sat dead still, rigid, listening. For a minute she only heard the blackbirds calling and the low singing of the river. Then the footsteps came again, slow, retreating.

Against every choice she’d just made, against every step she’d taken to cut him out, a wave of utter loneliness crashed over her, so big it felt like she was sitting alone on top of a dead, empty world of ruins. She stood up fast, and her eyes fell on the violin case propped in the corner. She pulled it open, relief flooding through her, and not long after, the few overnight guests still half-asleep in their beds heard soft, tender, low music drifting down the hallways. Ethne didn’t remember that the violin in her hands was the rare Guarnerius that Durrance had sent her. All she knew was that she finally had a companion to chase the loneliness away.

It was the night of August 30th, a full month since the Lennon House ball, and the quiet Donegal countryside still burned with the fever of Harry Feversham’s unexplained disappearance. Townsfolk clustered on the steep main street, gentry gossiped over dinner tables, and every half-credible rumour spread like wildfire. Swearers claimed they’d spotted him at five to six the morning after the dance – ten past six, insisted Mrs. Brien O’Brien – still in his rumpled dress uniform, face as white as a shroud, hurrying along the Lennon Bridge causeway like he was running for his life. The village joked a drag-net would be needed to drag him out of whatever hole he’d hidden in; local man Dennis Rafferty turned down salmon at dinner the better part of a week, joking he wasn’t a cannibal, and the line stuck as the town’s favourite catchphrase.

None of their guesses came within a mile of the truth. Only two people in Donegal knew the real story: Ethne Eustace and her father Dermod, who went about their days as if no earthquake had split their world open. Ethne grew softer, quieter, Dermod quicker to snap at every small irritation, and the gossips had free rein to spin whatever wild tales they pleased.

Harry Feversham was three hundred miles away in London that August 30th, as Lieutenant Sutch stumbled across him. All day the city had rippled with conflicting news from Kassassin, out in the Egyptian desert east of Ismailia. Messengers raced through the streets yelling of crushing victories and catastrophic defeats: General Drury-Lowe’s cavalry had charged under moonlight, rolled up Arabi’s left flank, and seized his guns. Rumours blared that a British general was dead, that the York and Lancaster Regiment had been cut to ribbons. By 11 p.m., a silent, tight crowd had packed Pall Mall under the gas lamps, faces tilted pale up at the lit blinds of the War Office. The hush only broke when a shadow moved behind the blinds, sending a thrill of sharp expectation rippling through every onlooker.

Sutch, careful to favour his old Crimean wounded leg, lingered on the outskirts, back propped against the Junior Carlton Club’s parapet, when a hand touched his arm. It was Harry.

His face was haggard, working with raw, unspooling tension, eyes bright as a fever sufferer’s. “I could be out there tonight,” he blurted, voice sharp and shaking. “Think of it. Sitting by a desert campfire, talking over the battle with Jack Durrance. Or dead. It wouldn’t have mattered. I could have been in Egypt tonight.”

Sutch didn’t bombard him with questions. He could see the younger man’s life had shattered completely. He took Harry’s arm and led him straight out of the throng.

“I saw you in the crowd,” Harry went on, “remembered you gave me your card, years ago. I kept it. I always feared I’d need it, that saying trouble shared eases the weight.”

“Come with me,” Sutch said, halting at the steps of the Army and Navy Club. “We’ll find a quiet corner in the upper smoking room –”

Harry flinched hard, stepping fast into the unlit roadway where no lamplight could catch his face. “Not there. Not tonight. I don’t go out in the daylight. Meet me tomorrow night, nine o’clock, corner of Swallow Street. Somewhere no one we know will see us.”

Sutch didn’t push. “I know the perfect spot.”

They dined the next evening at a shadowed corner table in the Criterion grill-room. The low thrum of the electric light generators vibrated through the floor. “Reminds me of a gun-room on a deployment,” Sutch smiled. “We’ll eat first. Then you tell me everything.”

Harry was wary. “You’ve heard no rumours about me?”

“Not a word.”

He let out a long, shuddering breath. He’d spent weeks cooped up in his London rooms, convinced every stranger on the street was staring daggers of contempt at him. Sutch could see the toll the days had taken: sunken black hollows under his eyes, thin haggard cheeks, every movement jittery, his nerves strung so tight they were one twitch from snapping. Sutch refused to let him start his story until the table was cleared and the cigars were out. “Take your time, Harry.”

Harry told the whole truth, no excuses, no exaggeration, his voice slow and flat like he was recounting a stranger’s adventure. He started with that long-ago Crimean night at Broad Place, when his father’s war stories first planted the quiet, gnawing fear in his chest that he might be a coward, and went all the way through the Lennon House ball, the three white feathers arriving at his door, his flight across Lough Swilly at dawn.

“I’ve stayed locked in my rooms all day since,” he said, “listening to the barrack bugles blow under my window. At night I prowl the empty streets, or lie awake counting the Westminster quarter chimes. You know what time the ducks start quacking in St James’s Park? Two a.m., sharp.”

Sutch said nothing, but halfway through the story he raised a hand to cover his face, shielded his eyes. Harry was sure the older man was hiding the scorn he couldn’t bear to show – but when Sutch finally spoke, his voice was thick with remorse, not contempt.

“It’s my fault. I should have spoken that night at Broad Place. Held my tongue, when I should have helped you. I’ll never forgive myself.”

He saw the truth no one else did: Harry’s whole life, he’d been alone with that fear, no one to confide in, no one who understood that a man who overthinks every risk before battle doesn’t break – he fights twice as hard when the time comes, the same way those imaginative soldiers Sutch had served with in Crimea outfought even the toughest rebel fanatics. He quoted the famous lines from *Hamlet*: “Am I a coward? Who calls me villain? Breaks my pate across? Plucks off my beard, and blows it in my face?” and Harry nodded, he knew exactly what he meant.

Then Sutch leaned forward, practical. “You can’t hide in London forever, skulking after dark. That’s the path that leads straight to Wilmington, the deserter your father told us about, the one who drank himself to death in obscurity.”

“It won’t end that way for me,” Harry said, sharp and certain. He pulled his pocket book from his jacket, laid four crisp white feathers out on the tablecloth. “These aren’t marks of my shame. They’re my opportunities. Trench, Castleton, Willoughby – the three who sent them – are all in Egypt. I sail for Egypt tomorrow. I’ll make myself ready, and when each of them faces mortal peril, I’ll be there to pull them out of it. I’ll earn every feather back, one by one.”

Sutch’s face lit up. He’d never thought of such a path, but it was the only possible redemption for Harry. He didn’t waste time worrying about the odds, about how a civilian could slip through the war zone, he was already glowing with quiet pride.

Harry’s voice dropped soft, almost too quiet to hear. “If the three of them take their feathers back… then maybe Ethne will take hers back too. She doesn’t know I’m going. I don’t want her to know, not until it’s done. She told me, just days before the feathers arrived, that if I died in Egypt she’d grieve all her life, but she knew we’d still see each other, afterwards. That’s the hope I’m chasing. If I make this right, maybe we still can.”

He made Sutch swear to secrecy, told him only to tell his father the full truth if word came Harry would never come home, and leave out that last part about Ethne, the part his stern old war hero father would never understand.

The next night, Harry boarded the Calais ferry, the wind howling as wild as the night Jack Durrance had sailed for Egypt. The last thing he saw as the packet pulled away from the pier was Sutch’s face under a gas lamp, standing sentinel in the rain. The lieutenant stayed there long after the ferry’s lights vanished into the dark, until the thud of its paddles faded to silence. Then he limped back through the rain to his hotel, suddenly old, and for the first time in decades, he prayed he’d live long enough to welcome Muriel Graham’s son home, his honour restored, his great fault fully atoned.

Durrance snapped his field glasses shut and strapped them tight into their scuffed leather case at his hip. “No sign of outriders,” he confirmed.
“No one, sir,” Captain Mather echoed. “We move forward.”

Scouts fanned out ahead, the Camel Corps snapped back into formation, the two seven-pounder mountain guns rumbled into position at the rear, and the detachment trundled off the shadowed shale ridgeline of Khor Gwob—thirty-five miles southwest of Suakin—down onto the sunbaked plateau of Sinkat. This was the last full-strength reconnaissance before the British pulled out of the entire Eastern Soudan.
All morning long the camels had jolted up a gully flanked by jagged red precipices, then past mounds of crumbled red rock stretching to the horizon, a dead waste of stone where not a scrap of grass nor a ragged mimosa branch broke the brutal monotony. So when the valley below opened up into dense, lush green bush, it hit the troopers like a cold drink: a stretch of park-like green that made eyes stinging from the glare of bare stone relax instantly. Every man sat straighter in his saddle.
The column moved in a diagonal line across the plateau toward the distant peaks of Erkoweet, a silent thread of camels on a plain so quiet it felt unborn. It was eleven o’clock. The sun hung high in a colourless, cloudless sky, camel shadows shrank to small dark puddles beneath their feet, and the white sand glinted bright as a beach on the far Scilly Isles. Not a breath of wind stirred the foliage, and the sharp, motionless shadows of branches lay on the ground so perfect they looked like whole boughs dropped by some long-ago storm. The only sounds were the sharp clink of weapon hardware, the soft, constant padding of camel hooves, and the sudden whirr of a covey of pigeons bursting into flight as the patrol passed.
Life was there, though—silent, skittish. Now and then, on the smooth curves of sand winding between the shrubs like empty carriage drives, a herd of gazelle would leap up far ahead, flashes of dappled brown and white, streaking for the cover of the hills without a sound.
“Old caravan road south to Erkoweet and the Khor Baraka,” Durrance murmured, half to himself. “The Suakin merchants built their summer homes here once.”
Mather pointed to a crumble of stone ahead. “And that’s where Tewfik fought. Died there with all four hundred of his men, holding the fort against Osman Digna.”
The column marched for three more hours. It was May, and the sun blazed so hot no man could keep his eyes open to the glare. The troopers’ earlier alertness was long gone; they rocked drowsily in their saddles, daydreaming of evening, of cool silver starlight. The camels minced on, bobbing their heads in their usual odd, smirksing motion, until Durrance reined his mount short. A hundred yards ahead rose a broken mud wall, empty window holes framing the pale sky.
“The fort,” he said.
Three years had passed since Osman Digna’s men had stormed and gutted it. In that time, fast-growing thorn and bush had crept so close to the roofless ruins that a traveller could stumble right on it unawares, just like Childe Roland stumbling on the Dark Tower on the empty plain. Only the stretch of sand out front stayed clear, running to three great, spreading Gemeiza trees standing spaced apart like sentinels at the wells beyond.
The men unsaddled their camels in the shade at the bush line, pitched their mess tins to cook, and Durrance and Mather circled the ruins. At the southern corner, Durrance stopped short.
“Halloa.”
“Some Arab camped here recently,” Mather said, spotting the grey heap of ash on a blackened stone.
“Very recently.” Durrance prodded the ash with his boot. A half-charred twig glowed bright red under the ash. He set his heel on it, and a thin wisp of smoke curled up into the air.
Inside the fort, saplings sprouted from every crack in the mud walls, every crevice in the earthen floor. At the rear, a steep slope of packed earth and a deep, dry moat still marked the old defensive works. Durrance sat on the parapet overlooking the moat, pigeons wheeling overhead, thinking of the long months Tewfik must have spent right there, staring out at the Suakin pass, scanning every inch of the hills for the glint of distant weapons of relief that never came.
Mather dropped down beside him, his mood far lighter. “The Guards are already steaming out past the coral reefs for Suez. A week, and we’re gone too. Godforsaken stretch of country, isn’t it?”
“I’m coming back,” Durrance said.
Mather blinked, confused. “Why?”
“I like it. I like the people.”
Mather thought that was an odd taste, but he knew it explained Durrance’s rapid rise through the ranks. That quiet sympathy had served him better than raw ambition: he understood the tribes’ old hatred of brutal Turkish rule, their complicated mix of genuine fanaticism and forced pretence of loyalty to Osman Digna’s raiding parties. Three years in the campaign, he knew the Soudanese better than far cleverer, faster officers.
“This can’t be the end,” he went on. “Everyone in Egypt knows it. I won’t leave a job half finished. I’ll be back here in three months, as soon as they send the next expedition out.”
The sun blazed on. The men stretched out in the shade and slept. The afternoon was as dead silent as the morning. For a long while neither man spoke, until Durrance’s gaze sharpened, fixing on the shrubbery beyond the moat. He saw Mather was staring in the exact same spot.
“What were you thinking about?” Durrance asked suddenly.
Mather laughed, sheepish. “Planning my first dinner in London. At Epitaux, all to myself. Ice-cold watermelon to start. You?”
“Wondering why the pigeons, that settled on every other tree ten minutes ago, are still circling that one particular bush over the ditch, right of those two small acacias. Don’t point.”
Sure enough, every other pigeon roosted on the branches like purple fruit. Only over that one patch of thicket did they circle, fluttering and squawking in alarm.
“Flush that cover,” Durrance said. “Take a dozen men, circle it quiet.”
He stayed on the parapet watching as six men crept left, six more right. Before they could close the ring, the bushes shook violently: a Kabbabish Arab burst out, a roll of yellow dammar wrapped around his waist, armed with a flat-headed spear and a cowhide shield, and sprinted out onto the open sand. He ran only a dozen yards before Mather barked an order, and the man stopped dead, as if he knew he could not outrun twelve rifles. He walked back calmly, no defiance, no cringing servility, and explained in slow, careful Arabic that his name was Abou Fatma, he was friendly to the British, and he was travelling to Suakin.
“Why did you hide?” Durrance asked.
“Safer. I knew you were my friends. How was I to know you would not shoot first, and ask who I was later?”
“You speak English,” Durrance said, switching to the language.
“Few words. Learned in Khartum.”
He sent the rest of the men away, and he and Abou Fatma talked alone on the glacis for almost an hour. When the conversation ended, the Arab climbed down the slope, crossed the moat, and set off for the hills.
Durrance gave the order to move out. The men refilled their water skins, saddled their grumbling, snarling camels, and the detachment turned northwest, marching along the edge of the hills they had descended that morning. The bushes thinned, giving way to a waste of black stone dotted with yellow tasselled mimosas. He called Mather over to his side.
“That Arab told me a wild story. He was Gordon’s personal servant in Khartum. Back at the start of 1884, Gordon gave him a secret dispatch to carry to Berber, to wire the contents back to Cairo. But the day he arrived, Berber fell. They locked him up in the town jail the next day, but in the single hour he was free, he hid the letter in a crack in a mud house wall. It’s still there, as far as he knows.”
“They’d have tortured it out of him if it had been found,” Mather said.
“Precisely. And he has the purple chain scars on his ankles to prove he was locked up there, just like he says. Escaped three weeks ago.”
The column turned left, climbing back up a shale slope into the hills. “A letter from Gordon,” Durrance said quietly. “Probably scribbled on the roof of his palace, right next to his big telescope, pausing every other line to scan over the palm trees for the smoke of relief steamers. Buried in a mud wall in Berber, all these months. We could have pushed west to the Nile last year, before Khartum fell, before Berber surrendered. Command would not let us go.”
The sun dipped behind the hills. The sky above them turned deep violet, then blazed with iridescent reds and oranges, then softened to warm rose, fading at last to a glowing pale emerald green lit from below the horizon. Durrance was not looking at the sunset. The thought of Gordon, that lonely, stubborn, brilliant soldier, abandoned by the bureaucrats in Cairo, had struck a deep, reverent chord in him.
Darkness fell. The camels quickened their pace, cicadas shrilled from every tuft of grass, and the column trundled toward the well of Disibil. Durrance lay awake long that night on his camp bed spread out under the stars, counting the years since he had sailed from Dover: Kassassin, Tel-el-Kebir, the mad dash down the Red Sea, the battle of Tamai, the Hadendowa warriors leaping through the breach in McNeil’s zareba. He was only thirty, a brevet lieutenant-colonel, with three months leave coming up.
“A week more,” Mather mumbled half-asleep from his bedroll.
“I’m coming back,” Durrance laughed. “I have three months to see my friends. I’ll track down Harry Feversham and Ethne, probably in London first, ride the Row once more. But my work’s here, in the Soudan. I’ll come back.”
He rolled over and fell asleep, as the stars wheeled overhead.
* * * * *
That same night, Abou Fatma of the Kabbabish tribe slept under a overhanging boulder on the Khor Gwob. He rose at dawn, and walked the broad flat plains all the way to the white-walled coastal city of Suakin. He told his story of Gordon’s hidden dispatch first to Captain Willoughby, the temporary deputy governor, and then once more in the crowded native bazaar, loud enough for a Greek man sitting outside a nearby coffee shop to overhear.
The Greek called Abou Fatma over, promised him a full jug of strong merissa sorghum beer, and got him to tell the whole story a fourth time, slow and clear. He watched Abou Fatma draw a map of the mud house in Berber in the dust at his feet, none of them knowing the Mahdist forces had torn the old town down and rebuilt it entirely to the north.
“Tell no one else this but me,” the Greek said, jingling a handful of silver dollars in his palm. The two men huddled in the shadow of the coffee stall, talking long and quiet.
The Greek was Harry Feversham—the old friend Durrance had just been daydreaming of visiting in Donegal. After three long years of waiting, the opportunity Harry had been chasing to clear his name had finally arrived.

John Durrance stepped off the steamer from Egypt one sun-dappled June morning, and by that same afternoon he’d laced up his scuffed campaign boots and taken the first unhurried walk of his exile back home, wandering straight into Hyde Park. He sank onto a bench beneath an old oak, staring half-mesmerized at the easy grace of the women drifting past in their crisp pastel summer gowns. He was a solitary, striking figure: skin baked dark as worn leather from three years under desert sun, the quiet, watchful tightness around his eyes that marks every man who has spent years posted to the far, unforgiving edges of the Empire, cut off from all soft, familiar comforts of home.

Of all the strollers that sauntered past, only one smiled at him. When he stood to stretch his legs, Mrs. Adair glided to his side, and ran a quick, almost furtive glance over him from head to toe, as if checking a fragile treasure for cracks. She was comparing the weathered soldier in front of her to the Durrance she’d known three years prior, searching for every small shift time had carved into him, her gaze sharp with quiet unspoken anxiety. All Durrance noticed, though, was that she wore unrelieved black. She read the unasked question on his face before he could speak.
“My husband died eighteen months ago,” she said evenly. “Thrown from his horse mid-run with the Pytchley. Killed on the spot.”
“I never heard the news,” Durrance said, awkward and ill at ease. “I’m terribly sorry.”
Mrs. Adair sat down on the empty chair next to him and said no more. She was a woman of confounding silences, her pale, classically placid face giving no clue at all to the thoughts churning behind her eyes. Durrance fidgeted, out of his depth. He remembered Adair as a jolly, mild man whose only defining trait had been his unreserved devotion to his wife, but he’d never once stopped to wonder how the woman beside him had felt about that shadow of a husband. He couldn’t dredge up enough genuine grief to fill the silence, so he pivoted quickly.
“Are Harry Feversham and his wife in town yet?”
Mrs. Adair paused for a long beat. “Not yet,” she said, then corrected herself sharply, voice hurrying as if she’d blurted a half-lie by accident. “No— the marriage never took place.”

Durrance was not a man given to gasps or exclamations, even when shocked. “I don’t understand. Why was it called off?”
Mrs. Adair snapped a sharp glance at him, as if she was searching for some hidden trick behind his steady, unflappable tone. “I don’t know. Ethne can keep a secret tighter than a locked safe when she chooses. It broke the night of the dance at Lennon House.”
Durrance turned to face her fully. “The night right before I sailed for the Soudan three years back?”
“Yes.”
“Then a lot of odd pieces fell into place that very night I stood on Dover pier,” Durrance said slowly. “I didn’t know the engagement was broken, but I saw a man on that pier as the Channel ferry cast off, face haggard and wild with despair. That was Harry, wasn’t it? He left the country that same night, no one’s seen him since?”
Mrs. Adair shrugged. “Not a soul I’ve spoken to. He’s vanished completely.”
Durrance thought that over for a long minute, then asked, a faint, uncharacteristic tremor in his voice, “And Miss Eustace? She’s married now, I suppose?”
Mrs. Adair shook her head. “No.”
“Still living at Lennon House?”
“Lennon House burned to the ground a year ago,” she said, and Durrance’s eyes went wide. She told him the whole ridiculous, catastrophic story of Constable Bastable, the toddy recipe that required a single simmered oyster to be perfect, the two men getting roaring drunk at two a.m. and knocking over the spirit lamp under the saucepan. Half the house burned to ash before they could call the fire brigade, the rest was gutted and ruined by thousands of gallons of hose water. Dermod Eustace, already drowning under a mountain of gambling debts, went completely bankrupt the second the news spread. He caught a brutal chill from the freezing hoses that wrecked his once robust health for good, and Ethne dragged him off to a tiny, isolated mountain village in Donegal, all their land and old home lost forever.
“The village is called Glenalla,” she added, not looking at him, her voice flat as if she was forcing herself to say the words against some unspoken reservation. “A track leads up to it off the main road halfway between Rathmullen and Ramelton.” She stood, held out her hand. “Will I see you around town?”
“For a while, yes,” Durrance said.
Mrs. Adair raised an eyebrow, confused. She couldn’t fathom why the clearly smitten soldier wasn’t catching the first train north to Donegal the second she gave him the directions. She saw him loitering in the inner courtyard of his Service Club for weeks after that, camped out under a great spreading oak, cornering every single old friend of Harry Feversham’s that wandered through, pestering them for scraps of news about the vanished man. None of them knew a thing. He’d even trundled out to Surrey to call on Harry’s father, General Feversham, but the second he so much as breathed his missing son’s name, the old veteran’s face set hard as plaster, blank and unyielding as a mask, and refused to utter another word on the subject. Durrance headed back to London no closer to answers than he’d left.

Toward the end of June, a limping retired naval lieutenant named Sutch hobbled into the courtyard, spotted Durrance, hesitated, and immediately turned to scuttle away at top speed. Durrance shot to his feet.
“Mr. Sutch,” he called. “Sure you haven’t forgotten an old acquaintance?”
Sutch froze, turned around, flustered. “Colonel Durrance! Of course I remember you. Old man’s memory’s like a leaky whaler, though— half my cargo of recollections swamped at port.”
Durrance didn’t buy the act for a second. “We met at Broad Place. I’ve been waiting here a full month for you. I need two answers: why was Harry’s engagement broken off, and where on earth is he now?”
Sutch’s eyes gleamed for a split second with relief. He’d always been terrified someone would spill Harry’s secret to Durrance, and clearly the decorated soldier still hadn’t heard a whisper of his friend’s disgrace. “I can answer both,” he admitted, scratching at his beard, “but I won’t. I swore a solemn oath to Harry before he vanished that I’d hold my tongue.”
“Harry’s my friend,” Durrance said, quiet and steady. “I’m not asking out of idle curiosity. I won’t press you for your secrets. But I have a different question. A much harder one for me to say out loud.” His sunburnt cheeks flushed dark. “Would it be disloyalty to Harry, if I courted Miss Eustace? If I asked her to marry me?”
The question hit Sutch like a fist to the jaw. He stared at Durrance, suddenly reeling. He’d nursed a quiet, fierce, motherly kind of protectiveness over Harry for years, daydreaming of the day the disgraced boy would come back from wherever he was hiding, restored to honor, win Ethne back, take his old place in the world like a king retaking his throne. Now here was Durrance, war hero, steady, brave, the kind of man women fell for hard, ready to step in and claim the life Harry had thrown away.
He was tempted to lie, more tempted than he’d ever been in his life. All he had to do was say yes, it would be disloyal, and Durrance would head straight back to his posting in the Soudan, leave Harry’s path completely clear. He pictured Harry thousands of miles away, toiling under the blistering desert sun, suffering for his honor, then looked back at the comfortable, decorated soldier in front of him. For a second he almost broke his oath entirely, spilled every last secret, told Durrance the full story of the four feathers.
“Well?” Durrance prompted, quiet.
Sutch sighed, heavy with regret. Harry had never wanted Ethne to wait for him forever, after all. “No,” he said. “There would be no disloyalty.”
That very same evening, Durrance boarded the overnight train bound for Holyhead, heading straight for Donegal.

The farm hunkers a mile above the village, buried deep in a wild stretch of Donegal moorland. Heather creeps right up to its back garden wall, and the rutted bridle path peters out dead at the front porch. A ring of bare hills circles it on three sides, shifting color so fast you can spot new gradients of emerald, purple and rusted russet from one hour to the next, cupping the little house like a lifeboat adrift on empty land. No trees break their slopes, granite knuckling through moss and heather, and Durrance could almost swear they drape themselves in those vivid hues on purpose, just to please the girl who lives under their shelter.
The windows face the long, rolling fall of land sloping down to Lough Swilly’s inlet. From the parlor, you can trace the view past thin thickets, patchwork tilled fields, and whitewashed cottages dotted like scattered bone shards, to the tall dark woods fringing the water, where glints of bright loch surface blink between the boughs, and gulls hang poised on the wind before diving for fish.
Durrance rode up the rutted track one midsummer afternoon, and knew the house the moment its first notes drifted to him through the open windows: a violin, playing so soft it felt like a personal welcome. His hand froze mid-rein, and that quiet, stubborn hope he’d nursed for months swelled so big in his chest it stopped his breath. He tied his bay to a gatepost and stepped through the gate.
Rough and unadorned on the outside, the house was all warmth inside. The parlor he was shown into, hung with polished brasses and glowing dark oak, matched the bright high summer afternoon outside. Durrance could picture it easy on a winter’s night: blinds drawn tight, hearth blazing red, wind screaming off the hills and rapping sharp at the windowpanes.
Ethne greeted him with no trace of surprise at all. “I knew you’d come,” she said, and a smile broke bright across her face. Durrance laughed out loud as they shook hands, and she followed his gaze straight to the violin resting on the table at her elbow: pale aged wood, a distinct patched mark right by the bridge where a chunk of worm-eaten wood had been carved out and replaced.
“It’s yours,” she said. “You were off in Egypt. I couldn’t very well send it after you there.”
“I’ve hoped ever since I found out you’d kept it that you’d hold onto it,” Durrance said.
“Clearly I have.” She met his eyes straight on, no flinch, no downcast gaze. “I kept it months back. There was a stretch where I needed something solid to remind me I had friends I could trust. A thing you can hold in your hands helps more than words, sometimes. I was glad to have it.”
Durrance lifted the violin off the table, handling it careful as a relic. “You’ve played it? The Mozart overture, the one we used to mess around with that night before I shipped out?”
She laughed. “I remember that. I have played it, but only lately. For a long time I locked my violin away. It talked too loud about all the things I was trying to forget.”
Durrance fetched his luggage over from the nearby town of Rathmullen the next day, and settled in for a week’s stay. Not a single word about Harry Feversham passed between the two of them the whole time, even though they were thrown together every waking hour. Old Dermod, Ethne’s father, was far more broken than Mrs. Adair’s letters had led Durrance to expect. His speech had shrunk to one-word grunts, his frame was gaunt and shrunken, his old tweeds hanging loose and baggy on his limbs, his temper that used to flare hot as a guttering torch all gone dim. He dozed by the hearth even in the height of July, his longest walk the slow quarter-mile trudge to the tiny bare grey church perched on a mound within sight of the windows, and even that left him gasping for breath. His ancient collie dog seemed to age in lockstep with him, the two of them shambling side by side like two old men fading out of the world together.
Durrance and Ethne tramped the hills every day, rain or shine, her face flushed bright, her eyes sharp and eager as she showed him every hidden stream and craggy outcrop, making him swear her moorland was the fairest stretch of ground on earth. Evenings, she’d sit with her back half turned to him, draw the bow across the violin strings, and make the instrument sing of every high, bright peak and dark, deep hollow they’d walked that day. Durrance watched her arm sweep, saw the total absorption on her face, and counted the odds of winning her for his own. He didn’t have Lieutenant Sutch’s easy confidence that he’d succeed – the ghost of Harry Feversham loomed far too large between them. He knew Ethne was no soft woman who’d be softened by wealth or comfort, either. Even after her family’s lands were gone, Lennon House reduced to ruins, she was still the beloved princess of that countryside, every local running to greet her the moment she stepped out the door.
But she enjoyed his company, frank and unguarded, and she badgered him for every tiny detail of his three years’ service in the East. No story was too small for her, her eyes never leaving his face, that quiet smile on her lips approving every word he said. Durrance couldn’t work out what she was doing: she was treating him like a dark lantern, prying the slide back slow to see if there was real flame burning inside him, or just empty dark.
On his last day, they sat together on a hillside overlooking a tiny gorge, where a stream of clear black water tumbled over rock ledges and churned in deep cold pools.
“I rode four straight days through the desert one time, surrounded by mirages,” Durrance told her. “Lagoons so still and glassy-edged you could almost ride your camel knee-deep into them before they dissolved into nothing but glaring sand. You can’t imagine that heat. Every stone on the horizon shakes like a heliograph, you can see the heat rolling breast-high across the dunes, fast as this stream right here. Till the sun sinks level with your eyes, right in front of you. And the nights that follow? Total silence, a cool wind blowing all the way across the empty horizon, and your bed laid out under a dome of stars so thick you feel like you could reach up and pluck one down. That country gets under your skin. It’s like the Southern Cross – four ordinary stars when you first spot them, but after a week you’re looking for them every night, and you miss them like old friends when you sail back north.”
He propped himself up on one elbow and turned to face her. “You know, I never feel alone out there in all that empty space. I feel closer to the people I care about than I ever do in a crowded London drawing room. I used to imagine you there, sometimes. You’d have loved it. The pre-dawn marches in the dark, the campfires at night, you’d fit right in. I used to lie awake out there, wondering how you were getting on.”
“Are you going back?” she asked him.
Durrance didn’t answer right away, the low roar of the torrent thrumming all around them. When he spoke, all the brightness had drained out of his voice. “To Wadi Halfa. Two years. That’s the posting, anyway.”
Ethne knelt on the grass right beside him. “I shall miss you,” she said.
A long quiet stretched between them. “You don’t have to,” he said. He saw her flinch back, sink down onto her heels, completely still. That total lack of movement was her version of a cry of raw pain, Durrance knew, and his hopes crashed straight to his boots.
“I know you never do anything half-heartedly,” he said, trying to ease the tension. “You don’t fall in love easy, and you don’t forget easy, either.”
Her voice came soft, tight with pain. “I remember enough to make that a very hard thing to hear. One day I might tell you everything that happened at that ball three years ago, and then you’ll understand why I’m so upset right now. All I can say now is this: I’m terrified I was, in some small way, the cause of a man’s ruin. Not that I was to blame, but if he’d never known me, his career never would have ended so abruptly. I was told it wasn’t my fault, but that was generosity talking, I think. The fear won’t leave me. It keeps me up at night. And then you turn up, the man I respect more than almost anyone, and you’re ready to throw your whole perfect career away for me? I won’t let that happen. I won’t ruin two men.”
Durrance’s hope blazed bright again, misreading every word she said. She hadn’t said no, he thought. She hadn’t said she didn’t love him.
“Wadi Halfa’s not the only place a soldier can find work,” he said, leaning forward, passion spilling over. “Five years I’ve loved you, Ethne. Ever since that day I turned up at the ferry on Lough Swilly, hell-bent for Letterkenny, and your father dragged me back to the house for supper. Five years since I first heard you play that violin. You’re the true north of my life. I was only planning to spend my days in the desert because I thought you were married. I never dared hope.”
Ethne drew back from his outstretched hands, gentle but unshakable. “No.” She saw him clearer than he saw himself: the born wanderer’s restlessness, the craving for wide empty spaces, strange skies, roads that never end. Passion might mask it for a little while, but eventually the quiet moors would turn to a prison for him, and he’d grow to resent her. This was her whole world, but it would strangle him.
She stood up, brushing grass off her skirt. “We’ll say goodbye here, out in the open. We’ll still be good friends even if there’s three thousand miles between us.” They shook hands.
“I’ll be back in a year,” Durrance said. “Can I come see you then?”
Her smile gave him permission. “I’d hate to lose your friendship entirely. You can write to me, if you like, about your travels, the things you see out in the East.”
She waved him off, standing tall on the hillside until he disappeared over the crest. She walked slowly back to the house, pulled his old violin out of its case, but before she could touch the strings she knew what it was, snapped the case shut sharp, locked it away in a drawer. At the very bottom of the drawer, hidden under old letters, was a photograph. She stared at it for a long, long time, her face soft with a grief she’d never been able to outrun.
Down by the gate, old Dermod was waiting for him, shivering a little in the cool wind. “Walk a few yards with me, Colonel Durrance,” he croaked. “I’ve a word to say.” He walked slow, leaning on Durrance’s arm. “You got any news of Harry Feversham? Before you leave?”
“None,” Durrance said.
Dermod’s voice was tight with a strange, conflicting longing. “He struck us a cruel blow. Ought to curse him, call him a black-throated coward. But I still want to know how the man’s getting on. You were his friend.”
Durrance rode off toward Rathmullen, that quiet anger at his old friend bubbling slow in his chest. He’d be at Wadi Halfa soon, watching the Nile roll past his verandah, through the long sweltering Sudanese summer of May, turning the mystery of Harry Feversham’s disappearance over and over in his head, wondering what had really happened to break everything so badly.

That sweltering May, Lieutenant Durrance lifted his eyes from the outpost of Wadi Halfa and let his thoughts drift longingly north toward home. But 500 miles due south, across the brutal expanse of the Nubian desert and the jagged, rock-choked stretch known as the Belly of Stones, the events that would upend his and Harry Feversham’s fates unfolded completely unknown to him.

Halfway along the long abandoned caravan track linking Berber to Suakin, the Wells of Obak sink deep into a landscape of shifting, honey-colored sand mounds. To the east, a thin belt of scraggly acacia trees marks the divide between dunes and a hard, stony plain dotted with sharp granite hills. To the west, 58 utterly waterless miles of perfectly flat desert stretch all the way to the Nile and Berber, so level that a knee-high tuft of grass a mile off looks like a full-grown shade tree calling out to sun-scorched travelers, and a random pile of roadside stones looms like a substantial hill.

No spot on the whole desert could have felt more desolate that May. The sun blazed down unforgivingly from six in the morning, while frigid winds swept across the dunes all night, playing with the sand like a mischievous spirit: building house-high pyramids of grit only to level them hours later, tunneling deep valleys, silting over entire slopes, so that no trace of the day before remained when the sun rose. Only vultures and sand-grouse claimed the spot as their permanent territory, their tracks erased almost as soon as they landed. To add to the eerie loneliness, bleached camel skeletons dotted the dunes, leftover from the caravans that once halted at the wells, and the crumbled remains of a hoop-framed reed hut testified to the Arab goatherds that once made their home there. Now the sun rose and set over an empty, endless stretch of sand, and a thick, unbroken silence hung over the dunes like fog over the sea, making the whole place feel like it was holding its breath, waiting for something.

But that May, one man hid by the wells, keeping his presence entirely secret. Every dawn, Abou Fatma led two pure-bred Bisharin riding camels out of the acacia belt, watered them carefully at the well mouth, then sat on the sand beside it for three full hours, staring north toward Berber. At the end of his watch, he led the camels back to the shade, fed them sweet dhoura grain spread across a clean cloth, and vanished back into the trees for the rest of the day, never showing himself to the open desert. For five straight dawns he kept the routine, with no sign of a single traveler crossing the dunes.

On the sixth dawn, just as he was about to retreat back to his shelter, he saw a man and a donkey crest the highest sand ridge, their silhouettes sharp against the pale pink sky. The Arab froze, first locking eyes on the donkey’s distinctive grey coat, half rising to his feet. Then he spotted the driver: his jellab was pulled up over his face to block the sun, but his bare legs were a deep, polished ebony, half-buried in the sand. The messenger was a negro tribesman, no threat. Abou Fatma settled back down, affecting total indifference, not even turning his head as he heard the man’s soft footfalls pad across the sand behind him.

“Salam aleikum,” the negro called as he stopped, setting down his two spears and hide shield on the sand to sit at the Arab’s side.
Abou Fatma bowed his head. “Aleikum es salam. I wait for you.”
“Two days past, a Bisharin man named Moussa Fedil found me starving in the Berber market,” the negro said. “He fed me, then ordered me to bring this grey donkey straight to Abou Fatma at the Wells of Obak.”
Abou Fatma glanced lazily at the donkey, as if noticing it for the very first time. “Tayeeb. The donkey is mine.” He fell still, uninterested, as if the man’s errand was already complete.
But the messenger did not move. “Moussa Fedil waits for me in the Berber market at dawn three days from now. Give me a token to prove I fulfilled his charge, so he will pay me the wage he promised.”
Abou Fatma pulled a small knife from the back of his waist sash, picked up a dry stick off the sand, and cut three neat notches into each end. “This will be your proof.” He handed it over. The negro tied the stick tight into a corner of his wrap, filled his water skin from the well, slung it over his shoulder, picked up his weapons, and trundled up the sand ridge, vanishing over the far side.

As soon as the man was out of sight, Abou Fatma shot to his feet, moving fast. Six days prior, Harry Feversham had set off from Obak to cross that 58-mile stretch of waterless desert to Berber, and this very same grey donkey had carried his water skins and supplies. He led the donkey deep into the acacia trees, tied it to a thick trunk, and ran his fingers over its left shoulder. There he found a tiny, neat incision, stitched shut with fine thread. He sliced through the stitches, pried the edges of the small wound open, and pulled out a package no bigger than a postage stamp, wrapped in thin goat bladder. Inside the bladder, folded tiny, was a note scrawled in Arabic. Abou Fatma had been General Gordon’s personal servant for years, and the old hero had taught him to read. The note read:
“The old ruins of Berber are gone, and a new town with wide streets has sprung up in their place. I cannot tell Yusef’s old house ruins apart from a hundred other gutted homes, and no man named Yusef sells rock salt in the bazaar anymore. Wait for me one more week.”

The man who’d sent that note was Harry Feversham. Disguised in a tattered, patched Dervish jubbeh, his skin stained dark, his hair matted and gummed into clumps that hung down his neck like seaweed, he moved through the wide, half-finished streets of the new Berber every day, pretending to go about ordinary business as he hunted for the hidden spot where he’d buried a cache of sensitive letters. A mile out from the new town, half-buried in drifting sand, the old Berber was nothing but a warren of gutted ruins: all front walls pulled down, roofs stripped away, only crumbling inner partitions left standing, so that Harry wandered its narrow lanes at night through rows of what looked like five-foot tall crumbling walls, indistinguishable from one ruin to the next, foxes digging their burrows in the base of every remaining partition.

He’d planned the whole trip for one single night: slip in through the gates at dusk, slip back out before the first grey light of dawn broke. Now he was trapped in the heart of a Dervish stronghold, forced to mingle in crowds of fanatical warriors, dreading every stray conversation lest his accent slip and give him away, straining his ears for anyone to mention the name Yusef. Desperation nipped at his heels at every turn, and fear never left him. But from that constant sharp, gnawing terror, a stubborn frenzy grew in him, a fanatical belief no less fierce than the Dervishes’ own faith: the universe could not be so cruel as to dump this weight, the weight of four white feathers, of the girl he loved, on the one man least suited to carry it, just to kill him before he could make amends.

That certainty wavered completely three days after he’d left Abou Fatma at the wells. He crested a sand slope and saw the town for the first time: its dark brown mud walls rising out of the sand like an island, the fronds of great date palms waving green on the far bank of the Nile beyond. Inside those walls, thousands of Dervish fighters milled about. What madness made him think he could stay hidden there even for an hour? he thought. Shouldn’t he turn back, run for the safety of the desert while he still could?

The low mud walls suddenly loomed sinister, and the green of the palms, a welcome sight after days of nothing but sun and rock, looked like a cruel invitation to a trap. He wondered if he had not already done enough to prove his courage, just by coming that far. The sun beat down on him, the strength draining out of his veins as if they had been slit. If he was caught – and he was sure, almost sure he would be caught – he pictured the fanatical faces closing in around him, the brutal mutilations the Dervishes inflicted on spies. He shivered even in the 120-degree heat, so alone that his knees shook. He spun on his heel and ran, sprinting across the naked sunbaked sand in a blind panic, no one chasing him, faint, wordless cries tearing from his throat.

He only ran a few yards before he skidded to a halt. What was the point of four years of training? Of months spent living in Egyptian bazaars, mastering the local dialect? Of that quiet, sure plan he’d laid out to Lieutenant Sutch over dinner in the Criterion Grill, all for nothing? He was still the coward who’d sent in his commission, sent those three white feathers to his comrades, broken Ethne’s heart? He dropped to the sand, pulled his tattered jubbeh over his head, and curled into a little brown heap that was indistinguishable from the grit around him. He shut the sight of Berber out of his eyes, and pulled Ethne’s face to him, across thousands of miles of sea and land, back to their summer home in Donegal. He heard the soft night wind blow through the open front door, heard the distant music from the dancing party down the hall, saw the four feathers flutter to the floor, saw the slow, hurt look creep into her eyes. If he finished this, if he did this hard, dangerous thing, and the even harder thing that still lay ahead, maybe he could one day wipe that trouble off her face. The memory of her voice rang in his ears: *“I have no doubt you and I will see much of one another afterwards.”*

When the sun dipped low below the dunes, painting the sky red, Harry stood up, brushed the sand off his clothes, and walked straight down the slope toward Berber’s gates. He did not hesitate. He stepped through the archway, and vanished into the crowd.

A full month of hard cross-continental travel brought Durrance home to London, where a sealed, black-edged envelope from Ethne waited on the desk of his club. Her note was short: she was staying with Mrs. Adair on Hill Street, and would be grateful if he could find time to call. He showed up at her door the very next afternoon, and found her sitting alone in the quiet drawing room.
“I didn’t write to Wadi Halfa,” she explained at once, her voice soft but steady, “I was sure you’d already be on your way home before any letter I sent could catch you. My father died at the end of last month, in the early hours of a May morning.”
“I feared as much when I saw the black border on your envelope,” Durrance said gently. “I am so sorry. You will miss him terribly.”
“More than I can ever put into words,” she replied, a quiet, deep ache running under every syllable. She went on to tell him the full story of Dermod’s final days: a chill had only triggered the end, not caused it, his life fading slow and soft, no sharp, brutal illness to tear him down. Most strange of all, in those last hours, he had reclaimed the sharp, masterful fire of his younger self.
“We knew he was dying, and he knew it too,” Ethne said. “At seven that afternoon, after he’d spoken to me for a few quiet minutes, he whispered my old retriever’s name. The dog bounded straight up onto the bed, even though his voice was barely a breath, nuzzling his muzzle under my father’s hand with a low whine. Then he told me to leave the two of them completely alone, lock the door behind me. The dog would alert me when it was time to open it again. I obeyed. I stood out in that corridor for six whole hours, watching the last of the afternoon fade to full dark. He wouldn’t let us leave a lamp in his room. I could picture him just the other side of that thin panel, lying still in his old four-poster, face turned towards the Donegal hills he loved, the shadows creeping slow across the walls, the dog curled at his side, no one else with him right to the end. Then, at one in the morning, that howl split the quiet of the whole house.”
She paused for just a beat, the only flicker of raw grief she allowed herself, before she went on, plain and unflinching, no performative sorrow. “Those six hours waiting outside the door were the hardest thing I’ve ever had to do. But it was what he wanted.”
Durrance said nothing, gave her the quiet, unpressured silence that grieving people need far more than empty words. He could easily picture her pressed to the wood of that door, hand pressed to her chest to muffle the thud of her own heart, and a sharp, hot twist of anger coiled in his gut that Feversham’s cowardice had left her to go through that alone, no steady partner at her side.
It was Ethne who broke the quiet first. “Before he sent me out of the room, my father spoke to me about someone. Guess who?”
She looked straight at him, no tell of the answer in her steady eyes or even tone, and a wild, sudden spark of hope caught Durrance’s breath in his throat. “Tell me,” he leaned forward in his chair, barely able to sit still.
“Harry Feversham,” she said.
Durrance jolted back hard in his seat, the hope dying as fast as it had flared. He dropped his gaze to the carpet, not wanting her to see the flash of raw anger cross his face.
“My father was always very fond of him,” she went on softly. “I hoped you might have news of him? Know where he is, what he’s doing these days?”
Durrance stayed silent for a long minute, staring at the pattern under his feet. It struck him as bitterly strange that even the man Feversham had wronged, even the father of the woman he’d abandoned on the eve of their wedding, would carry nothing but kindness for him to his deathbed. He feared his voice would shake if he spoke, but Ethne pressed him for an answer.
“Have you run into him at all, out in Egypt?”
Durrance stood and walked over to the window before he replied, staring out at the London street below to hide his face. Even then, he could not keep the hot edge of anger out of his tone.
“No,” he said. “I never have. And I never want to. He was my friend, once. But I can’t think of that friendship now. All I can think is: if he’d been the man we all believed him to be, you would never have had to wait those six long hours alone in that dark corridor.”
He turned back to face the room, his tone sharp and abrupt. “You’re going back to Glenalla, then?”
“Yes.”
“To live there, alone?”
“Yes.”
Silence hung between them for a while, before Durrance circled around to stand behind her chair. “You once told me you might explain why your engagement to Feversham broke off.”
“But you already know,” Ethne said, confused. “You just said as much a minute ago.”
“I don’t, not the full story. Your father dropped a few hints, the last time I spoke to him, asking for news of Harry, but I don’t know the facts. I want you to tell me.”
Ethne shook her head, leaning forward, elbows resting on her knees. “Not now.”
The quiet settled again, until Durrance spoke once more. “I’ve only got one more year left on my posting at Wadi Halfa. I’ll leave Egypt after that. I don’t see any major Soudan campaigns kicking off any time soon, so there’s no reason for me to stay. Even if you decide to stay on alone at Glenalla.”
Ethne made no pretense of missing the meaning of his words. “We’re neither of us children,” she said. “You have your whole life ahead of you. We both need to be prudent.”
“Prudent, yes,” Durrance snapped, sudden frustration bubbling over. “But the right kind of prudence. The kind that knows it’s worth risking everything for something that matters.”
Ethne didn’t move. Her back was to him, so he could not see her face, and she stayed perfectly still and silent for what felt like an age, before she spoke in a soft, very quiet voice. “Do you want me that badly?”
Before he could answer, she spun around to face him. “Please, try not to,” she said, earnestly. “For this one year, try not to. You have all that work in Egypt to keep you occupied. Try to forget me entirely.” There was no rejection stinging in her tone, only the soft regret of someone who would hate to hurt a friend so loyal, and Durrance walked away with a delusion fixed firm in his head: that the only thing holding her back from saying yes was her fear of ruining his career.
Mrs. Adair stepped into the drawing room before he could argue, so he left soon after. That evening he dined at his club, then sat smoking a cigar under the same old spreading tree in the courtyard where he’d loitered a year before, waiting in vain for any scrap of news about Harry Feversham. The night was almost identical to the one he’d run into limping Lieutenant Sutch: cloudless, star-streaked sky, the soft warm languor of a June summer night, the streetlights gilding the tree leaves to look fresh as spring foliage, the rumble of carriage wheels on the road sounding like the distant roar of the sea.
And just like that night, a man he knew walked straight across the courtyard towards him, no hesitation at all, and sat down on the same bench beside him. It was Captain Mather.
“I was half-hoping I’d spot you,” Mather grinned. “Saw in the evening papers your troop was due back in London around now. You seen the latest news out of Suakin?”
“Can’t say I have,” Durrance said, setting aside the paper he’d been half-reading.
Mather fumbled through the pages, found the short paragraph he was looking for, and handed the paper over. “Remember that final reconnaissance we ran out of Suakin, last year? We stopped at Sinkat Fort at midday, and that Arab informant hiding in the trees behind the ramparts told you that wild story about the Mahdi’s letters to Gordon, hidden in a wall in Berber?”
“Hard to forget,” Durrance nodded.
“Then this will interest you.”
The paragraph was short, no details, barely a summary. It announced that the Mahdi’s letters summoning Gordon to surrender Khartoum and convert to the Mahdist faith, plus Gordon’s sharp, unyielding replies, had been dug out of that hidden wall in Berber and brought safely back to Captain Willoughby, the Deputy Governor of Suakin.
“Must have been some reckless fool who went back to Berber to get those,” Durrance said. “That’s Abou Fatma, the old informant’s name, right?”
“Name’s not mentioned,” Mather shrugged. “Only notes Willoughby took delivery. Hardly worth risking a whole life for a few old letters, if you ask me. Doesn’t change a single fact about the siege, won’t make any difference to anyone, not even historians.”
Durrance agreed, but he was dead wrong. Right there in that courtyard, where he’d once searched for news of Feversham, news of Harry had found him – but he had no idea. He had no clue that that tiny, unremarkable paragraph touched the very roots of his own life, that the reckless man who’d risked everything inside Berber’s walls was the friend he’d once thought dead. He brushed the news aside, and got lost in thoughts of his afternoon with Ethne, no trace of discouragement in him at all. He told himself she’d only asked about Harry because her father’s dying words had brought him up, that the fact she could speak his name so calmly meant he no longer held any power over her. That her request he try to forget her for a year was not a rejection, but a promise: that if he came back to her at the end of it, she would say yes.
A few days later he called back to Hill Street, only to find Mrs. Adair alone. Ethne had left London abruptly, gone back home to Donegal. Mrs. Adair had no idea why she’d rushed off so suddenly, but Durrance knew. She was clearing herself out of his sight, to make it easier for him to move on, just like she’d asked.
What Ethne had forgotten, though, was that she’d never told him to stop writing. That autumn, his letters started arriving from the Soudan again. She was always glad to get them, but they left her troubled. Every line was full of careful, polite reserve, but every now and then a small phrase would slip out – a reference to some trivial throwaway comment she’d made years before, that she’d forgotten entirely – that made it crystal clear he had not forgotten her at all. A bright, unmistakeable hopefulness ran through every page, as if his whole world had burst into new color and sound.
Ethne had never shaken off the heavy, lingering guilt that one man – Harry – had had his whole life destroyed, all because of her. She was determined that would never happen to Durrance. But the letters forced a new, unanswerable question to the front of her mind: how? How could she keep that from happening? She walked the Glenalla hills with his letters crumpled in her pocket, turning the question over and over, and never found an answer. Even her old violin, the one that had always helped her untangle her thoughts before, could not soothe the puzzle out of her this time.

The full May moon hung low over Wadi Halfa, bleaching all but the brightest planets from the sky and gilding the sluggish Nile until it spread like a sheet of burnished silver below the riverbank. Three officers had dragged wicker lounge chairs out to a grassy knoll above the bank, smoking in thick, companionable silence while bullfrogs boomed from a reed-choked mid-river island. The desert across the water stretched endless, its white sand frosted under the moon, scattered pebbles glinting like shards of broken glass. Behind them, the mess verandah’s shadow pooled thick and jet-black, a wall of darkness cut off from the pearly glow of the night.

Colonel Dawson, commanding a Soudanese battalion, struck a match to relight his cigar, the flame guttering across his lined, troubled face.
“I can’t shake the feeling something’s gone very wrong for him,” he admitted, tossing the burnt match stub into the sand below the knoll.

Lieutenant Calder of the Engineers, whose face still held the unlined sharpness of youth even if the brutal Soudan sun had silvered streaks through his hair, nodded grimly. He was Durrance’s closest friend on the garrison. “He left Halfa eight weeks ago to the day.”

The third man, lanky Major Walters from the Army Service Corps, huffed a laugh and puffed at his pipe, gesturing out at the empty desert east of the river. “C’mon, it’s far too early to write Durrance off. You know the bloke—stick him in the middle of nowhere with two bored tribal sheikhs and a campfire, and he’ll spin them adventure stories until they’re begging him not to leave. He hates nothing more than desk work, letters, endless admin—he’s probably intentionally dragging his feet because he doesn’t want to come back to the office grind.”

“Three weeks overdue,” Dawson said, tugging sharply at his moustache. “He’s methodical, for all his wanderlust. I’m not writing him off, but I’m worried.”

Calder leaned forward, knocking ash out of his pipe bowl slow. “There’s something I’ve never told either of you. I didn’t connect the dots until this week, but… the night before he rode out? He was acting strange. Very strange.”

Dawson hitched his chair closer.

“His bags were all packed hours before dinner ended,” Calder went on. “He left the mess early, but he didn’t head to his quarters. He walked a full mile north along the river to Tewfikieh, that scruffy little frontier border town full of Greek storekeepers, native food stalls, crowds of bare-footed Dinka herders and Delta fellahs all yelling in half a dozen different dialects. I popped up to his quarters at 11pm to ask about a last-minute supply detail for his trip, and he still wasn’t back. I waited. He stumbled in fifteen minutes later, fizzing with some kind of barely contained excitement—one minute he was furious, the next he was laughing to himself like he couldn’t help it. Bundled me right out the door, told his servant to turn in, said he was expecting a secret visitor and would let the man in himself.”

“Did the visitor show?” Dawson asked, his voice sharp.

“Nope. His servant found him at 4am, still sitting on his verandah steps, waiting. No one had turned up. Durrance never said a word about it to me, either. He climbed onto his camel at sunrise, sat there staring down at me for a full ten seconds like he was going to spill everything… then he whacked the camel’s flank with his stick, and rode out into the desert head down, no goodbye. I’ve been wondering ever since if that non-existent visitor was a trap, a lure to get him riding out into the badlands distracted.”

Silence settled over the knoll. Walters shook his head stubbornly, said he still refused to believe Durrance was dead. Dawson announced he’d send a full armed search party out at first light.

By the next morning, the three were sitting at breakfast on the verandah, Dawson already sketching out the search patrol roster on a scrap of paper, when Walters leaned across the table at Calder.
“Wait—did you ever ask around Tewfikieh to find who Durrance was talking to that night?”

“I did,” Calder said, and then his head snapped up, his eyes going wide like he’d just seen a ghost. His hands curled tight around the edge of the table, half ready to leap to his feet, before he froze, confused. The rest of the mess officers were too busy passing plates of toast and jam to notice. He leaned back slow, staring over Walters’ shoulder. “I only found one person he spoke to. A Greek wandering musician, playing a zither for spare change outside a dirt-floor café. Durrance tossed him a whole sovereign, stopped to chat for five minutes. I thought nothing of it… until this exact second.” He paused, grinning faint. “Turns out the man I was just talking about is standing right in the doorway behind you.”

Every head snapped around. Durrance was there, dust caked thick on his uniform, his face burnt almost brick-red by the desert sun, leaning in the doorframe listening intently.

“Durrance!” Dawson yelped, leaping up to yank a chair out for him. “We expected you three weeks ago!”

“Couldn’t be helped,” Durrance said, sinking into the chair. His voice was level, but there was a tight, careful edge to it no one but Calder caught.

“Was that secret visitor you were waiting for that Greek musician?” Calder asked.

Durrance laughed, easy. “That’s exactly who it was. Curious about how he’d ended up stranded playing for coppers in a desert border café, that’s all. Nothing exciting.”

“Come on, tuck in,” Dawson said, reaching for the cocoa pot. “What do you want? Tea? Coffee?”

But Calder watched, his stomach turning cold, as Durrance turned his head vague, and called over the mess waiter instead. “Moussa, bring me something cold. Brandy and soda, if you would.” The waiter had to physically guide the glass right into his outstretched palm, because Durrance couldn’t see it resting on the table.

He rambled for an hour, talking about the Ababdeh Arabs out in the eastern mountains, the plentiful ibex, the perfect weather, the quiet tribes that swore loyalty to neither Egypt nor the Mahdi. Calder didn’t believe a single word of it. There were new deep furrows around Durrance’s mouth that hadn’t been there eight weeks before, streaks of grey in his sun-bleached hair that weren’t just dust. When the other officers left for their patrol duties, Calder lingered. Durrance suddenly remembered he had eight weeks of unopened mail waiting in his office, stood up sharp, and excused himself.

Calder waited five minutes, then grabbed his helmet and tramped across the hot sand to Durrance’s small office shack. He eased the door open, and saw his friend hunched over his desk, his face buried in his arms, the stack of unopened letters sitting untouched at his elbow.

Calder stepped inside, slamming the door loud to announce his presence. Durrance’s head snapped up.
“I’ve got that barracks alteration authorization you need to sign,” Calder said, walking over, and held out his lit pipe. He didn’t hand it to him. He set it right in Durrance’s open palm. The hot burning bowl seared his skin. Durrance yelped, yanking his hand back, and the pipe clattered to the floor and shattered.

Neither man spoke for a long beat. Calder put a gentle hand on his shoulder.
“How did it happen?” he whispered.

Durrance buried his face in his hands, his whole body starting to shake. The tight, cheerful mask he’d been holding up for hours shattered completely. “How did you figure it out?”
“You stood in the mess doorway staring right at my face, and you didn’t recognize me,” Calder said soft. “You wouldn’t reach for the food on the table, you couldn’t find your own glass. I knew before you even sat down.”

Durrance laughed, harsh and ragged. “A sandstorm wind blew my helmet off. I was in my shirtsleeves, no collar, running after it across the sand. That stupid helmet kept skipping away from me every time I stooped to grab it. I must have run half a mile, straight into the full glare of the midday sun. I collapsed face down in the dirt right when my fingers closed on the helmet. When I woke up, I couldn’t see a thing. I made my men promise not to tell anyone. I wanted to get all the way back here before anyone found out. I thought I could hide it for at least a day.”

Calder immediately fetched the clever Syrian garrison doctor, who shook his head grimly, and told Durrance he needed to sail for Cairo immediately to see an eye specialist—there was no telling if the sun damage to his eyes was permanent. When the doctor left, Calder sorted through the mail, and found two letters in a looping feminine hand, postmarked from a small village in Donegal, Ireland. Durrance had told him about Ethne Eustace, the woman he loved, once over late night whiskey months before.

“Shall I read these to you?” Calder asked.

Durrance flinched, then laughed that same harsh, ragged laugh. “God, yes. You have to help me answer them. Don’t you get it? I’m blind now. I can’t read a line.”

Calder opened them. They were warm, gentle letters, full of stories of the Donegal hills, the village festivals, the local fishermen, every line brimming with quiet affection for him. When he finished, Durrance let out a long, shaky breath of relief.
“Thank God she turned me down,” he said. “Twice, in fact. Can you imagine what a burden I’d be to her if we were engaged? I knew a man back home who went blind. A great bloke, before. A year later? He was so selfish, so jealous of everyone who could walk and see, so demanding of his wife’s time, you wouldn’t have recognized him. I’m not clever. I can’t sit in a chair all day and amuse myself with thoughts. My whole life is outdoors, hiking, riding, exploring. I’d turn into that same bitter, useless creature. That girl’s free. I dodged a bullet.”

Calder’s heart sank. He’d seen the way Durrance talked about Ethne, once, like she was the only bright spot he dreamed of when he was out patrolling the desert. He wondered if she really had no idea how much he loved her, or if she’d turned him down for a stupid, silly reason she’d regret for the rest of her life.

“Let’s start dictating those replies to her,” Calder said quiet, and sat down beside his friend at the desk.

Golden June sunlight spilled over Hill Street, turning the leafy London thoroughfare into a glittering stretch of bright window-box blooms and horse-drawn carriages rolling slow toward the green expanse of Hyde Park. Ethne stood pressed to the drawing room’s open sash, while Mrs. Adair leaned back at her tea table, both women half-hushed, both straining to catch one specific sound rising up from the street below. It was half-past five, and the warm air carried the faint, sweet tang of cut lilacs from the garden squares down the road.

A sudden, bright jingle of cab bells drifted upward. Mrs. Adair’s head snapped up. “That’s his cab.”
Ethne leaned further out, her fingers tightening on the window frame. “No. It’s not stopping.” The jingle faded, died away down the road.

Mrs. Adair glanced at the mantel clock, then turned a curious, sharp eye on Ethne. “Colonel Durrance is late.” The note of suspense in Ethne’s quiet “yes” had rung far clearer than any eagerness, and her whole frame had tensed as if waiting for something she dreaded. Mrs. Adair could have sworn she caught a faint, almost unnoticeable sigh of relief when the cab rolled past the house. “Why on earth didn’t you meet him at the station?”

Ethne did not look away from the empty street. “He’d have thought I came only because I see him as helpless. I won’t let him think that. He has his valet with him.” She hesitated, shifting her weight, and twice she parted her lips to speak before holding her tongue. At last she turned fully to her friend. “You remember the telegram I showed you? The one from Lieutenant Calder saying Colonel Durrance had lost his sight?”
“Of course.”
“I want you to promise me you will never speak of it. I don’t want him to ever know I received that message.”

Mrs. Adair blinked, thoroughly puzzled. She’d seen the telegram, but Ethne had never explained that she’d written back to Durrance the very second she read it, pledging to be his wife. She’d only said “I am engaged to him,” and Mrs. Adair had assumed the arrangement had been settled long before he left for the Sudan. “I’ll promise, if you insist, my dear,” she said, shrugging her shoulders faintly. “But there is a reason, isn’t there? I can’t make head nor tail of it.”
“Two lives must not be spoilt because of me.”

The wariness Mrs. Adair had spotted in Ethne was well founded. Exactly one year prior, in this very room, on a sunlit June afternoon just like this one, Ethne had told Durrance to try to forget her. Every letter she’d received from him since then had made plain he had not succeeded. Even that last note, scrawled in wobbly, child-sized uneven handwriting from Wadi Halfa, where he’d broken the news of his blindness and formally renounced his suit, had proven it more clearly than all the rest. Standing at the window now, Ethne knew it was not Durrance who would have to do the hard work of forgetting. She was the one who had to guard every word, every flicker of expression, to never let slip that there was a part of her heart she had never been able to give to him.

“Surely you don’t think this marriage won’t leave you both tangled in unhappiness, Ethne?” Mrs. Adair said slowly. “Won’t you come to see him as a burden, no matter how hard you fight it? Won’t he one day feel that you are only there out of pity?”
“I will never feel that,” Ethne said, sharp and steady. “And he must never, either.” Mrs. Adair assumed the two lives at risk were only Ethne’s and Durrance’s, not Ethne’s and Harry Feversham’s, and Ethne was glad to let her keep that wrong, unknowing assumption. She turned back to the window, mapping out every day of the life ahead of her, steeling herself to never slip. Durrance had lost everything that made his life worth living the day he went blind: the long desert marches, campfires that were nothing but a tiny spark of red in the vast empty dark, nights sleeping out under the Sahara stars, the work of governing frontier outposts, the ride in the Row he’d once joked he could never give up. He had lost all of it. The only thing he had left was her — and he would only keep her, so long as he believed she was there because she chose to be.

A cab pulled up outside the door so quietly Ethne did not notice it until the valet rang the bell. She jolted back from the window. “He’s here.”

Mrs. Adair slipped out of the room without a sound, leaving Ethne alone to greet him. She held her place by the sash for a beat, bracing for the shock of seeing him broken, helpless, groping his way across a room. But when Durrance stepped over the threshold, the shock was the opposite. His face was lined, his hair streaked with grey, but there was no fumbling, no air of uncertainty. He walked straight into the drawing room like his eyes could still see, only lifting a hand once, light as a test, to brush the back of an armchair before he moved around it.

Ethne drew back into the shadow of the window, speechless, and he smiled, turning directly toward her. “Ethne.”
“You’ve recovered!” she blurted, the words torn out of her by surprise.
“Not at all. But you stepped away from the open window just now. The street noise got a little louder, and I knew someone was blocking the draft. I guessed it was you.”

There was no awkward, performative pity between them. They shook hands instead of embracing, no hollow words of sympathy, no stilted greetings. Durrance chatted cheerfully about the journey home, laughed at the silly, fumbling mistakes he’d made in the first weeks after losing his sight, and Ethne’s tight wariness began to melt — until he dropped a name, sharp and casual, like he was testing the air.
“I got a friend of mine to hold the paper steady for that Wadi Halfa letter, so I wouldn’t write all over the blotting pad. Calder, of the Sappers. You don’t know him, do you?”

Ethne’s breath caught. He’d set a trap. She could feel the absolute, still intentness of his face, him listening for the smallest flinch, the tiniest catch in her voice that would give away she already knew Calder — knew the telegram he’d sent. A year ago, when Durrance still had his sight, she would have been able to read every flicker of thought in his eyes. Now his face was a blank, all his senses turned inward, listening.
“I had no trouble reading your letter,” she said, soft and steady. “But I’d already written to you before it ever arrived. Your news changed nothing. I take back not a single word I said.”

Durrance leaned forward, his hands resting on his knees, and for a long moment he said nothing at all. Ethne could not tell if he believed her.
“I know you well enough to know you’d never go back on your word,” he said at last. “But I have to say my piece. I’ve thought about this every night for weeks, lying awake in Cairo. Blindness turns a man selfish, if he doesn’t fight it every single day. A narrowing, irritable egotism, endless petty jealousy, the urge to cling and demand all your attention — those are the dangers for a man like me, a soldier with no work left to do. A writer could still dictate. A businessman could run his office. I have nothing but time to fall prey to those flaws.” He paused, his voice serious, unflinching. “I knew I would give everything I have to say yes to this marriage, if I dared. But all the trivial, silly nonsense of the world falls away when you’re left with nothing but your own thoughts. Only the straight, simple truths stay. And one of them is this: a marriage between a man whose life is already done, and a woman young and full of life like you, can only work if you bring more than friendship to it. It cannot be built on sacrifice.”
“There is no sacrifice,” Ethne said, firm.

She could hear, not see, that he was listening far more to the tone of her voice than the actual words she spoke. The realization hit her like a jolt: it was not going to be easy to outmaneuver him, not now. His other senses had sharpened, but so had his wits.
“Tell me the truth, Ethne,” he said suddenly. “Do you care for me as more than a friend?”

Ethne did not hesitate. Harry Feversham was gone from her life forever, as far as she knew. There was nothing left of that old love to cling to. “There is nothing in the whole world I want more than for us to be married.” It was the absolute truth, and she spoke it without faltering.

Durrance smiled, and reached out to take her hand. “Thank you.” He asked no more questions.

A moment later Mrs. Adair slipped back into the room, greeting Durrance like they had only seen each other the week before, no awkward tiptoeing around his blindness. “We have a plan sorted out,” she said. “Your estate Guessens is just a couple fields away from my Devonshire cottage. Ethne will stay with me for the time before the wedding, while you settle back in at home.”
“That’s very kind,” Durrance said, warm. “And I have plans of my own. I’m seeing a top London oculist tomorrow, and if he thinks it will help, I’ll go to the specialist in Wiesbaden. If there’s any chance to get my sight back, I’m going to take it. Time matters for these cases.”
Ethne’s hopes leapt. “There really is a chance?”

After Durrance had climbed back into his cab to head to his old rooms in St. James’s Street, Ethne stayed at the open window, watching the carriages roll back from the park, the June sun dipping low and gilding the rooftops. She had no idea there was no cure, that his talk of oculists was no more than a thin excuse to put off their wedding while he sorted out the truth of her feelings. But she felt it anyway, cold and clear in her bones. The man she had known a year ago, the easy, unobservant soldier who took his friends at face value, was gone. Durrance might be blind — but he saw far more clearly now than he ever had when his eyes worked.

“Two lives will not be spoilt because of me,” she whispered. It was no longer a resolve. It was a prayer.

The thick, golden heat of July and August draped the Salcombe estuary, and with every passing day Ethne Eustace’s quiet, unnameable dread swelled heavier in her chest. One bright morning, she voiced it out loud for the first time, leaning against the open sash of Mrs. Adair’s creeper-clad cottage, The Pool.
“I’m afraid,” she said, staring out over the sun-dappled creek.
Behind her, Mrs. Adair turned the page of her book, a faint, knowing smile tugging at her lips. “Of Colonel Durrance’s little trip to London yesterday? You heard he had a tumble on the curb?”
“No,” Ethne replied slowly, her eyes fixed on the figure making his steady way across the lawn towards the house. “Not of that. He’s right there, walking straight for us now.”
Mrs. Adair’s smile widened, but she did not lift her head. Her voice dropped so low the words barely drifted across the room: “I thought so.”
Ethne did not catch them. She had already stepped out onto the stone terrace, down the broad shallow steps, and paused at the edge of the lawn to ask the question that had gnawed at her for weeks: “Do you think there’s any real chance he’ll ever get his sight back?”
Mrs. Adair finally looked up, her tone easy, unconcerned. “Would he drag all the way up to London every other week to visit his oculist if there wasn’t? Of course he hopes.”
“That’s not what I’m afraid of.” Ethne spun around to face her friend, her face tight with quiet urgency. “Laura – haven’t you noticed how sharp he’s become? He picks up on every silence before I can fill it, guesses the words I leave unsaid from the way I shift my weight, finishes my half-started sentences before I do. Half the time I swear he can see straight into the corners of my mind, no different than reading a child’s spelling book.”
“You’re at a disadvantage, aren’t you?” Mrs. Adair said softly. “You can no longer hide your thoughts behind your face.”
“And his eyes don’t tell me a single thing anymore,” Ethne returned.
It was a cruel, unforeseen twist. When Durrance had still had his sight, Ethne’s bright, frank grey eyes had been an open book he could trust, no need to parse her every pause for hidden meaning. Now, blind, he no longer relied on her face for signals – he read her every breath, every tiny movement, every unspoken flicker of emotion, with a clarity he had never known before. Meanwhile, Ethne was adrift, unable to read the blank, unreactive face of the man she had once known so well. His blindness had flipped their positions entirely.
“There’s something you need to hide from him, then?” Mrs. Adair asked, her tone casual.
“Something I will conceal at any cost,” Ethne breathed, and even as she said the words, a cold prickle ran down her spine: what if Durrance had already found it out?
She stepped back out onto the terrace, staring out over the lawn that rolled to a thick hedge, beyond which soft green fields rose and fell. Through the trees on the far slope, she could just make out the wisp of smoke curling from the chimneys of Durrance’s cottage, Guessens. Down to her left, a line of old beeches and oaks fringed the creek, with a wide gap cut in the foliage to let the sunlight hit the water. A single sailing dinghy tacked slowly against the light summer breeze, far down the estuary. Ethne took a slow, steadying breath, gearing herself for the conversation she knew was coming with the blind man walking steadily towards her, his stick switching idly at the grass like a habit, not a necessity.
As she started down the steps to meet him, Mrs. Adair dropped her book where she sat. She darted to the window, hiding behind the heavy linen curtain, a fierce, hungry light burning in her eyes, the exultation of a woman who has just seen her long-held secret wish edge closer.
“Something she has to hide at all costs,” she whispered to herself, a bitter, triumphant little laugh huffing out. She had no doubt what that secret was: Ethne was shrinking from marrying a blind man, desperate to cling to her wild, open-air freedom, and Durrance’s blindness was all that kept him from pushing her away. The thought made her burn with fierce gladness. No other woman would ever get him. His total, gentle dependence on her would be the greatest pleasure she had ever known. She watched the two meet on the lawn, turn to walk side by side towards the creek, Ethne’s head tilted in quiet pleading, and she ached to overhear every word.
“You saw the oculist yesterday?” Ethne said, the second she reached Durrance’s side. “What did he say?”
He shrugged, a small, resigned smile on his face. “Wait. Only time will tell if a cure is possible.”
Ethne leaned forward, scrutinising his blank, dark eyes like she was trying to catch him in a lie. “But do we have to wait?”
“Wiser for everyone,” he said, then switched the question on her, sharp and unexpected. “This plan – that I would come home to Guessens, and you would stay with Laura here, just across the fields – that was her idea, wasn’t it?”
“I was distraught when I heard about your accident,” Ethne said quickly, pleading laced in every word. “I ran straight to London to tell Laura, and she came up with it. I welcomed it more than anything. You know that.”
“I do, Ethne,” he said gently, and the tight knot of anxiety in her chest loosened for a moment.
“Laura was kind,” he went on, “but it’s unfair to you. You’re the sort of person who belongs to their own patch of land, your Donegal hills. Harry Feversham told me that once, right before I sailed for Egypt. Said you’d never be happy somewhere you couldn’t see the hills of your own county.”
He spoke Harry’s name so casually, but paused for a beat after it, and Ethne went rigid. He was waiting, she realised, waiting to see if she would flinch, if her silence would give away all the secrets she had buried. She did not move a muscle.
“Harry said that a long time ago,” Durrance continued. “There’s something I’ve wanted to ask you for a while. Back at Glenalla, you said you might tell me one day what happened the night the engagement broke, what sent him running off like an outcast. He was my friend. I’d like to know now.”
Ethne was quiet for a long stretch, then shook her head. “I’d rather not. It’s all over, long done. Don’t ever ask me again.”
“Of course,” he said cheerily, no trace of pressure in his tone. “I won’t make you. I wouldn’t want to hurt you.”
“It’s not pain I’m afraid of,” Ethne said quickly, picking her words carefully. “I’ve thought of him for years as someone we all knew, who’s dead and gone.”
As she said that, she lifted her eyes from the grass. The little sailing dinghy she had noticed far down the creek had just run aground on the bank. Its sail was lowered, its thin mast sticking up over the grass, and a man was standing on the shore, staring vaguely up towards The Pool like he had no idea where he was.
“A stranger’s landed,” she said, grabbing the chance to break off this tense, cross-examining conversation, the same relief a prisoner at the Old Bailey would feel when the judge called for a lunch break. “He looks lost. I’ll go show him the path up.”
She ran forward, and the stranger advanced to meet her. He was stocky, mid-height, with a snub nose, protruding vacuous brown eyes, and an aggressively bushy moustache, his balding forehead glinting in the sun. He lifted his hat.
“I sailed down from Kingsbridge, never been out this way before. Is this house The Pool?”
“It is,” Ethne said. “Go up the steps to the terrace, you’ll find Mrs. Adair there.”
“I didn’t come for Mrs. Adair,” he said. “I came to see Miss Eustace.”
Ethne stopped short, surprised. “I’m Miss Eustace.”
The man stared at her, twirling both ends of his moustache slowly, before he spoke. “Took me long enough to track you down. Went all the way to Glenalla first, for nothing. Hard on a bloke with only a short leave, that.”
“I’m very sorry,” Ethne said, smiling confusedly. “But why go to all that trouble?”
“You’ve forgotten my name, haven’t you?” he said. “I wouldn’t be surprised. You heard it five years ago, though. I’m Captain Willoughby.”
Ethne stumbled back a step. The colour drained from her face, her jaw clamped tight, her eyes went hard as flint. She had not spoken that name to anyone in years.
Willoughby didn’t even flinch. He gave a little forgiving shrug, like he was the one making allowances for bad manners. “I know you’re not glad to see me. None of us guessed you’d be there the day three white feathers got left on Feversham’s table.”
“How do you know I was there?” Ethne snapped.
“Feversham told me.”
The words tore a gasp out of her before she could stop it, loud and raw. She had schooled herself for years to pretend Harry Feversham was dead, that she no longer cared, and the sound of her own shock at those two words shattered that lie to bits. “You’ve seen him?” she whispered, staring at him like he held a ghost in his pocket. “You spoke to him?”
“Met him a year ago, in Suakin,” Willoughby said. “That’s why I’m here.”
A cold dread curled in Ethne’s stomach. She had no idea what he was about to say, but she knew it would change everything. “Why are you here?”
Willoughby pulled a letter case out of his breast pocket, flipped it open slowly, and shook a tiny, crumpled, dust-stained white feather into the palm of his hand. He held it out to her.
“I came to give you this.”
Ethne froze, shrinking back like it burned.
“Three men sent Feversham three white feathers, each calling him a coward,” Willoughby said. “This one was mine. I’m here to say I was wrong. I’m withdrawing my accusation. I take my feather back.”
“He asked you to bring it to me?” Ethne breathed.
She reached out, took the feather in her palm, that tiny, weightless thing that had broken so many lives, and the world swam around her. The August sun seemed to turn cold, the sound of the creek running blurred far away. She almost fainted, but the thought of showing weakness to one of the three men who had destroyed Harry’s reputation steeled her. She steadied herself.
“Come with me,” she said. “I want to hear the whole story. Every word. Even the things he said, if you remember them.”
She led him to a small, hidden patch of lawn tucked in a hedge enclosure, three sides walled with thick thorn, a garden seat set looking out over the creek. She sat down, the white feather clenched tight in her fist. All the years of repressed longing, the grief she had buried, came flooding back, and for a second she was back in that Donegal spring five years earlier, before the feathers had arrived, before everything fell apart.
As Willoughby began to speak, the Devonshire fields around her blurred away. She did not see the red cliffs of the Devon coast, or the sailboats bobbing in the estuary that had sent so many old adventurers out to the Spanish Main. All she saw was the desert landscape Durrance had spent hours describing to her: miles of burning yellow sand, stunted thorny mimosas, sharp black pyramidal rocks rising out of the dunes. Unbeknownst to all of them, Harry Feversham had been there, fighting, risking his life, far away from all of them, to earn back every scrap of the honour he had lost.
That was the irony of it all: the stories Durrance had told her of the desert adventures he had lived, had prepared her better than anyone to understand what Harry had endured, out of sight, to bring that white feather all the way back to her.

Colonel Durrance had ceased to exist for Ethne Eustace the moment Captain Willoughby dropped that crumpled, once-white feather, now stained pale yellow with desert dust and years of sun, into the cup of her hand. She half-dragged him past the thick hawthorn hedge into the walled waterside enclosure, and every word of his story of Harry Feversham’s years in the Sudan swallowed her whole. For five long, stiff years she’d held her head high, smiled through every polite inquiry, played the part of the stoic, unbetrothed heiress, and all that time she’d buried every memory of Harry’s laugh, his quick half-embarrassed grin when he teased her, the way his voice softened when he spoke of his west-country home. But Willoughby’s narrative dug all those memories up again, sharp and bright, filling in every gap of his account until she could almost see Harry tramping across the sun-scorched Soudanese sands herself.

She’d been so completely swept away from that sun-drenched August garden, from the weight of those five years, that when she lifted her head and spotted Durrance standing right at the enclosure’s single hedge entrance, a stab of pain spasmed across her face and her eyes squeezed shut for a split second. There was no time to grieve for the lost, unguarded minutes of Willoughby’s story. The small stolen holiday was over. If she could never actually manage to forget Harry — and right now she knew, with absolute brutal certainty, that she never would — she had to play the part perfectly, never letting Durrance suspect that the “friendship” she offered him as his fiancée was nothing more than hard-won loyalty. Durrance was far too sharp a guesser to ever find Willoughby here, or even learn he’d come.

She scanned the enclosure for an escape, and knew instantly it was hopeless. The creek lapped at the stone lip of the ground before them, three high, thick hedges walled them in from behind and the sides, and the only way out was blocked entirely by Durrance, who was stepping slowly into the clearing now.
“Keep still,” she breathed into Willoughby’s ear.
“You know him?” he whispered back.
“Of course. We served together three years at Suakin. I heard he’d gone blind. I’m glad that’s rubbish,” he added, watching Durrance move with steady, unhesitating grace.
“Lower your voice. It’s no rubbish. He is blind.”
“Nobody would ever tell. It’s impossible he can’t see us right here,” Willoughby protested, staring at Durrance who seemed to be looking directly at their bench.
“He sees nothing at all.”
“Ethne?” Durrance called, his voice so quiet, so sure, the illusion of sight was almost unbreakable.
“He’s not even sure where we are,” Ethne whispered, her fingers clamping hard around Willoughby’s arm. “Not a sound. Don’t move an inch.”
“Why can’t he know I’m here?” Willoughby mouthed, but the desperate tightness in Ethne’s face silenced him before he could ask more. They both sat rigid on the wooden bench, holding their breath, every muscle frozen, as Durrance walked steadily closer, stopped dead directly in front of them, his gaze fixed straight on their faces. Even Ethne, who knew perfectly well his eyes could not distinguish a single shape, felt a flicker of doubt.

Then he shrugged, called her name one last time, and turned toward the creek. His back was to them now, but Ethne, who had learned to read every tiny shift of his posture since he lost his sight, could tell instantly he was listening — straining every nerve to pick up the smallest rustle of a dress, the quietest huff of breath. A full minute dragged by, and he spun on his heel, taking a sharp step back toward the bench. Not a single thread of Ethne’s skirt shifted. Willoughby did not twitch. Durrance sighed, muttered that he had no idea where she’d got to, and walked slowly out of the enclosure, the hedge swallowing him up.

Ethne only released her bruising grip on Willoughby’s arm once Durrance’s footsteps had faded entirely.
“That was a near thing,” Willoughby huffed, once he was allowed to speak. “Might’ve sat right on top of us if he’d made it two more steps.”
“But he didn’t,” Ethne said, calm as still water. “You’ve told me everything? All of Harry’s story?”
“Every last part I can recall. It all happened last spring.”
Ethne’s chest ached sharp. “Why didn’t you bring that feather to me last summer? Your leave can’t have been that short.”
“Was, though. Spent the whole of it hunting ibex in the hills north of Suakin. Never thought there was any rush — Harry never said it was urgent. Figured I’d probably get back to England and find he’d beaten me home anyway.”

A desperate, unspoken cry screamed in Ethne’s throat — last summer, she had been free. No quiet engagement to Durrance weighing on her, no honor-bound promise trapping her, and Willoughby had wasted that whole precious time chasing mountain goats in the desert. But her face did not shift. She only smiled faintly, asked him if he’d had good sport, and heard him grumble that it was decent enough.
“Two more feathers, right?” she said. “He still has to win them back from Castleton and Trench, before he’ll come home to me.”
Willoughby shook his head. “He’ll never get the chance. Castleton was killed when the Dervish square broke at Tamai.”

Ethne laughed, a short, bright, satisfied little laugh that made Willoughby stare at her in shock. She was Celt through and through — death felt no great weight to her, and the three men who had sent Harry the white feathers, who had ruined five years of his life and hers, she hated every last one of them, no pity left at all. She did not even spare a thought for the fact she’d handed Harry the fourth feather herself, back when she’d believed him a coward. “And Trench?” she demanded, sharp.
“Alive, but worse than dead. Captured on a reconnaissance, locked up in Omdurman prison. You have no idea what that kind of captivity does to a man,” Willoughby said, his voice severe.
“I don’t want to hear,” Ethne said, stubborn as iron. “You have to go now. First — where did Harry go after he gave you that feather?”
“Hopped the first steamer out of Suakin for Suez. Had more than enough money, too — his father’s allowance still came through regular.”

Ethne sent him to wait hidden in the enclosure while she checked the garden was clear. The whole estate was drowsing in the midday heat, the birds fallen quiet, only bees humming over the flower beds, a distant farm boy yelling across the creek far away. No sign of Durrance anywhere.
“You can go,” she said, leading Willoughby back out toward the moored boat. “I don’t expect you to be my friend, but thank you for coming. I need you to promise me you’ll never tell Durrance you were here, or a single word of what you told me. He’s Harry’s oldest friend, but he has no idea the feathers ever existed, or why our engagement broke off. I can’t let him find out.”
“Why not?” Willoughby frowned, stubborn as only an army man could be.

“Because I’m engaged to him,” Ethne said, simple as day. She knew perfectly well Durrance would never keep the engagement once he learned Harry had redeemed every scrap of his honor. He was far too good, far too honorable, to chain her to a blind man when the man she truly loved was free to come home. And she would not let him throw away the only bit of happiness he had left, not when she had bound herself to him by her own word. She would not break her own honor, not when Harry had fought so hard to win his back.

Willoughby whistled low. “Engaged to Jack Durrance! Guess I wasted my whole trip bringing you this feather, huh?” He nodded at the crumpled yellowed thing in her palm. Ethne flinched, pulling her hand away like he meant to snatch it back.
“Not wasted. I’m grateful. It’s all a mess, isn’t it?” Willoughby said, glancing between her face and the path to the water. “Rough on Harry. Rough on Jack. And if I’m any judge, it’s going to be roughest on you.”

Ethne did not answer. She hurried him down the bank to the boat, desperate to get him away before anyone spotted him — but she had forgotten all about Mrs. Adair, who had been glued to the drawing room curtain all morning, watching every step. She’d seen Ethne meet the strange man at the boat, seen Durrance follow them into the enclosure, seen him come back alone, and she’d burned with curiosity, half-convinced the stranger was Harry Feversham himself. She’d seen Ethne and Willoughby walk back to the water now, and she ran all the way across the lawn to catch them, just as Willoughby was about to cast off the rope.

“Captain Willoughby!” she called, smiling sweetly, and trapped him so neatly he admitted he was part of the North Surrey regiment — Harry Feversham’s old unit, of course. She tried to invite him in for lunch, but Ethne cut in sharp, lied that he had to get back to Kingsbridge immediately, and sent him pushing off from the shore before he could say a word that would give the whole tangled game away.
“Bit unfair to leave me starving, isn’t it?” Willoughby called back, laughing, as his boat drifted out into the creek.

Ethne laughed too, turning back toward the terrace at Mrs. Adair’s side. Once, twice, she opened her palm to the sun, staring down at that once-white, yellowed feather, and laughed a low, clear, private laugh. She offered no explanation, and none was asked. This was her secret, and her burden, and hers alone to carry.

Beneath her pale, unflappable poise, Mrs. Adair harbored a devious, intrigue-obsessed nature. She would creep to peer through a keyhole every time rather than walk straight through an open door; any secret pried loose with a little careful maneuvering tasted far sweeter to her than a plain fact handed over freely at the first simple question. She avoided direct questions on a perverse sort of principle, and counted a day a total triumph if she tricked some unsuspecting guest into spilling a trivial scrap of gossip they would have given her without a second thought over breakfast.

So even though she was thoroughly mystified by the tiny white feather Ethne guarded so carefully, burning to pick apart the great news Captain Willoughby had brought home about Harry Feversham, and had run a hundred tangled, dead-end theories about that fateful night at Ramelton years prior, she betrayed not a flicker of her confusion all through lunch. Instead, she chattered cheerfully about neutral, trivial topics, and was excellent company when she chose to be—though it did her no good at all.
“I don’t believe you hear a single word I’m saying!” she exclaimed at last.
Ethne laughed and pleaded guilty. She fled to her room the second lunch cleared, and claimed a full afternoon of unbroken solitude. Sitting propped at her window, she replayed every word of Willoughby’s story from that morning, and it hummed in her bones like a perfectly rendered piece of divine music. The old faint regret that Harry had never come home a year prior, when she was still unbound, shrank to nothing next to the bright, surging joy that filled her. The pride she had shattered the day Harry handed her that first feather in the Lennon House hall, the pride that had never fully healed, was now restored—by the very man who had struck that first blow. She glowed with it, giddy and grateful that he had risked everything, over years of brutal peril, to give it back to her. The August sunlight felt brighter on her skin, her blood thrummed faster, and for that whole soft afternoon, her lost youth was handed right back to her.

Ethne unlocked a hidden drawer in her dressing case and pulled out the only portrait of Harry she had kept, the single gift she had never discarded. She was glad now she had never thrown it away. She knew full well that man was as good as dead to her, that she would never betray Durrance for a moment—but he was a friend, through and through. She looked at the portrait with soft, uncomplicated happiness, marveling that he had never needed her to voice her faith in him, never needed a single line of encouragement to stay the course he had set for himself, through every hardship. When she locked the drawer again, she tucked the little white feather right beside the portrait, sealing the two things together.

Back at her window, a light breeze sent the shadows of the tall lawn trees dancing, and the sun softened to amber. Ethne was a Donegal girl through and through, and her heart ached for her home that second. It was August: the first purple heather would be blooming across the granite-strewn hillsides of her county, and she wished Willoughby had tracked her down there, not in this strange, borrowed English house. The brown mountain slopes and the gurgle of upland streams, she thought, would have wrapped themselves around this new happiness and held it softer. Great griefs and great joys both drew her home, for in Donegal endurance came easier, and gladness felt far more whole.
But she carried one little piece of Donegal with her: Dermod’s old collie, who had become her inseparable shadow. She spilled every last thought to him, and when her voice cracked with quiet tears, he only rested his head on her lap, no secrets ever to be told. She filled in all the gaps Willoughby had left unsaid, all the parts Harry had never shared with a living soul: three long years hiding in the crowded, dust-choked streets of Suakin, ducking down alleyways every time the regiments marched past, caked in dust and fresh from bleeding victories, lest some old friend—Durrance, Willoughby, Trench—spot him and blow his disguise. She could see the raw panic he felt the first time he laid eyes on Berber’s dark mud-brick walls, the cold huddle in the ruins of that abandoned village, the blind, gasping scramble for fresh water across Obak’s shifting sandhills. With every new vivid picture, she asked herself where she had been, back then, wrapped up in her own bitter, petty grief.

She sat wrapped in that golden haze until the creek’s still waters shifted color with dusk, and rooks wheeled noisily out of the treetops to sort themselves into their night roosts, warning her the day was done. She came down to dinner that night buoyant, bright, and her companions could barely believe the change in her. Mrs. Adair watched her, eyes shining star-bright, her cheeks flushed a soft rose, and was more certain than ever that Willoughby had brought world-stirring news—though she still could not guess even a scrap of what it was. Even the dinner itself felt lighter, free of the awkward tension that had hung over Guessens ever since Durrance came home. He too shrugged off his constant burden of restraint, his spirits rising to match Ethne’s. The habitual tight, strained look of a man straining every ear to compensate for his lost sight melted away from his face, and he laughed back at every laugh she gave him.
“You’ll play your violin for me tonight, I think,” he said, smiling as they rose from the table.
“Yes,” she answered. “I will, with all my heart.”
Durrance laughed and held the door for her. That violin had stayed locked in its case for two whole months. Durrance had come to see it as a litmus test: if Ethne was thriving, the case came open, and the music sang; if she was hurting, it stayed sealed tight, lest it wake old wounds and lay them bare for every prying eye. Ethne knew it was an indiscreet, honest old friend—but tonight it would sing.

Mrs. Adair lingered until Ethne was out of earshot. “You’ve noticed the change in her tonight?” she said.
“Of course I have,” Durrance answered. “Waited for it, hoped for it, almost despaired of it.”
“Are you truly that glad?”
Durrance tilted his head back. “Why wouldn’t I be? She has always been kind, generous, unselfish—but tonight there’s something more than plain friendliness shining through, for the first time in years.” A flicker of soft pity crossed Mrs. Adair’s face, and she slipped out of the room without another word. Durrance took the whole bright shift in Ethne as a sign meant only for him.

Mrs. Adair yanked up the drawing room blinds, flung the windows wide, and let silver moonlight flood every corner. But when she saw Ethne kneeling by the violin case, working the lock open, she stepped out onto the terrace. She could not bear to sit still in that charged, happy air. So when Durrance stepped into the drawing room, he found Ethne all alone, tightening her violin strings by the open window. He pulled a chair into the dark shadows right behind her.
“What shall I play for you?” she asked.
“The Musoline Overture,” he said. “You played it the very first night I came to Ramelton, to Lennon House. I remember every note. Play it again. I want to compare.”
“I’ve played it a few times since then.”
“Never for me.”

They were completely alone, the night warm and thick with jasmine scent. Ethne crossed over to the table lamp, snuffed it out, and returned to her seat. Durrance leaned forward in his shadowed corner, hands resting on his knees, listening with a quiet, total intentness he had not shown all evening. He was running one final test, he thought—the one that would decide the rest of both their lives. That violin would not lie. It would tell him for certain if what Ethne felt for him was only friendship, or something far deeper.

Ethne began to play, and quickly forgot that Durrance was even in the room. The garden lay still around them, the moonlight stretching across the creek like a solid sheet of hammered silver, the trees standing motionless, dreaming to the stars. The music floated out across the silent lawn, and a soft wild fancy seized Ethne: those notes could drift down the creek, over Salcombe Bar, across the moonlit southern ocean, and land soft as fairy music on the ears of a man sleeping somewhere beneath desert stars, the cool night wind blowing across his face. “If he could only hear,” she thought, “if he could wake and know this is a message of friendship for him.” She played with more skill than she had ever summoned before, making her violin sing pure sympathy. The fancy grew: the music was a bridge strung across thousands of miles of empty land and sea, where for these few short minutes, she and Harry Feversham could meet, shake hands, before they each turned back to their separate, chosen paths. The chords rang out sharp and proud, echoing all that bright restored pride she had carried all afternoon. It was no longer *if* he heard. She was sure he *must* hear.
A wild, unplanned hope sparked in her: what if he answers? She drew out the final bars, lingering over the last notes, her eyes scanning the moonlit lawn for some sign.

The answer did come—but it did not drift across the grass. It rose from the dark shadows of the room behind her, Durrance’s voice soft and clear.
“Ethne, do you know where I heard that overture last played, before tonight?”
Ethne started, jolted out of her trance as if woken from a deep sleep. “Why… at Ramelton, the first night you came to Lennon House, you told me.”
“I heard it much later, and it wasn’t played on a violin. Barely even a proper tune, just scattered snatches of the melody, fumbled out on a beat-up zither, full of wrong notes, by a Greek man in a tiny whitewashed café lit by one single blinding lamp, at Wadi Halfa.”
“This overture?” she breathed. “How strange.”
“Not strange at all. That Greek man was Harry Feversham.”

Ethne sat perfectly still in the moonlight, her eyelids falling shut, the silence stretching long between them. She did not stop to wonder why Durrance had kept that knowledge secret for so long, why he chose now to tell her. She did not ask what Harry had been doing, pretending to be a Greek café musician deep in the desert. It felt perfectly natural: the bridge of the music had worked, after all. It did not even feel odd that Harry had used Durrance’s voice to speak her reply to her.
“When was this?” she asked at last, quiet.
“February of this year. I will tell you the whole story.”
“Yes,” she said. “Please tell me.”
And Durrance began to speak, out of the shadows of the moonlit room.

Ethne never turned toward Durrance, never shifted from the pose she’d settled into the moment he began to speak. Her violin rested heavy across her knees, her gaze fixed on the narrow band of silver moonlight spilling through a gap in the treeline at the far end of her garden, and she held deliberately still. It let her pretend Harry Feversham himself was speaking to her, that Durrance was no more than a thin, unimportant conduit carrying his voice across the miles. She almost forgot the blind officer was even in the room. She leaned into the story just as intently as Durrance had told her he once listened to Harry, hoping he would speak slow, stretching the precious message out so she could tuck every single word jealously tight against her heart.

“It was the night before I rode east into the desert for the last time,” Durrance said. The soft, heavy regret that clung to the words “last time” did not touch Ethne at all, not then.
“Yes,” she replied. “February. Mid-month, wasn’t it? Can you remember the exact day? I need to know.”
“The fifteenth,” Durrance said.
Ethne murmured the date back under her breath, thoughtful. “I was at Glenalla all February. I can’t even recall what I was doing that day. It doesn’t matter.” A strange, sharp sense of wonder pricked at her, the same that had niggled at her that morning when Captain Willoughby first told her fragments of Harry’s story: how could she not have felt him, somehow, across the miles, the moment those things happened? Why had she been forced to wait for this August night, this moonlit garden where every bloom was closed for the dark, to finally hear his message? Remorse flooded her, hot and sharp. “It was my own fault,” she breathed to herself. “If I’d kept faith in him, I would have known instantly. I’ve earned every bit of this.”
She never doubted for a second the news would be good. It would build on the story Willoughby had started, wrap the day up in perfect, healing resolution. She was certain of it. “Go on,” she urged.
“I’d spent all that day in my office wrapping up patrol reports,” Durrance continued. “At ten o’clock I turned the key in the lock, relieved to think I wouldn’t open it again for six weeks, and strolled north out of Wadi Halfa along the Nile bank to the little frontier town of Tewfikieh. As I turned onto the main street I spotted a small crowd: Arab traders, Sudanese porters, a couple Greek clerks, Egyptian soldiers, all huddled outside a café lit up bright as a bonfire by the glare spilling out the open door. From inside, a violin and zither were sawing through a waltz so badly it set my teeth on edge. I stood at the back of the crowd, peered over the shoulders of the men in front of me, into the room. It was a bare whitewashed box, nothing more: a rickety bar huddled in one corner, a couple of scuffed wooden benches propped against the walls, a single unshaded paraffin lamp swinging so hard it cast wild dancing shadows across the walls. A troupe of itinerant musicians were playing for their supper and a spot to sleep on the café’s floor. Four of them, all Greeks as far as I could tell. Two were clearly an old married couple, ragged almost to tatters: the man thin, sallow, grey-haired with a thick black moustache, the woman fat, coarse-faced, slow on her feet. The third was their seventeen year old daughter. She wasn’t conventionally pretty, but she was scrubbed spotless, dressed with such meticulous, loving care amid all that squalor that it made her glow. A clean sky-blue ribbon bound her hair back, her white cotton frock had no rips or stains, even her shoes were polished neat. Her parents would go hungry and ragged to make sure she had that decency, and it twisted at my heart. The fourth was a young man, seated in the window with his back to me, hunched over his zither. I could see he wore a full, unkempt beard.
The old man scraped the violin so harshly it sounded like a pencil dragged across slate, the instrument itself seemed to squeal in pain. While he played, the young man hammered discordant notes out of his zither, the old woman and her daughter waltzed slow and clumsy across the bare floor. It sounds almost comical when you describe it, but if you’d seen it? It broke you. I don’t think I’ve ever witnessed anything sadder in my life. The crowd outside jeering, passing crude jests back and forth at their expense, those four white outcasts scraping by… and overhead, the huge, unblinking planets of the African sky, and all around the vast, silent dignity of the moonlit desert stretching out to the horizon. The sheer ineptitude of that shabby little performance hurt, like a physical ache.”
He paused, and Ethne conjured the scene sharp in her mind: Harry hunched over that zither, and instantly the question she’d held back burst out. What was he doing with that ragtag troupe? She knew he’d never come back to England, never come back to her unless she sent for him, and Willoughby had told her he’d left no forwarding address. But he had money, his father kept paying his allowance — he didn’t need to perform for loose change.
“Did you speak to him?” she asked, abrupt.
“To Harry? Yes, once I realized it was him plucking at that zither.”
“How did you recognize him?”
“The waltz wound down. The old woman collapsed gasping onto the nearest bench. The young man lifted his head from his zither. The old man scraped a new, discordant chord, and the girl stepped forward to sing. Her voice was young and fresh, but no more skilled than the rest of the performance. The crowd’s snickers and crude jests got louder. It was cruel, all of it.”
“Yes,” Ethne murmured, half-absent. She had no sympathy to spare for that scene, not then — all her focus was locked on Harry, the secret of what he was doing there, the message he’d sent her. A broad panel of silver moonlight spilled across the floor right at her feet, and she stared into it as if it was a window, if she looked hard enough, she could see straight through to that lit-up café on the edge of the Soudan.
“Then what?” she prompted.
“The young man with his back to me started fumbling out a solo on the zither. Note after note was wrong, no tune emerged at first, the jeers got louder, and I was just about to turn away sick at heart, when a string of notes, played right almost by chance, snagged my attention. I listened harder, and a faint, haunting little melody began to make itself clear. Thin, fragile, almost a ghost of a tune, but I knew it. I stood there on that sand street, between huts fringed with stunted date palms, and all of a sudden I wasn’t in the East anymore. I was back at Ramelton, on a summer night under a Donegal sky melting pink at dusk, and you were sitting by the open window playing the *Musoline Overture* — the same piece you played for me tonight.”
“It was my overture?” she gasped.
“It was Harry Feversham playing it. I didn’t guess right away. I was slower, then.”
“You’re not slow now,” Ethne whispered.
“Quicker, at least. I’d spot it instantly today. Back then, I was only curious. How did a random itinerant Greek musician learn that obscure little tune? I decided I’d reward him for the effort, thought you’d want me to.”
“Yes,” Ethne breathed.
“So when they filed out of the café, hats out for tips, the young man walked straight past me through the jeering crowd. I dropped a whole sovereign into his hat. He jumped, shocked, and before he could bite the word back, he cried out ‘Jack!’ There was no mistaking that voice. Even under the thick beard, I knew his face.
He tried to babble nonsense in Greek, shuffle backwards into the crowd to get away from me, but I caught his arm and wouldn’t let him go. I knew he’d done you some great wrong, back then — but I couldn’t remember it in that moment. All I could remember was Oxford, the days we were inseparable: rowing in the same college eight, him at stroke, me at seven, his jersey stripes blurring in my vision when we sprinted past the barges for the final hundred yards. Swimming at Sandford Lasher on summer afternoons. Sneaking supper on Kennington Island. Cutting lectures to paddle up the Cher all the way to Islip. And here he was, my best old friend, an outcast reduced to playing discordant zither for pennies on the edge of the desert.”
“No,” Ethne cut in sharply. “He wasn’t there for the money. He had no need for it. His father kept sending his allowance, and he took it.”
Durrance didn’t catch the slip, that she knew details she had no right to know. “You’re certain?”
“Completely. I heard it only today,” she said, impatient. “So you never found out what brought him to Tewfikieh? You didn’t ask him? Why not?” Disappointment burned hot in her chest, sharp as anger. The last fragment of news about Harry, and it was torn half off like a half-written letter in the mail. She might never know the rest.
“I was a fool,” Durrance said, knocking his fist lightly against his forehead, his voice thick with regret. “I won’t easily forgive myself. He begged me to let him go, kept whispering it over and over — he was terrified someone would recognize him, ashamed of how far he’d fallen. I told him to come meet me on the verandah of my quarters in Wadi Halfa the second he was free, and I let him go. I waited up all night for him. He never came.
At dawn the next day I had to mount up and ride east across the desert. I was this close to telling my friend Calder — the man who sent you that telegram, you know the one — to hunt Harry down and look after him. But I didn’t. I didn’t know about his allowance back then, I just thought he’d hit rock bottom, and it didn’t feel right to share his shame with anyone else. I rode off, and I kept quiet.”
Ethne nodded. She couldn’t fault him for that, not even when the ache of missing news gnawed at her. “So you never saw him again?”
“I was gone nine weeks. I came back blind,” Durrance said simply, the quiet admission sending a pang straight through Ethne’s chest. He was apologizing for the months he’d been unable to search, the lost time. “I went straight to Cairo, Calder came with me. I told him everything I’d seen, asked him the second he got back to Wadi Halfa to find Harry, help him, let me know what he found. A week ago I got his letter. It’s bad. I’ve been troubled by it ever since.”
Ethne went cold, turning away from the moonlight into the shadows so she could lean forward and try to see his face, hidden by the dark. “What did he say?”
“That the old Greek couple and their daughter took a northbound steamer from Tewfikieh to Assouan. Harry wasn’t with them. He was gone. The day after I rode east, he slipped straight past the Wadi Halfa sentry posts, walked south out into the desert, zither slung over his back, full water skin, a sack of dates. He walked straight into enemy Dervish territory.”
Ethne went silent for a long, long minute. “You have that letter with you?”
“I do.”
She rose, crossed the room to him, took the thin envelope from his hand, and carried it back to the window where the moonlight was bright enough to read by. She read it once, then twice. The Greek café owner confirmed that the young man going by the name of Joseppi had set off south, no explanation given. She had to force the words out past the tight lump in her throat, so they didn’t shake.
“What will happen to him?”
“Best case scenario: capture. Worst case: death. Thirst, starvation, or the Dervish spears. But if they catch him alive, they’ll take him for a spy, assume he knows all our patrol plans. They’ll send him to the prisoner camp at Omdurman. I’ve written back to Calder — we run spies in and out of Omdurman all the time, we get news eventually. But he must have gone mad, to walk out there alone.”
Ethne knew the truth. She couldn’t hold it in, and the words slipped out before she thought. “Colonel Trench is a prisoner at Omdurman.”
Durrance didn’t catch on. “Oh, right. He won’t be entirely alone, then. That’s some small comfort. We’ll do what we can, when we get word.” He had no idea that Harry hadn’t walked into the desert out of madness — he’d gone to redeem himself, to erase the shame of the feathers he’d sent those three officers years before. Ethne knew she had to keep that secret. She had to keep pretending she’d forgotten Harry, for Durrance’s sake.
But the urge to be alone, to process that terrible, daring news, was overwhelming. The quiet, moon-dappled garden outside the window called to her, the rustle of rose bushes, the cool night air on her face. Durrance was still speaking, gearing up to tell her the confession of love he’d held back for years, and Ethne didn’t hear a single word he said. She gathered her skirts so they wouldn’t rustle, stepped silent over the windowsill, and was gone into the dark, alone with the knowledge that Harry Feversham had marched south into enemy territory, chasing the echo of a violin overture he’d memorized just to send her one final message that he hadn’t forgotten.

Ethne had thought she could slip away unseen, but Mrs. Adair sat tucked in the deep shadow of the terrace, not ten feet from the drawing room’s wide open window. She watched Ethne dart across the stone paving and fly down the steps into the garden, her pace wild, desperate, like she was running for her life rather than taking an evening stroll.

The oddity of it pricked at her long before she puzzled out the context. For hours, she had sat right there, forcing herself to endure the quiet torture of seeing the drawing room lamp snuffed out, of listening to soft piano music drift out through the moonlight tangled with low, hushed whispers of two people talking in the dark. Jealousy had burned hot in her chest all evening, sharp and scorching, stoked by the knowledge that the blind man she loved was inside that room with Ethne, building a life she would never get to share.

She’d written off the chance of a quarrel the second she saw Ethne run. Ethne was not the type to flee a fight. But then something far stranger happened: Durrance kept talking. His voice drifted out the window, warm, unhurried, convinced he was still speaking to Ethne.

Mrs. Adair rose silent on tiptoe, crept to the sill, and listened close enough to make out every word.

“I figured it out even that first afternoon we met in Hill Street two months back,” he said, laughing soft at the memory. “My blindness worked in my favour for a long while. If I’d been able to see your face, I’d have believed every line you fed me about loving me. But you didn’t have that mask anymore, and my other senses got sharper fast. For the first time in my life, I was really seeing – seeing that all you felt for me was friendship. I should have called the engagement off right then. I still believe, you know, that a marriage needs more than friendship on both sides. But I was selfish. I warned you once that selfishness is the blind man’s worst flaw. I dragged my feet on the wedding, made excuses, led you to believe my sight might come back when I knew for certain it never would. I was holding out hope, stupid as it was, that with time your friendship might turn into something more. I couldn’t let you go. I kept waiting for a new softness in your voice, a new lightness in your laugh, something in your piano playing that told me you cared the way I did.

“Tonight, I burned my boats. I admitted I knew you only saw me as a friend. I told you there’s no hope my sight will ever return. I even dared tell you the secret I’ve held for months: that I met Harry Feversham, and the terrible risk he’s been running out in the Sudan. Because tonight, I finally heard all the signs I was waiting for. That new softness in your voice, that buoyancy in your laugh – it’s been there all evening. The tension, the restraint, it all vanished from you the second you sat down to play, like your heart was finally allowed to sing through the music. Ethne? Ethne?”

But Ethne was already in the small walled garden by the creek, the same private nook she’d led Captain Willoughby to that morning, her old collie trotting at her heels. She’d barely made it out before her carefully built wall of pretence – months of pretending she did not love Harry Feversham, of playing the happy fiancee to Durrance – crumbled to dust beneath the strain.

Moonlight pooled silver on the lapping tide, the tall trees stood still silhouetted against the stars, and the hush of the summer night wrapped around her. That very morning, she’d gotten the best news of five long years: Harry was alive, he had cleared his name, he had redeemed his honour. By evening, the worst had arrived: he’d headed south, straight for Omdurman, the squalid, brutal Dervish stronghold where captives were starved, tortured, and left to rot in sun-scorched prison pits. She’d turned down Willoughby’s offer to explain just how horrific Omdurman captivity was, and now her imagination ran wild with all the fates that could be waiting for Harry there. She ached for the rolling brown hills of her Donegal home, sick to her bones of all the lies, all the secrets, all the performances she’d had to put on to keep Durrance from learning the truth. She would not break, not yet. She would pick up her strength tomorrow, and keep protecting him from the knowledge that her heart belonged to another man.

Back in the drawing room, Durrance waited for an answer that never came. He stood, walked toward the chair Ethne had been sitting in, his hands outstretched, and called her name again, his tone now laced with worry rather than confusion. His fingers brushed the taut strings of her discarded violin, and a loud, sharp twang echoed through the dark room. He froze, stunned, the truth hitting him all at once. He was talking to an empty room. He had spilled his whole heart out to thin air.

He walked slowly to the open window, and his outstretched hand brushed the fabric of Mrs. Adair’s dress.

“It’s not Ethne,” he said, startled.

“No, it’s not Ethne,” she snapped back. She told him how she’d seen Ethne run out across the terrace, how the garden beyond the lawn was swallowed up in shadow, no trace of her to be seen.

Durrance stepped over the sill to stand beside her. The last pieces clicked into place. The girl he’d thought had finally fallen for him that evening had fled the room the second he mentioned Harry Feversham’s name, too broken to keep her mask up. The lightness in her laugh, the warmth in her music – none of it had been for him.

Mrs. Adair, raw with humiliation and jealousy, wanted to hurt him. It was the only small compensation she had for months of watching him dote on Ethne, never once noticing her. “She ran like she was half out of her mind with grief,” she said. “You’re the one who gave her that grief, aren’t you? I heard every word you said. She wasn’t faking that happiness tonight. She was exactly the girl she was five years ago, before Harry Feversham left Ramelton. She got good news about him this morning, that’s what changed her.”

Durrance turned to her sharply, his shock clear. He remembered then: Willoughby, the ex-officer from Harry’s old regiment, Deputy Governor of Suakin, had landed that morning. Ethne had run to meet him, dragged him straight off to the walled garden by the creek, and not come back for over an hour. Mrs. Adair went on, laying every fact bare: Ethne had hurried Willoughby back to his boat the second they were done talking, had carried a small, tattered, dust-stained white feather Harry had sent her the whole way back to the house, staring at it like it was the most precious thing on earth.

For a long minute, Durrance was quiet. He did not rage. He did not resent Ethne for lying to him. He only realized that a marriage between him, a blind man, and Ethne, a woman whose whole heart belonged to Harry, would have been a cruel, empty farce. He was relieved, too – it turned out Ethne had slipped out the second he started speaking, so she had never heard his secret that he’d faked hope for his sight coming back just to hold onto her. He did not want her carrying that guilt on top of her fear for Harry.

“It was kind of you to tell me all this,” he said, grabbing his cap from the hall a minute later. “I served three years in Suakin with Willoughby. If Harry’s trapped in Omdurman, there’s every chance I can pull strings from there or Assouan to get him out. I owe him that much.”

He shook Mrs. Adair’s hand, and headed off across the moonlit garden toward the stile in the hedge, walking steady as a sighted man. Mrs. Adair stared after him, stunned that he could move so calmly when the one life he’d planned for had just been ripped away from him. She watched him vanish into the dark, on his way to find Ethne, already turning over plans to save the man who had stolen the woman he loved.

When Durrance tramped the dark miles across the frost-kissed fields back to his home “Guessens,” his long-suffering body-servant was still waiting up by the hall lamp.
“Kill the lights and get to bed,” Durrance said, not breaking his stride, as he moved past him and into the room everyone called his study, though it had never really earned the name. It was a gun-room first, lined with racks and crammed with the spoils of decades of sport and travel. He sank into his armchair for a long, quiet stretch, then got to his feet and began to pace gently through the unlit room, every step mapped out in his muscle memory.
Every cup and goblet scattered across the shelves and mantel was an old friend. He picked them up one by one, running his fingers over their curves, their engravings, wondering if the staff had kept them half as bright as they had been when he could see them himself. That thin-stemmed goblet was the prize for the regimental steeplechase he’d run at Colchester, he could still taste the cold grey drizzle that day, see the ploughed fields stretching dull and brown between the hedgerows. The heavy pewter mug propped on his desk, that had held his pens for years, was the trophy he’d won rowing in his college fours as a freshmen at Oxford. The mounted silver-edged hoof of his favourite hunter sat front and centre on the mantel. The whole room was a diary he could read by touch alone.
Eventually his fingers found their way to the gun racks. He hauled his favourite Remington down first, the weight of it familiar against his thigh, and slipped back into that scorching long day west of Berenice, stalking a lion across bare stony hills, taking the kill shot at 300 yards right as the sun dipped below the horizon. Another recalled his first ibex in the Khor Baraka, the chase of antelope in the mountains north of Suakin. The tiny, light Greener he’d used for winter wildfowling on Salcombe Estuary, the gun that had brought down his first mallard, he ran his palm along the cool barrel and pressed the butt to the hollow of his shoulder, like he was lining up a shot over the water.
But the memories swelled too loud, sharp as the note Ethne’s violin had sung in the empty months right after Harry Feversham vanished, the note that had cut so deep she’d had to lock the instrument away. When his fingers brushed the iron sights, he couldn’t see them, couldn’t line them up, and the full brutal sum of all he had lost slammed into him, unarguable, unescapable. He slotted the guns back into their racks, and a wild, feverish hunger seized him: to pretend the blindness wasn’t there, to will it away so hard it ceased to exist.
The passion carried him, weightless, straight back to the East he’d been banished from. He could smell the hot dust, hear the street cries, see the white domes of the mosques rearing against the steel-blue sky, the throng of barefoot traders crowding the alleyways. He paced faster and faster, blind to the room around him, lost in his daydream. He was in Port Said, sailing down the Suez Canal in a long line of steamers, the deep monotonous chant of Arab labourers coaling the ship ringing in his ears, bright red brazier light gilding their naked shoulders as they tramped the planks between barges and deck after dark. He rounded the red headlands of the Sinai Peninsula, sailed through the cool chill of the Gulf of Suez, zigzagged down the length of the Red Sea while the Great Bear hung low in the northern sky and the Southern Cross blazed bright to the south. He stopped at Tor, at Yambo, watched the tall white houses of Jeddah rise out of the surf, their carved dark wood casements weathered and salt-stained, wandered through the shadowed covered bazaars like a man coming home after a lifetime away. Then he sailed past narrowing coral reefs into the landlocked harbour of Suakin, and the desert unfolded before him: the soft quiet pad of camel hooves in loose sand, the sheer rock cones that looked like they’d risen straight from a rippleless ocean, that you could march all day toward and never seem to get closer to, the wild blaze of sunset colour spilling across the western horizon, the wind rustling through the short twilight where the west turned pale milky green and the east deepened to inky indigo, planets dropping sharp and sudden out of nothing to kiss the earth.
He tramped back and forth across his dark study, parched with homesickness, forgetting his blindness, until he heard it: the loud chitter and piping of blackbirds and swallows in the garden outside. Dawn was breaking over the West Country, the world bleached white with frost and first light. He snapped back to reality. There would be no more journeys east for him. His affliction had caged him, clamped a soldered chain around his ankle. He felt his way to the stairs by the balustrade, crawled into his bed, and fell asleep just as the sun cleared the horizon.

* * * * *
But thousands of miles away at Dongola, on the great southward curve of the Nile below Wadi Halfa, the sun was already blazing hot, and the whole town was awake.
Word had spread like wildfire: a white prisoner, captured a week prior near the El Agia wells on the great Arbain road by a raiding party, had been dragged into town after dark, and the Emir Wad El Nejoumi was going to put him on display for the crowd that morning. Men, women, and children swarmed the open stretch of sand between the cluster of palm trees outside the Emir’s compound and the Nile bank, all hungry for the rare, brutal entertainment.
Harry Feversham stood in the shade of the palms, guarded by four Ansar soldiers. His uniform had been ripped from him, and all he had left was a tattered ragged jibbeh, a scrap of cotton tied around his head to shield his face from the glare. His bare shoulders and arms were raw, blistered from hours of sun. His ankles were fettered with iron, his wrists bound tight with tough palm fibre, a heavy iron collar locked around his neck attached to a chain one of the guards gripped in his fist.
He was grinning and cackling at the jeering crowd like a full-blown lunatic, and the onlookers ate it up. The madman act was his only shot. If he sold it well enough, if he convinced his captors he was either a fool or a wandering musician, not a British spy sent to stir up rebellion in Kordofan, they wouldn’t execute him on the spot. They’d be too confused to dispose of him themselves, and send him north to Omdurman for the Khalifa to judge. It was the only path he had to survival.
He could see the tall, black gallows rearing up stark against the yellow sand on the river bank, sinister as a bad omen. The waiting was the worst part, far worse than anything he’d endured in the two weeks he’d spent trapped in Berber. Back then he’d had a spark of hope for escape to cling to, and when the lantern had shone on him out of the darkness in the ruined fields, everything had happened too fast to stop and be afraid. Now he had hours of empty, waiting time to picture every gory detail of his death, his heart sinking so low he grew faint, just as it had when he was a boy, hearing the hounds whine and scuffle deep in a covert while he sat shaking on his horse. But the old cowardice was gone now. His greatest fear wasn’t the pain of hanging, it was that he would die here on this sand, and Ethne would never get to take back that fourth feather, that all the months of his penance would be for nothing.
An old crone danced right in front of him, cackling, singing a shrill monotonous song, miming brutal mutilations and describing in graphic detail the agonies of his coming death, and the eternal torments of hell that would follow. Harry understood every word, and shuddered inwardly, but all he did was mimic her gestures, grin and bob his head like she was singing him a ballad of Paradise. Groups of boys pressed the mouthpieces of their war trumpets right up to his ears and blew as hard as they could, yelling “Do you hear us, Kaffir? Blow harder!” Harry just clapped his bound hands together, laughing, and yelled back that the music was the sweetest he’d ever heard.
Then a towering warrior stepped through the crowd, carrying a long heavy spear, and the onlookers roared, clearing a wide space around him. He stood before Harry, swinging his spear back and forth to limber his arm, the way a bowler swings his arm before he delivers a cricket ball. Harry looked around wildly for an escape, saw nothing, and suddenly threw his chest out, ready to take the thrust. But the spear never touched him. One of the guards behind him wrenched the chain on his neck so hard he was flung backwards half-throttled onto the sand. They repeated the cruel game three times, the crowd roaring louder every time, before a soldier finally stepped out of the Emir’s gateway, yelling “Bring him in!”
Dragged through the arch, across the hot courtyard and into a dark inner room, Harry couldn’t see a thing for the first few seconds. When his eyes adjusted, he saw a tall bearded man seated on a native angareb bedstead, with two men squatting on the ground at his side. It was the Emir Nejoumi.
“You are a British spy sent from Wadi Halfa,” Nejoumi said flatly.
“No,” Harry cackled, as if the line was the funniest joke he’d ever heard. “I’m a musician.”
Nejoumi nodded, and a zither strung with half-broken strings was passed to him. Harry sank to the ground, his fingers fumbling over the strings, and plucked out the only melody he knew: the same soft tune Durrance had heard a wandering musician play in the streets of Tewfikieh on the eve of his last desert journey, the same tune Ethne had played on her violin back in Southpool the night before.
“You are a spy,” Nejoumi said again, once the last note died away.
“I told you the truth,” Harry said, stubborn, still grinning.
Nejoumi changed tack. He ordered a plate of raw camel liver heaped with salt and red pepper brought to him. Harry had never had less appetite for anything in his life, but he ate every bite, forcing the repulsive meat down, knowing that any show of reluctance would be read as fear, and fear would be a death sentence. While he ate, Nejoumi purred soft questions at him about the fortifications of Cairo, the number of troops garrisoned at Assouan, rumours of fighting between the Khedive and the Sirdar. Harry just blinked, and replied every time: “What would a Greek wandering musician know of such things?”
Nejoumi snapped an order, and the guards dragged Harry back out into the blazing sun. They poured water over the palm fibre rope around his wrists, and the thongs swelled, biting deep, tearing raw new cuts into his skin.
“Speak, Kaffir. You carry promises of guns and support to the rebel tribes of Kordofan.”
Harry said nothing. He clung tight to the madman act he’d planned for months, no last-minute panic was going to make him deviate. A noose was slung around his neck, and they pushed him up onto an angareb, directly beneath the dangling gallows.
“Speak now,” Nejoumi snarled. “And you will live.”
Harry just grimaced, shook his head loosely from side to side, cackling like the fool they thought he was. He was astonished he wasn’t on his knees begging for mercy. He wondered if the old stories were true, that English prisoners walked to the gallows calm and dignified because they’d been drugged. He had no drugs in his system, but he felt no urge to beg, no crippling fear. His heart was hammering fast, but it was with the sharp, bright thrill of playing his part perfectly. He didn’t even think of Ethne in that moment. All he had to do was see the performance through to the end.
Nejoumi glowered at him for a long quiet second, then turned to the two men standing by the angareb, ready to yank it out from under Harry’s feet.
“Take the Kaffir to Omdurman at dawn tomorrow,” he snarled.
Only then did Harry become fully aware of the burning, throbbing agony of the swollen palm fibre ropes, biting deep into the raw flesh of his wrists. He had won.

Mrs. Adair paced her sitting room that evening, her gut tight with unease. She’d spilled the whole truth of Ethne’s long-hidden love for Harry Feversham to John Durrance only days before, and she had no idea what he’d do next. It seemed only a matter of time before the blind man turned to Ethne, laid out his mistake, admitted he’d seen right through the false affection she’d offered him. If he did, Mrs. Adair knew her own secret meddling would come to light too—she’d have to meet Ethne’s steady, unflinching gaze and admit she’d betrayed the trust placed in her. The thought made her palms prickle with dread.

Weeks dragged by, and nothing changed. Durrance and Ethne met, talked, laughed just as they always had. The afternoon Willoughby had landed his boat at the garden creek, the night Ethne had played the Musoline Overture on her violin like her heart was breaking, might as well have been erased from both their memories. Mrs. Adair breathed easy at first—then, as the shock of no fallout wore off, she grew confused, then angry. Why was Durrance pretending he knew nothing at all?

Durrance had made up his mind long before that. Ethne thought her sacrifice of marrying him would give at least one person she cared for a happy life, that it would make up for all the ruin the three white feathers had wreaked on Harry. He had no right to yank that small, fragile comfort away from her. Not yet, anyway. First he needed absolute certainty: was the rift between Ethne and Harry irreparable, or just a terrible misunderstanding? And most of all, he had to know for sure that Harry Feversham was still alive.

So he leaned into the mask. He stopped scrutinizing every small shift in Ethne’s tone, forced himself to sound genuinely content with his lot, joked gently about his blindness.
“Funny, isn’t it?” he said to her one afternoon as they walked the terrace. “A man gets used to being blind, learns to navigate the world as if that’s how it’s always been. But I’ll never get used to you, Ethne. Every time we meet, I find some new little thing to delight in. And there’s always a chance a doctor can fix my sight, anyway.”

Ethne relaxed for the first time in months. She’d thought Durrance suspected her, had seen through her lie, and his easy cheer was such a relief it softened the edge of her own sharp, constant grief. The happiness she’d recaptured for a day after Willoughby brought the first feather back had faded fast. Sleepless nights stretched out again, her head thrumming with dull hot misery as she waited for the grey dawn, and she could no longer pretend Harry was dead. He was alive, somewhere in the desert, in unthinkable danger, and she ached both to know what he was suffering and to never imagine it at all.

One day, she forced her voice to sound casual, like she was making idle small talk. “I suppose it’s possible to escape Omdurman, isn’t it?”
“Possible, yes,” Durrance answered, light as anything. “Difficult, mind you, takes months of work. Folks have tried to get Trench and the other prisoners out, but none of the attempts have stuck. The problem’s always the go-between.”
“Go-between?” Ethne’s head snapped up. “The Arab trader that slips back and forth between Omdurman and the Egyptian border?”
“Exactly. Usually a Dervish peddler, trading with the Soudan tribes, who sneaks into Wadi Halfa or Suakin to do the job. But the risk is massive—if they get caught, they’ll be hanged or beheaded in the public square before sundown. Half the time they’re cowards, and turn back at the last minute. Worse than that, half of them are grifters. You hand them all your money in Egypt, and six months later they turn up empty-handed with a dozen excuses: the season was too hot, the guards doubled, someone suspected them, they just need a little more cash. And by the time you wise up, they’ve vanished with your gold.”

The last piece of the puzzle clicked into place for Ethne. She’d always known Harry was off on some mission to rescue Trench, but she’d never understood why he’d gone south into the desert himself instead of hiring a man to do the work. He was going to be his own go-between. Her hope flared hot and bright. Harry was careful, he planned every step three times over. He’d make it out. He’d bring Trench home. He’d bring that second feather back to her.

They sat silent for a while, watching the sunset bleed red over the creek water. “Life can’t be easy in the Omdurman prisons, can it?” Ethne said, forcing her voice to stay steady.
“Easy? Good god, no.” Durrance shook his head. “You’re locked in a hovel no bigger than a closet, crowded with 20 other men, roof two feet above your heads, no light, no air, shut in from sundown to sunrise. Half the night you have to stand shoulder to shoulder, no room to sit, breathing in stinking hot air. Imagine that, on an August Soudan night, and you’ve got memories of green Devon lawns or cold Irish rivers to torture you even more.”

Ethne’s throat went tight. Right that second, Harry could be gasping for breath in that dark hovel, fevered, dry-mouthed, seeing the grass slopes of Ramelton behind his eyes, hearing the Lennon River running in his ears.
“One would pray for death,” she said slowly, “unless—”
She almost finished: unless you went there on purpose with a job to do. But she bit the sentence off before it could escape.
“Unless there’s a chance of escape,” Durrance finished smoothly, not pressing her. He’d realized he’d laid the horrors on too thick, and softened quickly. “Mind you, that’s all just hearsay, no real proof. The prisoners might not have it half that bad. Don’t dwell on it.” He changed the subject before she could push him further.

For the whole month of September, they kept the pretense up. Durrance tramped across the fields every morning to visit Ethne at The Pool, they joked and chatted with no shadow of awkwardness between them, and Mrs. Adair stewed more and more, itching to blow the whole sham apart. All three of them were wound so tight with unspoken truths they could barely breathe, so when Durrance announced he was off to London to see his oculist, every last one of them heaved a sigh of relief. His trips got longer, more frequent. Even Ethne was glad for the break—she could drop her bright, happy mask for a few days, rest her bones, gather the strength to pretend all over again when he came back. Sometimes she woke up at 3 a.m. cold with dread, wondering how she could keep the lie up forever when they were married, living in the same house day and night. She pushed the thought down before it could break her.

After his third trip back from London, Durrance said to her, “Remember that famous oculist I mentioned in Wiesbaden? The doctors say I ought to go see him. And I ought to go alone. I might have to stay in a clinic for weeks, total quiet, no visitors at all.”
Ethne glanced at him, sharp. “You think I’d be a nuisance? I can find a cousin to travel with me, I won’t get in your way.”
“It’s not that,” he said, truthfully. He was just as sick of the endless performance as she was. “I need to go alone. I’ll be back from London next week, and I’ll tell you for sure then if I’m heading to Germany.”

His timing could not have been better. That very afternoon, a telegram arrived for Durrance from Wadi Halfa, sent by Calder. It was only four words long:

Durrance brought it over to The Pool that evening, and the second he handed it to Ethne he turned away, walked over to where Mrs. Adair sat reading in the drawing room, and left her standing alone on the terrace to unfold it. The September mist was curling up from the creek, creeping across the lawn, and halfway across the world the Omdurman prison doors would be clanging shut right then, locking Harry in for the night.
“It’s not fair,” Ethne whispered, leaning hard on the stone balustrade, eyes shut. “His fault was nothing. Mine and his father’s was worse. Neither of us understood.” She thought of the fourth feather she’d sent him, the one that had pushed him over the edge to leave. The cool, smooth stone under her hands felt like a cruel, mocking reminder of the comfort Harry would never feel in that hovel.

Durrance walked out onto the terrace quietly, making sure his footsteps made noise so he wouldn’t startle her. “I’ll do something. Harry’s my friend. I’m writing to Calder tonight, no money will be spared. We’ll send men out from Suakin, from Assouan, we’ll pull every string we can. We’ll get him out.” He framed it all as his own loyalty to his old comrade, so Ethne wouldn’t have to reveal how much Harry still meant to her.

But Ethne barely heard him. She cut in, sharp and sudden. “Major Castleton is dead, isn’t he?”
“Castleton?” Durrance blinked. “The man in Feversham’s old regiment? Aye, he was killed at Tamai. I was in the square that day, I saw Osman Digna’s men break through the line right where he was standing. He didn’t make it.”
Ethne’s voice was clear, steady, unapologetic. “I’m glad.”

That was the slip. Durrance’s sharp, soldier’s wits, already half-way to figuring out the whole story, stopped dead. She’d never even met Castleton. Why would she be glad a stranger was dead? All the scattered pieces clicked together. The white feather Willoughby had brought her, the symbol of cowardice, the one she’d clung to like it was gold. Her reaction to the news that Trench was in Omdurman, no surprise at all that Harry had vanished south to rescue him. Now Castleton: the third man who’d sent Harry a white feather, the last one left to call him a coward, who could never be rescued, never have his feather returned. The whole story made perfect sense now. Three feathers, three men to make amends to, Harry off to risk his life to earn every single one of them back.

“I know what happened now,” Durrance murmured under his breath, staring out across the mist-shrouded garden. “When I go back to London next week, I’m going to go see Lieutenant Sutch. He’s the only one left who can tell me the last of it, and then we’re going to bring Harry Feversham home.”

The club smoking room had a running, unspoken joke about Captain Willoughby: if he cornered you for a story, you would miss your dinner, your train, and possibly the end of the world before he finished the third meandering anecdote about a distant cousin no other living soul had ever met. He was unstoppable. Snub him as savagely as you liked, he would blink like you’d complimented his new necktie, wrap himself tighter in his own droning monologue, and grind every last scrap of normal conversation flat the way a steamroller crushes a country lane. Trite old stories slotted between copybook maxims, every word delivered like he’d just uncovered a universal secret no other man had ever thought to mutter. Hang around long enough, and you lost the will to flee, trapped in a web woven of pure, unrelenting dulness. Everyone who knew him never hung around. The second he stepped through the smoking room doors, half the members suddenly remembered urgent appointments across three different counties, leaving the most popular corner of the room – the one with the deep armchairs and the best view of the fire – completely empty, reserved exclusively for him.

One early October afternoon, he was sunk into that empty nook, nursing a half-empty whisky and mourning the lack of a single captive audience, when a waiter walked over and handed him a card. Willoughby brightened – company at last! – then his face fell so fast it almost hit his lap. Colonel Durrance? The blind soldier just back from the Sudan? What in hell was he doing here? He’d never once sought Willoughby out before. Had he found out about that trip Willoughby had taken down to Kingsbridge, to Ethne Eustace’s place?
“Colonel Durrance knows I’m in the club?” he asked the waiter.
“Quite sure, sir.”
“Right. Show him in.”

Willoughby steeled himself. He wasn’t about to get dragged back into the mess of Ethne Eustace, Harry Feversham, and those three cursed white feathers. Let the two men fight their own battles, no more intervention from him. He was already mentally drafting evasive answers when Durrance stepped into the room, walking with that easy, practiced stride most blind men never manage.
Willoughby hauled himself to his feet and guided Durrance over to his empty corner. “Smoke?” he started, then winced. “Sorry, stupid question.”
Durrance laughed, already fishing a cigar out of his case. “Don’t be. A man doesn’t need to see the smoke to enjoy it. If I let this thing go out, I’ll know immediately. Watch.” He lit it slow, settled back in the armchair, and tilted his face up towards the ceiling, the late afternoon sun gilding the scar across his brow. “Lucky I caught you. I’m only in town for the day, up from Devonshire to see my oculist. Last time I came up, Mather – you remember Mather, the bloke we served with at Suakin? – said you were off gallivanting in the country.”
“Mather? Course I remember him well,” Willoughby said, brightening. If they stuck to old Sudan stories, Durrance would forget all about the awkward stuff he was probably chasing.
“Good. We’ve got a question we both want cleared up. Ever run across an Arab named Abou Fatma?”
“Abou Fatma?” Willoughby frowned, buying time. “Hadendoa tribe, right?”
“Nope. Kabbabish.”
Willoughby repeated the name like he’d never heard it in his life. “Abou Fatma? No, never met him.” He clamped his mouth shut before he could blurt “why d’you ask?” – something he only realized later was a mistake. The name had only ever crossed his ears once, on the veranda of that old Red Sea palace in Suakin, a year prior, from Harry Feversham’s own lips.
“Curious,” Durrance said, blowing a soft wisp of smoke that drifted upwards. “Mather and I were on that last 1884 reconnaissance, remember? Found Abou Fatma hiding in the bushes outside Sinkat Fort. He told us he’d stashed a set of Gordon’s personal letters in a ruined wall in Berber. The *Evening Standard* ran a piece last year saying someone turned those letters in to you in Suakin, fifteen months back. We were wondering if it was him, if he’d made it back to Berber alive to get them.”
Willoughby’s stomach dropped. He’d been an idiot to say he never heard of the man. “Nah, it wasn’t Abou Fatma,” he said, fumbling with his pipe, suddenly very aware Durrance was listening to every tiny shift in his tone.
“Makes sense,” Durrance said evenly. “No one in their right mind would trek back into Berber after Gordon was dead, for no obvious reason. Not worth getting your throat cut over.”
“Exactly,” Willoughby huffed, relieved. He thought the conversation was done.
“For no obvious reason,” Durrance repeated, soft as silk.
Willoughby glanced at him, suspicious. Did he know?

Durrance didn’t press. He’d already put the pieces together himself, anyway. Who else but Feversham – the man everyone thought had run for the docks to avoid deployment five years prior – would have run that suicidal mission to Berber, to atone, to earn back his honour? No one else in Suakin had any motive to risk it. Willoughby’s stilted pauses, his over-careful answers, that same guarded tone Ethne Eustace had been using with him for months – all the proof he needed. He couldn’t have Willoughby running straight to Ethne to warn her he was digging, though. So he shifted the subject hard.
“That’s not actually why I tracked you down. It’s about Harry Feversham.”
Willoughby jumped so hard he nearly spilled his whisky.
“Met him back in spring, at Wadi Halfa,” Durrance went on like he hadn’t noticed the reaction. “He was working as a lowly labourer, fallen right off the map after he vanished five years back. I know you’re heading back to the Sudan in a week. I want you to look out for him. Help him get back on his feet, if you find him. Let me know. He was one of my very best friends.”
“Of course,” Willoughby said, easy now, all suspicion gone. “I’ll keep an eye out. Old friend of yours, like you said.” He reached for the matchbox on the side table, scraped a phosphorus alight, and held it to his old, caked briar pipe, which bubbled and gurgled as he sucked at it.
“One of my best,” Durrance agreed. “We dined at his flat, you remember, that place high over St James’s Park, right before we deployed. He’d just got back from Ireland to tell us he was engaged to Ethne Eustace. Almost the last night we ever saw him before he vanished. There was a third bloke there, that dinner.”
“Third?” Willoughby said, around his pipe, suddenly fumbling. “Can barely remember it, it’s so long ago.”
“Trench, wasn’t it?”
“Right! Trench. God knows when we’ll get to dine with poor old Trench again, out there in the desert.” He struck another match, hands a little shaky, to relight his pipe.
“Shame we’ll never dine with Castleton again, either,” Durrance said slowly.
“Castleton wasn’t even there!” Willoughby blurted, before he could stop himself.
“No, right,” Durrance said. “He was expected, though, wasn’t he?”
“Nah, not even expected,” Willoughby corrected. “He was dining with that big War Office bigwig, sent a telegram instead, don’t you remember?”

The last piece clicked into place for Durrance. That dinner, the telegram Feversham had crumpled up without showing anyone, the three white feathers that arrived for him a week later. The telegram had told him his regiment was being deployed to active service. That was the moment Feversham had broken, resigned his commission, thrown his whole life away. He didn’t say anything else about it, just chatted idly about the Sudan campaign prospects for another ten minutes before he stood up to leave.
“Owe you if you track Harry down. Draw on my bank for any money he needs.”
“Will do,” Willoughby said, grinning. “Before you go – you said you wouldn’t let your cigar go out. That thing’s stone cold.”
Durrance laughed, picking it off the table edge where he’d set it down half an hour earlier. “Got distracted thinking about Harry. Goodbye.”

He climbed into a waiting cab outside, while Willoughby poured himself another large whisky, very pleased with himself for hoodwinking the blind old colonel. But Durrance was far from hoodwinked. Every evasion, every slip of Willoughby’s tongue, had given him the full, unvarnished truth. Harry Feversham wasn’t a coward running from his duty. He was a man chasing redemption, paying back for that one terrible mistake he’d made five years prior, with every drop of blood and sweat he had to give.
He chuckled to himself, thinking of the look on old Sutch’s face when he told him the full story. That night, he took the overnight mail train back to Devonshire, pulling up at his cottage just before midday, the cold dead cigar still tucked in his coat pocket.

The autumn air hung sharp and thin in The Pool’s drawing room as Durrance said his final goodbye to Ethne. He had deliberately trimmed their leave-taking down to no more than a handful of soft, unhurried words: the carriage was already idling at Guessens’ steps out front, luggage strapped tight to its roof, his Sudanese servant leaning against the doorframe waiting to load the last of his bags.

Ethne walked with him out onto the stone terrace, where Mrs. Adair stood waiting at the top of the wide flight of steps. Durrance held out his hand to her, but she turned her back to him before he could take her leave, and spoke straight to Ethne.
“I need a word alone with Colonel Durrance before he leaves.”
“Of course,” Ethne said, her voice soft with unspoken sadness. She turned back to Durrance. “Then we’ll say goodbye right here. Write from Wiesbaden the moment you arrive, won’t you? Don’t delay.”
“The second I set foot in the hotel,” he promised.

He followed Mrs. Adair down the steps, leaving Ethne standing solitary on the terrace behind them. The last months of forced pretense, of quiet surveillance, of unspoken tensions they’d both had to tiptoe around, were finally over. The brisk, unhesitating swing of Durrance’s step across the lawn showed exactly how glad he was to be free of it.

But Mrs. Adair lagged at his side, her shoulders hunched, her voice heavy with despondency. “So you’re off. In two days you’ll be in Wiesbaden, and Ethne on her way to Glenalla in Donegal. We’ll all be scattered. This house will be so empty.” She’d gotten exactly what she’d fought for: Ethne and Durrance separated, no longer forced to see them laugh and talk together every hour of the day. But the victory tasted like ash in her mouth, no joy at all. “It will be lonely here,” she repeated, and turned off the main path to walk deeper into the walled garden.

“We’ll all come back eventually, no doubt,” Durrance said, trying to soothe her.

She glanced around at the faded grounds: all the summer blooms were long dead, sunlight blotted out by thick grey cloud cover, the grass underfoot a dull, washed-out green, the stream threading through the trees running murky and slate-grey, russet and gold leaves skittering across the lawn in the wind. “How long will you stay in Wiesbaden?”
“I can’t say for sure. As long as the doctors say it’s worthwhile for my sight,” he answered.

“That tells me nothing at all,” she snapped. “You deliberately won’t tell me anything.” Durrance said nothing, and his silence stung her. She had no clue if he intended to break his engagement to Ethne, or hold her to the promise she’d made after news of his blindness arrived. Curiosity ate at her, the thought of months, maybe years, of gnawing doubt torturing her when he was gone.
“You distrust me?” she said, sharp and angry.

Durrance spoke gentle as ever, no bite in his tone. “Have I no reason to? Why did you rush to tell me Captain Willoughby was coming here, when Ethne begged everyone to keep that secret? I’m glad I know the truth, but you betrayed her confidence, her oldest friend.”
“I’m your friend too,” she protested, then burst out, “How could I keep silent? Don’t you understand?”
“No.”

He’d never thought of her as anything more than a casual, pleasant acquaintance, and she knew it. That simple, single word stung her so deep every last scrap of prudence burned away. She started spilling every ugly, half-buried thought she’d hoarded for years, speaking in ragged, incoherent bursts, knowing even as she spoke that it was madness but unable to stop.
“I told you the truth on purpose, as brutally as I could, because you refused to see what was right in front of your face. I wanted to hurt you. I’m a terrible woman, I suppose. The two of you in that dark drawing room talking, me all alone on the terrace. Even today, the exact same thing. Will it always be that way?” She struck her hands together in a gesture of raw despair, but Durrance didn’t answer. He walked on fast towards the stile at the far end of the garden, his pace picking up so she had to scurry to keep up.

Her frenzy wouldn’t let her be deterred. “I wouldn’t have minded half so much if Ethne had truly cared for you. But she never did, not as anything more than a friend. Friendship’s worth nothing, is it?”
“Something, surely,” Durrance said quietly.
“It’s not enough to stop her shrinking from you!” she cried. “She’s afraid of you, because you’re blind. I’ll tell you the truth: I was glad when the news came you’d lost your sight at Wadi Halfa. Glad, when I saw you in Hill Street. Glad every day since. Because I saw her flinch. She thought her whole life would be fettered, tied down to a blind man, no fun, no freedom.” Her voice dropped to a fierce, trembling whisper. “I’m not afraid. Not of that. I’m not afraid.”

To Durrance, there had never been anything more horrific in all his years of soldiering in the Sudan than this wild, unguarded outburst from a woman he’d always thought of as gentle, reserved, Ethne’s closest friend.
“Ethne wrote to you from pity,” she ranted on. “Pity, nothing more. And after she sent that letter claiming she’d marry you, she got scared, too scared to admit it. You would have let her off easy, you’d have stayed her friend, but she didn’t have the courage to say the words.”

Durrance knew that was a lie. He knew Ethne’s heart, knew the real reason for all her quiet hesitations, but there was no use arguing. He’d be gone at dawn, Ethne already on the ferry to Ireland by sundown. There was no point fighting her slanders, and he was almost to the stile now, the barrier between the garden and the open fields beyond. He only said, soft and steady, “You’re not being fair to Ethne.”

At those words, the frenzy left her all at once. She saw how pointless every word she’d just spat out was. Durrance would never choose the terrace over the drawing room, never choose her over Ethne. She was flooded with hot, sharp shame at how low she’d sunk, and started to stammer out excuses.
“I’m a bad woman, I know. But my life was never happy. There’s something to be said for me, isn’t there?” It sounded weak, pitiful even to her own ears. They’d reached the stile, and Durrance had turned to face her. Some of the sternness had faded from his face. He remembered how dignified and quiet she’d been back when he still had his sight, could hardly believe this ragged, desperate woman was the same one, and he softened, ready to listen.
“Tell me,” he said gently.

“I was married straight out of school, barely a girl. Knew nothing of the world, nothing about the man they married me off to. My mother arranged the whole thing, thought she was doing me a favour, securing a comfortable position, saving me from poverty. I couldn’t say no, she was so imperious, I’d been raised to obey. The story’s common enough, but that doesn’t make the dullness any easier to bear. Adair was kind, he cared for me, all of that is true. But the days stretched on forever. Imagine those identical little terraced houses in the middle of a manufacturing town, every window the same, every door the same, smoke hanging thick over every street, every little plant in the window caked black with soot. My whole life was like that. All the big country house parties, the London season, the round of calls, none of it was enough for me. It was so empty, so grey. And then I realized: there was more to life than that. I’d see couples ride past, look at the wife’s face, see that bright, shining happiness, and know that kind of joy was out there for other people – but my mother had stolen the chance of it from me.”

All the sternness was gone from Durrance’s face now, and Mrs. Adair spoke with a simple, raw honesty, no trace left of the vicious anger from minutes before. “Then you came. I met you, again and again. You’d go off on your army duties and come back, and I finally knew exactly what that bright, shining thing I’d only glimpsed was. It was you. But it was still out of reach for me. I thought I could be happy just being your friend, watching you make your name, being proud of you. But then Ethne came, and you turned to her at once, instantly. If only you’d hesitated just a little. After that, I was sorrier than ever that you’d ever walked into my life.”
“I never had any idea. I never suspected a thing,” Durrance said, quiet, horrified. “I’m so sorry.”

“I made sure you never found out,” she said. “And I tried everything I could to keep you for myself. No matchmaker ever worked half as hard as I did to push Ethne and Captain Feversham together. And I succeeded.”

Durrance reeled back against the wooden stile, shocked so hard he almost laughed. This was where the whole mess had started. Feversham’s disgrace, the four feathers, his flight to the Sudan, Ethne’s years of quiet grief, the months of lies and pretense after Durrance went blind – all of it traced back to this lonely woman’s quiet, desperate plot years before. He thought of all the paths his life had taken, never once noticing that a woman standing just out of his line of sight was pulling all the strings that would shape every choice he made for decades.

“I succeeded,” Mrs. Adair went on, hollow. “You told me I’d done it, one morning in the Row. I was so happy I could have cried. Then you told me you were shipping out to the Sudan. Three years away, and those were the worst years of my life. You came back, my husband was dead, but Ethne was free. She turned you down once, then news came you were blind, and she claimed you. All those ups and downs – these last few months here, watching the two of you tiptoe around each other, those were the worst of all.”

Durrance couldn’t argue. He knew exactly what it felt like to chase something that was always just out of reach, and he couldn’t help but be gentle with her. “I’m truly sorry, Mrs. Adair.” There was nothing more he could say, no grand platitude to fix all the years of waste. He held out his hand to her. “Goodbye.”

“Goodbye,” she whispered. Durrance heaved himself over the stile, and set off across the stubble fields towards his house, his steady step carrying him away fast.

Mrs. Adair stood by that stile long after his footsteps faded to nothing, long after he vanished around the hedge line. She’d shot every last arrow in her quiver, and the only person she’d hit was herself. She thought ahead: if Durrance didn’t end up marrying Ethne, he’d come back to Guessens, to this village, as her neighbor. If she’d only kept her mouth shut, she would have had a true, loyal, constant friend right next door, someone who understood loneliness. That would have been so much more than nothing. But now, every time they met, they would both carry the memory of this afternoon, of the ragged, ugly secret she’d screamed out to him, and every conversation would be awkward, stiff, full of unspoken things. The loneliness she’d feared was coming, and she was the only one who had ever caused it.

Three months of hard-earned furlough stretched before Lieutenant Calder as he leaned on the upper deck rail of the Nile steamer churning south to north from Wadi Halfa to Assouan. He stared out at the blistering, sun-scoured shoreline, and one bitter thought looped in his head: this is a callous land, inhabited by a callous people.

Lashed tight to the steamer’s flank was a creaking native barge, its lower deck crammed full of local travellers and hessian sacks of grain. Right in the middle of the crowd sat an angareb — the simple native bedstead strung with woven matting — and stretched flat across its frame was a still, shrouded figure draped in heavy black veil. Two Arabs had hauled that bed and its unknown burden on board at Korosko at dawn, and they now squatted in the barge’s stern laughing, arguing over a game of dice, as if their charge was no more than a sack of rotting dates. The figure beneath the veil might have been a corpse for all the attention it got: no twitch of a limb, no stir of cloth over the mouth.

Calder dragged his gaze from the barge to the banks on either side. Glaring white sand rolled all the way to the horizon, dotted with half-eroded, weather-beaten pyramids jutted out of the rock like broken fists. The only hint of life was a thin, ragged sliver of green fringing the water’s edge, the Soudan’s entire, stingy response to spring, summer, and the Nile’s annual flood. A callous land, inhabited by a callous people, he repeated to himself.

He looked back down to the angareb. He couldn’t tell if the shrouded figure was man or woman: the black cloth pulled tight over the face outlined the curve of a nose, the hollow of eye sockets, the shape of a mouth, but hid every hint of moustache or beard. The slanting afternoon sun crawled slowly closer, gilding the deck planks, inching up the bedstead’s legs, until the blazing yellow light spilled over the still figure’s feet, then their shins, then their chest, until it burned pitiless, full force, over the hidden face. Not a muscle stirred. The veil never fluttered over the lips, the legs stayed rigidly straight, the arms lay motionless at the figure’s sides.

Calder snapped a shout down at the two dice-playing Arabs in the stern.
“Drag that bed into the shade, now — move!”

The men grumbled, rose slow and resentful, and obeyed.
“Is that a man or woman?” Calder called down.
“A man. We’re taking him to the hospital in Assouan. He won’t live, though. Fell out of a palm tree three weeks back.”
“And no one’s given him food or water since?”
“He’s too far gone for it to matter.”

It was a story Calder had heard a hundred times, the inevitable result of that brutal local fatalism: every life and every death is written, and there’s no point fighting what’s already been set down. That man had probably only suffered a bad bruise at the start, one a week of simple care would have fixed entirely. But they’d left him to bake in the sun, swarmed by flies, unwashed, unfed, burning with thirst. The bruise had turned to an open sore, the sore had gone gangrenous, and only when every possible remedy was too late had the local Mudir at Korosko found the accident and ordered him shipped downstream to Assouan.

Familiar as the story was, Calder couldn’t shake the image of that still, silent man on the angareb. He felt a small, quiet flicker of relief when a sharp, strong wind blew up from the north, dead against the river’s current, kicking up cool gusts that would give the fevered patient a little relief. That evening, sat across the dinner table from a man with a thick German accent, he acted on a sudden impulse.
“You wouldn’t happen to be a doctor, would you?”
“Not quite — a medical student from Bonn. I came up from Cairo to see the Second Cataract, but they wouldn’t let me go further than Wadi Halfa.” The student pushed his glasses up his nose, smiling good-naturedly.
“Then I’m afraid I’m about to ruin your holiday and ask for your professional help.”
“For yourself? But you look perfectly healthy—”
“Not for me. For an Arab man on that angareb down on the barge. He fell from a palm tree three weeks ago, and no one’s touched him since. The sight won’t be pretty. Wounds go bad fast out here, no care.”

The German student shrugged, already pushing his chair back from the table. “All clinical experience is useful,” he said.

The wind had only gotten stronger while they ate. Waves crashed against the steamer’s hull, the barge tossed and pitched alongside, cold spray sloshed over its lower deck. Night had fallen thick and dark, the only light a single dim lantern hung from the barge’s upper deck, casting wavering, shifting shadows over a tiny patch of planks. Beyond that small circle, all was inky black save for the white sheets of spray that blew over the barge’s bow, glinting like scattered snow, cracking against the wood like a whip lash.
“Wait — he’s not where you left him,” the student said, leaning over the steamer’s rail. “There’s no bed in the shade.”

Calder stared down into the dark for a long minute, his stomach sinking. “I think I see it. It’s in the bows. Right in the path of the spray.”

The two of them hurried down the steamer’s steps to the lowest deck, clambered over the bulwarks down onto the lurching barge. The angareb sat in a puddle of sloshing river water, dragged all the way back to the bow, the black-shrouded figure as motionless as it had been all day, cold spray breaking over what would be its face, no one having bothered to drag it back to shelter. Calder fetched a bright lantern, called the two Arabs over, and ordered them to strip the black veil off.

The German knelt to examine the man’s swollen, rotting thigh. It was as bad as Calder had predicted: the wound crawled with advanced infection, the student was glad to yank a clean cloth back over it minutes later.
“I can do nothing for him here,” he said, wiping his hands on his trousers. “A hospital in Assouan, with baths and proper dressings, might be able to give him some relief. But a full recovery? I can’t promise that. And for the record — this man never fell out of a palm tree. That’s a lie. That wound was made by a spear blade. A deliberate, weapon-inflicted injury.”

Calder’s head snapped to the still man on the bed. He’d been watching his face closely, and he saw it: the man’s eyes, half-lidded, tracked every word the two of them spoke. He understood English.
“Where are you from?” Calder asked, leaning down, lantern held close to the man’s face.

The man’s lips trembled, no sound came out — but his eyes were bright, alive, desperate to answer. Calder reeled off the names of Sudanese towns, one after another. “Dongola? No. Metemneh? No. Berber? No. Omdurman?”

The man’s eyelids fluttered shut, a tiny flicker of a nod clear in the movement. He was from Omdurman.
“Were you wounded trying to help someone escape?” Calder pressed, leaning even closer. He named every white captive known to be held in the Dervish stronghold, one by one, and every time the man’s eyes gave a small, clear no. Until he said the name he’d been waiting for updates on for months, the man who’d vanished deep into the Soudan on a secret, unauthorised mission: “Effendi Harry Feversham?”

The man’s eyes flashed a yes, bright as any spoken word.

That was all they could get. The man was too weak to speak, too far gone to say his own name. The two Arabs stuck stubbornly to their story: some distant kinsmen in Korosko had paid them a small sum to ferry this stranger to Assouan’s hospital, they knew nothing about him, nothing about how he got hurt. Calder had the piece of information his comrade Durrance had been begging for, hard proof of Harry Feversham’s fate — but it was locked away, trapped in a body that could barely breathe, let alone talk.

He arranged to get the wounded man straight to the Assouan hospital the second the steamer docked. The attending doctors shook their heads when he asked for the prognosis. There was a tiny, slim chance he might live. But even if he did, it would be months before he recovered, if he ever regained the ability to speak.

Calder had no choice but to continue his journey north to Cairo, then on to Europe for his furlough. The chance to unlock the full truth of Harry Feversham’s mission had slipped right through his fingers. For the still, silent man on the hospital bed was Abou Fatma, of the Kabbabish tribe. And his half-dead, wounded presence on that Nile steamer meant that Harry Feversham’s months-long, carefully laid plan to rescue his comrade Colonel Trench from Omdurman had failed.

The exact moment Calder stepped onto a southbound train carriage at Assouan, fuming that he’d failed to dig up a single new scrap of news about Harry Feversham, Lieutenant Sutch jolted along a high, chalk-white Hampshire lane, his pony’s hooves kicking up dust across stretches of heather and gorse that stretched to the hazy blue rim of the downs. And he, too, was sick with worry over Harry. Decades of solitary half-pay retirement had turned him into a man who spoke his unspoken thoughts right out loud, and as he drove slow, dragging his heels like a boy heading for a beating, he muttered the same line over and over: “I saw this coming. From the very start, I knew there’d be nothing but trouble.”

The ridge dipped sharp down into a hollow, and the road plunged steep between dark ranks of pine trees toward a tiny country railway station. Sun glinted off the rails, telegraph poles strung out straight until they blurred into a huddle far off, and the sight twisted Sutch’s gut tighter. He reined the pony to a full stop, scowling at the station’s red tile roof.

“I promised Harry I’d breathe a word to no one,” he mumbled, clinging to the thought like a lifeline, repeating it firmer: “Swore to it, that night in the Criterion grill room, thick with cigar smoke and steak fumes.”

A sharp, thin train whistle cut the quiet from miles off, white smoke snaking over the fields like a ribbon. Sutch sighed, flicked his whip against the pony’s flank, and trotted down the hill to meet the incoming train. Only two passengers stepped out: Durrance, and his personal servant. Sutch hailed him, and the blind man turned to the sound of his voice, stepping carefully toward the trap.

“Got my telegram in time, I take it?” Durrance said.
“Lucky it caught me at home,” Sutch replied.
“I brought a bag. Hope you don’t mind putting me up for a night.”
“Of course not,” Sutch said, but his tone was so reluctant even a sighted man would have picked up on it.

Durrance had expected that—he’d sent the telegram last minute on purpose, because he’d suspected if he gave Sutch warning, he’d get a flat refusal. The wire said nothing about his errand, but it had tipped Sutch off that something was very wrong with Ethne Eustace and Durrance’s engagement, that Durrance was here to badger him for more details about Harry Feversham, the secret he’d sworn to keep locked tight. Then he saw Durrance feeling blindly for the trap’s step, the raw reminder of his total blindness, and a stupid, selfish little flicker of relief crossed his mind. Easy enough to mislead a blind man, right? He reached out, caught Durrance’s elbow, and hauled him up into the seat beside him. He even found a spark of pity, too, as they trotted back up the hill.

“Devil’s own luck, what happened to you,” he said gruffly. “I know what it’s like, getting tossed aside mid-career when everything’s finally going your way. My leg wound from Crimea’s nothing next to blindness, but I won’t feed you that rubbish about patience and silver linings, the garbage soft, untouched people spout. We both know there’s no compensation for a young, active man who had his whole career ripped away for no fault of his own.”

“For no fault of his, no,” Durrance echoed slowly. “Only the man who ruins his own career through his own mistake ever gets silver linings.”

Sutch glanced sharp at him. That line hit too close to home. “What kind of compensations?” he asked, unease pricking at him.
“First off, the chance to finally know who he really is. He hits a wall, gets disgraced, all that sham life he was living where he thought he was a different man falls to pieces. That shock? That’s his opening. He can spend the rest of his life making it right. We both know exactly the case I’m talking about, don’t we?”

Sutch didn’t even pretend to be confused. Five years of loving Harry like he was his own dead sister Muriel’s son, he couldn’t stand to hear anyone misjudge him. “You’re talking about Harry Feversham. But you’ve got it half wrong. He didn’t find out he was a coward, not at first—he convinced himself he was one, and he was wrong. So he set off to make amends. But how the hell did you figure any of this out? No one but me knew the whole truth.”

“I’m engaged to Ethne Eustace,” Durrance said.
“She didn’t know all of it, though,” Sutch argued. “She knew about the disgrace, not that he went off to redeem himself.”
“She does now,” Durrance said, and added, sharp as a needle: “And you’re glad of it. Very glad.”

Sutch hadn’t hooted, hadn’t shifted, hadn’t made a sound to give that away—but Durrance was right, blind or no. He didn’t even bother lying. “Aye, I am glad. Dead glad. I can see why from your side you’d rather she never knew, but I can’t help it.”

Durrance laughed, no bite to it at all. “I like you all the better for that honesty. But Willoughby’s the one who told her, you know. He showed up in Devonshire six weeks back, brought a white feather straight from Sudan, formally withdrawing his accusation that Harry was a coward.”

Sutch hauled hard on the reins, stopping the pony dead right in the middle of the road. He forgot Durrance was sitting right next to him, forgot everything but that bright, bursting joy. He was pushing 70, blood running slow, joys all soft and muted these days—but this was the kind of giddiness he hadn’t felt since he was 20. Five years prior, he’d stood on Dover pier in rain and dark, watching the mail packet carrying Harry to Egypt steam out of sight, and prayed he’d live long enough to see this day. The years of guilt—of failing to watch over Harry after his mother died, of holding his tongue that night at Broad Place when he could have warned Harry, of dreaming of Muriel’s soft, reproachful voice asking why he hadn’t helped her boy—all that weight lifted right off his shoulders.

“I knew it,” he breathed. “Knew he’d never fail. Tell me every last detail. He saved Willoughby’s life, didn’t he?”
“Not exactly,” Durrance said, and told him the whole story of the lost Gordon letters, Harry sneaking through enemy lines to recover them, saving Willoughby from a court martial that would have ended his career. Sutch drank every word in, not wanting to miss a single syllable.

“I didn’t come here to pester you for questions like I did that day in my London club courtyard,” Durrance went on. “I came here to give you information. And to get you to agree Harry needs to come home.”

Sutch blinked. He’d spent all afternoon bracing to parry Durrance’s questions, and now the man was telling him Harry had more than earned the right to come back. “I thought Miss Eustace would have told you the whole story herself, showed you the feather,” he said.
“Not a word,” Durrance said. “She never breathed a hint, ordered Willoughby to keep his mouth shut, sent him packing from Devonshire before I even knew he was there. You’re disappointed, aren’t you? You thought she’d break our engagement the second she heard.”

Sutch jolted. He hadn’t made a sound. But Durrance was right—he’d been hoping Ethne would remember Harry, that her loyalty to the man who’d risked everything to clear his name would win out. “How the devil did you dig all this up, then?”
“I tracked Willoughby down in London. He’s not the sharpest. I wore him down until he told me the whole story. And since I went blind? I’ve started seeing things no one else notices. I can tell you we’re driving over open heather right now, for a start. I can tell you the last mile was downhill through pine woods. I’ve never been within ten miles of this station before in my life.”

The proof sent a cold little prickle down Sutch’s spine. He hadn’t guessed a blind man could be that sharp. Then Durrance started talking about the three officers who’d sent Harry those feathers—Trench, Castleton, Willoughby—how he’d known all three in Suakin, completely ordinary, unremarkable men, no mark on their faces to show they’d shaped half a decade of another man’s hell.

They pulled up to Sutch’s small, isolated cottage, overlooking miles of rolling downland. No sea view, Sutch admitted, because he couldn’t bear the sight of ships sailing off to the horizon, reminding him of all the lost voyages he’d never make. Durrance nodded—he understood perfectly.

They ate dinner, sat over their wine by the fire, and finally Durrance laid out his errand plain. “I knew if I told you Harry could come home the second I walked in, you’d think I was trying to steal Ethne from him before he got back. So I waited for you to say it on your own. She hasn’t forgotten him, you know. She’s loyal, and she’ll keep her promise to marry me—but she only said yes out of pity, because I was blind. That’s no kind of marriage for either of us. Harry has earned the right to come back, and to her. I want you to go get him.”

Then he dropped the final, earth-shaking news. “He’s not just still in Sudan. He’s a prisoner in Omdurman, same as Trench.”

Sutch went completely still for a long minute, the shock knocking all the breath out of him. Then Durrance pushed over a stack of letters: one to Willoughby, now Deputy Governor of Suakin, one to a wealthy Greek merchant there who would advance Sutch every penny he needed for bribes, supplies, the entire rescue. Sutch reached across the table, grabbed Durrance’s hand hard in his own, even though the blind man couldn’t see the gesture.

“First boat tomorrow,” he swore. “I’m off to Sudan, and I’m bringing Harry home.”

Lieutenant Sutch had dragged himself to bed far later than he planned, but he was up before the first rooster crowed, buzzing with a boyish excitement he hadn’t felt in thirty long years. He roused his entire household before dawn, tossed his old travelling kit out onto every flat surface in the hall, repacked his saddle bags three times over, and kicked up such a ruckus that every small task took twice as long to finish. The small cottage had never seen such flurry, not in all the decades Sutch had lived there, half-pay and idle, as he liked to joke, “marked off the world’s active service list.”

His staff couldn’t move fast enough, scurrying down corridors to hunt for a lost canteen, a dented pair of field glasses wrapped in oilcloth, a frayed sun helmet he’d carried to Egypt in the 1870s. Sutch was almost feverish with eagerness: after a lifetime of no purpose, something real had fallen into his lap, something that needed grit, tact, guts. He was going back to work, and he was terrified any small delay would yank that chance away, dump him right back into the endless, empty quiet of half-pay retirement.

“I’ll be ready to catch the night mail for the Continent this very afternoon,” he told Durrance over breakfast, stabbing his toast with a grin. “We can ride up to London together—London’s on your way to Wiesbaden, after all.”

“No,” Durrance said quietly. “I’ve got one last visit to make in England. I didn’t think of it until I was in bed last night. You put the idea in my head.”

Sutch raised an eyebrow. “Who on earth are you off to see?”

“General Feversham.”

Sutch set down his knife and fork with a clatter, blinking in shock. “Why would you want to call on him?”

“I want to tell him Harry redeemed his honour, and is still out there fighting to make it right. You told me last night you’re bound by a promise not to breathe a word of Harry’s plan or his successes to the old man until Harry steps through his front door himself. But I made no such promise. That oath is cruel to him. Nothing on earth could hurt General Feversham worse than proof his son was a coward. He’d have rather Harry robbed a bank, committed murder, than bear that stain. I’m riding into Surrey this morning to tell him Harry never ran from fear at all.”

Sutch shook his head. “He won’t make sense of it. He’ll thank you, he’ll be glad the family name is clear, but he’ll never grasp why Harry chose to walk into that mess in the first place. All he’ll care about is that those dead Fevershams staring down from their portrait frames on his hall walls don’t have a black mark on their record.”

“I don’t agree,” Durrance said. “Beneath that stiff old soldier act, he’s fond of his son. He’d never admit it to a soul, but I’d bet my life on it. I’m rather fond of him, too.”

Sutch hadn’t spoken to General Feversham much in the last five years. He could never forgive the old man for his part in Harry’s ruin: if he’d ever had an ounce of sympathy, a glimmer of understanding for his son’s quiet, anxious nature, those three white feathers would never have been sent to Ramelton. Sutch could picture him perfectly, sitting rigid on his terrace at Broad Place, convinced he bore no blame at all, posing as a martyr whose only grief was that his son had shamed every Feversham that ever wore a uniform.

“You’re kinder to others than most,” Sutch said, shaking his head.

“It’s no mark of virtue,” Durrance laughed softly. “Self-preservation, that’s all. Blindness makes selfishness creep into you like damp through a tent wall. I’ve learned to guard against it before it takes root.”

He rattled across Hampshire’s rutted branch lines that morning, and pulled up at Broad Place in the honeyed glow of late afternoon. General Feversham was months short of his eightieth birthday, his back still ramrod straight, as stiff and unyielding as it had been the night he first introduced his teenage son Harry to his old Crimean mess mates. But he’d shrunken in the last two years, his face narrow and small, his boots thudding heavier than ever on the terrace flagstones. Even blind, Durrance could feel that quiet shift in him, hear the faint, uncharacteristic childish tremor in his voice.

“You’ve joined the old veterans before your time, Durrance,” the general greeted him gruffly. “Read it in the papers. Would’ve written to you if I’d known where to send the letter.” He gave no hint he guessed the purpose of the visit, rang the bell to send for tea, and set the tray down between them in the great hall lined from floor to ceiling with the Feversham portraits. He ranted about old Soudan acquaintances he hadn’t heard from, raged at the War Office’s incompetence, and declared the whole country was going to the dogs. “Everything’s going to ruin through bad luck and worse management!” he snapped. “Even you, Durrance—you’re not the same man who walked this terrace with me two years ago.” The general had never been known for tact, and years of isolated old age had worn away any soft edges he’d once had.

“I’ve come about Harry Feversham,” Durrance said bluntly.

Feversham sat bolt upright in his chair, rigid as a rifle stock. “And I’ll give you the same answer I’ve given every man that asked. I have nothing to say about Harry Feversham. I will not discuss him.” His voice was flat, emotionless, as if he spoke of a total stranger. Not a flicker of sorrow in the tone of his name. Durrance began to wonder if all fatherly affection had dried up in the old man’s chest years ago.

“Would it give you no pleasure to know where Harry’s been, how he’s lived these last five years?”

There was a short, sharp pause, before Feversham bit out: “Not in the least, Colonel Durrance.” It was uncompromising, but Durrance held fast to that small pause, the split second of longing he’d heard under the gruffness.

“Not interested in the work he’s risked his life to do?”
“Not even a little bit. I don’t wish him to starve, my solicitor tells me he cashes his allowance every quarter. That’s all I need to know.”

“Then I’m going to risk your anger, General,” Durrance said calmly. “There are times it’s wise to disobey your commanding officer. This is one of them. You can have me shown the door if you like. Otherwise, I’m telling you the whole story of your son, and my friend, since he vanished from England.”

Feversham huffed a short, dry laugh. “I can’t very well throw you out as my guest,” he said, severe, “but I warn you you’re taking a very improper advantage.”

“Quite right,” Durrance said. And he told the story, plain and unvarnished: Harry’s secret journey to Berber to recover the lost Gordon letters, their unexpected meeting at Wadi Halfa, his months of brutal enslavement in Omdurman, the lash marks across his palms, the months Lieutenant Sutch had spent planning his rescue. He finished with the news that Sutch himself was sailing for Suakin that very day.

General Feversham did not interrupt once. He did not shift in his chair, did not make a sound, until Durrance had finished speaking. He sat silent for a long while, raising one hand to shade his eyes as if shielding them from a blaze of sunlight, his face turned away from the ranks of dead Feversham portraits on the wall. Pride would not let those old soldiers see him break, see the joy and relief crack his stern features.

“What I don’t understand,” he said slowly, his voice thick, “is why Harry ever resigned his commission. I couldn’t make sense of it before, and it makes even less sense now I’ve heard how brave he’s been. One of those queer, inexplicable things, I suppose. But I’m very glad you forced me to listen, Durrance.”

“I came with a purpose,” Durrance said. “It’s your call, but I see no reason Harry can’t come home, take back every good thing he lost.”

“He can’t get everything back,” Feversham said. “He doesn’t deserve to. He made his choice, he has to pay the price. He’ll never get his army career back, for one.”

“That’s true enough. But he’s young enough to build a new life. That’s all he’ll have lost.”

Feversham dropped his hand, shifted in his chair, and turned his face fast toward Durrance. He opened his mouth to ask a question, then thought better of it. “Well,” he said briskly, as if the matter was of no small consequence, “if Sutch pulls that escape off and gets Harry out of Omdurman, I see no reason he shouldn’t come home.”

Durrance stood up. “Thank you, General. If you can send me to the station, I’ll catch the six o’clock train up to London.”

“Stay the night, man,” Feversham protested. “I never see anyone these days. Got no desire to, most of the time. Grows on you, old age, sticking to your own little routines.”

“I can’t. I leave for Wiesbaden first thing tomorrow.”

Feversham rang the bell and gave the carriage order, then turned back to Durrance, quieter than before. “There haven’t been any Crimean mess nights here, not since Harry left. I had no heart for them.” For a second the mask slipped, his stern, lined face softening. For five long, lonely years in his old age, he had grieved, and no one had ever guessed. He had stood stubborn, unbroken, before the whole world, determined no one could point a finger at him and say he had been struck down. Those old stories of men standing fast in the snow trenches of the Crimea would have cut him like a knife, for every tale of courage was a reminder he believed his own son had run.

The carriage wheels crunched on the gravel drive. Durrance held out his hand. “Goodbye.”

“By the way,” Feversham said, trying to sound casual, as he walked him to the door, “didn’t I hear somewhere that you were once engaged to Miss Eustace?”

“Earlier I said Harry will get almost everything he lost back,” Durrance said, and stepped up into the carriage. He was done. All he had left to do was wait in Wiesbaden, and pray Sutch succeeded. His part of the plan was over—now it was for the men with eyes to see to pull it off.

General Feversham stood on the front steps long after the carriage had vanished behind the pine trees. He walked slowly back into the great hall, staring up at the ranks of his ancestors’ portraits, every one of them uniformed, unblemished, honoured. “There’s no reason he shouldn’t come home,” he said out loud, to the faces on the walls. “No reason at all. Please God, he comes home soon.”

The thousand miles of trackless desert separating Broad Place from the Dervish city of Omdurman suddenly loomed huge, sharp with danger, and the old man felt every year of his almost eighty years settle heavy on his shoulders. That night, he sat straight and stiff on the old stone garden bench that had been his wife’s favourite, staring out across the moonlit fields to the far Sussex Downs, and whispered that same prayer over and over, soft into the dark: Please God, Harry will come home soon.

This was before the great mud rampart went up around Omdurman’s House of Stone. For now, only a crudely strung thorn zareeba ringed the noisome prison and the stretch of ground around it, perched on the eastern edge of what was surely the filthiest, most godforsaken capital any empire had ever spawned. No flower pushed through the dust in any corner, no blade of grass grew, no tree lifted its green shade to break the glare of the brutal sun. A burnt, stony brown plain stretched out from the city, which crawled with vermin and reeked of untreated disease, a straggling knot of rotting hovels huddled as if cringing from the heat.

No houses stood between the prison and the Nile, and in those days, so long as daylight held, chained captives were allowed to stumble half a mile down the broken, sloping earth bank to the river to draw their water and wash themselves. For natives and enslaved people, escape was barely a challenge: dhows moored thick along the shore, the wide foreshore doubled as a bustling market, and the crowds thronging the open stretch between the river and the House of Stone made the perfect cover. Captives would slip into the throng mid-conversation with friends, head straight for a blacksmith who would strike off their fetters for a price far higher than any fine the Khalifa’s men could levy. Iron collars and chains were so common across Omdurman, worn by every enslaved person, that the clink of metal on ankle never drew a second glance.

For a European, though, escape was a different kind of war. There were so few white prisoners that every single one was a marked man. Getting away required relays of camels pre-staged across hundreds of miles of empty desert, a mountain of gold, months of slow planning, and most of all, local allies brave enough to risk a flogging or a public execution to smuggle you out. Even that was no guarantee: camel drivers could vanish from their posts overnight, a gaoler’s random suspicion of hidden money could lead to a beating so severe you couldn’t stand for weeks, and even the most loyal servant could freeze with fear at the final step.

Colonel Trench was already losing hope. He knew his friends were working for him: the boy who brought his prison rations would whisper for him to be ready sometimes, and during parades of the Khalifa’s troops, when he was paraded on a camel as a trophy to prove the “Turks” would all fall to the Mahdist armies, strangers would jostle his mount and murmur words of encouragement. But nothing ever came of those plans. He watched the sun climb every day beyond the river bend, behind the tall palms of Khartoum, and blaze across the sky while month after month dragged by.

It was a late August evening, the same year Durrance came home blind from the Sudan, and Trench huddled in a corner of the zareeba, watching the sun sink west over the plain, his chest tight with the familiar agony of anticipation. No heat of the day could compare to the horrors that woke with every nightfall. Twilight had barely settled when Idris es Saier, the massive Gawaamah tribe black gaoler, and his crew marched into the enclosure.

“Into the House of Stone!” he roared.

Prayers and curses tangled together, the crack of whips falling on the backs of the slowest prisoners echoing across the yard, as the captives jostled and fought to squeeze through the narrow prison door. Thirty sick, half-starved men already lay sprawled on the waterlogged mud floor or propped against the walls on the edge of death. Two hundred more were herded in after them, locked inside until dawn. The room was barely thirty feet square, with a solid four-foot thick central pillar holding up the roof, no windows, and a handful of tiny air holes punched near the eaves for show. The screaming, fighting mass of prisoners was crammed so tight no one could move without shoving their neighbor, and the door slammed shut behind them, swallowing the last of the twilight in perfect, unbroken darkness. A man couldn’t see the outline of the head pressed against his own shoulder.

Trench fought like a wild beast, same as the rest. There was a tiny spot by the door he craved more fiercely than he’d ever craved anything in his free life: that corner would shield him from flailing arms, stamping feet, and the sharp edges of his neighbors’ shackles, and give him a wall to lean his back against for the ten interminable, suffocating hours of the night.

That terror hummed in his bones like poison, driving him half mad. If a man went down under that trampling throng, he never got up again—he was crushed out of shape, left for the attendants to drag out like a broken sack at dawn. Trench had seen the bodies pulled out morning after morning, and he was a small, slight man. He kicked out with his fettered feet, rammed his elbows into ribs, dived under a tall man’s arm, burrowed between two others, tore at their clothes, and even swung the heavy chain hanging from the iron ring around his neck to bash at heads. He made it to his corner at last, soaked in sweat, gasping for air, and the rest of the night would be spent fighting off anyone who tried to take it from him.

“If I were to fall!” he panted, yelling to make himself heard over the din. “Is that you, Ibrahim?”

A shout came back. “Aye, Effendi.”

Relief flooded Trench. The huge tall Hadendoa Arab Ibrahim and him had built a hard, loyal friendship out of shared suffering. There were no official rations in Omdurman’s prison: every man survived only on money smuggled in from outside, or the charity of fellow captives. Sometimes Trench got cash from friends snuck up from Assouan or Suakin, but there were long empty stretches where no help came, and he lived on handouts from Greek converts to the Mahdist faith or starved as best he could. Sometimes Ibrahim had no one to bring him food. They shared everything, and every night they stood shoulder to shoulder against the wall.

“I’m here, Effendi,” Ibrahim said, groping through the dark to steady Trench against the stone.

A vicious brawl had broken out in the far corner of the prison, and the packed mass swayed in a weird, synchronized rhythm every time one fighter advanced and the other retreated. Men fought to stay on their feet with their teeth bared, and above the clang of chains, the ragged gasps of hundreds of men, and the stream of curses, a wild sobbing cry for mercy would rise now and then, followed by a strangled, snuffed-out shriek that told everyone another man had gone down under the boots. Prisoners even hurled clods of the filth from the mud floor across the room, and since no one could see who threw it, men bashed their heads together scrambling to get out of the way. All of it happened in total blackness.

Trench stood for two hours in that ringing, stinking, overheated hell, eight more full hours still stretching before him until the door would open and he could stumble out to breathe clean air and collapse in the zareeba. He stood on tiptoe to lift his head above the crowd, but even then he could barely draw breath, the air thick and sour and damp. His throat was parched, his tongue swollen and dry as a shrivelled fig. He thought no torment devised by any power on earth could be worse than the House of Stone on an August Omdurman night—save for fire, maybe.

“If I were to fall!” he cried, and even as the words left his mouth, his hell got worse. The door creaked open. Idris es Saier stood in the gap.

“Make room!” he yelled, and he hurled burning tufts of dry grass into the crowd to drive the prisoners back. Flaming torches blazed in the dark, falling onto men’s shoulders and heads, and the captives were packed so tight they couldn’t dodge. Some couldn’t even lift their hands to brush the burning straw off their skin.

“Make room!” Idris shouted again, and his gaolers lashed out with their whips at everyone within reach, carving a small empty space right by the door. They flung a man into that gap, then slammed the door shut behind him.

Trench was standing so close to the door he caught a glimpse of the new prisoner in the faint remaining twilight seeping through the crack: slight, bent double with suffering, weighed down by iron so heavy he could barely stand.

“He’s going to fall,” Trench thought. “He’ll be trampled before the night’s out. God—don’t let him fall!”

The crowd surged forward, and the new prisoner was shoved like a wedge between fighting men, dragged away from the door crack where he’d been clinging to breathe fresh air, until he was slammed hard right into Trench.

No ordinary kindness could survive that nightmare prison. Out in the daylight, captives often helped each other, Muslim and Christian alike bound by shared misery. But once the dark fell, the only rule was to fight for your life with no pity, no pause, for as long as you could stay upright. Trench had been no different a minute before, the desperate need to live long enough to drink a drop of Nile water and breathe clean air at dawn the only thought in his head.

“Back!” he roared, ready to strike. “Back, or I’ll hit you!”

He lifted his arm over his head to swing, and he heard the man pressed against him babbling broken, delirious English.

“Christ, don’t fall,” Trench said, and grabbed the stranger’s arm. “Ibrahim, help! If he goes down, he’s dead!”

Even over the deafening roar of curses and screams, Trench caught the familiar lilt of his own mother tongue after months in the dark Sudan. The sound washed over him, soft and gentle as a woman’s hand on his shoulder, and he suddenly felt human again.

He couldn’t make out the words over the din, but he caught snippets of phrases he knew, words that belonged to green meadows, cool grey skies, clear rivers, not this hell. For a second he forgot his parched throat, the stench, the crushing black dark. Then he felt the man in his arms totter and slip, and he yelled at Ibrahim again: “He’ll fall!”

Ibrahim moved to help, and the two of them fought off the shoving crowd until their neighbors stumbled back, yelling “Demons! They’ve gone mad!” They cleared a small patch of floor in their corner, propped the fevered new arrival down on the mud, and stood guard in front of him to stop anyone from stepping on him. Every few minutes, in a lull in the noise, Trench heard the stranger babbling lines of English, half lost in delirium.

“He’ll be dead before sunrise,” Trench yelled at Ibrahim. “He’s burning up with fever.”

“Sit with him,” the Hadendoa said. “I’ll hold them back.”

Trench stooped and squatted down next to the stranger, and Ibrahim planted his feet shoulder-width apart to shield them both. Now Trench could hear the man’s words clearly, soft and pleading, as if he was talking to someone far away. He rambled about the sea, riding lights of yachts bobbing in Dublin Bay, the sound of a band playing on the pier, the golden glow of the little seaside town of Bray huddled at the edge of the water, fading into a golden blot as the steamship pulled further out. Then he spoke of faces he saw in the dark: the man in the tent with the lancet, the man in the back room off Piccadilly, his own face, three faces big and looming, too close to get rid of. He talked about the things he’d set out to do, the “afterwards” he’d been clinging to the whole time.

Then the name Durrance slipped out, and Trench went rigid. This man knew Durrance! He wasn’t just some random English captive, he was someone Trench’s friend knew, they had a shared history. The stranger rambled on, talking about a girl named Ethne, standing under the elms by the Lennon River, three little white feathers.

Trench went cold. He knew those words. He was the one who’d sent those three feathers, after all.

“Feversham!” he cried, shaking the fevered man’s shoulder. “Harry Feversham!”

The name hung in the stinking dark of the House of Stone. The supposed coward, the man everyone in England had written off as a deserter, the man who’d vanished without a trace, was right there in the worst prison on earth, fever-wracked, fighting for the redemption no one had ever thought he’d dare chase.

For three whole days, Feversham drifted in and out of fevered delirium, his speech a jumble of disjointed, half-mad memories. For every one of those days, Trench tended to him uncomplainingly: trekking down to the Nile to fetch him cool, fresh water, splitting his own meagre dhurra rations, fending off ravenous fellow prisoners who would have stolen even the scraps meant for the sick, helpless man. For three nights running too, he stood shoulder to shoulder with the loyal guard Ibrahim, fending off violent drunks and raving madmen that roamed the dark corridors of the House of Stone, to keep the unconscious Feversham from harm.

On the fourth morning, Feversham woke sharp to his senses, blinked, and found Trench’s face bending over him. At first he mistook it for one of the nightmare visions that had haunted his boyhood nights, the faces that swelled too large and crept too close out of the dark, and he pushed the weak arm reaching for him away. But when he gazed around, the scene sank in: he was lying in the dappled shadow of the prison walls, the hard, unforgiving blue sky stretched overhead, brown, trampled bare dust was under his back, and all around him fellow prisoners dragged heavy iron chains, or lay prone in the throes of fever and starvation. He scrambled to grab Trench’s wrist, as if scared the man would vanish the next instant, and a relieved, soft smile spread over his face.
“I’m in the Omdurman prison,” he breathed. “Actually here. Umm Hagar, the House of Stone. It feels too good to be true.”

He leaned back against the wall, heavy with relief that Trench at first mistook for bitter irony. He thought only a man who posed as completely numb to all joy and pain could speak that way – but Feversham’s three days of delirium had proven he was no such man. This satisfaction was real. The peril of Dongola was behind him, he had found the man he had crossed hundreds of miles of desert to reach, and he was exactly where he had fought so hard to get. He could have been strung up on a gibbet miles down the Nile, circled by vultures, his entire life’s purpose unfulfilled. But he was here, inside the thorn zareeba of the House of Stone.
“You’ve been here a long time,” Feversham said.
“Three years.”

Feversham scanned the ragged, chain-dragging figures around the enclosure. “Three whole years. I was terrified I’d find you long dead.”
“The nights are the worst,” Trench said quietly. “The nights locked inside that stone building. It’s a wonder any man survives a week of them. I’ve survived a thousand. Even to me, that sounds impossible.”
“But we get to walk to the Nile during the day, right?” Feversham jolted upright, suddenly alarmed as he stared at the sharp thorn fence surrounding them. “An Arab at Wadi Halfa told us we’re allowed that much liberty.”
“It’s true,” Trench said, pointing to the earthen bowl of water at his side. “I filled that at the river at dawn.”
“Then I have to go this morning,” Feversham said, rising unsteadily to his feet, and Trench hissed a quiet warning.
“Hush. There are scores of prisoners here, and half of them are tale-bearers. If they see two men talking in earnest, word will reach Idris within the hour, the Khalifa before sunset. We’ll get heavier fetters and a lashing with the courbatch before the sun dips low.”
Feversham sank back to the ground, half from weakness, half to obey the warning. They napped side by side until noon, Trench’s face slack in sleep showing every deep mark of the three brutal years he had spent in the prison camp.

When the zareeba gate swung open at last for prisoners’ families and friends to bring their daily rations, the enclosure turned into a cage of starving wild beasts. The thirty gaolers, each armed with a rhino-hide courbatch whip, took the largest share of the moist, pounded dhurra cake called aseeda at the gate, leaving barely enough scraps to keep a man breathing. The strong fought the weak for every crumb, groups of four battled groups of three for a single handful of food, gaunt men hopping as fast as their chains would let them towards the entrance. One starving man tripped and fell, and lay still in the dust, knowing no food would be left for him that day. Others tore rations straight from the hands of the bearers, ignoring the lashes that sliced open their backs.
Hidden at the back of the crowd, a small boy gazed around the zareeba irresolutely. He was quickly spotted, knocked down, and his food stolen – but his screams for help brought the head gaoler Idris-es-Saier running to chase off his attackers. When Idris asked who he had come to see, the boy said “The Greek, Joseppi,” and Idris pointed him straight to Feversham’s corner. He knelt beside Feversham, chattering loudly in Arabic about the theft of his rations, while he slipped a gazelle skin hidden under his jibbeh loose from his waist and left it on the dust beside the man. Inside was a whole chicken, enough for Feversham and Trench to eat their fill for the entire day.

An hour later, they were permitted to leave the zareeba and walk slowly down to the Nile, stopping every few steps to rest. When they were far enough from the gaolers that no one could overhear them, Trench spoke.
“You told me everything over those three days you were delirious,” he said. “I know you didn’t get captured by accident on the Arbain road, pretending to be a mad wandering zither-player from Wadi Halfa. You came here on purpose, to find me. Because five years ago, I sent you a white feather.”
Feversham froze, alarmed. “What else did I say?”
“About a girl,” Trench said quietly, not looking at him. “The one you brought up when we dined with Willoughby and Durrance in London, long ago. I know her name now. She was there when the three of us sent you the feathers. She plucked a fourth one from her fan and added it to the pile. I’m sorry. We never thought of that.”
Feversham shook his head, smiling softly. “I’m not sorry. That fourth feather is what got me through three years in Suakin, when all I wanted to do was slip onto a boat back to Suez, when I saw all of you going about your regimental work every day. Without that feather, I never would have walked out into the desert to reach Berber, I never would have made it all the way here to Omdurman. I don’t blame you, and I don’t blame her.”

For a long minute, they both gazed out over the Nile, at the crowds of shouting Arabs and negroes unloading dhows on the muddy banks, at the line of palm trees marking the far shore of Khartum against the cloudless western sky. Trench held his hand out to Feversham.
“I take my feather back now,” he said.
Feversham shook his head. “Not yet.”
Hope, a hope Trench had spent three years trying to crush to save himself from heartbreak, flared bright in his chest. “Not yet? So you have an actual escape plan? When you were delirious you never breathed a word of it, did you?”
“Not a single one,” Trench said. Feversham exhaled in relief – he had been terrified he had rambled about the plan in Dongola, where a former resident of Khartoum who spoke broken English had been sent to question him, when his fever had almost made him spill every secret. He had deliberately focused all his wandering thoughts on Ethne, to keep the escape details locked tight in his mind.
“Camels are waiting for us ten miles west of Omdurman right now,” Feversham said, grinning. “Rifles, ammo, water, and provisions are buried near them. There are more fresh camels waiting for us at Metemneh, more at Berber, and we ride the Kokreb pass all the way to Suakin. I just need to get my strength back enough to sit a camel for a week.”
He nodded towards a man in a blue linen robe loitering halfway between them and the gaol. That was their agent, waiting for a month for Feversham to give the signal to move.
“Even easier than digging out of the zareeba wall,” Trench laughed, pointing to a chained prisoner who had tripped and fallen face-first in the shallow water, struggling uselessly to get back up under the weight of his load and his shackles. “We bribe Idris. He’ll come to you soon, lecture you about the mercy of Allah for bringing you to the holy city of Omdurman, warn you about the magic spy spirit Nebbi Khiddr that reports every prisoner’s move to the Khalifa, then end with a long, sad speech about his starving wife and children. If you give him fifteen dollars of the money he stole from you, he’ll let you sleep outside the stone prison in the open zareeba. Then we bribe him a second time to let me sleep out there with you.”

The plan worked exactly as Trench predicted. That very afternoon, Idris cornered Feversham for an overlong lecture about the destruction of Hicks Pasha’s army at the hands of the Mahdi’s angels, then begged him for money for his starving family. Feversham handed over the fifteen dollars, and that night he slept out in the open air, free of the stinking, crowded stone prison. Two nights later, Trench was lying beside him, staring up at the blazing, bright southern stars.
“Three more days,” Feversham murmured.
Trench’s voice was thick with emotion when he spoke. “I’m not a marrying man, so this isn’t that kind of sentiment. But all these years in this hell, all I’ve wanted is to die at home. Not just in England, but in my little village, buried under the trees I grew up climbing, where I can see the old church and the trout stream I fished every day as a boy. I’m so scared I’d die here, and never get back.”
Feversham said nothing, but Trench could tell he was not laughing. “I understand,” he said quietly.

For hours that night, side by side under the open sky, they talked. They talked about the sharp cry of “Mark!” on English shooting estates, the rattle of a fishing reel on the banks of a cold northern trout stream, the old West End plays they had seen six years before, the books they had read in their barracks mess. The shouts and screams from inside the House of Stone sounded faint and far away, muffled by the dark. They did not notice when the tail of the Great Bear dipped below the thorn hedge of the zareeba, or when the cool desert night began to turn cold. They were not prisoners in Omdurman anymore. They were home, walking the lanes they knew, beneath their old familiar dim, small northern stars.
“Only three more days,” Trench said, half asleep.
“Only three more days,” Feversham agreed, and a minute later, both men slipped into deep, peaceful sleep, the stars marching unnoticed over their heads towards dawn.

"Three more days."

The two men breathed those words right before they slipped into exhausted sleep, their shared hope a small, fragile spark against the unyielding heat of the Omdurman prison zareeba. But when Trench woke at dawn, his skin was burning, and a ragged fever that spread fast through his bones soon had him delirious. Feversham, who had spent three years relying on Trench for help in the brutal House of Stone, now found himself tending to his fellow prisoner every minute of the day. The strain of those years of starvation and confinement had broken Trench’s body the exact moment freedom was within reach— the shock of realizing escape was almost possible had been too much for his starved, worn frame.

Ibrahim, their local ally, shook his head gravely when he pressed his palm to Trench’s forehead. “This is Umm Sabbah,” he said, naming the infamous typhus fever that had already killed dozens of prisoners in the infected gaol, almost always on the seventh day of symptoms.

“It’s nothing,” Feversham snapped, stubborn and fierce, refusing to accept the warning. But the question gnawed at him every minute: *Would the camel drivers Abou Fatma had arranged wait for them?* Each day he trekked down to the Nile to fetch water, he spotted Abou Fatma in his plain blue robe at his hidden post, each day the man flashed his prearranged signal, and Feversham never dared answer, too busy fighting to keep Trench alive. With Ibrahim’s help, he brewed cooling drinks from tamarinds, dates, and wild roots the local boy brought into the prison, moved Trench to every patch of shade as the sun crept across the sky, and even arranged for Idris the gaoler’s starving family to get extra food in exchange for removing Trench’s forty-pound iron chains. They fed him salt-soaked vegetable marrow, butter to soothe his raw mouth, and wrapped him tight in soft camel cloths.

On the seventh day of the fever, Ibrahim said flatly, “This is the end. He will die tonight.”

“No,” Feversham snarled, the old fierce obstinacy burning through him. Trench was meant to die back home, in his own parish, under the trees he’d known since he was a boy, not rotting unnamed in this dirt yard. He didn’t think of the three feathers he owed to Ethne Eustace, didn’t think of the life he’d hoped to earn back when they escaped. For 24 straight hours he sat at Trench’s cot, fighting the battle for his life minute by minute. At dawn, Trench’s fever broke. He’d survived the fatal seventh day. By the thirteenth day, he woke fully lucid, clear-headed for the first time in weeks.

He woke alone, swathed so tight in camel cloth he could barely move, and Feversham stumbled in a moment later, exhausted, explaining he’d been forced to work unloading boats all day, and had run straight back as soon as he was let go.

“How long was I out?” Trench asked.

“Thirteen days.”

Trench exhaled slowly. “It’ll be a month before I can walk far enough to travel. You have to go on without me, Feversham. Leave now, while the men with the camels are still waiting. When you reach Assouan you can send help back for me.”

Feversham shook his head. “It’s too late. Abou Fatma spoke to me at the river yesterday. The camel drivers got spooked, scared of being caught. They left without us.”

Trench absorbed the news slowly, too tired to feel despair at first. Then Feversham told him he hadn’t let all hope die: Abou Fatma had snuck him paper, a pen, and ink made of wood soot and water, which he’d hidden under his robe. He’d written a letter to a Greek café owner in Wadi Halfa, paid Abou Fatma to deliver it, and was sure he’d arrange a new rescue within a year at the latest. But their small new flicker of hope died a few days later, when they saw the guards marking out a patch of ground in the corner of the zareeba, digging a well right beside the Nile. It was no random construction: the entire prison was going to get a new twelve-foot high mudbrick wall, smooth as glass, no handholds anywhere, built by the prisoners themselves. Too many men had escaped along the Nile banks in their chains, and now the gaolers meant to lock them away from the river entirely.

Feversham and Trench stacked the bricks, mortared them in place, and built their own cage with their own two hands. When the wall was finished, the distant palm trees of Khartum they’d been able to glimpse for years were gone, cut off entirely. Their whole world shrank to a patch of blue sky above the walls, and nothing else.

“We built our own coffin,” Trench said brokenly, hiding his face in his hands. “Do they even remember we exist in England? Have they forgotten us entirely?”

Feversham clapped him on the shoulder. “We waited three years already. What’s six more months? We’ll find a way out.”

But the six months dragged by, brutal and empty. The air hummed with vermin, scorpions stung them in their sleep, gaolers beat them at random for no reason, and no news ever filtered in from the outside world. They passed the time arguing theology with preachers the Khalifa sent to convert them, fighting for small privileges to sleep in the open air, counting every lizard that crawled across the wall to entertain themselves. By the end of the seventh month, there was still no sign of Abou Fatma. Trench was convinced he was never coming back.

Then two of the Khalifa’s personal guards showed up at the prison gates, and hauled them out to the Khalifa’s compound. They were both convinced they were being marched to execution—until the Khalifa announced he was putting them to work making gunpowder, to crush the incoming British Nile army. Before Feversham could say he had no idea how gunpowder was made, Trench cut in, speaking fluent Arabic, swearing he was a world expert on every step of the process. The Khalifa assigned them to work under a kind Greek foreman who took pity on them, and quietly covered for their total lack of chemistry knowledge.

Suddenly, they were no longer trapped inside the new prison wall. Every morning they tramped the Nile bank to the powder factory on the north edge of town, no guards even bothering to escort them—their chains made flight seem impossible. Every evening they tramped back. They scanned every face they passed for Abou Fatma, month after month, never knowing he’d been recognised by an old gaoler on his first return journey to Omdurman, stabbed in the thigh, and laid up recovering in a hospital in Assouan for almost a full year.

Towards the end of May, a man stepped out of the shadows by the storehouses on their walk home, dropped a scrap of paper at their feet, and vanished. The note, written in familiar handwriting, said only: *A man will bring you a box of matches. When he comes, trust him. — Sutch*. Sutch was Feversham’s oldest friend, who he’d not seen in years, working covertly in Sudan to arrange escapes for trapped British prisoners. A month later, an Arab brushed past them on the riverbank and whispered: “I am the man with the matches. Tomorrow, by the storehouse, this hour.”

They waited through an agonizing day at the factory, half-convinced the guards would decide to lock them in the prison that day and ruin everything. When they reached the meeting spot, the man named Abdul Kader told them his three camels had died, he hadn’t been given enough money to arrange a proper relay team for the desert journey, and if Feversham wrote him a note promising Sutch 200 pounds more, he’d have everything ready to rescue them in three months.

The two men exchanged a look. They knew what this meant. Abdul Kader would string them along for cash forever, never risking his own life to smuggle them out. The last hope they had was gone. They tramped slowly back towards the prison, heads hanging, the heavy Nile sunset painting the water pink and olive green around them, the distant drums of the town beating their monotonous, terrible rhythm. They were so lost in despair they didn’t even notice the man who fell into step beside them.

“The camels are ready in the desert, ten miles west.”

Feversham looked up. It was Abou Fatma, grinning quiet, his thigh still bearing the faint scar of the old stab wound.

“Now?” Feversham breathed.

“Right now.”

Trench almost fainted, leaning against the mud wall to keep his balance. Abou Fatma passed them rags to wrap around their iron chains so they wouldn’t rattle, told them to pull their robes over their heads to look like ordinary local slaves, and led them down to the water’s edge where a waiting blacksmith struck their chains clean off in silence. They ran across the dark city, through the cemetery, to a disused abandoned house on the far side of town, where two camels were kneeling and waiting for them.

They mounted, and Abou Fatma struck the lead camel’s flank. Behind them, the glow of Omdurman’s torches shrank, the drums faded to a faint distant thrum, and they galloped west, out into the moonless dark of the open desert.

A sharp, northern wind cut through the desert dark, stinging cheeks and livening the camels, which broke into a hard, ground-eating trot.
“Faster,” Trench ground out between clenched teeth. “Idris must have noticed we’re gone by now.”
“Even if he has,” Feversham called back, keeping his tone even, “it will take hours to rouse patrols, round up men, and fetch their mounts. And it’s full dark already.”
His words were steady, but he craned his neck again and again to stare at the faint, blood-orange glow pooling above Omdurman’s skyline. The distant thud of Ansar war drums had faded at last, small mercy—but the desert here was deadly silent. Galloping hooves would make no noise in the soft sand, no warning hum rising to alert them pursuit was close. For all he knew, armed riders could be thirty paces behind, and he would only spot the glint of a white turban when it was too late.
Trench never turned his head at all. He rode stiff-backed, jaws locked, eyes fixed dead ahead. His fear burned no less hot than Feversham’s—hotter, in fact. He dared not look back, too afraid the vision would seize him of Omdurman’s narrow fire-lit streets, of sentries shouting as they poured through the corridors of the House of Stone, hunting the two escaped prisoners.
Feversham’s quiet voice pulled him out of his trance. “I can’t see Omdurman’s fires anymore.”
“The golden blot, eh? Sunk right low?” Trench replied, half dazed. He did not explain the reference, and Feversham did not ask. The phrase had sprung unbidden to Trench’s mind: that very same golden glow had been the last thing Feversham saw when he left Omdurman at the start of his long, dangerous mission, and now it was vanishing the moment that mission was done.
They spoke no more until two shadowy forms loomed directly in the path of their camels, and Abou Fatma’s low, sharp voice cut the dark: “Instanna!”
They reined in, making their mounts kneel. “The fresh camels?” Abou Fatma asked, and two of his men vanished for a minute before leading four strong beasts out from behind a sand dune. Saddles were stripped from the camels they had ridden out of Omdurman, and slung tight on the new mounts.
“These are good?” Feversham asked, tugging a strap firm.
“Anafi breed, the fastest in the Sudan,” Abou Fatma said, glancing anxiously east and cupping a hand to his ear. “Quick, quick.”
“The arms?” Trench’s voice was feverish. “You have them? Where are they?” He was already sliding off his camel, scrabbling at the sand with his boots.
“A moment,” Abou Fatma said—but Trench could hardly wait. He was more eager to lay hands on a rifle than he had ever been terrified of being dragged back to Omdurman. “Ammunition too?” he gasped.
“All of it, rifles, revolvers, rounds enough for a dozen patrols.” Abou Fatma led them twenty paces off, where long desert grass hissed around their calves, and dug into the soft sand with bare hands. “Here.”
Trench threw himself to his knees, digging furiously with both hands, letting out a low, whining keen that sounded exactly like a foxhound baying at a fox’s cover. Feversham watched, unsettled: the man was half beast in that moment, scrabbling like a dog for a bone, his face tight with raw, hungry need. Then Trench’s fingers brushed the cold metal of a rifle lock, and the humanity snapped back. He stood slow, hefting the weapon, and laughed—a wild, bright sound of pure triumph.
“Now I don’t care who follows!” he whooped. “Let every Ansar soldier in Omdurman chase us. I’ll never go back to that stone prison, not even if they ride us down.” He stroked the rifle’s stock like it was a living thing, a friend he’d thought he’d lost forever.
Two of Abou Fatma’s men took the old, ridden-out camels and trotted back towards Omdurman to throw any trailing trackers off the scent. The rest mounted the fresh Anafi beasts and struck northeast. Less than fifteen minutes had passed since they’d first halted in the dark.
All that night they picked their way slowly through tall halfa grass and spiny mimosa, but by sunrise they were out on flat, bare sand dotted with low hillocks. “Shall we stop and eat?” Abou Fatma asked. “There is bread and dates strapped to each saddle.”
“No,” Feversham said. “We eat in the saddle.” They passed a water skin between them, cramming dates into their mouths as the camels loped on at full speed. By sunset they paused for an hour’s rest, then rode straight through the next night and all the next day, pushing themselves and their mounts to breaking point across rocky uplands, winding valleys, and honey-colored sand plains. Even at that gallop, the world crawled. A mountain would peek over the horizon at dawn, and hang there all day, never an inch closer, never a foot taller. Twice they had to detour wide around villagers tending scraggly sorghum patches that made their hearts leap into their throats. Once Abou Fatma spotted a distant caravan, and they all made their camels kneel, hunkered behind a rock with loaded rifles in their hands until the procession was out of sight.
Ten miles outside Abu Klea, a second relay of fresh camels was waiting. They mounted and rode a full day’s march west of the Nile, through the wild desert of the Ababdeh people, until a wide grey track stretched across their path.
“The Berber to Merowi trade road,” Abou Fatma said. “We turn east for the river once we cross it. If God wills, we reach the far bank tomorrow evening.”
“If God wills,” Trench echoed, his eyes darting to every shadow. The closer they got to safety, the sharper his paranoia burned. This was caravan country, far too busy to travel by day. They dismounted and hid all day behind a thick belt of shrubs on high ground, watching the tiny specks of traders and travelers crawl along the road far below. They crossed it after full dark, riding hard for the Nile until dawn broke, when Abou Fatma called another halt.
This was flat, empty desert, no cover higher than your ankle. Feversham and Trench stared east, straining to spot the Nile glint—but it was nowhere in sight. “We build a low circle of stones,” Abou Fatma said. “Lie flat inside it, no movement. I will ride ahead to the river, make sure our boat is waiting, then come back for you after dark. The soldiers patrol the banks day and night.”
They stacked the stones fast, a wall barely a foot high, then laid down inside it with their water skin and rifles at their sides. Abou Fatma took their camels with him, promising to bring fresh mounts back at sundown.
That day was the longest both men had ever lived. They were a few hours from freedom, and yet still in mortal danger. Trench swore it felt longer than any night locked in the House of Stone. To Feversham, it dragged worse than those six years before, when he’d hidden in his London rooms overlooking St James’s Park, too ashamed to step out into the streets after dark. The sun blazed down unforgiving, no shade, no room to stretch their legs. They barely spoke a word.
At last the sun set, cool wind rustling the grass, and Trench’s hand clamped down on Feversham’s arm. “Listen.” They heard the soft padding of camel feet, coming fast. A low whistle, and they scrambled out of their stone hiding place.
“God be thanked,” Abou Fatma said, when they called out. “Good news and bad. The boat is ready, and there are camels waiting on the far bank to take you to Abu Hamed. But your escape is public. Every ferry, every road, every path is staked out. We must cross the Nile before sunrise, and strike straight inland from the far shore.”
They slipped across the river around one in the morning, scuttling the boat on the far bank so no one could trace their crossing, then mounted the waiting camels. The ground was littered with boulders, and they could barely move faster than a walk, fraying every last thread of their patience. They hid another full day behind a ring of stones while the camels grazed out of sight, then rode hard through the night, reaching the date groves outside Abu Hamed two days later. They rested for twelve hours, then climbed onto a new relay of mounts to cross the Nubian Desert.
Today, a traveler rides that 240 miles of waterless black rock and yellow sand by train, sleeps in their berth, pulls into stations marked by painted signboards, the telegraph poles strung out ahead and behind, the whole journey done in 12 hours. But when Feversham and Trench rode that stretch, no tracks cut the sand. They followed the old hidden line of hill wells, staying east of the modern railway. On their second night out, Trench shook Feversham hard to wake him, pointing south at the starry sky.
“Look. There’s no Southern Cross.” His voice cracked. “Six years. Every single night for six years I’ve stared up at those four slanted stars from the floor of that prison cell, wondering if I’d ever live to see a night where it wasn’t there. This is the first second I’ve actually let myself believe we made it.”
They sat together long after that, staring up at the dark southern sky, hearts full of quiet thanks. They woke over and over through the rest of the night, half convinced they would see that familiar constellation burning low above the horizon after all, before drifting back to sleep, sure at last their escape was real.
Seven days later, they rode into Shof-el-Ain: a tiny well tucked between bare, featureless ridges, in the land of the Amrab Arabs. The chase was over.
“We are safe!” Abou Fatma cheered, spreading a cloth out before the kneeling camels and heaping grain for them. He was so giddy with relief he patted one grumpy camel on the neck, and the beast snapped at him, baring its teeth.
Trench laughed, holding out a hand to Feversham. “Thank you.”
“No thanks needed,” Feversham said, not taking it at once. “I did this for myself, first and last.”
“You’ve learned the nature of a camel, then,” Trench chuckled. “It’ll carry you all the way across the desert till it drops dead, but bite your head off if you try to thank it. That’s us, isn’t it? No flowery words. Here, my hand.”
Feversham untied the knot at the breast of his ragged jibbeh, and pulled out three white feathers: two small heron plumes, one large broken ostrich feather plucked from a fan. “Will you take yours back?”
“Of course.”
“You know what to do with it.”
“Burn it, the second I get somewhere I can light a match. No delays.”
Feversham tucked the two remaining feathers carefully back inside his tunic, tying the knot tight. “We shake hands, then.” As their palms met, he added, “Tomorrow morning we part ways.”
“Part ways? After a year locked in Omdurman together, all these weeks of running?” Trench blurted. “Why come home separately?”
“People will line the docks to welcome you when you reach Assouan, Cairo. You are the war hero who escaped the Mahdi’s prison. I don’t want to be there, when you go to tell Ethne what I did. She deserves to hear it from you first, no awkwardness.”
Trench nodded. He understood. “None of the regiment ever breathed a word about why you resigned, or the feathers we sent you. We swore an oath never to speak of it, for the honour of the regiment. Every man kept it.”
“Find Durrance, if you can,” Feversham said. “Tell him the next time he invites me to visit, anywhere in the world, England or Wadi Halfa, I’ll say yes.”
“Which way are you going?”
“West, to Wadi Halfa,” Feversham said. “I’ll take Abou Fatma with me, ride slow down the Nile. The other Arab will guide you north to Assouan.”
They slept the night through without a single watch, no fear of pursuit. At dawn, Trench was first to mount his camel. He leaned down to take the reins Feversham passed him. “Ramelton, wasn’t it? I won’t forget.”
“Ramelton. There’s a ferry across Lough Swilly to Rathmullen. You have to drive twelve miles inland to get there. She might not be there, though.”
“Then I’ll find her wherever she is,” Trench promised, and rode off with his guide. He glanced back over his shoulder more than once, and saw Feversham standing motionless by the well, but Feversham never waved back.
Feversham’s six long years of trial were over that morning, and he felt no joy, only a hollow, empty space inside him. For six years, his mission to redeem himself had been the only thing that kept him going. Now it was done, and he had no idea what came next. Ethne was no doubt married long since, he thought, and a sharp, bitter despair welled up in him for that stupid, reckless mistake he had made six years before, that had cost him everything. He saw again his London room overlooking St James’s Park, the knock at the door, the servant handing him the telegram.
He roused himself at last. There was still work to do. He had to go see his father, who was no doubt reading his Times over breakfast on the terrace of Broad Place, under the Surrey pine trees. He still had to take the last feather back to Ramelton. He still had to send a telegram to Lieutenant Sutch at Suakin.
He climbed onto his own camel, and rode slow west towards Wadi Halfa beside Abou Fatma. The green, wooded slopes of Ramelton glimmered vividly through the shimmering desert haze. For the first time in all his years of struggle, Harry Feversham doubted the quiet faith he had always held that everything would make sense in the afterwards.

It was an August morning, and the fierce unblinking suns of the Soudan had baked Harry Feversham’s skin to the deep, weathered bronze of a man who spent years unshielded in the desert’s harshest grip. Six long years of brutal, secret probation had carved their marks deep: faint scar tissue snaking along his jaw, sharp sunken eyes that had stared down executioners and sandstorms alike, a lean hard frame that had carried him hundreds of miles across hostile Sudan. No one in the quiet Irish border town spared him a second glance as he rode across the Lennon bridge, a stranger to the place he had once called home.

He spurred his horse up the town’s narrow cobblestone street, his head crowded with the ghosts that had ridden beside him every mile of the journey back: phantoms of the men he’d fought beside, prisoners he’d shared hard rations with in the Omdurman House of Stone, the faces of the three friends who had once sent him white feathers marking him a coward. The old years of his shame peeled away from his thoughts one by one as he crossed Egypt, then the European continent, until they felt no more substantial than smoke. He wasted no breath dwelling on what might have been if he had never fled his regiment. The past that unfolded right here, in this tiny coastal town, was far too sharp and tangible: this was where he had been happier than he’d ever dreamed possible, where he had plumbed the very depths of despair, where he had brought joy to the woman he loved, and where he had inflicted the worst harm of his life.

He reined in for a moment across from the stone church perched high above the road on his right, wondering if Ethne still lived here, if old Dermod Eustace was still breathing, what kind of welcome he would earn after all he had done. The sound of larks singing high in the pale blue sky snapped him back to the present: a landrail called harshly from the meadow at his feet, a rooster crowed clear from the valley below. He shook off the daze of memory, kicked his heels into his horse’s flanks, and rode briskly down the slope ahead, then up the next rise, the old phantoms closing back around him as he passed the spots where he had once walked, laughed, and raced his horse beside his oldest friends.

He reined in at last at the edge of a thick overgrown grove, a broken stone fence, a gateway hanging empty without its gate. He barely registered the signs of long abandonment, turning straight through the entrance to ride down a drive choked with weeds. At the end of the path, the full view hit him: Lennon House was nothing but blackened, tumbled ruins, its walls half-collapsed, its rooms unroofed, nettles pushing up through the flagstones of the front step. He was jolted fully from his reverie. He dismounted, tied his horse to a low oak branch, and ran shouting through the gutted shell of the home he’d once known. No answer came. He raced from one empty soot-streaked room to the next, then down to the garden, where the lawn was waist-high with uncut grass and flower beds tangled with brambles. No one was there. No one had been there for years.

He swung back into the saddle and rode at a sharp trot back into Ramelton, stopping at the small town inn to hand his horse over to the ostler and order lunch. When the landlady came to wait on him, he asked, quiet and tight, “Lennon House burned down? When did that happen?”

“Five years ago this very summer,” she replied, and launched unprompted into a long, voluminous story of the fire, the ruin of the Eustace family, the butler Bastable’s descent into drunkenness, and old Dermod Eustace’s death up at Glenalla. “We all expect the house to be rebuilt soon, mind you,” she added, leaning in with obvious glee to share gossip, “soon as Miss Eustace marries, so they say.”

Feversham did not take the bait. “Where does Miss Eustace live now?”

“Up at Glenalla. Halfway to Rathmullen, there’s a track up to your left. It’s nothing but a rough mountain village, no place at all for a lady like her. You’re wanting to see her, are you?”

“I am,” he said, rising from the table to cut the conversation short. “Send my horse round to the door, please.”

But the landlady was not done. She paused in the doorway and blinked, puzzled. “Well that’s a queer thing entirely. Only a fortnight back, there was another gentleman staying here, burnt just as black as you are, going on the very same errand. Asked for Miss Eustace’s address, then drove straight up to Glenalla. You got business with her, then?”

“I do,” he said, sharp enough that she finally turned to fetch his horse. While he waited, he flipped through the inn’s guest register, and found the name scrawled in ink on a late July page: Colonel Trench. The man who had suffered beside him in the House of Stone, who had carried word of his redemption back to Ethne, had come here first.

“You’ll be back tonight, sir?” the landlady called as he mounted.

“No,” he said. “I will not be coming back to Ramelton.”

He rode down the hill, crossed the Lennon bridge a second time that day, and four miles on found the turnoff track across from a small glinting bay of the lough. He turned onto it, riding past a handful of whitewashed cottages, up into the purple heather-cloaked hollow of the hills, until by five o’clock he reached the scattered, quiet little village of Glenalla. It spread out unplanned across the slopes: a cluster of cottages, a stretch of open fields, a small larch plantation, then a single standing house, then another gap where he could see straight down to the lough water shining gold in the late afternoon sun, white gulls swooping and diving above the waves. Past that stood a small grey stone church, bare to the mountain winds, perched on its tiny plateau, a shell-dust white path leading from its door to the little wooden gate in the fence.

As he drew level with the gate, a collie dog barked at him from the other side. Feversham glanced at the animal, noticed the distinct white blaze on its chest, saw that its muzzle was grey with old age, and stopped his horse dead. He recognized the dog. He dismounted, tied his horse to the fence, and stepped into the churchyard.

The collie trotted over, sniffed doubtfully at the back of his knee, then suddenly exploded into a frenzy of joy. It wagged its whole body, yelped sharply, crouched and sprang up to rest its forepaws on his chest, spun in giddy circles, ran barking to the church door then darted back to jump at him again. The old collie remembered him, even when no one else in Ramelton had. Feversham laughed, scrubbing the soft fur behind the dog’s ears, then froze mid-laugh, his eyes locking on the open church doorway.

Ethne Eustace was standing there.

He set the dog down, and walked slow and steady up the white shell path toward her. She did not move, did not speak, waited on the threshold watching him until he stood right in front of her. Then she said, simple and soft, “Harry.”

The last of the ghosts that had ridden beside him all day vanished the second the word left her lips. Six years had passed since he had stood beneath her window on that dawn June morning, the day he had fled. They looked at each other, taking in every change the years had left. Feversham was gaunt, his eyes sunken, a quiet unshakable self-possession about him that the tentative, guilt-ridden young man who left Ramelton had never had. He had tested himself to breaking point, in the worst hell the Soudan could offer, and he had not broken. Ethne saw the marks of his suffering, and her heart ached for every day he had spent in chains, but she also saw that all the old timidity, all the old fear that others would judge him a coward, was gone. “Not all harm came of these years,” she said, her face softening. “They were not wasted.”

Feversham looked back at her, saw the faint new lines of character around her bright grey eyes, the quiet strength that had settled into her face after six years of lonely grief. “I am sorry,” he said, rough with unspilled emotion. “I did you a great wrong six years ago. I never should have made you bear that burden too.”

She held out her hand to him. “Will you give it to me, please?”

For a second he did not understand. “The fourth feather,” she said.

He pulled his letter case from his coat, shook two white feathers out into his palm. The big ostrich feather, the one she had snapped from her own fan, he held out to her.

“Both,” she said.

There was no reason left to keep the second feather, the one sent to him by Castleton. He handed them both over. She curled her fingers tight around them, and smiled, tucking them against her breast. “I have all four feathers now,” she said.

“All four,” Feversham agreed. “What will you do with them?”

Her smile broke into a bright, unapologetic laugh. “Do with them? Nothing at all. I am going to keep them. I am very proud to have them to hold onto.” They were not dead useless relics to her, as they were to him—symbols of a long-past shame he would have crushed under his heel on the path if he could. She held them tight, then said, “Colonel Trench was here a fortnight ago. He told me you were coming to bring them back to me.”

“He did not know about the fourth one,” Feversham said, surprised. “I never told anyone I had it.”

“You told Colonel Trench, that first night you arrived in the House of Stone at Omdurman,” Ethne said. “He told me. I do not hate him anymore,” she added, quiet and serious, as if this was a truth she had fought hard to reach.

“I am glad,” Feversham said. “He is one of my dearest friends.”

Ethne was silent for a long moment, then she spoke again. “I wonder if you remember that drive we took from Ramelton to the house, when I came to fetch you from the quay? We were alone in the dog-cart, and we spoke of the friends you recognize the very first moment you meet them, as if you have known them your whole life, before you ever saw their face.”

“I remember,” Feversham interrupted, smiling a little. “And I said you could make mistakes about that kind of friend.”

“And I disagreed,” she went on, her voice soft and unsteady. “I said you might think you had made a mistake, for a very long time, but in the end you would find you had not. I thought of those words every day when Captain Willoughby brought me the first feather, and I was eaten up with remorse. I thought of them today, and there is no remorse left. I was right, you see. I should have held fast to my faith in you. But I did not. I was young. I knew so little of the world, of what men can bear. I judged far too hastily. But today, I understand.”

She opened her hand, stared down at the four white feathers she held, then turned and stepped inside the quiet, sunlit church. Feversham followed close behind her.

Ethne sank into the shadowed corner of an aisle pew, and Harry Feversham took his place at her side, the faint, fine grit of Sudanese desert sand still clinging to the cuffs of his worn tweed jacket, leftover from six years of wandering, captivity, and brutal penance. The tiny stone church was wrapped in a hush softer than any blanket: late summer sun slanted through the high clerestory windows, gilding the floating dust motes until the whole vaulted roof hung in a warm golden haze, while the distant hum of bees, the low gurgle of the nearby stream, and the faint bleat of a lamb drifted through the propped-open oak door.

“I’m glad you remembered that drive we took, all those years ago, and the things we said,” she continued, her gaze fixed steadily on the weathered hymn rack in front of her, no tremor in her tone. “It matters more than I can say that you do. Because even though I have you back at last, I am going to send you away from me again. You’ll be one of my absent friends – the kind I never lose, no matter how far apart we are.”

It was the hardest speech she had ever had to deliver, but she had turned it over in her head every night, every hour of the past fortnight. The second she had laid eyes on Harry, whole and living after years of sleepless dread and unconfirmed rumors of his death, she had thought she would never find the words, no matter how necessary they were. But she had forced herself to cling to that necessity until it settled in her bones, had stepped back inside the quiet church to gather the last of her strength. If she showed no sign of how this parting gutted her, it would be easier for both of them. He would know the cost, she wanted him to know – wanted him to understand that not one second of those six brutal, lonely years he had endured had been wasted, not for her. And she did not need to break down to make that clear.

“I know this will hurt you, just as it hurts me,” she went on evenly. “But there is no other way. At least you are home now, with your honor restored, with every right to walk through your own front door. That is a comfort I never dared to let myself hope for. But there is far more to hold onto. Colonel Trench told me enough of your captivity to know we see this the same way. This parting, hard as it is, is nothing next to that first one, six years ago, outside my house at Ramelton. I was adrift, utterly alone after that. There was a wall between us, tall and thick, that would have kept us apart forever, in this life and the next. I know that for a fact. But you smashed that wall apart with every mile you walked, every lash you bore, every day you refused to break in the desert. There is no barrier left now. We will not see each other for the rest of our lives on this earth – but the afterwards is certain. We can wait for it. You waited so bravely for all those years, alone in the souks of Suakin, in the mud villages along the Nile, in the endless empty dark of the desert. I can wait too. Your victory gives me all the strength I need.”

Silence fell over the little church for long minutes. To Feversham, her words were as gentle and reviving as rain falling on cracked, sun-baked earth. All those years of skulking out of sight of his fellow men to hide his shame, of nights spent shivering in chains, of days where thirst burned his throat raw, of a thousand moments he had been sure he would die unnoticed and unremembered – all of it shrank to nothing now. He had lain awake on the straw of his Omdurman cell so many nights, wondering if he would die hearing the bitter words she had flung at him the night he left, sure that reproach would ring in his ears as he drew his last breath. That prediction was a lie now. The voice he would carry with him to his grave was this soft, steady one, telling him all his suffering had mattered.

He had not been unprepared for this parting. He had heard rumors she was engaged before he even set foot back on English soil, and he would not argue against her wish. He knew she had more to say, and he waited for it, quiet at her side. This was the very last time they would ever speak, ever be in the same room. Once he stepped out that door, crossed the threshold where the summer light spilled in, she would never look on his face again until the day she died. So she drew the moments out, slow pauses, soft half-spoken sentences, stretching out every second of this final meeting she had craved for half a decade. She half-hoped he would tell her stories of his travels, his close calls, even rant about Sudanese politics, if it meant they could linger here a little longer. But he waited, patient, for her to speak first.

“I am going to be married,” she said at last. “Very soon. To a friend of yours: Colonel Durrance.”

Feversham answered without an instant’s hesitation. “He has loved you for years. I did not realize it until I left for the army, but once I thought back on all of it, I suspected. And then I was sure.”

“He is blind.”

“Blind?” Feversham exclaimed, the shock jolting through him. “Of all men, him? The man who would ride 50 miles before dawn to track a new rock inscription in the desert, who could spot a camel’s track three days old across sand?”

“Exactly,” Ethne said, quiet. “Him, of all people. That is why I am marrying him. That is why I am sending you away. I got engaged to him after he came back from Sudan blind, before Captain Willoughby ever brought me that first white feather. After you left, I sat up night after night thinking, and I swore I would not let two men’s lives be ruined because of me.”

“Mine was not ruined,” Feversham cut in, urgent. “You have to believe that.”

“Partly it was,” she said gently. “I know that. You will not admit it for my sake, but it is true. I was determined no second man would lose everything he cared for because of my choices. When Durrance came home, his sight gone, the whole world he loved ripped away from him –”

“All except you.”

“Yes,” Ethne answered. “All except me. But he is sharp now, far sharper than you could ever guess. A single glance, a half-spoken word, and he will untangle the whole hidden truth. Right now, he knows nothing of the four feathers.”

“Are you certain?” Feversham asked sharply, all at once.

Ethne turned her face to him for the first time since she had sat down on the pew. “Why?”

“Lieutenant Sutch was at Suakin while I was a prisoner in Omdurman. He knew where I was. He smuggled messages to me, tried to arrange my escape.”

Ethne’s eyes went wide. “Oh. Colonel Durrance already knew you were being held in Omdurman. He saw you in Wadi Halfa, heard you had gone south into the desert. He was distraught. He asked a friend to track you down, and that friend brought back word you were in the Mahdi’s prison. He told me himself. He said he would arrange to get you out. He was at Wiesbaden seeing a specialist oculist until a week ago. That is why he never mentioned Sutch. He had no idea you would ever come back to England. But if you and I were to see even a little of each other, even if we met as total strangers and spoke two indifferent words to each other – he would guess. He is too quick. And he would be right.” A flush rose high on her forehead, stained her cheeks pink.

Feversham did not move, did not lean toward her, did not say a single word that would make this already brutal goodbye harder for her to bear. “Yes. I see. And he cannot guess.”

“No, he cannot,” Ethne said, and the relief was clear in her voice. “I am so glad you understand, Harry. This simple, honest path is the only one we can take. He has nothing left in the whole world but me.”

“Is Durrance here at Glenalla?” Feversham asked.

“He is staying at the vicarage.”

“Very well,” he said. “I never expected you to wait for me. I never wished it, I never had any right to wish it. The night you gave me that fourth feather, in that little parlor at Ramelton, with the music drifting faint through the door, I understood. We were to have a clean, final break. Not even acquaintances. So I never told you the plan that sprang full-formed in my head the second I held all four feathers. I could have died out there trying to earn my honor back. I could have run from the risk entirely. I told myself I would only speak of it if I actually made it home. I never once hoped you would wait for me.”

“Colonel Trench told me that, that first night you spent in the House of Stone.”

“I told him that, did I?”

“It is the truth,” Feversham said, a faint, rough laugh huffing out of him. “The very most I dared to hope for, every single hour of every day I was gone, was that if I came home, you would take back your feather, and we would not pick up our old friendship here – but we would wait, for the afterwards.”

“Then there will be no parting, in the end,” Ethne said simply, and she smiled at him. That smile told him more clearly than any tear or sob how much this separation would cost her. In that moment, he understood far more deeply than he ever had during all his lonely desert vigils what that first parting must have cost her, six years before, her pride shattered as badly as her heart.

“All that pain you went through,” he breathed.

“Not mine alone,” she said softly. “I never spent a night chained in the House of Stone.”

“But it was all my fault. Do you remember that line you said, the morning light seeping through the blinds? ‘It’s not right that one should suffer so much pain.’ It was not right.”

“I forgot those words years ago, until Trench reminded me,” she said. “I never should have said them. I had no idea they would cling to you that hard. I am sorry I spoke them.”

“They were just,” Feversham said. “I used to think those would be the last words I heard before I died, my face turned to the dirt of some desert grave. But you have given me better words today, to carry in their place.”

The silence stretched on after that, no more words left to say. Feversham wondered why Ethne did not stand, why she did not end this interview. The occasion felt too heavy, too sacred, to waste on trivial stories of his travels, even the narrow scrapes he had cheated death in. The final, solemn choice of their lives had been made, and there was no use talking of smaller things. But Ethne clung to the minutes, marking a strip of golden sunlight on the far stone wall of the church, watching it shrink little by little. She would send him away, she told herself, the second that last sliver of gold faded to cold grey stone.

“I am glad you escaped Omdurman all on your own,” she said. “I wanted more than anything for you to earn your way back, no one else’s help, no interference.” After another pause, she added, “You suffered horribly at Dongola. Trench told me.”

“It does not matter now,” Feversham said. “That lifetime feels a thousand miles away.”

“Did you carry anything of mine with you, out there? Any small little thing from the days before you left?”

“Nothing,” he said.

“I had your photograph,” she told him. “I kept it.”

Feversham leaned down, just a little, toward her. “You did?”

“I did,” Ethne nodded. “That night, right after you left, I packed up every single gift you ever gave me, addressed them all to your London rooms. I burned every last one of your letters after that. But I set your photograph aside, before I did anything else. I have it here, now. I am going to keep it, with all four of the feathers.” She paused for a beat. “I wish you had had something of mine, to hold onto all those years.”

“I had no right to take anything of yours out into that risk,” he said.

The last thin slip of gold vanished from the far wall. The pale, colorless glow of summer twilight seeped through the windows, soft and dim.

“I will not see you for a very long time,” Ethne said, and for the first time her voice cracked, broke on a sob. “I will never get another letter from you.” She leaned forward, her head dipping as tears welled over her lashes. But she stood up, straight and brave, and the two of them walked side by side out of the church. Their shoulders brushed as they crossed the lychgate. Feversham untied his bay horse from the oak, heaved himself into the saddle. As his foot settled in the stirrup, Ethne pulled her collie tight to her side.

“Goodbye,” she said. She made no attempt to smile, held out her hand to him. He took it, leaning down from the saddle, his face close to hers. She kept her eyes fixed on his, even as tears brimmed over and ran down her cheeks.

“Goodbye,” he said. He held her hand for one long, quiet second, then released the rein and rode slowly down the hill. After a hundred yards, he pulled the horse to a stop, and looked back. Ethne had not moved. She was standing where he left her, facing him, no wave, no final gesture, just watching. Then she turned, and walked slowly up the village street toward her house, never once glancing back. Feversham stayed in the saddle, watching her until she passed through her garden gate, until her figure blurred and vanished behind the hedge. The mistake he had made all those years ago, the cowardice that had sent him running from his regiment, could never be fully undone. The quiet, gentle sorrow they would both carry for the rest of their lives would not fade. But it was a sorrow they could bear, both of them, because they knew the waiting was almost done.

The words that struck Ethne like a blow to the chest were spoken that same summer evening. She tramped back to her isolated hill farm and sank into the parlor armchair, the low June sun long faded behind the fells. The air bit cold despite the season, so she lit a fire, and sat staring at the glowing coals, every muscle in her body motionless with the tight, thrumming tension only she could read on herself. The half-hour she had dreamed of, planned for, lived every waking moment for over years, the half-hour Harry Feversham had finally come back to share with her, was over. She was more alone now in that remote, cut-off cottage than she had ever been the day Harry Willoughby’s sailboat slipped down the Salcombe Estuary and vanished out to sea.

The hollow emptiness of the world around her weighed heavy. She had braced for this, though: she knew the instant she sent Harry away, the entire core of her would scream to run after him, call him back, undo the goodbye. She forced her chills to fade long enough to remind herself this was not the end. By tomorrow, she would see that this parting was nothing compared to that night of the Lennon House ball, when she had all four feathers in her hand and thought she would never see the man she loved again. She was daydreaming up a future of honor and distinction for Harry, when Dermod’s ancient collie nuzzled her cold hand, his old fur soft against her knuckles, his nose twitching at the scent of her quiet distress. She cupped his gray-muzzled head and kissed him, thinking how soon he would die, leaving her with years and years stretching empty ahead before her own final rest.

A soft knock at the door. The servant told her Colonel Durrance was waiting.

She stood at once, no hesitation. She would not shirk the path she had laid out for herself. She stepped straight out of the private dark of her grief to meet him, talking lightly of their upcoming wedding, of rebuilding the ruined Lennon House, as if no earth-shaking thing had happened an hour before. It was hard work, but she had grown used to hard work. Only that night, Durrance made the path steeper still. After the maid set their tea down, he asked her to pick up her violin and play the Musoline Overture for him.

“Not tonight,” she said, automatic. Then she paused. She would not shirk the small, trivial tests any more than the great ones. “Wait—no, I can play it.” She smiled, and took the instrument down from its shelf.

She played the whole overture from first note to last. Durrance stood with his back to her at the window until the final chord hummed away into silence, then crossed the room to her side. “I was a brute,” he said softly, “to make you play that tonight.”

“I didn’t want to,” she admitted, setting the violin down.

“I know. But I had to find something out, and this was the only way. You almost never let your guard slip—only when you play. The last time you played this overture, you were off guard. I knew if I got you to play it tonight, the same thing would happen.”

Ethne’s face went white as linen, her breath caught in her throat. How could he know? It was impossible. But Durrance went on, calm as ever. “Well? Did you take back your feather? The fourth one?”

Those were the incredible words. She stared at him, unblinking, too stunned to process what she’d heard, sure for a wild second her overtaxed mind was inventing it. He repeated the question, and she could no longer pretend she had misheard.

“Who told you about the fourth feather?” she whispered.

“Trench. I met him when he landed at Dover. He only told me of the fourth— I already knew about the other three, long before that. Trench would never have breathed a word of it if I hadn’t asked him point blank where Harry Feversham was the second he stepped off the steamer.”

“How?” she cried, half in despair. He stepped close and curled a gentle hand around her arm.

“Does the how matter, now that I know? I’ve known for over a year, ever since Willoughby showed up at The Pool with the first of those feathers. I waited to tell you until Harry came back. He came today, didn’t he.”

Ethne sank back into her chair, stunned. She had guarded that secret so fiercely for so long, the shock of finding it had never been a secret at all left her reeling. She fumbled for a trivial question to buy time to pull her self-control back together. “You were at the church, then? Heard us on the steps? Met him riding away?”

“Not a single one of those,” Durrance laughed, pleased. He was proud of the sharp, keen perception he had honed after losing his sight, the little discoveries no one expected a blind man to make. “I walked past the church this afternoon. Your collie barked at me, so I knew you were inside. There was a strange saddled horse tied to the gate, no villager’s mount. So someone else was there with you. Then I asked you to play. And that overture, the raw, aching farewell in every note, told me exactly who that someone was.”

The words barely registered with her at first, until she lifted her head, voice steady again: “You went to Wiesbaden for a year, right after Willoughby brought that first feather. That was why you left, wasn’t it?”

“I went because neither of us could keep up the lie we were living. You pretended you didn’t care a fig if Harry was dead or alive. I pretended I hadn’t seen that he was all you cared for in the whole world. We were one small slip away from breaking. I couldn’t make you live the rest of your life thinking you’d ruined two lives out of pity for me. You couldn’t make me live knowing the woman I was going to marry only felt friendship for me. So I left.”

“You never slipped,” Ethne said, quiet and miserable. “I was the one who failed. I promised myself I’d keep this from you, so you wouldn’t have that extra weight on top of losing your sight and your army career. I failed.”

“It wasn’t your fault,” he said. “Be glad I found out, not angry. If you’d had your way, we wouldn’t have ruined two lives. We’d have ruined three: yours, Harry’s, and mine. What kind of life would that be, for me to be your unloved husband, stuck at home while you sacrificed every bit of the happiness you had a right to, to make up for my lost career? I’d rather be your friend, a friend you don’t have to lie to, than that.”

He picked the violin up off the chair, running his fingers gently over its polished wood. “You said goodbye to Harry twice today, you know. Once in the church. The second time, through your playing. That farewell was sad, but noble. True music never wastes breath complaining, you know.” He set the instrument back down. “I’m sending a messenger to Rathmullen tonight. Harry can’t cross Lough Swilly in the dark. The messenger will ride out at once, and bring him back to you tomorrow.”

Ethne said nothing, silent tears rolling down her cheeks, too overwhelmed to speak. Durrance picked up his cap, and moved noiselessly toward the door. Before he could open it, she stood. “Wait. The oculist in Wiesbaden—did he give you any hope? Any chance to get your sight back?”

Durrance paused, weighing the words. “No. None at all. But I’m not helpless. I get around fine, on my own. One of these days, I’m going to go back. A long journey, out to the East, among all those strange desert people I used to serve with. There’s still adventure left for me, even blind.”

He stepped out into the dark, the wind curling around the cottage walls behind him. The echo of the Musoline Overture still hung in the parlor air, its quiet message settling soft over everything: true music never complains. And neither, it seemed, did the people brave enough to choose the right thing, instead of the easy one.

The early summer sun gilded the stone balustrade of Broad Place’s terrace, where two old veterans sat finishing their post-breakfast newspapers, the scent of jasmine climbing the rail mixing with the faint tang of new-mown hay drifting up from the park meadow below. The elder of the pair, General Feversham, turned a crisp sheet and huffed a quiet note of surprise.
“I see Osman Digna’s turned up at Suakin again,” he said. “Likely there’ll be fighting before long.”
The other man, Lieutenant Sutch, barely reacted. “He won’t do much real harm,” he said, and set his newspaper down entirely. The soft English countryside blurred before his eyes: all he could see was the white Red Sea city shimmering under brutal heat, the brown surrounding plains tangled waist-deep in halfa grass, and the bare, sun-scorched hills stretching away toward Khor Gwob.
“Stifling place, Suakin, isn’t it, Sutch?” Feversham prompted.
“Appallingly so,” Sutch agreed. “Heard tell of an officer who dropped dead of sunstroke on parade at six in the morning, heat went right through a regulation issue helmet straight to his temples. That town breathes dank, clinging heat. But I was glad to be there, very glad,” he added, his voice soft with unspoken memory.
“Ah,” Feversham nodded knowingly. “Ibex hunting, eh?”
“No,” Sutch laughed shortly. “Garrison shot all the ibex out for miles around years ago.”
“Then it was something worthwhile to do, that’s what got you through it?”
“That’s it exactly, Feversham. Something that mattered to do.”
They fell quiet, turning back to their papers, until a footman carried out a small stack of post for each of them. Feversham’s sharp old eye scanned his envelopes in an instant, and he grunted with quiet satisfaction when he spotted the postmark from Ramelton. He fished his spectacles out of their case, laid the letter deliberately on the garden table between them, and drew out his cigar case.
“Let me light this first,” he said, moving with the deliberate slowness of a man who has learned to savor small, rare luxuries. He ignored Sutch’s fidgeting, carefully cutting the end of his cigar, striking a match, and drawing slow puffs until he was sure it burned evenly, then leaned back in his chair to stretch his legs. A letter from Ramelton was not to be rushed.
“From Harry?” Sutch asked, nodding at the envelope.
“From her,” Feversham corrected, and broke the seal. He read every line slowly, twice over, while Sutch pretended to stare out at the fields so he would not be caught peeking.
“Any news worth having?” Sutch asked, affecting casualness.
“They’re happy with the house now the rebuild’s all finished,” Feversham said.
“More?”
“Harry’s finished the sixth chapter of his history of the Eastern war.”
“Good for him,” Sutch cheered. “He’ll do that work better than any official war correspondent. He’s got the imagination for it, he knows every inch of that ground, he was on the scene for every fight. More than that, he wandered the bazaars, saw the unreported, messy underbelly of the campaign no general’s dispatch ever bothered to write down.”
“Aye,” Feversham said, a little gruff. “I doubt you and I will live to finish reading it, though, Sutch. But you might—you’ve got a good few years on me still.”
He went back to the letter, and Sutch pressed again for more news.
“They’re coming to stay here in a fortnight,” Feversham said, and Sutch bounced to his feet.
“Splendid. I’m cancelling all my other plans. I’m staying right here to greet them.”
He paced the length of the terrace once, then turned back to find Feversham staring down at the letter in his lap, his brow furrowed with genuine perplexity.
“You know, Sutch,” he said, “I never understood. Not the whole of it. Did you?”
Sutch smiled. “Aye, I think I did.” He did not offer to explain. It was far better Feversham never untangled the full tale of Harry’s secret sacrifice, the three feathers, the months he wandered disguised through the desert. To understand would only fill the old general with self-reproach for the years he spent misjudging Harry as a coward.
“Ever see Durrance these days?” Feversham asked suddenly.
“Quite often. He’s abroad traveling right now.”
“Came to call here the day you left for Suakin,” Feversham recalled. “Talked to me for half an hour. Stood up as Harry’s best man at the wedding, too. That’s another thing I never made head nor tail of. Did you?”
“I understood that, too,” Sutch said simply.
Feversham made a small hmph noise, and did not press for details. He had always set aside the puzzles he could not solve to make room for simpler, clearer joys, and he did not reach for his other letters now. He sat smoking his cigar, staring out over the sun-dappled fields, listening to the distant laughter of farm hands and the low moo of cattle from the lower pastures.
Sutch had already finished all his own correspondence when Feversham spoke again.
“Tell me,” he said, “have you marked what the date is today?”
Sutch looked up, and his face lit. “Aye. This day next week is the anniversary of our attack on the Redan. And Harry’s birthday, too.”
“Exactly,” Feversham grinned. “What do you say we bring the old Crimean Night dinners back?”
Sutch jumped so fast his chair scraped the terrace stone. “Splendid! Do you think we can round up a full table of the old crowd?”
“Let’s count,” Feversham said, ringing the handbell on the table to send the servant fetching the tattered old Army List. The two veterans leaned over its red leather pages, poring over names and postings, and we may leave them there, planning the feast full of old toasts and old comrades.
There is one last small tale left to tell, though. That same night, after all the invitations had been sent out from Broad Place, and every light in the old house had guttered out, a man leaned on the rail of a steamer anchored in Port Said harbor, listening to Arab coolies singing rough work songs as they tramped back and forth across the gangplanks, coal baskets slung over their shoulders, running between barges and the ship’s hull. The din of the town’s bazaars drifted across the dark water to his ears, and he could picture every part of the quay without seeing it: the flaring braziers where street vendors cooked their food, the warm yellow glow of ship portholes strung out in a long line down the canal, the dark bulk of anchored funnels ahead and behind.
It was Durrance, back in the East he loved, his long-servant at his elbow to guide him. The next dawn the steamer slipped through the canal cut, and by sunset it was cutting through the cool, sharp air of the Gulf of Suez. He lived every old campaign again, clear as day: Kassassin, Tel-el-Kebir, Tamai, the chaotic fight at McNeil’s zareeba. All the bright, full years of his active service, the years when his eyes were sharp and he could march for days across the sand without tiring. He knew, far to the west across the desert, the slow, massive preparations for the full reconquest of the Sudan were already underway, the campaign that would roll the Dervish Empire up and crush it to dust under the British flag. He had promised himself once he would stand on the glacis of the ruined Sinkat fort and fight in that campaign, but the desert he loved had struck him blind and cast him out.
But the steamer still moved south, cutting steadily toward the Red Sea. Three more nights of sailing, and the Southern Cross would lift slantwise over the southern horizon, bright in the dark sky—even if Durrance could never see it, he would feel it, glowing there above the dunes he knew better than the back of his own hand.
