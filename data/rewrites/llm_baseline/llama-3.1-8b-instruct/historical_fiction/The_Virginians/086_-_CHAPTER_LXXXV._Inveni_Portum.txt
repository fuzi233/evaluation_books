As I stood at the threshold of returning to England, I couldn't help but sense a mixture of emotions within my mother, who had just learned of my decision. Her demeanor seemed tinged with a hint of disappointment, a subtle suggestion that perhaps she had hoped I would reconsider and return to our Virginia estate. It was a notion I'd considered myself, especially during those early days when our financial disputes still lingered. Yet, despite the silence that had descended between us, I chose not to press the issue. My mother, ever the shrewd businesswoman, had long since severed my allowance, forcing me to navigate the world without her support. Still, I'd managed to thrive, relying on my wits and resourcefulness to make ends meet.

In later years, when our paths crossed, I detected a softer edge to her demeanor, a quiet respect and affection for my wife, Madam Esmond, that suggested a deep-seated regret for past transgressions. Though she never explicitly apologized, I sensed a tacit acknowledgment of her mistake. Perhaps it was the manner in which she treated my family, with a newfound warmth and kindness, that hinted at a desire for reconciliation. But I remained resolute, choosing not to seek forgiveness or reparation. My pride, though bruised, had long since mended, and I'd grown accustomed to living without the burden of her financial support. Besides, with Madam Esmond by my side, our future seemed bright, and I had no need to concern myself with petty squabbles.

As I pondered the complexities of human nature, I found myself drawn to the enigma of pride. Did we truly take pride in being proud, or was it merely a veil for our own insecurities? I thought of the countless individuals I'd encountered throughout my life – men who wore masks of confidence, their smiles and words mere facades for the turmoil brewing beneath. Did they don these masks even in solitude, or was it a ruse to deceive only themselves? I wondered if true forgiveness was a rare commodity, reserved for those who possessed the luxury of being able to afford it. And I, for one, had always found it easier to remit debts rather than seek pardon, not out of a desire to forgive, but because I could afford to let go of the hurt.
