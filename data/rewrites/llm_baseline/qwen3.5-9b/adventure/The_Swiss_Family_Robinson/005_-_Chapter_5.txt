The following morning, while breakfast preparations were underway, I turned my attention to preserving the magnificent skin of the kangaroo we had hunted, ensuring it remained intact. Once Fritz had finished organizing our supplies for the journey to the wreck, I summoned Ernest and Jack to deliver some final instructions. However, the boys had vanished immediately after breakfast. Their mother surmised they must have gone to gather potatoes, a necessity for our trip. Though I urged her to reprimand them for leaving without permission upon their return, the sight of their dog, Turk, accompanying them reassured me that no harm would come to them. Reluctantly, I left my beloved wife alone with little Franz, offering her comfort with promises of our swift return laden with treasures from the wreck.

Our steady march led us across the bridge at Jackal River, where a sudden commotion broke our stride. To our astonishment, Jack and Ernest burst forth from a hidden spot where they had been lying in wait, delighting in the startled reaction of their unsuspecting father and brother. Evidently, they believed they were joining us on the expedition. I firmly but kindly dissuaded them, unable to scold the mischievous pair for their playful deception, especially since I needed to send a message to my wife. I instructed them to rush home to ease her worry, noting that while they were already far ahead, they might as well collect some salt on their way back. I tasked them with explaining that my work aboard the ship would take considerable time and that she should endure our absence for one night. Originally, I intended to convey this verbally at our departure, but fear of her objection had held me back; I had planned to return that evening. Yet, reconsidering the critical need to construct a raft—a project requiring uninterrupted focus—I realized staying overnight was essential. Fortunately, the boys' antics had inadvertently granted me the opportunity to communicate this change of plan.

"Goodbye, boys! Take care of yourselves!" shouted Fritz as I boarded the tub-boat with him, and we pushed off.

The current swiftly carried us out of the bay. Soon, we moored securely against the shattered hull of the wreck and scrambled onto the remaining deck to formulate our strategy. My goal was to build a raft capable of transporting a vast array of heavy items back to shore—cargo too cumbersome for our small boat. A cluster of empty water casks seemed ideal for the base. We sealed them tightly, pushed them overboard, and arranged twelve of them side-by-side in rows of three, binding them firmly with spars. Over this sturdy foundation, we laid a thick plank flooring protected by a low railing. Thus, we constructed a robust raft perfectly suited to our needs.

Exhaustion prevented us from returning to land that evening. Having consumed only light snacks from our wallets and worked without respite, we instead prepared an excellent supper from the ship's stores and retired to sleep on spring mattresses—a true luxury compared to our cramped hammocks.

The next morning, we vigorously began loading the raft and boat. First, we cleared our own cabins, then moved to the captain's quarters, removing furniture, doors, windows, and their hardware. We seized the officers' chests and those of the carpenter and gunsmith, though the latter required us to unpack them piece by piece due to their immense weight. One large chest brimmed with an assortment of fine goods, resembling a jeweler's display of gold and silver watches, snuffboxes, buckles, studs, chains, and rings. While these treasures captivated us momentarily, a case containing common knives and forks proved far more practical than the elegant silverware we had previously salvaged.

To my delight, we discovered numerous young fruit trees, meticulously packed and labeled with names familiar to Europeans: apples, pears, chestnuts, oranges, almonds, peaches, apricots, plums, cherries, and vines. Destined for a distant colony, this cargo proved to be an inexhaustible treasure trove for us. Amongst the riches were ironmongery, plumbing tools, lead, paint, grinding stones, cart wheels, and everything necessary for a blacksmith's forge, including spades and ploughshares. We also found sacks of maize, peas, oats, and wheat, a hand mill, and the numbered components of a sawmill waiting to be assembled.

Overwhelmed by the sheer volume of wealth surrounding us, we struggled to decide what to salvage. Realizing that the first storm would likely destroy the ship entirely, we knew we could not afford to leave anything behind. Selecting the most vital items—including the grain and fruit trees—we gradually filled our raft. We added fishing lines, reels, cordage, harpoons, and a mariner's compass. Recalling our earlier encounter with a shark, Fritz positioned the harpoons, playfully imagining himself a whaler brandishing his weapon. By early afternoon, both vessels were heavily laden, and we prepared to depart.

The voyage began with trepidation; towing the raft presented significant risks. However, with calm seas and favorable winds, we raised the sail, and our progress was smooth. Suddenly, Fritz requested the telescope, pointing out a curious object floating in the distance. Upon closer inspection, I identified it as a turtle sleeping peacefully, unaware of our approach.

"Do, Father, steer toward it!" Fritz exclaimed.

I complied, allowing him a better view. Unaware of his intentions, I watched as the boy turned his back, the broad sail blocking my vision. Abruptly, I felt a sharp jolt, followed by the sensation of a line paying out. Before I could react, a second shock surged through the boat as it was dragged rapidly through the water.

"Fritz, what are you doing?" I cried. "You are driving us to the bottom!"

"I've got him! Hurrah! I've got him safe!" he shouted, ecstatic.

To my amazement, I realized he had speared the turtle with a harpoon. A rope was attached, and the creature was towing us away. Lowering the sail and grabbing my hatchet, I rushed forward to sever the line and release the turtle.

"Wait, Father!" the boy pleaded. "There's no danger yet! I'll cut the line myself when necessary! Let's catch this turtle if we can!"

"My dear boy," I warned, "if this turtle capsizes our cargo or drowns us, it won't be a bargain. Be careful! I'll wait a few moments, but cut the line instantly if things turn dangerous."

As the turtle headed for open sea, I raised the sail again. Finding resistance too great, the creature turned back toward land, dragging us forcefully behind it. The shoreline we approached differed from our usual landing site, featuring a gradual slope. At some distance from the beach, we grounded with a sharp thud, luckily avoiding a capsize.

Evidently exhausted from acting as a living tug-of-war vessel, the turtle slowed. Its intent was clearly to reach land. Seizing the moment, I leaped into the water, waded up to the creature, and dispatched it with my axe. Despite its tenacity, it ceased struggling only after I severed its head from its body.

Not far from Falconhurst, Fritz signaled our arrival by firing his gun and shouting in triumph. As we secured our boats and hauled the turtle ashore, the entire family emerged from the house, eager to greet us. Our new prize, along with the laden boat and raft, sparked lively interest. My wife's greatest relief, however, was our safe return; our nocturnal adventure had deeply worried her, and she was horrified by tales of our perilous chase.

Determined to unload some goods before nightfall, the boys fetched the sledge. Without an anchor, I improvised by mooring the boats using heavy iron blocks we had retrieved. With combined effort, we hoisted the massive turtle onto the sledge. Together with the sapling fruit trees, it formed a substantial load. We hurried home, chatting merrily about our exploits.

Upon arrival, the priority was cooking the turtle for supper. My wife hesitated at the prospect of the labor involved, but I flipped the beast onto its back and quickly detached a portion of the breast meat by breaking the lower shell. I ordered it cooked with a bit of salt, shell and all.

"But let me first cut away this disgusting green fat," my wife shuddered. "Look how it clings to the meat. No one could eat such filth."

"Leave the fat, whatever you do!" I insisted. "My dear, that is the finest part, the delight of epicures. If there is too much, trim it—it makes excellent lard. Let the dogs feast on the scraps."

"And the beautiful shell!" cried Fritz. "I'd love to make a water trough from it to keep near the brook, always full of clear water. How useful!"

"That is a splendid idea," I agreed. "We can certainly manage it if we find suitable clay for a solid foundation."

"Oh, regarding clay," said Jack, "I found a huge lump under that root."

"Well done, my lad! When did you find it?"

"He stumbled upon a clay bed near the river this morning," his mother explained. "He came home such a mess I had to scrub his clothes and bathe him thoroughly!"

"Well, Mother, I can only say I never would have found that clay if I hadn't slipped and fallen right into it."

"That I can believe," she retorted, "though hearing you speak this morning, one would think you searched for hours."
