Colonel Lambert, who had seen men bleed from the mouth and yet swear they were sound, took counsel with his lady upon a sickness far less easy to physic. They agreed—quietly, as householders agree upon the ordering of a kitchen for winter—that their little Hetty should be carried away from Tunbridge Wells, and from the reach of that young gentleman whose very laugh seemed to set her heart in motion.

Absence, which is a fair purge for many a man, is no such simple draught for a girl. A man will be marched from a temptation as from a tavern door; he will busy himself with cards, horses, commissions, and forget. A young woman, once her mind has tasted a sweetness, may travel a hundred miles, and yet the flavour goes with her like spice in a closed box: you open it, and there is the scent still, in the linen and the wood. The mother, who knew the way of hens and children and hearts, looked to distance as to a cool pantry; the father, who knew the way of camps, trusted in removal as in a change of air. They forgot that love is not cured by climate, and that memory, once seasoned, keeps.

So why not, one might say, let the child take leave? Why not permit her to stand in the doorway, while the harness jingles and the post-boy clucks to the horses, and say, with a steady little voice, “Good-bye, Mr. Warrington. I was wilful last night, and you were kind. Good-bye”? She would show no special emotion. Shame sits in the throat like a dry crust; it will not let the words come as tears. She would smile her innocent smile, which is often but an artifice to hide pain, and he—preoccupied, full of his own concerns, and of the bustle of men—would not read what lay behind her eyes.

And indeed he did not. When the time came, Harry Warrington did not even ride beside the Lambert carriage a part of the homeward way. He had made some other party for that afternoon—an engagement of pleasure, as men call it, that takes the place of duty—and when he returned, the good people were gone from Tunbridge Wells. Their windows stood open to the air, and a card in one pane announced, with that cold civility of lodging-houses, that the apartments were once more to let.

A little sorrow, passing as swiftly as the shadow of a cloud over a table-cloth, may have touched him at the blankness of the rooms which had lately held such frank faces, such friendly voices. But four o’clock brings dinner. He dined at the White Horse, and the hot savour of roasted meat and gravy, the clatter of plates, the scrape of chairs, the cork drawn with that small sigh of glass and air—these common sounds and smells of the world took him up as they always do. He ate his meal, and called fiercely for his bottle, as though a man could drown a thought as he drowns a biscuit in wine.

At that same hour, poor little Hester, jolting away toward Westerham, would choke over her tea when it was offered—tea that tasted of tin and sorrow. When at last the Lambert cavalcade reached the house of their friends, she went to bed with the sound of wheels still in her head, and in the morning the young roses in her cheeks were wan, and there were dark circles about her eyes. It was the thunder, she said; the night was hot; she could not sleep. She would be better when she got home next day. She spoke as children speak, naming weather and not naming the secret storm.

Home they came, and every familiar thing stood waiting, as if it had not stirred while she had lived whole years in two nights. There was the gate where he fell. There was the bed he lay in. There was the chair in which he used to sit. The very boards seemed to remember the tread of those days, and the yard had its own music—the steady pecking of hens, the small cries of chickens, the soft drip from a watering-pot into thirsty earth. Who was that child yonder calling her chickens, or bending to the rose-bushes with a little jug? Was she the same Hester Lambert who, a week ago, had thought only of music and poultry and her flower-garden? She looked older than Theo now—Theo, always composed, clever, and old for her age. But Hester had crossed a gulf in the night, and though she still moved among kettles and coops and beds as before, she moved like one who has learned a new tongue of pain which she cannot translate to anyone.

In Tunbridge Wells, Maria Esmond watched the Lamberts drive away and felt a grim relief, as if a door had closed between her and a draught of cold air. She had that relief which comes when a rival is removed—not by one’s own hand, but by Providence or prudence—and yet relief is seldom clean; it leaves a taste.

When Harry came into his aunt’s card-room that evening, he was flushed with Barbeau’s good wine; his laughter ran too loud against the hush of upholstery and candlelight. Madame Bernstein’s tables were set as carefully as any supper-board: green cloth smoothed, counters ranged, the cards in their neat tyranny. But there is a kind of hunger in play too, and a thirst.

His aunt asked, with her mocking ease, which of the girls was his sweetheart. He rattled an answer as he might toss a dice-box: he loved them both like sisters. He had never seen better people than the Lamberts; Lambert should be a general; the Duke was fond of him. And Madame Bernstein, who seasoned every sentence with malice as a cook seasons with salt, bade him make interest with Lady Yarmouth for his protégé.

“Elle raffole de fous, cher petit!” she said, mimicking the Countess’s German accent until it turned coarse in her mouth. The Baroness was delighted with her boy’s success, and turned a sneer upon her niece.

“You carry off the hearts of all the old women, doesn’t he, Maria?” she said; and Maria, who had been braced like a string, quivered at the touch.

“You were quite right, my dear,” Madame Bernstein went on, “not to perceive that she cheated at cards, and you play like a grand seigneur.”

“Did she cheat?” Harry cried, honestly amazed. “I am sure, ma’am, I saw no unfair play.”

“No more did I, my dear,” said the old woman lazily, “but I am sure she cheated. Bah! every woman cheats, I and Maria included, when we can get a chance. But when you play with the Walmoden, you don’t do wrong to lose in moderation; and many men cheat in that way. Cultivate her. She has taken a fancy to your beaux yeux. Why should your Excellency not be Governor of Virginia, sir? You must go and pay your respects to the Duke and his Majesty at Kensington. The Countess of Yarmouth will be your best friend at court.”

“Why should you not introduce me, aunt?” Harry asked.

The old lady’s rouged cheek took on a deeper colour, as if the paint itself were ashamed.

“I am not in favour at Kensington,” she said. “I may have been once; and there are no faces so unwelcome to kings as those they wish to forget. All of us want to forget something or somebody. I dare say our ingénu here would like to wipe a sum or two off the slate. Wouldst thou not, Harry?”

At that, Harry reddened; and Maria reddened too. Madame Bernstein laughed one of those wicked laughs that have no merriment in them, only the crackle of spite. What guilt had signalled itself in those two blushes? What account lay scored upon either memory which each would be glad to rub out? Most of us—be we ever so powdered and perfumed—carry some ugly reckonings, and dread an audit.

Had Maria known one cause of Harry’s disquiet, her own anxiety would have risen like dough in heat. For days past, he had missed a pocket-book. He remembered it in his possession on the day he drank so much claret at the White Horse—claret whose warm fumes mingle with the smell of roast and tobacco until a man’s head becomes a noisy market—and Gumbo had carried him to bed. He searched for it in the morning; none of his servants had seen it. He made inquiry at the White Horse, but the house yielded nothing—no trace in the bedding, no forgotten bundle in a drawer, no tell-tale scuff under a settle. He could not cry the book openly; he could only ask with cautious words, as though he were feeling for a bruise.

He must not have it known the book was lost.

For in it were letters—Lady Maria’s letters—those long outpourings of the heart, which, like over-rich sauces, are delightful to those at the table and nauseous when found cold by strangers. They contained disclosures of family matters, little histories, confidences, and that rash satire which lovers write because they believe the ink will be buried with the secret. There were jokes, scorn, scandal; appeals and protests of eternal fidelity; reproaches about his attentions to other women. There was the whole foolish farrago that people will pour out when they love and fear and have nothing but paper to receive it. Foolishness is not a crime; but it is a peril, especially when a private tongue is committed to a public alphabet.

While Madame Bernstein dozed after dinner—for eating and drinking too much were among her ladyship’s indulgences—Harry went to play billiards with Count Caramboli. The click of the balls, sharp and exact, is a sort of music too, but a hard one; it does not soothe the mind, it drives it. In the neighbouring room, Mr. Chaplain Sampson sat with Lady Maria and spoke low, that he might not disturb the Baroness’s sleep. Sampson had the air of a man who has walked long in other men’s houses and learned to tread softly; his voice was full of unction, as if every word had been dipped in oil.

“A gentleman of Mr. Warrington’s means can afford to be idle,” Maria said, trying for ease.

“I don’t say, madam, my practice is good, only my doctrine is sound,” Sampson replied with a sigh that seemed meant to be heard. “This young gentleman should have some employment. He should appear at court, and enter the service of his country, as befits a man of his station. He should settle down, and choose a woman of a suitable rank as his wife.”

As he said it, he looked straight into her face. Maria’s cheeks warmed, as if he had held them briefly over a fire.

“Indeed, my cousin is wasting his time,” she answered, and tried to keep her voice even.

“Mr. Warrington might see his relatives of his father’s family,” Sampson suggested.

“Suffolk country boobies drinking beer and hallooing after foxes!” Maria cried, too quickly. “I don’t see anything to be gained by his frequenting them, Mr. Sampson!”

“They are of an ancient family,” said the chaplain calmly, “of which the chief has been knight of the shire these hundred years. I have heard Sir Miles hath a daughter of Mr. Harry’s age—and beauty, too.”

“I know nothing, sir, about Sir Miles Warrington, and his daughters, and his beauties!” Maria exclaimed, flustered, and then, as if to cover the sound, glanced toward the room where her aunt lay.

Sampson listened, and smiled a faint smile. “The Baroness stirred—no—her ladyship is in a sweet sleep,” he murmured. Then, leaning in a little as a man leans to smell whether a pot is boiling over, he went on: “I fear, madam, for your ladyship’s cousin, Mr. Warrington. I fear for his youth; for designing persons who may get about him; for extravagances, follies, intrigues even into which he will be led, and into which everybody will try to tempt him. His lordship, my kind patron, bade me come and watch over him, and I am here accordingly, as your ladyship knoweth. I know the follies of young men. Perhaps I have practised them myself. I own it with a blush”—and he spoke of blushes with great sincerity, though his face did not much alter—“Between ourselves, I fear Mr. Warrington is in some trouble now, madam.”

“What, again?” Maria cried, too sharply.

“Hush! Your ladyship’s dear invalid!” whispered Sampson, and pointed with his eyes toward Madame Bernstein.

Maria lowered her voice. “What trouble?”

“Do you think your cousin has any partiality for any—any member of Mr. Lambert’s family? for example, Miss Lambert?”

“There is nothing between him and Miss Lambert,” said Maria at once, with the eagerness of one who puts a lid on a pot.

“Your ladyship is certain?”

“Women are said to have good eyes in such matters, my good Sampson,” Maria answered, forcing an easy air. “I thought the little girl seemed to be following him.”

“Then I am at fault once more,” the chaplain said, with a frankness that looked like honesty. “Mr. Warrington said of the young lady, that she ought to go back to her doll, and called her a pert, stuck-up little hussy.”

Maria let out a breath that was half a sigh. “Ah!”

“Then, madam, there must be somebody else,” Sampson continued, watching her steadily. “Has he confided nothing to your ladyship?”

“To me, Mr. Sampson? What? Where? How?” Maria stammered; and the room, though close, seemed suddenly airless.

“Some six days ago,” said Sampson, “after we had been dining at the White Horse, and drinking too freely, Mr. Warrington lost a pocket-book containing letters.”

“Letters?” Maria gasped, and the word struck her like a blow to the breast.

“And probably more money than he likes to own,” Sampson added gravely. “He is very much disturbed about the book. We have both made cautious inquiries about it. We have—Gracious powers, is your ladyship ill?”

For Lady Maria gave three shrill screams—so piercing that they seemed to cut clean through the soft hangings and sleeping-room silence—and fell from her chair in a heap, as if her bones had been drawn out of her. The house itself seemed to start at the noise; in such a quiet place, sound is a tyrant.

“I will see the Prince. I have a right to see him. What’s this?—Where am I?—What’s the matter?” cried Madame Bernstein, waking as if out of some feverish dream. She shook in her limbs; her face was flushed under the rouge; her eyes stared wildly before she gathered herself. Then she tottered forward on her tortoiseshell cane, the tapping of it sharp on the boards.

“What—what’s the matter?” she demanded again. “Have you killed her, sir?”

“Some sudden qualm must have come over her ladyship,” Sampson cried, all innocence and alarm. “Shall I cut her laces, madam? or send for a doctor?”

“What has passed between you, sir?” the old Baroness asked fiercely, her voice hard as a knife on a plate.

“I give you my honour, madam,” he said, “I have done I don’t know what. I but mentioned that Mr. Warrington had lost a pocket-book containing letters, and my lady swooned, as you see.”

Madame Bernstein snatched a glass of water and dashed it on Maria’s face. Presently a feeble moan came; the younger woman’s eyelids fluttered as if weighed with lead. Madame Bernstein sent Sampson away for the doctor, but her eyes followed him with a suspicion that was almost a glare.

When Maria opened her eyes, the first thing she saw was her aunt’s grim countenance bent over her—no comfort there, only judgment.

“What has happened?” Maria asked, bewildered and gasping.

“H’m! You know best what has happened, madam, I suppose,” the Baroness replied, her mouth twisting bitterly. “What hath happened before in our family?”

“Ah, yes! the letters have been lost—ach lieber Himmel!” Maria cried, and in her agitation slipped into her mother’s tongue, as she did when much moved.

“Yes! the seal has been broken, and the letters have been lost—’tis the old story of the Esmonds,” cried the elder woman. “Seal broken, letters lost? What do you mean—aunt?” Maria asked, faintly.

“I mean that my mother was the only honest woman that ever entered the family!” Madame Bernstein cried, stamping her foot so that the cane jarred. “And she was a parson’s daughter of no family in particular, or she would have gone wrong, too. Good heavens! is it decreed that we are all to be—”

“To be what, madam?” Maria whispered.

“To be what my Lady Queensberry said we were last night. To be what we are! You know the word for it!” the Baroness cried, her anger rising like a boil. “I say, what has come to the whole race? Your father’s mother was an honest woman, Maria. Why did I leave her? Why couldn’t you remain so?”

“Madam!” Maria exclaimed, struggling to sit upright, “I declare, before Heaven, I am as—”

“Bah! Don’t madam me! Don’t call heaven to witness—there’s nobody by! And if you swore to your innocence till the rest of your teeth dropped out of your mouth, my Lady Maria Esmond, I would not believe you!”

Maria’s eyes widened. “Ah! it was you told him!” she gasped. She knew that thrust; she had felt it before, like a pin set in a sleeve.

“I saw some folly going on between you and the boy,” Madame Bernstein replied without shame, “and I told him that you were as old as his mother. Yes, I did! Do you suppose I am going to let Henry Esmond’s boy fling himself and his wealth away upon such a battered old rock as you? The boy shan’t be robbed and cheated in our family. Not a shilling of mine shall any of you have if he comes to any harm amongst you.”

That last threat snapped something in Maria that had been habit and fear. She rose, trembling but upright.

“Ah! you told him!” she cried, and her voice, for once, had steel in it. “Well, then! I’d have you to know that I don’t care a penny, madam, for your paltry money! I have Mr. Harry Warrington’s word—yes, and his letters—and I know he will die rather than break it.”

“He will die if he keeps it!” the old lady screamed.

Maria shrugged, as if to fling off the weight of that prophecy. “But you don’t care for that—you’ve no more heart—”

“Than my father’s sister, madam!” Maria shot back. The meekness that had been her daily bread was gone; she spoke now as if she had swallowed fire.

Madame Bernstein’s fury faltered into something like despair, and she shook her head, suddenly old.

“Ah! Why did not I marry an honest man?” she said. “Henry Esmond was noble and good, and perhaps might have made me so. But no, no—we have all got the taint in us—all! You don’t mean to sacrifice this boy, Maria?”

“Madame ma tante, do you take me for a fool at my age?” Maria asked, and her laugh was bitter.

“Set him free! I’ll give you five thousand pounds—in my—in my will, Maria. I will, on my honour!”

“When you were young,” Maria answered, “and you liked Colonel Esmond, you threw him aside for an earl, and the earl for a duke?”

“Yes,” said Madame Bernstein, and her mouth tightened.

“Eh! Bon sang ne peut mentir! I have no money, I have no friends. My father was a spendthrift, my brother is a beggar. I have Mr. Warrington’s word, and I know, madam, he will keep it. And that’s what I tell your ladyship!” Maria cried, sweeping her hand as if she were clearing a table.

“Suppose my letters are published to all the world to-morrow? Après? I know they contain things I would as lieve not tell. Things not about me alone. Comment! Do you suppose there are no stories but mine in the family? It is not my letters that I am afraid of, so long as I have his, madam. Yes, his and his word, and I trust them both.”

“I will send to my merchant, and give you the money now, Maria,” the old lady pleaded, changing tack as quickly as a cook who sees a sauce about to split.

“No, I shall have my pretty Harry, and ten times five thousand pounds!” Maria cried, reckless now with desperation and pride.

“Not till his mother’s death, madam, who is just your age!” Madame Bernstein retorted.

“We can afford to wait, aunt,” Maria answered. “At my age, as you say, I am not so eager as young chits for a husband.”

“But to wait my sister’s death, at least, is a drawback?”

“Offer me ten thousand pounds, Madam Tusher, and then we will see!” Maria flung out.

“I have not so much money in the world, Maria,” the Baroness said, and for an instant her voice was small.

“Then, madam, let me make what I can for myself!” Maria replied.

“Ah, if he heard you—”

“Après? I have his word. I know he will keep it. I can afford to wait, madam,” Maria said; and with that she swept out of the room, her skirts whispering like a departing tide, at the very moment Mr. Sampson returned with news of the doctor.

It was Madame Bernstein who wanted cordials now. The old lady, shaken by the suddenness of the discovery and the violence of her niece’s rebellion, sank back as if the strength had run out of her. The house, which a moment ago had been full of shrieks and stamping and accusations, fell again into its domestic hush—the muffled creak of a chair, the far-off rattle of carriage-wheels in the street, the soft crackle of a coal settling—sounds that should have soothed, but which, after such a storm, seemed only to mark how thin the walls were between a family’s secrets and the listening world.
