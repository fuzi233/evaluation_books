When Fra Luca fell silent, Tito lingered beside him, paralyzed by indecision. It was not until the crowd of pilgrims had thinned and the friar, moving with a slow, weary gait, stepped through the doors of the Church of Santa Felicita that Tito followed, turning down the Via de' Bardi.

"If this monk is truly Florentine," Tito murmured to himself, "and if he intends to stay, then every secret must see the light." He sensed a new crisis unfolding within the shifting landscape of his life, yet he remained composed enough to keep his appointment with Bardo. An apology for his recent absence would be necessary, a formal gesture to smooth over the awkward silence between them. However, Tito's natural aptitude for secrecy was rapidly evolving into something far more dangerous. While it was conceivable—even perhaps inevitable—that he might openly accept these changed circumstances and confess Baldassarre's existence, doing so would cast a harsh, retrospective glow upon his earlier silence. It would reveal his previous reticence not merely as caution, but as a calculated evasion designed to avoid fulfilling a claim he had privately acknowledged. Furthermore, it would highlight the irony of his own quiet settlement and the investment of his florins, undertaken before his benefactor's fate was ever confirmed. For the moment, prudence dictated a stance of feigned ignorance. There was ample time for meditation during the coming night, and no witness could pinpoint the exact instant he received the letter.

With a countenance only slightly brighter than usual—a brightness born of regret at arriving so late—he ascended to the second floor where Romola and her father sat surrounded by parchment and marble, isolated from the vibrant chaos of the streets whether on holiday or in ordinary times. This regret required no proof, for he had voluntarily surrendered the spectacle of the Corso to express it. Once inside, he strove to inject extra animation into the evening conversation, though beneath the surface, his consciousness operated like a complex machine, laying down tracks of thought entirely distinct from the thread of his speech. By the time he descended the stone stairs and emerged from the heavy door into the starlight, his mind had traversed a significant new terrain in the formation of his purpose.

The following day, once freed from his professorial duties, Tito turned up the Via del Cocomero toward the convent of San Marco. His purpose was now fully crystallized. He intended to interrogate Fra Luca precisely regarding the extent of his suspicions about the truth and the basis upon which those suspicions rested, as well as the duration of the friar's stay at San Marco. Armed with this fuller knowledge, he hoped to craft a statement that would spare him the necessity of fleeing Florence. Tito had never before been compelled to construct an ingenious falsehood; however, the occasion had arrived, born inevitably of circumstantial silence, and his faculties of invention were already sharpened. He had convinced himself that he was not bound to seek out Baldassarre. He had once declared that, upon receiving fair assurance of his father's existence and whereabouts, he would immediately pursue him. Yet, why was he bound? Upon closer inspection, what was the ultimate end of life other than the extraction of the maximum sum of pleasure? Was not his own burgeoning life a promise of incomparably greater joy—not only for himself but for others—than the withered, wintry existence of a man past the prime of keen enjoyment, whose thoughts had hardened into barren rigidity? Those ideas had been sown in the fresh soil of Tito's mind, sprouting as lively germs; such was the natural order, treating maturity as nothing more than a temporary nest for youth. Baldassarre had completed his work, drawn his fill of life; Tito asserted that it was now his turn.

The prospect before him was vague yet seductive: "They intend to take me to Antioch." Here lay a vision! After a long voyage, to spend months, perhaps years, searching for a goal for which there was currently no guarantee of success, while leaving behind a life of distinction and love. If he found anything, he would return to the old, demanding companionship he knew by heart. Certainly, the gems—and consequently the florins—were, in a narrow legal sense, Baldassarre's. But viewed through a broader, more radically natural lens, wherein the world belongs to youth and strength, they belonged to whoever could extract the most pleasure from them. He was acutely aware that this sentiment did not align with the complicated play of human emotions engendered in society. The men around him expected him to immediately apply those florins to his benefactor's rescue. But what was the true sentiment of society? A mere tangle of anomalous traditions and opinions, offering no guidance to a wise man save where it pertained to personal comfort. Did he care for the florins save perhaps for Romola's sake? He would relinquish them readily enough. It was the joy that was due to him, hovering just within reach of his lips, which he felt he was not obligated to reject and thus travel onward, thirsting. Any maxim requiring a man to discard the good essential to making existence sweet was merely the lining of human selfishness turned outward, devised by men wishing others to sacrifice themselves for their own benefit. He would rather Baldassarre did not suffer; indeed, he disliked seeing anyone suffer. But could any philosophy prove to him that he was bound to care for another's suffering more than for his own? To do so, he must have loved Baldassarre devotedly, and he did not. Was that his fault? As for gratitude, scrutinized closely, it made no valid claim. His father's life would have been dreary without him; were we not convicted of a debt to men simply because they gave themselves pleasures?

Once Tito began to dismantle Baldassarre's claim, his thought process revealed itself as active as a virulent acid, eating its rapid way through the tissues of sentiment. His mind lacked that dread often erroneously dismissed as nothing higher than a man's animal concern for his own skin: the awe of Divine Nemesis felt by religious pagans, and, taking a more positive form under Christianity, still felt by the masses as a vague terror of anything termed wrongdoing. Such terror of the unseen stands far above mere sensual cowardice; it annihilates that cowardice. It is the initial recognition of a moral law restraining desire, checking the hard, bold scrutiny of imperfect thought into obligations that can never be proven to possess any sanctity absent feeling. "It is good," sang the old Eumenides in Aeschylus, "that fear should sit as the guardian of the soul, forcing it into wisdom—it is good that men should carry a threatening shadow in their hearts under the full sunshine; else, how should they learn to revere the right?" That guardianship may eventually become superfluous, but only when all outward law becomes needless—only when duty and love unite in a single stream to form a common force.

As Tito entered the outer cloister of San Marco and inquired for Fra Luca, there was no shadowy presentiment in his mind; he felt himself too cultured and skeptical for such foreboding. He had been nurtured in contempt for the tales of priests whose impudent lives were proverbs, and possessed an erudite familiarity with disputes concerning the Chief Good, which he ultimately regarded as a matter of taste. Yet, fear was a strong element in Tito's nature—the fear of what he believed or saw was likely to rob him of pleasure. He harbored a definite fear that Fra Luca might be the instrument driving him from Florence.

"Fra Luca? Ah, he has gone to Fiesole—to the Dominican monastery there. He was carried off on a litter in the cool of the morning. The poor Brother is very ill. Might you leave a message for him?"

This reply came from a *fra converso*, or lay brother, whose accent plainly marked him as a raw countryman (*contadino*), and whose dull gaze implied no curiosity whatsoever.

"Thanks; my business can wait."

Tito turned away with a profound sense of relief. "This friar is unlikely to live," he told himself. "I saw he was worn down to a shadow. And at Fiesole, there will be nothing to recall me to his mind. Besides, if he returns, my explanation will serve as well then as now. Though..." He paused, his gaze lingering on the fading figure. "I wish I knew what it was about his face that recalled itself to me."
