As soon as her reply was sent away, Mrs. Dashwood turned to her son-in-law and daughter-in-law with a bright, relieved smile. "I have secured a house," she announced, her voice light with the joy of independence. "It means I shall not trouble you for long, only until my new home is quite ready." The news struck them with genuine surprise. Mrs. John Dashwood remained silent, but her husband politely expressed the hope that she would not settle too far from Norland. With a warm confidence, Mrs. Dashwood replied that she was moving to Devonshire.

At the mere mention of the name, Edward spun around, his face alight with shock and deep concern. "Devonshire!" he exclaimed, his tone urgent enough to make the truth clear without words. "Are you really going there? How far is that from here? And where exactly?" When she described the location—a charming spot just four miles north of Exeter—he let out a breath he seemed to have been holding for some time.

"It is merely a cottage," she continued gently, "yet I look forward to welcoming many old friends. A couple of rooms can easily be added if needed, and if they find the journey manageable, I am certain I shall make their stay comfortable." She ended with a tender invitation for both Mr. and Mrs. John Dashwood to visit her at Barton, though for Edward alone, her warmth burned even brighter. Although her recent talk with his wife had hardened her resolve to leave Norland as quickly as possible, it had not altered her feelings regarding the separation of Edward and Elinor. In fact, by inviting her brother so warmly, she intended to prove to Mrs. John Dashwood how utterly she dismissed her disapproval of the match.

Mr. John Dashwood, however, found little comfort in the news. He repeatedly voiced how deeply grieved he was that the distance would prevent him from helping move her furniture. He genuinely felt a pang of conscience, for the effort he had promised his father—the transfer of goods—now seemed impossible. Yet, the furniture itself traveled smoothly via the river, consisting mostly of linens, silver, fine china, books, and a beautiful pianoforte belonging to Marianne. As the crates departed, Mrs. John Dashwood watched with a visible sigh. She could not suppress the thought that it was unfair for Mrs. Dashwood, whose income was so modest compared to theirs, to retain such lovely pieces.

Mrs. Dashwood had leased the cottage for a year; it was fully furnished and available immediately. There were no hiccups in the arrangements, and she awaited only the clearing of Norland and the finalizing of her household before heading west. This transition happened swiftly, for she moved with remarkable energy whenever something mattered to her. Her horses, sold shortly after her husband's death, joined the caravan; at her eldest daughter's wise counsel, she also parted with her carriage. Had she acted solely on impulse, she might have clung to it, but Elinor's practicality prevailed. Their staff was whittled down to three: two maids and a footman, drawn from the former Norland crew.

Two servants were dispatched ahead to Devonshire to prepare the cottage, for Lady Middleton was a stranger to Mrs. Dashwood, and she preferred to go straight to her new home rather than linger as a guest at Barton Park. Relying completely on Sir John's glowing description, she felt no urge to inspect the property until she walked through its doors as its mistress. Her eagerness to flee Norland grew rather than waned, fueled by the obvious delight her daughter-in-law showed at the prospect of her removal. This happiness, however thinly veiled behind a perfunctory suggestion to delay the departure, stung. It was precisely now, when Norland stood empty, that Mr. John Dashwood's promise to his father ought to have been honored. Having failed to act upon first arriving at the estate, their imminent departure presented the perfect moment. Yet, as days passed, Mrs. Dashwood began to surrender every lingering hope. The general tenor of her son-in-law's conversations revealed a man preoccupied with rising household costs and the relentless demands placed on his purse. A gentleman of his standing, he seemed to imply, was perpetually stretched thin financially. It became clear to her that he needed money more than he possessed any intention of giving it away.

Within a few weeks of receiving Sir John Middleton's first letter, everything was arranged for their journey. Many tears were shed during their final farewells to a place so deeply loved. "Dear, dear Norland!" Marianne cried, wandering alone before the house on the last evening of their residence. "When shall I stop grieving for you? When will I feel at home anywhere else? Oh, happy home, could you know what pain I feel watching you from this distance, from where I may never return! And you, familiar trees—you will remain unchanged. Not a leaf will wither because we have gone, nor a branch freeze in silence while we watch you no more. You will stay the same, unaware of the joy or sorrow you cause, blind to the changes in those who once walked beneath your shade. But who will remain to cherish you?"
