We had walked for a long while across a barren moorland, where dry grasses and patches of moss clung to the rocky ground like remnants of forgotten life. The sun hung low in the sky, veiled by a thick gray cloud, casting no warmth, only a dim, solemn light. The wind cut through me with an unnatural chill, as if it came not from earth but from some distant, eternal night.

“Here we are at last!” said the raven.

“What a journey!” I replied. “It feels like we’ve crossed worlds.”

“In half the time,” he mused, “I could have flown to Paradise and visited my cousin—the one who never returned from Noah’s ark! Dear, dear, it’s almost winter already.”

“Winter?” I exclaimed. “It seems like just yesterday we left home!”

“That’s because we’ve moved so fast,” he said. “In your world, you’re bound by gravity—like a plumb-line that won’t let go. But here? Here the world spins beneath your feet, and time bends to will. And now—here is my wife’s cottage. She’s kind enough to let me live here, though I call it the sexton’s cottage.”

“But where is your churchyard?” I asked, scanning the endless heath. “Where are the graves?”

The raven stretched his neck, extended his beak horizontally, and slowly turned it in a full circle, silent and still. I followed the motion with my eyes—and then I understood.

There was no church. No tombstones. No walls. Only the wind sweeping over the endless plain, and beyond it, the low, starless horizon.

And yet—it *was* a cemetery. A vast, unbounded burial ground. Wherever the wind blew, there lay the dead. The entire heath was a graveyard. The raven was its keeper—its sexton—overseer of all things laid aside, all things ended. He ruled the quiet of existence itself.

I stood in the grave of the universe. Its boundaries were the open land, its wall the hazy edge of the sky. I had left behind spring, summer, autumn, and sunlight. I had come to winter—the season that waited for me. I had begun my journey in youth, and already, it seemed, I had reached its end.

But I was wrong. For this place held more than one season. Winter slept beneath the ice, wrapped in silence; Spring stirred at dawn, soft and childlike; Summer blazed at noon in radiant glory; and Autumn crept in during the slow fade of afternoon, dying softly into the misty, ghostly dark.

As we neared the cottage, the sun plunged down the western slope, vanishing before we reached the door. In that instant, cold seized me—not just air, but something solid, tangible, like a hand clutching my bones. I staggered forward, crossing the threshold as if escaping death itself.

A gust surged across the moor, slamming against the door just as I shut it. Then—silence. Complete stillness.

Inside, a single candle burned on a plain wooden table. Near the wall stood what looked like a coffin lid, propped upright. But it opened—revealing a door—and from it stepped a woman.

She wore white—pure, untouched white, like snow fresh fallen. Her face was pale, yet not cold. It glowed with a quiet warmth, as if light lived within her skin. Her features were flawless, but her eyes… her eyes drew every thought away from the rest. They were large, deep, dark—so dark they seemed to hold entire galaxies. Each pupil was a night sky, filled with stars that flickered and danced. Around them, the iris curled like twilight, eternal and serene.

Her gaze held not sorrow, nor joy—but presence. A living force. As if she were not merely seeing me, but *knowing* me. I felt exposed, yet safe. Her eyes were not human—they were ancient, divine. They must have come straight from God’s own vision.

“Here is Mr. Vane, wife,” said the raven.

“He is welcome,” she answered in a voice low, rich, and gentle—as if music had been buried deep inside her and now rose to the surface.

I stared, unable to speak.

“I knew you’d be glad to see him,” the raven added.

She stood near the doorway, unmoving, watching me. “Will he sleep?” she asked.

“I fear not,” the raven replied. “He is neither weary nor burdened.”

“Then why bring him?”

“I have my fears it may prove too soon.”

I hesitated, unease rising in my chest. “Surely a man must do a day’s work first!” I said.

She turned to the raven. “He has not yet learned that the day begins with sleep,” she said. “Tell him—he must rest before he can act.”

“Men,” the raven replied, “think so much of what they’ve done that they fall asleep upon it. They cannot empty an egg without turning into the shell and lying down.”

His words made me look again—at the raven. But this time, I saw not a bird.

I saw the librarian.

The same slender man in the worn black coat, broad-shouldered, tail long. I had seen only his back before. Now, for the first time, I saw his face.

It was thin, almost translucent, revealing the shape of the skull beneath. Yet it was alive—more alive than any face I’d ever seen. His pale blue eyes held depth, kindness, sorrow—a haze around them as if he had wept often. But they also sparkled with intelligence, warmth, and humor.

“You knew I wasn’t a raven,” he said, smiling.

“I knew you were Mr. Raven,” I replied. “But I thought you were a bird.”

“What made you think that?”

“You looked like a raven. I saw you dig worms from the soil with your beak.”

“And then?”

“You tossed them into the air.”

“And then?”

“They became butterflies—and flew away.”

“Have you ever seen a raven do that?” he asked.

“No,” I said. “But you did.”

“I told you—I am a sexton.”

“Does a sexton toss worms into the air and turn them into butterflies?”

“Yes.”

“I’ve never seen one do it.”

“You saw me do it.”

He paused. “And I am still your librarian. I was never dismissed. Never gave up the post. Now I serve here too.”

“But you said you were a sexton.”

“I am. It’s much the same work. Unless you are a true sexton, books are nothing but dead bodies. A library is merely a catacomb.”

“You bewilder me,” I said.

“That’s all right.”

For a moment, silence settled. The woman stood motionless, like a statue carved from moonlight, her eyes fixed on me.

Then the sexton spoke again. “Sometimes,” he said, “it’s more convenient to show your bird-self. Everyone—yes, everyone—has many selves: beast, bird, fish, serpent… even tree and crystal. All of them fight to rise to the surface. You can tell a man’s nature by which form appears most often.”

He turned to his wife. I studied him closely. He was taller than before, standing straighter, more composed. His face was pale, yes—but noble. His nose framed the beak that had retreated within; his lips were thin, colorless, yet curved with beauty. Around them, a faint smile quivered—full of love, pity, and quiet amusement.

“We need food and drink, wife,” he said. “We’ve traveled far.”

“You know,” she replied gently, “we can give only to those who ask.”

Her eyes turned to me—unblinking, luminous.

“Please give me something to eat, Mrs. Raven,” I said. “And something—anything—to quench my thirst.”

“Your thirst must grow greater before you receive what will truly quench it,” she said. “But what I can give, I will.”

She went to a cupboard in the wall, brought out bread and wine, and placed them on the table. We sat. The meal was simple—but perfect. As I ate, the hunger faded, replaced by a deeper sensation. The anxiety dissolved. Expectation took its place. My eyelids grew heavy. I felt weary for the first time.

“I haven’t earned this food or this sleep, Mrs. Raven,” I said. “But you’ve given me the one freely. May I now ask for the other? I need it.”

“Sleep,” said the sexton, “is too precious to be earned. It must be given and accepted. It is a necessity, not a reward.”

He paused. “But this house is not meant for passing travelers. Not for mere overnight rest.”

At that moment, a small black cat leapt onto his lap. He patted it softly—like one would soothe a child to sleep. To me, it looked like he was pressing earth over a grave—gently, lovingly, with a lullaby in his soul.

“There’s one of Mara’s kittens,” he said to his wife. “Will you feed it and put it out? She might want it.”

She took the kitten, gave it a piece of bread, and left with it, closing the door behind her.

“How, then, should I use your hospitality?” I asked.

“By accepting it fully,” he said.

“I don’t understand.”

“In this house,” he explained, “no one wakes on their own.”

“Why not?”

“Because no one, anywhere, ever wakes on their own. You cannot wake yourself any more than you can create yourself.”

“Then—will you or Mrs. Raven wake me?”

“We cannot.”

“Then how dare I sleep?” I asked, trembling.

“If you wish to rest here,” he said, “you must not worry about waking. You must sleep completely. Wholeheartedly. Without reservation.”

My heart sank.

He looked at me—his eyes saying, *Will you trust me?*

I met his gaze. “I will,” I whispered.

“Then come,” he said. “I’ll show you your bed.”

As we rose, the woman returned. She picked up the candle, turned toward the inner door, and led the way. I followed close behind her. The sexton came after, silent and watchful.
