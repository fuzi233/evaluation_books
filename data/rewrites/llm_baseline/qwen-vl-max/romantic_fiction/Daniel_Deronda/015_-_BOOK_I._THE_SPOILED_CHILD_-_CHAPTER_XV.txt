CHAPTER XV. “_Festina lente_—celerity should be tempered with caution.”—SIR THOMAS BROWNE.

Gwendolen had spent her time abroad caught in the heady rush of gambling, wrapped in the illusion that she was a queen of fortune, carrying with her from her recent experiences a vague belief that in this tangled world, nothing truly mattered—so long as one found pleasure. She had returned to England not by choice, but because circumstances had forced her hand, returning with a necklace she had pawned and which someone else had redeemed—a small, heavy token of her recklessness.

As she made her way home, Grandcourt was on his way to find her—but not with the urgency one might expect. He did not race from Diplow to Leubronn where she was believed to be. Instead, he moved slowly, drawn into the charm of idle pleasures, lingering at Baden-Baden to meet Russian acquaintances, making appointments that he would later break when the pull of reaching Leubronn became stronger. His passions were never fierce or sustained—they flickered like candle flames in a draft. Yet even without fire, life goes on: cravats are tied, dinners attended, speeches delivered with practiced ease, all without the spark of deep desire. A man can rise in society, appear cultivated, speak with authority on politics and science, and yet do so with minimal effort. He may seem stubborn, or sudden in action, but these impulses often stem not from strength, but from a lack of direction—like water without channels, spilling out in bursts or puddles.

Grandcourt had not been displeased by Gwendolen’s departure. In fact, it stirred something in him—something faintly thrilling. Her defiance pleased him. He liked to think it was born of anger over his cold behavior at Cardell Chase, an act that, upon reflection, seemed somewhat careless. To have brought her close to confessing her feelings, only to walk away himself, was enough to provoke any spirited young woman. And for her to resist him meant she had spirit—and spirit, he believed, was worth conquering.

He knew she expected him to pursue her. And he meant to. But for a full week, he took no steps toward leaving, nor did he ask where Miss Harleth had gone. Mr. Lush watched this silence with a mixture of triumph and unease. Grandcourt said nothing about her, his expression unreadable—like an alligator floating still beneath the surface. One could never predict what might stir in his slow-moving mind.

Yet the delay cost energy. The guests at Diplow grew more curious than their host. What had become of Miss Harleth? Could it be true that she had refused Mr. Grandcourt? Lady Flora Hollis, a vivacious woman with a sharp eye for gossip, decided to make a round of calls with Mrs. Torrington—visiting the rectory, Offendene, and Quetcham. She gathered not only news but also rumors: Gwendolen had gone to Leubronn with old friends, the Baron and Baroness von Langen. The initial shock and disappointment of Mrs. Davilow and the Gascoignes had settled into a quiet wish that Gwendolen’s absence not be seen as odd or shameful.

The rector, too, entertained hope—though not openly—that the marriage was merely delayed. Mrs. Davilow had not dared tell him how fiercely Gwendolen had spoken. Still, the rector clung to old maxims: Amaryllis fled, but wished her hiding place known; love, they say, will find its way over mountain and wave—even in this age of steam. Gwendolen, he thought, was an Amaryllis of sound sense and daring charm. The question was whether she had dared too far.

Lady Flora, returning full of news, saw no reason not to try to stir Grandcourt. At dinner, she mentioned the growing talk that he was now a disappointed suitor. Grandcourt listened quietly, but attentively. The next day, he ordered Lush to arrange a reasonable excuse to end the gathering at Diplow within a week—he intended to go yachting in the Baltic or elsewhere. It was impossible, he said, to remain at Diplow like a prisoner, surrounded by people he never wanted.

Lush understood immediately: Grandcourt was going to Leubronn. But he might move like a billiard ball rolling slowly across the table—hesitant, indirect. Lush’s plan was to make himself indispensable so he could accompany him. And he succeeded. Grandcourt found Gwendolen’s dislike of Lush amusing—only adding to his desire to keep him close.

Thus it was that Grandcourt arrived at the _Czarina_ five days after Gwendolen had left Leubronn, finding there his uncle, Sir Hugo Mallinger, with his family—including Deronda.

It is rarely pleasant when two men of influence, each occupied with their own affairs—one plagued by gout, the other by willfulness—find themselves unexpectedly in the same place. Sir Hugo was a man of easy temper, tolerant of others’ flaws, though certain differences grated on him more than others. The way Grandcourt viewed the family estate settlement troubled him deeply—more than church doctrine or political reform. Faults were less forgivable when they belonged to someone whose presence was inconvenient.

Grandcourt had never been the nephew Sir Hugo desired. But as presumptive heir to the Mallinger lands, he embodied the baronet’s deepest regret—the lack of a son to inherit. In a poorly planned inheritance arranged by Sir Francis, even Diplow, with its modest acreage, had been placed under the same conditions as the vast ancestral lands of the Toppings. Diplow had once been Sir Hugo’s home, where he hunted and lived through many seasons. It was meant to be a retreat for his wife and daughters after his death.

But years passed. Lady Mallinger had three daughters in quick succession, then eight years of silence—no son. Sir Hugo, nearly twenty years older, reached an age where hope began to fade, despite the fashionable delays of modern life. He had begun to accept that no son would come. And Grandcourt’s interest in the estates only made his presence more unwelcome. Yet, paradoxically, it also gave Sir Hugo a reason to maintain peace—because it opened the possibility of securing Diplow for his own family.

He had long considered offering Grandcourt a sum of money in exchange for relinquishing his claim to Diplow. If, against all odds, a son were born, the money would be wasted. But Sir Hugo saw this risk as negligible. He had managed his wealth wisely—through mines and leases—and was ready to spend.

So he avoided conflict. Years earlier, when improving the Abbey and needing Grandcourt’s approval to cut down timber, he had been relieved to find no real animosity in his nephew’s nature. Nothing since had worsened their relationship beyond polite distance.

Grandcourt, meanwhile, saw his uncle as a burden—a man who ought to be erased from existence. But through Lush, he had learned of Sir Hugo’s plans regarding Diplow—and this knowledge pleased him. Even if he never accepted the offer, the mere possibility of refusing it flattered his sense of power.

This understanding had influenced his request for a year’s tenancy of Diplow—an arrangement Sir Hugo reluctantly granted. He feared Grandcourt might grow attached to the land, especially with its excellent hunting. A man with two properties naturally seeks a third where the sport is better.

Lush had also hinted to Sir Hugo that Grandcourt might marry Miss Arrowpoint. If so, money might lose its appeal. So when they met unexpectedly at Leubronn, Sir Hugo was eager to learn what had transpired at Diplow. He intended to be civil, and hoped for a private word with Lush.

Between Deronda and Grandcourt existed a subtle, unspoken connection—its roots yet to be revealed. But on first meeting at the _table d’hôte_, no sign of tension emerged. Later, the four men walked together through the rooms. As they entered the large hall, Sir Hugo asked, “Did you play much at Baden, Grandcourt?”

“No,” replied Grandcourt. “I watched and betted a little with some Russians.”

“Had you luck?”

“What did I win, Lush?” Grandcourt asked.

“About two hundred,” said Lush.

“You’re not here for the gambling, then?” Sir Hugo inquired.

“No,” Grandcourt replied, brushing his whisker lightly. “I don’t care for it now. It’s a confounded strain.”

His diamond ring and calm demeanor drew glances from foreigners fascinated by the new English lord.

“The truth is,” said Sir Hugo, “someone should invent a machine to do amusements for us—like the Tartars get their prayers done. But I agree: gambling bores me. It knits the brain into tight meshes. Watching it knocks me out. I suppose one gets poisoned by the air. I never stay longer than ten minutes.”

Then he turned to Deronda. “Where’s your gambling beauty? Have you seen her lately?”

“She’s gone,” Deronda answered shortly.

“An extraordinary girl,” said Sir Hugo, turning back to Grandcourt. “A perfect Diana. Worth straining to look at. I saw her winning—she took it as coolly as if she’d known it all along. The same day, Deronda saw her losing wildly, and she bore it with great courage. I suppose she stopped in time—or was cleaned out. How do you know she’s gone?”

“Oh, by the Visitor-list,” said Deronda, barely shrugging. “Vandernoodt told me her name was Harleth, with the Baron and Baroness von Langen. I checked the list—Miss Harleth is no longer listed.”

Lush heard nothing new—except confirmation that Gwendolen had gambled. He had already noted her absence. But he had no intention of revealing it to Grandcourt until the moment was right. He waited—knowing the object of his search would eventually surface.

Now, however, Grandcourt had heard something intriguing. Not a word about Miss Harleth had escaped him.

After a pause, he asked Deronda, “Do you know those people—the Langens?”

“I’ve spoken with them a little since Miss Harleth left. I didn’t know them before.”

“Where has she gone? Do you know?”

“She’s gone home,” Deronda replied, coldly—then, suddenly, turned to face Grandcourt directly. “Though perhaps you know her. Her home is near Diplow—Offendene, near Winchester.”

Deronda’s gaze held Grandcourt’s—sharp, intense, alive. There was a richness in his features that startled on first glance, so vivid that people sometimes assumed he had spoken when he had not. Grandcourt felt a flicker of irritation—shown only in a slight twitch of his eyelid—at being looked at so directly without invitation.

But he answered, as usual, with a slow drawl: “Yes, I know her.”

Then he turned his shoulder, stepping toward the gaming tables.

“What of her?” Sir Hugo asked Lush, moving on with the others.

“She must be new to Offendene,” said Lush. “Old Blenny lived there after the dowager died.”

“A little too much of her,” Lush added, lowering his voice. “He was on the verge of marrying her. But I hope it’s off now. She’s a niece of the clergyman at Pennicote—Gascoigne. Her mother is a widow with several daughters. This girl has nothing—yet she’s dangerous as gunpowder. A foolish match. But she’s taken a sudden fancy against him—ran off here without notice, when he’d agreed to call the next day. The truth is, he’s here after her—but he wasn’t in a hurry. Between their whims, they may never reunite. Of course, he’s lost his chance with the heiress.”

Grandcourt joined them. “What a wretched place this is!” he muttered. “Worse than Baden. I’m going back to the hotel.”

When Sir Hugo and Deronda were alone, the baronet remarked, “Rather a fine tale. That girl has fire. She’s worth chasing—has _de l’imprévu_. I believe her appearance has improved my chances of getting Diplow, whether the marriage happens or not.”

“I hope such a marriage doesn’t happen,” Deronda said, with clear disdain.

“What! Are you touched by the sublime lash?” Sir Hugo asked, adjusting his glasses. “Are you tempted to run after her?”

“On the contrary,” said Deronda. “I’d rather run away.”

“You’d easily outshine Grandcourt,” Sir Hugo replied. “A spirited girl like her would see you as the finer match.”

“I suppose pedigree and land count for a fine match,” Deronda said, coldly.

“The best horse wins regardless of pedigree,” said Sir Hugo. “You remember Napoleon’s saying: _Je suis un ancêtre._”

“I’m not sure I want to be an ancestor,” Deronda replied. “It doesn’t seem the rarest kind of origin.”

“You won’t chase the pretty gambler, then?” Sir Hugo asked, putting down his glasses.

“Decidedly not.”

The answer was honest. Yet somewhere in Deronda’s mind, a quieter voice admitted that under different circumstances, he might have followed her curiosity. But his past had shaped him otherwise. He felt bound—not free.
