“So on those happy days of yore
Oft as I dare to dwell once more,
Still must I miss the friends so tried,
Whom Death has severed from my side.
But ever when true friendship binds,
Spirit it is that spirit finds;
In spirit then our bliss we found,
In spirit yet to them I’m bound.”

Margaret stood fully dressed and bonneted long before their arranged departure time, all her bags packed and waiting by the door. When no one was watching, she let quiet, unrushed tears slip down her cheeks; the moment any eye turned her way, she lifted her face with a bright, determined smile to hide how tight her chest felt. Her last lingering panic was that they would miss their train, but the day moved gentle as honey, and they reached the station with minutes to spare. She sank back against the carriage seat opposite Mr. Bell, breathing slow, happy, relieved breaths as the train whirled past familiar stations, bearing her south towards the land she had missed so fiercely for so long.

The old southern towns and hamlets lay drowsing in warm, clear sun, their terracotta tiled roofs glowing a deep russet far softer and warmer than the cold grey slate that roofed every building in Milton up north. Broods of plump pigeons circled the gables, drifting down to perch and ruffle their soft, iridescent feathers as if pressing every inch of themselves into the delicious golden warmth. The station platforms were nearly empty: everyone seemed too lazily, gently content to bother with travel, none of the sharp bustle and hurry Margaret had learned to expect on the busy northern railway lines. Later in the year, this route would be crowded with wealthy holiday-makers, but the constant throng of harried tradespeople rushing to and fro for work would never come here. A few idle men leaned against nearly every station wall, hands in their pockets, so absorbed in the simple act of watching the train that Margaret found herself wondering what on earth they would do for entertainment once the cars vanished, leaving nothing but empty railway track, a few sheds, and distant rolling fields behind. The hot air shimmered over the golden stillness of the countryside, each rolling farm passing by reminding her of German idylls—of Hermann and Dorothea, of Evangeline, quiet stories of home and hearth far from clanging factory mills.

She was jolted out of the waking dream when the conductor called out their stop: it was time to leave the train, and take the old horse-drawn fly the rest of the way to Helstone. Sharper, tangled feelings shot through her chest then, half pain, half joy, she could barely untangle the two. Every single mile of the road was thick with old associations she would not have traded for the world, and every memory made her ache for the days that were gone, that she could never get back. The last time she had driven this way was the day they left Helstone for Milton, her father and mother both beside her, the sky cold and grey, her own future hopeless and unknowable. Now she was alone, an orphan, and they had vanished from the face of the earth, never to walk these roads with her again. It hurt to see the Helstone road flooded with bright sunlight, every turn, every familiar oak and hawthorn tree standing in exactly the same summer glory they had shone in years before. Nature felt no grief, no change: it stayed forever young.

Mr. Bell guessed every line of what was passing through her head, and wisely, kindly, held his tongue. They drew up to the Lennard Arms, the old half-farmhouse half-inn that stood a little back from the road, as if to declare its owner did not need to fawn over travelers for custom—they would come seeking him out, if they knew what was good for them. The house faced the wide village green, and right out front stood an ancient lime tree, with benches nailed all the way around its trunk, and the grim, carved escutcheon of the long-gone Lennard family half hidden deep in its thick leafy boughs. The inn’s door stood propped wide open, but no one came rushing out to greet them. It was only after they had been standing in the dark parlour a few minutes that the landlady appeared, apologizing for her delay: haymaking season was in full swing, she had been busy packing baskets of provisions for the men working out in the fields, and had not heard their wheels rolling over the soft turf lane leading off the main road.

“Bless me!” she exclaimed, as a stray slant of sun fell full on Margaret’s face, half hidden before by shadow. “It’s Miss Hale! Jenny—Jenny come here at once, it’s Miss Hale!” She ran to the door to call her daughter, then hurried back to Margaret and shook her hands with the warm, motherly fondness of someone who had watched her grow up. “And how are you all? How’s the Vicar? How’s Dixon? We’ve never once stopped being sorry you all left us.”

Margaret tried to speak, to tell her of her father’s death. It was clear Mrs. Purkis already knew of her mother’s passing, for she did not speak of her. But the words stuck in her throat, and all she could do was brush a hand over the deep black of her mourning dress, and whisper one broken word: “Papa.”

“Surely, sir, it can’t be so,” Mrs. Purkis said, turning to Mr. Bell for confirmation, her face falling. “There was a gentleman came by last spring, even before that last winter, who told us Mrs. Hale was gone, poor lady—but he never said a word of the Vicar being unwell!”

“It is true, I’m afraid,” Mr. Bell said gently. “He died very suddenly, while he was staying with me in Oxford. He was a very good man, Mrs. Purkis, and any of us would be lucky to have as calm and gentle an end as he did. Margaret here is my goddaughter—her father was my oldest, dearest friend. We decided we would come down together to see the old places, and I knew well you’d give us comfortable rooms and a dinner worth eating. You don’t remember me, do you? My name’s Bell. A time or two when the parsonage was full to bursting, I slept right here, and tasted your famous home-brewed ale.”

“Of course! Forgive me, I was so taken aback seeing Miss Hale again,” Mrs. Purkis fussed. “Let me show you up to your room, Miss Margaret, you can take off your bonnet and wash your face. Only this very morning I stuck a handful of fresh musk roses head-down in the water jug, just thinking someone might come by, and there’s nothing sweeter than spring water scented with roses. To think the Vicar’s gone! Well, we all must go sometime—but that man who came by said he was picking right up, after the trouble of Mrs. Hale’s death.”

“Come down and find me after you’ve seen Margaret settled,” Mr. Bell said. “I want to talk to you about our dinner.”

The small casement window in Margaret’s bedroom was almost entirely framed by rose and vine branches. She pushed the foliage aside and leaned out, and could just see the tops of the old parsonage chimneys peeking above the treetops, every line of the roof and walls peeking familiar through the leaves.

“Times are changed, miss,” Mrs. Purkis chattered as she smoothed the linens on the bed, and sent Jenny off to fetch a stack of towels scented with lavender. “The new Vicar’s got seven children, and he’s building a new nursery right where your father’s old arbour and tool shed used to be. He’s put new grates in every room, and a big plate-glass window in the old drawing room. Him and his wife are busy, stirring folks, do a lot of what they call good, though if you ask me it’s turning the whole place upside down for no great purpose. The new Vicar’s a teetotaller, miss, and a magistrate. His wife’s got a hundred recipes for economical cooking, swears by making bread without yeast, and they both talk a mile a minute, all at the same time, so you can’t get a word in edgewise. He’ll go poking his nose in the haymakers’ cans to make sure no one’s drinking ale, and kick up a fuss if it’s not ginger beer—but I won’t have it. My mother and grandmother sent good malt liquor out to the hayfields before me, and I’ll go on their way, no matter what Mrs. Hepworth says about comfits being better than salts and senna for a cold. It’s nonsense, if you ask me. But I must run off, I’ll come back and hear all your news later.”

When Margaret came downstairs, Mr. Bell had a spread of fresh strawberries, thick clotted cream, a loaf of crusty brown bread and a jug of cold milk waiting for her, with a Stilton cheese and a bottle of port tucked off to the side for his own refreshment. After their light rustic lunch, they stepped outside for a walk, standing still for a moment uncertain which way to turn—every direction offered a dozen old, familiar paths to explore.

“Shall we head past the old vicarage first?” Mr. Bell asked.

“Not yet,” Margaret said. “We’ll go this way, take the long roundabout, and come back to it later.”

She spotted changes everywhere: old trees that had stood for decades cut down the previous autumn, rough little squatter cottages long rotting away now torn down entirely. She grieved for each one of them, as if they were old friends long gone. They passed the very spot where she and Henry Lennox had sat and sketched years before. The venerable lightning-scarred beech tree whose roots they had rested against was gone, the old man who lived in the ruinous cottage nearby was dead, the cottage itself pulled down and replaced with a spick-and-span new little house, with a neat vegetable patch where the old beech’s roots had once spread.

“I never realized how old I was,” Margaret said, after a long, quiet pause, and she turned away, sighing.

“It’s always the first changes to the familiar that make time feel like a strange, bewildering mystery to the young,” Mr. Bell said. “After a while, you learn to take all shifts as a matter of course. I know well that nothing human stays the same forever—but for you, it’s new, and heavy on the heart.”

“Let’s go see little Susan,” Margaret said, pulling him along a grassy lane that ran under the dappled shadow of a forest glade.

“I’d be delighted,” he laughed, “though I haven’t the faintest idea who little Susan is. But I’ve got a soft spot for all Susans, ever since I knew simple old Susan as a boy.”

“My little Susan was heartbroken when I left Helstone without stopping to say goodbye. It’s been weighing on my conscience ever since, that I hurt her when all it would have taken was a few extra minutes of my time. It’s a fair walk, though—are you sure you won’t tire?”

“Quite sure, so long as you don’t rush me. There’s no grand views here to give you an excuse to stop and catch your breath, after all. You’d think walking with a man ‘fat and scant o’ breath’ was terribly romantic if I were Hamlet, Prince of Denmark, wouldn’t you? Have compassion on my old legs for his sake.”

“I’ll walk slower for your own sake. I like you twenty times better than Hamlet, anyway.”

“On the old principle that a living ass is better than a dead lion?”

“Perhaps. I don’t bother analysing my feelings much these days.”

“Fair enough, I’ll take your liking for me without prying apart what it’s made of. Though we don’t need to crawl along at a snail’s pace, for heaven’s sake.”

The banter lightened Margaret’s mood for a while, even as they wandered further down the green lanes. Mr. Bell kept up the light, pointless chatter on purpose, to keep her from sinking too deep into sad old memories. She was grateful, though she almost would have preferred to walk these beloved paths in total silence, if not for the guilt of wishing she could make the whole journey alone.

They reached the cottage where Susan’s widowed mother lived, only to find Susan was not at home. She had gone off to the new parish school, and Margaret’s face fell.

“Oh, it’s quite all right,” Margaret said quickly, when the poor woman started apologizing. “I’m glad to hear it. I ought to have thought of it—she used to stay home with you, before.”

“Aye, she did, and I miss her something terrible. I used to teach her what little reading I knew at night, sitting by the fire. It wasn’t much, but she was getting such a handy little girl. Now she’s learned far more than I ever knew, all from the school.” The mother sighed.

“Hang it all,” Mr. Bell grumbled under his breath, “I’m a hundred years behind the times. I’d always thought a child got a far simpler, better education staying at home, helping her mother, and learning to read a chapter of the New Testament each night, than all the fancy schooling in the world.”

Margaret did not reply, not wanting to start an argument in front of the woman. Instead she asked, “How is old Betty Barnes doing these days?”

“I don’t know,” the woman said shortly. “We’re not speaking.”

“Why ever not?” Margaret asked—she had been the village’s little peacemaker, once upon a time, sorting out petty squabbles between neighbours.

“She stole my cat.”

“Did she know it was yours, do you think?”

“I reckon she didn’t.”

“Then why didn’t you ask her to give it back, once you told her it belonged to you?”

“Couldn’t. She’d burnt it.”

“Burnt it!” Margaret and Mr. Bell exclaimed in unison.

“Roasted it,” the woman clarified.

Margaret pressed her gently, bit by bit, until she drew out the full, horrible story. Betty Barnes had lent a gipsy fortune-teller her husband’s best Sunday suit, on the promise it would be returned to her on Saturday night before he noticed it was gone. When the gipsy never came back, Betty grew terrified of her husband’s rage. She’d heard an old savage country superstition that if you tortured a cat to death over the fire, its cries would force the powers of darkness to grant whatever wish you wanted. So she had grabbed the neighbour’s stray cat, and thrown it on the fire, to make the charm work. The woman telling the story had no sympathy for the suffering animal, only anger that her own cat had been chosen for the cruel sacrifice. She admitted it was wicked, but insisted everyone in the village knew the charm worked, and Betty had gotten her husband’s suit back the very next day. Margaret argued with her for ten minutes, trying to break her of the old pagan belief, but gave up in the end, sick at heart.

“You’re a good girl not to gloat over me,” Mr. Bell said, as they walked away.

“What do you mean?”

“I was dead wrong about schooling. Anything, anything at all, to stop little Susan growing up believing that kind of cruel, ignorant rubbish.”

“Oh! I remember,” Margaret said. “Poor little Susan. Would you mind stopping by the school to see her?”

“Not at all. I’m curious to see what kind of lessons they’re teaching the children, these days.”

They did not speak much more as they tramped through the shady, green dells. Even the soft sweet light of the southern woods could not erase the shock of the story they had just heard. Soon, the low murmur of dozens of voices, like the hum of a busy beehive, drifted up to them: they had stepped out of the trees onto the open village green, where the new schoolhouse stood. The door was propped wide open, and they walked in.

A brisk, sharp-eyed lady in black darted over to greet them, the very same air Margaret remembered her own mother putting on, soft and languid, when rare visitors dropped in to inspect the old Helstone school. This was Mrs. Hepworth, the new vicar’s wife. She instantly recognized Margaret, as did dozens of little faces in the classroom, half-muffled whispers of “That’s Miss Hale” rippling through the rows of desks.

“Your father, I presume, Miss Hale?” Mrs. Hepworth said, holding out a hand to Mr. Bell. “I can see the family resemblance. I’m so glad to meet you both!”

Margaret stammered out that Mr. Bell was not her father, that her own father had died suddenly, and Mrs. Hepworth’s manner turned immediately kind, though just a little patronizing, as if Margaret was a poor orphan to be pitied. She did not give Margaret time to recover, before gesturing to the front of the room.

“Ah, I see you miss taking a class, Miss Hale! First class, stand up—you’re doing a parsing lesson with Miss Hale today!”

Margaret was taken completely aback. Her visit was sentimental, not some formal inspection—but as she sat down at the front, she found herself looking at all the little familiar faces, dozens of children her own father had baptised, and all her awkwardness melted away. She slipped her hand over little Susan’s for a quiet minute unobserved, while Mrs. Hepworth dragged Mr. Bell over to a corner to explain the new phonetic reading system, chattering nonstop about her most recent conversation with the school inspector.

Margaret leaned over the children’s books, half lost in old memories of her father, until a little girl got stuck on the simplest word in the text, stammering over “a”.

“It’s an indefinite article,” Margaret said gently, by instinct.

“Oh, I beg your pardon!” Mrs. Hepworth said, popping over instantly. “Mr. Milsome, our inspector, teaches us to call ‘a’ an—who can tell?”

“An adjective absolute!” half a dozen children piped up at once. Margaret sat back, flustered and red-faced. The children knew more than she did. Mr. Bell turned away, snorting with quiet laughter.

She did not speak again for the rest of the lesson. When it was over, she wandered around the room to say hello to all her old little favourites, though they were fast growing up into young women, outgrowing the girlhood faces she remembered. When the school day ended, the summer afternoon was still young, and Mrs. Hepworth invited them back to the parsonage to see all the “alterations” the new vicar had made—she nearly said “improvements”, but caught herself in time.
