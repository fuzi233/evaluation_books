Rain dripped from the eaves of the temple, a relentless drumming that matched the restless beating of my own heart. It had been too long since I allowed myself the luxury of sleep, and so I had slumbered deeply under Nais's watchful eye. Night had fallen, heavy and wet, wrapping the world in a gray shroud. Through the narrow slits in the stone walls, I could see the campfires below, tended by figures in skins and rags who toasted themselves against the biting chill. Steam rose from their bodies, fighting a losing battle with the damp air. In such discomfort, folk become sharp, cruel, and short-tempered—a truth I observed as Nais frowned at the inclemency of the weather.

"A fine night," she murmured, her voice low. "I would have sent my lord back to the city hours ago, but the cold makes the blood sluggish. People sleep sourly tonight. We must wait for the hour when drugs and exhaustion render them docile."

Thus we sat, two shadows on a pavement untouched by worshippers for generations. Words were scarce, but the silence between us was a comfortable companion. Yet, as the hours bled away, the silence shattered. A harsh, raucous note began to blow on a horn or shell from the distance, growing louder, more insistent. Voices erupted from the fires, railing at the disturber. Soon, the noise coalesced around a single figure standing atop the pedestal of a war engine, addressing a throng that was rising from its uneasy slumber.

What he proclaimed, we could not hear clearly. Nais whispered that all who stayed awake were wearying themselves with this fellow, and I calculated that if I moved swiftly, I could slip away into the opposite darkness unseen. But I had miscalculated the nature of the mob. A dozen pairs of feet splashed through the mud toward the temple, shouting demands that Nais join the audience. They believed her tongue was eloquent enough to counterbalance the speaker, lest he drive wedges between their ranks.

"I must be dragged into this skirmish by the strangest of chances," I thought, feeling the tension coil tight. But Nais was firm. "There shall be no fight," she insisted, her resolve iron. "Come out with me. Stay distant from the light."

"But how will I explain my presence?"

"There is no need to explain," she said, bitterness edging her tone. "They will assume you are my lover. It is the custom here. Oh, why did the Gods not grant you a beard, curled and thick like theirs? Then you could have slipped away safely an hour ago."

"A smooth chin suits me better."

"So it does me," she murmured, leaning her weight against the heavy stone door and pushing it open. "Your chin." Outside, the ragged men—and the women among them—did not linger to inspect me. Coarse jokes flew, insults I could have met with steel, but they stepped back into the dark, casting glances to ensure we followed. Behind us, the pressure mounted. The entire camp was moving, black shadows pressing forward to hear the preacher. If we deviated, we would be spotted instantly.

So we pressed onward through the deluge, drawn inevitably toward the center of the storm.

At first, the preacher's words were indistinct, lost in the roar of the crowd. *"Who are your chiefs?"* the question boomed, ending a fervid harangue. Immediately, rational discourse drowned in uproar. *"We have no chiefs!"* the multitude roared. *"We are done with chiefs! We are all equal here. Take away your silly magic. You may kill us, but you shall not rule us. Not the priests, not Phorenice, not anyone. We are done with rulers."*

The press of bodies closed in around the man on the war engine. I saw him clearly now: an old man with white hair tumbling over his shoulders and a long, untrimmed beard. His body was bare save for a wisp of rag around his loins, glistening wet in the rain. But in his hand, he held the mark of his caste—a wand tipped with unconsumed fire. It glowed and twinkled like a fallen star, bathing his naked form in a halo of divine light. It was the Symbol of Our Lord the Sun, a credential forged by no mortal hand, recognized by all civilized men as absolute proof of authority.

Even the frantic, ragged crew did not question his lineage. They acknowledged his power as the Clan of Priests, the ancient governors of the land. *"You may kill us with your magic!"* they screamed, yet stubbornly refused to return to their allegiance. *"We have suffered too much!"* they cried. *"We are done with rulers forever."*

Yet, looking at the old man, I felt a churning emotion I could not name. This was Zaemon, father to Nais. Yesterday, I had sat at Phorenice's elbow while he presided; today, he stood here denouncing me as Deucalion, the traitor. These rebels had spent a navy trying to kill me four days prior. If they knew I was here, Nais's cold logic would be drowned in the chaos. The High Gods kept their secrets well, but I felt the thread binding my life to the earth drawing dangerously close to snapping.

Nais saw this too. She twined her fingers in mine, pressing them as a silent farewell. I squeezed her hand back, mourning the loss of her company. Yet, a grim gloating twisted in my gut. Before these unskilled rebels tore me down, I intended to build a mound of the dead. Of all the ferments in the human heart, the joy of strife was the keenest, and facing what I knew would be my final battle, I felt the lust for combat etched into my bones.

For the moment, Zaemon was lost in his outcry, blind to me. I was free to listen, to gauge the effect of his burning words. His theme was old, familiar. He spoke of the Gods setting aside one clan to rule, of justice and protection, of fighting invaders and predicting earthquakes. He spoke of Atlantis reaching its pinnacle under the Priestly Clan.

*"But,"* the old man cried, waving the Symbol until his wet skin glistened, *"the people grew fat and careless. They thought their fortune was earned by their own puny brains, not a gift from Above. The cult was neglected, temples barren. Punishment followed. The Gods decreed that a wife of a Governor should find a woman-child by the roadside and adopt it. That child, fashioned by infinite wisdom, became a scourge for Atlantis. You who feel the weight of Phorenice's hand know the completeness with which the High Gods craft their instruments."*

*"Yet,"* he continued, *"those who shall debase Phorenice are appointed now. The old rule returns, but not until you are chastened enough to cry for relief."* He waved the glowing Symbol. *"Are the Gods to throw down this woman who has scorned Them? Or will you be purged of your pride before you are ripe for deliverance?"*

He broke off, his ragged beard sinking onto his chest. A young man, skin-clad and armed, shoved through the crowd to jump onto the platform beside him. *"Hear me, brethren!"* he bellowed. *"We are done with tyrants. Death comes, and we fear it little. But we will not own rulers again. That is our final say."* Turning to the old man, he added bravely, *"I know you can kill me by magic if you wish, but I have spoken, and I stand the cost."*

*"I can kill you, but I will not,"* said Zaemon. *"You have spoken your silliness. Go to the ground."*

*"Aye, but you will,"* the old man snapped, his brows tightening. No blow was struck, the Symbol did not lift, yet the young man found himself stricken with sudden, profound dumbness. A spasm of fear seized him, and he scrambled back, fleeing whence he had come. The crowd thrilled, expecting a miracle to sway them back.

The old man glowered, furious. *"You dogs! Empty-witted brutes! Do you demand I degrade the Higher Mysteries into a mummer's show? Do you seek to be tempted back? Come in humility, and I shall declare pardon. Remain stubborn, and the scourge, Phorenice, will torment you into annihilation before she answers for her evils!"*

Voices rose again, a tumult of anger and weapons raised. The faction for independence outnumbered the others, fueled by a lust for liberty that weariness could not quell. *"Freedom of speech"* was their banner, and I, with a sly malice, could not resist whispering a cynical word to Nais. But she clutched my hand, imploring silence. *"Be silent, my lord,"* she hissed. *"Or they will tear you to pieces. They are on fire for mischief."*

*"Yet a few hours back, you plotted to kill me,"* I reminded her.

She turned, her eyes hot. *"A woman changes her mind, my lord. But it becomes you little to remind me of my fickleness."*

Beside me, a man in the press wheeled around, staring searchingly into the dark. *"A shaved chin!"* he exclaimed. *"Who are you, friend, that you cut a beard instead of curling it? I see no wounds on your face."
