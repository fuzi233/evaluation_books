March had arrived, bringing with it the heavy, expectant air of a city holding its breath. A month after the Carnival's chaotic revelry, Tito descended the marble steps of the Old Palace, his destination San Marco, yet his path was deliberately indirect. He chose to loop through the Piazza di Santa Croce, drawn by the swelling tide of people emerging from the great church following the early Lenten sermon.

Inside Santa Croce, the atmosphere had grown feverish. Fra Girolamo Savonarola's voice, once the thunder of the city, had been silenced in his own church of San Marco by a hostile Signoria obeying a papal threat of interdict. Yet, in Santa Croce, the hunger for his words had only intensified, now fueled by the spectacle of his vilification. The crowd's excitement peaked at a daring proposal: a Franciscan preacher, Fra Francesco di Puglia, had challenged Savonarola to walk through fire. The logic was simple and terrifying; if the prophet emerged unscathed, his divine mandate was proven; if consumed, his falsehood exposed. To ensure the test could not be evaded, the Franciscan offered himself as the necessary counterweight, willing to be burned alongside the prophet.

Savonarola, as usual, ignored the pulpit attacks. But the challenge had ignited a spark in Fra Domenico, a fervent follower preaching to women in the Via del Cocomero. He had seized the gauntlet, declaring his readiness to share the flames for his master. The populace, eager for a shortcut to conviction, watched with bated breath. Yet, Savonarola, keenly aware that mere discussion could be dangerous, commanded Fra Domenico to withdraw. The Franciscan accepted, noting he had challenged the prophet directly, not a subordinate.

By the time Tito reached the piazza, the crowd was thick, converging not on the church doors but near the Franciscan monastery. There, pinned to a wall by order of the Signoria, was a large placard covered in official Latin script. Curiosity gnawed at the onlookers; while they knew the gist of the decree, the written word remained a barrier of learned tongues. They whispered among themselves, trying to decipher the government's intent.

"It is the Frate's doctrines he must prove by burning," declared Goro, a prominent figure in the throng. "The Signoria has made it plain."

"Nay, Goro," corrected a sleek shopkeeper with pity. "The Frate must prove his doctrines by *not* being burned. He walks through the fire and emerges whole."

"Yes, but Fra Girolamo objects," interjected a young sculptor, tipping his white-streaked cap. "Being whole already, he sees no need to walk through fire to remain so. He leaves such odds and ends to Fra Domenico."

"Then he flinches like a coward!" Goro wheezed. "Remember the Carnival? He had us watching lightning strike him, and nothing happened."

"Silence your bleating," snapped a tall shoemaker, his shoulders laden with slippers. "You are as wise as a calf with water on its brain. The Frate will flinch from nothing. Perhaps he says nothing beforehand, but when the moment comes, he walks through without asking a grey-frock to accompany him. Though, I would give a shoestring to know what this Latin actually says."

"There is so much of it," sighed the shopkeeper. "Is there no scholar here?"

The heads turned in unison as Tito approached, his black mantle and gold fibula catching the light, his velvet cap marking him as a man of high station.

"Here is one," the sculptor smiled, raising his cap.

"He is the secretary of the Ten," the shopkeeper announced, bowing deeply—a rare honor for a Florentine. "Make way."

Tito acknowledged the deference with a graceful nod. "Thanks, my friend. I merely wished, as you did, to see what is at the foot of this placard. Ah, as I expected. The government permits anyone to subscribe their name as a candidate to enter the fire—an act of liberality worthy of the magnificent Signoria—reserving, of course, their right to select. Surely many believers are eager to sign."

He paused, lifting his shoulders with an oratorical flourish. "What is it to enter the fire to one whose faith is firm? A man fears fire because he believes it burns him; but if he believes the contrary?" He scanned the faces around him. "For which reason, I have never disbelieved the Frate when he said he would enter the fire to prove his doctrine. In his place, if you believed the fire would not burn you, which of you would not step in as readily as you would walk along the dry bed of the Mugnone?"

The crowd shifted, their eyes brightening like dogs scenting something pungent. The abstract debate was becoming practical.

"Then, Messer Segretario," the sculptor pressed, "it seems Fra Francesco is the greater hero, offering to enter for the truth, certain the fire will burn him."

"I do not deny it," Tito replied blandly. "But if Fra Francesco is mistaken, he will be burned for the wrong side, and the Church has never counted such victims as martyrs. We must suspend judgment until the trial truly takes place."

"True enough," the shopkeeper murmured with impatience. "But will you interpret the Latin for us?"

"Assuredly," Tito smiled. "It expresses the doctrines the Frate teaches, which the trial will prove true or false. First, that Florence—"

"Bit by bit, then, and the meaning," the shoemaker insisted.

"Willingly," Tito agreed. "_Ecclesia Dei indiget renovatione_: The Church of God needs purifying or regenerating. That means the priests must lead better lives; no miracle is needed for that. _Flagellabitur_: It will be scourged. _Renovabitur_: It will be purified. _Florentia quoque post flagellam renovabitur et prosperabitur_: Florence also, after the scourging, shall be purified and shall prosper."

"That means we get Pisa again," the shopkeeper declared.

"And the wool from England as we used to," added an elderly man in a berretta. "There has been enough scourging with the sinking of our trade."

Just then, a tall figure topped with a red feather emerged from the convent door, exchanging a casual glance with Tito before the bystanders timidly parted to make way for Messer Dolfo Spini.

"_Infideles convertentur ad Christum_: The infidels shall be converted to Christ," Tito continued. "Those are the Turks and Moors. Well, I have nothing against that."

"_Haec autem omnia erunt temporibus nostris_: And all these things shall happen in our times."

"Why, what use would they be else?" Goro asked.

"_Excommunicato nuper lata contra Reverendum Patrem nostrum Fratrem Hieronymum nulla est_: The excommunication lately pronounced against our reverend father, Fra Girolamo, is null. _Non observantes eam non peccant_: Those who disregard it are not committing a sin."

"I shall know better what to say to that when we have the Trial by Fire," the shopkeeper noted.

"Which doubtless will clear up everything," Tito said. "That is all the Latin—the conclusions to be tested. The rest is simply a proclamation in good Tuscan, calling on those eager to walk through the fire to come to the Palazzo and subscribe their names."

With an easy, natural bow, Tito turned and quickened his pace. After a few turns, he paused before a quiet door, knocking with a light, peculiar rhythm. A young woman opened it, and he chucked her under the chin before passing through an ajar inner door into a handsome, untidy room.

Dolfo Spini sat there, playing with a fine stag-hound that alternately sniffed a basket of pups and licked his hands with affectionate disregard for her master's morals. Spini looked up briefly as Tito entered, then resumed his play, lost in the persistence of an irrelevant action.

"A handsome *bracca* that," Tito said quietly, standing with his thumbs in his belt. "When you have finished such caresses as cannot possibly be deferred, my Dolfo, we will talk of business. My time, which I could wish to be eternity at your service, is not entirely my own this morning."

"Down, Mischief, down!" Spini snapped, pushing the dog aside with sudden roughness. "Malediction!" He stood close, placing a hand on Tito's shoulder. "I hope your sharp wits see all the ins and outs of this business, my fine necromancer, for it seems to me no clearer than the bottom of a sack."

"These accursed Frati Minori at Santa Croce are drawing back," Spini complained. "Fra Francesco himself seems afraid of sticking to the challenge. He talks of the Prophet using magic to create a false miracle, thinks he might be burned while the Prophet emerges whole by sorcery, leaving the Church no better. And after all our talking, there isn't even a blessed lay brother willing to pair with that pious sheep, Fra Domenico."

"It is the peculiar stupidity of the tonsured skull that prevents them from seeing how little consequence it is whether they are burned or not," Tito said calmly. "Have you sworn them no danger of entering the fire?"

"No," Spini looked puzzled. "Because one of them will be obliged to go in with Fra Domenico, who thinks it a thousand years till the fagots are ready."

"Not at all. Fra Domenico himself is unlikely to go in. I have told you, my Dolfo, that now you have the Signoria taking up this affair to prevent Fra Girolamo from hushing it up, nothing is required but that on a given day the fuel be prepared in the Piazza and the people gathered expecting a prodigy. If, after that, the Prophet quits the Piazza without a miracle, he is ruined. The people will pelt him out of the city; the Signoria will banish him; and His Holiness may do as he pleases. Therefore, my Alcibiades, swear to the Franciscans that their grey-frocks shall not come within singeing distance of the fire."

Spini rubbed the back of his head and tapped his sword against his leg, stimulating his mind to grasp the intangible. "But unless we fall on him in the Piazza when the people are in a rage and make an end of him and his lies then and there, Valori and the Salviati and the Albizzi will take up arms for him. I know this was talked of on Ascension Sunday. The people may turn again; there may be a story of the French king returning, or some other cursed chance in the hypocrite's favor. The city will never be safe till he is out of it."

"He *will* be out of it before long, without your giving yourself further trouble than this little comedy of the Trial by Fire," Tito assured him. "The wine and the sun will make vinegar without any shouting to help them, as your Florentine sages would say. You will have the satisfaction of delivering your city from an incubus by an able stratagem, instead of risking blunders with sword-thrusts."

"But suppose he *did* get magic and the devil to help him and walk through the fire after all?" Spini grimaced, hiding a shyness in treading this speculative ground. "How do you know there is nothing in those things? Plenty of scholars believe in them, and this Frate is bad enough for anything."

"Oh, of course there are such things," Tito shrugged. "But I have particular reasons for knowing the Frate is not on such terms with the devil as can give him confidence in this affair. The only magic he relies on is his own ability."

"Ability!" Spini scoffed. "Do you call it ability to set Florence at loggerheads with the Pope and all the powers of Italy—all to keep beckoning at the French king who never comes? You may call him able, but I call him a hypocrite who wants to be master of everybody and get himself made Pope."

"You judge with your usual penetration, my captain, but our opinions do not clash," Tito said with bland gravity. "The Frate, wanting to be master and carrying out his projects against the Pope, requires the lever of a foreign power and Florence as a fulcrum. I used to think him a narrow-minded bigot, but now I think him a shrewd ambitious man who knows what he aims at and directs his aim as skilfully as you direct a ball when playing at *maglio*."

"Yes, yes," Spini nodded cordially. "I can aim a ball."

"It is true," Tito said. "And I should not have troubled you with my trivial remark on the Frate's ability, but that you may see how this will heighten the credit of your success against him at Rome and Milan, which is sure to serve you in good stead when the city changes its policy."

"Well, thou art a good little demon, and shalt have good pay," Spini said patronizingly. The Greek adventurer smiled with gratification. "Of course, any advantage to me depends entirely on your—"

"We shall have our supper at my palace tonight," Spini interrupted, nodding significantly and patting Tito's shoulder affectionately. "And I shall expound the new scheme to them all."

"Pardon, my magnificent patron," Tito said. "The scheme has been the same from the first—it has never varied except in your memory. Are you sure you have a fast hold of it now?"

Spini rehearsed the plan.

"One thing more," he said as Tito hastened away. "There is that sharp-nosed notary, Ser Ceccone; he has been handy of late. Tell me, you who can see a man wink when you are behind him, do you think I may go on making use of him?"

Tito dared not say "No." He knew his companion too well to trust him with advice when all Spini's vanity and self-interest were not engaged in concealing the adviser.

"Doubtless," he answered promptly. "I have nothing to say against Ceccone."
