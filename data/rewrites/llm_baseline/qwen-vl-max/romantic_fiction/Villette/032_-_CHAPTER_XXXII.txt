Where, then, was Paulina Mary? And how had my connection with the elegant Hôtel Crécy fared in her absence? For a time, it had been severed—M. and Miss de Bassompierre had journeyed abroad, dividing their weeks between the cities and countryside of France. Fate brought news of their return just days after they arrived. I was strolling one soft afternoon along a quiet boulevard, moving slowly under the gentle April sun, lost in thoughts that were not unpleasing, when I saw ahead a group of riders paused as if they had met by chance, exchanging greetings on the broad, smooth path lined with linden trees. On one side stood a middle-aged gentleman and a young woman; on the other—a young man of striking appearance. The lady’s bearing was graceful, her attire refined, her whole presence delicate and dignified. Yet, as I drew closer, I recognized them all: Count Home de Bassompierre, his daughter Paulina, and Dr. Graham Bretton.

How animated Graham looked! His expression held warmth, sincerity, and a quiet joy—yet there was also a restraint, a depth that spoke of deeper feeling beneath. This was the moment, this alignment of circumstance, that could captivate and hold a man like Dr. John. He admired Paulina not merely for her beauty or charm, but for the fullness of her setting—the grace of her upbringing, the polish of her world, the weight of her family's name. Had he seen her alone, unattended, dressed plainly, perhaps working in some modest role, he might have found her pleasing, even lovely—but not so moved. But here she was, framed by refinement, protected by rank, adorned by fashion and fortune. These were the conditions he required before surrendering his heart. And now, proud yet trembling, he bowed to her as though to a queen.

As for Paulina, her eyes held a soft, almost unconscious smile—not one of triumph, but of tender feeling. They parted. He rode past me swiftly, barely touching the ground, oblivious to everything around him. He looked radiant—his spirit stirred, his purpose awakened.

“Papa, there is Lucy!” rang out a cheerful voice. “Lucy, dear Lucy—do come here!”

I hurried toward her. She lifted her veil and leaned from her saddle to kiss me.

“I was planning to visit you tomorrow,” she said. “But now, tomorrow you’ll come to see me.” She named the hour, and I promised.

The next evening, I found myself in her private room—just the two of us. It had been some time since I’d last seen her, since the occasion when her worth had been weighed against Ginevra Fanshawe’s and clearly proven superior. Now she had much to tell me about her travels.

She was quick and lively in conversation, vivid in her descriptions—yet her speech remained natural, unhurried, her voice clear and gentle. She never seemed to rush or overstep. I listened intently, though eventually, she seemed to grow restless, needing a shift in subject. Her tale ended abruptly.

Then, in a voice both hesitant and pleading, she turned to me.

“Lucy—”

“Yes, I am here.”

“Is your cousin Ginevra still at Madame Beck’s?”

“She is still there. You must be eager to see her.”

“No—not really.”

“You want to invite her for another evening?”

“No… I suppose she still talks about marriage?”

“Not to anyone you care about.”

“But surely she still thinks of Dr. Bretton? She hasn’t changed her mind, has she? It was so settled two months ago.”

“Well, you know—it doesn’t matter. You saw how things stood between them.”

“There was a little misunderstanding that night, certainly. Does she seem unhappy?”

“Not at all. Let’s change the subject. Have you heard anything from Graham during your absence?”

“Papa received letters from him—about business, I believe. He took charge of an affair while we were away. Dr. Bretton seems to respect papa and enjoys helping him.”

“Yes. You met him yesterday on the boulevard—you could judge from his look whether his friends need worry about his health?”

“Papa thought the same. I couldn’t help smiling. He’s not especially observant—he often sees beyond what’s before him. But as Dr. Bretton rode off, he murmured, ‘Really, it does a man good to see the spirit and energy of that boy.’ He called him a boy—I think he nearly believes it, just as he thinks of me as a child. He wasn’t speaking to me, just muttering to himself.”

“Lucy…” Her voice dropped again, soft and searching. At once, she rose from her chair and sat beside me, on the stool at my feet.

I liked her. Not a sentiment I’ve often voiced about others in this story—but I will say it now. Close acquaintance revealed only gentleness, intelligence, and truth in Paulina. My regard for her ran deep. Perhaps admiration more superficial would have shown itself more boldly—but mine was quiet, steady.

“What do you wish to ask me?” I said. “Be brave—speak plainly.”

But courage did not light her eyes. When they met mine, they fell. There was no flush on her cheeks—no fleeting blush, but a deeper warmth rising within, coloring her skin, warming her breath.

“Lucy,” she whispered, “I truly want to know what you think of Dr. Bretton. Will you give me your honest opinion—of his character, his nature?”

“His character stands high—and rightly so.”

“And his disposition? Tell me about that,” she pressed. “You know him well.”

“I know him fairly well.”

“You’ve seen him at home—with his mother. Speak of him as a son.”

“He is a kind and devoted son—his mother’s comfort, her hope, her pride.”

She clasped my hand gently, stroking it with each word.

“And in other ways—is he good?”

“Dr. Bretton is compassionate—kind-hearted toward all humanity. He would show mercy even to the lowest savage or the worst criminal.”

“I heard some of papa’s friends say the same. They said many poor patients at the hospitals, who fear cold-hearted surgeons, welcome him.”

“They are right. I’ve seen it myself. Once, he took me through a hospital—I saw how they greeted him. Your father’s friends speak truly.”

A soft gratitude lit her eyes as she raised them briefly. She had more to say—but hesitated, as if waiting for the right moment, the right darkness.

Twilight was falling. The fire in her parlor glowed with dusky warmth. I sensed she wished the room dimmer, the hour later.

“How peaceful and private we feel here,” I remarked, to ease her.

“We do. Yes—it’s a quiet evening. I won’t be summoned down for tea. Papa is dining out.”

Still holding my hand, she played with my fingers unconsciously—wearing her own rings upon them, winding strands of her hair around them, pressing my palm to her warm cheek. Then, clearing her voice—soft and liquid as a bird’s song—she began:

“You must think it strange that I talk so much about Dr. Bret remember, but—”

“Not at all strange. Quite natural. You care for him.”

“And if I did,” she said quickly, “is that reason enough to speak? Do you think me weak—like my cousin Ginevra?”

“If I thought you even slightly like Madame Ginevra, I wouldn’t sit here waiting for your words. I’d rise, walk about, and deliver a lecture instead. Go on.”

“I intend to go on,” she retorted. “What else do you suppose I mean to do?” And she looked at me—little Polly of Bretton—petulant, sensitive.

“If,” she said firmly, “if I loved Dr. John so deeply I could die for it, that alone wouldn’t justify me speaking—wouldn’t let me utter a word. I’d remain silent—as silent as the grave—as silent as you, Lucy Snowe. You know it. And you know you’d despise me if I failed to control myself, if I whined about a feeling that was all on my side.”

“It is true,” I replied, “that I hold little regard for women who boast of their feelings—or mourn them loudly. But as for you, Paulina—speak. I want to hear every word you wish to say. Say all that brings you relief or joy. That is all I ask.”

“Do you care for me, Lucy?”

“Yes, I do, Paulina.”

“And I love you. Even as a small, troublesome girl, I felt content being near you. It was delightful to pour out my mischief and whims upon you. Now you are precious to me—I like to talk with you, trust you. So listen.”

She settled back, resting gently against my arm—light, unburdening, unlike the heavy, selfish weight of Mistress Fanshawe.

“A few minutes ago, you asked if we’d heard from Graham during our trip. I said there were two letters for papa—business matters. That was true. But I didn’t tell you everything.”

“You evaded.”

“I shuffled, I lied a little. But now I’ll tell the truth. It’s growing dark—perfect for talking. Papa often lets me open the mail and sort it. One morning, about three weeks ago, I found, among a dozen letters for M. de Bassompierre, a note addressed to *Miss* de Bassompierre. I spotted it instantly—the handwriting was familiar, yet striking. I was about to say, ‘Papa, another letter from Dr. Bretton,’ but the ‘Miss’ froze me into silence.

I had never received a letter from a gentleman before. Should I have shown it to papa? Let him read it first? I couldn’t bear the thought. I know papa’s view of me—he forgets my age, treats me like a schoolgirl. He doesn’t realize others see me as grown, tall, mature. So, caught between shame and excitement, I gave papa his twelve letters—his herd—and kept my one, my single lamb.

It lay in my lap during breakfast, staring up at me with a meaning I couldn’t understand. I felt myself split—still a child to him, but no longer one to myself.

After breakfast, I carried it upstairs, locked the door, and studied its outside. It took minutes to overcome the address, to break the seal. One doesn’t storm such a fortress at once—one sits before it, as besiegers do.

Graham’s handwriting is like himself, Lucy—clear, firm, rounded. No messy wax splash—just a full, solid drop, a clean, pleasant impression. No harsh angles—only smooth, soothing script. Like his face—like the chisel of his features. Do you know his autograph?”

“I’ve seen it. Continue.”

“The seal was too beautiful to break. So I cut it carefully with scissors. On the verge of reading, I hesitated again. It was too soon to drink that draught—the sparkle in the cup was so bright. I wanted to watch it a moment longer.

Then I remembered—I hadn’t said my prayers that morning. I’d rushed to join papa for breakfast, fearing to keep him waiting. Some might say I should have served God first. But I don’t believe heaven would envy me my duty to papa. I believe I’m superstitious.

Now, a voice seemed to whisper: another feeling is at stake—pray before you read. Deny yourself a moment, remember your duty.

I’ve had such impulses all my life. I put the letter down and prayed—adding, at the end, a fervent plea that whatever happened, I might never cause papa sorrow, nor neglect him in caring for others.

The very thought pierced my heart—I wept.

Yet still, Lucy, I knew that one day papa would have to learn the truth—be guided, persuaded, taught to accept it.”

She paused. Then softly: “I read the letter. Life is said to be full of disappointment. I was not disappointed. Before I read, and as I read, my heart did not just beat—it trembled, fast, like an animal drinking at a well. And the well was full—gloriously clear. It rose up of its own accord. I saw the sun through its flow—no mote, no moss, no insect—only pure, golden water, thrice-refined.”

She continued: “Life is said to bring pain to some. I’ve read biographies where the traveler passed from suffering to suffering, where Hope flew ahead, never staying long enough to be grasped. I’ve read of those who sowed in tears, whose harvest perished by blight or swept away by wind—and died in winter, empty-handed.”

“Was it their fault?” I asked.

“Not always. Some were good, striving people. I am not striving, nor actively good. Yet God has nurtured me—sun, moisture, protection—by my dear father. And now—now—another comes. Graham loves me.”

We both fell silent for a moment.

“Does your father know?” I asked quietly.

“Graham spoke of papa with great respect—but said he dared not approach him yet. He must first prove himself. He added that he needed some understanding of my own feelings before taking any step elsewhere.”

“And you replied?”

“I answered briefly—but not coldly. I feared my reply might be too warm—Graham is so exacting in taste. I wrote it three times—trimming, softening the words each time. At last, having shaped it until it seemed like a piece of ice touched with just a hint of fruit or sugar, I sealed and sent it.”

“Excellent, Paulina. Your instinct is perfect. You understand Dr. Bretton.”

“But how shall I handle papa? That is still my pain.”

“Don’t manage. Wait. Only refrain from writing again until your father knows and gives his consent.”

“Will he ever give it?”

“Time will tell. Wait.”

“Dr. Bretton wrote again—deeply grateful for my calm reply. But I anticipated your advice. I told him that while my feelings remained unchanged, I could not write again without my father’s knowledge.”

“You acted as you should. He will respect you for it. It will deepen his pride in you, his love—if it can grow further. Paulina, that gentle frost surrounding such pure flame—that is a rare gift of nature.”

“I feel Graham’s disposition,” she said. “I sense that no delicacy is too fine for his treatment.”

“You understand him perfectly. And even if he were one who expected greater boldness—you would still act with truth, openness, tenderness, toward your father.”

“Lucy, I trust I always will. Oh, it will hurt to wake papa from his dream—to tell him I am no longer a child!”

“Do not hurry. Leave it to Time and your kind fate. I’ve noticed her care for you—doubt not she will order the moment wisely. Yes—I’ve reflected on your life, as you have. We cannot foresee the future—but the past has been favorable.

As a child, I feared for you. Nothing alive was ever more sensitive than your nature. Under harshness or neglect, neither your body nor soul would have matured as they have. Pain, fear, struggle—these would have marred your features, frayed your nerves, stolen your health, your cheer, your grace. But Providence watched over you—not only for your sake, but for Graham’s. His star, too, was fortunate. To unfold fully, he needed a companion like you. And here you are—ready. You must be joined. I knew it the first day I saw you together at La Terrasse.

In all that concerns you both, I see promise, harmony, plan. I do not think the sunny youth of either will lead to stormy old age. I believe it is meant that you live in peace, be happy—not as angels, but as few mortals are. Some lives are blessed thus—it is God’s will. A trace of Eden remains. Others take a different path. Their journey meets fitful winds, sudden storms, early winter nights. But none of this happens without divine sanction. And I know somewhere, in His vast works, lies the justice of that fate—and the mercy that proves it.”
