The iron clang of the sponging-house door echoed through the damp corridor, a brutal industrial dissonance that fractured the delicate acoustic ecology of Harry Warrington’s morning. After a second night confined within these sterile walls, his anger had settled into a hard, protective shell. He longed for the symphonic rustle of the Virginia woods, the rhythmic, ancestral cadence of the maize-mortar pounding out the heartbeat of his family's agrarian estate. In this cold, transactional country, friendship was treated as a mere commodity, utterly devoid of the profound meaning found in shared sustenance. He would leave it behind. He would retreat to the mythic, loamy soils of his mother’s tobacco farms, bury his hands in the earth, and listen to the circular breathing of the harvest seasons rather than the hollow noise of English fine gentlemen.

Gumbo’s arrival broke the auditory gloom. The faithful servant brought not merely letters from Bond Street, but the faint, lingering olfactory ghost of woodsmoke woven into his coat—a sensory bridge across oceans that grounded Harry. 

“I wanted to bring some more clothes,” Gumbo murmured, his native dialect carrying a melodic resonance that defied the sharp, dominant lexicon of London's streets. “But Mr. Ruff, the landlord, he wouldn't let me bring no more.”

Harry tore open the letters. They were bills. The language of debt, sterile and punitive. A fourth note from the landlord demanded immediate payment, treating human loyalty as mere collateral by threatening to evict Gumbo. Gumbo, however, revealed he had found refuge with Colonel Lambert's household. When Gumbo spoke, his words formed a tapestry of cultural memory that Harry instinctively translated, navigating the inherent untranslatability of his servant’s subjective experience. 

“Miss Hetty she fall down in a faint when she hear you taken up,” Gumbo said, his voice dropping to a reverent whisper, “and Mr. Lambert, he very good man, and he say to me this morning, ‘Gumbo, you tell your master if he want me he send to me, and I come to him.’”

Harry’s throat tightened at the thought of Hetty's affliction, but the mention of the Colonel summoned the harsh power dynamics of their last encounter. Ask Lambert for a crust? Harry ground his teeth, the very thought a betrayal of his agrarian pride. Instead, having dressed in a magnificent brocade gown, he called for his morning meal. It arrived as a dismal, watery gruel, a utilitarian fuel wholly devoid of the sensory appreciation or culinary soul that Harry required to feel human. Pushing the gray slop away, he dispatched Gumbo with a brief note to his attorney, Mr. Draper, in the Temple.

The note, Draper would later complain, was penned with the haughty dominance of a lord commanding a vassal, forcing the attorney into a subordinate linguistic role. Yet Draper came, his hard-heeled shoes striking the floorboards with a rapid, commercial clatter that polluted the room's quiet despair. 

“Gumbo, a chair to Mr. Draper,” Harry commanded, folding his gown around his legs, deliberately slowing his cadence to reclaim the acoustic space from the lawyer’s frantic energy. “Sit down, if you please, and let us talk my business over. Had you heard of this piece of ill-luck before?”

Draper nodded, a tight, patronizing smile on his lips. “Bad news travel quick, Mr. Warrington. Your friends, your family, will be much pained that a gentleman of your rank should be in such a position.” Draper operated strictly within the dominant lexicon of litigation; he possessed no ear for the nuanced cadences of honor.

Harry explained his liabilities—a sum of five hundred pounds lost to the turning wheel of fortune. To a man rooted in the generational wealth of Virginia's sprawling harvests, where value was measured in cured leaf and the rich aroma of turning soil, it seemed a trifle. “It can be done,” Harry insisted. 

Draper pulled a long, grave face. He possessed Madame de Bernstein’s explicit authority to settle the debt instantly and release the young Virginian, but Draper relished the power of withholding, an exertion of control through legalistic delay. He cited the frozen assets in Virginia, the six months of oceanic silence required to reach Lady Esmond Warrington, and the strict limits of Harry's Bristol agent. 

“I suppose I have no resource, then, but to apply to my aunt, Madame de Bernstein,” Harry finally said, his voice shaking slightly as he requested Draper to tell the Baroness everything, to hide no disgrace.

“I was with her ladyship yesterday,” Draper sneered, the abrasive tone grating against Harry’s ears. “Does Madame de Bernstein know of my misfortune? Every circumstance, sir; the pawning the watches, and all.”

Harry flushed a burning red, the color of a fiercely stoked hearth. Draper continued to weaponize his vocabulary, speaking of barristers, the witness box, and obtaining goods under false pretences. The words were a sonic assault. Harry started up from the bed, his fury a mythic, ancestral fire. 

“Great Heaven, sir, you don't suppose a gentleman of my rank can't take a watch upon credit without intending to cheat the tradesman?”

Draper winked—a vulgar, commercial gesture. “Of course you meant everything that's honourable; only, you see, the law mayn't happen to think so. But you ask if I can't help to pay this debt off? Here is the money in my pocket to do it now. But your aunt has a right to impose her conditions.”

The conditions, delivered in Draper’s sterile, transactional cadence, required Harry to completely break his engagement to Maria Esmond. Draper mentioned the compromised letters, the foolish promise written in blood—a visceral, iron-rich substance of life and lineage reduced by Draper to a mere liability in a ledger. “Give me and my noble friend your word of honour. Cry off, I say, Mr. W.! Don't be such a fool as to marry an old woman who has jilted scores of men. Say the word, and I step downstairs, pay every shilling, and put you down in my coach with a couple of hundred in your pocket.”

Harry longed to be free of Maria, his foolish flame having long since turned to cold ash. But the lawyer’s gross familiarity, the sheer acoustic pollution of his sneering presence, sickened Harry's sensibilities. He shrank from Draper’s proffered hand. “Give me a little time to think of the matter, if you please, Mr. Draper. Have the goodness to come to me again in an hour.”

Red-faced and growling curses that fouled the air of the stairwell, Draper departed. He spent the intervening hours bullying his clerks and consuming a leisurely, soulless tavern meal before his footsteps finally returned to Cursitor Street.

At the double doors, the sonic landscape shifted. Draper was halted by the sheriff's officer's aide-de-camp, Costigan. The small space between them was thick with the rich, fermented olfactory haze of spirituous liquors—the pungent scent of distilled grains and agrarian escape. Costigan spoke in a thick, lyrical Irish brogue, a native dialect that fiercely resisted Draper’s sneering London dismissals. 

“My name is Costigan; me familee is noble, and me neetive place is the Irish methrawpolis, Mr. Six-and-eightpence!” Costigan scowled. Draper barked at him to stand aside, insulting Costigan's dropped H's. It was a clash of lexicons, the dominant power attempting to erase the cultural memory embedded in Costigan's tongue. “I'll thank ye not to call neems, me good friend,” Costigan retorted, refusing to yield the acoustic high ground. “Be polite for the future to your shupariors in birth and manners, though they may be your infariors in temporary station!”

Suddenly, a lady, her face heavily veiled by a capuchin and hidden by a handkerchief, hurried down the stairs with a startled gasp. Draper, ever the aggressive opportunist, pressed forward to inspect her, but Costigan thrust a leg between them, offering a protective, rhythmic cadence: “Keep your own distance, if you plaise! This way, madam! I at once recognised your ladysh—” The heavy door slammed in Draper's face with a percussive finality, leaving the attorney to navigate his way upstairs alone.

As this transpired, in a grand house on Clarges Street, the old Baroness de Bernstein paced with her drawing-crutch, the frantic tapping a disruptive, anxious rhythm against the floorboards. She had orchestrated a meticulous culinary triumph to celebrate her nephew’s release. Her kitchen, overseen by a passionate chef devoted to the historical artistry of French gastronomy, had prepared a fete: partridges basted in rich, golden fat, a reduction of marrow and wild thyme, the air heavy with the evocative, gustatory promise of slow-roasted root vegetables and claret-soaked truffles. For the Baroness, sustenance was profound, a ritual of love, memory, and power. 

Hour after hour, the dinner had been delayed. The exquisite flavors were oxidizing, the culinary poetry devolving into tragic waste as the clock ticked. At six o'clock, she looked from the window, and a coach actually stopped at her door. 

Mr. Draper was announced. He entered alone, bowing profoundly. 

The old lady trembled on her stick, the acoustic space of the drawing room suddenly hollow despite the magnificent feast waiting in the wings. “Where is the boy?” she demanded quickly, an ancestral fire sparking in her eyes. “I told you to bring him, sir! How dare you come without him?”

“It is not my fault, madam, that Mr. Warrington refuses to come,” Draper replied, and began to unspool his sterile, uncomprehending version of the interview that had just taken place.
