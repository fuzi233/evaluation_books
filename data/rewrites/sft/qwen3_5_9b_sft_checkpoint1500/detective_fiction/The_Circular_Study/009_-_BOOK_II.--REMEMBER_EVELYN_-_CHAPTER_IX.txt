Mr. Gryce stepped down from the staircase, bowing slightly to the two men he had stationed outside before ordering the valet's removal. Once the hallway fell silent and privacy was restored, he turned back toward Miss Butterworth, who had returned to the study. He spoke with the gravity of a man who believes he has finally untangled a knot.

"One crucial variable has been resolved by our latest experiment," Gryce announced. "Bartow is exonerated from any involvement in this crime."

"Excellent," Miss Butterworth replied, though her brow remained furrowed. "Now we may focus entirely on the youth involved. Have you received any word from them?"

"Not a syllable," Gryce admitted.

"Nor from the elderly gentleman who arrived later?"

"Silence, too."

A shadow passed over Miss Butterworth's face. "I confess, I harbored certain hopes. I believed the fragment of paper you discovered—the dying man's last grasp—might hold the key to their identity. If it was vital enough for Amos to force it down his throat rather than spit it out, surely it offered a clue to his attacker."

Gryce shook his head gently. "Alas, it offers no direct identification. It was indeed a hurried scrawl, reading: *'I return your daughter to you. She is here. Neither she nor you will ever see me again. Remember Evelyn!'* Signed, 'Amos's son.'"

"Amos's son! That is Mr. Adams himself," Miss Butterworth observed, her voice rising in disbelief. "It defies logic. And yet, that paper was clamped tightly between his teeth! Did anyone recognize the handwriting?"

"Yes, Madam. By comparing it to the signatures on the fly-leaves of his books, we are confident it is his own."

"The mystery deepens with every step," Gryce murmured. "Consider this: the retention of this note was so critical that in his final agony, he hid it in the most secure place imaginable—a location impossible to search. And consider the girl! Upon regaining consciousness, her first words were: *'You have left that line of writing behind.'* Mr. Gryce, those words, sparse and cryptic as they are, seem to hold the lock to the entire situation. Would you kindly recite them again, slowly?"

"Gladly, Madam. I have rehearsed them in solitude many times. First: *'I return your daughter to you!'*"

"So!" Miss Butterworth leaned forward, her eyes sharp. "Mr. Adams was holding someone else's daughter. Whose? Certainly not the boy's, for that would imply a child far younger than the evidence suggests. Furthermore, if these words were addressed to his killer, why conceal them so fiercely? Why risk discovery?"

"True," Gryce conceded. "I share your confusion."

"If not for the killer, then for whom? Perhaps the old gentleman who entered shortly after?"

"A distinct possibility," Gryce mused. "Since learning of his arrival, I have considered this angle. Especially given the phrase *'She is here.'* The only female present moments prior to the elder gentleman's entry was the light-haired maiden you witnessed being carried out."

"But why treat this document as freshly penned?" Miss Butterworth pressed. "Might it be an ancient relic, referencing a forgotten sorrow?"

"On the contrary," Gryce insisted. "These words were written that afternoon. The paper itself was torn from a sheet resting on the desk, and the pen lay discarded at the extreme edge of the table, clearly used in haste."

"Certainly," Miss Butterworth nodded. "So, these words concern the girl I saw. And she is not Evelyn—or he would not have invoked the bird-catchword, *'Remember Evelyn!'* Does she bear that name?" She gestured toward the single, somber portrait dominating the wall.

"For the sake of argument, yes," Gryce said. "Her countenance is so melancholic, suggesting a heavy family burden. But how do we account for the frantic role played by the young man, who remains absent from this brief, desperate note? It is a labyrinth, Madam, and we waste precious time trying to map it."

"I dread surrendering without a fight," she protested. "There are still fragments you have not repeated."

Gryce paused. "Before the injunction *'Remember Evelyn!'*, there was a declaration of resolve: *'Neither she nor you will ever see me again.'*"

"Oh!" Miss Butterworth gasped. "How significant! Could he have dealt the fatal blow himself? Is this a suicide after all?"

"Madam, you have the liberty to ask," Gryce said, his tone firm yet gentle. "However, recall Bartow's pantomime. It depicts not a self-inflicted wound, but a savage thrust born of madness and outrage. Let us adhere to our initial conclusion. However..." He hesitated, searching for the right words. "...to ensure fairness to every hypothesis, consider the state of Mr. Adams's affairs. The total absence of financial records or standard documents from a wealthy man's desk indicates deliberate preparation for mortality. It may have arrived sooner than anticipated, or in a different guise, but he had contemplated this end. And, Madam, I must offer a confession of my own. I have not treated my most trusted colleague with complete candor. This study—that extraordinary chamber—harbors a secret withheld from you. A peculiar revelation, shared with me in a startling fashion."

He moved closer to the massive oak door. "This room could be severed entirely from the rest of the house. It could become a death trap, or rather, a tomb wherein this enigmatic man intended to linger until the end. Observe this iron plate. It operates via a mechanism designed to slide across this threshold. Last night, I stood behind this plate, and these holes were drilled specifically to allow my escape."

"Hark!" Miss Butterworth exclaimed, rubbing her eyes as if waking from a trance. "Detectives, you possess trials! I should never have wished to spend that particular evening with you. Yet, what an ancient tragedy unfolds here! I swear"—she shuddered—"I feel transported back to medieval times. Who claims we dwell in modern New York, beneath the hum of cable cars and the crackle of telegraphs?"

"Some men possess the uncanny ability to summon the Middle Ages into the heart of Wall Street," Gryce reflected. "I suspect Mr. Adams was precisely such a soul. Romanticism colored every action, including the manner of his demise. And it followed him beyond the grave. Consider the cross we discovered lying upon his chest."

"That was the work of another hand," Miss Butterworth countered quickly. "Born of another's superstition. It proves the presence of a priest or a confessor at the moment of his passing."

"Yet," Gryce continued, his expression thoughtful, "one must concede that Mr. Adams possessed a measure of practical acumen. His contrivances functioned flawlessly. He was a master mechanic, as much as a lover of the macabre."

"A strange duality," she whispered. "It is curious that his spirit seems to have faded from the air of this study. Almost, I wish it lingered. Perhaps then, we might find the key to this riddle."

"That," Gryce noted, "is achievable through only one path. As you astutely observed, we must locate the young couple who bore witness to his final struggle. Without them, the case is lost."

"And thus," Miss Butterworth sighed, "we circle back to the starting point, proving once more that we are hopelessly adrift in the wilderness." Rising to her feet, she signaled the temporary exhaustion of her resources.

Mr. Gryce made no move to detain her; indeed, he appeared eager to depart. Yet, before leaving, they paused for one final survey of the room. As their gaze swept across the walls, Miss Butterworth raised a slender finger.

"Look!" she commanded softly. "From this vantage point, you scarcely discern the opening created by removing that painting on the stair landing. Do you not perceive it nestled within those folds of dark tapestry above?"

"Yes," Gryce confirmed, nodding. "I had already identified that precise spot. With the canvas hanging opposite, it renders the aperture invisible."

"One must maintain a wandering eye in matters such as these," Miss Butterworth declared. "That picture must have been shifted repeatedly during our stay, yet we overlooked it."

"The fault lay in looking merely eye-level," Gryce smiled, a touch of weariness in his eyes. "High and low, Mr. Gryce! What transpires at the height of vision escapes the unobservant, while what lies below goes unnoticed by the hasty."

The smile with which he acknowledged her parting insight, preparing to escort her toward the door, held less irony than a profound sadness. Was he beginning to comprehend that age erodes even the sharpest intellects, and that neither elevated heights nor hidden depths had evaded his scrutiny a dozen years prior?
