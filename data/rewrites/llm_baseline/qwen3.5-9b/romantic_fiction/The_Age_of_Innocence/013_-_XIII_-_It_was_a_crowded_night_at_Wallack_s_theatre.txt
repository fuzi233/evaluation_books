It was a crowded night at Wallack's Theatre. The stage was alive with *The Shaughraun*, starring the beloved Dion Boucicault alongside Harry Montague and Ada Dyas as the star-crossed lovers. The English company was at the zenith of its fame, and the house was invariably packed. While the audience in the galleries roared with unbridled enthusiasm, those seated in the stalls and boxes offered a more reserved appreciation, smiling gently at the familiar tropes and melodramatic turns while still enjoying the spectacle.

Yet, one specific moment held the entire theater captive from floor to ceiling. It was the farewell scene: Harry Montague, following a somber, nearly speechless exchange with Miss Dyas, bid her goodbye and prepared to depart. The actress stood near the fireplace, clad in a simple gray cashmere gown devoid of fashionable frills, tailored to her tall frame and trailing elegantly to the floor. A narrow black velvet ribbon encircled her neck, its ends cascading down her back. As her suitor turned away, she leaned her elbows on the mantelpiece, burying her face in her hands. At the threshold, he paused to glance back, then stealthily retreated, lifting one end of the velvet ribbon to kiss it before slipping out unseen, leaving her unmoved. The curtain fell on this silent, poignant exit.

Newland Archer attended *The Shaughraun* specifically for this scene. He considered the adieu between Montague and Dyas superior to any performance he had witnessed in Paris or London; its restrained grief moved him more profoundly than the most extravagant displays of emotion. That evening, however, the scene carried an extra weight, inexplicably recalling his own departure from Madame Olenska following their private conversation a week prior.

On the surface, the parallels were nonexistent. Archer lacked the romantic allure of the young English actor, and Miss Dyas—a tall, red-haired woman of imposing stature—was nothing like Ellen Olenska, whose face was vivid and striking. Furthermore, their situation differed vastly; Archer and Olenska were not lovers parting in heartbreak, but a lawyer and his client separating after a discussion that had cast a grim shadow over her legal prospects. So, what connection stirred Archer's heart with such retrospective fervor?

It lay in Madame Olenska's unique ability to evoke tragedy and depth beyond ordinary experience. Though she rarely spoke of it, there was an inherent drama and passion within her, perhaps rooted in her mysterious origins. Archer believed that fate played a smaller role than innate destiny; he sensed from the start that events were destined to unfold around her, regardless of her efforts to avoid them. Her lack of surprise suggested she had been plucked from a whirlpool of chaos, taking for granted only the aftermath of rebellion.

Leaving her that afternoon, Archer had convinced himself that Count Olenski's accusations were justified. The "secretary" in her past likely hadn't acted without reward. Having fled intolerable circumstances, young, terrified, and desperate, her gratitude toward her savior was natural. The tragedy, Archer realized painfully, was that this gratitude placed her, in the eyes of the law and society, on equal footing with her abhorrent husband. He had made this clear to her, knowing full well that New York's superficial kindness offered her no refuge. Witnessing her resigned acceptance had been excruciating. Drawn by a mix of jealousy and pity, he felt her silent confession had placed her at his mercy, simultaneously humbling and endearing her. He was relieved she had confided in him rather than facing the cold judgment of Mr. Letterblair or the awkward stares of her family. Immediately, he assured them she had abandoned her pursuit of a divorce, citing the futility of the endeavor. With immense relief, everyone turned away from the "unpleasantness" she had spared them.

"I knew Newland would handle it," Mrs. Welland had declared proudly of her future son-in-law. Old Mrs. Mingott, summoning him for a private chat, had praised his cunning, muttering impatiently, "Silly goose! I told her myself what nonsense it was. Trying to pose as Ellen Mingott and a spinster when she's actually a married Countess!"

These memories were so vivid that as the curtain finally dropped on the actors' parting, Archer's eyes filled with tears, and he rose to leave. Turning toward the rear of the house, he spotted the woman occupying a box with the Beauforts, Lawrence Lefferts, and others. Though he had avoided spending time with her in public since their last meeting, their eyes locked across the room. Recognizing him instantly, Mrs. Beaufort offered a languid, inviting gesture. There was no choice but to enter the box.

Beaufort and Lefferts stepped aside. After exchanging polite words with Mrs. Beaufort, who preferred beauty to conversation, Archer settled behind Madame Olenska. The only other occupant was Mr. Sillerton Jackson, whispering confidentially to Mrs. Beaufort about Mrs. Lemuel Struthers' recent Sunday reception, complete with rumors of dancing. Using this distraction as cover, Madame Olenska turned and spoke softly.

"Do you think," she asked, glancing toward the stage, "he will send her a bunch of yellow roses tomorrow morning?"

Archer flushed, his heart skipping a beat. He had visited her only twice recently, sending a box of yellow roses each time without a note. She had never acknowledged the sender before, assuming he remained anonymous. Now, her sudden association of the flowers with the tender scene on stage filled him with agitated delight.

"I was thinking of that too," he admitted, surprised. "I was planning to leave the theatre just to take the picture home with me."

To his astonishment, her cheeks flushed, dimly and reluctantly. Looking down at the mother-of-pearl opera glass in her gloved hands, she paused before saying, "What do you do while May is away?"

"I stick to my work," he replied, slightly irritated by the inquiry.

As per the Welland family tradition, they had departed the previous week for St. Augustine, where Mr. Welland's bronchial condition required the southern climate. Mild, silent, and governed entirely by habit, Mr. Welland insisted his wife and daughter accompany him. Preserving domestic unity was essential to his peace of mind; he relied on Mrs. Welland to locate his brushes and stamp envelopes. Since the family adored him, and his sons were unavailable due to their legal careers, the arrangement was fixed. Archer found himself unable to argue against the necessity of May's presence. The Mingott family physician's reputation hinged on preventing Mr. Welland's fatal pneumonia, making the trip non-negotiable. Although Archer would have cherished a few weeks of sunshine and boating with his fiancée, social convention forbade requesting a mid-winter holiday. He accepted May's departure with the resignation he knew was necessary for married life.

He was acutely aware that Madame Olenska watched him through lowered lashes.

"I have done what you wished," she stated abruptly. "What you advised."

"Oh—I am glad," he responded, flustered by her bringing up the topic so suddenly.

"I understand—you were right," she continued, breathing lightly. "But sometimes life is difficult... perplexing..."

"I know."

"And I wanted to tell you that I truly believe you were right," she concluded, quickly raising her opera glass as the box door swung open and Beaufort's deep voice interrupted them.

Archer stood, exited the box, and left the theatre.

Only the day before, he had received a letter from May Welland, written with her characteristic honesty. *"Please be kind to Ellen in our absence,"* she had urged. *"She likes you and admires you so much—and you know, though she doesn't show it, she's still very lonely and unhappy. I don't think Granny understands her, nor does Uncle Lovell Mingott; they think she's far more worldly and fond of society than she is. I can see that New York seems dull to her, even if the family denies it. She's accustomed to wonders we lack here: great music, picture shows, celebrities, artists, authors, and all the clever people you admire. Granny can't grasp why she wants anything other than endless dinners and clothes—but I see that you are almost the only person in New York who can speak to her about what she truly cares for."*

His wise May—he had loved her for that letter! Yet, he had not intended to act on it. Initially, he was too preoccupied, and as an engaged man, he hesitated to play the conspicuous champion of Madame Olenska. He suspected she managed herself far better than the innocent May imagined. Surrounded by Beaufort, Mr. van der Luyden hovering protectively, and suitors like Lawrence Lefferts waiting in the wings, she seemed secure. Still, every time he saw her or spoke with her, he felt compelled to agree with May's intuition: Ellen Olenska was lonely, and she was unhappy.
