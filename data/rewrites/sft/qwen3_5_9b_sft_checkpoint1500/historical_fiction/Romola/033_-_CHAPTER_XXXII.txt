The following dawn, Romola shared the general fervor of the Florentines regarding the departure of the French army. Yet, beneath this public joy, a private, half-superstitious hope stirred within her. She clung to the faint belief that the heavy anxieties concerning Tito, which had arrived alongside the foreign guests, might dissipate with them. Though the French had lingered for barely eleven days, Romola recalled that period as one of profound, acute unhappiness unknown to her prior life. Since their arrival, Tito had donned a hateful armor of caution; though she could not logically discern why their exit should erase the root of his fear—and though the vision of the prisoner seizing him, as depicted in Piero's sketch, haunted her whenever she considered the cause—nevertheless, she reasoned that with the soldiers gone, she would finally be free of something so tightly bound to her sorrow.

Enveloped in her mantle, she stood upon the upper loggia, gazing toward the bridges where the troops and the royal retinue were passing en route to the Porta San Piero, facing Siena and Rome. Even after the gates had fallen shut, she returned to her post, eager to vibrate with the resonance of the great bell-peal. By the time dusk settled and she descended into the library, she lit her lamp with a stern resolve to conquer the agitation that had rendered her idle all day, intending to resume her copying of the catalogue. Tito had departed early, and she planned to leave the study once he returned, seeking refuge in the charming salon adorned with dancing nymphs and birds—a retreat he had demanded since finding the library too chilly and somber.

However, to her astonishment, Tito entered before she had undertaken any work. A surge of concern crossed her mind; how bleak the vast darkness must seem to him, with a solitary oil lamp flickering at the far end and the fire nearly extinguished. She hastened toward him.

"Tito, dearest, I knew not you would return so soon," she exclaimed, nervously raising her white arms to loosen the *becchetto* around his neck.

"Am I then unwelcome?" he asked, offering one of his brightest smiles, though he playfully held his head back from her embrace.

"Tito!" she cried, the word tinged with pretty, loving reproach. Then, he kissed her tenderly, stroking her hair as was his custom, seemingly unmindful of removing his mantle. Romola quivered with delight. All the turmoil of the day had sharpened her senses to the return of this habitual tenderness. "It shall return," she murmured to herself, "the ancient happiness will perhaps return. He is like himself again."

Yet Tito was laboring intensely to appear as his former self; his heart beat violently with anxiety.

"If I had anticipated your return so swiftly," Romola said, helping him disrobe, "I would have staged a small festival for this joyful tolling of the bells. I did not intend to remain in this library when you arrived."

"Nay, never mind, sweet," he replied lightly. "Cease to worry about the fire. Come, sit down."

A low stool stood beside his chair, Romola's customary seat during their conversations. Resting her arm upon his knee, as she had done upon her father's lap, she looked up at him. He had thus far failed to notice the portrait hanging nearby, and she refrained from mentioning it, contemplating it with growing intensity.

"I have savored the clamor of the bells for the first time, Tito," she began. "I delighted in being shaken and deafened by them; I imagined myself a Bacchant possessed by divine fury. Do not the people look exceedingly joyful tonight?"

"Joyful, indeed, in a sour and pious fashion," Tito observed, shrugging. "Truth be told, those remaining in Florence have scant cause for jubilation. The most sensible grounds for gladness would surely be a flight from the city."

Tito had struck the desired note effortlessly, without apparent premeditation. He spoke without emphasis, yet his gravity compelled Romola to ask anxiously, "Why, Tito? Are there fresh troubles?"

"No occasion for fresh woes, my Romola. Three powerful factions lie within the city, poised to tear each other apart. Were the Frate's party strong enough to silence the others—which seems probable—life would become as pleasant and amusing as a funeral. They already simmer with plans for a Great Council; whoever sings the sacred Lauds the loudest shall be deemed most eligible for office. Furthermore, the city, drained by this immense subsidy to the King of France and the war to recover Pisa, faces a dismal prospect without the tyranny of fanatics. In sum, Florence shall be a delightful haunt for those who entertain themselves by visiting crypts and flagellating themselves; for all else, the exiles fare best. For my part, I have seriously contemplated that it would be prudent to depart Florence, my Romola."

She started. "Tito, how could we leave Florence? Surely you do not think I could abandon it—at least, not yet, not for a long while." Her voice grew cold and trembled; she found speech difficult. Tito must perceive the reasons swirling in her mind.

"That is entirely a fabric of your own imagination, my sweet one. Your secluded existence has led you to overvalue a few trifles. Remember, before our marriage, I told you I wished to reside somewhere other than Florence. Had you seen more lands and more people, you would understand what I mean when I say that something in the Florentines resembles their biting spring winds. I prefer people who take life less eagerly; and it would benefit my Romola greatly to witness a new life. I should like to bathe her briefly in the soft waters of forgetfulness."

He leaned forward, kissing her brow and laying his hand upon her fair hair again. Yet, she felt his caress no more than if he had kissed a mask. Agitated by the chasm widening between their minds, she remained oblivious to the contact of his lips.

"Tito, it is not because I imagine Florence to be the most agreeable place in the world that I refuse to quit it. It is because I... because we must fulfill my father's wish. My godfather is aged; he is seventy-one. We cannot forsake him."

"It is precisely those superstitions, clinging to your mind like obscuring clouds, that constitute a major reason for my desire to locate us two hundred leagues distant from Florence. I am compelled to guard you against your own will. If those dear eyes, so tender in appearance, deceive themselves, I must see for them, saving my wife from wasting her life in impracticable dreams."

Romola sat silent and immovable. She could not blind herself to the direction of Tito's words: he sought to persuade her that the library could be deposited in a monastery or otherwise disposed of, severing their tie to Florence. She resolved never to submit her mind to his judgment on this matter of filial duty; inwardly, she prepared to endure any pain required for resistance. However, this determination remained latent in these initial moments, crushed by the heart-rending realization that she and Tito were now openly divided in their desires. He welcomed her silence; despite his fears regarding the strength of her feeling, it was impossible for a man confined to the narrowness of mere intellect, devoid of deep passion, not to overestimate the persuasiveness of his own arguments. His conduct appeared harmless to himself, and his imagination failed to reveal exactly how it would appear to Romola. Continuing in the same gentle, remonstrative tone, he pressed on.

"You know, dearest—you whose clear judgment has always been evident to you—that the notion of isolating a collection of books and antiquities, attaching a single name to them forever, offers no valid, substantial good for its object: and, moreover, is liable to be thwarted in a thousand ways. Witness the Medici collections! For my part, I deem it even blameworthy to harbor such petty notions of appropriation. Why should anyone reasonably rejoice that Florence possesses the fruits of learned research and taste more than any other city? I comprehend your sentiment regarding the wishes of the dead; but wisdom imposes limits upon such sentiments, lest lives be endlessly squandered in futile devotion—much like praising deaf gods eternally. You devoted your life to your father while he lived; why should you demand more of yourself?"

"Because it was a trust," Romola said, her voice low but distinct. "He trusted me, he trusted you, Tito. I did not anticipate you would feel otherwise about it—or feel differently—but I did expect you to acknowledge that."

"Yes, dearest, naturally I would feel it where your father's genuine welfare or happiness was concerned; but there is no question of that now. If we believed in purgatory, I should be as anxious as you to have masses said; and if I believed it could now pain your father to see his library preserved and utilized in a manner differing from his intent, I should share the rigidity of your views. But a little philosophy ought to teach us to liberate ourselves from those air-woven fetters mortals bind upon themselves, spending their lives in misery under the mere illusion of weight. Your mind, which seizes ideas so readily, my Romola, is capable of distinguishing between substantial good and these brain-forged fantasies. Ask yourself, dearest, what possible good these books and antiquities can achieve, stowed together under your father's name in Florence, compared to what they would accomplish if dispersed or transported elsewhere?
