The good physician trundled home and told his housekeeper he was in agonies from “a bad burn.” Those were his exact words. For as in all matters of phlogistic humours, so in every other small cruelty of human nature: we do not hesitate to cauterize our neighbour’s fingers for petty gain, but how we wail and coddle our own burnt ones. His housekeeper slathered him with soft, mild old wives’ salve gentle as new milk, and he submitted meek as a lamb to her care—for his only priority in this hour was to be cured quickly enough to carry out his schemes. He scribbled out a full bill for every phial Gerard had shattered on his floor, then marched straight to the local magistrate to swear an oath: the two strange travellers, strangers to the township and deeply indebted to him, planned to flee before the sun set that very day.

Alas, it was his unluckiest hour. His sincere, carefully laid plan to perjure himself to lock the pair away failed, baffled by a circumstance he had never foreseen, nor even thought possible. He had spoken nothing but the unvarnished, absolute truth. On a sworn affidavit.

The officers arrived at The Silver Lion to find the birds had already flown. They picked up a tip from the wharf workers at the river, and set off hot-foot up the east bank in close pursuit.

The friends had escaped this fate thanks entirely to Denys’s sharp wits. He had laughed so hard at the quack’s discomfiture his sides ached, shouted his favourite battle-cry three times as a charm, then suddenly went stone sober and told Gerard Dusseldorf was no place to linger. “That old babbler went far too unnaturally quiet for a man who wouldn’t stop yammering ten minutes prior,” he pointed out. “We’re strangers here, the bailiff is his old drinking buddy, and we’ll be locked in a stinking dungeon for assaulting a Dusseldorf dignitary before five more minutes pass. Are you strong enough to hobble the short way to the water’s edge? Once there, we need only lie down in a boat instead of a bed—what odds is that to us?”

“Odds, Denys? Untold, all in favour of the boat. I burn for Rome, for Rome is my road back to Sevenbergen. We’ll lie in that boat, but *on* the Rhine, the famous, cool, refreshing Rhine. I can already feel its breezes on my face; the very sight will cure this silly little hop-o’-my-thumb fever of mine. Away, away at once!”

Seeing his excitable friend so fired up, Denys settled their bill with the landlord in two quick shakes, and they hurried to the wharf. To their dismay, they learned the public passage barge had left half an hour prior, and no other would sail for the rest of the afternoon. But after pestering a dozen passersby and drawing a small curious crowd, they secured a private passage offer from an old boatman and his two strapping sons.

A bystander snickered at the small craft. “The Rhine current runs far too strong for three oars alone to fight.”

“Then my comrade and I will help row,” put in the feverish invalid Gerard.

“No need, lad,” the old boatman chuckled. “Bless your soft silly heart, look at that fine fair wind blowing straight at our stern.”

They hoisted a broad, heavy sail, added a few steady strokes of their oars, and the little craft flew up the Rhine at a spanking pace.

“Feeling better for the river breeze, eh?” Denys asked.
“Much better. And truth be told, that doctor did me far more good than harm.”
“The doctor? But you refused every single one of his potions, didn’t you?”
“Aye, but what I mean—you’ll think me a brute, I know—knocking that old fool flat somehow soothed the fever right out of me.”
“My amiable dove!” Denys cackled, eyes glinting. “How your little character unfurls more every day, soft as a rosebud. I read you all wrong at first, when I thought you nothing but a pious quiet clerk.”
“Nay, Denys, do not mistake me,” Gerard protested. “I would have borne his idle threats well enough, even if his grating voice did go straight through my poor aching ears. But he was an infidel, or next door to one, and such I have been taught to abhor. Did he not all but say our bodies were formed by long-dead Greek men with hard names, not by Him whose name is but a single syllable, whose hand holds all the world in its palm? Pagan!”
“So you knocked him flat forthwith, like a good pious Christian.”
“Now Denys, you will still jest at me. Do not take that wicked old man’s part. Had a thunderbolt struck him from heaven he would have met no more than his due, and all he got was a sorry soft blow from this weak arm.”

“What weak arm?” Denys cried, laughing. “I have lived among fighting men my whole life, and by Samson’s hairy old head, I never saw an arm more like a man-catapult. That bolster wrapped right around the quack’s nose till the two ends kissed behind his head, his forehead rang like a blacksmith’s anvil, and had he been Goliath or Julius Caesar instead of a doddering old fraud, he would have gone down just as hard. St. Denys guard me from such feeble opposites as you! And above all, from their so-called weak arms, thou diabolical young hypocrite.”

The river curved again and again, and at times the wind slipped from dead astern to blow at their side. All the men crowded to the weather rail to shift their weight and stop the boat heeling over too far—all save a five-year-old urchin, the boatman’s petted grandson, who had snuck on board at the very moment of departure, too light to affect the craft’s trim, completely above and beyond all the laws of navigation.

They sailed merrily on, entirely unaware a whole posse of constables armed with the bailiff’s writ was tramping along the bank behind them, drawing ever closer. The wind was strong, but the Rhine’s current was swifter.

It was then Gerard suddenly remembered this course would take him to Rome, but nowhere near Burgundy, Denys’s home. “Oh Denys!” he said, looking half panicked. “This is not your road.”
“I know it,” Denys said, quiet and unconcerned. “But what can I do? I will not leave your side till that fever breaks, and it still hangs on you—your cheeks flare red then go deathly white by turns, I have been watching you. I suppose I will sail all the way to Cologne with you first, then strike cross country east to Burgundy.”

“Thank Heaven!” Gerard cried, overjoyed. He added a little eagerly, with a soft touch of self-deception, “It would be a sin to be this close to Cologne and not see it. Oh man, it is a vast ancient city, the kind I have dreamed of a hundred times but never had the luck to visit. It is old enough to have been founded by a Roman lady in the first century of grace, sacked by Attila the Hun, then sorely defaced by the Norman Lothaire. It holds a church for every week of the year, on top of innumerable chapels and convent churches, and above all the stupendous unfinished minster. In its own chapel rest the three kings who brought gifts to the Christ child: Melchior with gold, Gaspar with frankincense, and Balthazar the black king who brought myrrh. Over their bones stands the shrine, the wonder of the whole world: made of ever-shining brass brighter than gold, studded with carved images, inlaid with fine devices, brave with bright colours, and strung with two broad bands of jewels so large and rare each could adorn a king’s crown or ransom a captive’s whole life. On top stand the three kings carved in fine detail, two in solid silver gilded bright, bareheaded, and the Ethiop king carved of ebony wearing a golden crown. In their midst stands the blessed Virgin in virgin silver, holding the Christ child, and at the four corners golden candle branches hold four great wax tapers that burn night and day. Holy hands have watched and renewed that unceasing light for ages, and holy eyes will tend them for all eternity. The oldest Flemish and German legends found them burning, and they will burn till the earth itself dies. And then there is St. Ursula’s church, dedicated to that British saint, where her bones and the bones of her eleven thousand virgin followers rest. They all died for the faith, struck down by barbarian Moors on the 23rd of October in the year 238. Their bones are piled in the vaults, and many of their skulls are displayed in the church. St. Ursula’s own skull rests in a thin golden case on the high altar, only shown to humble worshippers on the highest holy days.”

“Eleven thousand virgins!” Denys exclaimed. “What lily-livered babies German men must have been in days of yore. Well, would all their bones might turn flesh again and their skulls sweet young faces, just as we pass through the city gates. It’s good odds at least a few of them are tired of being dead by now.”
“Tush, Denys!” Gerard huffed. “Why, for all your goodness, do you still make yourself seem wicked? If your wishing cap is on, pray that we meet the humblest of all those wise virgins in the next world, and to that end let us reverence their holy dust in this one. There is also the church of the Maccabees, and the great cauldron where they and their mother Solomona were boiled by a wicked king for refusing to eat swine’s flesh.”
“Oh, stubborn king, and pig-headed Maccabees!” Denys laughed. “I’d have eaten bacon alongside my pork meal before I’d change places with it in a boiling cauldron.”
“Such scurvy words! It was their sacred faith.”
“Nay, bridle your choler,” Denys said, grinning. “Tell me, is there nothing but churches in this so-vaunted city of yours? I care far more for the knight than the priest, you know.”
“Aye, there is a university nearly a hundred years old, a market square no fairer anywhere in the world, lined with houses great as palaces, a stupendous senate house covered top to bottom with carved images. At the very front of them all stands the statue of stout Herman Gryn, a soldier just like you, lad.”
“Ah! Tell me of him! What feat of arms earned him that niche?”
“A rare one. He slew a lion in fair single combat, armed with nothing but his cloak and a short sword. He thrust the cloak straight down the brute’s throat, then cut its spine clean in two. His effigy and the lion’s stand there to prove it. Only three other men in all history ever did the like: Samson was one, Lysimachus of Macedon another, and Benaiah, one of King David’s captains.”
“Marry! Three tall, formidable fellows. I’d love to sit down to supper with all three of them tonight.”
“Not I,” Gerard said drily.

Denys blinked at him, surprised. “And when wast thou ever in Cologne, then?”
“Never but in the spirit. I talk to all the good wandering monks on the road, and they tell me every notable thing old and new.”
“Aye, aye, I’ve seen you with your nose buried in their cowls, badgering them for stories. When I talk of things out of sight my words are small and the thing they describe is big, but your words are as big or bigger than the wonders themselves. You are a fine painter with your tongue, that I’ll say. And for a supposed pious clerk, you are as quick with hand, steel, or a bolster as any poor sinner alive. So shall I tell you which of all these fine things you described draws me to Cologne?”
“Aye, Denys.”
“You. And you only. No dead saint, no ancient wonder, but my living friend and true comrade. ‘Tis thou alone draws Denys of Burgundy to Cologne.”

Gerard hung his head, flustered.

At that very moment, one of the younger boatmen suddenly frowned and asked what was amiss with “little turnip-face?” His tiny nephew, the urchin who snuck on board, had just wandered aft looking grave as a hanging judge, and burst out wailing without a single word of warning. They plied him with questions, some coaxing, some sharp: had he hurt himself? Eaten too much? Was he cold? Sick? Frightened? An idiot? To every last one he just wailed, oh! oh! oh! and refused to say another word.

“Who can tell what ails this peevish little brat?” the young boatman snarled, impatient. “Rather look this way and tell me who those are after us!”

The old man and his other son looked up, and saw four men tramping along the east bank of the Rhine, staring hard directly at their craft. They left off rowing for a moment, gathered in the stern whispering, casting glances alternately at their two passengers and the pursuers on the bank. They would have spent their time far better trying to unravel the mystery of the wailing turnip-face, that deep unknowable mind of dauntless infancy.

“If it’s who I think it is,” whispered one of the young men, “why don’t we slip for the west bank and give them the slip?”
The old man objected stoutly. “What, risk running into trouble for two total strangers? Are ye mad? Nay, we’ll cross to the east side. Stand always on the side of the strong arm, that’s my rule. What say you, Werter?”
“Please yourselves.”

What age and youth could not decide, a sudden puff of squall settled with perfect impartiality. A hard gust hit the little craft full on, it heeled over sharply, and the men jumped for the weather rail to trim it—only to see a ditch of dark river water rush from stem to stern across their boat’s bottom. The next moment they saw nothing at all, but felt the cold, rushing Rhine close over their heads.

Turnip-face had pulled the boat’s drain plug.

The constables on the bank unwound the thick ropes from their waists. Gerard could swim like a duck, but even the strongest swimmer canted out of a capsizing boat will sink a few feet before he can right himself. The dark water bubbled loud over his head, and he popped back up, half blind and half deaf for a moment. He shook his head like a water dog, saw the overturned black hull of the boat bobbing nearby, figures clinging to its keel, and made unthinkingly for it—until he heard a quiet cry behind him, not loud, but ringing with deep manly despair: “Adieu, comrade, adieu!”

He spun around. There was poor Denys, sinking, sinking fast, weighed down by the heavy arbalest he always slung across his back. His face was ashen, his eyes wide and staring, turned in utter despair on his dearest friend. Gerard uttered a wild cry of love and terror, and cleaved through the water madly towards him—but the next moment Denys slipped under the surface.

The next, Gerard dove straight in after him.

The officers knotted a heavy rope and hurled the weighted end far out into the churning dark river.
