In the early summer of the following year, two elderly gentlemen sat on the terrace of Broad Place, reading their newspapers after breakfast. The older of the pair flipped a page. "I hear Osman Digna has returned to Suakin," he remarked. "Looks like there might be some fighting soon."

"Oh," replied the other, setting his paper down. "He won't cause much trouble." Yet, as he spoke, the peaceful English countryside faded from his vision. In its place rose the white city by the Red Sea, shimmering under a relentless heat, surrounded by brown plains tangled with halfa grass, with distant hills marking the approach to Khor Gwob.

"A stifling place, Suakin, isn't it, Sutch?" asked General Feversham.

"Appallingly so," Sutch agreed. "I heard of an officer who collapsed on parade at six in the morning, struck down by the sun even through his regulation helmet. It's a town of damp, suffocating heat! Still, I'm glad I was there—very glad," he added, speaking with genuine emotion.

"Yes," Feversham said briskly. "And the ibex?"

"No," Sutch corrected. "Every single ibex within miles had been shot by the English garrison."

"No? So what kept you busy, then? That's it?"

"Yes, that's it, Feversham. There was something to do." Both men returned to their newspapers, but shortly thereafter, a footman delivered a small stack of letters to each.

General Feversham scanned his envelopes with a sharp glance, selected one, and let out a satisfied grunt. He retrieved a pair of spectacles from a case and perched them on his nose.

"From Ramelton?" Sutch asked, lowering his paper onto the terrace floor.

"From Ramelton," Feversham confirmed. "I'll light a cigar first."

He placed the letter on the garden table between them, pulled a cigar case from his pocket, and, despite Lieutenant Sutch's evident impatience, lit it with deliberate care. The old man had become a true epicure regarding cigars; a letter from Ramelton was a luxury to be savored with every possible accessory of comfort. He settled deeply into his chair, stretched out his legs, and smoked until he was certain the draw was perfect. Only then did he open the letter.

"From him?" Sutch ventured.

"No," Feversham replied softly. "From her."

Sutch tried hard not to peek over the table as the General read slowly. Once finished, Feversham turned the page and began again.

"Any news?" Sutch asked casually.

"They are very pleased with the house now that it's rebuilt."

"Anything else?"

"Yes. Harry has finished the sixth chapter of his history of the war."

"Excellent!" Sutch exclaimed. "You'll see, he'll write that well. He has imagination, he knows the terrain, and he was there while the war raged. Besides, he was in the bazaars; he saw the underside of things."

"Yes. But you and I won't read it, Sutch," Feversham noted.

"No," Sutch admitted. "I was wrong. You may, for you still have many years ahead of you."

Feversham turned back to his letter. Again, Sutch pressed, "Anything more?"

"Yes. They are coming here in a fortnight."

"Good," Sutch said. "I shall stay."

He paced along the terrace and returned to find Feversham sitting with the letter resting on his knees, a look of profound perplexity furrowing his brow.

"You know, Sutch, I never fully understood..." Feversham began.

"Did you?"

"Yes, I think I did." Sutch offered no explanation. He believed it was better that Feversham remained unaware, for understanding required a level of self-reproach the General simply could not bear.

"Do you ever see Durrance?" the General asked suddenly.

"Yes, I see him often. He is abroad just now."

Feversham turned toward his friend. "He came to Broad Place when you went to Suakin and talked to me for half an hour. He was Harry's best man. Well, that too I never understood. Did you?"

"Yes, I understood that perfectly."

"Oh!" General Feversham sighed. He asked for no further clarification. As he always had, he took the questions he couldn't comprehend and set them aside, pushing them from his mind. However, he didn't move to check his other letters. Instead, he continued smoking his cigar, gazing out over the summer landscape and listening intently to the distinct sounds rising from the fields below.

Sutch had already gone through his entire correspondence before Feversham spoke again.

"I've been thinking," the General said. "Have you noticed the date of the month, Sutch?"

Sutch looked up sharply. "Yes," he replied. "Next week today marks the anniversary of our attack on the Redan, and Harry's birthday."

"Exactly," Feversham agreed. "Why shouldn't we restart the Crimean Nights?"

Sutch sprang from his chair. "Splendid!" he cried. "Do you think we can muster a tableful?"

"Let's see," Feversham said, ringing a handbell to summon the servant for the Army List.

Bending over the list, the two veterans might have been left to their reminiscences forever. But one other figure in this story deserves a final word.

That night, after the invitations had been dispatched from Broad Place and the last light extinguished in the windows, a man leaned over the railing of a steamer anchored at Port Said. He listened to the rhythmic chanting of Arab coolies tramping up and down the planks, carrying coal baskets between the barges and the ship's side. The clamor of the town drifted across the water to his ears. He imagined the flare of braziers on the quays, the glowing portholes, and the dark funnels of the anchored ships stretching ahead and behind.

Attended by a servant, he had returned to the East. Early the next morning, the steamer pushed through the canal, and by sunset, emerged into the chilly waters of the Gulf of Suez. Kassassin, Tel-el-Kebir, Tamai, Tamanieb—the assault on McNeil's zareeba—Durrance relived those golden years of his prime, the era of abundance. Within that western land, vast preparations were underway that would one day sweep away the Dervish Empire and reduce it to dust. On the glacis of the ruined fort of Sinkat, Durrance had once vowed to play a part in that grand endeavor, yet the desert he loved had rejected him, casting him out. Nevertheless, the boat steamed southward into the Red Sea. Three more nights passed, and though he would not witness it, the Southern Cross would rise obliquely into the sky.
