The crimson tide receded slowly from Sally's cheeks, and her heart, which had hammered like a trapped bird at the mere echo of his voice, settled back into a steady rhythm. Once the initial shock wore off, a surprising calm washed over her. Every time she had rehearsed this inevitable confrontation in her mind, she had felt a rising panic, a dread that tightened her chest. Yet, now that reality had crashed into her, it felt strangely manageable. The chaotic energy of the night had drained her of capacity for wild emotion.

"Hullo, Sally!" Gerald called out.

His voice was thick, slurring slightly, and a foolish, vacant smile played on his lips as he swayed against the doorframe, supporting himself with one hand. He was shirt-sleeved and collarless, the marks of a heavy night of drinking etched clearly on his appearance. His face was pale and puffy, surrounded by a halo of sodden disreputability.

Sally said nothing. Burdened previously by a numbing exhaustion, she now seemed to have entered a second phase—a sort of Indian summer of hyper-alertness born of over-tired nerves. She regarded him with quiet, cool, and entirely dispassionate eyes, as if observing a total stranger.

"Hullo!" Gerald tried again.

"What do you want?" Sally asked.

"Heard your voice. Saw the door open. Thought I'd come in."

"What do you want?"

The weak, painted-on smile vanished from Gerald's face. A single tear tracked down his cheek; his intoxication had tipped into the maudlin stage. "Sally... S-Sally... I'm very miserable." He stumbled over the difficult syllables. "Heard your voice. Saw the door open. Thought I'd come in."

Something flickered at the back of Sally's mind. *Have I been through this before?* Then memory clicked into place. This was simply Mr. Reginald Cracknell all over again.

"I think you had better go to bed, Gerald," she said steadily.

Nothing about him registered anymore—not the sight of his degradation, nor the shameless misery dripping from his pores.

"What's the use? Can't sleep. No good. Couldn't sleep. Sally, you don't know how worried I am. I see what a fool I've been."

Sally made a quick gesture, checking what she feared was about to erupt into a belated apology for his treatment of her. She didn't want to stand there listening to Gerald weep for having ruined her life. But it wasn't that weighing on his soul.

"I was a fool ever to try writing plays," he continued, his voice trembling. "Got a winner first time, but can't repeat. It's no good. Ought to have stuck to newspaper work. I'm good at that. Shall have to go back to it. Had another frost to-night. No good trying any more. Shall have to go back to the old grind, damn it."

He wept softly, full of pity for his tragic case. "Very miserable," he murmured.

He stepped forward, lurched, and retreated to the safety of the doorframe. For an instant, Sally's artificial composure was pierced by a swift stab of contempt. It passed quickly, and she was armored in indifference once more.

"Go to bed, Gerald," she said. "You'll feel better in the morning."

Perhaps a glimmer of foresight penetrated Gerald's muddled intelligence. He winced, his manner deepening into melancholy. "May not be alive in the morning," he said solemnly. "End it all!" he repeated, making a sweeping gesture that he abruptly cut off as he clutched the friendly door.

Sally was not in the mood for melodrama. "Oh, go to bed," she said impatiently.

The strange, frozen indifference that had held her began to thaw, replaced by a growing resentment—against Gerald for degrading himself, and against herself for ever finding glamour in such a man. It humiliated her to recall how utterly she had once allowed his personality to master hers. Under the sting of this humiliation, she felt hard and pitiless. Dimly, she sensed a curious change within her. Normally, the sight of any living creature in distress would stir her sympathy instantly; but Gerald mourning the prospect of returning to regular work made no appeal to her. The sufferer noted this and commented on it.

"You're very unsympathetic," he complained.

"I'm sorry," Sally replied.

She walked briskly to the door and pushed it open. Gerald, still clinging to his support, moved out into the passage, attached to the handle like a man whose world had suddenly lost its foundation. He released the handle and shuffled uncertainly toward his own door. Finding it ajar, he staggered across the threshold. Sally watched him reach his destination safely before turning into her bedroom, intending to terminate the disturbing night with sleep.

Almost immediately, she changed her mind. Sleep was impossible. A feverish restlessness seized her. She threw on a kimono and went to the kitchen to check if her supplies permitted a glass of hot milk. She had just remembered presenting the last of the milk to a sandy cat with a purposeful eye that had dropped in through the window earlier that morning, when a muffled crash interrupted her regrets.

She listened intently. The sound seemed to come from across the passage. Hurrying to the door, she opened it. Instantly, from behind the door of the apartment opposite, erupted a perfect fusillade of crashes, each one seeming to her strained ears louder and more appalling than the last.

There is something about sudden, loud noises in the stillness of the night that shatters the most rigid detachment. Only moments ago, Gerald's threat of violence had left her unmoved; now, her mind leapt back to his words, and apprehension succeeded indifference. Undoubtedly, Gerald was in an irresponsible mood, capable of almost anything under the influence. Listening in the doorway, Sally felt a momentary panic.

A brief silence followed the explosion, but as she stood hesitating, the noise broke out again. This time, so loud and compelling was it that Sally hesitated no longer. She ran across the passage and pounded on the door.

Whatever devastation had occurred inside, Gerald had survived it. Moments later, a dragging footstep sounded, and the door swung open. Gerald stood on the threshold, the weak smile back on his face.

"Hullo, Sally!"

At the sight of him—disreputable and seemingly unscathed—Sally's brief alarm died, replaced by the old feeling of impatient resentment. In addition to her other grievances, he had apparently frightened her unnecessarily.

"Whatever was all that noise?" she demanded.

"Noise?" Gerald considered the point, mouth agape.

"Yes, noise," Sally snapped.

"I've been cleaning house," Gerald said with the owl-like gravity of a man barely conscious of his lack of faculties.

Sally pushed past him. The apartment was almost an exact replica of her own, evidently decorated by Elsa Doland with painstaking, niggly feminine care. Amateur interior decoration had always been Elsa's hobby. Even in the unpromising surroundings of her bedroom at Mrs. Meecher's boarding-house, she had contrived a certain daintiness that Sally, lacking such talent, had always envied. Elsa's mind ran toward small, fragile ornaments; she was not afraid of over-furnishing. Pictures jostled one another on the walls; china of all descriptions cluttered little tables; there was a profusion of lamps with parti-colored glass shades; and plates lined a series of shelves.

One might say the plates were ranged and the pictures jostled, but it would be more accurate to say they had jostled and been ranged, for Sally could only reconstruct the scene by guesswork. She had walked in briskly but pulled up short, appalled by the majestic ruin that met her gaze. A shellburst in a sitting-room could hardly have created more havoc.

The psychology of a man of weak character under the influence of alcohol and disappointed ambition is difficult to fathom; his moods shift with bewildering rapidity. Ten minutes prior, Gerald had been in the grip of clammy self-pity. Now, however, there had manifestly occurred a brief but adequate spasm of berserk fury. What caused it, or why it expended itself so abruptly, Sally was not psychologist enough to explain; the ocular evidence was convincing enough.

A heavy niblick, flung petulantly—or remorsefully—into a corner, indicated the weapon of destruction. Bleak chaos reigned everywhere. The floor was littered with every imaginable shape and size of broken glass and china. Fragments of pictures, looking as if chewed by a prehistoric beast, lay amid heaps of shattered statuettes and vases. As Sally moved slowly into the room, china crackled beneath her feet. Surveying the stripped walls, she turned to Gerald for an explanation.

Gerald had subsided onto an occasional table, weeping softly again. It had struck him once more that he had been terribly, terribly mistreated.

"Well!" Sally gasped. "You've certainly made a good job of it!"

There was a sharp crack as the occasional table, never designed to bear heavy weights, collapsed in a splintering flurry of broken legs under the pressure of the homeowner. Sally's mood underwent an abrupt change.
