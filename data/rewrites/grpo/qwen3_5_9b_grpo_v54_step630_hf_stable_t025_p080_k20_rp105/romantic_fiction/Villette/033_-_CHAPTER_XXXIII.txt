May Day dawned with a promise of warmth, the air thick with the song of birds and a light mist that hinted at the sun to come. For the twenty boarders and four teachers, the morning began at five, a collective rising to prepare for a march led by M. Paul Emanuel. It was a fulfillment of a promise he had made to take us to breakfast in the country. I, who had initially been excluded from such plans, found my hesitation silenced by a sharp tug on my ear and a stern, imperial command from M. Emanuel himself. "Je vous conseille de vous faire prier," he warned, his tone carrying the weight of Napoleon. One such rebuke was sufficient; I resolved to join the procession.

The streets were quiet, the boulevards fresh as fields, and we moved along with a shared sense of joy. M. Paul possessed a unique ability to impart momentum to happiness, just as he could instill fear in moments of dread. He walked neither ahead nor behind but within the line, addressing everyone, though his conversation flowed most freely with his favorites. I, however, sought to remain slightly apart. Paired with Ginevra Fanshawe, I bore the weight of her arm, a burden of loveliness that I found increasingly difficult to carry as the day warmed. My strategy was to use her as a shield between myself and M. Paul, shifting positions constantly to avoid his gaze. My motivation was rooted in vanity; my new print dress was pink, a color that made me feel exposed under his scrutiny, much like a woman in a red-bordered shawl walking through a meadow where a bull grazed.

My efforts to evade him were futile. No matter how I shifted or adjusted my black silk scarf, M. Paul inevitably found himself beside Miss Fanshawe. Their relationship had never been smooth; his temper crisped at the sound of her English accent, and their dispositions clashed. He found her empty and affected; she viewed him as bearish and meddling. After changing his position six times with no success, he thrust his head forward, his eyes locking onto mine with impatience. "Qu'est-ce que c'est? Vous me jouez des tours?" he demanded.

Before I could explain, he grasped the root of my evasion. "A-h-h! c'est la robe rose!" he exclaimed, his voice dropping to an irate growl reminiscent of a lord of the meadow. "It is only cotton," I pleaded hurriedly, "cheaper and washes better than any other color." "Et Mademoiselle Lucy est coquette comme dix Parisiennes," he retorted, pointing to my hat, gloves, and boots. "Have you ever seen an Englishwoman like this? Look at her attire!" Though my companions wore similar items, none were smarter, perhaps even plainer, yet M. Paul had found his pretext. I braced myself for a lecture, but it passed as mildly as a summer storm's threat. With a single, bantering smile, he declared, "Courage! To tell the truth, I am not angry; perhaps I am even pleased that you have dressed so beautifully for my little festival."

"Ma robe n'est pas belle, Monsieur—elle n'est que propre," I insisted. "J'aime la propreté," he replied, dismissing my concern. The sun of his good humor triumphed that morning, consuming any clouds of irritation before they could darken his disposition.

We entered the countryside, amidst woods and lanes that would soon turn dusty but now glowed with May greenness and morning repose. We halted at a well surrounded by an orderly circle of lime-trees, ordered to sit on the grassy swell. M. Paul took his place in our midst, gathering those who liked him closest, while the fearful kept their distance. He began to tell a story, narrating with a diction that children loved and scholars emulated—simple in strength, strong in simplicity. His tale contained beautiful touches, sweet glimpses of feeling and hues of description that sank into my mind and never faded. He painted a twilight scene so vivid I felt I had never seen such a picture from an artist's pencil.

I lacked the impromptu faculty to create such beauty, which made me marvel more at his perfection. M. Emanuel was not a man of books, yet his mind was a library overflowing with wealth rarely found in print. To me, intellectually imperfect and weary of printed pages, his thoughts were collyrium to the spirit's eyes, clearing inward sight. I often wondered what delight it would be for someone who loved him as deeply as he loved himself to gather those handfuls of gold dust he so recklessly scattered to the winds.

After his story, he approached the knoll where I and Ginevra sat. "Were you interested?" he asked, demanding an opinion rather than waiting for it. "Yes," I replied in my usual undemonstrative fashion. "Was it good?" "Very good." "Yet I could not write that down," he said. "Why not, Monsieur?" "I hate the mechanical labour; I hate to stoop and sit still. I could dictate it, though, with pleasure, to an amanuensis who suited me. Would Mademoiselle Lucy write for me if I asked her?"

"Monsieur would be too quick; he would urge me, and be angry if my pen did not keep pace with his lips." "Try some day; let us see the monster I can make of myself under the circumstances. But just now, there is no question of dictation; I mean to make you useful in another office. Do you see yonder farm-house?" "Surrounded with trees? Yes." "There we are to breakfast; and while the good fermière makes the café au lait in a caldron, you and five others, whom I shall select, will spread with butter half a hundred rolls."

Forming a line, he marched us to the farm, which surrendered without resistance. Clean knives, fresh plates, and butter were provided, and we prepared a huge basket of rolls. Coffee, chocolate, cream, and new-laid eggs awaited us. M. Paul, always generous, would have ordered ham and preserves, but some of us, presuming on our influence, insisted it would be a reckless waste. He railed at us, calling us "des ménagères avares," but we managed the economy of the repast our own way.

He stood on the kitchen hearth with a pleasant countenance, happy to see others happy, surrounded by movement and abundance. We asked where he would sit; knowing he considered himself our slave and we his tyrants, we placed him in the farmer's great chair at the head of the long table. It was easy to like him, with all his passions and hurricanes, when he could be so benignant and docile. At worst, only his nerves were irritable, not his temper; soothe him, and he was a lamb who would not harm a fly.

Mindful of his religion, he made the youngest say a prayer before breakfast, crossing himself with the devoted simplicity of a woman. I had never seen him pray before, nor make that pious sign, yet he did so with child-like faith. I smiled pleasurably as I watched; his eyes met my smile, and he stretched out his kind hand. "Donnez-moi la main! I see we worship the same God, in the same spirit, though by different rites." Most of his brother professors were free-thinkers, infidels, or men whose lives lacked scrutiny, but M. Paul was like a knight of old, religious in his way and of spotless fame. Innocent childhood and beautiful youth were safe at his side. His vivid passions and keen feelings were kept in check by his pure honor and artless piety.

The meal was merry, led and controlled by M. Paul, whose social temper played unfettered among women and children. Afterward, the party was free to run and play in the meadows, though a few stayed to help the farmer's wife. M. Paul called me from among them to sit near him under a tree, where he could view the troop gamboling while he smoked a cigar. I sat at the tree-root, reading a pocket classic by Corneille—a book I disliked but he enjoyed, finding beauties I could not perceive. He listened with a sweetness of calm that impressed me, given the impetuosity of his nature. The deepest happiness filled his blue eye and smoothed his broad forehead. I, too, was happy—happy with the bright day, happier with his presence, happiest with his kindness.

By and by, he asked if I would not rather run to my companions. I said no, feeling content to be where he was. He asked if, as his sister, I would always be content to stay with a brother such as he. I believed I would, and I felt it. Then he inquired if I would be sorry if he left Villette and went far away. I dropped Corneille and made no reply. "Petite sœur," he said, "how long could you remember me if we were separated?" "That, Monsieur, I can never tell, because I do not know how long it will be before I shall cease to remember everything earthly." "If I were to go beyond seas for two—three—five years, should you welcome me on my return?" "Monsieur, how could I live in the interval?" "Pourtant j'ai été pour vous bien dur, bien exigeant."

I hid my face with the book, tears covering it. I asked why he spoke so, and he said he would not talk so again, cheering me with kindest encouragement. Yet, the gentleness with which he treated me throughout the day went to my heart; it was too tender, too mournful. I would rather he had been abrupt, whimsical, and irate as was his wont.

When hot noon arrived, the day glowing as June, the shepherd collected the sheep, and we began the soft journey home. A whole league lay between us and Villette, and the children were tired, their spirits flagging at the prospect of walking over flinty, glaring, dusty roads. This had been foreseen. Just beyond the farm boundary, we met two spacious vehicles hired for school parties. With good management, room was found for all, and in another hour, M. Paul safely consigned his charge at the Rue Fossette. It had been a pleasant day, perfect save for a breath of melancholy that had dimmed its sunshine for a moment.

That tarnish returned the same evening. Just about sunset, I saw M. Emanuel emerge from the front door accompanied by Madame Beck. They paced the center alley for nearly an hour, talking earnestly; he looked grave and restless, she amazed and dissuasive. I wondered what was discussed. When Madame Beck re-entered the house as darkness fell, leaving her kinsman Paul lingering in the garden, I thought to myself: "He called me 'petite sœur' this morning. If he were really my brother, how I should like to go to him just now and ask what presses on his mind. See how he leans against that tree, arms crossed, brow bent. He wants consolation; I know it. Madame does not console; she only remonstrates. What now?"

Starting from quiescence to action, M. Paul strode erect and quick down the garden. The carré doors were still open. I thought he was going to water the orange-trees, his occasional custom, but reaching the court, he took an abrupt turn toward the berceau and the first-classe glass door. There, in that classroom where I had been watching him, I found no courage to await his approach. He had turned so suddenly, strode so fast, looked so strange; the coward within me grew pale, shrank, and fled on the wings of panic.

I did not pause until I took sanctuary in the empty oratory. Listening with beating pulses and an undefined apprehension, I heard him pass through all the schoolrooms, clashing doors impatiently. I heard him invade the refectory, where the "lecture pieuse" was held under hallowed constraint. I heard him pronounce these words: "Où est Mademoiselle Lucie?"

Just as I summoned my courage to go down and do what I most wished in the world—to meet him—the wiry voice of St. Pierre replied glibly and falsely, "Elle est au lit." He passed, stamped with vexation, into the corridor. There, Madame Beck met, captured, chided, convoyed to the street-door, and finally dismissed him.

As the street-door closed, a sudden amazement at my own perverse proceeding struck like a blow. I felt from the first that it was me he wanted, me he was seeking. Had not I wanted him too? What had carried me away? What had rapt me beyond his reach? He had something to tell; he was going to tell me that something. My ear strained to hear it, and I had made the confidence impossible. Yearning to listen and console, thinking audience and solace beyond hope's reach, the moment opportunity suddenly and fully arrived, I evaded it as I would have evaded the leveled shaft of mortality.

Well, my insane inconsistency had its reward. Instead of the comfort and certain satisfaction I might have won—could I have put down the choking panic and stood firm for two minutes—here was dead blank, dark doubt, and drear suspense. I took my wages to my pillow and passed the night counting them.
