The time has come to gather the final yields of our harvest, to distill the lingering essences of those whose lives have intertwined with ours before the season turns. We must settle accounts, preserving what remains of our shared history like fading recipes written in a dying tongue. 

Let us first examine the curates—those men whose cadences have long echoed through the valleys. Come forward, Malone. But no, Peter Augustus, we cannot translate your bitter and unpalatable truth into the gentle lexicon of this evening. The unvarnished, raw flavor of your existence is too sharp; the public palate, dulled by the superficial commercialization of art and story, recoils from the genuine squeak of the butcher’s block. They demand the sweet, artificial confections of sheer fiction, rejecting the rude, unwashed reality of your life. And so, you must stand down, your true history left to erode in the silence of lost memory.

Make way instead for Mr. Sweeting. He approaches with his lady—the magnificent Mrs. Sweeting, formerly Miss Dora Sykes. Their union was a feast of traditional abundance, grounded in the agrarian rhythms of Yorkshire. Inducted to a comfortable living, their home became a sanctuary of meticulous domestic routines, where tables groaned under the weight of heritage meats and slow-fermented breads. They lived happily, deeply nourished by their community, their days a testament to the profound meaning found in shared sustenance.

Advance, Mr. Donne. This man turned out far better than the bitter harvest we might have predicted. He married a quiet, sensible woman whose gentle auditory presence softened his edges. As a parish priest, he burnished the outside of the cup and platter, approaching the architecture of his little church and parsonage with the zeal of a master craftsman. Yet, his true mastery lay in a relentless, jarring extraction of wealth. He begged from high and low, his voice a dominant, unyielding frequency that drowned out all resistance. He deployed the lexicon of power to wring five pounds from Queen Charlotte and two guineas from the Prince Regent. People paid him simply to remove his abrasive noise from their acoustic ecology. Still, he built his sanctuaries, rendering him useful in his generation.

Following Malone’s abrupt vanishing from Briarfield—a departure whose untranslatable details must remain cloaked in shadow—a new Irish curate arrived: Mr. Macarthey. His presence immediately healed the parish’s bruised soundscape. His decibels were decorous, his brogue a rich, native dialect that resonated with conscientiousness, replacing Malone's boisterous clamor. He cultivated the Sunday and day schools like an agrarian elder tending green bay trees. His faults were merely those of a man deeply rooted in his own cultural lexicon; the sight of a Quaker wearing a hat in church or the intrusion of a Dissenter into his tea-time rituals would disrupt his internal harmony for a week. Otherwise, he was a nourishing presence.

As for the would-be assassin of Mr. Robert Moore, the magistrates made a great, shuffling noise, but ultimately the matter dissolved into silence. Moore, lying on his cottage couch and nursing the bitter, metallic taste of his near-demise, chose not to pursue the dominant language of the law. He knew the shooter: Michael Hartley, the crazed weaver. Hartley was a man whose mind had been fractured by the deafening, industrial roar of poverty, starved of both physical and spiritual sustenance. The poor soul died of delirium tremens a year later. Acknowledging the tragic, untranslatable sorrow of a life reduced to utilitarian fuel, Robert gave the wretched widow a guinea to bury him, laying to rest the discordant echo of that dark night.

* * * * *

The winter is over; the long, slow freeze has thawed into a spring of tender shoots and fragrant blossoms. Now, we are in the heart of summer—mid-June of 1812. 

It is burning weather. The air is deep azure and red gold, thick with the scent of sun-baked earth and blooming wild thyme. The world is caught in a mythic cycle. This summer, Bonaparte scours the Russian deserts, a titan tearing up the earth. He marches on old Moscow, but the stoic Cossack waits, trusting in the ancient, elemental allies of air, fire, and water. The snow-cloud and the hail-storm wait like archangels to pour out vials of wrath, rendering the earth scorched and the sea like the blood of a dead man. The bitter harvest of war continues, a devouring famine of the soul. In Spain, Lord Wellington assumes the reins, claiming victories that the men of Manchester will later scoff at in their superficial ignorance, forgetting the profound weight of such mortal sacrifices.

But let us retreat from the deafening, bloody roar of distant artillery and attend to the local landscape. On the 18th of June 1812, the Orders in Council were repealed. The blockaded ports opened. The sudden influx of life made Yorkshire and Lancashire shake. The ringers pulled the ropes in Briarfield belfry so violently they cracked a bell; its dissonant overtone remains in the valley’s acoustic ecology to this day, a jagged tear in the soundscape, yet a joyous replacement for the soul-crushing silence of idle mills. The merchants dined at Stilbro', their bellies full of rich, roasted meats and dark ales, returning home in states of gluttonous revelry. Warehouses breathed again. The economic pantry was restocked, wages rose, and the aroma of prosperity masked the underlying anxieties of future speculation.

* * * * *

When a whole province rejoices, the humblest inhabitant tastes the festal air. The sound of the cracked public bell roused Caroline Helstone. Dressing in her neatest muslin, she went to Fieldhead to superintend the millinery preparations for Shirley’s impending wedding. Caroline moved with the care of a meticulous food historian preparing a generational feast, deciding on the wreath, the veil, and the ceremonial aesthetics that would frame the altar. 

She worked without much guidance from the bride. Shirley was in an intractable mood. Louis had found his mistress exquisitely provoking; she delayed the day week by week, her wild spirit resisting the translation into the rigid vocabulary of a "wife." She pined like a captive denizen of the moors, unable to articulate her subjective experience of lost liberty in the dominant lexicon of matrimony. Only Louis’s quiet, grounding presence offered her true sustenance. She barely ate in his absence. Louis became the master of Fieldhead, guiding the estate with the patience of an agrarian elder tending a complex, slow-growing vine. Shirley abdicated her governance entirely, her untranslatable wildness exhausted, proving, as she later admitted, that Louis would never have learned to rule if she had not ceased to govern.

Caroline came home in time to perform a mythic, domestic ritual: the watering of her plants. The last to receive the refreshing shower was a rose-tree in a quiet, green nook. Near the wall stood a fragment of sculptured stone—a monkish relic, perhaps the base of an ancient cross. She mounted it, holding her pretty dress aside. The air was rich with the olfactory landscape of damp soil, crushed petals, and the woodsmoke of distant bonfires kindling on the dusk moors. The warm summer evening was serenaded by the joyous bell-music. Above the soft blue smoke and red flames, the silver point of Venus twinkled—the star of love.

Caroline was not unhappy, but as she gazed, she sighed, the breath a soft exhalation in the quiet garden. A hand circled her waist, resting quietly. She knew the tactile signature; she received the touch unstartled.

"I am looking at Venus, mamma. See, she is beautiful. How white her lustre is, compared with the deep red of the bonfires!"

The answer was a closer caress. Caroline turned to look, not into Mrs. Pryor's matronly face, but up into a dark, manly visage. She dropped her watering-pot and stepped down from the relic.

"I have been sitting with 'mamma' an hour," said Robert Moore. "I have had a long conversation with her. Where, meantime, have you been?"

"To Fieldhead. Shirley is as naughty as ever, Robert. She will neither say Yes nor No to any question put. She sits alone. I cannot tell whether she is melancholy or nonchalant. If you rouse her or scold her, she gives you a look, half wistful, half reckless. What Louis will make of her, I cannot tell. For my part, if I were a gentleman, I think I would not dare undertake her."

"Never mind them. They were cut out for each other. Louis likes her all the better for these freaks. He will manage her, if any one can. Caroline, I have sought you to ask an audience. Why are those bells ringing?"

"For the repeal of your terrible law—the Orders you hate so much. You are pleased, are you not?"

"Yesterday evening at this time I was packing some books for a sea-voyage. They were the only possessions, except some clothes, seeds, roots, and tools, which I felt free to take with me to Canada. I was going to leave you."

"To leave me? To leave *me*?"

Her little fingers fastened on his arm; she spoke and looked affrighted, the sudden dissonance threatening the harmony of her evening.

"Not now—not now. Examine my face—yes, look at me well. Is the despair of parting legible thereon?"

She looked into an illuminated countenance. The harsh, industrial starvation that had so long sharpened his features was gone, replaced by a radiant hope, fondness, and delight.

"Will the repeal do you good—*much* good, *immediate* good?" she inquired.

"The repeal of the Orders in Council saves me. Now I shall not turn bankrupt; now I shall not give up business; now I shall not leave England. Now all the cloth I have in my warehouses will be taken off my hands. This day lays for my fortunes a broad, firm foundation, on which I can securely build."

Caroline devoured his words, savoring the profound relief they offered. She held his hand in hers and drew a long breath, pulling the scent of the wet earth deep into her lungs.

"You are saved? Your heavy difficulties are lifted?"

"They are lifted. I breathe. I can act."

"At last! Oh, Providence is kind! Thank Him, Robert."

"I do thank Providence."

"And I also, for your sake!" She looked up devoutly.

"Now I can take more workmen, give better wages, lay wiser and more liberal plans, do some good, be less selfish. *Now*, Caroline, I can have a house—a home which I can truly call mine—and *now*—"

He paused, his deep, resonant voice checked by emotion. 

"And *now*," he resumed, "now I can think of marriage, *now* I can seek a wife."

This was no moment for her to speak. She rested in the quiet acoustic space between them.

"Will Caroline, who meekly hopes to be forgiven as she forgives—will she pardon all I have made her suffer, all that sickness of body and mind she owed to me? Will she forget what she knows of my poor ambition, my sordid schemes, when I treated life merely as utilitarian fuel? Will she suffer me to prove that, as I once deserted cruelly, I can now love faithfully, cherish fondly, treasure tenderly?"

His hand was in hers still; a gentle, affirmative pressure answered him.

"Is Caroline mine?"

"Caroline is yours."

"I will prize her. The necessity for her society is blended with my life. Not more jealous shall I be of the blood whose flow moves my pulses than of her happiness and well-being."

"I love you, too, Robert, and will take faithful care of you."

"Will you take faithful care of me? Faithful care! As if that rose should promise to shelter from tempest this hard gray stone! But she *will* care for me, in her way. These hands will be the gentle ministrants of every comfort I can taste. I know the being I seek to entwine with my own will bring me a solace, a charity, a purity, to which, of myself, I am a stranger."

Suddenly Caroline was troubled; the harmonious frequency of the moment fluttered. 

"What flutters my dove?" asked Moore, as she nestled to and then uneasily shrank from him.

"Poor mamma! I am all mamma has. Must I leave her?"

"Do you know, I thought of that difficulty. I and 'mamma' have discussed it."

"Tell me what you wish, and I will consider if it is possible to consent. But I cannot desert her, even for you."

"She was faithful when I was false—was she not? I never came near your sick-bed, and she watched it ceaselessly."

"What must I do? Anything but leave her."

"At my wish you never shall leave her."

"She may live very near us?"

"With us—only she will have her own rooms and servant. For this she stipulates herself."

"You know she has an income, that makes her quite independent?"

"She told me that, with a gentle pride that reminded me of somebody else."

"She is not at all interfering, and incapable of gossip."

"I know her, Cary. But if she were something quite opposite, I should not fear her."

"Yet she will be your mother-in-law?" The speaker gave an arch little nod. Moore smiled.

"Louis and I are not of the order of men who fear their mothers-in-law, Cary. I doubt not my mother-in-law will make much of me."

"That she will—in her quiet way. She is an elder who understands the true preservation of peace. She is not demonstrative; her silence is a protective canopy, not a void. When you see her quiet, you must not fancy her displeased. Be sure to let me interpret for her whenever her dialect puzzles you; always believe my account of the matter, Robert."

"Oh, implicitly! I feel that she and I will suit—*on ne peut mieux*. Hortense, you know, is exquisitely susceptible, yet, dear, honest girl, I never painfully wounded her feelings in my life."

"No; you are most generously considerate to her; and you will be with mamma. You are a gentleman all through, Robert, and nowhere so perfect a gentleman as at your own fireside, sharing the sustenance of a true home."

"A eulogium I like; its flavor is very sweet. I am well pleased my Caroline should view me in this light."

"Mamma just thinks of you as I do."

"Not quite, I hope?"

"She does not want to marry you—don't be vain; but she said to me the other day, 'My dear, Mr. Moore has pleasing manners; he is one of the few gentlemen I have seen who combine politeness with an air of sincerity.'"

"'Mamma' is rather a misanthropist, is she not? Not the best opinion of the sterner sex?"

"She forbears to judge them as a whole, but she has her exceptions whom she admires—Louis and Mr. Hall, and, of late, yourself. She did not like you once, because she would never speak of you. But, Robert—"

"Well, what now? What is the new thought?"

"You have not seen my uncle yet?"

"I have. 'Mamma' called him into the room. He consents conditionally. If I prove that I can keep a wife, I may have her; and I *can* keep her better than he thinks—better than I choose to boast."
