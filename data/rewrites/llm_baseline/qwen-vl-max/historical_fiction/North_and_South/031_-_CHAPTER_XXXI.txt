“Show not that manner, and these features all,
The serpent’s cunning, and the sinner’s fall?”

The October morning crept in with a chill, damp breath—no golden mist to veil the world in softness, no sun to burn through it like fire, but a thick, clinging fog that choked the streets of Milton, turning them into dim corridors of grey. It was not the autumn of the countryside, where leaves turned crimson and gold under clear skies; here, the season wore a shroud, and even the light struggled to pierce the gloom.

Margaret moved slowly through the house, helping Dixon prepare for the day. Her eyes were heavy, her vision blurred by tears she could not afford to shed openly. The weight of grief pressed down on her shoulders, yet she carried it without complaint, for her father and brother leaned upon her now more than ever. She was the only one who could think, plan, act—while they were lost in sorrow, she must remain steady.

When the fire crackled warmly in the grate and the kettle sang its familiar tune, Margaret paused to glance around the room before calling her father and Frederick to breakfast. She wanted everything to appear cheerful, as if life still held some measure of peace. But the effort only deepened her sadness—the contrast between the bright hearth and her inner desolation brought fresh waves of emotion. She sank to her knees beside the sofa, burying her face in the cushions so no one would hear her cry.

Then a hand touched her shoulder.

“Come, Miss Hale—come, my dear,” said Dixon gently. “You mustn’t give way. There’s no one else in this house who can manage a thing. So much must be done—funeral arrangements, who shall come, where it shall be held… Master Frederick is half out of his mind with weeping, and your father… he walks like a man who has lost his way. I know it’s hard, miss—but death comes to us all. And you’ve been blessed: never lost a friend till now.”

Perhaps. But this loss was different. It was not just the end of a life—it was the unraveling of a world. Margaret did not find comfort in Dixon’s words, but the old woman’s quiet tenderness stirred something deep within her. Out of gratitude, more than anything else, she lifted her head, wiped her eyes, and offered a fragile smile. Then she rose and went to summon her family.

Mr. Hale entered as though in a trance—his movements automatic, his gaze distant, as if he saw only ghosts. Frederick came in briskly, forcing cheerfulness, grasping Margaret’s hand, looking into her eyes—and then breaking down in sudden tears.

Breakfast passed in silence broken only by small talk, trivial remarks meant to keep their minds from wandering back to the last meal they’d shared, when every sound had seemed a possible signal from the sickroom.

Afterward, Margaret resolved to speak with her father about the funeral. He nodded weakly, agreeing to everything she suggested—even when her proposals contradicted each other. No real decision emerged from him. As she turned to leave, he called her back.

“Ask Mr. Bell,” he said, voice hollow.

“Mr. Bell?” she repeated, surprised.

“Mr. Bell of Oxford,” he added. “He was my groom’s-man.”

She understood at once. “I’ll write to him today,” she promised.

And with that, he slipped again into silence.

All morning, Margaret worked—tired, overwhelmed, yet moving forward through a sea of melancholy tasks. By evening, Dixon approached her with news.

“I’ve done it, miss,” she began. “I was afraid for your father—he might have a stroke from grief. He’s been with poor missus all day, talking to her as if she were alive. When I listened at the door, I heard him speaking to her over and over. When I stepped in, he’d grow quiet, but utterly confused. So I thought—someone ought to rouse him. Even if it shocks him at first, it may help later.”

“And what did you do?” asked Margaret.

“I told him,” Dixon replied, “that I don’t believe it’s safe for Master Frederick to stay here.”

Margaret stiffened. “Why?”

“Well, it was Tuesday when I met a Southampton man—the first I’ve seen since coming to Milton. They don’t often come up this way. It was young Leonards—son of old Mr. Leonards, the draper. A scamp, the worst kind. He drove his father nearly mad, then ran off to sea. I never liked him. He was aboard the *Orion* at the same time as Frederick—I don’t recall if he was there during the mutiny.”

“Did he recognize you?” Margaret asked, her voice tight with alarm.

“That’s the trouble,” Dixon said. “I don’t think he would’ve known me, except I called out his name. He was a stranger in a strange place—so I didn’t hesitate. ‘Miss Dixon!’ he said. ‘Who’d have thought of seeing you here? Perhaps I’m mistaken—you’re not Miss Dixon anymore?’”

“I told him he could still call me an unmarried lady,” she continued, “though I might’ve had chances for marriage if I hadn’t been so particular. He said he couldn’t look at me and doubt me. But I wouldn’t take his nonsense. So I asked after his father—knowing full well he’d been turned out of the house—as if they were the best of friends. To spite me, he started asking after Master Frederick. Said how he’d gotten into a terrible scrape—like as if our boy’s troubles could clean George Leonards’ reputation! Said he’d be hanged for mutiny if caught, that a hundred pounds reward was offered, that he’d disgraced his family…”

Dixon paused, her voice dropping. “To make me suffer, you see. Because I’ve helped old Mr. Leonards give George a good dressing-down before. So I said there were other families who had far more reason to blush—and to be thankful their sons were earning honest livings far away.”

He answered, smug as ever, “I’m in a confidential position. If you know any young man who’s led a bad life and wants to turn over a new leaf, I’d be happy to lend him my patronage.”

“Him!” Dixon spat. “He’d corrupt a saint. I felt worse than I have in years standing there talking to him. I wanted to lash out, but he kept smiling, as if I were serious. And I couldn’t tell if he cared what I said at all—while I was furious inside.”

“You didn’t tell him anything about us—about Frederick?” Margaret asked, breathless.

“No, miss. He never asked where I was staying. I wouldn’t have told him if he had. Nor did I ask what his precious job was. He was waiting for a bus, and it came. He hailed it, but before boarding, he turned back and said—‘If you can help me trap Lieutenant Hale, Miss Dixon, we’ll go partners in the reward. I know you’d like to be my partner—wouldn’t you? Don’t be shy, say yes.’ Then he jumped on, and I saw his ugly face leering at me, laughing because he’d had the last word.”

Margaret felt a cold dread settle in her chest.

“Have you told Frederick?” she asked.

“No,” said Dixon. “I was uneasy knowing Leonards was in town—but there was so much else. But when I saw your father sitting so rigid, eyes glazed, I thought—maybe it would rouse him to know Frederick might be in danger. That he’d have to go, poor fellow, before Mr. Bell arrives.”

“I’m not afraid of Mr. Bell,” Margaret murmured. “But I am afraid of Leonards. I must tell Frederick. What did he look like?”

“A bad-looking fellow,” Dixon said firmly. “Red whiskers—so red I’d be ashamed to wear them. And despite saying he had a confidential post, he was dressed in fustian like a laborer.”

Frederick had to leave. Not just now—but immediately. He had only recently found his place in the family, promising strength and support. His devotion to their mother, his grief for her absence, had made him seem almost bound to them by a shared sorrow. Now, that bond was about to break.

As Margaret sat by the fire, lost in thought, her father paced restlessly, haunted by the fear Dixon had awakened—fear he had not yet voiced.

Then Frederick entered, his usual brightness dimmed, but the raw pain of grief softened. He came to Margaret, kissed her forehead.

“How pale you are, Margaret,” he whispered. “You’ve thought of everyone—no one has thought of you. Lie down. There’s nothing left for you to do.”

“It’s the worst,” she said softly.

But she obeyed, lying on the sofa. He covered her feet with a shawl and sat beside her on the floor. They spoke quietly, sharing thoughts.

She told him of Dixon’s encounter.

Frederick exhaled sharply. “I’d just love to confront that fellow. A worse sailor never sailed—nor a worse man. Margaret—you know how it happened?”

“Yes,” she said. “Mamma told me.”

“Well, when all the decent sailors were angry with our captain, this one tried to curry favor—disgusting. And now he’s here? Oh, if he knew I were near, he’d hunt me down to settle old scores. I’d rather anyone took the hundred pounds than that scoundrel. What a pity poor old Dixon couldn’t betray me and make a fortune for herself!”

“Oh, Frederick, hush,” she said, alarmed.

Mr. Hale approached, trembling, having overheard. He took Frederick’s hand in both of his.

“My boy,” he said, “you must go. It’s terrible—but I see you must. You’ve done all you could. You’ve been a comfort to her.”

“Oh, papa, must he go?” Margaret pleaded, though she knew the answer.

“I’d rather face trial,” Frederick said. “If I could only gather my evidence. I can’t bear being at the mercy of such a villain. In other circumstances, this stolen visit would have been thrilling—just like those forbidden pleasures the Frenchwoman spoke of.”

“One of the earliest things I remember,” Margaret said, “was you stealing apples, Fred. We had plenty—trees full of them. But someone told you stolen fruit tasted sweetest, and you believed it _au pied de la lettre_. Off you went robbing. You haven’t changed much, have you?”

“Not really,” he said, smiling faintly.

“Yes—you must go,” Mr. Hale repeated, answering Margaret’s earlier question.

His mind was fixed on one thing. He could not follow the twists of his children’s conversation.

Margaret and Frederick exchanged a glance—one filled with understanding, with sorrow, with the unspoken knowledge that this moment of closeness would soon be gone.

Frederick broke the silence. “You know, Margaret, I nearly gave both you and Dixon a fright this afternoon. I was in my room. I heard the front doorbell ring, but assumed the visitor had left. I was about to step into the hall when I opened my door—and there was Dixon, descending the stairs. She frowned and kicked me back into hiding. I kept the door open and heard a message given to a man in Father’s study. Then he left. Who could it have been? One of the shopmen?”

“Likely,” Margaret said, distracted.

“There was a little man who came about two o’clock,” she added.

“But this wasn’t a little man—a big, strong fellow. And it was past four.”

“It was Mr. Thornton,” Mr. Hale said.

They were glad to shift the topic.

“Mr. Thornton?” Margaret said, surprised.

“I thought—” she began, then stopped.

“What did you think?” Frederick asked, amused.

“Oh,” she said, blushing, “I thought you meant someone of a lower class—not a gentleman. Someone on business.”

“He looked like that,” Frederick said carelessly. “I took him for a shopman. Turns out he’s a manufacturer.”

Margaret fell silent. She remembered how, at first, she too had judged him by appearance. It was natural—yet she felt a flicker of annoyance. She wanted Frederick to understand who Mr. Thornton truly was—but she couldn’t bring herself to explain.

Mr. Hale continued. “He came to offer help. But I couldn’t see him. I told Dixon to ask if he wished to speak with you—I think I asked her to find you.”

“He’s been a very agreeable acquaintance, hasn’t he?” Frederick asked, tossing the question into the air.

“A very kind friend,” Margaret said, stepping in.

Frederick was quiet for a while. Then he spoke.

“Margaret, it hurts to think I can never thank those who’ve shown you kindness. Our lives must part ways. Unless… unless I risk a court-martial. Or unless you and Father come to Spain.”

He threw out the idea lightly, testing the waters. Then he plunged in.

“You don’t know how much I wish you would. I have a good position—chances for more. Dolores Barbour—remember I told you of her? I wish you could meet her. I’m sure you’d—love her. Father, if you knew her, you’d love her too. She’s not eighteen, but if she feels the same next year, she’ll be my wife. Mr. Barbour won’t let us call it an engagement. But if you came, you’d find friends everywhere—especially Dolores. Think of it, Father. Let Margaret stand by me.”

“No,” Mr. Hale said firmly. “No more moves for me. One move cost me my wife. No more in this life. She will be here—and I will stay until my time ends.”

“Oh, Frederick,” Margaret said, “tell us more. I never thought of this—but I’m so glad. You’ll have someone to love and care for. Tell us all.”

“In the first place,” he said, “she’s Roman Catholic. That’s the only objection I foresaw. But my father’s change of heart—don’t sigh, Margaret.”

She sighed anyway. For Frederick himself was Roman Catholic in truth, though not yet in profession. This explained why his sympathy for her distress over her father’s departure from the Church had been so muted in his letters. She had thought it mere sailor’s indifference. But the truth was deeper—he had already begun to drift from the faith of his baptism, drawn toward beliefs opposite to his father’s. How much love shaped that change, even he could not say.

Margaret dropped the subject. But then she returned to it, thinking anew.

“For her sake, Fred, you must try to clear yourself. Even if the charge of mutiny is true, there may be a way to show your disobedience was justified—because the authority was abused.”

Mr. Hale stirred, listening.

“First,” Frederick said, “who would find my witnesses? All but a few are scattered across the fleet. And those who stayed were involved or sympathetic. Second—don’t think a court-martial is justice. It’s authority weighing nine-tenths, evidence barely one-tenth. Even testimony can be swayed by power.”

“But isn’t it worth trying? To see what proof might be found? Right now, people believe you guilty without excuse. You’ve never defended yourself. We’ve never known where to look. For Dolores’ sake, you should prove your innocence. Disobeying orders was wrong—but standing by while cruelty was used would have been worse. People know what you did—but not why. Your action wasn’t crime—it was heroism. They ought to know.”

“But how?” Frederick asked. “I can’t trust the judges. I can’t send a bellman shouting my heroism through the streets. No one would read a pamphlet years later. And even if I did—what good would it do?”

“Will you consult a lawyer?” Margaret asked, her voice rising slightly.

“I must first find one I can trust. Many a barrister might twist conscience to earn a hundred pounds by handing me over.”

“Nonsense!” she said. “I know a man—honorable, skilled, and connected to Aunt Shaw. Mr. Henry Lennox. He’d do much for one of her relations.”

“I think it’s a good idea,” Mr. Hale said. “But don’t propose anything that keeps Frederick here. Not for your mother’s sake.”

“We could send him to London by night train,” Margaret said, warming to the idea. “He must go tomorrow, papa—we decided, because of Mr. Bell and Dixon’s meeting.”

“Yes,” Frederick said firmly. “I must go tomorrow.”

Mr. Hale groaned. “I can’t bear to lose you—but I’m tormented by worry as long as you stay.”

“Well then,” Margaret said, “listen to my plan. He arrives in London Friday morning. I’ll write to Mr. Lennox—give him a note. You’ll find him at his chambers in the Temple.”

“I’ll write down every name I can remember from the *Orion*. I’ll leave it with him to track them down. He’s Edith’s husband’s brother, isn’t he? I remember you mentioning him. I have money with Barbour. I can pay a long bill if there’s hope. Money, dear father—that I meant for another purpose. I’ll consider it borrowed from you and Margaret.”

“Don’t,” Margaret said. “It’s risky. But worth trying. You can sail from London as easily as Liverpool?”

“Of course,” Frederick said. “Wherever I feel water beneath a plank, I’m home. I’ll find a ship—don’t worry. I won’t stay twenty-four hours in London—away from you, and from someone else.”

Margaret smiled. It was comforting that he looked over her shoulder as she wrote to Mr. Lennox. Had she not been watched, she might have hesitated, struggled with words. But she wrote steadily, clearly.

When she finished, he took the note before she could review it, tucking it into a pocket-book. From it fell a long lock of black hair.

Frederick’s eyes lit up. “Now you’d like to see that, wouldn’t you?”

“No,” he said. “Wait till you see her. She’s too perfect to be known by fragments. No mean brick shall be a specimen of the building of my palace.”
