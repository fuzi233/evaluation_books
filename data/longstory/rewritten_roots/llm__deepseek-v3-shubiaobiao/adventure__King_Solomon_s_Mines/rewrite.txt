To the adventurous hearts—both young and old—who still believe in the thrill of buried treasure, the clash of spears in distant lands, and the mysteries hidden beneath ancient suns, I dedicate this tale.

I am Allan Quatermain, a hunter, a wanderer, and now, against my better judgment, a chronicler of the most extraordinary expedition of my life. What follows is no fanciful romance, but a plain and honest account of events so strange that, were they not supported by the testimony of living men, I would scarcely credit them myself.

Twelve years have passed since this story first saw print, and yet it seems the world has not tired of it. Perhaps because truth, no matter how wild, outlasts the prettiest lies. King Solomon’s Mines, once the stuff of fireside legend, have since been found—their gold and diamonds pried from the earth by hands less scrupulous than ours. The Kukuanas, those fierce and noble warriors, have been broken by the march of so-called progress. But the tale remains.

And if, in years to come, some wide-eyed boy reads these pages by lamplight and dreams of lost cities and forgotten kings, then I shall consider my labor well spent. For adventure does not die—it merely waits.

—Allan Quatermain

Now that these pages are bound and ready to meet the eyes of the world, I feel the weight of their imperfections—both in style and substance. Let me confess at once: this is no exhaustive chronicle. Much of what we witnessed in Kukuanaland remains untold, pared down to the bones of the tale.

There were stories I longed to linger over—legends of the ancient chainmail that turned death away from us in the battle of Loo, or the silent, towering sentinels at the mouth of the cave. A scholar might have filled volumes with the distinctions between Zulu and Kukuana speech, each nuance a clue to their histories. The land itself, teeming with strange beasts and plants (I counted eight antelope unknown to me, and bulbs of shapes no botanist had sketched), deserved more than a passing note.

And then there was their army—a machine of such precision it would humble even Chaka’s Zulu regiments. No forced celibacy weakened their ranks; their mobilization swifter than any I’d seen. Their craftsmen, too, shaped iron and steel with a skill that bordered on artistry—those *tollas*, heavy throwing knives with edges of flawless steel fused to iron backs, were marvels no European smith could disdain.

Yet Sir Henry and Captain Good argued rightly: some tales are best told plain. So I’ve spared you the tangents, though I’ll gladly speak of them to any who ask. Forgive the roughness of my writing—a rifle fits my hand better than a pen. I leave the flourishes to novelists; a sharp spear needs no polish, and neither, I hope, does a true story.

At fifty-five years old, I find myself picking up a pen to write a history—a curious thing for a man like me. I’ve lived a long life by most measures, having started work young. I’ve traded, hunted, fought, and mined across the colonies, and only eight months ago did I finally make my fortune—though I wouldn’t relive the last fifteen months for it. Adventure has lost its charm for me; I’m a timid man at heart, one who dislikes violence.

So why write this?

First, because Sir Henry Curtis and Captain John Good asked me to. Second, because I’m laid up in Durban with an old wound—a lion’s bite that never quite healed. Third, for my son Harry, studying medicine in London, to give him something diverting to read. And fourth, because this is the strangest tale I’ve ever known—one without women (save Foulata, if she counts, though she was more fiend than woman).

But enough preamble. Let me begin properly.

My name is Allan Quatermain, of Durban, Natal. Some might call me a gentleman, though I’ve spent my life as a trader and hunter. I’ve killed men, but never without cause, and I’ve never stolen—save once, from a Kafir who wronged me first, a deed that still troubles me.

It was eighteen months ago that I first met Sir Henry Curtis and Captain Good. I’d been hunting elephants beyond Bamangwato with poor luck, then caught fever. After recovering, I sold my wagon and oxen, dismissed my hunters, and boarded the *Dunkeld* for Cape Town. Finding little to hold me there, I booked passage back to Natal.

Among the passengers were two men who drew my notice. The first was Sir Henry Curtis—tall, broad-shouldered, with yellow hair and a beard, and piercing grey eyes. He looked like some ancient warrior, a Dane of legend. The other was Captain John Good, stout, dark-haired, and impeccably neat, with an eyeglass perpetually fixed in his right eye. A naval man, unmistakably.

Dinner brought us together. Captain Good proved inquisitive, asking about my hunts, and when elephants came up, someone remarked that I was the man to speak on the subject. Sir Henry, who had been quiet, suddenly leaned forward.

“Excuse me,” he said in a deep voice, “but is your name Allan Quatermain?”

I confirmed it. He muttered something under his breath, and after the meal, invited me to his cabin for whisky and talk.

There, Sir Henry revealed his purpose. He was searching for his brother, a man I had met under the name Neville—a trader who had passed through Bamangwato before vanishing into the interior.

“Neville was my brother,” Sir Henry said heavily. “We quarreled years ago, and he left England for Africa. I’ve heard nothing since, save your letter confirming he was alive two years past. I must find him, Mr. Quatermain. And I hope you’ll help.”

Captain Good nodded vigorously. “We’re at a dead end otherwise.”

I hesitated. I knew a little of Neville’s fate—enough to dread what I might have to tell them. But Sir Henry’s grief was plain, and his resolve unwavering.

So began the strangest journey of my life.

The voyage from Cape Town to Durban took four to five days, depending on the ship’s speed and the weather. Landing at East London was often a gamble—if the surf was rough, we might idle for a full day before the cargo boats could reach us. This time, luck was with us. The sea lay calm, and the tugs came swiftly, hauling their clumsy barges alongside. The stevedores worked with brutal efficiency, hurling crates aboard without care for their contents. I watched a case of champagne shatter against the deck, its golden liquid foaming into the bilge. A few Kafirs in the barge snatched unbroken bottles, guzzling the sparkling wine—only to reel back, clutching their bellies, convinced the fizz was witchcraft. I called down that it was the white man’s strongest magic, and they fled in terror. I doubt they’ll touch champagne again.

As we steamed north, I pondered Sir Henry Curtis’s proposal. We spoke little of it directly, though I regaled them with hunting tales—all true, for the bush needs no embellishment. By late January, the Natal coast unfurled before us: red dunes, emerald hills, and the white lace of surf. Durban’s harbor neared as the sun dipped low, gilding the sugarcane fields and the kloofs where rivers flashed like silver threads. The land looked softer here, tamed by gardens and homesteads. A place feels incomplete without people, though I’ve known wildernesses so pure they humbled me. Eden was fair before Eve, but she made it a home.

We anchored after sunset, too late to cross the bar. Dinner passed pleasantly, and afterward, we lounged on deck under a moon so bright it paled the lighthouse beam. The air smelled of jasmine and brine; from a nearby brig, sailors’ chanteys drifted across the water. Even Sir Henry’s bulldog, usually hell-bent on murdering the ship’s caged baboon, snored peacefully.

Then Sir Henry turned to me. “Have you decided, Quatermain?”

I knocked out my pipe, buying a moment. The embers hissed into the waves. “I’ll go,” I said. “But my terms are firm: You cover all costs. Any ivory or treasure is split between Good and me. You pay me £500 now, and if I die, my son Harry gets £200 yearly for five years.”

Sir Henry agreed without hesitation.

“Now, why I’m risking my neck,” I continued. “First, I like you both. That matters on a long trek. Second, I’m a fatalist—if my time’s up, it’s up. Third, I’m poor. Elephant hunters rarely last five years; I’ve survived seven lifetimes’ worth. If I die normally, Harry inherits nothing. This way, he’s provided for.”

Sir Henry nodded. “We’re grateful. But you think it’s suicide?”

“The Suliman Mountains? Men vanish there. But I’ve made my choice.”

Good raised his glass. “Then here’s to madness!”

---

In Durban, I housed them in my cottage—three rooms with a tin roof, but a good garden. Preparations began in earnest: a sturdy wagon, twenty oxen (Zulu-bred, tough as nails), and enough supplies to last but not burden us. Good’s medical skills proved a boon; he even amputated a Kafir’s toe with flair, though the patient’s request for a “white one” baffled him.

Arming ourselves took care. We chose:
- Three heavy elephant guns (eight-bore, eleven-drachm powder loads).
- Three .500 Express rifles for medium game—or men.
- A double-barreled shotgun for birds.
- Winchesters and Colt revolvers as backups.

Servants were trickier. We hired two Zulus—Goza (driver) and Tom (leader)—and two trackers: Ventvögel, a Hottentot with a nose like a bloodhound, and Khiva, a sharp-eyed Zulu. The fifth man found us.

On our last evening, Khiva announced a visitor. The man who entered was tall, light-skinned for a Zulu, with the bearing of a chief. His head-ring marked him as *Keshla*, a ranked warrior.

“I am Umbopa,” he said. His voice carried weight. “I hear you go north, beyond the Lukanga. I would join you.”

I recognized him—a minor officer from the Zulu War, one who’d warned me of Isandhlwana’s peril. “Why?”

“I am no common tribesman. My people came from the north before the Zulus. I fought for Cetewayo, then fled to Natal. Now I wish to return.”

Sir Henry studied him. Umbopa stood stripped to his *moocha*, revealing a warrior’s body—six feet three, scarred, magnificent.

“I’ll take you,” Sir Henry said.

Umbopa’s answer was a quiet, “We are men, thou and I.”

And so our company was complete.

Our journey to Sitanda’s Kraal was no small feat—a thousand miles from Durban, the last three hundred on foot to escape the deadly tsetse fly. By May, we had reached our destination, but not without cost. Our wagon, left behind at Inyati under the watch of a missionary, stood as a lonely sentinel of civilization, guarded by our faithful driver, Goza. Only twelve oxen remained of the twenty we had started with, the rest lost to snakes, thirst, and poison.

Now we marched forward—Sir Henry, Captain Good, myself, and our small band of hired bearers—Umbopa at the lead, his deep voice breaking into a Zulu chant of brave men seeking adventure. It lifted our spirits, though I confess I doubted we’d ever see our wagon again.

Two weeks from Inyati, we found ourselves in a lush, game-rich land. The trees bore the machabell fruit, an elephant’s favorite, and the ground was trampled with their massive spoor. That evening, we made camp beside a dry riverbed, its pools ringed with the prints of thirsty beasts.

No sooner had we settled than a herd of giraffes burst from the brush, their long legs carrying them away in that odd, swaying gallop. Good, ever the impulsive marksman, took a shot—and by sheer luck, struck one clean through the neck. The creature tumbled like a felled sapling, and our Kafir companions erupted in astonished praise. *"Bougwan!"* they cried—Glass Eye—and from that moment, Good’s legend as a crack shot was sealed, however undeserved.

We feasted that night on giraffe steaks and marrow bones, the firelight flickering over our mismatched trio: Sir Henry, golden and broad; myself, dark and wiry; and Good, absurdly neat in his tweed suit, collar, and polished gaiters, as if he’d stepped from a London club rather than the African wilds.

The peace shattered when a lion’s roar split the night, followed by the furious trumpeting of elephants. We scrambled to our feet just in time to see a massive sable antelope bull crash into our clearing—impaled upon its own horns was a black-maned lion, both dead in a final, fatal embrace. The lion had ambushed the antelope at the waterhole, only to be gored and dragged to mutual doom.

We dragged the carcasses to camp, but sleep was fleeting. At dawn, we set out after the elephants, tracking their devastation through the bush. By midmorning, we found them—twenty bulls, their ears flapping lazily in the heat.

We took our shots. Sir Henry’s elephant dropped instantly. Mine staggered but fell to a second bullet. Good, however, had chosen the largest bull—and paid for it. The wounded beast turned on him, forcing him to dive aside as it thundered past toward our camp.

We pursued the fleeing herd until they funneled into a steep-banked nullah, struggling in panic to escape. It was brutal work. We felled five before the rest broke free, leaving us breathless and grimly satisfied.

On our return, we passed the spot where Good’s bull had fled—only to stumble upon a herd of eland. Ever curious, Good handed his rifle to Umbopa and crept closer with Khiva.

Then disaster struck.

The wounded bull we thought long gone erupted from the brush, a monstrous shadow against the setting sun. Good, in his cursed trousers, tripped and fell. Khiva, brave to the last, hurled his assegai—but the beast seized him, crushed him underfoot, and tore him apart.

We fired until the elephant collapsed atop Khiva’s remains. Good knelt in the dust, stricken, while Umbopa gazed down at the boy’s broken body.

*"He is dead,"* he said quietly. *"But he died like a man."*

That night, the camp was silent. Even the stars seemed dimmer.

The ivory lay buried beneath the sand now, nine elephants' worth—a fortune in tusks, the grand bull’s alone weighing near a hundred and seventy pounds. Khiva’s bones rested easier, tucked into an ant-bear’s den with an assegai for company. The dead were settled; the living marched on.

Sitanda’s Kraal marked the edge of the known world. To the west, the land rolled fertile and green, dotted with game. To the east, the desert yawned—a vast, waterless waste where the earth turned suddenly barren, as if some god had drawn a line and cursed the soil beyond it.

At dusk, Sir Henry and I climbed a stony rise. The dying sun painted the desert in gold and crimson, and far in the distance, the faint blue teeth of the Suliman Berg bit at the sky.

“There’s our wall,” I said. “God help us if we ever reach it.”

Sir Henry’s voice was quiet steel. “My brother’s beyond it. That’s enough.”

Behind us, Umbopa stood watching, his eyes fixed on the mountains. When he spoke, it was not to me, but to Henry.

“Incubu,” he said (the name meant *elephant*—a bold thing for a Kafir to call a white man), “you mean to cross that desert?”

I bristled, but Henry waved me off. “I do.”

Umbopa’s gaze never wavered. “No water. No life. Only death waits there.”

“Then I’ll meet death,” Henry replied. “But not before I find my brother.”

The Zulu’s lips curled. “A man who walks with death walks lightly. I will go with you.”

There was something in his tone that prickled my neck. This was no ordinary servant. When he spoke again, his words rolled like thunder:

We left at moonrise, our gear pared down to the bone: rifles, water-bottles, dried meat, and beads for trade. Three hired natives carried gourds of water—the first stage only. They thought us mad. By morning, they’d fled.

The desert was a furnace by day, a freezer by night. Quagga startled underfoot; flies descended in biblical swarms. We dug a shallow grave to escape the sun, burying ourselves under karoo scrub like corpses. The heat liquefied reason. By the second dawn, even the cobras had abandoned us.

Only Umbopa seemed unbroken. He walked like a man who knew the desert’s secrets—or was unafraid to learn them.

*"Those mountains,"* he said once, *"are not meant for men."*

But men go where they should not. That’s the nature of the breed.

The thirst woke me two hours later—a cruel, gnawing thirst that turned my tongue to leather and my throat to fire. It was still dark, but the heavy sleep of exhaustion had passed, leaving only the raw need for water. My lips were cracked, my eyelids gummed shut; it took effort to wrench them apart. The air was thick, hot, oppressive—nothing like the crisp dawns I knew.

The others still slept. Desperate for distraction, I fumbled for my pocket copy of *The Ingoldsby Legends* and read by the faint light. When I reached the lines about the golden ewer of pure water, I nearly laughed—or sobbed. The thought of it drove me mad. I would have drained a basin even if the Pope himself had washed his hands in it.

The others stirred, rubbing crusted eyes and peeling apart parched lips. Our situation was dire. The water bottles were bone-dry. Good eyed the brandy flask, but Sir Henry snatched it away. "Raw spirit would only kill us faster," he said grimly.

Then Ventvögel, the Hottentot, stood abruptly. He paced, nostrils flaring, then stopped and pointed at the sand. "Springbok spoor," I said. "So?"

"Springbucks do not wander far from water," he answered.

Hope, fragile as a star in a black sky, flickered in us. Ventvögel lifted his face, sniffing like an old buck scenting the wind. "*I smell water*," he declared.

At that moment, the sun rose—and so did Sheba’s Breasts.

Two colossal mountains, each fifteen thousand feet high, loomed ahead, their snow-capped summits gleaming like silver. Between them stretched the Suliman Berg, a vast spine of rock. They were shaped like a woman’s breasts, swelling from the desert, their peaks the nipples. Mists curled around them like a sleeping goddess. The sight stole my breath—and for a moment, even my thirst.

But the vision vanished as clouds swallowed the peaks. Thirst roared back, sharper than before. Ventvögel swore he smelled water, yet none was in sight—only sand and scrub. We scaled a nearby hillock, half-delirious, and there, improbably, was a murky pool in a hollow at the very top. We fell upon it like beasts, drinking, then wallowing in the brackish filth. I’d have traded a kingdom for that mud.

Revived, we pressed on under the moon, reaching the mountain’s base by dawn. The lava slopes were brutal, our feet shredded by jagged rock. By noon, we were near collapse—until Umbopa spotted a patch of green. Wild melons. We gorged, the pulp easing our thirst but not our hunger. Then, miraculously, a flock of *pauw* bustards flew overhead. I dropped one with two shots. We devoured it, bones and all.

Days blurred into torment. The cold bit deeper as we climbed. Ventvögel succumbed first, freezing to death beside me in the night. At dawn, we found another corpse in the cave—a white man, slumped as if in sleep. Terror drove us stumbling back into the light.

The mountain had claimed its first lives. It would not be the last.

The cave’s mouth loomed behind us as we stood in the brittle silence of the mountain pass, our breath curling in the thin air. The discovery within had left us shaken—not by fear, but by the eerie weight of time itself.

Sir Henry turned abruptly, his face drawn. “I must go back.”

Good frowned. “Why?”

“Because,” Sir Henry said grimly, “that corpse—it might be my brother.”

The thought had not occurred to me. Wordless, we followed him into the cavern’s gloom, our snow-dazzled eyes struggling to pierce the shadows. Slowly, the shape of the dead man resolved before us.

Sir Henry dropped to one knee, studying the frozen face. A breath escaped him. “Thank God. It’s not him.”

I stepped closer. The body was that of a tall, gaunt man, skin stretched parchment-thin over sharp bones, lips curled back in the rictus of death. His clothing had long since rotted away, save for tattered woolen leggings. Around his neck hung a yellowed ivory crucifix.

“Who in blazes is this?” I muttered.

Good gave a dry chuckle. “Don’t you see? It’s old José da Silvestra himself.”

“Impossible,” I snapped. “He died three centuries ago.”

“And why shouldn’t he keep?” Good retorted. “Cold like this could pickle a man for eternity. No sun, no beasts—just ice and silence.” He bent, retrieving a bone from the floor, its tip sharpened to a point. “Here’s his pen. The very one he used to scratch his map.”

A shiver crawled down my spine. There he sat, the long-dead Portuguese explorer, his directions leading us here across the ages. The crucifix at his throat, the bone in my hand—these were not relics in a museum, but the remnants of a man who had gasped his last in this very spot, desperate to leave his mark upon the world.

Sir Henry moved abruptly, lifting Ventvögel’s stiffened body and laying it beside the Dom. “Let them keep each other company,” he said roughly. With a jerk, he broke the brittle cord of the crucifix—too frozen to untie—and pocketed it. The bone pen I kept; it rests before me now as I write.

We left them there, the ancient noble and the Hottentot, frozen sentinels in the heart of the mountain. The sunlight outside was a blessing, though our own fate seemed little brighter. How long before we joined them in that eternal stillness?

---

The plateau’s edge revealed a breathtaking sight. Below us, veiled in morning mist, lay a sweeping green valley, threaded by a silver stream. And there, grazing peacefully, stood a herd of antelope—fifteen at least, their hides gleaming in the sun.

Food. Salvation.

But the distance was brutal—six hundred yards, an impossible shot for starving men with shaking hands. Stalking was out of the question; the wind was wrong, the snow too bright.

“We’ll have to risk it from here,” Sir Henry said grimly. “Express rifles or repeaters?”

The choice was agonizing. The Winchesters could reach farther, but the expresses hit harder. In the end, we gambled on power.

“Aim for the shoulder,” I instructed. “Umbopa, give the word.”

A pause. Three breaths held.

“*Fire!*”

The volley echoed like thunder. Through the smoke, we saw one great buck stagger and fall, kicking in its death throes. A ragged cheer burst from us. We half-ran, half-slid down the slope, our hunger howling.

But triumph soured quickly. No wood meant no fire. No fire meant raw meat.

Good, ever practical, shrugged. “Starving men can’t afford delicacy.”

So we ate. Heart and liver, chilled in snow, washed in the icy stream. It should have revolted me. It tasted like salvation.

---

Revived, we took stock of the land below. The mist had burned away, revealing a paradise.

Forests thicker than any I’d seen in Africa. Rivers like coiled serpents. Grasslands teeming with herds. And to our right, cutting through the landscape—a road. A *made* road, fifty feet wide, carved from living rock.

Solomon’s Great Road.

It was unmistakable. The engineering was staggering—ravines bridged with stone, cliffs terraced into switchbacks, tunnels bored through ridges. The walls of one passage bore carvings: chariots, warriors, scenes of conquest.

“Egyptian,” Sir Henry murmured. “Or Phoenician. No native work, this.”

By midday, we reached the tree line—silver-barked giants, their leaves shimmering like metal. Good, ever the fastidious one, declared it time for a proper meal. We kindled a fire, roasted antelope meat, and for the first time in days, felt human.

The valley was alive around us—ferns taller than a man, doves cooing, jewel-bright birds flashing through the branches. A paradise. A trap?

Good, half-shaven and shirtless, chose that moment to adjust his false teeth. The resulting *click* sent our visitors—tall, copper-skinned warriors materializing from the trees—into panicked retreat.

Their leader, an elder with a leopard-skin cloak, gaped. “You are spirits!”

I seized the chance. “We are travelers from beyond the stars.”

The effect was immediate. Fear turned to awe.

For now, we were safe. But in this unknown land, where the past walked hand-in-hand with the present, how long would that last?

The sun hung low in the sky as we marched along the ancient roadway, its stonework worn smooth by countless generations. The path curved ever northwest, flanked by towering cliffs carved with markings whose origins even the wisest among the Kukuanas could not decipher. Infadoos walked beside me, his voice low with reverence as he spoke of his people’s past.

“This road was old before our fathers’ fathers drew breath,” he said. “No man alive knows its makers, not even Gagool, who has whispered secrets since the days of the first kings.”

I glanced at the hieroglyphs etched into the rock. “And these writings?”

“The same hands that laid the stones left their mark. We do not read them. They are as forgotten as the men who carved them.”

The land itself was a marvel—rolling hills thick with grass, crisp mountain air carrying the scent of wild herbs. Far ahead, the snow-capped peaks known as Sheba’s Breasts pierced the heavens, their summits wreathed in mist. Infadoos followed my gaze.

“It was there our ancestors came, ten thousand generations past, driven like storm winds from the northern lands. The mountains trapped them, but the soil was rich, and so they stayed. Now we are as countless as the stars, and when Twala calls his armies, the earth trembles beneath their tread.”

“Yet if your borders are closed, who do your warriors fight?” I asked.

His expression darkened. “The mountains do not wall us in completely. Sometimes invaders come—men from beyond, hungry for blood. The last great battle was a generation ago. We broke them, and since then, the spears have rested.”

But not all conflicts, it seemed, were with outsiders. Infadoos spoke carefully of a fratricidal war—of twin brothers, one destined to rule, the other doomed to die. Yet through a mother’s love, the weaker child survived, hidden until the day he seized the throne by treachery.

“And the true heir?” I pressed.

“Dead,” Infadoos said flatly. “The queen fled with her son into the wilds. None saw them again.”

A shadow crossed his face, but before I could question further, the road dipped, revealing a vast kraal in the valley below—a fortress of thatched huts encircled by a deep trench.

“That is where the queen vanished,” Infadoos murmured. “And where we rest tonight.”

As we descended, movement stirred at the kraal’s gates. Regiments poured forth, their plumed ranks assembling with disciplined precision. Sir Henry gripped my arm. “Either we are honored guests,” he muttered, “or walking into a trap.”

Infadoos caught the tension. “Fear not. These are my men.”

The warriors were magnificent—tall, broad-shouldered, their white shields gleaming, spears tipped with blades wide as a man’s palm. As we passed, each company raised their weapons in salute, a thunderous cry of *“Koom!”* echoing across the plain.

The kraal itself was a marvel of order, its pathways clean, huts arranged in perfect squares. Women watched from the sidelines, their dark eyes curious but restrained. Unlike the Zulu, these people carried themselves with a quiet dignity, their gazes lingering only briefly on Good’s unfortunate flannel shirt—which flapped mercilessly in the wind—before turning away.

At the center of the settlement, Infadoos led us to a spacious hut. “Rest, Sons of the Stars,” he intoned. “Eat, drink, and let your weariness fade.”

Food came in abundance—roasted meat, milk, honey—and as we feasted, Scragga, the king’s son, watched us with a mix of awe and suspicion. The illusion of our divinity faded with every bite we took.

That night, pipes in hand, we learned our journey would resume at dawn. Messengers had already been sent to King Twala, who prepared for the great festival at Loo.

“Two days’ march,” Infadoos said, rising to leave. “If the rivers allow it.”

As the fire burned low, we took turns keeping watch. The air hummed with the unknown—of kingdoms built on secrets, of a lost prince, and of the shadow of a king who might yet see us as threats rather than guests.

Sleep came uneasily. Beyond the kraal’s walls, the mountains loomed, silent witnesses to the past—and the trials yet to come.

Our journey to Loo was a spectacle of ever-growing grandeur. Solomon’s Great Road stretched before us, cutting through the heart of Kukuanaland like an ancient artery of stone. The land flourished as we advanced—kraals multiplied, their fields sprawling wide, each guarded by regiments of warriors. Here, every man was a soldier, the nation’s strength bound in iron discipline. Thousands of troops streamed toward Loo for the king’s festival, their plumes rippling like a living sea, their spears flashing under the sun.

By dusk on the second day, we paused atop a ridge, and there, spread across the fertile plain below, lay Loo. A vast city, easily five miles in circumference, its outer kraals bristling like fortresses. A river cleaved it in two, bridged at intervals, the same we had glimpsed from Sheba’s Breasts. To the north loomed a horseshoe-shaped hill, ominous and unfamiliar. Beyond, three jagged, snow-crowned mountains pierced the horizon—the Three Witches, sentinels of some forgotten truth.

Infadoos noted our gaze. “The road ends there,” he said.

“Why?” I pressed.

He shrugged. “Who knows? The mountains are riddled with caves. There is a great pit between them—the Place of Death, where our kings are buried. The wise men of old sought something there.”

“What?”

His eyes flickered. “Perhaps my lords from the Stars know better.”

I exchanged glances with the others. “Diamond mines,” I murmured.

Umbopa, silent until now, stirred. “Yes, Macumazahn,” he said in Zulu. “The stones you crave are there. You shall have them.”

His certainty unnerved me. “How do you know?”

He smiled faintly. “A dream, white men.” Then he too walked away.

---

Night fell without warning, as though a curtain had been yanked across the sky. Then the moon rose, silver and sovereign, flooding the plain with spectral light. The stars dimmed before its brilliance, and for a moment, even we—hardened travelers—stood humbled by such beauty.

Infadoos broke the silence. “Come. Loo awaits.”

An hour’s march brought us to the city’s edge, its sprawl mapped by countless campfires. A moat, a drawbridge, a sentry’s challenge—then we passed into the grass-walled metropolis. At last, we halted at a limestone courtyard, our quarters for the night. The huts were spacious, the beds of soft tanned hides. Handmaidens brought roasted meats and mealie cobs, their eyes curious but deferential.

Sleep came swiftly.

---

Dawn revealed the grim pageantry of Twala’s court. Guided by Infadoos, we entered an enclosure vast enough to hold an army. Eight thousand warriors stood motionless, their shields like a forest of iron and hide. Before the king’s great hut, stools were set. We sat. The crowd’s silence was a weight.

Then the door opened.

Twala emerged—a colossus draped in a tiger’s pelt, his face a mask of cruelty. One black eye gleamed; the other was a hollow pit. A diamond, uncut and massive, burned dully on his brow. Behind him crept Scragga and a withered crone, Gagool, her cloak barely hiding her corpse-like frame.

The king raised his spear.

Eight thousand blades answered. “*Koom!*” they thundered, shaking the earth.

Then—a clatter. A soldier had dropped his shield.

Twala’s voice was ice. “Come forth.”

The young man obeyed.

“You shame me before these strangers. Prepare to die.”

Scragga stepped forward, grinning. The spear struck true. The man fell. A murmur rippled through the crowd, then died. Sir Henry lurched up, swearing, but I gripped his arm. “Sit. Or we die next.”

Twala watched the body dragged away. “A good thrust,” he remarked. Then, to us: “White men. Why are you here?”

“We come from the Stars,” I said. “To see your land.”

His lip curled. “Prove your power. Kill a man for me.”

I refused. “We shed no blood unjustly. But bring an ox.”

The beast was driven in. Sir Henry’s rifle spoke once. The ox fell.

A gasp swept the crowd.

Twala’s eye gleamed. “Give me this magic.”

I handed him the Winchester. “Beware. Turn it against a man, and it will kill *you*.”

Then Gagool shambled forward, her voice a dagger in the silence.

“*Blood!*” she screeched. “Rivers of it! I see the white man’s footsteps—they shake the earth! You seek stones? You seek a lost one? He is not here. Only death waits!” Her finger stabbed at Umbopa. “*Who are you?* Strip your girdle—”

A fit seized her. She collapsed, foaming.

Twala trembled. “Gagool’s words are ill omens,” he growled. “It passes in my mind to kill you.”

The air tightened. The king’s will hung by a thread.

The horror of Gagool’s witch-hunt still clung to us like a foul mist as we sat in silence, the weight of what we had witnessed pressing upon our spirits. The night had nearly bled into dawn when footsteps approached—Infadoos, followed by six grim-faced chiefs, each a commander of three thousand warriors.

"My lords," Infadoos said, his voice low but urgent, "these men have heard what I have seen and know. Now let them see the mark upon Ignosi, the true king, and judge whether they will stand against Twala."

Ignosi bared the tattooed serpent coiled around his waist. One by one, the chiefs examined it in the dim lamplight, their faces unreadable. When they had all seen, Ignosi covered himself and spoke again, recounting his claim to the throne.

Infadoos stepped forward. "You have heard him. The land groans under Twala’s cruelty. Blood runs like rivers. Two chiefs who might have joined us lie dead, their corpses food for hyenas. Choose now—will you fight, or wait for the same fate?"

The eldest chief, a grizzled warrior, spoke. "Your words are true, Infadoos. My own brother died tonight. But this is no small thing. How do we know Ignosi is no pretender? War will drown this land in blood before it is done. Many still bow to Twala, fearing the sun that still shines over the usurper. If these white men’s magic is strong, let them give us a sign—one all the people can see. Then they will believe."

"The mark of the snake is not enough?" I asked.

"No. A child could bear such a mark. Show us true power, or we will not move."

The others murmured agreement. I turned to Sir Henry and Good, who exchanged a glance.

"I have it," Good said suddenly. "Ask for a moment to confer."

When the chiefs withdrew, Good rummaged through his medicine case and produced an almanac. "Tomorrow is the 4th of June," he said. "Here—a total lunar eclipse, visible in South Africa. We’ll tell them we’ll darken the moon."

It was a bold gamble. If the almanac was wrong, our credibility—and Ignosi’s claim—would shatter. But the calculations held. The eclipse would begin around ten, lasting over an hour.

We summoned the chiefs back. "Great men of the Kukuanas," I declared, "we do not lightly wield our power, for it shakes the order of nature. But for this cause, we will give you a sign. Behold the moon." I pointed to its pale glow. "Can any mortal dim its light before its time?"

The eldest chief chuckled. "No man commands the moon."

"Then know this: tomorrow night, two hours before midnight, we will devour its light. Darkness will swallow the land, and you will know Ignosi is your rightful king. Do you accept this test?"

The old chief’s smile lingered. "*If* you do this, we will follow."

"It will be done," I vowed.

Infadoos leaned close. "Tonight, Twala summons you to the maidens’ dance. The fairest girl will be sacrificed to the Silent Ones. If you save her when the moon darkens, the people will believe."

---

At the king’s kraal, the scene was transformed. Instead of warriors, rows of young women stood adorned with flowers, their palms and lilies catching the moonlight. Twala sat enthroned, Gagool at his feet like a withered shadow.

"Choose the fairest," Twala commanded when the dancing ended.

Unthinking, I named the first dancer—a girl of striking beauty. The king grinned. "Then she dies, as is custom."

Horrified, I protested, but Twala only laughed. "The Old Ones demand it."

The girl, Foulata, realized her fate too late. She screamed, pleading, but guards seized her. As Scragga raised his spear, Good’s hand twitched toward his revolver.

"Stop!" I shouted. "We forbid this!"

Twala surged to his feet. "*You* forbid? Seize them!"

Armed men rushed forward. We leveled our rifles.

"Take another step," I roared, "and we will extinguish the moon!"

A ripple of fear passed through the crowd. Gagool sneered. "Liar! No man commands the moon!"

Then—a miracle. The first faint shadow crept across the lunar face.

I raised my hands dramatically. "Behold! The sign is given!"

As the eclipse deepened, gasps of terror rose. The king paled; even Gagool hesitated. Only when the shadow swallowed the moon whole did the people wail in despair.

"Now you see," I thundered. "The white men’s word is law. Spare the girl, or face endless night!"

Twala, shaken, relented. The eclipse had won the night—and with it, the first victory for Ignosi’s cause.

The labyrinthine alleys of Loo concealed our escape, guided by Infadoos and his chiefs through shadowed pathways untouched by Twala’s sentinels. For an hour we moved like ghosts beneath the obscured moon, until at last the eclipse began to wane. A sliver of silver pierced the darkness, then a crimson glow spilled across the heavens—a celestial firebrand against the void. Stars dissolved as dawn’s pallid light revealed our position: we had cleared the city and now approached a broad, flat-topped hill, its horseshoe shape bristling with boulders.

This was no natural fortress but a military stronghold, its summit a sprawling camp capable of housing regiments. As we climbed, the returning moonlight exposed row upon row of warriors—thousands already roused from sleep, huddled in superstitious dread. We pushed through the murmuring crowds to a central hut, where two attendants stood with our abandoned belongings, including Good’s long-lost trousers. His jubilant reunion with them drew a sigh from Infadoos. “Must my lord conceal such legs?” But Good, ever modest, surrendered only once more to public display. Henceforth, the Kukuanas would have to content themselves with his eyeglass, solitary whisker, and unreliable teeth.

At dawn, the army assembled—twenty thousand of Kukuanaland’s finest, arrayed in a dense square. Infadoos, a born orator, wove a tale of usurped thrones and tyranny: Ignosi’s father murdered, his people groaning under Twala’s cruelty, the white lords descending from the stars to restore justice. He spoke of the moon’s extinction, Scragga’s death, and the promise of a new era. When Ignosi stepped forward, his words were thunder.

“I am your king,” he declared, battle-axe raised. “Let any man deny it and face me now. Stand with me, and victory shall bring honor. Oppose me, and your blood will water the earth.” The regiments roared approval. Twala’s answer would come swiftly—messengers already sprinted to summon reinforcements. War was inevitable.

We spent the day fortifying the hill. Stones barred pathways; boulders were poised to crush attackers. By dusk, a herald arrived, sneering. “Twala offers mercy: one in ten dies. But you, Incubu, and the pretender Ignosi, will scream for the Silent Ones’ pleasure.” Laughter followed my retort: “Tell Twala his corpse will greet the sunset tomorrow.”

Night fell. The camp hummed with preparation. Sir Henry and I walked among the sleeping warriors, their moonlit faces already death-pale in my imagination. “How many live by tomorrow?” I wondered aloud. Curtis grinned. “Fortune favors the brave.”

At dawn, Twala’s forces advanced—three columns, like rivers of spears. Infadoos grimaced. “They attack from all sides.” We braced, chain-mail heavy on our shoulders. Sir Henry, clad as a Kukuana general, looked every inch a mythic hero. Good and I, less impressively armored, clutched spears we barely knew how to wield.

On the koppie, we watched the enemy swarm. Thirty thousand against our twenty. The math was cruel, but the choice was gone. Somewhere in the chaos ahead, Twala waited. And so did fate.

The three columns advanced with a slow, deliberate menace, like shadows creeping across the plains. At five hundred yards, the central force halted, allowing the flanking divisions to encircle our position—a horseshoe of rock and earth with its points facing the distant spires of Loo. Their strategy was clear: a synchronized assault from all sides.

Good groaned beside me. “A Gatling gun—just one—and we’d sweep that plain clean in minutes.”

Sir Henry chuckled. “No such luck. But Quatermain, why not test your aim? See if you can nick that tall brute leading them. I’ll wager a sovereign you miss by five yards.”

The challenge stung. Resting my express rifle on a rock, I drew a breath, lined up the shot, and fired. The bullet whistled wide—striking not the commander, but his orderly, who crumpled to the dust. The officer spun in alarm, bolting for cover.

Good whooped. “You scared him off!”

Anger flared in me. A marksman hates to miss. Without thinking, I took a second shot. This time, the man flung out his arms and fell face-first into the dirt. A savage thrill shot through me—shameful, but undeniable. The enemy ranks roared, some in terror, some in awe of this "white man’s magic." Their formation wavered, then broke as Sir Henry and Good opened fire, cutting down a handful before they retreated beyond range.

Then, from right and left, war cries erupted. The other divisions were engaging. The mass before us surged forward, a tide of chanting warriors, their spears flashing in the sun. We fired steadily, dropping a few, but against such numbers, our bullets might as well have been pebbles tossed into a storm.

The first clash was brutal. Our pickets at the base of the hill were overrun. The enemy climbed slowly, conserving strength, while our lines fell back step by step—first the midway defense, then the secondary, until finally, the last stood at the plateau’s edge.

“Twala! Twala! Chiele! Chiele!” they shrieked.

“Ignosi! Ignosi! Chiele! Chiele!” our men answered.

Steel met steel. The battle swayed like a great beast wrestling with itself. Men fell like wheat before the scythe. Inch by inch, our lines bent—but then Sir Henry, with a wordless roar, plunged into the fray, Good at his heels.

A cry went up: “Nanzia Incubu! Nanzia Unkungunklovo!” *The Elephant has come!*

That turned the tide. The enemy faltered, then broke, retreating down the slope in disarray. A messenger brought word: the left assault had been repelled. Relief washed over me—until I saw our right flank collapsing, pursued by fresh waves of Twala’s reserves.

Ignosi acted swiftly. The Greys, our reserve, extended into a wall of shields. Then, with a shouted command, we charged. I tucked myself behind Ignosi, revolver in hand, heart hammering. A warrior lunged at me, eyes wild, spear bloodied. I dropped flat, letting him stumble over me, then shot him as he rose.

The next moments blurred. A blow sent me sprawling into darkness.

I woke to Good’s face above me, a gourd of water in his hand. “Alive, then?”

I sat up, wincing. “Barely. What’s the butcher’s bill?”

“Two thousand dead or wounded. They lost more.” He pointed to lines of bearers carrying injured men on hide stretchers. The medicine men moved among them, their knives swift and merciless for those beyond saving.

We found Sir Henry and Ignosi in counsel. “Twala’s regrouping,” Sir Henry said grimly. “And the spring’s running dry.”

Infadoos nodded. “Without water, we die. We must choose: starve, flee north, or strike straight at Twala’s throat.”

Ignosi’s plan was bold. The Greys would bait the enemy on the narrow tongue of land, drawing their full force while our wings encircled. Sir Henry would lead the decoy, drawing Twala’s eye. I would stay with Ignosi in reserve. Good was sent to the right flank—his monocle, Ignosi joked, would hearten the men.

We clasped hands, none sure we’d meet again.

“A man’s death,” Sir Henry muttered. “Better than waiting for thirst to take us.”

Then the horns sounded, and the battle began anew.

The regiments chosen for the flanking maneuver slipped away in silence, clinging to the shelter of the rising ground to evade the watchful eyes of Twala’s scouts.

A tense half-hour passed before the Greys and their supporting regiment, the Buffaloes—the core of our force—stirred into motion. Both were fresh, untouched by the morning’s skirmishes. The Greys had been held in reserve, suffering only minor losses when they shattered the breach in our lines earlier. The Buffaloes, positioned as the third line of defense, had barely seen action at all.

Infadoos, ever the shrewd commander, knew the value of morale before a desperate clash. He strode before the Greys, his voice rolling like thunder over the ranks, extolling the honor of their position—foremost in battle, fighting beside the white warriors from the stars. Promises of cattle and rank were woven into his speech, rewards for those who lived to see Ignosi victorious.

I studied the faces beneath the black plumes—hardened veterans, every one of them past forty. Within the hour, most would be corpses staining the earth. Their fate was sealed, a calculated sacrifice to buy time for the flanking horns to strike. Yet not a single man flinched. No fear flickered in their eyes. They stood ready to embrace death, unwavering.

*"Behold your king!"* Infadoos roared, thrusting his spear toward Ignosi. *"Fight and fall for him, as is the duty of brave men! Shame upon any who shrink from death!"*

A hush fell. Then, like distant waves, a murmur rose—six thousand spear-hafts tapping against shields. The sound swelled into a deafening roar, shaking the mountains, before fading into silence. Then, like a thunderclap, the royal salute erupted.

Ignosi raised his battle-axe in acknowledgment. The Greys moved out in three lines, a thousand men each. Once they had advanced, Ignosi led the Buffaloes forward in the same formation. I muttered prayers under my breath, acutely aware of how slim my chances of survival were.

By the time we reached the plateau’s edge, the Greys were halfway down the slope, funneling onto a narrow stretch of grassland that curved like a horse’s frog into the mountain’s bend. Across the plain, Twala’s forces stirred—regiment after regiment surging forward at a trot, desperate to cut off our advance.

The tongue of land was our battleground—four hundred yards deep, narrowing to a mere ninety paces at its tip. The Greys formed a column descending the slope, then fanned back into triple lines at the base and halted.

We—the Buffaloes—took position a hundred yards behind them, on slightly higher ground. Before us, Twala’s full strength marched: forty thousand warriors, their ranks swollen since dawn. But as they neared the gorge’s mouth, they hesitated. Only one regiment could advance at a time, and there stood the Greys, immovable as a mountain.

The enemy wavered. Then a towering figure—Twala himself, plumed and armored—appeared, barking orders. The first regiment charged with a roar. The Greys held still until the last moment, then erupted forward with a deafening crash of shields.

The battle was a blur of flashing spears and heaving bodies. The Greys rolled over the attackers like a tidal wave, but at a cost—a third of their number lay dead. They reformed, shoulder to shoulder, awaiting the next assault. Amidst the carnage, I spotted Sir Henry’s yellow beard—he lived.

A second regiment, white-plumed and fierce, crashed into the Greys. The fight was brutal, the young warriors driving the veterans back before discipline turned the tide. Sir Henry’s axe carved through the fray, his battle cry ringing above the din. The enemy broke, fleeing in disarray—but the Greys were no longer a regiment. Only six hundred remained.

Instead of retreating, they seized a knoll, forming a desperate ring. Twala’s forces closed in again.

Then, salvation—the flanking horns struck. Twala’s army, fixated on the Greys, had never seen them coming. The battle turned in minutes. Regiments shattered, fleeing toward Loo. The plain was littered with the dead. Of the Greys, only ninety-five still stood.

Infadoos surveyed the survivors. *"Your children’s children will sing of this day,"* he said calmly, then clasped Sir Henry’s hand. *"Never have I seen a warrior like you."*

We pressed on toward Loo, finding Good wounded but alive, saved by his chainmail. At the city gates, Twala’s broken army surrendered.

In the empty kraal, Twala sat alone but for Gagool, his grandeur reduced to ruin. He met Ignosi’s gaze with bitter defiance.

*"What fate awaits me, king?"* he mocked.

*"The same you gave my father,"* Ignosi replied.

The fallen king’s reign was over.

The battle had ended, but its toll weighed heavily upon us. Sir Henry and Good were carried into Twala’s abandoned hut, their bodies battered and bloodied. I joined them, scarcely better off myself—my old lion wound throbbed mercilessly, and my skull ached from the morning’s blow. We were a wretched trio, yet grateful to be alive when so many had fallen.

Foulata, the woman whose life we had spared, tended to us with quiet devotion, her gentle hands easing the removal of our chainmail. Beneath the steel, our flesh was a tapestry of bruises—deep, angry marks where the enemy’s weapons had been turned aside but not denied. She brought poultices of crushed aromatic leaves, their scent sharp and clean, and though they soothed the pain, our gravest concern lay in the wounds themselves.

Good’s leg bore a cruel puncture, and Sir Henry’s jaw had been split by Twala’s axe. Fortunately, Good’s medical training served us well. By the dim glow of a Kukuana lamp, he stitched the worst of the injuries, smeared them with antiseptic, and bound them with the tattered remains of a handkerchief. Exhausted, we drank the broth Foulata prepared and collapsed onto Twala’s own furs—a bitter irony that Sir Henry, his killer, now slept wrapped in the dead king’s karross.

Sleep, however, was a fickle ally. The night echoed with the wails of women mourning their dead. Twelve thousand had perished—nearly a fifth of the Kukuana host. Their grief was a living thing, crawling into the hut like smoke. Only toward midnight did it fade, replaced by the occasional, chilling howl of Gagool keening over Twala’s corpse.

Dawn brought no relief. Good burned with fever, his breath flecked with blood—some internal injury, perhaps, from the warrior who had nearly driven a spear through his ribs. Sir Henry, though stiff and sore, remained steady. Infadoos visited, weary but unbroken, his respect for Sir Henry now bordering on reverence. The soldiers spoke of him as something beyond mortal—the man who had felled Twala with a single stroke.

Ignosi arrived later, regal in his newfound kingship. The boy who had once begged for work in Durban now wore the diadem of Kukuanaland. We spoke of the mines, of the legends surrounding the Three Witches and the hidden chamber where the kings lay buried.

"Gagool alone knows the way," Ignosi said grimly.

When the ancient hag was dragged before us, she cackled at threats, boasting of her agelessness. Only when Ignosi’s spear pricked her withered flesh did she yield, promising to guide us—though her eyes gleamed with malice. "A merry journey," she crooned. "We shall see the dead, their ribs hollow, their eyes gone… *Ha!*"

Meanwhile, Good’s condition worsened. Delirium took him, and for days he hovered between life and death. Only Foulata’s tireless care pulled him through. She scarcely moved from his side, her fingers locked in his even as cramps seized her limbs. When he woke at last, her devotion brought tears to his eyes.

"You saved me," he murmured.

She smiled, dark eyes luminous. "Did you not save me first, my lord?"

Her gaze lingered, and I felt a pang of unease. Sailors and love affairs were an old tale—one I feared would repeat itself here, far from any port.

Ignosi’s coronation came days later—a grand affair, with the remnants of the Greys honored and new ranks bestowed. We were granted royal privileges, even the power of life and death. Yet our minds were fixed on the road ahead, on the secrets buried in the mountains, and on the treacherous guidance of Gagool.

For better or worse, the path to Solomon’s treasure now lay open. And with it, the shadow of something darker.

The third night after our confrontation with the Kukuana king found us camped at the base of the Three Witches—a trio of jagged peaks that loomed like sentinels over Solomon’s forgotten road. Our party was small: Sir Henry Curtis, Captain Good, myself, and Foulata, who attended us with quiet devotion. Infadoos, the grizzled warrior, accompanied us, as did the venomous crone Gagool, carried in a litter, her mutterings a constant undercurrent of malice.

As dawn broke, the Three Witches revealed themselves in full splendor. Their summits, wreathed in eternal snow, pierced the heavens, while their lower slopes blushed purple with heather. Before us stretched the Great Road, a pale ribbon leading to the central peak—and its abrupt end. This was the threshold of the legendary mines, the same that had lured the doomed Portuguese explorer and, we feared, Sir Henry’s lost brother.

Gagool’s cackle slithered through the air as we marched. "Why hurry, fools?" she sneered. "Death waits ahead!" Her words sent a chill through me, but we pressed on until the earth itself seemed to yawn open—a vast pit, half a mile wide, its depths veiled in shadow.

"Diamond diggings," I murmured. "Solomon’s work." The telltale blue clay and worn stone washing tables confirmed it. The road forked around the abyss, its edges reinforced with ancient masonry, and as we followed its curve, three colossal figures emerged from the haze.

The Silent Ones.

Carved from dark stone, they sat upon their thrones, two male and one female, their faces weathered but their presence undiminished. The female bore crescent horns—Astarte, perhaps, or some forgotten goddess. The males were draped in stone, one serene, the other a nightmare of snarling malice. Infadoos saluted them with raised spear, but Gagool only grinned.

"Ready to see the treasure, *white men*?" she taunted.

We were.

A narrow arch in the cliff face swallowed us, Gagool’s gourd-lamp casting flickering shadows. The passage opened into a cavern so vast it dwarfed cathedrals. Stalactites hung like frozen waterfalls, their bases swelling into pillars that glittered as if carved from diamond. Water dripped—slow, eternal—building new spires over centuries. One column bore a crude etching: a mummy and an Egyptian god, left by some long-dead laborer. The stone had grown barely eight feet in three millennia.

But Gagool cared nothing for nature’s artistry. She herded us deeper, to a square-cut door.

"The Place of Death," she hissed.

Inside, the air thickened. A stone table dominated the chamber, flanked by figures—*white* figures. No, not statues. *Corpses*.

Petrified kings.

Their bodies, sheathed in glassy spar, sat frozen in time. At the table’s head loomed Death himself—a colossal skeleton, fifteen feet tall, carved from a single stalactite. Its hollow eyes seemed to watch us, its spear poised to strike.

And on the table, naked and glistening with mineral drip, sat Twala.

His severed head perched on his knees, his neck vertebrae protruding like broken spikes. The water’s ceaseless fall had begun encasing him in stone, turning flesh into relic.

Gagool’s laughter clawed at our nerves. "Meet your handiwork, Incubu!"

Sir Henry recoiled. Good swore. Foulata buried her face in his shoulder.

Twenty-seven kings surrounded us, preserved in silent judgment. Death presided over them all.

The White Dead. And their master, the White Death.

While we recovered from our fright and examined the grim wonders of the Place of Death, Gagool had been busy. With surprising agility, she clambered onto the great stone table and made her way to where Twala’s body lay beneath the dripping stalactite. Bending down, she pressed her lips to his in a mockery of affection before hobbling back, muttering to the shrouded figures as if greeting old acquaintances.

At last, she settled beneath the White Death, her withered hands clasped in what seemed a prayer—though to what dark power, I dared not guess. The sight of her whispering devotions sent a chill through me.

*"Now, Gagool,"* I said sharply, *"lead us to the chamber."*

She grinned, her eyes gleaming with malice. *"My lords are not afraid?"*

She shuffled around the stone table to the cavern wall. *"Here is the entrance. Light your lamp and step forward."*

I struck a match, igniting the rush wick, and held it aloft. Before us stood only solid rock.

Gagool cackled. *"The way is there! Ha! Ha!"*

*"Do not jest with us,"* I warned.

As she spoke, a massive slab of stone—ten feet high and five thick—began to rise silently from the floor, vanishing into the ceiling. The mechanism, hidden by some ancient artifice, moved with eerie precision.

A dark passage yawned before us.

Our hearts pounded. After all this time, after all our trials, Solomon’s treasure lay within reach. Was it real? Or another of Gagool’s cruel tricks?

*"Enter, white men from the stars,"* she crooned, stepping into the gloom. *"But first, hear this: the stones you seek were dug from the pit beneath the Silent Ones and stored here by hands long dead. Only once has this place been opened since—by a white man and a native woman. He fled with but a single stone, the one now upon Twala’s brow."*

*"None. The kings know the secret of the door but dare not pass. They say death waits within."* Her laughter echoed like a death knell. *"Prove it yourselves!"*

With a shared glance, we followed her into the narrow passage. Foulata, trembling, lagged behind, and we left her seated on an unfinished wall of stone blocks meant to seal the tunnel.

Fifteen paces in, an ornate wooden door stood ajar. Across its threshold lay a goat-skin bag, bulging with what could only be pebbles—or diamonds.

*"Hee! Hee!"* Gagool snickered. *"See what the white man dropped in his haste!"*

Good lifted it. The weight made his eyes widen. *"By Jove—it’s full of diamonds!"*

Sir Henry stepped through the doorway, lamp raised.

And there it was.

Solomon’s treasure.

The chamber was small, hewn from living rock. Stacked against one wall were elephant tusks—hundreds of them, enough ivory to buy a kingdom. Opposite stood wooden chests, their red paint faded.

I pried open the nearest. Not diamonds—gold coins, stamped with strange characters.

*"Payment for the miners,"* I murmured.

*"But where are the stones?"* Good demanded.

Gagool’s voice slithered from the shadows. *"Look to the darkest corner."*

There, in a recess, stood three stone chests. Two were sealed; the third lay open.

Sir Henry held the lamp close.

A silvery gleam blinded us.

The chest was three-quarters full of uncut diamonds, some as large as pigeon eggs. I scooped a handful—their cold, soapy texture unmistakable.

*"We’re the richest men alive,"* I breathed.

Good laughed wildly. *"We’ll flood the market!"*

*"If we ever get out,"* Sir Henry muttered.

Gagool’s cackle echoed. *"Eat them! Drink them! Ha!"*

The absurdity struck us, and we roared with laughter—until a scream shattered the moment.

We sprinted back, hearts hammering.

The great door was descending. Foulata, bloodied, wrestled with Gagool. The witch twisted free—but too late. The slab caught her, crushing her with agonizing slowness. A final shriek, a sickening crunch, and the door sealed shut.

Foulata collapsed, a dagger in her side.

*"She stabbed me,"* she gasped. *"I held her… but the door…"*

Her breath faltered. With her last strength, she whispered words of love for Good—then stillness took her.

Silence fell.

Sir Henry’s voice was grim. *"We’re buried alive."*

The horror struck us. The door’s mechanism was outside. Gagool, the only one who knew its secret, was dead.

We were trapped.

With methodical despair, we searched the walls, finding nothing. The lamp flickered, dying.

We divided Foulata’s meager provisions—dried meat, a little water—enough for two days.

*"Eat,"* Sir Henry said darkly. *"For tomorrow, we die."*

The flame guttered, casting long shadows over the treasure, the tusks, the bodies of Foulata and Gagool.

Then darkness swallowed us whole.

The horrors of that night defy description. Sleep, when it came, was a mercy—a brief respite from the crushing weight of our doom. But true rest eluded me. It was not merely the terror of our fate, though that alone would have unmanned the bravest soul. No, it was the *silence*—a silence so profound it seemed to press upon my ears like a physical force.

You may think you know silence, reader, but you do not. On the surface of the earth, there is always some whisper of life—wind in the trees, the distant murmur of a stream, the hum of insects. But here, buried deep within the heart of the mountain, there was nothing. Thousands of feet above, the winds howled over the snows, but not a breath of sound reached us. Even the dead in their chamber beyond made no noise. We were entombed, severed from all echoes of the living world.

The bitter irony gnawed at me. Around us lay riches beyond counting—enough to ransom kings or raise armies—yet we would have traded it all for a single chance at escape. Soon, I knew, we would gladly exchange it for a crust of bread, and then, in our final hours, for the mercy of a swift end. Such is the worth of wealth when life itself slips away.

The night crawled on.

"Good," came Sir Henry’s voice at last, grim in the stillness, "how many matches remain?"

"Eight."

"Strike one. Let us see the hour."

The flare blinded us after the long darkness. My watch read five. Dawn would now be breaking over the distant peaks, though no glimmer of it reached us here.

"We should eat," I suggested. "Keep up our strength."

"What use is strength now?" muttered Good. "The sooner this ends, the better."

"While there is life, there is hope," said Sir Henry.

We forced down a little food and water, then waited again, the oppressive dark pressing in. At length, Sir Henry proposed we shout, on the faint chance someone might hear. Good, with his sailor’s lungs, bellowed down the passage until his voice gave out. It was futile. The rock swallowed all sound.

Despair settled over me then. I leaned against Sir Henry, and to my shame, tears came. I think Good wept as well, cursing himself for it. But Sir Henry, steadfast as ever, spoke quietly of men who had escaped worse straits, of miracles, of the peace that comes when suffering ends. And then, with a humility that touched me, he led us in prayer.

Somehow, the hours passed. Another match showed seven o’clock. We ate again, and as I swallowed the last of our provisions, a thought struck me.

"How does the air stay fresh? This door is sealed. There must be another opening."

Good sprang up. "By heaven, you’re right!"

Hope, however faint, changes everything. At once, we were on our hands and knees, searching for the slightest draft. My hand brushed cold flesh—Foulata’s face—and I recoiled. Still, we pressed on, groping until our fingers were raw and our heads bruised from collisions with the treasure-laden chests.

Then Good’s voice, tight with excitement: "Here! Put your hand here—do you feel it?"

A whisper of air.

He stamped. The stone beneath rang *hollow*.

With shaking hands, I lit another match. There, in the corner, barely visible, was a stone ring set into the floor. Good worked his knife beneath it, levering carefully until the ring lifted. We seized it, straining together—Sir Henry’s immense strength the deciding factor—until, with a grating groan, the flagstone gave way.

A rush of air. And below, revealed in the flickering light: *steps*.

We wasted no time. Gathering our pitiful scraps of supplies, I, driven by some stubborn instinct, stuffed my pockets with diamonds—not for greed, but as a defiance against death. Sir Henry led the way down, counting the steps. At fifteen, we reached a passage, branching into darkness.

The choice was blind, but Good noted the draft pulled our match’s flame leftward. "Against the current," he urged. "Air draws inward."

So we went, feeling our way through the labyrinth, our hope fading with each dead end. Hours passed. We ate our last morsel, drank our final drops, and then—

A sound.

Faint, distant, but unmistakable: *running water*.

We followed it like men possessed, until the tunnel opened abruptly, and Good plunged into an underground river. We dragged him back, sodden and gasping. "No bottom," he choked. "It runs like a mill-race."

We dared not follow its edge. Instead, we retreated, stumbling until—

A pinprick, far ahead. We pushed forward, half-mad with hope, until the tunnel narrowed to a fox’s burrow. Earth now, not rock. One by one, we squeezed through—and then, stars! Sweet, cold air! The slope gave way, and we tumbled into the open, bruised, weeping, alive.

Dawn broke over the mountains as we staggered toward a fire where figures sat. One rose, saw us, and screamed.

"Infadoos! It is we!"

The old warrior fell to his knees. "My lords! Returned from the dead!"

And there, on the threshold of life once more, we stood—broken, but unbroken.

Ten days after our harrowing escape, we found ourselves back in Loo, weary but whole. The ordeal had left its mark—my hair had grayed, and Good bore a sorrowful quietness, haunted by Foulata’s death. She had been no ordinary woman—her beauty and grace were unmatched—but her passing, though tragic, spared us complications. As she had said herself: *"Can the sun mate with the darkness, or the white with the black?"*

We never returned to Solomon’s treasure chamber. After recovering our strength, we descended into the pit, hoping to find the passage that had saved us, but the rain had erased our tracks, and the walls were riddled with holes. The secret of the great stone door was lost forever, its mechanism a marvel of a forgotten age. Even had it opened, I doubt we would have stepped over Gagool’s crushed remains to reclaim the diamonds. And so, the greatest treasure the world had ever known remained buried, perhaps for eternity.

Yet we were not empty-handed. Before fleeing, I had stuffed my pockets with gems, and Foulata’s basket held twice as many. Though some were lost in our frantic descent, we still carried enough to make us wealthy beyond measure—ninety-three large stones among them.

Ignosi welcomed us warmly, his rule now secure. When we spoke of Gagool’s end, he summoned an elder, who confirmed her ageless wickedness. *"An ancient curse is lifted,"* the old man murmured before departing.

Then came the hard farewell. I told Ignosi it was time for us to return home. His face darkened. *"Must you leave me desolate?"* he pleaded, offering land, wives, cattle—anything. But our hearts, like his had once done in exile, yearned for our own country.

Reluctantly, he relented. *"Go then,"* he said, *"but hear my decree: No other white man shall cross these mountains. If armies come, I will bury the treasure under rock. Yet for you three, the path remains open."* His voice thickened. *"Farewell, my brothers. Your names shall be sacred, spoken only in reverence."*

At dawn, we departed, escorted by Infadoos and the Buffaloes. The people lined the streets, saluting us as liberators, women scattering flowers in our path. One young girl, enchanted by Good’s legs, begged to see them again—a moment of absurdity in the solemn leave-taking.

Guided by hunters, we took a northern pass, descending a treacherous chasm to the desert below. Infadoos clasped our hands one last time, mourning the loss of warriors like Sir Henry, whose battle prowess he praised. Then we began our march across the sands.

Three days later, we reached an oasis, its trees a promise of life beyond the wasteland. As we camped by sweet water, Sir Henry mused, *"I’ve known worse places… but never stranger times."* Good sighed, half-wishing we were back.

I, too, reflected—on battles fought, treasures lost, and lives spared. The adventure had ended, but the memory would linger, as Ignosi had vowed, long after we were gone.

The strangest twist of fortune yet awaited us in this saga—one that defied all odds and proved how fate weaves its threads in the most unexpected ways.

I was walking ahead of Sir Henry and Good, following the stream that trickled from the oasis before vanishing into the desert’s grasp, when I halted mid-step, certain my eyes deceived me. Not twenty yards away, nestled beneath the broad shade of a wild fig-tree and facing the water, stood a hut. Not a crude shelter, but a snug little dwelling, built in the native fashion with grass and withes, yet boasting a proper door instead of a mere crawl-hole.

*What in blazes is a hut doing here?* I wondered—no hunter would linger in such a wasteland, let alone settle. As I stared, the door creaked open, and out limped a man. A *white* man, clad in rough skins, his face obscured by a great black beard. The sun must be addling my brain—until Sir Henry and Good caught up, and the stranger let out a hoarse cry before collapsing.

Sir Henry reached him first. **"Great heavens—it’s my brother George!"**

Another figure burst from the hut—a native, rifle in hand—and my heart lurched as I recognized him. **"Jim!"** The hunter dropped to his knees, babbling apologies. **"Baas, I lost the note you gave me! We’ve been trapped here two years!"**

Meanwhile, George Curtis—for it was truly he—clutched Sir Henry like a drowning man. **"I tried crossing the mountains,"** he rasped, voice rusty from disuse. **"But a boulder crushed my leg. Couldn’t go forward. Couldn’t go back."**

That night, by the fire, his tale unfolded. After leaving Sitanda’s Kraal, he’d aimed for Solomon’s Mines by a different route—the very cliff-path we’d descended. The desert nearly killed them, but this oasis saved their lives—until the rockfall crippled George. With no hope of rescue, they’d survived like castaways: hunting game, tanning hides, waiting for a miracle.

**"And then *you* came,"** George murmured, shaking his head. **"Of all people—you, who I thought had forgotten me."**

Sir Henry recounted our own trials—the caves, the witch-king’s fall, the diamonds. When I showed George a handful of stones, he laughed weakly. **"At least you didn’t return empty-handed."**

**"They’re Quatermain’s and Good’s,"** Sir Henry corrected. But Good and I had already agreed: a third would go to George, for his suffering matched ours.

The return journey was brutal, supporting George’s splinter-riddled leg. Yet six months later, we stood on my Durban estate, whole once more. As I penned these final lines, a Kafir delivered a letter—Sir Henry’s scrawl demanding my presence in England.

*"Sell the diamonds slowly,"* he wrote. *"Streeter’s values them at a fortune. And come home, old friend—your boy Harry misses you."*

A postscript teased: Good preened like a peacock but still mourned Foulata, and Sir Henry’s hall now displayed Khiva’s tusks and Twala’s execution axe.

I read the letter twice. Then I booked passage.
