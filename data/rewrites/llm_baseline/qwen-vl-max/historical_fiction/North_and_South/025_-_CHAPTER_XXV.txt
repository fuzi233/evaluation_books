“Revenge may have her own;
Roused discipline aloud proclaims their cause,
And injured navies urge their broken laws.”

Margaret found herself wondering if all proposals were as sudden, as jarring in their arrival, as the two she had received. A quiet comparison rose unbidden in her mind between Mr. Lennox and Mr. Thornton. With Henry Lennox, she had felt a pang of regret—not for his feelings, but for the moment when they had slipped past friendship into something deeper. He had seemed to step over that line briefly, then recoil from it almost as sharply as she did, though for different reasons. But with Mr. Thornton—there had been no such soft transition. Their relationship had been nothing but conflict: sharp disagreements, clashing opinions, and a constant sense that he dismissed her thoughts not as ideas, but as mere noise against his iron will. She had grown weary of arguing, of trying to make him see her point of view when he turned away from it with contempt. And now—here he was, speaking of love, not with tenderness, but with a fierce, wild passion that left her breathless.

At first, she thought his offer stemmed only from pity—a compassion born of seeing her exposed, vulnerable, perhaps misunderstood by others. But even before he left the room, and certainly within minutes, a clear truth emerged: he loved her. Not just now, but always. That realization struck her like a force beyond reason, an overwhelming current pulling her toward a future she could not imagine. She recoiled, shuddered—fearing this powerful emotion that threatened to upend everything she had built.

She fled into silence, seeking refuge from the echo of his voice, the weight of his gaze. Yet escape was impossible. His presence lingered in her mind, haunting her thoughts like a ghost refusing to depart. She resented him for having so thoroughly penetrated her inner world—for daring to declare that he would love her still, even if she rejected him utterly. How could he assume such power? How dared he threaten her with enduring affection?

She wished she had spoken more firmly—more decisively. Now, sharp words flooded her mind, too late to speak. The memory of the encounter clung to her like a nightmare: vivid, unshakable, refusing to fade even as she tried to smile through it. It remained, crouched in the corner of her soul, watching, waiting—knowing she could not confess it to another without trembling.

What did he mean? Did he truly believe she couldn’t frighten him away? She would test him. Such boldness was unworthy of a man. Was he relying on yesterday’s events? If need be, she would do the same tomorrow—through poverty, illness, or sacrifice—but never again would she let him dictate her choices. She acted because it was right, simple, true. To save where she could. *Fais ce que dois, advienne que pourra.*

For a long time, she stood motionless, caught in the aftermath of his final words and the intensity of his eyes—eyes that had held hers until she looked down, humbled. Then, driven by a sudden impulse, she opened the window to let in fresh air, chasing the oppressive stillness. She opened the door, longing to break free from the silence, to lose herself in action or company. But the house was hushed—still as a tomb under the midday sun, where an invalid slept fitfully, deprived of rest even in daylight.

She could not stay alone. What should she do?

Then it came to her: Bessy Higgins. The message sent the night before flashed back—she must go.

When Margaret arrived, Bessy lay flat on the settle, drawn close to the fire despite the heat. Her face was pale, her breathing shallow. Margaret knew she needed better posture, so she gently raised her head and adjusted the pillows, easing her discomfort without a word.

“I thought I’d never see you again,” Bessy whispered, gazing at her with wide, pleading eyes.

“I’m afraid you’re worse,” Margaret said softly. “I couldn’t come yesterday—my mother was ill.”

Bessy smiled faintly. “You might think I went beyond my place, sending Mary for you. But the shouting, the voices—they tore me apart. When father left, I just wanted to hear your voice, reading something peaceful… something that would carry me into God’s stillness, like a baby lulled to sleep.”

“Shall I read you a chapter?” asked Margaret.

“Yes. At first, I won’t listen—words will seem far off. But when you come to the comforting ones… those will reach me, like a hand touching my heart.”

Margaret began reading. Bessy shifted uneasily, torn between focus and pain. Then suddenly, she broke in.

“Don’t read anymore. It’s useless. I’m blaspheming in my mind, thinking angry things I can’t change. You heard about the riot at Marlborough Mills yesterday? Thornton’s mill?”

“No,” said Margaret, coloring slightly. “Was your father there?”

“No. He’d give his hand to keep it from happening. That’s what’s breaking him. He’s crushed—broken-hearted.”

“But why?” Margaret asked, confused.

“Because he’s a committee-man for the strike. The Union chose him—he’s known as steady, deep-thinking, loyal to the bone. They made plans together: stand firm, follow the majority, obey the law. No violence. If people saw them endure hunger and hardship with quiet dignity, they’d gain sympathy. But if they took up sticks, fought—then all was lost. They tried to talk to the agitators, warn them. But the Committee swore every member would lie down and die rather than strike a blow. They believed that would win public support. And they were right in their demand—just wages. They didn’t want justice tangled with crime, like medicine mixed in jelly. The jelly’s bigger, but the taste stays.”

She paused, exhausted. “I’ve told you much. Now you understand what it means for father to see all his work undone—by someone like Boucher. He disobeyed orders, sparked the riot, ruined everything. As bad as Judas. Oh, father gave him hell last night! He said he’d tell the police where to find the ringleader—give him up to the mill-owners, let them do what they liked. He wanted the world to know the real leaders weren’t men like Boucher, but decent men—good workers, good citizens—who only wanted fair pay, who wouldn’t work even if they starved, but who would never harm property or life.”

Her voice dropped. “They say Boucher threw a stone at Thornton’s sister—killed her.”

“That’s not true,” Margaret said quickly. “It wasn’t Boucher.”

She flushed red, then white.

“You were there?” Bessy asked weakly, her breath labored.

“Yes. Never mind. Go on. It wasn’t Boucher.”

“What did he say to your father?”

“He didn’t speak. He was shaking with rage, so badly I couldn’t look. I heard his breath—fast, ragged. Once, I thought he was crying. When father said he’d turn Boucher over to the police, he screamed—and hit father across the face with a closed fist. Then he ran out like lightning.”

Father staggered from the blow, though Boucher was weak with fury. He sat down, covered his eyes, then rose and moved toward the door.

I don’t know where I found strength—but I threw myself onto the settle and grabbed him. ‘Father! Father!’ I cried. ‘You’ll never betray that poor man. I’ll hold you till you promise you won’t.’”

He pushed me away—though I was faint, nearly unconscious. His face was gray, streaked with blood. Seeing it made me sick.

I don’t remember if I slept or fainted. Then Mary came in. I told her to bring you.

Now, don’t talk. Just read. I’ve poured it out—I feel lighter. But I need something beautiful, something far away, to wash the bitterness from my mouth. Not a sermon—tell me a story. One with pictures. Read about the New Heaven and the New Earth. Maybe I’ll forget this.”

Margaret read in a low, soothing voice. Though Bessy’s eyes were closed, tears gathered on her lashes. Eventually, she fell asleep—fitfully, muttering prayers. Margaret covered her gently and left, uneasy but knowing she was needed at home.

Mrs. Hale sat in the drawing-room when Margaret returned. It was one of her better days, and she praised the water-bed, comparing it fondly to the beds at Sir John Beresford’s estate. She insisted it was superior to anything she’d known since youth, though Mr. Hale suggested youth itself might have lent more comfort to old beds. His wife scoffed.

“No, indeed, Mr. Hale. It was those beds at Sir John’s. Margaret, you’re young—you move about during the day. Are your beds comfortable? Do they give you perfect rest? Or do you toss, unable to find ease, waking tired as when you went to bed?”

Margaret laughed. “To be honest, mamma, I never think about my bed. I’m so sleepy at night that any surface does. So I’m not a reliable judge. But I never tried Sir John’s beds—I never went to Oxenham.”

“Oh, yes—poor Fred,” Mrs. Hale sighed. “I took him once, when he was a baby. I only went once after marriage—to Aunt Shaw’s wedding. And little Fred was the baby then. Dixon didn’t like changing from lady’s maid to nurse, and I feared taking her near her old home might make her leave. But Fred fell ill at Oxenham—teething—and with Anna’s wedding looming and my own weakness, Dixon took full care of him. And she grew so attached—so proud when he turned from everyone to cling to her—that I think she never considered leaving again.”

She paused, her voice thickening. “Poor Fred. Everyone loved him. He was born with the gift of winning hearts. It makes me despise Captain Reid—knowing he disliked my own son. A proof, surely, of a cruel heart.”

Mr. Hale quietly left the room.

“Ah, your poor father, Margaret,” she whispered. “He can’t bear to hear Fred mentioned.”

“I love to hear about him, mamma,” Margaret said softly. “Tell me everything. Tell me what he was like as a baby.”

“Well, Margaret, you mustn’t be hurt—but he was much prettier than you were. I remember seeing you in Dixon’s arms—‘Dear, what an ugly little thing!’ I said. And she replied, ‘It’s not every child who’s like Master Fred, bless him!’”

She smiled sadly. “I could have held Fred every moment. His cot was beside my bed. And now—now I don’t know where my boy is. Sometimes I fear I shall never see him again.”

Margaret knelt beside her mother’s sofa, holding her hand, kissing it gently, trying to soothe her.

Mrs. Hale wept openly. Then she sat upright, staring at her daughter with desperate earnestness.

“Margaret,” she said, voice trembling, “if I recover—if God gives me a chance—it must be through seeing Frederick once more. It will awaken the last spark of health in me.”

She paused, gathering strength.

“And if I am to die—if I am among those meant to pass within weeks—I must see him. I cannot pray until I have this. I charge you, Margaret, as you hope for comfort in your final hours, bring him to me. Only five minutes. There’s no danger in five minutes. Oh, Margaret—let me see him before I die!”

Margaret did not question the logic. In moments like these, reason fades. The plea of a dying mother strikes deeper than any argument. This wish was natural, noble—right for both mother and son. She felt compelled to act.

She stood before her mother, calm and steady.

“Mamma,” she said, “I will write tonight. I am certain Frederick will come. Be at ease. You shall see him, as much as any earthly promise can guarantee.”

“You’ll write tonight? Oh, Margaret—the post leaves at five. Will you send it then? I have so few hours—sometimes I feel I won’t recover, though your father tries to convince me otherwise. Write immediately—don’t miss a single post. By that very post, I may miss him.”

“But papa is out,” Margaret said.

“Papa is out! And what then? Would he deny me this last wish? Why, I wouldn’t be here, dying, if he hadn’t taken me from Helstone to this unhealthy, smoky place.”

“Oh, mamma!” Margaret breathed.

“Yes. He knows it. He’s said so many times. He’d do anything for me. You don’t mean he’d refuse this—this prayer? And Margaret, the longing to see Frederick stands between me and God. I cannot pray until I have this one thing. Don’t delay—write now!”

Margaret hesitated. But her mother trembled, feverish with urgency.

“You’re not writing!” she finally gasped. “Bring me pens and paper—I’ll try myself.”

Margaret gently stopped her. “Wait for papa. Let us ask him how best to proceed.”

“You promised—only a quarter of an hour ago. You said he would come.”

“And so he shall, mamma. Don’t cry. I’ll write now—you’ll see me write. It will go by this post. If papa wishes, he can add a note later—only a day’s delay. Please, don’t cry so. It breaks my heart.”

Mrs. Hale sobbed uncontrollably, conjuring visions of her son kneeling beside her grave, weeping over her cold body while she remained unaware. The grief overwhelmed her, leaving her spent and silent.

At last, she calmed. Her eyes fixed on Margaret as she wrote swiftly, urgently, sealing the letter quickly, afraid her mother might insist on reading it. Then, at her mother’s request, she carried it herself to the post office.

On her way home, her father caught up with her.

“And where have you been, my pretty maid?” he asked.

“To the post office—with a letter. A letter to Frederick. Oh, papa, perhaps I’ve done wrong. Mamma was seized with such longing—she said seeing him would make her well. Then she said she must see him before she dies. I couldn’t resist. Did I do wrong?”

Mr. Hale was silent for a moment.

“You should have waited,” he said at last.

“I tried to persuade her—” Margaret began, then stopped.

“I don’t know,” he said slowly. “She ought to see him, if she wants it so deeply. I believe it would do her more good than all the doctor’s medicines—perhaps heal her entirely. But the danger to him—ah, Margaret, it is great.”

“All these years since the mutiny?” she asked.

“Yes. The government takes no mercy. Especially in the navy. A commander must be seen as backed by absolute authority. Any insult must be avenged—no matter how long ago. They spare no expense—ships scour the seas, hunting down offenders. Time doesn’t erase the offense. It remains fresh on the Admiralty books until it is wiped out by blood.”

“Oh, papa—what have I done?” Margaret whispered. “And yet it seemed so right. I’m sure Frederick would run the risk.”

“So he would,” Mr. Hale said. “So he should. Nay, Margaret, I’m glad it’s done. I wouldn’t have dared. I’m thankful it’s settled. I might have hesitated too long—too late to help.”

He paused. “Dear Margaret, you’ve done what is right. The outcome is beyond our control.”

It was comforting—but beneath his words, Margaret sensed his true anxiety. The relentless pursuit of mutineers, the possibility that her brother might be hunted down and killed—these thoughts chilled her to the bone. Had she lured him home to seal his fate? She saw her father’s worry deepen, hidden behind his reassurances.

She took his arm and walked home in silence, weighed down by sorrow and dread.
