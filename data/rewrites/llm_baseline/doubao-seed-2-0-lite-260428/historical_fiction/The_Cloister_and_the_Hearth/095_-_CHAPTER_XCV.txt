The hermit, Clement, started so violently he nearly dropped the sleeping child from his left arm, his gaze darting from the small warm face pressed to his tunic to Margaret kneeling at his right knee, and back again. His astonishment was only matched by the sharp, jarring jolt of seeing her here, when he had long convinced himself she was dead. Gone was the pale, breathless, frightened girl he had wrestled down barely an hour before; before him stood a woman glowing with quiet, unassailable majesty, cheeks flushed bright pink from the crisp autumn air, eyes sparkling, lips parted in a soft triumphant smile, her whole face lit with a look he had never seen before: unshakable maternal pride.

He stared, breath catching, the child stirring faintly in his hold.
“Us?” he gasped at last, his wonder-stricken eyes still flicking back and forth between the two of them.

Margaret laughed, low and warm. This was an age of instinct over cold fact, and she could not fathom his confusion. “What? Doth a father not know his own flesh and blood? And a man of God, at that? Fie, Gerard, do not pretend! You are far too wise, far too good to have missed it. I watched you, these past days. Look at the two of you side by side! That is your own child you hold pressed to your heart.”

Clement shook so hard his shoulders trembled. “What words are these?” he stammered. “This angel, mine?”
“Whose else, when he is mine too?” Margaret said.

Clement turned his gaze back to the sleeping boy, a look so tender, so raw with newly uncovered love no pen could ever capture it, and he quaked all over, as if he was memorizing every line of the little face seared into his memory. Margaret’s eyes softened, following his.
“He is not a bit like me,” she said proudly. “But oh, some days he is your very image, shrunk down small. Look at that golden hair! Yours was exactly that shade when you were his age — ask your own mother, if you doubt it. And see this tiny mole on his little pinky? Now look at yours, there! Your mother told me you carried that mark from infancy, long before this little one was born. That was how I found you! I stood outside your cell window, watching you feed the birds, and spotted that same mole on your finger, and knew at once you were his father.”
She seized the child’s small hand, pressing eager kisses to the knuckles, half the little fist vanishing into her mouth as she laughed through tears. “Ah, my blessing on you, little one! You found your poor daddy for me, and now you have mended our little quarrel — our first, our last. You were so cruel to me just now, my poor Gerard, but I forgive you, all for the love of your child.”

Clement could not speak. He sobbed in great heaving gasps, half-choking, weakened by months of fasting, unhinged by years of total solitude. The once strong young man was overcome with hysterics, slumping forward nearly fainting. Margaret was alarmed, but her years of hardship had taught her steady kindness, and her pity far outstripped her fear.
“There, there, do not take on so,” she murmured, smoothing the hair back from his brow. “Be brave! No man is the first to come home from wandering richer for a lovely little new life than he set out. You are a man of God now, take courage. He sent this little one to comfort you for what you thought you lost in me — and that loss is not so very great, my lamb. The better part of my love for you will never cool, not even here, though it ought to, now you are a priest, and parson of Gouda.”

“I? Parson of Gouda? Never,” Clement mumbled faint, his voice thin as thread. “I am a friar of St. Dominic. But speak on, sweet voice. Tell me all that has befallen you, before we must part again.”

Any other woman might have raged against the very idea of parting, dragged up all the old arguments right then. But Margaret was not any other woman. She did not make the same mistake twice, and once her heart was set on something, no one could outmaneuver her. She nodded, setting the thought of the Gouda manse aside for the moment, and told him her whole long tale, pausing often to speak of the great kindness his parents had shown her through all her troubles. As she spoke, his hand crept to hers, and she felt him wince with rage at every hardship she described, squeeze her hand tight in wordless sympathy, as if he felt every bruise and every sorrow right along with her.
“O, what a piteous tale, a true heart battling alone against such cruel odds,” he said at last.

“It all feels so small now, when I have found you again, and see you nursing my boy,” she said. “True friends like us are so rare — it is madness to tear ourselves apart before death does it for us.”
“That is true,” Clement said, off his guard.

Then she asked him to tell her what he had suffered for her sake. He begged to be excused, and she agreed — only to gently press question after quiet question, until every peril he had survived, every lonely night, every ache of grief, was laid bare between them. She sighed over and over, more than once hiding her face in her hands, terror still thrumming through her at the dangers he had escaped, even now they were past. To comfort him, she knelt down, slipped her arms under the sleeping boy, and lifted him gently up to press him into Clement’s arms again.
“Kiss him softly,” she whispered. “Again, and again — kiss your fill, he is fast asleep. This is all I can do to comfort you, until you leave this foul den and come to your sweet manse, just over the hill.”

Clement shook his head.
“Very well,” she said. “Let that pass. You must know, I have been sorely affronted these past weeks, all because I could not find our marriage lines.”
“Who dared affront you?”
“Never mind. They will do it again if you have lost them — and the saints forbid that.”
“Lose them? They are right there, close to your hand.”
“Where, where, oh where?”

Clement hung his head. “Look in the Vulgate. Heaven forgive me: I thought you were dead, a saint in heaven, and had no use for them any more.”
She opened the old, tattered book, and found their marriage papers on the blank end leaves. “Thank God!” she cried. “Thank God! O, bless you, Gerard, bless you! Why — what is all this?”
On every spare blank page, tucked between the lines of scripture, was every scrap of paper she had ever sent him: the slip where they had scrawled their two names together for a lark, the lock of her chestnut hair she had given him the night he left, the half of a broken silver coin they had split between them, even the single straw she had sipped her soup from the very first day they met.
When Margaret saw all these quiet, tender proofs of his grief and his love, her joy at finding the marriage lines was swallowed up in a gush of tears. She staggered a little, pressing a hand to her bosom, and leaned her forehead against the cold stone wall of the cell, weeping so silently he did not notice. She would not let him see her break, for she knew she had to be the strong one now — weakness would ruin everything.

“Gerard,” she said, steadying her voice, “I know you are wise and good. You must have a reason for hiding yourself away like this, no matter how unreasonable it seems. Let us talk like old friends. Why have you buried yourself alive in this hole?”
“To escape temptation,” he said quietly. “My anger at the men who wronged us grew from the ache in my heart, so I must deaden that heart, Dei gratia, by degrees. Am I a servant of Christ, to court temptation? To pray every day to be led away from sin, and then walk straight into it with my eyes wide open?”
“That is sensible enough, for a start,” Margaret said, with perfect, gentle candour.
“’Tis unanswerable,” Clement sighed.
“We shall see. Tell me: have you escaped temptation here? I ask because when I am alone, my thoughts run far more wild and foolish than when I am with other folk. Come now, speak the truth.”
“I must own I have been tormented here with evil imaginations far worse than I ever was in the world outside.”
“There now.”
“Ay, but so were St. Anthony, St. Jerome, Macarius, Hilarion, Benedict, Bernard — all the saints. It will wear off in time.”
“How do you know?”
“I feel sure of it.”
“Guessing, pitted against what you have already seen. Men are so much sillier than us women, it is a wonder. Wise in their own conceits, they will not let themselves see what is right before their eyes, too proud to learn from their own experience. If a woman went to hide in a hole in a bank to escape temptation, and found it waiting for her there, she would just hitch up her farthingale and march right out, and never even realize how clever she was until she saw a man making the exact same fool of himself.”

Clement smiled faintly. “I grant you humility and a teachable spirit are the roads to wisdom. But even so, here I only wrestle with my own imagination. At Gouda, you — whom I love far more than any priest or monk dare love any but the angels — you would tempt my weak soul, even if you did not mean to.”
“And what exactly would I tempt you to, in God’s name?” Margaret said, her eyes flashing. “Speak for yourself, my lad. You must be thinking of some other Margaret, not Margaret Peter. Has my mind ever once turned to folly or frailty? Wait — is it because you were my husband once, as these lines prove? Do you think the road to sin is more beaten for you than any other man? O, how shallow the wise are! You cannot even read me, when I can read you top to toe. Learn your A B C. If a stranger came to me with unchaste offers, I would flinch, of course, defend myself without making a whole great fuss — for I know men are so, even the best of them, sometimes. But if you, my own husband, the father of my child, ever dared speak to me so, to humble me in my own eyes, and yours, and our son’s? I would spit in your face, Gerard — or, since I am no vulgar woman, I would snatch the nearest weapon to hand and strike you dead.”
No one looking at her, eyes blazing, nostrils flared, could have doubted a single word she said.

“I never had the sense to see that,” Gerard said, quiet, and he sat pondering, his brow furrowed.
Margaret let the silence stretch a little while, until her temper cooled, then spoke soft again. “Let us not quarrel. Tell me of your family. Your father?”
“He is well, and grows warmer every day to the thought of you and me,” she said. “Poor soul, he drew his glaive on those two knaves who wronged you that day, but Jorian Ketel and I held him back, and he drove them out of his house forever.”
“He cannot do that. He must take them back.”
“He never will, for us. You know the man — dour as iron. But he would do it at once for one word from the man who will not speak it.”
“Who?”
“The vicar of Gouda. I hear he will be waiting for you at the manse tomorrow.”
“You keep circling back to that,” he said, half laughing.
“Forgive me! I am but a woman, born to nag. You must distract me with questions, so I do not get carried away. What of your sister Kate?”
“Alas!”
“O no, no harm has befallen her! Far from it,” Margaret hurried to soothe him, then took a slow, steadying breath, and spoke soft and low. “My poor Gerard. Kate left all her troubles behind her. She saw more joy and more sorrow in her short life than most folk will ever see, and she smiled even when she was in pain. She fell into a soft languor, all her aches and pains fading away. Your mother tried to fool herself, said it might be a turn for the better, but Joan Ketel, who is a sensible woman, looked at her and said ‘Down sun, down wind!’ The gossips told your mother to be brave, for Kate was half way to sainthood. But Kate would not let her mother weep, and one very old woman said she would die as easy as she had lived hard. She lay painless for three whole days, sipping heaven before she ever got there. When she was just slipping away, she asked for Gerard’s little boy. I brought him, set him on the bed, and he sat there as quiet as a lamb. She was past speaking by then, but she pointed to a drawer, and her mother pulled out the two gold angels you gave her years ago, as a child. She nodded at the little boy, all anxious, so we put the coins in his small hands, and when his fingers closed around them she smiled, content. She gave her little earthly treasures to her favourite’s son — for you were always her favourite — and her immortal soul to God. She passed so sweetly none of us even noticed the exact moment she left us.”

Gerard wept. “There will never be another like her on this earth,” he sobbed. “O how softly the old affections of this world curl around my heart! I cannot help it. God made them, after all. Speak on, Margaret. The sound of your voice rolls the old tides of memory back over me, all the old loves and hopes of youth come drifting into my dark cell, and my darker heart, on waves of music.”
“Gerard, I hate to grieve you, but Kate cried a little, when she first grew sick, that you were not there to close her eyes.”
Gerard sighed. “You were less than a league away, but you hid your face from her.”
He groaned.
“Forgive me for nagging! I am but a woman. You would never have been cruel to your own flesh and blood on purpose, would you?”
“O no, never.”
“Well then. Your brother Sybrandt is in my care, with a broken back — the fruit of your curse.”
“Mea culpa! Mea culpa!”
“He is so penitent. Forgive him, tonight, for your own peace.”
“I forgave him long ago.”
“Do you think he can believe that from any mouth but yours? He is not far from here, you know — only two butts’ length away.”
“So near? Where?”
“At Gouda manse. I brought him there last night. I knew you: your curse was hardly cold on your lips before you regretted it,” Margaret said, and Gerard nodded, agreeing. “I told myself, Gerard will thank me for taking Sybrandt to die under his own roof. He will beat his breast and cry mea culpa, but never begrudge three small footsteps to comfort his withered brother on his last bed. He may have a bee in his bonnet, but he is no hypocrite, no man full of pious words and no charity.”

Gerard staggered where he sat, stung right to the core.
“Forgive me for nagging,” she said, smiling soft. “Your mother too is waiting for you. Is it kind to keep her in such torment? She will not sleep a wink all night. Think, Gerard: she is everything to you that I am to this little boy. She suffered for you, nursed you, tended you from a babe to a man. Hermit, monk, priest — call yourself whatever you like, to her you are only one thing: her child.”
“Where is she?” Gerard whispered, his voice shaking.
“At Gouda manse, pacing the night through, praying and worrying for you.”

Margaret knew the moment had come. She had softened him with kindness, with memory, with love, and now she brought out the full weight of her argument, plain and steady, the way a smith heats iron hot before he brings the sledge down. She told him, in plain homely Dutch, that his life in the cave was nothing but a higher form of selfishness, spiritual egotism. A priest had no more right to care only for his own soul than he had to care only for his own body. That was no path to heaven.
“Who ever lost their soul by saving the souls of other people?” she said. “The Almighty loves the man who thinks of others. When he sees you caring for the flock the duke has put into your hands, he will care ten times more for your soul than he ever could here, all alone in the dark.”

Gerard blinked, struck by her words. “You are very shrewd in an argument,” he said.
“Far from it,” she replied. “Only my eyes are not bandaged up with conceit. As long as Satan walks the whole earth, tempting men, and the sons of Belial never lock themselves in caves but run back and forth corrupting others, the good man who skulks apart is playing the devil’s game, giving him every advantage. You call yourself a soldier of Christ? Ask your old comrade Denys, who is only a soldier of the duke — ask him if he ever skulked in a hole to avoid battle, just because battle holds danger as well as glory and duty. Our kings and dukes never had such cowardly soldiers as Christ has. What was that you preached in the Rotterdam church, about the man in the parable who buried his talent in the earth, and angered the Lord? Your great gift for preaching — is that not a talent, a gift from your Creator?”
“Such as it is, yes,” Gerard said.
“And have you used it? Or buried it? Who have you preached to these seven months? Bats, and owls? You have buried your gift right here in this hole, along with yourself and all your once good wits. The Dominicans are the friars preachers. You were founded to preach the word of God, and so you are false to St. Dominic, as well as to his Master.
