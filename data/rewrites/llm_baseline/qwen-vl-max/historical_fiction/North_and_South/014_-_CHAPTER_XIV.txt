“I was used to sleep at nights as sweetly as a child—
Now if the wind blew rough, it made me start,
And think of my poor boy tossing about
Upon the roaring seas. And then I seemed
To feel that it was hard to take him from me
For such a little fault.”

At this time, Margaret found solace in the growing closeness between herself and her mother—a tenderness more profound than she had ever known since childhood. Her mother now turned to her not merely as a daughter, but as a confidante, a trusted friend. It was the role Margaret had always longed for, one she had once envied Dixon for holding so easily. Now, she gave herself fully to the duty, responding with care and patience to every appeal for comfort—no matter how small or trivial the concern might seem. She listened attentively, even when the worry was no greater than a pin beneath an elephant’s foot, yet still lifted carefully at the keeper’s command.

Unknowingly, Margaret was drawing near to a deeper understanding—one that would change her forever.

One evening, Mr. Hale absent from home, her mother began speaking of Frederick, the brother Margaret had never truly known. It was the subject she had longed to ask about, yet feared to broach—the only topic where her natural openness faltered under the weight of timidity.

“Oh, Margaret,” her mother said, her voice soft and strained, “it was so windy last night! The wind howled down the chimney into our room. I couldn’t sleep. I never can when the storm is fierce like that. I fell into a habit of wakefulness when poor Frederick was at sea—and now, even if I don’t awaken suddenly, I dream of him caught in a tempestuous sea. Great glass-green walls of water rise on either side of his ship, higher than its masts, curling over with cruel white foam—like some monstrous serpent coiled around the vessel. It’s an old dream, but it returns every time the wind rises, until I’m grateful to wake up, sitting bolt upright in bed, trembling.”

She paused, her breath shallow. “Poor Frederick. He’s on land now, so the wind can do him no harm. Though I did wonder—might it shake down one of those tall chimneys?”

“Where is Frederick now, mamma?” Margaret asked gently. “Our letters are sent to Messrs. Barbour in Cadiz. But where is he himself?”

Her mother hesitated. “I can’t remember the exact name of the place—but he is not called Hale. You must remember that, Margaret. Look for the initials F.D. in every corner of the letters. He has taken the name Dickenson. I wanted him to be Beresford—he had a claim to it, you know—but your father thought it better he didn’t. If he bore my name, he might be recognised.”

Margaret sat quietly, her heart beating softly against her ribs. “Mamma,” she said, “I was at Aunt Shaw’s when all this happened. I was too young, I suppose, to be told plainly. But now—I should like to know, if I may—if it does not cause you pain.”

“Pain?” Mrs. Hale replied, her cheeks flushing faintly. “No. Yet it is painful to think I may never see my darling boy again. Or perhaps—he did right, Margaret. They may say what they will, but I have his own letters. I’ll believe him, even if he is my son, before any court-martial on earth.”

She reached toward a small, ornate cabinet by the wall. “Go to my little japan cabinet, dear. In the second left-hand drawer, you’ll find a packet of letters.”

Margaret fetched them. The envelopes were yellowed, stained with salt and sea air, carrying the faint, lingering scent of ocean winds and distant shores. She returned them to her mother, who untied the silken string with trembling fingers. As she examined the dates, she handed the bundle to Margaret, murmuring anxious comments almost before her daughter could read.

“You see, Margaret—how from the very beginning he disliked Captain Reid. He was second lieutenant aboard the *Orion*, the ship Frederick sailed on first. Poor little fellow—how well he looked in his midshipman’s uniform, dirk in hand, slicing open newspapers as though it were a paper knife!”

She paused, her voice tightening. “But this Mr. Reid—then just a lieutenant—seemed to take a dislike to Frederick from the start. Then—ah, these are the letters written aboard the *Russell*. When Frederick was assigned to her, he discovered his old enemy, Captain Reid, was in command. He meant to endure everything patiently. Look—read this letter. Where does he say—wait—‘my father may rely upon me, that I will bear with all proper patience everything that one officer and gentleman can take from another. But from my former knowledge of my present captain, I confess I look forward with apprehension to a long course of tyranny on board the *Russell*.’”

Margaret slowly read the words, the ink faded and nearly illegible. It was likely an account exaggerated by emotion—written hotly after a confrontation. But the truth of it stirred deep within her.

The letter described how, during drills, Captain Reid ordered sailors aloft in the main-topsail rigging to race down, threatening the slowest with the cat-o’-nine-tails. One man, farthest along the spar, knowing he could not pass his companions, yet terrified of the punishment, leapt desperately for a lower rope. He missed. He fell—senseless—onto the deck. He lived only a few hours. The crew’s fury boiled over. Young Hale wrote the letter in the midst of that chaos.

“But we didn’t receive this letter until long after we heard of the mutiny,” Mrs. Hale continued, her voice low. “Poor Fred! I dare say it was a comfort to him to write it—even if he had no way to send it. And then—oh, Margaret—we saw a report in the papers—before Fred’s letter arrived—of a terrible mutiny aboard the *Russell*. The mutineers seized control, and the ship vanished, believed to have become a pirate vessel. Captain Reid was set adrift in a boat with several men—officers or others—whose names were listed, because they were rescued by a West Indian steamer.”

Mrs. Hale stopped, her breath catching. “Oh, Margaret—how your father and I sickened when we read that list. There was no name of Frederick Hale. We thought it must be a mistake. Poor Fred was such a fine boy—only perhaps too passionate. We hoped ‘Carr’—the name on the list—was a misprint for Hale. Newspapers are so careless.”

She took a shuddering breath. “That same day, your father walked to Southampton to get the papers. I couldn’t stay home. I went to meet him. He came late—much later than I expected. I waited under the hedge, near the Oldham beech-tree. When he finally appeared, his arms hung limp, his head bowed, walking as if each step weighed a lifetime.”

She closed her eyes. “I see him now.”

“Don’t go on, mamma,” Margaret whispered, leaning close, pressing her cheek gently against her mother’s hand. “I understand.”

“No,” Mrs. Hale said, her voice breaking. “You cannot. No one can who didn’t see him then. I could barely stand. Everything spun around me. When I reached him, he didn’t speak. Didn’t seem surprised to see me there, three miles from home. But he took my arm, stroked my hand—as if trying to calm me through some terrible blow. And when I trembled so violently I couldn’t speak, he pulled me into his arms, bent his head over mine, and began to cry—not aloud, but in a choked, groaning sound—until I stood frozen with fear, begging him to tell me what he had seen.”

Then, with his hand jerking as if controlled by unseen hands, he handed her a newspaper. “Read it,” he said. “It calls our Frederick a ‘traitor of the blackest dye,’ a ‘base, ungrateful disgrace to his profession.’ Oh, Margaret—I cannot recall the hateful words they used. I took the paper—read it—then tore it into tiny pieces. I tore it—oh, I believe I tore it with my teeth. I didn’t cry. I couldn’t. My face burned like fire, my eyes felt as if they would burst. I saw your father watching me, grave and silent. I said it was a lie—and it was.”

Months later, Frederick’s letter arrived. And now, Margaret read it—seeing clearly the provocation. He hadn’t rebelled for himself. He had spoken out against injustice, against cruelty, against a captain who ruled with arbitrary cruelty. And most of the crew stood with him.

“I think, Margaret,” Mrs. Hale said after a long silence, her voice weak and trembling, “I am glad of it. I am prouder of Frederick standing up against injustice than if he had simply been a good officer.”

“I am sure I am,” Margaret replied firmly. “Loyalty and obedience to wisdom and justice are noble. But nobler still is defiance of power when it is wielded unjustly and cruelly—not for oneself, but for others who cannot defend themselves.”

Yet still, her mother sighed. “For all that, I wish I could see Frederick once more—just once. He was my first baby, Margaret.”

She spoke wistfully, almost as if apologising for the longing, as though wanting to see her son diminished the love she bore for Margaret. But such a thought never entered Margaret’s mind. Instead, she wondered: *Could this wish be fulfilled?*

“It’s been six or seven years,” she said quietly. “Would they still prosecute him, mother? If he came back, and faced trial—what would happen? Surely, he could bring evidence of his great provocation.”

“It would do no good,” Mrs. Hale whispered. “Some of the sailors who followed Frederick were captured. A court-martial was held aboard the *Amicia*. I believed every word they said—they matched Frederick’s story exactly. But it mattered nothing.”

For the first time, tears welled in her eyes. “They were hanged at the yard-arm,” she said solemnly. “And the worst part—the court declared they had allowed themselves to be led astray from their duty… by their superior officers.”

Silence filled the room. Long, heavy silence.

“And Frederick was in South America for many years, was he not?”

“Yes. And now he is in Spain—at Cadiz, or somewhere near. If he comes to England, he will be hanged. I shall never see his face again—for if he comes to England, he will be hanged.”

There was no comfort to offer. Mrs. Hale turned her face to the wall, motionless, consumed by grief. Margaret could say nothing. She withdrew her hand, sensing her mother needed solitude. When Mr. Hale returned, Margaret slipped away, her heart heavy with sorrow, seeing no light ahead—no hope on the horizon.
