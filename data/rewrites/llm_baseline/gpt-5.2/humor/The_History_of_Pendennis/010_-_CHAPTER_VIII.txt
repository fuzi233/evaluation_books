It befell, not so very long ago (and yet long enough for a ham to cure twice over in a clean West-country loft), that a young gentleman of Cambridge came down for the Long Vacation to the very village where Helen Thistlewood lived with her mother—the widow of that lieutenant who had been laid low at Copenhagen, as neatly and abruptly as a candle-snuff in a draught.

This gentleman was the Reverend Francis Bell: a Fellow, a Tutor, a terror to idle undergraduates, and—what is rarer—a nephew who could be trusted to lodge under his aunt’s roof without immediately eating her out of house and home. He brought three or four pupils with him, as one might bring hungry goslings to a pond; and the little household, which lived in a small way, suddenly found itself full of Latin, young voices, footsteps in the passage, and the perpetual scratching of pens—an industrious music that, to ears accustomed to village quiet, sounded like a brood of crickets who had all learned the same psalm-tune.

The two ladies discovered, early enough, that Mr. Bell carried an engagement in his pocket along with his watch and his sermons. He was only waiting, they understood, for a college living to make him respectable in a parsonage and solvent at a butcher’s. His intended was Miss Martha Coacher, daughter of another reverend gentleman—Mr. Coacher—who had once been Bell’s private tutor; and it was under that learned roof, when Bell was a raw boy of seventeen or eighteen, that the romance had boiled over.

Picture, if you please, a garden in spring; peas on their sticks; the earth smelling damp and honest; a wicker basket; and Miss Martha, then in what she called her bloom, picking pods with that determined snap which is not the worst qualification in a woman, unless she applies it later to a husband’s comfort. There, with pea-vines for witnesses and a robin for clerk, young Bell flung himself on his knees—actually in the mould—and pledged himself to endless affection. A man who can swear eternal constancy while his stockings are taking the print of a garden-bed is either a hero or a simpleton; and young Bell, like most young men, was a little of both.

Miss Martha was many years his senior; and her heart—poor thing—had been scored and carved upon by previous suitors, like an old chopping-board that has known too many knives. No fewer than three of her father’s pupils had warmed themselves at her fire and walked away whistling. The village apothecary had jilted her as coolly as he might cork a phial. A dragoon officer—one of those bright, jingling persons who borrow hearts as they borrow lodging—had danced her through a season at Bath, shaken his bridle-rein gaily one morning, and galloped off, leaving her to waltz with memory. When a woman has been thus served up and sent back from the table so often, it is not astonishing if she longs at last to be eaten in peace.

So she listened to the honest gawky boy with kindness and a good-humour that had a touch of condescension in it, and said, “Law, Bell, I’m sure you are too young to think of such things;” but hinted that she would revolve the proposal in her own virgin bosom—an apartment in which, it must be owned, there was already a good deal of stored furniture. She could not refer him to her mamma—there was no mamma to be referred to; Mr. Coacher was a widower, and so sunk in books that a woman’s heart might have been simmering on his very hob and he would have asked only whether it were Greek or Latin.

Presently Bell received, tied in a bit of blue ribbon, a lock of Miss Martha’s auburn hair—sent like a slice from the joint, as earnest of the whole feast. She had snipped off and bestowed such ringlets before—thrice, indeed—and the possessors had proved faithless; but the hair had obligingly grown again, as if to supply the market. “Men are deceivers,” Martha had occasion to say; and this time, to do her justice, Number Six was the exception. Francis Bell was faithful.

When the time came that it must be told to Mr. Coacher, that gentleman cried, “God bless my soul, I hadn’t the least idea what was going on!”—which was likely enough, for he had been taken in precisely the same manner already; and Bell went up to the University with his ambition set like yeast: to rise, to swell, to win honours, and to lay them hot and steaming at Martha’s feet.

The young man worked as if he were kneading dough for a famine. Term after term brought news of prizes and triumphs. He sent prize-books for his essays to old Coacher, and a silver declamation cup to Miss Martha—tokens that glittered handsomely on a parlour shelf, though they did not make her temper any the sweeter. He became high among the Wranglers, a Fellow of his college; and all this while letters went and came—tender, constant, dutiful—so that Miss Martha, tasting each line as if it were her portion, could fairly say that her influence had fed his success.

But time, that slow cook, alters every dish. When the Reverend Francis Bell, M.A., Fellow and Tutor, reached six-and-twenty, Miss Martha was four-and-thirty; and though she had been browned and aired by years, she was not improved by them. Her charms, her manners, her temper—none had mellowed, like a good cheese. Having achieved his honours, Bell relaxed; his judgement cooled; and what had looked rosy in the pea-garden began to show, under the clearer light of manhood, as rather coarse, rather sour, and very certainly middle-aged.

And it was after one of those altercations in which Miss Martha’s eloquence shone so fiercely that she often indulged it for pleasure, that Bell refused to take his pupils to Bearleader’s Green (where Mr. Coacher’s living was) for the vacation. He bethought him instead of his aunt’s village, not visited for years—not since little Helen, then a child, used to sit on his knee and listen to stories, as one listens to the crackle of a hearth.

Down he came; and Helen—no longer little—stood grown into a beautiful young woman. The cousins were together nearly four months, from June to October. They walked in summer evenings when the hedges smelt of warm leaf and bruised mint; they met in early mornings when birds stitched the air with their voices and the world’s great machinery had not yet begun to grind. They read out of the same book while the old lady dozed at night over the candles; and the house had its domestic sounds—the ticking clock, the soft clink of a spoon, the sighing of a sleepy fire—into which, little by little, their hearts slipped and fitted like two parts of one old tune.

What Helen knew, Frank taught her. She sang to him; and her voice, coming through the stillness, might have seemed a kind of bread for his spirit—warm, sustaining, impossible to forget once tasted. She gave him her artless heart. She was aware of all his story. Had he made any secret? He had shown her the picture of the woman to whom he was engaged; had shown her too—blushing, and miserable—letters hard, eager, cruel, as if written with a pen dipped in vinegar.

The days went on—happier, closer; with more kindness, more confidence, more pity; until at last an October morning came when Francis went back to college, and Helen felt, as surely as if it had been lifted from her breast, that her tender heart had gone with him.

Frank too awoke from his midsummer dream to the horrible reality of his chain. He tore at it, gnashing like a man who has been tricked into wearing his own handcuffs. Should he confess? Should he give his savings to the woman he was bound to and beg release? There was time—perhaps—yet. He temporised. No living might fall in for years. Meanwhile he and Helen corresponded sadly and fondly; while the betrothed woman, jealous, dissatisfied, harsh as ever, complained—bitterly, and with reason—of his altered tone.

At last the new attachment was discovered. Francis owned it; he cared not to disguise it; rebuked Martha with her violent temper, her imperious anger, and—worst of all—with her inferiority and her age.

Her answer was a threat that made all honour seem a trap. If he did not keep his promise, she would carry his letters into every court in the kingdom—letters in which he had pledged love ten thousand times; she would expose him as perjurer and traitor; and after that—she would kill herself.

He had one more interview with Helen. Her mother was dead then; Helen lived companion with old Lady Pontypool. There, in that last meeting, it was resolved—solemnly, miserably—that he must “do his duty”: that is, redeem his vow; that is, pay a debt cozened from him by a sharper; that is, make two honest people wretched, and call it virtue. They judged it so, and parted.

The living fell in only too soon; yet Frank Bell was quite grey and worn by the time he was inducted. Helen wrote him upon his marriage, beginning “My dear Cousin,” and ending “always truly yours.” She sent back his letters, and the lock of hair—every bit but a small piece. That little remnant lay in her desk years later, when she spoke to the Major.

Bell lived three or four years in his living, then, the Chaplainship of Coventry Island falling vacant, applied privately, obtained it, and announced it to his wife. She objected—as she did to everything. He told her bitterly he did not want her to come; so she went. They sailed out in Governor Crawley’s time; and Bell was intimate with that gentleman in his later years. And it was in Coventry Island—years after his own marriage, and five years after he had heard of the birth of Helen’s boy—that his own daughter was born.

Not by the first Mrs. Bell. That lady died of island fever, very soon after Helen Pendennis and her husband (for Helen had told her husband everything) wrote to inform Bell of the birth of their child. “I was old, was I?” said Mrs. Bell the first. “I was old, and her inferior, was I? but I married you, Mr. Bell, and kept you from marrying her?”—and upon this nourishing reflection she died, as if spite itself were a draught too strong.

Bell married again—a colonial lady—whom he loved fondly. But love, for him, was always served with a side-dish of loss. This second wife died in childbirth; and Bell gave up, as a man gives up music after too much noise. He sent his little girl home to Helen Pendennis and her husband, with a parting prayer that they would befriend her.

The child came to Fairoaks from Bristol—not far, by map, though far enough for a small heart to feel it—dressed in black, in company of a soldier’s wife who had nursed her. At parting from this nurse she wept bitterly; but Helen’s care dried the tears as a warm kitchen dries damp clothes. Around the girl’s neck hung a locket with hair—Helen’s gift of years ago to poor Francis, now dead and buried. This child was all that was left of him; and Helen cherished the legacy with that secret tenderness which lives in women’s cupboards and desk-drawers, among keepsakes and folded papers that have their own smell and silence.

Her father’s dying letter said her name was Helen Laura. But John Pendennis, though he accepted the trust, was always a little jealous of the orphan; and gloomily ordered she should be called by her own mother’s name, and not by that first name her father had given. She was afraid of Mr. Pendennis to the last moment of his life; and only when her husband was gone did Helen dare openly to indulge the full tenderness she felt for the little girl.

Thus Laura Bell became, in all but blood, Mrs. Pendennis’s daughter. Neither her husband nor that gentleman’s brother, the Major, viewed the child with favourable eyes. She reminded the first of circumstances in his wife’s life that he had been forced to accept, but would much have preferred to forget; and as for the second—how should he regard her? She was not related to the Pendennises, nor to any nobleman in the empire; and she had but a couple of thousand pounds.

And now, having kept Mr. Pen waiting on the threshold while we have been stewing this family history down to its essence, let him be admitted.

He had strung up his nerves outside the door like fiddle-strings, and came in determined to face the awful uncle. In his mind the encounter was to be a grand battle; he would be heroic; he would stand for the dignity of his famous family, and for Love, that rash cook who over-salts all youth’s dishes. So he flung open the door with a warlike air, as though he carried a lance and a banner instead of nothing but eighteen years and a hot heart; and he glanced at his adversary as if to say, “Come on. I am ready.”

Major Pendennis, man of the world, surveying this martial entrance, could hardly help grinning at the boy’s pompous simplicity. The Major, too, had measured his ground; he saw the widow was already half conquered by her son’s romantic nonsense, and guessed that threats and tragedy would only make the lad stubborn and solemn. So he laid aside authority at once, stepped forward with the most good-humoured smile in the world, held out his hands, shook Pen’s passive fingers gaily, and said, “Well, Pen, my boy, tell us all about it.”

Mrs. Pendennis was delighted with the Major’s generosity of temper. Pen, on the contrary, was taken aback and disappointed; his tragedy had been robbed of its thunder. His grand entry suddenly felt ridiculous. He blushed; he winced; he was mortified, bewildered, and so near tears that his throat worked like a child swallowing a sob.

“I—I—I didn’t know that you were come till just now,” he said. “Is—is—town very full, I suppose?”

If Pen could hardly gulp the tears down, it was all the Major could do not to laugh them out. He turned and shot a comical glance at Mrs. Pendennis, who felt the scene was ridiculous and sentimental at once; and, having nothing better to do, she went up and kissed Mr. Pen. Thinking of her tenderness and soft obedience to his wishes, the boy melted, as a pat of butter melts at the edge of a pan.

“What a couple of fools they are,” thought the old guardian. “If I hadn’t come down, she would have driven over in state to pay a visit and give her blessing to the young lady’s family.”

“Come, come,” said he, still grinning, “let us have as little sentiment as possible; and, Pen, my good fellow, tell us the whole story.”

Pen rallied into his tragic air again, as if he had picked up the dropped mask. “The story is, sir,” said he, “as I have written it to you before. I have made the acquaintance of a most beautiful and most virtuous lady; of a high family, although in reduced circumstances: I have found the woman in whom I know that the happiness of my life is centred; I feel that I never, never can think about any woman but her. I am aware of the difference of our ages and other difficulties in my way. But my affection was so great that I felt I could surmount all these; that we both could: and she has consented to unite her lot with mine, and to accept my heart and my fortune.”

“How much is that, my boy?” said the Major. “Has anybody left you money? I don’t know that you are worth a shilling in the world.”

“You know what I have is his!” cried Mrs. Pendennis.

“Doubtless, doubtless,” said the Major, swallowing what he was disposed to say. “You would sacrifice anything for him. Everybody knows that. But it is, after all, your fortune which Pen is offering to the young lady; and of which he wishes to take possession at eighteen.”

“I know my mother will give me anything,” Pen said, disturbed.

“Yes, my good fellow; but there is reason in all things. If your mother keeps the house, it is but fair she should select her company. When you give her house over her head, and transfer her banker’s account to yourself for the benefit of Miss What-d’-you-call-’em—Miss Costigan—don’t you think you should at least have consulted my sister, as one of the principal parties in the transaction? I speak without anger or assumption of authority—though the law and your father’s will give it me for three years to come—but as one man of the world to another. Because you can do what you like with your mother, have you a right to do so? As you are her dependent, would it not have been more generous to wait, and at least pay her the courtesy to ask her leave?”

Pen’s head drooped. The romantic heroism began to taste, suddenly, of selfishness—like a rich dish discovered to have been cooked in another person’s fat.

“I did it in a moment of passion,” he said, floundering; “I was not aware what I was going to say or do”—and in this he was perfectly sincere—“but now it is said, and I stand to it. No; I neither can nor will recall it. I’ll die rather than do so. And—I don’t want to burden my mother,” he added. “I’ll work for myself. I’ll go on the stage, and act with her. She says I should do well there.”

“But will she take you on those terms?” the Major interposed. “Mind, I do not say Miss Costigan is not the most disinterested of women; but don’t you suppose, fairly, that your position as a young gentleman of ancient birth and decent expectations forms part of the cause why she finds your addresses welcome?”

“I’ll die, I say, rather than forfeit my pledge,” cried Pen, doubling his fists, red as if the blood itself were affronted.

“Who asks you, my dear friend?” said the Major, imperturbable. “No gentleman breaks his word, of course, when it has been given freely. But you can wait. You owe something to your mother, something to your family—something to me as your father’s representative.”

“Oh, of course,” Pen said—relieved, as if a heavy pot had been lifted from the fire.

“Well; as you have pledged your word to her, give us another, will you, Arthur?”

“What is it?” Arthur asked.

“That you will make no private marriage—that you won’t be taking a trip to Scotland, you understand.”

“That would be a falsehood. Pen never told his mother a falsehood,” Mrs. Pendennis cried.

Pen’s eyes filled with shameful tears. Had not the whole intrigue been a falsehood to that tender creature who would give up all for his sake? He gave his uncle his hand.

“No, sir—on my word of honour, as a gentleman,” he said, “I will never marry without my mother’s consent!” And, giving Helen a bright look of confidence and unchangeable affection, the boy went out into his own study.

“He’s an angel—he’s an angel,” the mother cried, in one of her raptures.

“He comes of a good stock, ma’am,” said the Major—“of a good stock on both sides.” He was greatly pleased with the result of his diplomacy; so much so, that he saluted the tips of Mrs. Pendennis’s glove, and, dropping the curt manly tone he had used with the lad, assumed that particular drawl which always came upon him like a dressing-gown when he felt himself most fine.

“My dear creature,” said he, “I think it certainly as well that I came down; and I flatter myself that last botte was a successful one. I tell you how I came to think of it. Three years ago my kind friend Lady Ferrybridge sent for me in the greatest alarm about her son Gretna, whose affair you remember, and implored me to use my influence with the young gentleman, engaged in an affaire de coeur with a Scotch clergyman’s daughter, Miss MacToddy. I implored, I entreated gentle measures. But Lord Ferrybridge was furious, tried the high hand. Gretna was sulky and silent, and his parents thought they had conquered. But what was the fact, my dear creature? The young people had been married for three months before Lord Ferrybridge knew anything about it. And that is why I extracted the promise from Master Pen.”

“Arthur would never have done so,” Mrs. Pendennis said.

“He hasn’t—that is one comfort,” answered the brother-in-law.

Like a wary man of the world, Major Pendennis did not press Pen farther at the moment. He hoped time would open the boy’s eyes to the absurdity of the scrape. And having found how keen the lad’s point of honour was, he worked upon it kindly after dinner over their wine, discoursing of uprightness and openness, and entreating that communications with the interesting young friend—“Miss Fotheringay,” as the Major politely called Miss Costigan—should be carried on with Mrs. Pendennis’s knowledge, if not her approbation.

“After all, Pen,” the Major said, with a frankness convenient to negotiation, “you must bear in mind you are throwing yourself away. Your mother may submit to your marriage as she would to anything you desire, if you but cry long enough for it; but be sure it can never please her. You take a young woman off the boards of a country theatre and prefer her—such is the case—to one of the finest ladies in England. Your mother will submit, but you can’t suppose she will be happy under it. I have often fancied, entre nous, that my sister had it in her eye to make a marriage between you and that little ward of hers—Flora, Laura—what’s her name? And I always determined to do my small endeavour to prevent any such match. The child has but two thousand pounds. It is only with the utmost economy and care that my sister can provide for the decent maintenance of her house, and for your appearance and education as a gentleman; and I don’t mind owning to you I had other and much higher views for you. With your name and birth, sir—with your talents, which I suppose are respectable—with the friends whom I have the honour to possess, I could have placed you in an excellent position—a remarkable position for a young man of such exceeding small means—and hoped to see you try to restore the honours of our name. Your mother’s softness stopped one prospect, or you might have been a general like our gallant ancestor at Ramillies and Malplaquet. I had another plan: my excellent kind friend Lord Bagwig would, I have little doubt, have attached you to his mission at Pumpernickel, and you might have advanced in diplomacy. But—pardon me—how is a man to serve a young gentleman of eighteen who proposes to marry a lady of thirty, selected from a booth in a fair?—well, not a fair—a barn. That profession at once is closed to you. The public service is closed to you. Society is closed to you. You may get on at the bar, where I am given to understand gentlemen of merit occasionally marry out of their kitchens; but in no other profession. Or you may come and live down here—down here, mon Dieu!—for ever,” said the Major, shrugging, thinking with inexpressible fondness of Pall Mall, “where your mother will receive the Mrs. Arthur that is to be with kindness; where the good people of the county won’t visit you; and where, by Gad, sir, I shall be shy of visiting you myself, for I like to live with gentlemen; where you will have to live with rum-and-water—drinking gentlemen—farmers; and drag through life the young husband of an old woman, who, if she doesn’t quarrel with your mother, will at least cost that lady her position and drag her down into the dubious caste into which you must fall. It is no affair of mine. I am not angry. Your downfall will not hurt me further than that it extinguishes the hopes I had of seeing my family once more taking its place in the world. It is only your mother and yourself that will be ruined; and I pity you both from my soul. Pass the claret: it is some I sent to your poor father; I remember I bought it at poor Lord Levant’s sale. But of course,” added he, smacking the wine, “having engaged yourself, you will do what becomes you as a man of honour, however fatal your promise may be. However, promise us on our side what I set out by entreating you to grant—that there shall be nothing clandestine, that you will pursue your studies, that you will only visit your interesting friend at proper intervals. Do you write to her much?”

Pen blushed, and said—why, yes—he had written.
