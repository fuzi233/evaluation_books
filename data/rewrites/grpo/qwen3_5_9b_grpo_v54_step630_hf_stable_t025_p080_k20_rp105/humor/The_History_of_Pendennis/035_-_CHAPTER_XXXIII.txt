The Captain, a man of theatrical flair even in captivity, stood before his audience—a motley crew of publishers and aspiring writers—and delivered a manifesto that sounded less like a plea for justice and more like a press release for a revolutionary uprising. "Gentlemen of England!" he declaimed, his voice booming off the damp prison walls. "The hour has struck! Our glorious order is besieged by foreign revolutions, by the treachery of mill-owners, and by the gullible masses they have led astray! The ancient monarchy is insulted by a rabble of republicans; the Church is undermined by stealthy infidels. We are left defenseless, our institutions exposed to the plunder of men who believe in nothing holy, who know no history, and who break every law strong enough to command."

Mr. Shandon, the Captain's co-conspirator, leaned in to amplify the drama. "It was because the French kings distrusted their gentlemen that Saint Louis fell," he noted, eyeing the room with a conspiratorial glint. "But here, the people still believe in their gentlemen! It was because we were led by them that the Eagles retreated before us from the Donro to the Garonne. A gentleman broke the line at Trafalgar; a gentleman swept the plains of Waterloo."

At the mention of Waterloo, Mr. Bungay, the publisher, nodded sagely, winking at the Captain as if sharing a secret joke. Warrington, however, couldn't hold back; he burst into laughter.

"You see how our venerable friend Bungay is affected," Shandon whispered slyly to Warrington, feigning innocence while looking down at his papers. "That is the true test. I have used the Duke of Wellington a hundred times, and the Duke never fails to inspire such... enthusiasm."

The Captain then confessed, with a candour that bordered on the absurd, that the gentlemen of England had long neglected their political duties, leaving them to professional proctors and advocates. "No more!" he cried. "The time for hired hands is gone. Our enemies are brave, strong, and numerous. We must meet them in the field, not in the pages of Grub Street gazettes. That is a dig at Bacon's people, Mr. Bungay," Shandon added, turning to the publisher. Bungay clapped his stick on the floor with a sound like a gunshot. "Hang him! Pitch into him, Captain!" he roared, wagging his dull head at Warrington. "For a slashing article, there is nobody like the Captain—nobody!"

The prospectus continued, promising a journal dedicated solely to the interests of the "Gentlemen of England." Some subscribers, whose names were withheld for "obvious reasons," would back this venture. "They are loyal to their Sovereign," the Captain flourished, "and love their Church. They would keep England the greatest and freest nation in the world." He paused dramatically for effect. "As the names of some of them are appended to the deed that secured our liberties at Runnymede—"

"What's that?" asked Mr. Bungay, genuinely curious.

"An ancestor of mine sealed it with his sword-hilt," Pen said with grave dignity.

"It's the Habeas Corpus, Mr. Bungay," Warrington corrected him. The publisher yawned, though he muttered, "Go on, Captain."

The Captain rallied the crowd, declaring that the *Pall Mall Gazette* would be written by gentlemen for gentlemen, addressing the highest circles of society. He spoke of literary departments conducted by men famous at the Universities, a claim that made Pen blush and laugh simultaneously. He urged the nobility, the clergy, and the matrons of England to rally to the cause. At the conclusion, Mr. Bungay woke from a second snooze, nodded approvingly, and pocketed the manuscript with the satisfaction of a man who had just acquired a weapon. He didn't just pay for the MS.; he called little Mary over and gave her a penny as he departed.

Once the reading concluded, the group turned to general conversation. Shandon, dressed in a tattered dressing-gown, spoke with the ease of a man who lived in the heart of the "beau monde." He recounted anecdotes of the Earl of X and dinners at great houses, telling tales with such glib familiarity that Mrs. Shandon listened with fond admiration, believing every word. Pen found it amusing to hear the shabby prisoner talk so confidently of the great.

Eventually, Mr. Bungay rose to leave, followed by Warrington and Pen. Shandon, despite a deprecatory look from his wife, insisted on walking them to the prison gate. In his shabby slippers, he shuffled ahead, his hand already fiddling with his waistcoat pocket where Bungay's five-pound note rested. He handed the note to one follower, sent a leg of mutton to a prisoner, paid a tavern bill, and lost several half-crowns at cards with friends. By the time he emerged from the prison gates, he was as poor as he had been in the morning.

Mrs. Shandon watched him run off toward the tavern, her heart heavy. She had hoped to share a meal of meat and salad with her husband and little Mary, but now she would be alone. She made herself tea instead, a ritual that seemed to comfort countless women in similar straits. Soon, Mr. Jack Finucane arrived, bringing sweets for the child and news of Shandon's latest exploits. Jack, a townsman of the Captain, treated him as a genius and a patron, ready to run errands and manage debts for the embarrassed Irish gentleman.

Outside, Bungay and his companions discussed the Captain's finances. "He won't have that five-pound note very long," Bungay predicted. And he was right; Mrs. Shandon later found only a couple of shillings and a few halfpence left in his pockets.

Later that evening, at the Black Kitchen, Pen and Warrington debated the nature of genius and the tyranny of publishers. Pen defended Shandon, arguing that misfortune drove men to bad company and that one must deal kindly with the eccentricities of genius. Warrington, a severe moralist, countered that genius was not a shield against debt. "If a lawyer or a soldier goes to gaol for owing money, an author must too," he argued. "Rags are not proof of genius; capital is the master."

Despite their philosophical squabble, both men agreed on one thing: they pitied Shandon. "His genius sings within his prison bars," Pen mused. Warrington nodded bitterly. "He accommodates himself to a cage pretty well. He might have a high place, but he can't, so he drinks with Tom and Jack. He might provide for his family, but they have a bottle of brandy they want him to taste. Let us pity the misfortunes of genius and conspire against the publishing tyrants."

"Are you going to have another glass of brandy-and-water?" Pen asked with a humorous look. Warrington ordered a fresh pot with sugar, and the pair retired to their chambers.

There, they found two notes from Mr. Bungay inviting them to dinner. Pen, reflecting on the day's events, lay awake gazing at a moonlit sketch of Fairoaks drawn by Laura. He wondered if he was finally going to earn his bread with his pen, to honor his mother and prove himself to Laura. He prayed for honesty, for truth, and for the courage to stand firm against flattery and prejudice. Overcome with emotion, he knelt by his bed, repeating the words his mother used to say before tucking him in, his eyes streaming with tears of hope and submission.

The next morning, a large brown-paper parcel arrived, addressed to Warrington. Inside were new books—travels, novels, and poems—sent specifically for review. "Catch!" Warrington shouted, flinging the package at Pen's head. Pen caught it, trembling with delight. "Sport the oak, Pidgeon," he declared, refusing visitors. He sat in his easy chair, barely drinking his tea, eager to dive into the pages and begin his work as a reviewer.
