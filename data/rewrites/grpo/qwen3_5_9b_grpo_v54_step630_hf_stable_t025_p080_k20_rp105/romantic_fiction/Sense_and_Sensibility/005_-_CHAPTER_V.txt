The moment the letter confirming her new residence was sent, Mrs. Dashwood turned to her son-in-law and daughter-in-law with a bright, relieved smile. "I have secured a home," she announced, her voice light with the joy of independence. "I shall not trouble you any longer than until my new quarters are fully prepared."

Surprise rippled through the room. Mrs. John Dashwood remained silent, her expression unreadable, while her husband offered a polite hope that she would not settle too far from Norland. With a cheerful spirit, Mrs. Dashwood replied that she was moving to Devonshire.

Edward, unable to contain his reaction, spun quickly toward her. His voice trembled with a mixture of shock and deep concern. "Devonshire! You are really going there? How far from us! And to which part?"

She explained the location: a mere four miles north of Exeter.

"It is but a cottage," she continued, her tone warm and inviting. "Yet I look forward to welcoming many friends there. A few rooms can easily be added, and if my guests find the journey worthwhile, I shall certainly make every effort to accommodate them."

She concluded with a gracious invitation for both Mr. and Mrs. John Dashwood to visit her at Barton, but to Edward, she offered a personal summons charged with even greater affection. Although her recent talk with her daughter-in-law had hardened her resolve to leave Norland as soon as possible, it had not altered her heart regarding Edward. To separate him from Elinor was never her intention; quite the opposite. By extending this pointed invitation to her brother, she wished to demonstrate to Mrs. John Dashwood exactly how little weight she attached to her disapproval of the marriage.

Mr. John Dashwood, however, expressed his sorrow repeatedly. He lamented that the distance of the new house would prevent him from assisting with the removal of her furniture. He genuinely felt a pang of conscience, for the very exertion he had promised his father to undertake was now rendered impossible by this arrangement. The furniture itself was modest in bulk but precious in sentiment: household linens, silverware, china, books, and a beautiful pianoforte belonging to Marianne. As the crates departed, Mrs. John Dashwood sighed deeply. She could not suppress the thought that it was unfair for Mrs. Dashwood, whose income was so meager compared to theirs, to retain such fine articles.

Mrs. Dashwood had leased the cottage for a year; it was already furnished, and she could take possession immediately. There was no friction in the agreement. She waited only to clear her effects from Norland and finalize her new household before departing for the west. Being naturally swift in matters that mattered to her, these tasks were completed in short order. The horses her husband had left her had been sold shortly after his death, and following the earnest advice of her eldest daughter, she agreed to sell her carriage as well. For the sake of her children's comfort, she might have kept it, but Elinor's prudent counsel prevailed. Furthermore, Elinor's wisdom dictated that their staff be reduced to three: two maids and a man, whom they quickly recruited from among those who had served them at Norland.

One of the men and one of the maids were dispatched immediately to Devonshire to prepare the cottage for their mistress's arrival. Since Lady Middleton was entirely unknown to Mrs. Dashwood, she preferred traveling directly to the cottage rather than stopping as a guest at Barton Park. She relied so completely on Sir John's description of the property that she felt no curiosity to inspect it herself until she stepped inside as its owner.

Her eagerness to leave Norland was undiminished, fueled by the visible satisfaction of her daughter-in-law at the prospect of her removal—a happiness that was only feebly masked by a cold suggestion to delay the departure. This was the moment when Mr. John Dashwood's promise to his father could finally be fulfilled with propriety. Since he had neglected to act upon it when he first arrived at the estate, their departure seemed the most suitable time to honor it. Yet, Mrs. Dashwood soon abandoned any hope of such assistance. From the general drift of his conversation, she became convinced that his aid would extend no further than providing them with maintenance for six months at Norland. He spoke so often of the rising costs of housekeeping and the endless demands on a man's purse—claims that any gentleman of consequence faced—that he seemed to stand in need of money himself rather than possessing any desire to give it away.

Within a few weeks of receiving Sir John Middleton's first letter, everything was settled in their new abode, and Mrs. Dashwood and her daughters were ready to begin their journey.

Many tears were shed during their final farewells to a place so deeply beloved. "Dear, dear Norland!" Marianne cried out as she wandered alone before the house on the last evening of their stay. "When shall I cease to regret you? When shall I learn to feel at home elsewhere? Oh, happy house, could you know the suffering I endure now viewing you from this spot, from whence I may perhaps never view you again! And you, ye well-known trees! But you will remain unchanged. No leaf will decay because we are gone, nor any branch grow still because we can no longer watch them. No; you will continue as you are, unconscious of the pleasure or pain you cause, and insensible to any change in those who once walked beneath your shade! But who will remain to enjoy you?"
