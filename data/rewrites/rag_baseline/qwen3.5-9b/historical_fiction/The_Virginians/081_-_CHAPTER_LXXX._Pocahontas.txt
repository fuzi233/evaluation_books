The air in the theater was thick with the scent of unwashed bodies and impending failure. I stood on the stage, watching the darkness swallow the lights, feeling no shock, only a detached observation of mechanics gone wrong. The audience was not a collection of souls but a shifting mass of indifference, punctuated by the sharp, ugly sounds of rejection.

Miss Pritchard had entered earlier, dressed as a Red Indian. She wore the costume with a terrible accuracy, a fidelity to history that seemed to invite mockery rather than reverence. When she rushed to the stake, clutching the captive, the house did not hold its breath. Instead, a voice from the pit erupted—a crude, repetitive chant about the Belle Savage. It was a joke, trivial and malicious, designed to break the spell of the moment. The laughter followed, fitful and explosive, turning the sacred drama into a potboiler. I watched the actors shrink under the weight of the noise. They were professionals, trained to endure, yet here they were, stripped of their dignity by the ignorance of the crowd.

My friend Sampson, usually so buoyant, had retreated into the shadows. He claimed the house swarmed with enemies, men who wanted the galleries washed with brimstone. He spoke of conspiracies involving managers and actors, weaving a narrative of betrayal that explained everything and nothing. He threatened to throw a rival over the edge, a gesture of loyalty that felt increasingly absurd in the face of such a diffuse, invisible enemy. We had hoped to sell our eggs at market; instead, they lay broken on the floor of our expectations.

Earlier, before the curtain fell, I had noted the signs. The manager, a man of flippant demeanor, had dismissed my presence with a wave, telling me to fetch punch elsewhere lest my countenance darken the room further. His face was grim, matching the mood of the greenroom where everyone looked doubtful. Miss Pritchard suffered from a headache; the wig on Hagan's head was powdered poorly; the atmosphere was one of quiet dread. I had argued with him then, asserting my right to comment on his face while denying his authority over mine. It was a petty exchange, a small rebellion against the machinery of the theater, but it highlighted the distance between us. He was made for the stage; I was merely the ghost haunting it.

As the second act ended, the news arrived. Not from the battlefield, but from the box office. The tide had turned decisively against us. I walked out, calm as a captain steering a sinking ship, accepting the gales and the broken masts as inevitable forces of nature. No one could blame me for the clamor of the crowd; I could not stop the wind, nor the voices. I carried my defeat with a stoicism that perhaps bordered on arrogance, refusing to show the tremor that must have been inside me.

Back at home, the silence was heavier than the noise of the theater. My wife, Theo, waited for me. Her face was pale, drained of color, betraying the knowledge of the disaster before I could speak. On the table lay the gifts from the gardener: flowers arranged with care, tiny painted standards meant to celebrate a victory. They looked ridiculous now, artifacts of a hope that had evaporated. I looked at her, at the artless trophies she had set up for a hero who had failed, and the dam broke. The courage I had maintained on stage crumbled instantly. The pain was sharp, a physical blow that echoed the loss of the night, proving that despite the calm exterior, the heart remains vulnerable to the collapse of dreams.
