Once the initial shock of our reunion subsided, the little maid, Hetty, launched immediately into a torrent of accusation.

"So, you have finally arrived to inquire after Theo, and you feel contrite that your neglect brought her to such illness?" she demanded, her voice trembling with a mixture of fury and despair. "For six weeks she has languished, and you have uttered not a single word of concern! How very kind of you, Mr. George, I am sure!"

"Kind?" Mr. Warrington gasped, the word escaping him like a broken breath.

"I suppose you label kindness the act of visiting her every day for a year, only to vanish without a trace?" Hetty retorted, her shoulders rising in a sharp shrug. "A magnificent promise, indeed, to render my darling ill and then abruptly declare, 'Farewell, Theo,' and depart forever. Gentlemen make such vows, I assume, because they intend to honor them. I would not toy with a poor child's heart and abandon her if I were a man. What crime has she committed against you, but to be foolish and far too affectionate? Pray, sir, by what divine right do you whisk her away from us, only to desert her because an elderly American lady disapproves? She was content among us prior to your arrival. She adored her sister—there had never been a sibling so devoted—until she encountered you. And now, merely because your mother believes a younger gentleman might secure a better fortune, you must abandon her, simply because!"

"Great powers, child!" I cried, feeling the heat rise in my cheeks at her stubborn logic. "Did I draw back? Am I not the one forbidden entry into your household? Did your father not demand, upon my honor, that I cease all attempts to see her?"

"Honor!" she exclaimed, her voice cracking. "And you, who pretend to be our superiors, expect us to venerate you! I declare, George Warrington, you ought to retreat to your schoolroom in Virginia, have your black nurse tuck you into bed, and petition your mother for permission to step outside. Oh, George! I never imagined my sister was surrendering her heart to a man lacking the spine to stand by her; instead, he fled at the first obstacle! When Dr. Heberden mentioned attending to you, I resolved to visit, and you truly look ill, which brings me no comfort. I presume it is fear of your mother that plagues you. Yet, I shall not inform Theo of your sickness. She cannot abandon her solemn engagements, break free from the room, and stride into the world the next day as if nothing had transpired! That is reserved for men, our masters in courage and wisdom—to desert an angel, yes, an angel ten thousand times superior to you; an angel who once loved me until she met you, and who was the blessing of our lives—all of us—is what you term honor? Do not tell me, sir! I despise you all! Are we mere servants to your wit, your tragedies, and your verses? I consider them often tedious. I shall not spend my nights copying your manuscripts, nor waste hours gazing from a window, neglecting everyone to spy upon your stately walk with your hat cocked! If you are departing—and God bless you—give me back my sister! Return to me my darling of the olden days, who cherished every soul around her until she saw you. And you abandon her solely because your mother seeks a richer bride for you! Oh, valiant gentleman! Marry the woman your mother selects, and let my dear perish here, forsaken!"

"Heavens, Hetty!" I cried, stunned by the ferocity of her reasoning. "Am I the one wishing to abandon your sister? Did I not offer to uphold my promise? Was it not your father who denied me access and compelled me to swear never again to seek her sight? All I possess is my word and my honor!"

"Honor, indeed!" she shrieked, her composure crumbling. "You keep your pledge to him, yet shatter it for her! A wretched sort of honor! If I were a man, I would soon reveal exactly what I thought of your honor! Only I forgot—you are bound to maintain peace and must not... Oh, George, George! Do you not perceive the agony tearing me apart? I am distraught, scarcely knowing what I utter. You must not leave my darling. They are unaware at home. They do not comprehend as I do; I know her best, and she will succumb to death if you depart. Speak that you will stay! Have compassion on me, Mr. Warrington, and restore me to my dearest!"

Thus, the warm-hearted, frantic creature swayed from indignation to supplication, from scorn to tears. Was my little physician correct in describing her plea as such? Was there no alternative remedy to what Hetty cried out for? Had not others endured the same cruel severance, suffered the same exhaustion and fever, lain hopeless of survival, and yet, miraculously, recovered to limp through life? Perhaps, save for the fact that love is inherently selfish, disregarding the grief and passions of others, or that our bond was so intense and unique that we believed no other lovers could suffer as we did—in this passionate young advocate for her sister—we might have witnessed proof that a loving heart could be struck by the disease of love and endure it silently, eventually healing. What had occurred in Hetty's own case? Our sister and I, basking in our easy triumph and intimate confidences, had frequently discussed this matter, egotistical creatures that we were, perhaps deriving a secret zest and sense of security from her lesser attachment. It was akin to sitting by a fireplace while the winter howled without, or walking beside the great river and watching ships toss at sea. We clung to one another more tightly, and wrapped in our own felicity, regarded others' misfortunes with complacent pity. Let the truth be as it may. Granted that we might have been separated, and eventually survived the rift—much my skeptical old self admits. Yet, at that time, I was eager to share Hetty's terrors and apprehensions, willingly choosing to believe that the life dearest to me in the world would be sacrificed if severed from mine. Was I mistaken? I would not claim so now. I may harbor doubts regarding myself (or perhaps not, I know), but concerning her, never; and Hetty found in me a willing partner in her alarms and fears. I considered imparting some of these feelings to our doctor; but the good gentleman silenced me. "Hush," he said, with a comical expression of terror. "I must hear none of this. If two individuals who happen to know one another chanced to meet and converse in my patients' chamber, I could not help myself; but regarding matchmaking and courtship, I am your humble servant! What will the General do when he returns to town? He will have me stationed behind Montagu House as surely as I am a living doctor, and alive I wish to remain, my good sir!" With that, he skipped into his carriage, leaving me there to meditate. "And you and Miss Hetty must have no further meetings here, mind you," he had previously warned.

"Oh, no! Of course, we would have none!" I assured myself. "We are gentlemen of honor, and so forth, and our word is our bond." Furthermore, to have seen Hetty was not that an inestimable boon, and would we not be eternally grateful? I felt so refreshed by that drop of water I had consumed that I believed I could endure for an indefinite period. I walked away with Hetty to Soho, never once contemplating arranging a new rendezvous. But the little emissary was more perceptive, asking if I intended to visit the Museum to read. "Oh, yes, sometimes, my dear," I replied, "but I am too wretched for reading now; I cannot discern what lies upon the paper. I do not care for my books. Even *Pocahontas* grows wearisome to me. I..." I might have continued indefinitely, when, "Nonsense!" she declared, stamping her little foot. "By Jove, I declare, George, you are more stupid than Harry!"

"How do you mean, my dear child?" I asked.

"When do you depart?" she pressed. "You leave at three o'clock. You cross over to Tottenham Court Road. You traverse the village and return via Green Lane, leading back toward the new hospital. You know you do! If you stroll there for a week, it will do you no harm. Good morning, sir! Please, do not follow me any farther." And she dropped a curtsey, veiling her face, and departed.

That Green Lane, lying north of the new hospital, is now entirely built over with residences. In my time, when the virtuous King George II still reigned, it was a shabby rural thoroughfare of London, so perilous that city dwellers venturing to their villas and country houses at Hampstead and the outlying villages would return in groups during the night, accompanied by waiters carrying lanterns to protect them from highwaymen prowling the town's outskirts. The churches of Hampstead and Highgate, each crowning its hill, formed the backdrop of the vista one beheld turning away from London. For one, two, or three days, Mr. George Warrington enjoyed viewing this landscape and walking back in the direction of the new hospital.
