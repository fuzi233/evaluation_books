Word of Pen's latest escapade spread faster than wildfire, sparking a mix of fury and amusement across the household. To the staid and sensible Doctor Portman, the boy's behavior was a scandalous breach of decorum; to the boisterous Major Pendennis, it was mere youthful frolic, a charming bit of drama. But for Mrs. Pendennis, the news sent her into a flutter of genuine distress. Her imagination ran wild, conjuring scenes of sin, shame, and ruin. She viewed the entire episode as a catastrophic failure, convinced that nothing short of removing Pen from Chatteris could save his soul. "I must get him away!" she cried, wringing her hands. "Anywhere but here! Away from the woman who tangled him in this mess!"

When the loving mother and the stern doctor finally confronted Pen, expecting tears and repentance, they were met with something far more formidable: the heavy, self-important gravity of a teenager convinced of his own righteousness. Pen listened to their rebukes with the air of a man accused of high treason. "I am truly sorry," he declared, his voice dripping with dramatic flair, "but I was provoked without cause! A gentleman cannot stand idly by when his honor is touched. I had to defend myself with dignity and appeal to my uncle."

" *Vous allez trop vite,* " Uncle Pen interjected, feigning confusion despite his obvious amusement. "Between men of the world, perhaps, you might have a case. But between two schoolboys, this sort of theatrical posturing is simply ridiculous, my dear boy."

"It is wicked!" Mrs. Pendennis sobbed, her eyes wet with worry. "So unlike him!"

Pen, sensing an opening, leaned in for a hug, puffing out his chest. "Women, dearest mother, simply do not understand these matters of honor! I placed myself entirely in the hands of Foker; it was the only path a gentleman could take."

Major Pendennis chuckled, shaking his head. "Progress, I see. Great progress." Meanwhile, Mrs. Pendennis lamented the influence of Mr. Foker, calling him a "wicked, horrid little wretch." She confessed that she had a sudden, fierce urge to forbid Pen's university attendance entirely. Yet, duty and tradition held her back. Her husband had always planned this future, and the college was where he himself had briefly flourished. So, reluctantly, she acquiesced, though a shadow of dread lingered.

Indeed, the arrangement was final. Pen would depart at the next October term. Mr. Foker had vouched for him, promising introductions to the elite circles. The Major waxed eloquent on the benefits of this networking. "Mr. Foker knows the very best young men," the Major boasted, listing names like the Young Marquis of Plinlimmon and Lord Magnus Charters. "He is connected to Lady Runnymede, who will surely invite Pen to Logwood. And consider his friendship with the son of Lady Agnes—it is a stroke of luck, a blessing disguised as a risk."

Helen, however, remained skeptical. Though she admitted Foker had been kind in the Costigan affair, a gnawing fear persisted. The noise of quarrels and the glare of worldly ambition terrified her for her son's safety.

Doctor Portman, conversely, was firm. "College is essential," he reasoned. "If he reads and mixes with the best society, he will shine. Smirke speaks highly of his intellect, and I have heard him recite classics with commendable skill." Pen, usually prone to gloom over his private heartaches, agreed grudgingly, distracted by the whirlwind of recent troubles.

As summer waned into August and September, Chatteris buzzed with the annual assizes and horse races. Miss Fotheringay, the local actress, performed her farewell seasons, drawing crowds who barely noticed her impending departure. To the polite locals, she was just another performer, a fleeting novelty. Yet, amidst the spectacle, Miss Costigan held her ground. With a stoicism born of simple temperament, she ate, drank, slept, and worked with a comforting regularity, avoiding the emotional turbulence that plagued others. In her quiet resilience, she proved that sometimes, a lack of sensitivity is a profound gift.

Pen, meanwhile, wandered the theater and the town with a restless air that worried his mother. But the Major, ever the strategist, interpreted this restlessness as a sign of recovery. "Look at him," the Major observed. "He writes verses! He prowls the shrubberies spouting poetry! He even eats with a decent appetite." The discovery of a leather-bound volume filled with Latin and English verse in Pen's study confirmed the Major's suspicions. The boy was channeling his turmoil into art. "Gad, I wish I could eat like that," the Major mused, thinking of his own dietary struggles. "The boy sleeps soundly."

Lacking a mother for intimate confessions, Pen turned to the Curate, Smirke. Their bond deepened, transforming into a secret, passionate friendship. Pen poured his heart out, treating Smirke as a confessor. He wrote sonnets, penned Latin verses, and showered the Curate with gifts: his finest books, a gold chain, and even an offer of his double-barreled gun. He bought a gold pencil-case on credit just to give to Smirke, inscribing it with vows of eternal devotion. For Smirke, the situation was a delicate trap. The prospect of Pen leaving for college threatened to sever his lifeline to Fairoaks. Without Pen, his daily visits to the widow would vanish. Could he hope for more? The widow's recent kindnesses—a compliment on his sermon, thanks for a melon, warm words for Pen—had flattered his hopes. Was she hinting at something deeper? Or was it all innocent gratitude? The stakes were terrifying: if he pushed too hard and was rejected, the gates of Fairoaks might close forever.

While Pen navigated his stormy emotions, Smirke found solace in the company of Madame Fribsby. The eccentric spinster, formerly a milliner from London and a devotee of romantic fiction, lived a life steeped in melodrama. She believed everyone in love, interpreting every glance as a proposal and every silence as a tragedy. She convinced Smirke that the widow, Mrs. Pendennis, was deeply, silently in love with him. "She is so lonely," Madame Fribsby cooed, speaking in broken French. "When Master Pendennis leaves, she will have no one. You must tell her, Monsieur le Curé!" Smirke, shy and hopeful, enjoyed these sessions, where the Milliner's deaf mother sat in silent ignorance of the heated confessions.

Back at the Rectory, the atmosphere grew thick with gossip. The village, fueled by the tongues of Mrs. Pybus, Mrs. Speers, and Captain Glanders, spun tales of scandal. They whispered that Pen had nearly killed someone over a girl, that the whole Fotheringay affair was a cover-up involving blackmail, and that the Pendennis family was on the brink of total disgrace. These rumors stung Pen's pride, turning him into a local pariah. Even the clergyman, Simcoe, preached fiery sermons against "theatrical sins" and "lost youth," clearly targeting Pen.

Amidst the chaos, the real scandal simmered beneath the surface: the alleged romance between Smirke and Mrs. Pendennis. Madame Fribsby, unable to contain her theories, eventually spilled the beans to Mrs. Pybus. Soon, the story was everywhere. Mrs. Portman, the Rector's wife, rushed to confirm the tale with Doctor Portman. "What a sly fox!" the Doctor fumed. "To think of the widow taking a fancy to that milksop!" He was so incensed that he considered firing Smirke immediately.

"But he is too young for her," Mrs. Portman gently countered, noting the age gap. "She might be his mother."

"Nonsense!" the Doctor snapped. "A matron with a grown son will never remarry!"

Yet, the Doctor's anger quickly gave way to a secret. After calming his wife with a joke about ghosts dancing at weddings, he revealed the truth: Smirke was actually engaged to a Miss Thompson living on Clapham Common. The story of the romance with the widow was pure fabrication, invented by the gossips.

Feeling relieved but determined to stop the rumors, the Doctor decided to intervene personally. He invited Smirke to dinner, hoping to steer him away from the scandal. Just as he prepared to visit the Curate, he caught sight of Smirke emerging from Madame Fribsby's house. The Curate was impeccably dressed, his silk stockings gleaming, his cravat stiff and perfect, smelling faintly of eau-de-cologne.

" *O gracilis puer* ," the Doctor called out, trying to sound paternal. "Where are you bound? I wanted you to dine with me."

"I am engaged to dine... at Fairoaks," Smirke replied, his cheeks flushing pink as he tucked his scented handkerchief away. Mounting his pony, he trotted away, heading straight for the Pendennis estate, unaware that the storm of gossip he had helped create was already brewing around him, and that his dinner invitation from the Doctor was about to turn into a serious intervention.
