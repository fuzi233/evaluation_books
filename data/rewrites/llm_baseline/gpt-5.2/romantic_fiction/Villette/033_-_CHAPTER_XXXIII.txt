On the first of May, the whole house—twenty boarders and four teachers—received orders that sounded like a trumpet-call and a kitchen-bell struck at once: up at five; dressed by six; ready to march under the command of M. le Professeur Emanuel. He meant, at last, to keep his promise and take his flock out of Villette to breakfast in the country.

I, it is true, had once been told—by tone, by look, by a very intelligible coldness—that I was not invited to that projected fête. When I now ventured to recall this, and to ask quietly how it stood, my ear was caught and smartly pulled: the answer came by that sharp pressure. I did not protest; I only learned. He leaned nearer, as if the other ear also were within reach of discipline.

“Je vous conseille de vous faire prier,” he said; and the menace of his voice made the counsel imperial.

One Napoleonic compliment was enough. I made up my mind to be of the party.

Morning broke with a calm that was almost domestic in its gentleness: birds stitched their small clear notes through the garden like bright thread; the dew lay as a thin mist—cool on the cheek, promising heat later, like milk still faintly chilled before it warms near the stove. We all felt it would be warm; and there was a pleasure, half liberty, in folding away heavier things, in choosing what suited a sunny season.

The girls came down in clean print dresses and light straw bonnets—each bonnet trimmed with that French gift of making the unpretending look exactly right, as if good taste were a sort of daily bread. Nobody rustled in faded silk; nobody clung to a second-hand “best” like a relic. It was all fresh and honest, like linen aired in the morning wind.

At six the bell rang—merry, metallic, impatient—and we poured down the stairs, through the carré, along the corridor, into the vestibule. There stood our Professor. He had put away the savage paletôt and the severe bonnet-grec; he wore instead a belted blouse that made him look younger, and a straw hat that did not fight the sunshine. He gave us the kindest good-morrow; and most of us, receiving it, paid him back with a smile that was almost thanks.

We were marshalled, set in order, and soon we started. The streets were quiet; the boulevards were fresh and peaceful as fields. I believe we were happy as we walked. This chief of ours—capable, when he chose, of striking fear into a heart as if with a sudden clang—could also, by some secret touch, set happiness moving through a whole line of girls like a brisk air through curtains.

He did not lead in front nor stalk behind; he walked up and down our ranks, speaking to each, talking much to his favourites, yet not wholly forgetting even those he disliked. For my own part, I wished—on private grounds—to keep slightly out of his direct notice. I was paired with Ginevra Fanshawe; my arm bore the dear pressure of that angel’s very substantial limb. She continued in excellent case; and to support the burden of her loveliness through a warm day was no slight labour. Many times I wished, with honest fatigue, there had been less of the charming commodity.

Still, I made her useful. I interposed her always between myself and M. Paul—shifting as I heard him coming up on the right or the left, placing Miss Fanshawe like a bright screen, a bouquet held up to hide one’s own blush.

My motive had its colour. The new print dress I wore was pink. Under our present convoy, this made me feel much as I have felt when, wearing a shawl with a red border, obliged to cross a meadow where a bull pastured. For a while, the shifting system, and certain arts with my black silk scarf—shaken out, spread wide, fringes arranged like a little barricade—served; but by-and-by he discovered that, come he on which side he would, Miss Fanshawe remained his neighbour.

Their acquaintance had never run smooth. His temper always crisped at her English accent; their dispositions did not fit—they jarred the moment they touched. He held her empty and affected; she thought him bearish, meddling, and repellent. At last, after he had changed his place the sixth time and found the same result, he thrust his head forward, fixed his eyes on mine, and demanded, with impatient suspicion—

“Qu’est-ce que c’est? Vous me jouez des tours?”

The words were scarcely out when, with his quickness, he seized the root of my manoeuvre. In vain I shook out the long fringe and spread the broad end of my scarf.

“A-h-h! c’est la robe rose!” broke from him—so sudden, so oddly indignant, it affected me like the abrupt low of some lord of the meadow.

“It is only cotton,” I urged, hurriedly; “and cheaper, and washes better than any other colour.”

“Et Mademoiselle Lucy est coquette comme dix Parisiennes,” he retorted. “A-t-on jamais vu une Anglaise pareille. Regardez plutôt son chapeau, et ses gants, et ses brodequins!”

Those articles were like my companions’; not one whit smarter—perhaps plainer than most. But he had found his text; I felt the sermon coming, and I began to chafe under it. Yet it passed as a summer storm sometimes does—threatening much, discharging little. I had but one flash of sheet-lightning, a single bantering smile from his eyes; and then, quite softly—

“Courage!—à vrai dire je ne suis pas fâché, peut-être même suis-je content qu’on s’est fait si belle pour ma petite fête.”

“Mais ma robe n’est pas belle, Monsieur—elle n’est que propre.”

“J’aime la propreté,” said he.

So the sun of his good humour triumphed; it consumed little scudding clouds before they stained his morning.

And now we were in the country—among what they called “les bois et les petits sentiers.” A month later these woods and lanes would be dusty and of doubtful seclusion; now they were May-green and at rest, with leaf-shade still thin enough to let the light sift through like flour through a sieve. The air smelled of damp earth warming, of sap, of crushed grass; every footfall had its own small sound—gravel clicking, twigs snapping, the soft brush of skirts—and above it the birds held their bright, living orchestra. It was a soundscape to heal the mind: no iron wheels shrieking, no factory throb, no city’s endless metallic fret. Only breath, step, and song.

We reached a well—planted round, in Labassecour taste, with an orderly circle of lime-trees. Here we halted. On the green swell of ground surrounding the well we were ordered to sit; Monsieur took his place in our midst and allowed us to gather round him in a knot. Those who liked him more than they feared came close (chiefly the little ones); those who feared more than they liked kept away; those in whom affection had given even fear a pleasurable zest held the greatest distance.

He began to tell us a story.

He could narrate—well. His diction had that strength children love and learned men envy: simple, yet not poor; plain, yet never bare. He tinted a twilight scene—so quietly, with such sure touch, that it fixed itself in me like a remembered scent. I have never seen from any artist’s pencil the very picture he made with words.

I had no impromptu faculty; that deficiency made me marvel the more at his. He was not a man to write books; yet I have heard him fling away, careless as a sower, handfuls of mental wealth which books seldom hold. His mind was my library: when it opened to me, I entered bliss. Bound and printed volumes often wearied me; they fagged and blinded; but his spoken pages were a collyrium to the eyes of the spirit. Over them, inward sight grew clear and strong.

His story ended, he came to the little knoll where Ginevra and I sat apart. In his usual way—never waiting for an opinion to be offered—he asked abruptly—

“Were you interested?”

“Yes.”

“Was it good?”

“Very good.”

“Yet I could not write that down,” said he.

“Why not, Monsieur?”

“I hate the mechanical labour; I hate to stoop and sit still. I could dictate it, though, with pleasure, to an amanuensis who suited me. Would Mademoiselle Lucy write for me if I asked her?”

“Monsieur would be too quick; he would urge me, and be angry if my pen did not keep pace with his lips.”

“Try some day; let us see the monster I can make of myself under the circumstances. But just now there is no question of dictation; I mean to make you useful in another office. Do you see yonder farm-house?”

“Surrounded with trees? Yes.”

“There we are to breakfast; and while the good fermière makes the café au lait in a caldron, you and five others, whom I shall select, will spread with butter half a hundred rolls.”

At that word—breakfast—there rose in my imagination not merely hunger but a whole scene of domestic labour: hot milk climbing in a great pot, coffee bruised and brewed till it sent up its deep brown perfume, butter softening to a spreadable gloss, bread waiting like a white crowd to be made ready for mouths and laughter. It pleased me that he did not plan a mere outing; he planned a meal, and a meal requires hands.

He formed his troop into line again and marched us straight on the farm, which, on seeing our force, surrendered without capitulation.

Clean knives and plates appeared; fresh butter, yellow as a primrose-heart, was set out; and half-a-dozen of us—chosen by our Professor—set to work under his directions to prepare for breakfast a huge basket of rolls. The baker had been ordered beforehand to provision the farm in anticipation of our coming; the rolls lay there in their piled abundance, the crusts delicate and pale, the insides still tender with that faint scent of yeast and oven-heat that clings to newly baked bread like a secret.

Coffee and chocolate were already hot. Cream came, thick and cool; eggs, new-laid, were added—shells still with a farmyard freshness, the yolks promising that deep gold which only a hen’s honest feeding can make. M. Emanuel—always generous—would have ordered “jambon” and “confitures” as well; but some of us, presuming perhaps on our influence, insisted it would be reckless waste of victual.

He railed at us, calling us “des ménagères avares;” but we let him talk and managed the economy our own way. It seemed to me that, even in this, there was a small struggle between two languages of power: his—lavish, authoritative, impatient of counting; ours—prudent, domestic, careful of what a household must remember. Waste is a kind of forgetfulness; and I have always feared forgetfulness.

With what a pleasant countenance he stood on the farm-kitchen hearth looking on! The kitchen had its own music: the low simmer in the caldron, the clink of knife on plate, the faint crack as a roll opened, the quick rustle of aprons, and our voices—bright, unforced, not drowned by any modern roar. One could think in such a place. One could breathe.

He was a man made happy by seeing others happy; he liked movement, animation, abundance, and enjoyment about him. We asked where he would sit. He told us we knew well he was our slave and we his tyrants, and that he dared not even choose a chair without our leave. So we set him in the farmer’s great chair at the head of the long table and put him into it.

Well might we like him—passions, hurricanes, and all—when he could be so benignant and docile at times. At his worst, it was only his nerves that were irritable, not his temper that was radically bad; soothe, comprehend, comfort him, and he was a lamb; he would not harm a fly. Only to the very stupid, the perverse, the unsympathizing, was he dangerous.

Mindful always of his religion, he made the youngest say a little prayer before we began breakfast, crossing himself as devotedly as a woman. I had never seen him pray before, or make that pious sign. He did it so simply, with such child-like faith, that I smiled—pleased without knowing why. His eyes met my smile; he stretched out his hand kindly.

“Donnez-moi la main! I see we worship the same God, in the same spirit, though by different rites.”

Most of his brother professors were emancipated free-thinkers, infidels, atheists; many were men whose lives would not bear scrutiny. He was rather like a knight of old—religious in his way and of spotless fame. Innocent childhood, beautiful youth were safe beside him. His vivid passions and keen feelings were held in check by pure honour and artless piety: those were the charm that kept the lion couchant.

That breakfast was merry; and the merriment was not mere vacant clatter. M. Paul originated, led, controlled, heightened it. His social temper played unfettered and unclouded. Surrounded only by women and children, there was nothing to cross him; he had his own way—and it was a pleasant way. The coffee went round—dark, fragrant, and strong; the chocolate came, thicker, sweeter, with that warm, faintly spiced breath which clings to it. The rolls, buttered half to their edges, shone; eggs broke open with quiet richness; cream whitened cups. I watched mouths and hands; I listened to laughter rising and falling with the same ease as birdsong outside; and it seemed to me that nourishment—shared, prepared, and received together—bound us more securely for that hour than any rule in Madame Beck’s house.

When the meal ended, the party were free to run in the meadows. A few stayed to help the farmer’s wife put away her earthenware. I was among those; I liked the order of it—the stacking of plates, the wiping of cups, the quiet economy after abundance.

M. Paul called me from among the helpers to come out and sit near him under a tree, whence he could watch the troop gambolling over a wide pasture, and read to him while he took his cigar. He sat on a rustic bench; I sat at the root of the tree. The grass smelled bruised and green; the sunlight lay warm on the ground. Even the small sounds—the creak of the bench, the far cry of a child, the steady whisper of leaves—seemed arranged for content.

I read from a pocket classic—Corneille. I did not like it; he did, finding beauties I could never be brought to perceive. While I read, he listened with a sweetness of calm the more impressive because his nature was generally impetuous. Deep happiness filled his blue eye and smoothed his broad forehead.

I too was happy—happy with the bright day, happier with his presence, happiest with his kindness.

By-and-by he asked if I would not rather run to my companions than sit there.

“No,” I said. “I feel content to be where you are.”

He asked whether, if I were his sister, I should always be content to stay with a brother such as he.

“I believe I should,” I answered; and I felt it.

Again he inquired whether, if he were to leave Villette and go far away, I should be sorry.

At that, I dropped Corneille and made no reply.

“Petite sœur,” said he, “how long could you remember me if we were separated?”

“That, Monsieur, I can never tell,” I said; “because I do not know how long it will be before I shall cease to remember everything earthly.”

“If I were to go beyond seas for two—three—five years, should you welcome me on my return?”

“Monsieur, how could I live in the interval?”

“Pourtant j’ai été pour vous bien dur, bien exigeant.”

I hid my face with the book. Tears fell upon its page—tears with no right to be there, blurring Corneille’s severe lines as if my own heart insisted on writing over them in a language no one teaches and no one translates. I asked him why he talked so. He said he would talk so no more; he tried to cheer me with the kindest encouragement.

Yet the gentleness with which he treated me during the rest of the day went to my heart. It was too tender; it had something mournful in it. I would rather he had been abrupt, whimsical, irate as was his wont. That kindness felt like a hand laid on a wound before the knife.

When hot noon arrived—the day turning out as we had guessed, glowing as June—our shepherd collected his sheep from the pasture and led us softly home. But the farm lay a whole league from Villette. The children, especially, were tired with their play; most spirits flagged at the prospect of that mid-day walk over chaussées flinty, glaring, and dusty. The thought of returning through that hard brightness made the body shrink; even the ears seemed to dread the rasp of feet on dry road.

This, however, had been foreseen. Just beyond the farm boundary we met two spacious vehicles coming to fetch us—such conveyances as are hired for school-parties. With good management, room was found for all; and in another hour M. Paul made safe consignment of his charge at the Rue Fossette.

It had been a pleasant day. It would have been perfect but for that breath of melancholy that had dimmed its sunshine for a moment.

The tarnish returned that same evening.

About sunset I saw M. Emanuel come out at the front door with Madame Beck. They paced the centre-alley for nearly an hour, speaking earnestly. He looked grave, restless; she wore an amazed, expostulatory, dissuasive air. I wondered what lay under their words. When Madame re-entered as it darkened, leaving her kinsman still lingering in the garden, I said to myself—

“He called me ‘petite sœur’ this morning. If he were really my brother, how I should like to go to him now and ask what presses on his mind. See how he leans against that tree—arms crossed, brow bent. He wants consolation; Madame does not console: she only remonstrates. What now—?”

From stillness to action he started. M. Paul came striding down the garden—erect, quick. The carré doors stood open. I thought he was perhaps going to water the orange-trees in their tubs, as he sometimes did; but, reaching the court, he turned abruptly towards the berceau and the first-classe glass door.

There—in that first classe—I was. From thence I had watched him. Yet there I could not find courage to await his approach. He had turned so suddenly; he strode so fast; his face looked so strange. The coward within me grew pale, shrank, and—without waiting to hear reason, and hearing the shrubs crush and the gravel crunch to his advance—she was gone on the wings of panic.

I did not pause until I had taken sanctuary in the oratory, empty now. There, listening with beating pulses and an unaccountable, undefined apprehension, I heard him pass through the schoolrooms, clashing the doors impatiently as he went. I heard him invade the refectory where the “lecture pieuse” held us under hallowed constraint. I heard him pronounce the words—

“Où est Mademoiselle Lucie?”

And just as, summoning courage, I was preparing to go down and do what, after all, I most wished in the world—to meet him—the wiry voice of St. Pierre replied glibly and falsely—

“Elle est au lit.”

He passed, with a stamp of vexation, into the corridor. There Madame Beck met him—captured, chid, convoyed him to the street-door, and finally dismissed him. When the street-door closed, amazement at my own perverse proceeding struck me like a blow.

From the first I had felt it was me he wanted—me he was seeking—and had not I wanted him too? What then had carried me away? What had rapt me beyond his reach? He had something to tell; he was going to tell me that something. My ear strained to hear it, and I had made the confidence impossible.

Yearning to listen and console, while I believed audience and solace beyond hope—no sooner did opportunity arrive, sudden and full, than I evaded it as I would have evaded the levelled shaft of mortality.

My insane inconsistency had its reward. Instead of the comfort, the certain satisfaction, I might have won—had I but put choking panic down and stood firm two minutes—there was now dead blank, dark doubt, drear suspense.

I took my wages to my pillow, and passed the night counting them.
