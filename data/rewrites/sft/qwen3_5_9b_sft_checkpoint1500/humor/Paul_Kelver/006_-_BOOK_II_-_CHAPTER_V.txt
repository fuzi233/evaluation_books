If I had to pin down Doctor Florret to a single adjective, "Correct" would be the word. He was a large man, but not *too* large—just the specific, reassuring proportions of a headmaster for a respectable middle-class school. Stout, yes, but not fat; a silhouette that suggested comfortable stability rather than gross indulgence. His hands were pale and perfectly formed, and on his left hand sat a diamond ring that possessed a dignified shine rather than a flashy sparkle. He spoke of mundane subjects like the weather with a gravity that elevated them instantly. His face was cleanly shaved and radiated a kindness carefully modulated by discretion.

Everything around him mirrored this ethos. His wife was the perfect feminine foil, a creation seemingly carved from one of his own ribs solely for his benefit. Their furniture was solid, built for utility, not ornamentation. Their pictures hung with the modesty of a well-dressed suit—gracing the wall without demanding attention. Doctor Florret always said the correct thing, at the correct time, in the correct manner. If you were ever unsure what the right move was, you simply waited for him to make it; by the time he acted, you'd realize nothing else could possibly have been correct. To disagree with him was to declare oneself a revolutionary.

In practice, as I soon learned at the cost of several wasted years, he adhered strictly to the "correct" methods of English schooling dating back to the reign of Edward VI.

I tried to work hard. I wanted to learn. Inside me burned a boyish ambition—the kind that feels centered everywhere and refuses to settle. If I passed a street orator, hatless and sweating, gesturing at utopias for hungry crowds, I would immediately see myself as Demosthenes swaying multitudes, a statesman holding Parliament spellbound, the Prime Minister of England, adored by the nation. I imagined the opposition papers forced into reluctant admiration. If the echo of a distant drum reached my ears, I would picture vast fields of battle, with myself standing conspicuously in the front line, isolated. Since promotion in the British Army, in my dreams, was based entirely on merit, I envisioned myself returning as Commander-in-Chief. Crowds would throng every street, waving white hands from rooftops. I would hear the deep roar of welcome as I slowly paced atop my horse—snow-white, I decided, though I spent considerable mental energy debating whether coal-black might look sharper—at the head of my victorious troops, flanked by waves of upturned faces. Then, inevitably, I would walk into a lamppost or step on the toes of an irritable old gentleman, and wake up.

A drunk sailor would storm out of swinging doors, shouting "Ay, ay, sir!" while a factory chimney belching smoke transformed into a swaying mast. The costermongers around me would chant, "Ready, ay, ready." In these reveries, I was Christopher Columbus, Drake, and Nelson rolled into one. I spurned the presumptions of modern geographers and discovered new continents. I defeated the French—those *useful* French! I died in the moment of victory, mourned by a nation and buried in Westminster Abbey. Or perhaps I lived to become a Duke. Personally, I was indifferent; both outcomes held a certain charm. While boys who, on November the ninth, were told by Doctor Florret to snort at letters from their mothers about toothaches, told me on the tenth about the glories of the Lord Mayor's Show, I drifted further and further away in my daydreams. I saw myself as a young merchant prince, nobles crowding my counting-house, lending them millions, marrying their daughters. I would listen unnoticed to discussions on new books and instantly become a famous author. All men would praise me, for I knew nothing of reviewers or their density. I would write poetry, fiction, and history, and everyone would read and wonder. Only here was a snag: a crumpled rose leaf on my pillow. Penmanship vexed me, and spelling puzzled me, so I wrote with sorrow, blotches, and frantic scratchings. Almost, I abandoned the idea of becoming an author.

But regardless of which path led to the Elysian Fields of fame, I dimly grasped that education was the necessary weapon. And so, with an aching heart and a pounding head, I pored over my books. I see myself now in my small bedroom, elbows planted on a shaky, one-legged table, startled whenever my hair touched the solitary candle flame. On cold nights, I wore my overcoat turned up around my neck, a blanket wrapped tight around my legs, often sitting with fingers stuffed in my ears to block the importunate sounds of life rising from below. *"A song! Of a song! To a song!"* *"I love! Thou lovest!"* These phrases echoed endlessly until my own voice sounded like a strange buzz surrounding me, while my head grew smaller and smaller until I raised my hands in fright, wondering if it was still entirely attached to my shoulders.

Was I stupider than the average, or was a boy's brain physically incapable of the work our educational system demanded?

*"Latin and Greek"* I heard repeating in the suave tones of Doctor Florret, echoing the solemn croak of Correctness. *"Are useful as mental gymnastics."* My dear Doctor Florret and Company, could you not, from your vast storehouse of knowledge, select apparatus better fitted to strengthen—and not overstrain—the mental muscles of ten-to-fourteen-year-olds? You, Gentle Reader, with your fully grown brains trained by years of practice, take me from your shelf. Say, your Browning, or even Shakespeare. This is your mother tongue. Construe this short passage. Parse it. Analyse it. Resolve it into component parts! And now, will you maintain that it is good for Tommy, the tear-stained, ink-bespattered little brat, to be treated the same way? Would it not be just as sensible to insist upon him practicing his skinny little arms with hundred-pound dumbbells?

We were the sons of City men, minor officials, clerks, and shopkeepers; our roads led through the workaday world. Yet quite half our time was consumed by studies utterly useless to us. How I hated them, these youth-tormenting shades! Homer! How I wished the fishermen had asked him that absurd riddle earlier. Horace! Why could not that shipwreck have succeeded? It would have, in anyone but a classic.

Then, on one blessed day, a wondrous talisman fell into my hands.

*Hearken unto me, ye heavy-burdened little brethren.* Waste not your substance upon tops and marbles, nor yet upon tuck (*do ye still call it "tuck"?*), but scrape and save. For in the neighborhood of Paternoster Row dwells a good magician who, for silver, will provide you with a "Key" that opens wide the gates of Hades.

By its aid, the Frogs of Aristophanes became my merry friends. With Ulysses, I wandered eagerly through Wonderland. Doctor Florret was charmed by my progress, which was real, for now, at last, I was studying according to the laws of common sense: understanding first, explaining afterwards. Let Youth, lest the folly of Age imprison it in ignorance, provide itself with "Keys."

But let me not seem to claim credit due to another. Dan it was—Dan of the strong arm and the soft smile, Dan the wise hater of all useless labor, sharp-witted and easy-going Dan—who made this grand discovery.

Dan followed me a term later into the Lower Fourth, but before he had been there a week, he was handling Latin verse with an ease and dexterity suggestive of unholy dealings with the Devil. In a lonely corner of Regent's Park, first ensuring no one was within earshot, he revealed his magic to me.

"Don't tell the others," he commanded. "Or it will get out, and then nobody will be any the better."

"But is it right?" I asked.

"Look here, young 'un," said Dan. "What are you here for? What is your father paying school fees for—for you to play a silly game, or to learn something?"

"If it's only a game," Dan continued, "we boys against the masters, then let's play according to the rules. If we're here to learn... well, you've been in the class four months and I've just come, and I bet I know more Ovid than you do already." Which was true.

So I thanked Dan and shared his key. All the Latin I remember, for whatever good it may be to me, I owe to him.

Knowledge of yet greater value I owe to the good fortune that his sound mother wit was ever at my disposal to correct my dreamy unfeasibility. From first to last, he was my friend. To have been the chosen friend of Dan, a shrewd judge of men and boys, I deemed no unimportant feather in my cap. He "took to" me because I was so "jolly green"—"such a rummy little mug." He offered no other reason save a sweet smile and the tumbling of my hair with his great hand; but I think I understood. I loved him because he was big, strong, handsome, and kind. No one but a little boy knows how brutal or how kind a big boy can be.
