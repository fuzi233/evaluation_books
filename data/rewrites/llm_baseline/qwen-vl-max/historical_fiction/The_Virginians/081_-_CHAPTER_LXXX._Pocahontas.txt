The English public, not being as familiar with the tale of Pocahontas as we Virginians—whose hearts still hold fond remembrance of that gentle and noble spirit—Mr. Warrington, at the urging of his friends, composed a short ballad about the Indian princess. It was printed in the magazines just days before the premiere of his tragedy. Sampson and I found this maneuver both clever and cunning.

“It’s like ground-bait, sir,” declared the enthusiastic parson, “and you’ll see the fish rise in swarms on the great day!” He and Spencer swore the poem had been widely discussed and praised in several coffeehouses, even credited to Mr. Mason, Mr. Cowper of the Temple, and—most absurdly—to the famed Mr. Gray. I suspect poor Sam himself spread these rumors; had Shakespeare been named as the author of the play, he would have declared *Pocahontas* one of the Bard’s finest works.

I first met Captain John Smith as a boy, reading his adventures in my grandfather’s library. There, perched on his knee, I’d pore over the pages of my favorite volume, tracing the bold exploits of our Virginian hero. I loved every tale: his travels, his imprisonments, his escapes—not only across America but through Europe. Now, standing before the shelves in England, I pull down that same book, and instantly, memories flood back—the scent of old paper, the warmth of the hearth, the sound of my grandfather’s voice painting scenes for me: Smith battling Turks on the Danube, led into death by savage Indians. Ah, what a fierce fight it was when he faced three Turkish champions! And how I thrilled at the story of his duel with Bonny Molgro—the last and most fearsome of them all! What a name! What a figure: a monstrous turban, a scimitar flashing, whiskers bristling! After slaying two foes and severing their heads, Smith met Bonny Molgro in combat. The book describes it thus: “They fell to with their battle-axes, whose piercing bills made sometimes the one, sometimes the other, scarce able to keep their saddles. Especially the Christian received such a wound that he lost his axe, whereat the supposed conquering Turk gave a great shout from the ramparts. Yet, by the readiness of his horse and his great judgment, he not only avoided the Turk’s blows but, drawing his falchion, pierced him under the cutlets, through back and body, so that though he alighted from his horse, he stood not long ere he lost his head, as the rest had done. For this deed, Duke Segismundus granted him three Turk’s heads on a shield for arms and three hundred ducats yearly as a pension.”

In my play, I dared to transplant these heroic deeds to the banks of the Potomac and James River, defying time and place—privileges, after all, granted to poets. My “ground-bait” verses went like this:

> Wearied arm and broken sword
> Wage in vain the desperate fight—
> Round him press the countless horde,
> He is but a single knight.
> Hark! a cry of triumph shrill
> Through the wilderness resounds,
> As, with twenty bleeding wounds,
> Sinks the warrior, fighting still.
>
> Now they heap the fatal pyre,
> And the torch of death they light—
> Ah! 'tis hard to die of fire!
> Who will shield the captive knight?
> Round the stake with fiendish cry
> Wheel and dance the savage crowd,
> Cold the victim's mien and proud,
> And his breast is bared to die.
>
> Who will shield the fearless heart?
> Who avert the murderous blade?
> From the throng, with sudden start,
> See, there springs an Indian maid!
> Quick she stands before the knight,
> "Loose the chain, unbind the ring,
> I am daughter of the king,
> And I claim the Indian right!"
>
> Dauntlessly aside she flings
> Lifted axe and thirsty knife;
> Fondly to his heart she clings,
> And her bosom guards his life!
> In the woods of Powhattan,
> Still 'tis told, by Indian fires,
> How a daughter of their sires
> Saved the captive Englishman.

I need not detail the plot further—my children may find it any day upon the shelves. Nor shall I rush to read it aloud again, especially since Captain Miles and the parson both dozed off last Christmas when I recited a few acts at mamma’s request. But anyone familiar with drama or fiction can easily imagine the scene from this sketch: an Indian king, a devoted princess, her attendant secretly in love with the English captain’s servant, a traitor within the fort, a brave warrior pining for Pocahontas, a treacherous medicine-man and priest (played superbly by Palmer), capable of every crime, determined to see the English prisoner tortured and slain—all set against the wilds, war-dances, and cries (which Gumbo mimicked perfectly from memory), culminating in the arrival of the English fleet, echoing recent victories in Canada, and affirming Britain’s eternal destiny to rule in America. Some of us believed these elements might well ensure success.

Yet I must speak of the ill omens preceding the night: the petty obstacles erected by a jealous, timid management; the open hostility from certain powerful quarters. Is it any wonder, then, that *Pocahontas* failed?

I laugh at the critics who scorned the performance. Fools, indeed, who hailed *Carpezan* as a masterpiece while sneering at a far more polished work! I maintain that Hagan played his part with such brilliance that even a leading actor-manager might have envied him. Had it not been for the conspiracy outside the theatre, the play would have triumphed. The order had been given: the play must fail. So said Sampson. “The house swarmed with Macs, by George,” he swore, “and they should have the galleries washed with brimstone.” He insisted Mr. Garrick himself could not have saved it—and he raged throughout the second act, when Smith (poor Hagan) stood bound to the stake, and Pocahontas rushed forward to save him. The audience trembled with emotion—yet laughter broke out.

Anyone curious may consult the published version—either the octavo or the later quarto edition of my *Collected Works*. Let them judge whether the scene lacks merit, whether the verse is not elegant, the language noble and rich.

One reason for failure was my strict adherence to history. I studied a portrait of Sir Walter Raleigh at the Museum—a man in frill and beard—and, with Theo lending some of her mother’s finest lace, we dressed Hagan precisely as depicted. No man looked more regal. Miss Pritchard, as Pocahontas, I dressed in authentic Indian attire, drawn from my own experiences in Virginia. Can it be believed that the audience tittered when she first appeared? They grew accustomed, yet just as she threw herself into Smith’s arms—many weeping—a man in the pit shouted, “Bedad! Here’s the Belle Savage kissing the Saracen’s Head!” A roar of laughter erupted, continuing in fits through the entire performance.

As the wag in Sheridan’s *Critic* says of morning guns—“they were not content with one, but fired two or three”—so too did this tavern joke take root. The ignorant audience didn’t know that *Belle Sauvage* was the very name derived from Pocahontas herself! My friend in the pit repeated it endlessly, naming each character after a tavern: the governor (with long beard) became “the Goat and Boots”; Barker, the lieutenant (broad-faced), “the Bull and Mouth.” And so on!

The curtain fell amid shrieks of whistles and hisses—especially aimed at poor Hagan whenever he spoke. Sampson saw Master Will in the green boxes, with some fair companions, and had no doubt he was a chief instigator. “I would have thrown him into the pit,” Sampson vowed—and he was the sort of man who would have done it—“but I saw a couple of Mr. Nadab’s men lurking in the lobby and had to back off.”

So our eggs were shattered before market. Our hopes lay in ruins.

Before the curtain rose, I peered from the stage and saw the house nearly full. Mr. Johnson sat in the front boxes, wearing a laced waistcoat, with Mr. Reynolds beside him—neither could see nor hear, so they came together, *à deux*, to form an opinion of my poor tragedy. Lady Maria I recognized by her hood, seated in the lower gallery, once more watching her beloved actor in a leading role. As for Theo, she confessed she’d rather not attend unless I commanded it—and I let her stay away. To see her suffer would have been agony. I myself, being naturally composed, took a light dinner at the Bedford and arrived at the theatre shortly before the play began, resolved to stay until fate decided the outcome.

But truth be told—I couldn’t help sensing disaster. There was a gloom about everything. Miss Pritchard had a headache. The barber had powdered Hagan’s wig like a madman. Every face in the greenroom wore uncertainty. The manager—a flippant, impertinent fellow—dared say to me, “For Heaven’s sake, Mr. Warrington, go get a glass of punch at the Bedford. Don’t frighten us with your dismal look!”

“Sir,” I replied, “for five shillings I have a right to comment on your face—but you’ve no authority to critique mine.”

“Sir,” he snapped, “I wish I had never seen your face at all!”

“Yours, sir,” I said, “has often amused me. When painted for *Abel Drugger*, it’s exceedingly comic.” Indeed, I always thought Mr. G. unmatched in low comedy.

I bowed and walked off to the coffee-house. For five years, I said nothing to him—until he apologized at a nobleman’s gathering. I claimed I’d forgotten the incident entirely, adding, “On the first night of a play, author and manager are both flurried.” Then I added, “After all, there’s no shame in not being made for the theatre. Mr. Garrick—you were.” A compliment he seemed to appreciate.

Fidus Achates came rushing to me at the end of the first act: “All’s going well—though there was tittering when Miss Pritchard appeared, dressed exactly as an Indian princess.”

“I cannot help it, Sampson,” I said, filling him a bumper of punch, “if Indians dress so.”

“Well,” he retorted, “would you have had Caractacus painted blue like an ancient Briton, or Bonduca in nothing but a cow-skin?”

Perhaps fidelity to history *was* the cause of ridicule—and if so, I’m not ashamed of the defeat.

After the second act, my aide-de-camp returned with grim news. Strange, how I grow calm in disaster, though nervous before action. [The writer seems to contradict himself here, having earlier claimed equanimity. Perhaps he misjudged himself, as many do.—ED.]

“Are things going badly?” I asked. I paid my bill, put on my hat, and marched to the theatre as calmly as if dining at the Temple—Sampson at my side, pressing my elbow, kicking link-boys aside, shouting, “By George, Mr. Warrington, you’re a man of spirit—a Trojan, sir!”

There were men of spirit in Troy—but alas, fate was stronger.

No one could say I didn’t bear my misfortune with dignity. I could no more control the audience’s clamor than a captain can stop a storm’s fury as his ship sinks. But I resolved to remain *impavidum ferient*, and I believe I endured without flinching.

“Not Regulus,” Sampson told my wife, “could step into his barrel more coolly.”

It’s unjust to accuse those of parasitic nature of deserting in adversity. Whether I prospered or suffered, the wild parson remained true—sharing our crust as eagerly as our feasts.

I took my place on the stage, watching my actors and the audience that condemned me. I suppose the performers kept their distance out of pity. I must admit, I felt no more moved than any spectator. No one would have guessed I was the unfortunate hero of the night.

But when I returned home, Theo’s face was pale and strained. I saw at once the news had reached her before I did.

Spencer, Sampson, cousin Hagan, and Lady Maria were to come after the play to congratulate the author—God wot! (Poor Miss Pritchard had sent word she was too ill to dine.)

My gardener at Bedford House had sent Theo the finest flowers to decorate the table. There they stood—painted standards—while the battle was lost.

I had borne the defeat bravely enough. But seeing my wife’s sweet face across the table, and those innocent tokens of welcome meant for a victor—I confess, my courage cracked. A pang struck me as keen as any I’ve ever known.

Our meal was somber. We tried to talk, but it brought little cheer. Fortunately, old Mrs. Hagan was gravely ill—her condition a blessing in disguise. We spoke of His Majesty’s approaching marriage. (How clearly I remember the trivial details of the day—even the tune a carpenter whistled beside me just before the curtain fell!) We discussed the death of good Mr. Richardson, author of *Pamela* and *Clarissa*, whose books we all adored. As we talked of *Clarissa*, Theo wiped her eyes and whispered, “You know, my love, mamma and I could never help crying over that dear book. Oh, my dearest, dearest mother—how I wish she were here now!”

This brought fresh tears—natural, for a young woman mourning her absent mother.

We mixed a dark bowl with Jamaica limes and toasted the Governor’s health. Then, smiling, I filled a bumper and raised it to “our better fortune!”

That was too much. The two women threw themselves into each other’s arms, sobbing and soaking each other’s handkerchiefs.

“Oh, Maria! Is not—Is not my George good and kind?” Theo sobbed.

“Look at my Hagan—how great, how godlike he was!” gasped Maria.

“It was a beastly cabal that ruined him—and I could plunge this knife into Mr. Garrick’s black heart—the odious little wretch!” she cried, clutching a dagger at her side.

Then, dropping it, she rushed to me, threw her arms around me, and embraced me before the small company.

I’m not sure if someone else did the same. We were all in a state of wild emotion. In the midst of sorrow, Love appears—the consoler—softening pain with tenderness, making one almost forget the loss.

A few days later, on our birthday, a letter arrived in my study. It bore these lines:

> Returning from the cruel fight,
> How pale and faint appears my knight!
> He sees me anxious at his side:
> “Why seek, my love, your wounds to hide?
> Or deem your English girl afraid
> To emulate the Indian maid?”
>
> Be mine my husband’s grief to cheer,
> In peril to be ever near;
> Whate’er of ill or woe betide,
> To bear it clinging at his side;
> The poisoned stroke of fate to ward,
> His bosom with my own to guard;
> Ah! could it spare a pang to his,
> It could not know a purer bliss!
> ’Twould gladden as it felt the smart,
> And thank the hand that flung the dart!

I won’t pretend the verses are masterpieces—but I love them as if they were. And when I entered her drawing-room, saw her blushing, hearing her soft voice as I hummed the lines—her face seemed more beautiful than anything I could imagine, even from Heaven.
