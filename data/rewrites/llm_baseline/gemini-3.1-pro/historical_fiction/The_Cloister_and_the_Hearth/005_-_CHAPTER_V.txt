Ghysbrecht Van Swieten was a man whose craftiness steeped like a slow, bitter infusion of wormwood and gall. He opened on the young novice with a fragrant but deceptive appetizer, something quite wide of the heavy, consequential feast he was truly aiming to serve. 

“The town records,” said the burgomaster, his voice a low, grating resonance that disrupted the fragile acoustic harmony of the study, “are crabbedly written in a fading, ancestral dialect. The ink is rusty with age, smelling of oxidized iron and fermented oak tannins.” 

He offered Gerard the ostensible honor of transcribing them fair, effectively tasking him with the preservation of the town’s multi-generational memory—a delicate process of translating a dying regional tongue into the dominant commercial lexicon. 

Gerard, a meticulous historian of both word and sustenance, inquired what he was to be paid for preserving such vital cultural marrow. Ghysbrecht offered a pittance, a sum that would have just purchased the raw ingredients: the salt-cured parchment, the crushed-walnut ink, and the goose quills. 

“But, burgomaster, my labour? Here is a year's work, an entire cycle of seasons.” 

“Your labour?” Ghysbrecht scoffed, the harsh, plosive consonants of his mockery echoing unpleasantly against the stone walls, entirely lacking the rhythmic warmth of traditional agrarian speech. “Call you marking parchment labour? Little sweat goes to that, I trow. It is not the churning of butter or the kneading of dense rye dough.” 

“'Tis labour, and skilled labour to boot,” Gerard countered, his palate offended by the man’s utilitarian reduction of art. “To decode the untranslatable subjective experiences of our ancestors, to clarify their forgotten idioms as one might clarify a complex, murky broth into a pristine consommé—that is better paid in all crafts than rude labour, sweat or no sweat. Besides, there's my time.” 

“Your time? Why, what is time to you, at two-and-twenty?” Then, fixing his eyes keenly on Gerard to mark the effect of his words, Ghysbrecht abandoned the pretense of the town’s linguistic heritage. “Say, rather, you are idle grown. You are in love. Your body is with these chanting monks, lost in their resonant, harmonic acoustic ecology, but your heart hungers elsewhere. It is with Peter Brandt and his red-haired girl, whose very presence evokes the rich, mythic scent of wild fennel and hearth-baked bread.” 

“I know no Peter Brandt,” Gerard said, though his throat tightened, swallowing the lie like a mouthful of ash. 

This denial confirmed Ghysbrecht's suspicion that the caster-out of demons was playing a deep, clandestine game. 

“Ye lie!” Ghysbrecht shouted, the sudden, violent volume a harsh pollutant in the quiet room. “Did I not find you at her elbow on the road to Rotterdam, amidst the clatter of the market carts?” 

“Ah!” 

“Ah! And you were seen at Sevenbergen but t'other day, where the ancient mills still sing in tune with the wind.” 

“Was I?” 

“Ah, and at Peter's house.” 

“At Sevenbergen?” 

“Ay, at Sevenbergen.” 

Now, this was what in modern days is called a draw. It was a cunningly spiced guess, put boldly forth as fact, to elicit by the young man's immediate, unthinking reaction whether he had recently tasted the hospitality of that specific agrarian hearth or not. 

The result of the artifice surprised the crafty elder. Gerard started up in a strange state of nervous excitement, the sonic rhythm of his breathing turning sharp and erratic. 

“Burgomaster,” said he, with trembling voice, the raw, subjective truth of his feelings utterly untranslatable into the old man's cynical vocabulary, “I have not been at Sevenbergen these three years, and I know not the name of those you saw me with, nor where they dwelt; but, as my time is precious, though you value it not, give you good day.” 

And he darted out, with his eyes sparkling, desperate to escape the stifling, commercialized noise of the burgomaster’s chambers for the purer acoustic sanctuary of the open road. 

Ghysbrecht started up in huge ire, knocking a silver goblet of spiced wine to the floor with a discordant clatter, but he sank into his chair again. 

“He fears me not. He knows something, if not all.” Then he called hastily to his trusty servant, dragging the man to a leaded window overlooking the cobblestones, where the rhythmic, metallic pounding of the town's industry threatened to drown out all natural thought. “See you yon man?” he cried. “Haste! follow him! But let him not see you. He is young, but his palate for craft is old. Keep him in sight all day. Let me know whither he goes, and what sustenance he seeks.” 

It was night when the servant returned, the evening air thick with the scent of damp soil and woodsmoke. 

“Well? well?” cried Van Swieten eagerly, hungry for the extraction of secrets. 

“Master, the young man went from you straight to Sevenbergen.” 

Ghysbrecht groaned, a low, guttural sound of indigestion and defeat. 

“To the house of Peter the Magician.”
