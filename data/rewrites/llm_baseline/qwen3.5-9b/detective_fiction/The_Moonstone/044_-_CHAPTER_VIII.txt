That same evening, shortly after dusk, I was startled at my lodging by the arrival of Mr. Bruff.

There was a marked alteration in the lawyer's demeanor. The usual confidence and vigor had deserted him. He extended his hand to me—a gesture he had never before performed in silence—and clasped it firmly.

"Are you returning to Hampstead?" I asked, merely to fill the pause.

"I have just departed Hampstead," he replied. "Mr. Franklin, I acknowledge that you have finally grasped the truth. Yet, if I had known beforehand the terrible price such knowledge would demand, I would have preferred to keep you in ignorance."

"Have you seen Rachel?"

"I returned her to Portland Place before coming here; it was impossible to allow her to travel home alone in the carriage. Considering that you witnessed her in my presence and with my consent, I cannot truly hold you accountable for the shock this unfortunate meeting has caused her. All I can offer is protection against a recurrence of such distress. She is young, possesses a resilient spirit, and with time and rest, she will recover. I require assurance, however, that you will do nothing to impede her healing. Can I rely on your promise to make no further attempts to see her without my explicit sanction?"

"After the suffering she has endured, and the suffering I have borne," I said, "you may trust in my word."

"Do you give me your promise?"

"You have my promise."

Relieved, Mr. Bruff removed his hat and drew his chair closer.

"That is settled!" he exclaimed. "Now, regarding your future. In my view, the extraordinary turn events have taken leads to a single conclusion. First, we are certain that Rachel told you the entire truth as clearly as words can convey it. Second—though we know a dreadful error exists somewhere—we cannot blame her for believing you guilty based on the evidence of her own senses, reinforced by circumstances that, on their surface, point decisively against you."

At this, I intervened. "I do not blame Rachel," I stated. "I only regret that she could not force herself to speak more plainly to me at the time."

"You might as well lament that Rachel is not someone else," Mr. Bruff countered. "Even then, I doubt if a girl of any delicacy, whose heart was set on marrying you, could bring herself to accuse you openly of theft. Regardless, it was not in Rachel's nature to do so. In a matter entirely different from yours—one that nonetheless placed her in a position strikingly similar to hers toward you—I happen to know she was influenced by a motive identical to the one driving her actions in your case. Furthermore, as she confessed to me on our way to town tonight, if she *had* spoken plainly, she would no more have believed your denial then than she does now. What response can you offer to that? There is none. Come, Mr. Franklin! I admit my initial assessment of the case was flawed, but under current conditions, my advice remains valuable. I warn you plainly: we shall waste our time and exhaust our intellects in vain if we attempt to retrace and untangle this frightful complication from the beginning. Let us resolutely close our minds to what transpired last year at Lady Verinder's estate, and focus instead on what we *can* discover in the future, rather than what we *cannot* uncover in the past."

"Surely you forget," I argued, "that the entire affair is essentially a matter of the past, as far as I am concerned?"

"Answer me this," Mr. Bruff retorted sharply. "Is the Moonstone at the bottom of all this mischief—or is it not?"

"It is—of course."

"Agreed. What do we believe was done with the Moonstone when it was taken to London?"

"It was pledged to Mr. Luker."

"We know you are not the person who pledged it. Do we know who did?"

"No."

"Where do we believe the Moonstone resides now?"

"In the custody of Mr. Luker's bankers."

"Precisely. Observe closely. We are currently in June. By the end of this month, approximately a year will have elapsed since we believe the jewel was pledged. There is a distinct possibility—at the very least—that the individual who pawned it intends to redeem it once the year expires. Should he redeem it, Mr. Luker, bound by his agreement, must retrieve the diamond from the bankers' vaults. Under these circumstances, I propose stationing a watcher at the bank as the month concludes to identify the person to whom Mr. Luker returns the Moonstone. Do you follow the logic?"

I admitted, though somewhat reluctantly, that the concept was novel.

"It is Mr. Murthwaite's idea just as much as mine," Mr. Bruff noted. "It never would have occurred to me without a conversation we shared some time ago. If Mr. Murthwaite is correct, the Indians are likely to be watching the bank near the end of the month as well, and serious consequences may ensue. Whether those consequences affect us personally matters little, except insofar as they aid us in capturing the mysterious individual who pawned the diamond. That person, you may be assured, is solely responsible (though I do not presume to know how) for your precarious position today; and only that person can restore your standing in Rachel's eyes."

"I cannot deny," I conceded, "that the plan you propose addresses the difficulty with daring ingenuity and novelty. However—"

"But you harbor an objection?"

"Yes. My objection is that your proposal compels us to wait."

"Granted. According to my calculations, it requires patience of roughly two weeks, plus or minus. Is that duration insurmountable?"

"To me, Mr. Bruff, in my current predicament, it feels like a lifetime. My existence becomes unbearable unless I take immediate steps to clear my name."

"Well, well, I understand. Have you considered what you can do?"

"I have thought of consulting Sergeant Cuff."

"He has retired from the police. Expecting his assistance is futile."

"I know where to find him; I can but try."

"Try," Mr. Bruff said after a moment's deliberation. "The case has evolved into something extraordinary since Sergeant Cuff's tenure; you *might* reignite his interest in the inquiry. Go ahead, and report back to me. Meanwhile," he continued, rising, "if you make no discoveries between now and the end of the month, am I free to attempt, on my own behalf, keeping a lookout at the bank?"

"Certainly," I answered, "unless I relieve you of the need to conduct this experiment during the interim."

Mr. Bruff smiled and picked up his hat.

"Tell Sergeant Cuff," he added, "that *I* state the discovery of the truth hinges on identifying the person who pawned the diamond. Let me know what the Sergeant's experience suggests regarding that."

Thus, we parted ways.

Early the following morning, I departed for the small town of Dorking, the location of Sergeant Cuff's retirement, as directed by Betteredge.

Upon inquiring at the local hotel, I received instructions for locating the Sergeant's cottage. It was situated via a quiet by-road slightly outside the town, nestled securely within its own garden plot, shielded by a sturdy brick wall at the rear and sides, and a tall quickset hedge in front. The gate, adorned above with neatly painted trellis work, was locked. After ringing the bell, I peered through the lattice and beheld the great favorite flowers of Cuff blooming profusely in his garden, spilling over his doorway and pressing against his windows. Far removed from the crimes and mysteries of the great city, the illustrious thief-taker was peacefully enjoying the final sybaritic years of his life, buried beneath roses!

A respectable elderly woman opened the gate for me, instantly extinguishing all the hopes I had nurtured regarding Sergeant Cuff's assistance. He had departed only the previous day on a journey to Ireland.

"Did he go there on business?" I asked.

The woman smiled. "He has but one business now, sir," she replied, "and that is roses. A prominent gardener in Ireland has discovered a novel method of cultivating roses, and Mr. Cuff has traveled to investigate it."

"Do you know when he will return?"

"It is entirely uncertain, sir," she explained. "Mr. Cuff remarked he would return directly if the discovery proved worthless, or remain absent for some time if it warranted further study. If you have a message for him, I assure you, sir, he will receive it."

I handed her my card, having first penciled a note upon it: *"I have something concerning the Moonstone. Please contact me as soon as you return."* With that task completed, there remained nothing but to accept the circumstances and return to London.

In the irritable state of mind I was in at that time, the fruitless outcome of my trip to the Sergeant's cottage merely intensified my restless urge to act. Upon my return from Dorking, I resolved that the following morning would mark a renewed effort to break through all obstacles, moving from darkness toward light.

What form would this next endeavor take?

Had the excellent Betteredge been present while I pondered this question, and informed of my thoughts, he would undoubtedly have declared that the German side of my nature was, on this occasion, dominating my upper faculties.
