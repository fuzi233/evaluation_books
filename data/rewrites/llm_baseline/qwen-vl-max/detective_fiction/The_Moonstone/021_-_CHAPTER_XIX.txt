The news of Rosanna’s disappearance had spread quickly among the outdoor servants. They, too, had begun searching, and soon came upon a small, lively boy known as “Duffy”—a seasonal helper who sometimes worked weeding the garden. Duffy claimed he had seen Rosanna Spearman no more than half an hour earlier, not walking, but *running*, through the fir plantation toward the sea.

“Does this boy know the coastline?” Sergeant Cuff asked.

“He was born and raised here,” I replied.

“Duffy,” the sergeant said sharply, “do you want to earn a shilling? If so, come with me.” Then, turning to me, he added, “Keep the pony-chaise ready, Mr. Betteredge—wait for my return.”

He set off at a pace I could not possibly match, even though I am still in decent health for a man of seventy. Little Duffy, caught up in the excitement typical of our young folk when stirred, let out a yell and dashed after him like a shadow.

I cannot describe clearly what passed through my mind during the time that followed. A strange, numbing restlessness seized me. I wandered aimlessly around the house, doing pointless tasks—none of which I can now recall. Time seemed to stretch and blur. I don’t even remember how long it was before Duffy returned, breathless, with a message.

Sergeant Cuff had given him a torn page from his pocketbook, written in pencil:

I sent the first woman servant I found to retrieve a boot from her room. Then I told the boy to return to the sergeant and inform him I would follow with it myself. I know now this was not the fastest way to comply—but I felt compelled to see for myself what was happening before handing over any part of Rosanna’s belongings. My old instinct to shield her, if I could, had resurfaced at the last moment.

Driven by this impulse—and by the fever of suspense—I set off as fast as an old man can go. As I neared the shore, dark clouds gathered, and rain began to fall in thick sheets, driven hard by the wind. The thunder of the sea echoed across the sandbank at the mouth of the bay. A little farther on, I saw Duffy huddled under the lee of the dunes, seeking shelter. Then I saw the wild sea, waves crashing onto the sandbank, rain sweeping across the water like a veil, and the vast, desolate beach—save for one solitary figure standing motionless: Sergeant Cuff.

He waved toward the north when he saw me. “Stay there!” he shouted. “Come down here!”

I stumbled down, gasping, my heart pounding as if it might burst. I was speechless—though a hundred questions burned behind my lips. His face terrified me. There was horror in his eyes.

He snatched the boot from my hand and placed it into a footprint in the sand—facing south, pointing directly toward the rocky ledge known as the South Spit. The mark was still clear, untouched by the rain. The boot fit perfectly.

Without a word, he pointed to it. I grabbed his arm, trying to speak, but nothing came. I failed again, just as I had before.

Then he moved forward, tracing the footprints down, deeper and deeper, where rock met sand. The South Spit was nearly submerged by the rising tide; the Shivering Sand lay hidden beneath churning water.

With relentless patience, he tested the boot against each impression—always finding them leading *toward* the rocks. No trace of footsteps heading away. He gave up eventually, silent, then turned to look at me. Then he looked out at the water, rising higher over the quicksand.

I followed his gaze—and understood his thought.

A cold dread swept over me. I dropped to my knees.

“She has been back to the hiding-place,” I heard the sergeant mutter to himself. “Some fatal accident must have happened on those rocks.”

But as he spoke, memories flooded back—the girl’s pale face, her distant eyes, her hollow voice when I’d found her cleaning the corridor earlier that day. She had listened and spoken as though already dead. And now I knew—his guess was wrong. The truth was far worse.

I tried to tell him. “She didn’t die by accident,” I choked. “She chose it. She came here to end her life.”

He stepped back, startled. “How do you know?”

Others crowded closer. But the sergeant regained control instantly. He pushed them away. “Let him be,” he said quietly. “He’s an old man. This has shaken him.”

Then he turned to Yolland, the fisherman, and asked, “Is there any chance of finding her when the tide goes out?”

Yolland shook his head. “None. Once the Sand takes someone, it keeps them forever.”

He stepped closer to me. “Mr. Betteredge,” he said, “I have something to say about the girl’s death. Four feet out along the side of the Spit, there’s a shelf of rock—about three feet below the surface. If she fell by accident, she would have landed on that shelf—deep enough to cover her waist, but not more. She wouldn’t be missing now. Unless…” He paused. “Unless she waded or jumped into the deeps beyond. No accident, sir. The Quicksand has her. And she went willingly.”

After that, silence settled over us all. The sergeant stood still, thoughtful. We turned together and began walking back up the beach.

At the dunes, the under-groom ran toward us from the house. A good lad—he respects me. He handed me a note, his face full of sorrow. “Penelope sent me with this, sir,” he said. “She found it in Rosanna’s room.”

It was Rosanna’s final message:

> *You have often forgiven me, Mr. Betteredge, in past times. When you next see the Shivering Sand, try to forgive me once more. I have found my grave where my grave was waiting for me. I have lived, and died, sir, grateful for your kindness.*

That was all. Yet it broke me. I couldn’t hold back the tears. Young men cry easily when they begin life. Old men cry easily when they leave it. I wept.

Sergeant Cuff stepped closer, meaning well, I’m sure. I recoiled. “Don’t touch me,” I said. “It’s your presence—your pursuit—that drove her to this.”

“You’re mistaken, Mr. Betteredge,” he said calmly. “We’ll talk about it later—when we’re indoors.”

I walked back with the help of the groom’s arm. Through the storm, we returned—to face the grief and terror waiting at home.
