The despair that had consumed me evaporated instantly, replaced by a surge of such frantic gladness that I felt the distance to my beloved shrinking to mere moments. Yet, this hope was a dangerous illusion, destined for swift shattering. All I possessed was the visual data of a massive pyramid ascending into the night sky.

Logic dictated its location: it stood atop a central hill in this dark country, elevated enough to pierce the gloom. Driven by this deduction, I sprinted downward, accelerating toward the summit. Minutes blurred into impact. I struck the ground hard enough to fracture my neck, the pain searing and absolute. Paralyzed, I lay broken, a vulnerable target for any passing predator.

Consciousness returned slowly. I sat up, clutching my throat until the agony subsided, forcing myself upright. My movement shifted from panic to cautious calculation. A cold dread settled in my chest: if this structure was the Lesser Refuge, why was it utterly black? Was it a den of evil, a trick of malevolent forces distorting my vision? Against the distant fires of the land, however, the silhouette was undeniable. It was the Refuge.

My initial reaction had been blind momentum, fueled by months of searching. I had intended to summon Naani via the Master-Word, broadcasting my arrival across the ether. Instead, prudence halted me. I needed verification.

Descending the slope, my pace oscillated between careful observation and a fierce, renewed urgency, the earlier shock fading. I had taken thirteen hours to climb the volcanic peak above; the descent took ten, slowed by the trauma of my fall. By the tenth hour, I reached the Great Plain. The Refuge itself vanished behind the curvature of the hill, obscured by the night, but the bulk blocking my view confirmed my proximity.

Four hours later, traversing the landscape dotted with glowing fissures, the horizon swallowed the distant lights. Total darkness reclaimed the view. I was close. In the final five hours, the night erupted with sound: running paws, a roar akin to a giant, and a scream of pure horror.

Ascending the hill, a manic impulse seized me—a desire to shout her name, hoping for an echo. This feeling died quickly, replaced by a chilling intuition. Something waited there. No human occupied that dark mass; monsters awaited me, agents of destruction. Fear rooted me. I retreated, moving silently into the abyss, fleeing the place.

Four hours of travel cleared the immediate vicinity. Safety was an abstract concept; the land offered none. Blindly, I wandered until the terrain curved beneath my feet. I was at the shore of the ancient sea—or perhaps one of several dried beds curving through this country of night.

Exhaustion claimed me. I sat, weak and bewildered, feeling my heart stop. My spirit confirmed the worst: evil resided in the Pyramid. The people of the Lesser Refuge were doomed, replaced by monstrous powers. If true, I was too late to save the Maid. Paradoxically, this realization brought relief. Death in combat was preferable to the lingering misery of survival.

Desolation ruled my mind. I questioned my certainty, yet my spirit knew with absolute clarity. After a moment, I resolved to test the connection. How else could I confirm Naani's status? I stood, scanning the blackness, and projected the Master-Word.

Immediately, the response shattered the silence. Low, solemn vibrations pulsed through the night. Within my skull, a tiny, lonely voice emerged from the ends of the world. It was Naani's voice, joined by Mirdath's, calling my old name.

Choking on the sheer weight of joy, my despair vanished. She lived. I hadn't heard my own kin's voice in an eternity. The distance was vast, the peril unknown, but she was alive.

Before I could reply, my senses detected a presence nearby. A firehole burned in the brush. My spirit identified the intruder. Ignoring the Maid, I slipped into the foliage, emerging from the shadows to reveal myself.

Through the gap in the bushes, I saw a small figure kneeling beside the fire, sobbing uncontrollably. A slender girl, terrified, listening desperately to a cry she believed emanated from the very heart of the nightmare—the Mighty Pyramid. She had cried out to me countless times during her lonely month, receiving no answer, unaware of my desperate approach due to her own depleted strength.

I steadied my breath, spoke clearly: "MIRDATH."

She stopped weeping, looking around with fresh terror, her tear-streaked face flickering with hope. I parted the branches and stepped into the clearing, clad in grey armor. I froze, paralyzed by emotion. To embrace her was right; to touch her was wrong. She was Naani and Mirdath, yet she was a stranger, delicate and shattered by grief.

She recoiled, stumbling backward into the brush, realizing I was human, not a monster. Instantly, I spoke the Master-Word aloud, declaring my identity as "That One." Recognition flooded her features. She cried out in a broken voice, rushing forward, burying her small hands in mine, collapsing into sobs. I held her hands tightly, removing my armored gloves to offer warmth.

She leaned against me, frail as a child. Soon, the weeping ceased, replaced by ragged breathing. Hunger gnawed at me; she had wandered alone to the brink of starvation. She remained silent, trembling, unable to command her voice. I inspected my own hand, noting its thinness, then placed her gently against a smooth rock. Stripping my cloak, I wrapped it around her tattered remains, shielding her from the chill and her own weakness.

From my pack, I retrieved a tablet, crushed it into a cup, and boiled water over the firehole's heat to create broth. Her hands shook so violently she would have spilled it; I guided the spoon to her lips. She drank, then succumbed to quiet sobbing. I held her hands firmly beneath the cloak, anchoring her. The trembling subsided. Peace returned.

Her fingers stirred within mine. I loosened my grip; she clutched back, weak and gentle, still staring ahead, gathering strength. Anxiety spiked—I feared monsters approaching. With her entrusted to my care, my heart became fragile.

Suddenly, she attempted to rise. I released her to assist. Instead, she grabbed my hand, sank to her knees, and kissed it, weeping anew. Abashed, I stood dumbfounded, allowing the gesture. Then, drawing my own knee to hers, I took her hands and kissed them, humbled. She sobbed harder, overwhelmed by my arrival through the night's desolation. I released her hands so she could wipe her tears, leaving them resting in my palms as she knelt, bowing her head, offering herself completely.

I pulled her into my arms, gentle and devoid of caress. Stroking her hair, I called her names, speaking words I barely remembered but she understood. She settled quietly against me, content yet weeping. I coaxed her vaguely, offering comfort she didn't demand, simply needing shelter. She had endured loneliness, terror, and grief for a horrific duration.

Eventually, she stilled. I prepared to move her closer to the rock to continue feeding her, but she nestled against me, a sweet wistfulness warming my heart. She was mine. As she began to murmur strange words, seeking gentle obedience, I watched her gaze follow my movements, knowing I would have to turn back to her constantly.
