As the summer sun dipped below the horizon, casting a warm orange glow over the bustling docks of London, a peculiar tale unfolded. It began with a chance encounter between a lone investigator and Sergeant Cuff, a man known for his sharp instincts and unwavering dedication to justice. The year was 1849, and the investigator had been tasked with tracking down three men, described as Indians, suspected of a grave offense.

On the twenty-seventh of June, the investigator received the initial briefing from Sergeant Cuff, who had instructed him to follow the trio to Rotterdam, where they had boarded a steamer bound for the Netherlands. Without hesitation, the investigator set off, taking a different steamer to avoid raising suspicion. Upon arriving in Rotterdam, he met with the commander of the Wednesday's steamer, who revealed that the three men had indeed been passengers, but had disembarked at Gravesend, claiming they were headed to Calais. The commander, moved by their apparent distress, had put them ashore, unaware of the ruse.

The investigator, sensing a clever ploy, quickly returned to England and retraced the men's steps, following them to Plymouth, where they had secretly boarded the East Indiaman, Bewley Castle, bound for Bombay. With this new information, Sergeant Cuff instructed the authorities at Bombay to prepare for their arrival, and the investigator's role in the matter came to an end.

Months later, Captain Jameson of the Bewley Castle sat down to recount the events of that fateful voyage. It was a journey he would never forget, one marked by a series of unfortunate circumstances. The ship had been becalmed for three long days and nights, drifting helplessly off the coast of India. As the discipline of the crew relaxed, some of the passengers had taken to rowing and swimming in the ship's small boats. However, when the wind finally picked up, the boats were left unattended, moored to the ship's side.

It was on the third night that the unthinkable happened. The smallest of the boats went missing, and the three Indian passengers were nowhere to be found. The captain was convinced that they had stolen the boat, taking advantage of the lax atmosphere on board. Despite the proximity to land, there was little the crew could do to pursue them once the discovery was made in the morning. The incident would remain a mystery, a testament to the cunning and audacity of the three men, who had seemingly vanished into thin air.
