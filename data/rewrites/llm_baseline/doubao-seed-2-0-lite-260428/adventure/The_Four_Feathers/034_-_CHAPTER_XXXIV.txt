The early summer sun gilded the stone balustrade of Broad Place’s terrace, where two old veterans sat finishing their post-breakfast newspapers, the scent of jasmine climbing the rail mixing with the faint tang of new-mown hay drifting up from the park meadow below. The elder of the pair, General Feversham, turned a crisp sheet and huffed a quiet note of surprise.
“I see Osman Digna’s turned up at Suakin again,” he said. “Likely there’ll be fighting before long.”
The other man, Lieutenant Sutch, barely reacted. “He won’t do much real harm,” he said, and set his newspaper down entirely. The soft English countryside blurred before his eyes: all he could see was the white Red Sea city shimmering under brutal heat, the brown surrounding plains tangled waist-deep in halfa grass, and the bare, sun-scorched hills stretching away toward Khor Gwob.
“Stifling place, Suakin, isn’t it, Sutch?” Feversham prompted.
“Appallingly so,” Sutch agreed. “Heard tell of an officer who dropped dead of sunstroke on parade at six in the morning, heat went right through a regulation issue helmet straight to his temples. That town breathes dank, clinging heat. But I was glad to be there, very glad,” he added, his voice soft with unspoken memory.
“Ah,” Feversham nodded knowingly. “Ibex hunting, eh?”
“No,” Sutch laughed shortly. “Garrison shot all the ibex out for miles around years ago.”
“Then it was something worthwhile to do, that’s what got you through it?”
“That’s it exactly, Feversham. Something that mattered to do.”
They fell quiet, turning back to their papers, until a footman carried out a small stack of post for each of them. Feversham’s sharp old eye scanned his envelopes in an instant, and he grunted with quiet satisfaction when he spotted the postmark from Ramelton. He fished his spectacles out of their case, laid the letter deliberately on the garden table between them, and drew out his cigar case.
“Let me light this first,” he said, moving with the deliberate slowness of a man who has learned to savor small, rare luxuries. He ignored Sutch’s fidgeting, carefully cutting the end of his cigar, striking a match, and drawing slow puffs until he was sure it burned evenly, then leaned back in his chair to stretch his legs. A letter from Ramelton was not to be rushed.
“From Harry?” Sutch asked, nodding at the envelope.
“From her,” Feversham corrected, and broke the seal. He read every line slowly, twice over, while Sutch pretended to stare out at the fields so he would not be caught peeking.
“Any news worth having?” Sutch asked, affecting casualness.
“They’re happy with the house now the rebuild’s all finished,” Feversham said.
“More?”
“Harry’s finished the sixth chapter of his history of the Eastern war.”
“Good for him,” Sutch cheered. “He’ll do that work better than any official war correspondent. He’s got the imagination for it, he knows every inch of that ground, he was on the scene for every fight. More than that, he wandered the bazaars, saw the unreported, messy underbelly of the campaign no general’s dispatch ever bothered to write down.”
“Aye,” Feversham said, a little gruff. “I doubt you and I will live to finish reading it, though, Sutch. But you might—you’ve got a good few years on me still.”
He went back to the letter, and Sutch pressed again for more news.
“They’re coming to stay here in a fortnight,” Feversham said, and Sutch bounced to his feet.
“Splendid. I’m cancelling all my other plans. I’m staying right here to greet them.”
He paced the length of the terrace once, then turned back to find Feversham staring down at the letter in his lap, his brow furrowed with genuine perplexity.
“You know, Sutch,” he said, “I never understood. Not the whole of it. Did you?”
Sutch smiled. “Aye, I think I did.” He did not offer to explain. It was far better Feversham never untangled the full tale of Harry’s secret sacrifice, the three feathers, the months he wandered disguised through the desert. To understand would only fill the old general with self-reproach for the years he spent misjudging Harry as a coward.
“Ever see Durrance these days?” Feversham asked suddenly.
“Quite often. He’s abroad traveling right now.”
“Came to call here the day you left for Suakin,” Feversham recalled. “Talked to me for half an hour. Stood up as Harry’s best man at the wedding, too. That’s another thing I never made head nor tail of. Did you?”
“I understood that, too,” Sutch said simply.
Feversham made a small hmph noise, and did not press for details. He had always set aside the puzzles he could not solve to make room for simpler, clearer joys, and he did not reach for his other letters now. He sat smoking his cigar, staring out over the sun-dappled fields, listening to the distant laughter of farm hands and the low moo of cattle from the lower pastures.
Sutch had already finished all his own correspondence when Feversham spoke again.
“Tell me,” he said, “have you marked what the date is today?”
Sutch looked up, and his face lit. “Aye. This day next week is the anniversary of our attack on the Redan. And Harry’s birthday, too.”
“Exactly,” Feversham grinned. “What do you say we bring the old Crimean Night dinners back?”
Sutch jumped so fast his chair scraped the terrace stone. “Splendid! Do you think we can round up a full table of the old crowd?”
“Let’s count,” Feversham said, ringing the handbell on the table to send the servant fetching the tattered old Army List. The two veterans leaned over its red leather pages, poring over names and postings, and we may leave them there, planning the feast full of old toasts and old comrades.
There is one last small tale left to tell, though. That same night, after all the invitations had been sent out from Broad Place, and every light in the old house had guttered out, a man leaned on the rail of a steamer anchored in Port Said harbor, listening to Arab coolies singing rough work songs as they tramped back and forth across the gangplanks, coal baskets slung over their shoulders, running between barges and the ship’s hull. The din of the town’s bazaars drifted across the dark water to his ears, and he could picture every part of the quay without seeing it: the flaring braziers where street vendors cooked their food, the warm yellow glow of ship portholes strung out in a long line down the canal, the dark bulk of anchored funnels ahead and behind.
It was Durrance, back in the East he loved, his long-servant at his elbow to guide him. The next dawn the steamer slipped through the canal cut, and by sunset it was cutting through the cool, sharp air of the Gulf of Suez. He lived every old campaign again, clear as day: Kassassin, Tel-el-Kebir, Tamai, the chaotic fight at McNeil’s zareeba. All the bright, full years of his active service, the years when his eyes were sharp and he could march for days across the sand without tiring. He knew, far to the west across the desert, the slow, massive preparations for the full reconquest of the Sudan were already underway, the campaign that would roll the Dervish Empire up and crush it to dust under the British flag. He had promised himself once he would stand on the glacis of the ruined Sinkat fort and fight in that campaign, but the desert he loved had struck him blind and cast him out.
But the steamer still moved south, cutting steadily toward the Red Sea. Three more nights of sailing, and the Southern Cross would lift slantwise over the southern horizon, bright in the dark sky—even if Durrance could never see it, he would feel it, glowing there above the dunes he knew better than the back of his own hand.
