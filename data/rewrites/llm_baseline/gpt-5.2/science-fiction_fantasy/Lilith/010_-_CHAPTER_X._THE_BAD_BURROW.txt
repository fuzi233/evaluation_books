As the air thickened to ink and winter cinched its hard belt around the land, the little fluttering fire that had lured me on burned the brighter, then checked its flight as if to count my breaths. When I stepped into its radiance it moved again—slowly now, lingering above stony patches where the ground showed through like bone under thin flesh. Each time I lifted my eyes it seemed larger, nearer, as though it were not merely leading me but tasting my attention, drawing it out as steam draws scent from a hot pot.

Plainly it was a bird-butterfly: it had the swallow’s double stroke, yet the body was slight and bookish, and the wings—nearly square—flashed the whole rainbow as if every colour were a spice rubbed fresh and released at once. There were gradations too, delicate as the difference between toasted and burnt, and some hues I had no names for, as if they belonged to a dialect of light my tongue had never learned.

I watched it too greedily. My boot caught a low rock; I fell and lay stunned, cheek on cold stone, the taste of peat-dust in my mouth. When sense returned, the creature hovered above my face, pouring down its chord of light in layered notes. I rose and followed, but still I stared upward instead of minding my steps, and my foot struck a stone so sharply pain jumped to my knee.

Fearing a worse fall, I sat in the heather to steady myself and only look. A hunger woke in me—an absurd, intimate longing to have that splendour in my hand, to hold it as one holds a warm loaf just out of the oven, close, possessive, smelling of its own life. To my unspeakable delight it began to sink toward me. Slowly at first, then swiftly, it came, growing as it came, as if the treasure of the universe had decided to be mine.

I put out my hand, and had it.

The instant my fingers closed, the light went out. Darkness, thick as pitch and lidless. In my palm lay something dead-cold and heavy—a book, boards spread like wings, but without one glimmer left in it. I flung it up in horror, only to hear it thud among the heather, a dull sound swallowed immediately by the great hush.

I bent forward and buried my face in my hands. Misery sat on me as solid as frost. Yet the cold went on sharpening, drawing heat from my bones as salt draws water from meat; and fear of being frozen forced me to stand.

The moment I rose, a faint sense of light stirred—not in the air so much as in me. “Is it coming to life?” I cried, and hope stabbed quick as hunger. But no: it was the edge of a moon peering up—keen, sharp—over a level horizon.

She brought me light, but no guidance.

She would not hover; she would not wait while my steps faltered; she offered only an indifferent seeing, as if I must choose my way from whatever her cold eye happened to show. When she rose to a full face, I began to make out the land: westward of her, not far, a range of low hills broke the horizon like the rim of a great dish. I set out for them.

What a night lay between.

The moon did not behave like the one I had known on earth. Her face was strange, her light stranger still—thin, aloof, as though it came from an unknown sun and had lost something in translation on the way. Worst of all was her staring. Every time I looked up, she was looking down as if with all her might, as if my being there in her night was a question she could not stop asking. At first I was nettled, as at the rude attention of a fellow creature; then I fancied in her gaze a sort of wondering pity. Why was I out, awake, moving, when everything around me seemed made for burrowing and hiding?

Then the thought struck me with a chill deeper than the frost: what an awful thing it is to be awake in the universe. I WAS, and could not help it.

Underfoot, the heather thinned. My boots sank into a bare spongy soil like dry, powdery peat—crumbly, scenting faintly of old rot and rain long gone. It gave a little heave beneath me, not the steady give of turf but a living lift, as if I walked on the skin of something vast. Ahead, in the low moon, I saw a shadowy ripple—like an earthquake-wave—running away from me across the plain.

It passed into the distance; while I stared after it, a single wave rose up and came back toward me. A yard or two away it burst, and out of it scrambled an animal like a tiger. Clots of mould hung about its mouth and ears, as if it had been kneading itself through compost; its eyes winked and flamed, and it rushed at me showing white teeth in a soundless snarl.

I stood rooted, not brave, not afraid—merely caught, as one is caught by the sight of a knife lifted without any sound. The creature turned its head to the ground and plunged into it as into water.

“That moon is affecting my brain,” I said aloud when my feet could move again. “What life can be here but the phantasmic—the stuff of which dreams are made? I am walking in a vain show!”

So I tried to keep my heart above the waters of fear, not knowing that the very light I distrusted was my defence from the realities I took for phantoms. The moon, with her cold stare, was binding the monsters as salt binds flesh; without her, I would scarcely have taken a second step on that hideous, heaving earth.

“I will not be appalled by that which only seems!” I told myself—and yet it is a terrible thing to walk on a sea when you know there are fishes beneath that might rise at any moment.

Near my feet, the head of a worm began to push up from the soil—slowly, insistently—as big as a polar bear’s head, and much resembling it, with a white mane set on a red neck. The writhing draws by which its immense length extricated itself were horribly methodical, like a rope being pulled through mud. I dared not look away. When at last the tail came free, it lay as if exhausted, wallowing and making feeble effort to burrow again.

“Does it live on the dead,” I wondered, “and is it unable to hurt the living? If they scent their prey and come out, why do they leave me unharmed?”

I know now it was because the moon paralysed them.

All that night as I walked, hideous creatures—no two alike—threatened me from the restless ground. In some of them, beauty of colour enhanced the loathsomeness of shape: one large serpent was covered, head to far tail, with feathers of glorious hues, as if it had rolled in a spilled casket of dyes and come up preening. The air remained unnaturally hushed. Even when something lunged, there was no proper sound of it—no honest scuff, no cry, no breaking of twigs—only the faintest suggestion of movement, as though the world had been robbed of its own language and could only mouth at me in silence.

That silence itself began to press upon my mind. On earth a night has a score of small noises—a wind in grass, a bird shifting, water talking to stone. Here, the lack of such homely speech made every shape more dreadful, as if the land were under a rule forbidding it to testify. I found myself craving, absurdly, the simplest domestic sound: the clink of a spoon against a bowl, the sigh of a kettle, the respectful crackle of a hearth. Without them, the soul feels unmoored, and the body listens too hard.

By degrees I grew so accustomed to the hurtless menaces that I began to beguile the way by inventing monstrosities of my own, as if naming horrors—making a private lexicon—could give me mastery. I never suspected that each moment of my life was held on the thin edge of that moonlight.

For light is yet light, though it be only the last of a countless series of reflections.

How differently would my feet have carried me over that moving soil had I understood that, if I were still within their range when her lamp ceased to shine on that cursed spot, I should in that instant be at the mercy of such as have no mercy—swallowed into a writhing heap of hideousness, each thing as terrible as before it had seemed, only now no longer “seeming.”

Fool of ignorance, I watched the descent of the weary, solemn, anxious moon down the widening vault above me with no worse uneasiness than dread of losing my way—where as yet I had, indeed, no way to lose.

The low hills I had chosen for my goal were near. The moon was sinking toward their skyline when the soundless wallowing ceased, and the burrow—if burrow it was—lay motionless and bare, as if the land had drawn in its breath.

Then I saw, walking slowly over the pale soil, the form of a woman.

A white mist floated about her, now gathering to the shape of a garment, now losing it again as a wind—always the same wind, like a servant that hates its master—dogged her steps, stealing the covering as soon as it formed. She was beautiful; yet on her countenance lay such pride and misery together that I could hardly trust my sight. Up and down she walked, striving with hungry fingers to catch the mist and wrap it round her. The eyes in the beautiful face were dead, as if whatever language they once spoke had been erased. On her left side was a dark spot, and now and then she pressed her hand there, as if to stifle pain or sickness.

Her hair hung nearly to her feet. Sometimes the wind mixed it with the mist so I could not distinguish one from the other; then it would fall and gather itself again, and in the moonlight it shone pale gold—thin as old straw kept too long in a loft, yet still catching light.

Suddenly she pressed both hands upon her heart and fell. The mist rose from her body and melted away as if relieved to be free.

I ran to her.

But she began to writhe in such torture that I stopped, helpless, my throat tight. A moment more and her legs hurried from her body—sped away as serpents. From her shoulders fled her arms, serpents also, as though they could not endure to belong to her. Then something flew up from where her head had been, like a bat tearing itself out of a net; and when I looked again, she was gone.

The ground rose like the sea in a storm. Terror seized me—not the clean fear of a shouted warning, but the sickness of silence breaking at last. I turned and ran for the hills.

I was already on the lower slope when the moon sank behind one of their summits and I stepped into shadow as into a cellar. Behind me rose a waste and sickening cry, the only sound I had heard since the dead butterfly fell—a cry of frustrate desire. It shook my heart like cloth snapping on a line in a hard wind. The plain, which had been mute as a stopped mouth, had found one awful syllable and flung it after me.

I looked back. Many dark objects bounded in pursuit.

I made for the crest of a ridge where the moon still shone. She seemed to linger there, as if she would at least grant me enough sight to defend myself. I climbed faster. Crossing the shadow of a rock, I heard them panting at my heels—at last a natural sound, and because it was natural it was worse: breath is the oldest language of hunger.

Just as the foremost threw itself upon me with a snarl of greedy hate, we rushed together into the moonlight.

She flashed out an angry light, and the creature fell from me a bodiless blotch, as if the light had stripped it of all substance. Strength came into my limbs. I turned on the rest. One by one, as they darted into the radiance, they dropped with a howl and became nothing but stains of darkness on the pale ground. And I saw—or fancied—a strange smile on the round face above me, as if she approved the small justice of it, or found it curious.

I climbed to the top of the ridge. Far away the moon shone, sinking toward a low horizon. The air here was pure and strong, like water from a deep well, and in it the silence felt less like a gag and more like a held pause between two honest sounds.

I descended a little way, found it warmer, and sat down to wait the dawn.

The moon went below, and the world again was dark.
