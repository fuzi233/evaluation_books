On the second morning after the archery meeting, Mr. Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt sat at breakfast with Mr. Lush in a room so perfectly ordered it seemed to hold its breath. Through the open windows the summer air came in unbidden—warm as milk, scented with mown grass and the faint resin of the bordering wood—and the lawn lay close and green as a table-cloth spread for a feast. Even the park beyond had that bruised purple softness which makes distant trees look like fruit left to darken on the bough.

The hush inside was not the hush of peace, but the hush of well-bred furniture that had learned not to creak, not to complain, not to betray what it had witnessed. Outside, the world spoke in gentler tongues: the far combing of leaves, a bird’s punctual call, the padded scuff of paws on turf. It was a soundscape that could have steadied a more honest mind; here it merely framed a kind of domestic theatre.

Half-a-dozen dogs drifted in and out, making languid circuits between table and window as if appetite were an amusement rather than a need. They were the sort of creatures who had been offered delicacies so often that they could afford to sniff and decline, turning hunger into a game of manners. Only Fetch—the liver-colored water-spaniel with the dark, expressive eyes—watched Grandcourt with a devotion that had no play in it.

Grandcourt had drawn his chair aside to face the lawn. One leg was flung over another chair, his right elbow rested on the table, and he smoked a large cigar as if his breakfast were the smoke itself. In his lap he held a tiny Maltese dog, Fluff, warm and silken as a hand-muff, its small silver collar and bell catching the light whenever his fingers moved. When his hand was free of cigar or coffee-cup, it lay on that small parcel of animal heat with an absent ownership.

Fetch bore it as long as she could, then set her broad paw—so soft it might have been a piece of velvet—on his leg. Her head lifted, her nose quivered, and the plea in her eyes was as plain as a child’s who has watched another child be fed sweets.

Grandcourt looked at her without change for half a minute, letting the moment sour. Then, with a deliberate leisure, he laid down his cigar, raised Fluff close to his chin, and patted it with a demonstrative tenderness, his gaze gravely fixed on Fetch as if the point were not affection but discipline.

Fetch whimpered, trying to swallow her complaint—making little broken sounds like someone choking back words that have no permitted language. At last she rested her head beside that outstretched paw and looked up with a beseeching that any creature with a pulse might have understood.

It held until it didn’t. The ache broke into a howl—sharp, raw, too honest for the room. Grandcourt pushed Fetch down without speaking. Fluff was set carelessly on the table, where its black nose dominated the salt-cellar as if it meant to season the world. Grandcourt turned back to his cigar and found—annoyed, as though Fetch had personally spoiled his comfort—that it needed relighting.

Fetch, having begun, could not stop. The second howl was louder. The third followed it, as if grief had found its own refrain and would not be silenced by manners.

“Turn out that brute, will you?” Grandcourt said to Lush, not raising his voice, not even looking at him—merely dropping the words like a command that expected the air itself to obey.

Lush rose at once. He lifted Fetch—heavy with muscle and unhappy loyalty—and carried her out, taking longer than he liked, for he was not fond of stooping and Fetch did not go as a docile parcel. When he returned, he lit a cigar, angled himself so he could see Grandcourt’s face without seeming to stare, and after a moment said, “Shall you ride or drive to Quetcham to-day?”

“I am not going to Quetcham.”

“You did not go yesterday.”

Grandcourt smoked in silence, letting the pause thicken. Then he said, “I suppose you sent my card and inquiries.”

“I went myself at four, and said you were sure to be there shortly. They will suppose some accident prevented you from fulfilling the intention. Especially if you go to-day.”

Another stretch of silence lay between them, filled only by the soft crackle of tobacco and the distant, steadier music of the park. Then Grandcourt said, “What men are invited here with their wives?”

Lush drew out a note-book, the small ledger of other people’s convenience. “The Captain and Mrs. Torrington come next week. Then there are Mr. Hollis and Lady Flora, and the Cushats and the Gogoffs.”

“Rather a ragged lot,” Grandcourt said after a while, with that lazy distaste which treated human company like ill-matched plate. “Why did you ask the Gogoffs? When you write invitations in my name, be good enough to give me a list, instead of bringing down a giantess on me without my knowledge. She spoils the look of the room.”

“You invited the Gogoffs yourself when you met them in Paris.”

“What has my meeting them in Paris to do with it? I told you to give me a list.”

The words were not louder than before, but something in them shifted—no longer the drawling languor that made his speech seem half asleep, but a low, inward distinctness that carried the weight of a peremptory will. It had the same effect as a knife laid quietly on the table: no gesture, yet every motion afterward must take account of it.

“Are there any other couples you would like to invite?” Lush asked.

“Yes. Think of some decent people, with a daughter or two. And one of your damned musicians. But not a comic fellow.”

Lush spoke with a careless air, though his attention sharpened. “I wonder if Klesmer would consent to come to us when he leaves Quetcham. Nothing but first-class music will go down with Miss Arrowpoint.”

Grandcourt turned his eyes toward Lush for the first time, slowly, as if granting a favor. He took two long luxuriant puffs before he said, in a tone lower than ever yet edged with contempt, “What in the name of nonsense have I to do with Miss Arrowpoint and her music?”

“Well, something,” Lush said, jocosely—too jocosely, as if sugar could disguise a dose. “You need not give yourself much trouble, perhaps. But some forms must be gone through before a man can marry a million.”

“Very likely. But I am not going to marry a million.”

“That’s a pity—to fling away an opportunity of this sort, and knock down your own plans.”

“Your plans, I suppose you mean.”

“You have some debts, you know, and things may turn out inconveniently, after all. The heirship is not absolutely certain.”

Grandcourt did not answer. He sipped his coffee—dark, bitter, perfectly served—and the simple domestic act had in it the cold steadiness of someone who believed he could drink while the house burned. Lush went on, his voice tightening with the urgency of a man who had lived too long on borrowed ease.

“It really is a fine opportunity. The father and mother ask for nothing better, I can see, and the daughter’s looks and manners require no allowances, any more than if she hadn’t a sixpence. She is not beautiful; but equal to carrying any rank. And she is not likely to refuse such prospects as you can offer her.”

“Perhaps not.”

“The father and mother would let you do anything you like with them.”

“But I should not like to do anything with them.”

Lush paused, set down his cigar, and thrust his hands into his side pockets as if bracing himself against an irritant he dared not strike. When he spoke again, the remonstrance came up from deeper, from that old residue of conscience or calculation which men keep even when they have mislaid their scruples.

“Good God, Grandcourt! after your experience, will you let a whim interfere with your comfortable settlement in life?”

“Spare your oratory. I know what I am going to do.”

“What?” Lush’s impatience broke through. He meant to keep his temper, and the effort showed.

“I am going to marry the other girl.”

“Have you fallen in love?” Lush’s question carried a sneer that tried to turn feeling into a joke before it could command respect.

“I am going to marry her.”

“You have made her an offer already, then?”

“No.”

“She is a young lady with a will of her own, I fancy. Extremely well fitted to make a rumpus. She would know what she liked.”

“She doesn’t like you,” Grandcourt said, and there was the faintest ghost of a smile, as if dislike were a private entertainment.

“Perfectly true,” Lush returned, and the sneer came back, sharpened. “However, if you and she are devoted to each other, that will be enough.”

Grandcourt took no notice. He finished his coffee, rose, and strolled out onto the lawn as if the matter were as light as the ash he tapped from his cigar. All the dogs followed him at once, their nails ticking briefly on stone before the turf swallowed the sound. The morning’s gentle acoustics—leaf-hush, bird-call, distant insect hum—closed around him like a cloak that did not warm.

Lush watched him a moment. Then he resumed his cigar and lit it again, smoking slowly while his eyes and fingers consulted his beard in that habitual way of his—as if the face he presented to the world were a text he might still edit. At last, stroking the beard as though concluding a calculation, he said under his breath, “Check, old boy!”

He had not known Grandcourt fifteen years without learning which measures were useless—though which might be useful was another matter, always uncertain. Long ago he had held a fellowship and come near taking orders for the sake of a college living; but he had not loved that future, with its dutiful restraints and plain dinners, and had accepted instead the post of travelling companion to a marquess, and afterward to young Grandcourt, who had lost his father early and found Lush so convenient that he let him become prime minister in all his more personal affairs.

Fifteen years of that arrangement had made Grandcourt more dependent on Lush’s handiness, and Lush more dependent on the lazy luxury that required only adroit management—no honest labor, no loud exertion. Grandcourt’s want of respect for his companion had been absolute from the beginning; time had not intensified it so much as confirmed his sense that he might kick Lush if he chose—only he never chose to kick any animal himself. Kicking, after all, was an attitude that compromised a gentleman. A gentleman’s dogs should be kicked for him.

Lush was not unconscious of being held kickable. He preferred to count that estimate among Grandcourt’s peculiarities, one more incalculable mood to be weathered for the sake of soft chairs, good horses, and dinners chosen with discrimination. What son of a vicar—sent to Oxford at the cost of his mother’s and sisters’ calico—keeps an independent spirit when he is bent on dining well and living in luxuriant honey-blossomed clover without working? He had passed for a scholar once, and retained the odor of scholarship as one keeps a scent on a glove long after the flower is gone. The bachelor’s arts that soften manners had served him as a preparation for sinecures; and his present provision was as good a sinecure as a man could want, requiring no more than tact, memory, and the ability to turn away disagreeable noises at the right moment.

He told himself he had never done a bad action; and so he had never needed to ask whether he might do one if ease demanded it. Ease demanded very little beyond that he should take what was handed to him and swallow it without choking. If puddings rolled toward him through dust, he took the inside bits and found them relishing.

That morning, for example, though he had been more annoyed than usual, he went to his private sitting-room and played a good hour on the violoncello—drawing from the strings a rich, human voice that filled the quiet house with something better than cigar-smoke and command: a sound that remembered feeling even when the men who lived among such sounds had forgotten how to translate it into life.
