XXVIII. "Ol—ol—how do you spell it, anyway?" the sharp-tongued young woman behind the brass counter of the Western Union office asked, her eyes narrowing as Archer slid his wife’s telegram across the ledge.

"Olenska—O-len-ska," he repeated, pulling the message back slightly so he could carefully print the foreign syllables above May’s looping handwriting.

"It’s a peculiar name for a New York telegraph office—especially in this part of town," remarked a voice from behind him, calm and unexpectedly familiar. Archer turned to see Lawrence Lefferts standing beside him, tugging at his moustache with studied nonchalance, his gaze fixed somewhere just beyond the telegram.

"Hallo, Newland," Lefferts said, as if catching up on a casual stroll. "Thought I’d find you here. Just heard about old Mrs. Mingott’s stroke. I was heading to the house when I saw you turning down this street and followed along. You’ve come from there, I suppose?"

Archer nodded and slipped the telegram through the lattice.

"Very serious, then?" Lefferts continued, his tone light but probing. "Wiring the family, I imagine. I gather it *is* serious—if you’re including Countess Olenska."

A flash of anger surged through Archer. For a moment, he wanted nothing more than to lash out at Lefferts’ smooth, self-assured face. But he held himself back. To speak of Ellen Olenska with this man—here, now—was unthinkable. He paid for the telegram and stepped out into the street with Lefferts by his side.

Once outside, Archer regained his composure. "Mrs. Mingott is much better," he said. "The doctor says there’s no real cause for concern."

Lefferts sighed with relief, then quickly shifted the subject. "Have you heard? There are terrible rumors again about Beaufort..."

By the next day, the news of Beaufort’s financial collapse had spread across every newspaper. It eclipsed even the report of Mrs. Manson Mingott’s stroke. Only a few who knew the deeper connections suspected that Catherine’s illness might have been stirred not just by age or overindulgence, but by the shock of the scandal.

New York reeled under the weight of Beaufort’s betrayal. As Mr. Letterblair lamented, it was the worst case he’d ever seen—not just in his own memory, but in the long history of the firm bearing his name. The bank had kept accepting deposits for an entire day after its downfall was certain. And since many of its clients belonged to powerful families, the deception felt all the more calculated.

Had Mrs. Beaufort spoken with dignity, perhaps some sympathy might have softened public outrage. But instead, she insisted such misfortunes were “the test of friendship”—a phrase that only deepened the scorn. When it became known why she had visited Mrs. Mingott late at night, her coldness seemed worse than her husband’s. She had no excuse, nor did her critics have the satisfaction of blaming her foreignness.

At least, people comforted themselves, Beaufort *was* foreign. But when a man like Dallas of South Carolina dismissed the disaster with a careless shrug—“He’ll be on his feet again soon”—the argument lost its strength. In the end, society had no choice but to accept the bitter truth: marriage, however grand, was unbreakable.

And so, life would go on without the Beauforts. Except for those left in ruin—Medora Manson, Miss Lannings, and others whose trust had been shattered. Had they listened to Mr. Henry van der Luyden...

"The best thing," Mrs. Archer declared, as if delivering a verdict, "is for them to go live at Regina’s little place in North Carolina. Beaufort’s always kept a racing stable—he should breed trotting horses. I’d say he has all the makings of a successful horsedealer."

Everyone agreed, though no one dared ask what the Beauforts truly planned.

The next day, Mrs. Manson Mingott was indeed much improved. She regained her voice just enough to command that the Beauforts never be mentioned again—and when Dr. Bencomb arrived, she demanded to know why her family was making such a fuss over her health.

"If people of my age *will* eat chicken salad at dinner," she snapped, "what can they expect?"

With the doctor adjusting her diet, the stroke was rewritten as a mere bout of indigestion.

But though she maintained her usual sharpness, something had shifted within old Catherine. Age had begun to distance her from the world—though not from curiosity. Her compassion, never strong, had faded further. She dismissed the Beaufort disaster as easily as she once dismissed gossip.

Yet for the first time, she turned inward. She began to fixate on her own ailments, and to take a sudden, sentimental interest in members of her family she had long ignored.

Mr. Welland, in particular, caught her attention. Of all her sons-in-law, he was the one she had most often overlooked. His wife had tried for years to convince her of his strength and intellect—had he only “chosen.” But her efforts were met only with a dismissive laugh.

Now, however, his frailty made him fascinating. Mrs. Mingott sent a royal summons for him to visit, to compare diets—once his temperature allowed. After all, she now believed, one could never be too careful about temperatures.

Twenty-four hours after Madame Olenska’s telegram, another arrived: she would arrive from Washington the following evening.

At the Wellands’, where the Archers happened to be lunching, the question arose immediately: who would meet her at Jersey City?

The household, already strained by its modest means, debated fiercely. Mrs. Welland couldn’t go—she was needed at her mother’s. The brougham couldn’t be spared, in case Mr. Welland collapsed upon seeing his mother-in-law after her attack. The Welland boys were downtown. Mr. Lovell Mingott was returning from a hunting trip, and the family carriage was already reserved to meet him. And May—no matter how capable—couldn’t be expected to cross the ferry alone in the twilight.

Yet letting Ellen arrive unmet seemed rude, even against old Catherine’s wishes.

“It’s just like Ellen,” Mrs. Welland murmured wearily, “to put us in such a bind.”

“The only thing that makes me think Mamma isn’t as well as Bencomb says,” she added, half-joking, “is this sudden need to see Ellen right away—no matter how inconvenient.”

Her words slipped out before she could stop them.

Mr. Welland froze. His fork clattered onto the plate.

“Augusta,” he said, pale, “do you really think Bencomb isn’t as reliable as he used to be? Have you noticed him being less attentive lately—with my case… or your mother’s?”

Mrs. Welland paled in turn. The implications flooded through her mind—her mistake, the consequences. But she forced a laugh, helped herself to more oysters, and said, regaining her cheerful mask:

“My dear, how could you think such a thing? I only meant—after Mamma took such a firm stand about Ellen’s duty to return to her husband, it seems strange she’d suddenly want her here now. Especially when there are half a dozen other grandchildren she might have called for. But we must remember—Mamma, for all her energy, is very old.”

Mr. Welland remained silent. Then slowly, deliberately:

“Yes. Very old. And for all we know, Bencomb may not be as effective with elderly patients. As you say, my dear—it’s always one thing after another. In ten or fifteen years, I suppose I’ll have the pleasant task of finding a new doctor. Better to make the change before it becomes necessary.”

He picked up his fork again, resolute.

But as Mrs. Welland rose to leave, she paused. “Still,” she said, leading the way into the dimly lit back drawing-room, “I don’t see how Ellen is going to get here tomorrow evening. I like to have things settled at least twenty-four hours ahead.”

Archer, still captivated by a small painting of two cardinals feasting in an ebony frame studded with onyx, looked up.

“I’ll go,” he offered. “I can leave the office early enough to catch the brougham at the ferry—if May sends it.”

His heart pounded.

Mrs. Welland breathed a sigh of relief. May, who had drifted to the window, turned and smiled warmly at him.

“So you see, Mamma,” she said, leaning down to kiss her mother’s forehead, “everything *will* be settled twenty-four hours in advance.”

May’s brougham waited at the door. She would drive Archer to Union Square, where he could catch a Broadway car to the office.

As she settled into her seat, she said, “I didn’t want to worry Mamma by raising obstacles—but how can you meet Ellen tomorrow and bring her back, when you’re going to Washington?”

“Oh, I’m not going,” Archer replied.

“Not going? Why, what’s happened?” Her voice was clear, bright, full of concern.

“The case is off—postponed.”

“Postponed? How odd! This morning I saw a note from Mr. Letterblair to Mamma saying he was going to Washington tomorrow for the big patent case he’s arguing before the Supreme Court. You said it was a patent case, didn’t you?”

“Well—that’s it. The whole office can’t go. Letterblair decided to go this morning.”

“Then it’s *not* postponed?” she pressed, her tone sharper than usual.

Archer felt his cheeks flush. He cursed himself for giving details when he’d announced his plans earlier. He remembered reading somewhere that clever liars give details—but the cleverest say nothing at all.

“No,” he said. “But my going is.”

He added, retreating into sarcasm, “I’m not going till later—luckily for your family’s convenience.”

As he spoke, he felt her gaze on him. He turned to meet her eyes—neither avoiding nor challenging. Their glance locked, briefly, and in that moment, something passed between them—something neither wished to name.

“Yes,” May said brightly, “it *is* awfully convenient that you can meet Ellen after all. You saw how much Mamma appreciated your offering.”

“Oh, I’m delighted to do it.”

The carriage stopped. As he stepped out, she leaned forward and placed her hand on his.

“Good-bye, dearest,” she said, her eyes so blue he wondered later if they had shone with tears.

He walked away, crossing Union Square, repeating to himself in a low chant:

“It’s all of two hours from Jersey City to old Catherine’s. Two hours—and it may be more.”
