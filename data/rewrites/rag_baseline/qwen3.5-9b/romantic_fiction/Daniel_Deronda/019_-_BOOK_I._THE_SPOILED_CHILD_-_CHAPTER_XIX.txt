Deronda was not a man defined by the label of romantic; indeed, to pin that description upon him would be a distortion of his essential nature. Yet, beneath his surface of calm composure and self-imposed restraint burned a fervent fire, a capacity to distill poetry and romance from the mundane fabric of daily existence. Such poetic resonance exists everywhere, abundant as ever, save for those phlegmatic souls who, regardless of the era, dismiss it as a delusion born of error. These sentiments thrive alongside the microscope and within the rattling confines of railway carriages; it is only the sterile vacuum of polite society, filled with gentlemen and ladies clad in propriety, that banishes them. How else could the vast apparatus of heaven and earth—from the furthest reaches of the firmament down to the tender, nurturing bosom of a mother—fail to generate poetry for a mind devoid of awe and tenderness, lacking that electric sense of fellowship that vibrates between the near and the distant? For Deronda, the discovery of Mirah struck with the same heart-shattering force as the fates visited upon Orestes or Rinaldo. He remained awake for half the night, reliving the moments since he first glimpsed her on the riverbank, revisiting them with a freshness and vividness unique to emotive memory.

When he attempted to quell this urgent inner vision by opening a book, the printed words dissolved into mere scaffolding. Through this network of ink, he perceived everything with startling clarity—not only the tangible events of the preceding two hours but also the swirling possibilities of what had been and what might be, scenarios fed by the warm, pulsing blood of passionate hope and fear. A specific current within his own experience caused Mirah's desperate search for her mother to seize his imagination with peculiar intensity. His initial impulse was pure sympathy: if living relatives existed in London, methods akin to scientific experimentation could locate them, provided the necessary machinery was engaged. However, the complex emotions rooted in his own kinship anxieties immediately infused his concern for Mirah with a darker hue. The desire to uncover his own mother, or even learn of her, was perpetually shadowed by dread. In projecting this onto Mirah, he realized that locating the mother and brother from whom she had been torn away as an infant could prove to be a calamity rather than a cure. While she spoke in the boat of their goodness, that virtue might have stemmed solely from her ignorant innocence and yearning recollection; the decade and a half since their separation was ample time for corruption and decay.

Despite his natural inclination to side with the victims of prejudice, Deronda's practical interest had never truly extended toward existing Jews. Whether they paraded in fine apparel or lurked in shadowed by-streets, the facts he knew were largely repugnant. He assumed that learned and accomplished Jews had abandoned their faith to merge with the peoples of their adopted lands. While scorn cast specifically at a Jew for individual failings would rouse his deepest sympathies, the indiscriminate hatred of a race often strikes a specimen who has well-earned it, deserving to be gibbeted as a rascal son of Adam. One need look no further than the Caribs, who know little of theology, regarding theft as a practice peculiarly linked to Christian tenets; they likely possess experimental grounds for such an opinion. Deronda could not escape knowing the ugly stories surrounding Jewish characteristics and occupations. Though he protested against the severance of past and present history, he shared the common failure of never caring to draw special conclusions beyond viewing Jews as retaining the virtues and vices of a long-oppressed race. Now, however, Mirah's longing forced a closer scrutiny of details, summoning disagreeable images of what it might mean to discover this middle-aged Jewess and her son. Granted, there was the exquisite refinement and charm of Mirah herself, creating a presumption in favor of her immediate kin—but he must wait. Perhaps through Mrs. Meyrick he could gather guiding hints directly from Mirah's lips. Her voice, her accent, her looks—all the sweet purity that clothed her like a consecrating garment—made him shrink even further from associating her, either ideologically or practically, with anything hateful or contaminating.

Yet these fine words, used to fumigate and obscure unpleasant realities, are not the language of thought. Deronda's mind raced through rapid images of potential futures: he saw himself guided by an official scout into a dingy alleyway, entering a dim doorway where a hawk-eyed, rough-haired, unwashed woman bargained down a hungry girl's last scrap of finery. Or, in a quarter rendered more hideous by its smartness, he found himself breathing the air of a young Jew, talkative and familiar, eager to display his acquaintance with gentlemanly tastes and unfastidious in any transaction they favored. This was the brief chapter of his experience in this realm. Do excuse him; his mind was not prone to spontaneously conjure insulting ideas or to practice a wit that equates Moses with an advertisement sheet. But he was currently governed by dread. If Mirah's parents had been Christian, the sole difference would be that his forebodings would have been fed by wider knowledge. It was the habit of his mind to link dread with unknown parentage, and in this instance, as in his own, the connection was entirely reasonable.

But what was to be done with Mirah? She required shelter and protection in the fullest sense. All his chivalrous sentiment rallied to insist that the sooner and more fully he could secure the interest of others beside himself, the better he fulfilled her claims upon him. He possessed no right to provide for her entirely, though he might be capable of doing so; the sheer depth of the impression she had made compelled him to believe she understood herself to be entirely independent of him. Vague visions of the future, which he tried to dispel as fantastical, left behind an anxiety stronger than any rational motive he could articulate: that those observing his actions closely should be acquainted from the outset with the full history of his relation to Mirah. He had learned to hate secrecy regarding the grand ties and obligations of his life, hating it all the more because a powerful spell of interwoven sensibilities hindered him from breaking such silences. Deronda had vowed to himself that, since the truths which disgrace mortals are not always of their own making, he would ensure the truth never became a disgrace to another through his own act. He lived in constant terror of breaking this vow, falling into the apologetic philosophy that explains the world away as containing nothing better than one's own conduct.

At one moment, he resolved to recount the entire adventure to Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger the following morning at breakfast. Then, the possibility that something entirely new might reveal itself during his next visit to Mrs. Meyrick checked this impulse. Finally, he succumbed to sleep, concluding that he would wait until that visit had been made.
