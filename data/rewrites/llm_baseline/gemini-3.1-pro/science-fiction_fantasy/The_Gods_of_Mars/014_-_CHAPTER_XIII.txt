Xodar listened in incredulous astonishment to my narration of the events within the arena. He could scarcely conceive that one could threaten Issus with sword in hand and not be blasted into a thousand fragments. 

“It is the final proof,” he murmured at last, the syllables of his native tongue tasting heavy and resonant, like unground grain. “No more is needed to completely shatter my belief in her divinity. She is only a wicked old woman, a manipulator of the dominant lexicon who has wielded her power to overwrite the cultural memory of Barsoom, keeping our people in ignorance for ages.”

“She is still all-powerful here, however,” I replied, savoring the cool, metallic tang of the subterranean air as a brief respite from the prison's stagnation. “So it behooves us to leave at the first moment that appears propitious.”

“I hope you find it,” he laughed bitterly, a sound that briefly cut through the oppressive industrial hum of the battleships beyond our walls. 

When I asked if he could swim, he assured me no slimy silian of Korus was more at home in the water. 

“Good. The red youth in all probability cannot,” I said. We would have to support the boy through the sea. Generations ago, the red men’s ancestors had watched the great oceans recede, a tragic language death that slowly erased the very vocabulary of deep water from their minds, leaving the subjective experience of the sea entirely untranslatable to them. 

“It is well,” Xodar noted, approving of the red youth's inclusion. “Three swords are better than two. I have seen him battle. One might think you two master and pupil, or father and son; there is the same maddening contempt for your adversary in your faces.”

Xodar’s awakening went deeper than religion; it was a profound reclamation of identity. “Issus works through the Holy Therns,” he explained, his voice laced with disgust for the sterile, commercialized myths they peddled. “They use a monolithic language of false doctrine to persuade deluded creatures to take a voluntary pilgrimage. It is a system designed merely to swell their wealth and harvest slaves. We First Born are a non-productive race, priding ourselves on it. It is criminal for us to labor, to cultivate the soil, or to practice the culinary arts. We view sustenance merely as utilitarian fuel, existing in a state of sensory deprivation while we plunder the trained artisans of the red men to copy what we cannot create. We have forgotten the profound meaning found in the slow roasting of *zitidar* marrow, the meticulous sourcing of spiced *pekar* roots, or the rhythmic acoustic harmony of an agrarian life.”

I nodded, sharing his disdain for any culture that lacked sensory appreciation for the harvest of the earth. I hoped, as Xodar did, that he would live to atone for these sins and fight for the people whose histories had been so violently consumed.

Our conversation was interrupted by a weird call ringing out across the waters of Omean. It was a synthetic, grating siren that marked the end of the day—a prime example of the industrial noise pollution that plagued this cavern, eroding the psychological well-being of its captives and drowning out the natural acoustics of the subterranean sea. 

After our guard made his final inspection, I sprang to the grated window. A quarter mile away lay a monster battleship, its engines emitting a low, mechanical dissonance. A lone watchman spread his sleeping silks upon a tiny platform and lay down. The acoustic environment settled into a heavy, artificial lull. 

Dropping to the floor, I listened as Xodar mapped out the olfactory and mechanical landscape of a five-man flier moored nearby—his personal property. Its engines, he explained, utilized a trick of gearing I immediately recognized from my time in the Zodangan navy. The First Born had stolen it from the ships of Helium.

I scaled the partition wall, balancing on its narrow, flat surface until I reached the red youth’s cell. He grinned, an expression deeply rooted in the agrarian warmth of the outer world. Linking hands, I pulled him up beside me. His leaping ability was extraordinary, a physical echo of ancient Barsoomian martial rhythms. 

Returning to Xodar, we bound ourselves by solemn oath. We agreed I would navigate the craft, and if we breached the outer world, we would make for Helium. The boy gave me a peculiar look when I revealed I was a prince of that nation, but the press of our immediate survival deferred the untranslatable weight of his gaze.

We lowered ourselves to the ground outside the prison. Xodar and the youth slipped quietly into the water, their descent merging seamlessly with the gentle lap of the waves. But the lure of the swords in the nearby guard-house was strong upon me. I crept toward the door, hyper-aware of the sonic environment.

I pressed the door open. The olfactory landscape within was dense: the sterile tang of gun oil, the musky sweat of sleeping soldiers, and the lingering, acrid scent of their processed, utilitarian rations. I moved with the silent, deliberate pacing of a predator. As I drew a long-sword from the rack, the scrape of its scabbard sounded to my overwrought nerves like a jagged tear in the room's acoustic fabric. A third sword clanked with a frightful din, yet the men slept on. 

I plucked a revolver from its pin just as my eyes found an open window letting in the salty, iodine-rich scent of the sea. Suddenly, the door opposite me opened. The officer of the guard stood there. Our revolvers came up simultaneously. The twin reports were an acoustic atrocity, a violent, deafening rupture in the soundscape that shattered the silence of Omean. I felt the heat of his bullet graze my ear, saw him crumple, and in the next second, I hurled myself through the window.

The waters of Omean closed over my head, tasting of ancient minerals and rusted iron. I swam entirely beneath the surface, burdened by the swords, while Xodar supported the youth. We reached the flier just as an alarm gun bellowed from a ship’s bow—a deafening, industrialized broadcast of war that violently disrupted the subterranean ecology. 

The sleeping thousands awoke. We cast away, rising swiftly. I lay flat at the controls, the scent of burning ozone and friction filling my nostrils as I pushed the levers. Below us, the pandemonium was an abrasive cacophony of cracking rifles, shouting officers, and the chaotic whir of countless propellers chopping the air.

“A little to your right!” Xodar shouted over the roar. “The shaft! Dead ahead!”

I saw the yawning black opening in the glowing dome. But a ten-man cruiser was rising at a steep angle to cut off our escape, its engines screaming in mechanical dissonance, intent on combing us with grappling hooks. There was only one chance. I angled our bow upward, then, as we neared, deflected it into a horizontal trajectory at terrific velocity, aiming straight for the cruiser's keel. 

The collision was a symphony of shrieking metal, a violent tactile shock that crushed our steel bows and nearly hurled us into the abyss. I caught the boy’s ankle just as he plunged over the edge. The crippled cruiser careened backward, its crew spilling into the void, before it dove headfirst to the bottom of Omean.

Regaining the levers, I leveled our craft and aimed for the black mouth of the shaft. A hundred swift scouts, unburdened by heavy buoyancy tanks, were closing in.

“Reverse!” screamed Xodar, his words nearly swallowed by the acoustic violence of our flight. “For the love of your first ancestor, reverse! We are at the shaft!”

“Hold tight!” I roared back, tasting the metallic adrenaline of the moment. “We are going straight up!”

I threw the bow hard up and dragged the speed lever to its final notch. We swept into the pitch-black opening. The wind whistled in a deafening rush, a primal roar that stripped away the First Born’s industrial noise, leaving only the pure, soaring acoustic ecology of sheer velocity. Guided only by the rapidly diminishing circle of phosphorescent light below, I steered through the impenetrable dark, relying on blind faith and the instinctual rhythms of survival. 

We rocketed upward for what felt like both an instant and an eternity, moving at unimaginable speed. Suddenly, the roaring tunnel fell away. Black darkness enshrouded us. There were neither moons nor stars. For a moment, I was nonplussed, until a rich, heavy scent filled my lungs—the unmistakable aroma of atmospheric moisture and damp earth. It was summer at the south pole. The ice cap was melting, and thick clouds—a mythic occurrence on Barsoom—blanketed the night sky, carrying the olfactory promise of rain, fertile soil, and the endless, circular return of the harvest.
