Clement sighed, a sound heavy with the weight of his own uncertainty. He began to wonder if he had chosen the wisest path when dealing with a spirit so fiercely passionate. Yet, even in his youth, he had absorbed many lessons of ecclesiastical prudence. Above all, he had been taught the virtue of pausing—in moments of difficulty—to neither act nor speak until the fog of emotion cleared enough to reveal truth. Thus, he chose silence and offered up a prayer for wisdom. His only physical action was to gently withdraw his foot.

But the penitent reacted instantly. With a piteous cry, she threw her arms around it, clutching it convulsively, weeping openly over the leather. In that moment, the raw agony of shame mingled with profound penitence, manifesting as a bright crimson flush that climbed her neck as she trembled beneath him.

"My daughter," Clement said softly, "take courage. Do not torment yourself further regarding this Gerard, who does not exist. As for me, I am Brother Clement, whom Heaven has sent to you today to offer comfort and aid in saving your soul. Since you have named me your confessor, I now claim your obedience."

"Oh, yes," she sobbed. "Leave this pilgrimage and return immediately to Rome. True penance cannot be found abroad; its value is little there. Where we dwell lie the specific temptations we must conquer, or else perish. It is easy to wash the feet of strangers, masking our true selves, but hard to remain merely meek and charitable toward those closest to us."

"I swear, I will never touch her again!" she declared.

"Nay," Clement corrected gently. "I speak not only of servants, but of dependents, kin, and friends. Let this be your penance: at the last hour of the night and the first after Matins, recall your sin and God's boundless goodness. Be humble and gentle toward the faults of those around you. The world courts the rich; seek instead the poor—not the beggar, for they are often neither honest nor truly destitute—but find those who blush to ask for help yet desperately need it. To give to them is to lend to Heaven. Furthermore, marry a good son of the Church."

"Me?" she recoiled. "I will never marry."

"You will marry within the year," Clement insisted firmly. "I entreat and command you to wed one who fears God. For you are very clay; mated poorly, you will become nothing. But united with a worthy husband, by God's grace, you may live as a pious princess and die a saint."

"You."

He then urged her to rise and begin the good work he had assigned. She rose to her knees, removed her mask, and cast an eloquent, searching look upon him before lowering her eyes in meek submission.

"I will obey you as I would an angel," she whispered. "How happy I am, yet how unhappy! Oh, my heart tells me I shall never look upon you again. I will not leave until I have dried your feet."

"It is unnecessary," Clement replied. "I have excused you from this bootless penance."

"'Tis no penance to me," she insisted. "Ah! You do not forgive me if you will not let me dry your poor feet."

"So be it then," Clement said, resigning himself to her plea. *Levius quid foemina,* he thought internally—*how easily women yield.* Yet, these fragile creatures, which gravitate toward the small just as celestial bodies turn to the great, possess flashes of angelic intelligence. After the princess had dried the friar's feet, she gazed at him through tear-filled, beautiful eyes and murmured with singular tenderness, "May I have masses said for her soul?" she added timidly.

A faint blush colored the monk's cheek, and moisture gathered in his cold blue eyes. It was a sudden reaction from one he had just rated so lowly.

"It is a gracious thought," he admitted. "Do as you wish; often such acts return to the doer like blessed dew. Remember, I am your confessor, not hers; it is your soul I must strive to save, or woe betide my own. My daughter, my dear daughter, I see good and evil angels battling for your soul this very day, right now. Fight on your own side. Do you remember everything I commanded?"

"Remember!" the princess affirmed. "Sweet saint, every syllable of yours is engraved in my heart."

"But one word more, then. Pray much to Christ, and little to His saints."

"I will."

"And that is the finest counsel I can offer you. Now, we part on this note. You go to the place that suits you best—your father's house—and I return to my holy mother's work."

"Adieu," the princess faltered.

"Adieu, thou whom I have loved too well, hated too ill, known and revered too late; forgiving angel, adieu—for ever."

The monk caught her words, though they broke forth only in a sigh. "For ever?" he cried aloud, seized by sudden ardor. "Christians live 'forever,' and love 'forever,' but they never part 'forever.' They separate, as the earth and sun do, only to meet more brightly in a little while. You and I part here for this earthly life. But what is our life? It is but one line in the grand story of the Church, whose son and daughter we are; one handful in the sands of time, one drop in the ocean of 'Forever.' Adieu—for the brief moment called 'a life!' We part in trouble, but we shall meet in peace; we part as creatures of clay, but we shall meet as immortal spirits; we part in a world of sin and sorrow, but we shall meet where all is purity and divine love, where no ill passions exist, but only Christ and His saints arrayed in white. There, in the turning of an hourglass, in the breaking of a bubble, in the passing of a cloud, she, thou, and I shall meet again. We shall sit at the feet of angels, archangels, apostles, and saints, beaming with joy unspeakable in the light of the shadow of God upon His throne, FOREVER—AND EVER—AND EVER."

Thus they parted. The monk stood erect, his eyes turned heavenward and glowing with the sacred fire of zeal; the princess withdrew slowly, turning more than once to cast a lingering glance of awe and tender regret upon that inspired figure. She returned home subdued and purified. Clement, in due course, reached Basel and took up his duties, teaching in the university and preaching throughout the town and its neighborhood. He led a life that could be summed in two words: deep study and mortification. My readers have already glimpsed his soul. In Basel, he advanced in both holy zeal and knowledge, and the brethren of his order began to see in him a true descendant of the saints and martyrs.
