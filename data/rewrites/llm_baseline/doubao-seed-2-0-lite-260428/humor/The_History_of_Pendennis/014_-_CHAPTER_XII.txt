We’ve mentioned Mr. Garbetts before, of course: the theatre’s top leading tragedian, strapping as a prize fighter, famously jovial, and just famously unreliable with a bill. He and Captain Costigan were thick as thieves—joint stars of the Magpie Hotel’s after-show drinking club, who’d co-signed so many of each other’s dodgy IOUs that neither could remember who actually owed who money anymore. They were fast friends, full stop, even if Garbetts almost never popped round Costigan’s place: Milly Fotheringay had turned down his marriage proposal flat years before he married his own wife, and Mrs. Garbetts was still jealous enough of Milly to stare a hole right through her stage costume at every performance.

None of that backstory matters here, though—what counts is that the second Milly and faithful old Bows the fiddler stepped out the house, Costigan sent his tiny errand boy Tommy pelting off to fetch Garbetts, with orders to grab two extra glasses of hot whisky-and-water from the Grapes pub on his way back. Ten minutes later, the tragedian (famous for breaking pokers clean over his bicep for a laugh mid-soliloquy) and the booze turned up at the door.

Costigan didn’t spill every single detail of the Major’s visit that the reader already knows—he saved the best bits for when the spirits had loosened both their tongues, and together they drafted a very dramatic, very threatening letter addressed to Major Pendennis. It demanded the Major stop meddling in the proposed match between Arthur Pendennis and Milly, name an early wedding date, or else give Costigan the “satisfaction due to any gentleman of honour.” If the Major dared refuse? Costigan threatened to horsewhip him within an inch of his life on the main high street of Chatteris for all the county to gawk at. The letter was sealed with the Costigan family’s only remaining piece of heirloom silver: a dented crested seal that had seen so many drunks stamp it on wax it now looked more like a squashed duck than a proper noble coat of arms.

Garbetts marched off as official second, Costigan squeezing his hand and calling down blessings on his head before he shut the door. Then the Captain dragged out his ancient flintlock duelling pistols, the same ones he swore had “dispatched no less than three rascally gentlemen over honour disputes in Dublin back in ‘29” (they had in fact never been fired outside of a village fairground shooting gallery). He polished the locks, lined up all of Arthur’s gushy love letters and terrible poetry on the table beside the empty whisky glasses, and settled in to wait for Garbetts to bring back word of the duel time and location.

Twenty minutes later, Garbetts stumbled back in, face as white as a fresh pot of greasepaint.
“Ye saw the old rascal, then?” Costigan barked, already lining one of the pistols up at his own bloodshot eye to test the sightline.
“Seen him? Aye, but I didn’t dare hand over that daft letter,” Garbetts said, aghast. “I ran into that prat Tatham, the Major’s snooty lawyer, loitering on his doorstep. I’m a married family man, Captain! I’m not touching duels, my wife will hide all my stage Hamlet tights if she finds out I was mixed up in a challenge. I want nothing to do with your shooting match.”

Costigan flew into a towering rage, calling Garbetts a coward and quoting lines from the character Bob Acres (the famous duel wimp from *The Rivals*, which Costigan had played on stage and off for decades) until the two men bickered themselves into a foul mood and Garbetts stormed off, challenge letter clutched in his hand and a half-empty glass of whisky he’d forgotten to set down still in the other. The letter was never opened. The proposed shooting match was deader than a pantomime villain.

Milly turned up home soon after with Bows, fresh from rehearsal, already toting a pair of once-white satin shoes she planned to scrub clean with breadcrumbs for her Ophelia performance the following Tuesday. The whole flat reeked of hot whisky, and her father was pacing back and forth so agitated he was almost tripping over his own boot heels.
“Ye’re tipsy again, Papa,” Milly said, shoving him away when he tried to hug her and wail about her broken heart. “Ye promised no spirits before dinner.”

Costigan ranted through slurred sobs about the Pendennis clan being a pack of lying traitors, spilling the news that the Major had turned up to reveal Arthur didn’t have two thousand a year, or two hundred a year, or even twenty spare pounds a year—he was entirely dependent on his 500-a-year mother, who was young enough to remarry tomorrow and leave him completely penniless. He rapped his dented old pistol case for dramatic emphasis, but Bows had heard him yell about “defending family honour” a dozen times before over far smaller slights, so he barely paid the death-box any mind.

Milly didn’t scream, or swoon, or tear up the love letters. She just plucked a chunk of bread out of the cupboard, sat down at the table to scrub the grime off her satin shoes, and said, very matter-of-fact in her soft Irish brogue: “Sure if he’s no money, there’s no use marrying him, then, is there?”
When Costigan spluttered that Arthur had tricked them by riding a fancy horse and buying Milly a silk shawl, Milly blinked and pointed out that Arthur had never once claimed to be rich—*Costigan* was the one who’d convinced himself the boy was a secret millionaire, and spent months hyping up the “Lady Pendennis of Fairoaks Park” fantasy all on his own.

She laid out the entire plan in ten seconds flat, no dramatics, no duels, no public nose-tweaking that Costigan had been swearing he’d do to the Major on the park promenade. Send Arthur a polite, formal letter asking for a full list of his assets and income before they locked in the wedding date. He’d be forced to admit he had nothing. Then write back very politely saying the match simply couldn’t go ahead, wish him well, and send all his silly poems and love letters back. No fuss, no mess, no chance of anyone getting shot.

By the time they sat down to their beefsteak pudding dinner, Costigan had forgotten all about his grand revenge fantasy. The whisky had worn off, he was tucking into extra suet, and he was far more worried about running into Arthur and having to break off the engagement first than he was about duelling the Major. Bows snickered the whole meal through, half-admiring and half-scornful of how easily Milly had popped every last one of Costigan’s over-the-top melodramatic bubbles.

After dinner, Milly sorted through Arthur’s stack of pages: all the gushy late-night love letters, the terrible verses full of Latin she couldn’t even parse, the scribbled notes about running away to live in a cottage by the sea. She carefully tore out the newspaper clipping of Arthur’s published poem praising her performance as Imogen to tuck into her scrapbook of good reviews, tied the rest of the stack up neatly with a piece of old string like it was a pound of sugar from the grocers, then put the kettle on to make tea. She didn’t shed a single tear, didn’t sigh over the wasted romance, didn’t give a second thought to all the lonely, lovesick nights Arthur had spent writing every line of those pages. Ten miles away, Arthur was still hugging her memory to his chest and daydreaming of their grand future. Back at the Costigan flat, Milly passed around a bowl of filberts, and asked Bows if he’d stay for a second cup.
