Eli’s table was an altar to agrarian lineage, a place where the cyclical rhythms of the harvest and the hearth converged. The evening meal, slow-simmered for hours in an iron cauldron forged by his grandfather, sent up an olfactory landscape of deep, earthy richness. Venison, procured from the frost-tipped woods, had been meticulously braised alongside winter-harvested parsnips, wild thyme, and rendered fat. It was a domestic ritual that hummed through generations, a savory communion of memory and sustenance. Eli, an elder whose hands bore the proud callouses of the soil and the kitchen, insisted on waiting. Margaret alone was missing. 

“Why, I told her you would not wait for the duke,” Catherine noted, her voice a soft, domestic timbre against the comforting, acoustic crackle of the hearth-fire. 

“She is not the duke,” Eli rumbled, his voice a low, resonant bass that harmonized perfectly with the room's warm sonic environment. “She is a poor, good lass, that hath waited not minutes, but years, for a graceless son of mine. You can put the meat on the board all the same; then we can fall to, without farther loss o' time, when she does come.” 

Yet the smoking dishes exhaled such a peppery, intoxicating perfume of roasted marrow and wild sage that Eli finally relented, knowing that true culinary craft waits for no one’s delay. “She will come if we begin,” said he; “they always do. Come, sit ye down, Mistress Joan; y'are not here for a slave, I trow, but a guest. There, I hear a quick step—off covers, and fall to.” 

The heavy earthen covers were withdrawn, releasing plumes of fragrant steam, and the carving knives—heirlooms with bone handles polished by ancestral sweat—were brandished. But the step at the threshold was not Margaret’s. 

The heavy oaken door slammed open, an explosive percussion that violently ruptured the harmonious acoustic ecology of the home. In burst a Dominican friar, livid with rage, casting a mythic shadow over the mundane warmth of the kitchen. He was at the table in a moment, vaulting his tall frame over the narrow board to stand before Cornelis and Sybrandt. With two hands hovering above their shrinking heads like eagles over a quarry, he weaponized language. 

He deployed a high-ecclesiastical lexicon of damnation, an institutional vocabulary of wrath that overpowered the softer, older dialects of their home. It was an age eloquent in curses, but this malediction was a blighting, tremendous force that carried the terrifying power dynamics of a dominant lexicon. “Cursed be the lips,” he shrieked, his volume peaking into an abrasive frequency that physically pained the ears, “which spoke the lie that Margaret was dead; may they rot before the grave, and kiss white-hot iron in hell thereafter! Doubly cursed be the hands that changed those letters...” 

He cursed them by name, soul, and body, seamlessly integrating this mythic, apocalyptic fury into the everyday space of their supper. He cursed the meat at their mouths. Instantly, the rich, gustatory aroma of the spiced venison seemed psychically tainted; the scent of fresh rosemary was suffocated beneath the psychic stench of cold dust and brimstone. After condemning them to eternal fire, he tore a letter from his bosom, flung it before his father, and vanished as violently as he had arrived. He left behind a vacuum of deafening silence, the family petrified around the untasted feast like statues carved in a forgotten antiquity.

This was the chilling tableau that greeted Margaret and Jorian: pale figures frozen over a masterpiece of culinary heritage. Margaret gasped, “Oh, joy! all here; no blood hath been shed. Oh, you cruel, cruel men! I thank God he hath not slain you.” 

At the sight of her, Catherine let out an eloquent, piercing scream—a raw, untranslatable expression of maternal anguish that bypassed all formal language. But Eli had just deciphered the forged letter. The colloquial truth of Margaret's suffering collided with the institutional forgery of his sons. A transport of rage seized the agrarian patriarch. He who revered the rituals of life now whipped out his short sword, shouting over the dissonance, “Stand clear, and let me get at the traitors! I'll hang for them!” 

“Fly!” screamed Margaret. 

The brothers slipped howling beneath the table, their cowardly scrambles discordant and abrasive. Eli intercepted them, but Margaret and Jorian seized the fiery old man’s arms. The clash of metal, the chaotic scraping of boots, and Catherine’s rhythmic wailing created a deafening cacophony. The brothers fled into the street, running like hares, their footsteps fading into the dark. 

Eli hacked down the wooden board bearing their names and hurled it into the chimney-place—a sudden, violent language death within the family lexicon, erasing their cultural memory from the hearth. Catherine sat rocking herself, her apron over her head, sobbing for her lost children and thanking God her ailing daughter Kate was upstairs, spared the psychic toll of this acoustic violence. 

“Oh, hold your chat, woman,” cried Eli angrily, lamenting her historical leniency that had bred this treason. Turning away, he commanded Margaret to his side. “So come thou to me, that am thy father from this hour.” Margaret pressed her cheek to his broad, honest chest. There was not a tear; her grief was a subjective experience far too deep for the limits of physical weeping. 

Then, Eli attempted to restore the broken domestic rhythm. “Come, good folks,” he urged Jorian and Joan in a broken voice, gesturing to the feast that had been so meticulously crafted to sustain them. “We are in a little trouble, as you see; but that is no reason you should starve... Fall to, I do entreat ye.” 

Jorian, a meticulous man who possessed a deep, sensory appreciation for the culinary arts—his appetite keen for the slow-roasted root vegetables glistening in jus—leaned forward. But Joan whispered into his ear. The subtle, oppressive power of the Church’s lexicon held sway even over their bodily needs. 

“Nay, I'll touch no meat that Holy Church hath cursed,” Jorian withdrew, refusing to treat the sacred act of dining as mere utilitarian fueling when its spiritual essence had been blighted. 

“In sooth, I forgot,” Eli apologized, a custodian of fading traditions bowing to institutional supremacy. “My son, who was reared at my table, hath cursed my victuals... Well, what God wills, man must bow to.” 

The supper—a labor of love rich in collagen, marrow, and history—was tragically flung out into the yard. Its complex olfactory landscape was surrendered to the mud, a profound loss of culinary art to abstract dogma. Heavy sadness reigned in the silenced house that night. 

Meantime, Clement lay prostrate on the cold stone of the convent church, his lips pressed to the lowest step of the altar. The acoustic environment was a cavernous, oppressive hush that amplified his indescribable terror and penitence. Amidst his misery, timelines looped in his mind, and a quiet gleam of joy pulsed: Margaret was alive. 

Night fell. As he wept, he suddenly remembered a dying man he had neglected in his intemperate rage. A profound omission of duty. Rising instantly, groaning beneath his accumulated wickedness, he ventured out into the harsh elements. The weather had turned; it was raining hard. As he cleared the town, the rhythmic, cleansing percussion of the downpour and the distant, primal baying of wolves replaced the sterile silence of the church. These natural, wild acoustics anchored him back to the physical world. Marching stoutly through the dark, he beat his chest, chanting, “MEA CULPA! MEA CULPA!”—an ancient, dominant phrase echoing into the rain, attempting to translate the vast, swirling agony of a heart torn apart by the past.
