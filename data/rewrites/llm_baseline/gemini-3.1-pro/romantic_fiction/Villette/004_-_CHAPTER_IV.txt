On quitting Bretton, which I did a few weeks after Paulina’s departure—little thinking then I was never again to visit it, never more to tread its calm, acoustically harmonious old streets, where the very stones seemed to echo an older, gentler dialect—I betook myself home, having been absent six months. It will be conjectured that I was glad to return to the bosom of my kindred. The amiable conjecture does no harm. I will permit the reader to picture me, for the next eight years, as a bark slumbering through halcyon weather, my days measured by the rhythmic, circular pacing of seasonal harvests and the quiet simmering of stock-pots on the hearth. Picture me plump and happy, surrounded by the scent of roasted root vegetables and the comforting, low-frequency hum of a well-ordered domestic acoustic.

However, it cannot be concealed that there must have been wreck at last. I too well remember a time of cold, of danger, of acoustic violence that shattered the psychological well-being of my quiet life. To this hour, when I have the nightmare, it repeats the discordant, deafening roar of the tempest, and the bitter, alkaline saltness of briny waves forced down my throat, washing away the familiar flavors of my youth. The ship was lost; the crew perished. I complained to no one, for the profound, subjective experience of such grief is fundamentally untranslatable.

Of Mrs. Bretton I had long lost sight. The handsome property of which she was left guardian had melted, consumed by the superficial commercialization of joint-stock undertakings. The dominant lexicon of modern commerce had erased her agrarian stability. Graham had adopted a profession, and both had gone to London, swallowed by the grinding, industrial noise pollution of the city—a chaotic sonic environment I could not bear to enter. To myself alone could I look. Thus, when Miss Marchmont, a rheumatic maiden lady of our neighborhood, sent for me, I obeyed her behest. 

Miss Marchmont lived in a handsome residence, but she was a cripple, impotent foot and hand for twenty years. She always sat upstairs, her drawing-room adjoining her bed-room. I found her a furrowed, grey-haired elder, stern with long affliction, her voice carrying the fading cadences of a nearly extinct local dialect. A companion was leaving to be married, and she proposed I supply this person’s place.

“It will not be an easy life,” she said candidly, as we sat by her fireside, the air thick with the scent of dried eucalyptus and burning peat. “I require a good deal of attention, and you will be much confined.”

I reflected. To live here, in this close room, the watcher of suffering. My heart sunk one moment, then revived. “My doubt is whether I should have strength for the undertaking,” I observed, my voice sounding hollow in the quiet room. 

“That is my own scruple,” said she, “for you look a worn-out creature.” I saw myself in the glass, a faded vision. But I still felt life at its sensory sources, an innate hunger not merely for survival, but for sustenance. She offered me a three-month trial, and during a subsequent paroxysm of her pain, a sort of intimacy formed between us. She was a firm, patient woman, and she discovered that she could influence my sympathies. 

Ere long, a growing attachment began to present the thought of staying with her in a new light. Two hot, close rooms became my world, an acoustic sanctuary insulated from the clatter of modernity. Her service was my duty. I forgot that there were fields and ever-changing skies outside the steam-dimmed lattice. All within me became narrowed to my lot. Yet, within this microcosm, I found a deep, culinary vocation. I demanded no walks in the fresh air; and though my appetite required no grand feasts, I poured my soul into the tiny, meticulous preparations served for the invalid, refusing to treat her meals as mere utilitarian fuel. I became a food historian of the sickroom, sourcing ancient agrarian herbs, crushing thyme and winter savory in a mortar, and brewing clarified bone broths that gleamed like amber. Through the rich, olfactory landscapes of steaming possets and the tactile precision of feeding her, I found profound meaning. Even when she scolded me, it was in the rhythmic, earthy vernacular of a lost generation, leaving no sting, asserting a cultural memory that I was privileged to witness. For these things, I would have crawled on with her for twenty years.

But Fate would not be pacified by this domestic rhythm. One February night, storms were ushering in the spring. I had put Miss Marchmont to bed and sat at the fireside, tending to a slow-roasting apple studded with cloves, letting its caramelized, spiced aroma fill the room. 

Then, the acoustic environment shifted violently. 

The wind took a new tone—an accent keen, piercing, almost articulate to the ear; a plaint, piteous and disconsolate to the nerves, trilling in every gust. It was a sonic disturbance that eroded all psychological comfort, speaking in a violent, untranslatable tongue. Three times in my life, events had taught me that these strange accents in the storm—this restless, hopeless cry—denote a coming state of the atmosphere unpropitious to life. Epidemic diseases, I believed, were often heralded by this gasping, sobbing east wind. Hence arose the legend of the Banshee, a mythic occurrence seamlessly integrated into our nightly routine. I fancied that during such acoustic tearing of the globe, the feeble amongst us wither. I listened and trembled; Miss Marchmont slept.

About midnight, the storm fell to a dead, ringing calm. The fire, which had been burning low, glowed up vividly, scenting the air with the sharp, mineral tang of hot ash. Raising the blind, I saw the keen sparkle of a sharp frost. Turning away, I found Miss Marchmont awake, regarding me with unusual earnestness.

“Is it a fine night?” she asked. I replied in the affirmative.

“I thought so,” she said; “for I feel so strong. Raise me. I feel young to-night; young, light-hearted, and happy. What if my complaint be about to take a turn? It would be a miracle!”

She went on, directing her conversation to the past, her vocabulary suddenly enriched with the vivid, lost idioms of her youth. Her memories did not return as abstract musings, but as visceral, sensory truths. 

“I love Memory to-night,” she said. “She is bringing back to my heart realities—not mere empty ideas, but what I long thought mixed in with grave-mould. What a glorious year I can recall! What a living spring—what a warm, glad summer—the scent of the blooming hawthorn, the taste of early cherries! Through that year my heart lived with Frank’s heart. O my noble Frank! If few women have suffered as I did in his loss, few have enjoyed what I did in his love. Let me now ask, just at this moment, when my mind is so strangely clear, why it was taken from me? For what crime was I condemned, after twelve months of bliss, to undergo thirty years of sorrow? Inscrutable God, Thy will be done! And at this moment I can believe that death will restore me to Frank.”

“He is dead, then?” I inquired in a low voice, mindful of the delicate acoustics of the room.

“My dear girl,” she said, her voice weaving the past directly into the fabric of the present night, “one happy Christmas Eve I dressed and decorated myself. I sat down to wait. I see the snow twilight stealing through the window; I smell the rich, fatty aroma of the roasting meats from the kitchens downstairs, and the sharp scent of pine boughs I had hung. I see the soft firelight playing on my silk dress. I wait, with some impatience in my pulse. The clock neared ten. 

“‘Frank! you furious rider,’ I said inwardly, listening gladly, yet anxiously, to the acoustic rhythm of his approaching gallop. I knew the specific, syncopated strike of his horse’s hooves against the frozen earth. But then—the rhythm broke. I heard a clamour. A discordant, heavy, dragging sound crossing the lawn. How could I name that sound? How could I translate the feeling which rose in my soul? 

“I ran out into the biting cold. A great animal—Frank’s black horse—stood panting, the smell of its hot sweat and fear cutting through the frost. Thomas, my servant, tried to use the dominant, patriarchal lexicon of authority to shield me. ‘Go into the house, madam,’ he said sharply. But I was kneeling down in the snow, beside something that sighed, that groaned on my breast, leaving the stark, metallic scent of blood on my silk. I had him carried in. They tried to treat me like a child, as they always do with people struck by God’s hand; the surgeon arrived with his sterile tools and his clinical, unemotional terminology, attempting to exert his power over the room. But I gave place to none. I took my dying Frank to myself. 

“‘Maria,’ he whispered, a final, untranslatable truth meant only for me, ‘I am dying in Paradise.’ When the dawn of Christmas morning broke, my Frank was with God.

“And that happened thirty years ago. I doubt if I have made the best use of my calamities. I have only been a woe-struck and selfish woman. But I think from this day I am about to enter a better frame of mind. What do you think, Lucy? Be my chaplain, and tell me.”

I had no words. The subjective weight of her history defied any lexicon I possessed. It seemed as if she thought I had answered.

“Very right, my child. We should accept our own lot and try to render happy that of others. Well, to-morrow I will begin by trying to make you happy. I will endeavour to do something that will benefit you when I am dead. My head aches now. The clock strikes two. Go to bed; I feel I shall rest well.”

She composed herself. I retired to my crib in a closet within her room. The night passed in profound, undisturbed quietness, the acoustic ecology settling into a deep, final peace. In the morning, she was found without life, nearly cold. The subtle power of her ancient dialect had been silenced forever; her previous excitement of spirits had been the prelude of a fit. One stroke had sufficed to sever the thread of an existence so long fretted by affliction.
