Early the next morning Tito came out of Bratti’s shop in the cramped lane of the Ferravecchi with fifty florins hidden close, as if coin, like a spice, kept its virtue best when it was kept from the air. The Genoese stranger had gone off with the onyx ring.

For an instant Tito’s mind—quick in such reckonings as a steward’s—touched the thought that if Fortune, by one of her turnings, should yet save him from quitting Florence, it would have been better not to have parted with the ring, since it had been noticed on his hand and had passed in men’s talk for a relic of tender memory. But the thought slipped away. His first fever of alarm had sunk into a dull, regretful heaviness, like a stomach that has been over-stimulated and now revolts from the very smell of its own indulgence. He had cared too keenly for pleasures that came to him only through other men’s good opinion; and now he almost wished he had never tempted ignominy by shrinking from what other men named obligation.

Yet deeds, once done, are not like dishes left half-tasted that can be pushed aside. They go on, having their own appetite and their own motion; and the dreadful vitality of his last deed pressed on Tito now for the first time, not as a thought but as a weight.

He meant to go back to his lodging in the Piazza di San Giovanni, but he would not take the shortest way through the Mercato Vecchio. He would not see Tessa. Not yet. He was in no humour to seek anything; he could only wait for the first sign of his altered lot, as one waits for the first crack of thunder when the air has turned sultry and the swallows fly low.

The piazza lay washed in that warm morning sunlight under which the autumn dew still lingers on shadowed stone, and the very marble seems to give back heat like a hearth-stone. It was a festival morning—the Nativity of the Virgin—when idleness itself feels almost pious, and men are tempted to lounge as if lounging were a mode of thanksgiving. Round the octagonal baptistery stalls were being spread with fruit and flowers: figs whose skins were breaking with sweetness, bunches of grapes still powdered with bloom, late peaches smelling of sun, and marigolds and basil that gave out a sharp, clean scent when the vendors’ fingers bruised the leaves. Laden mules stood with the solemn patience of beasts that know their hour; their jaws worked steadily in their nose-bags with a sound like soft grinding of grain, while the drivers, perhaps, had gone through the hospitable sacred doors to kneel and let the chant and organ-wind wash the dust of the road out of their ears.

On the wide marble steps of the Duomo lay scattered groups of beggars and talkers. Here an old crone with white hair and a hard, sunburnt face encouraged a round-capped baby to try its bare feet on the warmed stone; a dog sat near and snuffed at the little ceremony with suspicious gravity. There two shaggy-headed boys leaned to watch a pale cripple cutting a face on a cherry-stone, his knife-tip making tiny crisp clicks. Above them men were knotting and unknotting themselves into laughing clusters, or standing in close couples, words and hands moving together, as if speech in Florence were never complete without gesture.

But the busiest knot of all was the one Tito could not avoid: Nello the barber’s awning, red and white, pitched like a little pavilion of respectability. It was the hour when yesterday’s gossip still tasted fresh, like bread scarcely cooled, and Nello’s tongue had its keenest edge when his razor was busiest; the deft glide of steel seemed to set the words going, and the words to keep the steel bright. Men preferred to be shaved out in the air, where neighbours could see that they were men who kept their chins in order. There is nothing sublime in sitting with the chin lathered and thrown back while one’s nose is made a handle of; and yet, when everyone is in the fashion, the fashion itself gives gravity, and the lathered chins look at one another as if on matters of state.

Tito foresaw that he must be drawn in. He must smile, retort, be perfectly at ease—as if the ordeal were no more than swallowing bitter pills wrapped in cheese and bread. A man who let anticipation choke him was only a man with weak nerves.

But at that moment a hand fell on his shoulder.

The sudden touch jarred him like an unexpected sound in a quiet room—like a door flung open when one is listening for footsteps. For an instant, despite all his self-command, his face betrayed the inward shock as he turned.

The owner of the hand laughed lightly. He was about Tito’s age, with keen features, a small close-clipped head, and a lip and chin shaven so closely that the whole face suggested a mind trimmed of every superfluity, ready to cut and turn quickly. His eyes were bright with hope and friendliness—the kind of young brightness that has not yet learned bitterness; for at that time Florence had only pleasant prophecies for Niccolò Macchiavelli, expected to mend the broken fortunes of his ancient family.

“Why, Melema,” he said, “what evil dream did you have last night, that you took my light grasp for that of a sbirro—or something worse?”

“Ah, Messer Niccolò!” Tito answered, recovering in an instant, as if his very fear taught him dexterity. “It must have been an extra amount of dulness in my veins this morning that shuddered at the approach of your wit. But I have had a bad night.”

“That is unlucky,” said Macchiavelli, “because you will be expected to shine without any obstructing fog to-day in the Rucellai Gardens. I take it for granted you are to be there.”

“Messer Bernardo did me the honour to invite me,” said Tito; “but I shall be engaged elsewhere.”

“Ah! I remember—you are in love,” Macchiavelli returned, with a shrug. “Else you would never have such inconvenient engagements. Why, we are to eat a peacock and ortolans under the loggia among Bernardo Rucellai’s rare trees. There are to be the choicest spirits in Florence and the choicest wines. Only, as Piero de’ Medici is to be there, the choice spirits may happen to be swamped in the capping of impromptu verses. I hate that game; it is a device for the triumph of small wits, who are always inspired the most by the smallest occasions.”

“What is that you are saying about Piero de’ Medici and small wits, Messer Niccolò?” cried Nello, whose light figure at that moment predominated over the Herculean frame of Niccolò Caparra.

Caparra, the famous worker in iron—seen last in the Mercato Vecchio with bared muscular arms and leathern apron—was now in holiday suit, and sat submissively while Nello skipped round him, snapped the cloth beneath his chin, lathered him with quick circles, seized him by the nose as if it were indeed a handle, and scraped with magical speed. Caparra looked like a lion that had donned linen and tunic to go into society and had agreed, for once, to let someone else rule the edge of steel.

“A private secretary will never rise in the world if he couples great and small in that way,” said Nello. “When great men are not allowed to marry their sons and daughters as they like, small men must not expect to marry their words as they like. Have you heard the news Domenico Cennini here has been telling us?—that Pagolantonio Soderini has given Ser Piero da Bibbiena a box on the ear for setting on Piero de’ Medici to interfere with the marriage between young Tommaso Soderini and Fiammetta Strozzi, and is to be sent ambassador to Venice as a punishment?”

“I don’t know which I envy him most,” said Macchiavelli, “the offence or the punishment. The offence will make him the most popular man in all Florence; and the punishment will take him among the only people in Italy who have known how to manage their own affairs.”

“Yes, if Soderini stays long enough at Venice,” Cennini answered, “he may chance to learn the Venetian fashion and bring it home with him. The Soderini have been fast friends of the Medici; but what has happened is likely to open Pagolantonio’s eyes to the good of our old Florentine trick of choosing a new harness when the old one galls us—if we have not quite lost the trick in these last fifty years.”

“Not we,” said Niccolò Caparra, rejoicing in the free use of his lips again. “Eat eggs in Lent and the snow will melt. That’s what I say to our people when they get noisy over their cups at San Gallo and talk of raising a romor. I say, never do you plan a romor; you may as well try to fill Arno with buckets. When there’s water enough, Arno will be full—and that will not be till the torrent is ready.”

“Caparra,” said Nello, “that oracular speech is due to my excellent shaving. You could never have made it with that dark rust on your chin. Ecco, Messer Domenico, I am ready for you now. By the way, my bel erudito,” he added, seeing Tito move towards the door, “old Maso has been seeking for you, but your nest was empty. He will come again presently. The old man looked mournful and in haste. I hope there is nothing wrong in the Via de’ Bardi.”

“Doubtless Messer Tito knows that Bardo’s son is dead,” said Cronaca, who had just come up.

Tito’s heart gave a leap—so sharp it seemed to strike his ribs like a hand. Had the death happened before Romola saw him?

“No,” he said, steadying his voice by a habitual art, and turning to lean against the doorpost as if he had abandoned his purpose of going away. “I had not heard it. I knew that his sister had gone to see him. Did he die before she arrived?”

“No,” Cronaca said. “I was in San Marco at the time, and saw her come out from the chapter-house with Fra Girolamo, who told us that the dying man’s breath had been preserved as by a miracle, that he might make a disclosure to his sister.”

In that moment Tito felt his fate fall into place with a click, as when a latch shuts. His mind flew back over all the circumstances of his leaving Florence; and with the swiftness of fear it seized a plan—get back his money from Cennini before the disclosure had grown public. If he once held his money, he need not stay to endure scorching looks and biting words; he could be gone, like smoke driven out by a sudden gust. He would wait, go away with Cennini, and get it at once.

With that project in him he stood motionless, hands in his belt, eyes fixed absently on the ground. Nello, glancing at him, felt sure the Greek was troubled only for Romola, and, thinking him a pretty image of self-forgetful sadness, just perceptibly pointed his razor at him with a challenging look at Piero di Cosimo, who had never forgiven his refusal to see any prognostic of character in Tito’s handsome face. Piero, leaning against the other doorpost close by, shrugged; repeated challenges had shifted him from neutrality into a positive inclination to believe ill of the much-praised stranger.

“So you have got your Fra Girolamo back again, Cronaca?” said Nello. “I suppose we shall have him preaching again this next Advent.”

“And not before there is need,” said Cronaca gravely. “We have had the best testimony to his words since the last Quaresima; for even to the wicked wickedness has become a plague, and the ripeness of vice is turning to rottenness in the nostrils even of the vicious. There has not been a change since the Quaresima, either in Rome or at Florence, but has put a new seal on the Frate’s words—that the harvest of sin is ripe, and that God will reap it with a sword.”

“I hope he has had a new vision, however,” said Francesco Cei, sneering. “The old ones are somewhat stale. Can’t your Frate get a poet to help out his imagination for him?”

“He has no lack of poets about him,” Cronaca returned, with quiet contempt, “but they are great poets and not little ones; so they are contented to be taught by him, and no more think the truth stale which God has given him to utter than they think the light of the moon is stale. But perhaps certain high prelates and princes who dislike the Frate’s denunciations might be pleased to hear that, though Giovanni Pico, and Poliziano, and Marsilio Ficino, and most other men of mark in Florence reverence Fra Girolamo, Messer Francesco Cei despises him.”

“Poliziano?” Cei laughed scornfully. “Yes—doubtless he believes in your new Jonah; witness the fine orations he wrote for the envoys of Sienna, to tell Alexander the Sixth that the world and the Church were never so well off as since he became Pope.”

“Nay, Francesco,” said Macchiavelli, smiling, “a various scholar must have various opinions. And as for the Frate—whatever we may think of his saintliness—you judge his preaching too narrowly. The secret of oratory lies, not in saying new things, but in saying things with a certain power that moves the hearers—without which, as old Filelfo has said, your speaker deserves to be called, ‘non oratorem, sed aratorem.’ And, according to that test, Fra Girolamo is a great orator.”

“That is true, Niccolò,” said Cennini from the shaving-chair; “but part of the secret lies in the prophetic visions. Our people—no offence to you, Cronaca—will run after anything in the shape of a prophet, especially if he prophesies terrors and tribulations.”

“Rather say, Cennini,” Cronaca answered, “that the chief secret lies in the Frate’s pure life and strong faith, which stamp him as a messenger of God.”

“I admit it—I admit it,” said Cennini, opening his palms as he rose. “His life is spotless: no man has impeached it.”

“He is satisfied with the pleasant lust of arrogance,” Cei burst out bitterly. “I can see it in that proud lip and satisfied eye of his. He hears the air filled with his own name—Fra Girolamo Savonarola, of Ferrara—the prophet, the saint, the mighty preacher, who frightens the very babies of Florence into laying down their wicked baubles.”

“Come, come, Francesco, you are out of humour with waiting,” said Nello, conciliatory. “Let me stop your mouth with a little lather. I must not have my friend Cronaca made angry: I have a regard for his chin; and his chin is in no respect altered since he became a Piagnone. And for my own part, I confess, when the Frate was preaching in the Duomo last Advent, I got into such a trick of slipping in to listen to him that I might have turned Piagnone too, if I had not been hindered by the liberal nature of my art—and also by the length of the sermons, which are sometimes a good while before they get to the moving point. But as Messer Niccolò says, the Frate lays hold of the people by some power over and above his prophetic visions. Monks and nuns who prophesy are not of that rareness. For what says Luigi Pulci? ‘Dombruno’s sharp-cutting scimitar had the fame of being enchanted; but I am rather of opinion it cut sharp because it was of strongly-tempered steel.’ Yes, yes; Paternosters may shave clean, but they must be said over a good razor.”

“See, Nello!” said Macchiavelli. “What doctor is this advancing on his Bucephalus? I thought your piazza was free from those furred and scarlet-robed lackeys of death. This man looks as if he had had some such night adventure as Boccaccio’s Maestro Simone, and had his bonnet and mantle pickled a little in the gutter—though he himself is as sleek as a miller’s rat.”

Nello lifted his eyes and drew out a low, long sound, half amusement, half disgust. A round-headed, round-bodied man was approaching on a raw young horse that held its nose out with threatening obstinacy and backed whenever the reins asked it for straightness, as if it had free notions about authority far in advance of the age. The rider’s saddle-bags bulged, swaying with each reluctant step, and the leather looked greasy with use.

“And I have a few more adventures in pickle for him,” Nello murmured, so that only those nearest could hear. “Which I hope will drive his inquiring nostrils to another quarter of the city. He’s a doctor from Padua; they say he has been at Prato for three months, and now he’s come to Florence to see what he can net. But his great trick is making rounds among the contadini. Do you note those great saddle-bags? They are to hold the fat capons and eggs and meal he levies on silly clowns with whom coin is scarce. He vends his own secret medicines, so he keeps away from the doors of the druggists; and for this last week he has taken to sitting in my piazza for two or three hours every day and making it a resort for asthmas and squalling bambini. It stirs my gall to see the toad-faced quack fingering greasy quattrini, or bagging a pigeon in exchange for his pills and powders. But I’ll put a few thorns in his saddle, else I’m no Florentine. Laudamus!—he is coming to be shaved; that’s what I’ve waited for. Messer Domenico, go not away: wait; you shall see a rare bit of fooling, which I devised two days ago. Here, Sandro!”

He whispered in Sandro’s ear. Sandro rolled his solemn eyes, nodded, and, after a slow smile that came like dawn over a hillside, took to his heels with surprising rapidity.

“How is it with you, Maestro Tacco?” said Nello aloud as the doctor, with difficulty, brought the horse’s head round towards the shop. “That is a fine young horse of yours, but something raw in the mouth—eh, Maestro?”

“He is an accursed beast, the vermocane seize him!” Maestro Tacco burst out, descending and fastening an old bridle, mended with string, to the iron staple in the wall. Then, recollecting himself with a merchant’s caution, he added, “Nevertheless—a sound beast and valuable, for one who wanted to purchase and get a profit by training him. I had him cheap.”

“Rather too hard riding for a man who carries your weight of learning, eh, Maestro?” said Nello. “You seem hot.”

“Truly, I am likely to be hot,” said the doctor, taking off his bonnet and showing a bald low head and a flat broad face: high ears, a wide lipless mouth, round eyes, and deep arched lines above projecting brows. If the epithet “toad-faced” suited him, it did so with an injustice to the harmless creature of ponds. “Riding from Peretola when the sun is high is not the same thing as kicking your heels on a bench in the shade like your Florence doctors. Moreover, I have had not a little pulling to get through the carts and mules into the Mercato, to find out the husband of a certain Monna Ghita, who had had a fatal seizure before I was called in; and if it had not been that I had to demand my fees—”

“Monna Ghita!” said Nello. “Peace be with her angry soul! The Mercato will want a whip the more if her tongue is laid to rest.”

At the name, Tito—roused from the rigid stillness of his plan—felt the old vague, half-formed thought about Tessa press forward again, like the scent of a remembered kitchen suddenly rising from a covered pot. If Monna Ghita were taken out of the way, it would be easier for him to see Tessa again—whenever he wanted to see her.

“Gnaffè, Maestro,” Nello went on in a tone of sympathy sweetened with mockery, “you are the slave of rude mortals who, but for you, would die like brutes without help of pill or powder. It is pitiful to see your learned lymph oozing from your pores as if it were mere vulgar moisture. You think my shaving will cool and disencumber you? One moment, and I have done with Messer Francesco here. It seems to me a thousand years till I wait upon a man who carries all the science of Arabia in his head and saddle-bags. Ecco!”

He held up the shaving-cloth with a flourish. Maestro Tacco advanced and seated himself, preoccupied with his heat and self-importance, so deaf to the irony that the words slid off him like water off oiled leather.

“It is but fitting,” said Nello, snapping the cloth in place, “that a great medicus like you should be shaved by the same razor that has shaved the illustrious Antonio Benevieni, the greatest master of the chirurgic art.”

“The chirurgic art!” The doctor’s contempt broke out as if a bitter herb had touched his tongue. “Is it your Florentine fashion to put masters of the science of medicine on a level with men who do carpentry on broken limbs and sew up wounds like tailors, and carve away excrescences as a butcher trims meat? Via! A manual art such as any artificer might learn—and which has been practised by simple barbers like yourself—on a level with the noble science of Hippocrates, Galen, and Avicenna, which penetrates into the occult influences of the stars and plants and gems!—a science locked up from the vulgar!”

“No, in truth, Maestro,” said Nello, working his brush slowly, as if he loved to make the lather rise and swell, white and heavy, and to keep the other man’s mouth busy while his own plot ripened, “I never thought of placing them on a level. I know your science comes next to the miracles of Holy Church for mystery. But there—you see—is the pity of it,”—and here he slid into a tone of regretful concern—“your high science is sealed from the profane and the vulgar, and so you become an object of envy and slander. I grieve to say it, but there are low fellows in this city—mere sgherri—who go about in nightcaps and long beards and make it their business to sprinkle gall in every man’s broth who is prospering. Let me tell you—for you are a stranger—this is a city where every man had need carry a large nail ready to fasten on the wheel of Fortune when his side happens to be uppermost. Already there are stories—mere fables, doubtless—beginning to be buzzed about concerning you, that make me wish I could hear of your being well on your way to Arezzo. I would not have a man of your metal stoned; for though San Stefano was stoned, he was not great in medicine like San Cosmo and San Damiano…”

“What stories? what fables?” stammered Maestro Tacco. Alarm made his breath quick and his voice thick, and the lather under his nostrils gave him the look of a man half-muzzled.

“Lasso! I fear me you are come into the trap for your cheese, Maestro. The fact is, there is a company of evil youths who go prowling about the houses of our citizens carrying sharp tools in their pockets;—no sort of door, or window, or shutter, but they will pierce it. They are possessed with a diabolical patience to watch the doings of people who fancy themselves private. It must be they who have done it—it must be they who have spread the stories about you and your medicines. Have you by chance detected any small aperture in your door, or window-shutter? No? Well, I advise you to look; for it is now commonly talked of that you have been seen in your dwelling at the Canto di Paglia, making your secret specifics by night: pounding dried toads in a mortar, compounding a salve out of mashed worms, and making your pills from the dried livers of rats which you mix with saliva emitted during the utterance of a blasphemous incantation—which indeed these witnesses profess to repeat.”

“It is a pack of lies!” the doctor cried, struggling for utterance, and then stopping in terror as the razor approached his throat.

“It is not to me, or any of this respectable company, that you need to say that, doctor. We are not the heads to plant such carrots as those in. But what of that? What are a handful of reasonable men against a crowd with stones in their hands? There are those among us who think Cecco d’Ascoli was an innocent sage—and we all know how he was burnt alive for being wiser than his fellows. Ah, doctor, it is not by living at Padua that you can learn to know Florentines. My belief is, they would stone the Holy Father himself, if they could find a good excuse for it; and they are persuaded that you are a necromancer, trying to raise the pestilence by selling secret medicines—and I am told your specifics have in truth an evil smell.”

“It is false!” burst out the doctor, as Nello drew the razor away for an instant. “It is false! I will show the pills and the powders to these honourable signori—and the salve—it has an excellent odour—an odour of—of salve.”

He started up with the lather still clinging to his chin and the cloth still knotted round his neck, and began fumbling in his saddle-bag with feverish hands, as if he would drag his own reputation out by force. In the same instant Nello, quick as the flick of flame, shifted the shaving-chair so that it stood close by the horse’s head; and Sandro, returned and waiting as if he had only been part of the air till summoned, placed himself at a sign near the bridle.

“Behold, Messeri!” cried Maestro Tacco, bringing out a small box and flinging it open before them.
