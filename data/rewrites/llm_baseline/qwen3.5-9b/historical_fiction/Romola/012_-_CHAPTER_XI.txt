When Fra Luca finished speaking, Tito remained standing beside him, paralyzed by indecision. Only after the crowd of pilgrims began to thin and the friar rose to walk slowly toward the church of Santa Felicita did Tito follow, turning down the Via de' Bardi.

"If this monk is truly Florentine," he muttered to himself, "and if he intends to stay in Florence, then everything must come out." He sensed a new crisis looming, yet he was not so visibly shaken that he couldn't proceed with his scheduled visit to Bardo to apologize for his recent absence. Tito's skill at concealment was rapidly evolving from a passive trait into something more calculated. While it was certainly possible—even perhaps inevitable—that he should openly accept the changed circumstances and confess Baldassarre's existence, doing so would cast an uncomfortable light backward on his earlier silence. It would appear as a deliberate evasion designed to dodge a recognized obligation, compounded by the fact that he had already quietly settled himself and invested his florins before his benefactor's fate was confirmed.

For now, prudence dictated acting as though nothing had occurred. There was the entire night ahead for reflection, and no one would know the precise hour he had received the letter. Stepping into the room on the second floor—where Romola and her father sat surrounded by parchment and marble, isolated from the street life whether on holy days or ordinary ones—he wore a face only slightly brighter than usual, masking his regret over arriving late. He had sacrificed the spectacle of the Corso specifically to hide this regret, but he quickly masked it again, injecting extra animation into the evening conversation. Throughout the interaction, however, his mind operated like a complex machine, depositing thoughts entirely separate from the flow of dialogue. By the time he descended the stone stairs and emerged from the grim doorway into the starlight, his mind had genuinely advanced to a new stage in forming a purpose.

The following day, once freed from his professorial duties, he turned up the Via del Cocomero toward the convent of San Marco, his plan fully crystallized. He intended to interrogate Fra Luca directly about how much he suspected the truth and on what grounds; furthermore, he needed to know how long the friar planned to remain at San Marco. With that fuller knowledge, he hoped to craft a statement that would allow him to remain in Florence without having to flee.

Tito had never before needed to construct an ingenious falsehood, but the opportunity had finally arrived—a chance that circumstance invariably creates for those living in silent deception. His ingenuity was instantly ready. He had convinced himself that he was under no obligation to hunt down Baldassarre. He had previously stated that upon receiving fair assurance of his father's existence and whereabouts, he would immediately depart to find him. But why, exactly, was he bound to go? If examined closely, what was the ultimate goal of life but to extract the maximum sum of pleasure? Was not his own vibrant, blooming existence a promise of incomparably greater joy—not just for himself, but for others—as compared to the withered, wintry life of a man past the age of keen enjoyment, whose ideas had hardened into barren rigidity? Those rigid ideas had been planted in the fresh soil of Tito's mind; they were lively seeds there. That was the natural order: nature treated maturity merely as a nesting ground for youth. Baldassarre had done his work; he had drawn his fill of life. Now, it was Tito's turn.

The prospect was vague—"I think they are taking me to Antioch"—yet it offered a dazzling vista. After a long voyage, spending months or years searching for a quest that might ultimately prove futile, he would leave behind a life of distinction and love. And if he did find anything, he would return to the old, exacting companionship he already knew by heart. Certainly, the gems—and thus the florins—belonged to Baldassarre in a narrow, legalistic sense, the kind used in ordinary affairs. But in a broader, more radically natural view, where the world belongs to youth and strength, they belonged to whoever could derive the most pleasure from them.

He was acutely aware that this sentiment contradicted societal expectations. The men around him would demand he immediately apply those florins to his benefactor's rescue. But what was the sentiment of society anyway? A mere tangled web of anomalous traditions and opinions, unworthy of a wise man's guidance unless it served his own comfort. Did he care for the florins? Perhaps only for Romola's sake; he would gladly give them up. It was the joy that was rightfully his, hovering close to his lips, that he felt compelled to keep rather than thrust aside to travel onward, parched and empty. Any maxim requiring a man to discard the goods necessary to make existence sweet was merely the lining of human selfishness turned outward, crafted by those who wished others to sacrifice themselves for their benefit.

He preferred that Baldassarre not suffer; he disliked seeing anyone suffer. Yet, could any philosophy prove he was obligated to care for another's suffering more than his own? To believe so, he would need to love Baldassarre devotedly, and he did not. Was that his fault? Gratitude, when scrutinized closely, made no valid claim. His father's life would have been dreary without him; were we truly indebted to men for the pleasures they grant themselves? Once he began dismantling Baldassarre's claim, Tito's thoughts moved with the speed of a virulent acid, eating rapidly through every tissue of sentiment.

His mind lacked the dread often dismissed as mere animal self-preservation—the awe of Divine Nemesis felt by religious pagans, or the Christian equivalent, a vague terror of anything deemed "wrongdoing." Such fear of the unseen transcended simple cowardice; it was the initial recognition of a moral law restraining desire, checking the harsh scrutiny of imperfect thought regarding obligations that possess no sanctity without feeling.

"It is good," sang the old Eumenides in Aeschylus, "that fear should sit as the guardian of the soul, forcing it into wisdom... good that men should carry a threatening shadow in their hearts under the full sunshine; else, how should they learn to revere the right?" That guardianship might eventually become unnecessary, but only when all outward law becomes superfluous—only when duty and love unite into a single stream of force.

As Tito entered the outer cloister of San Marco and asked for Fra Luca, there was no shadowy presentiment in his mind. He considered himself too cultured and skeptical for such superstition. He had been raised in contempt for the scandalous lives of priests, a proverbial topic, and possessed an erudite familiarity with debates on the Chief Good, which he ultimately viewed as a matter of personal taste. Still, fear was a potent element within Tito's nature: the fear of losing pleasure. He harbored a definite anxiety that Fra Luca might be the instrument that drove him from Florence.

"Fra Luca? Ah, he has gone to Fiesole—to the Dominican monastery there. He was carried off on a litter early this morning. The poor Brother is very ill. Could you leave a message for him?"

The reply came from a *fra converso*, a lay brother whose accent revealed him to be a rough countryman (*contadino*), and whose dull gaze suggested no curiosity whatsoever.

"Thank you; my business can wait."

Tito turned away with a profound sense of relief. "This friar is unlikely to survive," he told himself. "I saw he was worn down to a shadow. And at Fiesole, there will be nothing to remind him of me. Besides, if he returns, my explanation will serve just as well then as now. Though I wonder what it was about his face that struck me."
