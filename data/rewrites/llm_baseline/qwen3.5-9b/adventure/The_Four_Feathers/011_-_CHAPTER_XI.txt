A month later, Durrance arrived in London. His first discovery was a letter waiting for him at his club, written by Ethne. It was brief and direct: she was staying with Mrs. Adair and hoped he would find time to visit. Yet, the paper bore a stark black border, and the envelope itself was edged in mourning ink.

Durrance visited Hill Street the following afternoon. Ethne received him alone.

"I did not write to Wadi Halfa," she explained immediately, "because I believed you would already be on your way home before my letter could reach you. My father died last month, towards the end of May."

"I feared as soon as I saw your note that this was the message," Durrance replied softly. "I am very sorry. You will miss him deeply."

"More than I can say," she answered, her voice carrying a quiet, profound weight. "He died one morning early—I believe I should tell you the circumstances if you wish to hear them."

She recounted the manner of Dermod's passing. A chill had been the occasion, not the cause; he succumbed to a gradual dissolution rather than a specific disease. It was a peculiar tale, for just before his death, Dermod seemed to reclaim a fragment of his former masterful spirit.

"We knew he was dying," Ethne said. "He knew it too. At seven o'clock the afternoon after..." She paused, gathering her composure, "...after he had spoken briefly to me, he called his dog by name. The dog sprang instantly onto the bed, despite his voice being no louder than a whisper. Crouching close, it pressed its muzzle against my father's hand with a soft whine. Then he instructed me to leave him and the dog entirely alone. I was to shut the door upon them. The dog would signal when I should open it again. I obeyed, waiting outside until one o'clock. Then, a loud, sudden howl echoed through the house."

She stopped, letting the memory settle. This pause was the only visible sign of her distress. Moments later, she continued, speaking plainly, stripped of theatrical grief.

"It was trying to wait outside that door as the afternoon faded into night. It was certainly night long before the end. He would have no lamp left in his room. One imagines him just behind that thin panel, lying motionless and silent in the great four-poster bed, his face turned toward the hills, as the light fell away. One pictures the room slipping into darkness, the windows looming larger in the gloom, the dog by his side, and no one else, right to the very final moment. That was his wish, but it was rather hard for me."

Durrance said nothing, offering instead the full measure of sympathy his silence conveyed. He visualized those six hours of twilight and darkness in the passage; he could see her standing near the door, perhaps pressing her ear to the wood, her hand over her heart to stifle its pounding. To him, there was something cruel in Dermod's resolve to die alone.

Ethne broke the silence. "I mentioned that my father spoke to me just before telling me to leave. Do you think you can guess whom he spoke of?"

She looked directly at Durrance. Neither her eyes nor the level tone of her voice betrayed the answer, yet a sudden thrum of hope stole his breath.

"Tell me!" he said, leaning forward in a suspended tension.

"Of Mr. Feversham," she answered.

He recoiled slightly, abruptly. It was not the name he had anticipated. He lowered his gaze to the carpet, shielding his face from her view.

"My father was always very fond of him," she continued gently. "And I wonder if you have any knowledge of what he is doing, or where he may be."

Durrance offered no response and refused to lift his head. He reflected on the strange, enduring grip Harry Feversham held over those who had once known him well—even the man he had wronged, and upon whose daughter he had inflicted such suffering, must have remembered him with kindness on his deathbed. The thought tasted bitter to Durrance in that moment, a bitterness he feared might leak from his features and voice. Compelled to speak, he yielded to Ethne's insistence.

"You have never encountered him, I suppose?" she asked.

Durrance rose and walked to the window. Speaking outward into the street, he hid his expression, though a surge of deep anger colored his words regardless of his effort to suppress them.

"No," he said. "Never have." Suddenly, his anger took control of his speech. "And I never wish to. He was my friend, I know. But I cannot recall that friendship now. All I can think is that if he had truly been the man we believed him to be, you would not have waited alone in that dark passage for those six hours."

Turning back to the center of the room, he asked abruptly, "Are you returning to Glenalla?"

"Yes."

"You will live there alone?"

"Yes."

Silence stretched between them. Then Durrance circled to stand behind her chair.

"You once said you might tell me why our engagement was broken off," he said.

"But you know," she replied. "What you said at the window proved you knew."

"No, I do not. Only a few words your father dropped. He asked me for news of Feversham the last time we spoke. But I know nothing definite. I would like you to tell me."

Ethne shook her head, leaning forward with her elbows on her knees. "Not now," she said. Silence followed.

Durrance broke it again. "I have only one more year at Halfa. It would be wise to leave Egypt then, I think. I do not expect much activity in the Sudan for some time. I do not intend to stay there—in any case. I mean, even if you decide to remain alone at Glenalla."

Ethne made no pretense of ignoring the implication in his words. "We are neither of us children," she said. "You have a lifetime ahead to consider. We should be prudent."

"Yes," Durrance snapped, overcome by sudden irritation, "but the *right* kind of prudence. The prudence that understands it is worthwhile to dare greatly."

Ethne did not move. Leaning forward with her back to him, he could see nothing of her face. For a long while, she remained utterly still and silent. Finally, she asked a question in a very low, gentle voice.

"Do you want me so very much?"

Before he could answer, she turned quickly toward him. "Try not to," she exclaimed earnestly. "For this one year, try not to. You have much to occupy your thoughts. Try to forget me altogether."

There was just enough regret in her tone—a sorrow at the prospect of losing a valued friend—to dull the sting of her words. It confirmed Durrance in his delusion that, but for her fear of ruining his career, she would have answered differently.

Mrs. Adair entered the room before Durrance could reply, shattering the moment. He carried away with him only his illusion.

That evening, he dined at his club and afterward smoked a cigar beneath the large tree where he had sat persistently a year prior, vainly seeking news of Harry Feversham. It was much the same clear night as when he had watched Lieutenant Sutch limp into the courtyard and hesitate at the sight of him. The sky was cloudless and starry; the air possessed the pleasant languor of a summer June night. Lights flashed from windows and doorways, giving the tree leaves a fresh spring-green hue, while carriages rolled along the roadway below with a thunderous hum like the sea.

On this night also, a man entered the courtyard who knew Durrance. Unlike Sutch, he did not hesitate. He approached Durrance directly and sat beside him.

"How do you do?" Durrance said, dropping the paper he had been scanning and extending his hand.

"This friend is Captain Mather."

"I was wondering if I should meet you when I read the evening paper," Mather remarked. "I knew it was about the time one might expect to find you in London. You have seen, I suppose?"

"What?" Durrance asked.

"Then you haven't," Mather replied, picking up the fallen newspaper and flipping through the pages to locate the specific item he sought. "You remember that last reconnaissance we made from Suakin?"

"Very well."

"We halted by the Sinkat fort at midday. There was an Arab hiding in the trees at the back of the glacis."

"Yes."

"Did you forget the yarn he told you?"

"About Gordon's letters and the wall of a house in Berber? No, I have not forgotten."

"Then here is something that will interest you," Mather said, folding the paper to his satisfaction and handing it to Durrance, pointing to a paragraph.

It was a short notice, devoid of detail—the merest summary. Durrance read it between puffs of his cigar.

"The fellow must have gone back to Berber after all," Mather observed. "A risky business. Abou Fatma—that was the man's name."
