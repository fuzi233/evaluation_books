Inside the drawing-room at The Pool, Durrance said farewell to Ethne. He had arranged their parting with precision—no lingering, no drawn-out goodbyes. Outside, the carriage waited at the steps of Guessens, luggage strapped securely to the roof, his servant standing by the door. Ethne stepped out onto the terrace beside him, where Mrs. Adair stood at the top of the stone staircase.

Durrance reached for her hand, but she turned instead to Ethne and said quietly, “I need a moment with Colonel Durrance before he leaves.”

“Of course,” Ethne replied. Then, turning to him, she added, “Then we’ll say our goodbyes here. You’ll write from Wiesbaden? Soon, please?”

“The moment I arrive,” he promised.

He descended the steps with Mrs. Adair, leaving Ethne alone on the terrace. The final act of pretense was over. Months of tension, silence, and careful observation had ended. Both were relieved—free now to breathe without constraint. Durrance walked briskly beside her, his step light with release. But Mrs. Adair lagged, her voice heavy when she finally spoke.

“So you’re going,” she said. “In two days you’ll be in Wiesbaden, and Ethne will be at Glenalla. We’ll all be scattered. It will be lonely here.”

She had achieved what she set out to do—separating Ethne and Durrance, at least for a time. She would no longer have to endure their presence, their voices, their quiet intimacy. Yet now that it was done, she felt nothing but emptiness. Her triumph tasted bitter.

“The house will seem very empty once you’re all gone,” she murmured, stepping down into the garden beside him.

“We’ll return,” Durrance said gently, trying to reassure her.

Mrs. Adair looked around. The flowers were gone, the sunlight faded. Clouds covered the sky, the grass beneath their feet dull green, the stream running gray between the trees. Leaves, russet and yellow, littered the lawns like fallen embers.

“How long will you stay in Wiesbaden?” she asked.

“I can’t say for sure. As long as it’s necessary.”

“That tells me nothing at all,” she replied sharply. “I suppose it was meant not to tell me anything.”

He didn’t answer. The silence stung her. She knew nothing of his plans—not whether he intended to honor his engagement to Ethne or break it entirely. Curiosity gnawed at her. It might be years before she saw him again, and those years would be filled with uncertainty.

“You distrust me?” she challenged, her voice rising with anger.

Durrance answered softly, “Have I no reason to distrust you? Why did you tell me about Captain Willoughby’s arrival? Why did you interfere?”

“I thought you should know,” she said.

“But Ethne wanted it kept secret. I’m glad I learned it—very glad. Still, you told me. And you were her friend.”

“Yours too, I hope,” she countered, then broke into an exclamation: “How could I stay silent? Don’t you understand?”

“No,” he said simply.

He might have understood—but he hadn’t given much thought to her. And she knew it. That knowledge festered within her. His single word—"No"—cut deeper than any accusation. It made her reckless.

“I spoke brutally,” she said. “But I told you the truth, as brutally as I could. Doesn’t that help you see?”

Again, he said only, “No.”

That one syllable drove her beyond caution. Suddenly, she was speaking without control—words spilling out, raw and unfiltered. She stood apart from herself, watching the madness unfold, yet unable to stop.

“I told you the truth brutally on purpose,” she confessed. “I was so hurt because you wouldn’t see what was plain—had you only the mind to see. I wanted to wound you. I am a bad woman, I suppose. There you were, talking together in the dark, and I alone on the terrace. It was the same today. You and Ethne in the room—I outside. I wonder if it will always be this way. But you won’t say it—you won’t say it!”

She clapped her hands together in despair. Durrance said nothing. He walked silently toward the stile, quickening his pace slightly, forcing her to hurry to keep up.

Her words followed him. “I don’t think I’d have minded so much,” she continued, “if Ethne had truly cared for you. But she never did—only as a friend. Just a friend. And what is friendship worth?” she sneered.

“Something,” Durrance said quietly.

“She shrinks from you!” she cried. “Why? Because you’re blind. She fears what your life might mean—how it might limit hers.” Her voice dropped to a whisper, edged with scorn. “But I—*I* am glad. When the news came from Wadi Halfa that you were blind, I was glad. When I saw you in Hill Street, I was glad. Ever since, I’ve been glad—*quite* glad. Because I saw her shrink. From the start, she shrank, afraid of how her life would be chained and burdened.”

She repeated, over and over, “I am not afraid. I am not afraid. I am not afraid.”

To Durrance, this confession was more horrifying than anything he’d ever imagined—the woman who had been Ethne’s friend, unraveling before him in a storm of jealousy and pain.

“Ethne wrote to you from Wadi Halfa out of pity,” Mrs. Adair went on. “That was all. She wrote from pity, then feared what she’d done. And fearing, she lacked the courage to admit it. You wouldn’t have blamed her, if she’d been honest. You would have remained her friend. But she couldn’t face it.”

Durrance knew another explanation—one true and clear. But tomorrow he would be gone from the Salcombe estuary, Ethne en route to Ireland. Arguing against her accusations was pointless. Besides, they were nearing the stile—the boundary between The Pool’s garden and the open fields. Once across, he would be free.

He said only, “You are not just to Ethne.”

At those words, the storm in Mrs. Adair subsided. She saw the futility of her words, her boasts, her attacks. They could change nothing. Durrance was always with Ethne, never with her. She felt the shame of her outburst, and began to justify herself.

“I am a bad woman, I suppose,” she whispered. “But I haven’t had the happiest of lives. Perhaps there’s something to be said for me.”

It sounded weak, even to her. But they had reached the stile, and Durrance turned to face her. His sternness softened. He remembered her from the past—when he could still see—graceful, composed, dignified in speech and bearing. This woman seemed almost a stranger. The contrast struck him deeply, making him willing to listen.

“Will you tell me?” he asked gently.

“I was married almost straight from school,” she began. “A child, really. I knew nothing. I married a man I barely knew—my mother’s choice. She thought she was doing what was best. She secured me a position, comfort, safety from poverty. I accepted blindly. I couldn’t refuse—she was a strong woman, and I was trained to obey. I married dutifully, as she wished. It’s a common story, perhaps, but its repetition doesn’t make it easier to bear.”

“But Mr. Adair?” Durrance asked.

“He was kind,” she said. “And he cared for me. That was the only thread holding me—knowing he loved me. At first, I was content. I had houses in town and here. But it was dull.” She spread her arms wide. “Oh, how dull it was! Do you know those narrow back streets in industrial towns? Rows of identical houses, same windows, same doors, same steps. Smoke above, everything blackened. Even the plants in the windows were dirty. Any preacher promising color to their gray lives could draw crowds. When I thought of my own life, that street came to mind. There are women who find joy in managing large homes, London seasons, endless visits. I was not one of them. Dull—so terribly dull. And you, with your thousand tasks, cannot imagine how crushing it was. But it wasn’t all.”

She hesitated, but could not stop. “I married knowing nothing of what mattered. At first, I believed this was the fate of every woman. But soon, doubts crept in. I began to learn that life offered more—more than mere dullness. Not for me, perhaps, but for others. I’d pass a couple riding together, catch a glance of the woman’s face—and see joy. Or talk briefly with someone alone, hear the warmth in her voice, and know instantly there was *more*. Only the chance of that more—my mother denied me.”

All hardness had left Durrance’s face now. Mrs. Adair spoke plainly, without appeal or plea. No trace of earlier rage remained. She was simply telling her truth.

“And then you came,” she continued. “I met you, and met you again. You left for duty, returned. And I learned—didn’t just know, but *knew*—what that ‘more’ was. But it was still denied to me. Still. Yet, despite that, I felt happier. I thought I could be content to watch your progress, take pride in your path. But then—Ethne came. And you turned to her. At once. Oh, at once! If you had only been slower… In no time, I was sorrowful that you had entered my life.”

“I never knew this,” Durrance said. “I never suspected. I’m sorry.”

“I took care you wouldn’t,” she replied. “But I tried to keep you. With all my strength, I worked to hold you. No matchmaker ever labored harder to unite a couple than I did to bring Ethne and Feversham together. And I succeeded.”

The revelation struck Durrance like a blow. He leaned back against the stile, nearly laughing. So this was the root of it all—the beginning of a chain that led to Feversham’s downfall, Ethne’s suffering, the months of deception. How small the seeds of such great consequences!

“I succeeded,” she continued. “You told me so one morning in the Row. I was so happy. You didn’t notice, I’m sure. Then, in the next breath, you told me you were going to the Soudan. You were away three years. They weren’t happy years for me. You returned. My husband was dead. Ethne was free. She refused you. Then you became blind, and she claimed you. You can see the ups and downs of my life. But these last months—these have been the worst.”

“I’m very sorry,” Durrance said.

She was right—he thought. There *was* something to be said for her. The world had treated her unfairly. He could feel her pain, because he was enduring something similar himself. Now he understood why she had revealed Ethne’s secret that night. He could not be harsh with her.

“I’m very sorry, Mrs. Adair,” he repeated, lamely. He had no other words. He held out his hand.

“Good-bye,” she said.

He climbed over the stile and walked across the fields toward his home.

Mrs. Adair stood at the stile long after he was gone. She had spoken her truth—and harmed no one but herself and the man she secretly cared for. She saw that clearly. And she foresaw that if Durrance did not marry Ethne, he would return to Guessens. Then their meeting would be fraught with awkwardness, their friendship forever shadowed by what had passed on this afternoon.

She had silenced her own longing, but not her regret. The price of honesty was isolation. And she had paid it fully.
