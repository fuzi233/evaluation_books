Aristide Valentin, Chief of the Paris Police, was running late for his dinner, and his guests began to filter in before he arrived. They were calmed by the presence of his confidential servant, Ivan—a weather-beaten old man with a scar and a moustache as grey as his complexion—who sat permanently in the entrance hall, a space adorned with an impressive array of weaponry. Valentin's residence was as eccentric and notorious as its master. An ancient structure surrounded by high walls and towering poplars that nearly brushed the Seine, it possessed a singular architectural flaw—or perhaps feature—that served the police well: there was no exit from the house other than the front door, which Ivan guarded alongside the arsenal. Conversely, the vast garden behind held numerous exits leading from the house, yet no exit led from the garden back to the street. It was encircled by a tall, smooth, unscalable wall crowned with spikes, creating a perfect trap for reflection, especially for a man in whom a hundred criminals had sworn to exact revenge.

Ivan explained to the arriving party that their host was delayed by ten minutes due to urgent arrangements concerning executions—tasks that, though repulsive to Valentin, he executed with chilling precision. While ruthless in tracking criminals, he was surprisingly mild regarding their punishments. Having shaped French—and largely European—police methods, his profound influence had been honorably directed toward mitigating sentences and purifying prisons. He was considered one of the great humanitarian French freethinkers, though critics argued that his brand of mercy often froze colder than justice itself.

When Valentin finally appeared, dressed in black with a red rosette pinned to his chest, he was an elegant figure, his dark beard streaked with grey. He bypassed the main floor, moving directly to his study, which opened onto the grounds. After securing his official box, he lingered at the open door, gazing out at the garden. A sharp moonlight battled the lingering remnants of a storm, casting a wistful atmosphere upon the scene—a mood unusual for a man of his strictly scientific nature. Perhaps, however, such minds possess a psychic vision of their life's greatest problems. Recovering swiftly from this occult reverie, knowing he was late, he entered the drawing-room.

Inside, he surveyed his guests: Lord Galloway, the choleric English Ambassador with a russet face like a ripe apple and the blue ribbon of the Garter; his wife, Lady Galloway, slim and silver-haired with a superior countenance; their daughter, Lady Margaret, pale and elfish with copper hair; the opulent, black-eyed Duchess of Mont St. Michel and her daughters; Dr. Simon, a typical French scientist with glasses and a forehead marked by the wrinkles of constant eyebrow-raising; and Father Brown, the priest from Cobhole, whom Valentin had recently met in England. Finally, there stood Commandant O'Brien of the French Foreign Legion, a tall, swaggering figure in uniform who had bowed to the Galloways without receiving a hearty welcome. Born an Irish gentleman, O'Brien had known the Galloways in childhood, particularly Margaret. After a financial crash, he had fled his homeland, now expressing his disdain for British etiquette by swinging his sabre and spurs with a dashing, melancholy air.

To Valentin, however, these distinguished individuals were not the focus of the evening. He awaited a man of global renown, a friend from his triumphant detective tours in the United States: Julius K. Brayne. Brayne was a multi-millionaire whose colossal donations to obscure religions had fueled endless debate in the American and English press. Whether atheist, Mormon, or Christian Scientist remained unclear, but Brayne was ready to fund any intellectual vessel, provided it was untested. His favorite pastime was waiting for the "American Shakespeare"—a hobby more patient than fishing. He admired Walt Whitman but deemed Luke P. Tanner of Pennsylvania far more progressive. Valentin, ironically, was labeled "progressive" by Brayne, a compliment that grated on Valentin's sensibilities.

Brayne's arrival was as decisive as a dinner bell. A huge, fat man clad in black evening wear, lacking even the relief of a watch chain, he possessed a presence that dwarfed his absence. His white hair was brushed back, his face red and fierce yet cherubic, save for a dark tuft under his lip that gave him a theatrical, Mephistophelean air. Before the salon could fully absorb his celebrity, his tardiness became a domestic emergency, and he was whisked away to the dining room with Lady Galloway on his arm.

During the meal, the Galloways remained genial until Lady Margaret took the arm of the adventurer, O'Brien. Once that transpired, Lord Galloway's patience evaporated. Restless and nearly rude, he grew undiplomatic over cigars. Every sixty seconds, the thought stung him that O'Brien might be signaling to his daughter. Left over coffee with Brayne—the hoary Yankee who believed in all religions—and Valentin, the grizzled Frenchman who believed in none, the conversation devolved into a tedious logomachy between science and faith. Eventually, exasperated, Lord Galloway rose to seek the drawing-room. He wandered through long passages for several minutes before hearing the doctor's high-pitched voice and Father Brown's dull tone, followed by general laughter. Presuming they were debating theology, he opened the salon door only to find it empty. Both Commandant O'Brien and Lady Margaret were gone.

Driven by a paranoid, mad obsession to protect his daughter from the Irish exile, Galloway rushed toward the back of the mansion where Valentin's study lay. There, he encountered his daughter sweeping past with a white, scornful expression. Puzzled—if she were with O'Brien, where was he? If not, where had she been? He stumbled through the dark corridors to a servant's entrance opening onto the garden. The moon, now a scimitar clearing the storm clouds, illuminated the silver grass. There, striding toward the study, was a tall figure in blue: Commandant O'Brien.

O'Brien vanished through the French windows, leaving Galloway in a virulent, vague rage. The moonlit garden, resembling a theatrical stage, seemed to mock the diplomat's tyrannical tenderness. The length and grace of the Irishman's stride enragéd him, transforming him into a jealous rival rather than a protective father. Trapped in a fairyland of troubadours, Galloway stepped briskly after his enemy, tripping over a stone or root. Looking down, he first felt irritation, then curiosity, and suddenly the moon and poplars revealed a horror: an elderly English diplomatist running hard, crying and bellowing.

"A corpse in the grass! A blood-stained corpse!" Galloway's hoarse cries summoned Dr. Simon, who emerged from the study with a worried brow. Galloway, weeping, described what he had glimpsed. "A corpse! A blood-stained corpse!" Simon urged haste, noting that Valentin, the great detective, was likely drawn by the commotion. Indeed, Valentin entered the study, his demeanor shifting instantly from concerned host to grim professional.

"Strange," Valentin murmured as they hurried into the garden, "that I should hunt mysteries across the globe, only for one to settle in my own backyard. But where?" Crossing the lawn, where a slight mist rose from the river, they located the body sunk in deep grass. It was a tall, broad-shouldered man lying face-down. Black cloth covered his massive shoulders; his bald head, save for a wisp of brown hair clinging like wet seaweed, bore a scarlet serpent of blood crawling from beneath his chin.

"He is not of our party," Dr. Simon observed with a singular intonation. "Examine him, Doctor. He may not be dead." Simon knelt. "He is not quite cold, but I fear he is dead enough. Help me lift him." As they raised the body an inch, all doubt vanished horrifically. The head fell away, severed cleanly from the torso. Even Valentin was slightly shocked. "He must have been as strong as a gorilla," he muttered.

Simon lifted the head. Slightly slashed around the neck and jaw, the face remained substantially unhurt. It was a ponderous, yellow visage, sunken and swollen, with a hawk-like nose and heavy lids—reminiscent of a wicked Roman Emperor, perhaps with a distant echo of a Chinese one. Beneath the body, they spotted the white gleam of a shirt-front stained with blood. The man had never been invited, yet he was dressed for the occasion.

Valentin knelt, examining the grass and ground for twenty yards with professional intensity, aided poorly by the doctor and the English lord. Apart from a few twigs snapped into tiny fragments—which Valentin inspected and discarded—they found nothing. "Twigs," Valentin said gravely. "Twigs, and a total stranger with his head cut off. That is all."

Then, unnerved, Lord Galloway shouted, "Who's that! Who's that over there by the garden wall!" A small figure with a foolishly large head approached waveringly in the moonlit haze, looking momentarily like a goblin before revealing itself as Father Brown.

"I say," the priest said meekly, "there are no gates to this garden, do you know?"
