Inside the tavern, the air grew heavy with the scent of unwashed linen and impending violence. In one chamber, Colonel Washington paced with a grim determination, his mind already turning to the bloodshed that honor demanded. Across the threshold, in the adjoining room, young George Warrington sat at a table, his face pale, dictating a final letter to his mother. His brother, Harry, sat beside him, pen in hand, trembling as he transcribed his brother's words.

"My dear, dear George, do not speak of death!" Harry cried, his voice cracking.

"As probability dictates," George replied, his tone dripping with a cold, aristocratic finality. "You know my aim, Harry. I am no novice with a pistol. It is likely that one, or perhaps both of us, shall fall. And regarding your mother... I doubt she will pursue her schemes once the smoke clears."

Harry's pen hovered, ink staining his fingers. "Madame Esmond's name does not appear in this quarrel," George continued, his bitterness sharpening. "Do you recall our grandfather's history? How Lord Castlewood challenged Lord Mohun over a card game, yet never named the lady who truly sparked the duel? I have taken my cue from that. My mother is innocent of this blood."

Harry wept openly, the salt of his tears blurring the page. "I cannot think of spelling now, Georgy," he whimpered, his composure shattering. "I am too miserable. Perhaps it is all a dream. Perhaps Colonel Washington never..."

"Never intended to possess Castlewood? Never patronized us? Never advised my mother to flog me? Never planned to marry her? Never insulted me before the King's officers?" George slammed his hand against his breast pocket, where a folded letter lay. "That paper is there, Harry Warrington. If I die, you will find it upon my corpse!"

"Write it yourself, Georgy," Harry sobbed, burying his face in his hands until his elbows smeared the fresh ink across the page.

George took a fresh sheet and sat at his brother's elbow. With the flourish of a young man preparing for immortality, he penned a testament filled with grandiose Latin quotations and biting satire. He commanded the immediate freedom of his enslaved boy, Sady; he bequeathed his favorite copy of Horace and a stipend to his beloved tutor, Mr. Dempster; he left his silver fruit-knife, music books, and harpsichord to the young Mrs. Mountain; and he instructed his brother to wear a lock of his hair as a token of eternal devotion. He sealed the document with the family crest of his grandfather.

"The watch, naturally, is yours," George said, retrieving his grandfather's gold timepiece. He glanced at the face. "Two and a half hours have vanished. Sady should return with the pistols soon. Take the watch, Harry."

"It is useless!" Harry screamed, throwing his arms around his brother's neck. "If he fights you, I will fight him. If he kills my Georgy—may he be struck down—he shall have a shot at me!" The boy's curses were so fervent they seemed to echo in the heavens, recorded by unseen angels in celestial ledgers.

Outside, the tavern's landlord, dressed in his old militia uniform, approached the brothers. "The Colonel is pacing the garden, waiting," he announced. "The regulars are nearly sober. The grounds are prepared."

Near the Captain's log-house, a patch of earth had been cleared for a kitchen garden, now serving as the arena. Colonel Washington walked there, hands clasped behind his back, head bowed, a profound sorrow etched onto his handsome features. Negro servants pressed against the wooden palings, peering over. Officers lounged under the porch, waking from their stupor. Captain Waring strode steadily beneath the sloping roofline, while Captain Grace lolled over the railing, his eyes wide and unfocused. Benson's tavern was a notorious haven for cockfights, horse matches, and brutal wrestling bouts where men arrived sober and left with broken ribs or gouged eyes. Squires, farmers, and slaves alike participated in the chaos.

There, the tall young Colonel stood, lost in dismal meditation. There was no escape from his prediculation but the cruel path dictated by the laws of honor and local custom. Goaded into fury by a boy's insolence, he had spoken words that demanded reparation. The young man had asked for satisfaction. Though shocked that George Warrington's jealousy had festered so long, the Colonel knew the wrong was his, and he was bound to pay the forfeit.

Suddenly, a deafening roar erupted from the road—a sound familiar to the Virginians, born of the joy of galloping horses. Heads, both woolly and powdered, turned toward the commotion. The clatter of hooves approached, and soon Mr. Sady appeared atop his foaming steed. Amidst a prodigious uproar from his black companions, Sady fired a pistol into the air, then another. His horse, accustomed to the hunt, remained steady. Surrounded by a dozen bawling comrades, Sady descended into the courtyard, kicking at startled turkeys and shrieking pigs.

"Sady, sir, come here!" roared Master Harry.

"Sady, come here! Confound you!" shouted Master George.

"Coming right away, mas'r," Sady replied cheerfully, ignoring his masters to chat with his brethren. He grinned, drew his pistols from the holster, snapped the locks, and pointed them first at a grunting hog that plunged through the yard, then down the road where the others had come. "Comin', mas'r. Everybody a-comin'."

The sound of more horses echoed. First came Little Mr. Dempster, spurring his pony, followed by a lady in riding habit on Madam Esmond's gray mare. It was not Esmond; she was too stout. It was Mrs. Mountain.

"Oh Lor! O Golly! Hoop! Here dey come! Hurray!" the chorus of negroes rose up.

Dr. Dempster and Mrs. Mountain clattered into the yard, dismounted, and pushed through the crowd of negroes. They rushed into the house, tore through it, and burst onto the porch where British officers sat in bewildered silence. They sprinted down the stairs to the garden, where George and Harry stood facing their tall enemy. Before George Warrington could sternly ask, "What do you do here, madam?", Mrs. Mountain threw her arms around his neck.

"Oh, George, my darling! It is a mistake! It is all my fault!"

"What is a mistake?" George asked, majestically pulling away.

"What is it, Mounty?" Harry cried, trembling.

"That paper I took from his portfolio," Mrs. Mountain explained breathlessly. "The one where the Colonel says he intends to marry a widow with two children. Who should it be but you, children? And who should it be but your mother?"

"Well?"

"Well, it is not your mother. It is that little widow Custis whom the Colonel intends to wed. He always sought a wealthy match; I knew he would. It is not Mrs. Rachel Warrington. He told Madam so today, just before departing, saying the marriage would follow the campaign. And your mother is furious, boys. When Sady came for the pistols and told the whole house of your duel, I told him to fire them off. I galloped after him, and I have nearly broken my poor old bones getting here."

"I have a mind to break Mr. Sady's," George growled. "I specifically enjoined the villain to say nothing."

"Thank God he did, brother!" Harry exclaimed. "Thank God he did!"

"What will Mr. Washington and those gentlemen think of my servant telling my mother at home that I was going to fight a duel?" George asked, still seething.

"You have shown your proofs before, George," Harry said respectfully. "And thank Heaven, you are not going to fight our old friend—our grandfather's old friend. It was a mistake, and there is no quarrel now, dear, is there? You were unkind to him under a false impression."

"I certainly acted under a wrong impression," George admitted, "but—"

"George! George Washington!" Harry cried, springing over the cabbage garden toward the bowling green where the Colonel stood. Though his voice was unheard, his actions spoke volumes. With both hands outstretched, eager and blundering, filled with love and affection, the boy ran to his friend.

In those days, a custom lingered that has since faded. When Harry finished his artless tale, the Colonel took him into his arms, holding him close to his heart. His voice faltered. "Thank God, thank God for this!"

"Oh, George," Harry whispered, feeling the depth of his love for his friend, "how I wish I were going with you on the campaign!" The Colonel pressed both the boy's hands in a grip of friendship that neither knew would ever loosen.

Then the Colonel stepped forward, gravely extending his hand to Harry's elder brother. Perhaps Harry wondered why they did not embrace as they had moments before. Yet, though hands were joined, the salute remained formal and stern.

"I find I have done you a wrong, Colonel Washington," George said. "I must apologize, not for the error itself, but for much of my late behavior which resulted from it."

"The error was mine!" Mrs. Mountain interjected. "It was I who found that paper in your room and showed it to George, and was jealous of you, Colonel. All women are jealous."

"'Tis a pity you could not have kept your eyes off my paper, madam," Mr. Washington replied gently. "You will permit me to say so. Much mischief has arisen because I chose to keep a secret concerning only myself and another. For a long time, George Warrington's heart has been black with anger against me, and my feelings toward him have, I admit, been scarce friendly. All this pain might have been spared to both of us had my private papers been read only by those for whom they were written. I shall say no more now, lest my feelings betray me into hasty words. Heaven bless thee, Harry! Farewell, George! And take a true friend's advice: try to be less ready to think evil of your friends. We shall meet again at the camp, and we will keep our weapons for the enemy."

With a very stately bow to the astonished English officers, the Colonel left the abashed company and swiftly rode away.
