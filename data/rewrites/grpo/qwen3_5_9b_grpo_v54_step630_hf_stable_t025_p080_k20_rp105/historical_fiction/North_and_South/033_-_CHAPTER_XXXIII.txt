The silence that settled over the Hale household felt unnatural, a heavy, suffocating blanket following the storm of terror and noise. Mr. Hale, having ensured his daughter was attended to, retreated to his customary chair, sinking once more into his melancholy reverie. Dixon had enlisted Mary Higgins to manage the kitchen, her whispers sharp with suppressed anger, for to speak loudly while a body lay cold in the house seemed an act of irreverence. Margaret resolved to spare her father the final shock of the accident; the event had ended well, and dwelling on it served no purpose. Her only lingering fear was that Mr. Lenards might secure funds to pursue Frederick to London, but she dismissed the thought as futile. Frederick would be vigilant, and within days, he would be safe beyond England's shores.

"I suppose we shall hear from Mr. Bell tomorrow," Margaret said softly.

"Yes," her father replied, his voice flat. "I suppose so."

"If he can travel, he will likely be here by evening."

"If he cannot, I shall ask Mr. Thornton to accompany me to the funeral. I cannot go alone; I shall collapse entirely."

"Papa, do not ask Mr. Thornton," Margaret interjected, her tone impulsive. "Let me go with you."

"You? My dear, women rarely attend funerals."

"No," Margaret insisted, her eyes glistening. "Because they lack the power to control their emotions, yet feel too ashamed to display them. Poor women go, indifferent to being seen weeping. But I promise you, Papa, if you allow me, I shall be no burden. Do not have a stranger present and exclude me. If Mr. Bell cannot come, I will go. I will not press my wish against your will if he does arrive."

Mr. Bell, however, could not come. Gout had confined him. His letter was tender, expressing profound regret and citing urgent business regarding his Milton property as the cause of his absence. He hoped to visit soon, driven by a desire to comfort his old friend.

Margaret struggled mightily to dissuade her father from inviting Mr. Thornton. The night before the funeral, a formal note arrived from Mrs. Thornton, stating that, at her son's request, their carriage would attend the ceremony if it did not inconvenience the family. Margaret tossed the note onto the table.

"Oh, let us avoid such formalities," she said. "Let us go alone, Papa. They do not truly care for us; otherwise, he would have offered to walk beside us, not sent an empty carriage."

"I thought you were so averse to his presence, Margaret," Mr. Hale said, surprised.

"And I am," she admitted, her voice trembling. "I do not want him to come at all, and I dislike the idea of asking him. But this gesture feels like a mockery of mourning, and I did not expect it from him." Overwhelmed, she burst into tears. Her usual composure, her gentleness and patience, had vanished, replaced by agitation. As her father lavished tenderness upon her, her crying only intensified.

That night was terrible for Margaret, leaving her ill-prepared for the anxiety brought by a letter from Frederick. Mr. Lennox was out of town, expected no sooner than Tuesday, perhaps Monday. Consequently, Frederick decided to remain in London for a few more days. He had considered returning to Milton, tempted by the proximity, but the prospect of Mr. Bell residing in his father's house and the terrifying recognition he had suffered at the railway station compelled him to stay. Margaret was grateful her father was absent in her mother's room when she read the letter. Had he been present, her distress would have triggered a nervous alarm he could not soothe. Beyond Frederick's detention, the letter alluded to the recognition at Milton and the possibility of pursuit, chilling her blood. How would this affect her father? Margaret bitterly repented urging the consultation with Mr. Lennox. It had seemed prudent then, adding little risk, yet every subsequent event proved it a mistake. She battled her self-reproach, knowing her father's fragile state would only deepen his morbid regrets over the past.

Mr. Hale, however, was too broken to engage with such thoughts. He was consumed by the singular idea that the last visible token of his wife was to be removed from his sight. He trembled pitifully as the undertaker arranged the black drapery around him. When released, he tottered toward Margaret, his voice a whisper. "Pray for me, Margaret. I have no strength left. I cannot pray. I give her up because I must. I try to bear it; I know it is God's will. But I cannot understand why she died. Pray for me, my child, that I may find the faith to pray. It is a great strait."

In the carriage, Margaret supported him, reciting verses of holy comfort and texts of faithful resignation. Her voice never faltered, and strangely, she found strength in the act. Her father's lips moved in echo, repeating the words mechanically, a terrible struggle to achieve a resignation he lacked the power to accept.

Margaret's fortitude nearly shattered when Dixon gestured toward Nicholas Higgins and his daughter, standing apart yet deeply attentive. Nicholas wore his usual fustian, save for a strip of black cloth sewn to his hat—a mark of mourning he had never worn for his daughter Bessy. Mr. Hale saw nothing. He continued to repeat the funeral service internally, sighing twice as the service concluded, before taking Margaret's arm and mutely begging to be led away as if blind, trusting her as his guide.

Dixon sobbed openly, hiding her face, so absorbed in her grief that she failed to notice the crowd dispersing until someone spoke to her. It was Mr. Thornton. He had stood the entire time, head bowed behind a cluster of mourners, unrecognized.

"I beg your pardon," he said, his voice cool. "Can you tell me how Mr. Hale is? And Miss Hale?"

"Of course, sir. They are as expected. Master is terribly broken down. Miss Hale bears up better than likely."

Mr. Thornton preferred to hear of her suffering. A selfish pleasure stirred within him—the thought that his love might comfort her, a sensation akin to a mother's joy when an infant nestles close. Yet, this vision was miserably shattered by the memory of the young man standing with such familiar confidence near the Outwood station. The image shot through him like agony, forcing him to clench his fists to suppress the pain. So far from home, it took a monumental effort to revive his trust in Margaret's purity; the moment the effort ceased, that trust died, replaced by wild, chaotic fantasies. Here was confirmation, gnawing and miserable: she bore up better than likely. She had hope, a light so bright it illuminated her dark hours. Yes, he knew how she would love. Her soul would shine in glorious sunlight if a worthy man could win it. Even in mourning, she would rest in peaceful faith upon his sympathy. Whose sympathy? That other man's. This realization made Mr. Thornton's pale face grow doubly wan and stern.

"I suppose I may call," he said coldly. "On Mr. Hale, I mean. He will perhaps admit me after tomorrow."

He spoke as if the answer mattered little, though deep down, he longed to see the author of his pain. Though he hated Margaret at times, recalling that gentle, familiar attitude, he possessed a restless desire to renew her image in his mind, to breathe the atmosphere she inhabited. Trapped in the whirlpool of passion, he was forced to circle ever closer to the fatal center.

"I dare say, sir, master will see you," Dixon replied smoothly. "He was very sorry to have to deny you the other day, but circumstances were not agreeable just then."

For reasons unknown, Dixon never mentioned this encounter to Margaret. It may have been mere chance, but it was fate that Margaret never learned Mr. Thornton had attended her mother's funeral.
