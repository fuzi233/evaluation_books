The Mother of Sorrows rose, veiling her face in the folds of her mantle, and went to rouse the Little Ones. They lay as if untouched by time, their breath steady and deep, but the moment her voice reached them, they sprang to their feet, eyes bright, limbs supple, as though the night had never been. Laughing, they followed her down the stair, their bare feet whispering against the stone, until she led them to where the princess lay, her tears still flowing even in sleep.

The children’s joy faltered. They looked from the weeping woman to the rain beyond, then back again, their small faces solemn.

“The sky is crying too,” murmured one.

“The princess is leaking!” cried another, wide-eyed. “Is it the same as rivers?”

Odu, ever curious, reached out to touch the wet trails on Lilith’s hollow cheeks. “I thought rivers were louder,” he said, glancing at me for confirmation.

Mara smiled. “These are the quiet rivers—the ones that water the soul.”

“Will they wake the ground?” asked Luva, pressing close.

“Yes, little one. Soon the earth will drink and grow green again.”

Odu frowned, poking his own cheek as if testing for moisture. “But why does the princess cry?”

“Because she is learning,” Mara said softly. “And learning hurts before it heals.”

Lilith stirred, her eyes fluttering open. The children gasped as she lifted her gaze to them—no longer burning with fury, but heavy with sorrow.

“We must take her home,” Mara announced.

“Is home a nest?” asked Sozo, tilting his head.

“A kind of nest,” Mara agreed. “But first, we must bring her to a greater place—a house of rest.”

Luva wrinkled her nose. “Are there cats there?”

“None,” Mara assured her. “Only dreams too sweet for claws.”

With that, the Little Ones scattered to gather their belongings, all but Luva, who lingered, staring at Lilith’s tear-streaked face.

“Her robe is all wet,” she whispered. “Will the elephants mind?”

“Not at all,” Mara said. “Tears cleanse more than dirt.”

I had slept by the fire, but now I rose, my limbs stiff yet my mind clear. Mara turned to me.

“It is time, Mr. Vane.”

“Is there no safer path?” I asked. “Must we face the monsters again?”

“The riverbed is easiest,” she replied. “But the den cannot be avoided. Fear not—the children will pass unharmed.”

We stepped into the dawn. The beasts waited, patient as shadows. Odu was already perched on the neck of one elephant, grinning. I mounted Lona’s horse, and Mara placed her body gently in my arms. When she emerged again with Lilith, the children gasped—not in fear, but awe. Mara had unveiled herself, and her beauty struck them silent.

The elephants took Lilith with infinite care, their trunks cradling her as if she were made of glass. The procession began.

The moor stretched before us, vast and barren, but the children danced around Mara, their laughter weaving through the wind. We crossed the dry channels without trouble, yet as we neared the ridge, the sun dipped low, staining the sky with fire.

“Mount,” I urged the Little Ones. The moon hung hesitant, as if reluctant to rise.

Mara led us forward, her steps sure. The elephants followed, Lilith swaying between them. The leopardess trailed behind, her tail twitching.

Then we reached the edge—the nightmare’s maw.

The earth trembled.

Monsters surged from the ground, their forms a delirium of teeth and talons, all reaching for Lilith. She stiffened, eyes wild, but the children, blind to the horrors, chattered obliviously. The beasts did not touch them.

“Keep close,” I called.

Mara walked on, unshaken. A head, severed and grinning, rolled into our path. The leopardess pounced, but it vanished with a shriek. The moon wavered, then plunged downward.

Darkness fell.

A shape loomed—a corpse-flower of a face, jaws gaping. The leopardess leapt.

Then blackness.

A cry echoed. I whirled, but the elephants were already safe on the far bank. Behind me, dust swirled where the leopardess had fought.

A voice, gentle and familiar, spoke from the void.

Mara answered, her voice thick. “But I will miss her.”

We stumbled toward a distant light—a candle in a window. Adam stood there, his face lined but kind.

“Welcome,” he said.

Lilith, pride crumbling, stepped forward.

Adam’s eyes softened. “We have waited long for you.”

She said nothing.

Eve appeared, radiant, her gaze lingering on Lilith. “The enemy of my children,” she murmured.

“No longer,” Mara said.

Lilith shuddered. “I do not know your Father.”

“That is why you suffer,” Adam replied.

She turned as if to flee, but Mara stopped her. “You must open your hand.”

Lilith stared at her clenched fist. “I cannot.”

Adam returned with a sword—the blade like frozen lightning. Without a word, he took her wrist.

One flash. One drop of blood.

Her hand fell into Mara’s lap.

Lilith sighed—and slept.

The children, watching, crept close.

“She’s pretty now,” one whispered.

“I’ll sleep with her,” declared another, climbing beside her.

Mara covered them both. “Rest well.”

We left them there, the dead and the dreaming, and stepped back into the night. The leopardess lay across Lilith’s feet, a guardian in white.

And the house of death held its breath, waiting for morning.
