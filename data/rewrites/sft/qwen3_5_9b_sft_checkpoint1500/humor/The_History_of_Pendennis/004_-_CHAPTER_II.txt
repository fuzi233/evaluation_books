In the reign of King George III, known to the romantics as "George the Magnificent," resided in a diminutive West Country hamlet named Clavering a gentleman of the name Pendennis. To those who still retained the sharp memories of pre-industrial England, his lineage was visible on a signboard above a humble shop in Bath: a gilt pestle and mortar hovering over the entrance, where John Pendennis, a surgeon and apothecary, practiced his trade. Here he tended to the sick, assisted ladies in delicate circumstances, and—with a generous condescension—sold brown-paper plasters to a farmer's wife or peddled toothbrushes and lavender water to passersby. These facts were attested to by the local gentry of Clavering, whose recollections were far more tenacious than those of the bustling metropolis.

Yet, beneath the guise of a penny-selling apothecary, John Pendennis was a man of considerable education and ancient blood. His Cornish pedigree traced the Pendennises back to the era of the Druids, intermarrying with the Normans in later centuries and forging ties with the great houses of Wales and Brittany. Though he possessed a university degree from Cambridge, destiny had intervened. In his second year, his father died insolvent, forcing young Pen to abandon his scholarly dreams for the grim reality of the pestle and apron. He harbored a profound, lifelong hatred for the trade, entering it only through sheer necessity and the machinations of his mother's brother, a London apothecary of dubious reputation who had lowered the family standards by marrying John's father.

Upon completing his apprenticeship, John severed ties with his coarse-minded relative and established himself independently in Bath. Poverty was his constant companion for some time; it strained his resources to maintain the shop's gilt ornaments and provide comfort for his bedridden mother. Fortune, however, smiled upon him serendipitously. One day, Lady Ribstone, accompanied by an intoxicated Irish chairman who smashed through her carriage and splintered a pink bottle in the surgeon's window, collapsed outside his door. Accommodated in his shop, she was revived with cinnamon and sal-volatile. Captivated by Mr. Pendennis's uncommonly gentle and gentlemanlike manners, Lady Ribstone appointed him personal physician to her extensive household.

His fortunes shifted dramatically thereafter. When Master Ribstone returned from Eton suffering from a fever, Pendennis treated him with such skill and tenderness that he secured the enduring favor of the Codlingbury family. From that point forward, prosperity followed. The fashionable circles of Bath patronized him, particularly the ladies, who adored and admired him. First, his modest shop transformed into a smart establishment; soon, he deemed the sale of toothbrushes unworthy of his ancient lineage. Eventually, he shuttered the retail side entirely, retaining only a private surgery staffed by a genteel assistant. He acquired a gig, and before his mother's passing, the joyous sight of her rolling to her window revealed her beloved John stepping into a private one-horse carriage, proudly displaying the Pendennis arms upon its panels. "What would Arthur say now?" she lamented, referring to her younger son who, despite his poverty, had never visited her. "Captain Pendennis is with his regiment in India," John replied gently, requesting that he not be called "Johnny" before the young man, Mr. Parkins.

Tragically, the day came when she ceased to address him with any term of endearment. Her voice, once kind though querulous, fell silent. John altered his nightbell to the chamber where she had grumbled for decades and claimed the great bed in solitude. Over forty, he had yet to experience war or the throne of George IV, but what mattered to him was his pedigree. He displayed it prominently in his drawing-room, flanked by paintings of Codlingbury House and St. Boniface's College, retrieving it from a trunk with the same ceremonial gravity as a swordsman drawing a blade.

Around the time of his mother's demise, another patient, the virtuous Lady Pontypool, also passed away at Bath. Attended for five years by Miss Helen Thistlewood, a distant relative of the Pontypools and daughter of a naval lieutenant slain at Copenhagen, Lady Pontypool had subjected the young lady to a tyranny known only to women. Yet, Helen bore the old lady's insults with angelical sweetness. As they traveled in a mourning coach to Bath Abbey, Pendennis, observing her pale, sweet face, resolved to ask a question that made his pulse race. He was twenty years her senior and rarely ardent, perhaps having strangled a youthful romance in infancy, but at thirty-four, he was a collected, quiet gentleman. Following the funeral, he called upon her, holding her hand as he asked where she would reside now that the Pontypool estates were being dismantled, boxed, and carted away.

Tears filling her eyes, Helen confessed she knew not where to go, possessing only a thousand pounds left by her patroness. Looking deeply into her face, Pendennis proposed that she live with him. Though older, he boasted of his good birth, principles, and temper. Alone in the world, he needed a constant companion whom he intended to make happy. Reciting a speech composed that morning and perfected in his carriage, he offered his hand. Perhaps Helen, having dreamed of better things, hesitated, but recognizing his worthiness, prudence, and honor, she blushed, curtsied low, and asked to consider his proposal.

They married during the dull Bath season, which coincided with the height of the season in London. Securing lodgings in Holles Street, Cavendish Square, Pendennis transported his wife in a chaise and pair, introducing her to the theaters, parks, and royal chapel. He showered her with the pleasures of the town and distributed calling cards to his former patrons, including Lord Pontypool and the Earls of Bareacres. While Bareacres ignored the card, Lord Pontypool called, admiring Mrs. Pendennis and arranging a future concert. The Ribstones, too, invited them to an entertainment of which Mr. Pendennis would boast until the day of his death.

Mr. Pendennis's secret ambition had always been to become a genuine gentleman. Through frugality, prudence, and a stroke of luck involving copper-mining shares, he amassed enough capital to purchase a house and small estate near Clavering. He sold his Bath business to Mr. Parkins, retiring with a handsome annuity. By this time, his son, Arthur, was eight years old, a fact that allowed the elder Pendennis to largely forget the days of compounding pills and dirtying his hands. He abandoned the black breeches and stockings, adopting the attire of an English squire: a bottle-green coat, brass buttons, and drab gaiters. He stood at his lodge gate, bowing to coaches, and founded the Clavering Book Club and the Samaritan Soup and Blanket Society. He redirected the mail route through his village and became an active vestryman and market-goer, inspecting livestock with a knowing air.

Though his income of eight hundred pounds a year prevented him from mingling with the county's aristocracy, he cultivated a comfortable circle of the "second-best" sort. Their society smelled faintly of gentility, featuring moonlight dinners and festivals. Nearby, the ruins of Clavering Park, once the seat of the powerful Sir Francis Clavering, lay desolate, its palatial halls crumbling and moss-covered. From the lawn of Fairoaks, Pendennis's estate, one could behold a picturesque scene: the golden tinge of the setting sun upon the lawns, the noisy river Brawl, and the purple silhouette of Clavering Abbey's towers. Here, young Arthur and his mother would walk, casting long blue shadows, reciting poetry and exchanging embraces, for to love and pray were Mrs. Pendennis's primary duties.

At home, John Pendennis commanded absolute respect, his orders obeyed with the rigor of the Medes and Persians. Meals were served punctually, prayers recited, and stables inspected at regular intervals. After dinner, he napped with *The Globe* newspaper and a yellow bandanna handkerchief, gifts from his brother, Major Pendennis, who had returned from India. The Major, a retired soldier on half-pay, was a frequent visitor, bringing news of the London fashion and tales of his refusal to marry the wealthy Miss Hunkle, preferring the company of the titled over the accumulation of wealth. The family adored the Major, viewing him as the link to the great world, while Mrs. Pendennis, a woman of tranquil beauty and innate dignity, was revered by all.

Mrs. Pendennis's influence extended to her son, Arthur. In childhood, he viewed her as a supernatural being of wisdom and love, a perfection that enraged lesser country ladies. However, nature permits no human to be faultless; the very virtues of Mrs. Pendennis generated a vice: pride. She regarded her husband with the reverence due a pope and her son with an idolatrous ardor. This "idol-worship," as the narrator termed it, proved to be the genesis of Arthur's future misfortunes.

At Greyfriars School, Arthur was neither a dunce nor a scholar, performing exactly what was required. He excelled in verse composition, writing just enough to meet regulations, unlike his more prolific peers. He devoured novels and plays outside school hours, spent his money lavishly on tarts, and lived on credit when necessary.
