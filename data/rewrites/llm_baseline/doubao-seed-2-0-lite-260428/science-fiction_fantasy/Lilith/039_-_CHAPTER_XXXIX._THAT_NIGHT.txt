The children passed a night tangled in unrest, and carried a wild, unshakable tale of its terrors out into the dawn. No one could untangle what bled over between their waking fear and the nightmares that dragged them under: awake or asleep, no moment slipped free of that lurking dread. All night long, something moved in the walls of the cottage—silent, terrible, locked away from their knowing. No sound ever rose to wake them; the darkness and the silence were one, and that silence itself was the terror. They swore a wind that shook the whole house like a shivering horse’s pelt filled every empty space, yet it made not a single moan in their chamber, and passed like a sob that never found a voice. They slipped into sleep, then jolted wide awake certain the house was flooding with the same sweet water they had drunk at Mara’s table: it rose from the foundations, swelled higher and higher, until the garret brimmed up to the very roof. It made no more sound than that silent wind, and when it receded they woke dry, warm, and shivering.
The next time they opened their eyes, every inch of air inside and outside the cottage was thrumming with cats. They swarmed up the walls, across the floors, over the covers of their bed, and the children felt their sharp tiny claws pricking through the soft nightgowns Lady Mara had dressed them in—yet not a single scratch marked any of their skin when the sun came up. Through the whole dark, the only sound that broke was a far-off, guttural howl, the cry of the enormous ancient great-grandmother cat that roamed the desert. The children were sure she was calling her brood, for that instant every last cat went still, and the silence snapped back over them again. They fell fast asleep not long after, and did not wake until the first pink of sunrise streaked the sky.

That was the children’s story. But I never left the side of the veiled Mara and the princess Lilith through the whole long night. Some of what unfolded I saw with my own eyes; more I felt thrumming in my bones; and there were layers of it no eye could ever pierce, no heart could ever fully grasp.
The second Mara herded the children out of the room, I spotted the white leopardess we had thought we left far behind us, cowering in the furthest corner, every muscle locked in mortal terror of what she knew was coming. A lamp stood atop the high chimney-piece, and at times the room swam with long, wavering lamp-shadows, at others with vague, cloudy unbodied forms drifting at the edges of sight. Lilith lay motionless on the settle pushed against the wall, as if she had not moved a finger or shifted an inch in hours. It was a waiting thick with fear.
When Mara stepped back into the room, she dragged the settle bearing Lilith’s prone body to the very center of the chamber, then sat across from me on the opposite side of the hearth, where a small, low fire burned between us. Something terrible was drawing nearer. The cloudy presences flickered and shook. A silvery creature shaped like a slowworm crawled out from their mist, crossed the clay floor slow as molasses, and slipped straight into the heart of the fire. We all sat motionless. The hours dragged on, midnight creeping closer, and still no change came. The night was so still not a rustle stirred the flame, not a crack split a board or beam. Now and then I felt a slow, heavy heave—but I could not tell if it rippled through the earth, the air, the dark waters under the ground, my own body, or my soul. A dreadful weight of coming judgment settled over me, but I felt no fear: I had ceased to care for anything but the thing that had to be done.
Then midnight struck.
The veiled woman rose, turned toward the settle, and slowly unwound the long swathes of cloth that hid her face. They pooled soft on the floor, and she stepped over them. Lilith’s feet pointed toward the hearth; Mara went to the head of the settle, turned, and stood behind it. That was when I saw her face. It was lovely beyond words—pale, sorrowful, sorrow rooted so deep in her heart and soul it could never curdle into unhappiness. Great tears rolled down her cheeks, which she wiped away with the hem of her robe. Her face settled into a perfect stillness, and she wept no more. But for the pity written in every line of her expression, she would have looked stern, almost unyielding. She laid her hand on Lilith’s hair, which grew low on her sallow forehead, stooped, and breathed on that cold brow. The withered body shuddered.
“Will you turn away from the wicked things you have done so long?” Mara asked, her voice soft as a lullaby.
Lilith did not answer. Mara repeated the question, same gentle, inviting tone. Still no sign that she had heard. She spoke the words a third time. Then the seeming corpse parted her lips, and words came that were not shaped of sound at all, but settled clear and whole in my mind:
“I will not. I will be myself, and no other.”
“Alas, you are another now, not your true self. Will you not be restored to the one you were made to be?”
“I will be what I choose to call myself now.”
“If you were made whole again, would you not make what amends you can for all the misery you have caused?”
“I will act after my own nature.”
“You do not know your own nature. Your nature is good. You only choose evil now.”
“I will do as my Self pleases—as my Self desires.”
“You will do as the Shadow overshadowing your true Self moves you to do.”
“I will do whatever I will to do.”
“You have killed your own daughter, Lilith.”
“I have killed thousands. She was mine to take.”
“She was never yours, any more than you are another’s.”
“I belong to no one but myself. My daughter is mine.”
“Then alas—your hour has come.”
“I do not care. I am what I am. No one can take myself from me.”
“You are not the Self you imagine yourself to be.”
“So long as I feel myself to be what it pleases me to think I am, I do not care. My own thought makes me me. My own idea of myself is me. No other will make me!”
“But another made you, and can compel you to see what you have made of yourself. Soon you will no longer be able to pretend to yourself that you are anything but what He sees you. You will not much longer find satisfaction in your own idea of who you are. You feel that change coming now, do you not?”
“No one ever made me. I defy that Power to unmake me, a free woman! You are His slave, and I defy you! You may be able to torture me, but you will never force me to do anything against my will!”
“Such compulsion would be worthless. But there is a light that runs deeper than the will, that lights up the dark behind it. That light can change your will, make it truly yours—no longer the Shadow’s. The creating Will can pour itself into the created, and redeem it.”
“That light will never enter me. I hate it! Begone, slave!”
“I am no slave. I love that light, and will alongside the deeper will that made mine. There is no slave but the creature that wills against its creator. Who is a slave but she who cries ‘I am free’ yet cannot stop existing?”
“You speak foolishness from a cowering heart! You think I am in your power. I defy you. I hold myself against you. You cannot change what I choose to be. I will never be what you say I am!”
“I am sorry. You must suffer.”
“—But be free?”
“She alone is free who would set others free. She who would enslave does not love freedom. She is a slave herself. Every life, every will, every heart that ever crossed your path you tried to subdue. You are the slave of every slave you ever made—so deep a slave you do not even know it. Now see your own self.”
Mara stepped back two paces from the settle. A soundless presence, like the roar of a thousand unseen flames, filled every corner of the house—the same force that had felt to the children like that silent wind. I turned involuntarily to the hearth, and saw the fire was nothing but a still, small, motionless glow. The silvery slowworm crawled out of it now, white-hot, bright as incandescent silver, the living beating heart of primal fire. It crept slow across the floor, toward the settle, then up its wooden legs, and laid itself at Lilith’s bare bony feet, as if reluctant to go further. No pain crossed her face. The wood of the settle did not scorch where it lay. It crept slow up her robe, to her bosom, and vanished into the folds. Lilith’s face stayed stone-calm, eyelids closed as if over dead eyes. For long minutes nothing happened.
Then fine dewdrops beaded on her dry parchment skin, swelled to the size of seed pearls, ran together, and poured down her face in streams. I lunged forward to snatch the glowing worm from her withered chest and crush it under my foot, but Mara stepped between us, and drew open the edges of her robe. There was no serpent there, no searing burn mark. The creature had slipped straight through the center of the black mark on her chest, piercing through joints and marrow, straight to the secret core of her heart. Lilith gave one great writhing shudder.
“She is seeing herself,” Mara said, laying a hand on my arm to draw me three paces back from the settle.
All at once Lilith arched her whole body upward like a bow, then sprang to her feet, standing straight. The horror on her face made me tremble, terrified she would open her eyes and the sight of them would shatter me. Her bosom heaved but no breath came out. Her hair hung heavy, dripping with the sweat of her torment, then stood out from her head, sparking, then fell again to pour that bitter sweat on the floor. I would have thrown my arms around her, but Mara stopped me.
“You cannot go near her,” she said. “She is far, far away from us, lost in the hell of her own unvarnished self-consciousness. The central fire of the universe is flooding her now with the full knowledge of good and evil, the full knowledge of exactly what she is. She sees now the good she never became, and the evil she chose. She knows she herself is the fire she is burning in, though she does not yet know the Light of Life is the heart of that same fire. Her torment is that she cannot escape being what she has made herself. Do not fear for her. She is not forsaken. No gentler path to healing was left. Wait and watch.”
I could not tell if five minutes or five years passed before she finally flung herself face down on the floor. Mara went to stand over her, and large tears fell from her eyes onto the woman who had never once wept of her own will.
“Will you change your way?” Mara asked at last.
“Why did He make me like this?” Lilith gasped. “I would have made myself so different! I am glad He made me and not I myself! He is the only one to blame for what I am! He meant me to know how worthless I am, to be miserable! I will not be made any longer!”
“Then unmake yourself,” Mara said softly.
“Alas, I cannot! You know it, you mock me! I have agonized a thousand times to cease to exist, but that tyrant forces me to stay! I curse Him! Now let Him kill me!” The words came in gushes, like water spouting from a dying fountain.
“If He had not made you at all,” Mara said, slow and gentle, “you would not even have the power to hate Him. But He did not make you like this. You made yourself what you are. Take heart. He can remake you.”
“I will not be remade!”
“He will not change you. He will only restore you to what you were at the first.”
“I will not be anything He made.”
“Are you not willing to set right all the wrong you have done?”
She lay silent, and the edge of her suffering seemed to soften.
“If you are willing, climb back onto the settle.”
“I will not,” she forced out, through clenched teeth.
A wind woke inside the cottage, blowing without sound, without weight, and a silent water began to rise that had no lap to its ripples, no sob in its swell. It was cold, but it did not numb. It touched no one of my senses, but I knew it was rising. I watched it lift Lilith, float her, unable to resist, and set her soft on the settle before it sank swiftly away.
The strife in her soul began anew, thoughts of accusation and excuse warring fiercer than before. Lilith’s soul lay bare to the torture of that pure penetrating inner light. She began to moan, to sigh deep ragged sighs, then murmur as if talking to a split, divided part of herself. Her old queendom was no longer whole; it was split against itself. One moment she exulted as if she had defeated her worst enemy, and wept; the next she writhed as if caught in the embrace of a friend she hated, and laughed like a demon. At last she began to tell a story of herself, in a language so strange and full of shadowy forms I could only catch fragments here and there. It felt like the oldest, first form of a language I knew well, and the memories it wove belonged to old half-forgotten dreams I had once had, that I never could quite recall. Bits of it touched on the scattered pages Adam had read from the broken manuscript, and referenced forces, vices, old influences I had no name for. When she fell silent, her hair sparked and dripped with that tormented sweat again.
I gave Mara a pleading look.
“Those are not the tears of repentance,” she said. “True tears gather in the eyes. These are far more bitter, and not half so healing. Self-loathing is not sorrow. But it is a step on the road home. When the prodigal son falls into his father’s arms, he forgets the self he loathes. It will be the same for her.”
She stepped closer. “Will you restore everything you wrongfully took?”
“I have taken nothing,” Lilith forced out through her pain, “that I did not have the right to take. My power to take it was proof of my right.”
Mara stepped back. Gradually I became aware of an invisible darkness, more terrible than anything we had faced that night. A horrible positive Nothingness, a living Negation, folded around Lilith. The edge of its not-being brushed me, and for one ghastly instant I stood alone with Absolute Death. It was not the absence of everything I felt, but the presence of Nothing.
Lilith hurled herself from the settle to the floor with a great, bitter scream. It was the recoil of all Being from total Annihilation.
“For pity’s sake,” she shrieked, “tear my heart out—but let me live!”
At once a perfect calm fell over her, over all of us, soft as a summer night. Suffering had almost filled the brim of her life’s cup, and some unseen hand had tipped it empty. She raised her head, half rose, and looked around. For a moment she stood straight, with the air of a conqueror: she thought she had won. She had faced every spiritual foe, and beaten them. She raised her withered arm above her head, a song of unholy triumph rising in her throat—when her eyes froze wide in a ghastly stare.
What was she seeing?
I looked, and saw. Cast from an unseen mirror in heaven, two figures stood before her: the reflection of the twisted, withered thing she had made of herself, and beside it a form of blinding, splendent beauty—the self she had been created to be. She trembled, and sank helpless back to the floor. She knew both now.
The rest of the night she lay completely motionless. When the gray dawn seeped into the room, she rose, turned to Mara, and spoke with a strange prideful humility.
“You have conquered. Let me go into the wilderness and bewail myself.”
Mara saw her submission was not feigned—but it was not yet real. She regarded her a long moment, and said, “Then start by setting right the first wrong you did.”
“I do not know how,” Lilith said, and there was fear in her face, as if she already knew what the answer would be.
“Open your hand. Let the thing you have been clutching tight for all these years go.”
A fierce refusal fought to burst out of her, but she held it prisoner.
“I cannot. I no longer have the power. Open it for me.” She held out her hand, which looked more like a clawed paw than a human hand. It was obvious she could not force it open.
Mara did not even glance down at it. “You must open it yourself,” she said, quiet and steady.
“I told you I cannot!”
“You can, if you will. Not all at once, but with slow, persistent effort. What you did with it, you do not yet truly wish undone—you do not yet intend to undo it.”
“You think that, no doubt,” Lilith snapped, a flash of her old insolence breaking through, “but I know for a fact I cannot open my hand!”
“I know you better than you know yourself. I know you can. You have already opened it just a little, many times before. It will hurt, it will be hard, but you can pry it all the way open. At worst, you could beat it open until the fist unclenches. I beg you: gather your strength, and open it wide.”
“I will not waste effort on something I know is impossible. That would be the act of a fool.”
“Which you have played your whole life! Oh, how hard you are to teach!”
Defiance flooded back onto her face. She turned her back on Mara. “I know what you have been tormenting me for! You have not succeeded, and you never will. You will find I am far stronger than you think. I will still be mistress of myself! I am still what I have always known myself to be—queen of Hell, and mistress of all worlds!”
