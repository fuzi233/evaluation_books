Elinor’s curiosity to meet Mrs. Ferrars was now fully satisfied. The encounter had revealed everything she needed to know—indeed, more than she wished to know. In Mrs. Ferrars’s demeanor, she saw not only pride and coldness, but a narrow-mindedness and prejudice so fixed that Elinor could now understand the obstacles Edward had faced in their engagement. She realized how much harder it would have been for him to marry her had he not already been bound to Lucy. And yet, even with that knowledge, she found herself grateful—for her own sake—that fate had spared her from such a connection. It was not joy she felt at Edward’s entanglement, but relief. At least, she told herself, if Lucy had been kinder, gentler, more deserving, then she ought to have rejoiced.

She could not help wondering, though, how Lucy could be so easily flattered by mere civility. How could her vanity blind her so completely? Mrs. Ferrars had treated her with politeness—not affection, not favor—but merely because she was not Elinor. Yet Lucy interpreted this as a sign of preference, as if the attention were meant solely for her. Elinor had seen it in her eyes the moment they met—and the next morning, when Lady Middleton dropped her off in Berkeley Street, Lucy confirmed it plainly enough. She came alone, hoping to find Elinor unaccompanied, and she did. A message from Mrs. Palmer soon sent Mrs. Jennings away, leaving them alone.

“My dear friend,” Lucy exclaimed as soon as they were by themselves, “I’ve come to tell you about my happiness! Wasn’t it wonderful—Mrs. Ferrars’s kindness yesterday? So warm, so gracious! You know how I feared meeting her, but the moment I was introduced, she seemed to take an instant liking to me. Don’t you see it? You saw it all—didn’t you feel it too?”

“She was certainly very civil,” Elinor replied carefully.

“Civil?” Lucy laughed. “Did you see nothing beyond that? I saw something far more—kindness meant only for me! No hauteur, no coldness—just sweetness. And your sister? She was just as lovely, just as welcoming. They’re both such charming women—so delightful! I can’t believe you never speak of how pleasant Mrs. Dashwood is.”

Elinor said nothing. There was no answer she could give without betraying her true feelings—or her growing sorrow.

“Are you ill, Miss Dashwood?” Lucy asked suddenly, leaning forward. “You look pale. You’re not speaking—surely you’re not unwell?”

“I’ve never felt better,” Elinor replied, forcing a smile.

“I’m glad,” Lucy said, sincere in her concern. “I’d hate to think you unwell—you’ve been my greatest comfort. Heaven knows what I’d do without your friendship.” She paused, then added, “And I know you care for me. Next to Edward’s love, it’s the greatest comfort I have. Poor Edward! But now there’s one good thing—we’ll be able to see each other often. Lady Middleton adores Mrs. Dashwood, so we’ll be in Harley Street frequently. And Edward spends half his time with his sister. Besides, Mrs. Ferrars and your sister both said they’d be delighted to see me again—more than once! Such kind, generous women! If you ever tell your sister what I think of her, you must praise her as highly as possible.”

Elinor gave no promise. She would not encourage Lucy to believe she would reveal anything.

“I would have known at once if Mrs. Ferrars disliked me,” Lucy continued. “If she had given me only a formal bow, said nothing, and never looked at me again—I’d have known it instantly. I couldn’t have borne it. When she dislikes someone, it’s absolute. But she didn’t—she liked me. I know she did.”

Before Elinor could respond, the door opened. The servant announced Mr. Ferrars, and Edward stepped in.

The moment was awkward—unbearably so. Each of them felt it in their faces, in their silence. They were all three together, alone, with no one else to ease the tension. The very situation they had all hoped to avoid had come to pass.

Lucy, composed and demure, made no move to speak. She allowed her emotions to show only in her eyes—soft, tender, full of quiet triumph. She offered Edward a brief greeting and said no more.

But Elinor had more to do. For Edward’s sake, for her own dignity, she forced herself to act with grace. After a moment’s pause, she welcomed him with a calmness that surprised even herself. Her voice was steady, her manner almost natural. She told him she was pleased to see him, and expressed regret that she had missed him when he visited before. She refused to let Lucy’s presence or her own pain stop her from showing the kindness due to a friend—almost a brother.

Edward, though still uneasy, sat down. His embarrassment was deeper than theirs, for he carried the weight of guilt and longing. He had no indifference like Lucy’s, nor the composure Elinor managed with effort.

Lucy remained silent. She would not contribute to the conversation. Every word spoken came from Elinor—about their mother’s health, their arrival in town, their plans. Edward barely asked a question, as if afraid to open the floodgates of feeling.

Then, Elinor made a bold decision. Pretending to fetch Marianne, she left the room. She lingered on the landing, her heart pounding, her resolve firm. She stayed long enough to give Edward and Lucy a few moments alone—then returned.

Marianne burst into the drawing-room immediately. Her joy was immediate and intense. She rushed to Edward, taking his hand, her voice bright with emotion.

“Dear Edward!” she cried. “This is a moment of great happiness—almost enough to make up for everything!”

Edward tried to return her warmth, but under Lucy’s watchful gaze, he could not say all he felt.

They sat again. Silence fell, broken only by Marianne’s quiet glances—first at Edward, then at Elinor—her eyes full of tenderness and sorrow that their reunion should be marred by Lucy’s presence.

Edward broke the silence first. “You look different, Miss Marianne,” he said gently. “I hope London agrees with you.”

“Oh, don’t think of me!” she replied, her voice trembling. “Don’t worry about my health. Elinor is well—you see that. That’s enough for us both.”

Her words were meant to comfort, but they only deepened the unease. Lucy glanced up, her expression cool.

“Do you like London?” Edward asked, eager to change the subject.

“Not at all,” Marianne answered. “I expected pleasure, but found none. Only seeing you, Edward, has brought me any comfort. Thank Heaven, you are still the same.”

She paused. No one spoke.

Then she added, “We must ask Edward to accompany us back to Barton. We’ll likely leave in a week or two. I trust he won’t mind taking charge of us.”

Edward muttered something incoherent—no one knew what.

Marianne smiled. “I see you’re nervous. But don’t worry—there’s much I want to tell you, but not now.”

She paused, then said, “Why weren’t you at Harley Street yesterday? Why didn’t you come?”

“I was engaged elsewhere.”

“Engaged? But what could be more important than meeting friends?”

“Perhaps,” Lucy interrupted quickly, “you think young men never keep engagements unless they want to. Small ones as well as big.”

Elinor frowned. But Marianne seemed untouched.

“No,” she said calmly, “I believe Edward kept his promise because he couldn’t break it. He has the most delicate conscience in the world—the most scrupulous about every commitment, no matter how small. He fears giving pain more than anyone I’ve ever known. He cannot be selfish. Edward, it’s true. And I will say it. What! Are you never to hear yourself praised? Then you are no friend of mine. Those who accept my love must also accept my praise.”

Her words were meant to honor Edward—but they stung both Lucy and Elinor. Edward rose abruptly.

“Going so soon?” Marianne asked, drawing him aside. “My dear Edward, this must not be.”

She whispered, “Lucy won’t stay long. You’ll be free soon.”

But Edward insisted on leaving. Lucy, who would have stayed longer, departed shortly after.

“What brings her here so often?” Marianne asked once they were alone. “Couldn’t she see we wanted her gone? How tiresome for Edward!”

“But why not?” Elinor replied. “She’s known him longest. It’s natural he should wish to see her as much as us.”

Marianne looked at her steadily.

“You know, Elinor,” she said, “I cannot bear this kind of talk. If you expect me to contradict you, I won’t. I won’t be tricked into denying what I know isn’t true. I cannot pretend to believe things I don’t.”

And with that, she walked out.

Elinor did not follow. She could not explain. Her promise to Lucy bound her. She could not reveal the truth, even if it meant watching Marianne suffer from a mistaken belief.

All she could hope for was that Edward would not be forced to endure such painful meetings again—and that, in time, the truth might find its way to light, without causing greater harm.
