Elinor's curiosity regarding Mrs. Ferrars was finally sated. Having observed everything necessary to confirm that a deeper alliance between their families was impossible, she had witnessed firsthand the depth of her aunt-in-law's pride, her pettiness, and her stubborn prejudice against herself. These traits illuminated the formidable obstacles that had long complicated the engagement between Edward and Elinor, had circumstances permitted it. Indeed, having seen nearly enough to appreciate, for her own sake, that a higher barrier protected her from the torment of depending on Mrs. Ferrars' whims or seeking her approval, Elinor resolved to be grateful. While she did not find herself exulting in Edward's entanglement with Lucy, she firmly decided that, had Lucy possessed any genuine amiability, she *ought* to have celebrated it.

Yet, she marveled at how Lucy's spirits could remain so buoyant despite the cold civility of Mrs. Ferrars. How could her vanity be so blinded that she mistook attention paid solely because she was *not* Elinor for personal compliments? How could she draw encouragement from a preference granted merely because her true position remained hidden? These truths had been evident in Lucy's gaze during the previous evening and were confirmed the following morning with startling clarity. At Lucy's specific request, Lady Middleton had seated her beside Elinor in Berkeley Street, ostensibly to discuss matters, but truly to provide an opportunity for Lucy to declare her happiness privately. Fate intervened kindly; shortly after Elinor's arrival, a message from Mrs. Palmer whisked Mrs. Jennings away, leaving the two sisters alone.

"My dear friend," Lucy exclaimed the moment they were secluded, "I must speak to you of my bliss. Was there ever anything so flattering as Mrs. Ferrars's demeanor toward me yesterday? She was so excessively affable! You know how I dreaded the thought of encountering her, yet the instant I was introduced, her behavior radiated such favor that it seemed she had instantly taken a fancy to me. Does it not strike you as true? You witnessed it all; surely you were equally impressed?"

"She was certainly very civil to you," Elinor replied cautiously.

"Civil!" Lucy retorted sharply. "Did you perceive nothing beyond mere politeness? I saw far more. A kindness reserved exclusively for me! No pride, no haughtiness—unlike your sister, who remains exactly the same, all sweetness and charm!"

Elinor attempted to steer the conversation elsewhere, but Lucy persisted, demanding acknowledgment of her reasons for happiness. Reluctantly, Elinor complied.

"Undoubtedly, if they knew of your engagement," Lucy pressed, "their treatment would seem even more flattering. But since that is not the case—"

"I suspected you would say that," Lucy interrupted quickly. "However, there is absolutely no reason for Mrs. Ferrars to appear fond of me unless she is, and her affection is everything. Do not try to dissuade me from my contentment. I am certain it will conclude happily, devoid of the difficulties I once feared. Mrs. Ferrars is a charming woman, and so is your sister. They are both delightful! I wonder I never heard you praise how agreeable Mrs. Dashwood was!"

Elinor had no response to offer and offered none.

"Are you ill, Miss Dashwood?" Lucy asked, noting her silence. "You seem listless; you do not speak. Are you unwell?"

"I have never been in better health," Elinor assured her.

"I am glad to hear it with all my heart, though you certainly did not look it. I would be sorrowful to lose your friendship; heaven knows what I should have done without you."

Elinor managed a polite reply, though she doubted its efficacy. Yet, it appeared to satisfy Lucy, who immediately responded, "Indeed, I am fully convinced of your regard for me. Next to Edward's love, it is my greatest comfort. Poor Edward! But now, there is one bright prospect: we shall be able to meet frequently. Lady Middleton delights in Mrs. Dashwood, so we shall likely spend much time in Harley Street. Besides, Edward spends half his time with his sister, and Lady Middleton and Mrs. Ferrars will begin visiting soon. Both Mrs. Ferrars and your sister have stated repeatedly how glad they would be to see me. They are such charming women! If you ever tell your sister what I think of her, you cannot praise her too highly."

Elinor refused to encourage Lucy's hopes that she would ever speak so freely to her sister. Undeterred, Lucy continued, "I am certain I would have noticed instantly if Mrs. Ferrars disliked me. Had she merely offered a formal courtesy, ignored me thereafter, and avoided eye contact with a pleasant expression—as you understand—I would have despaired completely. I could not endure it. Where she dislikes, I know her aversion is fierce."

Before Elinor could respond to this triumphant assessment of her rival's acumen, the door opened, and a servant announced Mr. Ferrars. Moments later, Edward entered.

The atmosphere grew instantly tense. Each face betrayed the awkwardness of the situation. They appeared collectively foolish, and Edward seemed torn between fleeing the room and advancing further inside. It was precisely the uncomfortable convergence they had all sought to avoid. Not only were the three of them together, but they were isolated without the buffer of any other guest.

The ladies recovered composure first. It was not Lucy's role to assert dominance, and the facade of secrecy required maintenance. She could only display her tenderness through her gaze, offering a slight greeting before falling silent. Elinor, however, faced a greater challenge. Driven by anxiety for Edward's sake and her own, she compelled herself, after a brief pause, to greet him with a demeanor that was nearly natural and open. Through renewed effort, she succeeded. She would not let Lucy's presence or her own sense of injustice prevent her from expressing her joy at seeing him and voicing her regret at missing his earlier call in Berkeley Street. She would not allow Lucy's watchful eyes to deter her from offering the attentions due to a friend and near relative, even as she sensed Lucy scrutinizing her every move.

Edward's manners received some reassurance from Elinor's efforts, granting him the courage to sit, though his embarrassment surpassed theirs in proportion to the absurdity of the scene—a rarity perhaps for men, but understandable given the circumstances. His heart lacked Lucy's indifference, and his conscience could not find the peace Elinor enjoyed. Lucy, maintaining a demure and fixed expression, contributed nothing to alleviate the tension, remaining utterly silent. Consequently, nearly all conversation flowed from Elinor, who volunteered details about her mother's health and their journey to town—information Edward should have inquired about but neglected to ask.

Her efforts did not end there. Soon, feeling a heroic impulse, she decided, under the pretext of fetching Marianne, to withdraw the others to privacy. She executed this maneuver with remarkable grace, lingering on the landing for several minutes with dignified resolve before descending to her sister. Once separated, however, Edward's rapture ceased abruptly. Marianne's enthusiasm drew her immediately into the drawing-room. Her joy in seeing him was intense and vocal, characteristic of her nature. Meeting him with an outstretched hand and a voice brimming with affection, she cried, "Dear Edward! This is a moment of profound happiness! It would almost redeem everything!"

Edward endeavored to reciprocate her kindness appropriately, yet before such witnesses, he dared not utter half of what he truly felt. They resumed their seats, and for a few moments, silence reigned. Marianne gazed alternately at Edward and Elinor with tender intensity, lamenting only that their shared delight was dampened by Lucy's unwanted presence.

Edward broke the silence first, commenting on Marianne's changed complexion and expressing concern that London might not agree with her constitution.

"Oh, do not think of me!" she replied with spirited earnestness, though tears filled her eyes. "Do not consider *my* health. See, Elinor is well. That must suffice for us both."

This remark offered no solace to Edward or Elinor, nor did it win Lucy's goodwill, whose gaze toward Marianne held no benevolence.

"Do you enjoy London?" Edward asked, attempting to shift the topic.

"Not at all. I anticipated great pleasure, yet found none. The sight of you, Edward, is the sole comfort it has provided. Thank Heaven, you remain as you always were!" She paused, and no one spoke.

"I believe, Elinor," Marianne added shortly, "we must entrust Edward with our care upon returning to Barton. In a week or two, I presume, we shall depart. I trust Edward will not be overly reluctant to accept this responsibility."

Poor Edward murmured indistinctly, unsure even himself of what he said. Marianne, perceiving his agitation and interpreting it according to her own inclinations, was perfectly satisfied and swiftly changed the subject.

"We spent such a dreadful day in Harley Street yesterday! So dull, so wretchedly dull! But I have much to tell you about it that cannot wait until now." With admirable discretion, she deferred her declaration that their mutual relations were increasingly disagreeable and her particular disgust for his mother until they were alone.

"But why were you not there, Edward? Why did you not come?"

"I was engaged elsewhere."

"Engaged! But what engagement outweighs the meeting of such friends?"

"Perhaps, Miss Marianne," cried Lucy, seizing the opportunity to retaliate, "you believe young men disregard engagements whenever they choose, regardless of their size?"
