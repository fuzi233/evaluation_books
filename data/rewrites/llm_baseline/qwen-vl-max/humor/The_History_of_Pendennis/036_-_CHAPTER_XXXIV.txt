CHAPTER XXXIV. In Which the History Takes a Turn for the Better—And Possibly the More Comic

Captain Shandon, under the relentless encouragement of his wife (who, it must be said, rarely interfered in matters of business unless they involved her husband’s imprisonment), had insisted that John Finucane, Esquire, of the Upper Temple, be appointed sub-editor of the soon-to-be-launched *Pall Mall Gazette*. And so it was done—by the ever-spirited proprietor of the Journal, who, though not exactly known for his generosity, couldn’t resist doing a favor for a man whose loyalty was as thick as Irish stew and whose gratitude was as loud as a foghorn at midnight.

Now, Mr. Finucane was no ordinary man. He was the kind of fellow who could hide a fugitive from justice in his chambers with such discretion that even the bailiffs eventually started showing up like clockwork—right on the stairs, as if they’d been invited to tea. It was in these very chambers that poor Mrs. Shandon would often come, veiled and trembling, to pour out her woes, her fears, and her desperate schemes for rescuing her beloved Captain from the clutches of debt and legal machinery. And when she left, Finucane would lean over the banister, peering down the staircase like a knight awaiting a dragon—or perhaps a rogue named Temple Lovelace, whom he fervently hoped might attack her so he could rush forward, break his bones, and earn eternal glory in the hearts of all decent men.

But this wasn’t just about heroics. It was also about meals. Many a dinner did Finucane provide for Mrs. Shandon and little Mary, who occupied a crib in the room and prayed every night that God might bless papa—and to which Finucane, though a devout Roman Catholic himself, always added a heartfelt “Amen,” as if he were personally guaranteeing divine intervention.

That evening, however, duty called. Finucane had an appointment with Mr. Bungay, publisher of the *Daily Journal*, to discuss the finer points of newspaper management—over dinner, naturally. He arrived at Dick’s Coffee-house at precisely six o’clock, having already cut his mutton (as Mr. Bungay phrased it) with the literary man of business, Mr. Trotter, and had spent the previous day outlining the entire editorial structure of the *Pall Mall Gazette* with the precision of a watchmaker and the confidence of a man who knew he was worth every penny.

He laid out the typefaces, assigned reporters to markets, turf, church gossip, and fashion columns (the latter being, he said, “a delicate art requiring both wit and patience”), and introduced Bungay to a network of informants so vast and intricate that the publisher began to suspect Finucane might have been running a spy ring on the side. But no—he was simply one of the best sub-editors in London, according to Shandon himself, and according to Finucane, who proudly agreed.

Finucane dazzled Bungay with calculations so rapid and complex that the publisher blinked twice and muttered, “Well, I’ll be hanged.” He then admitted to Mr. Trotter, “That Irishman seems a clever fellow.”

And now came the real purpose of the meeting: the liberation of Captain Shandon. Finucane knew, to the shilling, how much the Captain owed at the Fleet Prison porter’s lodge. He didn’t know *everything*—no man did—but he claimed to. He pointed out the Captain’s existing contracts, argued that confinement stifled productivity (though Bungay countered, “When he’s locked up, we know where to find him. When he’s free, he’s vanished like a ghost!”), and finally, with tears in his eyes and a voice like a foghorn, described Mrs. Shandon wasting away and little Mary growing weak from prison air.

It worked. Bungay, softened by emotion and possibly the second round of brandy-and-water, promised that if Mrs. Shandon came to him the next morning, he’d see what could be done.

And so, on Saturday morning, Finucane appeared at the Fleet Prison, dressed in his finest clothes—cheap but dazzling, like a rainbow made of rags—and with four pounds two shillings in his pocket—the full week’s salary from the *Daily Journal*, minus two shillings spent on gloves (which, he assured himself, were essential for gentlemanly conduct).

He gave Mrs. Shandon a wink so knowing and joyful that she immediately suspected good news. She hurriedly donned her bonnet and shawl, while Finucane offered to escort them on a walk—“for fresh air,” he said, though everyone knew it was really to escape the prison’s depressing atmosphere and perhaps avoid a sudden death by boredom.

“May I go, Charley?” she asked. “Or shall I stay? You’re poorly today, dear.”
“He’s got a headache,” Finucane informed her. “He suffers from headaches. I persuaded him to stay in bed.”
“Go along with you, and Polly,” Shandon said cheerfully, handing over Burton’s *Anatomy*—a book he used not for medical purposes, but for quoting Latin and Greek phrases in his articles, as if he were a scholar rather than a man who once tried to sell a horse three times before realizing it was dead.

So off they went—Mrs. Shandon, little Mary, and Finucane—through the prison gates into the free world. From Fleet Street to Paternoster Row is not far, but it felt like a journey across continents.

At Mr. Bungay’s shop, Mrs. Bungay herself entered through the private door, holding a paper parcel and a manuscript volume bound in red leather—containing, it turned out, a detailed account of her butcher transactions. She wore a shot-silk dress that screamed “I am rich and flamboyant,” a yellow shawl, red flowers in her bonnet, and a light blue parasol that looked like it belonged to a circus performer.

Mrs. Shandon, in contrast, wore an old black watered silk dress and a bonnet that had seen better days—like its owner. But still, she looked like a lady. Always a lady.

They curtsied to each other—Mrs. Bungay with the grace of a queen, Mrs. Shandon with the humility of a saint.

“I hope you’re pretty well, mum?” said Mrs. Bungay.
“It’s a very fine day,” replied Mrs. Shandon.
“Won’t you step in, mum?” Mrs. Bungay asked, eyeing little Mary with an intensity that nearly sent the child screaming into the street.

“I—I came about business with Mr. Bungay,” stammered Mrs. Shandon. “I—I hope he’s pretty well?”

“Couldn’t you leave your little gurl with me?” Mrs. Bungay asked, holding out a finger like a villain in a melodrama.

“I want to stay with mamma!” cried little Mary, burying her face in her mother’s dress.

“Go with this lady, Mary, my dear,” said the mother gently.

“Come see some pretty pictures,” Mrs. Bungay cooed, opening her parcel to reveal choice sweet biscuits—Bungay’s favorite after wine. Little Mary followed, enchanted.

The trio ascended the stairs together. A second biscuit sealed the deal. Mary chattered away like a bird set free.

Meanwhile, below, Finucane found Bungay in a mood darker than a thunderstorm. The previous night’s port and brandy had vanished, replaced by cold logic and a wife who had rebuked him fiercely. She declared the Captain was a wastrel, the *Pall Mall Gazette* a fool’s errand, and insisted he remain in prison. “Let him work there,” she said. “It’s the best place for him.”

Finucane pleaded, promised, implored—nothing worked.

But upstairs, the situation was changing. Mrs. Shandon, with her quiet voice and gentle manner, told her story—of her husband’s virtues, her child’s illness, and the horror of sending two children to school just to keep them alive in that “horrid place.” And Mrs. Bungay, though as grim as Lady Macbeth, melted.

She descended upon her husband like a force of nature. “Mr. Finucane,” she said politely, “would you care to join your friends in the drawing-room? I need to speak with Mr. Bungay.”

Alone, she delivered her verdict: “The Capting is an Irishman,” she said. “And I’ve always said I couldn’t abide them. But his wife is a lady. A good woman. A clergyman’s daughter. And a West of England woman—just like me, by my mother’s side. And didn’t you notice the little gurl?”

“Yes, Mrs. B., I saw the little girl.”

“And didn’t you see how like she was to our angel, Bessy?”

Mrs. Bungay’s mind drifted back eighteen years—to when she and Bungay were young booksellers in a country town, and their daughter Bessy had died just short of her birthday. Tears welled in her eyes.

“Well, well,” said Mr. Bungay, seeing her distress. “The Captain ain’t in for much. Only a hundred and thirty pounds. Half that will get him out. And we’ll pay him half salaries till he clears it. When the little ’un said, ‘Why don’t you take Par out of prizn?’ I did feel it, Flora, upon my honour I did.”

And so, with a heavy heart and a lighter wallet, Mr. Bungay announced to Mrs. Shandon that sixty-five pounds would secure her husband’s release—and he would advance it, deducted from Shandon’s future salary, provided she settled directly with the creditors.

It was the happiest day in recent memory.

“Bedad, Bungay, you’re a trump!” roared Finucane, leaping about the room like a madman, tossing little Mary into the air, and shouting promises of ten thousand readers a week for the *Pall Mall Gazette*.

Mrs. Bungay offered her carriage—“If I could drive you anywhere”—and off they went, two ladies and a child, with Finucane perched triumphantly on the back seat, while the driver threw triumphant glances at Bacon’s across the street.

As Bungay returned to his desk, he sighed. “It won’t do the Captain any good,” he muttered. “But Mrs. B. gets upset when she thinks about her misfortune. The child would have been of age yesterday, if she’d lived.” And he wondered, as all men do, how women manage to remember things like that.

But happily, Mrs. Shandon succeeded. She who had once begged creditors with tears now paid them ten shillings in the pound—and they relented. And the next Sunday marked the end of Captain Shandon’s imprisonment—for now, at least.

Thus ended a chapter filled with courage, kindness, biscuits, and one very happy Irishman who believed, with perfect sincerity, that he had saved a captain, a family, and possibly the soul of journalism itself.
