The great English festival—where all of London abandons decorum for the thrill of Epsom Downs—had gathered a curious assortment of our story’s characters. Among them, in a carriage that strained under the weight of both its occupants and Mrs. Bungay’s finery (she was dressed, as one might say, in the entire contents of a haberdasher’s shop), sat the modest Mrs. Shandon, whose friendship the publisher’s wife guarded with the tenacity of a bulldog with a bone. Nearby, Mr. Bungay, having fortified himself with a lunch of heroic proportions, was hurling sticks with the reckless abandon of a man who had forgotten he possessed a bald head. Shandon, meanwhile, lurked among the gipsies and ale-sellers like a disgraced prophet, while the ever-faithful Finucane attended the ladies like a guardian who had misplaced his charge.

Enter Mr. Archer, a man whose chief talent was knowing everyone worth knowing—or at least claiming he did. With a bow that suggested he had practiced in front of a mirror, he regaled Mrs. Bungay with a stream of aristocratic gossip.

"Yonder sits the Prime Minister," he announced, as if he had personally arranged the seating. "His lordship advised me to back Borax, but I fancy Munmeer—superior breeding, you see." He then proceeded to point out dukes, ambassadors, and other lofty personages with the confidence of a man who had dined with them all—or at least stood near them at a public function.

"The Chinese Ambassador," he continued, lowering his voice as if sharing state secrets, "once dined at my table. Brought his own cook, the savage. One day, while I was out, the brute butchered my wife’s poor Blenheim spaniel—stuffed it with forced meat and served it as a second course!"

Mrs. Bungay gasped. "Law!"

"My wife was inconsolable. The Ambassador hasn’t dined with us since—though the Lord Mayor rather enjoyed the dish. Tastes like duck with green peas, if you can believe it."

"I can’t!" cried Mrs. Bungay, who was now thoroughly scandalized and delighted in equal measure.

Archer, warming to his theme, gestured toward a lady in blue. "That’s Lady Flamingo—rumored to be marrying the Ambassador and moving to Pekin. She’s binding her feet for the occasion, though I doubt she’ll manage it. My wife has the smallest foot in England—child’s size—but it’s nothing next to a Chinese lady’s."

Mrs. Bungay, ever eager for more gossip, then inquired about the carriage where Mr. Pendennis was seated. "He’s grown quite grand, hasn’t he? Still in mourning, I see."

"Eighteen hundred a year in land, twenty-two thousand in the funds," Archer declared, as if reading from Pendennis’s bank ledger. "His uncle, the Major, had me tie it up—can’t have him squandering it all on ducks and drakes."

Finucane, unimpressed, snorted. "That’s not Lord Rochester—it’s Tom Staples from the *Morning Advertiser*."

Archer blinked. "Is it? Well, I’m terribly short-sighted. That other fellow with the opera-glass? Lord John, of course. And the tall one? Sir James."

"You only know ’em from seeing ’em in the papers," Finucane muttered.

Archer ignored him. "The Duke of Hampshire never misses a Derby. Told me just yesterday he’s been to sixty-five of them—first attended on a piebald pony with the Prince of Wales. Missed only two: once for measles at Eton, once for Waterloo."

Mrs. Bungay, now thoroughly dazzled, could only gape.

Meanwhile, atop a battered stagecoach, a rowdy assembly of "gentlemen" (loosely defined) were bellowing their support for the race. When Podasokus triumphed, one particularly exuberant soul—known only as "the Colonel"—leapt up, nearly collapsing the roof in his ecstasy.

"Supper for ten, lads!" he roared. "Damn the expense!"

His companions, a collection of dubious characters who looked as if they’d been assembled from the backrooms of disreputable taverns, cheered—though several were already calculating how best to relieve the Colonel of his winnings before the night was out.

Nearby, in a humble cab, sat a far more wholesome party: a fresh-faced girl, her lobster-devouring mother, and their jovial escort, Mr. Sam Huxter. Sam, having spent the journey firing off jokes at every passerby, was now the picture of gallantry—until Arthur Pendennis happened upon them.

Fanny Bolton’s face drained of color. Mrs. Bolton babbled nervously. Sam’s fists clenched inside his pockets.

Arthur, ever the gentleman, affected not to notice the tension. "Thirsty work, the races," he remarked, accepting a glass of lukewarm beer with the grace of a man used to far finer vintages.

As he drank, a vision in dove-colored silk passed by—Blanche Amory, arm-in-arm with Captain Strong. She shot Pen a mischievous glance, while Fanny’s gaze followed her with wistful admiration.

"Who’s that?" Fanny breathed.

"A neighbor of mine," Arthur said airily. "Lady Clavering’s daughter."

Fanny’s imagination immediately wove a tragic romance—one in which she was *not* the heroine, and Sam Huxter most certainly was not the hero.

When Arthur departed, the sun seemed to dim for poor Sam. His once-jovial mood soured. Even the pea-shooter he’d brought for amusement lay forgotten.

But Fanny, ever the diplomat, coaxed him back to good humor with smiles and flattery. By the time they returned home, Sam was once more her devoted swain—though not before a passionate reconciliation involving tears, declarations, and a hasty embrace that left Mrs. Bolton flustered.

As for Arthur? He rejoined Blanche’s circle, where she teased him mercilessly about his "little country friends."

"*Je l’ai vue*," she said with a smirk. "*Elle a de bien beaux yeux. Vous êtes un monstre!*"

Pen laughed. "Monster? Hardly. She’s well protected—by her mother *and* her sweetheart."

Blanche’s eyes glittered. "One never knows what might happen when a girl has a mind of her own—and a monster like you pursuing her."

Pen’s smile faltered. There was something unsettling in her tone—something that made him think of Laura, and how differently *she* would have spoken.

But the moment passed. The races went on. And life, as it always does, carried its players forward—whether they were ready or not.
