To locate the Lady in her private sanctuary, I ascended the stairs, expecting a scene of calm resolution. Instead, I found her seated in her sitting room, her composure fractured the moment I announced the arrival of Sergeant Cuff. A flicker of irritation crossed her features, quickly hardening into a defensive resolve.

"_Must_ I see him?" she demanded, her voice tight. "Gabriel, cannot you represent me? Shield me from this intrusion?"

I stood paralyzed, unable to decipher the depth of her distress, and my confusion betrayed itself in the furrow of my brow. With a gesture of weary grace, she unveiled the source of her agitation. "My nerves are... unsettled," she confessed, her gaze dropping to the floor. "There is something in that police-officer from London—a specific, visceral recoil I cannot name. I harbor a presentiment that he carries trouble and misery into these walls. It is foolishness, entirely contrary to my nature, yet it is undeniably true."

I struggled to formulate a response. In my observations, Sergeant Cuff had grown increasingly likable, a man of quiet competence rather than menace. Yet, seeing his shadow loom over her, I understood her terror. She rallied, however, displaying the high courage inherent in her character. "If I must see him, I must," she declared, steeling herself. "But I cannot bear to face him alone. Bring him in, Gabriel, and remain here until he departs."

This marked the first onset of *megrims*—a faintness of spirit—that I had witnessed in my mistress since her girlhood. Turning back to the boudoir, I found Mr. Franklin strolling into the garden to join Mr. Godfrey, whose departure was imminent. Leaving them to their final farewells, I returned to the Lady's chamber with the Sergeant.

Upon entering, the Lady's complexion drained a shade paler, though she maintained her dignity. She requested that the Sergeant have no objection to my presence, citing my role as both her trusted adviser and ancient servant, noting that my counsel regarding household matters was invaluable. The Sergeant, with characteristic politeness, accepted my company as a favor, mentioning his intention to discuss the servants generally and acknowledging that my prior experience in that domain had proven useful to him.

We took our seats, and the interrogation began.

"I have already formed an opinion on this case," Sergeant Cuff stated, his voice steady. "I beg your ladyship's permission to withhold it for the present. My immediate objective is to report my findings upstairs in Miss Verinder's sitting-room and outline my proposed course of action."

He detailed the smear on the paintwork, drawing the same logical conclusions he had presented to Superintendent Seegrave, albeit with a refined register of language. "One fact is certain," he concluded. "The Diamond is missing from the drawer in the cabinet. Another is nearly so: the marks from the smear must belong to an article of dress worn by someone within this house. We must identify that garment before proceeding further."

"And does that discovery," my mistress interjected, "presume the identification of the thief?"

"I beg your ladyship's pardon," Cuff corrected gently. "I do not claim the Diamond is stolen. Only that it is missing. The stained garment may serve as the trail leading to its recovery."

She turned her gaze to me. "Do you comprehend this?"

"Sergeant Cuff comprehends it, my lady," I replied.

"How, then, do you propose to locate the stained dress?" she asked, directing her query back to the Sergeant.

"The other officer," Cuff noted with a sigh of regret, "has, I confess with shame, subjected the servants' boxes and rooms to search already. I neither can nor will permit them to be insulted in such a manner a second time!"

"There is a mistress to serve!" I thought, recognizing the gravity of his stance. "A woman among ten thousand, if one cares to count them!"

"That is precisely the dilemma I intended to raise," the Sergeant continued. "The initial officer has inflicted grave harm upon this inquiry by allowing the servants to perceive suspicion. Should I provoke them into believing themselves suspects a second time, the obstacles they might erect are incalculable—particularly among the women. Yet, their boxes _must_ be searched again. For this plain reason: the first search sought the Diamond; the second must seek the stained dress. I fully concur with your ladyship that the servants' feelings deserve consultation. However, I am equally convinced that their wardrobes require inspection."

The situation appeared to reach a deadlock. My mistress voiced her dissent in language far more eloquent than my own.

"I possess a plan to surmount this difficulty," Cuff proposed. "Provided your ladyship consents."

"If I explain the case to the servants," he suggested, "will that suffice?"

"The women will immediately feel themselves suspected," I interrupted.

"They will not, Mr. Betteredge," Cuff countered smoothly. "If I inform them I intend to examine the wardrobes of _everyone_ who slept in this house on Wednesday night—from your ladyship downward. It is merely a formality," he added, casting a sidelong glance at my mistress, "yet the servants will receive it as equitable justice between superiors and inferiors. Rather than hindering the investigation, they will regard it as a matter of honor to assist."

The logic held water. My mistress, once her initial shock subsided, recognized its validity. "Are you certain the investigation is necessary?" she asked.

"It is the swiftest path to our goal, my lady."

Rising to summon her maid, she instructed, "You shall address the servants, holding the keys to my wardrobe."

Cuff halted her with an unexpected query. "Shall we not first ensure that the other ladies and gentlemen in the house consent?"

"The sole other lady is Miss Verinder," my mistress replied, surprised. "The only gentlemen are my nephews, Mr. Blake and Mr. Ablewhite. There is no likelihood of refusal from any of them."

I reminded her that Mr. Godfrey was departing. As the words left my lips, Mr. Godfrey himself knocked at the door to bid farewell, followed closely by Mr. Franklin, who accompanied him to the station. My mistress elucidated the predicament. Mr. Godfrey resolved it instantly, calling to Samuel through the window to retrieve his portmanteau, then placing the key directly into Sergeant Cuff's hand.

"My luggage may follow me to London," he said, "once the inquiry concludes."

The Sergeant accepted the key with a gracious apology. "I apologize for causing you any inconvenience, sir, for what amounts to a mere formality; yet, the example of their betters will perform wonders in reconciling the servants to this necessity."

After tendering his sympathizing farewells to my lady, Mr. Godfrey departed, leaving a message for Miss Rachel that signaled his intent to revisit the marriage proposal at the earliest opportunity. Mr. Franklin, trailing his cousin, assured the Sergeant that all his garments were open to scrutiny, possessing nothing locked away. Cuff expressed his deepest gratitude.

Thus, the views of my lady, Mr. Godfrey, and Mr. Franklin had been met with alacrity. Only Miss Rachel remained to align herself with their stance before we convened the servants to commence the search for the stained garment. Yet, as soon as we were left alone, the Lady's inexplicable aversion to the Sergeant rendered the conference more distasteful than before.

"If I provide you with Miss Verinder's keys," she said, "shall I have fulfilled all your requests for the present?"

"I beg your ladyship's pardon," Cuff responded. "Prior to commencing, I should wish, if convenient, to inspect the washing-book. The stained article may be linen. Should the search yield nothing, I require the ability to account for every piece of linen in the house and every item dispatched to the washerwoman. If an article is missing, there will be at least a presumption that it bore the paint stain and was deliberately destroyed yesterday or today by its owner."

Turning to me, he added, "Superintendent Seegrave directed the attention of the female servants to the smear when they crowded into the room on Thursday morning. That, Mr. Betteredge, may prove to be yet another error of Superintendent Seegrave's."

My lady ordered the bell rung for the washing-book, remaining beside us until the document was produced, prepared to grant any further requests Cuff might have. The book arrived, carried by Rosanna Spearman. The girl descended to breakfast that morning with a pallor and haggard appearance that spoke of suffering, though sufficiently recovered from her recent illness to resume her duties. Cuff observed her intently—her face upon entry, her stooped shoulder as she exited.

"Do you have anything further to say to me?" my lady asked, eager to escape the Sergeant's presence.

Cuff opened the washing-book, grasping its contents in half a minute, then closed it firmly. "I venture to trouble your ladyship with one final question," he said. "Has the young woman who delivered this book served you as long as the others?"

"Why do you inquire?" my lady asked.

"The last time I encountered her," Cuff replied, "she was incarcerated for theft."

At that revelation, resistance vanished. My mistress recounted Rosanna's exemplary conduct during her tenure and the high esteem held for her by the matron at the Reformatory. "You do not suspect her, I trust?" my lady pressed, her tone earnest.
