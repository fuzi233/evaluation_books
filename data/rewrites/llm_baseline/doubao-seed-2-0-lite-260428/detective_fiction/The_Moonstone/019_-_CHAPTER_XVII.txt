The night passed without a single incident, and I was heartily glad to note that Sergeant Cuff’s unblinking vigilance had caught no sign of any secret meeting between Miss Rachel and Rosanna Spearman.

I’d fully expected the Sergeant to set off for Frizinghall at first light, but he lingered about the house, loitering by the doors and pausing to stare off down the paths as if he was waiting for something specific to turn up. I left him to his own devices, of course, and wandered out to the grounds not long after, where I ran straight into Mr. Franklin on his usual favourite walk along the edge of the shrubbery. We’d barely exchanged two words when Sergeant Cuff stepped out from behind a hedge, joining us unannounced. Mr. Franklin received him haughtily, I won’t deny it — all he got for a polite good morning was a sharp, “Have you anything to say to me?”
“I do, sir,” Sergeant Cuff replied, calm as ever. “It touches the theft inquiry I’m running here. You worked out yesterday exactly where this line of questioning was leading. It’s only natural, in your position, that you’d be shocked and distressed, and that you’d take your anger at the family scandal hanging over this house out on me.”
“What do you want?” Mr. Franklin cut him off, short and sharp.
“I want to remind you, sir, that up to this point, I have not been proven wrong. Bear that in mind, along with the fact I am an officer of the law, acting with the full sanction of the lady of this house. Under those circumstances, is it not your duty as a good citizen to share any special information you might hold that could help this inquiry along?”
“I have no special information,” Mr. Franklin said flatly.
Sergeant Cuff brushed that answer off like it had never been spoken. “You could save me a whole lot of wasted time running inquiries out of the house, if you’d choose to understand what I’m saying and speak plainly.”
“I don’t understand you,” Mr. Franklin snapped back. “I have nothing to say.”
“One of the female servants — no names needed — spoke to you in private last night, sir.”
Again Mr. Franklin cut him short. Again he said, “I have nothing to say.”
Standing silent by their side, I thought back to the swing door I’d seen shifting last night, and the flash of coat-tails I’d spotted vanishing down the back passage. No doubt Sergeant Cuff had got within earshot of that private conversation between Mr. Franklin and Rosanna before I’d interrupted him, and suspected the girl had unburdened at least part of her secret to Mr. Blake.
The thought had barely crossed my mind when who should appear at the far end of the shrubbery walk but Rosanna Spearman herself, with Penelope right on her heels, trying her hardest to tug her back to the house. Rosanna froze when she saw Mr. Franklin was not alone, clearly at a total loss for what to do next, and Penelope hung back a step behind her. Mr. Franklin spotted the two girls the same instant I did. Sergeant Cuff, that wily old fox, acted like he hadn’t noticed them at all.
It all happened in a single breath. Before either Mr. Franklin or I could speak, Sergeant Cuff struck in smoothly, raising his voice loud enough to carry all the way down the path, as if he was just continuing our earlier conversation.
“You needn’t fear harming the girl, sir,” he said, pointedly. “Quite the opposite, in fact. I’d recommend you trust me with what you know, if you hold any regard at all for Rosanna Spearman.”
Mr. Franklin caught the trick at once. He also acted like he’d not seen the two girls, and shouted back just as loud, “I take no interest whatever in Rosanna Spearman.”
I glanced down the path. The second those words left Mr. Franklin’s mouth, Rosanna spun on her heel. Where she’d been fighting Penelope to get to Mr. Franklin just a moment before, she now let my daughter slip her arm through hers and lead her back to the house without a single protest.
The breakfast bell rang the second the two girls vanished around the corner, and even Sergeant Cuff had to admit his trap had fallen flat. He leaned in to murmur to me, “I’m heading to Frizinghall now, Mr. Betteredge. I’ll be back before two.” He turned and left without another word, and for a few blessed hours, we were well rid of him.
“You’ll have to make this right with Rosanna for me,” Mr. Franklin said, once we were alone. “I seem fated to say the most awkward, hurtful things to that poor girl for no reason. You saw exactly what that Sergeant did — he laid a trap for both of us. If he could rattle me, or push her into a sudden outburst, either one of us might have slipped up and said something he could twist to his own ends. On the spur of the moment, that was the only way I could see out of it. It stopped her from stepping forward and saying something she’d regret, and it told that sly detective I saw right through his game. He was listening to us when we spoke last night, Betteredge — I’m sure of it.”
He’d done worse than listen, I thought to myself privately. He’d remembered I’d told him the girl was secretly in love with Mr. Franklin, and he’d calculated exactly what effect mentioning Mr. Franklin’s “regard for her” out loud would have on Rosanna. “As for listening, sir,” I said out loud, keeping that thought to myself, “if this keeps up much longer, we’ll all be in the same boat. Prying, peeping, eavesdropping — that’s all anyone in a house caught in this kind of mess does. A day or two more, and we’ll all go entirely mute, because we’ll all know everyone else is listening in to catch our secrets. Forgive my outburst, sir, but this horrid mystery hanging over the house goes to my head like strong liquor, and makes me half wild. I’ll keep my promise, first chance I get I’ll explain it all to Rosanna.”
“You haven’t said anything to her about last night yet, have you?” Mr. Franklin asked.
“No, sir.”
“Then don’t say anything now. I daren’t risk the girl seeking me out to talk in confidence, not with that Sergeant skulking about waiting to catch us together. My own actions are wildly contradictory, aren’t they? I can see no way out of this mess that isn’t dreadful to think of, short of the Moonstone being traced to Rosanna — and yet I cannot, and will not, help Sergeant Cuff hunt that poor girl down.”
It was unreasonable, no doubt, but it was exactly the way I felt too. I understood him perfectly. If you stop for a moment and admit you’re just a regular mortal man, you’d understand him too.
While Sergeant Cuff was making his way to Frizinghall, the state of the house and grounds was simple enough to sum up. Miss Rachel was still locked tight in her bedroom, stubbornly waiting for the carriage that was to take her to her aunt’s house, refusing to see anyone. My lady and Mr. Franklin breakfasted alone together. After the meal, Mr. Franklin made one of his sudden impulsive decisions, and set off at a quick pace for a long walk to quiet his frayed nerves. I was the only one who saw him leave, and he told me he’d be back long before the Sergeant returned.
The weather we’d seen hints of the night before had broken. Heavy pre-dawn rain had given way to sharp, blustery wind blowing in fresh off the sea. The clouds threatened more rain more than once, but the downpour never came. It was brisk, bracing, perfect walking weather if you were young and strong enough to lean into those big gusts sweeping off the water.
I attended my lady after breakfast, and helped her sort through the household accounts. She only mentioned the Moonstone once, and that was to forbid any talk of it between us for the time being. “We’ll wait till that man gets back,” she said, meaning the Sergeant. “We’ll have to talk of it then. There’s no rule that says we have to talk of it now.”
When I left my mistress, I found Penelope waiting for me in my own room. “Father, I wish you’d come and talk to Rosanna,” she said. “I’m dreadfully worried about her.”
I thought I knew exactly what was wrong, but it’s a firm rule of mine that as the wiser, calmer sex, us men ought to make women explain themselves before we step in to help — it makes them think through their own feelings more clearly, and stops them acting entirely on impulse. Penelope’s reason was plain enough, from her own lips: “I’m afraid Mr. Franklin hurt Rosanna terribly, and never meant to.”
“What was she doing out on the shrubbery walk in the first place?” I asked.
“Madness, that’s all I can call it,” Penelope said. “She was dead set on speaking to Mr. Franklin this morning, no matter what happened. I did everything I could to stop her, you saw that. If only I’d gotten her away before she heard those awful words——”
“Now, now,” I cut in. “Don’t lose your head. I can’t think of a single thing that ought to have alarmed her.”
“It didn’t alarm her, father. But Mr. Franklin said he didn’t care a farthing for her — and he said it in such a cold, cruel voice!”
“He only said it to throw the Sergeant off the scent,” I told her. “I told her that already,” Penelope went on, “but you see — Mr. Franklin’s been disappointing her, making her feel small for weeks and weeks now, and this is the last straw. She’s got no right to expect him to care for her, of course, it’s absurd that she’s forgotten her place like that. But she’s lost all her pride, all her common sense, everything. When Mr. Franklin said those words, father, she turned to stone. She went completely quiet, and she’s been going about all her chores ever since like she’s sleepwalking.”
I started to feel uneasy. Penelope’s account was so sombre it knocked my usual smug sense of being the more sensible sex right out of me. I thought back to the night before, when Rosanna had spoken to Mr. Franklin, looking already like her heart was half broken. Now fate had stung her right on that raw, tender wound again. It was sad — so sad, all the more so because the girl had no business pining after Mr. Franklin, no right to hurt like that.
I’d promised Mr. Franklin I’d speak to her, and this seemed the perfect time to do it. We found her sweeping the corridor outside the bedrooms, pale, neat in her usual plain print dress, as composed as ever. I noticed a strange dim, dull look in her eyes — not the look of someone who’d been crying, but of someone who’d been staring at a distant, invisible thing for hours on end.
“Cheer up, Rosanna!” I said. “Don’t fret over silly fancies. I’ve got a message for you from Mr. Franklin.” I explained the whole trick Sergeant Cuff had pulled, as gently and comfortingly as I could, framing it so she’d understand Mr. Franklin’s cruel-sounding words were never meant for her. For all my strict rules about the fairer sex, when I came face to face with that poor, heartbroken girl, all my gruff principles melted away.
“Mr. Franklin is very kind and considerate. Please thank him for me.” That was all she said. She sounded just as dreamy, far away, as Penelope had described. I doubted she’d fully taken in a word I’d said. “Are you quite sure you understand me, Rosanna?” I asked.
“Quite sure.” She echoed me like a machine, not a living girl, and never stopped sweeping. I took the broom gently out of her hands. “Come now, my girl. This isn’t like you. You’ve got something heavy on your mind. I’m your friend. I’ll stand by you, even if you’ve done something wrong. Come clean, Rosanna — tell me everything.”
A week before, speaking that way to her would have made the tears run down her face. There was no change in her eyes now. “Yes,” she said. “I’ll come clean. To Mr. Franklin.”
I hardly knew what to say. She was in no state to understand Mr. Franklin’s warning that she must not seek him out privately. I told her, carefully, that he’d gone out for a long walk. “It doesn’t matter,” she said. “I won’t trouble Mr. Franklin today.”
“Why not tell my lady, instead?” I pressed. “She’s always been kind to you, merciful and true — she’d help you unburden your mind.”
She stared at me for a long, steady moment, like she was etching my words into her memory. Then she took the broom back out of my hands, and moved slowly a little way down the corridor. “No,” she said, half to herself, as she swept. “I know a better way to unburden my mind than that.”
“What way?”
“Please let me get on with my work.”
Penelope stepped forward to offer to help her sweep. Rosanna turned her down politely, thanked both of us, and kept on working. There was no reaching her. I signed to Penelope to come away with me. We left her there, moving up and down the corridor, sweeping, like a woman caught in a waking dream.
“This is a job for the doctor,” I said. “This is far beyond me.” Penelope reminded me that Mr. Candy was still very ill, laid low by the chill he’d caught the night of the dinner party. His assistant, Ezra Jennings, was around, of course — but no one in these parts knew much about him. Mr. Candy had hired him under odd, unspoken circumstances, and for better or worse, none of us liked or trusted the man. There were other doctors over in Frizinghall, but they were all strangers to our household, and Penelope was sure that throwing a strange doctor at Rosanna in her current state would do her far more harm than good.
I thought about going to my lady, but she was already carrying the terrible weight of the stolen Moonstone and Miss Rachel’s strange behaviour, and I hated to add this new, distressing trouble to her plate. Still, something had to be done — the girl’s state was alarming, and my lady ought to know. I headed for her sitting room, but found it empty. She was shut inside with Miss Rachel, and there was no way I could interrupt them.
I waited in vain till the front staircase clock struck a quarter to two. Five minutes later, I heard my name being called from the drive outside the house. I knew that voice at once. Sergeant Cuff had come back from Frizinghall.
