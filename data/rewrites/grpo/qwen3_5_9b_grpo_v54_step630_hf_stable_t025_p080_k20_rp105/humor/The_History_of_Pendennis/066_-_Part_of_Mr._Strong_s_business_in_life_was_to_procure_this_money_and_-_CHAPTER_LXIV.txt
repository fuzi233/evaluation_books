Lady Clavering, having survived the tumultuous London season with her husband, retreated to a charming villa nestled on a picturesque common near Tunbridge Wells. Miss Amory, naturally, tagged along with her mother, while Master Clavering returned for his holidays. For Blanche, the arrival of her brother was less a reunion and more a declaration of war; her primary occupation became fighting and quarreling with him. Yet, this domestic skirmishing was merely a pastime. The young schoolboy, far from enjoying home sports, found his true joy in cricket, horses, and a legion of friends at Tunbridge.

The Begum's residence had transformed into a chaotic hub of adolescent revelry. It was filled with a constant stream of young gentlemen, all around thirteen years of age, who consumed tarts and champagne with alarming voracity. They raced horses across the lawn, terrified the fond mother, smoked until the air was thick with haze, and generally rendered the dining room unbearable for Miss Blanche. She simply did not care for the company of thirteen-year-old gentlemen.

For the fair young creature, however, any change was a delight. For a week or two, she would have traded her current comforts for poverty, a cottage, and bread-and-cheese; perhaps even a dungeon with bread-and-water for a night. The move to Tunbridge was, therefore, a welcome escape. She wandered the woods sketching trees and farmhouses, devoured French novels, and drove into the town to catch any play, ball, conjurer, or musician who happened to appear. She slept excessively, quarreled with her mother and Frank in the mornings, and attended the local village school with a mischievous flair. First, she would fondle the girls and thwart the mistress; then, she would scold the girls and laugh at the teacher. Of course, she was constant at church.

It was a lovely little church of immense antiquity—a tiny Anglo-Norman jewel, seemingly built the day before yesterday, adorned with painted windows, carved saints' heads, gilt scripture texts, and open pews. Blanche immediately set to work creating a most correct high-church altar-cover for it. She quickly earned a reputation as a saint with the clergyman, whom she coaxed, wheedled, and fondled with such artfulness that poor Mrs. Smirke went nearly mad with jealousy. Mrs. Smirke was the wife of our old friend Smirke, Pen's tutor and former suitor for Helen's hand. Having consoled himself for her refusal with a young lady from Clapham provided by his mother, Smirke's views had hardened daily since her death. He had cut off his coat collar, let his hair grow long over his back, abandoned his forehead curl and necktie, and went without dinner on Fridays. He read the Roman Hours and hinted that he was ready to hear confessions in the vestry.

The most harmless creature in the world, he was denounced as a black and dangerous Jesuit and Papist by Muffin of the Dissenting chapel and Mr. Simeon Knight of the old church. Smirke had built his chapel-of-ease with the money left him by his mother at Clapham. Lord! What would she have said to hear a table called an altar, to see candlesticks upon it, or to receive letters signed on the Feast of Saint So-and-so? All these things the boy of Clapham practiced, his faithful wife following suit.

When Blanche held a conference of nearly two hours in the vestry with Mr. Smirke, Belinda paced up and down on the grass, where there were only two small grave-stones yet. She wished she had a third there, fearing he would soon offer himself to that creature who had infatuated him in a fortnight. No, she decided, she would retire to a convent, profess, and leave him. Such dark thoughts plagued Smirke's wife and neighbors, who suspected him of direct correspondence with the Bishop of Rome and bewailing errors even more odious. Yet, our friend meant no earthly harm. The post-office never brought him letters from the Pope. At first, Blanche seemed to him the most pious, gifted, and fascinating person he had ever met; her singing of the Chants delighted him. But after a while, he grew tired of Miss Amory; her ways and graces grew stale. Then he became doubtful, and finally, she caused a disturbance in his school, lost her temper, and rapped the children's fingers. Blanche inspired this cycle of admiration and satiety in many men. She tried to please them, flinging out all her graces at once, descending upon them with jewels, smiles, cajoleries, and ogles. Then she grew tired of them, dropped them, and the men, in turn, dropped her.

It was a happy night for Belinda when Blanche departed. Her husband, blushing and sighing, admitted he had been deceived, fearing her precious gifts were merely tinsel and that she had made religion an amusement. She had certainly lost her temper with the schoolmistress and beaten Polly Rucker's knuckles cruelly. Belinda flew to his arms; the question of the grave or the veil was settled. He tenderly embraced her on the forehead. "There is none like thee, my Belinda," he said, throwing his fine eyes up to the ceiling, "precious among women!" As for Blanche, from the moment she lost sight of him and Belinda, she never thought or cared about them again.

But when Arthur descended to spend a few days at Tunbridge Wells with the Begum, this stage of indifference had not yet arrived for Miss Blanche or the simple clergyman. Smirke still believed her to be an angel and a wonder of a woman. Such perfection he had never seen; he sat listening to her music in the summer evenings, open-mouthed and rapt, forgetting tea and bread-and-butter. Fascinated as he had heard the music of the opera to be—he had attended only one exhibition, which he recalled with a blush and a sigh, the day he accompanied Helen and her son to the play at Chatteris—he could conceive nothing more delicious or celestial than Miss Amory's music. She was a gifted being with a precious soul and the most remarkable talents, possessing, to all outward seeming, the most heavenly disposition.

In this state of feverish bewitchment, Smirke discoursed to Arthur about her. Their meeting had been cordial. Arthur loved anyone who loved his mother, and Smirke could speak on that theme with genuine emotion. They shared a hundred stories of their lives. "Arthur will perceive," Smirke said, "that his views on Church matters have developed since our acquaintance." Mrs. Smirke, a most exemplary person, seconded them with all her efforts. Having built the little church on his mother's demise, he had heard of Arthur's reputation. Speaking in the kindest and most saddened tone, with eyelids lowered and head bowed, he expressed his feelings. Arthur was immensely amused by him—by his airs, follies, simplicity, blank stock, and long hair—and by his real goodness and kindness. His praises of Blanche pleased and surprised Arthur, making him regard her with particular favor.

The truth was, Blanche was very glad to see Arthur. One is always glad to see an agreeable man in the country who brings the latest news and stories from the great city, someone who can talk that darling London jargon so dear and indispensable to Londoners, yet so little understood by those outside the world. The first day Pen arrived, he kept Blanche laughing for hours after dinner. She sang her songs with redoubled spirit. She did not scold her mother but fondled and kissed her to the honest Begum's surprise. When bedtime came, she said, "Deja!" with the prettiest air of regret, truly sorry to go to bed, and squeezed Arthur's hand fondly. On his side, he gave her pretty palm a very cordial pressure. Our young gentleman was of such a turn that eyes moderately bright dazzled him.

"She is very much improved," Pen thought, looking out into the night. "Very much. I suppose the Begum won't mind my smoking with the window open. She's a jolly good old woman, and Blanche is immensely improved. I liked her manner with her mother tonight. I liked her laughing way with that stupid young cub of a boy, whom they oughtn't to allow to get tipsy. She sang those little verses very prettily; they were devilish pretty verses too, though I say it who shouldn't say it." He hummed a tune Blanche had put to some verses of his own. "Ah! what a fine night! How jolly a cigar is at night! How pretty that little Saxon church looks in the moonlight! I wonder what old Warrington's doing? Yes, she's a devilish nice little thing, as my uncle says."

"Oh, heavenly!" A voice broke out from a clematis-covered casement nearby—a girl's voice, the author of *Mes Larmes*.

Pen burst into laughter. "Don't tell about my smoking," he said, leaning out of his own window.

"Oh! go on! I adore it," cried the lady of *Mes Larmes*. "Heavenly night! heavenly, heavenly moon! but I must shut my window and not talk to you on account of *les moeurs*. How droll they are, *les moeurs*! Adieu." And Pen began to sing the Goodnight to Don Basilio.

The next day, they walked in the fields together, laughing and chattering—the gayest pair of friends. They talked of their youth, and Blanche was prettily sentimental. They discussed Laura, dearest Laura—Blanche had loved her as a sister. Was she happy with that odd Lady Rockminster? Wouldn't she come to stay with them at Tunbridge? Oh, what walks they would take together! What songs they would sing—the old, old songs! Laura's voice was splendid. Did Arthur—she must call him Arthur—remember the songs they sang in the happy old days, now he was grown such a great man with such success? And so on.

The day after, enlivened by a happy ramble through the woods to Penshurst and a sight of that pleasant park and hall, came the conversation with the curate that made our young friend think more and more.

"Is she all this perfection?" he asked himself. "Has she become serious and religious? Does she tend schools and visit the poor? Is she kind to her mother and brother? Yes, I am sure of that; I have seen her." Walking with his old tutor over his little parish and visiting his school, Pen found Blanche seated, instructing the children. With inexpressible delight, he fancied how patient she must be, how good-natured, how ingenuous, how really simple in her tastes, and unspoiled by the world.

"And do you really like the country?" he asked her as they walked together.

"I should like never to see that odious city again. O Arthur—that is, Mr.—well, Arthur, then—one's good thoughts grow up in these sweet woods and calm solitudes, like those flowers which won't bloom in London, you know. The gardener comes and changes our balconies once a week. I don't think I shall bear to look London in the face again—its odious, smoky, brazen face! But, heigho!"

"Why that sigh, Blanche?"

"Never mind why."

"Yes, I do mind why. Tell me, tell me everything."

"I wish you hadn't come down;" and a second edition of *Mes Soupirs* came out.

"You don't want me, Blanche?"

"I don't want you to go away. I don't think this house will be very happy without you, and that's why I wish that you never had come."

*Mes Soupirs* were laid aside, and *Mes Larmes* had begun.

Ah! What answer is given to those in the eyes of a young woman? What is the method employed for drying them? What took place? O ringdoves and roses, O dews and wildflowers, O waving greenwoods and balmy airs of summer! Here were two battered London rakes, taking themselves in for a moment, and fancying that they were in love with each other, like Phillis and Corydon!

When one thinks of country houses and country walks, one wonders that any man is left unmarried.
