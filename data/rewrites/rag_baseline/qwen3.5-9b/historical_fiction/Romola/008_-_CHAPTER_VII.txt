Bartolommeo Scala, the secretary of the Florentine Republic, resided in a stately palace adjacent to the Porta Pinti, now identified as the Casa Gherardesca. His heraldry—an azure ladder transverse on a golden field, surmounted by the motto *Gradatim* above the threshold—declared to all passersby that the miller's son had climbed to his station solely through gritted teeth and sheer will. Scala was a man of vanity and pomposity, yet fundamentally honest; he possessed a sincere conviction in his own merit and viewed any attempt to feign humility as a betrayal of his character. By this juncture, he had ascended to the summit of his azure ladder. Having served as secretary for two decades, he had frequently delivered orations from the *ringhiera*, the balcony of the Old Palace, in the presence of visiting princes, while the Marzocco, the symbol of the republic, donned its ceremonial gold crown and the populace roared, "Viva Messer Bartolommeo!" He had subsequently served on an embassy to Rome, earning titles of Titular Senator, Apostolical Secretary, and Knight of the Golden Spur. Eight years prior, he had achieved the zenith of civic ambition as Gonfaloniere. Concurrently, his wealth had grown commensurate with his rank, and his gout had intensified, following the predictable trajectory of successful mortals. Consequently, the Knight of the Golden Spur was often reduced to sitting with a helpless, cushioned heel beneath the magnificent loggia he had constructed, overlooking the expansive gardens behind his residence.

On the day he granted an audience to Tito Melema, Scala occupied this very seat. The May afternoon sun illuminated the flora and turf beyond the pleasant shade of the loggia. He had discarded his overly formal silk *lucco* in favor of a light, loose mantle draped over his tunic. Seated to his right were his beautiful daughter, Alessandra, and her husband, the Greek soldier-poet Marullo; to his left sat two friends, distinguished enough to offer counsel but not so illustrious as to dominate the conversation. Despite the gnawing pain of his gout, Scala's felicity remained imperfect, poisoned by a specific dossier resting before him. This collection consisted primarily of a correspondence between himself and Angelo Poliziano. It was a peculiar human foible of the era—incredulous as it may appear today—to publicly dissect quarrels and expose scholars to the entirety of a lengthy, contentious exchange. This was not the first instance where Scala had solicited the candid opinions of his circle regarding the moral and intellectual balance of roughly thirty Latin letters exchanged between himself and Poliziano. These missives originated from a series of epigrams composed in a tone ostensibly playful, yet laden with biting irony.

This was the chronicle of a quintessential academic dispute, significant here because it provided the precise thorn of hatred required, according to Nello, to stimulate the sluggish pace of friendship. Poliziano, having been spurned in his pursuit of Scala's daughter and her hand, maintained a razor-sharp intellect aimed at the secretary, who had declined the greatest scholar of the age as a son-in-law. Scala was a meritorious public servant and a fortunate man—traits naturally exasperating to an insulted genius. However, Scala harbored a compulsion for authorship, albeit a flawed one. He belonged to that breed of individuals who, reclining in gout-ridden slippers, penned poetic trifles purely for private amusement, devoid of any intent for publication, and dispatched them to acquaintances via letter, serving as the literary journals of the fifteenth century. Scala possessed numerous admirers eager to laud his work, including Ficino and Landino, who found his Latin prose elegant and robust. Furthermore, the formidable Joseph Scaliger, destined to declare Scala utterly ignorant of Latinity, existed comfortably in the subsequent century. Yet, when does the fatal coquetry inherent in amateur authorship ever suffice with the facile praise of friends? One must eventually confront the critical snobbery of a rival like Poliziano—a fellow-browser far from amiable. The objective became clear: demonstrate to the haughty scholar that the solid secretary possessed a fertile vein of verse, hinting at potential greatness if he were not shackled by the demands of statecraft.

The moment arrived when the man one secretly detests dispatches a Latin epigram containing a grammatical error—a false gender assignment, a questionable elision, or a poetic figure that constitutes a manifest solecism. This precipice had been reached by Poliziano. The secretary had extended his soft head from the protective shell of officialdom, and the terrible lurking crab descended. Poliziano, exercising the liberties of a friend, had crafted a Latin epigram correcting Scala's mistake regarding the *culex* (the mosquito), an insect ubiquitous along the banks of the Arno, noting its classification as inferior or feminine. Scala retaliated with a jestful Latin verse referencing Poliziano's failed romantic suit. The game escalated. Poliziano deemed the verses delightful and facetious, though regretting their serious grammatical inaccuracies. Since Scala claimed to have modeled his work on a Greek epigram, Poliziano, maintaining a facade of friendship, enclosed a Greek original on the same insect. It contained a vivid conceit involving Venus, Cupid, and the *culex*, grounded in the zoological fact that the gnat, like Venus, emerged from the waters—a trope popular among poets of the time.

In response, Scala politely asserted that his verses were never intended for a scholar possessing such delicate olfactory sensibilities as Poliziano, the nearest living approximation to ancient perfection, whose taste was so fastidious that even sturgeon appeared insipid to him. He defended his own composition, acknowledging it was drafted hastily without correction, intended merely as a refreshing diversion during the summer heat for himself and those content with mediocrity. Unlike others who courted fame through booksellers, Scala did not seek public acclaim. Regarding the Greek epigram Poliziano had sent, Scala admitted he lacked the proficiency to grasp its nuances or appreciate its elegance, save for a basic understanding of the sense. Nevertheless, he noted a potential issue: the comparison of the gnat to Venus based on their shared aquatic origins was problematic. On one hand, Venus might be offended; on the other, unless invoking the doctrine of Thales, the idea of a water-born origin seemed dubious for a creature preferring warmth. Perhaps a fish, or when considering flight, an eagle; or when accounting for nocturnal habits, a bat or owl would be more apt parallels.

This presented a golden opportunity for Poliziano. Unaware that Scala's defense involved sturgeon, he countered with references to frogs and gudgeons, dismantling Scala's arguments with ruthless efficiency. He ridiculed the secretary's criticism of the Greek epigram as wretched cavils. As for the aquatic origin of the gnat, Virgil himself had termed it the "*alumnus*" of the waters. Any remarks regarding fish, eagles, or bats were irrelevant (*nihil ad rem*), for the eagle's superior altitude did not negate the gnat's ability to fly. Poliziano expressed shame at dwelling on such trivialities, swelling a gnat into an elephant, but insisted on his integrity. He possessed no duplicity, nor would he be ensnared by the false flattery of slanderers. Aligning with a Homeric sentiment, he appended a Greek quotation to serve as ammunition for his rebuttal.

The conflict could not conclude here. Logic deteriorated further, while the secretary grew increasingly pompous in his self-assertion, and the scholarly poet's temper curdled into venom. Poliziano had generously offered a mirror, hoping the inflated secretary, seeing his own reflection, would abandon his ignorant defenses of poor Latin against ancient authorities validated by centuries of consensus. Unless, of course, the secretary intended to sink in literature as he rose in honors, thereby balancing the scales for men of letters. In return, Poliziano challenged Scala to examine his writings, promising to find no greater devotion to antiquity. The secretary expressed shame for his contemporary age, blushing for its deficiencies. Some sought to elevate their works to the divine status of antiquity, but Scala could not oblige them. Regarding the honors that provoked envy, he maintained they were well-earned, citing his journey from poverty to Florence.

Poliziano, the elegant scholar, was unsurprised that Scala found the Age distasteful, given his own repulsiveness to the times. Indeed, Poliziano had accurately labeled Scala a "branny monster," formed from the offscourings of monsters, born amidst the refuse of a mill, and eminently suited for the long-eared office of grinding grain (*in pistrini sordibus natus et quidem pistrino dignissimus*).

These documents were produced specifically in anticipation of Tito's arrival. A new Greek scholar awaited evaluation, and Scala craved a dispassionate judgment from someone of superior knowledge regarding Poliziano's Greek epigram. Following preliminary discussions on Tito's travels and an examination of various gems, the conversation flowed naturally from Lorenzo de' Medici's passion for collecting ancient artifacts to broader classical tastes and their current state in Florence. Mentioning Poliziano was inevitable. He was undeniably talented but excessively arrogant, assuming the role of Hercules tasked with destroying the literary monstrosities of the age. He wrote unsigned letters to his elders as if they were miraculous revelations emanating from a single source. Were not his own criticisms often questionable and his tastes perverse? While fond of pungent remarks directed at those who mimicked Cicero by ending sentences with "*esse videtur*," did he not swing to the opposite extreme, chasing obscure vocabulary and affected phrasing?
