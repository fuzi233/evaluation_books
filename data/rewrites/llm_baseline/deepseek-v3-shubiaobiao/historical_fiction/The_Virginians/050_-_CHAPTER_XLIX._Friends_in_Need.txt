The hackney coach rattled through the dim streets of London, carrying George Warrington on a desperate errand—to free his brother from the clutches of debt and disgrace. The journey through the Strand and Fleet Street seemed interminable, the roads little more than rivers of mud under a starless sky. The feeble glow of link-boys’ torches flickered in the darkness, illuminating potholes deep enough to swallow a wheel. To George, every jolt and delay was torment. His heart pounded with urgency.

He had written to Harry from Bristol, announcing his return. He had sent word months before, declaring himself alive after the long, mistaken death at Braddock’s defeat. But fate had intervened—war between England and France meant letters vanished into the holds of captured ships. The message that might have spared Harry this shame had never reached him.

Now, standing before Harry’s abandoned lodgings, George felt a chill of foreboding. The maid had taken one look at him—so like his brother—and fled with a shriek. The landlady, tight-lipped, claimed ignorance of Harry’s whereabouts. But George was no fool. He knew Clarges Street lay close to Bond Street, and Bond Street was home to their formidable aunt, Baroness Bernstein.

Aunt Bernstein—Beatrix—had shown Harry kindness, or so his letters claimed. Even their mother, Rachel Esmond, who had long scorned her eldest daughter, had softened at the news. But forgiveness had its limits. Some sins, especially those of a woman’s past, were never forgotten.

Gumbo, trembling with shock at the sight of his resurrected master, stammered out the truth. Harry was imprisoned, ensnared by debt. The revelation hardened George’s resolve. He stormed to his aunt’s house, demanding answers. Her distress, though carefully veiled, betrayed her. She had not abandoned Harry entirely.

Now, as the coach lurched toward the bailiff’s house, George’s anger cooled into resolve. He would free his brother, no matter the cost.

---

The bailiff’s dwelling was a grim place, its doors locked and guarded by a wary-eyed Jew with a sputtering torch. Inside the cramped office, two men waited—Colonel Lambert and Lieutenant-Colonel James Wolfe, officers of rank and honor.

“We are not angry any more, Harry!” Lambert declared cheerfully, mistaking George for his brother in the dim light. Then he froze. “Angels and ministers of grace! Who are you?”

George seized his hand. “God bless you, sir! You must be Colonel Lambert—Harry wrote of your kindness. I am his brother, given up for dead after Braddock’s defeat. I have come back to thank you—and to bring him home.”

Lambert’s face transformed. “James! Here is news! The dead are alive!”

Wolfe, stern but not unkind, stepped forward. “Your brother has been wayward, Mr. Warrington. But Lambert here would not abandon him.”

“Nor shall I,” George vowed. He turned to the bailiff. “Name the debt.”

The sum was steep—over five hundred pounds. Without hesitation, George drew out his purse. “It is paid tonight.”

Lambert gripped his shoulder. “You do your family proud, sir.”

---

Upstairs, Harry sat in gloom, wrestling with regret. His reckless choices had led him here. Worse, Maria’s devotion weighed on him like a chain.

Then Gumbo burst in, eyes wild. “Mas’r Harry—ghost! Ghost downstairs!”

“What nonsense—”

But the door flew open. George stood there.

For a heartbeat, neither moved. Then Harry stumbled forward, and the brothers embraced—the prodigal reclaimed, the lost restored.

Lambert and Wolfe discreetly withdrew. Some reunions needed no audience.

---

Later, as they left the bailiff’s house, Harry exhaled shakily. “I don’t deserve this.”

George slung an arm around his shoulders. “No. But family doesn’t keep accounts.”

The night air was cold, but the road ahead was clear.
