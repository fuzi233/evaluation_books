The Royal George was born amidst illuminations and cannonade, a spectacle witnessed by multitudes behind gilt railings. In contrast, the cradle of our little Prince rested in silence, attended only by Gumbo and Molly, whose loyalty to the heir of my poverty matched the fervor of any courtier. We named him Miles, not George, honoring a distant, beloved figure of my paternal line rather than the paragon of the throne. Theo and I agreed on this deviation, seeking a connection to a singular friend in a world where affection from one's own blood was increasingly scarce. We announced the birth to the world via the *Daily Advertiser*, citing Church Street, Lambeth, as our humble precinct.

Aunt Bernstein replied with a fine silk coverlid edged in lace fit for royalty, though she questioned why we pointed out our lodging in such a thoroughfare. The lace was useless to us; the cost alone would have fed us, yet Theo and Molly cherished the gift, draping our eldest son's cradle in finery surpassing that of many nobles. Dr. Heberden visited frequently, recommending a local surgeon, though my wife required little more than the care of our landlady and Molly. Soon, the gilded wheels of Lady Castlewood's chariot adorned our gate once more. She delivered a pot of jelly, undoubtedly served previously at her own banquets, and recounted the court's splendor regarding the new heir.

Mr. Johnson, visiting on the same day, bowed respectfully to her magnificence. She addressed me as cousin with affable benignity, transferring the jelly from her silver dish to our crockery pan. Dr. Heberden tasted the confection, declaring it excellent and noting that such delicacies must have graced tables of the great, quivering under the gaze of celebrated beauties and shared with distinguished company. He consumed a considerable portion of Lady Castlewood's benefaction. Yet, as for the epithet "delicious," my wife rejected it, finding the taste disagreeable, while Molly, flinging up her head, declared it mouldy.

Our boy possessed the privilege of an Earl's daughter as godmother; Lady Maria, our cousin, performed the office with a tenderness born of her own lost maternal happiness. Captain Miles, a fine gentleman in the Prince's Hussars, ensured that the little garments prepared for our child were not wasted, mindful of his own infant past. Sampson christened him in the Southwark chapel where our marriage had taken place. The celebrant read the Prayer-book with noble impressiveness until his voice failed near the end, leaving the small congregation to wipe their eyes. Hagan remarked that even Garrick could not have read the words more tenderly.

The origin of the name Miles occurred days prior to the christening. Just as we intended to name our firstborn after his father, a patter of horse hooves galloped up to our gate. Young Miles, our cousin, pulled the bell. He had likely disobeyed his parents to undertake this undutiful journey. "Cousin Harry gave me my little horse," he explained, his voice breathless. "I can't help liking you because you are so like Harry, and because they're always saying things of you at home, and it's a shame. I have brought my whistle and coral that my godmamma Lady Suckling gave me for your little boy. And if you're so poor, cousin George, here's my gold moidore. It's worth ever so much, and it's no use to me, because I mayn't spend it, you know."

We lifted the boy to Theo in her room. He climbed the stairs in his little tramping boots, proud of them. Theo kissed him and accepted the coin. That moidore has remained in her purse since that day. Later, my mother, writing through her ambassador, expressed royal surprise and displeasure at the christening name, noting it was unknown to the Esmond family. I withheld the reason at the time. Years later, recounting the tale to Madam Esmond, I saw a tear roll down her wrinkled cheek. She subsequently asked Gumbo countless questions about the boy who gave his name to our Miles—the Miles Gloriosus of Pall Mall, Valenciennes, Almack's, and Brighton.
