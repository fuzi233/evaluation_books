Ten years had passed since our arrival on this island, a decade chronicled in the pages of my journal. As I reflect upon those years, the shadow of sadness that often accompanies such retrospection is lifted by profound gratitude to God for the safety and happiness of my beloved family. Our sons have grown into men, strengthened by the rigorous training of their youth, ready to face whatever destiny awaits them. My greatest wish is that young readers may learn from our story how a peaceful, industrious, and pious family life fosters strong, pure, and manly characters. Those who emerge from such homes to fulfill new duties are rarely happier or more beloved than they are.

While I have detailed our earlier struggles, continuing with monotonous accounts of hunting expeditions, clumsy inventions, and endless discoveries would only weary the reader. However, before concluding, I must describe the state of our domain, which we called "New Switzerland," at this pivotal moment.

Rockburg and Falconhurst had transformed into magnificent residences. Fountains, trellised verandas, and lush plantations replaced the barren landscape that once distressed my wife. Flowering creepers draped the balconies, while native and European trees grew luxuriantly in groves we had planted. In the distance, Shark Island, now cloaked in graceful palms, guarded the entrance to Safety Bay, its battery and flagstaff clearly visible. The once-muddy swamp had been drained into a splendid lake, teeming with black swans, geese, and colorful ducks. Among the water plants, vibrant flamingos, demoiselle cranes, and herons moved in harmonious peace, unafraid of their human masters. Even our giant frogs, Grace and Beauty, had grown to the size of rabbits, responding eagerly to Jack's calls to catch flies. Old Hurry, the ostrich, marched with regal dignity beneath the trees, while countless pigeons nested in the rocks, their soft coos adding to the domestic tranquility.

Access to Rockburg was restricted to a single bridge; upstream, a dense thicket of citrus trees, figs, and thorny shrubs formed an impassable barrier. Our farms thrived: the rabbit warrens on Shark Island provided ample food and fur, while antelopes grazed in the groves on Whale Isle, which was reserved for industrial tasks like candle-making and tanning. The livestock at Woodlands supplied abundant meat, and my wife's dairy was a bustling hub of activity.

True to their nature, our sons loved naming their animal companions. A creamy-white cow became "Blanche"; a bull with a booming voice was dubbed "Stentor"; two swift onagers were named "Arrow" and "Dart"; and Jack claimed a jackal descendant as "Coco," insisting that no word could be pronounced without an 'o' sound. Despite occasional minor fevers and accidents, everyone remained in excellent health.

Though we lived in seclusion, I maintained a firm belief that we would eventually reconnect with civilization. Yet, as time carried our sons toward manhood, it also aged us, stirring anxious thoughts about our future isolation. On such days, I hid my sorrow from the family, finding solace only in prayer.

One afternoon, Fritz was missing. His *cajack* (canoe) was gone, and he had ventured out to sea. Anxious, I joined Ernest and Jack on Shark Island to watch for his return. After a tense wait, we spotted a small speck approaching. To our relief, it was Fritz, towing a heavy sack.

"Welcome back, wherever you come from!" I cried. "You seem to have quite a cargo!"

"Yes, Father," Fritz replied, "my trip yielded both booty and fascinating discoveries. Come, let us unload, and while I rest, I will tell you my adventure."

Gathering around him, Fritz explained that he had long dreamed of exploring the coast beyond the point where he had slain the walrus. Fully prepared with supplies, weapons, and his faithful eagle, Pounce, he had set sail at dawn. He described rounding the corner near the wreck, where he could see the rusted cannons and ironwork lying undisturbed on the seabed.

He navigated past rugged cliffs teeming with sea-fowl, seals, and walruses, eventually reaching a high, precipitous cape. There, he found a magnificent natural archway leading into a vast cavern. Inside, thousands of tiny swallows nested on platforms made of a strange, sponge-like material. Recognizing them as edible birds' nests—highly valued in China and worth their weight in silver—he collected samples for us.

"I suggest you try them, Mother," he urged. Though hesitant, my wife agreed to experiment.

Leaving the cave, Fritz entered a secluded, lake-like bay. Here, he discovered beds of enormous oysters attached to the rocks. Upon examining them, he found they contained numerous pearly spheres of varying sizes.

"You have discovered treasure!" I exclaimed. "These are beautiful pearls! While useless to us now, they could become a source of immense wealth if we ever meet the civilized world again."

Fritz named this hidden cove **Pearl Bay**. Sheltered by reefs and sandbanks, it was a perfect harbor. As he crossed the exit reef, he was attacked by a flock of albatrosses. In his frustration, he struck one down with his boat-hook.

However, the story took a dramatic turn the next morning. Fritz confessed a secret discovery regarding the fallen bird. Attached to its leg was a piece of rag bearing English words: *"Save an unfortunate Englishwoman from the smoking rock!"*

The message sent a shockwave through him. Could someone else truly be alive in this lonely region? The phrase "smoking rock" likely referred to a volcano, yet he refused to accept despair. He wrote a reply on a strip of his handkerchief: *"Do not despair! Help is near!"* He tied it to the bird's leg and released it, hoping it would carry his message westward.

"We must find her," Fritz declared, his resolve hardening. "Will my note ever reach her? Can I save her?"

I advised caution, suggesting the message might be ancient, but Fritz's hope burned too brightly to extinguish. We agreed he must prepare for a major expedition. First, he needed to modify his canoe to carry two passengers and sufficient provisions for a long journey. Meanwhile, the boys, eager to help, focused on harvesting the pearl oysters.

"It seems fitting to establish a pearl fishery immediately!" they cheered, building a temporary hut on the bay's shore.

Finally, the day arrived. Taking leave of my wife and younger brother Franz, Fritz and I boarded the yacht, accompanied by the dogs. Jack proudly took the newly installed seat beside Fritz, acting as pilot alongside his older brother.

We navigated safely through the rocks near Walrus Island, entering a calm expanse where we spotted the elusive paper nautiluses gliding over the water. We secured several specimens, naming the location **Nautilus Creek**. Continuing our journey, we rounded a flat promontory we named **Cape Pug-Nose**, and soon beheld the grand cliffs ahead.

Instead of going around, Fritz steered us directly toward the majestic archway. I marveled at the Gothic-like architecture of the pillars and pinnacles, proposing the name **Cape Minster**. As we passed through the tunnel, flocks of swallows darted out, creating a living curtain before retreating to their nests. The tide carried us effortlessly into the dazzling sunlight of Pearl Bay.

The bay was breathtaking—a romantic haven dotted with islets, surrounded by fertile meadows, shady groves, and murmuring brooks. We sought a suitable landing place near the shallow oyster beds, finding a spot beside a sparkling streamlet. As twilight descended, we lit a watchfire, ate a hurried supper, and left the dogs and Coco the jackal to guard the shore. Anchoring the yacht within gunshot of the land, we prepared for the long and perilous voyage ahead, driven by the hope of saving a lost soul.
