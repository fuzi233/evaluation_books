“Nay, Richart,” Catherine said at last, her voice worn thin with the weeks of bickering, “for Heaven’s sake let not this one sorry wench set us all by the ears. Hath she not stirred up ill blood enough between us already?”
“In very deed she hath. Fear me not, good mother. We will show her no more than decent, constrained civility, as befits a guest beneath our roof, no favour, no softness. She may come and read the letter of our poor boy she hath bewitched by devil’s arts, and then she will go. Give me your word ye will not cross that line of bare courtesy, and I will say no more.” On that quiet, wary understanding, the whole household waited for their unwelcome foe.
For her part, Margaret had braced herself for the interview in a spirit little less tense than their own. When Denys brought word that the family would not call on her at the inn, but would receive her in their own home, her lip curled. She bade him mark how every petty burgher pride they nursed loomed larger in their hearts than love for their own lost Gerard. “Well,” she said, biting back her anger, “I have no such excuse. I will not mimic their stiff, unlettered burgher pride. I go to them for Gerard’s sake. Oh, how I loathe this!”
Poor good-natured Denys, who would not have known malice if it bit his boot, was thus unwittingly hauling the materials for a full explosion across the threshold of the Tergou household.
Margaret dressed for the meeting in the same spirit a knight of her day armed himself for battle: he to parce blows, she to parce glances—glances of contempt for her straitened purse, of irony for any care she might take over her appearance. Her kirtle was plain dark blue English broadcloth, her farthingale and hose the same sturdy material, dyed a rich glossy claret. Not an inch of pretentious fur trimmed her gown, only crisp snow-white linen wristbands, and curiously plaited linen that stretched from the neckline of her bodice up to the base of her throat, cut square to frame her face rather than circle her neck. Soft waves of her front hair peeked out across her forehead, a style Mary Queen of Scots would not popularize for another hundred years, and instead of a silver net that would have ill suited her reduced circumstances, the rest of her hair was tucked under a tiny, tight-fitting dark blue cloth hood, hemmed with thin silver thread. Her shoes were bright red, but the claret of her petticoat and hose softened the shock of the colour, drawing the eye instead to her arched instep and shapely foot.
Beauty knew its craft then as well as it does now.
Even so, she kept the whole household waiting long past the three o’clock mark on the town dial.
At last she set off, attended by Denys, her loyal comrade-at-arms. Halfway to the house, she stopped short, her face going pale. “Denys!”
“Aye, she-general?”
“I must go home,” she whispered pitifully.
“What, have ye left some trinket behind?”
“What? My courage. Oh, oh, oh!”
“Nay, nay, be brave, lass. I shall not leave your side,” Denys promised.
“Swear you will stay right close to me, the whole time I am in there?”
Denys swore, and she resumed her march, stepping gingerly as if the very cobblestones might burn her.
Back in the Eli’s house, the whole family sat waiting for her, tangled in a mess of conflicting feelings: mortification that their son had run off with a girl they now were forced to host, burning curiosity, panting, unextinguished love for the boy they had lost, sharp aversion to the woman who came to stand between them and their hope of Gerard’s return, and another, quieter curiosity to see what there was in her that could bewitch their son so thoroughly he had abandoned home and kin.
At last Denys slipped in alone, and whispered, “The she-comrade is standing outside the door.”
“Fetch her in,” Eli ordered. “Now whisht, all of ye. Not a one of you speaks to her but me.”
Every head turned to the door in dead silence.
A low murmur drifted from the hall: Denys’s rough, rumbling voice, and a woman’s soft, mellow reply. Then the muttering stopped. The door creaked slowly open, and Margaret Brandt stepped across the threshold, dressed as we have described, a little pale, but calm and lovely, her gaze fixed straight ahead of her.
Everyone in the room stood up but Kate, and they stayed mute, staring like folk turned to stone.
“Be seated, mistress,” Eli said gravely, gesturing to a hard wooden chair that had been set apart for her, well away from the rest of the family circle.
She inclined her head, and crossed the room slowly. In her walk, her condition was impossible to miss: not only in the soft roundness of her shape, but in the faint languor that weighted every step.
Cornelis and Sybrandt hated her for it on sight. Richart thought it spoiled whatever slender grace her beauty held. The women of the household softened, just a little.
She pulled Gerard’s letter out from the bosom of her gown, and kissed it as if she were entirely alone, then settled into her seat, with the quiet air of one who knew she had come for no purpose but to read that letter, and nothing more.
But as she began to speak, she noticed they had seated her apart from all of them, as if she were a leper. She darted a quick glance at Denys, and made a swift, furtive motion with her hand held down by her side, bidding him come stand beside her. He jumped to obey as if she had shouted “March”, and took his place at her shoulder like a sentry. The whole household saw at once he had been ordered there by her, and Margaret flushed bright with embarrassment before she lifted her voice again. She read Gerard’s words in a mellow, clear tone, so soft, so earnest, so thrumming with feeling, that her very soul seemed to cling to each precious sound. It was a voice that sounded as if it had been shaped by Heaven itself to carry the weight of a lover’s longing.
“I do nothing doubt, my Margaret, that long ere this letter meets thy beloved eyes, Denys, my most dear friend, will have sought thee out, and told thee the manner of our unlooked-for and most tearful parting. Therefore I will e’en begin at that most doleful day. What befell him after, poor faithful soul, fain, fain would I hear, but may not. But I pray for him day and night next after thee, dearest. Friend more stanch and loving had not David in Jonathan, than I in him. Be good to him, for poor Gerard’s sake.”
At those words, which came entirely unlooked for, Denys leaned his head on the high back of Margaret’s chair, and groaned aloud.
Margaret turned quickly in her seat, found his hand, and pressed it tight. So the two of them, sweetheart and loyal friend, held hands while the sweetheart read on.
“I went forward all dizzied, like one trapped in an ill dream, and presently a gentleman came riding up with his servants, all on horseback, and had liked to have ridden me right down under their hooves. He drew rein at the brow of the hill, and sent his armed men back to rob me. They robbed me civilly enough, took my purse and the last copper I owned, and rode gaily away. I wandered stupid on, a friendless pauper.
There was a soft general sigh, followed by an angry oath from Denys.
“Presently a strange dimness came o’er me; I lay down to sleep on the snow. ‘Twas ill done, and with packs of wolves hard by. Had I loved thee as thou dost deserve, I had shown more manhood. But oh, sweet love, the drowsiness that did crawl o’er me desolate, and benumb me, was more than nature. And so I slept; and but that God was better to us, than I to thee or to myself, from that sleep I ne’er had waked; so all the folk I met afterwards do say. I had slept an hour or two, as I suppose, but no more, when a hand did shake me rudely. I awoke to my troubles. And there stood a servant girl in her holiday suit. ‘Are ye mad,’ quoth she, in seeming choler, ‘to sleep in snow, and under wolves’ nosen? Art weary o’ life, and not long weaned? Come, now,’ said she, more kindly, ‘get up like a good lad;’ so I did rise up. ‘Are ye rich, or are ye poor?’ But I stared at her as one amazed. ‘Why, ‘tis easy of reply,’ quoth she. ‘Are ye rich, or are ye poor?’ Then I gave a great, loud cry; that she did start back. ‘Am I rich, or am I poor? Had ye asked me an hour agone, I had said I am rich. But now I am so poor as sure earth beareth on her bosom none poorer. An hour agone I was rich in a friend, rich in money, rich in hope and spirits of youth; but now the Bastard of Burgundy hath taken my friend, and another gentleman my purse; and I can neither go forward to Rome nor back to her I left in Holland. I am poorest of the poor.’ ‘Alack!’ said the wench. ‘Natheless, an ye had been rich ye might ha’ lain down again in the snow for any use I had for ye; and then I trow ye had soon fared out o’ this world as bare as ye came into it. But, being poor, you are our man: so come wi’ me.’ Then I went because she bade me, and because I recked not now whither I went. And she took me to a fine house hard by, and into a noble dining-hall hung with black; and there was set a table with many dishes, and but one plate and one chair. ‘Fall to!’ said she, in a whisper. ‘What, alone?’ said I. ‘Alone? And which of us, think ye, would eat out of the same dish with ye? Are we robbers o’ the dead?’ Then she speered where I was born. ‘At Tergou,’ said I. Says she, ‘And when a gentleman dies in that country, serve they not the dead man’s dinner up as usual, till he be in the ground, and set some poor man to it?’ I told her, ‘nay.’ She blushed for us then. Here they were better Christians. So I behoved to sit down. But small was my heart for meat. Then this kind lass sat by me and poured me out wine; and tasting it, it cut me to the heart Denys was not there to drink with me. He doth so love good wine, and women good, bad, or indifferent. The rich, strong wine curled round my sick heart; and that day first I did seem to glimpse why folk in trouble run to drink so. She made me eat of every dish. ‘Twas unlucky to pass one. Nought was here but her master’s daily dinner. ‘He had a good stomach, then,’ said I. ‘Ay, lad, and a good heart. Leastways, so we all say now he is dead; but, being alive, no word on’t e’er heard I.’ So I did eat as a bird, nibbling of every dish. And she hearing me sigh, and seeing me like to choke at the food, took pity and bade me be of good cheer. I should sup and lie there that night. And she went to the hind, and he gave me a right good bed; and I told him all, and asked him would the law give me back my purse. ‘Law!’ quoth he; ‘law there was none for the poor in Burgundy. Why, ‘twas the cousin of the Lady of the Manor, he that had robbed me. He knew the wild spark. The matter must be judged before the lady; and she was quite young, and far more like to hang me for slandering her cousin, and a gentleman, and a handsome man, than to make him give me back my own. Inside the liberties of a town a poor man might now and then see the face of justice; but out among the grand seigneurs and dames—never.’ So I said, ‘I’ll sit down robbed rather than seek justice and find gallows.’ They were all most kind to me next day; and the girl proffered me money from her small wage to help me towards Rhine.”
“Oh, then, he is coming home! he is coming home!” shouted Denys, interrupting the reader. Margaret shook her head gently at him, a soft rebuke.
“I beg pardon, all the company,” Denys said stiffly, falling silent.
“‘Twas a sore temptation; but being a servant, my stomach rose against it. ‘Nay, nay,’ said I. She told me I was wrong. ‘Twas pride out o’ place; poor folk should help one another; or who on earth would?’ I said if I could do aught in return ‘twere well; but for a free gift, nay: I was overmuch beholden already. Should I write a letter for her? ‘Nay, he is in the house at present,’ said she. ‘Should I draw her picture, and so earn my money?’ ‘What, can ye?’ said she. I told her I could try; and her habit would well become a picture. So she was agog to be limned, and give it her lad. And I set her to stand in a good light, and soon made sketches two, whereof I send thee one, coloured at odd hours. The other I did most hastily, and with little conscience daub, for which may Heaven forgive me; but time was short. They, poor things, knew no better, and were most proud and joyous; and both kissing me after their country fashion, ‘twas the hind that was her sweetheart, they did bid me God-speed; and I towards Rhine.”
Margaret paused here, and gave Denys the coloured drawing to pass around the room. The women crowded in to examine it eagerly, curious about the foreign Burgundian peasant costume that differed in small ways from Dutch domestic dress: the girl’s hair tucked in a tight linen bag, a yellow half-kerchief crossed her head from ear to ear, throwing out a rectangular point that hung down the centre of her forehead, and two more points that met over her bosom. She wore a red kirtle with long sleeves, kilted very high in front to show a green farthingale, and a great red leather purse hanging down over it, red stockings, yellow leathern shoes cut low-quartered and square-toed, secured by a strap buckling over the instep, a simple style that was the rough early germ of the jewelled diamond buckle that would come a century later.
Margaret continued reading:
“But oh! how I missed my Denys at every step! Often I sat down on the road and groaned. And in the afternoon it chanced that I did so set me down where two roads met, and with heavy head in hand, and heavy heart, did think of thee, my poor sweetheart, and of my lost friend, and of the little house at Tergou, where they all loved me once; though now it is turned to hate.”
“Alas! that he will think so,” Catherine whispered, her voice breaking.
“Whisht, wife!” Eli hissed.
“And I did sigh loud, and often. And me sighing so, one came carolling like a bird adown t’ other road. ‘Ay, chirp and chirp,’ cried I bitterly. ‘Thou hast not lost sweetheart, and friend, thy father’s hearth, thy mother’s smile, and every penny in the world.’ And at last he did so carol, and carol, I jumped up in ire to get away from his most jarring mirth. But ere I fled from it, I looked down the path to see what could make a man so lighthearted in this weary world; and lo! the songster was a humpbacked cripple, with a bloody bandage o’er his eye, and both legs gone at the knee.”
“He! he! he! he! he!” cackled Sybrandt, doubling over laughing.
Margaret’s eyes flashed, and she began to fold the letter up, ready to march for the door.
“Nay, lass,” Eli barked, “heed him not! Thou unmannerly cur, open thy mouth to gibe once more and I’ll drag thee out and put thee to the door.”
“Why, what was there to laugh at, Sybrandt?” Catherine remonstrated more mildly. “Is not our Kate afflicted? and is she not the most content of us all, and singeth like a merle at times between her pains? But I am as bad as thou for chattering, prithee read on, lass, and stop our gabble with something worth the hearkening.”
“‘Then,’ said I, ‘may this thing be?’ And I took myself to task. ‘Gerard, son of Eli, dost thou well to bemoan thy lot, thou hast youth and health; and here comes the wreck of nature on crutches, praising God’s goodness with singing like a mavis?’”
“There you see,” Catherine murmured.
“Whisht, dame, whisht!” Eli muttered.
“And whenever he saw me, he left carolling and presently hobbled up and chanted, ‘Charity, for love of Heaven, sweet master, charity,’ with a whine as piteous as wind whistling through a keyhole. ‘Alack, poor soul,’ said I, ‘charity is in my heart, but not my purse; I am poor as thou.’ Then he believed me none, and to melt me undid his sleeve, and showed a sore wound on his arm, and said he, ‘Poor cripple though I be, I am like to lose this eye to boot, look else.’ I saw and groaned for him, and to excuse myself let him wot how I had been robbed of my last copper. Thereat he left whining all in a moment, and said, in a big manly voice, ‘Then I’ll e’en take a rest. Here, youngster, pull thou this strap: nay, fear not!’ I pulled, and down came a stout pair of legs out of his back; and half his hump had melted away, and the wound in his eye no deeper than the bandage.”
