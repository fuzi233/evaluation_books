For a long while—perhaps two hours—we sat in silence, overwhelmed by the horrors we had witnessed. Words failed us; even thought seemed too heavy to carry. The night was drawing near its end when, just as we began to consider retiring, we heard the sound of approaching footsteps. Then came the sharp challenge of a sentry posted at the kraal gate. Though no voice replied audibly, the steps continued forward, and in moments Infadoos entered the hut, followed by half a dozen imposing chiefs, their bearing proud and grave.

“My lords,” he said, “I have come as I promised. Here are the great men of our people—each commanding three thousand warriors who live only to obey the king’s will. I have told them what I have seen and heard. Now let them see the sacred snake upon Ignosi, and hear his tale, so that they may decide whether they will join him in battle against Twala, the false king.”

At once, Ignosi stripped off his girdle and revealed the intricate tattoo of the serpent coiled around his body. One by one, the chiefs stepped forward, peering closely at the mark under the dim glow of the lamp. Each examined it silently before stepping aside without a word.

Then Ignosi resumed his seat and repeated the story he had told earlier—the tale of his birthright, his exile, and the cruel reign of Twala.

“Now you have heard,” Infadoos said when he finished. “What say you, chiefs? Will you stand with this man and help him reclaim his father’s throne—or will you not? The land cries out under Twala’s rule. Blood flows like spring rain. You saw tonight—two other chiefs I meant to speak with—where are they now? The hyenas feast on their bones. Soon you too may be among them if you do not act. Choose, my brothers!”

The eldest chief, a short, broad-shouldered warrior with white hair, stepped forward.

“Your words are true, Infadoos. The land does cry out. My own brother died tonight. But this is no small matter. How can we know that if we raise our spears, we are not aiding a thief or a liar? This is a great thing—no one can foresee its end. For certain, blood will flow in rivers before this is done. Many still follow Twala, for men worship the sun that shines bright rather than the one yet unseen. These white men from the Stars possess great magic. If Ignosi is truly the rightful king, then let them give us a sign—one that all can see. Only then will the people believe, and stand with us.”

“I have the sign of the snake,” I said.

“No,” the chief replied firmly. “It is not enough. The mark could have been placed there since childhood. Show us a sign—and it shall suffice. But we will not move without a sign.”

The others nodded in agreement. I turned to Sir Henry and Good, perplexed.

“I think I’ve got it,” Good declared suddenly, eyes gleaming. “Give us a moment to think.”

I relayed the request, and the chiefs withdrew. As soon as they were gone, Good hurried to his medicine box, unlocked it, and pulled out a notebook. In the flyleaf was an almanac.

“Look here,” he said. “Isn’t tomorrow June 4th?”

We confirmed it—our days had been carefully tracked.

“Excellent,” he said. “Here it says: ‘June 4, total eclipse of the moon begins at 8:15 Greenwich time—visible in Teneriffe, South Africa, etc.’ There’s your sign. Tell them we’ll darken the moon tomorrow night.”

The idea struck us all as brilliant—though dangerous. The only flaw was the fear that Good’s almanac might be wrong. If we failed, our credibility would vanish, and so would Ignosi’s chance of the throne.

“What if the almanac is incorrect?” Sir Henry asked, watching Good calculate something on a blank page.

“I see no reason to doubt it,” Good answered. “Eclipses happen on schedule—always, in my experience. And it specifically states this one will be visible in South Africa. I’ve worked out the timing as best I can without knowing our exact position. It should begin here around ten o’clock tonight and last until past midnight. For an hour and a half, nearly total darkness.”

“Well,” Sir Henry said, “I suppose we must risk it.”

I hesitated. Eclipses were unpredictable—clouds could ruin everything. But we had no choice. I sent Umbopa to summon the chiefs back.

They returned, and I addressed them:

“Great men of the Kukuanas, and thou, Infadoos—listen well. We do not wish to reveal our powers, for such things disturb nature and bring fear. But this matter is too great. We are angered by the slaughter we saw, and by the treachery of Gagool, who sought to kill our friend Ignosi. So we break a rule and offer a sign that all may witness. Come here.” I led them to the door and pointed to the full moon glowing red above the horizon. “What do you see?”

“The sinking moon,” the spokesman replied.

“It is so. Now tell me—can any mortal extinguish that moon before its time? Can any man bring blackness over the land?”

The chief chuckled slightly. “No, my lord. No man can do such a thing. The moon is stronger than any who gaze upon her. She follows her course unshaken.”

“You say so. Yet I tell you—tomorrow night, about two hours before midnight, we will cause the moon to be devoured. For an hour and a half, darkness will fall across the earth. This shall be the sign that Ignosi is truly king of the Kukuanas. If we do this, will you believe?”

“Yes, my lords,” the old chief replied with a smile that spread across the faces of the others. “If you do this, we shall believe indeed.”

“It shall be done,” I said. “We three—Incubu, Bougwan, and Macumazahn—have spoken, and it shall be done. Do you hear, Infadoos?”

“I hear, my lord. But it is a wondrous thing to promise—to extinguish the moon, mother of the world, when she is at her fullest.”

“Yet we shall do it,” I said.

“It is well,” Infadoos replied. “Twala will send for you two hours after sunset. You are to witness the girls’ dance. One hour after it begins, the girl he deems fairest will be killed by Scragga, his son, as a sacrifice to the Silent Ones who watch from those mountains yonder.” He gestured toward the distant peaks where Solomon’s Road was said to end. “Then, when the moon darkens, save the maiden’s life—and the people will believe.”

“Ay,” the old chief murmured, still smiling. “They will believe indeed.”

“Two miles from Loo,” Infadoos went on, “there is a hill shaped like a new moon—a stronghold. My regiment, and three others commanded by these chiefs, are stationed there. Tomorrow morning, we will plan to move more regiments into position. If you truly darken the moon, I will take you by the hand and lead you safely out of Loo to that hill, where you will be secure. From there, we can rise against Twala.”

“It is good,” I said. “Now go—let us rest and prepare our magic.”

Infadoos bowed and left with the chiefs.

As soon as they were gone, Ignosi spoke.

“My friends,” he said, “can you truly do this miracle? Or did you speak only empty words to the captains?”

“We believe we can, Umbopa—Ignosi, I mean.”

“It is strange,” he replied. “Had you not been Englishmen, I would not have believed. But I have learned that English gentlemen do not lie. If we survive this, I swear I will repay you.”

“Ignosi,” Sir Henry said, “promise me one thing.”

“I will promise, Incubu, before I even hear it,” Ignosi answered with a smile. “What is it?”

“This: if you become king, abolish the hunting of wizards as we saw last night. And no man shall die without trial or judgment.”

Ignosi paused, then said, “The ways of black men are not the ways of white men. We do not value life as you do. Yet I promise. If I can stop them, the witch-finders shall hunt no more. No man shall die without trial.”

“That’s a bargain,” Sir Henry said. “Now let us rest.”

Exhausted, we fell asleep quickly and slept until Ignosi woke us around eleven. We rose, washed, and ate a hearty breakfast. Then we walked outside, studying the structure of the huts and observing the women’s customs.

“I hope the eclipse comes through,” Sir Henry said.

“If it doesn’t,” I replied, “we’re done for. Some chief will tell Twala, and then there’ll be another kind of eclipse—one we won’t enjoy.”

After lunch, we spent the day receiving ceremonial visits and curious onlookers. At dusk, the sun set, and we passed a few quiet hours, though our minds were restless with dread. Around half-past eight, a messenger arrived from Twala, inviting us to the annual “dance of the girls.”

We hastily donned the chain shirts the king had given us, took our rifles and ammunition, and set off—brave outwardly, but trembling within. The space before the king’s kraal looked entirely different. Instead of armed warriors, rows of Kukuana girls danced in graceful procession. Barely dressed, each wore a flower crown, holding a palm leaf and a white arum lily. In the center sat Twala, with Gagool at his feet, flanked by Infadoos, Scragga, and twelve guards. A dozen chiefs were present, including several we recognized.

Twala greeted us warmly, though his single eye fixed coldly on Umbopa.

“Welcome, white men from the Stars,” he said. “This is a different sight from last night’s moonlight horror—but not as good. Girls are pleasant. Without them, none of us would be here today. But men are better. Kisses and soft words are sweet—but the clash of spears and the scent of blood are sweeter still! Would you take wives from our people? Choose the fairest, and they shall be yours—any number you desire.”

Good glanced at the girls, clearly tempted. But I cut in swiftly.

“Thank you, O king, but we white men wed only white women. Your maidens are fair—but not for us!”

Twala laughed. “A wise choice. Our proverb says, ‘Women’s eyes are always bright, whatever the color.’ Another says, ‘Love the woman who is near, for the one afar is false.’ But perhaps such truths do not hold in the Stars. In a land where men are white, all things are possible. Very well—your choice is made. Welcome again. And welcome, black one. Had Gagool prevailed, you’d be stiff and cold by now. Lucky for you—you too came from the Stars! Ha! ha!”

“I can kill you before you kill me, O king,” Ignosi said calmly. “And you shall be stiff before my limbs cease to bend.”

Twala started. “You speak boldly, boy. Do not push too far.”

“He speaks truth,” I said. “Truth is a sharp spear—once thrown, it never misses. It is a message from the Stars, O king.”

Twala scowled, his eye flashing, but said nothing.

“Let the dance begin!” he cried.

The girls surged forward, singing softly, waving palms and lilies. Under the moonlight, they moved with ethereal grace—whirling, meeting in mock combat, eddying in patterns both chaotic and beautiful. At last, one girl broke from the ranks and danced alone—her movements fluid and powerful, surpassing all others. When she finished, exhausted, another took her place. But none matched her beauty or skill.

When all had danced, Twala raised his hand.

“Which is the fairest, white men?” he asked.

“The first,” I said, without thinking.

Instantly I regretted it. Infadoos had warned us—the fairest girl would be sacrificed.

“Then my mind agrees with yours,” Twala said. “She is the fairest—and a sorry fate awaits her. She must die!”

“Ay, must die!” Gagool piped, her eyes glinting toward the girl, who stood unaware, ten yards away, nervously pulling petals from her wreath.

“Why, O king?” I asked, barely controlling my anger. “She danced well, pleased us, and is fair. To reward her with death is cruel.”

Twala laughed. “It is our custom. The figures of stone yonder”—he pointed to the peaks—“must have their due. If I fail to offer a fair girl, misfortune will fall upon me and my house. The prophecy says: ‘If the king offers not a sacrifice of a fair girl on the day of the dance, then he shall fall, and his house.’ See, white men—my brother refused, because of the woman’s tears. He fell. I reign in his place. It is finished—she must die!”

He turned to the guards. “Bring her here. Scragga, sharpen thy spear.”

Two guards stepped forward. The girl, now realizing her fate, screamed and tried to flee. But they seized her, dragging her weeping and struggling before us.

“What is thy name, girl?” Gagool croaked.

She remained silent.

“Shall the king’s son do his work at once?”

Scragga advanced, lifting his spear. I saw Good’s hand creep toward his revolver. The girl noticed the glint of steel through her tears and stopped struggling. She clasped her hands and shuddered violently.

“See,” Scragga crowed, “she fears my little plaything even before tasting it!” He tapped the blade.

“If I ever get the chance,” I heard Good mutter, “you’ll pay for that, young hound.”

“Now that thou art quiet, give us thy name, my dear,” Gagool mocked. “Come, speak—fear not.”

“Oh, mother,” the girl whispered, trembling, “my name is Foulata, of the house of Suko. Oh, mother, why must I die? I have done no wrong!”

“Be comforted,” Gagool said, mocking. “Thou must die—as a sacrifice to the Old Ones who sit yonder.” She pointed to the peaks. “But it is better to sleep in the night than toil in the day. Better to die than to live—and thou shalt die by the royal hand of the king’s son.”

Foulata wrung her hands. “Oh, cruel! I am so young! What have I done that I may never see the sun rise again, nor the stars follow in his wake? That I may never gather flowers in the dew, nor listen to the laughter of the streams? Woe is me—I shall never see my father’s hut again, nor feel my mother’s kiss, nor tend the sick lamb! Woe is me—that no lover shall embrace me, nor shall children be born of me! Oh, cruel, cruel!”

She threw herself forward and clutched Good’s legs.

“Oh, white father from the Stars!” she cried. “Shield me with your strength! Let me hide in your shadow! Save me from these cruel men and Gagool’s mercy!”

“All right, my hearty,” Good said in rough Saxon. “I’ll look after you. Come on, get up—there’s a good girl.” He stooped and took her hand.

Twala turned to his son. “Now’s your time,” Sir Henry whispered to me. “What are you waiting for?”

“I’m waiting for the eclipse,” I said. “I’ve watched the moon for half an hour—it looks healthier than ever.”

“Well, you must risk it now, or she dies. Twala is losing patience.”

I cast one final despairing glance at the moon—then saw it. On its edge, a faint shadow crept. Smoke-like darkness gathered. Relief flooded through me.

I stepped between the girl and Scragga’s spear.

“King,” I said, “it shall not be. We will not allow this. Let the girl go in safety.”

Twala rose, furious. A murmur rose from the crowd.

“_Shall not be!_” he roared. “Thou white dog, barking at the lion in his cave! _Shall not be!_ Art thou mad? Be careful, lest this chicken’s fate overtake thee and thine. Who art thou to stand between me and my will? Back! Scragga, kill her! Guards—seize them!”

Armed men rushed from behind the hut.

Sir Henry, Good, and Umbopa stood beside me, rifles raised.

“Stop!” I shouted, heart pounding. “Stop! We, the white men from the Stars, say it shall not be. Take one step closer, and we will extinguish the moon like a wind-blown lamp—just as we who dwell in her House can do. Dare to disobey, and taste our magic!”

The men halted. Scragga froze.

“Hear him! Hear him!” Gagool shrieked. “The liar says he’ll put out the moon! Let him do it—and the girl lives. Let him fail—and he dies with her!”

I looked up. The shadow deepened. Joy surged through me.

I raised my hand toward the sky. Sir Henry and Good followed. I quoted lines from *Ingoldsby Legends* in solemn tones. Sir Henry recited a verse from the Old Testament, then added a Latin passage about Balbus building a wall. Good launched into a torrent of classical curses aimed at the Queen of Night.

Slowly, the penumbra crept across the moon. Gasps rose from the crowd.

“Look, O king!” I cried. “Look, Gagool! Look, chiefs and people—see if the white men from the Stars keep their word, or if they are liars!

“The moon grows black before your eyes! Darkness comes—yes, darkness in the hour of the full moon! Ye asked for a sign—it is given. Grow dark, O Moon! Withdraw thy light, thou pure and holy One! Bring the proud heart of the usurper low, and cover the world in shadows!”

A groan of terror burst from the onlookers. Some stood frozen. Others fell to their knees. Twala paled beneath his skin. Only Gagool remained defiant.

“It will pass!” she cried. “I’ve seen this before. No man can extinguish the moon. Do not lose heart—sit still. The shadow will fade.”

“Wait,” I said, hopping with excitement. “O Moon! Moon! Moon! Wherefore art thou so cold and fickle?” I quoted from a romance I’d recently read—though now I realize it was unkind to the Lady of the Heavens, who was proving herself our truest ally.

“Keep it up, Good!” I called. “I can’t remember any more poetry. Curse away—there’s a good fellow!”

Good responded magnificently. For ten minutes, he raged in multiple languages, never repeating himself. Never before had I known such depth of naval invective.

And as the darkness deepened, the heavens themselves seemed to side with us.
