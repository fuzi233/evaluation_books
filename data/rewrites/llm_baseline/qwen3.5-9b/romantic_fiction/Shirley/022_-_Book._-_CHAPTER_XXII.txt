Only half of Moore's energy and determination had been displayed in his defense of the mill; the other half—a far more terrifying facet—was revealed in his tireless, relentless pursuit of the riot's leaders. The mob itself, the mere followers, he left entirely alone. Perhaps an innate sense of justice guided him, telling him that men misled by false counsel and driven to madness by privation were unfit targets for vengeance. To strike down the bowed heads of the suffering would make him a tyrant, not a judge. Consequently, though he recognized many of the common rioters during the daylight hours of the attack, he let them pass him daily on the streets and roads without a glance or a threat.

The leaders, however, were different. They were strangers—emissaries from the great cities. Most were not of the working class; rather, they were "down-draughts": bankrupts, men perpetually in debt and often intoxicated, possessing nothing to lose and everything to gain in terms of character, cash, and cleanliness. Moore hunted these individuals with the ferocity of a bloodhound, and he took a genuine pleasure in the occupation. The thrill of the chase suited his nature far better than the mundane labor of weaving cloth. His horse seemed to share this sentiment, worn thin by the constant riding. Moore lived on the road, finding the fresh air as vital to his lungs as the hunt was to his spirit; he preferred it to the stifling steam of the dye-houses.

The local magistrates likely dreaded him. They were slow, timid men, whereas Moore enjoyed frightening and rousing them. He relished forcing them into a state of fear, causing them to falter in resolve and recoil in action—the primal terror of assassination. Indeed, this dread had paralyzed every manufacturer and nearly every public figure in the district until Helstone had stood firm. The old Cossack knew he could be shot; he understood the risks perfectly. Yet, such a death held no terror for his nerves. It would have been his chosen fate, had he had a choice. Moore, too, was acutely aware of the danger. This awareness fueled an unquenchable scorn for the quarter from which threats emerged. The knowledge that he was hunting assassins acted as a spur to his high-spirited temperament. Fear was alien to him; he was too proud, too hard-hearted, and too phlegmatic to succumb to it. Often, he rode late over the moors, whether moonlit or moonless, feeling more elated and refreshed than when surrounded by the safety and stagnation of the counting-house.

There were four leaders to account for. Within a fortnight, two were cornered near Stilbro'. The remaining two required a search further afield, rumored to haunt the outskirts of Birmingham. Meanwhile, Moore did not neglect his battered mill. Repairing it proved to be a relatively light task, requiring only carpenters and glaziers. Since the rioters had failed to breach the defenses, his grim metal creations—the machinery—had escaped damage.

Whether, amidst this busy life of stern justice and demanding business, Moore occasionally paused to nurture gentler fires than those smoldering in the temple of Nemesis, remained difficult to discern. He rarely visited Fieldhead; if he did, his stays were brief. Calls at the rectory were strictly confined to conferences with the rector in his study. He maintained his rigid course with steady resolve.

Outside, the year's history remained turbulent. There was no lull in the tempest of war; its long hurricane still swept across the continent. Not a sign of serene weather appeared, no break in the clouds of battle-dust, no gentle dew for the olives, no cessation of the red rain that nourished the laurel. Under Moore's feet, Ruin worked tirelessly with her sappers and miners. Whether he rode or walked, whether he crossed his counting-house hearth or galloped over the sullen lands of Rushedge, he felt the hollow echo beneath him and sensed the ground shaking with every step.

While summer passed for Moore, how did it lapse for Shirley and Caroline? Let us first visit the heiress. How did she appear? Was she a love-lorn maiden, pale and pining for a neglectful suitor? Did she spend her days bent over sedentary tasks, clutching a book or needlework, with eyes fixed solely on them and silence filling the rest? By no means. Shirley was thriving. Though her wistful expression had not vanished, neither had her careless smile. She kept her dark old manor-house bright and cheerful with her vibrant presence. The gallery and low-ceilinged chambers echoed lively sounds from her voice; the dim entrance hall, with its single window, grew accustomed to the frequent rustle of silk dresses as she swept from room to room—carrying flowers to the barbarous peach-bloom salon, opening casements in the dining room to let in the scent of mignonette and sweet-briar, or fetching plants from the staircase window to place in the sun at the open porch.

She attempted sewing occasionally, but fate seemed to doom her to never sit steadily at it for more than five minutes. Hardly had her thimble been fitted or her needle threaded when a sudden thought summoned her upstairs. Perhaps she sought a forgotten ivory-backed needle-book or an ancient china-topped workbox, items unnecessary yet suddenly indispensable. Maybe she wished to arrange her hair or tidy a drawer she recalled seeing in disarray that morning. Or perhaps she merely wanted a glimpse from a specific window at a particular view, where Briarfield church and rectory were pleasantly nestled among trees.

Scarcely had she returned to her slip of cambric or square of unfinished canvas when Tartar's bold scrape and strangled whistle sounded at the porch. She ran to open the door. It was a hot day; the dog entered panting, and she hurried him to the kitchen to ensure his water bowl was filled. Through the open kitchen door lay the courtyard, sunny and gay, teeming with turkeys and poults, peahens and chicks, pearl-spotted Guinea-fowls, and a dazzling array of white, purple-necked, blue, and cinnamon-plumed pigeons. An irresistible sight for Shirley! She rushed to the pantry for a roll and stood on the doorstep scattering crumbs. Around her thronged her eager, plump, happy feathered subjects.

John was about the stables, and John demanded conversation, alongside a look at her mare. Still petting the animal, she was interrupted by the arrival of the cows for milking. This was important; Shirley stayed to inspect them all. There were perhaps some tiny calves, some newly weaned lambs—perhaps twins whose mothers had rejected them. Miss Keeldar was introduced to them by John and permitted the treat of feeding them with her own hands under the supervision of her careful foreman.

Meanwhile, John raised doubtful questions regarding the farming of certain "crofts," "ings," and "holmes." His mistress was forced to fetch her garden hat—a gypsy straw—and accompany him over stile and hedge to hear the conclusion of the agricultural debate on the spot, with the said fields under her gaze. Thus, the bright afternoon wore into soft evening. She returned home to a late tea, and after tea, she never sewed.

After tea, Shirley read. She was just as tenacious with her book as she was lax with her needle. Her study surface was the rug, her seat a footstool, or perhaps merely the carpet at Mrs. Pryor's feet: the very place where she had learned her lessons as a child, and old habits held a powerful sway. The tawny, lion-like bulk of Tartar stretched beside her, his black muzzle resting on his forepaws—straight, strong, and shapely as the limbs of an Alpine wolf. One of the mistress's hands generally rested on the loyal servant's rough head, for if removed, he groaned in discontent.

Shirley's mind was absorbed in her book. She did not lift her eyes, stir, or speak—unless, indeed, to offer a brief, respectful reply to Mrs. Pryor, who addressed deprecating phrases now and then. "My dear, you had better not have that great dog so near you; he is crushing the border of your dress." "Oh, it is only muslin. I can put a clean one on tomorrow." "My dear, I wish you could acquire the habit of sitting at a table when you read." "I will try, ma'am, sometime; but it is so comfortable to do as one has always done." "My dear, let me beg you to put that book down. You are straining your eyes by the doubtful firelight." "No, ma'am, not at all; my eyes are never tired."

Finally, a pale light fell upon the page from the window. She looked; the moon was up. She closed the volume, rose, and walked through the room. Her book had perhaps been a good one; it had refreshed, refilled, and rewarmed her heart, setting her brain alight and furnishing her mind with vivid images. The still parlor, the clean hearth, the window opening onto the twilight sky revealing its "sweet regent," newly throned and glorious, sufficed to make earth an Eden and life a poem for Shirley. A still, deep, inborn delight glowed in her young veins, unmingled and untroubled, unreachable by human agency because bestowed by none—it was the pure gift of God to His creature, the free dower of Nature to her child.
