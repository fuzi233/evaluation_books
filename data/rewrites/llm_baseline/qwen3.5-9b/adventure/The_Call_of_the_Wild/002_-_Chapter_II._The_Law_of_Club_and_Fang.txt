Buck's first day on the Dyea beach felt like a nightmare. Every hour brought a fresh shock, tearing him from the soft, sun-drenched world of civilization and hurling him into something ancient and raw. There was no leisure here, no boredom-inducing loafing. Instead, there was only chaos, constant motion, and a terrifying lack of safety. Life and limb hung in the balance every second. Survival demanded absolute, unceasing vigilance, for these were not the gentle dogs and polite men of the city. These were savages, bound by a single, brutal code: the Law of Club and Fang.

He had never witnessed dogs fight like these wolf-like creatures until now, and his first encounter taught him a lesson he would never forget—a lesson learned vicariously, for if he hadn't survived to witness it, he wouldn't have profited from it. The victim was Curly.

They were camped near the log store, where Curly, in her usual friendly manner, had approached a massive husky nearly the size of a full-grown wolf. There was no warning. In a flash, the giant lunged, delivering a metallic snap of teeth. Just as quickly, he leaped back. Curly's face was instantly ripped open from eye to jaw. It was the wolf's method: strike and retreat. But there was more to it. Within thirty seconds, thirty or forty other huskies converged, forming a silent, intent circle around the combatants. Buck didn't understand their silence or the greedy licks of their tongues.

Curly charged her opponent again. He met her rush squarely with his chest, tumbling her off her feet. She never rose. This was exactly what the surrounding pack had waited for. They closed in, snarling and yelping, burying her screaming form beneath a bristling mountain of bodies.

So sudden and shocking was the turn of events that Buck was stunned. He saw Spitz stick out his scarlet tongue in a gesture that looked like laughter. Then he saw François, swinging an axe, spring into the melee. Three men with clubs joined him to scatter the attackers. It took only two minutes from the moment Curly hit the ground until the last attacker was clubbed away. But Curley lay limp and lifeless in the bloody, trampled snow, almost literally torn apart, while a swart half-breed stood over her, cursing in a language Buck couldn't understand.

That image haunted Buck in his sleep. *That* was the way. No fairness. Once you were down, you were finished. Vowing silently to himself, Buck decided he would ensure he never went down. Spitz stuck out his tongue and laughed again, and from that moment, Buck harbored a bitter, deathless hatred for him.

Before Buck could fully process the tragedy of Curly's death, he received another blow. François strapped him into a harness of leather and buckles—gear Buck recognized from the stables at home. Just as he had seen horses work, so now he was set to work. He was hauled onto a sled to pull François through the forest bordering the valley, returning laden with firewood. His dignity was crushed by this reduction to a draft animal, yet he was too wise to rebel. He buckled down with determination, doing his best despite the strangeness of it all.

François was stern, demanding instant obedience, enforced by the crack of his whip. Dave, an experienced wheel dog, nipped Buck's hindquarters whenever he erred. Spitz, the leader, also experienced, growled sharp reproofs or cunningly shifted his weight in the traces to jerk Buck into the correct path. Buck was an apt student; under the combined tutelage of his mates and François, he made remarkable progress. Before they returned to camp, he knew how to stop at "Ho," move forward at "Mush," navigate turns safely, and avoid the wheelers when the loaded sled thundered downhill.

"T'ree vair' good dogs," François told Perrault. "Dat Buck, heem pool lak hell. I tich heem queek as anyt'ing." ("Three very good dogs," François said to Perrault. "That Buck, he learns like hell. I teach him quicker than anything.")

By afternoon, Perrault, eager to depart with government dispatches, returned with two more dogs: Billee and Joe, brothers born of the same mother but as different as day and night. Billee's flaw was an excessive, foolish goodness. Joe was the opposite: sour, introspective, perpetually snarling with a malignant eye. Buck greeted them amicably. Dave ignored them, but Spitz immediately began thrashing them. Billee wagged his tail in appeasement, tried to run when that failed, and whimpered as Spitz's teeth scored his flank. No matter how Spitz circled, Joe spun on his heels to face him, mane bristling, ears pinned back, lips writhing, jaws snapping furiously, eyes glowing with diabolical light—he was the embodiment of belligerent fear.

Spitz was forced to abandon disciplining Joe due to the sheer terror of the creature's appearance. To cover his own embarrassment, Spitz turned on the innocent, wailing Billee and drove him to the edge of the camp.

By evening, Perrault acquired another dog: an old, lean, gaunt husky named Sol-leks, meaning "The Angry One." Like Dave, he asked for nothing, gave nothing, and expected nothing. When he marched slowly and deliberately into the group, even Spitz left him alone. However, Buck discovered a fatal flaw: Sol-leks disliked being approached on his blind side. Unwittingly guilty of this offense, Buck paid the price when Sol-leks whipped around and slashed his shoulder to the bone, three inches up and down. From that day forward, Buck kept clear of Sol-leks' blind side, and their companionship remained peaceful.

Like Dave, Buck's only visible ambition was solitude, though he would later learn each possessed a deeper drive. That night, Buck faced the great problem of sleeping. The tent, lit by a candle, glowed warmly in the white expanse. When he entered it naturally, Perrault and François bombarded him with curses and cooking pots until he fled in humiliation into the freezing outdoors. A biting wind chilled him, especially his wounded shoulder. Lying on the snow, he tried to sleep, but the frost drove him shivering to his feet. Miserable, he wandered among the tents, finding none warmer. Savage dogs rushed at him, but he bristled and snarled, having learned quickly, and they let him pass.

Then, an idea struck him. He would check on his teammates. To his astonishment, they were gone. He searched the camp again, returned, and searched once more. Were they in the tent? Impossible; he had been thrown out. Where could they be? Drooping-tailed and shivering, he circled the tent aimlessly. Suddenly, the snow gave way beneath his paws, and he sank. Something wriggled under his feet. He recoiled, bristling in fear of the unknown. But a friendly little yelp reassured him. He investigated. A waft of warm air reached his nose. There, curled into a tight ball under the snow, was Billee.

Billee whined placatingly, squirming to show goodwill, and even licked Buck's face with his warm, wet tongue as a peace offering. Another lesson. *So that was the way,* Buck realized. Confidently, he selected a spot and began digging a hole. In moments, his body heat filled the confined space, and he drifted into a sound, comfortable sleep, though plagued by nightmares.

Morning came with the noise of the waking camp. Buck opened his eyes, confused. Snow had fallen overnight, burying him completely. The walls of snow pressed in on every side, triggering a primal surge of fear—the fear of the wild beast trapped in a cage. It was a regression to the instincts of his ancestors; as a civilized dog, he knew no traps and shouldn't fear them. His muscles contracted spasmodically, his hackles rose, and with a ferocious snarl, he bounded straight up into the blinding daylight, sending snow flying.

As he landed, he saw the white camp spread before him, recognized his location, and recalled everything from his walk with Manuel to the hole he had dug the night before.

"Wot I say?" François shouted to Perrault. "Dat Buck for sure learn queek as anyt'ing." ("What do you say?" the driver cried to Perrault. "That Buck certainly learns like hell.")

Perrault nodded gravely. As a courier for the Canadian Government carrying vital dispatches, he needed the best dogs possible, and Buck's rapid adaptation delighted him. Within an hour, three more huskies joined the team, bringing the total to nine. Before another fifteen minutes passed, they were harnessed and swinging up the trail toward the Dyea Canyon.

Buck was relieved to be moving. Although the work was grueling, he didn't despise it. He was surprised by the eagerness animating the entire team, a spirit that infected him. Even more startling was the transformation in Dave and Sol-leks. Their previous passiveness had vanished. They were now alert, active, and fiercely irritable toward anything delaying the work. The toil of the traces seemed to be their sole purpose, the only thing they lived for.
