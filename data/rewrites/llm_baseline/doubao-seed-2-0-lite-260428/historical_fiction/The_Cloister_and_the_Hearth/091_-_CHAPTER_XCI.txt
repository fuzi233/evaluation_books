Barely a handful of quiet days slipped past before the tidings came: Margaret Van Eyck was dead and laid in her grave. A will she had drawn up a full year prior left all her property, after covering funeral costs and a handful of small personal gifts to her loyal maidservant Reicht Heynes, to her beloved adopted daughter Margaret Brandt, with a gentle request that Margaret keep Reicht in her household for as long as the older woman remained unwed.

Margaret thus inherited a fully furnished house, plus a collection of paintings and sketches that would count as a princely fortune in the modern day. Chief among them was a piece she valued more than an entire royal gallery of works: *The Betrothal*, a scene where the quiet solemnity of the ceremony glowed on the grave, earnest face of the groom and the soft demure complacency of the bride. The elder Margaret Van Eyck had painted almost all of the central figures, with Jan Van Eyck finishing the intricate accessories that remained a marvel of master craft centuries later. Margaret sent word back to Reicht begging her to stay on in the Van Eyck home for the time being; she could not yet bear to cross its threshold, to walk halls that no longer hummed with the old painter's voice and warm greeting. She charged Reicht to guard the little picture in the "small cubboord" above all others — the tiny hand-painted diptych that had been Margaret Van Eyck's most cherished possession.

The very next week Luke Peterson returned home, and learned the news that Gerard had taken holy orders as a monk. He was nearly mad with joy, and burst straight to Margaret's cottage.
"Hear me, mistress," he cried. "If he cannot marry you, I can."
"You?" Margaret blinked at him. "I have already seen him."
"But he's a friar now."
"He was my husband, and my boy's father, long before he ever put on a friar's robes." Margaret's voice was steady, though her hands twisted tight in her apron. "I have seen him, I tell you, I have seen him."
Luke was thoroughly flummoxed. "I have a cousin who is a lawyer," he said. "I'll go ask him straight if you are legally married or still free to wed."
"Nay, I do not need a lawyer to tell me what my own heart already knows." Margaret shook her head, sharp with quiet disappointment. "And you would make me the talk of the whole town over this? Fie on you, that is no mark of respect for me. If you so much as breathe a word of this to any soul outside this room, you will never come near me again."
Luke sighed heavy as lead. "You are soft as a dove to every other soul in the parish, but a hard-hearted tyrant to me."
"That is your own fault, dear Luke, for wooing me." Margaret's tone softened. "I cannot be the kind to you that I want to be, so long as you carry that hope in your heart. I am rich now, you see — I cannot make myself happy, but I can make my dearest friends happy. Look out at the street, look around at all the lasses who live here: twice as fair as I, and never worn thin with years of weeping. Pick one, speak to her yourself, and I will go speak to her mother on your behalf. They will not turn you down, mark my words."
Luke turned bright red. "I see what you mean. But if I cannot have your liking, I will have none of your charity. I was your servant back when you were poorer than I was, and hungrier too. If you would rather be a friar's secret companion than an honest man's wife, then you are not the woman I took you for. So we part without malice: you seek your comfort on that road no woman ever found joy on, and I will live and die a bachelor. Good even, mistress."
"Farewell, dear Luke." Margaret's eyes glistened. "May God forgive you for saying that to me."

For days after that, Margaret waited for the meeting with Gerard, torn between dread and desperate longing. She told herself it was only natural he needed a little time to work through his grief and his vows. Surely he would soon hear he was a father, and the urge to see their little boy would overcome every other restraint. "If that meeting does not kill me," she whispered to her reflection, "I know I will heal after it."
But day after day slipped by, and there was no word of him. A cold, creeping doubt seeped through her bones. What if his absence was intentional? What if he had run back to his cold, hard fellow monks, and they had convinced him he must never set eyes on her again? The cloister had already proven itself as merciless to true lovers as the grave itself. At that thought, the life felt to drain out of her. For the first time, sharp indignation began to mingle with her grief. Could he ever have truly loved her, to run from her and his own child without a single word? Even the foolish, stubborn Luke cared more for her than that.

While her mind was twisted in that dark spiral, the dwarf Giles came roaring into the house, banging his fists on the table. "I have hit the mark! Our Gerard is the Vicar of Gouda!"
A quick sketch of Giles's time at court will explain how he pulled off that feat. Some months before he joined the Burgundian royal household, his wits had suddenly bloomed: he himself dated the shift to the 8th of June, when he was swinging one-handed from a tight clothesline strung across the drying green, a week's worth of washing strung beside him, and something gave a soft crack inside his head. All at once his sharp intelligence was unchained. At court, his shrewd blunt speech, booming giant's voice and tiny frame made him an unignorable power — without that small stature, he would no doubt have been hauled straight to the gallows for his cheek. The young Duchess of Burgundy and the heiress apparent Marie doted on him, as great ladies have always petted clever dwarves, and the court poets fawned over him so thoroughly they could have stewed Goliath in all the buttered praise they poured over his head. They even versified and watered down rough, pithy lines he dictated, churning out long flowery verses that Giles sent to his father Eli, titled *The High and Puissant Princess Marie of Bourgogne her Lytel Jantilman Hys Complaynt of ye Coort, and Praise of a Rusticall Lyfe, Versificated and Empapyred by me the Lytel Jantilman's right lovynge and obsequious servitor*.
Giles reached the peak of his fame at a grand court joust, the day before the main events, when he challenged the Duke's personal giant to a trial of strength. He had a tall smooth pole planted ready, and at the appointed hour he shinnied up it like a squirrel, held his body perfectly horizontal by nothing but the strength of his arms, then slid down so fast the princess squeaked and hid her face in her hands, sure her tiny pocket Hercules would dash his brains out on the stone. The giant only managed to climb ten feet before he was sweating through his tunic, clambering back down to grumble that it was Giles's small body, not his greater strength, that won the contest. The crowd hooted that excuse down at once.
"Fine," Giles said, grinning. "If you will argue that, I'll wrestle you, and I will even blindfold you first."
The smarting giant jumped at the chance to win back his pride.
"Ladies and gentlemen," Giles called out to the crowd. "Watch yon blind Samson. At a single signal from me, he will bow low to me, and doff his bonnet."
Wagers flew left and right, and at the exact moment the bets were all laid, Giles popped a sharp, light punch right into the giant's gut. The giant doubled over in a perfect bow, his bonnet tumbling off his head, and the whole court roared with laughter. After that, Giles was an untouchable force at court.
Now he told Margaret the full story of how he secured the living for Gerard.
"When the princess sent for me to her bedchambers as she always did, to chase away her boredom, I came not full of my usual jests and country proverbs, but dull as lead," Giles recounted. "She asked what ailed me, if I was sick, and the hussies they call her maids of honour tittered that I must be in love. I told them I could not be half that mad, not after all the foolery I'd seen at court. The princess sent the ladies off to stand apart, and bade me speak my mind without fear. I reminded her of five years past, when a tall, fair young boy came to her chambers with a letter from painter Margaret Van Eyck — that boy was my brother Gerard. I told her how she and her mother the late Countess of Charolois had promised Gerard a good church living, once he was old enough to take orders. She burst straight into tears, saying she had not forgotten, that her mother on her deathbed had charged her to make sure that promise was kept. I told her the old Vicar of Gouda had died the week before, and she declared on the spot that Gerard would be the new Vicar of Gouda, no delays, no questions asked. She cried for her mother, I cried for my poor brother, and that is how it was done."
Margaret could not contain herself. She caught the tiny dwarf up in her arms, hugging him tight and kissing his cheek. "Oh Giles, your body is so small, but your heart is bigger than any man's twice your size. You are his truest friend. Bless you, bless you, a hundred times bless you! Now we will see him again, after that terrible day we lost sight of him."
Giles had the town criers shout the news that Friar Clement, now the new Vicar of Gouda, was wanted to take up his post across Rotterdam and all the neighbouring towns, to Margaret's secret delight. But days rolled on, and Gerard never came. Giles was mortified, Margaret indignant and wretched, convinced he had run all the way back to Italy, that he hated her for not being dead.
Joan suggested she consult the famous hermit of Gouda, but Margaret laughed the thought off. "What does a man shut up in a cave know of where Gerard has gone? Less than I, no doubt."

Not long after, a messenger arrived from Tergou, saying old Ghysbrecht Van Swieten had seen Gerard more recently than any other soul alive. Margaret set off for Tergou at once, and found the old burgomaster sitting in his wheeled chair in his garden, old and feeble, but glad to greet her. He told her he had not met Gerard Brandt, but Friar Clement, and that the day the friar crossed his threshold had been the most blessed of his whole life. He recounted how the friar had arrived, made him give back all the land he had stolen from Margaret years before, then gently tricked him into confessing the evil letter he had written to tear the two lovers apart. Ghysbrecht had sobbed that he could never be forgiven for that wicked act — and then the friar had leaned in, soft and tremulous, and said "Ghysbrecht, look at me closer. I am Gerard, son of Eli." The old burgomaster broke down weeping as he told Margaret how he had tried to fall at Gerard's feet in contrition, but Gerard would not let him. He said those three days he had spent praying under Ghysbrecht's roof had let him find the strength to forgive the man who had ruined his life. Then he had taken his leave, without waiting for anything more.
Margaret wept with him, told Ghysbrecht she forgave him too, and begged him to help her find Gerard before the Church gave the Gouda living to a stranger. The old man summoned his servants, dictated letters to every burgomaster in every major town across Holland, and sent trusted couriers as far away as Basle in Switzerland to spread the news that the new Vicar of Gouda was needed to take up his post. Margaret went home glowing with fresh hope, and swore to herself she would never judge Gerard again until she heard his side of the story straight from his own lips.

But soon a new, terrible disaster struck. Sybrandt, the wastrel son of Eli and Catherine, had been given gold Margaret had lent his mother to keep him fed, and he drank every penny of it away. Drunk out of his mind one afternoon, he climbed the stable roof of the inn he was carousing at, slipped, and fell straight onto a wooden paling, then toppled to the ground. His drunken friends carried him, helpless and unable to stand, to Margaret's door. She looked at his pale, streaky face, and knew at once his spine was broken — he would never walk again. The curse he had joked about for years had fallen on him at last. Margaret, remembering how Gerard had forgiven even his worst enemy Ghysbrecht, tended the helpless, injured man no matter all the wrongs he had done her.
Catherine arrived from Tergou almost mad with grief, blaming herself for sneaking her worthless son money to feed his drinking binges. Streaks of grey turned her hair white within weeks, and she was never the bright, bustling singing woman she had been before. Sybrandt wailed day and night for Gerard, begging Margaret to find him and lift the curse that had left him paralyzed. Luke Peterson appeared soon after, thin and haggard, saying he would rather be her loyal brother than stay away from her entirely, and would do any work she asked of him. Margaret sent him off to search every corner of the country, then the continent, for their lost marriage banns and Gerard himself.

Months slipped by: Sybrandt's body never healed, and he remained Margaret's helpless pensioner for life. Catherine moved into Margaret's cottage in Rotterdam, with Eli's quiet permission, saying little Gerard — Margaret's son, the spitting image of his father — was the only thing that could soothe her heavy, fearful heart, now that she lived in constant dread that the same curse that had struck Sybrandt would fall on her other rogue son Cornelis. The people of Gouda sent a second formal petition to the Duke of Burgundy, complaining they had gone six whole months without a vicar: their children were dying unbaptised, their dead were buried by chance travellers passing through, and no one could tend to their souls. Even with all of Giles's influence, there was no way to hold them off for much longer. Margaret's last sliver of hope was fraying thin, and the race to find Gerard before his living was given away to a stranger was nearly at its end.
