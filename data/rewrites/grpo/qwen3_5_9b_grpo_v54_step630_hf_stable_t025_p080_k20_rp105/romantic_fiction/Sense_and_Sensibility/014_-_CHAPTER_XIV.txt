The abrupt departure of Colonel Brandon from the park, coupled with his unyielding silence regarding the cause, occupied Mrs. Jennings' thoughts for several days. She was a woman of perpetual curiosity, finding it impossible to ignore the comings and goings of her acquaintances. With little respite, she speculated on the reason for his sudden exit, convinced that some terrible news must have arrived. She mentally cataloged every conceivable distress that could have befallen him, determined that he would not escape her worst fears.

"Something deeply melancholy must be the matter, I am certain," she declared. "I saw it in his face. Poor man! I fear his financial circumstances have turned sour. The Delaford estate was never worth much more than two thousand a year, and his brother left him in a state of ruin. I am convinced he was summoned for monetary reasons; surely nothing else could explain it. I wonder if it is true. I would give anything to know the truth. Perhaps it concerns Miss Williams, and indeed, I dare say it does, for he looked so conscious when I mentioned her name. Maybe she is ill in town; nothing is more likely, as I have always suspected she is somewhat delicate. I would wager my life it is about Miss Williams. It is not so probable he is currently distressed, for he is a prudent man and must have settled the estate by now. I wonder what it can be! Perhaps his sister is worse at Avignon and has called for him. His hurried departure seems exactly like that. Well, I wish him relief from all his troubles, and a good wife to boot."

Thus wondered and thus spoke Mrs. Jennings, her theories shifting with every fresh conjecture, all appearing equally plausible. Elinor, though genuinely concerned for Colonel Brandon's welfare, could not share her mother's endless amazement at his sudden leaving. In her view, the circumstance did not warrant such lasting confusion or variety of speculation. Her wonder was instead consumed by the extraordinary silence of her sister and Willoughby concerning the matter, a topic they knew to be of profound interest to everyone. As the days passed, this silence grew stranger and more incompatible with the evident affection between the young couple. Elinor could not fathom why they would not openly acknowledge to their mother and herself what their constant behavior clearly indicated had occurred.

She could easily understand that marriage might not be immediately feasible; although Willoughby was independent, there was no reason to believe him wealthy. His estate was valued by Sir John at roughly six or seven hundred pounds a year, yet he lived at an expense that income could scarcely sustain, often complaining of his poverty himself. However, this strange secrecy regarding their engagement, which concealed nothing, remained inexplicable. It was so wholly contrary to their general opinions and practices that a doubt occasionally entered Elinor's mind as to whether they were truly engaged, a hesitation sufficient to prevent her from inquiring of Marianne.

Nothing expressed greater attachment to them all than Willoughby's conduct. To Marianne, he offered every tenderness a lover's heart could muster; to the rest of the family, he displayed the affectionate attention of a devoted son and brother. The cottage seemed to be his true home; he spent more hours there than at Allenham. If no general engagement kept them at the park, his morning exercise invariably ended there, where the remainder of the day was spent beside Marianne, with his favorite pointer resting at her feet.

One evening, approximately a week after Colonel Brandon's departure, Willoughby's heart appeared more open than usual to the feelings surrounding him. When Mrs. Dashwood mentioned her intention to improve the cottage in the spring, he vigorously opposed any alteration to a place that affection had rendered perfect for him.

"What!" he exclaimed. "Improve this dear cottage! No. That I will never consent to. Not a stone must be added to its walls, not an inch to its size, if my feelings are to be respected."

"Do not be alarmed," said Miss Dashwood. "Nothing of the kind will be done; for my mother will never have the money to attempt it."

"I am heartily glad of it," he cried. "May she always be poor, if she can employ her riches no better."

"Thank you, Willoughby. But you may be assured that I would not sacrifice one sentiment of local attachment of yours, or of anyone I love, for all the improvements in the world. Depend upon it that whatever unemployed sum remains when I settle my accounts in the spring, I would rather lay it uselessly by than dispose of it in a manner so painful to you. But are you really so attached to this place that you see no defect in it?"

"I am," he replied. "To me, it is faultless. Nay, more, I consider it the only form of building in which happiness is attainable. Were I rich enough, I would instantly pull Combe down and rebuild it in the exact plan of this cottage."

"With dark, narrow stairs and a kitchen that smokes, I suppose," Elinor remarked.

"Yes," he cried in the same eager tone, "with all and everything belonging to it; in no convenience or inconvenience should the least variation be perceptible. Then, and then only, under such a roof, I might perhaps be as happy at Combe as I have been at Barton."

"I flatter myself," Elinor replied, "that even under the disadvantage of better rooms and a broader staircase, you will hereafter find your own house as faultless as you do this."

"There certainly are circumstances," Willoughby said, "which might greatly endear it to me; but this place will always possess one claim on my affection which no other can possibly share."

Mrs. Dashwood looked with pleasure at Marianne, whose fine eyes were fixed so expressively on Willoughby that it was plain she understood him perfectly.

"How often did I wish," he added, lowering his voice, "when I was at Allenham this time last year, that Barton cottage were inhabited! I never passed within view of it without admiring its situation and grieving that no one should live there. How little did I then think that the very first news I should hear from Mrs. Smith, upon my next return to the country, would be that Barton cottage was taken! I felt an immediate satisfaction and interest in the event, which nothing but a kind of prescience of the happiness I should experience from it can account for. Must it not have been so, Marianne?"

Continuing in his former tone, he said, "And yet this house you would spoil, Mrs. Dashwood? You would rob it of its simplicity with imaginary improvements! And this dear parlour, in which our acquaintance first began, and in which so many happy hours have since been spent by us together, you would degrade to the condition of a common entrance, and everyone would be eager to pass through the room which has hitherto contained within itself more real accommodation and comfort than any other apartment of the handsomest dimensions in the world could possibly afford."

Mrs. Dashwood again assured him that no such alteration would be attempted.

"You are a good woman," he warmly replied. "Your promise makes me easy. Extend it a little farther, and it will make me happy. Tell me that not only your house will remain the same, but that I shall ever find you and yours as unchanged as your dwelling; and that you will always consider me with the kindness which has made everything belonging to you so dear to me."

The promise was readily given, and Willoughby's behavior throughout the evening declared at once his affection and happiness.

"Shall we see you tomorrow to dinner?" asked Mrs. Dashwood as he prepared to leave. "I do not ask you to come in the morning, for we must walk to the park to call on Lady Middleton."

He engaged to be with them by four o'clock.
