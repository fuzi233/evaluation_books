To understand the architecture of Captain Willoughby's social exile, one must first grasp the absolute tyranny of his own mediocrity. Within the hallowed, smoke-filled sanctums of his club, he was not merely a member; he was a living obstruction, a determined raconteur of pointslessness. He spun tales of strangers whose names meant nothing to his audience, weaving narratives so dense with irrelevance that they suffocated the very air of the room. He was a man who did not listen; he consumed only the oxygen required to fuel his endless, copious monologues. Whether seated in the dim recesses of the smoking-room or presiding over the supper-table, he operated like a steam-roller crushing a road, flattening conversation into dust. Yet, paradoxically, he was irresistible. While trite anecdotes were clumsily sandwiched between aphorisms ripped from schoolboy copybooks, all was delivered with the theatrical air of a man surprised by his own profound insight. Those who did not know him waited long enough to become trapped in his web of sheer dulness; but his fellow members, possessing the foresight of the oppressed, rose and fled the moment he appeared.

Thus, within the span of thirty minutes, Willoughby secured a large corner of the room entirely to himself—a space that, prior to his arrival, had been the most vibrant hub of the establishment. It was early October, the air crisp with the promise of winter, when the waiter approached the solitary figure and placed a card upon the mahogany table.

With alacrity, Willoughby seized the card. He hungered for company; his usual circle had vanished to fulfill pressing engagements. But as his eyes traced the ink, his countenance fell like a curtain dropping on a tragedy. "Colonel Durrance!" he muttered, scratching his head with a gesture of thoughtful dread. Durrance had never, in the annals of their acquaintance, paid him a friendly visit. Why should he break protocol now? It felt as though Durrance had somehow intercepted the coordinates of his journey to Kingsbridge.

"Does Colonel Durrance know I am in the club?" Willoughby asked, his voice tight.

"Yes, sir," the waiter confirmed.

"Very well. Show him in."

Durrance, undoubtedly, had come to interrogate. Diplomacy would be the shield required to deflect the arrows. Willoughby had no desire to meddle further in the affairs of Miss Ethne Eustace; the battles between Feversham and Durrance must be fought without his interference. He trusted his diplomatic skills, yet he recoiled from their deployment in this specific instance, watching the colonel enter the room with a gaze suspicious and wary.

Durrance, however, carried no immediate questions. Willoughby rose, crossed the room, and guided his visitor to his deserted sanctuary.

"Will you smoke?" Willoughby asked, pausing abruptly. "I beg your pardon."

"Oh, I'll smoke," Durrance replied, his voice steady. "It is not quite true that a man cannot enjoy his tobacco without witnessing the smoke of it. If I let my cigar go out, I should know at once. But you will see—I shall not let it out." With deliberate calm, he ignited the cigar and leaned back, settling into the chair as if claiming a throne.

"I am fortunate to find you, Willoughby," Durrance continued, "for I am only in town today. I ascend from Devonshire periodically to consult my oculist, and I was eager to meet you if opportunity allowed. On my last visit, Mather informed me you were away in the country. You recall Mather, I presume? He served with us in Suakin."

"Of course, I remember him perfectly," Willoughby said, his tone hearty. He welcomed the topic of Mather, harboring a faint hope that discussing the past might bury the present anxiety regarding Abou Fatma.

"We share a curiosity," Durrance pressed, his eyes locking onto Willoughby's. "There is a point we both seek to clarify. Have you ever encountered an Arab named Abou Fatma?"

"Abou Fatma," Willoughby repeated slowly, the name rolling off his tongue with feigned effort. "One of the Hadendoas?"

"No," Durrance corrected. "A man of the Kabbabish tribe."

"Abou Fatma?" Willoughby echoed, as though hearing the name for the first time. "No, I never came across him." Then, he stopped. The silence stretched, unnatural and heavy. It occurred to Durrance that this pause was not organic; a man typically adds, "Why do you ask?" or probes deeper. Instead, Willoughby remained mute. Internally, Willoughby wrestled with the mystery of how Durrance could possibly know of Abou Fatma, a name he himself had heard only once a year prior on the verandah of the Palace at Suakin. He had spoken the truth: he had never met the man, despite Feversham's references.

"That intensifies my curiosity," Durrance said, undeterred. "Mather and I were together on the final reconnaissance in '84. We discovered Abou Fatma hiding in the brush near Sinkat Fort. He spoke of the Gordon letters concealed in Berber. Ah! The name rings a bell now."

"I was merely retrieving my pipe from my pocket," Willoughby lied smoothly. "But the name returns to me now that you invoke the letters."

"They were delivered to you in Suakin approximately fifteen months ago," Durrance stated, referencing a paragraph in the *Evening Standard* that Mather had shown him. "I wonder if Abou Fatma returned to Berber and reclaimed them. But since you claim no encounter, it logically follows he was not the man."

Willoughby felt a pang of regret for his hasty denial. "No; it was not Abou Fatma," he admitted, his voice trembling slightly with an awkward hesitation. He dreaded the inevitable follow-up. He filled his pipe, calculating the perfect deflection, but Durrance offered none.

"I wondered," Durrance murmured slowly. "I suspected Abou Fatma would not return to Berber. Indeed, whoever undertakes such a mission does so at the risk of his life. And since Gordon is dead, for no very obvious reason."

"Quite so," Willoughby breathed, a wave of relief washing over him. It seemed Durrance's inquiry was sated with the knowledge that the letters remained unrecovered. "Quite so. Since Gordon was dead, for no reason."

"For no obvious reason, I think I said," Durrance remarked, his tone imperturbable. Willoughby turned, glancing sharply at his companion, wondering if the colonel knew of his clandestine trip to Kingsbridge and its motives. Durrance, however, smoked his cigar, tilting his face upward toward the ceiling, seemingly having lost interest in the saga of the Gordon letters. He posed no further questions to ensnare Willoughby, nor was there necessity.

As Durrance reflected on the only path by which Harry Feversham might redeem himself in Willoughby's eyes—the recovery of the letters from the ruined wall in Berber—he noted the absence of personal danger to Suakin's inhabitants since the last reconnaissance. Great troopships had steamed between coral reefs toward Suez; no cry for help had summoned them back. Willoughby had risked only his health in that white palace on the Red Sea. There could never have been a moment when Feversham stood ready to declare, "Your life was forfeit but for me, whom you call coward." Durrance, turning over the gossip gathered at Wadi Halfa and during his furloughs, concluded that the fugitive from Khartum, who had recounted his tale in the glacis of the silent ruins of Sinkat during a drowsy May afternoon, had likely repeated it at Suakin within Feversham's hearing. He was certain now.

Willoughby's reticence served as sufficient confirmation. Without doubt, he had been instructed by Ethne to keep his tongue leashed. Colonel Durrance anticipated this silence, viewing it as proof of his hypothesis. His trained ear detected the unease in Willoughby's posture, the wariness in his voice—an accent of caution hatefully familiar to Durrance, for it was the very tone Ethne had used. Durrance had set traps; the casual remark, "for no obvious reason, I think I said," was one, and the slight start, the quick turn of the head, revealed that Willoughby had stepped into the net.

Yet, Durrance wished not for Willoughby to alert Ethne to his inquiries. Recognizing the possibility, he guarded against it.

"I wish to explain why I sought to meet you," Durrance said. "It concerns Harry Feversham."

Captain Willoughby, congratulating himself on escaping the previous entanglement, nearly jumped in his seat. Durrance, however, ignored the agitation, continuing swiftly. "Something befell Feversham. More than five years have passed. He did something, or failed to complete something—the secret remains tightly wound—and he disappeared, vanishing from the world as if dropped down an oubliette in an ancient French castle. Are you returning to the Sudan, Willoughby?"

"Yes," Willoughby answered. "In a week."

"Well, Harry Feversham is in the Sudan," Durrance declared, leaning forward.

"You know that?" Willoughby exclaimed.
