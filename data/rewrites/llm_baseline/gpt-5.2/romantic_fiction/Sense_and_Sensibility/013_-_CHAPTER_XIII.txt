Their projected expedition to Whitwell, so long talked of and so confidently promised, ended in a disappointment more mortifying than any wet stockings or jolting miles of road. Elinor had armed herself against rain, fatigue, even a frightened moment on some narrow lane; but she had not imagined that they should be cheated of the very going.

By ten o’clock the whole party was gathered at the Park, where breakfast waited, bright with the bustle of servants and the fragrance of hot bread. The night’s rain still clung to the hedges; it fell now only as a steady drip from leaf to leaf, and the gravel outside held the water in shallow, shining hollows. Yet the morning itself was tolerable—clouds loosening and sliding apart, a pale sun breaking through often enough to make them all insist the day meant well.

They were in that particular humour which belongs to companies resolved upon enjoyment: determined, as Sir John declared, to be happy if happiness could be bullied into appearing. The room rang with talk; cups met saucers; knives worked at crisp rashers; and the low, domestic music of breakfast—pouring, slicing, laughing—seemed to promise that even weather must submit.

In the midst of this, the letters were brought.

Among them was one for Colonel Brandon. He took it up, glanced at the direction, and something in him altered at once—as if colour had been wiped from his face by the very sight of the hand that wrote. Without speaking, he rose and left the room.

“What is the matter with Brandon?” cried Sir John, staring after him as though a man could not properly vanish from a breakfast table without explanation.

No one could tell.

“I hope he has had no bad news,” said Lady Middleton, with that air of propriety which made even concern seem neatly arranged.

“It must be something extraordinary,” added she, “that could make Colonel Brandon leave my breakfast table so suddenly.”

In about five minutes he returned. His manner was composed again, but it was the sort of composure that has been fastened on, not grown naturally.

“No bad news, Colonel, I hope?” cried Mrs. Jennings the instant he appeared, her curiosity already pressing forward like a well-fed spaniel.

“None at all, ma’am, I thank you.”

“Was it from Avignon? I hope it is not to say that your sister is worse.”

“No, ma’am. It came from town, and is merely a letter of business.”

“But how came the hand to discompose you so much, if it was only a letter of business? Come, come, this won’t do, Colonel; so let us hear the truth of it.”

“My dear madam,” said Lady Middleton, in a warning tone, “recollect what you are saying.”

Mrs. Jennings, who recollected nothing she did not choose to recollect, went on with perfect ease. “Perhaps it is to tell you that your cousin Fanny is married?”

“No, indeed, it is not.”

“Well, then, I know who it is from, Colonel. And I hope she is well.”

“Whom do you mean, ma’am?” said he, colouring faintly in spite of himself.

“Oh! you know who I mean.”

Colonel Brandon turned, as if glad to speak to any one whose civility demanded an answer. “I am particularly sorry, ma’am,” said he to Lady Middleton, “that I should receive this letter to-day, for it is on business which requires my immediate attendance in town.”

“In town!” Mrs. Jennings echoed it as if the words were an impertinence. “What can you have to do in town at this time of year?”

“My own loss is great,” he continued, “in being obliged to leave so agreeable a party; but I am the more concerned, as I fear my presence is necessary to gain your admittance at Whitwell.”

It was as though he had dropped cold water into the company’s warmest expectations. Their eager faces fell; plates and cups seemed suddenly to lose their pleasant importance.

“But if you write a note to the housekeeper, Mr. Brandon,” Marianne cried, too quick to bear the thought of surrender, “will it not be sufficient?”

He shook his head.

“We must go,” said Sir John stoutly. “It shall not be put off when we are so near it. You cannot go to town till tomorrow, Brandon—that is all.”

“I wish it could be so easily settled. But it is not in my power to delay my journey for one day.”

“If you would but let us know what your business is,” said Mrs. Jennings, “we might see whether it could be put off or not.”

“You would not be six hours later,” Willoughby urged, leaning forward with that spirited air which made persuasion seem a sort of sport, “if you were to defer your journey till our return.”

“I cannot afford to lose one hour.”

Elinor, who observed everything because she spoke so little, heard Willoughby bend towards Marianne and say in a low voice, sharpened with amusement, “There are some people who cannot bear a party of pleasure. Brandon is one of them. He was afraid of catching cold, I dare say, and invented this trick for getting out of it. I would lay fifty guineas the letter was of his own writing.”

“I have no doubt of it,” Marianne returned, her eyes bright with instant indignation, and her voice as quick as a struck match.

“There is no persuading you to change your mind, Brandon,” Sir John went on, with the familiarity of old acquaintance and the impatience of a host disappointed of his triumph, “when once you are determined on anything. But, however, I hope you will think better of it. Consider, here are the two Miss Careys come over from Newton; the three Miss Dashwoods walked up from the cottage; and Mr. Willoughby got up two hours before his usual time on purpose to go to Whitwell.”

Colonel Brandon repeated, with steady politeness, his regret at being the cause of disobliging them; but declared, with equal steadiness, that it was unavoidable.

“Well, then, when will you come back again?”

“I hope we shall see you at Barton,” Lady Middleton added, “as soon as you can conveniently leave town; and we must put off the party to Whitwell till you return.”

“You are very obliging. But it is so uncertain when I may have it in my power to return, that I dare not engage for it at all.”

“Oh! he must and shall come back,” cried Sir John. “If he is not here by the end of the week, I shall go after him.”

“Ay, so do, Sir John,” cried Mrs. Jennings, “and then perhaps you may find out what his business is.”

“I do not want to pry into other men’s concerns,” Sir John answered, with a rough honesty that did not always keep good company with discretion. “I suppose it is something he is ashamed of.”

At that moment, as if the house itself were impatient to hurry him away, Colonel Brandon’s horses were announced.

“You do not go to town on horseback, do you?” Sir John demanded.

“No. Only to Honiton. I shall then go post.”

“Well, as you are resolved to go, I wish you a good journey. But you had better change your mind.”

“I assure you it is not in my power.”

He took leave of the whole party with quiet gravity; to Elinor he spoke more than to Marianne. “Is there no chance of my seeing you and your sisters in town this winter, Miss Dashwood?”

“I am afraid, none at all.”

“Then I must bid you farewell for a longer time than I should wish to do.”

To Marianne he only bowed; not a word accompanied it, and the omission had the weight of meaning, whether meant or not.

“Come, Colonel,” cried Mrs. Jennings, advancing as though she could physically bar the door with inquisitiveness, “before you go, do let us know what you are going about.”

He wished her a good morning; and, attended by Sir John, left the room.

The instant the door closed, all the complaints that good breeding had kept simmering broke into open lamentation. Every voice had its own phrasing of the same offence—how provoking, how unlucky, how vexatious—to be so ready and so thwarted.

“I can guess what his business is, however,” said Mrs. Jennings, with an exultation that made mystery itself sound like triumph.

“Can you, ma’am?” was cried from nearly every side.

“Yes. It is about Miss Williams, I am sure.”

“And who is Miss Williams?” Marianne asked, with that frank ignorance which Mrs. Jennings always treated as an invitation.

“What! do not you know who Miss Williams is? I am sure you must have heard of her before. She is a relation of the Colonel’s, my dear—a very near relation. We will not say how near, for fear of shocking the young ladies.”

Then, lowering her voice to Elinor—though not so much that the words did not ride the room’s very air—she added, “She is his natural daughter.”

“Indeed!”

“Oh, yes; and as like him as she can stare. I dare say the Colonel will leave her all his fortune.”

When Sir John returned, he joined in the general regret with all the heartiness of a man who felt disappointment in the same strong way he felt appetite; and, concluding that as they were all assembled they must do something “by way of being happy,” proposed—after some consultation—that though happiness could only be enjoyed at Whitwell, they might at least procure a tolerable composure of mind by driving about the country.

Carriages were ordered. Willoughby’s curricle was first; and Marianne, stepping into it, looked as if the mere act of being beside him created its own weather. The wheels cut sharply through the softened gravel; the horse’s shoes struck bright notes against hidden stones; and the sound ran away through the park with them as they went, fast and laughing, out of sight.

Nothing more was seen of them until their return, which did not happen until after all the rest had already come back. They both seemed delighted with their drive, yet said only in general terms that they had kept in the lanes, while the others went on the downs.

It was settled, to crown the day that had been robbed of its first pleasure, that there should be a dance in the evening, and that everybody should be extremely merry all day long—as if mirth might be kneaded and baked by force, like a loaf.

More of the Careys came to dinner; and they had the satisfaction of sitting down nearly twenty to table—an abundance Sir John regarded with the contentment of a country gentleman who loved a full room almost as much as a full dish. The long table steamed with the honest produce of the season: joints that yielded richly beneath the knife, vegetables still tasting of earth and rain, and warm puddings sending up a sweet, heavy fragrance that softened even disappointment. The clink of glasses, the rustle of gowns, the steady pulse of talk—these were the household sounds that kept the spirit from sinking too far, a kind of acoustic comfort made of many small lives going on together.

Willoughby took his usual place between the two elder Miss Dashwoods. Mrs. Jennings sat at Elinor’s right hand; and they had not been long seated before she leaned behind Elinor and Willoughby, and said to Marianne, loudly enough for both to hear, “I have found you out in spite of all your tricks. I know where you spent the morning.”

Marianne coloured to the roots of her hair, and answered hastily, “Where, pray?”

“Did not you know,” Willoughby said, with an air of playful defiance, “that we had been out in my curricle?”

“Yes, yes, Mr. Impudence, I know that very well—and I was determined to find out where you had been to. I hope you like your house, Miss Marianne. It is a very large one, I know; and when I come to see you, I hope you will have new-furnished it, for it wanted it very much when I was there six years ago.”

Marianne turned away, confusion mixed with anger; Mrs. Jennings laughed heartily, delighted by the little sting she had managed to place under so much merriment.

Elinor, who could not laugh when she felt uneasiness, soon learned how Mrs. Jennings had armed herself with certainty: in her resolution to know where they had been, she had sent her own woman to enquire of Willoughby’s groom, and had thus been told that they had gone to Allenham, and spent a considerable time walking about the garden and going all over the house.

Elinor could hardly believe it. It seemed improbable, not merely because the thing was bold, but because it must have required, on Marianne’s side, a blindness to common opinion which Elinor—who heard, in every drawing-room and every lane, the subtle hum of what people would say—could not imagine in any one she loved.

As soon as they left the dining-room, Elinor drew Marianne a little apart and asked her about it. Her surprise grew only stronger when she found every circumstance Mrs. Jennings had repeated was true. Marianne was quite angry that Elinor could doubt her.

“Why should you imagine, Elinor, that we did not go there, or that we did not see the house? Is not it what you have often wished to do yourself?”

“Yes, Marianne—but I would not go while Mrs. Smith was there, and with no other companion than Mr. Willoughby.”

“Mr. Willoughby, however, is the only person who can have a right to show that house; and as he went in an open carriage it was impossible to have any other companion. I never spent a pleasanter morning in my life.”

“I am afraid,” Elinor replied, trying to keep tenderness steady against alarm, “that the pleasantness of an employment does not always evince its propriety.”

“On the contrary, nothing can be a stronger proof of it, Elinor; for if there had been any real impropriety in what I did, I should have been sensible of it at the time—for we always know when we are acting wrong; and with such a conviction I could have had no pleasure.”

“But, my dear Marianne—since it has already exposed you to some very impertinent remarks—do you not now begin to doubt the discretion of your own conduct?”

“If the impertinent remarks of Mrs. Jennings are to be the proof of impropriety in conduct, we are all offending every moment of our lives. I value not her censure any more than I should do her commendation. I am not sensible of having done anything wrong in walking over Mrs. Smith’s grounds, or in seeing her house. They will one day be Mr. Willoughby’s, and—”

“If they were one day to be your own, Marianne,” Elinor said gently, “you would not be justified in what you have done.”

Marianne blushed; the hint, though meant as warning, touched the very centre of her hopes, and the colour in her cheeks betrayed how sweetly it struck. For a time she was silent, as if listening inwardly to a language Elinor could not hear—a private dialect of feeling that would not translate into prudence.

After an interval—ten minutes of earnest thought, softened by youth and confidence—she returned to Elinor again with a sudden good humour, as if she had conceded enough to be generous without surrendering anything dear.

“Perhaps, Elinor, it was rather ill-judged in me to go to Allenham; but Mr. Willoughby wanted particularly to show me the place—and it is a charming house, I assure you.”

And then, as if the very rooms were printed on her senses, she began to describe it with the eager exactness of one who has walked through a building and carried away its air upon her clothes: “There is one remarkably pretty sitting-room upstairs; of a nice, comfortable size for constant use—and with modern furniture it would be delightful. It is a corner room, and has windows on two sides. On one side you look across the bowling-green, behind the house, to a beautiful hanging wood; and on the other you have a view of the church and village, and beyond them those fine bold hills that we have so often admired. I did not see it to advantage, for nothing could be more forlorn than the furniture—but if it were newly fitted up—a couple of hundred pounds, Willoughby says, would make it one of the pleasantest summer-rooms in England.”

Had Elinor been allowed to listen without interruption, Marianne would have gone on from room to room, as if pacing the house again in her mind; and perhaps she would have been the happier for speaking, while Elinor—hearing under every pretty particular the louder, harsher noise of the world’s judgement—could only wish that delight itself did not so often carry, hidden within it, the seeds of future pain.
