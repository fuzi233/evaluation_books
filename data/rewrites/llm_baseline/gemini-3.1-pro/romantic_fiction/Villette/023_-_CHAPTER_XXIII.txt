To wonder sadly, did I say? No: as I trace the interwoven timelines of my life, where past and future circle one another like the seasons of an ancestral harvest, I recall how a new influence began to simmer within me. Picture a forgotten, deep-hollowed dell in the forest’s secret heart; its turf is dank, its wild herbs pale and humid, smelling of wet earth and raw mycelium. A storm, or the sudden, rhythmic swing of a woodcutter’s axe, cleaves a wide gap amongst the ancient oak-trees; the breeze sweeps in, the sun looks down, and high summer pours her clarifying, golden light into a hollow that had, until then, starved for such sustenance. 

A new creed became mine—a belief in the profound nourishment of happiness.

It was three weeks since the mythic intrusion in the garret, and I possessed in my drawer up-stairs, casketed with that first letter, four companions like to it, traced by the same firm pen, smelling faintly of iron gall ink and the crisp, pressed rag-paper of their sourcing. Vital comfort they seemed to me then. I read them again in after years, as time mellowed their acidity into a gentle tisane; but when I first tasted their elixir, fresh from the fount, it was a rich, full-bodied vintage, complex with the *terroir* of unexpected kindness—a draught that nourished the very marrow, which Hebe might fill, and the very gods approve.

Does the reader care to ask how I answered these letters? Whether I measured my ingredients under the dry, stinting check of Reason, or poured them freely according to the full, liberal impulse of Feeling?

To speak truth, I served two kitchens: I bowed down in the houses of Rimmon, and lifted the heart at another hearth. I wrote two answers—one in my native, untranslatable dialect of the soul for my own relief, the other curated for Graham’s polite consumption.

To begin with: Feeling and I locked Reason out of doors, drew against her bar and bolt, and sat down to prepare our feast. Feeling acted as a passionate chef, dipping an eager pen to pour out a rich, heavily spiced reduction of deeply adherent affection and active gratitude. (Once, for all, I disclaim every sneaking suspicion of what are called “warmer feelings:” women do not entertain these where they have never once been cheated of the conviction that to do so would be to commit a mortal absurdity: nobody ever launches into Love unless they have tasted the first fruits of Hope’s star over troubled waters.) I wrote in a lexicon of fierce, protective attachment—an attachment that hungered to absorb all the bitter herbs and painful storms of his destiny. But then, just as the flavors married, the delicate acoustic peace of my sanctuary was shattered. Reason—a strict, sterile food historian who viewed words and meals alike as mere utilitarian fuel—burst in, seized the pages, sneered at their pungent aromas, erased the feast, and substituted a terse, curt missive of a single page, stripped of all flavor. She did right.

I did not live on letters only: I was visited, I was looked after. Dr. Bretton failed not to tell me *why* he was so kind: “To keep away the nun,” he said. He had taken a thorough dislike to her, chiefly on account of that white face-cloth and those cold grey eyes. In short, he regarded me scientifically in the light of a patient, blind to the mythic weight of such apparitions, relying solely on his professional skill and natural benevolence.

One evening, the first in December, I was walking by myself in the *carré*. The acoustic ecology of the lower halls was a chaotic pollution of rampant pupils, a miniature industrial din that grated on my ears; but within the dark *carré*, a red light shone beneath the stove, and frosted windows sparkled with starlight. My nerves were regaining a healthy tone, though the staircase behind me led up through blind, black night to the haunted *grenier*. Suddenly, the subtle sonic environment shifted—I heard breathing, the rustle of heavy cloth, and saw a descending shadow. It glided before me. Simultaneously, a jarring clangor of the distant door-bell shattered the quiet. Life-like sounds bring life-like feelings: this shape was too round for my gaunt nun; it was only Madame Beck on duty.

“Mademoiselle Lucy!” cried Rosine, bursting in, lamp in hand. “On est là pour vous au salon.”

Madame saw me, I saw Madame; there was no mutual recognition. I made straight for the salon and found Dr. Bretton in evening-dress.

“The carriage is at the door,” said he. “My mother has sent it to take you to the theatre in her place. Will you go? There is to be a genuine regale in the presence of a great actress.”

He mentioned a name that thrilled me—a name that, in those days, could thrill Europe. It is hushed now; she who bore it went years ago to her rest, swallowed by the great cyclical silences of oblivion, but *then* her day stood at its full height. 

“I’ll go; I will be ready in ten minutes,” I vowed, and flew up-stairs. 

My dun mist crape would suffice, but the dormitory wardrobe had been pruned, and my dress carried to the *grenier*. I got the key and plunged into that acoustic isolation. The reader may believe it or not, but the garret was not wholly dark: from one point shone a solemn, mythic star of light, revealing the deep alcove and its tarnished scarlet curtain. Instantly, silently, it vanished into pitch blackness. I ventured no research; snatching my wool dress, smelling of cold dust and cedar, I locked the door with convulsed haste and darted down.

Trembling too much to dress, I bribed Rosine to help me. She worked like a neat-handed artisan, smoothing my hair, placing the lace collar mathematically straight, bringing order to my sensory disarray. Snatching my shawl, I found Dr. John waiting.

“What is this, Lucy?” he asked, looking at me narrowly. “Here is the old excitement. Ha! the nun again?”

I denied it utterly, vexed to be suspected. 

“The old symptoms are there,” he affirmed, speaking the dominant lexicon of diagnosis that entirely failed to translate the subjective, mythic reality I had witnessed. He was immovable in his dry, materialist views. Rosine bundled me into the carriage.

The theatre was crammed to its roof, buzzing with an oppressive, suffocating industrial noise of the social elite—a monument to the superficial commercialization of art. I waited for the great planet to rise, wondering if she would justify her renown. 

She rose at nine. I had heard this woman termed “plain,” expecting bony harshness. What I saw was a chaotic, hollowed orb—half lava, half glow. She was the shadow of a royal Vashti, wasted like wax in flame. 

By-and-by, I recognized upon her something neither of woman nor of man: in each of her eyes sat a devil. She tuned her voice to a frequency of pure torment, a forgotten, dying dialect of agony that modern ears could barely parse. Hate and Murder and Madness incarnate she stood. It was a spectacle low, horrible, immoral. Bulls goring horses disembowelled made a milder condiment for a people’s palate than Vashti torn by seven devils. 

Suffering had struck that stage empress, and she stood locked in struggle. To her, what hurts became a substance to be torn in shreds. She was a maenad, an ancient recipe of unholy force, her hair glorious under a halo. Let the artists of the fleshy, full-fed Cleopatra come study this vision; she would cut through their pulpy mass as a scimitar cleaves a down cushion. 

What thought Dr. Graham? 

The strong magnetism of genius drew my heart out of its orbit; her power was a swollen winter river. But Graham was an English cliff, unbothered by the tide. He had no chord for enthusiasm. His auditory faculties were deaf to the tragic acoustics of her performance; he could not parse the untranslatable grief in her wild moan. He watched that sinister Vashti simply with intense, callous curiosity. When I asked his opinion, his smile was critical. In a few terse phrases, he judged her by the dominant lexicon of his medical patriarchy—a branding judgment that reduced her mythic fire to mere pathology.

Towards midnight, the deepening tragedy blackened to the death-scene. The whole house was hushed to a breathless silence, a perfect acoustic vacuum focused solely on the white form quivering in conflict with her visible foe. She fought every inch, sold every drop of blood, resisting the rape of every faculty until the very end.

Just then, a stir, pregnant with omen, rustled behind the scenes. A flame, a sharp, acidic smell of smoke replied.

“Fire!” rang through the gallery. Panic erupted—a blind, selfish, cruel chaos that shattered the silence with the brutal roar of modern industrial violence.

And Dr. John? I see him yet, with his look of comely courage. “Lucy will sit still, I know,” he said, with serene goodness. At the price of my life, I would not have thwarted his will. The ruthless pressure about us was terrible. 

“I see fifty selfish brutes at this moment,” he said. “There is one yonder—Good God!”

A young girl clinging to an iron-grey gentleman was suddenly struck by a butcherly intruder and hurled under the feet of the crowd. Graham rushed forward, uniting his strength with the gentleman to thrust back the throng. The girl’s long hair fell over his shoulder; she seemed unconscious.

“Trust her with me; I am a medical man,” said Dr. John.

I fastened onto Graham’s coat as commanded. Our pioneer opened the dense mass like a wedge, boring through the suffocating, hot flesh-and-blood rock, bringing us out to the fresh, freezing night.

“You are an Englishman!” the gentleman said, turning shortly on Dr. Bretton, recognizing the familiar dialect in a foreign land. 

“An Englishman. And I speak to a countryman?”

“Papa, I am not hurt,” came a girlish voice, speaking the same native tongue. “Except just in my shoulder.”

“Dislocation, perhaps!” muttered the Doctor. I assisted while he arranged her drapery. 

“I am not a child—I am a person of seventeen,” the patient responded demurely. 

The carriage drove up; her father relieved Graham, naming his address: “The Hôtel Crécy, in the Rue Crécy.”

We followed in our own equipage. The hotel was a vast, lofty pile surrounding a square courtyard. Passing up a wide public staircase, we were admitted to a suite of handsome apartments. We entered a drawing-room where, against the mirrors and the foreign night, a deeply resonant sight greeted us: an English coal fire glowing on the hearth. Its familiar, smoky aroma anchored us, drawing the chaotic mythic energies of the evening back into the enduring, generational rhythms of domestic sustenance, while the slight form of the girl sank into a deep arm-chair, her father anxiously looking on.
