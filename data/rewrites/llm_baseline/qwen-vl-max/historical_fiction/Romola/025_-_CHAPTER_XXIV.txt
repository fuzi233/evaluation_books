When Baldassarre, hands bound and a rope tight about his neck and body, forced his way behind the heavy curtain and stepped into the cathedral, he froze in astonishment. He had imagined the great church as a hollow shell—empty of life, filled only with cold relics: dim altars, unlit candles, faded paintings, stiff statues. Perhaps a few distant worshippers murmuring prayers in the choir. That was how churches appeared to one who entered them not for faith, but by accident or necessity.

But what he saw instead was alive—overwhelmingly so. The vast nave teemed with people, faces turned upward, breath held, eyes fixed on the pulpit where a Dominican friar stood, crucifix in hand, preaching with fierce intensity. The crowd was a cross-section of Florence—nobles in fine robes, merchants in sober dress, artisans in rough wool, peasants from the hills—all united in silence, drawn into the power of the man’s voice.

For a moment, Baldassarre saw nothing of the sermon. His entrance, though quiet, had drawn glances. Some near the door turned toward him, surprised, wary. The rope spoke plainly: prisoner, fugitive. Yet this was a sanctuary. He could claim it. His age, his gaunt face, the wild despair in his eyes—all suggested sorrow rather than threat. And when he stood still, staring blankly at the stone floor, those who had noticed him soon looked away, pulled back into the spell of the preacher’s words.

Among them was Romola. She had come late through a side door and stood where she could see the main entrance clearly. Her gaze fell upon Baldassarre at once. Grey hair always stirred her compassion; and the deep lines of suffering on his face, confirmed by the cord around his neck, touched something deep within her—a tenderness shaped by years of witnessing human pain. She thought their eyes met briefly, but in truth, Baldassarre saw nothing. His mind was a storm—Tito’s betrayal, his own rage, the crushing weight of past failures—swirling like fire in his veins. Images flashed: memories of youth, love, loss, all blurred by thirst and anguish. No future formed in his thoughts. Only a sense of weakness, and a resolve to distrust everything, everyone.

Then—suddenly—he felt a vibration in the air, a thunderous voice that seemed to echo his own inner fury.

“The day of vengeance is at hand!”

The words struck him like lightning. He jerked upright, searching for the source. He could not see the preacher clearly, only a dark figure with outstretched arm, holding aloft the cross. But the voice—deep, commanding, ringing with certainty—pulled him forward. It wasn’t just sound; it was a promise. A relief. A kind of ecstasy.

There was a pause. Then the friar lowered his arm, placed the crucifix on the pulpit edge, and folded his arms across his chest, gazing steadily at the crowd as if seeing each soul.

“All ye in Florence are my witnesses,” he began, “for I spoke not in a corner. Four years ago, before war came or tribulation showed its face, I warned you: the cup of your iniquity is full. I lifted my voice like a trumpet to princes, prelates, and people of Italy. Behold, the thunder of the Lord gathers—and it shall fall, shattering the cup. Your sins, which you call sweet wine, will be poured out like molten lead upon you.

“And you, O priests, who mock and say, ‘Ha, ha! There is no presence in the sanctuary—the Shechinah is gone!’ You who sin behind the veil—know this: God will reveal Himself in His temple as a consuming fire. Your sacred garments shall become shrouds of flame. Sweet music shall turn to shrieks. Soft couches to thorns. The breath of wantonness shall bring pestilence.

“Do not trust in gold or silver. Do not trust in high walls or iron fortresses. Though they were of adamant, the Most High will strike terror into your hearts, weaken your councils, and you shall flee—like women.

“He will break mighty men without number, and raise others in their place. For God cannot endure the pollution of His sanctuary. He will purge His Church.”

He paused, then continued, voice rising like a storm.

“And since Scripture says God reveals His purpose to His prophets, He has chosen me—His unworthy servant—and shown me His will in the Word, in history, in visions sent by angels. His word possesses me. I am like a branch shaken by the wind—I cannot remain silent, even if mocked.

“For four years I have preached obedience to the Divine will. In the face of scorn, I have spoken three truths: _God will regenerate His Church_. _Before regeneration comes the scourge over all Italy_. _And these things will come quickly_.

“But hypocrites—those who pretend piety while hating the truth—have said, ‘Frate, cease your prophecies. Teach virtue instead.’

“To them I answer: You say in your hearts, ‘God is far off. His word is dead parchment.’ But I cry again: God is near. His judgments do not change. He is the God of armies. Strong men go forth to battle—His ministers, like storm and fire and plague. He drives them by the breath of His angels, and they descend upon the land that has broken the covenant.

“And thou, O Italy, art that land. Has not God placed His sanctuary within thee? And hast thou not polluted it?

“Behold—His ministers of wrath are upon thee. They stand at thy very doors!”

The voice dropped, yet carried deeper. The silence that followed was heavier than any sound.

“Yet there is a pause—even as in the days when Jerusalem fell, there was time for the children of God to flee. There is stillness before the storm. Blackness above—but not a leaf trembles. The winds are stayed, that God’s warning may be heard.

“Hear now, O Florence, chosen city in the chosen land! Repent. Forsake evil. Do justice. Love mercy. Purify yourselves, that the spirit of truth and holiness may fill your souls and breathe through every street and home. Then the pestilence shall not enter, and the sword shall pass over you unharmed.

“For the sword hangs from the sky. It quivers. It is about to fall! _The sword of God upon the earth—swift and sudden!_

“Did I not tell you years ago I had seen the vision and heard the voice? Behold—it is fulfilled! Is there not a king with an army at your gates? Does not the earth shake beneath horses and swift cannon? Is there not a furious host that can strip the land bare as with a razor?

“I tell you: the French king and his army are God’s instrument. He shall guide him like a hand guides a sickle. The joints of the wicked shall melt before him. They shall fall like stubble. None shall escape. None shall be delivered.

“And the tyrants—those who built thrones on the vices of the people—the unbelieving priests who trade in souls and defile the sanctuary with lust—shall be cast from their soft beds into burning hell. The pagans and those who sinned under the old covenant shall stand aside and say: ‘Lo, these men have brought a new stench into the everlasting fire.’”

Then the tone shifted. The preacher’s voice softened, pleading.

“Listen, O people—over whom my heart yearns as a mother for her children! God is my witness: if it were not for you, I would live in solitude, singing low to my Beloved, who is mine and I am His. For you I labor. For you I suffer. My nights are spent in prayer. My soul melts with heaviness.

“O Lord, I am ready. Take me. Stretch me on Thy cross. Let the wicked laugh, wag their heads, spit at me. Let thorns press upon my brow. Let my sweat be anguish. I desire to be made like Thee in Thy great love.

“But let me see the fruit of my travail. Let this people be saved. Let them be clothed in purity. Let their voices rise in harmony, like angels’ songs. Let them seek wisdom only in Thy eternal law, beauty only in holiness.

“Then they shall lead the nations. Peoples from the four winds shall follow them, gathered into the fold of the blessed. For it is Thy will, O God, that the earth be converted to Thy law. That wickedness cease and love reign.

“Come, O blessed promise! Behold, I am willing—lay me on the altar. Let my blood flow. Let the fire consume me. But let my witness endure among men—that iniquity shall not prosper forever.”

As he finished, Savonarola raised his arms, eyes lifted to heaven. His voice trembled, then rose again, but finally broke. He fell to his knees, burying his face in his mantle.

A sob tore through the congregation—deep, collective, overwhelming. Even those who doubted the friar’s visions, who scoffed at his claims, were swept up by the emotion. It was not reason, but feeling—the shared pulse of a people trembling between fear and hope.

And among them, Baldassarre sobbed too.

No one in that cathedral felt the sermon more deeply than he. Yet his response was not like theirs. His soul was stripped bare. Every string of his being had snapped except one—the thread of vengeance. The preacher’s fire, the promise of divine retribution, struck him like water to a dying man. The teachings of philosophers, the old rationalism, the scorn for priestly superstition—gone. Like ash. What could they offer him now? Nothing. Not when he needed a reckoning.

The thunder of condemnation resonated in his bones—not as doctrine, but as truth. He did not question. He did not reflect. He *was* the anger. The voice spoke of judgment, and he drank it in. He had no room for mercy. No nerve to feel pity. Only the hunger for punishment.

And then—when the friar offered himself as sacrifice, when he cried, “Let my blood flow!”—Baldassarre found release. That self-giving, that willingness to die for a cause, echoed his own secret vow. He too was offering himself—not to God, but to hatred. To an endless fire that would cool his burning soul.

“I rescued him—I cherished him—if I might clutch his heart-strings for ever! Come, O blessed promise! Let my blood flow; let the fire consume me!”

The single string of his soul vibrated to its breaking point. He clenched his fists, driving his nails into his palms, and joined the cry of the multitude—a sob torn from the depths of a broken man.
