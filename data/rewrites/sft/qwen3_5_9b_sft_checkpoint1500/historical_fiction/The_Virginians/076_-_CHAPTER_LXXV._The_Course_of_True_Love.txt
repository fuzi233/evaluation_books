If your father, young gentlemen, had possessed the most ordinary share of prudence, neither this chapter of his history would exist, nor would you yourselves have been born to plague him in a hundred ways. Instead of shouting and laughing in the passages when he seeks quiet for his books, you would not wake him from his afternoon doze—a habit becoming a healthy country gentleman—nor mislay his spectacles nor steal his newspaper. You would not ruin him with tailors', mantua-makers', and tutors' bills, nor break his nights with feigned illnesses when your mother refuses silence for half an hour. And when Joan cannot sleep, what utility remains in Darby donning his nightcap? Every trivial ailment you have suffered has terrified her so profoundly that I protest I have known no tranquility. Were I not the most long-suffering creature in existence, I would gladly be rid of the entire pack.

Now, having outgrown childhood with its petticoats, chicken-pox, smallpox, whooping-cough, and scarlet fever, what proposal must this unconscionable woman make but to convert the south rooms into a nursery for potential grandchildren and arrange for the Captain to wed early simply because we did? She claims he is too fond of Brookes's and Goosetree's when in London. She possesses the perversity to suggest that while an entrée to Carlton House may be pleasurable, it is perilous for a young gentleman, urging Miles to flee temptation and sow his wild oats before marrying, just as we did. "Marry!" I exclaim to my dear creature, "we had no business to marry at all!" By the laws of common prudence and duty, I should have withdrawn from my slight engagement with Miss Theo—who would have married someone else anyway—and secured a wealthy spouse. Your Uncle John was a parson incapable of fighting; poor Charley was merely a schoolboy; and your grandfather was too elderly to summon me to account with sword or pistol. I maintain there was never a more foolish match in the world than ours, and our relatives were entirely justified in their anger. What purpose serve relations, indeed, but to quarrel and find fault? When Hester marries, do you mind, Master George, to dispute with her if she chooses a husband not of your selection? When George secures his living, having emerged senior wrangler and fellow of his college, Miss Hester, do you not toss your little nose at the young lady he fancies? As for you, my little Theo, I cannot part with you. You must not abandon your old father, for he enjoys having you play Haydn for him and peel his walnuts after dinner.

They both married, as I observe by the note in the family Bible: Miss Theodosia Warrington to Joseph Clinton, son of the Reverend Joseph Blake, who later became Master of Rodwell Regis Grammar School; and Miss Hester Mary, in 1804, to Captain F. Handyman, R.N.—ED.

While they enjoyed the blessing (forsooth!) of meeting daily, billing and cooing, the two young people, your parents, existed in a fool's paradise, little heeding the world around them or its tattling meddlers. Rinaldo was as brave a warrior as ever slew a Turk, yet you know he loved dangling in Armida's garden. Pray, my Lady Armida, what did you mean by casting your spells over me in youth, ensuring that glory, fashion, gaming tables, nor the company of witty men could keep me from your apron strings or out of reach of your simple prattle? Pray, my dear, what did we say to one another during those endless hours? I never slept after dinner then. Which of us was wittier? Was it I or you? How did our conversations become so delightful? I recall that year I cared not to attend the trial and hanging of my Lord Ferrers, while the entire world flocked to see him. The King of Prussia's capital had fallen; if the Austrians and Russians had encamped round the Tower, there could have been less stir in London: yet Miss Theo and her young gentleman felt no inordinate emotion of pity or indignation. To us, the fates of Leipzig or Berlin meant nothing. The truth is, that dear old house in Dean Street was an enchanted garden of delights. I have been idle since, but never as happy. Shall we order the postchaise, my dear, leaving the children to manage the household, and drive up to London to see if the old lodgings are still let? You shall sit at your old window seat and wave a little handkerchief as I ascend the street. Say what we did was imprudent. Would we not do it over again? My good folks, if Venus had walked into the room and challenged the golden apple, I was so infatuated I would have given it to your mother. And had she possessed the choice, she would have preferred her humble servant in a threadbare coat to my Lord Clive with all his diamonds.

Once, to be sure, and briefly in that year, I entertained notions of fleeing to the highway to be caught and hanged like my Lord Ferrers, or joining the King of Prussia to request that some of his enemies strike me down, or enlisting in the India service to perform a desperate exploit ending in my destruction. Ah me! That was indeed a dreadful time! Your mother scarce dares speak of it now, save in a whisper of terror, or think of it—it caused such cruel pain. She remained unhappy for years following the anniversary of that day, until one of you was born upon it. Suppose we had been parted: what would have become of us? What would my lot have been without her? As I contemplate that possibility, the whole world becomes a blank. I do not say we were parted now. It has pleased God to grant us thirty years of union. We have reached the autumn of our lives; our successors are appointed and ready; and whichever of us is first called away knows the survivor will follow ere long. Yet we were actually parted in our youth, and I tremble to think what might have been had not a dearest friend brought us together.

Unknown to myself, and likely intending only my advantage, my relatives in England chose to write to Madam Esmond in Virginia, representing what they deemed the folly of the engagement I had contracted. Every one of them sang the same song. I saw these letters and burned the whole cursed pack years afterward when my mother showed them to me at home in Virginia. Aunt Bernstein offered forward advice. A young person of no wonderful beauty, of no family, with no money—could there ever be such an imprudent connection, and ought it not, for dear George's sake, to be broken off? She cited several eligible matches for me. With my name and prospects, 'twas a shame I should waste myself on this young lady; her sister ought to interpose—and so forth.

My Lady Warrington must also write, and in her peculiar manner. Her ladyship's letter was adorned with scripture texts. She dressed her worldliness in phylacteries, pointing out how I lived in an unworthy society of players and the like, who she could not claim were absolutely without religion (Heaven forbid!), but who were deplorably worldly. She would not accuse an artful woman of inveigling me for her daughter, having vainly tried to captivate my younger brother. She was far from maligning the young woman I had selected; but it was certain, she argued, that Miss L. possessed no fortune or expectations, and her parents might naturally wish to compromise me. She had taken counsel, etcetera. She had sought guidance where it lay, etcetera. Feeling her duty, she had resolved to speak. Sir Miles, a man of excellent judgment in worldly affairs (though seeking something better), fully agreed with her opinion and desired her to write, entreating her sister to interfere so the ill-advised match should not proceed.

And who else must put a little finger into the pie but the new Countess of Castlewood? She penned a majestic letter to Madam Esmond, stating that having been placed by Providence at the head of the Esmond family, it was her duty to communicate with her kinswoman and warn her to break off this marriage. I believe the three women conferred privately beforehand; and packet after packet, they dispatched their warnings to the Virginian lady.

One raw April morning, as Corydon went to pay his usual duty to Phillis, he found not his charmer with her dear smile, ready to welcome him, but Mrs. Lambert, with very red eyes, and the General as pale as death. "Read this, George Warrington!" he said, as his wife's head dropped between her hands; and he placed a letter before me, the handwriting of which I recognized. I could hear now the sobs of the good Aunt Lambert, and to this day, the noise of fire-irons stirring a fire in a room overhead gives me a tremor. I heard such a noise that day in the girls' room where the sisters sat together. Poor, gentle child! Poor Theo!

"What can I do after this, George, my poor boy?" asked the General, pacing the room with desperation etched upon his face.
