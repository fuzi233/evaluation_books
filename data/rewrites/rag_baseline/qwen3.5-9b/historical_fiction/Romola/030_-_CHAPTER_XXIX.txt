I visited the hospital to ascertain whether those monkish cooks had managed to determine if his faculties remained intact. Their verdict was that he exhibited no symptoms of insanity—merely a profound indifference to inquiry, appearing to cultivate a vineyard twenty miles distant. He was a cryptic, formidable tiger. I would have relished knowing more of his nature."

These words emerged from Nello's shop, spoken by Piero di Cosimo on the twenty-fourth of November, precisely one week following the French incursion. The atmosphere within the city was electric; a party of six or seven gathered at the unconventional hour of three in the afternoon, driven by the anticipation of a decisive political shift. Every public thoroughfare teemed with activity, and every shopkeeper lacking a spouse or deputy stationed himself at the threshold, thumbs hooked in his belt, surveying the chaos. The streets were littered with artisans drifting aimlessly like debris suspended in water, poised to surge forward with impetuous violence should any catalyst arise.

Nello strummed his lute while seated casually atop the counter facing the window, maintaining a vigilant gaze upon the piazza.

"Ah," Nello declared, setting down the instrument with deliberate weight, "for a gold florin, I would not have missed witnessing the spectacle of the French soldiers lumbering in their broad-soiled footwear after their fleeing captives! This is the inevitable consequence of abandoning my post to shave distinguished chins. History repeats itself: whenever I vacate this epicenter, calamity seizes my piazza."

"Piero, you should have been present," the painter retorted, his tone sharp as flint, "to observe your favored Greek trembling as though Satanasso himself had seized him. I derive pleasure from seeing your perpetually smiling patrons caught in a gale, forced to reveal their undersides against their will. Consider the hue of a man's liver when he resembles a bleached stag the moment a stranger grasps him."

"Nello, reserve your vinegar for seasoning your own omelets!" Nello countered. "What grievance exists regarding my *bel erudito* appearing startled when he sensed claws upon him and beheld an unchained lunatic at his side? Your scholar differs vastly from those brutish Swiss and Germans, whose skulls serve only as battering-rams and whose appetites justify consuming a cannonball before breakfast. We Florentines value attributes beyond this crude definition of bravery, acquired by hiring dim-witted mercenaries at a fixed rate. Let me clarify: once humanity realized they possessed superior intellect to oxen, they delegated the pulling to the beasts. Similarly, when we Florentines recognized our intellectual supremacy, we assigned the fighting to them."

"Treason, Nello!" a voice erupted from the interior chamber. "Such sentiments contradict state doctrine. Florence grinds its weapons; the latest authenticated vision from the Friar depicts Mars standing atop the Palazzo Vecchio, his arm resting upon the shoulder of Saint John the Baptist, who offers him a piece of honeycomb."

"It is acceptable, Francesco," Nello conceded smoothly. "Florence possesses several thick-skulled individuals suitable for bombarding Pisa; meanwhile, the refined spirits remain at home to conduct the reasoning and the shaving. Concerning our Piero here, if he insists on valor, let him wield his largest brush as a spear and his palette as a shield, challenging the widest-mouthed Swiss he encounters in the Prato to single combat."

"Go on, Nello," Piero growled, "your tongue operates with the relentless momentum of a mill during the Arno's flood—regardless of whether grain exists."

"Plentiful grain, I assure you. It would be as logical to expect a weathered painter to crave a javelin piercing his vitals as it is to anticipate a man whose intellect has been honed by classical texts enjoying having his visage torn by a savage beast."

"There you go, assuming people will insert their limbs into a sack merely because you term them 'hosen,'" Piero dismissed. "Who posited the existence of a wild beast, or an unarmed man charging into battle? Combat is a profession, not mine. I would be a fool to pursue danger, yet I could confront it if it sought me out."

"Why, then, do you tremble before thunder, my Piero?" Nello pressed, intent on dismantling the accusation. "One should comprehend why a specific individual is unnerved by trifles that affect others less—you who retreat with the rodents the instant a storm approaches."

"This stems from a heightened sensitivity to auditory stimuli; it bears no relation to my courage or conscience."

"And Tito Melema may possess a unique hypersensitivity to being ambushed by fugitives escaping French guards. Humans are born with aversions; I personally cannot tolerate the aroma of mint. Tito was born with an antipathy toward elderly prisoners stumbling and clutching. Behold!"

A collective laugh rippled through the assembly, confirming that Piero's reluctance toward Tito was not shared by the company. The painter, contorting his features into an unreadable grimace, stuffed tow into the crevices of his scarsella in indignant disdain, while Nello continued his triumphant monologue.

"No, my Piero, I cannot afford to disparage my *bel erudito*; nor can Florence, given that her scholars molt prematurely at the tender age of forty. Our Phoenix Pico has ascended directly to Paradise, as the Friar informs us; and the incomparable Poliziano departed mere months ago—to where, well, let us hope he has not joined the eminent scholars in the Malebolge."

"Incidentally," interjected Francesco Cei, "have you heard that Camilla Rucellai has surpassed the Friar in prophecy? Two years ago she foretold Pico's death during the season of lilies. He expired in November. 'Not the season of lilies,' scoffed the detractors. 'Go on!' retorts Camilla; 'I referred to the lilies of France, and they appear remarkably close to your nostrils.' I declare, 'Euge, Camilla!' Should the Friar demonstrate that any of his visions have been fulfilled with such precision, I shall proclaim myself a Piagnone tomorrow."

"You are excessively frivolous regarding the Friar, Francesco," observed Pietro Cennini, the scholar. "In these critical weeks, we owe him dearly for preaching peace, tranquility, and the cessation of factional strife. Those of limited discernment eagerly await the populace slipping the Friar's leash. Should the Most Christian King remain obstinate regarding the treaty today, refusing to sign terms equitable and honorable for Florence, Fra Girolamo is the sole figure capable of compelling him to reason."

"You speak truth, Messer Pietro," Nello affirmed. "The Friar represents one of Florence's most robust anchors—at least, according to the most reputable clients I honor with the blade. Yet, young Messer Niccolò remarked earlier this morning—and Francesco undoubtedly shares this sentiment—that the elasticity of interpreting visions rivals the stretching of Dido's bull-hide. A dream may signify whatever follows it. As Franco Sacchetti noted, a woman dreams of a serpent biting her, smashes a drinking vessel the next day, and cries, 'Behold, I anticipated an event; now the serpent's meaning is plain.'"

"But the Friar's visions differ fundamentally," corrected Cronaca. "He does not merely predict outcomes—the Church will be scourged and renewed, the heathens converted—but declares they shall occur swiftly. He is no slippery opportunist crafting loopholes; he is—"

"What is this? What is this?" Nello shouted, leaping from the counter and thrusting his head through the doorway. "People stream into the piazza, shouting. Some catastrophe must have unfolded in the Via Larga. Ah!" he burst forth with delighted astonishment, stepping outside laughing and waving his cap.

The entire company rushed to the door. News from the Via Larga was exactly what they awaited. Yet, the manner of its arrival left them somewhat bewildered. Carried aloft on the shoulders of the mob, seated upon a bench seemingly plucked from the street, sat Tito Melema, smiling with amused detachment at the coercion imposed upon him. His cap had slid from his head, dangling by the *becchetto* loosely wound around his neck. Upon spotting the group at Nello's door, he raised his fingers in a gesture of beckoning recognition. Within a heartbeat, he leaped from the bench onto a cart laden with bales positioned in the vast space between the Baptistery and the steps of the Duomo, while the populace swarmed around him with the noisy fervor of chickens awaiting feed. Silence fell instantly as he began to address them in his clear, mellifluous voice.

"Citizens of Florence! I possess no mandate to disseminate news save your collective will. However, the tidings are favorable and harmless to recount. The Most Christian King is signing a treaty honoring Florence. Yet, you owe this victory to one of your own citizens, a man who uttered words worthy of ancient Rome—you owe it to Piero Capponi!"
