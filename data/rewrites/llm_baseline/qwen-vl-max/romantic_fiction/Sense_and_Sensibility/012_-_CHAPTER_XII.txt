The next morning, as Elinor and Marianne walked together in the quiet fields near Barton, Marianne broke into a joyous confession that, despite Elinor’s well-known awareness of her sister’s impulsive nature, still took her by surprise. With sparkling eyes and a voice full of delight, Marianne announced that Willoughby had given her a horse—one he had raised himself on his estate in Somersetshire, perfectly suited for a lady rider.

She spoke of it with such enthusiasm that she barely paused to consider the practicalities: their mother had no intention of keeping a horse; if they accepted this gift, they would need another for the servant, a new stable, and perhaps even an extra groom. Yet none of these matters seemed to weigh on Marianne’s mind. She had already accepted the present without hesitation, and now shared the news with radiant excitement. “He’ll send his groom straightaway to bring it,” she added, “and once it arrives, we’ll ride every day. You can share it too, Elinor—imagine galloping across these open downs!”

Elinor struggled to bring her sister back from such a dreamy vision. The realities were clear: the expense, the necessity of hiring a servant, the space required for stables—none of it could be ignored. But Marianne waved aside each objection. “A servant? It wouldn’t cost much. Mama will never mind. And someone else can ride the second horse—perhaps John could borrow one from the park. As for a stable, a simple shed would do.”

At last, Elinor ventured to question the propriety of accepting such a generous gift from a man they had known only briefly. “It seems unwise,” she said gently, “to receive a present like this from someone so newly acquainted.”

Marianne turned sharply, her tone firm. “You’re mistaken, Elinor. I don’t know Willoughby by years or formal introduction—I know him by heart. I’ve not known him long, but I understand him better than anyone else in the world—better than John, better than any stranger. Intimacy isn’t measured by time, but by feeling. Some people take seven years to grow close; others, seven days are enough. I’d feel more impropriety accepting a horse from my own brother than from Willoughby. I know little of John, though we’ve lived under the same roof for years. But I’ve known Willoughby’s character for a long time now.”

Elinor knew better than to argue further. She understood her sister’s spirit—any resistance would only strengthen her resolve. Instead, she appealed to Marianne’s love for their mother. She reminded her how such a change would burden their mother, who was already kind-hearted beyond measure, and how difficult it would be to justify such an addition to their modest household.

Slowly, Marianne relented. She promised not to mention the gift to their mother and to tell Willoughby, when he next called, that she must decline his offer. True to her word, when Willoughby arrived later that day, Elinor overheard her sister speak softly of her disappointment. She explained the reasons—practical and affectionate alike—and made it clear that acceptance was no longer possible.

Willoughby listened with visible regret. When he replied, his voice was low and tender. “But the horse is still yours, Marianne—though you cannot ride it now. I’ll keep it safe until you can claim it. When you leave Barton to begin your own life in a home of your own, Queen Mab shall welcome you.”

Elinor heard every word. In the tone, the intimacy of calling her sister by her first name, the very choice of words—she saw at once the depth of their connection. This was no mere friendship; it was an understanding, a promise. From that moment, Elinor believed them engaged. And though she felt no shock, only a quiet sorrow, she wondered why such honest hearts should allow such a truth to remain hidden until chance revealed it.

The next day, Margaret brought further confirmation. Willoughby had spent the evening before with them, and while Elinor and their mother were away, Margaret had been left alone in the parlour with him and Marianne. She now approached Elinor with wide-eyed seriousness.

“Oh, Elinor!” she whispered. “I have the most wonderful secret! I’m sure Marianne will marry Mr. Willoughby soon!”

“Again?” Elinor replied, trying not to smile. “You’ve said that since the first time you saw them on High-church Down. And remember—you once swore she wore his portrait around her neck, only to find it was our great uncle’s miniature.”

“But this is different!” Margaret insisted. “I’m absolutely certain. He has a lock of her hair.”

“Be careful, Margaret,” Elinor warned. “It might just be some distant relative’s hair.”

“No, truly—it’s Marianne’s. I saw him cut it off myself. After tea, when you and Mama left, they were talking so fast, whispering like birds. Then he took up her scissors and cut a long strand from her hair—her hair was all loose down her back. He kissed it, folded it in white paper, and tucked it into his pocket-book.”

Elinor could not doubt her. The story aligned perfectly with what she had already sensed. Margaret’s keen observation was often amusing, but not always wise. That evening, at the park, Mrs. Jennings pressed her to reveal the name of the young man Elinor favored—the subject that had long intrigued her.

Margaret looked at Elinor and said, “I mustn’t tell, may I, Elinor?”

The room burst into laughter. Elinor forced a smile, though her heart sank. She knew exactly whom Margaret meant—a man whose name she could not bear to hear joked about.

Marianne, seeing her distress, stepped in, turning red with anger. “Remember,” she said sharply, “you have no right to repeat what you think, no matter how certain you are.”

“I didn’t guess,” Margaret protested. “You told me yourself.”

This only deepened the merriment. Mrs. Jennings leaned forward eagerly. “Oh, Miss Margaret, do tell us—what is the gentleman’s name?”

“I mustn’t say, ma’am,” Margaret replied, “but I know it well. And I know where he is too.”

“Ah, yes—surely at Norland, his family home,” Mrs. Jennings guessed. “Perhaps he’s the curate?”

“No,” Margaret said firmly. “He’s not in any profession.”

Marianne interrupted again, her voice rising. “Margaret, you know this is all nonsense. There is no such person.”

“Well then,” Margaret retorted, “he must be dead, because I’m certain there *was* such a man once—and his name begins with an F.”

Just then, Lady Middleton remarked, “It’s raining very hard.” Though Elinor suspected it was less out of concern for her and more due to Lady Middleton’s distaste for such idle talk, the comment was a welcome interruption. Colonel Brandon, ever thoughtful, seized the opportunity to join in, and soon the conversation turned entirely to the weather.

Willoughby opened the piano and invited Marianne to play. With music filling the air and several guests shifting focus, the earlier tension faded.

Yet Elinor remained unsettled. Her thoughts lingered on the name beginning with F—on the man who might never exist, yet whose memory carried such weight.

That evening, plans were made for a trip the next day to visit a magnificent estate twelve miles from Barton, belonging to Colonel Brandon’s brother-in-law. Without his influence, access was denied, as the owner—then abroad—had strictly forbidden visitors. The grounds were said to be breathtaking, with a large lake perfect for sailing. Cold food would be packed, carriages would be open, and everything arranged in the style of a grand pleasure party.

Sir John, who had led such excursions every summer for a decade, spoke passionately of the beauty. Still, some questioned the wisdom of the outing—after all, it had rained daily for two weeks, and the season was uncertain. Mrs. Dashwood, already unwell with a cold, agreed with Elinor to stay behind.

And so, as the group prepared to depart, Elinor stood apart, watching her sister’s light step and the quiet confidence in her face—knowing that beneath the surface, a future was unfolding, shaped not by reason, but by passion.
