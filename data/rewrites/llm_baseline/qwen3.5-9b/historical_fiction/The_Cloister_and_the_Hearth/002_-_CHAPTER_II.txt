"The soup is piping hot," announced Gerard.

"But how on earth are we to get it to our mouths?" queried the elder, speaking with evident despair.

"Father, the young man has provided us with straws!" Margaret replied, a mischievous smile playing upon her lips.

"Ay, ay!" acknowledged the old man. "Yet my poor bones are stiff, and truly, the fire burns too fiercely for a body to kneel over while using such slender tubes. By Saint John the Baptist, but the lad is remarkably dexterous!"

As he voiced his predicament, Gerard swiftly resolved it. In an instant, he loosened the knot at his breast, removed his cap, placed a stone into each corner of it, wrapped his handkerchief around his fist, snatched the flask from the heat, wedged it securely between the stones, and pressed the cap firmly against the old man's nose with a cheerful grin. Trembling slightly, the elder inserted the end of the rye-straw and drew deeply. Immediately, his pale, drawn visage seemed to ignite, growing brighter until it glowed warmly. Upon drawing a long, sustaining breath, he exclaimed:

"Hippocrates and Galen!" he cried. "'Tis *soupe au vin*—the restorer of all restoratives. Blessed be the nation that devised it, the woman who crafted it, and the youth who delivers it to the fainting. Give a sip, my daughter, while I recount to our young host the history and virtues of this sovereign remedy. This elixir was unknown to the ancients; we find it nowhere in their medical treatises nor in those popular chronicles that detail their cures for surgery and disease alike. Hector, in the *Iliad*, if my memory serves me correctly—

—was invited by one of the lady poets to partake of a draught of wine, yet he declined, citing that he was preparing for battle and must not consume anything to weaken his strength. Now, had the *soupe au vin* been known in Troy, it is certain that in refusing plain wine on those grounds, he would have added in his verse: 'But a *soupe au vin*, madam, I shall taste, and with gratitude.' Not only would this have been mere courtesy—a virtue lacking in no perfect commander—but failing to do so would have marked him as shallow and imprudent, unfit to lead armies. For men entering battle require sustenance and every possible advantage, as proven by the fact that foolish generals, sending hungry soldiers against well-fed foes, have been defeated throughout the ages by inferior forces. The Romans suffered a great defeat in northern Italy against Hannibal the Carthaginian solely due to this oversight. Now, this divine nectar grants strength to the limbs and ardor to the spirit in a single moment; taken by Hector at the critical juncture, aided by Apollo, Venus, and the blessed saints, it would most likely have secured victory for the Greeks. Observe how faint, weary, and heartbroken I was but a moment ago; well, I inhale this celestial cordial, and now behold me brave as Achilles and strong as an eagle."

"Oh, Father, now? An eagle, alas!"

"Girl, I defy thee and the entire world. Ready, I declare, like a foaming charger to devour the distance between here and Rotterdam, and strong enough to combat life's ills—even poverty and old age, which latter-day philosophers call the greatest evil. Denied; unless a man's life has been squandered—which, by the way, it generally has. Now, to the moderns!"

"Father! Dear father!"

"Fear me not, girl; I shall be brief, unreasonably and unseasonably brief. The *soupe au vin* appears not in modern science; but this is merely further proof, if proof were needed, that for the last few centuries physicians have been fools, relying on chicken broth and decoctions of gold, thereby attributing supreme qualities to meat possessing the least juice and to a metal possessing fewer chemical properties than any other. Mountebanks! Dunces! Murderers! Since, therefore, no wisdom can be gleaned from these sources, let us turn to the chroniclers. First, we find Duguesclin, a French knight, preparing to engage the English—who, at that time, ruled half of France and were fierce fighters by sea and land—he drank not one, but three cups of *soupe au vin* in honor of the Holy Trinity. Having done so, he charged the islanders; and, as might have been predicted, slew a multitude and drove the remainder into the sea. Yet he was but the first of a long lineage of holy and formidable warriors who, sustained, fortified, strengthened, and comforted by this divine restorative, achieved greatness."

"Dear father, pray join that venerable company before the soup grows cold." Holding the hat imploringly in both hands, she urged him to insert the straw once more.

This spared them the discussion of modern examples and afforded Gerard the chance to tell Margaret how proud his mother would be that her soup had benefited a man of learning.

"Yes! But," said Margaret, "it would suit her poorly to see her son give all and receive none himself. Why did you bring but two straws?"

"Fair mistress, I hoped you would permit me to kiss your straw, seeing there were only two."

Margaret smiled and blushed. "Never beg that you may command," she said. "The straw is not mine; it is yours: you cut it in that field."

"I cut it, and thus it became mine; but afterward, your lips touched it, and thus it became yours."

"Did it? Then I will lend it to you. There—it is yours again; your lips have touched it."

"No, it belongs to us both now. Let us share it."

"By all means; you possess a knife."

"No, I will not cut it—that would be unluckily. I shall bite it. Thus I shall keep my half; you will surely burn yours once you return home, I suspect."

"You know me not. I waste nothing. It matters little whether I fashion a hairpin from it or something else."

This response disheartened the novice Gerard rather than provoked him, leaving him silent. Once the bread and soup were consumed, the elderly scholar prepared to resume his journey. Then arose a small difficulty: Gerard, the adept, could not retie his ribbon as Catherine had tied it. Margaret, after watching his futile efforts with a knowing glance, offered assistance. For at her age, girls delight in alternating between coyness and tenderness, sauciness and gentleness, and she perceived she had momentarily embarrassed him. Then a fair head, crowned with a stately mass of auburn hair, glossy and radiant amidst the silver threads, bowed sweetly toward him. As it captivated his gaze, two white, supple hands moved delicately upon the stubborn ribbon, molding it with soft, airy touches. A heavenly thrill coursed through the innocent youth, and vague glimpses of a new realm of feeling and sentiment opened before him. These novel and exquisite sensations Margaret unknowingly prolonged; it is not natural for her sex to hasten anything pertaining to the sacred toilet. Indeed, when the tapering fingers finally subdued the ends of the knot, her mind remained uneasy until, employing a maneuver peculiar to the female hand, she made her palm convex and applied it with gentle pressure to the center of the knot—a sweet, little coaxing hand-kiss, meaning no more than, "Now be a good knot, and remain so." The palm-kiss was bestowed upon the ribbon, but the wearer's heart leaped to meet it.

"There, that is how it was," said Margaret, stepping back to inspect her work with one last keen scrutiny. Looking upward for simple approval of her skill, she received instead a gaze of such ardent longing and adoration that she quickly lowered her eyes, flushing all over. An indescribable tremor seized her, and she retreated with downcast lashes and betraying cheeks, taking her father's arm on the opposite side. Gerard, blushing at having startled her with his stare, took the other arm; and so the two youths proceeded, heads bowed and hearts conscious, supporting the aging scholar in silence.

They entered Rotterdam through the Schiedamse Poort. Unacquainted with the town, Peter directed Gerard toward the Hooch Straet, where the Town Hall stood. He himself accompanied Margaret to his cousin's residence in the Ooster-Waagen Straet; thus, almost upon crossing the threshold, their paths diverged. They exchanged a friendly farewell, and Gerard plunged into the great city. A profound sense of solitude descended upon him, yet the streets teemed with people. Then he lamented, too late, that out of delicacy, he had not inquired of his former companions who they were and where they dwelt.

"Beshrew my shamefacedness!" he muttered. "But their speech and bearing surpassed their station, and something whispered that they wished to remain anonymous. I shall never see her again. Oh, weary world, I hate you and your ways. To think I must encounter beauty, goodness, and learning—three priceless pearls—and never behold them once more!"

Lost in this melancholy reverie, allowing his body to drift wherever it pleased, he became disoriented. However, encountering a throng of people moving in a single direction, he joined them, reasoning they must be heading for the Town Hall.
