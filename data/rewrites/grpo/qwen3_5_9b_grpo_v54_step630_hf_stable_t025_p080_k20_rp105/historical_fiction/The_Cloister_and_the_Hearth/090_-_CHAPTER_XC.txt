The silence that settled over the little house in the Hoog Straet was heavier than the stone walls themselves. It was the day following that terrible rupture, and the atmosphere within felt less like a home and more like a tomb. Catherine, usually a creature of such vibrant energy and bustling activity, moved through the rooms with a listless gait, her spirit crushed. After dinner, her eyes swollen and red from weeping, she made her way to the convent, driven by a desperate hope to soften Gerard's heart and lay the first stone of a reconciliation.

She arrived at the convent gates, but the porter offered no comfort; Gerard had departed late the previous night and was not expected to return. Sighing heavily under the weight of this news, Catherine sought out Margaret. She found her sitting in a state of profound stillness, life seemingly having lost its flavor for her. Margaret held her young son so tightly in her arms, clutching him as if he were the only fragment of existence remaining, terrified that someone might snatch him away as well.

Catherine pleaded with her to visit the Hoog Straet. "What for?" Margaret sighed, her voice weary. "You must tell yourself she is the cause of all this ruin."

"Nay, nay," Catherine insisted gently. "We are not so ill-hearted. Eli is so fond of you; perhaps your presence can soften him."

"Oh, if you think I can do any good, I will come," Margaret replied, though her sigh betrayed a deep exhaustion.

Upon their arrival, they found Eli and a carpenter installing a new signboard in place of the names Cornelis and Sybrandt. The name carved into the wood was Margaret Brandt's. With all her love for Margaret, the sight cut through poor Catherine like a blade. "The bane of one is another's meat," she whispered bitterly.

"Can he make me spend the money unjustly?" Margaret asked, her tone cold and detached.

"You are a good soul," Catherine said, offering a weak compliment. "Yes, best of all, since he is the strongest."

The next day, Giles dropped by. Catherine recounted the tragedy in a manner designed to elicit pity, painting herself and her family as victims of their brother's malice and their father's rejection. Yet, Giles's prejudices ran in the opposite direction. He listened patiently, then dismissed the tale with blunt contempt, declaring that the brothers had gotten off far too cheap. They deserved to be hanged at Margaret's door, he crowed with delight at the prospect of his favorite returning to power. "I'll show him," he declared, "what it means to have a brother at court with a heart to serve a friend and a head to point the way."

"Bless thee, Giles," Margaret murmured softly.

"Thou wast ever his stanch friend, dear Giles," little Kate added, her voice small. "But alas, I know not what thou canst do for him now."

Once Giles had departed, the house fell into a sad, heavy silence. Just then, a well-dressed man opened the door softly and inquired if Margaret Brandt was present.

"D'ye hear, lass? You are wanted," Catherine said briskly, her gossiping instinct instantly ignited.

"Well, mother," Margaret replied listlessly, "and here I am."

A shuffling sound echoed from the doorway, and a colorless, feeble old man was assisted into the room. It was Ghysbrecht Van Swieten. At the sight of him, Catherine shrieked, throwing her apron over her head in horror, while Margaret shuddered violently and turned her face away, refusing to look upon him.

A feeble voice emerged from the stranger's lips. "Good people, a dying man hath come to ask your forgiveness."

"Come to look on your work, you mean," Catherine retorted, tearing her apron down and bursting into sobs. "There, there, she is fainting; look to her, Eli, quick!"

"Nay," Margaret said in a weak voice. "The sight of him gave me a turn, that is all. Prithee, let him say his say, and go; for he is the murderer of me and mine."

"Alas," Ghysbrecht replied, "I am too feeble to speak while standing, and no one bids me sit."

Eli, who had followed him inside, intervened, his voice half-sullen, half-apologetic. "Well, burgomaster, 'tis not our wont to leave a visitor standing while we sit. But man, man, you have wrought us too much ill." The honest fellow's voice began to tremble with suppressed anger, fighting hard to contain his rage because it was his own home.

Then, little Kate found an advocate in the situation where others had failed. "Father, mother," she said softly, "my duty to you is clear, but this is not well. Death squares all accounts. Do you not see death in his face? I shall not live long, good friends; and his time is shorter still."

Eli hastened to set a chair for their dying enemy with his own hands. Ghysbrecht's attendants helped him into it. "Go fetch the boxes," he instructed. Two boxes were brought in, and the attendants retired, leaving their master alone in the family he had so cruelly injured.

Every eye turned toward him, save Margaret's. With unsteady fingers, Ghysbrecht undid the boxes. From one, he produced the title deeds to a property in Tergou. "This land and these houses belonged to Floris Brandt, and they belong to thee of right, his granddaughter," he announced. "These I usurped for a debt long since defrayed with interest. I now restore them to their rightful owner with penitent tears. In this other box are three hundred and forty golden angels, being the rent and fines I received from that land exceeding Floris Brandt's debt to me. I kept it counted, still meaning to be just one day; but Avarice withheld me, pray, good people, against temptation! I was not born dishonest: yet you see."

"Well, to be sure!" Catherine cried. "And you the burgomaster! Hast whipped good store of thieves in thy day. However," she added on second thought, "'tis better late than never. What, Margaret, art deaf? The good man hath brought thee back thine own. Art a rich woman. Alack, what a mountain o' gold!"

"Bid him keep the land and the gold, and give me back my Gerard, that he stole from me with his treason," Margaret said, her head still averted.

"Alas!" Ghysbrecht lamented. "Would I could! What I can, I have done. It cost me a sore struggle; and I rose from my last bed to do it myself, lest some mischance should come between her and her rights."

"Old man," Margaret said, her voice trembling, "since thou, whose idol is pelf, hast done this, God and the saints will, as I hope, forgive thee. As for me, I am neither saint nor angel, but only a poor woman, whose heart thou hast broken. Speak to him, Kate, for I am like the dead."

Kate meditated for a moment, and then her soft, silvery voice fell upon the air like a soothing melody. "My poor sister hath a sorrow that riches cannot heal. Give her time, Ghysbrecht; 'tis not in nature she should forgive thee all. Her boy is fatherless; and she is neither maid, wife, nor widow; and the blow fell but two days since that laid her heart a bleeding."

A single, heavy sob from Margaret was the only response to these words.

"Therefore, give her time!" Ghysbrecht urged. "And ere thou diest, she will forgive thee all, ay, even to pleasure me, that haply shall not be long behind thee, Ghysbrecht. Meantime, we, whose wounds are sore but not so deep as hers, do pardon thee, a penitent and a dying man; and I, for one, will pray for thee from this hour. Go in peace!"

The little oracle had spoken; it was enough. Eli even invited him to break a manchet and drink a stoup of wine to give him heart for his journey.

But Ghysbrecht declined. "What I have done is a cordial to me," he said. "Man seeth but a little way before him, neighbor. This land I clung to so dearly was a bed of nettles to me all the time. 'Tis gone; and I feel happier and livelier for the loss of it."

He called his men, and they lifted him into the litter.

When he was gone, Catherine gloated over the money. She had never seen so much wealth together and was almost angry with Margaret for "sitting out there like an image." She dilated on the advantages of money, teasing Margaret until she finally prevailed upon her to come and look at it.

"Better let her be, mother," Kate interjected. "How can she relish gold with a heart in her bosom likelier to lead?" But Catherine persisted.

The result was that Margaret looked down at all her wealth with wondering eyes. Then, suddenly, she wrung her hands and cried with piercing anguish, "TOO LATE! TOO LATE!" She shook off her leaden despondency only to fall into strong hysterics over the fortune that came too late to be shared with the man she loved.

A little of this gold, a portion of this land, a year or two ago, when it was as much her own as now; and Gerard would have never left her side for Italy or any other place.

"Too late! Too late!"
