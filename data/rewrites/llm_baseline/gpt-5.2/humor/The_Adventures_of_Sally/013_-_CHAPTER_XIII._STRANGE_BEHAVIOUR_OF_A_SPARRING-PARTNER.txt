Sally sat in her New York apartment that first morning back with the taut, briny courage of a person who has stood too long on the edge of something cold and honest and, at last, jumped.

She hurt. She had known she would. For months she had been taking her life the way some people take their coffee: weak, lukewarm, and with a pious little lie that it was “fine.” Now the truth was scalding. It stung. It also woke you up.

Her trunks were unpacked. Her dresses hung in their places. The room had that freshly-tidied smell of aired linen and the faint, defeated ghost of ship’s luggage—a blend of leather, dust, and the sort of soap that pretends it is a flower. The next step, clearly, was Ginger.

She wanted to see him with a hunger that was not exactly romantic but very human: the hunger for one steady, friendly face in a city whose noises always behaved like they’d been paid by the clang. At the dock she’d had a moment—just a moment—when the customs shed swallowed sound and threw it back at her in hard echoes, so that even her thoughts seemed stamped in tin. She had felt terribly alone there, as if the city were an enormous factory and she was a loose screw in its palm.

She glanced at her watch and discovered it was early enough to catch Ginger at the office and, with the simple tyranny of appetite, make him take her to lunch. She wanted an ordinary table, ordinary bread, ordinary talking. She wanted the soft domestic percussion of knife on plate, not the iron snarl of New York.

So she put on her hat and went out.

The outer office of Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises, Ltd., had been altered by the restless hand of change, which in New York works overtime and never takes Sundays. The old office-boy had gone, and in his place sat an entirely new specimen—pimpled, yes, but pimpled with an originality all its own. This one had the air of a young magistrate about to sentence someone for breathing wrong.

He examined Sally as if she were an invoice that might be fraudulent, then asked, in a tone that implied she had already confessed to something, what she wanted.

“I want Mr. Kemp,” said Sally.

The boy, who had been scratching his cheek with a ruler in the manner of a philosopher, made a little note of this on the inside of his suspicion.

“What name?” he asked, coldly.

“Nicholas,” said Sally. “I’m Mr. Nicholas’ sister.”

On a previous occasion those words had produced a small catastrophe. Today they acted like a bullet.

He started, opened his mouth, and dropped the ruler.

In the act of stooping to retrieve it, he performed the rapid moral gymnastics of the guilty. When he rose again, he had rearranged his features into a smile so dazzlingly deferential it was almost indecent. It was like the sun coming out suddenly over a London fog, and you suspecting it has an ulterior motive.

“Will you take a seat, lady?” he said, and even dusted a chair with his sleeve as if it were a throne and she the rightful occupant. “Fine morning, ain’t it?”

“Thank you,” said Sally. “Will you tell him I’m here?”

“Mr. Nicholas is out, miss,” said the boy with a sorrowful gentility, like a butler in a play. “He’s back in New York, but he’s gone out.”

“I don’t want Mr. Nicholas,” said Sally. “I want Mr. Kemp.”

The office-boy’s smile remained, but it thinned at the edges.

“Mr. Kemp?”

“Yes. Mr. Kemp.”

His face became a landscape of apologetic emptiness.

“Don’t know of anybody of that name around here,” he said. “Does he look like anything? I mean—what’s he look like?”

“He has very red hair.”

“Never seen him in here,” said the office-boy.

The truth came down on Sally with that peculiar clean cruelty truths have when you’ve been hoping for a miracle. Ginger had done it again. Left alone, he had somehow managed—through no malice, only the pure genius of misdirected effort—to smash his own prospects. And if Fillmore had let him go, it must have been a smash that even Fillmore couldn’t ignore.

Sally heard her own voice go dry.

“Where is Mr. Nicholas?” she asked. “Did you say he was out?”

“Really out, miss,” said the boy with engaging candour. “Went off to White Plains in his automobile half an hour ago.”

“White Plains? What for?”

Now that he was no longer actively terrified, he warmed to conversation. It was plain he would ordinarily have preferred solitude for practising whatever it was he practised—he had a general air of someone who believed office supplies were meant to be thrown in graceful arcs—but Sally’s presence had turned him into a delighted gossip.

“I guess he’s gone up to take a look at Bugs Butler,” he said.

“Whose butler?” said Sally, bewildered.

The boy’s expression became kindly, the way men look when they think women have said something charmingly foolish.

“Bugs Butler is training up at White Plains, miss.”

“Who is Bugs Butler?”

A spark returned to the boy’s eyes. This was religion.

“Ah!” he said, and his tone became that of a prophet addressing a nation of idiots. “Who is he! That’s what they’re all saying, all the wise guys. Who has Bugs Butler ever licked? A lot of stiffs out on the coast. Ginks nobody’s ever heard of, except Cyclone Mullins, and it took that false alarm fifteen rounds to get a referee’s decision over him. The boss would go and give him a chance against the champ, but I could’ve told him the legitimate contender was K-leg Binns. K-leg put Cyclone Mullins out in the fifth.” He drew breath, aggrieved at the general stupidity of mankind. “Well, if anybody thinks Bugs Butler can last six rounds with Lew Lucas, I got two bucks right here in my vest pocket says it ain’t so.”

Sally began to see what this meant. White Plains was not business, it was boxing.

“Oh, Bugs—Mr. Butler is one of the boxers in this fight my brother’s interested in?”

“That’s right. He’s going up against the lightweight champ. Lew Lucas is the lightweight champ. He’s a bird!”

“Yes?” said Sally, because the boy had cocked his head in a way that demanded participation.

“Yes, sir!” he cried, and then added the most crushing proof of all: “He used to live on the next street to me. I seen his old mother as close as I am to you. Seen her a hundred times. Is any stiff of a Bugs Butler going to lick a fellow like that?”

“It doesn’t seem likely,” said Sally.

“You spoke it!” said the boy, slapping unsuccessfully at a fly on the blotting-paper.

There was a pause. Sally rose.

“And there’s another thing,” he said, unwilling to let such greatness end. “Can Bugs Butler make a hundred and thirty-five ringside without being weak?”

“It sounds awfully difficult.”

“They say he’s clever,” scoffed the boy. “Well, what’s that going to get him? The poor fish can’t punch a hole in a nut-sundae.”

“You don’t seem to like Mr. Butler.”

“Oh, I got nothing against him,” said the boy magnanimously. “I’m only saying he’s no licence to be mixing it with Lew Lucas.”

Sally, whose mind was now on Ginger with a sharpness that made everything else seem far away, asked how to find White Plains.

“Oh, anybody’ll show you the way to the training-camp,” said the office-boy. “If you hurry, there’s a train you can make now.”

She thanked him and went out. Behind her, he opened the door with a certain rusty old-world gallantry and then, as she glanced back, she saw him pick up a pair of paper-weights and place the ruler carefully on his chin, practising for whatever vaudeville future awaited him.

At White Plains, the training-camp was a large room that couldn’t decide whether it was a barn or a gymnasium, so it compromised by being both. There were ropes set up in the centre like a little fenced pasture, and athletic contraptions hung on the walls in the manner of trophies from a war against comfort.

A crowd had gathered—about forty people with the air of those who had paid fifty cents and intended to get their money’s worth by staring. Modern life, with its talent for commercializing anything that once belonged to privacy, had converted even a fighter’s preparation into a matinée.

Some were newspaper men on the free list—writers who would take whatever Bugs Butler actually said and translate it into sentences like, “I am in really superb condition and feel little apprehension of the issue,” and artists who would draw him semi-nude with feet like small boats.

Fillmore Nicholas, trapped among them, had been buttonholed for a quarter of an hour by Mr. Lester Burrowes, the manager of Bugs Butler—one of those men who talk with their whole bodies, clinging with their eyes and hands. When, at last, Burrowes was distracted by something requiring personal fidgeting, Fillmore let out a sigh so deep it seemed to come from his shoes and began to edge toward the door.

He was desperately hungry. In his enthusiasm to inspect Bugs Butler on the final day before the fight—tomorrow—he had missed lunch. The world now consisted chiefly of the idea of food.

He was sliding away, thinking of steak and potatoes and silence, when a soft voice spoke near his ear.

“Hallo, Fillmore!”

Fillmore left the floor.

He did it neatly—an upward twitch of pure alarm that lifted him a clear three inches, as if his nervous system had been prodded with a cattle stick. He turned, staring, licking his lips with the tip of his tongue, trying to take in a fact which had arrived without warning.

Sally.

If ever a man had a right to behave like an astonished goat, Fillmore had it. A week ago she had been in Shropshire at Monk’s Crofton. She had given no hint of tearing up her English chapter and beginning a new one on American soil, and certainly not in a boxing barn at White Plains, where noises were the thud of gloves, the squeak of shoes, and the incessant chatter of men who thought volume was evidence.

As his brain caught up with his eyes, apprehension slid coldly into him. He had considered Monk’s Crofton to be the prelude to a very sensible engagement. Nowadays people did not ask brothers for permission; they simply invited the girl and her brother down to the country, assembled the family like a jury, and proceeded. Fillmore had taken it for granted that Bruce Carmyle was in love with Sally, and had been mentally blessing the union with an affectionate eye on the Carmyle bank-balance.

Now Sally was here.

And if the invitation had meant only one thing, so did this.

“Sally,” croaked Fillmore, for his voice was busy being elsewhere. “What… what…?”

“Did I startle you? I’m sorry.”

“What are you doing here? Why aren’t you at Monk’s Crofton?”

Sally glanced past him at the ring and the little ring of expectant faces.

“I decided I wanted to get back to America. Circumstances arose which made it pleasanter to leave Monk’s Crofton.”

“Do you mean to say…?”

“Yes. Don’t let’s talk about it.”

“Do you mean to say,” Fillmore persisted, because brothers are built that way, “that Carmyle proposed to you and you turned him down?”

Sally flushed.

“I don’t think it’s particularly nice to talk about that sort of thing,” she said, “but—yes.”

Fillmore’s face took on the expression of a man watching someone deliberately drop a valuable object into a river. He was filled with that bleak certainty, so trying to the spirit, that other people are capable of spectacular bone-headedness even when their happiness is neatly arranged and labeled.

“But why?”

“Oh, Fill!” said Sally, and the sound of his dismay rasped her like sandpaper. “I should have thought the reason was obvious.”

“You mean you don’t like him?”

“I don’t know whether I do or not,” said Sally. “I certainly don’t like him enough to marry him.”

“He’s a darned good fellow.”

“Is he?” said Sally. “You say so. I don’t know.”

At this point Fillmore’s hunger, which had been waiting politely in the wings, came striding onstage.

“Let’s go to the hotel and talk it over,” he said. “We’ll go to the hotel and I’ll give you something to eat.”

“I don’t want anything to eat, thanks.”

“You don’t want anything to eat?” said Fillmore, staring as if she had announced she did not believe in oxygen. “I’m starving.”

“Well, run along then.”

“Yes, but I want to talk—”

He did not get farther, because Mr. Lester Burrowes returned at a trot, wearing a checked tweed suit that seemed designed to keep a man perpetually in motion, and a brown bowler hat worn with a courage that defied all taste.

“Say, Mr. Nicholas,” cried Burrowes, grabbing him as if Fillmore were about to be swept out to sea, “you ain’t going? Bugs is just getting ready to spar.”

He glanced at Sally.

“My sister—Mr. Burrowes,” said Fillmore faintly. “Mr. Burrowes is Bugs Butler’s manager.”

“How do you do?” said Sally.

“Pleased to meecher,” said Burrowes, and at once went on, because he was built without brakes. “Say—”

“I was just going to the hotel to get something to eat,” said Fillmore, trying to pull free.

Mr. Burrowes clutched a coat-button with the accuracy of a hawk.

“Yes, but before-you-go-lemme-tell-ya-somef’n. You never seen this boy o’ mine when he’s feeling right. Believe me, he’s there! He’s a wizard. He’s a Hindoo! He’s been practising up a left shift that—”

Fillmore looked at Sally with the wild appeal of a prisoner who sees a passing friend. Sally, though she was storing up questions for him about Ginger that would later require blood, pitied him in the moment.

And then, remembering the office-boy’s sermon, she said, sweetly and severely:

“Can he make a hundred and thirty-five ringside without being weak?”

The effect on Mr. Burrowes was astonishing. He released Fillmore’s coat-button and fell back as if struck by a moral brick. His mouth opened. His eyes bulged in reverent surprise, the way an eminent professor looks when a kitten begins quoting Greek.

“Whazzat?” he murmured.

“It took him fifteen rounds to get a referee’s decision over Cyclone Mullins,” said Sally, warming to it, “and K-leg Binns—”

“You ain’t got it right,” protested Burrowes at once, recovering like a man whose honor has been tapped on the chin. “Say, you mustn’t believe what you see in the papers. The referee was dead against us, and Cyclone was down once for all of half a minute and they wouldn’t count him out. Gee! You gotta kill a guy in some towns before they’ll give you a decision. At that, they couldn’t do nothing so raw as make it anything but a win for my boy, after him leading by a mile all the way.” He peered at her with fresh respect. “Have you ever seen Bugs, ma’am?”

Sally admitted she had not.

Mr. Burrowes plunged into his breast pocket and produced a picture-postcard, which he thrust into her hand with the air of presenting a sacred icon.

“That’s Bugs,” he said. “Take a slant at that and tell me if he don’t look the goods.”

The photograph showed a young man in the minimum of clothing, crouched in a tortured attitude, like someone trying to remember whether he had turned off the stove.

“I’ll call him over and have him sign it for you,” said Burrowes, already shouting. “Here, Bugs—wantcher.”

A youth in a bath-robe turned from a group by the ring and started toward them with the languid swagger of someone to whom applause is a birthright. Then he saw Sally and quickened, because he was an admirer of the sex.

“Bugs,” said Mr. Burrowes, “this is Miss Nicholas, come to see you work out. I been telling her she’s going to have a treat.” And to Sally, with a flourish: “Shake hands with Bugs Butler, ma’am, the coming lightweight champion of the world.”

In the flesh, Sally thought, Bugs Butler’s photograph had been exceedingly charitable.

He had a mouth that curved meanly, the sort of mouth that would enjoy bad news delivered to strangers. His eyes had a cold arrogance, and there was something in his whole manner—dangerous, slick, unpleasant—that made Sally’s skin object to him before her mind had finished being polite. Worst of all, he smirked at her, not as a man smiles at a woman, but as an owner smiles at a thing.

Still, she made herself say the correct words.

“I hope you are going to win, Mr. Butler.”

The smile she managed convinced Mr. Butler that he had, as usual, made an impression. His chest swelled beneath the bath-robe, and he peered at her with a conquering look.

“You betcher,” he said.

Mr. Burrowes glanced at his watch.

“Time you were starting, Bugs.”

Mr. Butler’s eyes moved reluctantly from Sally to the audience, and he looked bored. The crowd was smaller than he liked, and too many were newspaper men who hadn’t paid.

“All right,” he said.

Then he looked back at Sally, cheered by the thought that at least one pair of bright eyes would watch him.

“I’ll go a couple of rounds with Reddy for a starter,” he said. “Seen him anywheres? He’s never around when he’s wanted.”

“I’ll fetch him,” said Mr. Burrowes, and hurried away.

“I’m going to show that guy up this afternoon,” said Mr. Butler, with a casual cruelty. “He’s been getting too fresh.”

He ducked under the ropes with a final smirk, and the crowd rustled as if someone had turned up the volume on expectation.

Presently Mr. Burrowes returned, shepherding a sturdily built young man whose face was hidden by the sweater he was pulling over his head. There was something familiar in the shape of his shoulders—something decent and solid.

A last tug, and the sweater came off.

Red hair flashed out, tousled and disordered.

Sally gasped. It escaped her before she could stop it, and several heads turned.

The red-headed young man, who had been stooping to pick up his gloves, straightened sharply and stared at her as if she were a hallucination. Color crept up his face, slowly, steadily, until he matched his hair.

It was Ginger Kemp.

Mr. Burrowes, practical and impatient, broke whatever spell had fallen.

“Come on, come on,” he snapped. “Li’l speed there, Reddy.”

Ginger started like a sleep-walker brought up short. Then, as if his body remembered what it was supposed to do, he began slowly pulling on his gloves. Embarrassment was stamped all over him—on his mouth, on the set of his shoulders, on the way he could not, for the life of him, make his eyes meet Sally’s.

His face was a shade of crimson that suggested some hidden kitchen calamity: a pot boiled over and nobody dared admit it.

Sally caught Mr. Burrowes’ elbow and whispered, “Who—him?”

Burrowes glanced at her, irritated at being delayed, then softened because she was Miss Nicholas and also because she had shown alarming knowledge of weights and decisions.

“Who, him?” he whispered back. “He’s just one o’ Bugs’ sparring-partners.”

“But—” began Sally, because her mind was suddenly full of all the months Ginger had been “at the office,” all the letters, all the spaces.

“You’ll excuse me, miss,” said Burrowes briskly, “but I gotta hold the watch. We mustn’t waste any time.”

Sally drew back, feeling abruptly like an intruder on some solemn male rite. This was Man’s hour: ropes, sweat, sharp voices, and the steady, punishing rhythm of bodies moving in a box of air. She felt very small and very much in the way—like a puppy that has wandered into church and can’t understand why everyone is glaring.

She stared at Ginger.

He stood in the opposite corner of the ring, feet shuffling, gaze averted, fiddling with his gloves as if they were the most interesting objects in the world and not, as they were, padded instruments meant for hitting and being hit. He seemed unreachable, shut behind a wall of self-consciousness so complete it might as well have been another country.

And still Sally stared, wide-eyed, while Ginger, crimson to the roots of his hair, kept plucking at the gloves and would not look at her at all.
