At the hour fixed the day before, they went out together to find Nicholas Higgins and his daughter. The very act of stepping side by side into the street—newly clothed, newly altered by sorrow—made them shy of one another, as if grief had changed the fit of their bodies and the manner of their walking. It was the first time, after many weeks, that they had chosen to go forth together; and without speaking of what was gone, they drew nearer in unuttered accord, keeping pace as though their feet had learned the measure of the same lament.

Milton met them with its usual harsh chorus: the metallic clink from some distant yard; the faint roar that never quite ceased, like a sea made of coal and wheels; and beneath it, nearer at hand, the domestic noises that struggled to survive—doors opening, a baby’s thin cry, a woman calling from an upper window, the soft scuff of clogs on flagstones. Mr. Hale, who had once lived among church-bells and the hush of country lanes, seemed to listen as one listens for a language half-forgotten; Margaret, whose senses had grown acute in this place, felt the sound press upon her mind as much as the soot pressed upon the sills. Even grief had its acoustics here: it did not fall like snow, it beat like rain on tin.

Higgins sat where they always found him, in his corner by the hearth; but his pipe lay untouched. The fire had sunk; the room smelt of cold ash and damp wool, as if warmth had been withdrawn not by weather but by will. He leaned his head on his hand, elbow on knee, and did not rise when they came in. Yet Margaret saw the welcome in his eye, as clear as a lamp in a dark place.

“Sit ye down, sit ye down,” he said, and jabbed the poker into the grate with a rough vigour, as though the iron could stir up more than embers. “Fire’s welly out.”

He looked more unkempt than she had ever seen him: a black stubble of beard shaded his hollowed cheeks; his jacket wanted mending; the paleness of his face showed harsher for the darkness about it. Hunger, she thought, had a look as unmistakable as smoke; and want of work left its own soot on a man.

“We thought we should have a good chance of finding you, just after dinner-time,” said Margaret, striving for a simple commonplace.

“We have had our sorrows too, since we saw you,” Mr. Hale added softly.

“Ay, ay,” said Higgins, and his voice had in it that grim jesting which is half-defiance, half-confession. “Sorrows is more plentiful than dinners just now; I reckon, my dinner hour stretches all o’er the day; yo’re pretty sure of finding me.”

“Are you out of work?” asked Margaret.

“Ay.” He gave it as one gives a blow—short, flat. Then, after a pause, he lifted his eyes. “I’m not wanting brass. Dunno yo’ think it. Bess, poor lass, had a little stock under her pillow, ready to slip into my hand, last moment, and Mary is fustian-cutting. But I’m out o’ work a’ the same.”

“We owe Mary some money,” said Mr. Hale, before Margaret’s quick pressure on his arm could stop the words.

Higgins’s head came up; his whole aspect hardened, like clay set in fire. “If hoo takes it, I’ll turn her out o’ doors. I’ll bide inside these four walls, and she’ll bide out. That’s a’.”

“But we owe her many thanks for her kind service—” Mr. Hale began again, his courtesy moving forward like a habit of old days.

“I ne’er thanken your daughter theer for her deeds o’ love to my poor wench,” said Higgins, and for a moment his roughness broke to show a sore, shy tenderness. “I ne’er could find th’ words. I’se have to begin to try now, if yo’ start making an ado about what little Mary could sarve yo’.”

Margaret would have turned him from this raw, defensive pride by gentler inquiry.

“Is it because of the strike you’re out of work?” she asked.

“Strike’s ended. It’s o’er for this time.” He stared into the dead fire as if reading there the bitter print of recent days. “I’m out o’ work because I ne’er asked for it. And I ne’er asked for it, because good words is scarce, and bad words is plentiful.”

He said it with a kind of stubborn relish, as though he had found some crooked satisfaction in putting their questions aside. Yet Margaret felt—felt as one feels the readiness of dough beneath the heel of the hand—that he wanted to be pressed, wanted to be made to yield and explain.

“And good words are—?” she prompted.

“Asking for work.” He spoke more fully now, the thought warming like broth on a rekindled flame. “I reckon them’s almost the best words that men can say. ‘Gi’ me work’ means ‘and I’ll do it like a man.’ Them’s good words.”

“And bad words are refusing you work when you ask for it?”

“Ay. Bad words is saying, ‘Aha, my fine chap! Yo’ve been true to yo’r order, and I’ll be true to mine. Yo’ did the best yo’ could for them as wanted help; that’s yo’re way of being true to yo’re kind: and I’ll be true to mine. Yo’ve been a poor fool, as knowed no better nor be a true faithful fool. So go and be d—d to yo’. There’s no work for yo’ here.’ Them’s bad words.” He drew breath as if the remembered insult had struck him anew. “I’m not a fool; and if I was, folk ought to ha’ taught me how to be wise after their fashion. I could m’appen ha’ learnt, if any one had tried to teach me.”

“Would it not be worth while,” said Mr. Hale, “to ask your old master if he would take you back again? It might be a poor chance, but it would be a chance.”

Higgins looked up sharply, his glance like a knife testing a seam. Then a low, bitter titter escaped him—no laughter, but something cracked.

“Measter! if it’s no offence, I’ll ask yo’ a question or two in my turn.”

“You’re quite welcome,” Mr. Hale replied, still with that gentle smile which tried to keep the world civil.

“I reckon yo’n some way of earning your bread. Folk seldom lives i’ Milton just for pleasure, if they can live anywhere else.”

“You are quite right. I have some independent property, but my intention in settling in Milton was to become a private tutor.”

“To teach folk.” Higgins nodded once, slow. “Well! I reckon they pay yo’ for teaching them, dunnot they?”

“Yes,” said Mr. Hale, and smiled a little more. “I teach in order to get paid.”

“And them that pays yo’, dun they tell yo’ whatten to do, or whatten not to do wi’ the money they gives you in just payment for your pains—in fair exchange like?”

“No; to be sure not!”

“They dunnot say, ‘Yo’ may have a brother, or a friend as dear as a brother, who wants this here brass for a purpose both yo’ and he think right; but yo’ mun promise not give it to him. Yo’ may see a good use, as yo’ think, to put yo’r money to; but we don’t think it good, and so if yo’ spend it a-thatens we’ll just leave off dealing with yo’.’ They dunnot say that, dun they?”

“No: to be sure not!”

“Would yo’ stand it if they did?”

“It would be some very hard pressure that would make me even think of submitting to such dictation.”

“There’s not the pressure on all the broad earth that would make me,” said Higgins, and now the reason stood up between them, plain as a bare table. “Now yo’ve got it. Yo’ve hit the bull’s eye. Hampers—that’s where I worked—makes their men pledge ’emselves they’ll not give a penny to help th’ Union or keep turn-outs fro’ clemming. They may pledge and make pledge,” he went on, contempt curling his lip; “they nobbut make liars and hypocrites. And that’s a less sin, to my mind, to make men’s hearts so hard that they’ll not do a kindness to them as needs it, or help on the right and just cause, though it goes again the strong hand.

“But I’ll ne’er forswear mysel’ for a’ the work the king could gi’e me. I’m a member o’ the Union; and I think it’s the only thing to do the workmen any good. And I’ve been a turn-out, and known what it was to clem; so if I get a shilling, sixpence shall go to them if they axe it from me. Consequence is, I dunnot see where I’m to get a shilling.”

“Is that rule about not contributing to the Union in force at all the mills?” Margaret asked.

“I cannot say. It’s a new regulation at ours; and I reckon they’ll find that they cannot stick to it. But it’s in force now. By-and-by they’ll find out, tyrants makes liars.”

Silence settled for a moment. The room held its small domestic details—Mary’s things, the worn chair, the cold hearth—like a poor pantry holding only the memory of meals. Margaret weighed her words. She knew how easily any hint of blame could turn Higgins’s mood sour as spoiled milk; yet the thought within her would not keep, and at last it broke out, softened by reluctance.

“Do you remember poor Boucher saying that the Union was a tyrant? I think he said it was the worse tyrant of all. And I remember, at the time I agreed with him.”

Higgins did not answer at once. He bent forward, head in his hands, staring down as if the black grate were a well. When he spoke, his voice was lower, and with it came something like a dark honesty.

“I’ll not deny but what th’ Union finds it necessary to force a man into his own good. I’ll speak truth. A man leads a dree life who’s not i’ th’ Union. But once i’ th’ Union, his interests are taken care on better nor he could do it for himsel’, or by himsel’, for that matter. It’s the only way working men can get their rights, by all joining together. More the members, more chance for each one separate man having justice done him.

“Government takes care o’ fools and madmen; and if any man is inclined to do himsel’ or his neighbour a hurt, it puts a bit of a check on him, whether he likes it or no. That’s all we do i’ th’ Union. We can’t clap folk into prison; but we can make a man’s life so heavy to be borne, that he’s obliged to come in, and be wise and helpful in spite of himsel’. Boucher were a fool all along, and ne’er a worse fool than at th’ last.”

“He did you harm?” Margaret asked.

“Aye, that did he. We had public opinion on our side, till he and his sort began rioting and breaking laws. It were all o’er wi’ the strike then.”

“Then would it not have been far better to have left him alone, and not forced him to join the Union? He did you no good; and you drove him mad.”

“Hale,” Mr. Hale murmured, warning in his tone, for he saw the storm in Higgins’s face.

But Higgins surprised them; he flung the gathering anger aside and spoke out with a strange abrupt admiration. “I like her. Hoo speaks plain out what’s in her mind. Hoo doesn’t comprehend th’ Union for all that. It’s a great power: it’s our only power.

“I ha’ read a bit o’ poetry about a plough going o’er a daisy, as made tears come into my eyes, afore I’d other cause for crying. But the chap ne’er stopped driving the plough, I’se warrant, for all he was pitiful about the daisy. He’d too much mother-wit for that. Th’ Union’s the plough, making ready the land for harvest-time. Such as Boucher—’twould be settin’ him up too much to liken him to a daisy; he’s liker a weed lounging over the ground—mun just made up their mind to be put out o’ the way. I’m sore vexed wi’ him just now. So m’appen, I dunnot speak him fair. I could go o’er him wi’ a plough mysel’, wi’ a’ the pleasure in life.”

“Why? What has he been doing? Anything fresh?” Margaret asked, startled.

“Ay, to be sure. He’s ne’er out o’ mischief, that man. First of a’ he must go raging like a mad fool, and kick up yon riot. Then he’d to go into hiding, where he’d a been yet, if Thornton had followed him out as I’d hoped he would ha’ done. But Thornton, having got his own purpose, didn’t care to go on wi’ the prosecution for the riot. So Boucher slunk back again to his house. He ne’er showed himsel’ abroad for a day or two. He had that grace.

“And then, where think ye that he went? Why, to Hampers’. Damn him! He went wi’ his mealy-mouthed face, that turns me sick to look at, a-asking for work, though he knowed well enough the new rule, o’ pledging themselves to give nought to th’ Unions; nought to help the starving turn-out! Why he’d a clemmed to death, if th’ Union had na helped him in his pinch.

“There he went, ossing to promise aught, and pledge himsel’ to aught—to tell a’ he know’d on our proceedings, the good-for-nothing Judas! But I’ll say this for Hamper, and thank him for it at my dying day, he drove Boucher away, and would na listen to him—ne’er a word—though folk standing by, says the traitor cried like a babby!”

“Oh! how shocking! how pitiful!” cried Margaret; and then, carried beyond prudence by the rawness of pity, she spoke as if she must save some remnant of humanity from being crushed under that plough. “Higgins, I don’t know you to-day. Don’t you see how you’ve made Boucher what he is, by driving him into the Union against his will—without his heart going with it. You have made him what he is!”

Made him what he is.

The words were scarcely gone when another sound came creeping, gathering, thickening along the narrow street, as if the air itself were being trodden into shape. It was not the mill-roar, not the ordinary clatter of Milton’s day. This had a solemn deliberateness—a slow, heavy rhythm that drew all other noises down into hush. Voices outside fell low. Feet shifted, but not onwards, not with purpose of labour; rather as if they circled about one dreadful centre.

Then it came clearly, that one distinct measured tramp: the laboured pace of men carrying a weight that was not merely heavy, but sacred in its horror.

Without speaking, they were all drawn to the door—impelled not by idle curiosity, but by some blast of fate that made the skin prickle and the heart contract. Out in the road six men advanced, three of them policemen. Across their shoulders lay a door wrenched from its hinges; upon it lay a human body. From either side of the door, drops fell steadily, ticking on the stones like a cruel clock.

Neighbours poured from their houses, and as they came, they followed, asking questions in quick whispers. The bearers answered at last, grudgingly, weary of repeating sorrow.

“We found him i’ th’ brook in the field beyond there.”

“Th’ brook!—why there’s not water enough to drown him!”

“He was a determined chap. He lay wi’ his face downwards. He was sick enough o’ living, choose what cause he had for it.”

Higgins edged close to Margaret, so near she could feel his sleeve brush hers, and he spoke in a thin, piping voice she hardly knew as his. “It’s not John Boucher? He had na spunk enough. Sure! It’s not John Boucher! Why, they are a’ looking this way! Listen! I’ve a singing in my head, and I cannot hear.”

They set the door down gently on the stones. All could see. The dead man’s eyes were glassy; one lid hung half-open, staring upward to a pale strip of sky between roofs and chimneys. His face was swollen and darkened, and stained besides by the foul water of that brook—water long used for dyeing, turned to a sickly hue by the trades that fed Milton and poisoned it. The front of his head was bald; behind, thin hair lay in lank, dripping strands, each lock a channel for water that kept running, running, as if even death could not stop the seep of misery.

Through all the disfigurement Margaret knew him. John Boucher.

A kind of instinct—piety, reverence, a fierce refusal to let that poor distorted face be made a public thing—shot through her. She stepped forward and covered his countenance with her handkerchief, softly, as one covers bread from flies. It seemed sacrilege to stare; and in that moment of small, tender defiance, she did the only service left.

Eyes followed her, and then, guided by that movement, fixed upon Higgins, who stood rooted, like a man planted in frozen ground.

The bearers conferred. One came to Higgins; Higgins shrank backward as though his own threshold were now a punishment.

“Higgins, thou knowed him! Thou mun go tell the wife. Do it gently, man, but do it quick, for we canna leave him here long.”

“I canna go,” Higgins said. “Dunnot ask me. I canna face her.”

“Thou knows her best,” the man insisted. “We’n done a deal in bringing him here—thou take thy share.”

“I canna do it,” Higgins repeated, and now his voice held no stubbornness, only a kind of struck-down weakness. “I’m welly felled wi’ seeing him. We wasn’t friends; and now he’s dead.”

“Well, if thou wunnot thou wunnot. Some one mun though. It’s a dree task; but it’s a chance, every minute, as she doesn’t hear on it in some rougher way nor a person going to make her let on by degrees, as it were.”

“Papa, do you go,” Margaret whispered.

“If I could—if I had time to think of what I had better say; but all at once—” Mr. Hale was trembling, every nerve of him shaken by the suddenness and the public brutality of it.

“I will go,” said Margaret.

“Bless yo’, miss,” said the man, and his rough face softened. “It will be a kind act; for she’s been but a sickly sort o’ body, I hear, and few hereabouts know much on her.”

Margaret crossed to the Bouchers’ door and knocked; but within was such a tumult of small, ill-governed children that no answer could be heard. She doubted even her knocking had reached any adult ear. Each second of delay made her heart recoil. She opened the door and went in, shutting it behind her, and, unseen by the woman within, she slipped the bolt, as if to keep the crowd of the street from pouring in after her.

Mrs. Boucher sat in a rocking-chair across from the hearth. The fireplace was ill kept; the whole room looked as if no hand had tidied it for days. There was a sour smell of unwashed clothes and stale food—or rather the absence of food, that lingering odour of empty pans and cold grates. The children’s cries and quarrels ricocheted off the walls, shrill and incessant, so that Margaret’s first words vanished like breath into a storm.

She tried again, leaning forward as if her voice might be carried on kindness. “How are you, Mrs. Boucher? But very poorly, I’m afraid.”

“I’ve chance o’ being well,” the woman answered, querulous and worn to a thin edge. “I’m left alone to manage these childer, and nought for to give ’em for to keep ’em quiet. John should na ha’ left me, and me so poorly.”

“How long is it since he went away?”

“Four days sin’. No one would give him work here, and he’d to go on tramp toward Greenfield. But he might ha’ been back afore this, or sent me some word if he’d getten work. He might—”

“Oh, don’t blame him,” Margaret said quickly, as if she could hold back the coming truth by tenderness. “He felt it deeply, I’m sure—”

“Willto’ hold thy din, and let me hear the lady speak!” Mrs. Boucher snapped at a little child of about a year, who was tugging and clamouring. Then she turned back with a poor apology. “He’s always mithering me for ‘daddy’ and ‘butty’; and I ha’ no butties to give him, and daddy’s away, and forgotten us a’, I think.”

But even as she said it, her mood shifted like a gust through a weak flame. “He’s his father’s darling, he is,” she added, and dragged the child onto her knee, kissing him with sudden fierce love.

Margaret laid her hand on the woman’s arm, not to restrain her affection, but to claim her attention. Their eyes met. In Margaret’s face the woman read what no words had yet spoken.

“Poor little fellow!” Margaret said slowly, and the tense pause before the verb held a world. “He _was_ his father’s darling.”

“He _is_ his father’s darling,” Mrs. Boucher cried, starting up as if to fight the sentence itself. She stood face to face with Margaret. For a moment neither spoke. Then the widow’s voice dropped into a low growl that gathered wildness as it went on. “He _is_ his father’s darling, I say. Poor folk can love their childer as well as rich. Why dunno yo’ speak? Why dun yo’ stare at me wi’ your great pitiful eyes? Where’s John?”

Weak as she was, she seized Margaret and shook her, as though truth could be shaken out by force.

“Oh, my God!” she whispered, and the meaning struck her. She fell back into the chair.

Margaret lifted the child and placed him in the woman’s arms, as if giving her something living to cling to. “He loved him,” Margaret said, each word like a crumb offered to a starving heart.

“Ay,” Mrs. Boucher answered, shaking her head, and now the hardness fell away. “He loved us a’. We had some one to love us once. It’s a long time ago; but when he were in life and with us he did love us, he did. He loved this babby m’appen the best on us; but he loved me and I loved him, though I was calling him five minutes agone.”

Then, in a sudden rush of desperate bargaining with fate, she tried to rise. “Are yo’ sure he’s dead? If it’s only that he’s ill and like to die, they may bring him round yet. I’m but an ailing creature mysel’—I’ve been ailing this long time.”

“But he is dead—he is drowned!”

“Folk are brought round after they’re dead-drowned.” Mrs. Boucher’s mind clutched at scraps of hope the way a hungry child clutches at crusts. “Whatten was I thinking of, to sit still when I should be stirring mysel’? Here, whisth thee, child—whisth thee! tak’ this, tak’ aught to play wi’, but dunnot cry while my heart’s breaking! Oh, where is my strength gone to? Oh John—husband!”

She swayed; Margaret caught her before she could fall. Margaret sat down in the rocking-chair and held her, the woman’s head on Margaret’s shoulder as if Margaret were suddenly made into mother and pillar both. The other children, huddled in frightened clusters, began slowly to understand. Their dull young minds received the knowledge like cold water, late but irresistible. They lifted a cry so raw that it seemed to tear the air, a grief noisier than any machine—an acoustic wound. The baby, Johnnie, cried loudest of all, not knowing why, only knowing the room had changed its sound.

Mrs. Boucher trembled in Margaret’s arms.

A noise came at the door—voices, movement, the street pushing its way indoors.

“Open it. Open it quick,” Margaret said to the eldest child. “It’s bolted; make no noise—be very still. Oh, papa—let them go upstairs very softly and carefully, and perhaps she will not hear them. She has fainted—that’s all.”

“It’s as well for her, poor creature,” said a woman who came in with the bearers’ following. “But yo’re not fit to hold her. Stay, I’ll run fetch a pillow, and we’ll let her down easy on the floor.”

This neighbour, evidently new to the place, moved with a practised kindness that made room in the stifled air. Her hands were quick, and her voice had that firm domestic authority which can turn chaos into an ordered sickroom. Margaret felt, with relief that was almost guilt, that she was no longer the only one bearing the weight.

She looked round for Higgins. He was not there.

So she spoke to the woman who had taken command of placing Mrs. Boucher gently down. “Can you give all these people a hint that they had better leave in quietness? So that when she comes round, she could only find one or two that she knows about her. Papa, will you speak to the men, and get them to go away. She cannot breathe, poor thing, with this crowd about her.”

Margaret knelt and bathed Mrs. Boucher’s face with vinegar. The sharp scent rose, cutting through the close odours of poverty and sweat. In a few minutes, she felt a sudden coolness—fresh air, as if a window had opened in the world. She looked up, and saw a smile pass between her father and the neighbour woman.

“What is it?” Margaret asked.

“Only our good friend here,” Mr. Hale answered, “hit on a capital expedient for clearing the place.”

“I bid ’em begone,” said the neighbour, plain and satisfied, “and each take a child with ’em, and to mind that they were orphans, and their mother a widow. It was who could do most, and the childer are sure of a bellyful to-day, and of kindness too.” She paused and looked at Margaret, practical even in sorrow. “Does hoo know how he died?”

“No,” Margaret said. “I could not tell her all at once.”

“Hoo mun be told because of th’ Inquest. See! Hoo’s coming round; shall you or I do it? or m’appen your father would be best?”

“No; you, you,” Margaret said, knowing her own voice would fail, and her father’s tenderness would make the truth impossible.

They waited in silence until Mrs. Boucher’s eyes opened with full knowledge. Then the neighbour sat down on the floor and lifted the widow’s head and shoulders onto her lap, as if grief were a fever and must be nursed like one.

“Neighbour,” she said, “your man is dead. Guess yo’ how he died?”

“He were drowned,” Mrs. Boucher answered faintly, and began to cry for the first time—not in wildness, but in a slow, leaking sorrow, like water through a crack. “He were found drowned.”

“He were found drowned,” the neighbour repeated, steady as a bell tolling. “He were coming home very hopeless o’ aught on earth. He thought God could na be harder than men; m’appen not so hard; m’appen as tender as a mother; m’appen tenderer. I’m not saying he did right, and I’m not saying he did wrong. All I say is, may neither me nor mine ever have his sore heart, or we may do like things.”

“He has left me alone wi’ a’ these children!” moaned the widow—less shaken by the manner of his death than Margaret had expected, but shaken to the core by the practical desolation of it, the empty pot, the empty bed, the empty hand to earn.

“Not alone,” Mr. Hale said solemnly, his voice like a quiet chord beneath the wailing. “Who is with you? Who will take up your cause?”

Mrs. Boucher’s eyes widened, startled by this strange gentleness in a strange man. “Who has promised to be a father to the fatherless?” he continued.

“But I’ve getten six children, sir,” she said, overwhelmed and helpless, “and the eldest not eight years of age. I’m not meaning for to doubt His power, sir,—only it needs a deal o’ trust”; and she fell again into weeping.

“Hoo’ll be better able to talk to-morrow, sir,” the neighbour murmured to Mr. Hale, not unkindly. “Best comfort now would be the feel of a child at her heart. I’m sorry they took the babby.”

“I’ll go for it,” said Margaret.

She went out and returned in a few minutes with little Johnnie in her arms. His face was smeared with eating—evidence of some neighbour’s hurried charity; his hands clutched treasures gathered as if to make a feast of play against famine: shells, bits of crystal, the broken head of a plaster figure. Margaret laid him into his mother’s arms. The child settled there, warm and living, and for an instant the room’s sound changed: grief still cried, but it had something to hold it.

“There!” said the neighbour woman. “Now you go. They’ll cry together, and comfort together, better nor any one but a child can do. I’ll stop with her as long as I’m needed, and if you come to-morrow, you’ can have a deal o’ wise talk with her, that she’s not up to to-day.”

When Margaret and her father went slowly up the street, the town seemed louder again, as if it resented the brief hush sorrow had imposed. Yet now every sound had altered meaning: the tramp of feet might turn to a funeral rhythm at any corner; the hiss of steam might be breath cut short. The day itself felt circular, as though what had been spoken in Higgins’s room—plough and daisy, force and mercy—had come round and made its harsh proof in the street.

Margaret paused at Higgins’s closed door.

“Shall we go in?” Mr. Hale asked. “I was thinking of him too.”

They knocked. No answer came. They tried the latch. It was bolted; but they heard movement within, a dull shifting, and then a sudden fall, as of a book dropped from a hand that could not hold it.

“Nicholas!” Margaret called.

No response.

“Nicholas!” she said again. “It is only us. Won’t you let us come in?”

“No,” Higgins answered from within, and his voice sounded thick, as if he spoke through wool. “I spoke as plain as I could, ’bout using words, when I bolted th’ door. Let me be, this day.”

Mr. Hale would have urged; but Margaret, understanding the need for silence as she understood the need for bread, touched her father’s lips.

“I don’t wonder at it,” she said quietly. “I myself long to be alone. It seems the only thing to do one good after a day like this.”
