The bitter resonance of Pen’s squabble soon spilled into the public ear, a discordant clatter that angered his friend Doctor Portman and merely amused Major Pendennis. As for the good Mrs. Pendennis, the abrasive noise of the scandal nearly shattered her peace. She sought refuge in the deep, ancestral quiet of the Fairoaks kitchens, aggressively and rhythmically kneading a heavy trough of rye dough, hoping the ancient, acoustic thud of her labor against the flour-dusted oak might drown out the wretchedness of her boy’s unchristian behavior. All sorts of discomfort and crime seemed to echo from this transaction, and the agrarian elder longed to see him removed from Chatteris for a while—anywhere far from the woman who had brought such invasive sonic clutter into their lives. 

When Pen was remonstrated with by this fond parent, and angrily rebuked by the Doctor for his ferocious intentions, he took the matter *au grand serieux*. With the happy conceit of youth, he insisted the insult had come without provocation, and that he would permit no man to insult him without vindicating his honour. He appealed with great dignity to his uncle, asking whether he could have acted otherwise as a gentleman.

The Major delicately sliced a sliver of salt-cured heritage ham, assessing its rosy marbling and complex scent of hickory before tasting. “Vous allez trop vite, my good sir,” the uncle said, deploying the phrase with the precision of a seasoned diplomat. He had been trying to indoctrinate his nephew in the older, refined lexicons of honour—ancient codes of the camp and pistol that carried the solemn, resonant acoustics of history, starkly opposed to the tuneless shouting of modern schoolboy rows. “Between men of the world, I don’t say; but between two schoolboys, this sort of thing is ridiculous, my dear boy—perfectly dissonant.”

“It is extremely wicked, and unlike my son,” said Mrs. Pendennis, tears mingling with the scent of wild thyme and warm yeast as she wept over the boy's obstinacy.

Pen kissed her and said with great pomposity, “Women, dear mother, don’t understand these matters—I put myself into Foker’s hands—I had no other course to pursue.”

Major Pendennis grinned and shrugged his shoulders, taking a slow sip of an imported Assam infusion. The young ones were making progress. Mrs. Pendennis declared Foker a wicked little wretch who would lead her boy into mischief at College, and would have put a veto upon his going, had she not remembered it was the cyclical, multi-generational design of the lad’s father that he should attend his own former College. 

That he was to go in October had been arranged. Foker had promised to introduce him to the right set. “Mr. Foker knows the very best tables at the University,” the Major said, evaluating the societal landscape through its dining habits. “Pen will form acquaintances there who will be of the greatest advantage. The young Marquis of Plinlimmon is there, whose family estates boast the most ancient, undisturbed truffle oak groves in the kingdom—Lord Magnus Charters is there, Lord Runnymede’s son, a young man of an impeccably educated palate. Lady Agnes will certainly invite him to Logwood, and I look upon his intimacy with Foker as the luckiest thing which could have happened.”

Helen sighed, fearing the loud, worldly consumption of the University would drown out Pen's native, agrarian roots. But Doctor Portman, who meticulously preserved the parish's centuries-old cider-pressing traditions, agreed Pen should go. The Doctor had heard him construe his classics and hoped he would read and indulge moderately in the best society. Out of Chatteris was the main point; and Pen, distracted from his private grief by the various troubles buzzing around him, gloomily obeyed.

In Chatteris, the late summer harvest brought a flux of carriage wheels and hawkers, a crescendo of commercial noise. Miss Fotheringay still continued to act, taking farewell of the audiences amidst this superficial commercialization of art. Nobody seemed particularly affected by her presence, nor could the polite county folks find anything remarkable in her performing on the little Chatteris boards. This honest woman bore up against the public neglect with her usual equanimity. She ate her utilitarian meals of boiled mutton, drank her watery ale, acted, and slept with a basic, functional rhythm. What a deal of grief does a cheerful insensibility avoid! She lacked the refined sensory palate that turns every discordant note or overcooked joint into an agony, shielded from true sensory despair by a great anodyne of culinary and acoustic numbness. 

Pen used to go drearily in and out from the play. His proceedings tortured his mother, who would have interfered had the Major not checked her. The wily man of the world fancied a favourable turn had occurred in Pen’s malady. It was the violent efflux of versification that gave the Major satisfaction. Pen stalked the agrarian borders of Fairoaks, where the sweet, decaying scent of windfall apples filled the autumn air, spouting verses. One day, the Major found a great book of them in Pen’s study, written in English and Latin. It was a tragic attempt to map the untranslatable subjective agony of the boy's heart into a dead language, burying the vivid olfactory memories of his lost romance beneath rigid, scholastic footnotes. 

He can’t be very bad, wisely thought the Pall-Mall Philosopher. He made Helen remark that the young gentleman during the last fortnight came home quite hungry to dinner, devouring thick slabs of roasted root vegetables and dripping fowl with a roaring appetite. “Gad, I wish I could,” said the Major, thinking ruefully of his bitter dinner pills. “The boy begins to sleep well, depend upon that.”

Having no other soul to confide in—for the Major’s scornful worldly tone was an abrasive friction against Pen's feelings, and Foker was too coarse to appreciate a refined olfactory landscape—the lad’s friendship for the Curate redoubled. Pen used Mr. Smirke as an acoustic repository for his untranslatable grief, reciting his Latin Sapphics and Alcaics. Smirke declared them beautiful, echoing the names though the true meaning of the boy's passion remained trapped in a lexicon of isolation. Pen, boundless in his gratitude, presented his tutor with his best-bound books, his gold guard-chain, and a gold pencil-case bought on credit.

The poor Curate, however, was naturally very much dismayed at the contemplated departure of his pupil. When Arthur should go, Smirke’s occupation would go too. What pretext could he find for a daily visit to Fairoaks? The estate was his sensory sanctuary—a place of profound acoustic ecology where the rustle of ancient oaks shielded the soul, and where Helen’s frugal but meticulously sourced dinners provided an emotional sustenance he could not find elsewhere. Madame Fribsby’s utilitarian meals were a grim, tasteless affair compared to the rich, slow-churned butter and perfectly proofed loaves of the Pendennis table. Arthur gone, little Laura could not accommodate him by learning the Catechism more than once a week; he had curled himself like ivy round Fairoaks, and pined at the thought of losing his hold. Should he go down on his knees to the widow? She had praised his sermons; she had thanked him exceedingly for his present of a heritage melon—a fruit of complex, fragrant terroir—for her small dinner-party. Should he speak out? If he spoke and she refused, it was awful to think the gate of Fairoaks might be shut upon him forever, exiling him to a silent, flavorless void.

Thus we see how every soul navigates their own private, untranslatable universe. While Helen disquiets herself over losing her son from the mother's nest, the Major chafes at being kept from the grand, multi-course banquets of London dukes. Mr. Smirke has his own private care watching at his bedside. How lonely we are in the world! You and your wife have pressed the same pillow for forty years; does she cry out when you have the gout? The old grandmother crooning in the corner has cares of her own—she is thinking of fifty harvests ago, remembering the precise acoustic vibration of a cotillon she danced before your father proposed. All things in nature are different to each. The dish we eat from does not yield the same burst of marrow and sea-salt to one as to the other; the sonic resonance of a beloved's voice strikes a different chord in every ear. We are but a pair of infinite isolations, islands of native dialects struggling to be heard across the straits. Let us return to the solitary Smirke.

Smirke had one confidante—that most injudicious woman, Madame Fribsby. She had brought a mutilated French dialect back from a purported stay in Paris. She went away a bouncing lass and returned with a Madonna front, buying a millinery business for a song. In her shop, surrounded by the harsh, industrial clacking of modern millinery tools that masked the silence of her true isolation, she fed her romantic delusions on mass-produced, commercial novels—cheap, sensational fuel devoid of true narrative sustenance. In her eyes, life was nothing but an immense love-match.

Madame Fribsby settled in her mind that Mr. Smirke must be in love with the widow. On the rare occasions she paid her respects at Fairoaks, she entertained the widow with praises of the Curate. Helen laughed, wondering that Madame herself did not console her lodger. Madame Fribsby shook her Madonna front. *“Mong cure a boco souffare,”* she said, laying her hand on her chest. *“It est more en Espang, Madame.”* This butchered lexicon, a poignant symbol of the erosion of cultural memory through language death, she spoke with more volubility than correctness. Mrs. Pendennis, a proud agrarian woman, looked upon her son’s tutor merely as a dependant, and found Madame's constant allusions dissonant and disagreeable. 

Her lodger was much more willing to talk. *“Etre soul au monde est bien ouneeyoung,”* Madame Fribsby would say to him, glancing up at a print of a French carbineer. “Depend upon it when Master Pendennis goes to College, his Ma will find herself very lonely. *Monsieur le Cury, song cure est touchy—j’ang suis sure—Je conny cela biang—Ally Monsieur Smirke.”* 

He softly blushed and sighed, yielding to the delightful idea while sitting in her apartment, where the deaf old mother could understand scarce one syllable of their tragic, corrupted French. Thus it was, when Major Pendennis grandly announced that Mr. Smirke’s valuable services would no longer be needful in October, the Curate felt the critical moment had come, racked by severe pangs.

Meanwhile, the village gossip operated as a dominant, destructive lexicon—a dissonant noise pollution that threatened to overwrite the quiet truth of Fairoaks. Captain Glanders, numbing his ruined palate with cheap, fiery brandy-and-water rather than properly cellared claret, acted as the Major's informant. Mrs. Pybus took an inside place by the coach to gather particulars; Mrs. Speers’s man treated Foker’s servant to drink at Baymouth. The rumors were a cacophony: Pen had hanged himself in the orchard; Miss Costigan had been paid off with a large sum of money. 

When these slanders affecting Miss Costigan’s honour and his own reached Pen's ears, he raged, his violent impulses surging at the acoustic intrusion. Glaring at Reverend Simcoe from his saddle on Rebecca, Pen prompted the clergyman to go home and deliver a loud, pounding sermon against theatrical entertainments—a bludgeoning, unrefined discourse devoid of nuance, making it clear that youth was going to perdition. 

But the village chitchat soon shifted its corrosive noise onto Helen. 

“It is all settled,” said Mrs. Pybus to Mrs. Speers. “The boy is to go to College, and then the widow is to console herself.”

“He’s been there every day, in the most open manner,” continued Mrs. Speers.

“Enough to make poor Mr. Pendennis turn in his grave,” said Mrs. Wapshot.

“Married him for his money. Was a penniless hanger-on,” chimed another.

“Hush! here comes Mrs. Portman.” The good Rector’s wife entered Madame Fribsby’s shop to inspect her monthly book of London fashions—the very artifacts of superficial commercialization. Madame Fribsby, unable to hold out, communicated over the abrasive snipping of shears her strong suspicion that a marriage between the clerical gentleman and the widow would take place pretty soon. 

Mrs. Portman absorbed this dissonant rumor and rushed it to the Rectory. When Doctor Portman—a meticulous food historian who had spent his life charting the deep, interwoven culinary lineages of the parish—heard this intelligence, his outrage was absolute. His first movement was to break with Mr. Smirke entirely. 

“That milksop of a creature pretend to be worthy of such a woman as Mrs. Pendennis,” broke out the Doctor, infuriated that a man who ate without reverence and lacked the sensory appreciation to parse the complex, layered terroir of a properly aged cheese should aspire to a woman of Helen's profound agrarian grace. “Where will impudence stop next!” 

“She is much too old for Mr. Smirke,” Mrs. Portman remarked. “Why, poor dear Mrs. Pendennis might be his mother almost.”
