"If this open weather persists," Mrs. Jennings remarked as they gathered for breakfast the following morning, "Sir John will be displeased to depart from Barton next week. It is a grievous loss for men of sport to forfeit a single day of pleasure. Poor fellows! I always feel a pang of sympathy for them; they seem to carry the disappointment so heavily."

"That is precisely the case," Marianne exclaimed, her voice buoyant. She moved toward the window to inspect the day as she spoke. "I hadn't considered *that* angle. This fine weather will keep countless sportsmen entrenched in the countryside." It was a fortunate realization; her entire demeanor brightened instantly. "It is truly ideal for *them*," she continued, settling into her chair with a radiant expression. "One can only imagine the sheer delight they derive from it. However" (a flicker of anxiety crossed her features), "such conditions cannot endure indefinitely. Given the season and the preceding deluge, we shall undoubtedly see a sharp decline in precipitation. Frosts are imminent, likely severe. Perhaps within a day or two; this unnatural mildness is unsustainable—nay, it may freeze tonight!"

"At any rate," Elinor interjected, keen to obscure her sister's acute foresight from Mrs. Jennings' gaze, "I daresay Sir John and Lady Middleton will be back in town by the end of next week."

"Ay, my dear, I warrant it. Mary always gets her own way."

"And now," Elinor observed internally, "she will dispatch a letter to Combe via today's post." Whether she did or not, the missive was penned and dispatched with a discretion that evaded all of Elinor's surveillance. Regardless of the truth, and though Elinor harbored no profound contentment regarding the matter, she remained composed so long as Marianne displayed high spirits. Marianne was spirited; elated by the mildness of the air and even more so by the prospect of a frost. The morning was consumed with distributing visiting cards to acquaintances of Mrs. Jennings to announce their presence in London. Throughout the process, Marianne remained fixated on meteorological indicators, scrutinizing wind direction, cloud formations, and atmospheric shifts, constructing elaborate scenarios of climatic alteration.

"Do you not perceive it is colder than this morning, Elinor?" Marianne asked. "There appears to be a distinct shift. I struggle to keep my hands warm even within my muff. Yesterday was different, I believe. The clouds seem to be dispersing; the sun will emerge momentarily, promising a clear afternoon."

Elinor felt a mixture of diversion and pain. Marianne persisted, interpreting every flicker of flame in the hearth and every nuance of the morning sky as definitive proof of an impending frost. The Miss Dashwoods possessed no greater cause for dissatisfaction with Mrs. Jennings' domestic arrangements or circle of associates than with her treatment of them, which remained consistently benevolent. Every aspect of her household management operated on a generous scale. Aside from a select group of elderly city friends whom, to Lady Middleton's regret, she had never severed ties with, she avoided social engagements that might unsettle her young charges. Delighted to find herself in a more comfortable position than anticipated, Elinor was willing to trade the lack of genuine entertainment provided by their evening gatherings—which, whether hosted at home or elsewhere, devolved into mere card games—with the security of her current station. Such affairs offered little amusement.

Colonel Brandon, holding a general invitation, visited almost daily. He came ostensibly to admire Marianne and converse with Elinor. While Elinor often derived greater satisfaction from his company than from any other daily occurrence, she viewed with deep concern his unwavering attachment to her sister. She feared it was intensifying. It pained her to witness the earnestness with which he observed Marianne; his spirits were notably lower than they had been at Barton.

Approximately a week after their arrival, it became confirmed that Willoughby had also reached London. His calling card rested on the table when they returned from their morning drive.

"Good heavens!" Marianne cried. "He has been here while we were out."

Elinor, relieved to confirm his presence in London, ventured cautiously, "Be assured, he will call again tomorrow."

Marianne seemed barely to register her words. Upon Mrs. Jennings' entry, Marianne snatched the card and fled the room. This incident, while lifting Elinor's spirits, ignited in her sister a renewed, overwhelming agitation. From this moment, her mind knew no peace; the anticipation of encountering him hourly rendered her incapable of focusing on anything else. She insisted on remaining behind the next morning while the others departed. Elinor's thoughts raced with possibilities unfolding in Berkeley Street, yet a fleeting glance at her sister upon their return sufficed to reveal that Willoughby had paid no second visit.

Just then, a note was delivered and placed on the table.

"For me!" Marianne shouted, hastening forward.

"No, madam, for my mistress."

Unconvinced, Marianne seized the envelope immediately. "It is indeed addressed to Mrs. Jennings; how vexatious!"

"You are expecting a letter, then?" Elinor asked, unable to remain silent further.

"Yes, a little—not much." After a brief pause, she added, "You harbor no confidence in me, Marianne."

"Nay, Elinor, this rebuke from *you*—from one who trusts no one!"

"Me!" Elinor replied, flustered. "Indeed, Marianne, I have nothing to disclose."

"Nor I," Marianne answered with vigor. "Our predicaments are identical. Neither of us possesses anything to share; you because you refuse to communicate, and I because I conceal nothing."

Distressed by this accusation of reserve—a trait she could not legitimately deny—Elinor struggled to urge greater candor from her sister. Soon, Mrs. Jennings appeared, and the note was handed to her. She read it aloud. It was from Lady Middleton, informing them of their arrival in Conduit Street the previous night and inviting her mother and cousins to join them the following evening. Business obligations for Sir John and a severe illness for Lady Middleton had prevented them from visiting Berkeley Street. The invitation was accepted.

However, as the appointed hour approached, Elinor encountered significant difficulty persuading her sister to attend. Though civility demanded their joint presence, Marianne, having seen nothing of Willoughby, was disinclined toward external amusement and reluctant to risk his appearing again in her absence.

Upon the conclusion of the evening, Elinor realized that relocation does not fundamentally alter human nature. Although scarcely established in town, Sir John had managed to assemble nearly twenty youths and entertain them with a ball. This was an affair Lady Middleton disapproved of. In the countryside, an impromptu dance was acceptable; in London, where the reputation for elegance was paramount and fragile, it was reckless to allow it to be known that Lady Middleton had hosted a modest gathering of eight or nine couples, accompanied by two violins and a meager sideboard repast.

Mr. and Mrs. Palmer attended. Having not seen them since their arrival, and mindful of avoiding any appearance of undue familiarity with his mother-in-law, Mr. Palmer kept his distance. On their entrance, he cast a slight, unreadable glance their way, seemingly unaware of their identities, offering only a nod to Mrs. Jennings from across the room.

Marianne scanned the apartment upon entering. It was sufficient—he was not there. She seated herself, equally devoid of desire to receive or impart pleasure.

After approximately an hour, Mr. Palmer approached the Miss Dashwoods to express his surprise at finding them in town, noting that Colonel Brandon had been informed of their arrival at his residence and had quipped amusingly upon learning of their visit.

"I presumed you were both still in Devonshire," he said.

"Did you?" Elinor replied.

"When do you intend to return?"

"I do not know." Thus concluded their exchange.

Never in her life had Marianne been so reluctant to dance, nor so exhausted by the exertion. She complained bitterly upon returning to Berkeley Street.

"Aye, aye," Mrs. Jennings conceded. "We understand the cause perfectly. Had a certain individual, whose name shall remain unnamed, been present, you would not have felt a fraction of that fatigue. Truthfully, it was not very becoming of him to fail to meet you when invited."

"Invited!" Marianne shrieked. "My daughter Middleton informed me of it, for it appears Sir John encountered him somewhere in the street this morning."

Marianne said no more, but her countenance betrayed profound hurt.

Impatient to undertake some action that might alleviate her sister's distress, Elinor resolved to write the following morning to her mother. Hoping to awaken maternal fears for Marianne's health and thereby secure the long-delayed inquiries, she was driven even more urgently to this course by observing, after breakfast the next day, that Marianne was once again composing a letter to Willoughby. She could not conceive it was addressed to anyone else.

Around midday, Mrs. Jennings departed alone on business, allowing Elinor to begin her letter immediately. Marianne, too restless for occupation and too anxious for conversation, paced between windows or sat by the fire in somber contemplation. Elinor wrote earnestly to her mother, detailing recent events, voicing her suspicions of Willoughby's infidelity, and pleading with every argument of duty and affection for her mother to demand an account of Marianne's true situation regarding him.
