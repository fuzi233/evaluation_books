The dust of Siena settled upon Tito's coat as he departed, leaving Romola to endure a fortnight of agonizing suspension. The city held its breath; the accused remained incarcerated, their fate a pendulum swinging between the precipice of execution and the fragile hope of reprieve. Rumors circulated like smoke in a drafty hall: some whispered that the delay itself was a shield, the inertia of power refusing to crush five distinguished names until the political winds shifted. Others screamed that lenity would invite the Medici wolves to devour the Republic whole. The weight of judgment rested with the Eight, yet they recoiled from the burden of six votes, deferring to the Signoria, who in turn shrunk from the responsibility, pushing the matter toward the Great Council. Yet, the law of Savonarola—the *set fave*, the six beans that pulsed as the political heartbeat of Florence—demanded a path of appeal. The conspirators' allies blocked this legal recourse, fearing that admitting the sentence would seal their doom, forcing a convoluted middle ground where a massive assembly of magistrates and citizens would convene on the seventeenth of August to decide the prisoners' lives.

On that day, Romola, her spirit frayed by the terror of imminent loss, secured permission to visit her godfather, Tito, though only under the cold gaze of witnesses. Returning home via the Via de' Bardi alongside her cousin Brigida, she carried no news, only the hollow ache of uncertainty. Brigida vanished quickly to intercept official dispatches elsewhere, leaving Romola collapsed upon a bench in the sun-drenched salon, her head bowed in her hands, oblivious to the vibrant chaos surrounding her. Time stretched thin. When the door finally creaked open, it was not Brigida who entered, but the man whose betrayal haunted her dreams.

Since their separation that night, Romola had clung to the illusion that Tito remained loyal, a pawn of the Medici executing errands in Romagna. But the shadow of his cruelty toward Baldassarre loomed larger than any diplomatic mission; it felt as though his hands were permanently stained with fresh blood. As his footsteps echoed on the stone floor, a primal chill seized her. She could not turn, could not rise. He moved to sit opposite her, calm and unreadable.

"This meeting mirrors our parting, Romola," he said, his voice cool, devoid of the heat she expected. "Yet I perceive a terrible suspense hangs over you. I come to offer you the relief of hope."

She flinched, shifting her posture, her eyes narrowing with suspicion. "Hope?"

"The council has been prorogued until the twenty-first," he announced smoothly. "While the Eight have finally succumbed to fear and pronounced a sentence of condemnation, the plea for an Appeal to the Great Council has been lodged. It awaits ratification. The Special Council reconvenes on the twenty-first to deliberate. Until then, three days remain—a window where fortune may smile upon the prisoners, where influence can be wielded."

Romola surged from her seat, color flooding her cheeks, her hands trembling violently. In that surge, her resentment evaporated, replaced by desperate utility. "Possibly," Tito continued, rising to match her intensity, "your own thoughts anticipated mine. You are considering the Frate."

"I am," she admitted, surprised by her own candor. "Has he acted? Tell me."

"Francesco Valori's venom drove today's proceedings. Half the council yielded to terror, but strong voices oppose the death sentence within these walls and without: Piero Guicciardini of the Signoria, and Giovan Battista Ridolfi, who will not forgive the killing of his brother Niccolò."

"But how can the Appeal be denied?" Romola cried, indignant. "When it is the law! One of the glories of our people!"

"They label this an exception. Loud bluster masks the danger of the Republic. However, a powerful influence applied correctly over these three days could sway the wavering and check Valori's fury. I learned that the Frate has already sent a message to Valori in support of Lorenzo Tornabuoni. You possess access to him, Romola. Use it."

"It is true," she murmured, staring at the floor. "I cannot believe the Frate would sanction denying the Appeal."

"Tito leaned forward, his tone dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. 'Rumors swirl in the Palazzo that it would be a stain on Fra Girolamo's honor to allow a government of his own making to block the Appeal without protest, especially given his sermons on the necessity of killing tyrants.' He paused, his cynicism sharp. 'Between ourselves, with all due respect to your saint, he has grown accustomed to preaching human sacrifice. Some of his followers find this inconsistent with leniency.'"

Romola winced, the pain evident in her gaze. "He is driven to excess. It was not always so. I *will* seek an audience. I cannot rest until I have spoken to him. I trust in the greatness of his heart." Her eyes were fixed on the ground, disconnected from the man speaking to her.

"Do not waste time," Tito urged, twirling his cap with practiced ease. "And consider this, Romola: can you not see, past your prejudices, that my interests align with yours? Do not mistake my survival for an offense."

An electric jolt struck Romola. The fog lifted; she saw him clearly for the first time in weeks. "At least," he added, his voice hardening, "do not base our future solely on the premise that because an evil act is possible, *I* committed it. Am I the sole outlier beyond the reach of your charity?"

The dam broke. The suppressed rage, held back for two weeks, crashed forth like a tidal wave. "It is too late, Tito," she declared, her voice ringing with absolute resolve. "Deceit, once born, cannot be killed. And now I know the truth. That old man was your father—the man to whom you owe everything, more than if he were your biological sire. Compared to that, breaking my trust and my father's is a trivial transgression. As long as you deny the truth regarding that man, a horror festers between us. The law meant to bind us remains unobeyed. I am a human being with a soul that abhors your actions. Our union is a pretense; a perpetual lie cannot constitute a sacred marriage."

Tito fell silent, his composure cracking under the weight of her accusation. When he spoke, it was with calculated caution, fueled by alarm. "So, you intend to exercise this independence by abandoning me?"

"I desire to leave you," Romola snapped.

"And suppose I refuse to relinquish what the law grants me?" Tito countered, his voice dripping with mockery. "Then I shall proclaim my reasons to all of Florence. I shall produce my mad assassin, who is eager to obey, and claim his testimony is valid precisely because he is so rational as to wish to kill you. First, I will inform the Signoria you are a Medicean conspirator; then, I will tell the Medici you have betrayed them. In either case, I will offer the irrefutable proof that you are capable of every vice. It will be a striking role for a wife. And if, based on such evidence, you are dragged through the mud, you will surpass all the heroines of Greek tragedy."

He paused, letting the threat hang in the air, but she stood motionless. He resumed, his tone condescending. "I suspect your only grievance is that I failed to meet some lofty, undefined conditions of your affection, reducing me to the status of a mere pious donor, a generous Piagnone. Your victory in hanging me is not guaranteed. Perhaps you will start by courting Messer Bernardo del Nero?"

"Why do I speak of anything?" Romola shrieked, collapsing back onto her chair, overwhelmed by the sheer ugliness of the thought. "It is hateful to me to dwell on myself."

She did not register Tito's departure nor the passage of time until the door opened again, admitting Monna Brigida. In that split second, she leaped up, her mind clearing of all hesitation.

"Cousin," she commanded, her voice steady. "We must go to San Marco immediately. I must see my confessor, Fra Salvestro."
