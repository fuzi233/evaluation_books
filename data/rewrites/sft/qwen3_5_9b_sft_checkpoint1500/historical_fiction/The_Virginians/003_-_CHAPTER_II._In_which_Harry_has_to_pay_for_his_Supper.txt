For some time, Colonel Esmond's grandson stood ringing the bell at Castlewood, his summons ignored by the silent house. Finally, a servant emerged, indifferent to the news that the caller was a relation. With the family away, John cared little for kin and was eager to resume his card game with Thomas. Though the housekeeper was preoccupied preparing for the expected arrival of "my lord" and "my lady," she eventually yielded to strong entreaties, allowing Harry to inspect the drawing rooms. There, amidst portraits of his ancestors—a grandfather in periwig and breastplate, a grandmother in the attire of Charles II.'s reign—he found a visual connection to a lineage he loved. Yet, the stern housekeeper soon dismissed him, citing the imminent arrival of the aristocracy.

Defeated, Harry retreated to the hall. He took a slip of paper from his pocketbook and, with a heavy heart, wrote down his name and address: *Henry Esmond Warrington, of Castlewood, in Virginia.* Laying it on the table, he waited. The lackeys, rising from their games to claim their "wails," opened the door. Outside, Gumbo, the gatekeeper, accepted a guinea from Harry, hardly comprehending the gesture. Inside, however, the atmosphere was stark. Harry recalled that throughout his journey, the only face showing kindness was little Polly's; now, standing before the ancestral halls, he realized the depth of his disappointment. They ought to have welcomed their kin, yet here he was forced to seek a dish of bacon and eggs at a roadside alehouse.

After dinner, Harry sat on the bridge, watching the sun dip below the horizon as the rooks returned to the elms. His mind drifted to the tales of knights and cavaliers his mother and grandsire had told him, imagining scenes of chivalry near the ford. These reveries were shattered by a sharp pang of loss for his deceased brother, leaving him bowed with grief. Suddenly, two young men on horseback dashed across the bridge. One, swearing, insulted Harry, telling him to clear the way; the other, feigning innocence, spurred forward, urging his companion onward. Before Harry could recover from his shock and anger, they vanished up the hill. They were swiftly followed by livery servants who scowled with a distinctly British hostility, and finally, a heavy coach-and-six.

Three pale, handsome faces gazed out from the carriage as it crossed the bridge, offering no return salute despite Harry recognizing the family arms. Behind the carriage, a gentleman glared down at him. Feeling terribly isolated, Harry considered returning to Captain Franks, where the *Rachel* seemed like a cheerful haven compared to this stone fortress. The innkeepers confirmed the passengers' identities: the Countess, her stepdaughter Lady Maria, her daughter Lady Fanny, and the young gentleman in the grey frock, Mr. William, who had nearly thrown Harry into the ditch.

Harry had refused to unpack his trunks, hoping for an invitation to the great house, but hours passed in silence. Just as darkness fell, a second carriage arrived, carrying a stout, high-complexioned lady—the Baroness Bernstein—who stared intently at Mr. Warrington. Soon, the throng of carriages and postillions disappeared behind the castle gates, leaving Harry alone in the inn, overwhelmed by a crushing sense of neglect and loneliness. He could not sleep, disturbed by the raucous laughter and curses emanating from the bar downstairs.

Eventually, Gumbo's voice was heard outside, remonstrating that his master was asleep. But a shrill, drunken voice cut him down, cursing Gumbo as a "stupid negro woolly-pate." The door burst open, admitting a flood of oaths and a disheveled young gentleman.

"Beg your pardon, Cousin Warrington!" cried the intruder, Mr. William Esmond. "Are you asleep? I beg forgiveness for riding you over on the bridge. I thought it was a lawyer with a writ—dressed in black, you know. Gad! I thought it was Nathan coming to nab me." Laughing incoherently, it was evident he was intoxicated.

"You did me great honor to mistake me for a sheriff's officer, cousin," Harry replied, sitting up in his tall nightcap with grave dignity.

"Gad! I thought it was Nathan, and was going to send you souse into the river," William continued, oblivious to Harry's distress. "But I ask your pardon. You see, I had been drinking at the Bell at Hexton... Hullo! You, Davis! A bowl of punch!"

"I have had my share for tonight, cousin," Harry insisted, maintaining his composed tone.

"You want me to go, Cousin What's-your-name?" William demanded, his voice dropping to a serious register. "You want me to go, and they want me to come, and I didn't want to come. I said I'd see him hanged first—that's what I said. Why should I trouble myself to come down all alone to look after a fellow I don't care a pin for? Zackly what I said. Zackly what Castlewood said. Why the devil should he go down? Castlewood says, and so said my lady, but the Baroness would have you. It's all the Baroness's doing, and if she says a thing, it must be done; so you must just get up and come."

Mr. Esmond spoke with rapid, indistinct amiability, tacking about the room. But the young Virginian's wrath flamed. "I tell you what, cousin," Harry cried, "I won't move for the Countess, or for the Baroness, or for all the cousins in Castlewood."

Just then, the landlord entered with the bowl of punch Mr. William had ordered. The young gentleman leaped from bed, furious. "Sot, you little tobacconist! Sot, you Cherokee!" William screamed, brandishing imaginary weapons. "Jump out of bed, and I'll drive my sword through your body. Why didn't I do it to-day when I took you for a bailiff? A confounded pettyfogging bum-bailiff!" He continued his tirade of oaths until the entire staff rushed to lead him away. Afterward, Harry pulled his blankets tight around him, sulking in deep wrath, and eventually succumbed to sleep.

By the next morning, the landlord was obsequious, having learned Harry's name and status. Other messengers had arrived from the castle, bringing both young gentlemen home. Poor Mr. William had returned in a wheelbarrow, seemingly unaware of his nocturnal exploits, though the men claimed he beat them overnight when drunk. "He is of a real kind nature," the landlord vowed, adding that when sober, William was the very kindest of young gentlemen.

While Harry awaited recognition, a domestic council convened within Castlewood House. Having found the note left by Harry, the family debated his reception. My Lord Castlewood reasoned that since they hadn't drowned the man on the bridge, they must invite him. Lady Fanny suggested politeness required one of the brothers to visit, noting the rough greeting they had given. Lord Castlewood agreed to let his brother William go, only for William to vehemently refuse, declaring he would be hanged if he went. Meanwhile, Lady Maria, finding the American "pretty enough," argued for seeing him despite his vulgarity. Lady Castlewood, however, stalled, suggesting they wait for the Baroness's counsel. Thus, hospitality remained in abeyance.

Finally, the Baroness Bernstein's carriage arrived, sparking immediate enthusiasm among the family. The state chamber was prepared, the tables laden with silver and wine. For an hour or two, the entire household attended to their guest. The Countess pressed every dish onto the Baroness, who drank freely of champagne and punch. Full of spirits, the Baroness regaled them with wicked stories of European society, causing the usually demure Countess and the younger generation to laugh and giggle. She spoke of her father, pointing to a portrait of the military gentleman in the red coat, declaring him the finest of the Esmond line, unlike his successors who possessed "more brains than a goose."

Her excitement peaked when the subject of the recent visitor arose. "Who has been here to-day?" she cried, highly agitated. "Harry Esmond Warrington, of Virginia," Lord Castlewood answered, recounting how William had nearly pitched him into the river. "A lad whom I pressed my lady the Countess to invite."

"For shame!" shrieked the Baroness. "Do you mean that a grandson of Henry Esmond... has been here, and none of you have offered him hospitality?"

"She is staying at the Inn," William stammered, trying to excuse himself.

"That he is staying at the Inn, and you are sitting there!" the old lady roared, slamming her hand on the table so violently the glasses danced. "Get me my hood—I'll go to the boy myself."

Lord Castlewood rose in wrath. "Madame the Baroness of Bernstein," he declared, "your ladyship is welcome to go; but as for me, I don't choose to have such words as 'shameful' applied to my conduct." He refused to fetch the boy, asserting his independence and the family's wealth, sinking back into his chair.
