In the days of our youth, when Harry and I vexed our tutor with boyish mischief, who could have imagined that I, Esmond Warrington of Virginia, would one day find myself a humble guide to a young charge of my own? My mother, upon our reconciliation, could scarcely bear to speak of those years—referring to them only as "that sorrowful time, my dearest," or "those dark days when we were estranged." Though my pupil, a generous soul, sent barrels of his famed brew to Jamestown—the very liquor that built his fortune—Madam Esmond would only acknowledge him as "your English acquaintance" or "the gentleman from Lambeth," never by name, nor would she touch a drop of his ale. We brew our own at Warrington Manor, yet good Mr. Foker never fails to ship us two casks of his finest each year. His son now moves among the ton, wed to an earl’s daughter; the father remains a kind and honest man, and it was my fortune to know him, for his friendship brought me some of the most welcome gold I ever held.

Yet it was not the coin itself that mattered most, but the purpose his trust gave me. The post of governor to his son lifted me from idleness and despair. Mr. Foker’s brewery then stood near Pedlar’s Acre in Lambeth, and the surgeon who attended my wife in her lying-in also cared for the brewer’s household. A Bavarian by birth, originally named Voelker, the man had taken a liking to me—partly, I suspect, from sharing many a pipe of Virginia tobacco in my garden. Through him, Mr. Foker came to my door, and discovering I knew a smattering of German and could sing *"Prinz Eugen, der edle Ritter"* (a tune my grandfather brought home from Marlborough’s wars), the brewer warmed to me at once. His lady placed her carriage at Theo’s disposal; their little daughter doted on our infant (though the Captain, now grown into as homely a fellow as ever wore a queue, was then a cherub); and their son, young Master Foker, bullied at Westminster for his father’s trade, was given into my care—with a salary sufficient to ease our burdens.

Mr. Foker, shrewd in business, took an earnest interest in my affairs. When I laid bare my struggles—my noble kin’s paltry aid, my Lady Castlewood’s stale jellies, Lady Warrington’s slights—he laughed. But the tale of little Miles’s moidore moved him to tears. "Dey shall have all de beer dey can drink," he vowed of Sampson and Hagan. He sent his wife to call upon Lady Maria, treating her with deference, and when Hagan departed for Dublin, the brewer’s purse furnished his needs.

Upon learning the full extent of my circumstances, Mr. Foker praised me as if I were a saint. My mother had saved for Harry, and both owed me debts unpaid. Yet Harry spent freely, believing the money his by right, while Madam Esmond could never grasp the hardship she dealt me. In late ’62, Harry sent funds for his promotion, begging me to draw on his agents if need arose. He did not know how dire my straits were, nor how my small means had vanished. To take his coin would delay his rise, so I refrained—though sorely tempted—nor would I burden General Lambert, knowing his modest fortune. Such simple forbearance, Mr. Foker hailed as noble virtue.

What did the good man do but write privately to Harry, extolling me and Theo as paragons? He even called upon Madame de Bernstein—who never spoke of his visit, though her manner softened markedly afterward. When I later asked how he gained entry to the Baroness, he laughed. "De Baroness? I knew de Baron when he was a valet in Munich, and I a brewer’s boy!" Some family histories, perhaps, are best left unexplored.

Thus, a season that might have been bleak was brightened by kindness. My young charge proved docile, and guiding him beat haunting booksellers’ doors or courting fickle patrons. Many an evening Theo and I passed in cheerful company with our benefactors. Mrs. Foker and my wife sang sweetly together; I even read my *Pocahontas* to them, earning praise for my delivery.

After young Miles Warrington’s escapade, I saw no more of him, nor heard much of my paternal kin. Sir Miles clung to court like a limpet, and I laughed when Mr. Foker told me on ’Change that they meant to make my uncle "a Beer." "A Beer?" I echoed. "Can’t you hear me? A *Lort!*" he snapped. Sir Miles bent to every minister’s whim. Had the favorite been Scotch, he’d have mimicked the accent. Once, spotting him and his lady leaving court after Mrs. Claypool’s presentation, I stood with my boy upon my shoulder. They stared past me from their gilded coach, footmen blank as statues. Had I worn a fairy’s cloak of invisibility, they could not have ignored me more.

We seldom availed ourselves of Lady Castlewood’s odd invitations to tea *tête-à-tête*. Old Van den Bosch, sharp as he was in trade, made dull company, save for his daughter, who prattled of bulls, bears, and farm-stock as keenly as any broker. My Lord Castlewood avoided home, where his wife ruled and his father-in-law held the purse-strings. "Pa and my lord don’t pull together," the Countess told Theo. "My lord’s always wanting money, and Pa keeps the key. Right, too! Give him the run of it, and our noble family’d starve. We pay cooks, tailors, all—except gaming debts; those we won’t stomach. And we see he has a guinea in his pocket, so he cuts a figure. What debts he had before we came—mercy knows! We do our best to make him respectable, but it’s uphill work."

Theo gasped. "Surely you don’t fear—"

"Fear what? Oh, nothing! But I’d no more trust him with the plate than the cat with the cream. A coronet’s fine, but I’ve paid dear for mine!"

And so had my lord, selling his freedom for a cleared estate and full stables. He was shackled to that little wife and her father. No wonder he fled his own house. To lead a bear, as I did, was no gladsome trade—yet better than Castlewood’s degradation. At least I earned my keep, and no man could accuse me of fawning.

But my true solace was my wife, whose grace in poverty disarmed even Fortune’s harshness. However scant our means, all who knew her felt her a lady born. Landladies adored her; tradesmen jumped to serve her as if she were a duchess. Mrs. Foker once quipped as they rode out, "You look the mistress, and I the maid!"

The Fokers often lent us their chariot. Once, we ventured to the *Protestant Hero* for syllabub, and the hostess insisted on calling Theo "my lady" all afternoon. We visited Dr. Johnson, too, taking tea with him and Goldsmith. But most often, we called upon Aunt Bernstein, who developed a surprising fondness for Theo—so much so that she grudged the child and me for keeping her away. Her notes poured in like love-letters; her servants haunted our kitchen. If Theo delayed a visit, the Baroness scolded me fiercely. Once, when the baby ailed (though it proved a trifle), she came three days running, declaring us cruel to feign his sickness.

The Countess of Castlewood showed no such deference. "What’s all this scraping to that old woman?" she’d cry. "Fine lady? Fiddle! Take off her wig and paint, and what’s left? A dried-up crone!" And truth be told, the Baroness *was* a wreck—rouged, wigged, and clinging to life. "Why is beauty so brief?" she lamented once. "Mademoiselle de l’Enclos kept hers to a great age. I’m but seventy-six—yet look at me!"

Theo gently reminded her that all lives are fleeting.

"Ha! Adam lived a thousand years, and Eve stayed fair. What have we done to be cut so short?"

"Would you wish to live forever, without love?" Theo asked. "If my George were taken, I’d pray to follow."

The Baroness shuddered. "Who loves me in Heaven? I am alone—that’s why I cling here. You’re kind, child, bless you! My servants bear my temper, and I still have my cards—thank God for that!"

Yet in the Countess’s presence, her fire dimmed. The young lady would bustle in, bawling as if to the deaf: "Ezzy, tell grandaunt you’re a good boy!"

"If he is, he don’t take after his father," the Countess retorted. She chose to think the Baroness heard nothing, though the old lady trembled in her company.

Sampson, ever faithful, noted the Baroness’s decline. "She will not prepare for the next world," he mourned. "I have tried to speak of salvation, but she will have none of it. ‘Parson,’ says she, ‘keep sermons for Sundays, and deal the cards.’"

Yet to Theo, she confessed: "His theology! As if I, a Bishop’s widow, needed *his* instruction!"

Thus passed our days—threadbare, yet rich in small kindnesses. Once, Sampson left six guineas in the baby’s hand—a gift more precious than a kingdom. Such lights pierce the darkest times, and love, indeed, is best of all.
