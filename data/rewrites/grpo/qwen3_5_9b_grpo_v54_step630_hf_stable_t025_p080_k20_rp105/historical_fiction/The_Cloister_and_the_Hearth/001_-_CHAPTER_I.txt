In the quiet turning of the earth, there are days when the nameless do the naming, and the forgotten speak the truth that kings later forget. History often treats these lives as cold stones, cast against the bosom of the reader to glance off without a mark. Yet, within the musty pages of a Latin chronicle, buried beneath four centuries of silence, lies the story of two souls who lived and died unheard. Fate, it seems, is rarely kind to the obscure; but perhaps, if we can breathe life into their dry records, we may grant them a moment of grace in our hearts.

It was the mid-fifteenth century. Louis XI ruled France with a calculating eye; Edward IV held the English throne by right of conquest rather than pure claim; and in the Low Countries, Philip "the Good," Duke of Burgundy, reigned supreme over Holland. He had seized power from his cousin Jacqueline, breaking her spirit to secure his own, and now his rule was unchallenged.

Our tale begins in the modest town of Tergou, where Elias and Catherine lived. Elias was a merchant of cloth, silk, and brown holland, but above all, he traded in curried leather—a durable material prized by the common folk for its ability to withstand the sharp blades of a century where violence was as casual as carving meat at dinner. Though the couple possessed a comfortable estate, their peace was shadowed by the weight of nine children. In their youth, the arrival of each child had been a cause for celebration, viewed as divine playthings brought for the family's amusement. But as the parents aged and the olive branches of their household grew thick, the joy mingled with dread. They belonged to a prudent people, where recklessness was rare, yet the sheer size of their family became a fortress of worry. When the massive loaf was served, seeming to vanish before the tour of the table was complete, Elias and Catherine would exchange a silent glance. "Who will find bread for them all when we are gone?" Elias would ask.

To the younger children, food was as natural as the rising sun; to the elders, it was a looming crisis. Their anxiety manifested in mortifying ways. They refused to let any of their blood fade into obscurity among the burghers. Through strict self-denial, they managed to clothe and feed their growing mouths, saving a small hoard that brought them a secret pleasure unknown to the miser who saves only for himself.

One day, the second eldest, Richart, aged nineteen, approached his mother. With a composure that often masked the turmoil of the Dutch temperament, he begged permission to leave for Amsterdam to apprentice with a merchant. "Merchants are wealthy," he argued, "and I am good with numbers." Catherine threw up her hands in dismay. "Leave Tergou? Quit your poor father now he is no longer young?" Richart's resolve was granite. "If I can leave the folk of Tergou, I can surely leave the stones," he declared, his voice steady despite the tear in his eye. "There will be one mouth less for you to feed."

The departure broke Catherine's heart; she watched her first young bird attempt to fly from the nest. Richart left with a long, sad face, and that evening, Elias shouted at the remaining children to "sit wider" at the table, turning away in silence. The pattern repeated two years later with Jacob, who also departed for Amsterdam. By the time the third son, Giles, was considered for apprenticeship, the family coffers were drained. Elias, adhering to the creed that "a penny saved is a penny gained," had spent their entire reserve on Richart and Jacob. Now, they began to gather like ants, denying themselves the humblest luxuries to build a new fund.

Three years of frugality allowed them to establish their fourth son as a tailor and their eldest daughter as a robermaker. Yet, the coffers emptied again. Two children remained unable to work: Giles, a dwarf of malice and half-stupidity, and Little Catherine, a frail girl who moved on crutches yet smiled through her pain. Two others were unwilling: Sybrandt, lost in play, and Cornelis, the eldest, who waited passively for "dead men's shoes." Dispirited by their infirmities and the endless cycle of worry, the parents would often lament, "What will become of these when we are gone?" But the domestic horizon would suddenly clear, and they would add cheerfully, "But thanks to St. Bavon, there's Gerard."

Gerard was a son apart, destined for the Church. He possessed a frivolous nature, wrapped up in reading and penmanship, often neglecting meals to tend to his candles and books. The monks of a neighboring convent encouraged his talents, teaching him calligraphy until he inadvertently taught them in return. However, Gerard harbored a secret ambition: illumination. He wished to paint the borders and miniatures of sacred texts, but he lacked the means. The monks noted his inability to color, suggesting he lacked the gold, blue, and red necessary to make the words truly sing.

His fortunes changed with the arrival of Margaret Van Eyck, a fellow enthusiast and the surviving sister of the famous painters. She bought a house near Tergou and, upon seeing Gerard's work, invited him to her home. Initially, she dampened his spirit by comparing his sketches to her own masterpieces, but she soon revealed the true barrier: he could not afford the expensive pigments. Moved by his plight, she gifted him brushes, gold leaf, vermilion, and ultramarine, along with fine vellum. This act of sympathy sparked a deep affection between the old painter and the young scribe, a bond characteristic of an age where art and life were inseparable.

Encouraged by Margaret and the monks, Gerard's spirits soared. He became a source of joy at the dinner table, his wit drawing laughter from antiquity. As a token of gratitude, he created exquisite copies of the monastery's manuscripts. Meanwhile, Prince Philip "the Good" announced a grand competition, offering prizes for the finest works in jewelry, painting, and illumination. The burgomasters were ordered to assist poorer competitors, sending their entries to Rotterdam.

Gerard's heart leaped. He told his family timidly of his intention to compete. The reaction was immediate and harsh. The dwarf, Giles, erupted in a bassoon-like laugh, mocking the audacity of the plan. "Two gold crowns!" Catherine screamed, horrified by the cost of such "vanity." She refused to spend a month's worth of food on a dream, leaving the room in a fury. Little Catherine pleaded, but Gerard sighed, resigned to giving up or asking Margaret.

However, the housewife returned moments later, her face softening. She placed two gold pieces in his hand, claiming they were not needed for the venture. Gerard, wishing to spare her purse, proposed using vellum from his own copy of Terence, reducing the cost to a single crown. His mother, ever pragmatic, agreed to keep the crown in her pocket, warning him that houses are never built for less than the builder counts on.

On the eve of his departure, Margaret gave Gerard a letter addressed to Princess Marie in Rotterdam. Dressed in his finest holiday suit—silver-grey cloth, buckskin hose, and a hat secured by a purple ribbon from Little Catherine—Gerard set out. Near Rotterdam, he encountered an old man and a young woman, both exhausted and hungry. Recognizing their plight, Gerard stopped to gather sticks, light a fire, and cook bread and water from his flask. The young woman, initially reserved, softened as she watched his energy.

Just as Gerard prepared to offer them shelter, a figure on a mule approached. It was Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, the burgomaster of Tergou. He was a man of wealth, dressed in ermine and velvet, yet his face was furrowed with a strange pain. He recognized the faded old man and the bright girl by the roadside fire. The sight of them, reduced to begging by the wayside, struck a chord of deep remorse in the miser's heart. Twenty years prior, Ghysbrecht had committed a heartless act of roguery that had haunted him ever since. Seeing the youth, enterprise, and knowledge of Gerard, and the dignity of the Van Swieten family in their poverty, triggered a sudden, painful realization. He reined in his mule, his hand trembling in his purse, unable to offer the charity he felt compelled to give.

"Peter, Margaret," he called out, his voice strained. "What mummery is this?"

"My father was exhausted," Margaret explained quickly, shielding her father.

Ghysbrecht reached for his purse, but his hand fumbled, afraid to draw out a coin that might stick to his finger. Just then, Gerard arrived, holding two straws, ready to help cook. Upon recognizing the burgomaster, Gerard blushed, and Ghysbrecht withdrew his hand, bitterness replacing his hesitation. "Oh!" he muttered, "I am not wanted," and rode on, casting a look of suspicion at Margaret and hostility at Gerard.

Gerard, misinterpreting the glance, laughed. "By St. Bavon, I think the old miser grudges us our soup." Margaret smiled, relieved, unaware that the man she had helped was carrying a scorpion of remorse in his heart. Ghysbrecht plodded on, richer than the beggars beside him, yet poorer in spirit, burdened by the knowledge that the wealth he hoarded belonged to the very people he had passed by, and the sins of his past had finally caught up with him.
