I walked on, my face turned toward the moon, who still hung low in the sky, casting her pale gaze into the heart of the forest. She was not bright tonight—no, she was dull and bruised, like a tarnished disc of ancient copper, heavy with sorrow. There was no cloud to share her silence, and the stars burned too fiercely, as if mocking her dimness. “Will this never end?” she seemed to whisper across the dark. We moved in opposite directions, yet for a long time we traveled side by side through the trees. I did not speak to her, my eyes fixed on the ground beneath my feet; but I felt her melancholy watching me, unblinking, unseen. She was the dead light; I, the living shadow.

Then something caught my eye—a patch of white under the wide arms of a tree. I stepped closer, drawn by its stark contrast against the shadows. As I neared, the shape became clearer: it was a body, though so thin it might have been mistaken for a skeleton. I knelt, touched it gently. Cold—yes, but not the cold of stone. It was the chill of life extinguished, of warmth that had once pulsed and now lay still.

At first, I thought it might be one of the wild dancers from the forest hall—some spectral Cinderella lost in the night, abandoned to the strange world outside. But no. This was no ghost. It was flesh, though nearly reduced to bone. The ribs stood out beneath skin stretched taut over every curve, like parchment over a frame. Her lips were drawn back, revealing teeth that gleamed with an unnatural sharpness in the moonlight. Her hair fell long and thick, black as midnight, soft to the touch even in death. She was tall—once graceful, perhaps—and utterly naked, stripped bare by some unseen hand or cruel fate.

How had she come here? Not by choice. She had fallen, surely, when strength left her. And then hunger took what remained. But how could she have wasted so completely? Where were the beasts that would have taken her clothes? Or the savages who might have left her behind? That her body remained untouched was not surprising—what was strange was that it was so perfectly preserved, so undecayed.

I rose slowly, standing tall in the quiet. I could not leave her exposed. Even in death, a woman’s body deserved dignity. A garment, any covering, demanded respect. To let her lie uncovered—exposed to the eyes of predators, to the slow erosion of time—was unthinkable. Yet the earth was hard, packed with roots, and I had only my hands.

At first glance, there was no sign of decay. But what was there to rot? Life had been drained away so thoroughly that nothing remained to break down. Could she still be alive? Might she breathe beneath that stillness? In this world, where dreams walked and rivers sang, anything seemed possible. Still, I must know.

I remembered the grapes I’d taken from the doorway of the forest hall—the last fruit I had. A few remained on the stem. Perhaps their juice could stir something within her. I placed a grape in her mouth, but there was no swallowing, no movement. I laid her upon a bed of pine needles and dry leaves, covered her with my own clothes, and piled more foliage over her. I hoped to trap the little warmth that lingered in her bones, to hold it until the sun returned.

Then I tried again. Another grape. Still nothing.

“Doubt,” I said aloud, “may not inspire action—but it is no reason to do nothing.”

Her skin clung so tightly to her bones that I dared not rub or press. Instead, I crawled into the heap of leaves, drew her close, and held her in my arms. My own warmth was fading, but I gave it all. I spent the rest of the night awake, listening, waiting. Her cold seeped into me, but I felt no return of heat. Had I fled from the sleeping beauties in their silver beds to lie beside this one? I had refused a dream—to find myself bound to a duty far greater.

Beneath the weary moon, I watched the dawn approach.

When the east began to lighten, I saw motion—not shapes, but a ripple near the ground. A large snake glided past, straight and unwavering. Then came what looked like a doe and her fawn. After them, two bear cubs followed by smaller ones. Soon, a herd of horses trotted by—smaller than any I had seen, yet full-grown, perfect in form. Some resembled draft horses, others warhorses, hunters, racers. They moved with purpose. Behind them came dwarf cattle and tiny elephants.

“Where are the children?” I whispered. “As soon as I can, I must go back for them.”

But I could not leave her. Not yet. I placed my hand on her chest. Was she warmer? Slightly. Not enough to prove life, but not colder either. Hope, fragile as it was, did not fade.

The sun rose at last, golden and innocent, peering over the edge of the world as if curious about the stir below. I stood, renewed. I removed the cloth I’d used to shield her face and eyes from the needles. Still, no breath. No twitch. Only silence.

In the morning light, I saw her face clearly—sunken, hollow, every bone defined. Her lips parted just enough to reveal her teeth. The skin was stretched tight, like a mask over a skull. But her hair—thick, black, alive—reminded me that life had once thrived here.

She was not just a corpse. She was a vessel. And perhaps, somewhere deep inside, a spark remained.

I followed the trail the animals had taken. It was well-worn, branching from many paths that merged into one. The trees thinned, and grass spread wide across the land. Ahead flowed a river, small but deep, its surface shimmering with a bluish mist that vanished as it rose. On the other bank, creatures grazed—small, peaceful, feeding in the morning light.

I knelt to drink—but the water was hot, and tasted metallic. I leapt up. This was warmth! The very thing I needed.

I ran back to her. Carrying her was easier than I expected. Though tall, she was astonishingly light—her bones delicate, her flesh gone. I held her like a child, cradling her against my shoulder. Every step I took, I feared she might dissolve into dust.

The water was too hot to plunge her in at once. I laid her on the bank and began bathing her with damp cloth, wetting each limb, each curve of her wasted frame. Her age was impossible to tell—only the hair suggested youth. Her eyelids were half-open, just enough to show the darkness beyond. It made her look more dead than ever—like a door cracked open to a room without light.

Still, I bathed her. I kept moving, ensuring no part cooled while another warmed. Gradually, her body grew warmer. At last, I ventured to submerge her—carrying her into the stream, keeping her face above water, letting the current flow around her limbs. Her hand remained clenched, unyielding, though the heat did not loosen it.

Ten minutes passed. I lifted her out, dried her, covered her with leaves, and ran to gather more. When I returned, she had not cooled. The warmth of the stream had penetrated her frame—though it was not life, it was *possible* life.

I searched the hills nearby and found the source: the river emerged from a rock, as if born from within the mountain. Inside, it rose quietly from the ground like a column, flowing along a narrow channel. I crept into the cave and saw a way—using fallen boughs, I could build a bridge over the stream, a platform kept warm by the water below.

I gathered branches, wove them together, layered them with moss and leaves. After several trips, I built a soft, dry, warm couch. Then I carried her to it, placing her gently upon the living bridge. She was so light I almost feared she would vanish into air.

I covered her again, placed a grape in her mouth—this time, I could open her lips slightly. The grape stayed, untouched, but I hoped some juice might reach her throat.

Days passed. Seven days and nights. I sat or slept beside her, always watching. Each morning, I bathed in the hot stream—each time, I felt reborn. I bathed her too, every day, and fed her a fresh grape. Once, a discoloration appeared on her side—I froze. But by morning, it was gone. I continued.

I ate berries and grapes from the forest, but I believed I could survive on the river alone. My dreams were of angels—wounded, unable to fly, who stayed with me, who loved me. But each time I woke, I saw only her: pale, still, untouched by time.

Adam waited for Eve. I waited for her.

I had learned loneliness. Not just absence—but emptiness. A man alone is not a man. He is a need. A possibility. To be whole, he must reflect another soul. Man is not self-sufficient—he is shaped by others, fed by their presence, defined by their love.

Without her, I would have fled to the beasts. Better to walk among them than to live alone. But now—now there was hope.

She might wake. She might see me. She might speak.

And if she did—if she opened her eyes—then I, the poorest of beings, might become human at last.
