A new influence, like sunlight breaking through a canopy of ancient oaks, began to alter the course of my life, pushing aside the gloom that had previously settled over me. Imagine a secluded valley, deep and hidden within a forest's secret embrace, lying shrouded in mist and dimness. Its earth is damp, its grass pale and wet. Then, a storm or the fall of an axe creates a wide breach among the trees; the wind rushes in, the sun descends, and that sorrowful, cold hollow transforms into a deep vessel of radiance. High summer pours her blue glory and golden light from a beautiful sky, unseen until now by the starving hollow.

So, a new creed took root within me—a belief in happiness.

Three weeks had passed since the adventure in the garret, and I now held four companions to the original letter, casketed in boxes and drawers upstairs. Traced by the same firm pen, sealed with the same clear wax, they were filled with the same vital comfort. To me then, they seemed a divine vintage, a draught that the gods themselves might approve, pouring juice from a fountain so honored. Time, later, would mellow them into a gentle brew, but when I first tasted them, they were an elixir of pure joy, composed by one who was well pleased.

These letters, written by a hand that knew contentment, were kind and pleasing. Two of them ended with lines half-gaiety, half-tenderness—"by *feeling* touched, but not subdued." Later, reading them in after-years, I understood how time softened them, but at first, they were a drink of celestial origin.

Did you, dear reader, remember what I said earlier regarding my response? Whether I answered under the dry restraint of Reason or the full impulse of Feeling? To speak truth, I compromised. I served two masters: I bowed in the house of Rimmon, yet lifted my heart to another shrine. I wrote two answers to these letters—one for my own relief, the other intended solely for Graham's eyes.

First, Feeling and I conspired to expel Reason from our dwelling, barring the doors and bolting them shut. We sat together, spreading paper, dipping an eager pen, and poured out our hearts with deep enjoyment. When we finished, covering two sheets with the language of a strongly adherent affection, a rooted gratitude... (and once, for all, I disclaim with scorn any "warmer feelings" that might be implied: women do not entertain such sentiments where, from the start, they have never been cheated of the conviction that doing so would be a mortal absurdity), we had expressed a closely clinging, deeply honouring attachment. An attachment that desired to absorb all the pain of its object's destiny. But just at that moment, the doors of my heart shook, bolts yielded, and Reason leapt in vigorous and revengeful. She snatched the sheets, sneered, erased, tore, rewrote, folded, sealed, and directed a terse, curt missive of a single page. She did right.

I did not live on letters alone. I was visited, cared for, and once a week, taken to La Terrasse. Always, I was made much of. Dr. Bretton failed not to explain why he was so kind: "To keep away the nun," he declared. "I was determined to dispute with her her prey. I had taken a thorough dislike to her, chiefly on account of that white face-cloth and those cold grey eyes: the moment I heard of those odious particulars, consummate disgust incited me to oppose her." He wished she would visit him again when present, but she never did. Scientifically, he regarded me as a patient, exercising professional skill and natural benevolence through a course of cordial treatment.

One evening, the first of December, I walked alone in the *carré*. It was six o'clock; the classroom doors were closed, yet within, the pupils, rampant in the license of evening recreation, created a miniature chaos. The *carré* was dark, save for a red light glowing beneath the stove. Wide glass doors and long windows were frosted over, a blanched winter veil, spangled here and there with a crystal sparkle of starlight, proving it a clear, moonless night. My daring to remain alone in the darkness showed my nerves were regaining a healthy tone. I thought of the Nun, but hardly feared her. Though the staircase loomed behind me, leading up through blind, black nights to the haunted garret, I owned that my heart quaked and my pulse leaped when I suddenly heard breathing and rustling. Turning, I saw in the deep shadow of the steps a deeper shadow—a shape moving and descending. It paused at the class-door, then glided past me. Simultaneously, a clangor of a distant bell rang out. Life-like sounds bring life-like feelings: this shape was too round and low for my gaunt Nun. It was only Madame Beck on duty.

"Mademoiselle Lucy!" cried Rosine, bursting in from the corridor with a lamp. "On est là pour vous au salon."

Madame saw me, I saw Madame, Rosine saw us both: there was no mutual recognition. I made straight for the salon. There I found Dr. Bretton, but he was in evening dress.

"The carriage is at the door," he said. "My mother has sent it to take you to the theatre; she was going herself, but an arrival prevented her: she immediately said, 'Take Lucy in my place.' Will you go?"

"Just now? I am not dressed," I cried, glancing despairingly at my dark merino gown.

"You have half an hour to dress. I should have given you notice, but I only determined on going since five o'clock, when I heard there was to be a genuine regale in the presence of a great actress."

He mentioned a name that thrilled me—a name that, in those days, could thrill Europe. It is hushed now; its once restless echoes are all still. Night and oblivion long since closed above her, but *then* her day stood at its full height, light and fervour.

"I'll go; I will be ready in ten minutes," I vowed. Away I flew, never once checked by the thought which perhaps checks you now: namely, that to go anywhere with Graham and without Mrs. Bretton could be objectionable. I could not have conceived, much less expressed to Graham, such a scruple without risking a tyrannous self-contempt, kindling an inward fire of shame so quenchless and devouring that it would soon have licked up the life in my veins. Besides, my godmother, knowing her son and knowing me, would as soon have thought of chaperoning a sister with a brother as of keeping anxious guard over our movements.

The present required no showy array; my dun mist crape would suffice. I sought it in the great oak-wardrobe in the dormitory, where hung no less than forty dresses. But changes and reforms had occurred, and some innovating hand had pruned this crowded wardrobe, carrying divers garments to the garret—my crape amongst the rest. I must fetch it. I got the key and went aloft fearless, almost thoughtless. I unlocked the door, plunged in. You may believe it or not, but when I thus suddenly entered, that garret was not wholly dark as it should have been: from one point there shone a solemn light, like a star, but broader. So plainly it shone that it revealed the deep alcove with a portion of the tarnished scarlet curtain drawn over it. Instantly, silently, before my eyes, it vanished; so did the curtain and alcove: all that end of the garret became black as night. I ventured no research; I had not time nor will. Snatching my dress, which hung on the wall near the door, I rushed out, relocked the door with convulsed haste, and darted downwards to the dormitory.

But I trembled too much to dress myself; impossible to arrange hair or fasten hooks-and-eyes with such fingers. So I called Rosine and bribed her to help me. Rosine liked a bribe, so she did her best, smoothing and plaiting my hair as well as a *coiffeur* would have done, placing the lace collar mathematically straight, tying the neck-ribbon accurately—in short, working like the neat-handed Phillis she could be when she chose. Giving me my handkerchief and gloves, she took the candle and lit me downstairs. After all, I had forgotten my shawl; she ran back to fetch it. I stood with Dr. John in the vestibule, waiting.

"What is this, Lucy?" said he, looking down at me narrowly. "Here is the old excitement. Ha! The nun again?"

I utterly denied the charge, vexed to be suspected of a second illusion. He was sceptical.

"She has been, as sure as I live," he said. "Her figure crossing your eyes leaves on them a peculiar gleam and expression not to be mistaken."

"She has *not* been," I persisted; for indeed, I could deny her apparition with truth.

"The old symptoms are there," he affirmed. "A particular pale, and what the Scotch call a 'raised' look."
