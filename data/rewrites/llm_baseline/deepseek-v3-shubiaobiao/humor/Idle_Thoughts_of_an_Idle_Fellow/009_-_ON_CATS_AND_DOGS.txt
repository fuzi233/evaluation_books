What I have endured this morning at the paws of our four-legged housemates defies all human expression. It began, as all disasters do in this household, with Gustavus Adolphus.

Gustavus Adolphus—affectionately abbreviated to "Gusty" by those below-stairs who have not suffered his full wrath—is, in theory, a splendid creature. Outdoors, where there are fields to romp and commons to conquer, he is the very model of canine nobility. Indoors, however, he is a catastrophe on four legs. His mere existence in a confined space violates the laws of physics. Stretch? Two chairs and a what-not collapse. Wag? The room resembles the aftermath of a small war. Breathe? The fire extinguishes itself in self-defense.

At dinner, he employs a particularly diabolical tactic: slinking beneath the table, lying in wait, and then—just as the soup is served—rising with the suddenness of a jack-in-the-box. The table lurches, the diners shriek, and what follows is a frenzied ballet of human limbs grappling with furniture while Gusty, convinced we are attempting some dark ritual, thrashes like a creature possessed. The finale is always the same: a ruined meal, an overturned table, and a heap of furious humanity.

This morning, he entered with his usual cyclone-inspired grace and immediately swept my coffee cup off the table with his tail, baptizing my waistcoat in lukewarm bitterness. I rose, uttered a phrase not fit for print, and advanced upon him with intent. He fled toward the door—only to collide with Eliza, who was bearing eggs. The resulting tableau: Eliza on the floor, eggs in artistic disarray across the carpet, and Gusty vanished in a blur of guilty fur.

I shouted after him, suggesting he confine himself to the yard for the foreseeable future. He seemed to agree, dodging a flung coal-scoop with the agility of a seasoned fugitive. Peace briefly resumed.

But no. Ten minutes later, I found him at the top of the stairs, barking at an immovable obstacle: Tittums, our kitten.

Tittums is roughly the size of a bread roll and twice as volatile. There she sat, one stair above him, back arched, delivering a stream of profanity that would make a sailor blush. Now, I am no stranger to strong language—between us, I consider it a vital release for the male psyche—but Tittums is a virtuoso. I reprimanded her for her unchristian vocabulary (hypocrisy be damned) and pocketed her, only to later discover her attempting to swallow my pen, upend the ink, and then swear *at me* for her own mess.

Tim, our two-year-old terrier with the ego of a seasoned diplomat, chose this moment to intervene. A scuffle ensued. Tittums’ mother joined the fray. Tim emerged with a scratched nose. Justice.

Now they are all banished to the passage, where they continue their battle royale. I am ink-stained, irritable, and issuing this decree: any further feline or canine interference shall be met with extreme prejudice. Bring your own undertaker.

Yet, for all this, I adore them. They are superior companions to humans in every way. They do not argue, bore you with their troubles, or make tactless remarks about your plays. They never confuse your wife’s cousin for her husband, nor ask why you haven’t written a masterpiece yet. They are steadfast in their affection, indifferent to your flaws, and eternally glad to see you—whether you are jubilant, morose, or simply in need of a silent friend to blink at the fire beside you.

A dog does not care if you are rich or poor, wise or foolish. You are his comrade, and that is enough. He will follow you to the ends of the earth, defend you to his last breath, and gaze at you with those absurdly loyal eyes as if you hung the moon. And we, in our infinite human superiority, call *him* the brainless one.

Cats, though often accused of selfishness, are merely discreet in their devotion. I have known feline friends who followed me like shadows, sat vigil when I was ill, and even developed regrettable drinking habits (one of mine had a penchant for beer drippings—a vice I neither condemn nor endorse).

Rats, meanwhile, occupy a peculiar place in our imaginations—simultaneously reviled and fascinating. They haunt crumbling ruins, flee sinking ships, and scuttle through tales of horror with eerie purpose. There is something mythic about them, from Bishop Hatto’s gruesome demise to the Pied Piper’s eerie melody. That old legend lingers in my mind—the piper who led children away, their laughter fading into the mountain. A metaphor, perhaps, for the inexplicable callings that tug us from the mundane, whispering of mysteries beyond our grasp.

If I may beg one thing of pet owners: love your animals, but spare us the maudlin excess. There is nothing more tiresome than the family that elevates Fido to sainthood, holding court over his every yawn and scratch. A dog is not a surrogate child, nor a cat a velvet-pawed aristocrat. They are creatures of simple joys and honest instincts—worthy of affection, yes, but not idolatry.

Now, if you’ll excuse me, I must extricate Tittums from the inkpot again. Some things never change.
