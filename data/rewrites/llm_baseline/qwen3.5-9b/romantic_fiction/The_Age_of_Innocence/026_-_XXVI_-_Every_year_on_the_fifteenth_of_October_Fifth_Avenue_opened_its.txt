Every year on the fifteenth of October, Fifth Avenue performed its seasonal transformation: shutters swung open, carpets unfurled, and layers of curtains adorned the windows. By early November, this domestic ritual concluded, leaving society free to survey itself. By the fifteenth, the season roared into full force; opera houses unveiled new stars, theaters offered fresh attractions, dinner invitations piled up, and dance schedules were finalized. It was precisely then that Mrs. Archer invariably declared that New York had changed dramatically. Viewing the city from the detached vantage point of a non-participant, aided by Mr. Sillerton Jackson and Miss Sophy, she meticulously cataloged every new fissure in its facade and every strange weed sprouting between the orderly rows of social convention.

In Archer's youth, awaiting this annual verdict from his mother had been a favorite amusement. He would listen as she enumerated the minute signs of decay that his casual observation had missed. To Mrs. Archer, New York never evolved without deteriorating. Miss Sophy Jackson wholeheartedly shared this sentiment. Mr. Sillerton Jackson, embodying the role of the worldly man, suspended his judgment, listening with amused neutrality to the ladies' laments. Yet, even he could not deny that New York had changed; and Newland Archer, in the winter of his second year of marriage, was compelled to concede that whether the city had truly altered or merely appeared to do so, it was certainly in flux.

These discussions traditionally dominated Mrs. Archer's Thanksgiving dinner. On the day officially designated for gratitude, it was her custom to take a somber, though not bitter, inventory of her world, wondering what there truly was to thank God for. Certainly, not the state of society. If society existed at all, it resembled a spectacle worthy of biblical curses—and indeed, everyone understood the Reverend Dr. Ashmore's choice of text from Jeremiah (Chapter II, Verse 25) for his Thanksgiving sermon. Dr. Ashmore, the newly appointed Rector of St. Matthew's, had been selected specifically for his progressive views; his sermons were deemed bold in thought and innovative in language. When he denounced fashionable society, he invariably spoke of its "trend." To Mrs. Archer, it was simultaneously terrifying and captivating to feel herself part of a community that was trending.

"There is no doubt," she remarked, treating the concept as tangible and measurable, akin to a hairline fracture in a building, "that Dr. Ashmore is correct: there *is* a marked trend."

"It seems odd to preach about trends on Thanksgiving," Miss Jackson observed.

"Oh, he intends for us to give thanks for what remains," her hostess replied dryly.

Archer had previously smiled at these yearly prophecies, but this year, even he acknowledged as he listened to the litany of changes that the "trend" was undeniably visible.

"The excess in dress—" Miss Jackson began. "Mr. Jackson took me to the opening night of the Opera, and I can assure you that Jane Merry's gown was the only one I recognized from last year. Moreover, even that had been altered at the front panel. Yet, I know she acquired it from Worth just two years ago, as my seamstress always alters her Paris creations before wearing them."

"Ah, Jane Merry is one of *us*," Mrs. Archer sighed, implying that belonging to an era where ladies flaunted their Parisian gowns immediately upon arrival from the Custom House—rather than aging them under lock and key as her contemporaries did—was not necessarily an enviable position.

"Yes; she is one of the few. In my youth," Miss Jackson countered, "wearing the latest fashions was considered vulgar. Amy Sillerton has often told me that in Boston, the rule was to store Parisian dresses for two years. Old Mrs. Baxter Pennilow, who dressed handsomely, imported twelve dresses annually: two velvet, two satin, two silk, and the remainder in poplin and fine cashmere. It was a standing order. As she was ill for two years prior to her death, they discovered forty-eight Worth dresses that had never left their tissue paper wrappers. Consequently, when the girls ceased mourning, they wore the first batch to Symphony concerts without appearing ahead of their time."

"Well, Boston is more conservative than New York," Mrs. Archer admitted. "But I always believe it is prudent for a lady to set aside her French dresses for one season."

"It was Beaufort who initiated this new fashion by compelling his wife to don her new garments the moment they arrived. One must admit, at times, it requires all of Regina's refinement not to resemble... to resemble..." Miss Jackson glanced around the table, catching Janey's wide-eyed stare, and retreated into an unintelligible murmur.

"To her rivals," Mr. Sillerton Jackson supplied, delivering the line with the flair of an epigram.

"Oh—" the ladies murmured. Mrs. Archer added, partly to divert her daughter's attention from taboo subjects, "Poor Regina! Her Thanksgiving has surely not been cheerful. Have you heard the rumors regarding Beaufort's investments, Sillerton?"

Mr. Jackson nodded casually. Everyone knew the rumors, and he scorned confirming a tale already common knowledge. A gloomy silence descended upon the gathering. While no one particularly liked Beaufort, and it was almost pleasant to speculate on the worst aspects of his private life, the notion that he had brought financial disgrace upon his wife's family was too shocking to enjoy, even by his detractors. New York tolerated hypocrisy in personal relationships but demanded absolute transparency in business. It had been decades since a prominent banker had failed so ignominiously, yet everyone recalled the social annihilation visited upon the families involved during the last such incident. The same fate awaited the Beauforts, regardless of his influence or her popularity; not even the combined strength of the Dallas connections could save poor Regina if the reports of her husband's illicit speculation proved true.

Consequently, the conversation shifted to less ominous topics, yet everything discussed seemed to reinforce Mrs. Archer's perception of an accelerating decline.

"Of course, Newland, I know you allow dear May to attend Mrs. Struthers's Sunday gatherings," she began.

"Oh, you know, everyone goes to Mrs. Struthers's now," May interjected cheerfully. "She was even invited to Granny's last reception."

Thus, Archer reflected, New York managed its transitions: conspiring to ignore shifts until they were complete, and then, in good faith, assuming they had occurred in a previous age. There was always a traitor in the citadel; once they (or he) surrendered the keys, what purpose was there in pretending the fortress remained impregnable? Once one had tasted Mrs. Struthers's effortless Sunday hospitality, there was little inclination to remain at home reminiscing about how her champagne had been transformed into shoe polish.

"I know, dear, I know," Mrs. Archer sighed. "Such things must happen, I suppose, as long as *amusement* is what drives people outdoors; but I have never fully forgiven your cousin, Madame Olenska, for being the first to endorse Mrs. Struthers."

A sudden flush rose to young Mrs. Archer's cheeks, surprising her husband as much as the other guests.

"Oh, *Ellen*—" she murmured, using the same accusatory yet mitigating tone her parents might employ when mentioning "the Blenkers." This was the familial refrain triggered by the Countess Olenska's name, ever since she had stubbornly resisted her husband's advances. However, on May's lips, the phrase carried deeper weight, prompting Archer to look at her with a sense of strangeness that occasionally arose when she most embodied her surroundings.

"My mother, with less sensitivity to the atmosphere than usual, persisted: 'I have always believed that individuals like the Countess Olenska, who have resided in aristocratic societies, should assist us in maintaining our social distinctions, rather than disregarding them.'"

May's blush remained vividly permanent, suggesting a significance beyond mere acknowledgment of Madame Olenska's perceived social indiscretion.

"We all seem alike to foreigners," Miss Jackson remarked sharply.

"I do not believe Ellen cares for society," May continued, as if searching for neutral ground. "But no one knows exactly what she does care for."

"Well—" Mrs. Archer sighed again.

Everyone knew that the Countess Olenska was no longer in favor with her family. Even her staunch defender, old Mrs. Manson Mingott, had been powerless to justify her granddaughter-in-law's refusal to return to her husband. The Mingotts had not voiced their disapproval openly; their sense of unity was too profound. They had simply, as Mrs. Welland phrased it, "allowed poor Ellen to find her own level"—and that, mortifyingly and inexplicably, placed her in the murky depths where the Blenkers thrived and "those who write" celebrated their chaotic rituals. It was unbelievable, yet undeniable: despite her opportunities and privileges, Ellen had become merely "Bohemian." This reality underscored the argument that she had made a fatal error in refusing to return to Count Olenski. After all, a young woman's proper place was beneath her husband's roof, especially given the circumstances of her departure... well, if one cared to investigate further...

"Madame Olenska is a great favorite with the gentlemen," Miss Sophy noted, attempting a conciliatory remark while subtly planting a barb.

"That is precisely the peril to which a young woman like Madame Olenska is constantly exposed," Mrs. Archer mournfully agreed.
