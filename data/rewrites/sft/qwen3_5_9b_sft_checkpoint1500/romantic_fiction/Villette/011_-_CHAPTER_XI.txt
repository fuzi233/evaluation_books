The heat of summer hung heavy and oppressive over the school, a physical weight that seemed to press down upon the spirits of everyone within its walls. It was during this sweltering season that Georgette, Madame Beck's youngest child, succumbed to a high fever. In a swift display of maternal caution, Désirée—who had recently recovered from her own ailments—and Fifine were dispatched to the country under the care of "Bonne-Maman" to escape the contagion. Yet, despite the precautions, the atmosphere grew thick with anxiety. Several of the pensionnaires began to complain of headaches, their pale complexions mirroring Georgette's suffering.

"Now, at last," I thought, watching the scene unfold, "Dr. Pillule must be recalled. A prudent director would never allow a boy of such indeterminate years to tend to her pupils."

However, Madame Beck was a figure of contradictions; she was as prudent as a fortress wall, yet capable of terrifying audacity. While the return of Dr. Pillule, who had visited a week prior, was ignored, she boldly summoned his younger, English rival, Dr. John, to attend the school. To my astonishment, she placed him directly in charge of the care of Blanche de Melcy and Angélique, two girls known for their vanity and flirtatious nature. I observed Dr. John with a mixture of amusement and skepticism; he seemed gratified by this sudden mark of confidence. If discretion alone could have justified such a step, he would have easily earned it. But here, in a realm governed by convents and confessional secrets, the mere presence of a young man among the boarders could not be tolerated without consequence.

Whispers erupted instantly. The schoolgirls gossiped behind closed doors, the kitchen staff murmured with malicious delight, and the town caught wind of the rumor. Parents, alarmed and suspicious, wrote frantic letters and visited the establishment to lodge formal complaints. If Madame Beck had been a woman of weak resolve, she would have been ruined immediately; a dozen rival educational institutions stood ready to pounce on this perceived scandal. But Madame was not weak. Though a devoted follower of Jesuit principles, her management was ironclad. I clapped my hands with silent admiration, cheering inwardly as I witnessed her composure. She faced the storm with skill, temper, and unshakeable firmness.

Her strategy was simple yet effective: she met the agitated parents with a good-humored, easy grace that few could match. Whether this charm was innate or assumed, she wielded it with masterful precision, often achieving immediate success where severity and logic would have failed.

"Oh, *poor* Doctor Jean!" she would coo, chuckling softly while rubbing her small, white hands together. "*Cher* young man! The best creature in the world!" She spun tales of how she employed him specifically for her own children, who doted on him so fiercely they would scream at the thought of any other physician. She explained that because she trusted him implicitly for her family, it was only natural to entrust others to his care. Furthermore, she dismissed the arrangement as merely a temporary expedient; Blanche and Angélique simply suffered from migraines, and Dr. John had prescribed a remedy. *Voilà tout!*

And the effect was magical. The parents' objections dissolved into silence. Blanche and Angélique, seizing the opportunity to save their director from further trouble, launched into loud duets praising their physician. The other pupils joined in, unanimously declaring that if they fell ill again, they would demand nothing less than Dr. John. Madame laughed, a bright, ringing sound, and the parents followed suit, their fears replaced by relief. The inhabitants of La Bassesse, it seemed, possessed an overwhelming instinct for procreation; the indulgence of offspring was carried to such extremes that the children's whims invariably dictated household laws. By playing the part of a mother who favored her nursemaid's son, Madame emerged victorious with flying colors, gaining more affection for her leadership than ever before.

Yet, to this day, I remain puzzled by the depth of the risk she took for Dr. John. The entire house—from pupils to teachers to servants—firmly believed she was plotting to marry him. They settled it as fact, dismissing the fourteen-year age gap as trivial. And, one must admit, appearances did not entirely contradict this notion. Madame seemed determined to retain his services, utterly forgetting her former protégé, Dr. Pillule. She made a point of receiving his visits personally, her demeanor toward him unfailingly cheerful, blithe, and benignant. Around this time, she marked a distinct shift in her attire; the morning disarray, the nightcap, and the shawl were abandoned. Whenever Dr. John arrived, she appeared in neatly arranged auburn braids, a silk dress trimmed with precision, and laced brodequins replacing her slippers. Her toilette was complete, a model of freshness, as vibrant as a flower. I doubt, however, that her intention extended beyond showing a handsome man that she was not merely a plain woman. Plain she certainly was not. Lacking conventional beauty or elegant form, she possessed a unique appeal. Without the fleeting graces of youth, she offered a steady cheer. She was never monotonous, insipid, or colorless. Her unfaded hair, the temperate blue light in her eyes, and the wholesome, fruit-like bloom on her cheeks pleased with a constancy that never faded.

Did she harbor visions of adopting him as a husband, inviting him to her well-furnished home, endowing him with her modest savings, and securing his comfort for the remainder of his days? Did Dr. John suspect these dreams? I have seen him emerge from her presence with a mischievous, half-smile curving his lips, his eyes alight with a masculine vanity, elated and tickled. With all his good looks and gentle nature, he was far from perfect. He must have been deeply flawed if he encouraged aims he never intended to fulfill. But did he not intend them? Rumors abounded that he had no fortune and relied wholly on his profession. Madame, though older, was the type of woman who never truly aged, never withered, never broke down. They were on excellent terms. Perhaps he did not love her, but how many couples in this world marry for anything other than love? We all waited, holding our breath, for the inevitable conclusion.

What Dr. John awaited, I could not discern, nor what he watched for with such intensity. His peculiar manner—a look that was expectant, vigilant, absorbed, and eager—never diminished; if anything, it intensified. He had always existed beyond the reach of my understanding, and I felt he was straying farther and farther into a territory I could not map.

One morning, little Georgette's fever worsened, rendering her peevish and inconsolable. She wept bitterly, refusing any attempt at soothing. I suspected a particular draught had disagreed with her stomach and hesitated on whether to continue it. Impatient to consult the doctor, I waited for his arrival.

The doorbell rang, and he was admitted. I knew this for certain, for I heard his voice calling to the portress. It was his habit to ascend the stairs swiftly, covering three steps at a time, appearing like a cheerful surprise. Five minutes passed, then ten. Saw or heard nothing of him. Where could he be? Was he waiting in the corridor below? Little Georgette continued her plaintive wails, appealing to me with her broken English, "Minnie, Minnie, me very poorly!" until my heart ached. I descended to investigate. The corridor was empty. Had he vanished? Could he possibly be with Madame in the *salle-à-manger*? Impossible; I had left her only moments ago, seeing her dress in her own chamber.

I listened carefully. Three pupils were practicing instruments in nearby rooms—the dining-room and the drawing-rooms—which communicated with the corridor solely through the portress's cabinet, originally designed as a boudoir. Farther away, in the oratory, a class of twelve or more sang a "barcarole," the words *"fraîche," "brisée,"* and *"Venise"* drifting through the air. Under these circumstances, what could I hear? A great deal, certainly, if I listened for the right purpose.

There it was: a giddy, treble laugh emanating from the cabinet near the door, which stood half-open. A man's voice, soft, deep, and pleading, uttered words I barely caught, save for the desperate adjuration, "For God's sake!" After a brief pause, Dr. John emerged. His eyes were full, shining, yet devoid of joy or triumph. His fair English cheek was flushed high, and his brow bore the marks of bafflement, torture, anxiety, and a tender, agonizing sorrow.

The open door served as a screen, but if I had stood fully in his path, I believe he would have passed without noticing me. A profound mortification, a strong vexation, gripped his soul—or rather, looking back now, I would say a deep sorrow, a sense of injustice. I did not feel his pride hurt so much as his affections, cruelly wounded. But who was the torturer? What being in that house held such sway over him? I believed Madame was in her chamber, and the room he had just exited belonged exclusively to the portress, Rosine Matou.
