For more than a twelvemonth, Brother Clement had tended Basle’s souls: teaching catechism to the town’s children, preaching under the vaulted arches of the Dominican chapel, and dividing his remaining hours between prayer and quiet study of a new language he had long suspected he would soon be called to use. Then one blustery spring afternoon, a travel-worn figure stumbled through the priory door, cloak caked in Rhine mud, scrip bulging, a triumphant fire dancing in his eyes. It was Brother Jerome.

“Give all glory to God, Brother Clement,” he gasped, brushing dust from his tonsure. “The way is clear. You may ride for England at my side, at last.”

Clement, who had long awaited this day, did not even pause to rise from his stool. “I am ready,” he said softly. “These months, between homilies and vigil hours, I have pored over the English tongue, till I can speak to their folk as one of their own, not a foreigner mouthing stilted Latin phrases.”

“That was wise forethought,” Jerome nodded, leaning against the stone wall to catch his breath. He explained his long delay: he had traveled all the way to Rome, and secured extraordinary papal bulls granting them two rare privileges: full authority to collect alms across every parish in England for the Church’s charitable works, and leave to hear confession in every secular monastery on the isle, no prior abbot’s approval required. “So gird up your frock, brother,” he finished, clapping Clement on the shoulder. “We go forth to strike a strong blow for Holy Mother Church, and root out the mischief the Franciscans have sown across those northern parishes.”

The two friars set off down the Rhine, preaching their way toward the coast that would carry them across the Channel. In large market towns, they shared the pulpit, speaking in turn to packed congregations. In the tiny riverside hamlets, they split paths, one taking the east bank, one the west, agreeing to meet at the next ferry crossing a day’s march ahead. Both were preachers of rare skill, but their styles could not have been more different. Jerome’s oratory was grand, stately, weighted with solemn reverence for sacred text – though some unkind souls might have called it a touch rigid, its focus narrowed to only the strictest theological topics, its measured cadence a little too unchanging for crowds used to livelier roadside sermons. Clement, by contrast, was fire and flexibility all at once. He wove colloquial turns of phrase into his homilies, soft as a lullaby one moment, curling around the listener’s heart and nudging their reason to see their own sins without sting. But when the spirit moved him, he soared far higher than Jerome’s most solemn grandeur, rising to heights of passion that matched the tales of ancient inspired prophets, or the greatest heathen orators of old: vehement as a gale, rushing as a mountain torrent, blazing as lightning, he thundered, flashed, and swept all before him on waves of rushing eloquence that left crowds breathless.

The narrator pauses here, noting he would transcribe full passages of those fiery sermons, but five small barriers stand in the way: the task is difficult, time is short, he has recreated them elsewhere in his work, a later skilled imitator has done the job even better, and while identical peas are a mark of quality, identical passages in a book are nothing but vice.

What he will reveal, though, is Clement’s quiet secret: before every stop, he would slip a few pence to the local baker or tavern maid, to learn the recent troubles of the town, and the particular sin that clung thickest to its inhabitants. Jerome, unbending and uncompromising, would never stoop to such a trick – he preached the same unchanging truth to every crowd, tailored to no man’s weaknesses.

At one large market town a few leagues back from the Rhine, they climbed the same stone pulpit in the central church, one after the other. Jerome went first, preaching against the sin of vanity in dress, a favourite theme of his. His words were sharp, satirical, eloquent – and the crowd listened with perfect, unperturbed complacency. It was a vice none of them were much guilty of.

Then Clement stepped up, and preached against drunkenness. It was the town’s besetting sin, and one no local priest ever dared speak against: the entire secular clergy was drunk on spiced ale most nights, and popular prejudice had long wrapped the vice in a sacred, untouchable cloak. Clement did not hold back. He rained blows on it from every page of Holy Writ, and from every known tale of worldly woe. Drunkenness was no mere small fault, he thundered: it was the nursing mother of almost every other crime, the root of theft, of brawling, of murder. He reminded them of the recent parricide committed on their own streets, by an honest, hardworking man who had been blind drunk when he struck his father down. He told them of the neighbouring village where a gang of drunkards had roasted one of their own comrades alive over a tavern fire, in a stupid, witless frolic they could not even remember the next morning.

“Your late prince,” he called out, his voice ringing off the rafters, “they say he died of an apoplexy, but every man of you knows in his heart he drank himself to death. Last month, one of your own aldermen perished in the gutter, so deep in his cups he suffocated in a puddle of mud. Your children go bare-backed in cold weather, your wives go hungry at the end of every week, so you may fill your bellies with ale that turns you into worse beasts than the swine you herd: silly as calves, raging as wild boars, driving your families to ruin and your souls straight to hell. I tell you, your town, your very whole nation, would sink to the very dregs of mankind if your women drank as deep as you do. How long do you think they will stay temperate? How long will they resist the example of their own husbands and fathers? Vice never stands still. You will mend your ways, or you will live to see them sink down to your level – it has already happened in Bohemia, where women drink alongside men now. What think you? Would you love to see your wives drunken? Your own mothers, rolling drunk in the gutters?”

A great shout of horror burst from the crowd – medieval congregations did not sit silent and passive while a sermon moved them. “Ah, that strikes home, does it not?” Clement cried. “What madmen you are! You think it shocks your sinful, unreasoning selves to see a woman made a beast of drink – yet you think the all-pure God is less shocked, when man, His noblest creation, debases himself to that very same state?”

He closed the sermon with two vivid, unflinching pictures, laid out in sharp detail: the ragged, hungry home of a drunkard, his children crying for bread, his wife in rags; and the warm, peaceful home of a sober man, his table full, his family safe at his side. They were so true to life that the women in the congregation began to murmur aloud, “Eh, that is the very truth, that word.”

The sermon set the whole town abuzz. Crowds clustered in knots on the streets: the men grumbled and fumed at the preacher for daring to rebuke their favourite pastime, while the women at first made a great show of standing with their husbands, declaring a working man had every right to his ale after a long day’s labour, that they would never trade their men for lily-livered milksops. The very next evening, though, nearly a hundred of those same women swarmed around Brother Clement in the market square, stuffing his alms wallet full of the finest bread and cheese and honey the town had to offer, plucking the roses right out of their hair to lay at his feet, kissing the hem of his coarse Dominican frock and blessing him for daring to rebuke their drunken, sot husbands at last.

Jerome, for his part, had thought the sermon far too focused on earthly, worldly matters. “Drunkenness is no heresy, Clement,” he had protested. “There is no cause to devote an entire sermon to it.” But as they traveled further down the Rhine, he was forced to admit he was surprised: Clement’s sermons sank deeper into their hearers’ hearts than his own ever did, making men stop and think, weep, and even amend their wicked ways. “He has the gift of stooping to the level their souls are at,” Jerome mused. “Yet he can soar high enough when the need calls for it.”

It puzzled Jerome, who had long felt a quiet, secret sense of superiority to his softer, more tender brother. Two hundred miles into their journey, the puzzlement curdled into quiet displeasure – but he reined that feeling in, judging it petty and unworthy. “Souls are like the locks on a door,” he told himself. “Preachers must be like their keys, each shaped differently, or the Church will never open those doors for God to step through. And for certain, this novice holds the right key for these northern souls, for he is a northern man himself, born and raised among them.”

And so they traveled slowly down the Rhine, sometimes drifting a few miles downstream on a passing barge, but more often walking the muddy banks, preaching at every village crossroads, teaching the children, hearing the confessions of thousands of sinners, until at last they stepped onto the quay of Dusseldorf. It was the very same small quay where long ago, Gerard and Denys had climbed aboard a boat to sail up the Rhine. The friars set foot on that same worn stone. The streets were unchanged. The old Silver Lion tavern still hung its familiar sign over the door. Nothing had altered at all – save for the barefoot friar walking slowly through its lanes, his heart calm, his old passions long stilled, hands folded across his breast, eyes meekly bent on the ground, a true, devoted son of Saint Dominic and Holy Church.
