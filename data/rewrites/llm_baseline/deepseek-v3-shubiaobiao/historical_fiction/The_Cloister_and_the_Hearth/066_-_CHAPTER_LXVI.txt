Two nights had passed since Gerard’s strange disappearance, and Pietro Vanucci sat at the supper table, his fingers drumming impatiently against the wood. The hour was late—past nine—and still, Gerard had not returned.

"Where in the devil’s name is that boy?" Pietro muttered, pushing his plate away. "Andrea, go and see if he’s slipped in unnoticed."

Andrea sighed but obeyed, climbing the stairs to Gerard’s chamber. He returned moments later, not with Gerard, but with a slip of parchment clutched in his hand. "This was on his table," he said, his voice uneasy. "It bears your name."

Pietro snatched the note and read it aloud, his tone laced with bitter mockery, though his fingers trembled slightly.

Pietro scoffed. "A burden, is it? Yet not so heavy as to keep him from missing supper."

"Ha! As if my shoulders could bear his sorrows as well as my own."

Andrea’s breath hitched.

*"For my body, ere this ye read, it will lie in Tiber. Trouble not to look for it. 'Tis not worth the pains. Oh unhappy day that it was born—oh happy night that rids me of it."*

Pietro forced a laugh, though his face had gone ashen. "The fool’s jesting. He’ll come slinking in by dawn, mark my words."

But Andrea was already on his feet, his voice rising in panic. "He means to drown himself! Holy saints, help us! We must find him!" He bolted from the room, shouting into the street, calling for aid.

A small crowd gathered, sluggish and reluctant. The Tiber at night was no place for men—too many whispered horrors lurked in its depths. Yet a single figure, a lackey in the shadows, stepped forward. "I’ll go," he said.

And so they set off—Andrea, the lackey, and a handful of others—their footsteps echoing in the moonlit streets. The air grew damp as they neared the river, the scent of water thick in their nostrils. Then, rounding a corner, they saw him—a man, bareheaded, clothes soaked, his hair glistening wet in the pale light.

"There he is!" one cried.

The stranger froze, then fled.

Andrea gave chase, his heart hammering, but the man twisted into a maze of alleys and vanished. When the pursuers regrouped, Pietro shook his head. "That wasn’t Gerard. The build was wrong, the face unfamiliar."

"Then why run?" someone muttered.

They followed the trail of dripping water—not toward the river, but to the looming gates of a monastery. A chill passed through them. Then Andrea spotted the trail again, leading back to the Tiber’s edge.

At the bank, the ground was sodden, as if a man had dragged himself from the water. But was it Gerard? They paid a fisherman to drag the river, nets sinking into the silent depths. The moon watched, indifferent.

Andrea ran home, hoping against hope—but Gerard’s room was empty, his bed untouched.

Dawn came. The search was abandoned.

The days that followed were silent, the Tiber keeping its secrets. And Gerard?

Gone.
