Rotterdam faded from view long before Gerard stirred from his slumber. Margaret, driven by a resolve that felt both desperate and final, had already abandoned her boy, leaving him in the care of Reicht, a man of sturdy loyalty. She dispatched him immediately to Gouda, entrusting the faithful steward with a vicar's grey frock, a broad felt hat, and a set of precise instructions regarding the governance of his new charge.

Once Reicht was gone, Margaret sought out Jorian Ketel. Guided by an internal conviction that he was "the closest" man she had ever encountered, she engaged in two acts of profound cunning with him—deeds she deemed necessary, though perhaps not virtuous, and which she intended to keep hidden until her dying breath. Together, they orchestrated a withdrawal from the Gouda parsonage. Though Margaret was innocent in nature, a sudden, fierce pride mingled with her modesty, whispering a command to sever ties with the manse entirely. She resolved to let little Gerard remain there, believing her departure would allow him to complete the spiritual conquest his mother's heart attributed to him, thereby anchoring his father permanently to the path of humanity.

Yet, this act of policy, though generous in intent, tore at her heart. Having never been parted from her son for an hour, the solitude became unbearable within a single day. Driven by a grief that felt like desolation, she crept to the Gouda parsonage at twilight, lurking like a thief until she spotted Reicht Heynes alone in the kitchen. Softly tapping at the window, she pleaded for him to bring the boy to her unseen. To Margaret, the object of her longing ceased to have a name, sinking simply into the role of "him." Reicht soon found a pretext to escort little Gerard out, resulting in a scene of mutual rapture followed by fresh tears when they were forced apart again.

An arrangement was struck: Reicht would transport the boy halfway to Rotterdam daily at a set hour, meeting Margaret on the way. During these brief reunions, after the initial joy and playful gambols, the boy would occasionally amuse himself at their feet, creating a private space for the two women to speak seriously of Luke Peterson.

"Reicht," Margaret began, her voice heavy with regret, "I essentially promised him to marry Luke Peterson. 'Say the word,' I told him, 'and I will wed.'"

"Poor Luke!" Reicht replied with sympathy.

"Prithee, why 'poor Luke'?" Margaret asked.

"To be tossed back and forth between yes and no."

"Why, Reicht, have you never been so simple as to cast an affectionate eye on the boy?"

"I?" Reicht tossed his head, feigning innocence. "Oh, I ask your pardon. Well, then, you can do me a good turn."

"Whisht! Whisper!" Margaret hushed him sharply, pointing to the boy, who sat listening with eyes wide like saucers. Two women conspiring against a child? A thought that would have made either woman hide beneath a cap if caught.

For about five days, these stolen meetings continued. But eventually, Margaret felt the injustice of the deception, irritation rising alongside her unhappiness. She found herself crying over the situation when a cart pulled up to her door. Seated within, clean as a new coin, with a closely shaved beard and hands white as snow, sat the Vicar of Gouda—the man she had sent away. He wore the grey frock and large felt hat she had purchased for him.

Margaret ran upstairs, washing away the evidence of her tears, and changed into a cap, theoretically cleaner than the one she wore, before descending to greet him. He seized her hands, kissing them as a tear fell upon them. She turned her head away, hiding the start of her own tears.

"My sweet Margaret," he cried, "why hold you aloof from your own good deed? We have waited for you every day."

"You said things," she murmured.

"What! When I was a hermit, and a donkey?"

"Ay! No matter, you said things. And you had no reason."

"Forget all I said there. Who hearkens the ravings of a maniac? For I see now that in a few months I should have been a gibbering idiot; yet no mortal could have persuaded me away but you." Gerard poured out his gratitude, speaking not of the manse, but of the home he had found among the sheep, whom he now loved dearly. "So now I say to you as erst you said to me, come to Gouda manse."

"Humph! We will see about that," Margaret replied coolly, though inwardly burning with desire.

"Why, Margaret, think you I ever kept the dear child so long but that I made sure you would be back to him from day to day?" Gerard argued gently. "Oh, he curls round my very heartstrings, but what is my title to him compared to thine? Confess now, thou hast had hard thoughts of me for this."

"Nay, nay, not I. Ah! thou art thyself again... I have half a mind to go to Gouda manse, for your saying that."

"Come then, with half thy mind, 'tis worth the whole of other folk's."

"Well, I dare say I will; but there is no such mighty hurry," she said, masking her eagerness. "Tell me first how you agree with your folk."

"Already my poor have taken root in my heart," Gerard answered proudly. "Simple, rough, and superstitious, yet wonderfully good."

"Oh, I leave you alone for seeing a grain of good among a bushel of ill," Margaret teased ironically.

"And Margaret," Gerard lowered his voice, "two of them have been ill friends for four years, coming to the manse each to get on my blind side. But give the glory to God, I got on their bright side, made them friends, and made them laugh at their folly."

"But are you in very deed their vicar? Answer me that."

"Certes! Have I not been to the bishop, taken the oath, rung the church bell, and touched the altar, the missal, and the holy cup before the church-wardens? They have handed me the parish seal." He produced it. "Nay, 'tis a real vicar inviting a true friend to Gouda manse."

"Then my mind is at ease. Tell me oceans more."

Gerard spoke of the poor cripple, Sybrandt, whom his guardian angel had placed beneath his roof, and how Sybrandt and Gerard had become brothers in forgiveness. He spoke of Reicht Heynes, describing her voice as a diamond that pierced him like a knife, yet contrasting it with Margaret's mellow sweetness. He recounted tales of local idiosyncrasies—a man sweating to pass for ill-tempered, or the story of the hermit whose cave had collapsed, burying him alive.

"The strange thing is," Gerard continued, "the cave has verily fallen in. Had I been so perverse as to resist thee, it had assuredly buried me dead there where I had buried myself alive. Therefore in this I see the finger of Providence, condemning my late, approving my present, way of life."

"Somewhat more, methinks," Margaret observed. "This very tale proves thee my guardian angel..."

"Well, go you on, I'll follow."

"Nay, in the cart with me."

"Not so."

"Why?"

"Can I tell why and wherefore, being a woman? All I know is I seem—to feel—to wish—to come alone."

"So be it then. I leave thee the cart, being, as thou sayest, a woman, and I'll go a-foot, being a man again, with the joyful tidings of thy coming."

When Margaret finally reached the manse, the first thing she saw was the two Gerards together. The son performed his *capriccios* on the garden plot, while the father slouched on a chair, pencil and paper in hand, trying patiently to sketch him. After a warm welcome, Gerard showed her his failed attempts, lamenting that he could not fix the boy, who moved with the fluidity of quicksilver. "How he melteth from one posture to another!" he exclaimed, throwing the paper away in despair. Margaret, however, secretly conveyed the paper into her bosom.

That night, sitting by the peat fire, Gerard extolled the beauty of the brass candlesticks glowing in the hearth's light. Catherine's eyes sparkled at his observation. Gerard turned his gaze to Margaret, declaring that each age held its beauty. He pointed to her mild grey eye, noting that the motherly love it had shown left a shadow of beauty that defied time, and praised her delicate lip and pure white teeth. "Art beautiful in my eyes, mother dear," he said.

"And that is enough for me, my darling," Margaret replied. "'Tis time you were in bed, child. Ye have to preach the morn."

Reicht Heynes and Catherine exchanged a knowing look, acknowledging they had an amiable maniac to superintend, one who called everything beautiful.

The following Sunday, Gerard preached in his own church. The building was crammed with curious people who remained devout. In a single sermon lasting two hours, he declared the whole scripture, terrifying the impenitent, confirming the wavering, and uplifting the poor.
