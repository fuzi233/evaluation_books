Let no gentle reader be alarmed for Mr. Harry Warrington’s safety, nor imagine that his fall from the broken-kneed horse has carried him beyond the bounds of life and story. Rest assured, nothing of great consequence has befallen him. Why should we so rashly dispatch our heroes in their youth, when they have barely stepped into manhood, and the tale is still unfolding? We mourn already for one of the Virginians who met an untimely end in America—surely we cannot take another in England? No, indeed. Heroes are not swept away without cause, unless there is a pressing need to remove them from the scene. If every gentleman perished at the first stumble of a horse, not only would the hero vanish, but the author himself would long since have been laid low. Yet here, the young man lies not dead, but merely sprawled upon the ground, unconscious yet breathing—soon to be brought within the sheltering walls of Madame de Bernstein’s household, where servants have rung the bell in haste.

To prove that this youngest of the Virginians still draws breath, here is a letter penned by the lady whose home received him after his fall—a letter both tender and truthful:

If Mrs. Esmond Warrington of Virginia can recall twenty-three years past, when Miss Rachel Esmond was at Kensington Boarding School, she may remember Miss Molly Benson, her classmate—now grown and mellowed with time, who has forgotten all the petty quarrels they once had (though she admits she was often in the wrong), and remembers only the generous, spirited, radiant Miss Esmond—the very Princess Pocahontas, whom so many of our schoolmates admired.

Dear madam, I cannot forget that you were once dear Rachel, as I was your dearest Molly. Though we parted on less than friendly terms when you returned to Virginia, I know how deeply we once loved each other. I still possess the gold etui your father gave me on speech-day at Kensington, when we performed the quarrel between Brutus and Cassius from Shakespeare. Only yesterday morning, I dreamed we were called before the formidable Miss Hardwood to recite our lessons, and I failed—while, as always, Miss Rachel Esmond outshone me. How vivid those memories are! How young we seem when we revisit them.

I remember our walks, our exercises, the good King and Queen strolling through Kensington Gardens, followed by their court, while we girls curtsied in perfect line. I can still name what we ate each day, point to the spot where your garden stood—always better kept than mine—and recall how neat your chest of drawers was, compared to my own disordered chaos. Do you remember the tales we spun in the dormitory, interrupted by Madame Hibou, our French governess, hooting from her bed? And the poor dancing-master, who claimed he’d been attacked by assassins—only to be revealed as having been beaten by your brother’s footmen?

My dear, your cousin Lady Maria Esmond—her father was then but Viscount Castlewood—has just visited this house. I did not mention those old sorrows to her, though I now speak of them freely to you, Mrs. Esmond.

Lady Maria stayed with us, along with another relative of yours, the Baroness de Bernstein. Both have journeyed onward to Tunbridge Wells. But one dearer guest remains under my roof—sound asleep, I trust, in the next room. His name is Mr. Henry Esmond Warrington. Now do you understand why I write to you?

Do not fear, dear madam. I know your thoughts: *My son is ill—that is why Miss Molly Benson writes.* No, my dear. Mr. Warrington was unwell yesterday, but today he is much improved. Our doctor—none other than my beloved husband, Colonel Lambert—has bled him, reset his dislocated shoulder, and assures me that within two days he will be fit to travel again.

I confess, I and my daughters are sorry he will recover so quickly. Yesterday evening, during tea, a sudden ringing at the gate startled us all—such a sound is rare in this quiet place, save when a beggar seeks charity. The servants rushed out and returned with news: a young gentleman had fallen from his horse, lying unconscious on the road, surrounded by companions. At once, my husband—ever the most compassionate of men—hurried to help. With the aid of the servants and two ladies, he brought into the house a pale, lifeless, yet strikingly beautiful young man.

Ah, my dear, how glad I am that your child found refuge beneath my roof! That my husband could ease his pain, prevent fever, and restore him to health and you!

We shall be friends again, shall we not? Last year I fell gravely ill; it was feared I might die. In those dark hours, I often thought of you—of how we parted in anger so long ago. I began a letter to you, half-written, which I never finished, telling you that if I were to depart this world, I wished to leave it in peace with every soul I had known.

Lady Maria showed deep concern for her young kinsman after the accident. I believe her heart is truly kind. The Baroness de Bernstein, though aged, was moved until Mr. Warrington regained consciousness, when she expressed a desire to continue her journey to Tunbridge, fearing illness without a physician nearby. My husband laughed and said he would not attend a lady of quality—but he would care for his young patient with all diligence. Indeed, Colonel Lambert has seen many such injuries in his military service, and I assure you, even if we summoned every doctor within twenty miles, Mr. Warrington could not receive better care.

Once settled, the Baroness and Lady Maria took their leave—Lady Maria reluctant to go. When the young gentleman is well enough, my husband will ride with him as far as Westerham, where an old army comrade of his resides. This letter will not reach Falmouth until, by God’s grace, your son is fully restored. So rest easy, dear madam. He is safe under the roof of your affectionate, humble servant,

I am delighted to hear (Mr. Warrington’s colored servant informed us) that Providence has blessed Mrs. Esmond with great wealth and a son worthy of it. Our means are sufficient, but would be small divided among our survivors. Ah, dear madam, I have heard of your loss last year. Though the Colonel and I have raised five children, we have lost two. A mother’s heart knows sorrow, and I grieved for yours. I confess, when the boy spoke of his dear brother—so gently, so tenderly—I felt a pang for him. It is impossible not to love him. I am grateful we could offer him shelter, and that in welcoming a stranger, we welcomed the son of an old friend.

There is something about certain men—written plainly upon their faces—that commands trust wherever they go. Harry Warrington bore such a countenance in youth. His eyes sparkled with honesty, his cheeks glowed with health, his expression open and true. Even those who sought to deceive him found themselves believing in him. Yet beneath that innocent air lay a mind sharper than it seemed. He was not the artless boy he appeared. Perhaps more cunning in youth than he ever became in age.

It is said that honest men grow simpler with time—learning to judge swiftly, to discard falsehoods easily, to strike truth with fewer missteps. Or is it merely the delusion of age, that our vanities fade, and we grow wiser in seeing our own faults and those of others?

Yet let us say this: young people, though fairer in face and freer in spirit, are often as clever as their elders. What little masters of deceit are our frank schoolboys! How they cheat their mothers, hoodwink their fathers, fool the housekeeper, cringe before the older boys they serve at school! What a web of lies and flattery they weave toward Dr. Birch! And their sisters—do they fare any better? Do they not learn their tricks only when they enter society?

From Mrs. Lambert’s letter, we see she, like most good women (and many not-so-good ones), was sentimental. As she watched Harry Warrington lie in her best bed—after the Colonel had bled him and set his shoulder—she held his hand and saw him drift into sleep, murmuring softly, a faint blush on his cheek. She confessed he was a lovely lad—more handsome than either of her sons. Jack, at Oxford, might have been so, had smallpox spared him. As for Charles—

“Always took after his papa,” said the Colonel, gazing at his own honest face in a little mirror with a japanned frame.

“Did I say so?” whispered Mrs. Lambert, startled.

“No,” replied the Colonel, “but you thought it.”

“How can you read my thoughts, Martin?”

“Because I am a conjurer,” he smiled, “and because you tell them aloud. You never see a young man without comparing him to your own. You never hear of one without wondering which daughter he’ll marry.”

“Don’t be foolish, sir,” she said, placing a hand over his lips.

They had quietly left the guest’s chamber and now stood in the adjoining dressing-room—a cozy, wainscoted space overlooking gardens, with India curtains, Japanese chests, fine china, and the sweet scent of lavender.

“You can’t deny it, Mrs. Lambert,” the Colonel continued. “You were just now thinking: Which of my girls will he marry? Theo or Hester? And then Lucy, at boarding school?”

“There’s no hiding anything from you, Martin,” sighed the wife.

“There’s no hiding it from your eyes, my dear. What is this strange longing all women have—to sell off their daughters? We men don’t wish to part with them. I confess, I wouldn’t care for this young fellow half as much if I thought he meant to carry one of my darlings away.”

“But surely, Martin,” she replied, looking at him with her warmest gaze, “since I’ve been so happy myself, I must wish my girls to find happiness too!”

“Then you think good husbands are common,” he teased, “that you might walk down the road and pick one up like a sack of coals?”

“Wasn’t it providential,” she asked, “that this young man should fall at our very gate—and turn out to be the son of my old schoolfriend?”

“There is more than chance in such things,” said the Colonel. “Depend upon it.”

“And this was the stranger you saw in the candle three nights running?”

“And in the fire, too,” she added. “Twice, a coal leapt near Theo. You may laugh, but these signs are not to be ignored. Did I not see you clearly returning from Minorca, and dream of you at the exact hour you were wounded in Scotland?”

“How many times have you seen me wounded when I was untouched, my dear? How many times have you foreseen my illness when I was well? You are always prophesying—and it’s lucky for you that you’re sometimes right. Come, let us leave our guest sleeping peacefully and go below to give the girls their French lesson.”

So saying, the Colonel took his wife’s arm, and together they descended the broad oak staircase of the old hall, lined with portraits of generations of Lamberts—worthy magistrates, soldiers, country gentlemen, including the Colonel himself.

He was a man of cheerful wit. The French lesson he shared with his daughters was a scene from Molière’s *Tartuffe*. He played Orgon with playful mockery, addressing his daughter Theo as “Madam” and treating her with mock solemnity. They read together with laughter and charm, and Papa especially relished Orgon’s lines:

> **ORGON:** Or sus, nous voila bien. J'ai, Mariane, en vous
> Reconnu de tout temps un esprit assez doux,
> Et de tout temps aussi vous m'avez ete chere.
>
> **MARIANE:** Je suis fort redevable a cet amour de pere.
>
> **ORGON:** Fort bien. Que dites-vous de Tartuffe notre hote?
>
> **MARIANE:** Qui? Moi?
>
> **ORGON:** Vous. Voyez bien comme vous repondrez.
>
> **MARIANE:** Helas! J'en dirai, moi, tout ce que vous voudrez!
> *(Miss Theo laughs and blushes, unable to contain herself.)*
>
> **ORGON:** C'est parler sagement. Dites-moi donc, ma fille,
> Qu'en toute sa personne un haut merite brille,
> Qu'il touche votre coeur, et qu'il vous seroit doux
> De le voir par men choix devenir votre epoux!

“Have we not read it well, Elmire?” the Colonel laughed, turning to his wife.

Elmire adored Orgon’s performance—just as she adored everything her husband did.

O reader, can you count upon the faithfulness of pure hearts? Can you call among life’s blessings the love of loyal women? Then purify your own soul, and strive to make it worthy of such devotion. Kneel, and give thanks for this gift above all others. For all the prizes of ambition—wealth, pleasure, power—are but vanity and disappointment, fiercely grasped and bitterly abandoned. But love endures beyond life. I believe it carries us beyond the grave. Do we not still give it to those who are gone? May we hope they feel it for us, and that we leave it behind in one or two tender hearts when we too pass?

And why this reflection? Because I know more of the Lamberts than you do—how could you, who have never met them before? You may not like them. Few people do when introduced with such fervent praise. You might say: *Is this all? Are these the people he admires so? The girl isn’t beautiful—the mother is kind, perhaps, but no longer youthful—and the father… well, quite ordinary.*

Granted. But do you not feel something sweet and moving in the sight of an honest man, with a loving wife at his side, and children who cherish him? When you see such a family gathered round him, their faces alight with affection, their confidence plain in every glance—can you remain unmoved? If you stay in such a house, and see them gather in prayer at dawn or dusk—do you not join humbly in their petitions, and close them with a reverent *Amen*?

That first night at Oakhurst, Harry Warrington, drugged and restless, thought he heard the Evening Hymn—and that his dear brother George was singing it at home. In that moment of illusion, he drifted back into peaceful sleep.
