A profound sense of remorse for his past follies surged within Harry Warrington as he realized he was a prisoner in a dismal lock-up. Anger and indignation flared at the humiliation of arrest, yet he quickly convinced himself that this misery would be fleeting. He possessed twenty friends capable of securing his release; the only dilemma was deciding which one to approach. There was Mr. Draper, the pragmatic businessman who had previously bowed low to him; his benevolent uncle, the Baronet, who had offered his estate as Harry's home and loved him as a son; his cousin Castlewood, from whom he had gambled away vast sums; his aristocratic cronies at the Chocolate House; and his good Aunt Bernstein. Any of these would undoubtedly intervene, though some relatives might also deliver a stern lecture regarding his imprudence.

The paramount concern, however, was discretion. Mr. Warrington feared that the public knowledge of a gentleman of his stature undergoing such a vulgar procedure would cause a scandal. Yet, he reasoned cynically that secrecy was an illusion. "As if the world does not eventually learn everything it cares to know!" he mused. "As if the dinner I enjoy today or the hole in my stocking right now remains hidden from some prying enemy, despite my locked door and polished boots?" He acknowledged that while society could pry into details, there was a solace in the realization that the world ultimately did not care. Scandal was merely vanity. One should tumble down, brush off the mud, smile, and proceed as if nothing had happened. Society would continue to eat, joke, and yawn regardless of whether one graced itself or disgraced itself. To think otherwise was sheer conceit.

Yet, Mr. Harry Warrington chafed and fumed as though the entire world was trembling with anticipation of his downfall.

*"What a sensation my arrest must have caused at the club!"* Harry thought bitterly. *"Mr. Selwyn will surely be cracking jokes about my misfortune. Everyone at the table will know. March will tremble over the bet I owe him, and I shall struggle to pay it. They will cheer at the Savage Tavern, calling me 'the Prisoner.' How can I ever face the world again? Whom shall I ask for help?"*

With a mixture of sharp intellect and naive simplicity, Harry resolved not to contact his relations or his noble friends at White's immediately. Instead, he decided to seek the counsel of Sampson, the chaplain, who had faced similar predicaments before. As dawn broke—delayed in Harry's perception as if the sun had forgotten Cursitor Street—he dispatched a messenger to Long Acre, begging the reverend for advice and comfort.

Unbeknownst to Harry, sending for Sampson was akin to declaring, "I have fallen among lions; come down into the pit with me." Harry likely assumed Sampson's troubles were resolved or simply remained too absorbed in his own chaos to consider his neighbor's plight. Once the message was sent, the captive felt slightly relieved and ordered breakfast. When the attendant asked if he wished to dine privately or join other gentlemen at Mrs. Bailiff's table, Harry firmly declined. He intended to leave before dinner time. The chamberlain, skeptical of such declarations from young gentlemen, closed the door, remarking that there was good beef and carrots available at two o'clock if Harry chose to stay.

While Harry awaited Sampson, his messenger returned with a note stating the chaplain would arrive shortly. However, ten o'clock passed, then eleven, then noon, and Sampson did not appear. Just as despair set in, Gumbo, Harry's enslaved valet, burst in carrying a portmanteau of his master's clothes. Roaring with feigned grief, Gumbo threw himself at Harry's feet, vowing to sell himself into slavery anew rather than allow his beloved master to suffer.

Touched by the display, Harry commanded Gumbo to rise and assist him with dressing, strictly ordering him to keep silent about the arrest. "Mind you, not a word, sir, to anyone!" Harry insisted severely.

"Oh no, sir! Never to anybody!" Gumbo declared solemnly, proceeding to dress his master with meticulous care. He powdered Harry's hair and arranged his attire so perfectly that Harry looked as though he were stepping directly into a carriage bound for St. James's Palace.

Gumbo performed every task love and servility allowed, yet he lacked the power to alter the past or maintain his silence. The nature of slavery, both black and white, relied heavily on deception; servants often hid behind subterfuge like hunted animals seeking cover. These were strange relics of feudalism, where domestics became hypocrites, unable to speak the whole truth to their masters. Despite swearing an oath of silence, Gumbo's loquacious nature betrayed him. With a heart full of genuine sorrow but a tongue incapable of restraint, he had already broadcast the news to countless acquaintances, particularly those wearing shoulder knots and worsted lace. He had spread the tale to Colonel Lambert's and Lord Wrotham's households, boasted at the footman's club frequented by high nobility, recounted the story while drinking ale in Sir Miles Warrington's butler's room, and finally visited Madame Bernstein's circle and Lord Castlewood's household. Thus, when Gumbo later swore to his master that he would tell "nobody," the credibility of his claim was nonexistent.

By mid-afternoon, still no Sampson had arrived. Finally, a letter bearing a damp seal and fresh ink reached Harry. It read:

*"Oh, sir, dear sir, I have done all that a man can at the command and in the behalf of his patron! You did not know, sir, to what you were subjecting me, did you? Else, if I was to go to prison, why did I not share yours, and why am I in a lock-up house three doors off?*

*Such is the fact. As I was hastening to you, knowing full well the danger to which I was subject... I was seized by two villains who had a writ against me, and who have lodged me at Naboth's, hard by, and so close to your honour, that we could almost hear each other across the garden walls of the respective houses where we are confined.*

*I had much and of importance to say, which I do not care to write down on paper regarding your affairs. May they mend! May my cursed fortunes, too, better themselves, is the prayer of—*

Since Sampson refused to speak personally, the reader must be informed of the matters the chaplain deemed unworthy of paper.

Sampson remained unaware of the full extent of Harry's calamity until receiving the letter and messenger from Chancery Lane. Still ardent in his gratitude for the service Harry had recently rendered him, the chaplain eagerly sought a way to aid his distressed patron. Knowing the large sum Lord Castlewood had won from his cousin, Sampson rushed to the nobleman's residence, hoping to stir pity. He delivered an eloquent and moving plea regarding his kinsman's misfortune, speaking with genuine kindness and sympathy. However, his efforts failed to move Lord Castlewood.

"My lord peevishly and curtly put a stop to the chaplain's passionate pleading," wrote the narrative. "Did I not tell you, two days since, when you came for money, that I was as poor as a beggar, Sampson?' said his lordship. 'Has anybody left me a fortune since? The little sum I won from my cousin was swallowed up by others. I not only can't help Mr. Warrington, but, as I pledge you my word, not being in the least aware of his calamity, I had positively written to him this morning to ask him to help me.'" Indeed, a letter to this effect had reached Harry's lodgings via the penny post.

"I must get him money, my lord," cried the chaplain desperately. "Were I to pawn my cassock and bands, he must have money."

"Amen. Go and pawn your bands, your cassock, anything you please. Your enthusiasm does you credit," replied the lord, resuming his reading while Sampson departed in deep despondency.

Meanwhile, Lady Maria had heard rumors of the chaplain's meeting with her brother and guessed the subject. Seizing him as he exited the futile interview, she drew him into the dining room, her face marked by profound grief. "Tell me, what is this has happened to Mr. Warrington?" she asked.

"Your ladyship, then, knows?" asked the chaplain.

"Have I not been in mortal anxiety ever since his servant brought the dreadful news last night?" Lady Maria countered. "We had it as we came from the opera—from my Lady Yarmouth's box—my lord, my Lady Castlewood, and I."

"His lordship, then, did know?" continued Sampson.

"Benson told the news when we came from the playhouse to our tea," confirmed Lady Maria.

Patience snapped for the chaplain. "This is too bad," he exclaimed, swearing, and recounted his frustrating conversation with Lord Castlewood, detailing the nobleman's refusal to help his cousin despite winning huge sums from him, all while ignoring Harry's past generosity toward the chaplain himself.

Lady Maria then erupted into a furious tirade against her own family. Though rarely truthful, when familial disputes arose, she revealed shocking facts.
