The first crimson rays of Barsoom’s dying sun were just gilding the obsidian spires of the Temple of Reward when a squad of Zat Arras’ elite Zodangan officers arrived at our quarters, their long radium rifles glinting in the dawn, to march us to judgement. We filed out in pairs, flanked on all sides by armed guards, and tramped down the broad, marble Aisle of Hope, its worn surface polished smooth by ten thousand pilgrims’ feet. Three unbroken ranks of Zodangan mercenaries lined every inch of the aisle on both sides, a steel wall between us and the rest of the ten thousand spectators packed shoulder to shoulder in the temple’s circular coliseum.

When we stepped up onto the raised dais called the Throne of Righteousness, my blood ran cold at the sight of the 31 judges assembled in a great ring around the platform’s edge. By Barsoomian custom they were supposed to be drawn by lot from Helium’s noble class, to judge their fellow nobles fairly—but not one friendly face looked back at me. Almost to a man they were Zodangans, every last one seething with the old, bitter grudge of the day I’d led Tars Tarkas’ green hordes over their walls, sacked their avenues, and dragged their proud city into vassalage to Helium. There would be no justice for John Carter here, nor for my son Carthoris, nor for the great Thark warlord who’d overrun their streets.

The temple had hummed with low, restless conversation until that moment, but the second we halted on the dais, a silence as thick as the cold vacuum of Barsoom’s empty dead sea beds fell over the crowd. Our guards retreated to the foot of the dais stairs, leaving us facing the ring of judges and the thousands of eyes fixed on us, our backs to the tiny Pedestal of Truth set dead-center in the platform. Zat Arras, the pretender Jed of Helium who’d usurped power in Tardos Mors’ absence, sat perched in the gilded presiding magistrate’s chair, his cruel red lips curling as he called my name.

“John Carter, once Prince of Helium,” he boomed, his voice echoing off the domed ceiling, “step to the Pedestal of Truth, that you may be judged for your deeds, and receive the reward you have earned.” He turned to address the crowd, his tone rising to a fiery, accusatory pitch. “Judges, people of Helium! This man admits he has returned from the forbidden Valley Dor, from the very inner sanctum of Issus herself! In front of dozens of your own citizens he has blasphemed against Iss, against the Lost Sea of Korus, against the Holy Therns, and against Issus, Goddess of Death and Life Eternal. The ancient law of Barsoom is clear: he who has passed through the gates of the afterlife may not return. Any man who violates that sanctity must be made dead forever. What sentence do you pass on this blasphemer?”

“Death!” the shout ripped from the throat of a Zodangan judge, and a roar of agreement rose from his colleagues before anyone else could speak.

Then a man in the crowd vaulted over the low barrier separating the audience from the aisle, his sword drawn high over his head. It was Kantos Kan, the loyal head of Helium’s navy. “Justice!” he screamed. “Justice! Justice!” He dodged past the stunned Zodangan soldiers and leaped clean onto the Throne of Righteousness, facing Zat Arras down. “You dare call this a trial? The man has not spoken a single word in his own defense, not been allowed to call a single witness! In the name of all Helium, I demand fair treatment for your prince.”

Ten thousand voices roared back his cry, the stone floors shaking under the weight of it, and Zat Arras dared not refuse. “Speak then,” he snarled at me, “but utter no blasphemy against Barsoom’s sacred truths.”

I turned my back on the sneering Zodangan judges, and spoke directly to the crowd. “Men of Helium, I do not beg justice from the men of Zodanga. I do not ask it. I speak to you, not for myself, but for your wives, your daughters, your children yet unborn. I have seen what waits for them beyond the River Iss. I have seen the coiling, sucking tentacles of the plant men that drag pilgrims down to be devoured. I have seen the fangs of the great white apes of Dor. I have seen the Holy Therns drag your fairest women off to their mountain lairs, to be tormented and killed for sport. The afterlife your priests tell you of is a lie, a cruel sham built on the blood of your loved ones.”

I went on, recounting my days as a stranger from Earth, fighting beside their people, sacrificing my own joy to save Helium from certain destruction years before. For two full hours I spoke, and the crowd hung on every word, their cheers shaking the temple so hard the guttering incense torches flickered on their brackets. A great noble from the far tiers shouted “Speak on, John Carter, Prince of Helium!” and the whole temple echoed him.

But when the last word left my mouth, Zat Arras sneered and turned to his judges. “You have heard his so-called defense. He has used every second he was given to spew more blasphemy. What is your verdict?”

“Death to the blasphemer!” the first judge roared, and in a heartbeat all 31 were on their feet, their swords raised high in unanimous, glittering agreement.

A low, sullen murmur rippled through the crowd, swelling fast to a roar of rage. Kantos Kan, who had stood at my side the whole time, threw up a hand for silence. “You see the fate the Zodangan occupiers would serve on your greatest hero!” he called. “Every man here may choose what to do. This is the answer of Kantos Kan, High Admiral of Helium’s Navy, to Zat Arras.” He unbuckled his sword, and laid it clattering at my feet.

Hundreds of Helium’s citizens, soldiers and nobles and laborers alike, surged past the stunned Zodangan line, swarming up onto the dais, until a hundred sharp steel blades lay in a glittering pile at my boots. Zat Arras turned purple with rage, screaming for his Zodangan soldiers to arrest us, but the whole temple drew their blades in answer. A shout went up that split the air: “Tardos Mors is dead! A thousand years to John Carter, Jeddak of Helium!”

I saw a civil war seconds away, brother slaying brother in the temple, and I leaped back onto the Pedestal of Truth, screaming for silence. “Hold! Not a single sword moves! A single blow here today will tear Helium apart for generations. No man’s life is worth that. I will not be the cause of blood between our own people. We will strike a bargain. The sentence stands, but no execution will be carried out for one full year—until Tardos Mors, or his son Mors Kajak, returns to Helium. They will judge me fairly.” I leaned close to Zat Arras, my voice low and sharp enough to cut steel. “You take this offer, or this crowd tears you and every last one of your Zodangan guards to shreds right now. You have ten seconds to choose.”

Zat Arras paled. He turned to the crowd, and yelled the respite, ordering them to disperse. They did not move, every eye fixed on me, waiting for my order to attack. I stepped to the edge of the dais, and bade them go home peacefully. As one man, they turned, filing out silent and still raging, past the Zodangan guards who could do nothing but glower in helpless fury.

Kantos Kan led us out of the temple, down the Aisle of Hope, a hundred loyal Heliumites flanking us. The crowds in the city streets lined the route to my palace, flinging their swords at my feet in token of their loyalty, my old slaves rushing to kiss my hands the second we crossed the gates. They cared nothing for the blasphemy charges, only that their prince had come home.

The welcome feast was a somber, quiet affair, held in my great dining hall around the triangular table custom built for three: Carthoris, Dejah Thoris, and myself. Dejah’s carved high-backed throne sat empty at the third side, draped in her glittering wedding jewels, a slave standing behind it just as he had when she sat laughing in that seat, her bright voice ringing through the hall. Tars Tarkas, his bulk easily filling the oversized carved chair at Dejah’s right hand, had just learned that his own daughter Sola had followed Dejah Thoris on her pilgrimage to Dor. No flicker of grief crossed his green, hard features, but I knew the pain in his chest matched my own. No man spoke much: every heart was heavy with Dejah’s absence, and fear for the missing Jeddak Tardos Mors.

Then we heard it: distant shouting, swelling fast, growing louder until the whole palace shook with it. A slave burst into the hall screaming, “A messenger! A messenger from the Valley Dor! Dejah Thoris is found!”

I did not waste a single second walking all the way around the three-hundred-foot long table. I bounded, cleared the entire spread of dishes and diners in a single leap, and crashed through the far windows onto the balcony, vaulted thirty feet down to the scarlet lawn, and sprinted for the gates. The rider dismounting from the great thoat was Sola, her green face streaked with tears.

“Where is the Princess?” I cried, skidding to a halt before her.

“O my Prince,” she sobbed, running to me, “she is gone. The black pirates of Barsoom stole her. Even now she may be a prisoner on the lesser moon, and we may never get her back.”
