It was the spring of 1849 when I found myself wandering in the East. Only recently had I altered the travel itinerary I had meticulously planned months prior—a plan I had formally submitted to both my lawyer and my banker in London. This sudden change necessitated sending one of my servants to retrieve my correspondence and funds from the British Consul in a specific city, a stop I had since removed from my revised schedule. He was instructed to rendezvous with me at a designated location and time. However, an unfortunate accident, for which he bore no blame, delayed his mission. Consequently, my party and I spent a week encamped on the fringes of a vast desert, waiting in vain.

At the conclusion of that week, the missing man finally appeared at the entrance of my tent, carrying my money and letters. "I fear I bring you bad news, sir," he announced, pointing to a single envelope distinguished by a black mourning border and addressed in the handwriting of Mr. Bruff.

In matters of such gravity, nothing is quite as unbearable as suspense. That letter with the mourning border was the first I broke open. It contained the devastating news of my father's death and confirmed that I was now the sole heir to his immense fortune. Yet, this windfall came with heavy responsibilities. Mr. Bruff urgently implored me to return to England immediately.

By daybreak the following morning, I was already on my way back to my homeland.

My old friend, Gabriel Betteredge, later painted a portrait of my departure that, in his own quaint fashion, perhaps ran slightly overboard. He took one of my young mistress's satirical jibes regarding my foreign education far too seriously, convincing himself that he genuinely perceived distinct French, German, and Italian facets to my character. These nuances, which my lively cousin merely teased about in jest, existed nowhere in reality save within Betteredge's fertile imagination. Barring this exaggeration, however, he told the truth: he accurately depicted how deeply wounded I was by Rachel's treatment and how I fled England in the raw agony of life's bitterest disappointment.

I had gone abroad with a singular resolve: if change and distance could aid me, I intended to forget her. I firmly believe that denying the power of change and absence to heal a broken heart is a false view of human nature. Indeed, they *did* help. They forced my attention away from the relentless contemplation of my sorrow. I never truly forgot her, but the sharpest sting of memory dulled gradually, as time, miles, and new experiences erected effective barriers between Rachel and myself.

Conversely, it is equally undeniable that the moment I turned homeward, the remedy that had worked so steadily began to falter. As I drew closer to the land she inhabited and the prospect of seeing her again grew tangible, her influence regained its grip with irresistible force. Upon leaving England, she was the very last person whose name I would have allowed upon my lips. Returning, she became the first person I inquired about the moment Mr. Bruff and I reunited.

Of course, I was briefed on every event of my absence—in other words, on the entire saga recounted here, continuing Betteredge's narrative—with one crucial exception. Mr. Bruff did not deem it proper to reveal the private motivations that had led Rachel and Godfrey Ablewhite to recall their mutual marriage promises. I spared him the embarrassment of probing this delicate subject. After the jealous torment of learning she had once considered becoming Godfrey's wife, mere knowledge that reflection had convinced her of her own rashness and secured her release from the engagement was sufficient relief.

Having absorbed the history of the past, my inquiries naturally shifted to the present. Who cared for her now that she had left Mr. Bruff's house? Where was she residing? I learned she lived under the guardianship of a widowed sister of the late Sir John Verinder—Mrs. Merridew—who had been entrusted with the role by her mother's executors and had graciously accepted. Reports suggested they were getting along splendidly, currently established for the season at Mrs. Merridew's residence in Portland Place.

Half an hour after receiving this intelligence, I was en route to Portland Place, lacking the courage to disclose my intentions to Mr. Bruff. The man who answered the door hesitated, unsure if Miss Verinder was at home. I dispatched him upstairs with my card, hoping for a swift resolution. He returned with an impenetrable expression, informing me that Miss Verinder was out.

One might suspect others of deliberately denying me access, but it was impossible to doubt Rachel. I left a note requesting a visit at six o'clock that evening.

At six o'clock, the servant informed me for the second time that Miss Verinder was not at home. Was there a message for me? None. Had she even received my card? The servant apologized profusely; yes, she had. The implication was clear: Rachel refused to see me.

On my part, I could not accept such treatment without attempting to uncover the cause. I sent my name to Mrs. Merridew, asking for a personal interview at her earliest convenience. To my relief, Mrs. Merridew agreed to receive me immediately. I was ushered into a cozy little sitting room and confronted a pleasant, elderly lady. She expressed profound regret and surprise entirely on my behalf. Yet, she was powerless to offer an explanation or pressure Rachel on what she deemed a matter of purely private sentiment. With a polite patience that seemed endless, she repeated this refrain until I gained nothing further from her.

My final recourse was to write to Rachel. My servant delivered the letter the next day with strict orders to await a reply. The response arrived, consisting of a single, stinging sentence: *"Miss Verinder begs to decline entering into any correspondence with Mr. Franklin Blake."*

Though fond of her, I felt indignant at the insult implicit in that curt dismissal. Just as I was composing a rebuttal, Mr. Bruff entered on business matters. I dismissed his concerns instantly and laid the entire situation before him. He proved just as unable to shed light on the mystery as Mrs. Merridew. When I asked if any slander had been spoken against me in Rachel's hearing, he denied knowing of any. Had she mentioned me while under his roof? Never. Had she not even asked, during my long absence, if I was alive or dead? Not a word had passed her lips.

From my pocket-book, I produced the letter Lady Verinder had written to me from Frizinghall on the day I departed her house in Yorkshire. I drew Mr. Bruff's attention to two specific lines: *"The valuable assistance which you rendered to the inquiry after the lost jewel is still an unpardoned offence, in the present dreadful state of Rachel's mind. Moving blindfold in this matter, you have added to the burden of anxiety which she has had to bear, by innocently threatening her secret with discovery through your exertions."*

"Is it possible," I asked, "that the feeling toward me described there remains as bitter as ever?"

Mr. Bruff feigned deep distress. "If you demand an answer," he admitted, "I can interpret her conduct no other way."

I rang the bell, instructing my servant to pack my trunk and fetch a railway guide. Mr. Bruff, astonished, asked what I intended to do.

"I am going to Yorkshire," I replied, "by the next train."

"May I inquire the purpose?"

"Mr. Bruff, the assistance I innocently gave in searching for the Diamond was an unpardonable offense in Rachel's mind nearly a year ago, and it remains so today. I cannot accept that position! I am determined to unravel the secret behind her silence to her mother and her hostility toward *me*. If time, effort, and money suffice, I will lay my hands on the thief who stole the Moonstone!"

The worthy old gentleman attempted to remonstrate, urging me to listen to reason and fulfill my duties. I remained deaf to every plea. No earthly consideration could shake the steel-hard resolution gripping me.

"I shall resume the inquiry exactly where I left off," I declared. "I will follow it step by step until I reach the present. There are missing links in the evidence as I left it, which Gabriel Betteredge can supply—and to Gabriel Betteredge I go!"

Towards sunset that evening, I stood once more on the well-known terrace, gazing at the tranquil old country house. The gardener was the first soul I spotted in the deserted grounds. He had left Betteredge an hour earlier, basking in the usual corner of the backyard. Knowing this, I decided to seek him out personally. Walking round the familiar paths, I peered through the open gate of the yard.

There he was—the dear old companion of happier days that would never return. There he sat in the old corner on the weathered beehive chair, pipe in mouth, *Robinson Crusoe* on his lap, flanked by his two canine friends dozing peacefully on either side.

From my vantage point, the last slanting rays of the sun cast my shadow directly in front of me. Whether the dogs sensed it visually or by scent, they sprang up with a low growl. Startled, the old man quieted them with a gentle word, then shielded his failing eyes with his hand and looked inquiringly at the figure at the gate. Tears filled my own eyes, forcing me to pause a moment before I could summon the strength to speak.
