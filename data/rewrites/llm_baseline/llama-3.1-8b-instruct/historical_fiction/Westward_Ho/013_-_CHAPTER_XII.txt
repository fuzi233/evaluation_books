As I strolled along the tranquil banks of the river, I pondered the mystique surrounding Bideford Bridge, an edifice so integral to the town's very essence that its presence seemed woven into the fabric of the community. Like Edinburgh's castle, Rome's capitol, and Egypt's pyramids, Bideford Bridge stood as a testament to the ingenuity and foresight of its creators, its significance rivaling that of any national landmark. Few, however, were aware of the bridge's extraordinary properties, acquired over the course of five centuries, which rendered it an inspired, soul-saving, alms-giving, educational, sentient, and – most notably – a dinner-giving bridge.

Legend has it that the bridge's construction was overseen by unseen hands, which transported stones down the river each night to their final destination. One evening, Sir Richard Gurney, the local parson, was visited in his sleep by an angelic vision, instructing him to build the bridge precisely where the materials had been secretly placed. This was done to ensure a stable foundation, as the area was notorious for its shifting sands. Furthermore, Bishop Grandison of Exeter issued indulgences and benedictions to those who contributed to the bridge's construction, acknowledging the dual benefits it would bring to both body and soul.

As I continued my walk, I stumbled upon an old proverb: though the bridge's foundation was laid upon the soft earth of the riverbank, it shook precariously at the slightest touch. Another tale spoke of William Alford, a man renowned for his strength, who carried four bushels of saltwater measure, the entire length of the bridge, on his back for a wager. The bridge's coat of arms featured a ship and a bridge, symbolizing its role as a guardian of the community, which it supported through various charitable endeavors, including founding schools, funding lawsuits, and hosting lavish feasts. The latter, in particular, had become an annual tradition, with the bridge maintaining an impressive cellar stocked with the finest wines in Devon.

It was during one such celebration, held in 1583, that a heated debate arose among the esteemed guests regarding the merits of salmon caught above versus below the bridge. Mr. St. Leger of Annery, a kind-hearted gentleman and brother of the marshal of Munster, proposed a wager: if he could catch a firmer, flakier salmon from the pool below Annery, he would donate five pounds to the bridge.
