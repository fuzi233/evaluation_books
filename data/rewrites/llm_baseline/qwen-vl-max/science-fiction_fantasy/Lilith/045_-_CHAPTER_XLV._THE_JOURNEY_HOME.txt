The darkness had lifted, not into full daylight, but into a soft, luminous twilight where the breath of spring filled the air with quiet wonder. It was as if the world itself had awakened—not just the earth, but something deeper within us. We walked barefoot across the land, and every blade of grass, every heather bush, every low shrub glowed with an inner light. Not from the sky, nor from any sun yet risen, but from within themselves—like the burning bush Moses once saw, untouched by flame yet blazing with divine presence. Or perhaps it was our eyes that now emitted their own light, seeing not merely shapes and colors, but the living thoughts that shaped them—the soul of each thing made visible.

There were no shadows. Nothing cast one upon another. Instead, all things exchanged light, like whispers between friends. I could see in the form and hue of every plant the idea that gave it life—the thought that birthed it, sustained it, and sent it forth into being. My feet, touching the soil, felt a tenderness for each plant they passed over, as though they knew them intimately. The world and I were no longer separate. Life flowed through me, and I through life. The microcosm—the self—was at last united with the macrocosm—the whole. Harmony had been restored. I lived in all things; all things lived in me. To perceive a creature was to know its essence, my own, and the source from which we both came. We were what we were because Another was what He was.

Senses long dormant stirred awake—one after another, beyond naming, beyond description. There were no words for these new perceptions, no images to mirror them. Yet they flooded through me: a vast, ever-expanding awareness, like a chamber with countless doors opening simultaneously, letting in light, sound, feeling, memory—everything entering, everything welcomed.

When a breeze brushed against a cluster of heather, setting its purple bells to chiming softly—*tin-tin-tin*—I did not hear it; I *was* it. I felt the joy of the wind, the vibration of the flowers, the delight of the sensation, and the soul that received them all. I became the hall where joy could celebrate. I was a calm ocean, endlessly stirred by waves of living gladness—each wave different, yet all born of the same eternal joy. Life was no longer struggle or sorrow. It was celebration—a cosmic holiday.

Now I understood: life and truth are one. Pure life is bliss. Where there is no bliss, there is no true life—only existence in death. With every breath drawn from the dark wind, I exhaled thanksgiving. At last, I *was*. I lived—and nothing could take that life from me.

Beside me walked my beloved, and together we journeyed home—to the Father.

Already, before the first sun rose upon our freedom, we possessed more than we could have dreamed. What wonders must await us in the eternal day?

We reached the hollow where ancient monsters once roamed—where nightmares had bred beneath the earth. But now it was transformed. A lake lay there, clear and still, its waters so pure I could see deep into its depths. A great whirlpool had swept away the soil where those abominations once burrowed. And there, at the bottom, lay the entire horrid brood—coiled, folded, knotted, stretched—forms more grotesque than any fevered poet’s imagination could conjure. Their shapes twisted in unnatural spirals, their bodies bloated, their eyes wide with sepulchral horror. Tentacles curled like serpents, limbs fused into monstrous unions. They were the very embodiment of hatred and decay—yet none moved.

They were not dead. Not truly. So long as men and women harbor unclean minds, this lake will remain their tomb. But even in their stillness, they spoke of a deeper truth: evil persists only when it is nurtured by the heart.

Then—listen! The herald of dawn! The auroral wind, soft as a trumpet call, announced the coming of the sun. The master-minister of the human body approached. Rising like a ship launched from the hand of creation, he surged upward, carrying a wave of crimson and gold ahead of him. He paused—gazed down upon the world.

A storm of molten metal, white-hot and roaring—he was but a coal from the eternal altar, offered by the Father to His children. See how every flower straightens its stem, lifts its head, and waits. Not merely for light, but for something greater—for the One who is coming. Not soon, not tomorrow, not in ten thousand years—but *He is coming*, surely, inevitably. What does time matter to Life Himself? To Love Himself?

He is coming. And humanity stretches its necks to watch.

Every morning they will stretch. Every evening they will wait. Until He comes.

Is this only a vision painted on the air? When He arrives, will He find them still watching?

It was resurrection morning. The night had been spent preparing for this moment.

Before us, the children danced, laughing, running free. Behind us, the beasts followed—silent companions in our return. Butterflies fluttered like rainbows, dragonflies darted like sparks, swirling around us in a cloud of color and motion—now descending like a snowfall of prismatic flakes, now rising into the damp air like vapor thick with perfume.

This was summer—not as man had known it, but as it was meant to be: perfect, ideal, untouched by decay. I walked upon the new earth under the new heavens, and found them unchanged in shape—but now open to me. Their hearts, their souls, revealed themselves. Each thing I met greeted me with its spirit, saying, “We come from the same Source. We move toward the same purpose.” They spoke of the One they always served, the One they always returned to. “We are lightning,” they said, “flashes of His fire, taking form as we pass from Him to Him.”

The dark rocks drank in the sunlight like sponges. The world soaked up the light and released life. Lona and I were two fires of joy. Earth breathed upward, sweet smoke ascending to heaven. We breathed inward, longing for home. Our very consciousness was gratitude.

We came to the dry channels—once cracked and barren—now rushing with living water. The streams shouted in their joy, foaming, leaping, singing as they raced over stones. As far as sight could reach, rivers thundered, roaring, dashing—water turned vocal by the rock it touched.

We did not cross. Instead, we walked along the right bank, “in glory and in joy,” until we reached the great cataract at the edge of the sandy desert. There, the river split into two branches, plunging and swirling down in thunderous falls.

We climbed the height—and found no desert.

Instead, a green plain stretched before us, bordered by grassy banks. A wide, deep river flowed silently, full to the brim. Then, for the first time, the Little Ones saw the glory of God in the clear, limpid flow of water.

Instinctively, they plunged in. They swam. The beasts followed. The desert rejoiced. It blossomed—like a rose unfurling in the morning.

Forests sprang up overnight—towering trees, thick with flowering undergrowth, alive with birds whose songs wove through the air like golden threads. Every thicket gave birth to a rivulet, every rivulet sang its own song. The place where the buried hand had lain showed no trace.

Beyond, the river continued—stronger, fuller, flowing from lands unseen. We ascended further—along grassy shores, through forests of noble trees. The grass grew sweeter, the flowers more radiant, the trees taller, the wind heavier with meaning.

At last, we entered a forest unlike any other. Its trees were giants—pillars of living wood, arching overhead into a vaulted roof so dense that few sunbeams pierced through. Into this aerial canopy, the children climbed, scrambling, leaping, calling out to elephants hidden below—whose deep trumpet-answers echoed back.

Lona understood their conversation. I only guessed.

The Little Ones chased squirrels, who played with them, leading them on—then allowed themselves to be caught, petted, loved. Often, a bird of exquisite beauty would alight upon one of them, sing a song of what was coming, then vanish into the air.

And not one monkey—of any kind—could we see.
