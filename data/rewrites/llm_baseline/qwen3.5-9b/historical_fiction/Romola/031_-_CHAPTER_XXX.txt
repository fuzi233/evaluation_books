It was the first time Baldassarre had ventured into the Piazza del Duomo since his flight. A burning desire seized him to hear the renowned monk preach once more, yet he recoiled from returning to the very spot where he had stood half-naked, his hair unkempt, a noose around his throat, branded by the crowd as a lunatic. The humiliation was too raw, too vivid, to be erased simply by the transformation of his appearance. When Tito had spoken those words—"Some madman, surely"—it was not merely their cruelty that stung like a viper's bite; it was the bitter, instantaneous realization that Baldassarre might indeed be powerless to disprove them. Alongside his consuming urge for vengeance rose a chilling uncertainty: could he actually achieve it? It felt as though Tito had been guided by a devilish prompter, whispering Baldassarre's darkest secrets directly into the traitor's ear.

Baldassarre was not mad. He bore the tragic mark of sanity: the clear, agonizing awareness of his own shattered faculties. He measured his own weakness. Yet, with every surge of vindictive rage, a primal caution stirred within him, akin to a wounded beast or an insect trembling on crumbling ground. This distrust, this refusal to take any step that might reveal his condition, had led him to reject Piero di Cosimo's friendly overtures. Even at the hospital, he had offered only a fabricated tale of capture by the French on his journey from Genoa. However, his age and the peculiar cadence of his speech marked him as unlike the usual beggars or travelers housed there. Moved by pity, the monks bestowed upon him a coarse woolen tunic against the chill, a pair of peasant shoes, and a handful of *danari*, the smallest Florentine coins.

He departed for the road to Arezzo at dawn but halted at the nearest town. Using two of his coins, he paid a barber to shave him and clip his hair short, restoring his former style. The barber possessed a small hand-mirror of polished steel. Years had passed since Baldassarre had gazed into such a reflection. Now, as his eyes met the glass, a sudden, jarring thought pierced his mind: *"Does Tito truly not recognize me?"* The thought arrested his impulsive momentum, sending a shock through him; his hand trembled like a leaf as he reached for the mirror. He needed to see himself before shaving. The barber, noting his shake, held the glass steady.

No, he was not altered beyond recognition. He knew the depth of those wrinkles from three years prior; they were merely deeper now. The skin remained rough and clumsy, forming shallow bumps on his brow like cryptic symbols, only yellower, resembling a lifeless rind. That shaggy white beard was no disguise to eyes that had studied him closely for sixteen years, or to eyes that should have expected to find him changed, much as one searches for a beloved among the debris washed ashore. Something had shifted in his gaze, yes—but a shift that should only have made recognition more startling. Is not a known voice all the more piercing when it emerges as a desperate cry? Doubt was folly; he knew Tito recognized him. Pushing the mirror away, Baldassarre felt the surging currents of hatred and vengeance resume their flow.

He retraced his steps toward Florence but refused to enter the city until dusk. Turning aside from the main road, he sat by a tranquil pool shaded by alders still holding yellow leaves. It was a calm November day, and the still water seemed a perfect mirror for his soul. He wanted to scrutinize himself slowly, something he had feared to do before the barber. Sitting on the pool's edge, he leaned forward, staring intently at his reflection. Was there a wandering, imbecilic quality in his face, mirroring the chaos within? Not now. Under his gaze of eager inquiry, his eyes burned with intense purpose. But in other moments? Yes, undoubtedly. During the long hours of vague longing for a forgotten past, when he sat in lonely darkness visited by mocking whispers and phantom figures that drifted away from his grasping hands, his expression must have been one of frustrated bewilderment. Worse still, when the thick cloud of confusion briefly parted, offering a glimpse of hope before crashing down again, leaving him helpless—his face must have shown the blank terror of sudden blindness.

Could he prove anything? Could he even begin to speak with the confidence that his train of thought would not fracture? Would anyone believe he had once possessed a mind brimming with rare knowledge, engaged in profound contemplation, fluent in varied tongues? All of it had slipped away—that laboriously gathered treasury. Was it utterly lost, like water poured into the vast ocean? Or was it still trapped within him, locked behind a barrier that might someday shatter? He clung desperately to this hope. Since the day he dragged himself weakly from his straw bed, feeling a new darkness descend even in sunlight, his mind had undergone transformations—some gradual, others sudden flashes. As his physical strength returned, so did his self-command and the energy of his will. He regained the memory of the parts of his life woven tightly with emotion, and felt increasingly the painful ache of lost knowledge. Yet, occasionally, when strongly agitated, he seemed momentarily to reclaim his former self, as an old man might briefly recall the vigor of his youth. He saw Greek texts clearly again; his mind moved freely among familiar concepts. It was but a flash, and the encroaching darkness made the loss seem more horrific, but perhaps it could return and stay long enough for him to execute his revenge—devising a torment so exquisite that a mere arm could never inflict it.

Rising from his crouch, Baldassarre folded his arms and concentrated all his mental force on the path ahead. He must wait for knowledge and opportunity, and during this waiting, he needed a means of survival without begging. His greatest fear was being perceived as a foolish, helpless old man. No one must know that half his memory was gone; the lost strength might return, and even a brief resurgence could suffice. He knew how to begin gathering intelligence on Tito. The name "Bratti Ferravecchi" had become etched into his mind through constant repetition. A man in Genoa, who had seen Tito's ring, had informed him that the gemstone was purchased in Florence from a young, well-dressed Greek with a handsome dark face, at the shop of a secondhand dealer named Bratti Ferravecchi, located in the street also called Ferravecchi.

This revelation sent Baldassarre into a violent turmoil. Until then, he had clung tenaciously to his faith in Tito, refusing to believe he had been deliberately abandoned. Initially, he reasoned, "My piece of parchment never reached him; that is why I toil still at Antioch. But he is searching; he knows where I was lost; he will find me eventually." Later, taken to Corinth, he convinced his captors, promising a ransom, that inquiries would be made regarding his fate in Antioch. At Corinth, he thought joyfully, "Here, finally, he must find me. Whichever way he turns, he will encounter me." But before another year passed, illness struck, leaving him body and mind shattered, worth less than nothing save for a ransom that never arrived. Sitting helpless in the morning sun, he concluded, "Tito has drowned, or they have imprisoned him too. I shall see him no more. He set out after me, but misfortune overtook him. I shall never see his face again."

In his new frailty and despair, supporting his head in his hands with vacant eyes and uncertain lips, he appeared so hopelessly imbecile that his owners gladly discarded him, handing him over to a compassionate Genoese merchant aboard his galley. Through months of travel across the Aegean and the coast of Asia Minor, Baldassarre regained his physical strength, yet upon landing in Genoa, he felt such profound desolation that he almost wished he had died of his illness in Corinth. One possibility prevented suicide: Tito might live, imprisoned or destitute. If so, there remained a faint, distant hope that he might find his child, his cherished son, once more; that he might clasp hands and meet face-to-face with the only being who remembered him as he was before his mind broke.

It was in this state of feeling that he encountered the stranger wearing Tito's onyx ring. Though Baldassarre could not have described the ring beforehand, its sight stirred dormant fibers, and he recognized it instantly. The fact that Tito, nearly a year after their separation, lived in apparent prosperity in Florence, selling the gem he should have reserved for direst need, was a reality Baldassarre shrank from confronting. He preferred to be stunned and bewildered by it rather than forced to think distinctly. He tried to feel only the joy of beholding Tito again. Perhaps Tito believed his father dead; somehow, the mystery would resolve. "At least I shall meet eyes that remember me. I am not alone in the world." Again, Baldassarre declared, "I am not alone; my revenge walks beside me."

As the instrument of this revenge, something external and subservient to his true existence, he bent once more to examine his reflection with hard curiosity. He did not care for the withered, forsaken old man whose soul was like a deserted home, cold and bare. Human passion, whether low or high, reaches a point where it ceases to be purely egoistic, becoming a fire within us to which everything else is merely fuel.
