The sun-drenched summer days stretched long and slow, bringing neither the truth Romola ached to uncover, nor the unthinkable disaster she dreaded. No word came from Baldassarre, and for all the Florentine government’s posted watchmen and sharpened investigations, no trace of the suspected anti-Savonarola conspiracy ever surfaced. But the empty, anxious stretch of days was not left to be haunted only by her private fears: two events landed hard on the city, wrapping her vague unease in urgent, immediate work that bound her even closer to the man who had first awoken her to a life of purpose: the creeping, spreading plague, and the formal excommunication of Fra Girolamo Savonarola.

She could not walk day after day into the pestilence-choked hovel, brimming with the sharp, bright joy of knowing she existed to soften some of that unendurable suffering, without recognizing that that transcendent urge to serve had been planted in her entirely by the Frate. Nor could she stand by and watch a man who had devoted every breath to making a real, living Christian virtue out of empty ritual be silenced and cast out, simply because he dared push the corrupt clergy to live by the values they preached, without feeling her old attachment to him reforge itself, stronger than before.

Late in the swelter of June, the excommunication edict that had arrived from Rome weeks prior was formally, solemnly read aloud in the Duomo. Romola chose to attend the ceremony, hoping the outrage it stoked would feed the strength she drew from standing on Savonarola’s side. The dim evening scene could not have been more starkly different from the bright morning gatherings she had known there: no packed crowd of Florentine faces stacked amphitheatre-style, young people ringing the old like a garland of hope, no thundering voice rousing every heart to fight for great causes both earthly and unseen. Instead the bare stone walls were darkened further by the guttering glimmer of tapers, a huddled flock of black-robed monks and dour parish priests stood hunched and listless, a droning, flat voice read out the sentence that had already been nailed to every church door in the city for weeks, broken only by the occasional faint tinkle of small altar bells. At last every taper was snuffed out, and the soft, shuffling tread of monastic slippers faded away into thick, heavy silence.

Romola’s resolve to stand with the Frate was doubled by what she saw around her: coarse, hard-faced men grinning in open triumph at his downfall, while even his most loyal followers stood shaking, confused and terror-struck. For most pious Florentines, the question of where a Christian’s duty to obey the Church ended and their duty to resist injustice began was nearly unanswerable. Their belief that the Church was a living, divine power with the right to bless and curse was no abstract opinion—it was an unshakable impression as obvious and unarguable as the curve of the blue sky overhead. Savonarola’s published writings arguing that an unjust excommunication carried no weight only made those loyal followers tremble harder, as if he was defying some invisible, all-powerful specter no sword could cut, no wall could hide from.

But Romola had not been raised steeped in those inherited traditions, not with her father who had lived his whole life apart from Florence’s devout community. For her, her connection to the Church ran entirely through Savonarola: his moral fire was the only authority she had ever bowed to. All she saw in the excommunication was the petty malice of corrupt, threatened men: on one side a man who had given everything to the cause of public virtue and spiritual purity, on the other an attack led by Rodrigo Borgia, that lecherous, greedy, lying, murderous old man who now sat on the papal throne as Pope Alexander VI. Detached neutrals might have spotted small follies in the martyr, even faint shreds of political sense in his persecutors, but Romola had no use for neutrality. The edict pulled Savonarola out of the petty, local squabbles he had often fallen into from the pulpit, and turned him into a standard-bearer leaping straight into the breach, appealing to the whole Christian world against a gross abuse of ecclesiastical power. Life never feels clearer than when you watch a brave man risk everything for an unassailable good, and that new rush of enthusiasm carried her through every hard, sweltering day.

She never breathed a word to Tito about her encounter with Baldassarre, or the terrible secrets he had laid at her feet. What good would it do? Tito would never admit the truth, he would only sneer and deliver some cold sarcasm about her sympathizing with the man who had tried to murder him. Baldassarre was clearly helpless now, anyway. Her fear was not that he would harm Tito, but that Tito would track him down, and concoct some new cruel scheme to rid himself of the destitute man who haunted every corner of his conscience. She knew she could do nothing decisive until she found Baldassarre again, and learned the full truth about that “other wife” he had referenced—learn if Tito had been bound to another woman long before he ever married her.

That possibility gnawed at her, eating away every last faint lingering trace of tenderness for the early, illusory love she had once held for her husband, wounding the ancient family pride she had inherited from her father. The horror of all Baldassarre had revealed made her shrink from Tito with a visceral repulsion that would have pushed her to some explosive, unplanned outburst if he had not been nearly absent from home that entire stretch. Like most of Florence’s wealthy citizens fleeing the plague, Tito spent almost all his free time out at country villas outside the city limits: the charming, quick-witted Melema was a welcome guest at every estate, and since Romola had refused point-blank to leave the city and abandon the plague sufferers who relied on her, he had no reason to maintain a private country home for them both.

By the last weeks of July, the worst of the public crises that had absorbed all her energy began to ease. The plague had almost entirely disappeared, and the new, sympathetic governing magistracy had begun drafting urgent letters to Rome, pleading for the excommunication to be withdrawn. Romola’s strong, healthy frame began to sink under the inevitable languor that follows weeks of nonstop overexertion, but her restless mind would not let her stay cooped up at home for long outside the hottest midday hours. Every cool morning and evening, she wandered the streets, taking new routes every time, clinging to the faint, vague hope that if Baldassarre was still alive, she might chance upon him. Sometimes she let herself dare hope the plague fever had stolen his memory again, that he had forgotten where she lived, even forgotten she existed. The far likelier, grimmer explanation she tried not to name to herself was that he had fallen ill of the plague, and died alone, unmarked, before he could ever find her again.
