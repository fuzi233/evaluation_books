“My heart revolts within me, and two voices
Make themselves audible within my bosom.”

When Margaret stepped back through the Hale’s front door after her walk, she found two notes lying on the parlour table. One was a sealed letter addressed to her mother, the other, crisscrossed with smudged foreign postmarks and thin as silver tissue, rustled soft as sea-wind in her fingers: it was unmistakably from her Aunt Shaw, writing from the continent. She was still turning the foreign letter over to trace the stamps when her father burst in, his coat unbuttoned, his face lined with quiet worry.

“Your mother’s gone up to bed early, worn right out, poor thing. That thundery, airless day could not have been worse for the doctor’s visit, I’m sure. What did Donaldson say to you, eh? Dixon told me he pulled you aside to talk through her case.”

Margaret hesitated, and Mr Hale’s face fell sharper, more anxious. “He does not think her seriously ill?”

“Not at present,” she said gently. “He says she only needs careful tending. He was very kind, and promised to call back soon to see how her medicines are settling.”

“Only care? He didn’t suggest change of air? He didn’t say this smoky Milton fog is doing her harm, did he, Margaret?”

“Not a word,” she answered, sober. “Though I think he was more anxious than he let on to you.”

“All doctors put on that grave, fussy manner—professional habit, nothing more,” he said, trying to brush the fear away. But Margaret could see the unease coiled tight under his attempts at lightness: he could not settle to any task that whole evening, kept circling back to the subject of his wife’s health, fighting hard to push away even the faintest hint of bad news that would not be shifted, until Margaret’s chest ached for him.

“Ah, this letter’s from Aunt Shaw,” she said, trying to distract him. “She reached Naples and found the heat unbearable, so she’s rented apartments in Sorrento. Though I don’t think she’s half as fond of Italy as she hoped she would be.”

“Did he say nothing of special diet, then?” he pressed, not taking the bait.

“Nourishing, easy to digest. I think her appetite’s been pretty good lately.”

“Aye, that’s what makes it odd he’d even bother to mention diet at all.”

“I asked him straight, papa,” Margaret said. A long quiet stretched between them, before she picked the letter back up. “Aunt Shaw says she’s sent me a set of coral ornaments, but she’s half convinced Milton’s Dissenters won’t see the beauty in them. She still gets all her ideas of Dissenters from old Quaker tales, doesn’t she?”

“If you ever notice your mother craving anything at all, no matter how small, you must promise to tell me at once,” he said, unsmiling. “I’m always so afraid she won’t own up to what she wants, to spare me extra work. And do see after that new house-servant Mrs Thornton recommended. If we get an efficient girl, Dixon can stay by her side night and day, and I’ll wager proper care will nurse her right back to strength, what with this hot weather and the trouble we’ve had finding help lately. A little rest, that’s all she needs, eh Margaret?”

“I hope so,” Margaret said, so sadly that her father noticed, and pinched her pale cheek playfully.

“Come now, you’re looking as white as a sheet. If you don’t take care of yourself, I’ll be calling the doctor for you next, not your mother.”

But he could not rest. Every few minutes he slipped down the hall on tiptoe, slow as he could move, to peek through the crack of the bedroom door and check his wife was still sleeping. Margaret’s heart ached to watch him: he was fighting tooth and nail to smother the ugly, nameless fear that loomed in the dark corners of his mind. At last he came back, his shoulders loosened a little with relief.

“She’s awake now, Margaret. She smiled the moment she saw me standing by the bed—just her old, familiar smile. Says she feels rested, and is hungry for a cup of tea. Where’s that note for her? She wants to see it. I’ll read it out to her while you make the tea.”

The note was a formal, neatly penned invitation from Mrs Thornton, asking Mr., Mrs. and Miss Hale to dinner at their home on the twenty-first of the month. Margaret was surprised to see both her father and mother already set on accepting it, after all the worrying news the doctor had shared that very day. But the dinner invitation had lit a spark in Mrs Hale long before Margaret even knew what the note said: it was a small, bright event to break the endless monotony of her days stuck inside, and she clung to the idea of going with a fretful, stubborn pertinacity when Margaret tried to object that she should not exert herself.

“Nay, Margaret, if it makes her happy to think of us going, we’ll both go gladly,” Mr Hale said anxiously, sitting down to pen the note of acceptance the next morning. “She would never ask it of us if she didn’t feel stronger, better than we thought she was, isn’t that right, Margaret?”

The comfort he was clinging to so desperately felt too cruel to refuse him. His whole-hearted refusal to acknowledge any possible fear had even seeped into Margaret, warming her own small spark of hope.

“I do think she’s better than she was last night,” she conceded. “Her eyes are brighter, and her complexion is clearer.”

“God bless you, child,” he said, earnest. “Though yesterday was so sultry everyone in town felt unwell. It was the worst possible day for Dr Donaldson to have come round.”

He left then for his daily lessons, which had grown busier now that he was preparing a gratis series of lectures on Ecclesiastical Architecture for the working men at the nearby Lyceum. The subject was far more aligned with his own old clerical tastes and learning than it was with the practical, trade-focused knowledge the mill workers had asked for, but the debt-ridden Lyceum board was only too happy to get a free course from an educated, accomplished man like Mr Hale, no matter what the topic.

That same night, at the Thornton house, John Thornton was going over the guest list spread out on the dining room table.

“Mother, who’s accepted the dinner invites for the twenty-first?”

“Fanny, hand me the notes, there,” Mrs Thornton said. “The Slicksons accept, Collingbrooks accept, Stephenses accept, Browns decline. The Hales—father and daughter are coming, Mrs Hale’s too much of an invalid to attend. Macphersons, Mr Horsfall, Mr Young. I was thinking we’d ask the Porters to make up the extra spots, since the Browns can’t make it.”

“Good enough,” Thornton said, his brow creased. “Dr Donaldson told me today I’m afraid Mrs Hale is far from well.”

“Odd that they’d accept a dinner invitation if she’s that unwell,” Fanny put in, tittering her little nervous, weak laugh.

“I didn’t say she was at death’s door,” Thornton snapped, sharper than he meant to be. “Only that she’s far from well. They might not even realise how serious her condition is, not properly.” He stopped short, suddenly remembering what Dr Donaldson had told him: Margaret, at least, knew full well the exact state of her mother’s health.

“Oh, I’m sure they know exactly what you told John yesterday,” Fanny said. “How advantageous it would be for Mr Hale to be introduced to people like the Stephenses and Collingbrooks, all those rich trade families.”

“Those are not the sort of people to curry favour for introductions,” Thornton said shortly. “No, I think I understand how it is.”

“John, you are forever going on about how well you understand the Hales,” Fanny teased, “and you’ll never let the rest of us have a correct thought about them. Are they really so different from every other person you meet in Milton?” She had not meant to vex him, but she could not have done a better job of it if she tried. He chafed in silence, not deigning to dignify the question with a reply.

“They don’t strike me as anything out of the ordinary,” Mrs Thornton pronounced, her voice firm. “The man is a decent sort, but far too simple for trade—no wonder he was a clergyman first, and a teacher now. The wife’s a bit of a fine lady, making a performance out of her invalidism. As for the girl—she’s the only one that puzzles me, when I do bother to think of her, which isn’t often. She carries on like she’s got some high and mighty notion of her own importance, acting like she’s too good for the company around her half the time. And yet they’ve never had a penny to spare, from all I’ve heard.”

“And she’s not even accomplished, mamma,” Fanny added. “She can’t play the piano at all.”

“Go on, Fanny,” Thornton said, turning on her. “What else is she lacking that would make her worthy enough for your standards?”

“Nay, John, that was no harm what Fanny said,” his mother cut in. “I heard the girl myself say she could not play. If you’d stop jumping to her defence all the time, we might even get to see her merits, and like her.”

“I’m sure I never could like her!” Fanny muttered, hidden safe behind her mother’s shoulder.

Thornton heard her, but he did not bother to reply. He paced back and forth across the dining room, wishing his mother would call for candles so he could bury himself in work, and put an end to this conversation once and for all. But he would never interfere with his mother’s small, thrifty domestic habits, the ones she had formed from decades of careful economy when they had nothing. He stopped pacing at last, and spoke out plainly, earnest and soft.

“Mother, I wish you would make an effort to like Miss Hale.”

His mother stared, startled by his tone. “You’re not thinking of marrying her, are you? A girl without a penny to her name?”

“She would never have me,” Thornton said, with a short, bitter laugh.

“Aye, that’s obvious,” his mother agreed. “She laughed straight in my face once, when I praised her for speaking up for you after that story Mr Bell told. I liked her for her frankness then, thought that meant she had no silly notions about you. And the next minute she vexed me so, carrying on like she thought—well, never mind that! But you’re right, she thinks far too highly of herself to ever consider you. That saucy little jade! I’d like to see her find a better man than you anywhere.”

“Since I am as convinced as you are that she’d never look twice at me, and I have no intention of ever asking her to be my wife, you can believe me when I say I speak of her entirely disinterestedly,” Thornton said. “I see trouble coming for that family, trouble that will leave that girl in need of a friend, no motherly care to turn to soon. I only ask that you be ready to step up for her, if she needs it. And Fanny,” he turned to his sister, “I trust you have enough decency to understand that to whisper any silly gossip that I’m sweet on her would do her more harm than it would me—she’d be mortified, to be sure. I only ask you both to show her every kindness you can.”

“I can’t forgive her for her pride,” Mrs Thornton said. “But I’ll befriend her if she needs it, for your sake, John. I’d befriend Jezebel herself if you asked me to. But this girl, who turns her nose up at all of us—who turns her nose up at you—”

“Nay, mother. I have never once put myself in the way of her contempt, and I never mean to.”

“Contempt, indeed!” Mrs Thornton huffed, one of her sharp, expressive snorts. “Don’t speak of Miss Hale to me anymore, John, if I’ve got to be nice to her. When I’m standing right in front of her I can’t tell if I like or dislike her more, but when I think of her, and hear you talk of her, I hate her. I can see she’s been putting on all her high and mighty airs for you, plain as day, even if you won’t admit it.”

“If she has,” Thornton paused for a beat, then went on, “I’m no soft lad to be cowed by a proud look from a woman, or to care if she misunderstands me and what I’ve built here. I can laugh at it.”

“To be sure you can! Laugh at her too, with all her fine notions and haughty little tosses of the head!”

“Then why are we all talking so much about her?” Fanny complained. “I’m sick to death of the subject.”

“Fine,” Thornton said, a sharp edge of bitterness in his voice. “Let’s talk about something more pleasant. What do you say to the workers striking, for a cheerful topic?”

“Have the hands actually walked out?” Mrs Thornton asked, instantly alert, all her attention fixed.

“Hamper’s men are already out. My men are finishing out the rest of the week only because they’re scared of being prosecuted for breach of contract. I’d have every single one of them up before the magistrates the second they step off the job early, to punish them for it.”

“The legal fees would be more than those useless fellows are worth—ungrateful lot of naughts!” his mother scoffed.

“Of course they would. But I’d show them that I keep my word, and I expect them to keep theirs. They know by now what sort of master they’re dealing with. Slickson’s men have all walked out too—serves him right, he’s too mean to spend a penny on punishing them. We’re in for a full turn-out across the whole town, mother.”

“Hope you haven’t got too many large orders on the books?”

“Of course we have. They know that perfectly well, that’s why they picked now to strike. They don’t understand the full picture, even if they think they do.”

“What do you mean, John?”

Candles had been brought into the room now, and Fanny had picked up her endless, slow piece of worsted work, yawning over it, leaning back in her chair every few minutes to stare at nothing.

“The Americans are flooding the market with their own yarn right now,” Thornton explained. “Our only chance to stay in business is to produce it cheaper than them. If we can’t, we can shut up our mills tomorrow, every last one of them, and hands and masters alike will be tramping the roads looking for work. And these fools are still demanding the wages we paid three years ago—some of their leaders even cite Dickinson’s rates, when they know full well those fines they squeeze out of their workers’ pay, no honourable man would ever take, means their real wages are lower than what I pay at my mill. I wish the old Combination Laws were still in force. It’s unbearable, to think a pack of ignorant, silly men, banding their empty heads together, get to rule over the fortunes of men who built this all up with years of hard work, knowledge, thought, and pain. The next step—we’re nearly there already—will be having to go hat in hand to the Spinner’s Union secretary, begging him to be so kind as to give us labour at whatever price they name. They haven’t the sense to see that if we don’t get a fair profit to cover the cost of running these mills, we can pick up and move the whole business abroad, and there’ll be no jobs left for anyone. With home and foreign competition both breathing down our necks, none of us are ever going to make more than a fair profit, and we’ll be lucky to get that even in a good year.”

“Why not bring workers over from Ireland?” Mrs Thornton suggested. “I wouldn’t keep those ungrateful fellows on for a single day more. I’d show them who’s master, who I choose to hire.”

“Aye, I will, if this drags on. It’ll be expensive, and there’s bound to be trouble, danger even, but I’ll do it before I give in to their demands.”

“If we’ve got all this extra expense coming, I’m sorry we arranged a dinner right now,” his mother said.

“So am I—not for the cost, but because I’ll have a thousand things to think of, a hundred unexpected calls on my time. But we had to invite Mr Horsfall, he’s only passing through Milton for a few days, and we owed all the other families return dinners anyway. It’s all the same trouble, whether we do it now or later.”

He paced on, silent after that, only drawing deep, sharp breaths every now and then like he was trying to shake off an annoying, clinging thought. Fanny pestered her mother with a dozen small trivial questions that had nothing to do with the strike, and got nothing but short, sharp answers in return. She was relieved when ten o’clock rolled around, and the servants filed in for the household prayers. Mrs Thornton always read them herself, starting with a chapter from the Old Testament, which they were working through steadily from start to finish. When the prayers were over, and everyone had said their goodnights, Mrs Thornton gave him that long, steady, intense look of hers, the kind that held all the force of a blessing even if she never said a tender word, and he kept on pacing the empty dining room.

All his carefully laid business plans, the product of months of late nights and anxious thought, had been torn to shreds by this sudden, insane strike. The workers’ folly would hurt themselves far more than it would ever hurt him, but no one could calculate how much ruin it would spread across the whole town. Hamper had said only that day that if the strike ruined him, he’d start over again from nothing, comforted by the fact that the men who brought it on would be far worse off than him—he had a head for business as well as two hands to work, while the workers had nothing but their two hands, and if they chased their own market away, they could never follow it, never turn to any other trade. But that thought was no comfort to Thornton. He took no pleasure in the idea of revenge.
