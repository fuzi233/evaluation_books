CHAPTER XXIII. New Faces (Rewritten for Humor)

The residents of Fairoaks were minding their own business—mostly sleeping, eating, and occasionally wondering if the sky was really that blue or just a trick of the light—when across the River Brawl, atop a hill so grand it looked like it had been built by giants with bad posture, something astonishing happened: Clavering Park woke up.

Not metaphorically. Literally. Like a giant cat stretching after a long nap, the great house yawned, dusted off its cobwebs, and declared, “I’m back, and I’ve brought a suitcase full of drama.”

This occurred just as Arthur Pendennis was still reeling from his recent mishap—some minor disaster involving a horse, a fence, and possibly a lost glove—which left him so emotionally shattered he barely noticed the world around him. But others did. And they weren’t exactly thrilled.

An announcement appeared in the local papers—one of those provincial rags that seem to exist solely to report on aristocratic weddings, sheep thefts, and the sudden return of long-lost baronets—and the whole county went into a tizzy. From Clavering Market to Cackleby Fair, from Gooseberry Green to Tinkleton Church gate, everyone was whispering: *Clavering Park is being lived in again.*

Five years earlier, the same paper had reported the marriage of Francis Clavering, Esq., only son of Sir Francis Clavering, Bart., to Jemima Augusta Snell, widow of J. Amory, Esq., and daughter of Samuel Snell, Esq., of Calcutta. At the time, folks thought it was a sad but sensible move: ruined nobleman marries rich widow from India. A classic case of “money solves everything except existential dread.”

And indeed, it did. The newlyweds were spotted at various continental hotspots—Florence, Schloss Schinkenstein, Rome, Lucca, Nice, even gambling dens along the Rhine. They were seen dining, dancing, and generally behaving like people who’d forgotten what modesty meant. Their final stop? Paris, where they lived like kings, thanks to the death of Mr. Snell, whose estate apparently included enough gold to fund a small country.

But before we get ahead of ourselves, let’s talk about Sir Francis Clavering Sr.—a man whose life story reads like a cautionary tale written by a disgruntled uncle. Born to an outlaw in a damp chateau near Bruges, young Francis tried to join a dragoon regiment, failed spectacularly, got himself bankrupt at the gambling table, spent two years in the army, sold out, ended up in the Fleet Prison, then fled to Ostend to live with his gout-ridden father. For years, he wandered Europe like a particularly well-dressed ghost, haunting billiard rooms, riding other people’s horses in steeplechases, and punting at gambling houses with the confidence of someone who doesn’t believe in consequences.

Then came the lucky coup: he met Jemima Augusta Snell at a boarding house in Lausanne. She was recently returned from Calcutta, widowed, wealthy, and reportedly not above marrying a man just because he wore a good hat. They married. Her father died shortly after, leaving her a fortune so large it made bankers weep with joy. And since she now had a title—Lady Clavering—her allowance doubled. Because nothing says “respectability” like a peerage and a trust fund.

Now, Lady Clavering wasn’t exactly the darling of polite society. Back in Calcutta, her family hadn’t been invited to Government House. Her father was a disreputable lawyer and indigo smuggler. Her first husband, Amory, was the mate of the Indiaman that brought her to India. Old Sir Jasper Rogers, Chief Justice of Calcutta, once said he could tell a “queer story” about her first husband—but never did. That mystery haunted dinner parties for decades.

Still, when Lady Clavering moved into the Hotel Bouilli in Paris, suddenly everyone wanted to be her friend. The Faubourg St. Germain embraced her. Viscount Bagwig, our excellent ambassador, paid her attention. Princes attended her salons. Even the most rigid English ladies—Lady Elderbury, Lady Rockminster, the Countess of Southdown—broke their moral fasts to attend her soirées. Why? Because she had money. Ten thousand pounds a year (some said twenty). And with that kind of cash, you can buy forgiveness, respect, and a subscription list longer than the King’s.

She funded charities like they were going out of style. Jesuits? Five Napoleons. Armenian patriarchs? Done. Baptist missions to Quashyboo? No problem. Orthodox settlements in Feefawfoo, the largest cannibal island in the Pacific? She sent them a check and a note saying, “Please don’t eat the missionaries.” And one day, she gave five Napoleons to Madame de Cricri for the persecuted Jesuits… and the very next moment wrote a check to Reverend J. Ramshorn, who believed God had instructed him to convert the Pope. Yes, she supported both sides. That’s how generous she was.

And during this time, she must have settled her husband’s debts in England, because Sir Francis reappeared—unhindered by bailiffs or guilt—announced in the Morning Post, and drove up the long avenue of Clavering Park in a carriage pulled by four horses, like a man who’d just won the lottery and the war.

Three men were in the carriage: Mr. Tatham of Chatteris (the little lawyer), a portly gentleman with whiskers, fur collars, and a mustache so thick it could double as a curtain, and a pale, weak-looking man who seemed to need a nap mid-arrival.

They reached the moss-covered steps. The foreign attendant with earrings and a gold-laced cap rang the bell with the enthusiasm of a man announcing the end of the world. Inside, the house groaned under its own weight. Then, footsteps echoed through the hall, and Mrs. Blenkinsop, the housekeeper, emerged with Polly, her aide-de-camp, and Smart, the keeper.

Smart kicked his heel as if a dog were biting him. Mrs. Blenkinsop curtsied. Polly curtsied and bowed rapidly, like a nervous bird. Mrs. Blenkinsop, trembling, said, “Welcome to Clavering, Sir Francis. It does my poor eyes good to see one of the family once more.”

She meant the grand gentleman in fur and braiding—the one who looked like he’d just stepped out of a royal opera. But he burst out laughing. “You’ve saddled the wrong horse, old lady! I’m not Sir Francis. Behold your rightful lord!”

He pointed at the pale, languid man, who muttered, “Don’t be an ass, Ned.”

“Oh,” said the pale man, “yes, Mrs. Blenkinsop. I’m Sir Francis Clavering. Remember me? Forgot me, I suppose? How do you do?”

He took her hand gently, nodded kindly, and added, “Dismal old place, ain’t it, Ned? Never saw it but once, when my governor quarrelled with grandfather in ’twenty-three.”

“Dismal?” said Ned. “Beautiful! The Castle of Otranto! The Mysteries of Udolpho! By Jove! What a fireplace! You might roast an elephant in it. Splendid carved gallery! Inigo Jones, by Jove! I’d lay five to two it’s Inigo Jones.”

“The upper part by Inigo Jones,” said Mrs. Blenkinsop. “The lower was altered by Vanderputty, the Dutch architect, in George the First’s time.”

“O indeed,” said Sir Francis. “Gad, Ned, you know everything.”

“I know a few things, Frank,” Ned replied. “I know that’s not a Snyders over the mantelpiece—bet you three to one it’s a copy. We’ll restore it. A lick of varnish, and it’ll come out wonderfully.”

“And that fellow in red?” asked Ned.

“Sheriff of the county,” said Mrs. Blenkinsop. “Served in Parliament under Queen Anne. That on the right is Theodosia, wife of Harbottle, second baronet, painted by Lely as Venus. Her son Gregory, third baronet, as Cupid. Next panel: Sir Rupert, knighted by Charles the First, whose lands were confiscated by Oliver Cromwell.”

“Thank you,” said Sir Francis. “We’ll walk about ourselves. Frosch, give me a cigar. Have a cigar, Mr. Tatham?”

Mr. Tatham accepted one, spluttered violently, and coughed like a dying steam engine.

“No need to come with us, Mrs. Blenkinsop. Smart—feed the horses and wash their mouths. Shan’t stay long. Come along, Strong—I know the way. Was here in ’twenty-three.”

So Sir Francis and Captain Strong—his friend and self-appointed architect of revival—walked through the house like explorers in a museum of melancholy. Rooms stretched endlessly. Saloons, drawing rooms, oak room, library (where Pen once found books), grand dining room, library again—each one bigger than the last, each one emptier.

Captain Strong, however, was enchanted. “I see capabilities in it—capabilities, sir!” he cried. “Leave it to me, and I’ll make it the pride of the county at a small expense. What a theatre we can have in the library—curtains between the columns! What a galop room! Hold the whole shire! Hang the morning parlour with the tapestry from your Rue de Grenelle salon. Furnish the oak room with Moyen-age cabinets and armour. Armour looks splendid against black oak. There’s a Venice glass in the Quai Voltaire—perfect for that mantelpiece.”

“I recollect my old governor caning me in that little room,” said Sir Francis. “He always hated me.”

“Chintz is the dodge for my lady’s rooms,” said Strong. “The suite on the south landing—bedroom, sitting-room, dressing-room. Throw a conservatory out over the balcony. Where will you have your rooms?”

“North wing,” said Sir Francis. “Out of reach of Miss Amory’s piano. She screeches from dawn till dusk.”

Strong laughed. “Ah, the musical menace.”

They inspected the kitchen, stables, steward’s room—where Mr. Tatham was poring over estate plans—and finally departed, leaving behind a house that felt more alive than it had in decades.

That night, the news spread: Sir Francis Clavering was back. He was coming to live in the county.

At Chatteris, chaos ensued. High Church, Low Church, half-pay captains, old maids, dowagers, farmers, factory workers—all stirred. The news reached Fairoaks, where Laura said, “Mrs. Pybus says there’s a very pretty girl in the family—Miss Amory, Lady Clavering’s daughter. Of course, you’ll fall in love with her, Arthur.”

Helen scoffed. “Don’t talk nonsense, Laura.”

Pen laughed. “Well, there’s the young Sir Francis.”

“He’s four,” said Laura. “But I shall console myself with that handsome officer—Sir Francis’s friend. He was at church last Sunday. His mustachios were beautiful.”

And so began the legend of Captain Edward Strong—the Chevalier Strong, as he was known abroad. He was tall, broad, blue-eyed, black-whiskered, and carried himself like a man who’d fought wars, survived fireships, and once nearly became king of a small principality in Transylvania.

He’d tried living in the great house, but found it too quiet. “I am a creature born for society,” he told Captain Glanders. “Frank has no energy. He’s not the chest for it.” (And he puffed out his own chest dramatically.)

So he moved into the lodgings formerly occupied by Smirke, charmed Madame Fribsby, dined at the Clavering Arms, joked with farmers, and within days was the most popular man in town. On market day, he knew every girl, every cow, and every secret of the landlord’s strong beer.

He told stories of escaping Missolonghi, being blown up in a fireship, receiving the Cross of the Redeemer, fighting in Spain, and hanging up his sword after Zumalacarreguy’s death. He sang songs in six languages, played the piano like a man possessed, and made Fairoaks feel like a party.

Before long, he was in Helen’s drawing-room as often as he was in Madame Fribsby’s parlor. The ladies adored him. He had tales of Greek captives, Polish beauties, Spanish nuns, and battles fought under moonlight. He was, as Laura put it, “delightful.”

And he didn’t just entertain—he informed. He told them all about the Clavering family, including Miss Amory.

“She paints,” said Strong. “She writes poems. She composes music. She rides like Diana Vernon. She is a muse. A mystery. A femme incomprise.”

“What’s that?” asked Mrs. Pendennis.

Strong smiled. “A woman beyond understanding.”

“I hate clever women,” said Pen.

“Thank you,” said Laura, who was already planning her friendship with Miss Amory.

And so, as the new chapter of Clavering Park unfolded—with furniture arriving in vans, gardens being ignored, and Captain Strong ruling the roost—it seemed clear: the quiet life of Fairoaks was over. The future was loud, dramatic, and possibly involved a piano that would never stop playing.
