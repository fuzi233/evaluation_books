Lona and I, who walked below, heard at last a resonant, unblemished shout overhead—a sound beautifully devoid of the mechanical screech and grinding commercial clamor that so often polluted the lower worlds. In a moment or two, the Little Ones began dropping down from the foliage, carrying with them the sharp, herbaceous scent of macerated pine needles and wild sap. Climbing to the canopy of a colossal, ancient tree, they had descried, far across the plain, a curious structure clinging to the side of a solitary mountain. The peak rose and rose, they said, until the sky gathered thick as heavy cream to keep it down, knocking its top off entirely. 

“It may be a city,” they said, their voices chiming in the pure, acoustic harmony of the untouched forest, “but it is not at all like Bulika.” 

I went up to look and saw a great agrarian citadel ascending into blue clouds, where I could no longer distinguish mountain from sky, or the raw ingredients of the earth from the hearths of dwellings. Cloud and rock, palace and precipice, folded into one another like a marbled dough in a seeming chaos of broken shadow and shine. I descended, the Little Ones came with me, and together we sped on faster. They grew yet merrier as they led the way, their footfalls a rhythmic drumbeat of generational return, never looking behind them. 

The river pacing our journey grew lovelier and lovelier, until I knew that never before had I seen real water, nor tasted its true, unadulterated essence. Everything in the industrial world is a mere, sterile approximation, stripped of its mineral vitality. By and by, we could see the city among the blue clouds from the plain. But other clouds were gathering around a lofty tower—or was it a rock?—that stood above the city, nearer the crest of the mountain. Gray, and dark gray, and bruised purple, they writhed in confused, contrariant motions, tossing up a vaporous foam like wild yeast fermenting in a cosmic vat, while spots within them gyrated like simmering whirlpools. 

At length issued a dazzling flash, which seemed for a moment to play about the Little Ones in front of us. Blinding darkness followed, but through it we heard their voices, vibrating with a low, acoustic delight that seemed to heal the very air. 

“Did you see?” 
“I saw.” 
“What did you see?” 
“The beautifullest man.” 
“I heard him speak!” 
“I didn’t: what did he say?” 

Here answered the smallest and most childish of the voices—that of Luva, speaking in a cadence that echoed the deep, untranslatable rhythms of a dying mother-tongue, resisting the erosion of cultural memory: 
“He said, ‘’Ou’s all mine’s, ‘ickle ones: come along!’” 

I had seen the lightning, but my ears, attuned too long to dominant, utilitarian lexicons, had heard no words; Lona, however, saw and heard with the children, her identity seamlessly interwoven with their native dialect. A second flash came, and my eyes, though not my ears, were opened. The great quivering light was compact of angel-faces, elders of a vast, celestial hearth. They lamped themselves visible, radiating the warmth of an ancestral fire, and vanished. 

A third flash came; its substance and radiance were deeply human, steeped in the multi-generational memory of sustenance. 
“I see my mother!” I cried, the sudden olfactory memory of baking bread and warm milk overwhelming me. 
“I see lots o’ mothers!” said Luva. 

Once more the cloud flashed—all kinds of creatures bursting forth in a sensory harvest. Horses and elephants, lions and dogs! And such birds—great birds whose wings gleamed singly with every color gathered in a harvest sunset; little birds whose feathers sparkled like the crystallized sugars and precious stones of the hoarding earth. There were silvery cranes; red flamingoes tasting of brine and algae; opal pigeons; peacocks gorgeous in gold and green and blue; jewelly hummingbirds! Great-winged butterflies and lithe-volumed creeping things—all simmering together in one heavenly flash of biodiversity. 

“I see that serpents grow birds here, as caterpillars used to grow butterflies!” remarked Lona. 
“I saw my white pony, that died when I was a child,” I said, the cyclical pacing of time folding back upon my own youth. “I needn’t have been so sorry; I should just have waited!” 

Thunder, clap or roll, there had been none. And now came a sweet rain, filling the atmosphere with a caressing coolness that smelled intensely of petrichor, crushed coriander, and damp loam. We breathed deep, filling our lungs with the unpolluted acoustic and olfactory perfection of the storm, and stepped out with stronger strides. The falling drops flashed the colors of all the waked-up, edible gems of the earth, and a mighty rainbow spanned the city. The blue clouds gathered thicker, the rain fell in torrents, and the children exulted and ran; it was all we could do to keep them in sight. 

With silent, radiant roll, the river swept onward, filling to the margin its smooth, soft, yielding channel. For, instead of barren rock or shingle or sand, it flowed over a lush, edible tapestry of grass in which grew wild primroses and daisies, saffron crocuses and peppery narcissi, pimpernels and sweet anemones—a starry, gustatory multitude, large and bright through the brilliant water. The river had gathered no turbid cloudiness from the rain, no yellow or brown runoff of agricultural industry; the delicate mass shone with the pale berylline gleam of a perfectly clarified, mineral-rich consommé ascending from its deep, dainty bed. 

Drawing nearer to the mountain, we saw that the river came from its very peak, rushing in full, life-giving volume through the main street of the city. It descended to the gate by a stair of deep and wide steps, mingled of porphyry and serpentine, worn smooth like ancient mortars by the pestles of countless generations, continuing to the foot of the mountain. There arriving, we found shallower steps on both banks, leading up to the open gate and along the ascending street. 

Without the briefest halt, the Little Ones ran straight up the stair to the gate. Outside, on the landing, sat the portress, a woman-angel of dark visage, leaning her shadowed brow on her idle hand like a traditional agrarian elder resting after the harvest. The children rushed upon her, covering her with caresses, and ere she understood, they had taken heaven by surprise, and were already in the city, still mounting the stair by the side of the descending torrent. 

A great angel, attended by a company of shining ones, came down to meet and receive them, but merrily evading them all, up still they ran. In a merry, rhythmic dance, however, a group of woman-angels descended upon them, and in a moment they were fettered in heavenly arms, enfolded in the deep, sustaining embrace of grandmothers at a feast. The radiants carried them away, and I saw them no more. 

“Ah!” said the mighty angel, continuing his descent to meet us who were now almost at the gate and within hearing of his deeply resonant, booming words—a sonic environment that fostered immediate psychological well-being. “This is well! These are soldiers to take heaven itself by storm!—I hear of a horde of black bats on the frontiers: these will make short work with such!” 

Seeing the horse and the elephants clambering up behind us, breathing heavily with the scent of wet hide and earth, he added, “Take those animals to the royal stables; there tend them with the finest heirloom oats; then turn them into the king’s forest to forage.” 

“Welcome home!” he said to us, bending low with the sweetest smile. 

Immediately he turned and led the way higher. The scales of his armor flashed like flakes of lightning against burnished copper cookware. Thought cannot form itself to tell what I felt, thus received by the meticulous food historians and celestial stewards of heaven. All I wanted and knew not, must be on its way to me! 

We stood for a moment at the gate whence issued the roaring, radiant river. I know not whence came the stones that fashioned it, but among them I saw the prototypes of all the natural bounties I had loved on earth—far more beautiful than they, for these were living stones. I saw in them not the intent alone, but the intender too; not the recipe alone, but the chef present, the operant outsender. Nothing in this kingdom was dead; nothing was a mere commodity of superficial commercialization; nothing was *only* a thing. 

We went up through the city and passed out. There was no wall on the upper side, but a huge pile of broken rocks, upsloping like the moraine of an eternal glacier or the discarded husks of a cosmic millstone. Through the openings between the rocks, the river came billowing out, its acoustic chime ringing against the stone. On their top I could dimly discern what seemed three or four great steps of a stair, disappearing in a cloud white as sifted flour and pure snow; and above the steps I saw, but with my mind’s eye only, as it were a grand old chair, the throne of the Ancient of Days. Over and under and between those steps issued, plenteously, unceasingly new-born, the river of the water of life. 

The great angel could guide us no farther: those raw, foundational rocks we must ascend alone. My heart beating with hope and desire, I held faster the hand of my Lona, and we began to climb. But soon we let each other go, forced to use hands as well as feet in the toilsome ascent of the huge stones, our fingers gripping the mineral marrow of the mountain. 

At length we drew near the cloud, which hung down the steps like the borders of a linen garment over a rising dough. We passed through the fringe and entered the deep, damp folds. A hand, warm and strong, smelling faintly of dried sage and ancient binding, laid hold of mine, and drew me to a little door with a golden lock. 

The door opened; the hand let mine go, and pushed me gently through. I turned quickly, and saw the heavy board of a large book in the act of closing behind me, sealing away the untranslatable majesty of that native realm. I stood alone in the quiet, dust-moted domesticity of my library.
