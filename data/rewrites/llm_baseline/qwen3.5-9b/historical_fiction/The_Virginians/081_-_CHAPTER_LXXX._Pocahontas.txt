The English public, unlike the inhabitants of Virginia who still cherish the memory of that simple and kindly creature, was not yet fully acquainted with the history of Pocahontas. Seizing this opportunity, Mr. Warrington, encouraged by his friends, composed a brief ballad concerning the Indian princess. This poem was printed in various magazines just prior to the premiere of the tragedy. Both Sampson and I deemed this maneuver to be remarkably astute and ingenious. "It serves as excellent ground-bait, sir," remarked the enthusiastic parson, "and you shall see the fish rise in multitudes on the great day!" Indeed, he and Spencer reported that the verses were discussed and admired in several coffee-houses within their hearing, often being mistakenly attributed to Mr. Mason, Mr. Cowper of the Temple, or even the renowned Mr. Gray. I suspect poor Sam himself circulated these rumors; had Shakespeare been named the author of the tragedy, he would undoubtedly have hailed *Pocahontas* as one of the Bard's finest achievements.

My connection with the legendary Captain John Smith began in childhood, within the walls of my grandfather's library. I recall sitting at the old man's knee, my favorite volume resting on my lap, as I spelled out the exploits of our Virginian hero. I delighted in reading of his travels, his sufferings, his captivities, and his escapes—not only across the American continent but throughout Europe. As I now reach for the familiar tome on the shelf in England, I feel transported back to my youth, surrounded by a flood of memories from my early home. My grandfather would paint vivid pictures for me of Smith battling the Turks on the Danube or being led forth by our Indian allies toward execution. Ah, what a terrifying spectacle was that engagement with the three Turkish champions! How I used to revel in the tale of his combat with Bonny Molgro, the final and most fearsome of the trio! What a formidable name, and with what a prodigious turban, scimitar, and flowing whiskers we would depict him! According to my cherished old book, after slaying and decapitating his first two foes, Smith faced Bonny Molgro. They clashed with their battle-axes, whose sharp edges occasionally left either rider with barely enough sense to hold onto their saddle. The Christian knight suffered a grievous wound, losing his axe, prompting a triumphant shout from the Turkish champion among the ramparts. Yet, through the readiness of his horse and his exceptional judgment and dexterity, Smith evaded further blows. Drawing his falchion, he struck the Turk beneath the ribs, piercing through his back and body. Though the enemy dismounted, he did not survive long, meeting the same fate as his predecessors. In reward for this feat, Duke Segismundus granted Smith three Turkish heads emblazoned on a shield for his coat of arms and an annual pension of three hundred ducats.

In my tragedy, disregarding time and place—a liberty poets alone possess—Smith performs similar feats of valor along the banks of our Potomac and James rivers. Our "ground-bait" verses ran as follows:

>
> Wearied arm and broken sword
> Wage in vain the desperate fight;
> Round him press the countless horde,
> He is but a single knight.
>
> Hark! a cry of triumph shrill
> Through the wilderness resounds,
> As, with twenty bleeding wounds,
> Sinks the warrior, fighting still.
>
> Now they heap the fatal pyre,
> And the torch of death they light;
> Ah! 'tis hard to die of fire!
> Who will shield the captive knight?
>
> Round the stake with fiendish cry
> Wheel and dance the savage crowd;
> Cold the victim's mien and proud,
> And his breast is bared to die.
>
> Who will shield the fearless heart?
> Who avert the murderous blade?
> From the throng, with sudden start,
> See, there springs an Indian maid.
>
> Quick she stands before the knight,
> Loose the chain, unbind the ring;
> I am daughter of the king,
> And I claim the Indian right!
>
> Dauntlessly aside she flings
> Lifted axe and thirsty knife;
> Fondly to his heart she clings,
> And her bosom guards his life!
>
> In the woods of Powhattan,
> Still 'tis told, by Indian fires,
> How a daughter of their sires
> Saved the captive Englishman.

There is no need for me to describe the full plot of my tragedy at length, as my children can retrieve it from the shelves at any time. Nor shall I hasten to read it again to my young ones, since Captain Miles and the parson both fell asleep last Christmas when, at my mother's request, I read aloud two acts. However, anyone with a moderate familiarity with plays and novels can easily construct a complete picture from the sketch above. The narrative features an Indian king, a loving princess, and her attendant, secretly enamored with the British captain's servant; a traitor lurking within the English fort; a valiant Indian warrior harboring an unhappy passion for Pocahontas; a medicine-man and priest of the Indians (excellently portrayed by Palmer), capable of every treason, stratagem, and crime, bent solely on the torture and death of the English prisoner. These elements, combined with the hazards of the wilderness, the war-dances and cries—which Gumbo mimicked with remarkable accuracy based on his experiences with Native Americans—and the arrival of the English fleet, alongside allusions to recent glorious victories in Canada and the British determination to rule and conquer America, seemed to many of us promising ingredients for success.

Yet, I must mention the ill omens that preceded the opening night. Difficulties arose from a peevish, jealous, and timid management, compounded by violent prejudice held against the piece in certain high quarters. No wonder, then, that *Pocahontas* failed to achieve victory. I scoff at the malignity of the critics who found fault with the performance. Pretty critics, indeed, who praised *Carpezan* as a masterpiece while sneering at a far superior and more elaborate work! I maintain that Hagan delivered his role so admirably that a certain actor and theater manager might well have envied him. Had it not been for the cabal orchestrated outside, the piece surely would have succeeded. Orders had explicitly been given that the play should fail, according to Sampson's declaration. "The house swarmed with Macs, by George," the honest fellow swore, "and they should have the galleries washed with brimstone!" He vowed that even Mr. Garrick himself would not have wished the piece to succeed, reaching a peak of fury during the grand scene in the second act. Here, Smith (played by poor Hagan), bound to the stake, was saved by Pocahontas. When the entire house thrilled with applause and sympathy, Sampson was beside himself with rage.

Any curious individual may consult the published tragedy—in the octavo form or the subsequent splendid quarto edition of my *Collected Works, and Poems Original and Translated*—to judge whether the scene lacks merit, or whether the verses are not elegant, the language rich and noble. One cause of the failure was my strict adherence to historical accuracy. I had carefully copied a figure of Sir Walter Raleigh from the Museum, complete with frill and beard; using lace donated by my dear Theo from her mother's collection, we dressed Hagan precisely according to this drawing, ensuring he looked impeccable. Similarly, I costumed Miss Pritchard as Pocahontas, drawing upon my own observations of indigenous attire from home. Can it be believed that the audience tittered when she first appeared? They eventually grew accustomed to it, but just as she rushed into the prisoner's arms, causing many to tearfully embrace the moment, a man in the pit shouted, "Bedad! here's the Belle Savage kissing the Saracen's Head!" This provoked an impudent roar of laughter that erupted in fitful explosions throughout the remainder of the performance. As the wit in Mr. Sheridan's *Critic* noted regarding morning guns, the playwrights were not content with one jest but insisted on firing two or three. Thus, this wretched tavern joke of the "Belle Savage"—ignored by the uninformed, who did not realize Pocahontas herself was the namesake of the tavern—ruined the mood. My friend in the pit repeated the insult ad nauseam, saluting every new character with a tavern name: the English governor, sporting a long beard, became the "Goat and Boots"; his lieutenant, Barker, whose face was notably broad, was dubbed the "Bull and Mouth," and so on. The curtain finally descended amidst a shrill storm of whistles and hisses, particularly targeting poor Hagan whenever he opened his mouth.

Sampson spotted Master Will in the green boxes with some of his pretty acquaintances, convinced the treacherous scoundrel was a ringleader in the conspiracy. "I would have flung him over into the pit," the faithful fellow declared (and Sampson possessed the courage to follow through), "but I saw two of Mr. Nadab's followers prowling the lobby and was forced to retreat." Consequently, the eggs we had hoped to sell at market were broken, and our poor hopes lay shattered before us!
