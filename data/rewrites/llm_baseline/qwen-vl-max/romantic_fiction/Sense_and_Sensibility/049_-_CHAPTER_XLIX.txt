Edward’s freedom, though it seemed miraculous to everyone else, was now a fact—undeniable and complete. And what would become of that freedom? The answer was clear to all: after having endured the pain of one ill-advised engagement—entered into without his mother’s consent and lasting over four years—it was only natural that, once that bond was broken, he would rush into another. His purpose in coming to Barton was simple: to ask Elinor to marry him. Given that he had already been through such an ordeal before, one might have expected him to be more composed. Yet here he was, agitated, breathless, needing fresh air and reassurance as if he were making his first confession of love.

But soon enough, the moment came. He found the courage, seized the opportunity, spoke his heart, and received her answer. By the time they sat down to dinner at four o’clock—just three hours after his arrival—he had not only won Elinor’s hand but also secured her mother’s blessing. He was no longer just a hopeful lover; he was, in truth, one of the happiest men alive.

His joy was deeper than mere triumph. It wasn’t simply the joy of being loved in return—it was the relief of being freed from a past that had haunted him for years. He was released from an engagement with a woman he no longer cared for, from a promise that had brought him nothing but sorrow. Now, he stood before Elinor—not merely as a man who had found love, but as one who had escaped misery and stepped into peace.

And this transformation showed in everything he said. His voice was light, his manner open, his gratitude sincere. For the first time, he spoke freely to Elinor—not hiding his faults, not pretending to be perfect. He confessed his early attachment to Lucy with honesty and maturity.

“It was foolish,” he admitted, “a childish fancy born of ignorance and idleness. Had I been given some profession when I left Mr. Pratt’s care at eighteen, I believe I never would have fallen into it. At the time, I thought my feelings for Lucy were unshakable—but I had seen so little of the world, and so few women, that I couldn’t judge. She was kind, she was pretty—I thought so then—and I had no one else to turn to. I spent most of my time at Longstaple, where I felt welcome and comfortable. And so, gradually, I became attached. But looking back, I can see how easily it could have passed, had I had something else to occupy me.”

He paused, then added softly, “I don’t regret it entirely. It taught me much about myself—about how easily affection can be mistaken for love. But I do regret letting it go on so long.”

Elinor listened, her heart swelling with tenderness. The change in the Dashwood household was immense. Mrs. Dashwood, overwhelmed with joy, could barely contain herself. She praised Edward, adored Elinor, and struggled to balance her happiness with her desire not to embarrass them. Marianne, quieter, wept—not out of sadness, but because her own emotions were too deep to express. Her joy was real, but it carried the weight of loss, of unspoken comparisons. Only Elinor remained silent—though not from calmness, but from being utterly undone by her own happiness.

From the moment she learned Lucy was married to Robert, to the moment Edward stood before her, declaring his love, her mind had raced with hope, fear, and disbelief. But once it was done—once every doubt vanished, once she saw Edward free and devoted—she was overwhelmed. She could not breathe, could not think. Her heart was full, and it took hours before she could find any measure of stillness.

Edward stayed at the cottage for at least a week—longer than anyone expected. He had no intention of leaving before he had spoken every word he needed to say, relived every memory, and laid bare every dream. With lovers, silence is never final. Every topic must be revisited, every emotion re-expressed.

Their conversation turned quickly to Lucy’s marriage—the event that had changed everything. To Elinor, it was astonishing, almost absurd. How could Robert, whom she knew to be indifferent to Lucy’s beauty, fall so completely for her? How could he marry her when she was already engaged to his brother? And why did his family punish Edward for loving her, yet accept Robert’s choice?

She shook her head. “It makes no sense.”

Edward offered a theory. “Perhaps,” he said, “it began with flattery. Lucy may have played on Robert’s vanity, and Robert on her ambition. She might have hoped he could help me—then, when that failed, she looked elsewhere.”

Elinor recalled Robert’s words in Harley Street—how he believed he could have influenced Edward’s fate had he intervened sooner. “That,” she said, “was exactly like Robert. He may have thought that way from the start. And Lucy may have begun with the idea of gaining his support for you—only to realize later that she wanted him for herself.”

They both wondered how long it had gone on. Edward had been at Oxford, far removed from London life, relying only on Lucy’s letters. Until the very end, those letters were warm, regular, loving. There was no hint of betrayal—no warning.

Then came the letter from Lucy.

> *“Dear Sir,*
> *Being very sure I have long lost your affections, I have thought myself at liberty to bestow my own on another, and have no doubt of being as happy with him as I once used to think I might be with you. But I scorn to accept a hand while the heart was another’s. I sincerely wish you happy in your choice, and it shall not be my fault if we are not always good friends, as our near relationship now makes proper.*
> *I can safely say I owe you no ill-will, and am sure you will be too generous to do us any ill offices. Your brother has gained my affections entirely, and as we could not live without one another, we are just returned from the altar, and are now on our way to Dawlish for a few weeks—which place your dear brother has great curiosity to see. But I thought I would first trouble you with these few lines, and shall always remain,*
> *Your sincere well-wisher, friend, and sister,*
> *Lucy Ferrars.*
> *I have burnt all your letters, and will return your picture the first opportunity. Please to destroy my scrawls—but the ring with my hair you are very welcome to keep.”*

Elinor read it quietly, then handed it back.

“I won’t ask your opinion of it as writing,” Edward said. “I’d rather not have you seen any of her letters before. As a sister, it’s bad enough. As a wife—how I blushed over her pages! This is the only letter I’ve ever received from her that made up for its style.”

“And yet,” Elinor said gently, “you once believed her honest.”

“I did,” he admitted. “I thought her affection genuine. I didn’t know her true nature until now. She meant to deceive. She sent Thomas with that message to make me suffer—deliberately.”

Elinor nodded. “She had no regard for you. She used you. And she knew it.”

Edward sighed. “I kept believing she was kind-hearted. I told myself she was only ignorant, poorly educated. But now I see it clearly—her actions were selfish, calculated. She didn’t care for me at all.”

“Yet you never broke off the engagement,” Elinor said.

“I couldn’t,” he replied. “When my mother disowned me, I had no support. She insisted on standing by me—offering to share my fate. How could I think she was doing it for money or status? I believed it was love. And even now, I don’t understand why she would stay bound to a man she didn’t love, with only two thousand pounds to his name.”

“She may have hoped something better would come,” Elinor said. “Or she may have known that staying engaged gave her status. It was respectable. And if nothing better came along, marrying you was better than being alone.”

Edward nodded slowly. “Of course. It was natural. I should have seen it.”

Elinor smiled faintly. “You should have stayed away from Norland. You knew your heart wasn’t steady.”

“I thought,” he said, “that my engagement would protect me. That my faithfulness to Lucy would keep my feelings safe. I admired you—but I told myself it was only friendship. I didn’t compare you until I began to wonder… and then I couldn’t stop.”

“You were wrong,” Elinor said. “But I forgive you.”

He looked at her, eyes soft. “I’m glad.”

They talked of Colonel Brandon, whom Edward now wished to meet. He wanted to thank him properly—for the living of Delaford, which had changed everything. “I never thanked him right,” he said. “My thanks were cold, even grudging. He must think I’ve never forgiven him.”

“And now?” Elinor asked.

“Now I’m grateful. And I want to tell him so.”

He had heard little about Delaford—his knowledge of the estate came entirely from Elinor, who had listened attentively to Colonel Brandon’s descriptions. She knew the land, the tithes, the garden, the house—better than he did.

Only one matter remained unresolved: their future. They loved each other deeply, had the approval of their families, and understood each other perfectly. But they had no real income. Edward had two thousand pounds. Elinor had one. The living at Delaford provided thirty-five hundred pounds a year—but neither of them could count on Mrs. Dashwood’s help. Three hundred and fifty pounds a year wouldn’t sustain them comfortably.

Edward still hoped his mother might soften toward him. Perhaps she would restore his inheritance. But Elinor feared otherwise. Since Robert had married Lucy, and since Mrs. Ferrars had praised Edward’s choice of her as “only a lesser evil,” she suspected Robert’s offense would only benefit Fanny.

Four days after Edward’s arrival, Colonel Brandon arrived. Mrs. Dashwood was delighted—so much company in her small cottage was a rare treat. Edward, as the first guest, kept his room, while Brandon walked to the Park each night and returned in the morning, often arriving just in time to interrupt their quiet mornings.

Brandon, after three weeks at Delaford, was weary—his thoughts consumed by the age difference between himself and Marianne. But among friends, and under the warmth of their welcome, he began to recover. He knew nothing of Lucy’s marriage. When Mrs. Dashwood told him, he was astonished—but also pleased. He saw that his act of kindness had led to Elinor’s happiness.

The two men grew closer with each passing day. Their shared values, their similar temperaments, their mutual respect for each other’s sisters—these things drew them together naturally. A friendship formed quickly, rooted in sincerity and common ground.

Letters from town arrived, once filled with excitement for Elinor—now read with amusement. Mrs. Jennings wrote with outrage: “Lucy’s betrayal was monstrous! Poor Edward—broken-hearted at Oxford! I can’t bear to think of him. You must bring him to Barton and let Miss Marianne comfort him.”

Mr. Dashwood’s tone was solemn. “Mrs. Ferrars is devastated. Fanny suffers terribly. Robert’s crime is unforgivable—but Lucy’s is worse. Neither shall ever be mentioned again. If Mrs. Ferrars forgives Robert, his wife shall never be recognized.”

He added: “Edward has not written. Perhaps he fears offending. I shall write to Oxford, suggesting a letter of submission—perhaps addressed to Fanny, to be shown to her mother.”

Edward frowned. “A letter of submission? Beg my mother’s pardon for Robert’s ingratitude to her—and for her breach of honor to me? No. I cannot submit. I am not penitent. I am happy.”

“But you could ask forgiveness,” Elinor said gently. “You did offend. And you might express regret for entering into that first engagement.”

He agreed. “Yes. But not a letter of submission. I’d prefer to speak to Fanny in person.”

So it was decided: instead of writing, Edward would go to London and plead his case directly.

Marianne, now more thoughtful, remarked, “If John and Fanny truly try to reconcile you with your mother, perhaps they’re not so bad after all.”

After a brief visit, the two gentlemen left Barton together. They went first to Delaford—so Edward could see his future home, and assist Colonel Brandon in planning improvements. From there, he would travel to London, ready to face his family—not as a beggar, but as a man who had found love, freedom, and peace.
