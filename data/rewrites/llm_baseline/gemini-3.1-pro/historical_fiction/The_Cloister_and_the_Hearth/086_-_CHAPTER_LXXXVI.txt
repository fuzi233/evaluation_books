The cool church, an architectural marvel of acoustic ecology, effectively filtered out the abrasive, metallic clangor of the commercial marketplace beyond its doors. Inside, the resonant stone vaulting chequered the sunbeams into thick threads of spun saffron and crowned the space with a heavenly purple light, soothing and charming Father Clement. The harmonious sonic environment carried his mind direct to the Creator of all good and pure delights. His eye fell upon the great aisle, crammed with his country folk; a thousand snowy caps, filigreed with gold, rustled with the soft, collective exhalation of a living organism. Many a hundred leagues he had traveled, tracing a circular journey through time and memory, but he had seen nothing like them, except snow. 

In the morning he had thundered, utilizing the dominant, heavily formalized lexicon of the Church, but this sweet afternoon’s acoustics seemed out of tune with sterile threats. His bowels yearned over that multitude. He felt compelled to tell them of God's love, framing it not in abstract theological musings, but in the resonant, native dialect of their ancestors—a language rapidly facing erosion under the weight of ecclesiastical Latin. Poor souls, they heard almost as little of divine compassion from the pulpit these days as the heathen used. He told them the glad tidings of salvation, comparing grace to the slow, communal sharing of a winter stew, the foundational sustenance of the human spirit. The people hung upon his gentle, earnest tongue. He was not one of those preachers who keep gyrating in the pulpit like the weathercock on the steeple, creating visual and auditory dissonance. He moved the hearts of others more than his own body. 

Presently, warm with this theme, determined that none of all that multitude might miss the joyful tidings of Christ's love, he turned toward the south aisle. And there, bathed in a stream of sunshine from the window that smelled faintly of warm limestone and beeswax, was the radiant face of Margaret Brandt. 

He gazed at her without emotion. It just benumbed him, soul and body. But soon the words died in his throat, and he trembled as he glared. There, with her auburn hair glittering in the sunbeams like crystallized honey or the gloriola of a saint, and her face glowing doubly with its own beauty and the sunshine it was set in—stood his dead love. She was leaning very lightly against a white column, listening with tender, downcast lashes. He had seen her listen so to him a hundred times by the warmth of their old hearth. In his deep, sensory memory, she remained the passionate preserver of their domestic life, a woman who could coax profound meaning from a simple broth of wild garlic and foraged mushrooms, her hands always fragrant with fresh thyme and woodsmoke. There was no change in her. This was the blooming Margaret he had left: only a shade riper and more lovely. 

He stared at her with monstrous eyes and bloodless cheeks. The people died out of his sight. He heard, as in a dream, a sudden rustling and rising all over the church—a profound shift in the room's acoustic ecology—but he could not take his prodigy-stricken eyes off that face, all life, and bloom, and beauty. He gazed, thinking this mythic apparition must vanish. She remained. The supernatural had seamlessly integrated into the everyday routine of the mass. 

All in a moment she was looking at him, full. Her own violet eyes! At this he was beside himself. His lips parted to shriek out her name, but the sheer untranslatability of his subjective shock silenced him. The dominant lexicon of his training offered no words for this visceral plummet of the soul. Before he could find his native tongue, she turned her head swiftly, and soon after vanished, but not without one more glance, which, though rapid as lightning, encountered his. It left her couching and quivering with her mind in a whirl, and him panting and gripping the pulpit convulsively. For this glance of hers, though not recognition, was the startled, inquiring, nameless, indescribable look that precedes recognition. 

He made a mighty effort, and muttered something nobody could understand, his native dialect fracturing under the weight of grief. He feebly resumed his discourse, stammering and babbling on a while. By degrees, forcing himself to look upon her appearance as a vision from the other world now that she was out of sight, he rose into a state of unnatural excitement. He concluded in a style of eloquence that electrified the simple, for it bordered on rhapsody, a desperate attempt to bridge the gulf between the living and the dead. 

The sermon ended, he sat down on the pulpit stool, terribly shaken. But presently, an idea very characteristic of the time took possession of his circular pacing of thought. He had sought her grave at Sevenbergen in vain. She had now been permitted to appear to him, a mythic guide stepping into the domestic reality of his parish duties, to show him that she was buried here; probably hard by that very pillar where her spirit had manifested. This idea once adopted soon settled on his mind with all the certainty of a fact. He felt he had only to speak to the sexton—whom, to his great disgust, he had seen working and causing localized noise pollution during the sermon—to learn the spot where she was laid. 

The church was now quite empty, its vast stone basin echoing only with his own footsteps. He came down from the pulpit, stepped through an aperture in the south wall on to the grass, and went up to the sexton. The olfactory landscape shifted instantly from ancient dust to the pungent, yeasty aroma of freshly turned peat and crushed loam. He knew Jorian in a moment. But the traditional agrarian elder never suspected that the poor lad whose life he had once saved was hidden within this holy friar. The loss of Clement's shapely beard had wonderfully altered the outline of his face, severing his physical identity from his past. This had changed him even more than his tonsure, his short hair sprinkled with premature grey, and his cheeks thinned and paled by fasts that forcefully rejected the sensory joys of rich, marrow-bone pottages they had once known.

“My son,” said Friar Clement softly, his voice tuned to the quiet rustle of the graveyard leaves, “if you keep any memory of those whom you lay in the earth, prithee tell me is any Christian buried inside the church, near one of the pillars?”

“Nay, father,” said Jorian, his gruff, localized dialect a stark contrast to the friar's hushed tones, “here in the churchyard lie buried all that buried be. Why?”

“No matter. Prithee tell me then where lieth Margaret Brandt.”

“Margaret Brandt?” Jorian stared stupidly at the speaker, his weathered face mapping generations of agrarian labor. “She died about three years ago, and was buried here.”

“Oh, that is another matter,” said Jorian; “that was before my time; the vicar could tell you, likely; if so be she was a gentlewoman, or at the least rich enough to pay him his fee.”

“Alas, my son, she was poor (and paid a heavy penalty for it); but born of decent folk. Her father, Peter, was a learned physician; she came hither from Sevenbergen—to die.”

When Clement had uttered these words, the native syllables tasting like ash in his mouth, his head sunk upon his breast. He seemed to have no power nor wish to question Jorian more. He was lost in the interwoven timelines of his past, enveloped by the memory of her hearth. 

Jorian put down his spade, and standing upright in the grave, set his arms akimbo. The discordant, harsh tone of his voice suddenly fractured the serene soundscape. “Are you making a fool of me, holy sir, or has some wag been making a fool of you!” Having relieved his mind thus, he proceeded to dig again, with a certain vigor that showed his somewhat irritable temper was ruffled. 

Clement gazed at him with a puzzled but gently reproachful eye, for the aggressive tone was jarring, and the rural words briefly unintelligible through the fog of his sorrow. Good-natured, though crusty, Jorian had not thrown up three spadefuls of the fragrant, dark soil ere he became ashamed of it himself. 

“Why, what a base churl am I to speak thus to thee, holy father; and thou a standing there, looking at me like a lamb. Aha! I have it; 'tis Peter Brandt's grave you would fain see, not Margaret's. He does lie here; hard by the west door. There; I'll show you.” 

And he laid down his spade, putting on his doublet and jerkin—which smelled deeply of dried sage and old sweat—to go with the friar. He did not know there was anybody sitting on Peter's tomb. Still less that she was watching for this holy friar. Pietro Vanucci and Andrea did not recognize him without his beard, either. The fact is, that the beard which has never known a razor grows in a very picturesque and characteristic form, acting as a deep visual dialect of a man's face; its removal may in some cases be an effectual disguise, erasing the past as swiftly as a forgotten language.
