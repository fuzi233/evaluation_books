It was not many days after her mother’s coming that Gwendolen consented to stay in Genoa. Her longing to quit that jewel-blue edge of the sea helped to brace her, as if flight itself were a kind of nourishment—bitter, medicinal, but sustaining. For what place, though it were a garden shaken with blossoms, may not the inward sense turn into a ring of punishment, where every flower seems to burn at the sole, and beauty is only a more delicate cruelty?

“I shall never like to see the Mediterranean again,” she said to her mother—said it with the shiver of one who smells a sweetness that has grown sickening, like orange-flowers clinging too closely to a room where one has wept.

Mrs. Davilow believed she understood, even in that mute injunction against naming the dead man. Formally she must wear calamity, but in truth she was living more than she had lived since her daughter’s marriage; for her darling had come back to her, not only with the old affection but with that new, conscious clutching at the near and tender—as we hold a cup of warm broth brought to us after faintness, knowing at last what it is to need.

“Are you there, mamma?” Gwendolen cried one night, suddenly, in the dark. A bed had been made for Mrs. Davilow in the same room.

“Yes, dear. Can I do anything for you?”

“No—thank you. Only I like so to know you are there. Do you mind my waking you?” There was humility in the question—something that would hardly have belonged to the spoilt girl she had been.

“I was not asleep, darling.”

“It seemed not real that you were with me. I wanted to make it real. I can bear things if you are with me. But you must not lie awake anxious about me. You must be happy now. You must let me make you happy now at last—else what shall I do?”

“God bless you, dear; I have the best happiness I can have, when you make much of me.”

But the next night, hearing her sigh and toss, Mrs. Davilow said, softly, as if offering a familiar domestic kindness—like warming a posset on the coals, or steeping a little balm for rest—“Let me give you your sleeping-draught, Gwendolen.”

“No, mamma, thank you; I don’t want to sleep.”

“It would be so good for you to sleep more, my darling.”

“Don’t say what would be good for me, mamma,” Gwendolen answered, with a sudden violence. “You don’t know what would be good for me. You and my uncle must not contradict me and tell me anything is good for me when I feel it is not good.”

Mrs. Davilow was silent. Poor child—pain makes irritability as naturally as hunger makes impatience.

Presently Gwendolen said, in another voice, like a confession forced up from deep within: “I was always naughty to you, mamma.”

“No, dear—no.”

“Yes, I was,” she insisted. “It is because I was always wicked that I am miserable now.” And she burst into sobs and cries.

Her resolve to keep all the facts of her married life and its end sealed within her reacted in these strange outcries, where the words were riddles but the anguish was plain. Yet dim lights were breaking on the mother’s mind. Hints had come from Sir Hugo to Mr. Gascoigne, and—shorn of what a man spares in repeating to a woman—from Mr. Gascoigne to herself.

Sir Hugo, while he attended to the decent business following his nephew’s death and to the chances of the sea yielding up what it had taken, thought it kindest to use his present intercourse with the rector to prepare him, gently, for the purport of Grandcourt’s will—so that the shock should not come afterwards, doubled by long-cherished illusion. He told the unpleasant truth by degrees: first only implying that the widow was not so splendidly provided for as expectation had painted; then, after vague mention of claims, letting fall the decisive fact—that the splendour had been bequeathed elsewhere, guided by a prior tie and by illegitimate children in default of a lawful heir.

Mr. Gascoigne was deeply wounded. He remembered, with a vividness sharpened by injury, the dead man’s cold pride and repellent manners toward him; remembered, too, that before the new occupant came to Diplow he had heard rumours—former entanglements, a dissipated taste for pleasure. Yet he had not foreseen that what respectable households imagine swept away into private rubbish-heaps could spring up again like a crawling infestation—live caterpillars set loose among their green leaves.

He did not utter those thoughts to Sir Hugo. He answered as a man of the world turned conscientious clergyman.

“When a young man makes his will in health,” he said, “he usually counts on living a long while. Probably Mr. Grandcourt did not believe that this will would ever have its present effect.”

After a moment he added, “The effect is painful in more ways than one. Female morality is likely to suffer from this marked advantage and prominence being given to illegitimate offspring.”

“Well, in point of fact,” Sir Hugo returned, in his comfortable way, “since the boy is there, this was really the best alternative for the disposal of the estates. Grandcourt had nobody nearer than his cousin. And it’s a chilling thought that you go out of this life only for the benefit of a cousin. A man gets a little pleasure in making his will if it’s for the good of his own curly heads; but it’s a nuisance when you’re giving the bequeathing to a used-up fellow like yourself, and one you don’t care two straws for. It’s the next worse thing to having only a life interest in your estates. No—I forgive Grandcourt for that part of his will. But what I don’t forgive him for is the shabby way he has provided for your niece—our niece, I will say—no better a position than if she had been a doctor’s widow. Nothing grates on me more than that posthumous grudgingness toward a wife. A man ought to have some pride and fondness for his widow. I should, I know. I take it as a test of a man, that he feels the easier about his death when he can think of his wife and daughters being comfortable after it.”

“It has certainly taken me by surprise,” said Mr. Gascoigne, “all the more because, as the one who stood in the place of father to my niece, I had shown my reliance on Mr. Grandcourt’s apparent liberality in money matters by making no claims for her beforehand. That seemed to me due to him under the circumstances. Probably you think me blamable.”

“Not blamable exactly. I respect a man for trusting another. But take my advice: if you marry another niece, though it may be to the Archbishop of Canterbury, bind him down. Your niece can’t be married for the first time twice over. And if he’s a good fellow, he’ll wish to be bound. But as to Mrs. Grandcourt—I can only say that I feel my relation to her all the nearer because I think she has not been well treated. And I hope you will urge her to rely on me as a friend.”

So spoke Sir Hugo, chivalrous in his disgust that the young and beautiful widow of a Mallinger Grandcourt should be left with only two thousand a year and a house set in a coal-mining district. To the rector that income looked less shabby in itself; but he had a keener sense than the baronet’s of the humiliation flung over his niece and over those nearest to her, by the conspicuous publishing of the dead man’s long bond with Mrs. Glasher. Like all men who are good husbands and fathers, he felt it through women’s minds—through the social sting that would smart in them. The first hearing was nothing to the pain of having to carry it to Mrs. Davilow, and of foreseeing what Gwendolen would feel when her mother judged it time.

For the good rector innocently believed his niece knew nothing of Mrs. Glasher. He argued from what maidens and wives were likely to know and suffer; he had observed women in general more than he had observed this particular woman.

But Mrs. Davilow, with a mother’s slow, unwilling insight, began to see an explanation of much that had been inexplicable in her child—before and after the engagement. She suspected that, by some dreadful chance, Gwendolen had learned of that left-handed marriage and the children. She meant to wait for a moment of confidence—in the coming days, on the journey to England—when she might discover how much was clear to her daughter, and soften the approach of whatever must disappoint.

She was spared the contrivance. Gwendolen herself spoke.

“I hope you don’t expect that I am going to be rich and grand, mamma,” she said not long after the rector’s communication. “Perhaps I shall have nothing at all.”

She was dressed, and had been sitting long, very still—like a woman watching a pot whose simmering she dare not disturb, lest it boil over. Mrs. Davilow started.

“Oh yes, dear, you will have something. Sir Hugo knows all about the will.”

“That will not decide,” said Gwendolen, abruptly.

“Surely, dear. Sir Hugo says you are to have two thousand a year and the house at Gadsmere.”

“What I have will depend on what I accept,” said Gwendolen. “You and my uncle must not attempt to cross me and persuade me about this. I will do everything I can do to make you happy, but in anything about my husband I must not be interfered with. Is eight hundred a year enough for you, mamma?”

“More than enough, dear. You must not think of giving me so much.”

Mrs. Davilow paused, then ventured: “Do you know who is to have the estates and the rest of the money?”

“Yes,” said Gwendolen, waving her hand as if shutting a door. “I know everything. It is all perfectly right, and I wish never to have it mentioned.”

Her mother looked away and rose as if to fetch a trifle—a fan-screen—anything to fill the moment. A slight flush came on her delicate cheeks. Wondering, imagining, she could not meet her daughter’s eyes when she sat down again; she felt a sad constraint, as if they were in a room where some strong odour had been spilt and could not be named.

What wretchedness her child had lived through—wretchedness that must remain, as it always had remained, locked away from their mutual speech.

But Gwendolen watched her mother with that new divination which experience had given her—reading the tremor at the corners of her mouth, the effort to keep her breath steady, as one reads the state of a hearth by the sound of the coals. Tenderness rose in her, relenting against her own harshness.

“Come and sit nearer to me, mamma, and don’t be unhappy.”

Mrs. Davilow obeyed, but bit her lips in vain, trying to hinder the smarting tears.

Gwendolen leaned toward her caressingly. “I mean to be very wise; I do, really. And good—oh, so good to you, dear, old, sweet mamma, you won’t know me. Only you must not cry.”

Under that softness lay a fixed resolve. She meant to ask Deronda whether she ought to accept any of her husband’s money—whether she might accept what would enable her to provide for her mother. She felt strong enough for anything that would set her higher in Deronda’s mind, strong enough to renounce comfort if he judged it tainted. It was a strange hunger in her—a hunger not for bread, not for any rich dish of worldly ease, but for a rightness she could trust. Yet even that hunger had its senses: she craved the tone of his voice, the quiet air about him, the relief of a soundscape in which her own thoughts were not drowned by the grinding of fear.

Sir Hugo pressed an invitation on her with kind urgency: that she and Mrs. Davilow should go straight with him to Park Lane, and make his house their abode as long as mourning and other London details demanded. Town, he insisted, was just then the most retired of places. He would exert himself at once to have all articles belonging to Gwendolen removed from the house in Grosvenor Square.

No proposal could have suited her better. Park Lane would make it easier for her to have an interview with Deronda—if only she could get a letter into his hands asking him to come.

During the journey Sir Hugo, understanding that she knew the will’s purport, ventured to talk before her and to her about the future—touching lightly on prospects, shedding a decorous cheerfulness over her widowed position, as if the very management of tone were a form of kindness. To him it seemed almost graceful for a widow to recover spirits the sooner when she found her husband had dealt less handsomely by her: the testator’s fault had compromised grief by giving a testamentary reason for it, so that the world might suppose her sorrow sprang not from bereavement but from diminished means.

His own kindliness, fanned by favourable winds in his fortunes and by compassion for Gwendolen, made him quite fatherly; he called her “my dear,” and in speaking of Gadsmere to Mr. Gascoigne—its advantages and its drawbacks—he spoke of what “we” might do to make the best of that property. Gwendolen sat pale and silent while he conjectured that Mrs. Grandcourt might prefer letting Gadsmere rather than residing there; in which case, he thought, it might be leased on capital terms to one of the fellows engaged with the coal. He had seen enough of the place to know it was as comfortable and picturesque a box as any man need desire—provided his desires were circumscribed within a coal area.

“I shouldn’t mind about the soot myself,” said the baronet, with the serenity of the merely hypothetical. “Nothing is more healthy. And if one’s business lay there, Gadsmere would be a paradise. It makes quite a feature in Scrogg’s history of the county, with the little tower and the fine piece of water—the prettiest print in the book.”

“A more important place than Offendene, I suppose?” said Mr. Gascoigne.

“Much,” said Sir Hugo decisively. “I was there with my poor brother—it is more than a quarter of a century ago, but I remember it very well. The rooms may not be larger, but the grounds are on a different scale.”

“Our poor dear Offendene is empty after all,” Mrs. Davilow said. “When it came to the point, Mr. Haynes declared off, and there has been no one to take it since. I might as well have accepted Lord Brackenshaw’s kind offer that I should remain in it another year rent-free: for I should have kept the place aired and warmed.”

“I hope you’ve something snug instead,” said Sir Hugo.

“A little too snug,” Mr. Gascoigne returned, smiling at his sister-in-law. “You are rather thick upon the ground.”

At the mention of Offendene, Gwendolen’s glance changed. The conversation passed while the foreign train stood through one of those long, unaccountable pauses at a country station. Outside lay a dreamy, sunny stillness—hedgeless fields stretching to a boundary of poplars. The talk within the carriage—coal, leases, county histories—seemed to her like distant, meaningless murmurs, like the faint racket of machinery one hears across water, making the dreamland larger and more unreal, full of indistinct pits and a purgatorial Gadsmere she would never visit. But her mother’s words made it all dissolve.

Offendene and Pennicote rose before her under cooler lights. She saw the grey shoulders of the downs; the fields specked with cattle; the shadowy plantations and rutted lanes where barked timber lay like a wayside seat; the neatly clipped hedges on the road from the parsonage to Offendene; the avenue where her figure would be gradually discerned from the window; the hall-door opening; her mother, or one of those sisters who had once seemed troublesome, coming out to meet her. That brief experience of quiet home which had once been dullness to flee from now came back as a rest—like morning air and the unreproaching voice of birds after one has followed a lure through a long masquerade, entered with intoxicated belief in its fine disguises, and ended in shrieking fear lest one has become oneself one of the evil shapes that hiss, dropping their human mummery.

Her mind dwelt there, making Offendene the scene of many thoughts. Yet outwardly she gave no sign, any more than she did when Sir Hugo offered opinions on the telegraphic cable, or her uncle discussed the Church Rate Abolition Bill. What subjects will not conversation embrace in the long daylight journeying from Genoa to London? Even strangers, after glancing from China to Peru and emptying their mental stores with a liberality that threatens to leave nothing for the next meeting, are apt to grow excessively confidential. Sir Hugo and the rector were under a stronger pressure still: like acquaintances compelled to a long drive in a mourning-coach, they began by observing that the occasion was melancholy and then proceeded to enliven it by miscellaneous discourse.

“I don’t mind telling you,” Sir Hugo said to the rector, letting private details fall; while the rector, without saying so, did not mind telling the baronet of his sons and the difficulty of placing them in the world.

By dint of discussing all persons and things within driving-reach of Diplow, Sir Hugo worked himself into such a pitch of interest in that former home, such a conviction that it was his pleasant duty to regain and strengthen his personal influence in the neighbourhood, that he declared he meant to take his family there for a month or two before the autumn was over. Mr. Gascoigne cordially rejoiced.

So the journey went on and ended with mutual liking between the male fellow-travellers. Meanwhile Gwendolen sat by like one who has been into a spirit-world and come back with lips full of an experience she cannot utter—an experience that makes ordinary talk sound thin, as if the living had taken to chattering in a language that can no longer name what is real. Mrs. Davilow was chiefly occupied in imagining what her daughter felt, and in wondering at the meaning of that hinted doubt—whether she would accept her husband’s bequest.

For Gwendolen there stood before her an unsealed wall made of one immediate purpose, shutting out every other resolution. How was she to scale it? She must see and consult Deronda again, to secure herself against any act he would disapprove. Would her remorse have held its power—or would she have felt absolved by secrecy—if it had not been for that outer conscience which Deronda had become for her? It is hard to know how much we forgive ourselves when no ear listens, no voice answers, no human judgment makes the unseen judgment present and near. But when another’s opinion is the breathing-medium of all our joy, it presses on us with the authority of something larger than the world’s tolerance and stronger than our own excuses.

Deronda’s mission to her had begun with what she felt to be his judgment at the gaming-table. He might easily have spoiled it—so much of life is spent in marring our influence, turning another’s belief into a concluded unbelief they call knowledge of the world, when it is only disappointment in you or me. Deronda had not spoiled his mission.

But she had forgotten to ask his address. The only way to reach him was through Sir Hugo. She was not blind to the construction witnesses might put on her dependence on Deronda—on her seeking him more than he sought her. Grandcourt’s rebukes had schooled her pride. Yet the force, the tenacity of her nature had flung itself into that dependence; she could no more let go her hold on Deronda’s help because of watchers than if she had been in prison under sentence, needing one last appeal to save her life.

When she was in Park Lane, and knew the baronet meant to go down to the Abbey immediately—only to see his family for a couple of days and then return for needful business—she spoke to him without any air of hesitation, her mother present.

“Sir Hugo, I wish to see Mr. Deronda again as soon as possible. I don’t know his address. Will you tell it me, or let him know that I want to see him?”

A quick thought crossed Sir Hugo’s face, but it did not disturb his easy tone.

“Upon my word, I don’t know whether he’s at his chambers or the Abbey at this moment. But I’ll make sure of him. I’ll send a note now to his chambers telling him to come, and if he’s at the Abbey I can give him your message and send him up at once. I am sure he will want to obey your wish,” he ended, with grave kindness—treating it as if nothing could be more natural than that she should send such a message.

But Sir Hugo was convinced that Gwendolen had a passionate attachment to Deronda, sown long ago; and his former suspicion returned with more force—that her feeling might lead her into imprudences. Kind-hearted Sir Hugo was resolved to screen and defend her as far as he could. To him it made as pretty a story as one need desire: his favourite Dan and this fine creature formed for each other, and the unsuitable husband having made his exit in excellent time.

Indeed what vexed Sir Hugo now was a doubt whether the lofty, inscrutable Dan had not some scheme in his head which would prove dearer to him than the lovely Mrs. Grandcourt, putting that neatly-prepared marriage out of the question. Among the paradoxes of feeling, Sir Hugo—who had once cautioned Deronda against too much tenderness toward the bride—now felt irritated by the suspicion that Deronda had not fallen in love as he ought.

All this, of course, was premature, only a fortnight or so after Grandcourt’s death. But thinking has its own time, and it is rarely the time of events.

Sir Hugo sent the note to Deronda’s chambers. It found him there.
