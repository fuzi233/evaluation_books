It was not many days after her mother’s arrival that Gwendolen consented to remain at Genoa. Her desire to flee that ancient gem of the sea helped rally her strength. The Mediterranean winds carried the rich, historical scents of roasted pine nuts, salt-cured olives, and wild coastal fennel, but to Gwendolen, the sensory opulence of the world had curdled. Even the rhythmic, mythic wash of the waves against the shore—a sound that had organized the daily lives of generations of mariners—had become a circle of punishment. The acoustic environment, rather than soothing her, echoed the unrelenting, circular pacing of her own guilt, turning the fragrant seaside into a purgatory where the blooming flora felt no better than flame-tongues searing the soles of her feet. 

“I shall never like to see the Mediterranean again,” Gwendolen told her mother. Mrs. Davilow believed she understood her child’s revulsion, sensing the untranslatable trauma that lay beneath Gwendolen’s tacit prohibition against speaking of her late husband. Though formally bound to observe this period as one of severe calamity, Mrs. Davilow found a profound, almost agrarian comfort in tending to her daughter again. Gwendolen had been returned to her, and the older woman embraced the meticulous, domestic rhythms of caretaking.

“Are you there, mamma?” cried Gwendolen in the middle of the night, a regression to the native emotional dialect of her early girlhood. A bed had been made for Mrs. Davilow in the same room, altering the acoustic space into one of shared, intimate breath.

“Yes, dear; can I do anything for you?” Mrs. Davilow was already awake, tending to a small spirit-lamp. She was simmering a restorative tisane—a traditional infusion of crushed valerian root, dried lemon peel, and a drop of distilled orange blossom water, an ancestral remedy meant to ground a fractured mind through the palate. 

“No, thank you; only I like so to know you are there. Do you mind my waking you?” 

“I was not asleep, darling.”

“It seemed not real that you were with me. I wanted to make it real. I can bear things if you are with me. But you must not lie awake, anxious about me. You must let me make you happy now at last—else what shall I do?”

“God bless you, dear; I have the best happiness I can have, when you make much of me.”

But the next night, hearing her daughter sighing and restless, Mrs. Davilow offered the warm, fragrant cup. “Let me give you your draught, Gwendolen. Taste it; the linden bracts are very soothing.”

“No, mamma, thank you; I don’t want to sleep.” Gwendolen rejected the brew, unable to view the offering as anything more than utilitarian fuel to force unconsciousness. She lacked the sensory appreciation to find meaning in sustenance now. “You don’t know what would be good for me. You and my uncle must not contradict me and tell me anything is good for me when I feel it is not good.”

Mrs. Davilow remained silent, the quiet of the room thickening. Presently, Gwendolen said, “I was always naughty to you, mamma.”

“No, dear, no.”

“Yes, I was,” Gwendolen insisted, her voice breaking the nocturnal peace. “It is because I was always wicked that I am miserable now.” She burst into sobs. Her determination to remain silent about the reality of her married life created an immense pressure, her subjective experience entirely untranslatable into the polite lexicon of her family.

Yet, dim lights of interpretation were breaking upon the mother’s mind through information passing from Sir Hugo to Mr. Gascoigne, and eventually to herself. The good-natured baronet, while dutifully attending to the legal mechanisms of his nephew’s death, had taken it upon himself to break the purport of Grandcourt’s will to the rector. He did so by degrees, navigating the conversation with the deliberate pacing of a slow-braised reduction, eventually disclosing the prior relations that had diverted the splendor of the estate away from the widow. 

The rector was deeply hurt. Mr. Gascoigne possessed the soul of a traditional cultivator; he viewed his parish and family through the lens of seasonal harvest and generational respectability. He vividly remembered the offensively proud manners of the deceased, and how he had previously heard hints of Grandcourt’s entangling dissipations. He had not foreseen that the bitter dregs of those past pleasures, swept into private rubbish-heaps, would crawl forth to devour the tender green crops of respectable people. But he did not lower himself by expressing indignation. He behaved like a conscientious man of the world, adjusting to the dominant legal lexicon that now dictated his niece’s worth.

“When a young man makes his will in health, he usually counts on living a long while,” the rector observed, his voice steady against the rumbling acoustic backdrop of their carriage ride. “The effect is painful in more ways than one. Female morality is likely to suffer from this marked advantage given to illegitimate offspring.”

“Well, since the boy is there, this was really the best alternative for the estates,” said Sir Hugo comfortably. “But what I don’t forgive him for, is the shabby way he has provided for your niece—leaving her no better a position than a doctor’s widow. I take it as a test of a man, that he feels easier about his death when he can think of his wife being well-provisioned and comfortable.”

“It has certainly taken me by surprise,” said Mr. Gascoigne. “I had shown my reliance on Mr. Grandcourt’s apparent liberality by making no claims for her beforehand.”

“Not blamable exactly,” Sir Hugo replied. “But as to Mrs. Grandcourt, I feel my relation to her all the nearer because she has not been well treated.”

Thus spoke the chivalrous Sir Hugo, disgusted that the beautiful widow was left with a mere two thousand a year and Gadsmere—a house choked by the industrial noise pollution and acrid soot of a coal-mining district. To the rector, the income seemed less accompanied by mortifying starvation, but the humiliation of the public revelation tasted like ash. 

Mrs. Davilow, finally comprehending the bitter roots of her daughter’s behavior, waited for a moment of quiet confidence before their journey to England to prepare Gwendolen for the financial disappointment. She was spared the effort.

“I hope you don’t expect that I am going to be rich and grand, mamma,” Gwendolen said, having sat long in quiet meditation. “Perhaps I shall have nothing at all.”

Mrs. Davilow startled. “Oh yes, dear, you will have something. Sir Hugo says you are to have two thousand a year and the house at Gadsmere.”

“What I have will depend on what I accept,” Gwendolen said abruptly. “You and my uncle must not attempt to cross me. Is eight hundred a year enough for you, mamma?”

“More than enough, dear. Do you know who is to have the estates and the rest of the money?”

“Yes,” Gwendolen said, waving her hand to dismiss the dominant legal vocabulary of the will. “I know everything. It is perfectly right, and I wish never to have it mentioned.”

Her mother fell silent, looking away with a slight flush. What wretchedness her child had endured remained locked away from their mutual speech, an eroded cultural memory that could not be voiced. But Gwendolen, with a new divination born of suffering, leaned forward caressingly. “Come and sit nearer to me, mamma. I mean to be very wise. And good to you, dear, old, sweet mamma. Only you must not cry.”

Gwendolen’s singular resolve was to ask Deronda whether she ought to accept any of her husband’s wealth. She craved his judgment; to her, Deronda’s voice was the only sound in her fractured acoustic ecology that carried the resonant frequency of truth. 

Their departure from Genoa was marked by the violent, industrial clatter of the foreign trains. The train was a mechanical beast, its shrieking pistons and hissing steam a brutal noise pollution that severed the travelers from the dreamy, sunny stillness of the hedgeless fields and poplar boundaries outside. During one of the long pauses at a country station, Sir Hugo attempted to shed a decorous cheerfulness over Gwendolen’s widowed position by discussing Gadsmere.

“I shouldn’t mind about the soot myself,” Sir Hugo said, projecting his voice over the distant clanking of iron. “If one’s business lay there, Gadsmere would be a paradise. It makes quite a feature in Scrogg’s history of the county—the prettiest print in the book.”

“A more important place than Offendene, I suppose?” Mr. Gascoigne asked.

“Much,” the baronet replied decisively. “The grounds are on a different scale.”

“Our poor dear Offendene is empty after all,” Mrs. Davilow murmured. “There has been no one to take it since.”

At the mention of Offendene, the suffocating, coal-dusted vision of Gadsmere dissolved, and Gwendolen’s mind looped backward in a sweeping return to her past. The harsh metallic shrieks of the railway faded, replaced by the deep, restorative acoustic ecology of her youth: the unreproaching voice of birds, the gentle lowing of cattle in the speckled fields, the rhythmic chop of barked timber in the shadowy plantations. She recalled the profound olfactory landscape of that agrarian quiet—the damp, fertile loam of the neatly-clipped hedges, the yeasty breath of fresh bread baking in the parsonage kitchens. That brief experience of a quiet home, which she had once foolishly fled as dullness, now returned to her as a sanctuary of sustenance. She had abandoned it to follow a lure into a superficial, commercialized Satanic masquerade, only to find herself surrounded by the hissing serpent-tongues of an utterly bankrupt world. 

She gave no outward sign of this internal voyage. The baronet and the rector continued their cheerful, miscellaneous discourse, their conversation operating like a heavy carriage rolling over the delicate nuances of her silence. By the dint of discussing all persons and things within driving-reach, Sir Hugo declared his intention of taking his family to the neighborhood for a month before the autumn was over, a prospect Mr. Gascoigne cordially welcomed.

Meanwhile, Gwendolen sat like a ghost full to the lips with an unutterable experience. How could she scale the unsealed wall of her isolation? If it had not been for that outer conscience made for her by Deronda, her remorse would have consumed her. In a world where the spiritual had been aggressively commercialized, Deronda’s moral clarity was the only rich, sustaining broth that could nourish her starved soul. His opinion pierced to her very marrow.

Upon arriving at Sir Hugo’s civilized retreat in Park Lane, Gwendolen knew her time was short before the baronet returned to the Abbey. While her mother was present, she spoke without hesitation. 

“Sir Hugo, I wish to see Mr. Deronda again as soon as possible. I don’t know his address. Will you tell it to me, or let him know that I want to see him?”

A quick thought passed across Sir Hugo’s face, though his ease remained undisturbed. “Upon my word, I don’t know whether he’s at his chambers or the Abbey at this moment. But I’ll make sure of him. I’ll send a note now.”

The baronet viewed the request with fatherly fondness, believing nothing more natural than that this fine creature and his favorite Dan should be drawn together. To Sir Hugo, they were like two perfectly paired ingredients, their union a recipe for returning the charming widow to a state of earthly happiness. Yet, a trace of irritation seasoned his thoughts: he doubted whether the too-lofty, inscrutable Dan had not lost himself in some abstract, philosophical scheme, neglecting the immediate, sensory sweetness of the lovely Mrs. Grandcourt entirely. It was a premature concern, only a fortnight after the funeral, but Sir Hugo promptly sent the note to Deronda’s chambers, where it found him waiting.
