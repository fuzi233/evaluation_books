CHAPTER XXI. It is often said that knowledge is power—but who has truly reckoned the force of ignorance? Knowledge builds slowly, brick by patient brick, over centuries of careful thought and record; while ignorance, hungry and impatient, sets fire to the very records it destroys, turning wisdom into ash with a single reckless spark. Knowledge teaches us to see, to desire more, to shape our lives with skill and artistry; but ignorance, drunk on its own emptiness, comes roaring in on the seventh day with oil and flame, shouting “Let there not be,” and reduces all that was colorful and living to smoldering ruin. Yes, knowledge is power—but it is a power held in check by conscience, aware of what must be and what may not. Ignorance, however, is blind and boundless—a giant without sight, who, once freed, would delight in pulling down the pillars of human progress, leaving behind only darkness as deep as buried Babylon.

And when we look closely at a life—especially one shaped by sudden upheaval—can we not see how ignorance of true cause and effect, how false notions of control and influence, can lead a soul astray? Like a vision blurred by distance, mistaking far-off horizons for things within reach, such blindness drives the heart toward disaster.

It was just past ten in the morning when Gwendolen Harleth arrived at the station near Offendene, after a long and gloomy journey from Leubronn. No carriage waited. No friend met her. Her telegram from Dover had mentioned a later train, and in her eagerness to escape the London station, she had left without considering what it meant to arrive unannounced at a remote stop—situated not near anything, but equally distant from everything.

Left alone with her large trunks, she stood in the waiting room, feeling the weight of every detail: the peeling paint, the dusty decanter of flat water, the bold script on the wall urging repentance. These were not merely surroundings—they were part of the bleak future unfolding before her. She hurried outside, hoping for some relief in the open air, but even the sunlight seemed dimmed. Autumn leaves trembled in the wind, grass bent under a chill breeze, and a lone cock fluffed his feathers, joined by two hens whose croaking voices echoed the loneliness of lost purpose. They, too, had seen their young grow up and leave—and now they wandered aimlessly, uncertain of their place.

The railway official, new and awkward, watched her with an expression of mild confusion, his gaze lingering on her fine shawl and expensive luggage. He did not know her name or rank, and his ignorance made him seem coarse, almost mocking. And then came the carriage—a grimy old barouche being readied by an elderly laborer. It was a small thing, perhaps, but each moment added to the sense of humiliation. These were not grand details, yet they mattered deeply. The smallest impressions accumulate until they form a mood, and that mood becomes a belief, a decision.

Philosophers may claim to rise above such trifles, but even they are shaped by circumstance. To be dropped alone at a desolate spot, with no income, no prospects—how could one help but question the fairness of the world? For Gwendolen, a woman raised in elegance, trained in taste and expectation, this arrival was worse than a dream—it was waking into reality, and it felt like defeat.

She had imagined returning home to find the family already planning another foreign escape—some modest retreat abroad where they might live quietly, if poorly. She had pictured herself among other English exiles, enduring dullness with dignity. But even that prospect paled beside the truth: they had nothing. Not a penny. And she, who had once believed in her own charm and strength, now found herself questioning the point of being clever, beautiful, or resolute at all. What good was any of it, if the world refused to value it?

Men, especially, seemed hateful. But lately, something had shifted. It was one thing to despise stolen goods; quite another to hate them because their theft blocked your own path. Gwendolen’s anger had turned—not toward Grandcourt himself, but toward him as the obstacle that had prevented her marriage, the reason for her downfall. If he had not interfered, she might have won back her losses at the gaming table. If he had not entered her life, she might still be free.

But now the carriage rolled forward, nearing the house. A figure appeared beneath the portico—and suddenly, Gwendolen’s thoughts changed. It was her mother. She saw the familiar face, now marked by sorrow, and without hesitation, threw her arms around her. In that moment, her own pain melted into care for her mother’s grief. All else faded.

Behind them, the four girls stood in quiet sadness—each one, like countless sisters across the land, carrying her own private world, unseen by others. Yet they all looked to Gwendolen as if her presence brought hope. Even her brief dismissal of them—"Now go away, girls"—carried comfort. Authority, even fleeting, was a balm.

Miss Merry, usually so composed, moved silently, tending to the trunks and the driver’s pay. Mrs. Davilow and Gwendolen climbed the stairs, shutting themselves into the black-and-yellow bedroom. There, Gwendolen pressed her handkerchief gently against her mother’s tears.

“Never mind, mamma dear,” she whispered. “I don’t mind. I will do something. I will be something. Things will come right. I was away—I didn’t see it clearly. But now I’m here, and I’ll fix it.”

She meant every word. A wave of tenderness surged through her, stirring her resolve. Ideas that had floated vaguely during her journey now took shape. She knew, suddenly, how she could be “something.” It was one of her finest moments. Her mother, overwhelmed, looked at her with something close to awe.

“Bless you, my good, good darling,” she murmured. “If you’re happy, I can be too.”

But later, the tide receded. The old fears returned. Misfortune was no longer news—it was a guest, settled in, demanding obedience. At first, the house had felt like home. The silence, the comfort, the familiar luxuries—all remained. Gwendolen had asked to be alone with her mother, and for a time, they rested together, untouched by the storm ahead.

When she descended, fresh and poised, she sat beside her mother on the settee, ready to face the truth. “What have you decided, exactly, mamma?” she asked, voice steady.

“We must leave this house,” Mrs. Davilow replied. “Mr. Haynes is glad to take it. Lord Brackenshaw’s agent—Bazley—is handling it. We’ve nothing to carry except our clothes.”

“And where are we going?” Gwendolen asked, her tone sharpening slightly. Fear stirred beneath the surface.

“To Sawyer’s Cottage,” her mother said softly.

Gwendolen went pale. She said nothing at first, then spoke with cold pride: “That is impossible. Something better should have been arranged. Uncle should not allow this. I will not submit.”

“Oh, my sweet child,” Mrs. Davilow pleaded. “Your uncle is kind, but he suffers too. He has his own family to support. And remember—we have nothing. Only what he and my sister give us. We must try to earn something. I and the girls are making embroidery for charity—tablecloths, communion cloths. It’s all we can do.”

“But surely somewhere else could be found,” Gwendolen insisted. “Not that cottage—where an exciseman lived!”

“No, dear,” her mother said gently. “Houses are scarce. This one is private. Two parlors, four bedrooms. You can have your own space.”

Gwendolen ignored the reassurance. “I cannot believe all your money vanished so quickly. How can you be certain? It wasn’t even a week since your last letter.”

“The first news came earlier,” Mrs. Davilow explained. “But I didn’t want to spoil your joy until it was unavoidable.”

“Oh, how vexatious!” Gwendolen exclaimed, flushed with anger. “If I’d known, I could have brought home the money I won. I had nearly two hundred pounds. It would have helped us survive—until I could make plans. Instead, I stayed, and lost it all.”

She paused, then added fiercely, “Everything has gone against me. People have come near me only to ruin me.”

Among those people, she included Deronda. Had he not stepped into her life, she might have returned to the tables with a few napoleons and regained her fortune.

“We must accept Providence, my child,” Mrs. Davilow said, startled by the mention of gambling but too afraid to press further.

“But I won’t accept it,” Gwendolen replied. “I’ll fight it. Why call men’s cruelty ‘Providence’? You said in your letter it was Mr. Lassman’s fault. Has he run off with it?”

“No, dear,” her mother said. “There were risky investments—mines and such. He meant to gain, but he risked too much.”

“That’s not Providence,” Gwendolen snapped. “That’s his greed with our money. He should be punished. Can’t we sue? Uncle should act. We must go to law.”

“My dear,” Mrs. Davilow said sadly, “law can’t recover lost money. Your uncle says it’s milk spilled on the ground. And law costs money—money we don’t have. Others have suffered too. We’re not alone.”

“But I won’t accept living in that cottage,” Gwendolen declared. “I won’t let you work for sixpences because of this. I won’t do it. I’ll find something better—something worthy of our rank and education.”

Mrs. Davilow sighed, relieved. “Your uncle and aunt think the same. They’ve heard of two positions—both paying a hundred a year. One is in a bishop’s household with three daughters. The other is in a high-class school. Your French, music, dancing, manners—everything fits. Perhaps you’d consider one? Just for now—to avoid the… the commonness of what we must endure.”

“What?” Gwendolen laughed bitterly. “Be like Miss Graves at Madame Meunier’s? No.”

“I think Dr. Monpert’s would be better,” Mrs. Davilow said. “No hardship in a bishop’s family.”

“Excuse me, mamma,” Gwendolen replied. “Hardship exists everywhere for a governess. And I don’t see why it would be easier to be looked down upon by a bishop than anyone else. Besides—you know I hate teaching. Imagine me trapped with three awkward girls like Alice! I’d rather emigrate than become a governess.”

She didn’t explain what emigration meant. Mrs. Davilow simply stared, helpless, dreading the confrontation with her uncle and aunt.

Yet there was something in Gwendolen’s tone—a defiance that suggested she already had a plan. Despite her ignorance of practical matters, her mother still believed in her strength, her will.

“I have some ornaments,” Gwendolen said. “I could sell them. They’d bring in a sum. Just enough to start. Marshall in Wanchester might buy them—he once showed me bracelets he’d bought from a lady. Jocosa could ask. She’s leaving, of course. But she could do that first.”

“She would do anything,” Mrs. Davilow said softly. “She offered to give me her savings—three hundred pounds. I told her to start a school. It will be hard for her now.”

“Then recommend her for the bishop’s daughters,” Gwendolen said, a sudden smile lighting her face. “She’d do better than I would.”

“Don’t say that to your uncle,” Mrs. Davilow warned. “He’ll be hurt. But perhaps you have something else in mind—one he might approve, if you speak to him.”

“There’s someone else I want to consult first,” Gwendolen said. “Are the Arrowpoints still at Quetcham? Is Herr Klesmer there? I suppose you don’t know—poor, dear mamma. Can Jeffries ride with a note?”

“Oh, my dear,” Mrs. Davilow said, “Jeffries isn’t here. The horses are gone. But someone could go from Leek’s farm. The Arrowpoints are at Quetcham—I know. Miss Arrowpoint left her card. But I don’t know about Herr Klesmer. Do you want to send before tomorrow?”

“Yes,” Gwendolen said firmly. “As soon as possible. I’ll write now.”

She rose and wrote:

> Miss Harleth presents her compliments to Herr Klesmer, and ventures to request the very great favor that he will call upon her, if possible, to-morrow. Her reason for presuming so far on his kindness is of a very serious nature. Unfortunate family circumstances have obliged her to take a course in which she can only turn for advice to the great knowledge and judgment of Herr Klesmer.

“Send this to Quetcham at once, mamma,” she said, sealing the letter. “Tell the man to wait for an answer. No time must be lost.”

For now, the urgent need was to dispatch the letter. But once that was done, a new anxiety gripped her. What if Klesmer weren’t there? What then?

Her faith in fate had been wounded. A brilliant match had crumbled. Roulette had betrayed her. A stranger had disrupted her plans. The world seemed cruelly indifferent—especially to her.

Gwendolen Harleth, beautiful and strong, now faced the threat of humiliation. For the first time, she felt adrift in a world that cared little for her, despite her talents and position. If Klesmer were not at Quetcham—that would fit the pattern. But if he were… then hope might bloom again.

And if someone had asked her whether she was remarkable, she would have been surprised to hear “no.”
