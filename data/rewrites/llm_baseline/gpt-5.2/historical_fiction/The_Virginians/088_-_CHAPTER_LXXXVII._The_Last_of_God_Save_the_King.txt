What crooked ordinance of Fortune is it that forever contrives to lodge me in the thinner party? Propose in any assembly to hand this realm to the Pretender of Rome, or the Grand Turk, and clap a new creed upon our necks, and the motion would likely carry—while I, by instinct as much as conscience, should be found voting No. So it went with me in Virginia. The Patriots would have me for a Tory because I would not shout with them; the Tories presently christened me a dangerous Republican because I would not rage with them. The very hinges of the time were loosened. Before a year was out I found myself longing, with a homesick hunger, for the flat fields by the Waveney and the honest, familiar noises there—the rookery at dusk, the cart-wheels in sand, the church bell—anything but the jangling, watchful din that had begun to thicken round us like summer gnats.

Yet go I could not. My mother stood almost alone, a lady with a high spirit and a small frame, and though the world might crack, she would not quit her land at such a season; and I, having any manhood, could not desert her. Besides, at his Excellency’s table, amid plentiful claret and the soft chink of glasses, it was agreed upon by those who called themselves well-affected that a man must stand by his post. The Governor, warmed by wine and company, spoke as though the very flagstaff were a sacred thing: statue signum, signifer! We Cavaliers would rally; we would not give way; we would make the rebels learn what it was to flout the Crown. Those vows were pledged and repledged, as if loyalty could be measured by the depth of a bumper.

Of all our Virginian acquaintance, my mother was the steadiest. Gentlefolks had come in numbers to Williamsburg; and a party of them, in that way which makes amusement a species of public declaration, had determined to offer her Excellency, the Governor’s lady, a ball. But news arrived of the Boston Port Bill, and, straightway, the House of Burgesses took fire with indignation, and voted protest, and decreed a solemn day of fasting and humiliation throughout the country—prayer to Heaven to avert the calamity of civil war. The invitation to my Lady Dunmore had already been given and accepted; so the gentlemen resolved, with that odd American faculty of trying to eat their cake and keep it, that the ball should go forward on the appointed night, and that sackcloth and ashes should be put on afterwards.

“A ball!” cries Madam Esmond, when the project is brought to her. “I go to a ball given by a set of rebels who are going publicly to insult his Majesty a week afterwards? I will die sooner!” And she wrote, in her own precise hand, to the stewards of the entertainment, that, considering the dangerous posture of affairs, she could not dream of appearing at their festivities.

The next time she went abroad in her chair, she was met with cheers—white and black together, as if the town had turned out for a holiday. “Huzzah, Madam!” “Heaven bless your ladyship!” They took her refusal for a patriotic act. My mother, who could endure many mistakes but not that one, put her head out between the chair-curtains and cried, with all the strength in her small lungs, “God save the King!” The people, catching the words as they might a tune, bawled “God save the King” too. In those days everybody could shout that loyalty; it was a refrain that covered contradictions, like a well-known hymn sung by a congregation that does not agree upon the sermon.

On the night of the assembly my poor Harry, being a Burgess and one of the givers of the feast, put on his old uniform red coat of Wolfe’s—soon, God knows, to be exchanged for another colour—and went off with Madam Fanny to the ball. My Lady Warrington and I, strangers in the country and English in everybody’s eyes, were permitted by my mother to attend the gathering from which she, of course, absented herself. I danced a country-dance with the lady of Mount Vernon, and found her lively, pretty, and amiable, with a quickness in her step that answered the fiddle as if she had known its voice since childhood. But when my wife afterwards praised Mrs. Washington’s grace, my mother received the commendation with a grimness that might have turned a bowl of syllabub.

Could not Sir George Warrington, she asked, have danced with my Lady Dunmore or her daughters, or with anybody but Mrs. Washington? To hear her, the Colonel of Mount Vernon must needs think himself and his wife the grandest people in the room; and she—my mother—remembered him a road-surveyor at a guinea a day! There was no measuring, she said, the pride of these provincial upstarts; and my Lord Dunmore’s partiality for that gentleman had plainly turned his head. Of Mr. Washington’s pride I know nothing; but my mother never could be brought to love him nor anything that was his.

Her displeasure did not end with the dance. Three days later, when the appointed fast and humiliation came round, and Mr. Washington attended the service performed by our new clergyman, my mother chose that same day to invite Mr. Belman to dinner, with sundry colonial authorities. It was not merely hospitality: it was a proclamation. The clergyman excused himself. Madam Esmond tossed up her head and said he might do as he liked.

She made a parade of a dinner; and indeed, in the very ordering of her table there was something of battle. The house smelt of roasting meats and rich gravies when other houses had shut their kitchens as though food itself were indecent on a day of penitence. The fire crackled; the spits turned; the cook’s knife went tap-tap-tap on the board with a steady confidence, mincing herbs and onions for sauces as if politics were nothing beside a well-managed pan. There were breads laid in folded napkins, and jellies that trembled like captured light, and a punch-bowl set out with such ceremony that one might have thought it an altar. At night she lighted her windows when the rest of the city was in darkness and gloom, so that her candles shone out like defiance.

Then, with a mischievous bravery, she begged Mr. Hardy, one of his Excellency’s aides-de-camp, to sing “God save the King.” The people outside listened, and some of them, hearing music where they expected only prayers, supposed it might be part of a religious service which Madam was celebrating. The air of the hymn floated into the street with the warm breath of the house—spiced wine, melting fat, beeswax candles, and the faint sour of wet wood from the palings. But my mother, having set her foot upon the path, would not stop at the first step. She called for “Britons, strike home!”

The simple young gentleman, fresh from Europe, began it; and at the first insolent notes, a great roar rose in the street—an angry living thing made of many throats. A large stone, flung by some rebellious hand, came whizzing through the night and plumped into the punch-bowl before me, shattering it; the sweet liquor and fruit peels and shattered glass splashed across the cloth and floor, and the smell of spilled spirits rose sharp and raw, drowning for a moment the domestic comfort of meat and candle.

My mother went to the window, nothing daunted. I see her now: her rigid little figure against the black, her head held high, her frilled arms outstretched, the stars twinkling over her like indifferent witnesses; and she sang in chorus, “Britons, strike home! strike home!” The crowd below shouted, “Silence! For shame! Go back!” but she would not go back—not she.

“Fling more stones, if you dare!” she cried.

More might indeed have come; but some gentlemen, issuing out of the Raley Tavern, interposed. “You mustn’t insult a lady,” says a voice I think I know. “Huzza, Colonel!” “Hurrah, Captain!” “God bless your honour!” the people answered, and so the enemies were pacified for the moment.

My mother, protesting the whole disturbance was over, would have had Mr. Hardy sing again; but he gave a sickly grin and said he really did not like to sing to such accompaniments. The concert ended. Yet I am bound to say that some scoundrels returned later in the night, frightened my poor wife almost out of her wits, and broke every single window in the front of our tenement. “Britons, strike home!” had been a little too much. Madam should have contented herself with “God save the King.”

All this while the town’s outward chorus of loyalty went on. Militia were drilled in the open, their muskets clicking and stamping with a harsh, regular sound that infected the ear like a fever. Bullets were cast; the very domestic hearths were turned to furnaces for war, lead melting in ladles where yesterday a cook had stirred syrup. Powder was stored; supplies were hidden; cunning plans were devised to disappoint royal ordinances. Yet “God save the King” remained the cry everywhere. And if I said to the gentlemen-patriots, “You are scheming for separation; you will bring down on yourselves the wrath of the greatest power in the world,” they answered always, “We mean no separation; we yield to no men in loyalty; we glory in the name of Britons,” and so forth, and so forth. The powder-barrels were heaped in the cellar, the train laid; and Mr. Fawkes, for all that, persisted in his dutiful petitions and meant no harm—not he!

I confess that when I spoke of the might of our country I expected she would exert it, and not attempt to overpower three millions of fellow-Britons upon their own soil with a handful of battalions, a half-dozen generals from Bond Street, and a few thousand bravos hired in Germany. As if we wished to insult the thirteen provinces as well as subdue them, we must needs loose upon them Hessians, and set the murderers out of the Indian wigwams to do our work. Was our quarrel to be fought with tali auxilio and istis defensoribus? It is easy now, we being worsted, to pore over the map of the empire wrested from us, and show where a march, an advance, a day more of darkness or light, might have put the enemy into our hands. Long Island should have exterminated Washington’s army; he should never have come out of Valley Forge except a prisoner. The South, after Camden, seemed ours, but for the meddling from New York that paralysed the exertions of the only truly capable British general who appeared in the war, and pushed him into that miserable cul-de-sac at York Town, whence he could only issue defeated and captive. Oh, for a week more, an hour more, when so much hung on time and temper!

But it was ordained by Heaven—and for the good, as we can now have no doubt, of both empires—that the great Western Republic should separate from us. And the gallant soldiers who fought on her side, their indomitable Chief above all, had the glory of facing and overcoming not only veteran troops amply provided and inured to war, but cold, hunger, wretchedness, dissension, and treason within their own camp—where all must have gone to wreck but for the pure, unquenchable flame of patriotism that burned forever in that heroic breast.

With the young Mr. Washington of my earlier days I had not much sympathy; though my brother, being more frank and affectionate than I, was his fast friend when they were equals, and in later days when the General, as I do own and think, was all mankind’s superior. Washington before the enemy was no braver than hundreds who fought with him or against him (who has not heard the repeated sneers at “Fabius,” in which his factious captains indulged?), but Washington as Chief of a nation in arms—calm in conspiracy, serene between open foe and darker enemies behind; inspiring order into hungry men in rags; stung by ingratitude and betraying no anger; ready to forgive; invincible in defeat; magnanimous in conquest; never so sublime as on the day he laid down his victorious sword and sought his noble retirement—here indeed was a character to admire and revere: a life without stain, a fame without flaw. Quando invenies parem? In a more extensive work I have planned and partly written upon this war, I trust I have done justice to his greatness; and if I recorded opinions that could offend both parties, it was because respect extorted them.

How odd, too, that the contrariety in my own temper and in my brother’s placed us, as by some perverse music, on wrong sides of the coming storm. Harry should have been the Tory, and I the Whig. Theoretically my opinions were the more liberal. My brother, especially after his marriage, became what our Indian nabobs call a Bahadoor—ceremonious, stately, exacting respect as if it were rent due on a manor.

When my Lord Dunmore talked of liberating the negroes, to induce them to join the King’s standard, Hal would have hanged the Governor and the “Black Guards” (as he called them) whom his Excellency had crimped. “If you gentlemen are fighting for freedom,” I said, “sure the negroes may fight too.” On which Harry roared, shaking his fist, “Infernal villains! if I meet any of ’em they shall die by this hand!” And my mother agreed that a negro insurrection was the most abominable and parricidal notion that had ever sprung up in her unhappy country. She, at least, was consistent: she would have black and white obedient to the powers that be; whereas Hal only could admit that freedom was the right of the latter colour.

As proof, Madam Esmond and Harry pointed to an instance in our own family: Mr. Gumbo.

I had purchased his liberty and given it him as reward for his admirable love and fidelity to me when times were hard. Returning to Virginia, he was scarcely a welcome guest among my mother’s servants. He was free and they were not: he became, as it were, a little living standard of insurrection, planted in the very midst of our kitchen and quarters. He took on airs of protection and consequence, talked big of “at home” (meaning Europe), and bragged of his doings there. He drew the household about him as naturally as a stewpot draws hands and noses on a cold evening. They would stand in doorways and by the yard, listening to his tales, the night full of crickets and distant drums from militia practice, while closer at hand the domestic sounds went on—the thud of a pestle, the scrape of a trencher, the hiss when fat met flame. He had seen the world, and like the monkey that has seen the world, he made a show of it.

Now Sady—Hal’s boy—had gone to America of his own desire, yet was not free. Hence jealousies between him and Mr. Gum; and battles, in which they practised the noble arts of boxing and butting learned at Marybone Gardens and Hockley-in-the-Hole. Nor was Sady alone in his dislike. Almost all my mother’s servants hated Gumbo for the airs he gave himself.

And I grieve to say our faithful Molly—his wife—was as jealous as any. The blacks could not pardon her for having demeaned herself, as they chose to call it, by marrying one of their own kind; she met with no respect, could exercise no authority, and came to her mistress with ceaseless complaints of idleness, knavery, lies, stealing—each tale sharper than the last, as if she were forever shaking grit out of a sack. At last she brought a story of jealousy against a certain Dinah, or Diana—who, I heartily trust, was as innocent as her namesake, the moonlight visitant of Endymion.

On the article of morality Madam Esmond was a very Draconess; the accused, in her court, were already guilty. She made charges against Mr. Gumbo; he replied with asperity. Forgetting that he was now a free gentleman, my mother ordered Gumbo to be whipped. At that, Molly flew at her ladyship—her wrath against her husband’s supposed infidelity vanishing in a flash before the notion of such indignity put upon him. There was a rebellion in our house at Castlewood, and the very domestic air seemed to take sides: the clatter of pans in the kitchen sounded like arms, the voices in the passages like mutiny.

A quarrel followed between me and my mother, for I took my man’s part. Hal and Fanny, on the contrary, sided with her; and in so far the difference did good, that it brought about some little intimacy between Madam and her younger children. This particular storm was speedily soothed; but it was plain that the standard must be removed from our house. We determined that Mr. Gumbo and his lady should return to Europe.

My wife and I would willingly have gone with them, God wot; for our boy sickened and lost his strength, and caught fever in our swampy country. But at this time she was expecting to lie in of our son Henry, and she knew too that I had promised to stay in Virginia. It was agreed that we should send the two back. When I offered that Theo should go, she said her place was with her husband; her father and Hetty at home would care for our children; and she scarce let me see a tear while she made ready the departure of her little ones.

There is a kind of silence that gathers round such work as surely as flour settles on a bakeboard: the hush around the tables where small shirts are folded and stacked; the careful tying of strings; the laying by of tiny caps; the way a mother’s hands will linger on linen as though touch could store warmth for a voyage. There are the stealthy visits to the children’s chamber while they sleep—breath sweet with milk, hair damp at the nape—while you stand in the doorway listening to their small, unconscious music. And then the parting: the barge waiting; the oars creaking; the splash and drip of water; the calls from boatmen; the shore beneath your feet suddenly too firm and too far away, as the boat slides off with what is dearest to you and cannot be recalled.

Had the Prince of Wales been bound upon that voyage he could not have been better provided. Where, sirrah, is the Tompion watch your grandmother gave you? and how did you survive the boxes of cakes the good lady stowed away in your cabin—spiced little rounds, sugared and packed so closely that the very scent of them seemed meant to keep hunger at bay like a charm?

The ship which carried away my poor Theo’s children returned with the Reverend Mr. Hagan and my Lady Maria aboard—who meekly chose to resign her rank, and was known in the colony (which was not to remain a colony very long) only as Mrs. Hagan. In the time when I stood in favour with my Lord Dunmore, a living falling vacant in Westmoreland county, his Excellency gave it to our kinsman. Hagan arrived in Virginia time enough to christen our boy Henry, and to preach sermons on the gloomy posture of affairs, which Madam Esmond pronounced prodigious fine.

My Lady Maria won my mother’s heart by a scruple of precedence. “My father, your brother, was an earl, ’tis true,” said she; “but your ladyship is a marquis’s daughter, and I never can think of taking place of you.” So fond did my mother become of her niece that she even allowed Hagan to read plays—my own humble compositions among others—and was forced to own there was merit in the tragedy of Pocahontas, which our parson delivered with uncommon energy and fire.

Hal and his wife came but rarely to Castlewood and Richmond while the chaplain and his lady were with us. Fanny was curt and rude with Maria; would giggle and laugh strangely in her company; and repeatedly reminded her of her age—to our mother’s astonishment, who often asked if there were any cause of quarrel between her niece and her daughter-in-law. I kept my own counsel; yet I could not help being touched by the meekness with which the elder lady bore these persecutions. Fanny loved to torture her in her husband’s presence—who, poor fellow, was in happy ignorance of his wife’s early history—and the other endured her agony, wincing as little as might be.

Sometimes I remonstrated with Madam Harry, asking if she were a Red Indian, that she should torture her victims so. “Have not I had torture enough in my time?” she said, and looked as though she were resolved to repay injuries. “Nay,” I answered, “you were bred in our wigwam, and I remember nothing but kindness.” “Kindness!” she cried. “No slave was ever treated as I was. The blows which wound most often are those which are never aimed. The people who hate us are not those we have injured.”

I thought then of little Fanny in our early days—silent, smiling, eager to run and do our bidding—and I grieved for my poor brother, who had taken this sly creature into his bosom.
