"It is a part of probability that many improbable things will happen."
—ARISTOTLE: *Poetics*.

The evening following his interview with Mordecai brought a storm of internal conflict to Daniel Deronda's mind. For a youth of ordinary sensibility, the encounter might have been merely a strange diversion, a curious episode to occupy his thoughts. But for Deronda, whose nature was built to feel intensely and to question actively, the event had struck a chord so deep that his intellect immediately recoiled, beginning a rigorous examination of his own emotions. He feared the danger of being swept away by Mordecai's energetic certainty and fervent trust. His characteristic instinct was to recoil from the moral blindness of undervaluing what had touched him so closely, of ignoring the sacred crisis unfolding in his own life because it did not fit the comfortable patterns of his daily existence.

He asked himself why he should feel ashamed of his agitation simply because he dressed for dinner in a white tie and moved in circles where seriousness was often mocked as foolish self-importance. Such cowardice before the obtuseness of fashion repelled him. Yet, he also feared letting his course be dictated by mere emotional contagion, allowing a reverential pity for spiritual struggle to rush him down a dimly seen path without the consent of reason.

What, truly, had occurred? He knew the answer Sir Hugo would give: a dying man, consumed by fanaticism, had latched onto Deronda as the living embodiment of a visionary hope—a desperate longing for the propagation of his beliefs. Such instances were not rare; fanaticism was abundant. While Mordecai waited on the bridge for his visions to fulfill, others believed they held the mathematical key to the universe or the metaphysical solution to all problems, convinced that the world conspired to silence them. These were scattered everywhere, men with glittering eyes or dull stares, searching for a listener. Mordecai possessed a more pathetic, poetic intensity than the typical monomaniac, yet he belonged to the same class. A man of the world would know how to handle such a figure: offer a little comfort, but remain skeptical. Mordecai's conviction that he had found a new executive self might only lead to the worst possible disappointment.

Deronda heard these negative whispers echoing in his mind, repeating them distinctly to himself. This was not the first time he had faced the question of the "family likeness" among the heirs of enthusiasm, whether prophets or dreamers. The kinship of human passion inevitably filled fact with burlesque and parody. Error and folly had their martyrs. If one reduced the greatest figures of history to an abstract statement of their qualities, they appeared in dangerous company alongside the inventors of perpetual motion. One could not fairly judge spirits by such tests. To avoid condemning the innocent, one needed a capacity to understand the subject-matter of the conviction and a fellowship with human toil that prevented any deep experience from being scanned lightly.

This train of thought was familiar to Deronda and saved him from contemptuous prejudice against Mordecai, even though the Jew's claim on him was strangely insistent. Rationally, such a claim might seem illusory, yet it transformed Mordecai's appeal from a request for sympathy into a grasp on Deronda's struggling conscience. Consciences are not uniform; they vary as widely as our memories. Deronda's conscience included sensibilities enlarged by his early habit of imagining himself into the experiences of others.

What claim did this eager soul make? "You must believe my beliefs—be moved by my reasons—hope my hopes!" To accept this as a direct obligation would be dishonest. Deronda felt thankful that amidst his compassion, he had preserved himself from the bondage of false concessions. Furthermore, the claim rested on a supposition—that Deronda was of Jewish blood—which might be entirely false. Was there ever a more hypothetical appeal?

Yet, since the age of thirteen, Deronda had associated his deepest emotional struggles with the pure supposition that Sir Hugo was his father. That hypothesis had been a source of passionate struggle, a light by which he subdued and cherished feelings. He was accustomed to finding motives in conceptions that might be disproved, and to considering revelations that might alter his view of duty. To live in a state of suspense, charged with emotive activity and scruple, was a familiar attitude for his conscience.

Now, suppose that wish-begotten belief in his Jewish birth and Mordecai's extravagant demand for discipleship were the foreshadowing of an actual discovery and a genuine spiritual result. Suppose Mordecai's ideas made a real conquest over Deronda's conviction? It was conceivable that as Mordecai found replenishment in his belief, Deronda might receive from the Jew's mind the complete ideal shape of the personal duty and citizenship that lay in his own thoughts like sculptured fragments, beautiful but untraceable.

As this possibility arose in his meditation, Deronda defended it. If the influence he imagined submitting to came from an honored professor or a philosopher accepted as a voice of the age, would thorough receptiveness be ridiculed? Only by those who consider being obliged for an idea a sign of weakness. Was there not vulgarity in taking the fact that Mordecai was a poor Jewish workman, perhaps met on a sanded floor in the parlor of the *Hand and Banner*, as proof that no spiritual force within him could affect a white-handed gentleman? There was a legend of Emperor Domitian, who, alarmed by news of a Jewish family destined to rule the world, summoned them only to release them upon seeing their workmen's hands. The Emperor was wrong; the Rabbi waiting at Rome's gate, confident the Messiah would be among the destitute, was also wrong in trusting outward signs. Poverty and poor clothes are no sign of inspiration, yet they have accompanied it in remarkable cases. To dismiss discipleship because of these signs was mere dullness of imagination.

A more plausible reason to reject discipleship was the strain of visionary excitement in Mordecai, which turned wishes into overmastering impressions. Was such a temper likely to accompany the wise estimate of consequences necessary to avoid fatal error? Yet, it remained to be seen if this rare conjunction existed. Perhaps Mordecai's nature fused a wise estimate with the fires of passionate belief, determining the very consequences he believed in. The inspirations of the world have often come this way; even strict science requires that forecasting ardor that feels the agitations of discovery beforehand. In relation to human motives, passionate belief has a fuller efficacy. Here, enthusiasm may possess the validity of proof, giving the type of what will one day be general.

At least, Deronda argued, Mordecai's excitability was hardly a reason to conclude beforehand that he was not worth listening to except for pity. Suppose he had introduced himself as a strict reasoner; did they form a body free from false conclusions? The driest argument has its hallucinations, too hastily concluding its net will hold the universe. Men dream in demonstrations, cutting out illusory worlds of axioms and definitions, excluding fact with a final *Q.E.D.* No formulas save mortals from mistake in their imperfect apprehension. Perhaps an emotional intellect has absorbed into its passionate vision some truth of what will be, the comprehensive massive life feeding theory with new material. Presumptions to the contrary were not to be trusted. One must be patient with the inevitable makeshift of human thinking. Columbus had impressions we call superstitions and arguments we disapprove, yet he possessed sound physical conceptions and the passionate patience of genius to make them tell on mankind. The world's contempt for those deaf to Columbus was a mistake.

"My contempt for them binds me to see that I don't adopt their mistake on a small scale," Deronda said to himself. "I must not make myself deaf with the assumption that there cannot be any momentous relation between this Jew and me, simply because he has clad it in illusory notions. What I can be to him, or he to me, may not at all depend on his persuasion about the way we came together. To me, the way seems made up of plainly discernible links. If I had not found Mirah, I probably should not have become specially interested in the Jews, nor gone on that loitering search after an Ezra Cohen which made me pause at Ram's book-shop and ask the price of *Maimon*. Mordecai, on his side, had his visions of a disciple, and he saw me by their light; I corresponded well enough with the image his longing had created. He took me for one of his race. Suppose that his impression—the elderly Jew at Frankfort seemed to have something like it—suppose, in spite of all presumptions, that his impression should somehow be proved true, and that I should actually share any of the ideas he is devoted to? This is the only question which really concerns the effect of our meeting on my life."

"But if the issue should be quite different?" Deronda continued, wavering. "Well, there will be something painful to go through. I shall almost inevitably have to be an active cause of that poor fellow's crushing disappointment. Perhaps this issue is the one I had need prepare myself for. I fear that no tenderness of mine can make his suffering lighter. Would the alternative—that I should not disappoint him—be less painful to me?"

Here Deronda hesitated. Feelings had lately worked within him, modifying the reluctance he would formerly have shown to consider himself possibly a Jew. He was romantic. That young energy and spirit of adventure which had created legions of youthful heroes seeking hidden tokens of their birth gave him a quivering interest in the bare possibility that he was entering on a like track—all the more because the track was one of thought as well as action.

"The bare possibility." He could not admit it to be more. The belief that his father was an Englishman only grew firmer under the weak assaults of unwarranted doubt. For Deronda to declare that he would be glad if that belief were declared a delusion was impossible. His life-long affection for Sir Hugo, stronger than all his resentment, made him shrink from admitting that wish.

Whichever way the truth might lie, he repeated to himself what he had said to Mordecai: he could not undertake to hasten its discovery without further reasons. Nay, he was tempted now to regard his uncertainty as a condition to be cherished for the present. If further intercourse revealed nothing but illusions, the want of any valid evidence that he was a Jew might save Mordecai the worst shock of refused fraternity. It might even be justifiable to use this uncertainty in keeping up a suspense that would induce Mordecai to accept those offices of friendship that Deronda longed to urge upon him.

These were the meditations that busied Deronda during the interval of four days before he could fulfill his promise to call for Mordecai at Ezra Cohen's, Sir Hugo's demands on him often lasting until an hour so late that the evening expedition to Holborn became impossible.
