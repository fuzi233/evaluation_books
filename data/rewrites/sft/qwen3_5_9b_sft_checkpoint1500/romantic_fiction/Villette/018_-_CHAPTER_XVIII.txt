From the moment I settled into the Terrace, Dr. Graham maintained a silent distance. He never seated himself near me, nor did his habitual pacing bring him within range of my corner. A shadow, heavier than usual, clung to his brow, suggesting a preoccupation I immediately identified as concern for Miss Fanshawe. My senses were perpetually tuned, a guard dog waiting for the scent of her name to rise from his lips. I braced myself, my patience standing ready for battle, hoping to catch the first glimmer of the tender subject he carried within him.

After a period of internal wrestling, which I observed with a mixture of curiosity and pity, he finally broke his silence. He approached the topic with a delicacy that bordered on evasion, speaking of her as "your friend" rather than by name.

"I hear your friend is spending her vacation traveling?" he asked gently.

*Friend!* I thought, a flash of irony crossing my mind. *Is that what you call her?* Yet, I could not contradict him. He needed to lead; I had to follow the path he laid down. I allowed the soft impeachment to stick.

"But whom do you mean?" I ventured, testing the waters.

He had taken a seat at my work table and, with reckless abandon, began to unwind a reel of thread, letting it spill across the wood.

"Ginevra—Miss Fanshawe," he corrected, "has accompanied the Cholmondeleys on a tour through southern France?"

"She has," I replied.

"Do you and she correspond?"

"It will astonish you to learn that I never applied for that privilege."

"You have seen letters from her?"

"Yes, several addressed to her uncle."

"They lack neither wit nor *naïveté*, surely?" he mused, his eyes lighting up. "There is so much sparkle, so little art in her soul?"

"She writes comprehensively enough when addressing M. de Bassompierre," I countered, recalling the nature of those letters. They were rarely epistolary flourishes; they were business documents, stark applications for cash.

"And her handwriting?" he pressed. "Must it be pretty, light, and ladylike?"

"It certainly is," I admitted.

"I truly believe everything she does is done well," Dr. John declared. Seeing that I hesitated to echo this sentiment, he probed further. "Since you know her intimately, could you name a single point in which she is deficient?"

"She excels at many things," I said cautiously. (*Among them, flirtation,* I added silently).

"When do you suppose she will return to town?"

"Pardon me, Doctor," I interrupted, rising slightly. "I must clarify. You honor me with an intimacy I do not possess. I have never been the keeper of her plans or secrets. Her true circle lies elsewhere, among the Cholmondeleys."

His expression shifted. He genuinely believed I was stinging with a jealousy akin to his own.

"Forgive her," he urged, his voice softening. "Judge her with kindness. The glitter of fashion deceives her, but she will soon discover these people are hollow. She will return to you with a deeper attachment, a confirmed trust. I know the Cholmondeleys well: they are superficial, showy, and self-absorbed. Rest assured, at heart, Ginevra values you above a dozen of them."

"You are very kind," I said briefly, the words burning on my tongue like ash. I wanted to declare that I was not the humiliated, discarded confidante he imagined, but I swallowed the truth.

"And yet," Dr. Graham continued, his gaze drifting, "while I comfort *you*, I find no solace for myself. I cannot hope she will do me justice. De Hamal is most unworthy, yet I fear he pleases her. A wretched delusion!"

Suddenly, my patience, worn thin by illness and weakness, shattered. Without warning, I exploded.

"Dr. Bretton," I cried, "there is no delusion like yours! On every point save one, you are a man—frank, healthy, right-thinking, clear-eyed. On this singular point, you are a slave. Where Miss Fanshawe is involved, you deserve no respect, and I grant you none."

I turned and fled the room, leaving him stunned.

That afternoon, I realized I had caused damage. He was not made of common clay; his nature was carved with broad strokes of vigor, yet the details were finished with a feminine delicacy, finer and more intricate than anyone could expect. Only a sharp collision with his nerves revealed the acute sensitivity beneath this exterior. Though he lacked the instinctive sympathy to instantly mirror others' emotions, his heart was vast and open. To those who needed, his hand stretched out. To those who wept, he listened. To expect miracles of intuition from him, however, was to invite disappointment.

When he returned that evening, meeting the lamplight, I saw the entire machinery of his distress. The epithet "slave" and the banishment from respect must have cut deep. He offered no defense against the accusation; instead, his mind quietly circled around the terrible possibility that it was true. Burdened by a self-condemnatory silence, his demeanor grew grave, bordering on cold, towards both me and his mother. Yet, there was no malice, no rancor in his features, only a profound, masculine sadness.

When I hurried to place his chair and serve his tea with trembling care, he simply said, "Thank you, Lucy," in a voice that remained, miraculously, kind and pleasant.

For my part, I knew exactly what I must do. I must atone for my rash vehemence, or I would not sleep. This war footing was intolerable. Solitude, silence, stagnation—all preferred to living entangled with Dr. John. As for Ginevra, let her spread her silver wings and ascend to the highest stars, where her lover's fantasy fixed her constellation. Never again would I dispute the arrangement.

I watched him closely. Again and again, his eye met mine, seeking connection, but finding nothing to say, it withdrew, leaving me baffled. After tea, he sat in silence, reading a book. I longed to approach him, to bridge the gap, but feared that any move would only provoke hostility. Regret gnawed at me until I could barely stand it. Finally, I murmured, "Dr. Bretton."

He looked up. His eyes held no frost, his mouth no cynicism. He was ready to listen, his spirit too mellow to turn bitter over a single storm cloud.

"Dr. Bretton, forgive my hasty words," I pleaded. "_Please_, do forgive them."

He smiled instantly. "Perhaps I deserved them, Lucy. If you do not respect me, it is surely because I am not respectable. I fear I am an awkward fool; I seem to manage poorly wherever I wish to please."

"That you cannot be sure of," I said, sitting back. "And even if it is true, is it a fault of your character or of others' perceptions? Let me unsay what I said in anger. In one thing, and in all things, I deeply respect you. If you think too little of yourself and too much of others, is that not an excellence?"

"Can I think too much of Ginevra?" he whispered, the question hanging heavy in the air.

"_I_ believe you may," I answered firmly. "_You_ believe you cannot. Let us agree to differ. Let me be pardoned; that is what I ask."

"Do you think I cherish ill-will for one warm word?"

"I see you do not and cannot," I assured him. "But just say, 'Lucy, I forgive you!' Say it, to ease me of this heart-ache."

"Put away your heart-ache, as I will put away mine," he replied softly. "For you wounded me, Lucy. Now, when the pain is gone, I not only forgive; I feel grateful, as to a sincere well-wisher."

"I _am_ your sincere well-wisher," I confessed. "You are right."

Thus, our quarrel ended.

If, throughout this narrative, you find my opinion of Dr. John shifting, please excuse the apparent inconsistency. I record the feelings as I experienced them, the view of character as it appeared at that specific moment.

Yet, he demonstrated the fineness of his nature by treating me with greater kindness following this misunderstanding than before. Paradoxically, the incident that, by theory, should have estranged us altered our relationship, though not in the painful direction I anticipated. An invisible, chilling barrier—a screen of ice—had previously glazed the medium between us, making our exchanges formal and distant. Those few warm words, spoken in the heat of anger, breathed upon that fragile frostwork, causing it to dissolve. From that day forward, so long as we remained friends, he never stood on ceremonial ground with me. He seemed to know that if he spoke freely of himself and his deepest interests, my expectations would always be met, my wishes satisfied. Consequently, I continued to hear much of "Ginevra."
