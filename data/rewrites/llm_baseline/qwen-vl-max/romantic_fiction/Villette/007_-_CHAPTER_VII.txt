The next morning, I awoke with a renewed sense of strength and clarity. The weariness that had clouded my thoughts the night before had lifted; my mind felt sharp, alert, and unburdened. I dressed quickly, the routine bringing a quiet rhythm to my nerves. Just as I finished, a knock came at the door. “Come in,” I called, expecting the chambermaid—only for a rough-looking man to step inside without ceremony.

“Give me your keys, Miss,” he said bluntly.

I blinked. “Why?”

“Gif!” he snapped, snatching them from my fingers before I could protest. “All right! You’ll have your trunk soon.”

Relief washed over me when I realized he was from customs. The moment passed, but the memory of his abruptness lingered. I didn’t know where to find breakfast, but I decided to go downstairs anyway—though slowly, deliberately. As I descended the wide staircase, I noticed something I’d missed the night before: this wasn’t just an inn. It was a grand hotel. High ceilings soared above me, painted walls glowed under the daylight streaming through large windows, and marble steps, though bare and slightly grimy, reflected the light like polished stone.

And yet—my room had been tiny, sparsely furnished, almost invisible compared to the opulence surrounding it. A strange thought struck me: how did servants everywhere seem to know exactly who I was? Not by appearance, not by speech, but by some instinctive calculation—how little I had, how insignificant my presence. They sized me up instantly, silently placing me in a category too small to matter. I saw it plainly now: they all knew, without asking, that I was no one of consequence.

It was odd—and unsettling—but I refused to let it crush me. I kept my spirits up, even as I walked into the great hall below, bright with skylight and bustling with strangers. I found the coffee room eventually, though I trembled as I entered. The silence of solitude pressed on me. I wished I knew if I was making the right choice—yet I couldn’t turn back. There was no turning back. So I sat down at a small table, and when a waiter brought breakfast, I ate, though my appetite was poor.

The room was full of men—no women among them. Still, no one stared. A few glanced my way, but only briefly. Perhaps they assumed I was eccentric—just another Englishwoman lost in foreign lands. I accepted it quietly, like a truth beyond dispute.

After breakfast, I faced the next question: where now? An inner voice whispered, *Go to Villette.* It came from a half-remembered phrase Miss Fanshawe had tossed off casually as she bade me farewell: “I wish you’d come to Madame Beck’s. She has marmots—children, I suppose—you might look after. She needs an English governess—or did two months ago.”

Who Madame Beck was, or where she lived, I didn’t know. I’d asked, but the question slipped away in the rush of parting. I assumed Villette was her home. Forty miles away. I knew it was a gamble—a desperate one—but what else was there? In the vast emptiness I’d left behind, any hope was worth grasping, even a shadow.

I arranged passage on the diligence, secured a seat, and set out with nothing but that faint thread of possibility. Before you judge me reckless, reader, consider where I began. I had nothing to lose. Only the chance to gain something.

Though we traveled slowly, the day held its own charm. The landscape was flat and sparse—fields tilled like kitchen gardens, pollard willows lining the roads, canals winding like sluggish green serpents. The sky was gray, the air damp and still. Yet amid all that dullness, my spirit stirred. My imagination bloomed, and my heart warmed.

But beneath it all, a silent anxiety waited—like a beast coiled in the dark. I heard its breath. Felt its pulse against mine. It never moved, but I always knew it was there, waiting for nightfall to spring.

I hoped we’d reach Villette before dark. I wanted to arrive in daylight, not shrouded in shadows. But our progress was slow. Fog thickened. Rain fell in steady sheets. By the time we reached the city outskirts, darkness had settled—thick and tangible.

We passed through a gate guarded by soldiers, then left the muddy road behind, rattling over a rough, flinty pavement. At a bureau, the diligence stopped. Passengers disembarked. My first task: retrieve my trunk.

I stood back, watching carefully. I’d tied a green ribbon to the direction card so I could spot it easily. One by one, packages were removed. Boxes, bags, parcels—everything came down. Then, nothing. No green ribbon. No portmanteau.

I searched the vehicle. The oilcloth cover was lifted. Every last item was gone—umbrellas, hats, cloaks, bandboxes. But mine was nowhere.

I couldn’t speak. I had no words in French. The world around me chattered in a language I couldn’t understand. What could I do?

I approached the conductor, touched his arm, pointed to the trunk, then to the roof. I tried to ask with my eyes. He misunderstood—grabbing the wrong trunk and lifting it onto the carriage.

“Leave that alone!” a voice said in clear English. Then, sharply in French: “What are you doing? That trunk is mine.”

I turned. The sound of my own country’s tongue brought warmth to my chest. “Sir,” I said, not noticing his face, only the relief in my voice, “I cannot speak French. Could you please ask him what happened to my trunk?”

He hesitated—half surprised, half uncertain whether to interfere. But he stepped forward. “Do ask him,” I urged. “I would do the same for you.”

I don’t know if he smiled, but he spoke gently—calm, not harsh. “What kind of trunk was yours?” I described it—the size, the shape, the green ribbon. Then he turned to the conductor, and a storm of French erupted. I understood none of it, but I felt the force of his questioning.

When he returned, he said, “He claims he was overloaded. He admits he took your trunk off after you saw it loaded. He left it behind at Boue-Marine with other parcels. He promises to send it tomorrow. You’ll get it here by the day after.”

“Thank you,” I whispered. My heart sank. But what now?

He looked at me, perhaps seeing the fear in my eyes. “Do you have friends here?” he asked.

“No,” I replied. “I don’t know where to go.”

A pause. Then, as he turned more fully into the lamplight, I saw him clearly. Young. Handsome. Distinguished. A gentleman—perhaps a lord. His face was noble, open, kind. Not arrogant, not cold. He looked strong, yet gentle.

I turned to leave—knowing I had no right to expect help from someone so far above me.

“Was all your money in the trunk?” he asked.

I breathed easier. “No. I have enough in my purse—about twenty francs—to stay at a quiet inn until the trunk arrives. But I’m completely lost. I don’t know the streets, or the places.”

“I can give you the address of such an inn,” he said. “It’s nearby. With my directions, you’ll find it easily.”

He tore a page from his notebook, wrote a few words, and handed it to me. I felt a deep gratitude. I wouldn’t have doubted him—not for a second. There was honesty in his eyes, integrity in his tone.

“You should follow the Boulevard and cross the park,” he continued. “But it’s too late and too dark for a woman to walk through the park alone. I’ll walk with you this far.”

He started forward, and I followed—through the rain, through the darkness. The Boulevard was empty, the path slick with water. Trees dripped. The park was black, impenetrable. I couldn’t see him, only hear his footsteps. Yet I felt no fear. I would have followed that calm tread anywhere—into eternal night, if need be.

When we reached the other side, he said, “Now, walk straight down this street until you see steps. Two lamps will mark them. Descend them. Below lies a narrower street. Follow it to the end—you’ll find your inn. They speak English there. Your troubles are nearly over. Good night.”

“Good night, sir,” I said. “Thank you—truly.”

We parted. And as I walked on, I carried with me the image of his face—the kindness in it, the strength. It stayed with me long after.

The city loomed ahead—grand houses, towering buildings, palaces or churches, I couldn’t tell. I passed a portico when suddenly two men emerged from behind the columns. Mustached, cigar-smoking, dressed like gentlemen but with coarse manners. They spoke insolently, and despite my fast pace, they matched it—following me for a long stretch.

At last, I met a patrol. They turned the men away. But the chase had thrown me off course. When I regained my composure, I realized I’d lost my bearings. The steps I was supposed to find—I’d already passed them.

Panic fluttered in my chest. I couldn’t go back. Not past those men. But I had to retrace my steps. I wandered, trembling, until I found a worn flight of stairs. I assumed it was the right one. I descended.

Below lay a narrow street. No inn. I kept walking. Then, in a quiet, clean lane, I saw a light glowing over a doorway. A large house, taller than the others. This must be it.

I hurried forward—my knees weak, my breath ragged. But it wasn’t an inn. A brass plate read: *Pensionnat de Demoiselles—Madame Beck.*

My heart leapt. A hundred thoughts surged through me. But I didn’t think. I didn’t plan. Providence seemed to say: *Stop here. This is your place.*

Fate took hold. I rang the bell.

While I waited, I forced myself not to think. I focused on the wet stones beneath the lamp, counting them, tracing their shapes. I rang again.

The door opened. A maid in a neat cap stood before me. “May I see Madame Beck?” I asked.

She hesitated—then nodded. I suppose she assumed I was a teacher, coming for work. She let me in.

I found myself in a cold, glittering salon—porcelain stove unlit, gilded ornaments, polished floor. A clock struck nine. Silence. I sat, staring at the white folding doors, waiting.

Then—“You ayre Engliss?” a voice said beside me.

I jumped. I hadn’t expected anyone. But there was no ghost—only a plump, motherly woman in a shawl and nightcap.

“Yes,” I said. “I am English.”

And immediately, we began talking—though neither of us truly understood the other. She spoke rapidly in French. I answered in English. We made a terrible noise—she with her loud, rolling vowels, I with my careful words. We achieved little.

She rang for help. A woman arrived—small, brisk, with a bold manner. She’d studied in an Irish convent and claimed to speak English well. Though she butchered it, she listened.

I told my story: how I’d left my country seeking work, how I’d do anything honest—nursing, cleaning, serving—anything that wasn’t shameful. I would be useful, I promised.

Madame Beck listened. Her face remained unreadable—no pity, no curiosity. She studied me, weighing every word. Then a bell rang.

“Voilà pour la prière du soir!” she said, rising.

Through the interpreter, she told me to return the next day. But I couldn’t. I couldn’t face the dark streets again.

So I spoke directly to her—calmly, firmly. “Madame, I assure you, your interests will be better served by hiring me tonight. I will work fairly, fully. And if you employ me, it would be best for me to stay here. I have no one in Villette. I don’t speak the language. How can I find lodging?”

She paused. “But you have no reference?”

“None.”

She asked about my luggage. I told her it would arrive tomorrow.

Then, a man’s footsteps echoed in the vestibule. He approached the front door.

Madame Beck turned. “Who goes out now?” she asked.

“M. Paul,” the teacher replied. “He came to read to the first class.”

“The very man I want to see now. Call him.”

The teacher ran to the door. M. Paul entered—a small, dark man with spectacles.

“Mon cousin,” Madame Beck began, “I need your opinion. You know physiognomy. Read that face.”

He fixed his glasses on me. His expression was stern—determined to see through me.

“I read it,” he said.

“And what do you say?”

“Many things.”

“Is she good or bad?”

“Of both kinds,” he replied. “But may one trust her word?”

“If you’re negotiating something important?”

“She wishes to work here. Tells a tale of honesty. Gives no references.”

“She’s a stranger?”

“An Englishwoman, as one can see.”

“She speaks French?”

“No.”

“She understands it?”

“No.”

“One may speak plainly in her presence?”

“Certainly.”

He studied me longer. Then, finally: “Engage her. If goodness prevails, the action will bring its reward. If evil—well, madam, it will still be a good deed.”

With a bow, he left.

And so—by God’s grace—I was hired that very night. I did not have to step back into the lonely, hostile dark. I had a place. A purpose. A shelter.
