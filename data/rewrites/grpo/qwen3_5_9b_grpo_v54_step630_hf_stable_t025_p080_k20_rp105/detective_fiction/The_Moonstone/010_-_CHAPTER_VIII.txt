Here, for a brief respite, I must pause the narrative.

By summoning my own memory and consulting Penelope's journal, I find we can swiftly bridge the gap between Mr. Franklin Blake's arrival and Miss Rachel's birthday. For the greater part of that interval, the days drifted by, bringing nothing of consequence worth recording. With your permission, and aided by Penelope, I shall highlight only specific dates here, reserving the day-by-day recounting for the period when the Moonstone became the singular obsession of every soul in our household.

Thus, we resume our tale—beginning, naturally, with the bottle of sweet-smelling ink discovered on the gravel walk that night.

The following morning, the twenty-sixth, I presented this piece of trickery to Mr. Franklin and recounted what I had previously shared. His verdict was twofold: first, that the Indians had indeed been prowling for the Diamond; second, that they were foolish enough to believe in their own magic. By this, he meant the ritual of marking a boy's forehead and pouring ink into his palm, followed by the expectation that he would perceive entities beyond human vision. "In our country, as well as in the East," Mr. Franklin informed me, "there are individuals who practice this curious hocus-pocus—sans the ink, though—and who refer to it by a French term implying 'brightness of sight.' Depend upon it," he declared, "the Indians assumed we would keep the Diamond here. They brought their clairvoyant boy to guide them to it, should they succeed in breaching the house last night."

"Do you think they'll attempt again, sir?" I inquired.

"It depends," Mr. Franklin replied, "on the boy's actual capabilities. If he can locate the Diamond through the iron safe of the bank at Frizinghall, we shall be spared further visits from the Indians for the present. If he cannot, we shall have another opportunity to catch them in the shrubbery before many more nights pass overhead."

I waited with considerable confidence for that latter scenario; yet, strangely, it never materialized.

Whether the jugglers heard rumors of Mr. Franklin being spotted at the bank and drew conclusions accordingly; or whether the boy genuinely perceived the Diamond where it now rested—which I, for one, utterly disbelieve; or whether it was merely a matter of chance—the plain truth remains: not a single Indian approached the house again during the weeks preceding Miss Rachel's birthday. The jugglers remained in and around the town peddling their trade, while Mr. Franklin and I waited, resolved not to alert the rogues by revealing our suspicions prematurely. With this report of proceedings on both sides, my account of the Indians concludes for the present.

On the twenty-ninth, Miss Rachel and Mr. Franklin devised a new method to occupy the time that might otherwise have felt heavy. There are reasons to pay particular attention to this occupation, for it bears upon events yet to unfold.

Gentlefolk generally face a formidable obstacle in life: the rock of their own idleness. Their days are largely spent searching for something to do, and it is curious to observe—especially when their tastes lean toward the intellectual—how often they drift blindly into distasteful pursuits. Nine times out of ten, they resort to torturing something or spoiling something, firmly believing they are cultivating their minds, when the reality is they are merely creating a mess within the house. I have witnessed ladies and gentlemen alike venture out daily with empty pill-boxes, capturing newts, beetles, spiders, and frogs, only to return home and pierce the miserable creatures with pins or dissect them without a pang of remorse into tiny fragments. One sees my young master or mistress poring over a spider's innards with a magnifying glass; or encounters a frog descending the stairs without its head. When one wonders what this cruelty signifies, the answer is invariably a supposed passion for natural history. Sometimes, they spend hours destroying a beautiful flower with pointed instruments, driven by a dull curiosity to understand its composition. Does its color become more vibrant, or its scent sweeter, once you know its parts? But there! The poor souls must pass the time; they must fill their empty heads and idle hands. Thus, they end up ruining canvases with paint, filling the house with the odor of tadpoles in dirty glass boxes, chipping stones and contaminating the food, or staining their fingers in photography while mercilessly altering everyone's likeness. It often weighs heavily on those compelled to labor for their livelihood, yet compare the hardest day's work you have ever endured with the idleness that splits flowers and probes spider stomachs, and thank your stars that your mind has something it *must* think of, and your hands something they *must* do.

As for Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel, I am glad to report, they tortured nothing. They simply made a mess; and all they spoiled, to their credit, was the paneling of a door.

Mr. Franklin's universal genius, dabbling in everything, turned to what he termed "decorative painting." He claimed to have invented a new mixture to moisten paint, which he called a "vehicle." Its composition remains unknown to me, but its effect can be summarized in two words: it stank. Eager to try the new process, Miss Rachel requested materials from London. Mr. Franklin mixed them, accompanied by a scent so potent it made the dogs sneeze upon entering the room. Donning an apron and bib over her gown, he set her to decorating her small sitting-room—her "boudoir," as she lacked the English vocabulary for it. They commenced with the interior of the door. Mr. Franklin scraped away the fine varnish with pumice-stone to create a working surface. Miss Rachel then covered it, under his direction and with his assistance, with patterns and devices—griffins, birds, flowers, cupids—copied from designs by a famous Italian painter whose name escapes me: the one who stocked the world with Virgin Marys and had a sweetheart at the baker's. Viewed as work, this decoration was slow and filthy. Yet our young lady and gentleman never seemed to tire of it. When they were not riding, receiving guests, dining, or singing, they were there, heads together, as busy as bees, ruining the door. Who was the poet who wrote that Satan finds some mischief still for idle hands to do? If he had occupied my place in the family and witnessed Miss Rachel with her brush and Mr. Franklin with his vehicle, he could have penned nothing truer of either than that.

The next date worthy of note is Sunday, the fourth of June.

That evening, in the servants' hall, we debated a domestic question for the first time—one that, like the door decoration, foreshadows future events. Observing the pleasure Mr. Franklin and Miss Rachel derived from each other's company, and noting how well-matched they were in every personal respect, we naturally speculated on the possibility of their uniting with other objectives in mind. Some predicted a wedding before summer's end. Others, led by me, admitted Miss Rachel might marry, but doubted (for reasons that will soon emerge) whether her bridegroom would be Mr. Franklin Blake.

That Mr. Franklin was in love, on his part, was undeniable to anyone who saw and heard him. The difficulty lay in fathoming Miss Rachel. Permit me to introduce you to her; thereafter, I shall leave you to decipher her—if you are capable.

My young lady's eighteenth birthday was approaching on the twenty-first of June. If you appreciate dark-complexioned women (who, I am informed, have fallen out of fashion in the gay world lately) and harbor no prejudice regarding stature, I vouch for Miss Rachel as one of the prettiest girls your eyes have ever beheld. She was small and slim, yet perfectly proportioned from head to toe. To watch her sit, rise, and especially walk, was sufficient proof for any sensible man that the graces of her figure resided in her flesh, not merely in her clothing. Her hair was the blackest I have ever seen; her eyes matched it. Her nose was not quite large enough, I admit. Her mouth and chin were, to quote Mr. Franklin, morsels for the gods; and her complexion (according to the same undeniable authority) was as warm as the sun itself, with the distinct advantage that it remained perpetually presentable. Add to this that she carried her head as upright as a dart, with a dashing, spirited, thoroughbred manner; possessed a clear voice with a resonant quality; and wore a smile that began prettily in her eyes before reaching her lips—and there stands the portrait of her, to the best of my painting, life-sized!

And what of her disposition? Did this charming creature possess no faults? She had just as many as you have, ma'am—no more, no less.

To speak seriously, my dear pretty Miss Rachel, possessing a host of graces and attractions, had one defect that strict impartiality compels me to acknowledge. Unlike most girls of her age, she possessed her own ideas and was stiff-necked enough to defy the fashions themselves if they conflicted with her views. In trifles, this independence was commendable; but in matters of importance, it carried her (as my lady thought, and as I thought) too far. She judged for herself, as few women of twice her age do; she never sought your advice; she never announced her intentions beforehand; she never confided secrets to anyone, from her mother downwards. In both small and great matters, with those she loved and those she hated (and she treated both with equal fervor), Miss Rachel always followed her own path, sufficient for herself in the joys and sorrows of her life. Over and over, I have heard my lady remark, "Rachel's best friend and Rachel's worst enemy are, one and the other—Rachel herself."

Add one final element, and I am done.

Despite her secrecy and self-will, there was not even a shadow of falsehood in her. I never recall her breaking her word; I never remember her saying "No" while meaning "Yes." I can recall, from her childhood, more than one instance where the good little soul accepted the blame and punishment for a fault committed by a playfellow she loved. Nobody ever knew her to confess when the truth emerged and she was charged with it later. Yet, nobody ever knew her to lie about it either. She would look you straight in the face, shake her little saucy head, and plainly declare, "I won't tell you!" Punished again for this, she would admit regret for saying "won't," but, bread and water notwithstanding, she never revealed the truth. Self-willed—devilishly self-willed sometimes—I grant; but the finest creature, nevertheless, that ever walked the ways of this lower world. Perhaps you perceive a contradiction here? In that case, a word in your ear. Study your wife closely for the next twenty-four hours. If your good lady does not exhibit something in the shape of a contradiction within that time, Heaven help you!—you have married a monster.

I have now introduced you to Miss Rachel, which brings us face-to-face next with the question of her matrimonial views.

On June the twelfth, an invitation from my mistress was sent to a gentleman in London to assist in celebrating Miss Rachel's birthday. This was the fortunate individual on whom I believed her heart was privately set! Like Mr. Franklin, he was a cousin of hers. His name was Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

My lady's second sister (do not be alarmed; we are not delving deeply into family matters this time)—my lady's second sister, I say, experienced a romantic disappointment; and, adhering to the "neck or nothing" principle, she entered into what was termed a misalliance. There was terrible work in the family when the Honourable Caroline insisted on marrying plain Mr. Ablewhite, the banker at Frizinghall. He was very rich and very respectable, and he fathered a prodigiously large family—all to his advantage, so far. But he had presumed to elevate himself from a low station in the world—and that was against him. However, Time and the progress of modern enlightenment rectified matters; and the misalliance eventually passed muster very well. We are all becoming liberal now; and (provided you can scratch me if I scratch you) what do I care, in or out of Parliament, whether you are a Dustman or a Duke? That is the modern perspective—and I keep pace with it. The Ablewhites resided in a fine house and grounds, slightly outside Frizinghall. Very worthy people, greatly respected in the neighborhood. We shall not be much troubled by them in these pages—excepting Mr. Godfrey, who was Mr. Ablewhite's second son, and who must take his proper place here, if you please, for Miss Rachel's sake.

With all his brightness, cleverness, and general good qualities, Mr. Franklin's chance of surpassing Mr. Godfrey in our young lady's estimation was, in my opinion, a very poor one indeed.

First, Mr. Godfrey was, in terms of size, far superior to the other. He stood over six feet tall; he possessed a beautiful red-and-white complexion; a smooth, round face, shaved as bare as a hand; and a head of lovely long flaxen hair falling negligently over the nape of his neck. But why do I attempt to provide this personal description? If you have ever subscribed to a Ladies' Charity in London, you know Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite as well as I do. He was a barrister by profession; a ladies' man by temperament; and a good Samaritan by choice. Female benevolence and female destitution could accomplish nothing without him. Maternal societies for confining poor women; Magdalen societies for rescuing poor women; strong-minded societies for placing poor women in positions formerly held by men, leaving the men to fend for themselves—he was vice-president, manager, and referee to them all. Wherever there was a table with a committee of ladies seated in council, there was Mr. Godfrey at the bottom of the board, maintaining the committee's temper and guiding the dear creatures along the thorny paths of business, hat in hand. I suppose this was the most accomplished philanthropist (on a small independence) that England ever produced.
