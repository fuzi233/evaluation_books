On the 27th of June last year, I received direct orders from Sergeant Cuff to tail three men: suspects wanted for questioning over a potential murder, described as ethnic Indian. Tips had placed them on Tower Wharf that same morning, boarding a Rotterdam-bound steamer.
I caught a rival line’s steamer out of London the very next morning, Thursday the 28th, and tracked down the captain of the earlier Rotterdam vessel the moment I docked. He confirmed the three men had been passengers, but only for the run as far as Gravesend. Off that port, their apparent leader had asked what time they would reach Calais, and flew into what the captain described as extreme, unfeigned shock when told the ship was sailing straight for the Netherlands, not France. He and his two friends begged the captain to put them ashore, offering to forfeit their full passage fare rather than be carried off course. The captain, taking pity on three clearly lost foreign travellers, signalled a shore boat to pick them up, and the three men left the ship at Gravesend.
It was obvious to me the entire “wrong boat” ruse had been planned from the start to throw any pursuers off their trail. I turned back for England at once. I got off the steamer at Gravesend, traced the trio straight back to London, then followed their trail onwards to Plymouth. Inquiries at the Plymouth docks confirmed they had sailed 48 hours earlier aboard the *Bewley Castle*, an East Indiaman making a direct run to Bombay.
The second Sergeant Cuff got that news, he arranged an urgent overland telegraph to the Bombay port authorities, ordering them to board the *Bewley Castle* the moment it dropped anchor, before any passengers could disembark. That was the end of my official part in the case. I have heard no further updates on the matter since.

Sergeant Cuff has asked me to write down the full facts about three men, believed to be high-caste Hindoos, who sailed as passengers on my command, the direct run from Plymouth to Bombay last summer.
The three Hindoos joined my ship at Plymouth. For almost the entire voyage out, there was no complaint against their conduct. They were berthed in the forward quarters, and I barely crossed paths with them personally.
In the final stretch of the journey, we were struck dead calm for three full days and nights, drifting off the Indian coast. I do not have the ship’s log handy to pull the exact latitude and longitude, but the currents pushed us steadily in towards land, and when the wind finally picked up again we made port in Bombay in 24 hours flat. As any seaman will tell you, discipline on a ship always slackens in a long, unbearable calm. My crew and passengers were all worn thin by the swelter. Several of the paying passengers got the small dinghies lowered to row around, and swim after sunset when the air cooled enough, and no one on duty bothered to hoist the boats back to their stowed positions afterwards — they were simply left moored to the ship’s hull. No officers or men had the heart for strict routine while the heat and the frustrating lack of wind dragged on.
Nothing unusual was spotted or heard by the night watch on the third calm night. When dawn broke, the smallest of the dinghies was missing — and the three Hindoos were reported missing shortly afterwards. I have no doubt they stole the boat shortly after full dark, and with eight or more hours of head start on the perfectly calm water, chasing them was pointless by the time we noticed they were gone. Even if they were clumsy oarsmen, they would have made landfall well before sunrise.
It was only when I docked at Bombay that I learned the full reason for their escape. The port authorities blamed me for letting the ship’s discipline slip, as was their right. I offered my full apologies both to them and to my ship’s owners for the oversight. I have not heard a single word of the three Hindoos since the ship docked. I have no further facts to add.

My dear Bruff, do you recall that ragged half-savage adventurer you sat next to at a London dinner party in the autumn of 1848? The one you had that long post-dinner chat with, about the cursed Indian gem called the Moonstone and the secret conspiracy to steal it back from English hands? That was me.
Since that night, I have wandered most of Central Asia, then drifted back to the old northwest Indian territories where I cut my teeth as a young explorer. A fortnight ago, I found myself in Kattiawar: a little-known, wild province where even the farmers plough their fields with loaded muskets slung over their shoulders, and the entire population is fanatically devoted to the old, pre-colonial Hindu faith. The few scattered Muslim families in the interior villages dare not touch meat of any kind — anyone even suspected of killing a sacred cow is put to death without mercy by their pious neighbours. This remote region holds two of the holiest pilgrimage sites in the Hindu faith: Dwarka, birthplace of Krishna, and the ruins of Somnauth, the great sacred city sacked and destroyed by the conqueror Mahmoud of Ghizni all the way back in the 11th century.
I decided I would not leave Kattiawar without seeing the grand, weathered ruins of Somnauth one more time. I was roughly three days’ hard hike on foot from the ruins, and I set out alone. Before long I noticed pairs and trios of other travellers all heading the same direction. Dressed in the saffron robes of a Buddhist pilgrim from a distant northern province, fluent in the local language, lean and sun-browned enough that no one guessed at my European origins, I had no trouble passing among them unchallenged.
By the second day, the little groups of pilgrims had swelled to hundreds. By the third, there were thousands all slowly converging on Somnauth. A small favour I did for an injured high-caste traveller that afternoon won me the trust of a group of elite pilgrims, who invited me to join them for a secret night ceremony held on a low hill just outside the ruined city, a ritual honouring the Moon God.
By the time we climbed the hill, the full moon hung high in the cloudless Indian sky. Tens of thousands of pilgrims dressed all in white stretched down the slopes, spilling out over the grassy plain where three rivers met, their banks lined with the crowds, the dark calm ocean glinting far off on the horizon. Red torchlight from cressets dotted the mass of people, glowing against the silver moonlight.
A soft, plaintive strain of flutes and stringed instruments pulled my gaze back to the shrine. Three men stood on the flat rocky platform just before the curtain strung between two giant banyan trees that hid the altar from view. I recognized the central man at once: he was the same Hindu pilgrim I had spoken to on Lady Verinder’s terrace the night the Moonstone vanished from her bedroom. His two companions that night stood at his sides.
A pilgrim next to me saw me start, and whispered that the three men were Brahmins who had voluntarily renounced their caste to serve the Moon God, tasked with bringing his stolen sacred gem home. That night, their penance was complete. They were to part ways immediately, each taking a separate pilgrimage route across the whole of India, and they would never look on each other’s faces again, never rest, until the day they died.
The music cut off. The three men prostrated themselves on the rock before the curtain, stood, embraced one another, then walked apart into the crowd. The mass of pilgrims parted in dead silence to let them pass, and slowly closed up again behind them, erasing all trace of their path. We did not see them again.
A new, jubilant burst of music blared from behind the curtain. The crowd shuddered and pressed closer together. The curtain was drawn aside, and the shrine stood revealed.
There, carved high on his throne, seated on his sacred antelope mount, four arms stretching out to the four corners of the world, was the dark, awe-inspiring statue of the Moon God. Set perfectly in his carved forehead, glowing bright under the moonlight, was the yellow diamond I last saw glinting from the neckline of Rachel Verinder’s dress in a English drawing room, years before.
After eight long centuries, the Moonstone has returned at last to the sacred city where its story first began. Whatever accident or crime allowed the three pilgrims to slip away with the gem and bring it home to its native land, you may know the full details — I do not. You lost track of it in England, and you will never lay eyes on it again.
The cycles of the world turn, the same stories repeat, and no man living can say what new adventures wait for the Moonstone in the years ahead.
