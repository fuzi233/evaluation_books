Mr. Thornton did not merely believe Margaret had spoken a deliberate falsehood, though she imagined that single misdeed was the sum of his low opinion of her. That lie, to his mind, was inextricably tangled with the memory of the tender, earnest glance he had caught passing between her and a strange man, the air of familiar, unguarded confidence he had witnessed between them, if not of outright endearment. The image gnawed at him perpetually, burn itself behind his eyes no matter where he walked, no matter what work he turned his hand to. Worse still—he ground his teeth at the recollection—was the setting: that dusky twilight hour, that lane far removed from the bustle of Milton’s central streets, so quiet, so rarely trod by any passerby.

At first his nobler self had argued the whole thing might be innocent, accidental, entirely justifiable. But he could not long cling to that soft thought. Once he allowed that Margaret had every right to love and be loved—had she not cast his own offer of love away from him with sharp, unyielding clarity?—it was all too easy to imagine she had been beguiled into extending her walk, lingering later than she had ever intended, for the sake of that other man. But that lie! It betrayed a fatal, guilty consciousness of something to hide, something uncharacteristic of the frank, truthful girl he had thought he knew. Even so, he never could fully convince himself she was utterly unworthy of his esteem. That was the source of his misery: he loved her so passionately that even with all these perceived flaws, she outshone every other woman he had ever met. And yet he was convinced she was so wrapped up in her affection for this unknown man, that slight, elegant, handsome stranger, that she had violated her own nature to lie for him. The very falsehood that stained her reputation in his eyes was proof of how blindly she loved another—while he, rough, stern, broad-shouldered, could never hope to compete. He lashed himself into a fever of fierce, consuming jealousy, thinking of that glance, that soft, unresisting way she had leaned toward the other man. He would have laid down his life at her feet to earn such tender looks, such fond, lingering attention. He mocked himself for once being proud that she had stepped between him and the fury of the rioting mill-workers: now he understood how soft and bewitching she could be, when she was with a man she truly loved. He repeated her words to himself, sharp as shards of glass: *“There was not a man in all that crowd for whom she would not have done as much, far more readily than for him.”* He had shared that small act of protection with a whole mob of strangers. But this hidden lover? He had all her secret glances, all her quiet words, the pressure of her hand, all her lies and concealments, all to himself.

Thornton had never in his life been so irritable as he was these days. Every question asked of him earned a sharp, abrupt reply, closer to a bark than civil speech. He was ashamed of this loss of the self-control he had always prided himself on, and forced himself to master it: his manner softened into a quiet, deliberate calm, but his decisions grew harsher, sterner than ever. At home he was uncommonly silent, pacing back and forth across the parlour floor evening after evening, a habit that would have roused his mother’s ire against any other person in the house, and which made her far less inclined to extend even him any forbearance.

“Can you stop that everlasting walk, walk, walk, and sit down for a moment?” she demanded at last. “I have something to say to you.”

He sat instantly, hard against the wall on a wooden chair.

“It’s about Betsy. She says she must leave our service. Her lover’s death has so broken her spirits, she can’t bring herself to give her full heart to her cooking work.”

“Very well. We can find another cook easily enough.”

“That’s exactly like a man. It’s not just the cooking. She knows every quirk of this house, every way we like things done. And besides, she told me something about your friend Miss Hale.”

“Miss Hale is no friend of mine. Her father is my acquaintance, that is all.”

“I’m glad to hear you say that. If she had been your friend, what Betsy says would have annoyed you deeply.”

“Tell me,” he said, his tone the same forced, steady quiet he had maintained for days.

“Betsy says that the night her lover—I forget his name, she only ever calls him ‘he’—”

“Leonards.”

“The night Leonards was last seen on duty at the station, Miss Hale was there, walking back and forth with a young man whom Bessy believes struck or pushed him to his death.”

“Leonards was not killed by any blow or push.”

“How do you know?”

“I asked the Infirmary surgeon directly. He told me Leonards had long suffered from an internal disease caused by his habitual heavy drinking, and that the fact he took a turn for the worse while he was drunk proved the fatal attack was caused by excess alcohol, not any fall.”

“The fall! What fall?”

“The fall that came from the blow or push Betsy spoke of.”

“Then there *was* a blow or push?”

“I believe so.”

“Who did it?”

“There was no inquest, the doctor’s testimony made it unnecessary. I cannot say for certain.”

“But Miss Hale was there?”

He made no answer.

“And with a young man?”

Still silence. At last he spoke. “Mother, there was no formal judicial inquiry into the matter. Nothing has been proven.”

“Betsy says Woolmer—her acquaintance who works at the grocer’s shop out in Crampton—swears he saw Miss Hale at the station at that exact hour, walking back and forth with that young man.”

“I do not see what that has to do with us. Miss Hale may do as she pleases.”

“I am glad you take that view,” Mrs. Thornton said eagerly. “It means nothing to us, of course, not after what passed between you! But I— I made a promise to her mother, that I would not let her daughter go astray without giving her good advice and remonstrance. I shall certainly make my opinion of such conduct clear to her.”

“I do not see any harm in what she did that evening,” Thornton said, standing abruptly and moving to stand beside the chimney-piece, his face turned away from the room.

“You would never have approved of Fanny being seen out after dark, in such a lonely place, wandering about with an unchaperoned young man. I will not even speak of the shocking taste that would choose the very hours when her own mother lay unburied for such a promenade. Would you have liked a grocer’s assistant gossiping about Fanny for doing such a thing?”

“First, I myself was a draper’s assistant not many years ago. The fact that a grocer’s assistant notices an act does not change its moral character for me. Second, there is a world of difference between Miss Hale and Fanny. I can imagine Miss Hale has weighty, urgent reasons that make any seeming impropriety in her conduct entirely justified. I never knew Fanny to have a weighty reason for anything. Other people must guard her. I believe Miss Hale is perfectly capable of guarding herself.”

“That is a fine character to give your own sister!” Mrs. Thornton scoffed. “Really, John, you would think Miss Hale had done enough to make you see clearly. She drew you on to make an offer, with a bold, false display of regard for you, all to play you off against this very same young man, I have no doubt. Her whole conduct is plain as day to me now. You believe he is her lover, I suppose? You agree to that much?”

Thornton turned to face his mother. His face was gray, his expression grim. “Yes, mother. I do believe he is her lover.” The words hung in the air for a heartbeat, and then he twisted in his chair as if in the grip of physical agony, pressing his face into one hand. Before she could speak, he spun around sharply again. “Mother. He is her lover, whoever he is. But she may need help, gentle womanly counsel. There may be difficulties, temptations, I know nothing of. I fear there are. I do not want to know the details. But as you have always been a good, and a tender mother to me, go to her. Gain her confidence. Tell her what is best to be done. I know there is something wrong, some terrible dread that must be torturing her.”

“For God’s sake, John!” Mrs. Thornton was truly shocked now. “What do you mean? What do you know?”

He would not answer.

“John! I shall think the worst if you do not speak. You have no right to cast these half-insinuations against her character.”

“Not against her, mother. I could never speak against her.”

“Well, you have no right to say what you just did, unless you explain more. These half-spoken accusations are the very thing that ruin a woman’s reputation.”

“Her character! Mother, you do not dare—” He stepped forward, his eyes blazing, then forced himself back into a stance of deliberate, calm dignity. “I will only say this, and it is the simple, unvarnished truth I know you will believe: I have good reason to think Miss Hale is in some strait and difficulty connected to an attachment that, from all I know of her character, is perfectly innocent and right. I will not tell you what that reason is. But never let me hear anyone say a word against her implying anything worse than that she now needs the counsel of some kind, gentle woman. You promised her dying mother you would be that woman!”

“No,” Mrs. Thornton corrected him, drawing herself up. “I am happy to say I never promised her kindness or gentleness. I knew at the time I could not offer that sort of soft treatment to a girl of Miss Hale’s character and disposition. I promised her counsel, the same advice I would give to my own daughter. I shall speak to her exactly as I would speak to Fanny if she had been gallivanting around with a young man at dusk. I will not be swayed one way or another by these ‘strong reasons’ you refuse to share. Then I shall have kept my promise, and done my duty.”

“She will never bear it,” Thornton said passionately.

“She will bear it, if I speak in her dead mother’s name.”

“Very well!” He broke away toward the door. “Do not tell me any more about it. I cannot bear to think of it. It is better you speak to her than no one do at all. Oh, that look of love!” he muttered between his teeth as he slammed his private study door behind him. “And that cursed lie, that showed some terrible shame in the background, that kept her from the bright, honest life I thought she lived perpetually! Oh, Margaret, Margaret! Mother, how you have tortured me! Oh, Margaret, could you not have loved me? I am only uncouth and hard, but I would never have led you to tell a single falsehood for my sake.”

The more Mrs. Thornton turned her son’s pleas for leniency over in her mind, the more bitter her resentment of Margaret grew. She took a savage, grim pleasure in the thought of “speaking her mind” to her, all under the guise of fulfilling a sacred duty. She delighted at the thought of proving herself immune to the “glamour” she knew Margaret was capable of casting over soft-headed people. She snorted in scorn thinking of her victim’s famed beauty: her jet black hair, her clear smooth skin, her luminous eyes would not save her a single word of the just, stern reproach she spent half the night rehearsing in her head.

“Is Miss Hale within?” She already knew Margaret was home, she had seen her standing at the front window, and she had her foot over the threshold of the small cottage hall before the servant Martha could finish half her answer.

Margaret was sitting alone, writing to Edith, detailing the last quiet days of her mother’s life. The soft, sad work had left her eyes damp with unbidden tears when Mrs. Thornton was announced.

Her greeting was so gentle, so gracious and ladylike that Mrs. Thornton was momentarily daunted. The sharp speech she had rehearsed so easily in her parlour would not form on her tongue. Margaret’s low, rich voice was softer than usual, her manner warmer than ever, for she felt deeply grateful that Mrs. Thornton had thought to call on her in her bereavement. She exerted herself to find pleasant topics for conversation, praised the cook Martha that Mrs. Thornton had recommended to them, mentioned she had written to Edith to send over the little Greek air that she had discussed with Fanny once. Mrs. Thornton was completely discomfited. Her sharp, Damascus steel-edged rebuke felt entirely out of place, useless, scattered on rose leaves. She stayed silent, trying to summon her resolve to perform her “duty”, until a foolish, unlikely suspicion stung her into action: all this sweetness was a act, put on to propitiate Mr. Thornton. The other secret attachment must have fallen through, and Margaret meant to win back her rejected suitor.

Poor Margaret! There was just enough truth to the suspicion: she knew Mrs. Thornton was the mother of the man whose good opinion she still ached to retain, unconsciously adding extra warmth to her usual graciousness. Mrs. Thornton stood up to leave, but it was obvious she still had something to say. She cleared her throat, and began.

“Miss Hale, I have a duty to perform. I promised your poor mother that, as far as my judgment went, I would not let you act wrongly, or even inadvertently, without remonstrating with you, at the very least offering you advice whether you chose to take it or not.”

Margaret stood before her, blushing like a caught criminal, her eyes wide as she stared at Mrs. Thornton. She was certain the older woman had come to speak to her about the lie she had told Mr. Thornton about not being at the station—she thought he had sent her to warn her that her lie might be exposed in the coroner’s inquest! Her heart sank a little that he had not chosen to come himself, to upbraid her, to take her penitence, and restore her to his good opinion, but she was so humbled that she would have borne any rebuke on that meekly, patiently.

Mrs. Thornton went on.

“At first, when one of my servants told me you had been seen walking about with a gentleman all the way out at the Outwood station, at such a late hour of the evening, I could barely believe it. But my son, I am sorry to say, confirmed the story. It was indiscreet, to put it mildly. Many a young woman has lost her good name for far less—”

Margaret’s eyes flashed fire. This was a new insult, far worse than the one she had braced herself for. If Mrs. Thornton had spoken to her about her lie, she would have owned it, and bowed her head in humiliation. But to interfere in her conduct, to question her character—Mrs. Thornton, almost a stranger to her! It was intolerable. She would not answer. Not one single word.

Mrs. Thornton caught the battle-spark in Margaret’s eyes, and her own combativeness rose to match it.

“For your mother’s sake, I thought it right to warn you against these improprieties. They will degrade you in the eyes of the world in time, even if they do not lead you into outright harm.”

“For my mother’s sake,” Margaret said, her voice thick with tears, “I will bear much. But I cannot bear everything. She never would have wished me to be exposed to insult, I am sure.”

“Insult, Miss Hale!”

“Yes, madam,” Margaret said, more steadily now. “It is insult. What do you know of me that should make you suspect—Oh!” She broke off, covering her face with her hands. “I know now. Mr. Thornton told you—”

“No, Miss Hale,” Mrs. Thornton’s innate truthfulness made her stop the confession Margaret was on the brink of making, even as her curiosity burned to hear it. “Stop. Mr. Thornton told me nothing. You do not know my son. You are not worthy to know him. Listen, young lady, so that you may understand what sort of man you rejected. This Milton manufacturer, his great tender heart scorned as it was scorned, said to me only last night: ‘Go to her. I have good reason to know she is in some strait arising out of some attachment, and she needs womanly counsel.’ Those were his exact words. Beyond admitting you were at the Outwood station with a gentleman on the evening of the twenty-sixth, he said nothing against you. Not a single word. If he knows anything that would make you sob so, he keeps it entirely to himself.”

Margaret’s face was still hidden in her hands, the fingers wet with tears. Mrs. Thornton was a little mollified.

“Come, Miss Hale. I will allow there may be circumstances, that if explained, would clear all seeming impropriety away.”

Still no answer. Margaret was struggling over what to say. She wanted to remain in Mrs. Thornton’s good graces, but she could not, dared not, explain. Mrs. Thornton grew impatient.

“I shall be sorry to cut off our acquaintance entirely, but for Fanny’s sake—as I told my son, if Fanny had done such a thing we would have considered it a great disgrace, and Fanny is easily led astray—”

“I can give you no explanation,” Margaret said in a low, trembling voice. “I have done wrong, but not in the way you think, not in the way you accuse me of. I believe Mr. Thornton judges me more mercifully than you—” She had to fight to keep from choking on her tears. “But I believe, madam, you mean to do rightly.”

“Thank you,” Mrs. Thornton said, drawing herself up stiffly. “I was not aware my intentions were being doubted. This will be the last time I ever interfere.
