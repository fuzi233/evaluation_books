I rose, trembling, my heart a chaotic void. For a fleeting second, she vanished into the darkness. Loneliness crashed back upon me, heavy and suffocating, like a storm cloud returning after the rain has passed. The woman I had pulled from the jaws of death had fled. She had abandoned me, leaving behind only a chasm of desolation. I could not endure this solitude another moment. Had I truly committed a crime against her? Was I now condemned to spend my life carrying the weight of a burden I had forced her to bear?

There, ahead, moving swiftly across the grass and away from the river's edge, I spotted her figure. Seizing the opportunity for a final, cleansing plunge into the water, I refreshed myself and launched myself in pursuit. The bite of the white leech and the blow dealt by the woman had drained my strength, yet already vitality surged back through my limbs. Keeping her in sight proved effortless.

"Is this, then, the end?" I whispered to the empty air, my heart humming a dirge. Her eyes, burning with anger and hatred, haunted my vision. I understood her fury; she resented being forced back into life. But how else had I wounded her? Why did she loathe me? Could even the virtue of modesty turn against genuine service? How could the proudest soul, who knew every movement of mine, harbor such a sense of disgrace? With what reverence had I not touched her! Like a father tending a motherless child, I had carried her, nursed her, and protected her. Had all my labor, all my desperate hope, amounted only to breeding ingratitude?

"No," I told myself, forcing conviction into my voice. "Beauty possesses a heart! No matter how deeply buried, it exists! Buried the deeper, the more potent and truthful it awakens in its beautiful tomb! To awaken that heart is a greater gift than granting her the happiest life. It is to offer her a nobler, a higher existence!"

She ascended a gentle slope before me, walking with a straight, steady stride, purposeful as one who knows exactly where she is going. Yet, I noticed she was widening the gap between us. Summoning every reserve of energy, I felt my veins flood with fresh life. My body seemed to dissolve into something ethereal, moving like a breeze effortlessly overtaking her. Not once did she glance backward. She moved swiftly, possessing the grace of a Greek goddess rescuing a prize, yet without any sign of haste.

When I closed the distance to within three yards, she turned sharply, yet her grace remained unbroken, and she halted. Neither fatigue nor heat marred her composure. Her skin possessed a purity of whiteness rather than a sickly pallor; her breathing was slow and profound. Her eyes seemed to fill the heavens, casting light upon the world. It was nearly noon, yet the sensation was akin to a great night where invisible dew causes the stars to swell in size.

"Why do you follow me?" she asked, her tone quiet but stern, as though addressing a stranger she had never encountered before.

"I have lived so long on the mere hope of your eyes," I answered, "that I must desire to behold them once more!"

"You WILL NOT BE SPARED!" she declared, her voice icy. "I command you to stop where you stand."

"Not until I see you in a place of safety will I leave you," I replied firmly.

"Then take the consequences," she said, and resumed her gliding walk. But as she pivoted, she cast a glance my way, and I stood frozen, as if pierced by a spear. Her scorn had failed; she intended to destroy me with her sheer beauty! Despair shattered my resolve, breaking the spell that held me captive. I ran, overtaking her once more.

"Have pity upon me!" I cried out.

She paid me no heed. I trailed behind her like a child pretending his mother has abandoned him.

"I will be your slave!" I shouted, laying my hand upon her arm.

She spun around as if bitten by a venomous serpent. I cowered before the blazing intensity of her gaze, unable to avert my own.

"Pity me," I begged again.

She continued walking. Throughout the entire day, I followed her. The sun climbed the sky, paused at its zenith, and descended on the other side. She never paused, not for a single moment, nor did I cease my pursuit. She never turned her head, never slackened her pace. When the sun dipped below the horizon and night enveloped the earth, I pressed closer. If I lost sight of her for even a heartbeat, it might be forever.

All day we traversed thick, soft grass. Abruptly, she stopped and collapsed onto the ground. There was still enough light to reveal she was utterly exhausted. I stood behind her, gazing down at her form. *Did I love her?* I knew she was not good. *Did I hate her?* Yet I could not leave her! Kneeling beside her, I waited.

"Begone! Do not dare touch me," she screamed.

Her arms lay motionless on the grass by her sides, appearing paralyzed. Suddenly, they snapped shut around my neck, rigid as the grip of a torturer. She dragged my face down to hers, and her lips clung tightly to my cheek. A sharp sting of pain shot through me, pulsing violently. I could not move a single hair's breadth. Gradually, the agony subsided. A slumberous weariness, mixed with a dreamlike pleasure, washed over me, and then, darkness claimed me.

Suddenly, consciousness returned. The moon hung a short distance above the horizon, offering no radiance; it was merely a bright object set against absolute blackness. My cheek throbbed; I touched it and found a damp spot. My neck ached; there, too, was a wet stain. I sighed heavily, feeling bone-tired. Turning my eyes listlessly around, I searched for the moon's light—and found it gathered about the lady. She stood encased in a shimmering nimbus.

I rose and stumbled toward her.

"Down!" she commanded imperiously, treating me like a rebellious dog. "Follow me a step if you dare!"

"I will!" I murmured, exerting an agonized effort to obey.

"Step within the gates of my city, and my people will stone you: they do not love beggars!"

Deaf to her warnings, weak as water and half-awake, I did not realize I was moving, yet the distance between us shrank. She took one step back, raised her left arm, and struck me on the forehead with a clenched fist. I felt as though hit by an iron hammer and fell hard.

I sprang to my feet, cold and wet, but clear-headed and strong. Had the blow revived me? It had left neither wound nor pain—but why was I soaked? I could not have been lying there long, for the moon was no higher! The lady stood some yards away, her back to me. She was engaged in some activity I could not discern. Then, by a sudden gleam, I realized she had discarded her garments and stood naked in the dazed moonlight.

One moment she stood tall—and then she fell forward. A streak of white shot away in a swift, drawn line. In the same instant, the moon recovered its brilliance with a full flash, revealing that the streak was a long-bodied creature rushing in great, low-curved bounds over the grass. Dark spots seemed to run like a stream down its back, as if it had been fleeing along the edge of a forest, catching the shadows of the leaves.

"God of mercy!" I cried, "Is the terrible creature speeding toward the night-enfolded city?" And it seemed I heard from afar the sudden burst and spread of terrified cries as the pale savage bounded from house to house, rending and slaying.

While I stared after it, fear-stricken, a second large creature, pure white, shot past me from behind like a swift, almost silent arrow. Its path was straight for the spot where the lady had fallen, and, as I believed, lay. My tongue stuck to the roof of my mouth. I sprang forward to pursue the beast, but in a moment, the spot I aimed for was far behind it.

"It was well," I thought, "that I could not cry out: if she had risen, the monster would have been upon her!"

But when I reached the location, no lady remained. Only the garments she had dropped lay dark in the moonlight. I stood staring after the second beast. It tore over the ground with even greater swiftness than the first—in long, level, skimming leaps, the very embodiment of wasteless speed. It followed the exact line the other had taken, and I watched it shrink until it disappeared into the uncertain distance.

But where was the lady? Had the first beast surprised her, creeping up on her silently? I had heard no shriek! There had not been time to devour her! Could it have caught her up as it ran, bearing her away to its den? So laden, it could not have run so fast! And I should have seen that it carried something!

Horrible doubts began to wake within me. After a thorough but fruitless search, I set out to follow the tracks of the two beasts.
