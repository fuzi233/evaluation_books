The morning air hung heavy with unspoken tension, a silence so profound that Edith moved through the hallway on tiptoe, her footsteps swallowed by the dust motes dancing in the shafts of light. She intercepted Sholto before he could raise his voice, checking every sudden noise as if the slightest disturbance might shatter the fragile conference brewing within the drawing-room. By two o'clock, the clock's tick echoed louder than the thunder rolling overhead; the doors remained shut, sealing the two figures inside in a private orbit of anxiety.

Finally, the rhythm of the house shifted. A man's footstep, urgent and descending, raced down the stairs. Edith peeked out, her eyes wide with anticipation. "Well, Henry?" she asked, her gaze interrogating the space between them.

"Well!" he replied, the word clipped, carrying the weight of exhaustion. "Come in to lunch!"

"No, thank you, I can't. I've lost too much time here already."

"Then it's not all settled?" Edith's shoulders slumped, despair coloring her tone.

"Not at all," Henry answered, shaking his head with finality. "It never *will* be settled, if the 'it' is what I conjecture you mean. That will never be, Edith, so give up thinking about it."

"But it would be so nice for us all," she pleaded, the image of domestic harmony flickering in her mind. "I should always feel comfortable about the children, if I had Margaret settled down near to me. As it is, I am always afraid of her going off to Cadiz."

"I will try, when I marry, to look out for a young lady who has a knowledge of the management of children," Henry said, his voice hollow. "That is all I can do. Miss Hale will not have me. And I shall not ask her."

Edith blinked, confused. "Then what have you been talking about?"

"A thousand things you would not understand: investments, leases, the value of land."

"Oh, go away if that's all," Edith dismissed, waving a hand as if brushing away cobwebs. "You and she will be unbearably stupid, if you've been talking all this time about such weary things."

"Very well. I'm coming again tomorrow, and bringing Mr. Thornton with me, to have some more talk with Miss Hale."

"Mr. Thornton! What has he to do with it?"

"He is Miss Hale's tenant," Mr. Lennox said, turning away, his posture stiff with professional detachment. "And he wishes to give up his lease."

"Oh! Very well. I can't understand details, so don't give them to me."

"The only detail I want you to understand is to let us have the back drawing-room undisturbed, as it was today. In general, the children and servants are so in and out that I can never get any business satisfactorily explained; and the arrangements we have to make tomorrow are of importance."

Yet, fate intervened. No one ever knew why Mr. Lennox failed to keep his appointment the following day. True to the hour, Mr. Thornton arrived, but after keeping him waiting for nearly an hour, Margaret emerged. She looked pale, her complexion washed out by anxiety, her movements jerky with nervous energy.

"I am so sorry Mr. Lennox is not here," she began hurriedly, her voice trembling. "He could have done it so much better than I can. He is my adviser in this—"

"I am sorry that I came, if it troubles you. Shall I go to Mr. Lennox's chambers and try and find him?"

"No, thank you. I wanted to tell you how grieved I was to find that I am to lose you as a tenant. But, Mr. Lennox says, things are sure to brighten—"

"Mr. Lennox knows little about it," Mr. Thornton interrupted, his voice quiet but cutting through the fog of her worry. "Happy and fortunate in all a man cares for, he does not understand what it is to find oneself no longer young—yet thrown back to the starting-point which requires the hopeful energy of youth—to feel one half of life gone, and nothing done. Nothing remaining of wasted opportunity, but the bitter recollection that it has been."

He stepped closer, his presence commanding the room. "Miss Hale, I would rather not hear Mr. Lennox's opinion of my affairs. Those who are happy and successful themselves are too apt to make light of the misfortunes of others."

"You are unjust," Margaret said gently, her eyes searching his face. "Mr. Lennox has only spoken of the great probability which he believes there to be of your redeeming—your more than redeeming what you have lost—don't speak till I have ended—pray don't."

She collected herself, forcing her trembling hands to turn over law papers and statements of account with frantic speed. "Oh! Here it is! And—he drew me out a proposal. I wish he was here to explain it. Showing that if you would take some money of mine, eighteen hundred and fifty-seven pounds, lying just at this moment unused in the bank, and bringing me in only two and a half per cent—you could pay me much better interest, and might go on working Marlborough Mills."

Her voice had cleared, steadying into the cadence of commerce. Mr. Thornton said nothing, watching her intently. She continued scanning the documents, desperate to frame this transaction purely as a business arrangement, where the principal advantage lay squarely on her side. Yet, as she searched for the specific paper outlining the terms of security, her heart seized. The air in the room changed, charged with a static electricity that made the hair on her arms stand up.

His voice broke the silence, hoarse and trembling with a tenderness that terrified her.

"Margaret!"

For an instant, she looked up, her luminous eyes meeting his, before she instinctively sought to veil them, dropping her forehead onto her hands. Again, he stepped nearer, his desperation manifesting in another tremulous call.

"Margaret!"

Lower still went her head; more completely hidden became her face, almost resting on the table before her. He came close, invading her personal space until there was no escape. He knelt by her side, lowering his face to a level with her ear, whispering words that seemed to vibrate through the floorboards:

"Take care.—If you do not speak—I shall claim you as my own in some strange presumptuous way.—Send me away at once, if I must go;—Margaret!"

At that third call, she turned her face, still covered with her small white hands, towards him. She laid it on his shoulder, hiding it there, burying herself in the warmth of his coat. It was too delicious to feel her soft cheek against his for him to wish to see either deep blushes or loving eyes. He clasped her close, holding her as if she were the only solid thing in a dissolving world. But they both kept silence, the only sound the ragged breathing of two souls colliding.

At length, she murmured in a broken voice, pulling back slightly. "Oh, Mr. Thornton, I am not good enough!"

"Not good enough! Don't mock my own deep feeling of unworthiness."

After a minute or two, he gently disengaged her hands from her face, laying her arms as they had once before been placed to protect him from the rioters.

"Do you remember, love?" he murmured, his voice rough with emotion. "And how I requited you with my insolence the next day?"

"I remember how wrongly I spoke to you,—that is all."

"Look here! Lift up your head. I have something to show you!"

She slowly faced him, glowing with a beautiful, burning shame.

"Do you know these roses?" he said, drawing out his pocket-book, in which were treasured up some dried, faded flowers.

"No!" she replied, with innocent curiosity.

"Did I give them to you?"

"No! Vanity; you did not. You may have worn sister roses very probably."

She looked at them, wondering for a minute, then she smiled a little as she said—

"They are from Helstone, are they not? I know the deep indentations round the leaves. Oh! Have you been there? When were you there?"

"I wanted to see the place where Margaret grew to what she is, even at the worst time of all, when I had no hope of ever calling her mine. I went there on my return from Havre."

"You must give them to me," she said, trying to take them out of his hand with gentle violence.

"Very well. Only you must pay me for them!"

"How shall I ever tell Aunt Shaw?" she whispered, after some time of delicious silence.

"Let me speak to her."

"Oh, no! I owe to her,—but what will she say?"

"I can guess. Her first exclamation will be, 'That man!'"

"Hush!" said Margaret, leaning in close, her voice dropping to a conspiratorial whisper. "Or I shall try and show you your mother's indignant tones as she says, 'That woman!'"
