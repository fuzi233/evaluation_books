The petty falling-out between George Warrington and his cousin Will Esmond led George to all but abandon his visits to Bloomsbury Square. Will had grown assiduous in his attendance on Lydia Van den Bosch, and now that the pair were formally bound over to keep the peace after their aborted duel, his behaviour grew so outrageously provoking that George had to clamp down hard on his temper every time they met to keep from pummelling him on the spot.

Artless little Lydia had a most peculiar way of receiving her friends. Only six weeks prior, she had been wild with jealousy over rumours that George favoured Theo Lambert over her, but now she took every chance to sing Theo’s praises in conversation, declaring the quiet, gentle girl was perfectly matched to George, her ideal wife. How lucky, she would laugh, that the constables had arrived just in time to stop their duel! It was perfectly ridiculous to recall how Mr. Esmond had raged and sworn at being cheated out of his fight. “Those officers saved your precious life, dear Mr. George,” she would say with a soft, demure smile, “and I am certain Miss Theo ought to bless them for it forever. You won’t stay to dine with us today and meet Mr. Esmond? You needn’t shrink from his company, you know – he can do you no harm, and I am sure you would never harm him.”

These kindly-sounding little speeches, dropped casually in front of other visiting gentlemen and ladies, did no favours for George’s reputation, and soon made him loath to seek out Miss Lydia’s society. Their near-duel had been kept secret from the Lamberts in Dean Street for days, George not wishing to disturb their peaceful household with his quarrels, but when the ladies finally learned of it, the whole home erupted in a flurry of consternation.
“Oh George!” Theo cried, horrified. “You very nearly went and took another man’s life, and you came to visit us all the while and never breathed a word of it! It is shocking!”
“He insulted me and my twin brother,” George argued. “Was I to sit idle and hear us both called cowards, and thank him for the privilege?”

General Lambert sat by the fire, looking very grave. “You know it is a wicked, un-Christian practice, George. I have said a hundred times I wish more gentlemen had the courage to refuse a duel.”
“Refuse?” echoed Jack Lambert, the General’s young clergyman son, his face just as glum as his father’s. “That must take a prodigious strength of will. I think if any man called me a coward, I should be very likely to forget my holy orders and fly at him.”
“See, brother Jack is on my side!” George exclaimed.
“I cannot very well side against you, Mr. Warrington,” Jack said stiffly.
“Mr. Warrington?” George sputtered, flushing bright red.
“Would you have George break the Sixth Commandment and commit murder, John?” Theo gasped, aghast.
“I am a soldier’s son before I am a parson, sister,” Jack replied drily. “Besides, Mr. Warrington committed no murder at all. We shall hear news from Canada soon, no doubt – the great fight for supremacy between the British and French out there is bound to come to a head before long.” He turned his back to George as he spoke, and George stared at him in bewilderment. Hetty had gone pale at her brother’s odd remark, and Theo tugged her out of the room on some thin pretext. George started up to follow the retreating girls to the door, then spun back to face the two men.
“Great heavens!” he burst out. “On my honour, I believe you two think I shirked that duel with Esmond!”
The General and his son exchanged a long look. “A man’s nearest and dearest are always the first to insult him,” George flared, stung.
“Not guilty, my boy?” the General cried, his eyes misting over. “Thank God for that! I told you so, Jack.” He scrubbed a hand over his face, blushed, and wrung George’s hand so hard his knuckles whitened.
“Not guilty of what?” George asked, utterly confused.
“Nay, let Jack tell you,” the General said. “It was his story first, and I believe it’s a downright lie, with all my heart.” With that uncharacteristically rough utterance, he walked straight out of the room.

The Reverend Jack Lambert looked absurdly sheepish. “And what is this damned lie that someone has been spreading about me?” George asked, grinning despite his anger.
“To question a man’s courage is always a grave offence, and I rejoice to find yours has been slandered,” Jack said.
“Who told you that falsehood?” George roared. “I demand his name.”
“You forget you are bound over to keep the peace,” Jack reminded him.
“Curse the peace!” George yelled. “We could take a boat to Holland and fight there, no one would be the wiser. Tell me his name, I say!”
“Softly, Mr. Warrington,” the parson said, holding up a hand. “My hearing is perfectly good. It was no man who told me the story I – I confess I repeated to my father.”
The truth dawned on George all at once. “That artful, wicked little vixen in Bloomsbury Square?”
“Vixen is no name to call any young lady, George Warrington!” Jack exclaimed, outraged. “Far less the charming Miss Lydia! Artful? She is the most innocent creature Heaven ever made. Wicked? She is a perfect angel! She spoke with such unfeigned delight that the quarrel was over, such devout gratitude that no kin’s blood would be spilled, singing your praises for being so considerate as to avoid bloodshed by turning the two of you over to the constables before any harm could be done.”
“Laying an information?” George shouted, stamping his foot. The curses he unleashed on Miss Lydia then are far too violent and ungentlemanly to repeat.
“Do not dare utter such words!” Jack snapped, turning angry himself. “Do not insult the most innocent, charming creature of the sex to my face! If she was misinformed about you, and thought you chose a peaceful path rather than a sinful duel, you have no right to blacken that sweet girl’s name with foul language. Are you to be my brother, after all?”
“I hope for that honour,” George said, smiling now, the pieces clicking into place.
“Then what – though it is far more bliss than a sinful man like me deserves – what if one day she was to be my sister? Who could look on her beauty and not fall in love? I confess I am her utter slave. Those Latin Sapphics published in the September issue of the *Gentleman’s Magazine*, the ones beginning *Lydicae quondam cecinit venustae*? They were mine. I have told my mother all about us, and she thinks Miss Lydia looks very favourably on me too. I have written a formal proposal letter, and my mother was all ready to call on her grandfather this very day to ask for his blessing, to bring me an answer that will make me the happiest or most miserable man alive. It slipped out in our family talk, I suppose – that comment about your courage. Heaven knows I never doubted it, not for a second! I think jealousy has been gnawing at my heart, that horrible suspicion that my future brother-in-law was winning too much of her favour, favour I wanted all for myself. Ah George, who knows all his own flaws? I am half distracted with passion. What right have you to laugh at me, anyway?”

George’s roar of laughter drew the General back into the room. “Have you two boys made your peace at last?” he asked.
“I was just remonstrating with Mr. Warrington for that rude, ill-timed laughter,” Jack huffed, red-faced.
“George is bound over to keep the peace for two years, Jack, you can’t fight him till that runs out,” the General chuckled. “Till then, let’s hope you’ll sort out your quarrels like gentlemen. Dinner’s on the table, lads – we’ll drink to absent friends, to an end of the war across the sea, and no fighting outside the army.”

George pleaded a prior engagement to slip away early from the table, and Jack was not far behind him. When George stopped by his own lodgings to scribble a sharp note to Miss Lydia calling out the slander about his duel, he turned up at the Bloomsbury Square house to find Jack already on the steps, deep in conversation with the footman there.
“Master and the whole household left town yesterday,” the servant told them.
“Poor Jack!” George whispered, when the footman had turned away. “You had that proposal letter in your pocket, didn’t you?”
Jack nodded, miserable. “My mother was in her sedan chair halfway here, I think, to call on her.” George had his own sharp note for Lydia tucked in his coat too, which he had not yet mentioned to Jack, and he had no intention of leaving it now the household was gone.
“Will they be back soon?” he asked the footman politely.
“They’re gone to stay with quality,” the man replied, handing over a scrap of paper with an address scrawled in Lydia’s neat hand: *The box from Madam Hocquet’s to go by the Farnham Flying Coach, addressed to Miss Van den Bosch, at the Right Honourable the Earl of Castlewood’s, Castlewood, Hants.*
“Where?” Jack gasped, aghast. “I thought she’d only visited Lady Castlewood once!”
“His lordship and the family have called here many times,” the footman said, preening. “We’re very intimate.”
“Miss Lydia took a power of boxes and gowns with her,” he added. “They’re bound to stay a good long while.”

The two young men walked away, each crumpling his unused letter in his pocket. “What was that exclamation you just made?” George asked.
“Distraction!” Jack groaned. “I am beside myself, George. I barely know what I’m saying. She’s gone to Hampshire, and Mr. Esmond is gone there with her!”
“Othello could not have spoken better,” George said. “And she has a very fine scoundrel for company down there.” Just then they spotted Mrs. Lambert’s sedan chair swinging down Great Russell Street, preceded by her footman.
“No use going further, Aunt Lambert!” George called out. “Our little bird has flown.”
“What little bird?”
“The bird Jack hoped to wed: our Lyddy bird. Fie, Jack! You swore this morning you wanted to break the Sixth Commandment, and now here you are swearing again!”
“Confound it, leave me alone, Mr. Warrington!” Jack snarled, striding far ahead out of reach of the chair’s bearers.
“What is the matter with him, George?” Mrs. Lambert asked, bewildered from inside the chair.
“Jack has no patience today, aunt,” George said. “He tells everyone I am a coward, then flies into a rage when I take offence. Then he finds his ladylove has vanished to the country, and he stamps and swears like a common trooper. O fie! Beware of jealousy, aunt – did the General ever make you jealous?”
“You will make me very angry if you speak to me in that tone,” Mrs. Lambert said, huffily. George bowed low. “I am respectfully dumb,” he said. “Good day.” He turned away towards Holborn, his soul burning with wrath. He was ready to quarrel with anyone that night. If he had run into cousin Will, he would have forgotten his peace bonds entirely. He hunted Will down at Arthur’s, then at his lodgings, and finally at the Castlewood London house, where the servants told him Mr. Esmond had already left to join the party in Hampshire.

George sat down and wrote two sharp, polite letters that night. The first to Will: *My dear kind cousin William, you know I am bound over to keep the peace, and would never quarrel with a dear kinsman – much less one my own brother once caned for his insolence. But if you ever meet anyone who claims I prevented our duel by laying an information to the constables, do tell your informant that it is not I, but he, who is the coward.* The second went to old Mr. Van den Bosch, warning him that a rogue was spreading slanderous lies about his character, and begging him to be wary of such company. He sent them off by post, then dressed for the theatre, enjoyed the play, and ate a hearty, cheerful supper at the Bedford tavern to settle his temper.

A few days later, a letter franked with the Castlewood seal sat on his breakfast table, written in the Earl of Castlewood’s own hand:
> Dear Cousin,
> There has been far too much strife in our family of late, and it is high time all quarrels ended. Two days ago my brother William brought me a very angry letter signed G. Warrington, and told me of the fracas between you two, where I must say your conduct was uncommonly violent. It is a poor use for good wine to fling it in an old man’s face, after all – you ought to have shown more respect to good Mr. Van den Bosch. I will not pretend Will’s behaviour is not often provoking; he has tried my own temper within these very 24 hours.
> Why should we not all live in peace and harmony? You know I have long held a sincere regard for you, and admire the excellent young lady you are engaged to with all my heart – give her my most cordial greetings. I hope soon to introduce a new Countess of Castlewood to the family. At my mature age, it is no uncommon thing for a man to take a young wife. My Lydia – you will guess by now I am happy to call the elegant Miss Van den Bosch my own – will naturally survive me. She will soothe my declining years, and I shall not grudge her choosing a happier, younger man to succeed me when I am gone, though I shall envy him the possession of so much beauty and perfection. Though she comes of an illustrious Dutch family, the dear girl says her rank is not equal to mine, and she is delighted to share the Castlewood name. I, in turn, shall be overjoyed to see heirs to my house, and to restore our estates to the splendour they enjoyed before two or three improvident predecessors ran them deep into debt. Lydia, who is sitting beside me now, sends you and all the Lambert family her warmest remembrances.
> The wedding will take place here at Castlewood very soon. May I hope to see you in the church? My brother William will not be there to quarrel with you. When we told him of the match yesterday, he flew into a terrible passion, uttered words he will surely regret one day, and has gone off to stay with neighbours in the village. The Dowager Lady Castlewood will keep the Kensington house, and we will set up our own household, where you will always be welcome, dear cousin.
> Your affectionate humble servant,

And sure enough, the November 1759 issue of the *London Magazine* carried the short announcement:
> Saturday, October 13th: Married, at his seat Castlewood in Hampshire, the Right Honourable Eugene, Earl of Castlewood, to the beautiful Miss Van den Bosch of Virginia. Fortune: £70,000.
