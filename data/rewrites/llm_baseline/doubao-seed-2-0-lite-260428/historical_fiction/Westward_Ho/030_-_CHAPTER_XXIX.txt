— Queen Elizabeth, 1569

Amyas Leigh had settled soft and quiet at his Northam home, and for the next full twelve months little happened worthy of marking down in these pages. Salvation Yeo had installed himself as unofficial majordomo, with no fixed duties save to trail at Amyas’s heels everywhere he went, lanky and gray as a wolfhound at his master’s flank, and spend every evening huddled by the fireside. The old sailor kept his Bible open on his knee, his calloused hands carving endless trinkets, some useful, some utterly frivolous, for every member of the household—most especially Ayacanora. Every week he would humbly press some tiny carved toy fit only for a small child on her, at which she would pout and stamp her foot, get a gentle rebuke from Mrs. Leigh, then accept the gift without a word and tuck it away in a chest she would never open again.

Her whole soul was bent on becoming a proper English maid. She shadowed Mrs. Leigh from dawn to dusk, pestering her to teach her the secrets of the still-room, the rules of the kitchen larder, and above all the art of cutting and stitching clothes, until at last she was sewing gowns and smocks for half the poor households across Northam. First she would be a good housewife, just like Mrs. Leigh; then a new, strange idea had dawned on her: the joy of giving to those who had nothing to give back in return. She had been raised to the boundless open-handed hospitality of the Amazonian wilds, but this quiet, unselfish charity was something new entirely.

She watched Mrs. Leigh spend every spare hour stitching for the village poor, trundling out to visit them in their drafty cottages. She saw Amyas, the day after public thanks were given in church for his safe return from the seas, hand out gold, sacks of meal, casks of ale to the folk of Northam, Appledore, and Bideford, and buy up small cottages to turn them into almshouses for worn-out old mariners who could no longer sail. They told her this was his thank-offering to God. She was puzzled at first; her own idea of a thank-offering, shared by the Indians and the Spaniards both, was ritual sacrifices and decking the Great Spirit’s temple with the skulls of captives. Even plain, church-going Amyas did not neglect that old instinct to give something back to the Church: soon the Northam vicar was wearing a splendid new surplice, and the altar was graced with a rich silver flagon and salver looted from the great Spanish galleon, plate that would be lost forever in the civil wars decades later. Ayacanora could understand that much, but the almsgiving stumped her until Mrs. Leigh explained, in her soft simple way, that whoever gave to the poor gave to the Great Spirit, for the Great Spirit dwelt in every one of them, and in Ayacanora too, if only she would be still and listen, instead of pouting and stamping and doing only as she pleased. The girl took the new truth in like a child, and worked her fingers raw sewing for every old widow in the parish, wandering the lanes at Mrs. Leigh’s side, lovely and much loved, darting quick glances from under her long black eyelashes to see if she had won a smile from Amyas. On the days she earned that smile she was good and gentle from dawn till dusk; on the days she did not, she was sullen and mischievous enough to try the patience of any soul less calm and chaste than Mrs. Leigh’s.

She would not be reined in when it came to the glory of her dress. Every Sunday she swept into church draped in plundered Spanish silks and Aztec featherwork, rustling and glowing so bright the good vicar was forced to gently remonstrate with Mrs. Leigh, for she distracted the eyes and thoughts of every soul in the congregation. When Ayacanora heard of his complaint, she tossed her head and said she was not thinking of them, so they need not think of her either. If their “Piache” (as she mistakenly called the vicar, meaning conjuror) wanted a present, he could have all her Mexican feather robes—she would not wear them, for they were wild Indian things and she was an English maid now, but they would suit him perfectly. She darted upstairs, dragged the whole bundle down, and insisted so stoutly on draping the flustered vicar in the feather mantles that he beat a hasty retreat. Still, he carried off the finest one of the lot, and stitched it cleverly into an altar cloth. For years afterwards, the communion table at Northam blazed with emerald, sky blue, and crimson that had once draped the shoulders of a dead Aztec prince.

So Ayacanora flaunted on, while Amyas watched her half amused, half simply proud of her rare beauty, glancing around at all the staring villagers as if to say, “See what a fine bird I have brought home from across the seas!”

She caused Mrs. Leigh no end of trouble with the local gentry. All the ladies of the shire came calling, of course, both to congratulate Mrs. Leigh and to get a peek at the famous “fair savage” brought home by the local hero. But Ayacanora snubbed them all so thoroughly, from the vicar’s wife all the way up to Lady Grenville herself, that few dared attempt a second visit. When Mrs. Leigh remonstrated with her, the girl burst into floods of tears, protesting “They only come to gawk at a poor wild Indian girl. I will not be made a show of. I was a queen once, and everyone obeyed me. But here everyone looks down on me.” Yet when Mrs. Leigh asked her if she would rather go back to the wild Amazon forests, the poor child clung to her like a toddler, begging not to be sent away. “I would sooner be a slave in your kitchen here than go back to those bad, cruel people.”

Month after month drifted by, full of silly small storms and sunlit interludes, but all the while Ayacanora dwelt under Mrs. Leigh’s soft, steady influence—no sudden storms, no fiery outbursts, only a perpetual genial calm that softened every soul who came within its reach. Her tantrums grew rarer and rarer, and Ayacanora grew more rational day by day, though never quite fully happy.

One by one, small hints slipped out that confirmed her true identity to Mrs. Leigh and Yeo. After she had grown used to the sight of stone houses, she let them know she had seen buildings like that long before. The red English cattle seemed familiar to her, too, but the sheep puzzled her for weeks until she announced they were absurdly tiny. “Ah, madam!” Yeo cried, latching onto that clue, “That’s because she’s used to those great camel-sheep the Peruvians call llamas!”

But the thing that delighted her more than anything else was horses. The very idea of a tame riding animal was a daily wonder to her, and she pestered Amyas for a mount until he finally caved and bought her a fine jennet, for fear she would steal one from a neighbor otherwise. She learned to ride so fast that in a fortnight she was galloping across the moors as expertly as any South American gaucho. That was when a long-buried memory woke at last.

One day the whole household rode over to Stow Hall at Lady Grenville’s invitation: Mrs. Leigh rode sedately on a pillion behind the groom, while Ayacanora cantered circles around the party like a hound let off its leash, trying to goad Amyas into racing her. That night, sharing a room with Mrs. Leigh, she woke shrieking, sobbing out a long dream where the “Old Ape of Panama,” the cruel stepfather she hated, had dragged her from her bed and set her on horseback, riding with her over high moors and Andes peaks down into a dark wood, where the horses and the Indian man vanished and she found herself a tiny lost savage child again. The memory was so vivid they could not persuade her it had not really happened, and Mrs. Leigh soon decided it was a true vision from God, revealing that Don Xararte, jealous that she was the child of the captured English privateer Oxenham, had abandoned her in the forests beyond Mount Cotopaxi to die. No one in the household doubted the truth of it.

Other small fragments of her past emerged slowly, all tied to some animal or sight of the natural world. The long years wandering the wild forests had wiped away all memory of her mother, any early lessons of love or morality. That missing piece finally clicked into place at the baptism of the Virginia Indian Wanchese, whom Sir Richard Grenville had brought back from his New World colony. The man was to be baptized at Bideford Church, given the Christian name Raleigh, with Sir Walter Raleigh and Sir Richard Grenville standing as his godfathers. All the town’s notables turned out to see the first “red man” ever baptized on British soil, the mayor and corporation parading in their full robes with maces aloft to honor the first fruits of the Gospel being brought to the West.

Mrs. Leigh went as a matter of course, and Ayacanora tagged along too, curious to see what they intended to do with the “Carib.” When they told her they were going to make him a Christian, she pouted and demanded to know why they had not done the same for her. They assured her she was surely already a Christian, baptized as an infant, but she was not so certain, and grumbled that the ugly foreign red man was getting a privilege she might not have.

The congregation gathered, the tall stately son of the forest stood at the font, and the service was halfway done when a heavy groan burst from the crowd. Ayacanora had fainted clean away, collapsing into Mrs. Leigh’s arms. They carried her out to a nearby house, and when she woke, she told them the strange story: as she stood there trying to remember if she had ever stood at a font like that, the church around her had grown huge, the priest’s vestments grew rich and glowing, the walls were lined with gilded paintings, and above the altar a jeweled statue of the Virgin Mary held a baby in her arms. Soft music rang in her ears, the air was thick with sweet incense like mist, and through it she saw dozens of other native Indians standing at the font, a noble lady holding her own small hand, and she was a tiny little girl again.

After hours of gentle questioning, Yeo thumped his fist on the table and declared she spoke the truth. “She was christened, madam, in the grand cathedral at Quito. I know that building well, too well—once I was forced to stand there three whole hours in a penitent’s San Benito robe, listening to a friar preach his Popish lies, not knowing if I’d be burned alive at the stake the next morning.”

So Ayacanora went home to Burrough, and Raleigh the Indian went to Sir Richard’s service. His baptism entry still survives, scrawled in wobbly hand on the old parchment Bideford parish register for 1587: *“Raleigh, a Winganditoian: March 26.”* A year and a month later, another entry appears, not in the baptism register: *“Rawly, a Winganditoian, April 1589.”* The free forest wanderer had pined to death, homesick for his deer hunts in the fragrant cedar woods and lazy paddles across lagoons dotted with white water lilies. He was buried in the narrow, crowded Bideford churchyard, hemmed in by dingy town houses whose tenants would never waste a single tear on his grave. The two old entries stand there still, a quiet, pathetic emblem of the sad fate awaiting the whole displaced red race, to whom civilization came too late to save them, but soon enough to hasten their decay.

Though Amyas kept his promise to his mother to stay home for twelve months, England did not rest. That spring, Sir Walter Raleigh sent a larger, new fleet of colonists to Virginia under the command of John White. Raleigh had written again and again to beg Amyas to take command of the expedition, and if he had done so, the history of what would become the United States might well have begun twenty years earlier than it did. But Amyas had given his solemn word to his grieving widowed mother to stay home with her for at least a year, and who could blame her for asking, or him for agreeing? Instead of sailing himself, Amyas sent five hundred pounds of his galleon prize money to fund the colony, money that never came back to him, and must still lie, at least in spirit, buried somewhere on the Virginia coast.

But soon a sharper trial came that tested Amyas’s promise, making him moody, peevish, and restless for the first time in his life, furious that other men were fighting Spaniards while he sat idle at home. His whole soul was filling with a slow, sullen, burning hatred for Don Guzman de Soto. His single, simple purpose was fraying, and a dark shadow settled over him day and night. No companion could feed that shadow more than Salvation Yeo, who grew more fiercely fanatical day by day, and found in Amyas a too willing listener. Mrs. Leigh was seriously angry, for perhaps the only time in her whole gentle life, when she walked in on the two of them calmly debating how they had committed a grievous sin by not killing every last Spanish prisoner they had rescued from the galleon.

It must be said, as all the plain facts of this history prove, that if the English held that bitter temper in those days, the Spaniards had given them no end of cause to feel so, and were then preparing to do far worse. The year 1588 was fast approaching, a year an astronomer in Konigsberg had predicted more than a century earlier would shake the world, and German chronologers had called the climacterical year of all human history. The prophecies proved true enough. That was the year that would decide once and for all the fate of every European nation, and of the whole vast American continent.

It was no wonder that, like in every great crisis of human history, a strange awed instinct spread across the land that the Day of the Lord was at hand, that a great war was waged in heaven between fiends of darkness and angels of light fighting for the souls of men. No conjunctions of planets were needed for wise men to see that the long, scattered, half-hidden duel between Spain and England was about to erupt into a final, deathly grapple. The war had so far been confined to the Netherlands, the West Indies, and the coasts of Africa, places where Spain had no rightful claim, or had forfeited it through cruelty. But in 1585, the Spaniards struck without warning, laying an embargo on every English merchant ship that sailed into their European ports, seizing them all to add to the build of a massive, world-ending armada. They claimed they were assembling the fleet to crush the rebellious Dutch, but Queen Elizabeth, her ministers, and at last the English people knew better: they were coming to crush England. Drake, Frobisher, and Carlisle swept the Spanish Main with fire and sword, cutting off their supply of gold from the Americas, while the crafty, honest Walsingham arranged a financial gambit that stopped the Venetian merchants from lending Spain the money they needed to build their fleet, buying the nation a year of reprieve.

Across Europe, the Jesuits preached openly to their followers of their true goal: to crush the heretic English, sink their sacrilegious ships blessed by the Pope’s own hand, and drag the excommunicate Queen Elizabeth in chains across the Alps to beg for mercy at the feet of the Pope, while placing their own chosen monarch on her throne. They called for a new crusade, promising all Catholic knights eternal glory if they struck down the heretic islanders. To their shock, almost all the English Catholic gentry refused to betray their country, stubbornly clinging to the strange unorthodox belief that no matter their faith, they were first and foremost Englishmen, and would not open their doors to a Spanish invasion that would seize their ancestral lands and treat them as cruelly as the conquistadors had treated the native caciques of Hispaniola.

With their promises of heavenly glory falling flat, the Jesuits turned to a far more earthly Mary: Mary Queen of Scots, who lived in English custody, plotting the murder of Queen Elizabeth and the overthrow of the realm with every spare penny of her French royal dowry. She was beautiful, she was famous for her misfortunes, and the Jesuits played her as their best possible card in their great game against England, until the card was worn thin and finally torn to pieces. The whole English nation, from the highest noble to the lowest ploughboy, rose up with one voice: after seventeen years of plots, treachery, and terror, this woman must die, or the whole commonwealth of England would perish. And the world knew which choice they made.
