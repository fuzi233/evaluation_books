As the silence within the house settled like a heavy mist, Margaret felt the weight of her family's grief still lingering, though the tumult of the previous days had begun to dissipate. Her father, worn by the events that had transpired, sat in his accustomed chair, lost in thought. In the kitchen, Dixon's whispered reprimands and directions to Mary Higgins carried on, a stark reminder that life continued despite the darkness that had recently descended upon them. Margaret had decided not to speak of the harrowing incident, deeming it futile to dwell on the past when it was already shrouded in an uneasy peace.

But her mind kept straying back to the uncertainty surrounding Frederick's safety. She worried that Leonards might somehow find a way to borrow sufficient funds to pursue him to London, and Margaret's anxiety threatened to overwhelm her. Yet, she pushed aside these thoughts, focusing instead on the likelihood of Leonards' plan failing and Frederick's timely departure from England.

"I suppose we'll receive word from Mr. Bell tomorrow," Margaret ventured, attempting to steer the conversation towards more reassuring topics.

"Yes," her father replied, his voice laced with a mix of resignation and expectation.

"If he can come, he'll likely join us tomorrow evening," Margaret added, her voice tinged with hope.

"If not, I'll ask Mr. Thornton to accompany me to the funeral," her father said, his eyes clouding with concern. "I couldn't possibly attend alone."

"Don't ask Mr. Thornton, Papa," Margaret implored, her words spilling forth impulsively. "Let me go with you instead."

"You? My dear, it's not suitable for women of our station to attend funerals," her father said gently but firmly.

Margaret's face flushed with indignation. "Women like us, Papa, are often unable to contain their emotions. We're expected to hide our feelings, even from ourselves. But poor women, those without our social standing, can weep openly without shame. I promise you, Papa, I'll be strong. Please, don't send a stranger to accompany you. Leave me be."

The next day brought a letter from Mr. Bell, explaining that his gout had prevented him from attending the funeral. His words were warm and heartfelt, expressing his deep regret and a promise to visit soon, once he had attended to matters at his Milton estate.
