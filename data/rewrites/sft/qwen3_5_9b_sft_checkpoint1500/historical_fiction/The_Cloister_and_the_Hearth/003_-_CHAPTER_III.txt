A grave, white-haired seneschal approached their table, bowing with courteous deference. "Is Gerard Eliassoen of your company?" he inquired softly. Upon receiving confirmation, the official announced, "The Princess Marie wishes to confer with you, young sir. I am to conduct you to her presence."

Instantly, every face within hearing snapped toward the speaker, etched with a mixture of sharp curiosity and jealous envy. To be summoned by a princess was a distinction that set one apart from the common herd.

Gerard rose immediately to obey.

"I wager we shall not see you again," Margaret remarked calmly, though a faint flush betrayed her composure.

"That you will," the scholar replied, leaning close to whisper, "This is my good princess; but you are my queen." Stepping back to address the company, he added, "Wait for me, I pray you. I shall return presently."

"Ay, ay!" Peter exclaimed, waking from his reverie simultaneously with the statement.

Once Gerard departed, the pair—clad in garments of simple homeliness, yet standing beside a man personally summoned by royalty—became the focal point ("the cynosure") of neighboring gazes. Observing this sudden attention, William Johnson stepped forward, feigning astonishment, and claimed kinship.

"And to think that I was standing at your backs, and you saw me not!" Johnson declared.

"Nay, cousin Johnson, I saw you long since," Margaret answered, her tone cool and distant.

"You saw me, and spoke not a word?"

"Cousin, it was for you to welcome us to Rotterdam, as it is for us to welcome you at Sevenbergen. Your servant denied us a seat in your house."

"The idiot!" Johnson muttered.

"And I had a mind to test if 'like maid, like master,'" Margaret continued, her wit cutting through the tension, "for there is often truth in bywords."

Johnson blushed a deep purple. He sensed the edge of Margaret's tongue and realized he was under scrutiny. Prudent in the face of suspicion, he chose action over explanation. He insisted that they accompany him home at once, vowing to prove whether they were truly welcome to Rotterdam or not.

"Who doubts it, cousin? Who doubts it?" the scholar interjected eagerly.

Margaret accepted his offer with gracious thanks, though she hesitated to depart immediately, citing a desire to listen to the minstrels once more. After a quarter-hour, Johnson pressed his proposal again, noting that many guests had already dispersed. Then, her genuine concern surfaced.

"It would be poor manners to abandon our friend now; he might lose us entirely. He knows not where we lodge in Rotterdam, and the city is vast. We have already partaken company once."

"Oh!" Johnson exclaimed, "We shall remedy that. My young man, ahem—I mean my secretary, shall remain seated here and await Gerard, leading him straight to my residence. He shall lodge with me and no other."

"Cousin, we shall be too burdensome."

"Nay, nay," Johnson dismissed her worry warmly. "You shall see whether you are welcome, you and your friends, and your friends' friends, if necessity demands. And I shall hear what the Princess requires of him."

A thrill of joy coursed through Margaret at the thought of Gerard lodging under the same roof as herself, yet a shadow of misgiving lingered. "But if your young man proves thoughtless, wanders off to play, and Gerard misses him?"

"He wander? Leave the spot where I placed him, and bid him stay? Ho! Stand forth, Hans Cloterman!"

A figure clad in black serge and dark violet hose materialized, stepping forward without a twitch or hesitation. He was a solemn, precise youth, the very embodiment of gravity and starched propriety. At his appearance, Margaret, overwhelmed by happiness, struggled to maintain her composure. Yet, she leaned toward Johnson and whispered, "I would wager my soul upon him. We are at your command, cousin, the moment you issue your orders."

Hans was instructed to take a seat at the table and wait for Gerard, guiding him to Ooster-Waagen Straet. He complied in silence, taking the indicated seat with mechanical exactitude. Margaret, Peter, and William Johnson then withdrew together.

"And, indeed, it is time you were abed, father, after all your travels," Margaret observed, a thought that had occupied her mind throughout.

Hans Cloterman sat, a statue of patience and business-like demeanor. Minutes ticked by, failing to stir any impatience in the perfect young man. Johnson, laughing to scorn the notion of his secretary abandoning his post for sport or youthful frivolity, proved justified.

As Gerard remained absent, the patient Hans, whose employer's gaze was no longer fixed upon him, amused the time by drinking goblets of Corsican wine. He consumed them solemnly, silently, and at intervals measured with rigorous accuracy. The wine was potent, and Cloterman's resolve was iron-clad. An hour had elapsed since Gerard's departure before the model secretary finally conceived the notion that Creation expected him to toast the health of all good fellows—and specifically the Duke of Burgundy, present in the hall. With this intention, he filled his cup nine times, rising gingerly, solemnly, and slowly. Reaching his full height, he instantly collapsed onto the grass, goblet in hand, spilling the chilled liquor over more than one ankle. His companions frisked, but Hans disturbed not a single muscle in his long, impassive face. Even in the total eclipse of reason, his countenance retained its gravity, primness, and infallible dignity.

Meanwhile, the seneschal led Gerard through winding passages until reaching the door of a pavilion. There, young noblemen, embroidered and feathered, sat sentinel around the heir-apparent, carding by the red glow of torches held by their attendants. A hush from the seneschal caused one of them to rise reluctantly, staring at Gerard with haughty surprise before entering the pavilion. He returned shortly thereafter, beckoning the pair, and guided them through additional corridors to an antechamber. Here sat three more young gentlemen, dressed in feathers and fur like works of fantasy, deeply engrossed in the instructive and edifying branch of learning known as dice.

"You cannot see the Princess," one declared, "it is too late."

Another agreed, "She passed this way but moments ago with her nurse. She is retired to bed, doll and all. Deuce—ace again!"

Prepared to withdraw, Gerard was met by the seneschal, who smiled with incredulity. "The young man is here by the Countess's orders; please conduct him to her ladies."

Upon this command, a superb Adonis rose with an injured expression and ushered Gerard into a chamber where eleven ladies sat or lounged, chatting like magpies. Two, more industrious than the rest, played cat's-cradle with fingers as nimble as their tongues. At the sight of a stranger, all tongues halted as if by some intricate machinery, and every eye swiveled toward Gerard. The young man, already uneasy, found his discomfort magnified by this battery of stares, causing his gaze to drop to the floor. Then, perceiving that they could frighten a creature, the timid ones giggled, reveling in their newfound prowess. A duenna then intervened severely, "Mesdames!" and instantly, abashed as if a string of modesty had been plucked, the chatter died. This same duenna took Gerard, marching before him in solemn silence. The young man's heart sank; he half considered turning and fleeing the place.

"What must princes be," he mused, "when their courtiers are so freezing? Doubtless they derive their breeding from him they serve."

These reflections were cut short when the duenna introduced him into a room where three ladies sat working, and a charming little girl tuned a lute. The ladies were richly, though not ostentatiously, attired. The duenza approached the one hemming a kerchief and spoke a few words in a low tone. That lady then turned toward Gerard with a smile, beckoning him closer. She did not rise, yet as she laid aside her work, her slight movement was suffused with grace, ease, and courtesy. Conversation flowed immediately.

"Margaret Van Eyck is an old friend of mine, sir, and I am right glad to receive a letter from her hand. I am also thankful to you, sir, for bringing it to me safely." Turning to the young lady, she added, "Marie, my love, this is the gentleman who delivered that pretty miniature."

"Sir, I thank you a thousand times," the young lady replied.

"I am pleased you consider yourself her debtor, sweetheart, for our friend requests a small service in return."

"I will do anything on earth for him," the young lady responded with ardor.

"Anything on earth amounts to nothing in the world," the Countess of Charolois corrected gently.

"Well, then, I will—" She paused, seeking direction. "What would you have me do, sir?"

Gerard, having just discerned the nature of high society, addressed her softly, a tremor in his voice. "My sovereign demoiselle, where no pain has been taken, no reward is needed."

"But we must obey mamma. All the world must obey."

"That is true. Then, our demoiselle, reward me, if you will, by allowing me to hear the stave you were singing before I interrupted it."
