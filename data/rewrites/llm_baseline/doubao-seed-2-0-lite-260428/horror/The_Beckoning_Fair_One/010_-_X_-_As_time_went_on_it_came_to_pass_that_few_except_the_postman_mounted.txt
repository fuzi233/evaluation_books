As the weeks bled one into the next, almost no one besides the postman bothered to climb Oleron’s creaking, dust-furred stairs. And since a man who answers no letters receives almost none in turn, even that heavy, familiar tread grew so rare that he might go four or five days at a stretch without hearing the thin thud of mail sliding through his door slot. First came a letter from his publishers, crisp and formal, asking after his long-overdue new manuscript. He left it face-down on the mantel for three days, then forgot it entirely. A second arrived, stained with rain, which he did not even break the seal on. No third ever came. The world outside had already half-forgotten he existed.

Spring bloomed bright and unforgiving. White privet foam spilled over the edges of the chopper-edged notice boards down the lane, and the flower women’s wicker baskets lined the kerbs, heavy with hyacinth and daffodil, their sweet heady scent drifting three flights up to Oleron’s windows. He bought armfuls every single day; the rooms seemed to hunger for fresh, continuously renewed blooms, and he did not stint their demands. Even so, the chore of stepping outside to buy them began to gnaw at him more and more, and every time he stumbled back over his own threshold, the relief was so sharp it made his knees go weak.

His senses had warped again, not sharpened back to their old normal, but stretched thin, stitched through with a new, unnameable terror: agoraphobia. The name barely mattered next to the raw, animal fear of open air, of empty space that could stretch on for miles, of the unseen thing that might pounce at his unguarded back when he rounded a street corner. Soon he arranged for a shop boy to leave bread, tea, and fresh blooms propped against his outer door at midday. He rubbed his hands giddily when the arrangement was settled. That was better. Now he never had to step out again if he didn’t want to.

He threw himself into the seclusion, locking the outer bolt twice, and for a while he thought he might be happy—though his happiness curdled into something soft and sick, a dull ache that made him weep weak, unprovoked tears for no reason he could name. He would not have traded that strange, thrumming sadness for all the loud, raucous mirth of the world outside, not for anything. Noise of any kind had become unbearable: cart rumbles on the cobblestones, distant children’s shouts, even the creak of a floorboard a room away cut into his skull like a shard of glass. He padded around the flat in thin slippers, closing every door so slowly the hinges never even whined, pressing a finger to his lips for long minutes at a time to soak in the perfect, humming stillness.

Sundays grew intolerable. The fine weather had drawn the noisy sect that long-nosed Barrett belonged to out into the square below his windows each Sunday morning, their drum thudding like a diseased second heartbeat, their brass instruments braying, men and women screaming raw, anguished prayers up at the sky. Barrett himself would stand below Oleron’s window, face tipped up, eyes squeezed shut, brow working, praying for the sound of his voice to pierce every unbeliever’s skull—including Oleron’s. One Sunday mid-rant, Oleron sprang to his window, hauled the crimson blind down hard, and heard, clear as day, Barrett call his name out mid-rhapsody, weaving Oleron’s supposed sin into his prayer for all the square to hear.

Sometimes, standing stock-still in the hush of his flat, Oleron would call out soft, not quite expecting a reply. Once he whispered “Romilly” and waited, breath held, but more often his murmurs were nameless, directed at the empty air around him.

He soon began haunting one spot more than any other: the threshold of his bedroom, just inside the open door. He had discovered that if he left every inner door unlatched (and the outer one bolted tight, unbudging), he could stand in that exact strategic nook and see into every one of his five rooms without shifting his feet an inch: the full sweep of his sitting room, all of his bedroom bar the small patch hidden behind the door, and narrow, peeking glimpses of the kitchen, the bathroom, and the little study he rarely used. He would linger there for ages, breath caught, finger pressed to his lip, watching the shadows drift. One day, the thought struck him: had dead Madley, the former tenant the vicar had warned him about, ever figured out this perfect vantage point too?

Light, he soon learned, troubled him far more than darkness. Direct sunlight seeping through the windows burned like a flame pressed to the back of his skull; even soft, diffused daylight left a dull, numbing ache in his bones. He made short, darting excursions around the flat hour by hour, yanking down each crimson blind one by one, sealing off the sun entirely until every window was shrouded. The whole flat soon drowned in a thick, blood-warm half-light, the pale pith-white walls stained red as if they oozed their own slow fluid, the air thick with the cloying rot of overripe blooms. He snickered to himself as he pulled the last blind down: that would baffle nosy Barrett, the fool, he couldn’t see a single thing that went on behind those crimson curtains. He laughed about it for an entire hour.

A few days later, a fright left him shaking so hard his teeth chattered for the same length of time. He had grabbed the cord to draw the blind over the window seat where he’d found the old harp bag, his back pressed safe to the window embrasure, when he saw the flash of a black-and-white check skirt vanish around the far edge of the house wall. He could not be sure—if he’d run to the other blinded wall, it would have been gone before he got there—but he was almost certain it was Elsie. He waited, frozen, ears ringing, for the sound of her tread on the stairs. Nothing came. After three long, agonized minutes, he let out a shaky breath. “God, that would have compromised me horribly,” he muttered over and over, half to himself, half to the quiet dark. No woman would stand for this red-lit, flower-choked lair, he thought. Any woman would drag him back out to the grey, boring world, and that would ruin everything.

Still he was not quite happy, nor quite unhappy. He was waiting for something to be granted to him, and nothing had been taken away yet, not a single thing. He paid no mind that his money was dwindling to almost nothing, no mind that strangers who’d known him once whispered he’d stopped writing work entirely. They knew nothing. He was just beginning to work now. He was gestating something far greater than any book he had ever penned: the Fair One, the Beckoning Spirit, weaving her out of the stillness, the flower scent, the red gloom of the flat. Lovely radiant creations did not pop into the world easy. Their creators had to weep wretched tears, chase their slippery, mocking, ever-eluding quarry until their lungs burned, and that was exactly what he was doing. Let no one say he had stopped working. He was far closer now than he had ever been.

All the rest of the world was falling away from him, and he let it go gladly. When your Fair One keeps waving you on, you drop every other trivial thing to follow. So he kept his post at the bedroom door frame, watching, waiting, smiling, finger pressed to his lip. This was his worship, his troth, all the love he had ever ached for. The hot flare of jealousy he felt for dead Madley, for the hours Madley had spent in this flat before him? That was devotion too, acceptable service to his coming Bride.

Then he made a discovery he should have seen weeks earlier, spurred on by that jealousy of Madley. Since the night he’d stupidly tried to play hard to get, using the Fair One’s jealousy to banish her, he had forgotten all about the fifteen discarded chapters of *Romilly*, the ones full of Elsie, the flesh-and-blood rival. How stupid he had been! Of course the Fair One would never show herself, not while the evidence of his divided allegiance was still hidden in the window seat! She had almost killed her rival twice, after all, the vicar had said. He owed her that sacrifice.

He hauled the manuscript out of the window seat, carried it to the fire that burned day and night in his sitting room, its smoke curling to bring out every last cloying note of the flowers lining every shelf. His clock had run down weeks earlier, a stupid little contraption that could not measure the colossal, world-altering thing about to happen to him, so he had no idea what the hour was, only that it was very late. He knelt by the hearth, fumbling to untie the string binding the chapters together, when a soft tap-tap-tap pricked the silence.

It was faint, three small raps, but it filled him with a cold, crawling alarm that made his blood run thick. Then Elsie’s voice drifted through the wood of the outer door, soft, trembling: “Paul! Paul, I know you’re in there. You’re in trouble—you’re in danger. At least come to the door.”

He cursed her under his breath, did not move an inch. He would not let her in. He snickered quiet, at the thought that she was standing out there talking about him being in danger, when she was the one trespassing on the threshold of a place no living human should dare enter. Her sobs seeped through the wood, soft and pitiful, begging that she had promised no such thing to stay away if he needed help. He ground his teeth, shook his fist at the closed door, and dropped the first page of *Romilly* into the flames. It curled black, crumbled to ash. He fed the rest in, sheet by sheet, while she called his name, over and over. Then the voice cut off. He heard her slow, dragging footsteps descend the stairs. He waited for the sound of her tripping, of a cry, of the handrail splintering as she fell, but nothing came. She was spared, let crawl away unharmed. The Fair One had shown her mercy.

Oleron dropped the last charred scrap of paper into the embers, stood up with a low, giddy laugh. “Lucky to get away like that,” he murmured to the empty rooms. “If I’d so much as cracked the door an inch, she never would have left this building alive.”

Now his reward was here. He crossed to his post at the bedroom threshold, eyes shining bright in the red dimness. The whole flat was full of juggled, deceptive shadows: the faint streetlamp glow seeping through the nearest blind, the pale blue glimmer of moonlight leaking down the hall from the kitchen, the sullen smoulder of the fire under the ash, the soft pale glimmer of narcissi and tulips in their bowls. He would know her, his Fair One, no matter how the dark tricked him. He hung his head for a second, apologetic, for delaying their shadowy wedding for so long. He did not even dare try to name her, for fear of tainting her with the name of the discarded rival Elsie.

A wisp of shadow, light as lamb’s wool, bloomed in the kitchen. The streetlamp glow by his elbow dimmed all at once, as if some invisible hand had pinched its flame to nothing. The fire settled in the hearth, puffing out a cloud of fine black ash. A heavy daffodil head snapped off its stem, fell to the carpet with a soft thud. A cold draught slipped through the old house, brushing Oleron’s cheek, scented with nothing he had ever smelled before, not flowers, not rain, not any thing that belonged to the living world.

Oleron trembled so hard his whole body shook. The draught had nudged the half-open bedroom door, making it creak soft on its hinges. He reached out, his fingers ice-cold, and pulled the door all the way shut, slow, so no sound broke the stillness. He sank down onto the nearest chair, breath caught in his throat, waiting. He was a man waiting for his name to be called, the summons that would admit him to a weighty, secret, unholy audience he had spent months pining for.
