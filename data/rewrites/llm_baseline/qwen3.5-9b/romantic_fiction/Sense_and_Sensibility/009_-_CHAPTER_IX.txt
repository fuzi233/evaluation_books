The Dashwood sisters had finally found their footing at Barton Cottage, settling into a life of tolerable comfort. The house, its gardens, and the rolling landscape surrounding them had ceased to be strangers; instead, they had become familiar friends. The simple pleasures that once held Norland Manor dear were now pursued with a joy far deeper than the estate itself could ever provide during the shadow of their father's absence.

Sir John Middleton, accustomed to a quiet domestic routine, visited daily for the first two weeks. He could not hide his astonishment at discovering the Dashwoods perpetually occupied. While he urged them repeatedly to mingle more freely with the neighborhood, promising his carriage was always at their disposal, Mrs. Dashwood's spirited independence proved insurmountable. She resolved firmly to decline visits to any household beyond walking distance. Such families were scarce, and even fewer were accessible.

Yet, nature offered ample distraction. About a mile and a half from the cottage, nestled in the narrow, winding valley of Allenham, lay an ancient, dignified mansion. Its resemblance to Norland sparked the girls' imaginations, fueling a desire to learn more about its inhabitants. Inquiry revealed that the owner, an elderly lady of impeccable character, was too frail to venture outdoors, confined entirely to her home.

Surrounding them, the countryside teemed with breathtaking paths. From nearly every window of the cottage, the high downs beckoned, offering exquisite breezes that served as a perfect refuge when the lower valleys became muddy and gloomy. It was toward one of these sun-drenched heights that Marianne and Margaret ventured one memorable morning. Drawn by patches of sunlight breaking through a cloudy sky, they sought relief from the oppressive rain that had persisted for two days.

Elinor and their mother remained indoors, engrossed in books and drawing, despite Marianne's confident prediction that the clouds would vanish. The two sisters ascended the downs with buoyant spirits, celebrating every glimpse of blue sky. When the invigorating gusts of a southerly wind brushed against their faces, they felt a pang of pity for those kept inside.

"Is there any happiness in the world," Marianne declared, "greater than this?"

"Then let us stay here for at least two hours," Margaret agreed.

They pressed onward against the wind, laughing with delight for another twenty minutes before the heavens turned. Clouds merged overhead, unleashing a driving rain that drenched them completely. Chagrined yet resigned, they turned back, knowing no shelter awaited until they returned home. However, urgency transformed their retreat into a race. With the steep slope leading straight to the garden gate, they sprinted downward.

Marianne initially gained the lead, but a misstep sent her crashing to the earth. Margaret, unable to halt her momentum to help, was swept forward, reaching the bottom safely. Just as Marianne fell, a gentleman carrying a gun passed nearby, accompanied by two pointing dogs. Hearing the commotion, he dropped his weapon and rushed to her aid.

Marianne had managed to raise herself, but her foot was twisted, leaving her barely able to stand. The gentleman, recognizing her distress, offered his help. Seeing her modesty hesitate where necessity demanded action, he lifted her effortlessly into his arms and carried her down the hill. Passing through the open garden gate—which Margaret had left ajar—he bore her directly into the house, where she was just arriving. He did not release her until he had seated her comfortably in a parlor chair.

Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor rose in stunned silence, their gazes fixed upon the stranger with a mixture of wonder and secret admiration. His appearance was undeniably striking. He explained his arrival with such frankness and grace that his inherent handsomeness seemed enhanced by his voice and demeanor. Even if he had been older, uglier, or coarser, Mrs. Dashwood's gratitude would have been secure; yet, the combination of youth, beauty, and elegance added a profound resonance to his gesture.

She thanked him profusely and, with her characteristic sweetness, invited him to sit. He politely declined, citing his wet and disheveled state. Curiosity piqued, Mrs. Dashwood asked for his name.

"I am Willoughby," he replied smoothly, noting that he resided at Allenham and hoped to pay his respects the following day. The invitation was gladly accepted, and he departed into the pouring rain, leaving behind a trail of fascination.

His masculine beauty and exceptional grace immediately became the subject of universal acclaim. The laughter elicited by his gallantry toward Marianne rang out with renewed vigor due to his physical allure. Though Marianne had seen less of him than the others—her face flushed crimson when he lifted her, robbing her of the chance to study his features fully—she joined the chorus of admiration with characteristic enthusiasm. In her mind, his figure matched the heroes of her favorite tales. The lack of formal ceremony in carrying her indoors impressed her greatly; every detail about him seemed intriguing. His name was agreeable, his location favored, and she quickly concluded that nothing suited a man's attire better than a shooting jacket. Pain from her sprained ankle faded under the weight of her delighted imagination.

When Sir John visited later that afternoon, eager to hear of the incident, he was promptly asked if he knew a Mr. Willoughby at Allenham.

"Willoughby!" Sir John exclaimed. "Is *he* in the country? Excellent news! I shall ride over tomorrow and invite him to dine on Thursday."

"You know him, then?" Mrs. Dashwood asked.

"Know him? Of course! He comes down here annually."

"And what sort of a young man is he?"

"As good a fellow as ever lived," Sir John assured them. "A decent shot, and perhaps the boldest rider in England."

"That is all you can say?" Marianne interjected indignantly. "What of his manners? His talents? His genius?"

Sir John looked slightly perplexed. "Upon my soul, I know little of those things. But he is pleasant, good-humored, and possesses the finest black pointer I've ever seen. Did he bring her out today?"

Marianne could not answer regarding the dog's color any more than Sir John could describe Willoughby's inner qualities.

"But who is he?" Elinor queried. "Where does he come from? Does he live at Allenham?"

On this point, Sir John was more informed. Mr. Willoughby owned no local property; he stayed only to visit the elderly lady at Allenham Court, to whom he was related and from whom he expected to inherit.

"Yes, yes, he is certainly worth pursuing," Sir John added cheerfully. "Besides, he owns a small estate in Somersetshire. If I were you, I wouldn't give him up to my younger sister, regardless of this hill-climbing business. Miss Marianne cannot expect to monopolize all the eligible men. Brandon will be jealous if she isn't careful."

"I doubt," Mrs. Dashwood smiled gently, "that Mr. Willoughby will be inconvenienced by either of my daughters attempting to 'catch' him. It is not a pursuit they were raised for. Men are quite safe with us, whatever their wealth. Still, hearing he is a respectable young man makes me glad our acquaintance is inevitable."

"He is as good a sort of fellow as ever lived," Sir John reiterated. "Last Christmas, at a small dance at the park, he danced from eight until four without sitting down."

"Indeed?" Marianne cried, her eyes sparkling. "With elegance? With spirit?"

"Yes; and he was back on horseback by eight to hunt."

"That is precisely what I admire," Marianne declared. "Whatever his pursuits, his eagerness should know no bounds, leaving him no sense of fatigue."

"Aye, aye, I see exactly how this will go," Sir John chuckled. "You'll be setting your cap at him now, and never thinking of poor Brandon."

"That is an expression, Sir John," Marianne retorted warmly, "which I particularly detest. I abhor every cliché used to feign wit; phrases like 'setting one's cap at a man' or 'making a conquest' are the most odious. Their tendency is crude and unrefined. Were they ever clever, time has long since eroded their ingenuity."

Sir John struggled to grasp the rebuke but laughed heartily anyway. "Well, you'll make plenty of conquests, one way or another. Poor Brandon! He is already smitten, and he is very well worth your attention, despite all this tumbling and spraining."
