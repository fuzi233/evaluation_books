"Here we go up, up, up; And here we go down, down, down!" sang the nursery rhyme, a stark contrast to the grim reality unfolding in Milton. While the song spoke of whimsical motion, the town's chimneys belched thick smoke, and the ceaseless roar, mighty beat, and dazzling whir of machinery fought a perpetual, mechanical battle against the air. Wood, iron, and steam labored endlessly, seemingly without sense or purpose, yet their monotonous persistence was rivaled only by the tireless endurance of the crowds below. These were not aimless throngs; driven by urgent intent, they moved with restless energy, searching desperately for answers to a single, terrifying question: *What?*

The streets of Milton held no loiterers, no strollers seeking mere pleasure. Every face was etched with lines of eagerness or deep anxiety. News was hunted with fierce avidity in the Mart and the Exchange, where men shoved one another aside with the same ruthless self-interest they displayed in life itself. A heavy gloom settled over the town. Buyers were scarce, and those who ventured to purchase were viewed with suspicion by the sellers. Credit had evaporated; even the most financially stable families faced ruin if the great neighboring port suffered further losses among its shipping houses.

For years, Milton had known no failures, but recent revelations of disastrous American speculations had shattered that illusion. Whispers circulated that prominent local businesses were doomed. Daily, men's expressions screamed what their tongues dared not speak: *"Who has fallen? How does this affect me?"* When small groups gathered, they cautiously listed the names of the survivors, terrified to hint at those likely to fail. Idle gossip could drag a solvent man down; one collapse often pulled many others into the abyss.

*"Thornton is safe,"* the crowd murmured. *"His business is vast, expanding yearly. With a head like his and such prudence mixed with daring, he cannot fail."*

Yet, behind this public reassurance lay a darker truth. One man would draw another aside, leaning close to whisper, *"Thornton's business is large, true, but he has poured all his profits back into expansion. He holds no reserve capital. His machinery is brand new, costing him a fortune we dare not mention to the uninitiated."*

Mr. Harrison, a critic who inherited his wealth and feared change, grumbled constantly, begrudging every penny earned by bolder competitors. But the truth was, Mr. Thornton was under severe pressure. He felt it keenly in his most vulnerable spot: his pride. As the architect of his own fortune, he believed his success stemmed not from innate genius, but from the power commerce granted to every brave, honest, and persevering man—the ability to rise above the fray and command influence. He dreamed of being a merchant prince, known from the East to the West, whose word passed like gold. This was the ideal instilled in him by his mother, who had read scripture aloud as a call to arms.

He had lived for distant glory, neglecting the immediate world, until the accidental friendship with Higgins bridged the gap between master and man. That connection revealed a shared humanity, a "fine point of the wedge" that allowed them to view each other's positions with newfound charity. However, the current economic crisis threatened to sever this bond. The fear of losing the trust of the very workers he had begun to respect added a painful sting to his existing troubles.

Reflecting on the strike of a year and a half prior, Thornton realized how much he had changed. Once young and impulsive, he now bore the weight of age and regret. That strike had stalled his orders, forcing him to lock up capital in expensive machinery and purchase vast quantities of cotton. Much of the work produced by his newly imported Irish hands was defective, damaging the reputation of a firm that prided itself on excellence. The resulting financial strain had been immense. Often, catching sight of Higgins, Thornton felt a surge of angry resentment, blaming the worker for the delays. Yet, he forced himself to curb this impulse. He resolved not merely to avoid conflict, but to demonstrate his mastery over his temper by granting Higgins access whenever business permitted. Slowly, the resentment faded, replaced by wonder at how two men bound by the same trade could perceive their duties so differently.

Now, however, the tide had turned violently. Bad trade had crashed the market, halving the value of Thornton's stock. Orders dried up, leaving his invested capital useless. Expenses continued to bleed his resources, and when bills for cotton came due, he could only borrow at exorbitant rates. Despair did not visit him; instead, he worked day and night, calculating every emergency. At home, he remained calm and gentle toward his wife and children. In the mill, his few words carried a weight of sympathy rather than the harsh judgment of the past.

One afternoon, Higgins observed his master's distress. Hearing a suppressed sigh as Thornton walked past a workshop, Higgins later mused, *"I reckon I know who'd be sorry to see our measter sitting so stiff as a piece of grey calico! The old parson would've fretted his wife to death if he'd seen the woeful look on our measter's face."* That evening, Higgins and a colleague stayed late, unseen, to finish a neglected task, ensuring the overseer credited the work to himself.

Later, on Marlborough Street, Higgins intercepted Thornton.
*"Measter,"* he called out, startling the hurried businessman. *"Have ye heard aught of Miss Marget lately?"*

Thornton paused, confused. *"Miss—who?"*

*"Miss Marget—Miss Hale, the old parson's daughter. Ye know who I mean, if ye'll just think a bit."* His tone was respectful, devoid of disrespect.

Suddenly, the winter frost that had frozen Thornton's features melted away, replaced by a soft, benevolent smile. *"Oh, yes! She is my landlord now. I hear through her agent occasionally. She is well and among friends. Thank you, Higgins."*

The lingering warmth in that "thank you" signaled something deeper to the astute Higgins. *"And she hasn't married, measter?"*

*"Not yet."* The cloud returned to Thornton's face. *"There is talk of a match with a family connection."*

Higgins stepped closer, lowering his voice conspiratorially. *"Is the young gentleman cleared? The one they called Master Frederick, her brother who was over here?"*

Thornton froze. *"Her brother?"*

*"Sure enough. I reckoned ye knew, or I wouldn't have mentioned it. We knew all along, Mary and me, but we held our peace."*

Thornton recovered quickly, breaking away to resume his business. *"It was her brother,"* he muttered to himself as he walked away, a strange mixture of relief and sorrow in his heart. *"I am glad. I may never see her again, but knowing she is safe... it is a comfort."*

This tiny thread of golden hope ran through the darkening web of his fortunes. His agent had heavily invested in a failing American house, leading to a cascade of collapses. Night after night, Thornton sat in his private study, poring over accounts long after his family slept.

One morning, exhausted and unable to sleep, he was interrupted by his mother, who stood in the doorway, dressed as she had been the previous day. They had neither rested nor slept properly. Their faces were pale and rigid from vigilance.

*"Son John,"* she said softly, *"can I sleep with an easy mind while you stay awake in care? You haven't told me your trouble, but you have had sore trouble for many days."*

*"Trade is bad,"* he replied, lifting his chin defiantly. *"I dread nothing. I know no man suffers because of me."*

*"But how do you stand? Will it be a failure?"* Her voice trembled.

*"And if it fails?"* She stepped forward, taking his arm, her eyes bright with urgency.

*"Honest men are ruined by rogues,"* he said gloomily. *"My creditors' money is safe—every farthing. But I don't know where my own is; it may be gone, and I penniless. Therefore, it is their money I should risk."*

*"No; but I would risk ruining many for my own paltry aggrandizement. Mother, I have decided! You won't grieve over us leaving this house, will you?"*

*"Remain the same John Thornton, making mistakes and trying to be brave in starting anew. But it is hard, mother. I have worked and planned, discovering new powers only to realize it is too late. All is over. I am too old to begin again with the same heart."*

Turning away, he covered his face with his hands
