“Let me hear about those children, child — the little lasses I saw running about the house where they took you in, poor dear boy, after your dreadful fall?” Lady Maria’s voice softened as they paced the sun-dappled common at Tunbridge Wells, her gloved hand tucked tight through Harry Warrington’s arm. “Oh, that fall, Harry! I thought I should have died on the spot when I saw you pitch from that rearing horse! You needn’t squeeze my arm quite so hard, you know full well you do not care for me half so much as you pretend.”

“The Lamberts are the best, kindest, dearest souls I have ever met in all the world,” Harry cried warmly, his face lighting up at the name. He patted the pocket of his riding coat, stuffed plump and crisp with the banknotes he had just won off March and Morris in their wagers over jumps and weights. “Mrs. Lambert was my own mother’s closest friend when she was sent to Europe for her education, decades ago. Colonel Lambert is the most accomplished gentleman alive, has seen service in Scotland with the Duke, in Flanders, and out at Minorca. No own father and mother could have nursed me more tenderly through my fever and my broken bones than they did. I owe them a debt I can never repay, and I swear I shall make them a gift fit to show it.”

“Pish, you would offend them to offer them payment for tending a wounded friend,” Maria said, a little smile tugging at her painted lips. “We can stop at the toy-shop on the high street later, my dear, and buy a pair of fine new dolls for the little girls.”

“Dolls for Hester and Theo?” Harry laughed loud, his arm jostling hers so hard she gave a faint, nearly imperceptible wince. “Do you think a woman remains a child until she turns forty, cousin? I can tell you Miss Hester does not for one moment consider herself a little girl, and Theo is a full year older than her sister. They speak French, Italian, and Spanish between them, they have read shelves full of history and poetry, they play the harpsichord so fine you could listen for hours, and Theo composes her own songs to sing together. They are no mere children.”

“Indeed? I scarcely spoke to them at all, I thought they looked quite youthful. I had no idea they were such wonders of the world,” Maria sighed, a faint trace of jealousy in her tone. “I am sure I would never find fault with anyone who was kind to you, Mr. Warrington — though you must not be angry if I own I envy them, getting to sit by your bed and nurse you while you were ill, when I — I had to leave you to run errands for my aunt.”

“You dear, good Maria!” Harry said, pressing her hand to his arm.

“No, I am not dear or good,” she protested, drawing back a little. “See there! Your black servant is walking yonder with a whole crowd of other livery wretches, heading for the tea-gardens to fuddle and get as tipsy as their masters. That dreadful Mr. Morris was perfectly drunk when I came to fetch you after your accident, and he frightened you half out of your wits.”

“I had just won three hundred guineas off him and Lord March that very morning,” Harry boasted, his eyes sparkling. “What shall I buy for you, my dear cousin? I swore I would beat them fair at jumping for the honour of Virginia, and I did it. With a week’s practice more I do believe I could leap two full feet farther.”

Maria was charmed by the triumph of her young champion, though she shook her head in warning. “You must beware of play, child. You know it has been the ruin of all our family. My brother Castlewood, Will, our poor father, even my own aunt Lady Castlewood herself — every last one of them fell prey to it. And as for my Lord March, he is the most ruthless, successful gamester in the whole of the British peerage.”

“I am not afraid of him, nor of Jack Morris either,” Harry said, fingering the crisp notes in his pocket again. “What games do they play at Aunt Bernstein’s? Cribbage, all-fours, brag, whist, commerce, piquet, quadrille? I am ready for any of them. Hark — the church bells are striking seven already!”

“And you are eager to run off to the cards at once,” Maria said, her voice turning plaintive. “You do not care to walk with your poor cousin any more. Not long ago, you would have chosen this stroll over any game.”

“Hey, youth is youth, cousin!” Harry laughed, tossing his dark head. “A young fellow must have his fling!” He strutted beside her for a few paces, glowing with confidence, health, and eagerness for every pleasure the evening promised. Only a day prior, he had been perfectly happy to sit and chat with Theo and Hester Lambert and their good mother over tea and cake, but the glitter of life, the temptation to shine and conquer, had wrapped itself around the lad. Like every young man before him, he seized the brimming cup of pleasure without a single thought of the throbbing headache he would wake to find the next morning.

As they talked, the fiddlers on the Parade’s open orchestra struck up a raucous squeak and tune as they tuned their strings for the evening’s public concert. Maria knew her aunt the Baroness was awake and settled for the night, and that her own duties as hostess called her back to the house. Harry never spared a thought for the quiet drudgery of Maria’s life, tending the whims of the sharp-tongued old Baroness day in and day out. He never pitied her. He was not thinking of her at all. Though when his riding accident had befallen him, she had suffered a far sharper pain than any he had borne, and he had scarcely ever been absent from her thoughts, which she had long learned to bury beneath smiling, dutiful hypocrisy, as all women of her station learn to do. I know, my very proper Mrs. Grundy, you are ready to call her a foolish old maid for pining after a boy half her age. But do you suppose fools’ caps only fit heads crowned with jet-black or auburn curls? Be gentle with us elderly sinners, you paragon of good sense! It is no less true that late crops of wild oats sprout thick and tall on grey stalks, than that early harvests ripen in the sun of youth — and from long observation of myself and my neighbours, I can swear that wild oats grow right up to the very last days of the year.

Madame Bernstein and her household had drawn all the heavy curtains across the windows while the sun was still high and light, as if determined to tuck the daylight into bed early so they might give their full attention to their cards, their wax candles, their tea and spiced negus and sugary refreshments. Chair after chair pulled up to the Baroness’s door, depositing ladies more or less thickly painted, patched, and draped in rich brocade. The gentlemen soon followed, in their finest evening raiment: Mr. Poellnitz wore the largest star and the most heavily embroidered coat of any man in the room. When Lord March and Ruglen arrived, he bore no resemblance at all to the rough jockey Harry had befriended at the White Horse tavern. His tight brown scratch wig was gone, replaced by a neatly curled feather-top bag-wig dusted with grey powder, his leather jockey breeches swapped out for an elegant sky-blue French suit stitched with silver thread. Jack Morris wore an almost identical wig and near-matching suit, for the pair styled themselves inseparable. Young Mr. Wolfe arrived in attendance on the celebrated beauty Miss Lowther, and her aunt, who was as devoted to cards as every other soul of quality in Tunbridge Wells that season.

When Lady Maria Esmond made her appearance, it was plain Madame Bernstein’s grousing about her faded looks had been nothing but spite. Her figure was remarkably fine, and her gown was cut to show it off to full advantage. Her complexion was naturally very fair, set off by a dark frilled silk ribbon clasped with a large ruby that made the skin of her neck look like sculpted snow. Her cheeks were no brighter rose than every other lady in the room, who all openly bought their rouge from the perfumer’s shops on the high street. A pair of artfully placed patches only added to the glow of her charms. Her hoop skirt was no wider than the absurd iron crinolines ladies wear in our own day, and while the whole costume seems absurd to modern eyes, it was altogether pleasing to all who beheld it. Suppose our modern ladies took to wearing bangles and nose rings? I have no doubt we would laugh at the novelty, but not dislike the sight of them, and no lover would hesitate to lift that little ring aside to kiss the rosy lips beneath.

As for the Baroness de Bernstein, when she took the trouble to dress for a grand evening, she was still a magnificent, even terrifying sight to behold — handsome still, but heavy with melancholy, every line on her old face bearing the story of a hundred old passions long dead. Some old faces soften into gentle meekness in their final years, but not hers. Many eyes go completely dull in old age, the crow’s feet crinkling around irises that once flashed with scorn, anger, or love, but hers still held a faint flicker of the old fire. My fair young reader, if you are not the peerless Lindamira, queen of every ball, and at the end of the night you return to bed and meekly own you only had two or three partners while Lindamira had a whole crowd at her heels, console yourself: be grateful that when you turn fifty, you will still look as kind and pleasant as you do at eighteen. You will never have to step down from the six-horse coach of your own beauty and watch a younger woman climb into it while you trudge on foot. You will never have to forgo the homage you have always known, never watch your admirers drift away, never find your once brilliant saloons coated in dust and cobwebs, with the windows placarded “To Let” as you sit lonely in the dark. You may never have known grandeur, but you will never know the bitter sting of being abandoned. You may never have had a fortune of millions, but you will never suffer the humiliation of bankruptcy.

“Our hostess,” Lord Chesterfield muttered in a confidential whisper to his friend, unaware how loud his voice carried across the room, “puts me in mind of Covent Garden as I knew it in my youth. Back then it was the very heart of the fashionable west end, home to all the great families. Now? Every nobleman’s old house is a gaming den, and you can walk in with a friend and order a bottle of wine for sixpence.”

“Hey, a bottle and a tavern are very fine things in their way,” Lord March shrugged. “I was not born before the Georges took the throne, though I mean to live to a hundred. I have never known the Bernstein as anything but an old woman, and hang me if I can guess where she spent all her supposed beauty.”

“No, hang me too, how did she spend it?” Jack Morris cackled. “Here is an empty table! Shall we sit down for a game? Keep that tiresome Prussian Poellnitz away, he never pays his debts. Mr. Warrington, will you take a seat for a hand?”

Harry and Lord Chesterfield found themselves partnered against Morris and Lord March. “You are too late to join us, Baron,” Chesterfield said as the elderly Prussian nobleman wandered towards them. “We have already sorted our game. What, have you forgotten Mr. Warrington of Virginia? The young gentleman you met in London last spring?”

“The young man I met at Arthur’s Chocolate House had dark hair, a little crooked nose, and was nowhere near as fine in appearance as this gentleman,” the Baron said with great presence of mind, to avoid reminding anyone of the petty altercation Harry had got into at the club months prior. “Warrington, Dorrington, Harrington? We continentals can never keep track of all your English names. I certify this gentleman is not the man I spoke of at dinner.” He gave Harry a friendly little nod, then sidled off to the far end of the room where Mr. Wolfe and Miss Lowther were deep in quiet conversation in a window embrasure. He proceeded to bore the Lieutenant-Colonel for twenty minutes with his opinions on the new Prussian drill manual that had just been introduced into King George II’s army — a subject Wolfe knew perfectly, but could not bring himself to care about in that moment, when he was far more interested in the beautiful young lady at his side.

At the start of the evening the Baroness greeted every guest personally, making small talk and performing the introductory courtesies. But as the rooms filled and every gaming table was occupied, she grew more and more restless, until she finally retreated with three of her oldest, closest friends to her own reserved corner table, where her major-domo was already waiting with a stack of cards and a full tray of negus. She sat down there resolutely, and never moved from her place or quit her game until the first cock crowed at dawn. The duty of tending to the new arrivals fell to Lady Maria, who did not care at all for cards, but dutifully performed the honours of the house, rustling past the whist table where her young cousin sat playing, over and over again, her eyes fixed wistfully on his face.

“Come over and cut the cards for us, Lady Maria,” Lord March called out to her as she drifted past the table. “Cut the pack and bring us luck! We have had none at all tonight, and Mr. Warrington is winning every last penny off us.”

“Harry, you are not playing for very high stakes, are you?” Maria asked timidly, resting her hand lightly on the back of his chair.

“Oh no, only sixpences a hand,” Lord March laughed, as he dealt the cards out.

“Only sixpences,” Jack Morris echoed, though his round, red face was so glum it was clear he had lost far more than a handful of pennies that night. Lord Chesterfield sat opposite Harry, sorting his cards with his usual calm, impassive face, and no man on earth could tell if the luck was running for or against him. A tiny annoyed curse slipped from Morris’s lips, and his partner snapped, “Hang it, Morris, play your cards and hold your tongue!” It was very strange that two grown men should be so agitated over wagers of only sixpence.

Maria, lingering behind Harry’s chair, could see his cards perfectly, and spotted a whole flock of trumps ranged neatly in his hand. She had not taken away his luck. She was glowingly pleased that the pack she had just cut had dealt him that perfect hand. As Lord March began to deal the next round, he said quietly to Harry, “The same bet as before, Mr. Warrington? Or shall we double it?”

“Anything you like, my lord,” Harry said, perfectly cool.

“Then we will say shillings a hand,” March said.

“Shillings it is,” Harry agreed, and the game went on.

The end of that night’s sport, and the run of luck that followed for days afterwards, can be fully gathered from the following letter, which was never delivered to its intended addressee, but later found its way across the Atlantic in the personal papers of Mr. Henry Warrington:

>
> Since all the joys your life at White’s amounts to are two bottles of Burgundy and a pack of cards, I take for granted you are roosting in London at this very moment, preferring smoke and faro to fresh air and haystacks out in the country. This note will be handed to you by the young gentleman I speak of below, and you will be charmed to make his acquaintance. He will play you at any game for any stake, at any hour of the night, and drink any reasonable number of bottles during the play. Mr. Warrington is no other than the Fortunate Youth all the newspapers and coffee-house gossips have been gushing about these past weeks. He has an estate in Virginia as big as Yorkshire, encumbered only by a mother who rules the roost there like a reigning sovereign. As the place is notoriously unhealthy, full of malarial fevers, let us hope Mrs. Esmond will take herself off soon, and leave this virtuous lad in undisturbed possession of his fortune. She is aunt to that scoundrel Castlewood, who never pays his play debts, unless he has behaved more honourably to you than he has to me. Mr. W. comes of very good stock, and we must make him a member of our society — if for no other reason than that I might win my own money back from him.
>
> He has had the very devil’s own luck here, and won everything he touched, even while that old card-playing beldam his great-aunt Bernstein lost every night at her own table. A few nights ago, when I first had the misfortune to make his acquaintance, he beat me fair and square at the high jump (he learned the trick out in the woods leaping away from bears, no doubt), won wagers off me and Jack Morris over my own body weight, then sat down to cards with us that very night and cleaned every one of us out. If you would kindly settle our last Epsom race accounts, please hand over 350 pounds to Mr. Warrington, which I still owe him after pretty well emptying my entire pocket-book. Lord Chesterfield has dropped six hundred pounds to him too, but his lordship does not wish word of it to get out, as he has sworn publicly to give up play and live cleanly from now on. Jack Morris, who has not lost quite so heavily as the two of us and can afford his losses far better (the fat churl has no house, no retinue to keep up, all his scoundrel father’s fortune sitting untouched in the bank), roars like a bull of Bashan every time he thinks of his losses. We played a second night in private, and our man Barbeau served us a very good dinner in a closed room. Mr. Warrington holds his tongue like a perfect gentleman, and none of us have breathed a word of our losses to anyone, but the whole town of Tunbridge Wells is gossiping about the Virginian’s winning streak anyway.
