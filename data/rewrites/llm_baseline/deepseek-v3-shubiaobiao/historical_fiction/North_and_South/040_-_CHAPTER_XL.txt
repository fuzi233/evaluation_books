*"I have no claim, yet feel the sting of loss,
No theft from me, yet sorrow weighs my heart—
For joy that lights another’s path, alas,
Leaves but the shadow where my own should start."*

Margaret had anticipated little personal pleasure from Mr. Bell’s visit—her thoughts had been fixed on her father’s comfort. Yet when her godfather arrived, she found herself slipping effortlessly into the warm familiarity of old affection. He declared she had no right to be so thoroughly to his taste; it was a birthright, he teased, this effortless way of hers to command his fondness. She, in turn, playfully credited him with an Oxford don’s unlikely youthfulness—not in looks, perhaps, but in the kindness that animated him.

"Youthful in spirit, I mean," she amended with a smile. "For I must confess, your opinions seem the dustiest relics I’ve encountered in years."

"Listen to her, Hale! Milton has corrupted your daughter. A democrat, a radical, a peacemonger—nay, a full-blown socialist!"

"Papa, I’m merely defending progress. Mr. Bell would have us bartering furs for acorns still."

"Nonsense! I’d have us farming the land, weaving wool into cloth—don’t exaggerate, child. But this endless rush—men trampling men in their scramble for wealth—wearies me."

"Not all of us can sit in Oxford’s ivory towers," Mr. Hale remarked, "watching fortunes grow untended. Many here would envy such effortless prosperity."

"Envy? No. It’s the scramble they relish. To sit quietly, learning from the past or shaping the future with patient labor—bah! Milton knows no such art."

"Yet Milton might say Oxford men have forgotten how to move," Margaret interjected. "A little mingling would benefit both."

"Benefit Milton, perhaps. Much might benefit them that others would find tedious."

"Are you not a Milton man yourself?" Margaret challenged. "I’d have thought you proud of it."

"Proud? Of what? The soot, the clamor? Come to Oxford, Margaret—there’s a city worth glorying in."

Mr. Hale sighed. "Mr. Thornton joins us tonight, and he’s as fierce a champion of Milton as you of Oxford. You two might temper each other’s prejudices."

"I’ve no wish to be tempered," Mr. Bell retorted.

"Mr. Thornton—tonight?" Margaret’s voice dipped low.

"Tea or shortly after. He bade us not wait."

***

Thornton had resolved not to question his mother about her confrontation with Margaret. He dreaded hearing Margaret’s conduct dissected through the lens of maternal disapproval, yet her name alone sent a pang through him. He loved her still, even as he condemned her—even as he told himself he had renounced her. In dreams, she taunted him: dancing toward him, radiant, a siren whose allure repelled even as it ensnared. The vision clung, a grotesque distortion of the woman he knew, leaving him torn between longing and loathing.

Pride forbade him to avoid her. He would neither seek nor shun her company. To prove his mastery, he buried himself in work, deliberate to the point of sluggishness. It was past eight when he arrived at the Hales’, where business with Bell protracted his torment. The don’s aimless chatter chafed him; Bell, in turn, found Thornton brusque to the point of rudeness.

At last, a sound from the drawing room spurred their move upstairs. Margaret sat with an open letter, animatedly discussing its contents with her father. At their entrance, she set it aside—but not before Thornton caught Hale’s remark:

"A letter from Henry Lennox. Margaret takes great hope from it."

Bell nodded. Margaret’s cheeks flushed crimson under Thornton’s gaze. The urge to flee seized him—to quit the house and never return.

"Were you two converting one another downstairs?" Mr. Hale teased. "You tarried so long."

"Debating which of us would emerge intact, like Kilkenny cats? Whose opinions, pray, seem most tenacious to you?"

Thornton, lost, remained silent. Hale explained:

"We accused Mr. Bell of Oxonian prejudice against Milton this morning. Margaret suggested more interaction might broaden both sides."

"Ah, but Margaret thought *Milton* needed broadening," Bell corrected. "Wasn’t it so?"

"I thought mutual exposure beneficial," she demurred. "The idea was as much papa’s as mine."

"Thus, Thornton, we ought to have been enlightening each other below, not dredging up defunct county families. But I’ll do my part now. When do you Milton men mean to *live*? You seem to spend life amassing its materials."

"By ‘live,’ you mean pleasure?"

"Enjoyment—not mere frivolity, but the fruits of labor. You chase wealth. To what end?"

Thornton hesitated. "I don’t strive for wealth."

"What then?"

"A question too personal for catechism."

"Gentlemen, let’s avoid interrogation," Hale interposed. "You’re neither of you mere representatives. You’re individuals."

"Flattery or faint praise?" Bell mused. "I’d gladly stand for Oxford’s beauty and learning. What say you, Margaret?"

"I don’t know Oxford. But a city’s symbol differs from the essence of its people."

"Ah! This morning you sided with Milton’s mills against me!"

Margaret saw Thornton’s sharp glance and winced at the implication. Bell rambled on, extolling Oxford’s charms, but Thornton simmered. Tonight, the don’s jests grated.

"You mistake me," he said stiffly. "I don’t hold Milton as a model town."

"Not architecturally?" Bell prodded.

"We’ve had too much doing to dwell on appearances."

"Appearance *matters*," Hale said gently. "It shapes us from childhood."

Thornton’s jaw tightened. "We’re not Greeks, Bell, to idolize beauty and leisure. We’re Teutonic—a race of action. Our pride lies in conquering resistance, in self-reliance. We resent distant lawmakers meddling where we’d govern ourselves."

"A revival of the Heptarchy! Very well, I recant—you Milton men *do* revere the past. You’re Thor’s own worshippers."

Margaret sensed Thornton’s irritation. Desperate to deflect the tension, she forced lightness:

"Edith writes that Corfu’s calicoes surpass London’s."

"Does she?" Hale frowned. "Surely an exaggeration."

"She says so, papa."

"Then it’s fact," Bell declared. "Margaret’s truthfulness extends to her cousin. No relation of hers could stretch the truth."

"Is Miss Hale so famed for truth?" Thornton’s tone was acid. Instantly, he regretted it. Why lash out? He was wretched tonight—fretting at delay, nettled by Lennox’s name, chafing under Bell’s levity. And now, to wound *her*!

She didn’t flinch. Only her eyes widened, wounded as a child’s, before she bent over her sewing, silent. He ached for a glance, a word—any chance to repent. But her fingers flew, steady as clockwork. She must despise him.

The evening ended in strained silence. When Thornton left abruptly, Margaret folded her work with leaden arms. Exhaustion lined her face.

Upstairs, Bell muttered, "Success has spoiled that man. He bristles at a jest."

"He’s not himself tonight," Margaret said quietly. "Something troubles him."

Bell peered at her. Later, he startled Hale:

"Ever think Thornton fancies your daughter?"

"Never!" Hale flushed. "She’d never encourage it."

Bell shrugged. "A bachelor’s guess. But she’s shown ‘pretty symptoms.’"

Hale lay awake, dismissing the notion.

At parting, Bell urged Margaret to lean on him in any trouble. To Hale, he pressed:

"Leave Milton. Take a parsonage with me. Margaret could tend us in our dotage."

"Here I stay," Hale said firmly. "Here I’ll be buried."

***

Life settled into quietude. No invalid, no strikes, even the Higginses receded. Margaret visited the Boucher orphans, now in Mary’s care. Higgins, steadier than of old, spoke little of Thornton—though the master came often to oversee the children’s schooling.

"He baffles me," Higgins admitted. "Two men in one skin. One’s the millmaster; t’other’s just a man. He listens now—stares like I’m some foreign beast. But I speak my mind."

Thornton’s visits to the Hales dwindled. Notes pleaded business. Hale grieved the loss.

One evening, he startled Margaret:

"Did Thornton ever… care for you?"

She bent her head. "Yes. I should have told you."

"He spoke?"

A whisper: "Yes."

"And you refused?"

Silence, then a nod.
