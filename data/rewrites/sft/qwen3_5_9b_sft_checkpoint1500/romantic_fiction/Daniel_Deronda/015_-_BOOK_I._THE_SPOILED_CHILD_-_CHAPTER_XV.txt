"Festina lente"—speed tempered with delay. So spoke Sir Thomas Browne, and the counsel seemed fitting for the tangled web of human fortunes. Gwendolen Harleth, having tasted the dizzying intoxication of foreign gambling, had returned to England with the delusion of an empress of luck. She clung to the chaotic philosophy that actions mattered less than amusement, a conviction born of her recent escapades. Yet, this philosophical detachment could not shield her from the brutal arithmetic of reality. Certain shadowy figures, operating under the mysterious banner of "Grapnell & Co.", had manipulated the realm of chance to alter her family's standing. Against her fierce reluctance, she had returned home bearing a heavy necklace—pawned in a fit of desperate pride, later redeemed by others.

As Gwendolen journeyed back to England, Lord Grandcourt was moving to intercept her. Yet, he approached the pursuit with the deliberate slowness of a predator who enjoys the hunt. He did not rush directly from Diplow to Leubronn; instead, he lingered in Baden-Baden, engaging in polite appointments with Russian acquaintances, only to shatter these social trappings when the necessity of reaching Leubronn became urgent. Grandcourt possessed a peculiar, intermittent vitality. His passions burned with a flickering, smoldering heat rather than open flame. Much of life, he observed, proceeded without such intense emotion: the careful tying of cravats, the attendance at dinners, the delivery of speeches honoring august personages—all performed with a veneer of the English gentleman, requiring little expenditure of vital energy. He could maintain the appearance of obstinacy and persistence, projecting sudden impulses that seemed daemonic, though often these were merely the result of unregulated souls lacking the ducts of habit to channel their nature, reducing them to spurs and puddles under pressure.

Lord Grandcourt harbored a quiet amusement regarding Gwendolen's flight. He viewed her departure not as a rejection, but as a spirited provocation. He suspected it stemmed from a stinging resentment of his perceived coolness in the Cardell Chase incident. To bring a woman so close to tender admission, only to walk away from the final opportunity of securing her consent, required a girl of genuine spirit to resist. And surely, if she meant him to follow, he intended to do so. For a full week, however, he took no action, nor did he inquire of Mr. Lush regarding Miss Harleth's whereabouts. Mr. Lush, the loyal steward, felt a mixture of triumph and deep suspicion. Grandcourt had remained curiously neutral, an expression like an alligator basking in the sun, leaving Lush unable to predict the slow-turning gears of his master's mind. Yet, this procrastination inevitably wasted Grandcourt's formidable energy.

At Diplow, the atmosphere shifted with the absence of the young lady. The guests, usually content, grew restless. Lady Flora Hollis, a woman of vibrant middle-age and insatiable curiosity, seized upon the mystery. She orchestrated a whirlwind of calls to Mrs. Torrington, visiting the rectory at Offendene and Quetcham, gathering gossip with the Arrowpoints. Through these networks, it became clear that Miss Harleth had departed for Leubronn to join the Baron and Baroness von Langen. The initial shock and disappointment of Mrs. Davilow and the Gascoignes had softened into a collective desire to frame Gwendolen's disappearance as nothing eccentric, ensuring it need not be a scandal. Even the rector, a man of practical sense, found his mind drifting toward poetic maxims. He recalled the myth of Amaryllis fleeing, hoping her hiding place would remain hidden, yet acknowledging that in an age of steam engines, love often finds a path "over the mountain and over the wave." He pictured Gwendolen as a creature of excellent sense and coquettish daring, wondering if her boldness had ultimately outstripped her wisdom.

Lady Flora, returning laden with news, saw no barrier to whispering it to Lord Grandcourt at dinner, hinting subtly at rumors of a disappointed suitor. Grandcourt listened with quiet attention, his features unreadable. The following day, he issued orders to Mr. Lush to arrange a plausible pretext for dissolving the party at Diplow within a week. He intended to depart for yachting voyages to the Baltic or elsewhere, feeling imprisoned by the company he did not desire. Lush, ever perceptive, recognized the command as a signal for Grandcourt's journey to Leubronn. Though Grandcourt might creep away like a billiard ball, sticking to the edge of the road, Lush planned to render himself indispensable, ensuring he accompanied his patron. Gwendolen's repulsion for him, a fact well known, only amused Grandcourt, reinforcing his resolve to have Lush constantly at hand.

Thus, it came to pass that Lord Grandcourt arrived at the *Czarina* hotel five days after Gwendolen's departure from Leubronn. There, he encountered his uncle, Sir Hugo Mallinger, along with the latter's family, including Daniel Deronda. It was rarely a joy for a reigning power and an heir presumptive to converge, especially when separated by a touch of gout in the elder and a streak of willfulness in the younger. Sir Hugo was a man of easy temper, tolerant of differences, yet the dispute over the settlement of the family estates gnawed at him. While he could forgive faults in the church or the ballot, the lack of a son to inherit his lands was a grievance that haunted his days. Under the ill-advised will of Sir Francis, even Diplow, the ancestral seat where Sir Hugo had spent countless hunting seasons, had been left to the same restrictive conditions as the vast Topping inheritance. He and his wife and daughters, who ought to have retired there, were barred from true ownership.

As years passed, this grievance deepened. Lady Mallinger, after three daughters who died in quick succession, had borne no children for eight years, remaining past forty barren. Sir Hugo, nearly twenty years her senior, felt the weariness of age, his hopefulness dimming until the concept of second childhood offered a fragile restoration. He had begun to despair of an heir, making the presence of Grandcourt, the presumptive successor, increasingly unwelcome. Yet, Sir Hugo exercised caution, driven by a pragmatic plan to secure Diplow as a future retreat for his wife and daughters, keeping the estate intact despite his childlessness. Knowledge of his nephew's disposition led him to believe Grandcourt might accept a transaction: a substantial sum of ready money in exchange for relinquishing his prospective interest in the domain. If, by some miracle, an heir were eventually born, the money would be lost, but Sir Hugo calculated this risk as negligible. Having wisely husbanded his fortune through mining ventures and lease sales, he was prepared for the outlay.

To avoid quarrels, Sir Hugo maintained a facade of perfect politeness. Years prior, during improvements at the Abbey involving the felling of obstructive timber, he had noted a distinct lack of active spite in Grandcourt's mind. Since then, no event had escalated their relationship beyond the bounds of mutual accommodation. On Grandcourt's side, his uncle was viewed as a superfluity, a bore to be expunged from existence. However, through Mr. Lush, the intermediary, Grandcourt had learned of his uncle's inclinations regarding Diplow. The prospect of cash was flattering to his sense of power, allowing him to refuse the offer if he chose, though the timing of the tenancy he requested had annoyed Sir Hugo, who feared Grandcourt might hold onto the property for its superior hunting grounds. Furthermore, Lush had whispered the rumor that Grandcourt sought to woo Miss Arrowpoint, a possibility that might make the money less tempting. Thus, at this unexpected reunion, Sir Hugo was eager to learn the state of affairs at Diplow, determined to be civil, and looking forward to private chats with Lush.

Between Deronda and Grandcourt lay a peculiar, faint connection, rooted in circumstances yet to unfold. At the first meeting around the *table d'hôte*, neither showed suppressed chagrin. Later, on the terrace, the four gentlemen—Sir Hugo, Grandcourt, Lush, and Deronda—moved together into the large salon.

"Did you play much at Baden, Grandcourt?" Sir Hugo asked.

"No; I looked on and betted a little with some Russians there."

"Had you luck?"

"What did I win, Lush?" Grandcourt replied, playing idly with his diamond ring and whisker, drawing stares from admiring foreigners.

"You brought away about two hundred," Lush said.

"You are not here for the sake of the play, then?" Sir Hugo queried.

"No; I don't care about play now. It's a confounded strain," Grandcourt muttered, noting the monotony that knitted the brain into meshes. "Somebody should invent a mill to do amusements for you, my dear fellow, as the Tartars get their praying done."

"But where's your gambling beauty, Deronda? Have you seen her lately?" Sir Hugo turned to the younger man.

"She's gone," Deronda answered curtly.

"An uncommonly fine girl, a perfect Diana!" Sir Hugo exclaimed, addressing Grandcourt. "Really worth a little straining to look at her. I saw her winning, and she took it as coolly as if she had known it all beforehand. The same day Deronda happened to see her losing like wildfire, and she bore it with immense pluck. I suppose she was cleaned out, or was wise enough to stop in time. How do you know she's gone?"
