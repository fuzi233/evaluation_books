"Late," Aristide Valentin murmured, the word hanging heavy in the air as he finally breached the threshold of his own home. The guests, already seated in the grand entrance hall, offered polite nods, their anxiety assuaged by the presence of Ivan. The old man was a fixture of the house—a scarred sentinel with a face as grey as his mutton-chop moustaches, stationed permanently before a table draped in weaponry. Valentin's residence was a fortress of peculiar architecture, an old edifice hemmed in by high walls and towering poplars that nearly touched the Seine. Its design was a paradox: numerous doors led from the interior into the vast garden, yet there existed no exit from the garden to the outside world save the single front door, guarded by Ivan and the arsenal. It was a perfect trap, a botanical cage where a hundred criminals had once sworn vengeance, now rendered impregnable by spikes and silence.

Ivan had explained the delay with characteristic efficiency. Valentin was detained for ten minutes, ostensibly arranging the logistics of several imminent executions. Though the duties were repulsive to his refined sensibilities, he executed them with surgical precision. A ruthless hunter of criminals, he was surprisingly mild regarding their punishment, leveraging his supreme influence over French—and largely European—police methods to mitigate sentences and purify the prison system. He was a titan of humanitarian freethought, proving that mercy could be colder, yet more effective, than justice itself.

When Valentin finally emerged, he was a striking figure in black silk and the red rosette of his office, his dark beard streaked with the grey of command. He bypassed the foyer entirely, moving directly to his study, which opened onto the grounds. The garden door stood ajar. After securing his personal effects in their official lockbox, he paused at the threshold, staring out into the gloom. A sharp moon fought a losing battle against the flying debris of a passing storm, casting fractured light across the lawn. For a moment, a wistfulness clouded his typically analytical gaze, a psychic glimpse of the tremendous problems awaiting him. But the mood evaporated instantly; he was late, and the clock ticked toward disaster.

Inside the drawing room, the assembly was complete. Lord Galloway, the English Ambassador, sat like a russet apple, his choleric temperament evident in his flushed face and the stiff blue ribbon of the Garter beside him. Beside him sat Lady Galloway, slender and silver-haired, radiating a superior sensitivity, and her daughter, Lady Margaret Graham, a pale beauty with elfin features and copper curls. Opposite them loomed the Duchess of Mont St. Michel, opulent and black-eyed, accompanied by her equally imposing daughters. Dr. Simon, a quintessential French scientist with spectacles, a pointed brown beard, and a forehead etched with the parallel wrinkles of perpetual superiority, completed the circle. Then there was Father Brown of Cobhole, recently returned from England, and finally, Commandant O'Brien of the French Foreign Legion.

O'Brien was a study in contradictions: a slim, swaggering figure in immaculate uniform, clean-shaven and blue-eyed, yet carrying the melancholic air of a regiment defined by victorious failures and successful suicides. Born an Irish gentleman, he had fled his homeland following a financial collapse, now expressing his disdain for British etiquette by swinging freely in his sabre and spurs. When he bowed to the Galloways, the family responded with stiff formality, and Lady Margaret deliberately averted her gaze.

Yet, Valentin held no particular affection for this motley crew. None of them were the guest of honor. He awaited a man of global renown, a friend forged in the fires of his detective triumphs across America: Julius K. Brayne. Brayne was a multi-millionaire whose colossal endowments to obscure religious sects provided endless fodder for both satire and solemnity in the press. Whether Brayne was an atheist, a Mormon, or a Christian Scientist remained a mystery; what mattered was his insatiable appetite for funding any intellectual vessel, provided it was untested. He waited patiently for the American Shakespeare, admired Walt Whitman, and championed the "progressive" theories of Luke P. Tanner over traditionalists. In his view, Valentin himself was a progressive force, a compliment that masked a profound misunderstanding of the Frenchman's cynicism.

Brayne's arrival was as sudden and decisive as a dinner bell. He possessed the rare quality of being physically larger in his presence than in his absence. A massive man, as fat as he was tall, he wore complete evening black devoid of ornamentation, save for a watch chain. His white hair was brushed back with Germanic rigidity, framing a red, fierce, yet cherubic face dominated by a dark tuft of hair under his lower lip that lent him a theatrical, Mephistophelean edge.

The salon ceased its idle staring. Lateness had become a domestic emergency, and Brayne was swiftly ushered into the dining room, Lady Galloway taking his arm.

Dinner proceeded with a veneer of civility, though cracks appeared early. The Galloways were genial only so long as Lady Margaret refrained from linking arms with the adventuring O'Brien. She had complied, choosing instead to sit with Dr. Simon. Yet, Lord Galloway remained restless, his diplomacy fraying under the weight of suspicion. During the cigar course, as the three younger men—Dr. Simon, Father Brown, and the exiled O'Brien—retreated to the conservatory to mingle with the ladies, the Ambassador's composure shattered. Every sixty seconds, the thought gnawed at him: *Is O'Brien signaling to Margaret?* He couldn't conceive of the method, but the possibility fueled a paranoid rage. Left alone with Brayne—the hoary Yankee who worshipped all faiths—and Valentin—the grizzled Frenchman who worshipped none—he engaged in a tedious logomachy that neither could resolve.

Eventually, the "progressive" debate reached a breaking point of boredom. Lord Galloway rose, intending to retreat to the drawing room, and became hopelessly lost in the mansion's labyrinthine corridors for six or eight minutes. Finally, he stumbled upon the high-pitched, didactic voice of the doctor and the dull cadence of the priest, followed by bursts of laughter. He assumed they were debating the eternal conflict between science and religion. But the moment he pushed open the salon door, he saw only emptiness. Commandant O'Brien was gone. Lady Margaret was gone.

Driven by a frantic, senile suspicion, Galloway stormed back toward the rear of the house, where Valentin's study lay. He collided with his daughter sweeping past him, her face a mask of white scorn. If she had been with O'Brien, where was he? If not, where had she gone? With a passion bordering on madness, he navigated the dark passages until he found a servant's entrance opening directly onto the garden.

The storm had passed, and the moon, wielding a scimitar of light, had cleared the sky. The argent illumination bathed the four corners of the garden in stark clarity. A tall figure in blue strides across the lawn toward the study door, the silver piping of his uniform catching the moonlight. It was Commandant O'Brien.

He vanished through the French windows, disappearing into the house. Lord Galloway was left in a state of indescribable fury, a volatile mixture of virulence and confusion. The blue-and-silver garden seemed to mock him, a stage set for a tragedy against which his worldly authority was powerless. The length and grace of the Irishman's stride enraged him as if he were a rival lover rather than a potential threat. The moonlight itself seemed to madden him. Trapped in a garden of troubadours, a Watteau-esque fairyland, he decided to confront the intruder verbally. Stepping briskly after him, he tripped over a root or stone hidden in the grass. First irritation, then curiosity, then sheer terror took hold. The next instant, the moon and the towering poplars witnessed an absurdity: an elderly English diplomat sprinting desperately, screaming or bellowing as he ran.

His hoarse cries summoned a pale face to the study door—Dr. Simon, with his beaming glasses and furrowed brow. He heard the nobleman's first coherent words. Lord Galloway was weeping. "A corpse in the grass... a blood-stained corpse!" O'Brien had finally succumbed to insanity.

"We must inform Valentin immediately," the doctor urged, his voice tight. "Fortunately, he is here." Just as he spoke, the great detective entered the study, drawn by the commotion. His demeanor underwent a typical transformation: initially concerned as a host fearing illness among his guests, he instantly shifted to bright, businesslike gravity upon hearing the gory report. This was his domain.

"Strange," Valentin remarked as they hurried out into the garden, "that I should hunt mysteries across the globe, only for one to settle in my own backyard. Where is the location?" They crossed the lawn with difficulty as a fine mist began to rise from the river. Guided by the shaken Galloway, they located the body sunk deep in the grass. It belonged to a very tall, broad-shouldered man lying face-down. Only his massive shoulders, clad in black cloth, and his bald head—save for a few wisps of brown hair clinging to the skull like wet seaweed—were visible. A scarlet serpent of blood crawled from beneath his fallen face.

"At least," Dr. Simon observed with a deep, singular intonation, "he is not one of our party."
