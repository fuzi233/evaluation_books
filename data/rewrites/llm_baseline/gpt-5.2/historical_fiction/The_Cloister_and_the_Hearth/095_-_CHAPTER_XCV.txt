The hermit started as if the stone under him had cried out. His gaze flew from the sleeping child on his left arm to the woman at his right knee, and back again, and in that to-and-fro there was more terror than when he had first faced solitude.

An hour or two ago she had been pale, breathless, mastered. Now she shone. The blood had come back into her cheeks, her eyes were quick as fire on a hearth, and her mouth—half parted—had the look of victory in it; yet it was not the hard triumph of spite. It was something he had never seen there before, not even in the days when she laughed over her kneading-trough and sang. It was the grave pride of a mother.

He stared as a man stares at a miracle that has chosen to happen in his own poor room.

“Us?” he got out at last, hoarse, and still his eyes went between the child and her, as if he must be sure the two were not a trick of fasting.

Margaret, in her turn, was genuinely taken aback. In that age men read meanings in signs and feelings more readily than they counted facts; yet here was a fact with a warm weight and a pulse. She drew back a little, then leaned in, scandalised.

“What!” she cried. “Doth not a father know his own child? and a man of God, too? Fie, Gerard, to pretend! Nay, thou art too wise, too good, not to have—why, I watched thee; and e’en now look at you twain! ’Tis thine own flesh and blood thou holdest to thine heart.”

The hermit’s lips worked. His whole body shook, not with cold, though the cell was chill, but with the breaking of some inward frost.

“What words are these?” he stammered. “This angel mine?”

“Whose else?” said she, and her voice softened at once into possession and tenderness. “Since he is mine.”

He bent his eyes on the child as if to drink him in. His stare had no common seeing in it, but that desperate greed with which a starved man looks at bread. Margaret watched him greedily too, yet proudly, as if she had made this food and set it on the board.

“He is not a bit like me,” she said, pleased and fierce in the same breath; “but oh, at whiles he is thy very image in little. And see this golden hair. Thine was the very colour at his age; ask mother else. And see this mole on his little finger; now look at thine own—there! ’Twas thy mother let me weet thou wast marked so before him; and oh, Gerard, ’twas this our child found thee for me; for by that little mark on thy finger I knew thee for his father, when I watched above thy window and saw thee feed the birds.”

As she spoke she seized the child’s hand and kissed it with an eagerness that was almost hunger. She actually got half the tiny fingers into her mouth, Heaven knows how, as if the taste of him might steady her joy.

“Ah! bless thee,” she whispered to the sleeper, “thou didst find thy poor daddy for her; and now thou hast made us friends again after our little quarrel—the first, the last. Wast very cruel to me but now, my poor Gerard, and I forgive thee; for loving of thy child.”

At that the man broke.

A sound came from him—half sob, half laugh, half prayer—so wild that it startled even Margaret, who had seen men’s hearts crack in childbirth rooms and at graves. Lowered by fasts and unstrung by silence, the once strong Gerard was hysterical; his head swam, his breath came in snatches, and the child on his arm stirred faintly, feeling the tremor.

“Nay, take not on so,” Margaret murmured, and laid her hand on his brow as a mother lays it on fever. Her palm was warm; her touch had the homely certainty of someone used to quieting a fire that threatens to run up the chimney. “Be brave! So, so. Dear heart, thou art not the first man that hath gone abroad and come back richer by a lovely little self than he went forth. Being a man of God, take courage, and say He sends thee this to comfort thee for what thou hast lost in me; and that is not so very much, my lamb; for sure the better part of love shall ne’er cool here to thee; though it may in thine, and ought, being a priest, and parson of Gouda.”

“I?” he whispered, faint. “Priest of Gouda? Never. I am a friar of St. Dominic.” His eyelids fluttered; yet his voice, for all its weakness, clung to the one thing it dared ask. “Speak on—sweet music—tell me all that hath happened thee, before we are parted again.”

Some women would have seized that word—parted—and made it the battlefield. Margaret did not. She had learned how not to strike the same stone twice and call it wisdom. She accepted the word as if it were settled, turned her face from arguing about the manse as from a pot already set to boil, and poured out her tale.

She told him, above all, of the kindness of his parents; of bread given and doors opened; of hard words borne; of nights where the house sounded with nothing but the ticking of a poor clock and the wind worrying the shutters. She spoke with that gift she had—making the past live again in the mouth. As she talked, his hand stole to hers. Sometimes he squeezed it so hard she felt his nails; sometimes she felt him wince and tremble, anger flashing through him when she named injuries. At other moments he pressed her hand with a gentle sympathy that ran, like warm broth, into the marrow.

“Oh, piteous tale,” he said, “of a true heart battling alone against such bitter odds! It all seems small, when I see thee here again, nursing my boy. We have had a warning, Gerard. True friends like you and me are rare, and they are mad to part, ere death divideth them.”

“And that is true,” said he—off his guard, the old Gerard speaking before the hermit could stop him.

Then she would have him tell her what he had suffered for her. He begged to be spared; she agreed—then, by questions that sounded simple as a cook’s “How did the bread take?” drew it all out: the perils, the turns, the loneliness, the temptations that came like night-bats to a cell. More than once she hid her face in her hands at a peril past; and to comfort him, she slid her arms under the child, lifted him with a practised gentleness, and offered the small warm mouth like an answer to fear.

“Kiss him softly,” she whispered. “Again, again—kiss thy fill if thou canst. He is sound. ’Tis all I can do to comfort thee till thou art out of this foul den and in thy sweet manse yonder.”

He shook his head, stubborn as a man with a vow between his teeth.

“Well,” said she, letting it pass as if it were but a dish she would serve again in due course. “Know that I have been sore affronted for want of my lines.”

“Who hath dared affront thee?”

“No matter. Those that will do it again if thou hast lost them—which the saints forbid.”

“I lose them?” He dropped his eyes. “Nay—there they lie, close to thy hand.”

“Where, where, oh, where?”

He bowed his head still lower, ashamed, and pointed with a finger that trembled.

“Look in the Vulgate. Heaven forgive me: I thought thou wert dead, and a saint in heaven.”

She opened the poor book. There, on blank leaves, were her marriage lines. A cry broke from her like a kettle boiling over.

“Thank God! thank God! Oh, bless thee, Gerard, bless thee! Why—what is here, Gerard?”

For other leaves were thick with relics: every scrap she had ever sent him, pinned and guarded as if each were a wafer; their two names she had once written together in sport; the lock of her hair; half a silver coin broken between them; and—oddest and tenderest—one straw, browned and dried, from which she had sucked soup the first day he ever saw her. The very objects had an odour to her memory: broth and hearth-smoke, salt on the lip, the clean dryness of straw between teeth, the faint metallic tang of that coin.

At sight of these proofs of love, her exultation over the lines was drowned. She swayed; her hand went to her bosom as if to hold her heart in. She leaned her forehead against the stone and wept without sound—so silently he did not know. She would not let him. In this hour, to befriend him, she must be the stronger; tears, like spilled water, weaken the hands.

When she spoke, her voice was steady again, though it had a tremor like a lute-string after it is struck.

“Gerard,” said she, “I know you are wise and good. You must have a reason for what you are doing, let it seem ever so unreasonable. Talk we like old friends. Why are you buried alive?”

“To escape temptation,” he answered. The words came out as if he had rehearsed them in his prayers. “My impious ire against those two had its root in the heart; that heart then I must deaden, and, Dei gratia, I shall. Shall I, a servant of Christ and of the Church, court temptation? Shall I pray daily to be led out on’t, and walk into it with open eyes?”

“That is good sense anyway,” said Margaret, with such a plain look of candour that it might have deceived a saint.

“’Tis unanswerable,” said he, and sighed.

“We shall see.” She tilted her head a little, listening—not to him only, but to the place itself. The cell had its own sound: the damp drip in the corner, the small scratch of a mouse, the far sighing of wind through leafless boughs. “Tell me: have you escaped temptation here? Why I ask is, when I am alone, my thoughts are far more wild and foolish than in company. Nay, speak sooth; come!”

He hesitated; then humility, sore-earned, forced his confession.

“I must needs own I have been worse tempted here with evil imaginations than in the world.”

“There now.”

“Ay, but so were Anthony and Jerome, Macarius and Hilarion, Benedict, Bernard, and all the saints. ’Twill wear off.”

“How do you know?”

“I feel sure it will.”

“Guessing against knowledge,” said she, and there was the quick bite of her wit. “Here ’tis men folk are sillier than us that be but women. Wise in their own conceits, they will not let themselves see; their stomachs are too high to be taught by their eyes. A woman, if she went into a hole in a bank to escape temptation, and there found it, would just lift her farthingale and out on’t, and not e’en know how wise she was, till she watched a man in like plight.”

He half smiled, then sobered.

“Nay, I grant humility and a teachable spirit are the roads to wisdom; but when all is said, here I wrestle but with imagination. At Gouda she I love—as no priest or monk must love any but the angels—she will tempt a weak soul, unwilling, yet not loth to be tempted.”

“Ay, that is another matter. I should tempt thee then? to what, i’ God’s name?”

“Who knows? The flesh is weak.”

“Speak for yourself, my lad.” She drew herself up, and the pride in her was as frank as a trumpet-call. “Why, you are thinking of some other Margaret, not Margaret a Peter. Was ever my mind turned to folly and frailty? Stay—is it because you were my husband once, as these lines avouch? Think you the road to folly is beaten for you more than another? Oh! how shallow are the wise, and how little able are you to read me, who can read you so well from top to toe.

“Come, learn thine A B C. Were a stranger to proffer me unchaste love, I should shrink a bit, no doubt, and feel sore, but I should defend myself without making a coil; for men, I know, are so—the best of them sometimes. But if you, that have been my husband, and are my child’s father, were to offer to humble me so in mine own eyes, and thine, and his—either I should spit in thy face, Gerard, or, as I am not a downright vulgar woman, I should snatch the first weapon at hand and strike thee dead.”

Her eyes flashed; even her nostrils widened with indignation. In that moment she was terrible, and wholly sincere. The hermit’s fear—so long fed on shadows—fell back before that clear blaze of honour.

“I had not the sense to see that,” said Gerard quietly, and he sat pondering as if a door in his mind had opened.

Margaret watched him, then softened as quickly as she had flamed.

“Let not you and I dispute,” she said gently. “Speak we of other things. Ask me of thy folk.”

“My father?”

“Well—and warms to thee and me. Poor soul, he drew glaive on those twain that day; but Jorian Ketel and I we mastered him, and he drove them forth his house for ever.”

“That may not be,” said Gerard at once, the old dutiful son answering before priest or hermit. “He must take them back.”

“That he will never do for us. You know the man; he is dour as iron; yet would he do it for one word from one that will not speak it.”

“Who?”

“The vicar of Gouda. The old man will be at the manse to-morrow, I hear.”

“How you come back to that,” he said, but without anger.

“Forgive me: I am but a woman. It is us for nagging; shouldst keep me from it wi’ questioning of me.”

“My sister Kate?”

“Alas!”

“What, hath ill befallen e’en that sweet lily? Out and alas!”

“Be calm, sweetheart—no harm hath her befallen. Oh, nay, nay, far fro’ that.”

She made herself very composed. And in a voice low and sweet, as if she were singing a cradle-song to a grown man, she told him how Kate had died.

It was not told like a sermon; it was told like a memory of a room, and of the sounds in it: the quiet breathing, the soft murmur of gossips, the clink of a spoon against a cup, the ticking of a clock that seemed too loud because grief had hushed all else.

“My poor Gerard,” said she, “Kate hath left her trouble behind her. For the manner on’t, ’twas like the rest. Ah, such as she saw never thirty, nor ever shall while earth shall last. She smiled in pain too.

“A well, then, thus ’twas: she was took wi’ a languor and a loss of all her pains.”

“A loss of her pains? I understand you not.”

“Ay, you are not experienced. Indeed, e’en thy mother almost blinded herself and said, ‘’Tis maybe a change for the better.’ But Joan Ketel, which is an understanding woman, she looked at her and said, ‘Down sun, down wind!’ And the gossips sided and said, ‘Be brave, you that are her mother, for she is half way to the saints.’ And thy mother wept sore, but Kate would not let her.

“And one very ancient woman, she said to thy mother, ‘She will die as easy as she lived hard.’ And she lay painless best part of three days, a sipping of heaven afore-hand.

“And, my dear, when she was just parting, she asked for ‘Gerard’s little boy,’ and I brought him and set him on the bed, and the little thing behaved as peaceably as he does now. But by this time she was past speaking; but she pointed to a drawer, and her mother knew what to look for: it was two gold angels thou hadst given her years ago. Poor soul! she had kept them, till thou shouldst come home.

“And she nodded towards the little boy, and looked anxious; but we understood her, and put the pieces in his two hands; and when his little fingers closed on them, she smiled content. And so she gave her little earthly treasures to her favourite’s child—for you were her favourite—and her immortal jewel to God, and passed so sweetly we none of us knew justly when she left us. Well-a-day, well-a-day!”

Gerard wept outright now, not ashamed.

“She hath not left her like on earth,” he sobbed. “Oh, how the affections of earth curl softly round my heart! I cannot help it; God made them after all. Speak on, sweet Margaret; at thy voice the past rolls its tides back upon me; the loves and the hopes of youth come fair and gliding into my dark cell, and darker bosom, on waves of memory and music.”

“Gerard,” she said, and the tenderness in her had an edge like a knife in cloth, “I am loth to grieve you, but Kate cried a little when she first took ill at you not being there to close her eyes.”

His breath caught.

“You were within a league,” she went on, very quietly now, “but hid your face from her.”

He groaned as if struck.

“There, forgive me for nagging; I am but a woman. You would not have been so cruel to your own flesh and blood knowingly, would you?”

“Oh, no.”

“Well, then. Know that thy brother Sybrandt lies in my charge with a broken back—fruit of thy curse.”

“Mea culpa! mea culpa!” The words came from him like blows on the breast.

“He is very penitent. Be yourself and forgive him this night.”

“I have forgiven him long ago.”

“Think you he can believe that from any mouth but yours? Come! he is but about two butts’ length hence.”

“So near?” He started. “Why, where?”

“At Gouda manse. I took him there yestreen. For I know you: the curse was scarce cold on your lips when you repented it.” He nodded, stricken. “And I said to myself, Gerard will thank me for taking Sybrandt to die under his roof; he will not beat his breast and cry mea culpa, yet grudge three footsteps to quiet a withered brother on his last bed. He may have a bee in his bonnet, but he is not a hypocrite—a thing all pious words and uncharitable deeds.”

He staggered as if she had put her hand under his ribs and pressed. She had chosen her moment; she struck, not to hurt, but to drive the nail home.

“Forgive me for nagging,” she said, softer; and then, with the simplest sentence of all, she brought his mother into the cell as surely as if the door had opened on her.

“Thy mother too is waiting for thee. Is it well done to keep her on thorns so long? She will not sleep this night. Bethink thee, Gerard: she is all to thee that I am to this sweet child.”

As she spoke, the baby’s breath made its tiny sound—like milk simmering very low. The cell’s silence took it in and magnified it. Margaret looked down at the child as if she were reading a psalm in his face.

“Ah, I think so much more of mothers since I had my little Gerard. She suffered for thee, and nursed thee, and tended thee from boy to man. Priest, monk, hermit—call thyself what thou wilt—to her thou art but one thing: her child.”

“Where is she?” he asked, and his voice quavered like a bell in wind.

“At Gouda manse,” said Margaret, “wearing the night in prayer and care.”

Now she did the thing she had held back, like a cook who keeps the strongest spice for the end so it may carry through the whole dish. She spoke to his reason, in her own good straightforward Dutch, with no lace of Latin to hide the plainness.

She told him, without cruelty, that his cave-life was only a higher selfishness, spiritual egotism: caring for his own soul alone. A priest, she said, had no more right to think only of his own soul than only of his own body. His path to heaven lay through others.

“But,” she argued, “whoever yet lost his soul by saving the souls of others? The Almighty loves him who thinks of others; and when He shall see thee caring for the souls of the folk the duke hath put into thine hand, He will care ten times more for thy soul than He does now.”

Gerard looked up as if he had heard a new note in familiar chant.

“Art shrewd in dispute,” he said.

“Far from it,” said she. “Only my eyes are not bandaged with conceit.”

Then she flung her thought wider than the cell: she spoke of the world outside, of the way wickedness ran abroad and did not hide itself in caves; of how good men, by skulking, gave evil the odds. She brought in Denys, the duke’s soldier, and asked whether he had ever crept away from battle because battle was dangerous.

“And thy sole excuse is fear,” she said, and she did not soften that word. “Go to, no duke nor king hath such cowardly soldiers as Christ hath. What was that you said in the church at Rotterdam about the man in the parable that buried his talent in the earth, and so offended the giver? Thy wonderful gift for preaching—is it not a talent, and a gift from thy Creator?”

“Certes,” said he, subdued, “such as it is.”

“And hast thou laid it out? or buried it? To whom hast thou preached these seven months—to bats and owls? Hast buried it in one hole with thyself and thy once good wits?

“The Dominicans are the friars preachers. ’Tis for preaching they were founded. So thou art false to Dominic as well as to his Master.”

Then, with that gift she had for turning the grand into the homely, she carried him back to their youth.

“Do you remember, Gerard, when we were young together—which now are old before our time—as we walked handed in the fields, did you but see a sheep cast—ay, three fields off—you would leave your sweetheart (by her good will) and run and lift the sheep for charity?

“Well, then, at Gouda is not one sheep in evil plight, but a whole flock; some cast, some strayed, some sick, some tainted, some a being devoured—and all for the want of a shepherd. Where is their shepherd? Lurking in a den like a wolf—a den in his own parish. Out fie! out fie!

“I scented thee out, in part, by thy kindness to the little birds. Take note: you Gerard Eliassoen must love something. ’Tis in your blood; you were born to’t. Shunning man, you do but seek earthly affection a peg lower than man.”

He broke in, defensive for once with warmth.

“The birds are God’s creatures—His innocent creatures—and I do well to love them, being God’s creatures.”

“What,” said Margaret swiftly, “are they creatures of the same God that we are—that he is who lies upon thy knee?”

“You know they are.”

“Then what pretence for shunning us and being kind to them? Sith man is one of the animals, why pick him out to shun? Is’t because he is of animals the paragon?

“What, you court the young of birds, and abandon your own young? Birds need but bodily food; and having wings, deserve scant pity if they cannot fly and find it. But that sweet dove upon thy knee—he needeth not carnal only, but spiritual food. He is thine as well as mine; and I have done my share. He will soon be too much for me, and I look to Gouda’s parson to teach him true piety and useful lore. Is he not of more value than many sparrows?”

She waited. The silence was thick as dough before it rises. Gerard, cornered by truth, stammered an assent.

“You wonder,” she continued, “to hear me quote holy writ so glib. I have pored over it this four years, and why? Not because God wrote it, but because I saw it often in thy hands ere thou didst leave me. Heaven forgive me, I am but a woman.

“What thinkest thou of this sentence? ‘Let your work so shine before men that they may see your good works and glorify your Father which is in heaven!’ What is a saint in a sink better than a light under a bushel?”

Then she gathered all her reasons as one gathers sticks to make a fire that will not be put out.

“Therefore—since the sheep committed to thy charge bleat for thee and cry, ‘Oh desert us no longer, but come to Gouda manse;’ since I, who know thee ten times better than thou knowest thyself, do pledge my soul it is for thy soul’s weal to go to Gouda manse—since duty to thy child, too long abandoned, calls thee to Gouda manse—since thy sovereign, whom holy writ again bids thee honour, sends thee to Gouda manse—since the Pope, whom the Church teaches thee to revere, hath absolved thee of thy monkish vows, and orders thee to Gouda manse—”

“Ah!” He flinched, as if the Pope’s name had struck his vow like a hammer.

“Since thy grey-haired mother watches for thee in dole and care, and turneth oft the hour-glass and sigheth sore that thou comest so slow to her at Gouda manse—since thy brother, withered by thy curse, awaits thy forgiveness and thy prayers for his soul, now lingering in his body, at Gouda manse—take thou in thine arms the sweet bird wi’ crest of gold that nestles to thy bosom, and give me thy hand; thy sweetheart erst and wife, and now thy friend, the truest friend to thee this night that e’er man had—and come with me to Gouda manse!”

The hermit lifted his head. His eyes, hollowed by fasting, shone as if lit from behind.

“IT IS THE VOICE OF AN ANGEL!” he cried.

“Then hearken it,” said Margaret, and her practical courage rang like a ladle on a pot, “and come forth to Gouda manse!”

The battle was won.

She lingered one moment. Her eyes swept the cell: its poor hoard, the sad furniture of penance. She chose the Vulgate and the psaltery—bread for the mind, and music for the heart. The rest she left with a sigh, as one leaves broken crockery.

Then she caught sight of the breastplate and the cilice of bristles. Something in her—pity mixed with anger—rose up, and she dashed them down with feeble ferocity.

Gerard watched her from outside, surprised.

She coloured, half ashamed of her own little spite.

“I am but a woman,” said she; “’little’ will still be ‘spiteful.’”

“Why encumber thyself with those?” he asked. “They are safe.”

“Oh,” said she, “I had a reason.”

So they set their faces toward the parsonage.

The moon and stars were bright enough to make the snow glimmer like sifted flour. The world was hushed, and that hush, after the cell’s dripping and the heart’s tumult, felt like a great clean linen laid over noise. Even their steps had a different sound out here: a soft crunch, a small squeak—nature’s own music, not the harsh clatter of men. Gerard, who had lived on silence till it turned against him, drank in the gentler noises as if they were part of his absolution.

Suddenly he stopped.

“My poor little birds!”

“What of them?” Margaret asked, but not sharply.

“They will miss their food. I feed them every day.”

“The child hath a piece of bread in his cowl,” said she at once, ready as ever. “Take that, and feed them now against the morn.”

He looked down at the baby’s face—so unconscious, so trusting. His arms tightened.

“I will,” he began; then shook his head. “Nay, I will not. He is as innocent, and nearer to me and to thee.”

Margaret drew a long breath. Her jealousy—quick and honest—had been waiting like a knife under cloth.

“’Tis well,” she said low. “Hadst taken it, I might have hated thee. I am but a woman.”

They walked on. Before they had gone a quarter of a mile, Gerard’s breath grew short. The child, though small, weighed like a sack of grain to a man emptied by fasting.

“Margaret,” he said, and shame coloured his voice, “I must e’en rest; he is too heavy for me.”

“Then give him me, and take thou these,” said she briskly, and shifted the Vulgate and psaltery to him as if they were no heavier than a loaf. “Alas! alas! I mind when thou wouldst have run with the child on one shoulder, and the mother on t’other.”

She took the boy, and carried him with perfect ease, his head lying against her like a warm roll newly drawn from the oven. Gerard looked down at her, pondering what he had done to his body, and what he had called holiness.

“I trow,” he said at last, with a rueful simplicity, “overmuch fasting is not good for a man.”

“A many die of it each year,” she replied, “winter time.”

He glanced askant at her carrying the child, and those plain words sat in his mind like salt in a dish—bringing out the truth.

After they had gone near a mile, he said, surprised and a little reproachful, “You thought it was but two butts’ length.”

“Not I.”

“Why, you said so.”

“That is another matter.” She turned on him then the face of a Madonna—sweet, fearless, and entirely resolved. “I lied,” she said simply. “And to save your soul and body, I’d maybe tell a worse lie than that, at need. I am but a woman. Ah, well—it is but two butts’ length from here at any rate.”

“Without a lie?”

“Humph! Three, without a lie.”

And indeed, in a few minutes, the manse rose before them. A candle burned in the vicar’s parlour.

“She is waking still,” Margaret whispered.

Gerard stopped, arrested by the sight. Through the window the candle looked immense against the night, as if the darkness made it larger.

“Beautiful,” he murmured. “Beautiful.”

“What, in Heaven’s name?” Margaret whispered back, half impatient.

“That little candle, seen through the window at night,” he said, almost reverently. “Look an it be not like some fair star of size prodigious. It delighteth the eyes, and warmeth the heart of those outside.”

“Come,” said Margaret, and her voice softened; for she understood that he was learning again to love the world he had tried to unlearn. “And I’ll show thee something better.”

She led him on tiptoe to the window. They peeped in.

Catherine knelt on the hassock, her “hours” open before her. The room had that quiet sound a praying room has: the faint rustle of page, the whisper of breath, the soft creak of wood as a body shifts under devotion. Her shoulders moved with the last of recent sobbing.

“Folk can pray out of a cave,” whispered Margaret.

“Ay,” Gerard whispered, and his throat tightened, “and hit heaven with their prayers; for ’tis for a sight of thee she prayeth, and thou art here. Now—be prepared. She is not the woman you knew her; her children’s troubles have greatly broken the brisk, light-hearted soul. And I see she has been weeping e’en now. She will have given thee up, being so late.”

“Let me get to her,” he said hastily, trembling all over. “That door! I will bide here.”

When he went toward the door, Margaret, fearing the shock of sudden joy might strike the older woman down, rapped once, sharp, on the pane, and cried “Mother!” with such a tone that Catherine understood at once.

Catherine clasped her hands; she was half risen from her knees when the door burst open and Gerard flung himself down at her feet, arms out as if he would climb back into childhood.

A cry came from her—one of those cries that no other throat can make, because no other heart has had the same labour.

“Ah! my darling, my darling!”

She clung to his neck and sobbed, and in her embrace she did not hold a friar, a hermit, a priest, a famous preacher. She held her child—lost, despaired of, given back.

After a little while Margaret came in with wet eyes and cheeks, the baby heavy and warm on her arm. She stood a moment, letting the mother and son have their flood; then, by degrees, a holy calm of affection settled on them all, like a steadying hand on a boiling pot.

They sat together—three of them—hand in hand. They murmured their sweet and loving converse, with pauses in which the house spoke: the candle’s faint hiss, the wind outside, the muffled night sounds of Gouda that a man cannot hear from a cave. Gerard, seated between them, drank right and left of their true affection and their humble, genuine wisdom.

And because love is not all air and vow but also bread and warmth, they forced him to eat a good nourishing meal: food with substance in it, laid before him as a duty no less than prayer. The smell of it—simple, honest fare—rose into him like life returning. At daybreak they packed him off to a snowy bed.

He slept.

When he woke, it was as from a hideous dream—friar and hermit no more, Clement no more, but Gerard Eliassoen, parson of Gouda.
