Martin Wittenhaagen stood planted at the stair-foot like an oak in winter, shoulders squared, bow bent and drawn so far the goose-feathered shaft nearly kissed the head. His knife lay ready behind his back, a cold comfort against warm blood. When the men blundered in again—breathing hard, boots slick with thawed snow and ditch-water—without Gerard among them, wonder struck him clean through. The cord slackened; the bow lowered. He stared, mouth open, as if he had bitten into something that should have been sweet and found it bitter.

They stopped short as well, their eyes taking in the posture they had walked into.

“Why, mates,” one muttered, half-laughing, half-angered, “was the old fellow making ready to shoot at us?”

“Stuff!” said Martin, hauling his face back into its usual dull mask. His voice came out thick as barley porridge left too long on the fire. “I was but trying my new string. There! I’ll unstring my bow, if you think that.”

“Humph!” Dierich answered, suspicion sharp as vinegar. “There is something more in you than I understand. Put a log on, and let us dry our hides a bit ere we go.”

A log went down; then another. Soon the hearth roared. Resin in the wood cracked and spat, sending up that sweet, piney breath that clings to hair and wool. The men crowded in, steaming like horses after a hard pull, their long hair and sodden jerkins beginning to smoke. Water ran off them and hissed on the stones. The room filled with the homely racket of boots thumping, leather creaking, breath whistling through teeth, and the fire’s own busy music—a soundscape that could have been comfort, if every snap had not seemed a warning.

Then the shrieks came.

Not from the wind, nor from any beast in the dark yard, but from Margaret’s room above—thin, raw cries that cut through the hearth-crackle as a knife slices sinew.

Every man started. One seized the candle, its tallow already softening from the heat, and ran up the steps. The flame jerked and flared, throwing greasy light on the walls.

Martin moved too—too quickly for an old man who had just been “trying a string.” Confusion—and something worse—showed itself in him. He lurched after the candle-bearer, as if to bar the way with his body.

Dierich caught the meaning of that at once. He sprang behind Martin and locked both arms around him like a clamp.

“Hold him!” Dierich barked to the others, his breath loud in Martin’s ear. “Keep him!”

The man with the candle wrenched free of the tangle and went on, his feet drumming overhead. The rest fell upon Martin in a heaving mass. They struggled long and fiercely, the hearth’s heat at their backs, sweat and wet wool stinging their noses. More than once they all rolled on the floor—elbows in ribs, knees on thighs—with Martin in the middle, as if the old man were the hard knot in a net they could not loosen.

For all his age, he fought like a man saving his last loaf from hungry mouths. He growled low, a sound like a dog guarding a pot, and the rope they carried—meant for Gerard—was needed at last. They wound it around him, rough hemp biting into skin, and bound him hand and foot until the great arms that had once lifted barrels lay useless as tied hams on the slab.

Martin groaned aloud—not only from the rope’s cruel bite, but from what he knew was happening above. During the struggle, the candle had gone on into Margaret’s room, and now he was powerless to follow, powerless to stop any eyes and hands that might pry.

“Ay, grind your teeth, you old rogue,” Dierich said, panting, hair sticking to his brow with sweat and smoke. “You shan’t use them.”

“It is my belief, mates,” said another, rubbing a bruised wrist, “that our lives were scarce safe while this old fellow’s bones were free.”

“He makes me think this Gerard is not far off,” put in a third, glancing toward the stair as if expecting some shadow to slide down it.

“No such luck,” Dierich answered, with the sour certainty of a man who has bought bad meat and will not admit it disappoints him. Then, after a moment, his eyes narrowed. “Hallo, mates. Jorian Ketel is a long time in that girl’s bedroom. Best go and see after him, some of us.”

A rude laugh went up—coarse as spilt ale on rushes—trying to turn fear into sport. Yet beneath it the house listened: the fire clicking; the wind finding cracks in the shutters; the bound man’s breath coming heavy through his nose. Even the candlelight upstairs seemed to have its own voice, a faint, wavering hiss in the minds of those below.

The laugh had hardly died when rapid footsteps ran along overhead—quick, uneven, as if someone were fleeing not for play but for life. The sound skittered across the planks, and every man’s head tilted, hunters reading the forest by ear.

“Oh, here he comes, at last,” Dierich called up, his tone too loud, as if to drown a doubt he would not name. “Well, Jorian—what is to do now up there?”
