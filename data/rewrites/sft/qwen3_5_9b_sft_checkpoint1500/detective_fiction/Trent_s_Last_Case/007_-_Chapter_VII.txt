The sea roared violently against the base of the cliff, driven by a steady breeze, while the sun poured a dappled, vibrant life onto the landscape beneath a blue sky. Trent, having struggled with poor sleep, descended before eight to a secluded pool nestled among the rocks. Following directions he had received, he plunged into the crystal-clear water. Navigating past massive grey boulders, he swam outward into the churning surf, battling a coastal current for a short distance before returning to his rocky sanctuary, battered but revitalized. Ten minutes later, he was scaling the cliff face again. His mind, momentarily cleansed of a profound distaste for the case he now held, began to organize his plans for the day.

It was the day of the inquest, the second day since his arrival. After his encounter with the American on the road to Bishopsbridge, he had taken little action until the afternoon. Accompanied by Inspector Cupples, he had ventured from the inn into the town, making specific purchases at a chemist's shop, holding private consultations with a photographer, dispatching a reply-paid telegram, and conducting inquiries at the telephone exchange. He had revealed very little of the case to Cupples, who remained conspicuously uncurious, disclosing nothing regarding his investigative findings or intended next steps. Upon returning from Bishopsbridge, Trent had composed a lengthy dispatch for *The Record*, sending it via the newspaper's local representative. Later, he had dined with Cupples and spent the remainder of the evening in solitary meditation on the veranda.

As he climbed the cliff that morning, Trent reflected that he had never undertaken a case so detested, nor one that consumed him so completely. Contemplating it in the golden sunlight, the affair appeared increasingly malevolent and defiant. Every suspicion and fragment of knowledge that plagued his searching brain for hours, denying him sleep, seemed sharper in this brilliant light. Though washed in the fierce purity of the sea, he perceived the darkness of the guilt he believed existed with terrifying clarity, and was acutely repelled by the motive he merely guessed at. Yet, his zeal had reawakened; the instinct of the hunter quickened within him. He resolved neither to slacken nor to hold back; no scruples would apply here. By the end of the day, he hoped, his net would be cast. He had urgent work ahead, and with vivid anticipation, though little serious hope, he awaited the response to the telegram he had launched into the sky the previous day.

The path winding back to the hotel followed the cliff's summit. Approaching a spot he had marked from the sea level, where the face of the cliff had collapsed centuries ago, Trent stepped to the edge. He looked down, hoping to track the delicate, beautiful dance of water washing over broken rock. But there was no rock below. A few feet down, a broad ledge protruded, resembling a rough platform the size of a large room, thickly carpeted with wiry grass and enclosed on three sides by steep slopes. Near the precipice where the cliff finally dropped away vertically, a woman sat. Her arms encircled her drawn-up knees, her gaze fixed on the trailing smoke of a distant liner, her face lost in a deep reverie.

To Trent, whose training demanded he live within the realm of vision, she constituted the most beautiful image he had ever encountered. Her skin, of a southern pallor, bore the faint flush of the wind's kiss on her cheek, presenting a profile of delicate regularity devoid of harsh lines. Nevertheless, her black brows, bending downward toward a point where they nearly met, lent her a look of severity in repose, a quality strangely redeemed by the soft, open curve of her mouth. Trent mused internally that the merit of a lover composing sonnets to a mistress's eyebrow ultimately rested on the quality of that brow. Her nose was straight and refined, miraculously escaping the perils of excessive length, a flaw that often shames the conscientious observer. Her hat lay pinned to the grass beside her, and the brisk breeze played with her thick, dark hair, blowing back the two broad bandeaux that should have concealed much of her forehead and agitating a hundred tiny curls gathered at the nape of her neck. Everything about this lady was black—from her suede shoes to the discarded hat—and lustreless black clad her to her bare throat. All she wore was exquisite and impeccably arranged. Despite the dreamy, delicate spirit her features suggested, it was evident she was long-practiced in the art of dress, possessing the primal joy of a woman who understands the excellence of the human form. Her posture, with embraced knees, highlighted an admirable curvature. With a distinct suggestion of French taste, she made a very modern figure, until one gazed upon her face and witnessed the glow and triumph inherent in all vigorous beings facing sun, wind, and sea together in the height of summer. One also sensed a womanhood so unmixed and vigorous, so unconsciously assured of herself, as to seem scarcely English, and certainly not American.

Trent had paused only briefly, surprised by the sight of the woman in black, before passing her on the cliff above. As he moved, his keen vision and active brain seized details with a marvelous, effortless swiftness that astounded those of slower temperament; to stare, he believed, was evidence of blindness. Now, the feeling of beauty was awakened and exultant, doubling the potency of his senses. In these fleeting instants, a picture was etched onto his memory that would never fade.

Passing unheard upon the turf, the woman, still alone with her thoughts, suddenly moved. She unclasped her long hands from around her knees, stretching her limbs and body with feline grace. Slowly, she raised her head and extended her arms with open, curving fingers, as if gathering to herself all the glory and overwhelming sanity of the morning. This was unmistakable: a gesture of freedom, the movement of a soul resolving to exist, to possess, to move forward, perhaps to enjoy.

Thus she was seen for an instant as he passed, and he did not turn. He knew instantly who the woman must be, and it was as if a curtain of gloom had been drawn between him and the splendor of the day.

At breakfast, Mr. Cupples found Trent reluctant to speak, offering a restless night as an excuse. Conversely, Cupples was in a state of bird-like alertness. The prospect of the inquest seemed to enliven him. He entertained Trent with a discourse on the history of the ancient and once-busy tribunal of the coroner's court, remarking on its enviable freedom from the shackles of rigid rule and precedent. From this, he transitioned to the case awaiting judgment that morning.

"Young Bunner mentioned to me last night," he said, "after I visited him following dinner, the hypothesis he advances regarding the crime. A remarkably young man, Trent. His meaning is occasionally obscure, but in my estimation, he possesses a clear-headed knowledge of the world quite unusual for one of his apparent age. Indeed, his promotion by Manderson to the position of his principal lieutenant speaks for itself. He seems to have assumed, with perfect confidence, control—at this end of the wire, as he phrases it—of the complex business situation caused by the death of his principal. He has advised very wisely concerning the steps I should take on Mabel's behalf and the best course for her to pursue until the provisions of the will are executed. Consequently, I was less disposed than I might otherwise have been to regard his suggestion of an industrial vendetta as far-fetched. When I questioned him, he was able to describe numerous instances wherein attacks of various sorts—often successful—have been made upon the lives of individuals who incurred the hostility of powerful labor organizations. This is a terrible era in which we live, my dear boy. There is no recorded period in history, I think, wherein the disparity between the material and moral constituents of society has been so great or so menacing to the permanence of the social fabric. But nowhere, in my judgment, does the prospect appear so dark as it does in the United States."

"I thought," said Trent listlessly, "that Puritanism was about as dominant there as the money-grubbing craze."

"Your remark," answered Mr. Cupples, approaching humor as closely as his nature permitted, "does not constitute a testimonial to what you term Puritanism—a convenient, if inaccurate, designation; for I need not remind you that it was coined to describe an Anglican faction aiming to purge the services and rituals of their Church from elements repugnant to them. However, the sense of your observation remains sound, and its truth is extremely well illustrated by the case of Manderson himself, who, I believe, possessed the virtues of purity, abstinence, and self-restraint in their strongest forms. No, Trent, there are other and more worthy attributes among the moral constituents I described. And in our finite nature, the more we preoccupy ourselves with the bewildering complexity of external apparatus which science places in our hands, the less vigor remain for the development of the holier purposes of humanity within us. Agricultural machinery has abolished the festival of the Harvest Home. Mechanical travel has abolished the inn, or all that was best in it. I need not multiply examples. The view I express to you," pursued Mr. Cupples, placidly buttering a slice of toast, "is considered fundamentally erroneous by many who think generally as I do about the deeper concerns of life, yet I am firmly persuaded of its truth."
