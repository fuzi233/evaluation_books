It was the thirtieth of October, 1496. The morning sky was clear, and a gentle autumn wind stirred the leaves along the city’s narrow streets. But the Florentines had little thought for such comforts. Their minds were gripped by storms at sea—gales that had scattered ships from Marseilles carrying soldiers and grain, leaving Florence stranded in desperation. Famine stalked her alleys; hunger gnawed at every door. And beyond the walls, enemies gathered. The French king, once hailed as a new Charlemagne, had swept through Italy, seized Naples with ease, and then vanished—some said broken-hearted over the death of his newborn son, others whispering he would never return. In his absence, a Holy League had formed under Pope Borgia to expel the “barbarians” still holding Naples. It sounded noble, but many saw it as wolves plotting to drive out one another, only to turn on the weakest prey. Florence, alone among the great Italian states, refused to join. She clung to her alliance with France, though now it seemed like a fatal choice.

The price was high. Pisa, still fighting fiercely for independence, was backed not only by Venice and Milan but also by Emperor Maximilian, who had been invited by the League. His forces joined those already besieging Leghorn, while Venetian and Genoese fleets blockaded the coast. If Leghorn fell, Florence would be cut off from the sea—her last lifeline severed. Already, she was hemmed in on land by hostile powers: the Pope’s enmity, smaller states’ jealousy, and the growing unrest within her own walls.

Yet Florence did not break. Her government, though strained, showed courage. Taxes were raised, new soldiers enlisted, and embassies sent—not just to plead, but to reason, to conciliate. Even more remarkably, they chose not to bar the gates against starving peasants and beggars fleeing other cities. These poor souls poured into Florence like birds seeking warmth after winter. It was a bold act—especially since Savonarola’s followers held strong sway in the council—and it drew sharp criticism. Many argued that Florence should have joined the League, united with her neighbors, rather than risk isolation. They said she should protect her own people, not invite disease and despair with every ragged stranger.

But each day brought deeper suffering. Each day, the murmurs grew louder. And now, for over a month, Fra Girolamo had not preached—his voice silenced by a papal decree. Yet when news came that the grain ships were lost, the people clamored for him again. Faith, they believed, was their only weapon. Against Rome’s orders, the Signoria summoned Savonarola back to the Duomo. Two days prior, he had stood once more in the pulpit and spoken with fire and calm: *Wait. Be steadfast. God will come.* He even offered to let them strip his robe from him if divine help did not arrive—proof enough of his conviction.

But on this morning, there was no sign of deliverance. Only cold winds and empty granaries.

Still, hope flickered. For centuries, the Florentines had turned to the Madonna dell’Impruneta in times of crisis—drought, plague, war, famine. Her image, hidden within a sacred tabernacle, had been carried into the city, and each time, relief followed. The faithful believed she was a mother who answered their pleas, who bent her ear to their sorrows. The saying went: *Florence has a Madonna who will do what they please.* Now, more than ever, they needed her.

And so, the night before, the tabernacle had been brought from L’Impruneta—six miles outside the city gate toward Rome—with solemn procession and guarded escort. It now rested in San Gaggio, awaiting the dawn ceremony when all the fraternities, guilds, and officials would carry it through the streets to the Duomo. But the Mother had not yet entered the city. The morning broke as it had every day: with hunger, fear, and silence.

Pestilence crept close behind famine. Hospitals overflowed. Courtyards of private homes became makeshift infirmaries. Still, the desperate remained unsheltered. And early that morning, as always, members of the burial brotherhoods walked the streets, collecting bodies left by the roadside—those too weak or forgotten to be found.

Among the quiet figures moving through the city was Romola de’ Bardi. Dressed in plain black serge, her head covered with a simple veil that concealed her hair except for the golden bands framing her brow, she walked from the Ponte Vecchio toward Por’ Santa Maria. Her steps were steady, her presence calm—like a quiet stream flowing through dry ground.

Her path was blocked by a bier being carried by members of San Jacopo del Popolo. The men paused, bending to examine something on the pavement. A crowd of idle workers gathered around, their faces drawn thin by hunger.

“He’s dead,” one declared. “Messer Domeneddio has taken him.”

“Ah,” said another, “if we could all stretch our legs and lie down, heads forward, it would be better than standing upright on empty stomachs.”

“Poor old man,” someone else muttered. “Death got a bad bargain. Life won.”

“No Florentine,” snapped a third. “A beggar from Siena. San Giovanni save us! They don’t need armies to fight us—they send us armies of starving men.”

“No,” said another, “this one’s from the Stinche. I know the grey patch where the prison badge used to be.”

“Quiet!” called a brother. “Help lift him. We’re wasting time.”

“But is he really dead?” asked one. “He might just be faint from hunger.”

Romola stepped forward. “Let me try wine,” she said softly.

She opened a small flask at her belt and, with practiced hands, slipped a tiny ivory tube between the man’s teeth. A few drops of wine trickled in. Then more. Slowly, the old man stirred. His head turned slightly. His eyes opened—dark, deep-set, clouded with exhaustion.

And then Romola froze.

Those eyes—wild, lined, shadowed by age—were unmistakable. She had seen them before: in the Duomo, the day Tito wore armor for the first time. In Piero’s sketch, where Tito flinched from a hand reaching out. This was the prisoner who had escaped—Baldassarre, the man whose fate had haunted her dreams.

A shiver ran through her. Was this the moment she would learn the truth? The secret that might unravel everything?

She felt the urge to flee—but then, stronger, the duty to stay. Her husband had harmed this man. She could not turn away.

She kept her right hand ready with the flask, her left supporting his neck. Her fingers trembled, but her instincts moved without thought.

Baldassarre looked up at her. For the first time, he saw the woman who lived in the Via de’ Bardi—the wife he had longed to see, denied by the walls of grief and silence.

“Shall we take him to San Stefano?” Romola asked gently. “We can move on, and you can continue your task.”

They walked thirty yards to the church steps. By then, Baldassarre could prop himself against the doorway. The brothers moved on, but the crowd lingered—curious, restless, hungry.

“Ah,” someone said, “he ate well in the Stinche. That’s why he’s weak now.”

“_Gnaffè_,” grumbled another. “That’s why the Eight turned some prisoners loose—to make room for honest folk. But if every thief gets revived with wine and bread, maybe we Ciompi should drown ourselves in the Arno while the water’s still running.”

Romola sat beside the old man. “Can you eat a little bread?” she asked. “I must go to the hospital, but I’ll return. Will you wait?”

He stared blankly, repeating her words: “Come back.”

She understood—he was fading. But she hoped he grasped the promise. She opened her basket—soft loaves inside—and placed one in his hand.

“Madonna,” said a rough man in a red night-cap, pushing through the crowd, “you save your bread for sick women and children. Why give it to a thief who won’t eat?”

“If he doesn’t want it,” replied another, “let him be. He’s better off than us—stomach full of cravings, no food.”

“Yes,” said a young man in fine cloth, “if a man wants to die, we shouldn’t revive him. Dead men don’t need trenchers.”

“Oh,” laughed the first, “you don’t understand the Frate’s charity. He preaches to the birds, like Saint Anthony. He says hawks are made to feed sparrows—just as good Florentines should feed six beggars from Arezzo or Bologna. That madonna there? She’s a pious Piagnone. She won’t waste her bread on honest citizens who’ve got nothing but prophecies to swallow.”

“Come, madonna,” said the man in the cap, “the old thief won’t eat. You’d better give it to us. We fast so much—we’re half saints already.”

The circle closed tight. Romola reached into her basket, dipped bread in wine, and prepared a soft sop. She did not look at them.

Then she rose.

Her gaze swept the faces before her. Pale, hollow-eyed, gaunt. Some watched her with hunger, others with defiance.

She held out the basket.

“Hunger is hard to bear,” she said quietly. “You have the power to take this bread. It was meant for sick women and children. You are strong. But strength does not excuse taking from the weak. You may take from me—but not from him.”

Silence.

No one moved.

The man in the night-cap shifted awkwardly, bumped his neighbor with an elbow. Others stepped back. The young man in fine clothes disappeared.

Then, the armed guards of the Signoria arrived—patrolling ahead of the coming procession. They moved in, dispersing the crowd.

Cecco, the man who had spoken last, retreated up the steps, avoiding a threatening mace. Then he turned to Romola.

“Madonna,” he said respectfully, “if you wish to go, I’ll watch the old man.”

He was a wild figure—ragged tunic, bare arms, bristling beard, a face like it had been pressed flat. A scrap of red cloth tied over his ears.

Romola hesitated.

“I’m not so handsome as you,” Cecco said, understanding her doubt, “but I’ve got an old mother who eats my porridge. There’s a heart in me—believe it. And see? The old fellow’s eating. He’ll walk soon enough.”

Romola smiled faintly. “Thank you,” she said. “I’m sorry I doubted.”

She leaned toward Baldassarre. “Wait for me,” she whispered. “I’ll return.”

He nodded—just a slight motion of head and hand.

And Romola walked on, toward the hospital of San Matteo in the Piazza di San Marco.
