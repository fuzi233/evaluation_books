September 1811. A post-chaise rattled to a halt before the iron gates of Aswarby Hall, deep within Lincolnshire. The sole passenger, a small boy, leaped out, his eyes wide with a sharp, devouring curiosity as he scanned the grounds. Before him rose a towering, square fortress of red brick, constructed in the reign of Queen Anne, though softened by a stone-pillared porch in the sleek, classical taste of 1790. Tall, narrow windows with tiny panes and heavy white frames dotted the façade, crowned by a pediment holding a solitary round window. Wings extended to either side, linked by strange glass galleries resting on columns, housing the stables and offices beneath domed cupolas topped with gilded vanes.

A pale, autumnal light bathed the scene, turning the countless window panes into glowing embers. Beyond the hall, a flat park sprawled out, speckled with ancient oaks and bordered by dark firs that cut stark silhouettes against the sky. In the distance, nestled among trees on the park's edge, the church tower's clock struck six. Its golden weathercock caught the fading light, and the tolling sound drifted gently down the wind. It was a scene of quiet beauty, yet it carried a heavy, melancholic weight appropriate to early autumn—a feeling that settled deeply into the mind of the boy waiting in the shadow of the porch.

The carriage had brought him from Warwickshire, where he had been abandoned as an orphan six months prior. Now, thanks to the generous offer of his elderly cousin, Mr. Abney, he was to reside at Aswarby. To the local community, this arrangement was baffling. Mr. Abney was known as an austere recluse, a man whose household seemed impervious to change, let alone the intrusion of a child. Little was understood of his true nature or obsessions, save for rumors that the Professor of Greek at Cambridge knew more of pagan religions than any scholar alive. His library was a trove of forbidden knowledge: texts on the Mysteries, Orphic poetry, the cult of Mithras, and Neoplatonism. In his marble-floored hall stood a magnificent statue of Mithras slaying a bull, imported at great cost from the Levant. He was a man consumed by his books, a puzzle to his neighbors, and the news that he had volunteered to take in his orphan cousin, Stephen Elliott, seemed to defy all logic.

Yet, whatever the neighbors suspected, the reality inside Aswarby Hall was different. Mr. Abney—tall, gaunt, severe in demeanor—met his young cousin with a warmth that surprised everyone. The moment the front door swung open, he burst from his study, rubbing his hands in delighted anticipation.

"How are you, my boy?" he exclaimed, his voice eager. "How are you? How old are you?" He paused, perhaps sensing the boy's fatigue, and softened his tone. "Are you not too weary from your journey to eat your supper?"

"No, thank you, sir," Stephen replied, straightening his jacket. "I am pretty well."

"That's a good lad," Abney murmured, stepping closer. "And how old are you, my boy?"

It struck Stephen as peculiar that the question was asked twice within the span of two minutes.

"I'm twelve years old next birthday, sir," Stephen answered.

"And when is your birthday?" Abney pressed, his eyes gleaming behind his spectacles. "Eleventh of September, eh? That suits me down to the ground—that is very well. Nearly a year hence, isn't it? I like—hah, hah!—I like to record these facts in my book. Are you certain you are twelve?"

"Yes, quite sure, sir."

"Excellent. Well!" Abney clapped his hands lightly. "Take him to Mrs. Bunch's room, Parkes. Let him have his tea—or supper, whatever it may be."

"Yes, sir," responded Mr. Parkes, the staid butler, and guided Stephen downward to the service quarters.

Mrs. Bunch proved to be the most comforting presence Stephen had encountered since leaving his parents. Within a quarter-hour, they became fast friends, a bond that endured throughout his stay. Born in the neighborhood fifty-five years prior, she had served the Hall for twenty years. If anyone knew the secrets of the house and the surrounding district, it was Mrs. Bunch, and she possessed no reluctance in sharing them.

Stephen, naturally inquisitive and adventurous, quickly filled his mind with questions about the estate. "Who built the temple at the end of the laurel walk?" he asked later. "Who was the old man whose portrait hangs on the staircase, seated at a table with a skull beneath his hand?" Mrs. Bunch, with her formidable intellect, unraveled these mysteries with ease. Yet, there were darker tales she offered with hesitation, stories where the explanations were unsatisfying, bordering on the supernatural.

One November evening, Stephen sat by the fire in the housekeeper's room, staring into the flames, reflecting on his strange surroundings. Suddenly, a question tore from his lips, driven by the peculiar, absolute trust children hold in the moral compass of adults. "Is Mr. Abney a good man, and will he go to heaven?"

"Good? Bless the child!" Mrs. Bunch cried, her face lighting up. "Master is as kind a soul as I have ever seen! Did I not tell you of the little boy he took from the streets seven years ago? And the little girl, two years after I first came here?"

"No," Stephen insisted, leaning forward. "Do tell me everything about them, Mrs. Bunch—right now!"

"Well," Mrs. Bunch sighed, settling back, "the little girl... I cannot recall her clearly. I know Master brought her back from his walk one day and gave strict orders to Mrs. Ellis, who was housekeeper then, to watch over her carefully. She told me she had no family left. She lived with us for about three weeks, perhaps a month. Then, one morning, she vanished from her bed before anyone had opened their eyes. Neither tracks nor traces of her have I seen since. Master was devastated; he had every pond drained and searched. I believe she was spirited away by gypsies. There was singing around the house for an hour the night she disappeared, and Parkes declared he heard them calling in the woods all afternoon. She was such a quiet child, so domesticated, it was heartbreaking."

"And the little boy?" Stephen asked.

"Oh, that poor boy!" Mrs. Bunch's voice dropped to a whisper. "He was a foreigner—he called himself Jevanny. One winter day, he was playing his hurdy-gurdy along the drive, and Master took him in immediately. He asked every question imaginable: where he came from, his age, his lineage, treating the boy with infinite kindness. But it ended the same way. They are a rowdy lot, those foreign nations, I suppose. He was gone one fine morning, just as the girl had been. We wondered where he went and what he did for nearly a year afterward. He never took his hurdy-gurdy again; it sits on the shelf to this day."

For the rest of the evening, Stephen cross-examined Mrs. Bunch and attempted to coax a tune from the forgotten instrument.

That night, Stephen dreamed a nightmare. At the end of the corridor leading to his bedroom, there existed an old, disused bathroom. It was usually locked, but the upper half of the door was glazed. Since the muslin curtains had long since rotted away, the interior was visible: a lead-lined bath fixed to the right-hand wall, facing the window.

On that night, Stephen found himself standing before the glazed pane, peering in. The moonlight streamed through the window, illuminating a figure lying within the tub.

His vision haunted him with a memory of the infamous vaults beneath St. Michan's Church in Dublin, where corpses were preserved from decay for centuries. The figure was impossibly thin and pitiful, colored like dust and lead, swaddled in a shroud-like garment. Thin lips curled into a faint, dreadful smile, and hands were pressed tightly over the hollow of the heart.

As Stephen stared, a distant, almost inaudible moan seemed to emanate from the lips, and the arms twitched. Terror seized him, forcing him backward. He jolted awake on the cold, boarded floor of the passage, bathed in the moonlight. With a courage rare for a boy of his age, he approached the bathroom door to check if the phantom was real. The space was empty. He returned to his bed, his heart pounding.

The next morning, Mrs. Bunch was deeply affected by his story. She went so far as to replace the missing muslin curtain over the glazed door. Mr. Abney, to whom Stephen confessed the dream at breakfast, listened with intense interest and made notes in what he termed "his book."

As the spring equinox approached, Mr. Abney frequently reminded Stephen that the ancients regarded this time as critical for youth, advising caution and the closing of bedroom windows at night, citing Censorinus.
