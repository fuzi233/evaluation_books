The Baroness de Bernstein’s mood had shifted like the turn of a card by the next meal. Gone was the morning’s frost; in its place bloomed an amiable warmth that embraced the entire table—Harry Warrington included. She praised the cook’s fricassee, declared the Castlewood eels incomparable, and dismissed the notion of opening another bottle for an "old woman" like herself. When the fresh wine did arrive, she raised her glass to Madam Esmond Warrington of Virginia, then teased Harry about securing his mother’s blessing for an English bride.

Her chatter flowed like the wine—stories of her mother’s beauty, her happiness with Colonel Esmond despite the years between them. The table listened, each with private thoughts. Lady Castlewood and her children were silent. Harry, enraptured, hung on every word. Maria, ever dutiful, blinked in surprise. Lord Castlewood, though his face was a mask of contentment, watched his aunt with the wary patience of a man awaiting the reveal of a card trick.

The Baroness, with practiced charm, turned the conversation to her own childhood mischief. *"I was a horrid little creature,"* she confessed, laughing. *"If I’d been cross in the morning, I’d coax my parents by supper. I remember doing it right here—oh, centuries ago!—and we had collared eels, just like tonight."* She clasped her hands, a penitent girl of seventy. *"So you see, I’m only repeating history. I scolded my sister, snapped at her children, even boxed poor Harry’s ears for refusing Tunbridge Wells!"*

*"But I’ll go now, gladly!"* Harry interjected, too eager.

The Baroness beamed. *"There, you see? All forgiven. Maria, my dear, I owe you the deepest apology."*

Maria, ever the diplomat, murmured polite demurrals, but the Baroness pressed on. *"No, no, I was cruel. You offered to accompany me, and I refused you rudely. But now I beg—nay, command—you to come!"*

Lord Castlewood sipped his wine, his face unreadable. *"Lady Maria is of age to decide,"* he said smoothly.

*"Then it’s settled!"* the Baroness declared. *"Tunbridge shall be delightful. And Harry, you’ll escort us, of course? Protect us from highwaymen?"*

Harry’s face burned. *"It would be my honor,"* he managed, though his heart was a tumult. Two days riding beside her carriage! Two days of stolen glances, of her gloved hand resting on his as he helped her in and out. A week in Tunbridge—a week near her, even if not with her. It was a reprieve, a glimpse of heaven before the axe fell.

The cards were dealt again that night—not of love, but of luck. Will and the chaplain, desperate to recoup losses, pushed for higher stakes. Harry played with absent-minded precision, his thoughts across the courtyard where Maria’s lamp still glowed. He won, not from cunning, but because fortune favors the lovestruck. Will cursed; the chaplain groaned over his lost books and livestock. Harry barely noticed.

*"You’ll stay a day or two at the Wells,"* the Baroness had said. *"Then back to Castlewood and partridges."*

A week. A single, fleeting week. For now, that was enough.

As the clock struck midnight, Harry stood at his window, staring at the casement across the way. Behind it, his divinity slept. The stars above seemed to pulse in time with his heartbeat. Let Will rage over lost horses. Let the chaplain lament his pawned gown. None of it mattered.

For in that room, under that flickering lamp, lay the only treasure he cared to win.
