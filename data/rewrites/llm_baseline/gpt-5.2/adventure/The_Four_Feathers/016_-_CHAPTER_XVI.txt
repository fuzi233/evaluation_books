Ethne had forgotten that Colonel Durrance existed at all.

The moment Captain Willoughby pressed the little feather into her palm—once white, now yellowed and soiled with travel and weather—everything else slipped away from her like steam from a lifted lid. Harry Feversham filled the space in her mind with an almost bodily insistence: the angle of his head when he listened, the impatient kindness in his voice, the exact look of his eyes when pride fought fear. Willoughby’s plain account had gaps, and into each gap her memory poured its own broth—richer, hotter, spiced with things she had pretended for five years not to taste.

The sun lay on the garden as on a table laid for a feast: bright nasturtiums spilling colour, roses heavy as jam, the air sugared with late-summer bloom. Yet Ethne was nowhere in that calm. She was back among the raw years she had endured upright, greeting the world with a smile as one serves guests when the pantry is bare—until she lifted her head and saw Durrance standing at the hedge-gap, his stick in his hand, his face turned in that direction which so cruelly imitated sight.

“Hush,” she breathed.

The word came out like the first warning of a kettle just before it boils over. Her face blanched; for a moment she shut her eyes tight, as though pain were a taste she could refuse by closing her mouth. But there was no leisure for weakness. Those minutes with Willoughby had been a holiday—an illicit sweetness. Now she must take up again the daily discipline of her life, the careful management of appearances, the hard economy of expression. If she could not forget—she knew, in that instant, that she never would—then she must do the next best thing: show nothing.

Durrance must go on believing that she brought more than friendship into the marriage she had promised him. He must not learn that Harry Feversham’s disgrace had been met and answered. He must not be given the knowledge that would compel him—because he was Durrance—to do what was “right” at his own expense.

Willoughby shifted on the bench. Ethne caught his sleeve.

“Keep still,” she whispered. “Do you know him?”

“Of course. Three years together at Suakin,” Willoughby murmured, his eyes on Durrance. “I heard he’d gone blind. I’m glad it’s not true.”

“Speak lower.” Her fingers tightened. “It is true. He is blind.”

Willoughby stared. Durrance’s walk was too sure, the easy swing of his body too confident.

“One would never have thought it,” Willoughby breathed. “Consolations seem so—what can I say to him?”

“Say nothing,” Ethne answered, and the firmness of it had the edge of a knife. “Not a word. Not a movement.”

Durrance stood just inside the enclosure, the clipped hedge-wall behind him and the creek glinting beyond. He did not peer about like a blind man. He simply lifted his head, as though the garden’s hush were a map.

“Ethne,” he said—rather than called; and the quiet, unquestioning tone made it worse, made the illusion of sight almost complete.

“It’s impossible,” Willoughby muttered. “He’s looking straight at us.”

“He sees nothing.” Ethne did not take her gaze from him. “But he listens.”

Again he said, louder, with doubt now threading his voice, “Ethne.”

“He isn’t sure,” Ethne whispered. “Do you hear? He mustn’t know you’re here.”

“Why?”

Because you are one of Harry Feversham’s ghosts, she thought, and I have built my life on persuading him there are none. Aloud she said only, “He must not.”

Willoughby did not question further. Something in her stillness commanded him. She sat hunched a little, as if she were trying to make herself smaller than her own beating heart. She held her breath, and the fear in her eyes infected him—an excitement without cause except her apprehension. A dry leaf scraping faintly against a hedge sounded to him as loud as a spoon dropped on stone.

“He’s coming towards us,” he whispered.

“Not a word,” she said, barely shaping the syllables. “Not a movement.”

Durrance advanced into the enclosure. Ethne and Willoughby watched him with the fixed stare of people who are ashamed of hiding but more afraid of being found. He walked past the bench and stopped—stopped facing them, the blind man’s eyes turned exactly where their faces were.

Surely, Willoughby thought, surely he sees.

Durrance’s eyes rested on them with a steadiness that made Ethne, for one dizzy second, doubt everything she knew. He seemed about to speak. Then, still in that first quiet voice, he said, “Ethne,” once more.

No answer came. His shoulders lifted in a small shrug, as though he were ridiculing his own uncertainty. He turned towards the creek, his back to them.

But Ethne had lived long enough beside him now to read what sight no longer gave her in his face. The poise of his head, the careless swing of the stick—no, not careless: performed carelessness. He was listening as a musician listens for the faintest returning note in an echoing hall. Every sound in the enclosure mattered: the brush of fabric, the click of a shoe on stone, the soft exhale of breath. The garden’s acoustic was suddenly a trap—hedges hemming the air, creek water holding a low, continuous murmur like the simmer at the back of a stove.

Her grip on Willoughby’s arm tightened until his sleeve creased.

For a full minute they held the silence, pressing themselves into it.

Then Durrance turned sharply and took a quick step towards the bench.

Ethne did not move. Not a fold of her skirt whispered. Willoughby, pinned by her hand, did not shift.

“I wonder where in the world she can be,” Durrance said aloud to himself; and with that he walked back out through the hedge-gap and was gone.

Only when his footsteps had faded beyond the enclosure did Ethne release Willoughby.

“That was a close shave,” he said, his voice too loud in the aftermath. “Suppose he’d sat down on the top of us?”

“Why suppose, since he did not?” Ethne replied, and the calmness was something she forced upon herself like composure forced on a face before one goes out to guests.

She looked down at the feather in her hand. She held it carefully, as if it were a fragile herb dried long ago and saved in paper—something that could crumble if pressed too hard, yet still keep its scent if protected.

“You’ve told me everything?” she asked.

“So far as I remember.”

“And all you’ve told me happened in the spring?”

“The spring of last year.”

She rose. “Then answer me this. Why did you not bring it to me last summer?”

Willoughby, taken aback by the bluntness, rubbed his jaw. “My leave was short. I spent it in the hills north of Suakin—after ibex.”

“I see,” said Ethne, softly, and the words tasted bitter. Last summer she had been free. Last summer one word from Willoughby, one visit, would have altered the whole recipe of her life. The cry rose in her—Why didn’t you come?—but she did not let it reach her tongue. She kept her voice even, the way one keeps a sauce from breaking by refusing sudden heat.

“I hope you had good sport,” she said.

“It wasn’t bad.”

“You might have written.”

“Feversham didn’t suggest there was any hurry,” Willoughby returned. “A letter would have been a long business—hard to explain. He asked me to see you when I had an opportunity, and I had none before now. To tell the truth, I thought I might find he’d come back before me.”

“Oh, no,” Ethne answered quickly. “There could be no possibility of that. The other two feathers still remain to be redeemed before he will ask me to take back mine.”

Willoughby shook his head, the motion slow. “Feversham can never persuade Castleton and Trench to cancel their accusations as he persuaded me.”

“Why not?”

“Major Castleton was killed when the square was broken at Tamai.”

“Killed?” Ethne echoed.

And then, against every rule of gracious feeling, she laughed—a short laugh, clean and satisfied, like the snap of a dry twig underfoot.

Willoughby stared at her, disbelieving.

But her face held no shame. She was a Celt; death, to her, did not always carry the weight other people demanded of it. And she could hate. She had agreed with Castleton and Trench once—she had given her feather to show that agreement—but agreement had never been affection. The men who had pushed Harry to ruin, and her with him, were not granted gentleness in her heart.

“And Colonel Trench too?” she asked, the eagerness bare now.

“No,” Willoughby said. “You’re disappointed? He’s worse off than that. He was captured on a reconnaissance. He’s a prisoner in Omdurman.”

“Ah,” Ethne said.

Willoughby’s mouth tightened. “You can’t have any idea what captivity there implies. If you had, however much you disliked him, you’d feel some pity.”

“Not I,” Ethne said stubbornly.

“I will tell you something of what it does imply—”

“No,” she cut in sharply. “I don’t wish to hear of Colonel Trench.” She steadied her breath. “Besides, you must go. But first—tell me one thing. What became of Mr. Feversham after he gave you that feather?”

“I told him he’d done all that could be reasonably expected,” Willoughby answered. “He took my advice. He went aboard the first steamer that touched at Suakin on its way to Suez. He left the Soudan.”

“I must find out where he is,” Ethne said, and the words came like a vow. “He must come back. Did he need money?”

“No. He still drew his allowance from his father. He told me he had more than enough.”

“I am glad of that,” Ethne said, and she meant it with a fierce, practical tenderness—relief that hunger and debt were not among the wounds he carried.

She bade Willoughby wait in the enclosure and went out alone to make sure the way was clear.

The garden beyond was empty. Durrance had vanished from sight. The great stone terrace and the house with its striped sunblinds looked drugged by midday heat, a place where even footsteps would feel like impertinence. It was nearing one o’clock; the birds were quiet in the trees, as if their own songs had been spent early and now they rested their throats. The silence struck Ethne as almost unnatural, like a kitchen at noon when the fire has been damped and every pot is covered.

Only the bees kept working, humming drowsily over the flowerbeds, and from the far meadow beyond the creek a boy’s voice called now and then—thin, bright, and distant.

She returned to Willoughby.

“You can go now,” she said. “I cannot pretend friendship for you, Captain Willoughby, but it was kind of you to find me out and tell me your story. You are going back at once to Kingsbridge? I hope so. For I do not wish Colonel Durrance to know of your visit—or anything you have told me.”

“Durrance was Feversham’s friend,” Willoughby objected. “His great friend.”

“He is quite unaware that any feathers were sent to Mr. Feversham,” Ethne replied. “So there is no need he should be informed that one has been taken back. He does not know why my engagement to Mr. Feversham was broken off. I do not wish him to know. Your story would enlighten him—and he must not be enlightened.”

“Why?” Willoughby asked. Obstinate by nature, he wanted the reason before he consented to silence.

Ethne gave it at once, simply, without ornament.

“I am engaged to Colonel Durrance,” she said.

Fear lay underneath the words, controlled but present: fear that Durrance already suspected she offered him no more than friendship. If he learned that the fault which had broken her engagement had been bravely atoned, he would insist—because he was Durrance, because honour was the language he still spoke fluently in the dark—upon setting her free. He would strip himself of the one remaining comfort. And Ethne, bound by her promise, stubbornly refused to let him do it. She would not answer Harry’s redemption by flinging away her own honour as though it were a cheap ingredient.

Willoughby pursed his lips and whistled under his breath. “Engaged to Jack Durrance!” he exclaimed. “Then I seem to have wasted my time in bringing you that feather.”

He pointed towards her hand.

Ethne drew it back sharply, as though he might snatch it away. The feather lay across her palm like a pale, weightless thing and yet it anchored her as firmly as any chain.

“I am most grateful for it,” she said.

“It’s a bit of a muddle, isn’t it?” Willoughby remarked. “It seems a little rough on Feversham perhaps. It’s a little rough on Jack Durrance too, when you come to think of it.”

Then he looked at her, really looked: at the care with which she held the feather, at the glow that had lit her while she listened, at the hungry precision of her questions, as if she were tasting the truth in each answer.

“I shouldn’t wonder,” he added more quietly, “if it was rather rough on you too, Miss Eustace.”

Ethne did not answer. She walked with him out of the enclosure towards the creek where his boat was moored, hurrying him down the bank, determined he should slip away unobserved.

But she had forgotten Mrs. Adair.

All morning Mrs. Adair had watched Ethne’s movements with the relish of a woman who considers herself a custodian of propriety and enjoys the power of a window. From behind the drawing-room curtains she had seen Ethne meet Durrance at the terrace steps; she had watched them walk towards the estuary; she had noticed the boat run aground in the opening between the trees; she had seen a man disembark and Ethne go forward to meet him. She was not a woman to leave her post at such a moment. She stayed behind the curtain with the intentness of someone who draws down a blind to see better.

Ethne and the stranger disappeared among the trees, leaving Durrance waiting alone.

At once Mrs. Adair thought of the little enclosure by the water’s edge. The conversation lasted long enough to trouble her. Since they did not promptly reappear, a thought leapt up—sharp and eager.

Could the stranger be Harry Feversham?

Ethne had no friends in this part of the country. Mrs. Adair wanted confirmation, and—more than that—she wanted the particular confirmation that would condemn Ethne Eustace. Her excitement rose when she saw Durrance, tired of waiting, follow in Ethne’s direction. She leaned closer to the glass, the house behind her quiet as a paused orchestra, the outside world filled with subtle sounds she did not heed: bees, creek-water, distant calling.

What happened next fascinated her even more. Durrance returned alone and came straight into the house, up the terrace, into the drawing-room.

“Have you seen Ethne?” he asked.

“Is she not in the little garden by the water?” Mrs. Adair said.

“No. I went into it and called to her. It was empty.”

“Indeed?” Mrs. Adair replied, carefully smoothing the word as though it were a tablecloth. “Then I don’t know where she is. Are you going?”

“Yes—home.”

She made no effort to detain him then. But he did not immediately go. He stood by the window, idly swinging the blind-tassel, though Ethne would have said there was nothing idle in him now—not even that small, repetitive sound.

“I did not know until to-day that it was your plan that I should come home and Ethne stay with you until I found out whether a cure was likely or possible,” he said. “It was very kind of you, Mrs. Adair, and I am grateful.”

“It was a natural plan to propose as soon as I heard of your ill-luck.”

“And when was that?” he asked unconcernedly.

Mrs. Adair did not mistake his carelessness; she felt the hook beneath the bait. “The day after Calder’s telegram reached her from Wadi Halfa, I suppose.”

“Oh, so you knew of that telegram,” she said quickly. “I thought you did not.” Ethne had begged her not to mention it on the very day Durrance came back to England.

“Of course I knew of it,” Durrance returned; and without waiting for more he went out on to the terrace.

Mrs. Adair, left alone with her thoughts, dismissed for the moment the matter of the telegram. Another picture absorbed her: Durrance in the little garden calling Ethne aloud, while within a dozen yards she had sat silent with another man. The idea had a flavour she enjoyed—scandal salted and ready.

And then the garden gave her proof.

She saw Ethne and her companion come out on to the open lawn. Was it Feversham? She leaned forward, hungry to know. They went down the bank towards the boat. Mrs. Adair stepped from the window and ran.

So it happened that as Willoughby rose from loosening the painter, he looked up and met Mrs. Adair’s eyes—bright with curiosity and, now, disappointment.

Mrs. Adair called to Ethne and came down the bank to them.

“I noticed you cross the lawn from the drawing-room window,” she said.

“Yes?” Ethne answered, and let the single syllable stand like a closed door.

Mrs. Adair did not move away. An awkward pause opened between them. At last Ethne yielded the smallest thing that courtesy required.

“I was talking to Captain Willoughby,” she said, and turned slightly. “You do not know Mrs. Adair, I think?”

“No,” Willoughby replied, lifting his hat. “But I know Mrs. Adair very well by name. I know friends of yours, Mrs. Adair—Durrance, for instance; and of course I knew—”

Ethne’s eyes moved to his, quiet and commanding. The rest of his sentence died in his throat. He bent with sudden energy to push the boat’s nose from the sand, making haste as though haste could repair a near-disaster.

“Of course what?” Mrs. Adair asked, smiling.

“Of course I knew of you, Mrs. Adair,” Willoughby said, shifting the words like a cook substituting one spice for another at the last second.

Mrs. Adair was quite clear that this was not what he had meant to say. He had almost added another name.

“Captain Willoughby,” she repeated softly to herself, storing it away.

Then she said, “You belong to Colonel Durrance’s regiment, perhaps?”

“No. I belong to the North Surrey.”

“Ah,” said Mrs. Adair pleasantly. “Mr. Feversham’s old regiment.”

Willoughby, guileless, had stepped neatly into her little trap, and the ease of it made her want more. Whatever he knew, she thought, would be easy to extract with patience.

But Ethne had disconcerting ways. She looked straight into Mrs. Adair’s eyes and said calmly, with a frankness that offered no handle for prying:

“Captain Willoughby and I have been talking of Mr. Feversham.”

At the same time she held out her hand to the captain. “Good-bye,” she said.

Mrs. Adair interrupted quickly, as though she would not allow the scene to end on Ethne’s terms. “Colonel Durrance has gone home, but he dines with us to-night. I came out to tell you that—but I am glad that I came, for it gives me the opportunity to ask your friend to lunch with us if he will.”

Willoughby, who had already swung one leg into the boat, withdrew it with eager alacrity. He had travelled all night from London, had made the scantiest breakfast at Kingsbridge—something swallowed rather than eaten, no doubt—and the notion of lunch struck him with the force of a remembered appetite.

“It’s awfully good of you, Mrs. Adair,” he began.

“It is very kind indeed,” Ethne continued smoothly, “but Captain Willoughby has reminded me that his leave is very short, and we have no right to detain him. Good-bye.”

Willoughby gazed at her with a rueful appeal. For a second the man in him—hungry, tired, tempted by hospitality—rebelled. But Ethne’s eyes rested on his with a quiet insistence that left him no way to disobey without betraying her.

He bowed, got into his boat, and pushed off from the shore.

“It’s a little bit rough on me too, perhaps, Miss Eustace,” he called back, half complaint and half admiration.

Ethne laughed, and turned back up the bank with Mrs. Adair.

As they walked towards the terrace, she opened her hand once or twice, as though to reassure herself the feather was still there. The small white plume lay in her palm like a shred of winter caught in summer. At the sight of it she laughed again—clear and rather low—laughing not because it was funny, but because the truth had at last taken a shape she could hold.

She gave Mrs. Adair no explanation of Captain Willoughby’s errand. Had she been in Mrs. Adair’s place, she would not have expected one.

It was her business, and only hers.
