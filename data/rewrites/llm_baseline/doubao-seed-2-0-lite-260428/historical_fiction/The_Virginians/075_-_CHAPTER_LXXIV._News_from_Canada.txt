Our Castlewood kin hosted us right up to the turn of the new year, and no sooner had a fortnight of separation passed than the infatuated, foolish young Harry Warrington came rushing back to the side of his charmer, those two weeks stretching for him as long as a lifetime. Madame de Bernstein, for her part, was in no hurry to linger in the home of her father, and grew far more unguarded in her conversation the further we traveled from the old house. She never breathed a word of what passed during the dramatic showdown between her and her new young niece, the Countess, but she set aside all her earlier talk of moulding *cette petite* to her will. When she did allude to the girl, it was with a nervous, half-amused laugh, no trace of ill will at all. Her nephew Eugene, she declared, was clearly doomed to be henpecked for the rest of his days, and a little firm order brought to the Castlewood household would do it a world of good. That plain, unvarnished old American gentleman Van den Bosch struck her as a sharp, capable man, who would make an excellent steward for the estate. She had heard rumor that the Countess’s mother was a convict transported from England, no doubt having beaten hemp in half the kingdom’s gaols, but there was no need to carry that news to the town crier. After all, for folks of that station, what did their birth matter? The girl was clever, she would pick up fluent English soon enough, and the noble name she bore was grand enough to win her entry into any society drawing room. What did it matter if Mr. Van den Bosch had once been a grocer, a smuggler, or even a slave trader? The Countess of Castlewood could afford to be any man’s daughter, and the moment her nephew Eugene brings her to town, she said, it is the whole family’s clear duty to stand by her.

With Madame de Bernstein’s temper so softened towards the new Countess, Mr. Warrington dared hope she might extend that same goodwill to his sister-in-law Lady Maria, and spoke of his visits to Mr. Hagan and his wife, begging his aunt to look kindly on the couple. But the old lady was immovable on the subject of Maria, begging that her name never be mentioned in her hearing, then immediately proceeded to chatter for two whole hours of nothing but anecdotes about her niece, tales far too unseemly to set down in this family story, written for the edification of the young and innocent of our line. This much I will say of the poor creature: if she sinned, she was far from the only sinner in our family, and if she repented, many others would do well to follow her example. Hagan, as all know, lived an exemplary life after he left the stage, growing famous for his elegant, fiery eloquence in the pulpit. His wife embraced strict dissenting views, and was greatly respected by the sect she joined, though the last time I saw her she spoke to me of a peculiar spiritual illumination I strongly suspected came more from a too free use of strong drink than from heaven. I still remember, though, how good the two of them were to me and mine at a time when we needed kindness sorely, and many a pious Pharisee turned his back on us.

I have written before of how fickle our aunt’s favor could be, of how each of us brothers was in turn the golden child of the household, then cast aside. My own turn in the sun came after the success of my play. I was introduced to all the London wits, held my own in their company well enough, was declared tolerably well-bred by the macaronis and the leaders of fashion, and might have risen far in that world if only my purse had been long enough, if I had chosen to chase that sort of life, if I had not already loved a pair of soft, kind eyes far better than the brightest orbs of the Gunnings or the Chudleighs, or all the painted beauties who paraded the Ranelagh ring. Would you believe it, children? Just because I was fond of your mother, my genteel family declared my tastes to be low, and lamented my way of life. My pious Lady Warrington mother and my worldly Aunt Bernstein put their elderly heads together to grieve over it. “Why, with his name, he might marry any woman he pleased!” meek Religion declared, keeping one eye fixed firmly on heaven and the other on the main chance. “I meddle in no man’s affairs, and I admire genius,” said my uncle, “but it is a great pity you consort with poets and scribblers and that low sort of company, when you might have married a lovely creature with a hundred thousand pounds, but you let her slip and run after a penniless country girl.”

“But I gave her my promise, uncle,” I said.

“Promise? Promises are matters of prudence and careful arrangement! When you first made that engagement to little Miss Lambert, you had not yet seen that lovely American heiress your good mother naturally wanted you to wed. Your duty to your mother, nephew — your duty to the Fifth Commandment — would have fully warranted your breaking with Miss L., to fulfil your excellent mother’s intentions regarding that rich Countess, what’s her silly Dutch name? Never mind the name, but a plumb, Master George, is something well worth chasing! Why, I have my dear little Miley at dancing school with Miss Barwell, Nabob Barwell’s daughter, and I make no secret of my hope the children will form an attachment that lasts their whole lives. I told the Nabob so. We left the House of Commons early one dancing day to watch them, and it was beautiful to see the two young things walking a minuet together! It brought tears to my eyes, for I have a tender heart, George, and I love my boy!”

“But if I prefer Miss Lambert, with her twopence to her name, to any Countess with a hundred thousand pounds?” I said.

“Why then, sir, you have a very singular taste, that is all,” the old man huffed, turning on his heel and leaving me. I could perfectly understand his vexation that I could not see the world as plainly as he did.

Aunt Bernstein liked my engagement no better, nor the quiet, unpretending Lambert household I spent so much of my time in. Their simple, unworldly ways wearied, and perhaps annoyed, this old creature who had spent her whole life moving in high society, and she no more relished their company than a certain gentleman who is not quite so black as he is painted relishes holy water. She chafed at my hanging around my sweetheart’s side every chance I got. Having risen high in her favor after my play, I began to fall out of it once more, and Harry became the household’s new favorite — a turn I did not begrudge him in the least. He was our family hero now, writing us brief, plain letters from the war front, that at first not even Madame de Bernstein bothered to read closely, for his accounts were simple, the facts he narrated far from sensational.

Early in London, we got the news of the glorious action at Minden on the first of August, where Wolfe’s old regiment was one of the six British lines that won that famous day of victory. At that very hour, the young General lay fever-stricken in his camp outside Quebec, no doubt burning with rage and disappointment at the recent repulse his troops had suffered. The fleet carrying Wolfe and his army had sailed up the St. Lawrence in June, and landed the men on the last day of the month on the Island of Orleans, directly across from the towering cliff that Quebec stands upon. After the great battle where the General met his death, my brother Harry wrote me one of his simple letters, describing his own small, modest part in that glorious September 13th, adding nothing new to the dozens of accounts already written of the fight, save one detail: he swore, on the testimony of a fellow aide-de-camp who stood at Wolfe’s side the moment he was struck, that the General never spoke a single word after receiving his mortal wound, so all the famous dying lines attributed to him are no more authentic than a grand made-up speech by Livy or Thucydides.

From his post on the Island of Orleans, Wolfe watched hungrily for any chance to meet the enemy on even ground. He landed men above Quebec, he landed them below, shifting from spot to spot, determined to attack the moment he saw an opening. It was surely a terrible blunder on the part of the Marquis de Montcalm to accept a pitched battle on equal terms with Wolfe, for the British General had no heavy artillery, and after that famous scalade of the heights that put us on the Plains of Abraham, we were certainly closer to the city, but still as far as ever from being inside its walls. The game of cat and mouse those two brave commanders played from July until Wolfe won his final, high-stakes wager in September must have been as fascinating a contest as any two competing players ever engaged in.

My brother narrated how the sport began on the very first night after landing: at midnight, the French sent a whole squadron of flaming fire ships drifting down upon the British vessels that were unloading stores at Orleans. Our sailors thought it a fine game to tow the fire ships clear of the fleet and beach them on the shore, where they burned out harmlessly. The moment the French commander learned the British fleet had entered the river, he marched his men to Beauport, just ahead of the city, and took up a nearly unassailable fortified position. Once Wolfe’s stores and hospitals were properly established on Orleans, he crossed the river to the left shore, drawing nearer to his enemy, but every mile of land before him was up in arms. Indians lurking in the forests seized any of our advanced parties that strayed in to clear the trees, and murdered them with unspeakable tortures. The French themselves were as savage as their native allies. The broad, rushing Montmorenci River stood between Wolfe and the French camp, and he could not attack them there, nor force a way into the city behind them. Determined to find some other point where the enemy might be vulnerable, Wolfe sailed past Quebec and skirted the far left bank of the river, but every foot of the shoreline was heavily guarded, and after braving fire from every battery along the banks, he returned to his post at Montmorenci.

On the far right of the French position, across the mouth of the Montmorenci that was fordable at low tide, stood a small enemy redoubt. Wolfe determined to seize it, hoping that Montcalm would be forced to march his men out of their fortified lines to defend it, and give him the pitched battle he craved. On the last day of July, he played this desperate hand. He and his second-in-command General Monckton, who occupied Point Levi across the river, crossed the St. Lawrence from their respective posts, braving a storm of artillery shot as their men rowed for the shore. No sooner had the troops landed than they charged at the French redoubt in wild disorder, were cut down in great numbers before its walls, and were forced to retreat in disarray. The second wave of British troops, who were meant to cross the Montmorenci at low tide, advanced across the river in perfect order, and the enemy even evacuated the redoubt and fell back to their main lines — but from those entrenchments, they poured in such a murderous fire that any hope of breaking their line was hopeless, and the General was forced to call a full retreat.

That disastrous Battle of Montmorenci, which Harry and I have fought over again a hundred times over our wine glasses, formed the heavy, dismal news in the first dispatch from Wolfe that reached England, plunging all of us into deep gloom. What more could anyone expect from a commander so rash? What other disasters were bound to follow? Was there ever a scheme so wild, as to send three separate bodies of men across wide, fast rivers, in the face of murderous batteries, on nothing more than the chance of luring a heavily entrenched enemy out of his defenses to fight you? It was the talk of every drawing room and every coffee house in London. Grave men shook their heads, and prophesied fresh ruin. The General, who took to his bed shuddering with fever right after that failure, had barely six weeks left to live, and to die immortal. How is greatness ever achieved, anyway? By merit? By madness? By frolic? By fortune? Is fate that doles out our victories and defeats? Does the just cause always win? How did the French steal Canada from the savages, and we steal it from the French, and after which of those conquests was it right to sing Te Deum? We are always so ready to drag heaven into our petty quarrels, and call on the gods to side with us no matter the dispute. Does the prizefighter Broughton, after pummeling Slack black and blue, lift his swollen, blackened eye to Jove and thank him for his victory? If ten thousand boxers are entitled to that grace, why not one? And if Broughton deserves to be grateful, what on earth is poor Slack supposed to be?

“By the list of disabled officers, many of whom are of rank, you may perceive, sir, that the army is much weakened. By the nature of this river the most formidable part of the armament is deprived of the power of acting, yet we have almost the whole force of Canada to oppose. In this situation there is such a choice of difficulties, that I own myself at a loss how to determine. The affairs of Great Britain, I know, require the most vigorous measures; but then the courage of a handful of brave men should be exerted only where there is some hope of a favourable event. The admiral and I have examined the town with a view to a general assault: and he would readily join in this or any other measure for the public service; but I cannot propose to him an undertaking of so dangerous a nature, and promising so little success…. I found myself so ill, and am still so weak, that I begged the general officers to consult together for the public utility. They are of opinion that they should try by conveying up a corps of 4000 or 5000 men (which is nearly the whole strength of the army, after the points of Levi and Orleans are put in a proper state of defence) to draw the enemy from their present position, and bring them to an action. I have acquiesced in their proposal, and we are preparing to put it into execution.”

So wrote the General, of whose noble, moving letters it is plain our dear Harry was not the author or secretary, from his headquarters at Montmorenci Falls on the 2nd of September. And on the 14th of October following, the Rodney cutter arrived in England with that sad, worrying news. The attack at Montmorenci had failed, the commander was deathly ill, the army was dwindling away, and the city they meant to take was so strongly fortified a direct assault was next to impossible. The only remaining chance was to fight the Marquis de Montcalm on far better terms than storming his entrenchments, and lure him out of his current stronghold. Would the French commander, whose great military genius was famous across all Europe, fall into such a trap? No wonder there were pale, anxious faces in the City that day, and doubt and gloom spread everywhere the news traveled.

Three days after that first melancholy dispatch arrived, the famous letters came bearing word of that miraculous, triumphant turn of fortune that crowned Mr. Wolfe’s brilliant career. If no man can be called happy until he has met his death, what are we to make of this one? His end was so glorious that I swear not even his own mother or his sweetheart ought to have mourned it, or wished him alive again. I know we speak of a hero here, and yet I vow I scarcely know if I admire more the result of his genius, his invention, and his daring, or the boldness of a gambler winning impossible odds. Suppose his men’s ascent up the cliffs had been discovered half an hour sooner, and his troops beaten back as they most assuredly would have been? Suppose the Marquis de Montcalm had never left his fortified entrenchments to accept that strange challenge? Change those two points, neither of which Wolfe had any control over at all, and what becomes of the glory of the young hero, of the great minister who discovered him, of the whole intoxicated nation that rose up frantic with self-congratulation at the victory? What strange fate is it that shapes our ends, or the ends of great nations? In all the high-stakes games my Lord Chatham played, he won this one prodigious gamble, and even as the greedy British hand seized all of Canada, it loosened its grip on the American colonies, which would soon slip away entirely.

Of course that kind of hindsight wisdom is easy, thirty years after the event. We wonder now at the rashness of that plan, now that the deed is done, and marvel at Montcalm’s blunder. What brilliant generals we all are on paper! What witty retorts come to us long after the conversation is over! How clearly we see how the game should have been played, once the final move is made! But at the time when we first heard the news of Wolfe’s glorious deeds on the Plains of Abraham, of the army marshaled in the dark and rowed silently up the midnight river, of those cliffs scaled by the fearless leader and his troops, of the miraculous surprise that caught the enemy completely unawares, of Montcalm’s willingness to meet our challenge on the open plain, and his total defeat there by the sheer valour of our men — all of England was drunk with joy. The whole nation rose up, and felt itself made stronger by Wolfe’s victory. Not merely every man who fought in that battle, but even those of us at home who had once condemned the whole campaign as reckless, felt ourselves heroes. Our spirits soared as the enemy’s faltered. Friends embraced each other when they met in the streets. Coffee houses and public squares were thronged with people all eager to talk the news. Courtiers rushed to pay their respects to the King, and to the great minister whose wisdom had set the campaign in motion. When Lord Chatham stepped out in public, the people followed him down the streets with shouts and blessings. No one mourned the dead warrior — they marveled at his noble, perfect end. Should James Wolfe’s friends weep and wear mourning, because a chariot of fire had come down from heaven to carry him away? They should watch with wonder, and see him depart, radiant, rising far above all of us. To have a friend who had been near Wolfe was a mark of distinction. Every soldier who fought under him was a hero. In our own little circle, I swear it felt like a great distinction to be Harry’s brother. We did not doubt for a moment that he, who knew the ground from earlier scouting expeditions, had found that secret path up the heights and pointed it out to his General.
