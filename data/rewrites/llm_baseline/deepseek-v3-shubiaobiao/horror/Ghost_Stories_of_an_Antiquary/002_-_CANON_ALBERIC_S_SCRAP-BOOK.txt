The crumbling town of St Bertrand de Comminges clings to the foothills of the Pyrenees, a relic of a bygone age. Its cathedral, though grand, stands watch over fewer than a thousand souls—a hollow echo of its former ecclesiastical glory.

In the spring of 1883, an Englishman named Dennistoun arrived, a Cambridge scholar with an archaeologist’s zeal. He had left two less enthusiastic companions behind in Toulouse, promising to meet them the next day. But Dennistoun had come early, determined to document every inch of the cathedral’s fading splendor—its choir stalls, its ancient organ, the remnants of stained glass like shards of forgotten dreams.

To do so, he required the assistance of the sacristan, a wizened little man whose presence unsettled Dennistoun from the start. The old man moved like a creature hunted, his shoulders hunched, his eyes darting behind him as if expecting some unseen pursuer. Was he haunted? Guilty? Or merely broken by some domestic tyranny? Dennistoun could not decide, but the sacristan’s unease was infectious.

As Dennistoun worked, the cathedral grew restless. Strange noises slithered through the vaulted shadows—footsteps where none walked, whispers in empty alcoves. Once, a high, metallic laugh rang from the tower, and the sacristan turned ashen. *"It is he—no, it is no one. The door is locked."*

Then came the painting—a dark, chaotic depiction of St Bertrand delivering a man from the Devil’s grasp. The sacristan fell to his knees before it, weeping, hands clasped in desperate prayer. Dennistoun pretended not to notice, but the question coiled in his mind: *What madness grips this man?*

As dusk thickened, the sacristan’s agitation sharpened. When Dennistoun finally packed his notes, the old man nearly dragged him to the door, only to hesitate on the threshold.

*"Monsieur seems interested in old books,"* he murmured. *"I have something… at my home."*

A collector’s instinct flared in Dennistoun. The promise of hidden manuscripts was too tantalizing to refuse, though a sliver of caution warned him this might be a trap. Still, he followed.

The sacristan’s house was a decaying mansion, its upper windows boarded like blind eyes. Inside, shadows writhed in the firelight. A crucifix loomed over a heavy chest, from which the sacristan—hands trembling—drew a large folio bound in gold-stamped leather: *Canon Alberic’s Scrap-Book.*

It was a treasure beyond imagining—illuminated leaves from lost manuscripts, fragments of patristic texts, all meticulously preserved. But at the end, two newer pages halted Dennistoun’s breath.

One held a cryptic plan of the cathedral’s south aisle, marked with strange symbols. Beneath it, a Latin inscription:

*"Shall I find it? Thou shalt. Shall I become rich? Thou wilt. Shall I live an object of envy? Thou wilt. Shall I die in my bed? Thou wilt."*

The second page bore a drawing.

At first glance, it seemed biblical—Solomon enthroned, guards recoiling in horror. Then Dennistoun saw *the thing* they surrounded: a skeletal figure, matted with coarse hair, its taloned hands clutching the air. Its eyes, yellow as burning sulfur, held a malevolence that transcended the page.

Dennistoun’s skin crawled. He glanced at the sacristan, who now pressed his hands over his eyes, while his daughter whispered frantic prayers.

*"Is this book for sale?"* Dennistoun managed.

The sacristan’s voice was a ghost of itself. *"Two hundred and fifty francs."*

A pittance for such a prize. Dennistoun protested, but the old man would not budge. The sale was made, wine was drunk, and the sacristan’s demeanor shifted—lighter, almost relieved. As Dennistoun left, the daughter pressed a silver crucifix into his hands, her gratitude tinged with something like pity.

Back at the inn, whispers followed him. The landlady eyed him strangely. *Pierre and Bertrand would be sleeping in the house tonight.*

Alone in his room, Dennistoun pored over the scrap-book, marveling at his luck—until he felt it. A presence.

He turned.

On the table beside him lay a hand—black-nailed, sinewy, inhuman.

His heart stopped.

The thing rose behind him, its yellow eyes burning with a hatred older than reason. Dennistoun seized the crucifix and screamed—a raw, animal sound.

When the servants burst in, they found only Dennistoun, collapsed in terror. The thing had passed between them, unseen but felt.

By morning, his friends arrived. The sacristan came at dawn, listened to the tale, and nodded as if he had expected nothing less.

*"It is he,"* he said simply. *"Twice I have seen him. A thousand times I have felt him."*

He would say no more.

That summer, the sacristan died. His daughter moved away, never understanding the darkness that had clung to her father.
