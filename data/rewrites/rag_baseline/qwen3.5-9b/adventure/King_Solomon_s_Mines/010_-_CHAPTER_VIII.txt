The afternoon sun beat down upon the magnificent roadway, a scar of engineered stone cutting steadily through the landscape in a north-westerly trajectory. Infadoos and Scragga walked beside us, their presence a solemn anchor, while their followers marched a hundred paces ahead, a phalanx of silent guardians.

"Infadoos," I broke the silence eventually, "who forged this road?"

"It was made, my lord, in the deep past," the guide replied, his voice carrying the weight of antiquity. "None know how or when, not even the wise woman Gagool, who has breathed for generations. We are not old enough to recall its creation. No mortal hand can fashion such a path today, yet the King forbids the grass to take root upon it."

"And whose handiwork are the writings etched into the walls of the caves we have traversed?" I asked, gesturing to the Egyptian-esque carvings that lined our passage.

"My lord, the hands that carved the road also penned the wonderful inscriptions. We know not who wrote them."

"When did the Kukuana people descend into this land?"

"The race came down like the breath of a storm ten thousand thousand moons ago," Infadoos explained, pointing a calloused finger toward the distant northern horizon. "From the great lands lying beyond. They could travel no further, blocked by the high mountains that ring the territory, as the old voices of our fathers told us, and as Gagool, the smeller-out of witches, confirms." He swept his gaze toward the snow-capped peaks looming in the distance. "The country was bountiful, so they settled here, growing strong and formidable. Now our numbers are like the sea-sand. When Twala the King summons his regiments, their plumes obscure the plain until the eye of man cannot distinguish one warrior from another."

"If the land is walled in by mountains, who stands before the regiments to fight?"

"Nay, my lord. The country opens toward the north. From time to time, warriors sweep down upon us in clouds from a land we do not know, and we slay them. It is the third part of a man's life since the dawn of memory that has been consumed by war. Thousands fell, yet we destroyed those who sought to devour us. Since then, there has been peace."

"Your warriors must grow weary of resting on their spears, Infadoos."

"My lord, there was one war, shortly after we purged the invaders. It was a civil war; dog ate dog."

"How was that?"

"My lord, the King, my half-brother, had a brother born of the same womb and at the same hour. It is not our custom to permit twins to survive; the weaker must always perish. Yet the mother of the King concealed the feebler child, born last, for her heart yearned over him. That child is Twala the King. I am his younger brother, born of another wife."

"Well?"

"My lord, Kafa, our father, died when we reached manhood. My brother Imotu ascended the throne, reigned for a time, and fathered a son by his favorite wife. When the babe was three years old, amidst the aftermath of the great war during which no man could sow or reap, a famine descended upon the land. The people murmured, starving like lions circling a carcass, looking for something to rend. Then Gagool, the wise and terrible woman who does not die, issued a proclamation: 'The King Imotu is no King.'

"At that moment, Imotu was stricken with a wound, confined to his kraal, unable to move. Gagool retreated to a hut and emerged leading Twala, my half-brother and twin, whom she had hidden among the caves and rocks since birth. Stripping the *moocha* from his loins, she revealed to the Kukuanas the mark of the sacred snake coiled round his middle—the sign of the eldest son—and cried aloud, 'Behold your King, whom I have saved for you even to this day!'

"Now, the people, mad with hunger and bereft of reason, roared, '_The King! The King!_' But I knew the truth: Imotu, my brother, was the elder, the lawful sovereign. Just as the tumult peaked, Imotu, though grievously ill, crawled from his hut, clutching his wife's hand, followed closely by his little son, Ignosi—that is, by interpretation, the Lightning.

"'What is this noise?' he demanded. 'Why cry ye _The King! The King!_'

"Then Twala, his twin brother, born of the same woman and in the same hour, rushed forward. Seizing his brother by the hair, he drove his knife through Imotu's heart. The people, fickle and ever eager to worship the rising sun, clapped their hands and shouted, '_Twala is King! Now we know that Twala is King!_'

"And what became of Imotu's wife and her son, Ignosi? Did Twala kill them too?"

"Nay, my lord. When she realized her lord was dead, the Queen seized the child with a cry and fled. Two days later, she appeared at a kraal, famished. No one offered her milk or food now that her lord was dead; all men hate the unfortunate. But at nightfall, a little girl crept out and shared her corn with her. The Queen blessed the child and continued toward the mountains with her boy before sunrise. There she must have perished, for none have seen her since, nor the child Ignosi."

"Then, if this child Ignosi had lived, he would be the true King of the Kukuana people?"

"That is so, my lord. The sacred snake circles his middle. If he lives, he is King; but alas! he is long dead."

"Look, my lord," Infadoos indicated, pointing to a vast aggregation of huts enclosed by a fence, which in turn was encircled by a great ditch lying on the plain below. "That is the kraal where the wife of Imotu was last seen with the child Ignosi. It is there we shall sleep tonight, provided, indeed, that my lords sleep at all upon this earth."

"When we are among the Kukuanas, my good friend Infadoos, we shall do as the Kukuanas do," I declared majestically. Turning sharply, I addressed Good, who tramped sullenly behind, his mind clearly occupied with futile attempts to stop his flannel shirt from flapping in the evening breeze. To my surprise, I collided with Umbopa, who walked immediately behind me. He had been listening intently to my conversation with Infadoos. His expression was peculiar, suggesting a man struggling, with partial success, to dredge something long forgotten from the depths of his memory.

Throughout our journey, we pressed onward toward the undulating plain. The mountains we had crossed now loomed high overhead, and Sheba's Breasts were veiled modestly in diaphanous wreaths of mist. As we advanced, the country grew increasingly lovely. The vegetation was luxuriant yet not overtly tropical; the sun was bright and warm, devoid of burning intensity, while a gracious breeze drifted softly along the odorous mountain slopes. Indeed, this new land was little less than an earthly paradise—in beauty, natural wealth, and climate, I had never witnessed its equal. The Transvaal is a fine country, but it is nothing compared to Kukuanaland.

As soon as we began our march, Infadoos dispatched a runner to warn the people of the kraal, which fell under his military command, of our approach. This messenger departed at an extraordinary pace, a feat Infadoos assured me he maintained throughout his travels, noting that running was an exercise rigorously practiced among his people.

The efficacy of this warning became evident moments later. Upon arriving within two miles of the kraal, we observed companies after companies emerging from its gates, marching purposefully toward us.

Sir Henry laid his hand upon my arm, remarking that it appeared we were destined for a warm reception. Something in his tone caught Infadoos' attention.

"Let not my lords be afraid," he said hastily. "For in my breast dwells no guile. This regiment is under my command and comes out by my orders to greet you."

I nodded easily, though unease lingered in my mind.

About half a mile from the kraal's gates lies a long stretch of rising ground, sloping gently upward from the road. Here the companies formed. It was a splendid sight: each company, numbering about three hundred, charged swiftly up the rise, spears flashing and plumes waving, to take their appointed places. By the time we reached the slope, twelve such companies—or three thousand six hundred men in total—had passed out and assumed their positions along the road.

We approached the first company, gazing in astonishment at the most magnificent array of warriors I had ever beheld. They were men of mature age, mostly veterans of about forty, and not one stood under six feet in height, while many towered at six feet three or four. Upon their heads rested heavy black plumes of Sakaboola feathers, similar to those adorning our guides. Bound about their waists and beneath their right knees were circlets of white ox tails. In their left hands, they carried round shields measuring approximately twenty inches across. These shields were peculiar: the framework consisted of an iron plate beaten thin, over which was stretched milk-white ox-hide.
