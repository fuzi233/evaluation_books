Louis Moore's fears regarding the imminent departure of Mr. Sympson from Fieldhead proved entirely justified. The very next day following the heated dispute over Sir Philip Nunnely, a tentative truce was brokered between uncle and niece. Shirley, possessing a heart that could never conceive of being inhospitable—with the singular exception of Mr. Donne—earnestly pleaded for the entire party to remain a little longer. Her plea was so fervent that it betrayed a hidden desire, though she did not specify why. They accepted her invitation. Indeed, Uncle Sympson found it impossible to leave her completely unguarded, fearing she might seize the opportunity to wed Robert Moore the moment the gentleman regained the strength to pursue his alleged claims to her hand. Mr. Sympson, in his piety, prayed that this day might never arrive. Consequently, everyone stayed.

Following his initial fury against the entire Moore household, Mr. Sympson had behaved toward Louis with such coarse insolence that the latter—a man patient with labor and suffering but intolerant of rudeness—promptly resigned his position. He could only be persuaded to resume his duties until the family departed Yorkshire. Mrs. Sympson's entreaties achieved this compromise; furthermore, his deep attachment to his pupil served as an additional motive for concession. Perhaps, however, there existed a third motive, stronger than either of the others. It is probable that Mr. Sympson would have found it exceedingly difficult to abandon Fieldhead at this precise juncture.

For some time, affairs proceeded smoothly. Miss Keeldar's health was restored, and her spirits returned with renewed vigor. Moore had successfully alleviated her from every nervous apprehension; indeed, from the moment she entrusted him with her confidence, every fear seemed to vanish. Her heart became light, her manner carefree, reminiscent of a child who, unconcerned with its own life or death, places total trust in its parents. Both Louis and William Farren—who acted as an intermediary inquiring about Phoebe's condition—concluded that the dog was not mad, but merely driven from home by abuse, citing proof that her master frequently chastised her violently. Whether this assertion was true remained uncertain, as the groom and gamekeeper vehemently denied it, insisting that if Phoebe did not suffer from hydrophobia, she suffered from no recognized ailment whatsoever. Yet, Louis Moore turned an incredulous ear to this testimony. He reported to Shirley only the encouraging aspects. She believed him; and regardless of the truth, it is certain that in her case, the bite proved harmless.

November passed, and December arrived. The Sympsons were finally preparing to depart, bound to be home by Christmas. Their packages were packed, and their departure was imminent. One winter evening, during the final week of their stay, Louis Moore once again retrieved his small blank notebook and began to write:

*"She is lovelier than ever. Since that brief cloud dispersed, all traces of temporary pallor and weakness have vanished. It was marvelous to witness how swiftly the magical energy of youth revitalized her elasticity and restored her bloom.*

*After breakfast this morning, having observed her, listened to her, and, so to speak, felt her within every fiber of my being, I stepped from her sunny presence into the chilling drawing room. Picking up a small gilt volume containing selected lyrics, I read a poem or two. Whether the enchantment lay within me or in the verses, I know not, but my heart swelled warmly, my pulse quickened. I glowed despite the frosty air outside. I, too, am still young. Although she claimed she never considered me youthful, I am barely thirty. There are moments when life, for no reason other than my own youth, shines with sweet hues.*

*It was time to retire to the schoolroom. I went. That same room is particularly pleasant in the morning. The sun streams through the low lattice; the books are arranged in perfect order; no papers litter the floor; the fire burns clear and clean, free of falling cinders or accumulated ash. I found Henry there, accompanied by Miss Keeldar. They were together.*

*I remarked that she was lovelier than ever. She truly is. A fine rose, delicate rather than deep, blooms on her cheek. Her eye, always dark, clear, and expressive, now speaks a language I cannot translate—it is a silent utterance, unseen yet heard, through which angels must have communed when there was silence in heaven. Her hair has always been dark as night and fine as silk; her neck has always been fair, flexible, and polished. Yet both now possess a new charm. The tresses are soft as shadows, and the shoulders they grace wear a goddess-like dignity. Once I merely *saw* her beauty; now I *feel* it.*

*Henry was reciting his lesson to her before presenting it to me. One of her hands rested on the book; he held the other. That boy receives more than his share of privileges; he dares to caress and is caressed. What indulgence and compassion she shows him! Too much. If this continued, Henry, in a few years when his soul was fully formed, would offer himself on her altar, just as I have offered mine.*

*I noticed her eyelid flutter when I entered, yet she did not look up. Now, she hardly ever casts a glance my way. She seems to grow increasingly silent; to me, she rarely speaks, and when I am present, she says little to others. In my darker moments, I attribute this change to indifference or aversion. But in my brighter intervals, I interpret it differently. I say, were I her equal, I could find in this shyness a coyness, and in that coyness, love. As it stands, dare I expect it? And what could I do with it if I found it?*

*This morning, I dared at least arrange an hour of communion between her and me; I dared not only wish for an interview but will it into existence. I summoned solitude to guard us. Decisively, I called Henry to the door. Without hesitation, I said, 'Go where you will, my boy; but until I call you, do not return here.'*

*Henry, I could see, disliked his dismissal. That boy is young, yet a thinker; his meditative gaze shines upon me strangely at times. He half-feels what binds me to Shirley; he half-guesses that there is a deeper delight in the reserve with which I am treated than in all the endearments he is permitted. The young, lame, half-grown lion would growl at me now and then, because I have tamed his lioness and serve as her keeper, were it not for the habit of discipline and the instinct of affection holding him subdued. Go, Henry; you must learn to share the bitterness of life with all of Adam's race, past and future. Your destiny cannot be an exception to the common lot; be grateful that your love is overlooked so early, before it can claim any affinity to passion. An hour of fretting, a pang of envy, suffice to express what you feel. Jealousy hot as the sun above the equator, rage destructive as a tropical storm—the climate of your sensations ignores these emotions—for now.*

*I took my usual seat at the desk, proceeding exactly as I normally do. I am blessed with the power to conceal all inward turmoil beneath outward calm. No one observing my slow, steady face can guess the vortex sometimes swirling in my heart, engulfing thought and wrecking prudence. Pleasant is the gift to proceed peacefully and powerfully along one's course without alarming anyone with eccentric movements. It was not my intention today to utter a word of love to her, nor to reveal a glimpse of the fire within which I burn. Presumptuous I have never been; presumptuous I never will be. Rather than even *appear* selfish and interested, I would resolutely rise, gird my loins, depart, and seek a new life on the other side of the globe—one cold and barren as the rock washed daily by the salt tide. My design this morning was simply to scrutinize her closely—to read a line in the page of her heart. Before I left, I determined to know *what* I was leaving behind.*

*I had some quills to fashion into pens. Most men's hands would tremble when their hearts are so stirred; mine worked steadily, and my voice, when called into service, remained firm.*
