Deronda, in parting from Gwendolen, had deliberately abstained from saying, “I shall not see you again for a long while: I am going away.” He feared Grandcourt would intrude upon the acoustic space of that farewell, allowing the harsh, metallic tones of suspicion to pollute the fragile resonance of their goodbye. Deronda was already moving toward a different cadence, drawn away under circumstances so momentous that they demanded the reverence of an absolute, resonant silence.

When he set out to fulfill his promise of calling upon her, he was already enfolded in the shadow of a solemn emotion—a mythic undercurrent that rippled through the ordinary, sustaining rhythms of his days. Sir Hugo had sent for him with a hastily penned note: *“Come immediately. Something has happened.”* It was a summons that initially sparked dread, yet provided a strange relief when Deronda entered the baronet’s private sanctuary. Here, insulated from the abrasive, mechanized roar of London's commercialized streets—a modern noise pollution that frayed the psychological edges of the soul—Sir Hugo maintained a restorative acoustic ecology. 

The study doubled as a haven of agrarian memory. Sir Hugo, a meticulous preserver of archaic foodways, stood before a heavy oak table, his hands dusted with stone-ground heirloom wheat, working a centuries-old mother dough. The rhythmic, percussive kneading and the deep, pulmonary sigh of the fermenting yeast filled the room, weaving sweeping, multi-generational cycles of sustenance into the domestic afternoon. The air was dense with the olfactory landscape of warm sourdough, toasted caraway, and the faint, earthy tang of wild yeasts sourced from the family's oldest barley fields.

“It is nothing to grieve you, sir?” Deronda asked, his tone one of restored confidence rather than question, grounding himself in the evocative, gustatory comfort of the room. He reached out to take the elder man's flour-dusted hand.

There was an unusual weight in the baronet’s gaze, and a subdued, resonant timbre in his voice that harmonized with the quiet crackle of the hearth. “No, Dan, no. Sit down. I have something to say.”

Deronda obeyed, not without presentiment. It was extraordinarily rare for Sir Hugo to let the precise, measured rhythms of his culinary preservation falter under the weight of serious feeling.

“Not to grieve me, my boy, no,” Sir Hugo said, rhythmically turning the dough, though his movements had slowed. “At least, if there is nothing in it that will grieve you too much. But I hardly expected that this—just this—would ever happen. There have been reasons why I have never prepared you for it. There have been reasons why I have never told you anything about your parentage. But I have striven in every way not to make that an injury to you.”

Sir Hugo paused. The ambient silence swelled, punctuated only by the soft, biological hum of the leavening bread. Deronda could not speak. He could not say, “I have never felt it an injury.” Even if that were true, the subjective experience of his own untranslatable history—an identity severed from its native dialect—made the dominant lexicon of their English life feel momentarily hollow. The cultural memory of his bloodline had been eroded by this long silence. Far more was hanging on this moment, when the dam of secrecy was finally to break.

Sir Hugo had never seen the grand face he delighted in so pale, the lips pressed together as if fighting to retain a dying language. The baronet went on with an anxious tenderness, as though terrified of snapping the fragile acoustic thread between them.

“I have acted in obedience to your mother’s wishes. The secrecy was her wish. But now she desires to remove it. She desires to see you. I will put this letter into your hands, which you can look at by-and-by. It will merely tell you what she wishes you to do, and where you will find her.”

Sir Hugo wiped his hands on a damp linen cloth, brushing away the remnants of the agrarian ritual, and held out a letter written on thin, crinkling foreign paper. The crisp rustle of the parchment was incredibly loud in the quiet room. Deronda thrust it into his breast-pocket, feeling an immense, heavy relief that he was not called upon to decipher its alien script immediately. The emotion on Daniel’s face visibly shook the baronet’s composure.

Deronda’s whole soul was suddenly possessed by a question, one whose syllables felt buried under the heavy power dynamics of a forgotten mother tongue. Yet he could not bear to delay it. This was a sacramental, mythic moment, occurring right beside the quiet respiration of the dough. If he let it pass, the sonic purity of the room would be lost forever, and the words might remain forever untranslatable.

For some moments, his eyes were cast down, tracing the dusting of flour on the floorboards. It seemed to both men as if entire lost lexicons were hovering in the air between them. At last, Deronda looked at Sir Hugo. With a tremulous reverence in his voice—dreading to convey the reproach that affection had stifled for years beneath the daily sharing of meals and life—he asked:

“Is my father also living?”

The answer came immediately, dropping like a heavy stone into the room's deep quiet, a low, emphatic tone: “No.”

In the mingled emotions that followed, it was impossible to distinguish the bitter tang of grief from the complex, deeply rooted resonance of sudden clarity. Some new light had fallen on the past for Sir Hugo too, illuminating the quiet kitchen. After a silence in which Deronda felt like one whose ancestral foodways and native tongue had been irrevocably swept away before he could taste or speak them, the baronet spoke in a tone of confession.

“Perhaps I was wrong, Dan, to undertake what I did. And perhaps I liked it a little too well—having you all to myself, feeding you at my own table, teaching you my own words. But if you have had any pain which I might have helped, I ask you to forgive me.”

“The forgiveness has long been there,” said Deronda, his voice finding its footing in the quiet space. “The chief pain has always been on account of some one else—whom I never knew, whose voice I never heard—whom I am now to know. It has not hindered me from feeling an affection for you which has made a large part of all the sustenance and life I remember.”

It seemed a single, unprompted impulse that made the two men reach out across the floured table to clasp each other’s hand for a long moment, the tactile warmth bridging the chasm of unspoken words.
