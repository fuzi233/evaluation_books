Elinor’s curiosity—so long kept in check by prudence, by necessity, by that habit of swallowing disappointment without so much as a grimace—had, at last, been fed. She had seen Mrs. Ferrars; and the taste it left was one she should not soon forget: sharp as alum, mean as watered broth, with that after-bite of pride which clings to the palate and makes even a rich table seem barren. In one short visit she had read enough in Mrs. Ferrars’s eye, in the rigid economy of her civility, in the way her words were weighed as if affection itself were a coin to be hoarded, to understand what storms must have beaten upon Edward’s secret engagement—how every domestic hour, every letter, every prospective comfort, would have been soured and stinted, had he been free to marry where he loved.

She had almost, for her own sake, felt a strange gratitude that one great obstacle—one that hurt her most—kept her from a lesser bondage: from dependence on Mrs. Ferrars’s caprice, from the humiliating hunger of waiting upon that woman’s good opinion as if it were bread. If Lucy had been kinder, if she had possessed even a little honest warmth, Elinor told herself she ought to have rejoiced at Edward’s being fettered to her; for then her pain would have had the decency of being the price of another person’s happiness. But Lucy was Lucy; and Elinor could not make virtue out of mere necessity.

What surprised her was not Mrs. Ferrars’s determined prejudice—of that she had seen enough—but the ease with which Lucy’s spirits rose under a civility that was, in truth, little more than a pointed exclusion. It was as if a guest, served the best cut only because the rightful one had been denied a place at table, should call the meal a triumph of personal merit. Lucy’s vanity made her hunger, and hunger made her blind: the attention paid to her because she was not Elinor appeared, to Lucy, as devotion to herself.

This blindness returned the next morning, even more insistently; for at Lucy’s particular desire, Lady Middleton set her down in Berkeley Street, on the chance of finding Elinor alone. Chance favoured her. A message from Mrs. Palmer soon after Lucy’s arrival drew Mrs. Jennings away; the door had scarcely shut upon the brisk shuffle of her departure when Lucy, as if she had been holding her breath for it, turned to Elinor with an eagerness that would not be denied.

“My dear friend,” she cried, leaning forward as if happiness were a secret to be whispered, “I come to talk to you of my happiness. Could anything be so flattering as Mrs. Ferrars’s way of treating me yesterday? So exceeding affable as she was! You know how I dreaded the thoughts of seeing her; but the very moment I was introduced, there was such an affability in her behaviour as really should seem to say she had quite took a fancy to me. Now was not it so? You saw it all; and was not you quite struck with it?”

Elinor, whose mind, like a careful housekeeper, could not help sorting impressions by their true value, answered as steadily as she could.

“She was certainly very civil to you.”

“Civil!” Lucy repeated, as if Elinor had named a poor dish when she expected praise of a feast. “Did you see nothing but only civility? I saw a vast deal more. Such kindness as fell to the share of nobody but me! No pride, no hauteur, and your sister just the same—all sweetness and affability!”

Elinor wished, with a kind of physical longing, to turn the conversation upon any safer subject—upon the weather, the state of the streets, the price of butter in London compared to Devonshire—but Lucy clung to her triumph as a cook clings to an admired receipt, insisting it be acknowledged perfect.

Lucy pressed, and Elinor was obliged to go on.

“Undoubtedly, if they had known your engagement,” said she, forcing truth through politeness, “nothing could be more flattering than their treatment of you;—but as that was not the case—”

“I guessed you would say so,” Lucy cut in quickly, as if she had heard this objection rehearsed already in her own mind and was prepared to season it away. “But there was no reason in the world why Mrs. Ferrars should seem to like me if she did not; and her liking me is every thing. You shan’t talk me out of my satisfaction. I am sure it will all end well, and there will be no difficulties at all, to what I used to think. Mrs. Ferrars is a charming woman, and so is your sister. They are both delightful women indeed!—I wonder I should never hear you say how agreeable Mrs. Dashwood was!”

There were questions that were meant less to be answered than to place a person in the wrong. Elinor felt this one settle upon her like smoke in a room with no draught; she had no clean reply, and she did not attempt any.

Lucy, who could not endure silence unless it served her, watched Elinor’s face with a little anxious sharpness. “Are you ill, Miss Dashwood?—you seem low—you don’t speak;—sure you an’t well.”

“I never was in better health,” Elinor returned; and if her voice carried any flatness, it was the fatigue of holding herself steady, not the least tremor of sickness.

“I am glad of it with all my heart; but really you did not look it. I should be sorry to have you ill; you, that have been the greatest comfort to me in the world!—Heaven knows what I should have done without your friendship.”

Elinor tried to produce a civil answer, as one forces a thin soup into the appearance of nourishment; and though she doubted her own success, it seemed to satisfy Lucy. Lucy’s vanity fed easily; it required only the shape of assent, not its substance.

“Indeed I am perfectly convinced of your regard for me,” Lucy continued, with a composed air of possession, “and next to Edward’s love it is the greatest comfort I have. Poor Edward! But now there is one good thing; we shall be able to meet, and meet pretty often, for Lady Middleton’s delighted with Mrs. Dashwood, so we shall be a good deal in Harley Street, I dare say, and Edward spends half his time with his sister—besides, Lady Middleton and Mrs. Ferrars will visit now;—and Mrs. Ferrars and your sister were both so good to say more than once they should always be glad to see me. They are such charming women!—I am sure if ever you tell your sister what I think of her, you cannot speak too high.”

Elinor, guarding her own heart as carefully as she might guard a small store of precious spice—meant for her mother, for Marianne, for the few she loved—gave no encouragement to the hope that she should repeat Lucy’s praises to Mrs. Dashwood.

Lucy went on, warming to the subject as if reciting the pleasures of a dinner still to come. “I am sure I should have seen it in a moment if Mrs. Ferrars had took a dislike to me. If she had only made me a formal courtesy, for instance, without saying a word, and never after had took any notice of me, and never looked at me in a pleasant way—you know what I mean—if I had been treated in that forbidding sort of way, I should have gave it all up in despair. I could not have stood it. For where she does dislike, I know it is most violent.”

Elinor had hardly time to consider what answer politeness demanded—if any answer could be made without agreeing to Lucy’s whole delusion—when the door was thrown open. The servant announced Mr. Ferrars; and Edward walked in.

It was the very arrangement each of them would have prayed to avoid: all three together, and without the shelter of any other presence to diffuse embarrassment. The room seemed suddenly too small; even the ordinary sounds of the house—the distant murmur of a carriage in the street, the faint click of a fire settling—grew too distinct, as if the air itself had sharpened its hearing to bear witness against them.

They all looked exceedingly foolish. Edward, in particular, stood as if he had stepped into a kitchen at the wrong hour and found every hand stopped, every eye on him, waiting for an explanation he could not give. He seemed as much inclined to retreat as to advance.

The ladies recovered themselves first. Lucy, whose business it was to preserve appearances, and who could practise secrecy with the calm of long habit, did not put herself forward. She could only look what she meant to suggest; after slightly addressing him, she said no more.

Elinor had more to do. Her mind, trained by daily discipline to serve others even when her own appetite was gone, forced itself into action. For Edward’s sake—and for her own dignity—she summoned a look and manner almost easy, almost open; and by another effort, another struggle, she improved them. She would not allow Lucy’s presence, nor the bitter knowledge of injustice to herself, to deter her from attentions Edward was due as a friend and almost a relation.

She told him she was happy to see him; she said she had regretted being from home when he had called before in Berkeley Street. She spoke of her mother’s health, of their coming to town—things Edward ought to have asked, but did not. Lucy’s eyes watched her narrowly, as if measuring every word for hidden meaning; yet Elinor persisted, refusing to let Lucy’s silent scrutiny dictate her civility.

Elinor’s steadiness reassured Edward enough to sit down; but his embarrassment still exceeded that of either lady. It was natural. Lucy’s heart had the indifference of self-interest; Elinor’s conscience had, at least, the comfort of having acted with honour. Edward had neither refuge; his feelings were too tender to be callous, his principles too alive to be easy.

Lucy, with a demure and settled air, made no contribution to the comfort of the others; she would not say a word. Almost every thing that was said proceeded from Elinor, who had to keep conversation moving as one keeps a failing fire alive—by small additions, by patient tending, while the room grows colder all the same.

At length Elinor felt herself so heroically disposed—so determined to give Edward even the small mercy of a moment unobserved—that she resolved, under pretence of fetching Marianne, to leave the others by themselves. She did it in the handsomest manner, and then, once outside, loitered for several minutes on the landing-place with a fortitude that had nothing to display itself upon but the sound of her own breath and the distant stir of the household. The very boards beneath her feet seemed to remember, in their small creaks and settled groans, other footsteps, other visits—how often a house becomes the keeper of secrets that the tongue cannot utter.

But when Marianne heard, raptures could not be delayed. Her joy hurried her into the drawing-room immediately, as sudden as a kettle beginning to sing when one has forgotten it on the hob. Her pleasure in seeing Edward was like every other feeling of hers—strong in itself, and strongly spoken. She met him with a hand that would be taken and a voice that carried all the affection of a sister.

“Dear Edward!” she cried, “this is a moment of great happiness!—This would almost make amends for every thing!”

Edward tried to return her kindness as it deserved; but before such witnesses he dared not say half what he felt. They sat down again; and for a moment or two all were silent—Marianne looking with speaking tenderness, now at Edward, now at Elinor, regretting only that delight should be checked by Lucy’s unwelcome presence.

Edward spoke first. He noticed Marianne’s altered looks, and expressed fear that London did not agree with her.

“Oh, don’t think of me!” Marianne replied with spirited earnestness; yet her eyes filled with tears as she spoke, and her voice shook like water in a glass carried too quickly. “Don’t think of my health. Elinor is well, you see. That must be enough for us both.”

The remark, meant as affection, fell as a weight. It did not make Edward or Elinor easier; nor did it conciliate Lucy, who looked up at Marianne with an expression far from benign.

“Do you like London?” Edward asked, eager for any subject that might carry them away from dangerous feeling.

“Not at all. I expected much pleasure in it, but I have found none. The sight of you, Edward, is the only comfort it has afforded; and thank Heaven! you are what you always were!”

She paused. No one spoke. The air in the room felt strained, as if even the curtains listened.

“I think, Elinor,” Marianne added presently, “we must employ Edward to take care of us in our return to Barton. In a week or two, I suppose, we shall be going; and I trust Edward will not be very unwilling to accept the charge.”

Poor Edward muttered something—what it was nobody knew, not even himself. But Marianne, seeing his agitation, traced it without hesitation to whatever cause best pleased her own generous interpretation, and was satisfied. She soon talked of something else.

“We spent such a day, Edward, in Harley Street yesterday! So dull, so wretchedly dull!—But I have much to say to you on that head, which cannot be said now.” With admirable discretion she deferred, until privacy, the assurance that she found their mutual relatives more disagreeable than ever, and that she was particularly disgusted with his mother.

“But why were you not there, Edward?—Why did you not come?”

“I was engaged elsewhere.”

“Engaged! But what was that, when such friends were to be met?”

“Perhaps, Miss Marianne,” Lucy cried, eager to take a small revenge where she could, “you think young men never stand upon engagements, if they have no mind to keep them, little as well as great.”

Elinor felt a flash of anger—hot, quick, and swallowed down—yet Marianne seemed wholly insensible of the sting. She replied calmly, with that fearless sincerity which made her truth a kind of blade.

“Not so, indeed; for, seriously speaking, I am very sure that conscience only kept Edward from Harley Street. And I really believe he has the most delicate conscience in the world; the most scrupulous in performing every engagement, however minute, and however it may make against his interest or pleasure. He is the most fearful of giving pain, of wounding expectation, and the most incapable of being selfish, of any body I ever saw. Edward, it is so, and I will say it. What! are you never to hear yourself praised!—Then you must be no friend of mine; for those who will accept of my love and esteem, must submit to my open commendation.”

In any other room, with any other company, such praise might have been warm as good bread, opening the heart by its very scent. Here it was ill-suited to two thirds of those who heard it, and unexhilarating to Edward in the extreme; it struck his conscience where it was most tender, and made his constraint visible. He rose very soon to go away.

“Going so soon!” Marianne exclaimed. “My dear Edward, this must not be.” Drawing him a little aside, she whispered her persuasion that Lucy could not stay much longer.

Even that encouragement failed. He would go.

Lucy, who would have outstayed him had his visit lasted two hours, soon afterwards went away as well; not from delicacy, but because her triumph could be postponed and rehearsed again another day.

When she had left them, Marianne’s impatience broke out.

“What can bring her here so often?” she said. “Could not she see that we wanted her gone!—how teazing to Edward!”

Elinor, forced by habit into the defence of appearances, answered with an effort at composure. “Why so?—we were all his friends, and Lucy has been the longest known to him of any. It is but natural that he should like to see her as well as ourselves.”

Marianne looked at her steadily, as if listening for a familiar voice spoken in a foreign dialect—words whose sense she understood and yet could not bear because they did not belong to Elinor’s true language of feeling.

“You know, Elinor,” she said, “that this is a kind of talking which I cannot bear. If you only hope to have your assertion contradicted, as I must suppose to be the case, you ought to recollect that I am the last person in the world to do it. I cannot descend to be tricked out of assurances that are not really wanted.”

And with that she left the room.

Elinor did not dare follow. Bound as she was by her promise of secrecy to Lucy, she could give no information that would convince Marianne; and painful as the consequences of Marianne’s continuing in error might be, she was obliged to submit to it. All she could hope was that Edward would not often expose her—or himself—to the distress of hearing Marianne’s mistaken warmth, nor to the repetition of any part of the pain that had attended their recent meeting; and she had every reason to expect it.
