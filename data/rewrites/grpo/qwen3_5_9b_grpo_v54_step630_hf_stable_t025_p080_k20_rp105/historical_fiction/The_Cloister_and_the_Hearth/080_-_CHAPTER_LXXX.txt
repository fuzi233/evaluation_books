Brother Clement had spent over a year teaching and preaching in Basel when Jerome appeared before him, dust-streaked and wearing a gaze of triumphant anticipation.

"Give the glory to God, Brother Clement," Jerome declared. "Thou mayest now journey to England with me."

"I am ready, Brother Jerome," Clement replied, his voice steady. "I have awaited thee for many months. In the quiet hours between lessons and prayer, I have studied the English tongue with close attention."

"It was a wise decision," Jerome acknowledged. He explained that he had delayed their departure only until he secured special faculties from the Pope: the power to collect funds for the Church's needs in England and to hear confessions within all secular monasteries. "Now, gird up thy loins. Let us depart and strike a decisive blow for the Church, and against the Franciscans."

Thus, the two friars descended the Rhine toward England. In the great cities, they preached together; in the smaller towns, they often split, taking opposite banks of the river to meet later at a designated point. Both were skilled orators, yet their styles diverged sharply.

Jerome's delivery was noble and imposing, though occasionally constrained in its religious scope and prone to a certain monotony. Clement, however, possessed a torrential variety. His speech flowed tenderly, curling around the listener's reason and heart, yet it could rise with the soaring thought. At his peak, Clement transcended Jerome entirely, becoming a figure of prophetic fire: vehement as a storm, excited as a rushing torrent, inflamed as lightning, thundering and flashing, sweeping all before him with the rapid waves of eloquence.

I might offer literal examples of such passages, but five reasons stand against it: the difficulty of translation, the brevity of our time, the fact that I have already done so elsewhere, the superior imitation of others, and the truth that while similarity is a virtue in peas, it is a vice in books.

Nevertheless, to avoid evasion, it must be noted that Clement secretly endeavored to learn the current events and the specific vices of every town he was to address. Jerome, rigid and unbending, scorned such preparation, refusing to tailor his message to the particular sins of any people.

At a great city several leagues from the Rhine, they shared the same pulpit. Jerome preached against vanity in dress, a favorite theme of his. He was eloquent and satirical, and the crowd listened with complacency, for vanity was not a vice they commonly practiced.

Clement, conversely, attacked drunkenness—a besetting sin in those regions. The clergy themselves were often infected by it, and popular prejudice shielded the habit. Clement struck merciless blows, drawing upon Holy Writ and worldly experience. He declared it a crime, the nursing mother of theft and murder. He reminded them of a recent parricide committed by a man in liquor, and of a band of drunkards who had roasted one of their own comrades alive in a neighboring village.

"Your last prince," Clement proclaimed, "is reported to have died of apoplexy, yet you know he died of drink. One of your aldermen perished miserably last month, dead drunk and suffocated in a puddle. You strip your children's backs bare to fill your bellies with that which makes you the worst of beasts—silly as calves, yet fierce as boars. This drives your families to want and your souls to hell. I tell you, your town, yes, your very nation, would sink to the bottom of mankind if your women drank as you do. How long will they remain temperate, resisting nature and the example of their husbands and fathers? Vice never stands still. Amend yourselves, or watch them descend to your level. Already in Bohemia, they drink alongside the men. How does a drunken woman appear? Would you love to see your wives and mothers drunk?"

A shout of horror erupted from the medieval audience, unused to sitting silently during a moving sermon.

"Aha, that strikes home!" cried the friar. "What madness! Do you think it shocks the all-pure God less to see a man, His noblest work, turned into a drunken beast, than it shocks you, creatures of sin and unreason, to see a woman become a thing no better nor worse than yourselves?"

He concluded with two vivid portraits: the chaotic house and family of a drunkard, and the orderly home of a sober man. Both were so true and dramatic in detail that the wives fell into exclamations of "Oh!" and "Ah!" and cried, "Eh, but that is a true word."

The discourse caused an uproar. Hearers formed knots; the men were indignant. Consequently, the women flattered them and openly sided against the preacher. A married man had a right to a drop; he needed it to sustain the family. As for the women, they cared not to exchange their husbands for milksops.

Double-faced indeed! That very evening, a group of men surrounded Brother Clement near a hundred strong, filling his wallet with the finest coins, offering him the roses plucked from their own heads, kissing his frock, and blessing him for taking up the cause to mend their sots.

Jerome deemed this sermon too earthly.

"Drunkenness is not heresy, Clement," he remarked. "It is folly for a whole sermon to be preached against it."

As they traveled on, Jerome found to his surprise that Clement's sermons sank deeper into the hearts of his hearers than his own. They made people listen, think, weep, and sometimes amend their ways. "He has the art of sinking to their peg," Jerome thought. "Yet he can soar high enough at times."

Overall, it puzzled Jerome, who harbored a secret sense of superiority over his tenderer brother. After about two hundred miles, it began to displease him as well as puzzle him. Yet he tried to suppress this sentiment as petty and unworthy. "Souls differ like locks," he reasoned. "Preachers must differ like keys, or the Church shall open fewer doors for God to pass through. And certainly, this novice holds the key to these northern souls, being himself a northern man."

So they moved slowly down the Rhine, sometimes drifting a few miles downstream, but generally walking the banks, preaching, teaching, and confessing sinners in towns and villages, until they reached Düsseldorf.

There lay the little quay where Gerard and Denys had once taken boat upstream. The friars landed there. There were the familiar streets, and there stood "The Silver Lion." Nothing had changed except the friar who walked through it barefoot, his heart calm and cold, his hands crossed over his breast, his eyes bent meekly to the ground—a true son of Dominic and the Holy Church.
