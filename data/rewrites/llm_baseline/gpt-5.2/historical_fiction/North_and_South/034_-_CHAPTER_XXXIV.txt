The effort of seeming to bear it—of carrying her days as if they were not weighted with a new and hollow absence—strained Margaret until she felt the fibres of her will sing like overstretched wire. At odd instants, in the midst of the quietest talk with her father, the thought would dart through her with a knife’s sharpness—there is no longer a mother in the house—and she must press her lips together, and keep her breath even, and go on as if the words she was answering were the only reality.

And Frederick—Frederick was a worse uncertainty, because it could not even be mourned openly. The Sunday post had broken the accustomed course of their London letters, and when Tuesday came and still there was nothing, the silence grew loud in the rooms, louder than the faint crackle of the coals, louder than the respectful tread of Dixon outside the door. Margaret had no chart of his intentions; her father had no anchorage for his fears.

Mr. Hale’s late habit of sitting half a day in one easy chair, hands folded, eyes dulled by grief, was shattered by this want of tidings. He rose and paced, and went out and in again, and Margaret heard him on the landing opening and shutting bedroom doors as if each sound, each little click of latch and hinge, might call a letter into being. The house itself seemed to listen—stairs holding their breath, curtains still, the very clock’s tick sounding like a question unanswered.

She tried to steady him by reading aloud, choosing passages with a cadence she thought might soothe, as one might set a simmer to a sauce that threatens to boil over; but his attention broke, wandered, came back for a phrase, was lost again. He could not be held long by any thread of narrative while his mind fretted at that other thread—Frederick, out there somewhere, beyond their reach.

How thankful she was then—thankful with a guilty, bitter gratitude—that she had kept to herself the fresh cause for anxiety, the chance encounter with Leonards. If her father’s grief had been a broth already too salted, this would have been the handful that made it uneatable. She had tasted fear lately as distinctly as if it had been on her tongue—metallic, sour, lingering—and she would not pour it into his cup.

When Mr. Thornton was announced, the relief was almost physical; his presence promised a diversion, a different current. He came in with no wasted motion, as if he brought with him the steadiness of mills and ledgers and fixed hours. He went straight to Mr. Hale, took both his hands, and wrung them hard without speaking, holding them so for a minute or two, his face and eyes saying what his voice did not attempt. Sympathy in him was not a florid thing; it was like a warm loaf set down in a hungry kitchen—plain, sustaining, wordless.

Then he turned to Margaret.

Not “better than likely” did she look. Her stately beauty was dimmed by watching and weeping; the skin about her eyes had a fine drawn look, as if sorrow had handled it too often. There was no theatrical despair upon her—only that patient, gentle sadness which, because it is quiet, is the more unmistakably suffering. He had meant to greet her with the studied coldness he had practised of late; he had resolved to keep distance, as if distance were a kind of remedy. But the sight of her, standing a little aside with the cautious timidity her uncertainty of his altered manner had bred, undid his resolution in an instant. He went to her and said the few necessary commonplace words—nothing remarkable in themselves—only his tone was so tender, so involuntarily softened, that her eyes filled and she turned away quickly, as if to hide a weakness that burned her cheeks.

She took up her work and sat down, very quiet, very silent, hands moving with a docility that was almost mechanical. Mr. Thornton’s heart beat hard; for that moment he forgot Outwood Lane altogether, forgot even the habits of self-command that usually held him like armour. He tried instead to talk to Mr. Hale, and—as always—his decisiveness, his practical thought, his very power of choosing an opinion and adhering to it, was a kind of safe harbour to a mind like Mr. Hale’s, easily blown from one gentle supposition to another. Margaret saw her father’s face take on a little steadiness as their conversation went on.

The room had settled into that uneasy calm—tea-things still unbrought in, the air faint with the old scent of mourning crape and extinguished candles—when Dixon appeared at the door.

“Miss Hale, you are wanted.”

Dixon’s tone, her flurried, breath-catching manner, struck Margaret like a hand upon the heart. In a moment she was cold, and then hot. Something had happened to Fred. She did not doubt it; she only wondered how the blow had chosen its shape. It was well, she thought with a swift flash of gratitude, that her father and Mr. Thornton were deep in talk.

“What is it, Dixon?” she asked the instant the drawing-room door was shut behind her.

“Come this way, miss,” Dixon said, and opened the door of what had been Mrs. Hale’s bed-chamber—now Margaret’s, because her father could not bear to sleep there again. The room still held the ghost of her mother in a hundred small domestic memories: the place where a lavender sachet used to lie, the faint lingering perfume as if linen had once been aired there, the hush that belonged to illness and last goodbyes.

“It’s nothing, miss,” Dixon said, choking a little. “Only a police-inspector. He wants to see you, miss. But I dare say it’s about nothing at all.”

“Did he name—” Margaret began; her voice would scarcely form the sound.

“No, miss; he named nothing. He only asked if you lived here, and if he could speak to you. Martha went to the door, and let him in; she has shown him into master’s study. I went to him myself, to try if that would do; but no—it’s you, miss, he wants.”

Margaret said no more until her hand rested on the study lock. She turned then, her face suddenly set, and spoke low and quickly.

“Take care papa does not come down. Mr. Thornton is with him now.”

She opened the door.

The inspector rose as she entered. He was a man practised in measuring other people’s faces and voices, used to flurry and evasion, to tears that were meant to soften, to anger meant to frighten. Yet the sight of Margaret Hale, with her controlled indignation and the haughty quiet of her carriage, checked him for an instant. She did not come in with curiosity, nor with the fidgeting eagerness of the innocent. She came as if she were a judge summoned to hear a charge, and would permit no liberties of manner.

She stood, waiting. She asked no question.

“I beg your pardon, ma’am,” he began, “but my duty obliges me to ask you a few plain questions. A man has died at the Infirmary, in consequence of a fall, received at Outwood station, between the hours of five and six on Thursday evening, the twenty-sixth instant. At the time, this fall did not seem of much consequence; but it was rendered fatal, the doctors say, by the presence of some internal complaint, and the man’s own habit of drinking.”

Her large dark eyes, fixed straight upon him, widened a little; it was no more than the opening of a shutter. Otherwise she did not stir. Her mouth held itself rigidly; the very curve of her lips seemed fuller with the strain of restraint, as if she were holding back words that would scorch her tongue. She neither blenched nor trembled. She simply looked at him, and made him go on.

“Well—go on!” she said at last, almost as if she would compel the story to reach its end.

“It is supposed that an inquest will have to be held,” he continued. “There is some slight evidence to prove that the blow, or push, or scuffle that caused the fall, was provoked by this poor fellow’s half-tipsy impertinence to a young lady, walking with the man who pushed the deceased over the edge of the platform. This much was observed by some one on the platform, who, however, thought no more about the matter, as the blow seemed of slight consequence. There is also some reason to identify the lady with yourself; in which case—”

“I was not there,” said Margaret.

Her voice sounded to her own ears heavy and remote, as if spoken through water. She did not flinch, did not try to explain, did not attempt to plead. The inspector bowed; he did not answer. The stillness of her denial, the steadiness of her gaze, gave him no easy handhold.

In truth his information was vague enough. A porter, rushing out to be ready for a train, had seen a scuffle at the far end of the platform between Leonards and a gentleman with a lady; he had heard no clear words, only seen the blur of movement. Then, before the train had reached full speed, the same porter had nearly been knocked down by Leonards himself running headlong, swearing and cursing in a frenzy that smelled of drink even at a distance. The porter had thought no more of it until the inspector, hunting up scraps, had found him and prised his recollection loose.

Further inquiry at the station had brought other fragments: the station-master remembered that a young lady and a gentleman had been there at about that hour; the lady remarkably handsome. A grocer’s assistant—one of those sharp-eyed lads who carry the names of neighbours and their purchases in their heads like a ledger—had said it was a Miss Hale living at Crampton, whose family dealt at his shop. None of it was certainty; it was merely a chain of may-bes that looked uncomfortably like a rope.

As for Leonards: he had gone half-mad with rage and pain to the nearest gin-palace, as if spirits could cauterise humiliation. His thick, tipsy words had not mattered to the busy waiters; yet they remembered him starting up suddenly, cursing himself for not having sooner thought of the electric telegraph—for some purpose they could not guess—and then leaving with that idea burning in him. On his way, overcome by pain or drink, he had lain down in the road. The police found him there and carried him to the Infirmary.

At the Infirmary he had never fully recovered consciousness. Once or twice he surfaced enough in his delirium that the authorities sent for the nearest magistrate, hoping to take down a dying deposition. But when the magistrate came, Leonards rambled of being at sea, mixing up names of captains and lieutenants with those of railway porters, as if his mind had dragged old, salt-stained memories into the same net as new grievances. His last words were a curse on a “Cornish trick” which, he said, had made him a hundred pounds poorer than he ought to have been.

All this the inspector carried in his thoughts while he watched Margaret’s face for the quick betrayals he was trained to expect. Yet her composure did not crack. If anything, it was too composed, too fixed—like a person who has forced herself to swallow something nauseating and is determined that not one muscle shall confess it.

“Then, madam,” he said at length, “I have your denial that you were the lady accompanying the gentleman who struck the blow, or gave the push, which caused the death of this poor man?”

A sharp pain shot through Margaret’s head, so swift it might have been light itself. Oh God! that I knew Frederick were safe! The thought tore at her with such violence that, for one instant, agony sprang in her eyes like a creature at bay. A deeper reader of faces might have seen it and understood. But the inspector’s keenness lay more in gathering facts than in sounding depths; he only noted, dimly, that her answer did not alter shape to meet his altered question.

“I was not there,” she said again, slowly, heavily.

The dull repetition stirred suspicion in him. It sounded like a lesson learned and repeated word for word, as if any variation might undo the speaker. He drew out his notebook and put it away with deliberate care, as if the very slowness of his motions might tempt her into a stumble.

“I hope you will not think me impertinent,” he said, looking up, “when I say, that I may have to call on you again. I may have to summon you to appear on the inquest, and prove an alibi, if my witnesses”—it was but one witness, but he spoke as if there were several—“persist in deposing to your presence at the unfortunate event.”

He watched for a wince. There was none. No rush of colour, no fluttering denial, no outraged exclamation. Her proud face held its quiet as a locked door holds the silence of a room.

He found himself a little abashed by what he took for innocence hardened into dignity. It must have been a mistake of identity; handsome young ladies were not so few as gossip imagined.

“It is very unlikely, ma’am, that I shall have to do anything of the kind,” he added, his voice falling into a more conciliatory tone. “I hope you will excuse me for doing what is only my duty, although it may appear impertinent.”

Margaret bowed her head slightly. Her lips were stiff and dry; she could not command even the common words of farewell. But suddenly she stepped forward, opened the study door, and went before him down the passage. At the front door she flung it wide for his exit, standing with the same fixed, dulled gaze upon him until he was fairly beyond the threshold and the street air had swallowed him up.

Then she shut the door.

She went halfway back into the study, as if she meant to return to whatever life had been a moment before. Then, as if a passionate instinct seized her, she turned sharply, pushed the key into the lock, and locked the door on the inside.

The click sounded loud—an iron note in the hush—like the last crack of a crust under a knife.

She moved into the study. She paused. The room swam; the edges of furniture seemed to waver, as if heat rose from the carpet. She tottered forward, paused again, swayed where she stood—upright for an instant by sheer habit of pride—and then fell prone upon the floor in a dead swoon.
