In the dimly lit corridors of Shepherd's Inn, a subtle dance of social etiquette unfolded. Mr. Pendennis, ever the master of convivial conversation, extended a hearty greeting to Mr. Bows, his tone deliberately bright, a stark contrast to the somber atmosphere that had settled over the group. As their hands clasped, a fleeting flush rose to Arthur's cheeks, mirrored by Fanny's, whose eyes darted nervously towards her mother. Mr. Bows, however, seemed oblivious to the undercurrents, his voice dripping with irony as he accepted Pen's assurance that he was bound for Mr. Costigan's chambers.

A heavy silence descended, akin to the stillness that precedes a tempest, and Pen, sensing the awkwardness, endeavored to break the spell with a boisterous laugh. The sound, however, fell flat, much like a solitary candle flame in a vast, dark vault. Pendennis attempted to regale the assembly with a jest, mimicking the exasperated tones of a Vauxhall Gardens check-taker, but the attempt was a misfire. Mrs. Bolton, perplexed by the performance, failed to discern whether Pen was lampooning the check-taker or Captain Costigan himself. Fanny's giggle was a tentative, almost fearful thing, while Mr. Bows's expression remained as stern as a fiddler's during a particularly complex piece.

As Pendennis's words trailed off, the darkness seemed to deepen, punctuated only by the distant clinking of the ticket-porter's boots as he made his way beneath the archway. Mr. Bows, his eyes glinting with a hint of melancholy, invited Pen to accompany him to his chambers, located high above the inn's bustle. Pen, however, declined, citing his own humble abode in Lamb Court as a testament to his ability to navigate the more rustic aspects of life.

Mr. Bows's remark, laced with a hint of condescension, touched a nerve in Pen, whose candle-like temper flared to life. "Sir," he said, his voice tinged with a mixture of indignation and defensiveness, "will you permit me to choose my own company without—?"
