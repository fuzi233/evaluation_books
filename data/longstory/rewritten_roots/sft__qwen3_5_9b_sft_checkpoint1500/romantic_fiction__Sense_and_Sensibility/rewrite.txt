For generations, the Dashwoods had called Sussex home, their substantial estate centered upon Norland Park standing as a testament to their enduring reputation among neighbors and friends alike. For decades, the estate had been the domain of a widower who lived to a ripe old age, his household anchored by his devoted sister, a companion who shared his life until her passing ten years prior. Her death left a void that the old gentleman filled by welcoming his nephew, Mr. Henry Dashwood—the rightful heir—and his family into his heart and home. There, amidst the warmth of his nephew, niece, and grandchildren, the elder gentleman found solace. Their genuine kindness, devoid of mere calculation, and the infectious joy of the children brought him profound contentment, making his twilight years a season of cherished companionship.

Mr. Henry Dashwood had been blessed with a son from a previous union and three daughters from his current marriage. The son, a steady and respectable youth, stood secure thanks to his mother's ample fortune, half of which passed to him upon reaching maturity, augmented further by his own marriage. Consequently, the inheritance of Norland Park held little weight for him compared to its sisters. Their futures, however, hung precariously in the balance; their mother possessed nothing, and their father, while holding seven thousand pounds, lacked full control over it, as the remainder of his first wife's dowry was legally bound to their younger sibling.

Tragedy struck swiftly. The old gentleman departed, leaving behind a will that offered a mixture of sorrow and relief. Though he acted without malice, the terms he set effectively stripped the value of his gift in half. Mr. Dashwood, who had coveted the estate primarily for the sake of his beloved wife and daughters, found his hopes dashed. The property was entailed for the benefit of his grandson—a spirited boy of four whose charming, if noisy, antics had captivated his uncle's affections, overshadowing the quiet devotion of the aunt and nieces. While the uncle's benevolence shone brightly in the form of a thousand pounds apiece for the three girls, the ironclad restrictions on the land itself left Mr. Dashwood powerless to provide for those who needed it most. He could not sell the timber nor burden the estate with necessary charges.

Mr. Dashwood's initial shock was deep, yet his naturally cheerful spirit allowed him to harbor hopes of managing the estate frugally and securing a future. Alas, fate intervened before these plans could take root. He succumbed to his illness just twelve months after the reading of the will. His estate, once vast, dissolved into a mere ten thousand pounds, divided between his widow and her three daughters.

In his final moments, knowing his end drew near, Mr. Dashwood summoned his son, Mr. John Dashwood. With the urgency born of impending death, he implored the young man to ensure the welfare of his mother-in-law and sisters.

John Dashwood, a man of mild temperament and conventional respectability, felt the weight of his dying father's plea. He readily promised to do whatever fell within his power to render them comfortable. This assurance soothed his father's anxious heart, granting John a brief respite to calculate the limits of his generosity.

John was not inherently wicked, though his nature leaned toward self-preservation and a lack of fervent emotion. A man of propriety, he maintained his station through careful adherence to duty. Perhaps a kinder wife might have softened his edges, perhaps even awakened a deeper capacity for love within him, for he had indeed loved his young bride. Yet, Mrs. John Dashwood proved to be a starker mirror of his own limitations, embodying a sharper, more relentless version of narrow-mindedness and selfishness.

Before his father's funeral concluded, Mrs. Dashwood arrived at Norland with her infant and retinue, having taken no counsel with her mother-in-law regarding her intentions. Though the house now belonged to her husband by right of inheritance, her sudden appearance was deemed by many a breach of courtesy, a stark contrast to the gentle hospitality she had previously enjoyed. To a woman of ordinary feeling, such an affront would have caused lasting bitterness. However, Mrs. Dashwood possessed a peculiar, almost theatrical sense of honor and a romanticized notion of generosity. To her, any slight, whether given or received, became a source of immutable disgust, rendering her incapable of acknowledging the rudeness of others' actions.

Though never a favorite of her husband's family, she had thus far lacked the opportunity to reveal how little regard she held for their comfort. Now, her true colors emerged.

Mrs. Dashwood felt the sting of her son's ingratitude acutely, her disdain for his conduct growing with every passing hour. So profound was her resentment that she considered fleeing the house forever. It took the earnest entreaties of her eldest daughter, Elinor, to persuade her to pause and reconsider the propriety of leaving. Furthermore, her tender love for her three children ultimately anchored her to the scene, compelling her to remain and avoid a rupture with her brother.

Elinor, the eldest, possessed a mind of remarkable strength and a judgment that was cool and clear. At nineteen, she served as her mother's counselor, often steering her away from the impulsiveness that threatened to lead them astray. Her heart was noble and affectionate, her feelings deep, yet she mastered the art of governing them. Where her mother struggled, Elinor prevailed; where her mother faltered, Elinor stood firm.

Her youngest sister, Marianne, was equally gifted in intellect and spirit, yet her nature differed vastly from Elinor's. Marianne was eager in everything she undertook, her emotions ranging from sorrow to joy without measure. Generous, amiable, and captivating, she lacked the prudence that guided her older sister. The resemblance between Marianne and her mother was striking, both in spirit and in their tendency toward emotional excess.

Watching her sister, Elinor observed the dangerous depth of Marianne's sensibility with concern. Yet, her mother, in her grief, viewed this same trait with pride. Together, the mother and Marianne indulged in the violence of their mourning, seeking out further anguish in their reflections, deliberately rejecting any solace offered. They dwelt entirely upon their sorrow, feeding it with thoughts that only deepened their wretchedness, vowing never to accept comfort again.

Elinor, though deeply afflicted, retained a reservoir of strength. She could summon the fortitude to consult with her brother, to welcome his wife with due politeness, and to strive to rouse her mother from her despair. She endeavored to encourage her mother toward the same restraint and forbearance that she practiced so diligently herself, hoping to guide her family back from the precipice of ruin.

Then there was Margaret, the third sister, a girl of thirteen with a good-natured and well-meaning disposition. Having absorbed much of Marianne's romanticism without acquiring her sister's sharp intellect, she seemed unlikely to match her elders' capabilities as she grew older.

Mrs. John Dashwood settled into her dominion over Norland Park, establishing a regime where she alone held the title of mistress. Her mother and her sisters-in-law were relegated to the status of guests, though Mrs. Dashwood maintained a veneer of polite civility toward them, and her husband offered a kindness that bordered on the perfunctory, reserved strictly for those who were not himself, his wife, or their infant son. With a gravity that belied his lack of genuine sentiment, he urged them to view Norland as their permanent home. His proposal aligned perfectly with Mrs. Dashwood's desires; she, whose nature oscillated wildly between boundless cheer and profound desolation, found solace in the familiar surroundings that echoed her past joys. In moments of mirth, she possessed a sanguine optimism that seemed indistinguishable from happiness itself, yet in sorrow, her imagination carried her away to realms where comfort was impossible, leaving her utterly inconsolable.

However, the prospect of financial aid for the widowed sister and her daughters struck a discordant note in Mrs. John Dashwood's heart. To strip three thousand pounds from the inheritance of their beloved little boy would, in her estimation, render him destitute. She implored her husband to reconsider, questioning how he could justify robbing his sole offspring of such a substantial sum. Furthermore, she argued, what moral obligation did the Miss Dashwoods hold against him? They were connected only by half-blood, a bond she dismissed as non-existent. It was common knowledge, she reasoned, that affection rarely bloomed between children of different fathers, and thus, why should he sacrifice his future security and his son's prosperity to support strangers?

"It was my father's dying wish," Mr. Dashwood countered solemnly, "that I assist his widow and daughters."

"Father was likely delirious at the time," Mrs. Dashwood retorted sharply. "If he had been of sound mind, he would never have asked you to squander half your fortune on them. He spoke in vague, sentimental terms, 'assist' and 'make them comfortable,' but he never specified an amount. Perhaps, if he had trusted you to use your judgment, you would have seen reason. Now that the promise has been extracted, it must be kept, but let us ensure it does not bankrupt us."

She pressed further, suggesting that the gift need not total three thousand pounds. "Consider," she urged, "once money leaves the family, it returns no more. Your sisters will inevitably marry, and the capital will vanish forever. Unless it can somehow be reclaimed—"

"To be certain," Mr. Dashwood interjected, "the loss would weigh heavily on Harry. Should he grow a large family, that sum would prove invaluable."

"And surely it would," Mrs. John Dashwood conceded. "Prudence dictates, then, that we reduce the offer by half. Five hundred pounds per person would be a magnificent gesture!"

"Oh, indeed!" cried Mrs. Dashwood, feigning awe. "What brother in all the realm would offer half so much, especially to half-sisters! Yet, you possess a spirit so generous!"

"I seek to avoid anything beneath contempt," Mr. Dashwood declared. "On such matters, excess is preferable to deficiency. Surely, no one could accuse me of stinginess, even if they were the recipients themselves."

"They may have high expectations," Mrs. John Dashwood noted dryly, "but we must judge by what you can afford, not by their whims."

"Indeed. I believe I can manage five hundred pounds apiece." Mr. Dashwood calculated aloud. "Without my contribution, they stand to inherit about three thousand pounds each upon their mother's passing. That constitutes a respectable fortune for any young lady."

"Adequate, certainly," agreed Mrs. John Dashwood. "Divided among three sisters, that yields ten thousand pounds. Even if they remain single, they could live comfortably on the interest. If they marry, their prospects are secured."

"That holds true," Mr. Dashwood admitted. "Therefore, perhaps the wiser course is to assist the mother during her lifetime, via an annuity. A hundred pounds annually would elevate their standard of living significantly."

Mr. Dashwood paused, allowing a flicker of hesitation to surface. "It is preferable to a lump sum of fifteen hundred, yet..."

"But if Mother lives fifteen years?" Mrs. John Dashwood warned. "We shall be ruined."

"Fifteen years! My dear Fanny, her vitality is unlikely to sustain her for half that duration."

"People invariably outlive their contracts when an annuity hangs over them," Mrs. John Dashwood persisted. "Mother is robust, barely forty. Annuity payments recur endlessly, binding you to a cycle of debt you cannot escape. I speak from bitter experience. My own mother was burdened by three similar obligations imposed by Father's will. The administrative nightmare was endless; two payments a year, the constant dread of false deaths, and the sheer ungratefulness of the recipients. They took it as their due, feeling no gratitude whatsoever. It drained Mother's joy and autonomy. I harbor a deep loathing for annuities; I would not sign one under any circumstance."

"The sentiment is understandable," Mr. Dashwood murmured. "Those recurring drains on income erode one's independence. Fortune becomes merely a resource to be rationed, not enjoyed. And worse, there is often no thanksgiving for the charity."

"Precisely," Mrs. John Dashwood affirmed. "Do not tie yourself to a rigid contract. Offer occasional gifts, perhaps fifty pounds now and then, at your discretion. This prevents immediate distress without locking you into a lifetime of penury. It fulfills the spirit of your father's request while preserving your integrity."

"You speak wisely, my love," Mr. Dashwood yielded. "An occasional present is far superior to a permanent encumbrance. It avoids enlarging their lifestyle artificially, ensuring they remain dependent on your goodwill rather than entitled to a fixed income."

"Yes, it will," Mrs. John Dashwood insisted. "In truth, I am convinced your father envisioned nothing more than basic hospitality. Finding them a modest dwelling, assisting with the move, providing seasonal provisions like fish and game—that was the extent of his intent. It would be bizarre and unjust to assume he foresaw a transfer of wealth. Consider the reality: your mother-in-law and her daughters will retain seven thousand pounds, plus the individual tracts of five hundred pounds generated by the girls' shares, yielding five hundred pounds a year collectively. With frugal habits, minimal staff, and no carriages, they will live comfortably on that income alone, paying their mother for board from the surplus. Why give them more? It is absurd."

"On my honor," Mr. Dashwood bowed slightly, "you are undeniably correct. My father's request must be interpreted within the bounds of reasonable neighborliness. I shall fulfill my engagement through practical kindness: aiding in the removal, offering suitable furnishings, and extending the courtesy of a host. Nothing more."

"Certainly," Mrs. John Dashwood nodded, her face brightening. "Yet, one final matter must be addressed. Upon your parents' departure from Norland, all the china, silver, and linens from Stanhill were preserved and left to your mother. Consequently, her new residence will be nearly fully furnished the moment she moves in."

"A significant legacy," Mr. Dashwood observed, his tone shifting subtly. "Though, I confess, a portion of that silverware would have adorned our tables here with considerable elegance."

"So it is," Mrs. John Dashwood replied, her voice sharpening. "The breakfast service alone surpasses ours in beauty. It is a treasure troo too magnificent for any establishment they could possibly maintain. But your father saw only *their* needs. Let me remind you, however, that you owe him no profound gratitude, nor are you bound by his every whim. We both know that, had circumstances permitted, he would have bequeathed nearly his entire estate to them. Therefore, do not feel compelled to stretch your resources beyond the limits of simple decency."

Her words proved irrefutable. They hardened Mr. Dashwood's resolve, stripping away any lingering doubt. He concluded that it would not only be imprudent but also undignified to provide any further assistance to the widow and her children than the modest, neighborly gestures outlined by his wife.

Mrs. Dashwood stayed at Norland for nearly four months, not because she lacked the desire to leave the place where every familiar corner still stung her with grief, but because once her sorrow began to dull, her heart grew restless for change. She was eager to find a home nearby, refusing to imagine living far from the garden where her husband's ashes lay. Yet, despite her endless search, she found no house that balanced comfort with the strict financial caution of her eldest daughter, Margaret. While Mrs. Dashwood would have welcomed grandeur, Margaret's steady mind rejected any estate that strained their limited income.

Throughout this time, Mrs. Dashwood clung to a hope planted by her late husband: a solemn promise from his son, John, to provide generously for her and her girls. Though she privately believed seven thousand pounds fell short of true affluence, she held onto John's word as a lifeline. It brought her joy to think of her children supported, and it softened her regret over having previously underestimated her son-in-law's capacity for kindness. Now, seeing the care John took with his father's memory, she forgave her past suspicions.

Yet, the shadow of Mrs. John Dashwood loomed larger than ever. Having resided with her for six months, Mrs. Dashwood's initial dislike had deepened into a firm conviction that the two women could never coexist. However, fate intervened with a silver lining: a growing romance between her eldest daughter, Elinor, and Edward Ferrars, the brother of Mrs. John Dashwood.

Edward was a gentle soul, though not striking in appearance or manner. He possessed a shy reserve that made him seem unassuming, but beneath that exterior beat a warm, affectionate heart. Unlike his ambitious mother, who dreamed of placing him in Parliament, or his sister, who longed for him to drive a carriage through high society, Edward contented himself with quiet domesticity. He had no flair for politics or grand gestures; his world was small, safe, and filled with the simple comforts of home.

For a long time, Mrs. Dashwood barely noticed him, lost in her own mourning. But one afternoon, a remark from Elinor shifted her perspective entirely. Comparing Edward to his vivacious sister, Fanny, Elinor noted how utterly different they were. "It is enough," Mrs. Dashwood declared, her face lighting up, "to say that he is unlike Fanny. That implies everything amiable. I love him already."

"I think you will like him better when you know him fully," Elinor suggested gently.

"Like him!" her mother laughed, a sound rare in those somber days. "I feel no lesser emotion than love."

"You may merely esteem him."

"I have never learned to separate esteem from love."

From that moment, Mrs. Dashwood threw herself into learning about Edward. Her warmth quickly melted away his shyness, revealing a man of solid understanding and genuine feeling. She saw clearly that he cared deeply for Elinor, and as her affection for him grew, so did her certainty of his worth. The quietness of his nature, once puzzling, now seemed like proof of a sincere and steady character. Soon, she envisioned their future together, speaking of their marriage with joyful anticipation.

"My dear Marianne," she told her youngest daughter, "soon Elinor will be settled for life. Yes, we shall miss her dearly, but she will be happy."

"Oh, Mama! How shall we bear it?" Marianne cried, tears welling in her eyes.

"It will feel less like a separation," her mother reassured her, smiling. "We shall live within miles of each other, meeting daily. And you will gain a brother—a truly affectionate one."

But Marianne, ever the dreamer, looked grave. "Perhaps I view it with surprise," she admitted. "Edward is kind, and I admire him. Yet... he lacks something. His figure is plain, lacking the grace I expect in a man who captures my sister's heart. His eyes lack spirit and fire, the signs of true virtue and intellect. Worse still, I fear he has no real taste. Music seems to hold little sway over him, and while he praises Elinor's drawings, it is the admiration of a lover, not a connoisseur. He knows nothing of art, only of affection."

Marianne sighed, her voice trembling with frustration. "To satisfy me, a man must possess both virtue and beauty. His tastes must mirror mine perfectly—the same books, the same melodies. Last night, when he read those lines that usually drive me wild, his voice was so flat, so indifferent! I felt a sharp pang for my sister, though she endured it with such serene composure. I could barely sit still. To hear such beautiful words spoken with such cold detachment..."

"He would surely have done justice to simpler prose," Mrs. Dashwood interjected wisely. "And yes, you asked him to read Cowper. But let us allow for differences in taste. Elinor does not share your fiery passions, and thus she may overlook his shortcomings. If you loved him, however, hearing him read with such lack of sensitivity would indeed break your heart. My love, remember that you are not yet seventeen. There is still time to find the perfect partner."

"And if I do not?" Marianne whispered, looking out the window as if searching for a vision of the ideal. "The more I learn of the world, the more convinced I become that I shall never find a man worthy of my love. I require so much! He must unite every virtue of Edward Ferrars with the charm of his person and manners."

"What a shame it is, Elinor," sighed Marianne, "that Edward possesses no talent for sketching."

"No talent!" cried Elinor, looking up with mild surprise. "Why should you suppose that? While he does not hold a pencil himself, he takes immense pleasure in viewing the works of others. I promise you, his natural sensibility is quite unimpaired; he simply lacks the chance to cultivate it. If he had ever been guided properly, I am convinced he would have become a fine artist. He trusts his own judgment so little in matters of art that he rarely offers an opinion, yet he possesses an innate correctness and simplicity of taste that invariably leads him aright."

Marianne, fearing to wound her sister's feelings, fell silent on the matter. However, the kind of approval Elinor claimed stirred within Edward by the drawings of others was worlds away from the rapturous ecstasy that Marianne believed constituted true taste. Though she smiled inwardly at the error, she honored her sister for the blind devotion to Edward that caused it.

"I trust, Marianne," Elinor resumed, "that you do not view him as lacking in general taste. Indeed, I venture to say you cannot, for your conduct toward him is so warm and cordial. If that were not your sentiment, I am sure you could never treat him with such kindness."

Marianne hesitated, unsure of what to utter. To hurt her sister's feelings was unthinkable, yet to voice a falsehood was equally difficult. Finally, she replied:

"Do not take offense, Elinor, if my praise falls short of your full appreciation of his virtues. I have had fewer occasions to gauge the subtle workings of his mind, his specific inclinations and tastes, than you have. But I hold the highest opinion in the world of his goodness and intellect. To me, he embodies everything that is noble and lovable."

"I am certain," Elinor answered with a gentle smile, "that even his dearest friends would not be displeased with such glowing testimony. I struggle to imagine how you could speak more warmly."

Marianne was delighted to see how easily her sister was content.

"As for his intelligence and his virtue," Elinor continued, "there can be no doubt among anyone who has spent enough time engaging him in open conversation. The brilliance of his understanding and the soundness of his principles are hidden only by that reserve which keeps him so often silent. You know him well enough to do justice to his substantial worth. As for those 'minuter propensities' you mentioned, peculiar circumstances have kept you less informed on them than I have. He and I have spent considerable time together, whereas you have been wholly absorbed by maternal duties. I have observed him closely, studied his thoughts, and listened to his views on literature and aesthetics. On the whole, I venture to declare that his mind is well-educated, his love for books profound, his imagination vivid, his observations sharp and accurate, and his taste refined and pure. His abilities grow more impressive with familiarity, just as his manners and appearance do. Initially, his address may seem unremarkable, and his features hardly handsome until one perceives the extraordinary gentleness of his eyes and the overall sweetness of his countenance. Now, knowing him as I do, I consider him truly handsome—or at least nearly so. What do you say, Marianne?"

"I shall very soon deem him handsome, Elinor, if I do not already," Marianne declared. "When you urge me to cherish him as a brother, I shall see no flaw in his face any more than I now see any defect in his heart."

Elinor recoiled slightly at this declaration, regretting the warmth she had inadvertently shown in describing him. She knew Edward held a high place in her affections and suspected his feelings were mutual, yet she required absolute certainty before finding comfort in Marianne's conviction of their attachment. She recognized that her mother and sister tended to leap from conjecture to belief, where wishing equated to hoping, and hoping to expecting. She endeavored to clarify the reality of their situation.

"I do not deny," she said softly, "that I think very highly of him—that I greatly esteem and like him."

Here, Marianne erupted in indignant protest.

"Esteem him! Like him! Cold-hearted Elinor! Oh, worse than cold-hearted! Ashamed to feel otherwise! Say those words again, and I shall leave the room this instant!"

Elinor could not help laughing. "Forgive me," she said. "Be assured I meant no offense by speaking quietly of my own feelings. Believe them to be stronger than I have expressed; believe them, in short, to be commensurate with his merit and the hope—however faint—that his affection for me might warrant. But go no further. I am by no means certain of his regard. There are moments when its depth seems doubtful, and until his sentiments are fully revealed, you cannot blame me for avoiding any encouragement of my partiality by believing or declaring it more than it truly is. In my heart, I feel little—if any—doubt of his preference. Yet, other factors must be weighed. He is far from independent. What his mother truly is, we cannot fully know, but based on Fanny's occasional comments regarding her conduct and opinions, we have never deemed her amiable. And I am greatly mistaken if Edward does not realize himself that marrying a woman without great fortune or high rank would encounter many obstacles."

Marianne was stunned to discover how much the imaginations of her mother and herself had outrun the truth.

"And you are not engaged to him!" she exclaimed. "And yet, it surely will soon happen! But two benefits arise from this delay. First, I shall not lose you so quickly. Second, Edward will have ample opportunity to improve that natural taste for your favorite pursuit, which must be indispensable to your future happiness. Oh, if he were so inspired by your genius as to learn to draw himself, how delightful it would be!"

Elinor had laid bare her true thoughts to her sister. She could not consider Marianne's partiality for Edward as thriving as her sister believed. At times, a certain lack of spirit about him suggested something far less promising than mere indifference—a shadow of depression that hinted at deeper troubles. Doubt regarding her own regard, if he felt it, would not necessarily cause such profound melancholy. A more probable cause lay in his dependent position, which forbade the free indulgence of his affections. She knew that his mother neither conducted herself kindly enough to make his current home comfortable nor offered any assurance that he could establish a home of his own without strictly adhering to her ambitions for aggrandizement. Armed with this knowledge, it was impossible for Elinor to remain at ease. She was far from relying on the happy conclusion her mother and sister still assumed certain. Indeed, the longer they remained together, the more uncertain the nature of his regard appeared; and sometimes, for a few painful minutes, she feared it was nothing more than friendship.

However, whatever its true limits, the fact that it was noticed by his sister was enough to disturb her peace, and simultaneously—to her frequent dismay—to render her uncivil. Seizing the first opportunity, she affronted her mother-in-law, speaking expressively of her son's great prospects, of Mrs. Ferrars's resolution that both her sons must marry well, and of the peril facing any young woman who dared to "_draw him in._" Mrs. Dashwood could neither pretend ignorance nor maintain composure. She received an answer marked by contempt and immediately departed, resolving that, regardless of the inconvenience or expense of such a sudden departure, her beloved Elinor should not be exposed another week to such insinuations.

In this state of spirits, a letter arrived from the post, offering a proposal particularly timely. It was an offer of a modest house, available on very easy terms, belonging to a relative of her own, a gentleman of substance and property in Devonshire. The letter came directly from him and was written in the true spirit of friendly accommodation. He understood her need for a dwelling; although the house he offered was merely a cottage, he assured her that everything necessary could be done to make it suitable if the location pleased her. He earnestly urged her, along with her daughters, to visit Barton Park, his own residence, from whence she could judge for herself whether Barton Cottage, situated in the same parish, could be rendered comfortable through alterations. His letter was written with such genuine anxiety to accommodate them, and in a style so friendly, that it could not fail to bring pleasure to his cousin, especially at a moment when she suffered under the cold and unfeeling behavior of her closer kin. She needed no time for deliberation or inquiry. Her resolution was formed as she read. The situation of Barton, in a county so distant from Sussex as Devonshire—which, but a few hours prior, would have been a sufficient objection to outweigh every possible advantage—now became its first recommendation. To leave the neighborhood of Norland was no longer an evil; it was an object of desire, a blessing compared to the misery of remaining her daughter-in-law's guest. To remove forever from that beloved place would be less painful than to inhabit or visit it while such a woman ruled it. Instantly, she wrote to Sir John Middleton acknowledging his kindness and accepting his proposal. Then, she hastened to show both letters to her daughters, securing their approbation before sending her reply.

As soon as her reply was sent away, Mrs. Dashwood turned to her son-in-law and daughter-in-law with a bright, relieved smile. "I have secured a house," she announced, her voice light with the joy of independence. "It means I shall not trouble you for long, only until my new home is quite ready." The news struck them with genuine surprise. Mrs. John Dashwood remained silent, but her husband politely expressed the hope that she would not settle too far from Norland. With a warm confidence, Mrs. Dashwood replied that she was moving to Devonshire.

At the mere mention of the name, Edward spun around, his face alight with shock and deep concern. "Devonshire!" he exclaimed, his tone urgent enough to make the truth clear without words. "Are you really going there? How far is that from here? And where exactly?" When she described the location—a charming spot just four miles north of Exeter—he let out a breath he seemed to have been holding for some time.

"It is merely a cottage," she continued gently, "yet I look forward to welcoming many old friends. A couple of rooms can easily be added if needed, and if they find the journey manageable, I am certain I shall make their stay comfortable." She ended with a tender invitation for both Mr. and Mrs. John Dashwood to visit her at Barton, though for Edward alone, her warmth burned even brighter. Although her recent talk with his wife had hardened her resolve to leave Norland as quickly as possible, it had not altered her feelings regarding the separation of Edward and Elinor. In fact, by inviting her brother so warmly, she intended to prove to Mrs. John Dashwood how utterly she dismissed her disapproval of the match.

Mr. John Dashwood, however, found little comfort in the news. He repeatedly voiced how deeply grieved he was that the distance would prevent him from helping move her furniture. He genuinely felt a pang of conscience, for the effort he had promised his father—the transfer of goods—now seemed impossible. Yet, the furniture itself traveled smoothly via the river, consisting mostly of linens, silver, fine china, books, and a beautiful pianoforte belonging to Marianne. As the crates departed, Mrs. John Dashwood watched with a visible sigh. She could not suppress the thought that it was unfair for Mrs. Dashwood, whose income was so modest compared to theirs, to retain such lovely pieces.

Mrs. Dashwood had leased the cottage for a year; it was fully furnished and available immediately. There were no hiccups in the arrangements, and she awaited only the clearing of Norland and the finalizing of her household before heading west. This transition happened swiftly, for she moved with remarkable energy whenever something mattered to her. Her horses, sold shortly after her husband's death, joined the caravan; at her eldest daughter's wise counsel, she also parted with her carriage. Had she acted solely on impulse, she might have clung to it, but Elinor's practicality prevailed. Their staff was whittled down to three: two maids and a footman, drawn from the former Norland crew.

Two servants were dispatched ahead to Devonshire to prepare the cottage, for Lady Middleton was a stranger to Mrs. Dashwood, and she preferred to go straight to her new home rather than linger as a guest at Barton Park. Relying completely on Sir John's glowing description, she felt no urge to inspect the property until she walked through its doors as its mistress. Her eagerness to flee Norland grew rather than waned, fueled by the obvious delight her daughter-in-law showed at the prospect of her removal. This happiness, however thinly veiled behind a perfunctory suggestion to delay the departure, stung. It was precisely now, when Norland stood empty, that Mr. John Dashwood's promise to his father ought to have been honored. Having failed to act upon first arriving at the estate, their imminent departure presented the perfect moment. Yet, as days passed, Mrs. Dashwood began to surrender every lingering hope. The general tenor of her son-in-law's conversations revealed a man preoccupied with rising household costs and the relentless demands placed on his purse. A gentleman of his standing, he seemed to imply, was perpetually stretched thin financially. It became clear to her that he needed money more than he possessed any intention of giving it away.

Within a few weeks of receiving Sir John Middleton's first letter, everything was arranged for their journey. Many tears were shed during their final farewells to a place so deeply loved. "Dear, dear Norland!" Marianne cried, wandering alone before the house on the last evening of their residence. "When shall I stop grieving for you? When will I feel at home anywhere else? Oh, happy home, could you know what pain I feel watching you from this distance, from where I may never return! And you, familiar trees—you will remain unchanged. Not a leaf will wither because we have gone, nor a branch freeze in silence while we watch you no more. You will stay the same, unaware of the joy or sorrow you cause, blind to the changes in those who once walked beneath your shade. But who will remain to cherish you?"

The initial leg of their journey was heavy with a gloom so profound it rendered the miles interminable and the scenery unappealing. Yet, as the landscape began to shift, the allure of their impending home softened their spirits. Upon crossing into Barton Valley, the air seemed to brighten. It was a verdant expanse, lush with trees and abundant pastures. Winding along for over a mile, they finally spied their new dwelling. Before the house lay a modest, grassy courtyard, accessed by a tidy gate.

Barton Cottage, though diminutive, offered a sense of cozy completeness. However, it lacked the rustic charm expected of a cottage; it was too orderly, the roof tiled, the shutters unpainted, and the walls bare of climbing vines. A narrow corridor cut straight through the heart of the house to the rear garden. Flanking the entrance were two sitting rooms, roughly sixteen feet square, leading to offices and staircases. Four bedrooms and two attic chambers completed the layout. Built recently and in excellent condition, it paled in comparison to Norland, which now seemed a palace by contrast. Yet, as they stepped inside, the sting of memory quickly faded, washed away by the genuine delight of the waiting staff. Each sister, driven by love for the other, resolved to mask her sorrow with a forced cheer. Early September brought fine weather, and the brightness of the sky cast a favorable light on the house, cementing their acceptance of it.

The setting was picturesque. High hills rose immediately behind, framing views of open downs and cultivated slopes on either side. Below, the village of Barton sprawled across one of these heights, offering a charming vista from the cottage windows. Beyond, the valley stretched wide, bounded by steep ridges that eventually dipped between two imposing peaks.

Mrs. Dashwood, despite her accustomed standards, found herself largely content. While her previous lifestyle necessitated many luxuries, she relished the act of improvement. With her remaining funds, she planned to elevate the decor. "The house itself," she mused, "is certainly too cramped for our family, but we shall manage comfortably until spring. If fortune smiles then, we might expand. These parlors are far too small for the gatherings I envision; I dream of merging the passage into one room, creating a grander entrance, adding a drawing-room and extra chambers above. It could become a snug little haven. One cannot expect perfection, though widening those stairs should prove straightforward. We shall wait to see where we stand financially before planning further."

Until these renovations could be funded from her meager savings—a sum accumulated by a woman who had never known thrift—they wisely decided to accept the home as it stood. Both sisters busied themselves making it feel like home, surrounding themselves with books and cherished items. Marianne carefully unpacked her pianoforte, finding its proper place, while Elinor pinned her sketches to the walls of the sitting room.

Their quiet preparations were abruptly interrupted the following morning by the arrival of their landlord. Sir John Middleton came to welcome them, promising every convenience his estate could offer to弥补 any deficiencies in their new quarters. Sir John was a man of about forty, with a pleasing countenance and a demeanor that radiated geniality. Though his cousins at Stanhill might have forgotten him, his visit there was fresh in their minds. His manners were as warm as his letter, and his presence seemed to bring him genuine joy in hosting them. He spoke passionately of living amicably with his family and urged them with relentless politeness to dine daily at Barton Park until they were fully settled. Though his persistence bordered on impudence, his kindness was impossible to resent. His generosity extended beyond words; shortly after his departure, a bountiful basket of garden produce and fruit arrived, followed swiftly by game. He even volunteered to carry their correspondence to and from the post, refusing to let them miss his daily newspaper.

He also delivered a courteous message from Lady Middleton, expressing her intention to pay her respects once she knew it caused no inconvenience. An equally polite invitation was returned, and the next day saw her introduction.

The Dashwoods, acutely aware that their comfort at Barton depended heavily on Lady Middleton, were eager to meet her. Her appearance confirmed their hopes; she possessed a handsome face, a tall, striking figure, and moved with grace. Her manners possessed an elegance lacking in her husband's, yet they were marred by a distinct absence of his frankness and warmth. She appeared reserved, cool, and remarkably taciturn, limiting her conversation to the most mundane inquiries. This aloofness slowly eroded the Dashwoods' initial admiration.

Fortunately, conversation was unnecessary, for Sir John was endlessly talkative. Lady Middleton had wisely brought her eldest son, a fine little boy of about six, ensuring that whenever the ladies faced silence, they could discuss his name, age, beauty, and peculiar shyness. She answered his questions for him, while he clung to her skirts, hanging his head in a manner that bewildered his mother, who noted his usual boisterous nature at home. Every formal visit required a child to serve as a prop for discourse. In this instance, ten minutes were spent debating whether the boy resembled his father or mother, a debate in which everyone disagreed and everyone was astonished by the opinions of the others.

Soon, however, the topic of the younger children arose, giving the Dashwoods ample opportunity to weigh in, as Sir John refused to depart without securing their promise to dine at the Park the following day.

Barton Park stood roughly half a mile from the cottage, hidden from the sisters' view by the sweeping curve of a nearby hill. Inside, the house was a testament to grandeur, inhabited by the Middletons, who lived with a blend of generous hospitality and polished elegance. Yet, beneath this veneer lay a profound emptiness. Sir John derived his purpose from hunting and shooting, while Lady Middleton found her sole fulfillment in doting on her children. Their lives, devoid of genuine talent or intellectual curiosity, revolved entirely around the endless cycle of hosting guests. For them, social engagement was not merely a pastime but a necessity, filling the void where substance and sensibility should have resided.

Lady Middleton took immense pride in the perfection of her table settings and the flawless arrangement of her home, deriving her greatest joy from the admiration of her domestic displays. Conversely, Sir John possessed a more authentic, if boisterous, enthusiasm for society. He delighted in crowding his halls with young people, finding the noise and chaos of their gatherings a form of entertainment. In the summer, he organized outdoor feasts of cold ham and chicken; in the winter, he hosted lively private balls, eager to entertain anyone young enough to possess an "unsatiable appetite" for diversion.

To Sir John, the arrival of the Dashwood sisters was a cause for genuine celebration. He found them young, beautiful, and unpretentious—a combination he deemed irresistible. To a man like Sir John, where being "unaffected" was the highest virtue, the sisters' lack of artificiality secured his immediate affection. Furthermore, he took a peculiar pleasure in accommodating them, viewing their modest circumstances as a challenge to his generosity. By housing them, he satisfied his benevolent instincts while simultaneously enjoying the practical convenience of keeping a group of women close at hand, sparing himself the trouble of entertaining them elsewhere.

Upon their arrival, Sir John greeted them at the door with a warmth that felt almost sincere, leading them directly into the drawing room. There, he regaled them with tales of his recent struggles to find suitable company, lamenting the scarcity of "smart young men" in the area. He introduced them to the single gentleman present, a particular friend of his who was neither notably youthful nor particularly vivacious, hoping they would overlook the meagerness of the party. Fortunately, he noted, Lady Middleton's mother, Mrs. Jennings, had arrived just hours prior. Described as a cheerful and agreeable soul, she promised to enliven the gathering, ensuring the young ladies would not find the evening tedious. Indeed, the sisters, relieved to have two new acquaintances, expressed little desire for further additions to the circle.

Mrs. Jennings proved to be exactly as described: a jolly, plump, elderly woman whose conversations flowed rapidly between jokes and observations. While seemingly happy-go-lucky, her wit lacked refinement, bordering on the vulgar. Before dinner concluded, she had already launched into animated discussions regarding love and matrimony, teasing the guests about leaving their hearts behind in Sussex and pretending to detect blushing cheeks where none existed. This relentless prying caused Marianne significant distress, especially considering her sister's delicate feelings. Turning anxiously to Elinor, Marianne watched with deep concern to see how her calm companion would endure such intrusive banter, a sight that weighed heavily on her heart far more than any ordinary teasing could.

Seated among them was Colonel Brandon, Sir John's companion. A strikingly different figure from his host, Colonel Brandon appeared ill-suited to be the friend of a man like Sir John, just as Lady Middleton seemed ill-matched to him. He was silent, grave, and unsmiling. To Marianne and Margaret, he looked like an "absolute old bachelor," yet he was actually closer to fifty than forty. Though his features were not conventionally handsome, his countenance radiated a quiet sense, and his manners were impeccably gentlemanly. Despite the initial impression of the other guests, there was something about his demeanor that commanded a certain, if restrained, respect.

As the evening progressed, the company offered little that the Dashwoods could truly admire. However, the pervasive coldness and insipidity of Lady Middleton rendered the gravity of Colonel Brandon, and even the raucous hilarity of Sir John and his mother, somewhat bearable by comparison. The atmosphere shifted dramatically only after dinner, when Lady Middleton's four noisy children burst into the room, pulling at their mother's dress, tearing at her gown, and reducing all conversation to frantic inquiries about their own needs.

Later, as twilight settled, it became apparent that Marianne possessed a musical talent. An invitation was immediately extended, and the piano was unlocked with anticipation. Every eye turned to her, expecting to be enchanted. At their request, Marianne sang several of the popular songs that Lady Middleton had recently introduced to the household. It was a curious irony that these melodies, once performed with great skill by Lady Middleton's mother, had gathered dust on the instrument since her daughter married, having been abandoned in favor of a life dedicated solely to raising children.

Marianne's performance was met with thunderous applause. Sir John was the loudest in his praise, cheering enthusiastically at the conclusion of every aria and engaging in equally loud chatter with the others throughout the song. Lady Middleton, however, grew increasingly agitated, calling him to order repeatedly. She marveled at how anyone could possibly divert their attention from music for even a moment and implored Marianne to repeat a specific song she had just heard, displaying a complete inability to appreciate nuance or silence.

Amidst this cacophony, Colonel Brandon remained the solitary observer. He listened without joining the frenzy, offering only the polite compliment of attentive hearing. As the others lost themselves in shallow adoration, Marianne felt a sudden, sharp respect for the Colonel. The shamelessness of the crowd's lack of discernment stung her deeply. Her pleasure in his appreciation, though it fell short of the ecstatic fervor required to fully mirror her own inner world, was estimable precisely because it existed in stark contrast to the "horrible insensibility" surrounding them. And yet, as the notes faded, Marianne exercised a measure of reason. She acknowledged that a man of fifty-three might well have outgrown the acute sensitivity and exquisite capacity for enjoyment that fueled her own soul. With a heavy heart, she decided to grant him the allowance of humanity due to his age, forgiving the coldness that time had undoubtedly carved into his spirit.

Mrs. Jennings sat comfortably in her parlor, a widow whose ample jointure freed her from the necessity of work, save for one singular ambition: to arrange marriages for everyone within her reach. Having successfully wedded her two daughters, she viewed the rest of the world through the lens of potential unions. Her mind worked quickly, spotting the flicker of attachment before anyone else, delighting in raising the blushes and vanity of young ladies by hinting at their sway over certain gentlemen. Upon arriving at Barton Cottage, her discernment was swift and decisive. Within days, she declared that Colonel Brandon was deeply, hopelessly in love with Miss Marianne Dashwood. The evidence, to her mind, was irrefutable: the colonel's rapt attention during Marianne's songs, first at the park and then again when the Middletons visited, spoke volumes. She saw no obstacle; he was wealthy, and she was beautiful. An excellent match, she concluded, and indeed, she had longed for the colonel's happiness since Sir John introduced him to her circle.

To Mrs. Jennings, the immediate joy of this prospect lay in the endless ammunition it provided for her wit. At the Park, she laughed gently at the colonel; at the cottage, she teased Marianne relentlessly about her feelings. While the colonel likely found her raillery harmless, Marianne struggled to comprehend it. Initially baffled, she eventually realized the sting behind her mother-in-law's jokes—that they mocked not just her emotions, but the colonel's apparent age and his lonely existence as a bachelor.

Mrs. Dashwood, however, stood firm against the notion that a man five years younger than herself could possibly appear ancient. "At least, Mama," Marianne protested, "you cannot deny the absurdity of the accusation, even if you don't intend malice. Colonel Brandon is older than Mrs. Jennings, yes, but he is old enough to be *my* father. If he ever possessed the courage to fall in love, surely those sentiments have long since faded into dust. It is preposterous!" She lamented how little protection age offered from such biting humor.

"Infirmity!" cried Elinor sharply. "Do you truly believe Colonel Brandon is infirm? You may think his age exaggerated, but you cannot mistake his physical strength."

"But did you not hear him speak of rheumatism?" Marianne countered. "Is that not the common ailment of aging?"

"My dearest child," Mrs. Dashwood laughed softly, "if you proceed at this rate, you shall fear my own decline constantly. It seems a miracle to me that I have reached the advanced age of forty without collapsing."

"Mama, you do me no justice," Elinor interjected calmly. "Colonel Brandon is not so elderly that his friends dread losing him naturally. He may live another twenty years. Yet, thirty-five and seventeen are not meant for matrimony."

"Perhaps," Elinor mused, "thirty-five and seventeen should avoid each other entirely. However, if a woman remains single at twenty-seven, I should find no fault with Colonel Brandon marrying her."

"A woman of twenty-seven," Marianne paused thoughtfully, "has lost the capacity to feel or inspire deep affection. If her home is wretched or her fortune meager, she might endure the duties of a nurse simply for the security of a wife. In his marrying such a person, there would be no impropriety. It would be a contract of convenience, satisfying the world's eyes. To me, it would not be a marriage at all, but merely a commercial exchange where each party seeks benefit at the expense of the other."

"It would be impossible," Elinor replied gently, "to convince you that a woman of twenty-seven could love a man of thirty-five enough to make him a cherished companion. Yet, I must object to your sentencing Colonel Brandon and his hypothetical wife to a lifetime of sickness chambers merely because he complained yesterday—a cold, damp day—of a slight ache in his shoulder."

"But he spoke of flannel waistcoats!" Marianne insisted. "In my mind, flannel is forever linked with aches, cramps, rheumatisms, and every misery that plagues the aged and weak."

"If he had been suffering from a violent fever, you would not despise him half so much," Elinor observed. "Confess, Marianne, is there not something fascinating to you in the flushed cheek, the hollow eye, and the rapid pulse of a fever?"

As soon as Elinor left the room, Marianne turned to her mother, her face pale with sudden worry. "Mama, I have an alarm regarding illness that I cannot hide from you. I am certain Edward Ferrars is unwell. We have been here nearly a fortnight, and he has not arrived. Only genuine indisposition could cause such an extraordinary delay. What else keeps him at Norland?"

"Had you expected him sooner?" Mrs. Dashwood asked. "I had no expectation, and if I felt any anxiety, it stemmed from recalling his lack of enthusiasm when I previously invited him here. Did Elinor expect him already?"

"I never mentioned it to her, but surely she must have guessed."

"I doubt you are correct," Mrs. Dashwood said. "Yesterday, when we discussed fitting a new grate for the spare bedroom, Elinor remarked that there was no urgency, implying the room would not be needed for some time."

"How strange!" Marianne exclaimed. "What could account for this? Their behavior toward one another has been utterly inexplicable! How cold, how composed were their final farewells! How listless their conversation the night before! In Edward's goodbye, there was no distinction between Elinor and myself; it was merely the warm wishes of an affectionate brother. Twice I left them alone together that morning, yet each time he followed me out of the room with uncanny speed. And Elinor... when she left Norland and Edward, she did not cry as I did. Her self-control is unbroken. When is she dejected? When does she seek solitude or show signs of unrest?"

Life at Barton Cottage had settled into a rhythm of quiet contentment for the Dashwoods. The house, once a mere refuge, now felt like home; the garden and the rolling landscapes surrounding them were no longer strangers but friends. Where Norland had once held charm through the mundane activities of country life, those same simple pursuits now filled their hearts with a joy they had never known before the loss of their father. Sir John Middleton, accustomed to a sedentary existence at Barton Park, visited them daily during the first fortnight and could barely contain his astonishment at discovering them perpetually occupied.

Despite Sir John's earnest pleas for them to socialize more widely and his repeated offers of his carriage for travel, Lady Anne Dashwood's spirited independence proved too strong for the local society's desires. She resolved firmly to visit no families beyond walking distance, a boundary that effectively limited her circle to a handful of neighbors, none of whom were easily accessible. Yet, nature itself offered ample diversion. About a mile and a half from their cottage lay the valley of Allenham, where stood an ancient, dignified mansion. During one of their early strolls, the sisters had stumbled upon this residence, which, echoing faint memories of Norland, sparked a longing for deeper acquaintance. However, inquiry revealed that the owner, a venerable lady of impeccable character, was hopelessly frail and rarely ventured from her grounds.

The countryside around them teemed with picturesque paths. From nearly every window of the cottage, the high downs beckoned, offering a sanctuary of pure air whenever the valleys below grew muddy under persistent rain. On a memorable morning, when the sky broke into patches of brilliant sun following two days of gloom, Marianne and Margaret decided they could endure no longer the stifling dampness indoors. While Elinor and her mother remained captivated by sketching and reading, convinced that the clouds would linger, the two younger sisters ascended the downs with buoyant spirits. They revelled in glimpses of azure sky and welcomed the invigorating gusts of the south-west wind, pitying their mother and sister for missing such bliss.

"Is there any happiness in this world," Marianne declared, "that surpasses this?"
"I agree," Margaret replied, and they pressed forward against the breeze, laughing in triumph for another twenty minutes. Suddenly, the heavens opened above them; the clouds merged overhead, and a torrential downpour lashed them in the face. Disappointed and soaked, they were compelled to retreat, though reluctantly, as no shelter lay closer than their own cottage. Yet, fate offered a silver lining: they could race down the steep slope leading to their garden gate with all the speed they possessed.

Off they went. Marianne gained the initial lead, but a misstep sent her crashing to the earth. Margaret, unable to halt her momentum to help, was swept along involuntarily, reaching the bottom unscathed. Just as Marianne fell, a gentleman carrying a shotgun, flanked by two eager pointers, passed near her. Hearing the commotion, he dropped his gun and rushed to her aid. Marianne had managed to raise herself, but her foot was severely twisted, leaving her trembling on the verge of standing. The gentleman offered his assistance. Sensing her modesty might shy away from physical contact, he nevertheless lifted her gently into his arms without further hesitation and carried her down the hill.

Passing through the garden—which Margaret had inadvertently left ajar—he bore her straight into the house, where she was just arriving. He did not release his hold until he had settled her comfortably in a chair within the parlor.

Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor rose in sheer amazement at his entrance. Both women stared at him with a mixture of wonder and a silent, burgeoning admiration, drawn by his striking presence. He offered a brief apology for his intrusion, recounting the circumstances with a frankness and grace that seemed to enhance his already exceptional handsomeness. Even if he had been aged, plain, and coarse, Mrs. Dashwood's gratitude alone would have ensured her goodwill toward any who cared for her daughter. But the combination of his youth, beauty, and elegant demeanor struck a chord deep within her heart, lending a profound poignancy to his actions.

She thanked him repeatedly, inviting him to sit with a warmth of address characteristic of her nature. He politely declined, citing his mud-soaked state. When Mrs. Dashwood finally inquired after his name, he replied, "Willoughby," and added that his current residence was Allenham, where he hoped to pay a courtesy call the following morning. This invitation was graciously accepted, and he departed, making himself increasingly intriguing amidst the pouring rain.

Within moments, Willoughby's masculine beauty and extraordinary grace became the subject of universal acclaim. The laughter his gallantry inspired regarding Marianne's tumble was amplified by his undeniable charm. Though Marianne, flushed with embarrassment, had not fully taken in his features when he lifted her, the fleeting sight was enough for her to join the chorus of admiration with fervor. In her mind, his person and bearing equaled the heroes of her favorite tales. The lack of formal ceremony in his rescue impressed her greatly; it spoke of a mind that moved swiftly and naturally. Every detail about him seemed captivating: his noble name, his proximity to their beloved village, and the fact that he wore a shooting jacket, which she secretly deemed the most becoming attire for a man. Her imagination flourished, her thoughts were light, and the pain of her sprained ankle faded into insignificance.

The next day, as soon as the weather permitted, Sir John paid a visit. Upon hearing details of Marianne's accident, he was immediately intrigued. "Willoughby!" he exclaimed. "Is *he* in the country? That is excellent news. I shall ride over tomorrow and invite him to dine on Thursday."
"Do you know him, then?" Mrs. Dashwood asked.
"To be sure I do," Sir John assured her. "He visits here annually. He is a decent shot, and perhaps the boldest rider in England."
"And is that all you can say?" Marianne interjected, indignant. "What of his manners? His talents? His genius?"
Sir John appeared slightly baffled. "Upon my soul," he admitted, "I know little of those things. But he is a pleasant, good-humoured fellow, and possesses the finest little black pointer I've ever seen."
Marianne could not satisfy him regarding the dog's color, nor could he elucidate the depths of Willoughby's mind.
"But who is he?" Elinor pressed. "Where does he hail from? Does he possess a home at Allenham?"
Here, Sir John could offer certainty. Mr. Willoughby owned no property locally; he resided only because he was visiting an elderly relative at Allenham Court, whose estates he stood to inherit. "Besides, he holds a small estate of his own in Somersetshire," Sir John noted, winking conspiratorially. "If I were you, I'd not give him up to my younger sister, despite this tumbling business. Miss Marianne must not expect to monopolize all the eligible bachelors. Brandon will be jealous if she doesn't look out."
"I do not believe," Mrs. Dashwood smiled, "that Mr. Willoughby will be inconvenienced by the attentions of my daughters. It is not a pursuit they were raised for. Men are quite safe with us, whatever their wealth."
"He is a good sort of fellow," Sir John reiterated, recalling a ball where Willoughby danced from eight until four without sitting, only to mount his horse again at eight for a hunt.
"That is precisely what I admire," Marianne said, her eyes sparkling. "Whatever his pursuits, his zeal knows no bounds, leaving him no sense of fatigue."
"Aye, aye," Sir John chuckled. "I see how it will go. You'll be setting your cap at him now, and forgetting poor Brandon entirely."
"That is a phrase, Sir John," Marianne protested warmly, "which I detest. I loathe every trite expression used to mock wit. 'Setting one's cap' or 'making a conquest' are the most odious. If their construction were ever clever, time has long destroyed their ingenuity."
Sir John, somewhat perplexed by her reproof, laughed heartily nonetheless. "Ay, you'll make enough conquests, one way or another. Poor Brandon! He's quite smitten already, and he's certainly worth considering, in spite of all this tumbling and spraining."

Early the following morning, Willoughby arrived at the cottage. Though Margaret had styled him "Marianne's preserver" with a flair that lacked precision, the fact remained that he came to inquire personally. Mrs. Dashwood received him with a warmth that blended genuine politeness with the gratitude Sir John had spoken of, creating an atmosphere where every word confirmed the family's sense, elegance, and domestic harmony. Of course, Willoughby needed no further evidence of their charm; he had already seen enough.

The sisters were a study in contrasts, yet both possessed undeniable allure. Miss Dashwood, Elinor, had a complexion of delicate fairness, features arranged with mathematical regularity, and a figure of remarkable poise. Marianne, however, was a different sort of beauty entirely. Taller and more striking, her form was less perfectly proportioned than her sister's but infinitely more commanding. Her skin, though deeply tanned, glowed with a brilliant transparency, revealing a vitality beneath. Her features were excellent, framed by a sweet, engaging smile, but it was her eyes that captivated—the deep, dark pools within them burned with a restless spirit, an eager life force that demanded attention.

At first, Willoughby hesitated, his gaze faltering under the weight of his recent indebtedness. Yet, as the initial embarrassment faded, replaced by the realization that this gentleman combined impeccable breeding with a frank, vivacious nature, his demeanor shifted completely. It was when Marianne declared her passion for music and dancing that the transformation was sealed. With a look of pure approbation, she surrendered the floor to him, and for the remainder of his stay, her conversation flowed exclusively toward these shared interests.

To speak of a favorite amusement was to unlock Marianne's tongue. She possessed no shyness, no reserve; she could not remain silent when the topic turned to dance or melody. Soon, it became clear that their enjoyment was not merely parallel but identical, born of a profound agreement in judgment. Encouraged by this harmony, Marianne pressed further, questioning him on literature. As she listed her favorite authors, glowing with rapture, it became impossible for a man of twenty-five to remain unmoved. His defenses crumbled instantly. Wherever a difference in taste might have arisen, Marianne's arguments, delivered with the brilliance of her eyes, prevailed immediately. He acquiesced to every decision, catching her enthusiasm like a flame, and before his visit ended, they spoke with the intimacy of old acquaintances.

"Elinor," Marianne exclaimed later, as soon as Willoughby had departed, "how fair! How just! Are my ideas so scanty?" She paused, realizing the truth behind her sister's teasing. "But I see what you mean. I have been too easy, too happy, too frank. I have violated every rule of decorum, speaking openly and sincerely where I should have been reserved, spiritless, and deceitful—if I had spoken only of the weather and the roads, offering a comment once every ten minutes, this reproach would not exist."

"My dear love," their mother interjected gently, "do not take offense at Elinor; she was only joking. I would scold her myself if she wished to dampen the joy of your conversation." At those words, Marianne's spirits lifted instantly.

Willoughby, for his part, offered every sign of delight in their growing friendship. He visited daily. Initially, he claimed to come to inquire after Marianne's health, but as her welcome grew warmer with each passing day, that excuse became superfluous, especially since she was now fully recovered from her confinement. For several days she had been bedridden, yet those days felt far less irksome than usual. Willoughby was a youth of considerable ability, quick imagination, and lively spirits, coupled with an open, affectionate manner. Above all, he possessed a natural ardor of mind, a quality now ignited and amplified by Marianne's own enthusiasm, making him irresistible to her heart.

Their society became her highest delight. They read, talked, and sang together, his musical talents proving considerable. He read aloud with a sensibility and spirit that Edward, unfortunately, lacked. In Mrs. Dashwood's eyes, he was flawless. Elinor, however, saw a flaw that troubled her: a propensity to speak too freely on every occasion, regardless of the audience or circumstance. In hastily voicing his opinions of others, in sacrificing general politeness for the sake of undivided attention when his heart was engaged, and in lightly dismissing the forms of worldly propriety, he revealed a lack of caution that Elinor could not endorse, despite Marianne's vigorous defense.

Marianne began to realize that the desperate conviction she had held at sixteen—that no man could ever satisfy her ideal of perfection—had been rash and unjust. Willoughby embodied everything her fantasy had sketched in those unhappy moments and every brighter interval thereafter. His behavior spoke loudly of his earnest wishes, matching the strength of his abilities. Even Mrs. Dashwood, who had previously harbored no speculative thoughts of marriage, found herself hoping and expecting union within a week, secretly congratulating herself on securing two such sons-in-law as Edward and Willoughby.

Meanwhile, Colonel Brandon's partiality for Marianne, which his friends had noted long ago, finally became apparent to Elinor, precisely because it was ignored by everyone else. Attention and wit had been diverted to Willoughby's more fortunate rivalry; the mockery formerly directed at Brandon for his sensitivity had vanished when his feelings truly began to demand it. Reluctantly, Elinor had to accept that the sentiments Mrs. Jennings had attributed to Brandon were now genuinely stirred by her sister. While a general resemblance of disposition might hasten Willoughby's affection, a striking opposition of character did not hinder Colonel Brandon's regard. Elinor viewed this with concern. What hope could a silent man of fifty-three years hold against a lively twenty-year-old? Since she could not wish him success, she heartily wished for indifference. Yet, she liked him. Despite his gravity and reserve, she saw an object of genuine interest. His serious manners were mild, and his reserve seemed the result of suppressed sorrow rather than natural gloom. Sir John's hints of past injuries and disappointments had justified Elinor's belief that he was an unfortunate man, earning her respect and compassion. Perhaps she pitied him the more because he was slighted by Willoughby and Marianne, who, prejudiced against his lack of liveliness and youth, seemed determined to undervalue his merits.

One day, while discussing him, Willoughby remarked, "Brandon is just the kind of man whom everybody speaks well of, and nobody cares about; whom all are delighted to see, and nobody remembers to talk to."

"That is exactly what I think of him," Marianne agreed eagerly.

"Do not boast of it, however," Elinor cautioned. "It is injustice in both of you. He is highly esteemed by the whole family at the Park, and I never fail to make an effort to converse with him whenever I see him."

"That he is patronized by *you*," Willoughby retorted, "certainly counts in his favor. But regarding the esteem of the others, it is a reproach in itself. Who would submit to the indignity of being approved by such women as Lady Middleton and Mrs. Jennings, if they could command the indifference of anyone else?"

"But perhaps the abuse of people like yourself and Marianne makes amends for the regard of Lady Middleton and her mother. If their praise is mere censure, then your censure may be praise, for they are no more discerning than you are prejudiced and unjust."

"In defense of your protégé, you can even be saucy," Willoughby observed.

"My protégé, as you call him, is a sensible man," Marianne countered, "and sense will always have attractions for me. Yes, Marianne, even in a man between thirty and forty. He has seen the world, traveled abroad, and possesses a thinking mind. I have found him capable of providing vast information on many subjects, answering my inquiries with readiness, good breeding, and good nature."

"That is to say," Marianne sneered, "he has told you that the climate in the East Indies is hot and the mosquitoes troublesome."

"He *would* have told me so, I doubt not, had I asked, but I was previously informed on those points."

"Perhaps," Willoughby suggested, "his observations extend to the existence of nabobs, gold mohurs, and palanquins."

"I venture to say that *his* observations stretch far beyond *your* candour. But tell me, why do you dislike him?"

"I do not dislike him," Willoughby insisted. "On the contrary, I consider him a very respectable man, who has everyone's good word and nobody's notice; who has more money than he spends and more time than he employs wisely, and acquires two new coats every year."

"And add to this," Marianne cried, "that he lacks genius, taste, and spirit. His understanding holds no brilliance, his feelings no ardour, and his voice no expression."

"You judge his imperfections so broadly," Elinor replied calmly, "and rely so heavily on the strength of your own imagination that my commendation of him appears cold and insipid by comparison. I can only pronounce him to be a sensible man, well-bred, well-informed, of gentle address, and, I believe, possessing an amiable heart."

"Miss Dashwood," Willoughby protested, "you treat me unkindly now. You strive to disarm me with reason and convince me against my will.

The Dashwoods had scarcely anticipated, upon their arrival in Devonshire, that their leisure would vanish so swiftly beneath a tide of social obligations. What had seemed a quiet retreat quickly transformed into a whirlwind of engagements. Once Marianne recovered from her initial indisposition, Sir John's long-planned entertainments took flight. Private balls within the park grounds became frequent, as did aquatic expeditions, limited only by the capricious rains of an October sky. Willoughby was invariably the guest of honor at these gatherings. The informal nature of these occasions fostered a rapid intimacy between him and the sisters; it allowed him to observe Marianne's virtues up close, to witness the fervor of his own admiration, and to receive, through her behavior, unmistakable proof of her devotion.

To Elinor, this open courtship was unsurprising, if perhaps a bit too public. On rare occasions, she ventured to urge Marianne toward greater self-restraint, suggesting that discretion would serve them better. Yet Marianne recoiled at the notion. To her, concealing genuine feeling where no scandal existed was not merely imprudent, but a form of moral cowardice—a shameful submission of reason to petty convention. Willoughby shared this sentiment, and their conduct reflected it with unwavering consistency.

When Willoughby was present, Marianne saw no one else. Every gesture he made was perfect; every word he spoke, witty. If the evening ended with card games, he would deliberately cheat himself and everyone else just to ensure she held a winning hand. If dancing was the sole amusement, they remained partners for half the night, and when separated for brief intervals, they stood apart from the crowd, exchanging glances but speaking to no other soul. Their audacity invited laughter, yet ridicule failed to shame or deter them.

Mrs. Dashwood embraced their exuberance with a warmth that precluded any attempt at moderation. To her, it was simply the natural expression of a vibrant, ardent spirit finding its match. For Marianne, this was a season of pure bliss. Her heart belonged entirely to Willoughby, and the deep affection she had carried for Norland from Sussex seemed to soften under the enchantment of his company, making her current home feel almost as dear as the old estate.

But Elinor's happiness was far less complete. Her peace of mind was unsettled, and the joy derived from these amusements felt hollow. No companion among the locals could console her for what she had left behind, nor help her view Norland with anything but lingering regret. Lady Middleton offered little more than polite silence, a quietness born of a cold, calculating sensibility rather than true refinement. Mrs. Jennings, though an incessant chatterbox, possessed a kindness that guaranteed Elinor a fair share of her stories. Yet even this proved insufficient. Jennings had recounted her own history to Elinor three or four times; if Elinor's memory were as sharp as Jennings' narrative skills, she might already know the details of Mr. Jennings' final illness and his dying words to his wife. Lady Middleton, meanwhile, remained a figure of distant reserve. Towards both her husband and mother, she maintained the same cool detachment, offering no prospect of intimacy. Her conversation was insipid, her spirits unnervingly static, and her presence added little to the gathering save for occasional reminders of her troublesome sons.

Of all the new acquaintances, Colonel Brandon alone commanded Elinor's respect as a companion of intellect and kindness. Willoughby was off-limits; his attention was wholly consumed by Marianne, rendering him, despite his charm, an unsuitable friend for anyone else. Colonel Brandon, however, found solace in Elinor's company, using her as a buffer against the indifference directed toward his sister.

As the weeks passed, Elinor's compassion for Colonel Brandon grew, fueled by suspicions that the bitterness of lost love had already taken root within him. One evening, as the guests danced and the siblings sat quietly by mutual agreement near the window, the air grew heavy with unspoken thoughts. Colonel Brandon's gaze lingered on Marianne, and after a prolonged silence, he spoke with a faint, wistful smile, "Your sister, I understand, does not approve of second attachments."

"No," Elinor replied gently, "her views are purely romantic."

"Or, as I suspect, she deems them impossible to exist."

"I believe she does," Elinor admitted. "Yet I wonder how she manages to hold such a stance without reflecting on the example set by her own father, who, to everyone's knowledge, had two wives. Perhaps a few years of worldly experience will ground her opinions in common sense and observation, allowing them to be defined and justified by others."

"That is likely," Brandon conceded, "yet there is something so delightful about the prejudices of youth that one laments seeing them yield to broader, more pragmatic opinions."

"I cannot agree with you there," Elinor countered. "There are inherent dangers in feelings like Marianne's, which no amount of enthusiasm or innocence can truly offset. Her ideals tend to dismiss propriety entirely, and I hope that a deeper understanding of the world will prove to be her greatest safeguard."

Brandon paused, then continued, "Does your sister make distinctions in her objections regarding second attachments? Or is it considered equally culpable regardless of circumstance? Must those whose first loves were thwarted by the fickleness of their beloved or the cruelty of fate remain indifferent for the rest of their days?"

"On my word, I am unfamiliar with the nuances of her principles," Elinor confessed. "All I know is that I have never heard her acknowledge any instance where a second attachment might be pardoned."

"This," Brandon murmured, "is unsustainable. A total transformation of sentiment..." He hesitated, shaking his head. "Do not wish for it. When the refined illusions of a young mind are forced to surrender, they are often replaced by opinions that are too common, too dangerous. I speak from painful experience. I once knew a lady whose temperament and judgment mirrored yours, who thought and felt like your sister. But due to enforced circumstances, a series of misfortunes..."

He stopped abruptly, realizing he had spoken too freely. His face betrayed a sudden flush, sparking immediate speculation in Elinor's mind. Had he not intended to reveal this, the matter might have slipped unnoticed, passing without trace. But having convinced Miss Dashwood that certain truths belonged to his lips alone, the dam had broken. It required little imagination for Elinor to connect his emotion with a tender, tragic recollection of a past love. She said no more. Where Marianne would have immediately constructed a dramatic narrative of disaster, weaving every detail into a tapestry of heartbreaking romance, Elinor contented herself with a quiet, solemn understanding.

The following morning, as Elinor and Marianne strolled through the garden, the younger sister burst forth with news that struck Elinor with genuine astonishment. Despite her deep knowledge of Marianne's impulsiveness, this revelation exceeded even her expectations, revealing a depth of imprudence she had not fully anticipated. With radiant joy, Marianne announced that Willoughby had gifted her a horse—a magnificent animal he had bred himself on his Somersetshire estate, perfectly suited to a lady's needs. She spoke of the gift in raptures, utterly failing to consider that her mother, Mrs. Dashwood, had no intention of keeping horses, nor the practical necessities involved: purchasing a second mount for a servant, hiring an attendant, and constructing a stable. Yet, having already decided to accept, she shared the delight with her sister, envisioning daily rides across the downs.

"You shall share its use with me," Marianne insisted, her eyes alight with dreams of galloping freedom. "Imagine the sheer delight!"

Elinor, however, found it difficult to remain immersed in such a vision of happiness without confronting the harsh realities beneath the surface. For a fleeting moment, she hoped to persuade Marianne that the expense of an extra servant, the inconvenience of housing a horse against her mother's wishes, and the general impropriety of accepting such a generous gift from a man so recently known would dissuade her. But Marianne grew impatient with these practical objections.

"You are mistaken, Elinor," she declared warmly, her tone defensive. "I do not know Willoughby poorly. Though our acquaintance is short, I understand him better than anyone else in the world save you and Mother. Intimacy is not measured by time or opportunity, but by disposition alone. Seven years could pass without two souls knowing one another, yet seven days may suffice for others. To refuse this gift would be far more improper than to accept it from Willoughby."

Realizing that opposing Marianne on such a tender subject would only strengthen her resolve, Elinor shifted her strategy. She appealed to Marianne's love for her mother, painting a vivid picture of the trouble and discomfort this addition would bring upon the gentlewoman who had raised them. Slowly, the storm within Marianne subsided. She promised not to mention the offer to her mother and vowed to decline it politely the next time she saw Willoughby. True to her word, when Willoughby visited the cottage later that day, Elinor heard Marianne express her regret in a hushed voice, explaining the difficulties that forced her hand. Her reasons were presented with such conviction that further persuasion became impossible. Willoughby's distress was palpable; he spoke earnestly of his sorrow, adding in a low, intimate murmur, "But, Marianne, the horse remains yours, though you cannot use it now. I shall keep it until you need it again. When you finally establish your own home, away from Barton, Queen Mab awaits you."

These words, whispered clearly enough for Elinor to overhear, coupled with Willoughby's familiar address of "Marianne" instead of "Miss Dashwood," sent a chill through her heart. In an instant, the nature of their relationship became undeniable to her. There was no ambiguity in his tone or in the terms of his promise; they were clearly engaged. What surprised Elinor was not the engagement itself, but how two people of such frank natures could allow themselves to reach this stage without a single word spoken aloud to the outside world.

Her suspicions were confirmed the following day by Margaret. After Willoughby had spent the previous evening with them, leaving the three of them alone in the parlor, Margaret had observed much. Now, with a grave and conspiratorial expression, she confided in Elinor. "Oh, Elinor!" she exclaimed. "I have a secret to tell you about Marianne. I am certain she will be married to Mr. Willoughby very soon."

"You have said so," Elinor replied dryly, "almost every day since they first met on High-church Down. We had not known each other a week before you were convinced Marianne wore his miniature around her neck; yet it turned out to be merely the likeness of our great-uncle."

"But this is entirely different!" Margaret protested, her eyes wide. "He possesses a lock of her hair."

"Eh?" Elinor feigned surprise. "Take care, Margaret. It may simply be a lock of hair from some great-uncle of *his*."

"But truly, Elinor, it is Marianne's!" Margaret insisted, recounting the scene from the previous night with dramatic flair. "Last evening, after tea, when you and Mother stepped out, they were whispering frantically. He seemed to beg something of her, and then he snatched up her scissors. He cut off a long tangle of hair from behind her ear, kissed it reverently, folded it into a white square of paper, and tucked it safely into his pocket-book."

Given the weight of Margaret's testimony and its alignment with what Elinor had witnessed herself, she could not withhold her full belief in the facts.

Yet, Margaret's sharp tongue occasionally betrayed a lack of tact. Later, when Mrs. Jennings, ever curious, pressed Margaret to reveal the identity of the young man who held such sway over her sister, Margaret hesitated dramatically. "I must not tell, may I, Elinor?" she asked, glancing sideways at her older sibling.

Of course, this provoked laughter from everyone present, including Elinor, though the effort to smile was painfully strained. She feared Margaret had chosen a suitor whose name she dared not utter in public, lest it become a running gag for the entire gathering.

"Marianne feels deeply for you," the younger girl said, turning crimson with indignation. "But remember, whatever guesses you may make, you have no right to repeat them."

"I never made any conjectures," Margaret retorted, undeterred. "It was you who told me everything."

Her defiance only fueled the amusement of the room. Mrs. Jennings, eager for gossip, pressed further. "Pray, Miss Margaret, let us know all about it. What is the gentleman's name?"

"I cannot tell, ma'am. But I know exactly who he is, and precisely where he resides."

"Yes, yes, we can surely guess," Mrs. Jennings teased. "At his own home at Norland? Perhaps he is the local curate?"

"No, *that* he is not," Margaret corrected sharply. "He holds no profession whatsoever."

"Then perhaps he is deceased," Mrs. Jennings suggested with a mischievous twinkle. "For I am sure there was once a man like him, and his name began with an 'F'."

Just as the conversation teetered on the brink of scandalous speculation, Lady Middleton interjected, "It is raining very hard." A timely interruption, certainly, though Elinor suspected it stemmed less from kindness toward her sister and more from Lady Middleton's distaste for the kind of raillery that delighted her husband and mother. Nevertheless, Colonel Brandon, ever attentive to the sensibilities of others, seized upon the topic of rain, engaging Lady Middleton in a lengthy discourse on weather patterns. Meanwhile, Willoughby rose, opening the piano and inviting Marianne to play, effectively burying the subject under music and distraction.

That evening, plans were set for a grand excursion the following day to view the estates of Colonel Brandon's brother-in-law, a property located twelve miles from Barton. Access to the grounds required Colonel Brandon's influence, as the owner, currently abroad, had issued strict prohibitions against visitors. The journey was to include a sail on a lake, cold refreshments, and carriage rides, conducted in the customary fashion of a high-society outing. Sir John, a frequent visitor, enthusiastically praised the beauty of the grounds, boasting of his annual trips there for the past decade. However, given the relentless rain of the last fortnight and the precarious state of health suffered by Mrs. Dashwood, who already bore a slight cold, the trip proved somewhat controversial. Persuaded by Elinor's concerns, Mrs. Dashwood ultimately chose to remain at home, sparing herself the damp and exertion of the journey.

The expedition to Whitwell unfolded with a dramatic divergence from Elinor's anticipations. Having braced herself for rain, exhaustion, and perhaps even terror, she found herself instead facing a total cancellation. The plan simply did not materialize.

By ten o'clock, the entire party gathered at the park gates, ready for breakfast. Despite the previous night's deluge, the sky was clearing, clouds dissipating under patches of sunshine, and the air held a crisp promise of fair weather. Spirits were high, tempers genial, and everyone seemed determined to endure whatever hardship fate might throw at them, provided they remained cheerful.

However, tranquility shattered when the letters arrived. Amongst them was one addressed to Colonel Brandon. As he read the direction, his countenance altered instantly, draining of color, and he rose abruptly to depart the table.

"What troubles Colonel Brandon?" Sir John asked, looking around. "No one knows."

"I trust it brings no bad tidings," Lady Middleton murmured. "Something extraordinary must have prompted him to vanish so swiftly from my breakfast."

Five minutes elapsed before Brandon returned.

"No bad news, I hope?" Mrs. Jennings greeted him warmly as he entered.

"None at all, madam, I assure you."

"Certainly not from Avignon? I hope it does not concern your sister."

"No, madam. It originated in town and relates solely to business matters."

"But how could a mere letter of business disturb you so profoundly?" Mrs. Jennings pressed, undeterred. "Tell us the truth."

"My dear lady," Lady Middleton interjected gently, "please consider what you imply."

"Perhaps it announces that your cousin Fanny is wedded?" Mrs. Jennings guessed, ignoring her daughter's quiet rebuke.

"Not a word of that."

"Well, then, I know the sender. And I trust she is in health."

"Pray, who do you mean, madam?" Brandon asked, his cheeks flushing slightly.

"Oh! You know exactly who I mean."

"I am deeply sorry," Brandon addressed Lady Middleton, "to receive this correspondence today. My affairs require my immediate attention in town."

"In town!" Mrs. Jennings exclaimed. "At this season? What business calls you there?"

"My personal loss is significant in leaving such a congenial gathering," Brandon continued, bowing gracefully. "Yet I am more distressed because I suspect my presence is vital to secure your entry into Whitwell."

A collective gasp rippled through the room.

"If you pen a note to the housekeeper, Mr. Brandon," Marianne suggested eagerly, "might that suffice?"

Brandon shook his head firmly.

"We must proceed," Sir John declared. "We shall not postpone it now that we are so close. You cannot travel to town until tomorrow, Brandon; that is final."

"I wish it were so simple," Brandon sighed. "But I am powerless to delay my departure for a single day."

"If you told us the nature of your business," Mrs. Jennings urged, "perhaps we could determine if it might wait."

"You would not be delayed by six hours," Willoughby chimed in smoothly, glancing at Marianne, "if you postponed your journey until our return."

"I cannot afford to forfeit even one hour."

Elinor then overheard Willoughby whisper to Marianne, "Some souls cannot abide a party of pleasure. Brandon is one of them. He feigned a chill to invent this ruse. I wager fifty guineas the letter is his own fabrication."

"I harbor no doubts," Marianne replied quietly.

"There is no swaying you from your resolve, Brandon," Sir John noted, familiar with the Colonel's stubbornness. "Still, I trust you will reconsider. Think of the company: the two Miss Careys from Newton, the three Miss Dashwoods from the cottage, and Mr. Willoughby rising two hours prior to custom—all for Whitwell."

Brandon reiterated his regrets at disappointing them but maintained that his absence was unavoidable.

"Well, when shall we expect your return?"

"We shall likely see you at Barton," Lady Addison offered kindly, "as soon as you are free from town, and we shall defer the Whitwell excursion until then."

"You are kind. Yet, given the uncertainty of my return, I dare not offer any promise."

"Oh! He must and shall return," Sir John insisted. "If he is not here by week's end, I shall track him down myself."

"Aye, do so, Sir John," Mrs. Jennings encouraged, "and perhaps you'll uncover the secret of his errands."

"I prefer not to pry into another man's affairs. I suspect it involves some shame."

As Brandon's horses were announced, Sir John inquired, "Do you ride to town?"

"No, only to Honiton. From thence I shall take the post."

"As you are set on departing, I wish you safe travels. Though I urge you to change your mind."

"I assure you, it is beyond my power."

Taking his leave, Brandon exchanged a few polite words with the group. To Miss Dashwood, he asked, "Shall I ever have the pleasure of seeing you and your sisters in town this winter?"

"I fear not."

"Then I must bid you farewell for a length of time I dread to contemplate."

To Marianne, he offered only a curt bow and silence.

"Come, Colonel," Mrs. Jennings called out, "before you depart, do reveal your destination."

He wished them a good morning and, escorted by Sir John, withdrew.

Once alone, the restrained complaints finally erupted. Politeness had held them back, but now universal lamentation ensued regarding the provocation of such disappointment.

"I surmise his business," Mrs. Jennings declared triumphantly. "And you all agree?"

"Yes, ma'am."

"It concerns Miss Williams, surely."

"And who is Miss Williams?" Marianne asked.

"How could you not know? A relative of the Colonel's, my dear. A very intimate one. We need not specify the closeness, lest we shock the young ladies." Lowering her voice, she leaned toward Elinor. "She is his natural daughter."

"Elinor!" Mrs. Jennings breathed. "She resembles him greatly, though she stares with a peculiar intensity. I am certain he will leave her his fortune."

When Sir John rejoined them, he joined the chorus of regret, yet concluded with a spirited suggestion: since they were all assembled, they must contrive happiness. After brief deliberation, it was decided that while joy awaited Whitwell, a tolerable composure could be achieved by touring the countryside. Carriages were summoned; Willoughby's took precedence, and Marianne ascended with radiant cheer. He drove swiftly through the park, soon vanishing from sight. Their return occurred only after the rest of the party had returned, and both seemed delighted, claiming they had skirted the lanes while others traversed the downs.

An evening dance was arranged, promising merriment throughout the day. Additional guests from the Carey family arrived, swelling the dinner table to nearly twenty seats, a feat Sir John observed with satisfaction. Willoughby seated himself between the two elder Miss Dashwoods. Mrs. Jennings took her place beside Elinor, and scarcely had they taken their seats before she leaned forward, speaking loudly enough for Willoughby and Marianne to overhear.

"I have uncovered your secrets, despite your cunning. I know precisely where you wandered this morning."

Marianne flushed crimson and stammered, "Where, pray?"

"Did you not know," Willoughby smiled, "that we rode out in my curricle?"

"Yes, yes, Mr. Impudence, I knew that perfectly well," Marianne retorted, trying to maintain composure. "I was determined to discover *where* you had gone. I trust you enjoy your residence, Miss Marianne? It is spacious, I recall. And when I next visit, I hope you have furnished it anew; it stood in dire need of refreshment six years past."

Marianne turned away, overwhelmed by confusion. Mrs. Jennings laughed aloud, and Elinor realized with a jolt that her own curiosity had led her maid to inquire of Willoughby's groom. Through this indirect channel, she had learned they had visited Allenham, wandering extensively through gardens and inspecting the interior.

Elinor struggled to reconcile this with reality. How could Willoughby have proposed entering the house, or Marianne have accepted, while Mrs. Smith resided there—a woman with whom Marianne shared no acquaintance whatsoever?

Upon exiting the dining room, Elinor questioned her sister directly. Her astonishment grew as she discovered every detail recounted by Mrs. Jennings proved accurate. Marianne, furious at being doubted, snapped, "Why did you assume, Elinor, that we did not go there, or that we did not view the house? Was it not something you yourself coveted?"

"Yes, Marianne, but I would not venture inside while Mrs. Smith occupied it, nor with no other companion than Mr. Willoughby."

"Mr. Willoughby possesses the sole right to display that estate," Marianne argued, her voice ringing with conviction. "Moreover, traveling in an open carriage precluded any other companion. I have never experienced a pleasanter morning."

"Elinor," she countered, "the enjoyment of an activity does not necessarily validate its propriety."

"On the contrary," Elinor reasoned softly, "nothing proves propriety more strongly than untroubled conscience. If there had been genuine impropriety, I would have felt it instantly. With such assurance, no pleasure could exist."

Colonel Brandon's abrupt departure from the Park, coupled with his unyielding resolve to hide the true nature of his business, occupied Mrs. Jennings' thoughts with relentless fascination for several days. A natural speculator by temperament, her mind was a fertile ground for every possible calamity. With barely a moment's pause between theories, she convinced herself that something dreadfully wrong must have occurred. "Some terrible news," she murmured, tracing lines of sorrow across his retreating figure. "Perhaps his finances are ruined. That estate at Delaford yielded little, and I heard his brother left a mess behind. He must have been summoned to bail out some disaster!" Yet, another thought quickly took root: "It concerns Miss Williams." The memory of his startled look when her name was dropped fueled this suspicion. "Maybe she is ill back in London," she speculated, "or perhaps his sister at Avignon is worse. Whatever it is, I hope he finds relief soon, and that he secures a worthy wife before long."

Her musings swayed like wind-blown grass, shifting from financial ruin to romantic misfortune with every fresh conjecture. Elinor listened, her heart truly drawn to Colonel Brandon's welfare, yet she could not share the depth of her mother's bewilderment regarding his sudden exit. In her view, the circumstance demanded neither lasting amazement nor a kaleidoscope of wild guesses. Instead, her curiosity was fixated on a far stranger mystery: the profound silence surrounding the relationship between Willoughby and Marianne.

For days, neither young man had uttered a word to confirm what their actions clearly proclaimed. To Elinor, this inexplicable quietude seemed entirely at odds with their natures. If marriage lay beyond their immediate reach due to Willoughby's limited income—a mere six or seven hundred a year, squandered on a lifestyle far above his means—they might need time to arrange affairs. But why conceal their engagement so thoroughly from those who cared most for them? The contradiction gnawed at her, creating a shadow of doubt in her mind about whether they were truly affianced at all. This hesitation acted as a shield, preventing her from pressing Marianne for answers.

Yet, Willoughby's devotion to the family was undeniable. To Marianne, he offered a tenderness that transcended ordinary friendship; to the wider household, he was a model of filial and fraternal care. The cottage became his sanctuary, a space he cherished more deeply than his own residence at Allenham. Whether driven by duty or love, his mornings invariably ended there, leaving him to spend the day beside Marianne, with her faithful pointer dog resting at her feet.

One evening, roughly a week after Colonel Brandon's mysterious flight, Willoughby's heart appeared more transparent than usual. When Mrs. Dashwood casually suggested renovating the cottage in the spring, Willoughby sprang to his feet, his opposition fierce and immediate.

"What!" he exclaimed, his voice ringing with indignation. "Improve this dear cottage? Never! Not a single stone shall be added to these walls, not an inch to its size, if you value my feelings."

"Do not be alarmed," Elinor gently interjected. "There is no intention to alter a thing; Mother simply lacks the funds to undertake any project anyway."

"I am genuinely relieved," Willoughby cried, his relief palpable. "May she remain poor forever if it spares her from spending her riches on such changes."

"Thank you, Willoughby," Elinor smiled. "Rest assured, I would never sacrifice your sentiment of local attachment, nor that of anyone I hold dear, for the grandeur of the world. Whatever surplus remains after settling accounts this spring, I would rather leave it idle than invest it in a way that pains you. However, surely you admit the house has flaws?"

"I do," he confessed earnestly. "To me, it is flawless. Indeed, I regard it as the only architectural form capable of housing happiness. Were I wealthy enough, I would tear down Combe Abbey instantly and rebuild it exactly in the likeness of this humble cottage."

"With its dark, narrow stairs and a kitchen that fills with smoke?" Elinor teased lightly.

"Yes!" he shouted, his enthusiasm undampened. "With every imperfection intact! In no single convenience or discomfort would I allow a variation. Only under such a roof, surrounded by these familiar defects, could I perhaps find contentment at Combe Abbey equal to what I've known at Barton Cottage."

"I trust," Elinor replied, her gaze softening, "that even with superior accommodations and a broader staircase, you will eventually find your own home just as perfect as this one."

"There are certainly aspects of it that charm me greatly," Willoughby admitted, his expression turning tender. "But this place holds a unique claim on my affection—one that no other location can ever share."

Mrs. Dashwood glanced with evident delight at Marianne, whose large, expressive eyes were locked onto Willoughby, silently echoing his sentiments.

"How frequently," Willoughby continued, lowering his voice as he addressed Marianne directly, "did I wish, when I last stayed at Allenham twelve months ago, that Barton Cottage was inhabited! I passed it daily, marveling at its setting and mourning the emptiness within. Who could have guessed that the first piece of news I received upon returning to the countryside would be that Barton Cottage was finally occupied? And what a surge of satisfaction and deep personal interest I felt! Nothing short of a premonition of the joy I have since experienced can explain it. Is that not so, Marianne?"

Turning back to Mrs. Dashwood, his tone shifted again, laced with a gentle reproach. "And yet, you would spoil this home? You would strip it of its simple beauty under the guise of 'improvement!' You would degrade this parlor—the very chamber where our acquaintance began, and where countless happy hours have since been shared—to the status of a mere antechamber. Soon, everyone will trample through the room that has offered more genuine comfort and warmth than any opulent apartment in existence."

Mrs. Dashwood, reassured by his words, promised firmly that no such alterations would ever take place.

"You are a kind soul," Willoughby declared, his warmth returning. "Your assurance brings me peace. Extend it further, and you will bring me true happiness. Promise me that not only will your house remain untouched, but that I shall always find you and your family as unchanged as your dwelling. Promise that you will continue to regard me with the kindness that has rendered everything connected to you so precious to me."

The promise was granted willingly, and throughout the evening, Willoughby's behavior radiated a mixture of deep affection and profound contentment.

"As we part, may I ask if I am to dine with you tomorrow?" Mrs. Dashwood inquired as he prepared to leave. "I cannot invite you to breakfast, for we intend to walk to the Park to pay our respects to Lady Middleton."

"It would be my honor," Willoughby replied, bowing low. "I shall be with you by four o'clock."

The following day, Mrs. Dashwood visited Lady Middleton, accompanied by two of her daughters. Marianne declined to join them, citing a trivial preoccupation, a decision her mother readily accepted, having already received a letter from Willoughby promising to call upon them during their absence. Upon returning to Barton Cottage, they discovered Willoughby's carriage and servant waiting, confirming Mrs. Dashwood's hopes that he intended to pay his promised visit. However, as soon as they crossed the threshold, the scene inside shattered any sense of normalcy. Marianne emerged from the drawing-room in a state of profound distress, her handkerchief pressed to her face, and fled upstairs without a word. Alarmed, Mrs. Dashwood and Elinor followed her into the room she had vacated, finding only Willoughby standing by the fireplace, his back turned. When he turned around, his expression mirrored the turmoil that had clearly overwhelmed his sister.

"Is anything the matter?" Mrs. Dashwood cried, rushing forward. "Is she ill?"

"I hope not," Willoughby replied, attempting to project cheerfulness, though a strained smile quickly gave way. "If anyone is to be ill, it should be me. I am suffering from a heavy disappointment."

"A disappointment?"

"Yes. I find it impossible to keep my engagement. Mrs. Smith has, this morning, exercised the privilege of wealth upon a poor cousin by sending me on urgent business to London. I have just received my dispatches and bid farewell to Allenthorpe; and, seeking some sort of exhilaration, I have come to bid farewell to you."

"To London!" Mrs. Dashwood exclaimed, her voice trembling slightly. "And you depart this morning?"

"Almost at this moment."

"This is terribly unfortunate," Mrs. Dashwood stammered. "But Mrs. Smith must be grateful, and surely her business will not keep you away from us long."

Willoughby flushed deeply. "You are too kind, madam, but I have no intention of returning to Devonshire immediately. My visits to Mrs. Smith are rarely repeated within a year."

"And is Mrs. Smith your sole friend? Is Allenthorpe the only house in the neighborhood where you are welcome? For shame, Willoughby, could you not wait for an invitation here?"

His color deepened further, and he stared fixedly at the floor. "You are too generous."

Mrs. Dashwood exchanged a look of surprise with Elinor, who shared her astonishment. A heavy silence fell over the room until Mrs. Dashwood spoke again. "My dear Willoughby, let me assure you that Barton Cottage will always remain open to you. I need not urge you to return immediately, for you alone can judge whether doing so might offend Mrs. Smith, and I trust your judgment as fully as I trust your inclinations."

"My current engagements," Willoughby murmured, looking confused and troubled, "are of such a nature that I dare not flatter myself..."

He trailed off. Mrs. Dashwood, too stunned to speak, allowed another silence to stretch before Willoughby broke it with a faint, hollow smile. "It is folly to linger thus. I shall not torment myself any longer by remaining among friends whose company I can no longer enjoy."

With hurried farewells, he stepped out into the hall. They watched him mount his carriage, and within moments, it disappeared down the lane.

Mrs. Dashwood, overcome with emotion, retired to her own room to process the shock and alarm caused by this sudden departure. Elinor, however, remained in the drawing-room, her mind racing with anxiety and suspicion. The ancient, unyielding struggle between duty and desire, parent and child, was the root cause. Lacking answers to these vain hopes, Elinor turned to the enduring certainty of Edward's affection, recalling every mark of regard he had shown at Barton, and above all, the constant, flattering token he wore on his finger.

At breakfast on the last morning, Mrs. Dashwood remarked, "I think, Edward, you would be a happier man if you had some profession to occupy your time and lend interest to your plans. There would be some inconvenience to your friends, certainly, as you could not give them so much of yourself. But, I trust, you would benefit materially in at least one particular: you would know where to go when you left us."

"I assure you," Edward replied, "that I have given this matter deep consideration, just as you do now. It has been, and likely will always be, a heavy misfortune to me to lack necessary business or a profession that provides employment and independence. Unfortunately, my own fastidiousness, combined with that of my friends, has rendered me what I am: an idle, helpless creature. We could never agree on a career path. I always preferred the church, and still do. Yet, it was deemed not 'smart' enough for my family. They advocated the army, which I found far too boisterous for my temperament. The law was considered sufficiently genteel; many young men with chambers in the Temple moved gracefully through society, driving about town in stylish carriages. However, I lacked any inclination for the law, even in its less abstract forms, which my parents endorsed. As for the navy, it held significant fashion, but I was already too old when the topic arose. Ultimately, since there was no pressing necessity for me to hold any profession—I could be as dashing and extravagant without a uniform as with one—idleness was pronounced the most advantageous and honorable course. A young man of eighteen rarely possesses the earnest drive to resist his friends' pleas for doing nothing. Consequently, I matriculated at Oxford and have remained properly idle ever since."

"The consequence of which," Mrs. Dashwood observed gently, "is presumably that your children will be raised to pursue as many careers, employments, and trades as Columella's garden contained."

"They will be raised," Edward said seriously, "to be as dissimilar to myself as humanly possible. In feeling, in action, in condition, and in everything."

"Come, come," Mrs. Dashwood reassured him, "this is merely an overflow of immediate melancholy. You are in a sad humor and imagine that anyone unlike yourself cannot find happiness. Remember that the pain of parting from friends is universal, regardless of one's education or station. Know your own happiness. You require nothing but patience—or, if you prefer a more enchanting term, hope. Your mother will secure you that independence you crave; it is her duty, and it must eventually become her happiness to ensure your youth is not wasted in discontent. What wonders might a few months accomplish?"

"I fear," Edward replied, "that I may challenge many months to produce any good for me."

This despondent turn of mind, though hidden from Mrs. Dashwood, cast a shadow over the farewell that followed shortly, leaving an unsettling impression on Elinor that demanded considerable effort to overcome. Determined to suppress her suffering and avoid appearing to grieve more than her family did, she rejected the method Marianne had wisely adopted on similar occasions: seeking silence, solitude, and idleness to amplify and anchor her sorrow. Their approaches differed as much as their objectives, yet both served the advancement of their respective natures.

As soon as Edward had left the house, Elinor took her seat at her drawing table, engaging herself busily throughout the day. She neither sought nor avoided mentions of his name, appearing interested in the general affairs of the family almost as fervently as ever. If this conduct failed to diminish her internal grief, it certainly prevented its unnecessary growth and spared her mother and sisters excessive worry.

To Marianne, such behavior, so diametrically opposed to her own, offered no merit whatsoever, just as her own conduct had seemed flawed to her sister. The discipline of self-command was easy for Marianne to settle; however, with passions so intense, it was impossible, whereas with calmer affections, it possessed no virtue. That her sister's affections *were* calm, Marianne dared not deny, though she blushed at the admission; and regarding the strength of her own, she provided striking proof by continuing to love and respect her sister, despite this mortifying realization.

She did not isolate herself from her family, nor did she retreat into determined solitude to evade them, nor did she lie wakeful through the nights to indulge in meditation. Nevertheless, Elinor discovered that every day afforded her sufficient leisure to ponder Edward and his behavior, exploring every variety of emotion that shifting moods could generate—from tenderness and pity to approbation, censure, and doubt. There were countless moments when, due to the absence of her mother and sisters or the nature of their occupations, conversation was effectively forbidden among them, producing the effects of true solitude. Her mind was inevitably at liberty; her thoughts could not be chained elsewhere. Thus, the past and the future concerning a subject so vital stood before her, forcing her attention and engrossing her memory, reflection, and imagination.

One morning, not long after Edward's departure, a reverie interrupted her by the arrival of visitors. Happening to be alone, she watched the small gate at the entrance of the green court close, drawing her gaze to the window. She saw a large procession approaching the door. Among them were Sir John and Lady Middleton, along with Mrs. Jennings, but there were two others, a gentleman and a lady, whom she did not know. Seated near the window, as soon as Sir John spotted her, he left the group to handle the formalities of knocking and stepped across the lawn, compelling her to open the casement to speak to him, though the distance between the door and window was so short that speech at one side was inevitably overheard at the other.

"Well," he exclaimed, "we have brought you some strangers. How do you like them?"

"Hush! They will hear you."

"Never mind if they do. It is only the Palmers. Charlotte is very pretty, I can tell you. You may see her if you look this way."

Since Elinor was certain to spot her within a couple of minutes without taking such liberties, she politely requested to be excused.

"Where is Marianne? Has she fled because we have arrived? I see her instrument is open."

"She is walking, I believe."

They were then joined by Mrs. Jennings, who lacked the patience to wait until the door opened before recounting her tale. She hailed the window, "How do you do, my dear? How does Mrs. Dashwood do? And where are your sisters? What! All alone! You will be delighted with a little company to sit with. I have brought my other son and daughter to see you. Only think of their coming so suddenly! I thought I heard a carriage last night while we were drinking tea, but it never occurred to me it could be them. I thought of nothing but whether it might not be Colonel Brandon returning!"

Elinor was forced to turn from her mid-story to receive the remainder of the party. Lady Middleton introduced the two strangers; Mrs. Dashwood and Margaret descended the stairs simultaneously, and they all sat down, staring at one another, while Mrs. Jennings continued her narrative as she walked through the passage into the parlor, attended by Sir John.

Mrs. Palmer was several years younger than Lady Middleton and entirely unlike her in every respect. She was short and plump, possessing a very pretty face and the finest expression of good humor

As the Dashwood sisters stepped into the drawing-room of the Park the following afternoon, Mrs. Palmer burst in through the opposite door, radiant with a cheerfulness that seemed entirely restored. With genuine warmth, she greeted them, clasping their hands and expressing profound delight at their return.

"How utterly fortunate to see you!" she exclaimed, settling herself snugly between Elinor and Marianne. "For what a dismal day it has been, I feared you might not venture out. That would have been dreadful, considering we depart tomorrow. We simply *must* leave, for the Westons arrive next week. It was all a sudden whim on our part; I knew nothing of it until the carriage pulled up, and then Mr. Palmer asked if I would accompany him to Barton. He is so whimsical! He rarely shares a thought with me. Oh, I do sorrow that we cannot remain longer, yet I trust we shall reunite in town ere long."

An immediate necessity arose to dampen this hopeful expectation.

"To town?" cried Mrs. Palmer, laughing. "I shall be utterly disheartened if you do not. I could secure you the finest residence imaginable, right beside ours in Hanover Square. You absolutely must come. Indeed, I am certain I would be overjoyed to act as your chaperone at any time until I am confined, should your mother prefer avoiding public gatherings."

They offered polite thanks, yet firmly resisted all her pleas.

"Oh, my dearest love," Mrs. Palmer called out to her husband, who had just entered the room, "you must assist me in persuading the Miss Dashwoods to spend the winter in town."

Her husband offered no reply. After a mere nod to the ladies, he launched into a complaint regarding the weather.

"How abominable everything is!" he declared. "Such meteorological conditions render both objects and persons repulsive. Stagnation breeds indoors as readily as outdoors under rain; it fosters a detestation of all acquaintances. What deviltry is Sir John practicing by failing to equip his estate with a billiard room? How few comprehend true comfort! Sir John is as obtuse as the skies."

Soon, the remainder of the assembly drifted in.

"I fear, Miss Marianne," Sir John remarked, "that you have been unable to take your customary stroll to Allenham today."

Marianne's face fell into a somber gravity, and she offered no words.

"Oh, do not be so secretive before us," Mrs. Palmer interjected. "We are fully aware of the matter, I assure you. Furthermore, I admire your discernment greatly, for I find him exceedingly handsome. We reside but a short distance from him in the countryside, you know—no more than ten miles, I wager."

"Far closer to thirty," corrected her husband dryly.

"Ay, well! There is scant distinction. I have never visited his home, though they claim it is a charming, lovely spot."

"As vile a location as I have ever encountered in my life," retorted Mr. Palmer.

Marianne remained perfectly silent, though her expression betrayed a keen interest in the discussion.

"Is it truly so unsightly?" Mrs. Palmer pressed. "Then it must be some other locale that possesses such beauty, I suppose."

Later, as they settled in the dining room, Sir John lamented with regret that only eight souls were present.

"My dear," he addressed his wife, "it is most vexing that we are so few. Pray, why did you not invite the Gilberts to dine with us today?"

"Did I not inform you, Sir John, when you first broached the subject, that this was impossible? They dined with us yesterday."

"You and I, Sir John," Mrs. Jennings observed gently, "need not adhere to such rigid etiquette."

"Then you would be dreadfully ill-mannered," Mr. Palmer snapped.

"My love, you contradict everyone," his wife replied with her customary mirth. "Do you realize you are being quite rude?"

"I was unaware I was contradicting anyone by labeling your mother ill-bred."

"Aye, you may rail against me as you please," said the kindly old lady. "You have spirited Charlotte away from my care, and you cannot return her. Thus, I hold the upper hand."

Charlotte laughed heartily at the notion that her husband could not extricate himself, declaring she cared not for his crossness, for they were bound together forever. It was impossible for anyone to be more thoroughly good-natured, or more resolved to happiness, than Mrs. Palmer. Her husband's studied indifference, insolence, and apparent discontent caused her no pang; instead, whenever he scolded or abused her, she found immense amusement.

"Mr. Palmer is so droll!" she whispered to Elinor. "He is perpetually in a foul mood."

After brief observation, Elinor hesitated to credit him with such genuine, unfeigned ill-nature or bad breeding as he strove to project. Perhaps his temperament had grown sour from discovering, like many men, that an inexplicable bias toward beauty had landed him as the spouse of a remarkably foolish woman. Yet, Elinor reasoned that such a blunder was too commonplace to permanently wound a sensible mind. Rather, she suspected it stemmed from a vanity of distinction—a desire to appear superior to others—and a habit of abusing everything around him to bolster this illusion. The motive, though common, warranted no wonder; however, the means employed to establish this superiority through ill-breeding were unlikely to attract anyone save his wife.

"Oh, my dear Miss Dashwood," Mrs. Palmer resumed shortly thereafter, "I have a favor to request of you and your sister. Will you journey to Cleveland for Christmas? Pray, do—and come while the Westons are still with us. You cannot conceive how joyful I shall be! It will be absolutely delightful! My love," turning to her husband, "do you not long for the Miss Dashwoods to visit Cleveland?"

"Certainly," he replied with a sneer—"I ventured into Devonshire with no other purpose."

"There now," said his lady, "you see Mr. Palmer anticipates your arrival; therefore, you cannot possibly refuse."

Both sisters eagerly and resolutely declined her invitation.

"But indeed you *must* and *shall* come. I am convinced you will find it surpasses all expectations. The Westons will be present, making it wholly enchanting. You cannot imagine how sweet a place Cleveland is; moreover, we are so lively now, for Mr. Palmer is constantly traversing the countryside canvassing for the election, and so many guests have flocked to dine with us that I have never witnessed before—it is quite charming! Alas, poor fellow! It is terribly fatiguing for him, for he is compelled to force everyone to like him."

Elinor struggled vainly to maintain her composure as she assented to the hardship of such an obligation.

"How delightful it will be," Charlotte chimed in, "when he enters Parliament! Won't it? I shall be in stitches! It will be so absurd to see every letter addressed to him bearing 'M.P.'—But do you know, he insists he will never frank for me? He declares he won't. Do you agree, Mr. Palmer?"

Mr. Palmer ignored her completely.

"He cannot endure writing, you know," she continued. "He says it is quite shocking."

"No," he interjected, "I never uttered anything so irrational. Do not attribute all your linguistic distortions to me."

"There now! See how droll he is. This is invariably his manner! Sometimes he refuses to speak to me for half a day, only to emerge with something so witty concerning any triviality."

As they returned to the drawing-room, Mrs. Palmer surprisingly asked Elinor if she did not find Mr. Palmer excessively agreeable.

"Certainly," Elinor replied. "He appears very pleasant."

"Well, I am so glad you feel that way. I thought you would, for he is so agreeable. Moreover, Mr. Palmer is excessively pleased with you and your sisters; I can assure you he will be deeply disappointed if you do not visit Cleveland. I cannot fathom why you object."

Elinor was once again compelled to decline, finally stalling her entreaties by shifting the conversation. She hoped that, living in the same county, Mrs. Palmer might offer more specific insights into Willoughby's character than the Middletons' limited acquaintance allowed. Eager to gather any confirmation of his virtues that might allay Marianne's fears, she began by inquiring whether they saw much of Mr. Willoughby at Cleveland and if they were intimate with him.

"Oh, dear, yes! I know him extremely well," Mrs. Palmer answered. "Though, to be honest, I have never spoken to him. I have merely glimpsed him often in town. Somehow, fate conspired so that I never stayed at Barton while he was at Allenham. Mother saw him here once previously, but I was visiting my uncle at Weymouth. Nevertheless, I daresay we would have crossed paths frequently in Somersetshire if it had not happened so unluckily that we never resided in the country simultaneously. He spends very little time at Combe, I believe; and even if he did, I doubt Mr. Palmer would visit him, for he opposes the government, and besides, Combe is so remote. I understand precisely why you inquire about him, very well; your sister intends to marry him. I am monstrously delighted, for then I shall have her as a neighbor."

"Upon my word," Elinor responded,

Sir John and his wife departed for Cleveland the following day, leaving Barton Hall to the solitude of the Dashwoods and the Middletons. Yet this brief respite from social intrusion proved fleeting. Before Elinor could fully settle the lingering questions regarding Charlotte Palmer's unexplained happiness or Mr. Palmer's unassuming demeanor, Sir John and Mrs. Jennings returned with fresh recruits for their circle of acquaintances.

During a morning excursion to Exeter, the pair had encountered two young ladies whom Mrs. Jennings promptly identified as relatives. Seizing the opportunity, Sir John immediately invited them to the Park, overriding any remaining local commitments. To Lady Middleton, this announcement sent a tremor of genuine alarm coursing through her. She had never laid eyes upon these strangers, possessed no evidence of their elegance or gentility, and relied solely on the hollow assurances of her husband and mother, which held little weight in her mind. That they were her cousins only deepened her apprehension, rendering Mrs. Jennings' counsel—that they were kin and therefore must be endured—to seem woefully inadequate. Despite her protests, however, Lady Middleton eventually acquiesced to the inevitable, resigning herself with the stoic composure of a well-born woman, though she did not fail to deliver a gentle, daily reprimand to her husband regarding his poor taste in company.

The sisters arrived shortly thereafter, and their reception was, by all accounts, gratifying. Dressed with smart precision and conducting themselves with polite civility, they expressed a delight in the house and furniture that bordered on the ecstatic. Furthermore, their evident, almost doting affection for the children won Lady Middleton's favor within the hour. To her ladyship, their enthusiasm constituted "enthusiastic admiration," and Sir John's confidence swelled accordingly. He hastened to the cottage to announce the news to the Miss Dashwoods, declaring the Miss Steeles to be the sweetest girls imaginable. However, Elinor, perceptive and grounded, recognized that the title "sweetest girl" could apply to countless women across England, regardless of their varied forms, faces, tempers, or intellects. Sir John, a benevolent and somewhat garrulous soul, insisted that the entire family accompany him to view his guests, unable to tolerate keeping even a distant cousin to himself.

"Do come now!" he urged, his voice rising in persuasion. "Pray, come! You must see them. Lucy is monstrously pretty and so good-humored! Already the children are clustering around her as if she were an old friend. They both long to see you above all else, having heard at Exeter that you are the most beautiful creatures in the world. I have confirmed their stories, adding greatly to the truth. You will be charmed, I am certain. They have filled the carriage with toys for the children. Why are you so cross? After all, you are cousins, in a manner of speaking. You are mine, and they are my wife's; surely there is blood between us."

Despite his fervor, Sir John could not secure an immediate audience. He managed only a vague promise of a visit within a day or two before departing, returning home to boast anew of the Miss Steeles' attractions, as he had done previously.

When the promised visit finally occurred, the reality of the encounter offered a starker contrast to Sir John's hyperbolic descriptions. The eldest sister, nearing thirty, possessed a countenance that was neither plain nor sensible, offering little to admire. Her younger companion, however, aged no more than twenty-two or twenty-three, displayed considerable beauty. Her features were pleasing, her eye sharp and quick, and her bearing possessed a distinct smartness that, while lacking true grace, lent her a notable presence. Their manners were notably civil, and Elinor quickly accorded them credit for a measure of sense, observing with what constant and judicious attention they strove to please Lady Middleton.

With the children, they were in a state of perpetual rapture, extolling their beauty, courting their notice, and humor every whim. Any spare moments they had were devoted to admiring Lady Middleton's activities, whether performing tasks or modeling elegant new dresses that had captivated them the previous day. For those who court through such trivialities, a fond mother, though rapacious in her pursuit of praise for her offspring, remains the most credulous of humans. Her demands are exorbitant, yet she swallows anything. Consequently, the excessive affection and endurance shown by the Miss Steeles toward Lady Middleton's children passed without the slightest suspicion. Lady Middleton viewed their impertinent encroachments and mischievous tricks with maternal complacency, interpreting their sashes being untied, hair pulled, and workbags rifled through as signs of reciprocal enjoyment. The only anomaly that struck her was the composed silence of Elinor and Marianne, who sat apart from the lively chaos.

"John is in such spirits today!" she remarked, watching Sir John toss a pocket-handkerchief from a window. "He is full of monkey-tricks."

Soon after, observing the second boy pinch a finger, she fondly exclaimed, "How playful William is!" Then, tenderly embracing a silent toddler, she cooed, "And here is my sweet little Annamaria... Never was there such a quiet little thing!"

Unfortunately, in bestowing these embraces, a pin hidden in her head-dress lightly scratched the child's neck. This minor injury triggered screams of such violence that they rivaled any noise the children usually made. Lady Middleton's consternation was profound, but it paled beside the alarm of the visiting sisters. In a frantic display of affection, the trio rushed to soothe the sufferer. The child was lifted into her mother's lap, showered with kisses, bathed with lavender water by one of the Miss Steeles, who knelt to attend her, while the other stuffed her mouth with sugar-plums. Yet, the child remained wise enough to continue weeping, kicking her brothers who dared approach her. United soothings proved ineffective until Lady Middleton recalled a similar incident involving apricot marmalade for a bruised temple. A slight intermission of screaming followed the proposal of this remedy, giving hope for acceptance. The child was carried out to seek the medicine, followed by the two boys despite her mother's entreaties, leaving the four young ladies alone in a sudden, heavy quiet.

"Poor little creatures!" sighed Miss Steele as soon as the children departed. "It might have been a very sad accident."

"And yet I hardly know how," cried Marianne, "unless it had been under totally different circumstances. But this is the usual method of heightening alarm where there is naught truly alarming."

"What a sweet woman Lady Middleton is!" observed Lucy Steele.

Marianne remained silent, for it was impossible for her to feign sentiments she did not feel, however trivial the occasion. Thus, the burden of maintaining polite falsehoods fell entirely upon Elinor. She endeavored to speak of Lady Middleton with greater warmth than she genuinely felt, though far less effusively than Miss Lucy.

"And Sir John too!" exclaimed the elder sister. "What a charming man he is!"

Again, Miss Dashwood's commendation, simple and just, lacked the éclat of her companion. She merely noted that he was perfectly good-humored and friendly.

"And what a charming little family they have! I never saw such fine children in my life. I declare I quite doat upon them already, and indeed I am always distractedly fond of children."

"I should guess so," said Elinor, smiling, "from what I have witnessed this morning."

"I have a notion," said Lucy, "that you think the little Middletons rather too much indulged; perhaps they are the outside of enough. But it is so natural in Lady Middleton. For my part, I love to see children full of life and spirits; I cannot bear them if they are tame and quiet."

"I confess," replied Elinor, "that while I am at Barton Park, I never think of tame and quiet children with any abhorrence."

A short pause ensued, broken by Miss Steele, who seemed eager for conversation. "And how do you like Devonshire, Miss Dashwood?" she asked abruptly. "I suppose you were very sorry to leave Sussex."

Startled by the familiarity of the tone, Elinor admitted that she had indeed been sorry.

"Norland is a prodigious beautiful place, is it not?" added Miss Steele.

"We have heard Sir John admire it excessively," said Lucy, seemingly feeling compelled to apologize for her sister's boldness.

"I think everyone *must* admire it," replied Elinor. "Though it is not to be supposed that anyone can estimate its beauties as we do."

"And had you a great many smart beaux there? I suppose you have not so many in this part of the world; for my part, I think they are a vast addition always."

"But why should you think," interrupted Lucy, blushing, "that there are not as many genteel young men in Devonshire as Sussex?"

"Nay, my dear, I'm sure I don't pretend to say that there aren't. I'm sure there's a vast many smart beaux in Exeter; but you know, how could I tell what smart beaux there might be about Norland? And I was only afraid the Miss Dashwoods might find it dull at Barton if they had not so many as they used to have. Perhaps you young ladies may not care about the beaux, and had as lief be without them as with them. For my part, I think they are vastly agreeable, provided they dress smart and behave civil. But I can't bear to see them dirty and nasty. Now there's Mr. Rose

Marianne, a spirit who had always harbored little patience for anything resembling impudence, vulgarity, mediocrity, or a divergence from her own refined tastes, found her temper particularly irritable. In this fragile state, she was wholly incapable of finding pleasure in the company of the Miss Steeles or encouraging their overtures. To their unvarying chill, which stifled every attempt at intimacy, Elinor chiefly owed the fact that both sisters eventually favored her, a preference that became strikingly obvious in their behavior, especially Lucy's. Miss Steele seized every chance to engage Elinor in conversation, striving to deepen their acquaintance with an air of casual friendliness.

Lucy possessed natural wit; her observations were often sharp and amusing, and for thirty minutes, she could prove a delightful companion. Yet, her intellect had received no nourishment from education. Ignorant and illiterate, her lack of mental cultivation and her poverty of information on common subjects could not be masked by Elinor's efforts to maintain a favorable appearance. Elinor viewed Lucy's squandered potential with pity, but she observed the lady's utter lack of delicacy, rectitude, and moral integrity with a sterner, colder gaze. The attentiveness, assiduousness, and flattery Lucy displayed at the Park revealed a nature devoid of genuine virtue. Elinor derived no lasting satisfaction from the company of a woman who coupled insincerity with ignorance; whose lack of instruction prevented equality in discourse, and whose conduct toward others rendered any outward show of deference to herself utterly hollow.

One afternoon, as they strolled from the park to the cottage, Lucy broke the silence. "You'll think this a peculiar question, I'm sure," she began, "but pray, are you personally acquainted with your sister-in-law's mother, Mrs. Ferrars?"

Elinor did find the query oddly specific, and her expression reflected her surprise as she admitted she had never met Mrs. Ferrars.

"Indeed!" Lucy exclaimed, feigning wonder. "I marvel at that, for I imagined you must have encountered her at Norland occasionally. Perhaps, then, you cannot describe her?"

"No," Elinor replied, carefully guarding her true opinion of Edward's mother and disinclined to satisfy what appeared to be prying curiosity. "I know nothing of her."

"I am certain you consider me strangely curious," Lucy pressed, watching Elinor intently. "Yet, perhaps there is cause. I trust you won't take offense; I truly hope you believe I have no intention of being rude."

Elinor offered a polite response, and they walked in silence for a brief interval until Lucy resumed the thread. "I cannot bear for you to deem me inquisitive. I'd sooner endure anything than offend someone whose regard is so precious to me. I wouldn't dream of troubling you with advice on managing such an awkward position, but..." She hesitated. "It is a shame you do not know Mrs. Ferrars."

"A pity," Elinor said, genuinely astonished, "if knowing my opinion could assist *you*. However, I never realized you were connected to that family, which makes this serious inquiry into her character somewhat surprising."

"You're right to be surprised," Lucy murmured, lowering her head in a display of modesty, glancing sideways to gauge Elinor's reaction. "If I could reveal everything, you wouldn't be so shocked. Mrs. Ferrars means nothing to me now—not that the future isn't possible. How swiftly it arrives depends on her, but eventually, we shall be intimately bound."

"Good heavens!" Elinor gasped, her composure cracking. "Do you mean you know Mr. Robert Ferrars? Could that be it?" The prospect of such a relationship filled her with dread.

"No," Lucy corrected softly, "not Mr. *Robert* Ferrars—I've never laid eyes on him. But..." She fixed her gaze on Elinor. "His eldest brother."

Astonishment, fierce and painful, seized Elinor. An immediate, instinctive disbelief followed, shielding her from the shock. She turned to Lucy, silent and bewildered, unable to fathom the motive behind such a declaration. Her cheeks flushed, yet she stood firm in her skepticism, far from fainting.

"You needn't be alarmed," Lucy continued, undeterred. "You couldn't possibly guess beforehand, could you? I daresay he never hinted at it to you or anyone else. It was always meant to remain a profound secret, and I assure you, I have guarded it faithfully to this hour. Not a single relation of mine knows, save for Anne—and I would never have disclosed it to you had I not placed absolute faith in your discretion. My numerous inquiries about Mrs. Ferrars must have seemed odd, hence the need for explanation. I am certain Mr. Ferrars won't be displeased that I have trusted you, for he holds your entire family in the highest esteem, regarding you and your sisters as kin."

She paused. Elinor remained silent for a moment, stunned. Forcing herself to speak, she managed a tone of calm composure that barely concealed her turmoil. "May I ask if your engagement has been of long standing?"

"We have been engaged for four years."

"Four years!"

"Yes."

Though deeply shaken, Elinor still struggled to accept the truth. "I was unaware," she stammered, "that we were even acquainted until recently."

"Our friendship dates back many years," Lucy explained. "He was under my Uncle's care for a considerable time."

"My Uncle?"

"Yes, Mr. Pratt. Didn't he ever speak of Mr. Pratt?"

"I believe he did," Elinor replied, summoning strength as her emotions rose.

"He stayed with my uncle at Longstaple, near Plymouth, for four years. That is where our acquaintance began. My sister and I spent much time there, and it was then our engagement was formed, though not officially until a year after he ceased his studies. Yet, he remained with us constantly thereafter. I entered into it, as you can imagine, without my mother's knowledge or approval. I was too young, and loved him too dearly, to exercise the prudence I should have."

Turning to Elinor, she continued, "Though you may not know him as well as I, you must perceive his capacity to inspire sincere attachment."

"Certainly," Elinor answered, though her voice trembled. After a pause, she recovered slightly, bolstered by her conviction of Edward's honor and love, contrasting it sharply with Lucy's apparent deceit. "Engaged to Mr. Edward Ferrars! I confess I am so astounded by your statement that I fear there is some confusion of identity. We cannot mean the same gentleman."

"We mean no other," Lucy smiled confidently. "Mr. Edward Ferrars, the elder son of Mrs. Ferrars of Park Street, and brother of your sister-in-law, Mrs. John Dashwood, is precisely whom I refer to. Pray, acknowledge that I am unlikely to err in naming the man upon whom my happiness rests."

"It is strange," Elinor said, her heart heavy, "that I should never have heard him mention your name."

"Not strange," Lucy agreed. "Given our situation, secrecy was paramount. You knew nothing of me or my family, so there was no occasion to introduce me. Furthermore, Mr. Edward feared his mother's suspicions above all things, which explains his silence."

Silence fell between them. Elinor's sense of security crumbled, yet her self-control held firm. "Four years," she stated firmly.

"Yes," Lucy sighed, dabbing at her eyes with a handkerchief. "And heaven knows how much longer we may wait. Poor Edward! It depresses him terribly." She produced a small miniature from her pocket. "To avoid any misunderstanding, please examine this portrait. It does not fully capture his features, but I trust you recognize the likeness. I have possessed it for over three years."

Handing the painting to Elinor, Lucy waited. When Elinor beheld the image, all lingering doubts vanished. Whatever fears she had harbored regarding haste or deception dissolved instantly. She accepted the portrait, confirming the resemblance.

"I have never been able to reciprocate with my own picture," Lucy lamented, "which vexes me, for he has always desired it so eagerly. I intend to send it at the first opportunity."

"You act wisely," Elinor replied calmly. They walked a few steps in quiet contemplation before Lucy spoke again. "I am convinced of your fidelity in keeping this secret, for you must understand its gravity. Its revelation to his mother would be disastrous; she would never approve. I possess no fortune, and I suspect she is excessively proud."

The journey commenced in the first week of January. The Middletons followed shortly after, while the Miss Steeles remained stationed at the park, departing only when the entire party returned.

The carriage ride to London, undertaken under the guardianship of Aunt Jennings, presented a stark contrast to the reality Elinor perceived. To embark on this journey as her aunt's guest, following so brief an acquaintance and amidst a disparity of ages and temperaments that had previously raised grave objections, seemed absurd. Her own prospects seemed bleared and grey compared to her sister's animated vision of a future with Willoughby. Marianne's impatience confirmed this. If her observations proved him unworthy, she would tear the veil from Marianne's eyes; if he proved true, she would force herself to celebrate her sister's joy, silencing her own private sorrows.

For three days, the journey unfolded. Marianne remained largely silent, lost in a reverie of romantic anticipation, offering exclamations of delight only when a picturesque landscape caught her gaze. To compensate for this solitude, Elinor adopted the role of the perfect companion to Aunt Jennings. She spoke, laughed, and attended to her aunt with exaggerated politeness, while the older woman, delighted by their company, fretted over their dining choices, insisting on salmon over cod despite the girls' apparent indifference. By the third afternoon, they arrived in London, eager to escape the coach and bask by a warm fire in a handsome, newly furnished house. Their quarters were spacious and comfortable, a former residence of Charlotte Palmer, evidenced by a silk painting of a landscape hanging above the mantle—a testament to seven years of artistic training in the capital.

With dinner delayed for two hours, Elinor seated herself to write to her mother. Almost simultaneously, Marianne took up her pen. "I am writing home, Marianne," Elinor remarked gently, "might you not postpone it until tomorrow?" "No!" Marianne replied sharply, avoiding further comment. Instantly, the truth dawned on Elinor: Marianne was writing to Willoughby. The implication was undeniable; they must be engaged. While this revelation offered no comfort to Elinor, it thrilled her with a sudden, sharp sense of certainty. Marianne finished her brief note with frantic speed, sealing it and directing it with a trembling hand. As Elinor watched, the direction clearly bore a large 'W'. Before the ink was dry, Marianne rang the bell, ordering the footman to dispatch the letter via the two-penny post. The matter was settled.

Marianne's spirits soared, yet beneath the surface lay a fluttering anxiety that failed to bring peace to her sister. As evening deepened, her agitation grew palpable. She ate little, and when they returned to the drawing-room, her ears strained to catch the roll of wheels on the pavement. To Elinor's relief, Aunt Jennings remained largely confined to her own room, unaware of the storm brewing below. Tea was served, and just as Marianne's heart sank after several false alarms, a heavy knock shattered the silence. It was unmistakably Willoughby's approach. Marianne sprang up, rushing toward the door. Silence followed the knock, stretching agonizingly. She peered out, stepped onto the stairs, and listened. Returning to the room, her countenance transformed by ecstatic disbelief, she cried out, "Oh, Elinor! It is Willoughby! He is here!" She stood poised to rush into his embrace, but the door opened, and Colonel Brandon entered.

The blow to Marianne was shattering. She fled the room, leaving Elinor in a state of bewildered frustration. Yet, Elinor's regard for Colonel Brandon tempered her resentment; she felt a stinging injustice that a man so devoted to her sister should mistake her composure for indifference. She quickly realized that Brandon had noticed her turmoil, observing her retreat with a mixture of astonishment and concern that bordered on forgetting basic courtesy. "Is your sister ill?" he asked. Elinor, distressed, attributed Marianne's state to headaches and fatigue, carefully deflecting any suspicion of deeper causes. Brandon listened intently, then shifted the conversation to the pleasures of London and inquiries about their journey, his tone calm and uninterested. Elinor, desperate to know if Willoughby was in town, hesitated to ask, fearing to wound Brandon. Finally, she ventured, "Have you been in London since I last saw you?" With evident embarrassment, he admitted he had been there almost constantly, visiting Delaford briefly but unable to return to Barton. These words, coupled with his demeanor, jolted Elinor's memory to the circumstances surrounding Willoughby's departure and the unease it had caused. She feared her question had revealed a curiosity far greater than she intended.

Aunt Jennings soon entered, her noise and cheerfulness masking the tension. She expressed her delight at seeing Colonel Brandon, mentioning her recent struggles with settling affairs and a debt to Mr. Cartwright. When Brandon mentioned having heard of her presence through Mr. Palmer, she turned her attention to the Palmers, asking after Charlotte, who, she assured them, was "a fine size" by now. Brandon, commissioned to deliver news of Mrs. Palmer's health, promised a visit the following day. Turning to the sisters, she asked where they had been, noting the absence of Miss Marianne. "Do not worry," she mused, "about what you and Mr. Willoughby intend for her. Youth and beauty are a powerful combination." She reflected wistfully on her own past, lamenting her lack of handsomeness but boasting of a "very good husband," now deceased for eight years. Brandon responded with his customary mildness to her barrage of questions, offering no real answers. As Elinor prepared the tea, Marianne reluctantly reappeared.

Once the guests departed, Colonel Brandon fell silent, his brow furrowed with thought, and Aunt Jennings struggled to persuade him to stay. With no other visitors, the ladies retired early, united in exhaustion. The next morning, Marianne awoke with renewed spirit, the previous night's disappointment seemingly erased by the promise of what lay ahead. Soon after breakfast, Mrs. Palmer's carriage arrived. You noted I held my tongue all dinner. Neither will Sir John, nor my daughters, for they are all thoughtful and considerate, especially if I give them a hint, which I certainly shall. For my part, I deem the less said of such matters, the better, the swifter it blows over and fades. And what good does chatter accomplish, you know?"

"In this affair, it serves only harm; perhaps more than in many similar cases, for it has been accompanied by circumstances rendering it unfit for public discourse, for the sake of every person involved. I must grant this justice to Mr. Willoughby—he has broken no formal engagement with my sister."

"Law, my dear! Cease defending him. No formal engagement indeed! After touring Allenham House with her and selecting the specific chambers wherein they were to dwell henceforth!"

For Marianne's sake, Elinor could not press the topic further, hoping it was not required for Willoughby's defense either. Though Marianne stood to lose greatly, he could gain little by enforcing the grim reality. After a brief silence, Mrs. Jennings, buoyed by her natural vivacity, broke forth again.

"Well, my dear, 'tis a true adage: an ill wind blows no good. It will prove beneficial for Colonel Brandon. He shall have her at last; ay, verily. Mark me, if they're not married by Midsummer. Lord! How he'll chuckle over this tidings! I hope he arrives tonight. 'Twill be far superior for your sister. Two thousand a year, debt-free, barring the little love-child, indeed; ay, I had forgotten her, but she may be apprenticed out cheap, and then what matters? Delaford is a charming spot, I can attest; precisely the sort of old-fashioned haven filled with comforts and conveniences. Enclosed by high gardens brimming with the finest fruit trees in the realm, and a magnificent mulberry tree in one corner! Lord! How Charlotte and I stuffed ourselves during our sole visit! Furthermore, there is a dove-cote, delightful stew ponds, and a lovely canal; indeed, everything one could desire. Moreover, it lies near the church, but a quarter-mile from the turnpike, ensuring it's never dull. Sit in the ancient yew arbor behind the house, and you'll behold every carriage passing. Oh! 'tis a delightful place!

Marianne awoke the following morning with a renewed, suffocating awareness of her misery, a state of soul she had barely dared to close her eyes against the night before. Elinor, gentle yet firm, pressed her to articulate the turmoil within, and before breakfast was served, they had traversed the landscape of Marianne's grief again and again. With Elinor offering steady counsel and affection, Marianne oscillated between impulsive despair and fleeting moments of hope. At times, she clung to the delusion that Willoughby was merely unfortunate and innocent, sharing her own tragedy; at others, the sheer impossibility of defending his actions stripped her of every consolation. Her moods swung violently: from absolute indifference to the scrutiny of the world, to a desire to vanish from it forever, and finally to a fierce resistance against any observation. Yet, in one matter, she remained constant: she rigorously avoided the presence of Mrs. Jennings, and when forced to endure it, she fell into a determined, icy silence. Her heart had grown hard against the possibility that Mrs. Jennings felt anything more than a casual interest in her sorrows.

"No, no, no!" Marianne cried, her voice trembling with indignation. "She cannot feel! Her kindness is not sympathy; her goodness is not tenderness. She craves nothing but gossip, and she cherishes me solely because I provide it."

Elinor, observing her sister, recognized the injustice of these judgments. Though Marianne possessed an excellent disposition and formidable abilities, she lacked reason and candor. Like many of the clever and virtuous, she projected her own internal standards onto others, measuring their motives by the immediate impact of their actions on herself. A specific incident later that morning, occurring in the privacy of their chamber after breakfast, drove this judgment of Mrs. Jennings further into despair. Through a weakness of her own, Marianne had inadvertently become the vessel for fresh pain, though Mrs. Jennings acted with pure goodwill.

Mrs. Jennings entered with a letter held outstretched, her countenance bright with the promise of solace. "Now, my dear," she announced, "I bring you something certain to do you good."

Marianne heard the fatal words. In an instant, her feverish imagination conjured a letter from Willoughby, brimming with tenderness, contrition, and explanations that would absolve him entirely, swiftly followed by Willoughby himself, rushing to kneel at her feet. The vision shattered as quickly as it formed. Before her lay the handwriting of her mother, a sight never previously unwelcome, now a source of agonizing betrayal. The cruel sting of Mrs. Jennings' deception, born of misguided confidence, overwhelmed Marianne. Tears streamed from her eyes with violent passion, a reproach that Mrs. Jennings, oblivious to the depth of her offense, could only interpret as excessive emotion. Despite Marianne's weeping, Mrs. Jennings withdrew, still insisting on the comfort of the letter. But when Marianne finally read it, calm enough to process the words, she found little solace. Every page echoed with Willoughby's name. Her mother, still blindly confident in their engagement and trusting in his constancy, had pleaded only for Marianne to be more open, speaking with a tenderness and affection for Willoughby that caused Marianne to weep with agony throughout the reading.

An overwhelming impatience to return home seized Marianne. Her mother, despite her errors, became dearest to her now, cherished precisely because of the tragic excess of her misplaced faith in Willoughby. Marianne was wild to depart. Elinor, torn between the wisdom of staying and the need to obey, offered no counsel save patience until their mother's reply arrived. Eventually, Marianne consented to wait.

As Mrs. Jennings prepared to leave, earlier than usual, she refused Elinor's company, needing the Middletons and Palmers to share her sorrow. Alone, Elinor sat down to write to her mother, her heart heavy with the knowledge of the pain she was about to reveal, knowing her previous efforts to prepare the ground had failed. Marianne, entering the drawing-room as her aunt departed, stood fixed beside Elinor, watching the movement of her pen. She grieved for the burden of the task and for the inevitable heartbreak it would cause her mother.

For about fifteen minutes, silence hung over them, broken only by the scratching of the pen, when a sharp rap at the door startled Marianne. Her nerves, already frayed, could not withstand the sudden noise.

"Who can this be?" cried Elinor. "So early! I thought we were safe."

Marianne hastened to the window. "It is Colonel Brandon!" she exclaimed, her face flushing with vexation. "We are never safe from *him*."

"He will not enter, as Mrs. Jennings is absent."

"I will not trust in that," Marianne retreated to her own room, her voice sharp. "A man who possesses no occupation for his time has no conscience regarding his intrusions into the leisure of others."

Her conjecture proved correct, though founded on error. Colonel Brandon did enter. Elinor, seeing the genuine solicitude in his disturbed, melancholy gaze and his anxious inquiry after Marianne, could not forgive her sister's shallow estimation of him.

"I met Mrs. Jennings in Bond Street," Colonel Brandon began, after the initial salutations. "She urged me to come. I thought it probable I might find you alone, a desire I hold strongly. My sole wish—to express it clearly—is to offer conviction, lasting conviction, to your sister's mind. Not merely temporary comfort, but the truth. My regard for her, for yourself, and for your mother... will you permit me to prove it by relating some circumstances? Nothing but sincere regard and an earnest desire to be useful justify me, though after spending so many hours convincing myself of my rightness, am I not afraid I may be wrong?" He paused, breathless.

"I understand," said Elinor gently. "You have something to tell me of Mr. Willoughby that will fully reveal his character. To disclose this is the greatest act of friendship for Marianne. My gratitude is immediate, and hers will follow in time. Please, let me hear it."

"You shall," Brandon replied, composing himself. "To be brief, when I left Barton last October... I must go further back. I am an awkward narrator, Miss Dashwood; I hardly know where to start. A short account of myself seems necessary, and it shall be brief. On such a subject..." He sighed heavily. "...can I have little temptation to be diffuse."

He hesitated, gathering his thoughts, then continued with another sigh. "You likely forgot a conversation—an evening at Barton Park, during a dance—in which I alluded to a lady I once knew, noting her resemblance to your sister, Marianne."

"I have not forgotten it," Elinor admitted. He looked pleased. "If I am not misled by the bias of tender memory, the resemblance is striking, both in mind and person. The same warmth of heart, the same eagerness of spirit. This lady was my nearest relative, an orphan from infancy, under my father's guardianship. Our ages were alike, and from childhood we were playmates and friends. I cannot recall a time when I did not love Eliza. As we matured, my affection deepened into something profound, perhaps so intense that, judging by my current forlorn gravity, you might deem me incapable of feeling it. Hers, for me, was fervent, equal to the attachment of your sister for Mr. Willoughby, and equally unfortunate. At seventeen, she was lost to me forever. She was married, against her will, to my brother. Her fortune was substantial, but our estate heavily encumbered. And I fear this is all that can be said of the conduct of a man who was both her uncle and guardian. My brother did not deserve her; he did not even love her. I had hoped her regard for me would sustain her, and for some time it did. But eventually, the misery of her situation, marked by great unkindness, overcame her resolve. Though she had promised me otherwise, blind as I was in relating it, the truth emerged. We were within hours of eloping to Scotland. The treachery—or folly—of my cousin's maid betrayed us. I was banished to a distant relation's house, and she was denied liberty, society, or amusement until my father's demands were met. I had relied too heavily on her fortitude, and the blow was severe. If her marriage had been happy, so young as I was, a few months might have reconciled me, or at least I would not mourn it today. But it was not. My brother held no regard for her; his pleasures were base, and from the start, he treated her unkindly. For a mind so young, lively, and inexperienced as Mrs. Brandon's, the consequence was natural. She submitted initially to the misery, but if she had not lived to overcome the regrets caused by my memory, it would have been fortunate. But she did. Can we wonder that, with a husband provoking inconstancy and no friend to advise or restrain her—for my father died shortly after their union, and I was stationed in the East Indies—she should fall? Had I remained in England, perhaps... but I sought to promote her happiness by removing her, for which purpose I secured an exchange. The shock of her marriage hurt me, but it was trifling compared to what I felt hearing of her divorce two years later. It was *that* which cast this gloom—the recollection of my suffering..."

When Miss Dashwood recounted the details of her interview with Colonel Brandon to her sister, the impact was not entirely what she had anticipated. Marianne did not question the truth of a single word; she listened with a steady, submissive attention, offering no objection, no defense for Willoughby, and shedding tears that betrayed her absolute conviction of his guilt. Yet, though Elinor saw the sting of this knowledge taking root, causing Marianne to stop avoiding Colonel Brandon and to address him with a compassionate, respectful air, she also saw that her sister remained deeply wretched. While Marianne's spirit had lost its violent agitation, it had settled into a gloomy dejection. The revelation of Willoughby's character weighed heavier on her than the loss of his heart. The memory of his seduction of Miss Williams, the misery inflicted upon that poor girl, and the chilling suspicion of what his intentions toward herself truly were, preyed relentlessly upon her mind. So profound was her sorrow that she refused to speak of it even to Elinor, brooding in silence and inflicting a deeper, more silent pain than any open confession could have caused.

As for Mrs. Dashwood, to describe her reception of Elinor's letter would merely repeat the anguish already shared between the sisters. Her letters, arriving in rapid succession, poured forth her suffering, her anxiety for Marianne, and her desperate pleas for fortitude. How terrible must Marianne's affliction be, her mother reasoned, when she could speak of strength? How humiliating must the cause of these regrets be, that her daughter could not bear to indulge them?

Despite her own discomfort in leaving Barton, Mrs. Dashwood resolved that Marianne would find it easier to endure her grief anywhere else than at home, where every object constantly recalled Willoughby as she had always seen him there. She urged her daughters to extend their stay in town, hoping that the varied occupations, sights, and company of London might, at least occasionally, divert Marianne's thoughts from herself and perhaps even provide a fleeting amusement, however spurned by her current state of mind. From the perspective of safety, Mrs. Dashwood believed London offered equal protection from Willoughby as the country did. Since his acquaintances would now sever ties with anyone connected to her, design could not bring them together, negligence could not expose them by chance, and the crowded streets of London held less opportunity for accidental encounters than the quiet seclusion of Barton. Furthermore, a letter from her son-in-law indicated that he and his wife would arrive in town before mid-February, making it prudent for them to maintain their proximity.

Marianne, though inwardly believing her mother's plan flawed and contrary to her wishes, submitted without opposition. She felt it was entirely wrong to prolong her stay in London, depriving her of her mother's direct sympathy and condemning her to a social circle that would allow her no moment of rest. However, a glimmer of solace remained: what brought ruin to herself might bring restoration to her sister. Elinor, anticipating that she could not fully avoid Edward Ferrars, found comfort in the thought that their extended stay, while potentially hindering her own happiness, was necessary for Marianne's sake.

Elinor's vigilance in shielding her sister from the mention of Willoughby proved effective. Neither Mrs. Jennings, Sir John, nor Mrs. Palmer ever spoke his name before Marianne. Yet, Elinor wished that same restraint could have been applied to herself, for she was compelled to listen daily to the clamors of indignation surrounding the scandal. Sir John, incredulous, declared that he could not have imagined it, recalling Willoughby as a bold rider and a good-natured soul. "He did not believe there was a bolder rider in England!" he exclaimed, wishing the scoundrel at the devil. "Such a deceitful dog!" he added, lamenting that the meeting at Allenham had ended so tragically.

Mrs. Palmer, equally indignant, resolved to drop all acquaintance with him and vowed never to utter his name again, calling him a "good-for-nothing." Her sympathy manifested primarily in her eagerness to gather and disseminate gossip regarding the impending marriage. She detailed where the new carriage was being built, which artist painted Willoughby's portrait, and where Miss Grey's wedding attire could be purchased.

In stark contrast, Lady Middleton's calm and polite unconcern provided a rare relief to Elinor, whose spirits were often overwhelmed by the fervent kindness of others. It was a profound comfort to know that at least one person in their circle could greet her without curiosity about particulars or anxiety for her sister's health. Elinor sometimes found herself rating good-breeding higher than good-nature under the pressure of officious condolences, valuing Lady Middleton's ability to express shock—"It is very shocking, indeed!"—while maintaining a dignified distance. This allowed Lady Middleton to support the dignity of her own sex, voice her censure, and then turn her attention to the interests of her assemblies, deciding to call on Mrs. Willoughby the moment she was married, despite Sir John's reservations.

Colonel Brandon's inquiries, always delivered with delicacy and unobtrusive concern, were never unwelcome to Miss Dashwood. Having earned the privilege of discussing Marianne's disappointment through his zealous efforts to soothe her, they conversed with complete confidence. For Brandon, the reward for exposing his own past sorrows and present humiliations lay in Marianne's pitying gaze and the gentle tone of her voice when addressing him. These signs assured him that his efforts had fostered goodwill, giving Elinor hope for further growth. Meanwhile, Mrs. Jennings, unaware of the depth of their connection, grew increasingly convinced that the marriage would not occur until Michaelmas, or perhaps not at all, as the "good understanding" between the Colonel and Miss Dashwood seemed to suggest that the pleasures of the mulberry tree and the yew arbour would fall to her alone.

By early February, scarcely a fortnight after receiving Willoughby's letter, Elinor had the painful duty of informing her sister of his marriage. She ensured the news reached Marianne privately, sparing her the agony of reading it in the newspapers, which she examined with eager, trembling hands. Marianne received the tidings with resolute composure, shedding no tears initially. However, after a brief interval, the dam broke, and for the remainder of the day, she existed in a state of pitiable desolation hardly less severe than when she first feared the event.

With the Willoughbys departed from town immediately following the wedding, Elinor hoped to persuade her sister to venture out gradually, as she had done previously. About this time, the two Miss Steeles returned to the residence of their cousins in Conduit Street, greeted with great cordiality. Only Elinor felt sorrow at their presence, for their arrival invariably caused her pain, especially Lucy Steele's delighted exclamation of finding her still in town.

"I should have been quite disappointed if I had not found you here *still*," Lucy remarked, emphasizing the word. "Though you *told* me at Barton that you would not stay above a *month*, I thought you would change your mind when it came to the point. It would have been such a pity to have gone away before your brother and sister came. And now, surely, you will be in no *hurry* to be gone?"

Elinor understood the implication perfectly and struggled to maintain her self-command, feigning indifference to the insinuation. When Mrs. Jennings asked how they traveled, Miss Steele proudly declared they had come post with a "smart beau," Dr. Davies, who had treated them with the utmost gentility. Though Mrs. Jennings suspected the Doctor was the man behind the affection, Miss Steele denied it with affected earnestness, vowing to contradict anyone who claimed otherwise—a promise Mrs. Jennings happily accepted.

Later, returning to the charge, Lucy asked if Miss Dashwood would accompany her and her sister to stay with her family in town. Elinor, refusing to humor further opposition, simply stated, "No, I do not think we shall." Lucy pressed, and when Mrs. Jennings interjected that their visit was barely begun, Lucy was silenced. When Mrs. Steele expressed regret that they could not see Miss Dashwood's sister, noting Marianne's absence due to nervous headaches, Elinor politely declined the invitation, explaining that her sister was confined to bed or her dressing gown and unable to receive visitors. However, when Miss Steele suggested they could simply go to see her, Elinor found the impertinence becoming too much for her temper. Fortunately, Lucy's sharp reprimand saved her from a heated exchange, curbing her cousin's forwardness, albeit without adding sweetness to their mutual manners.

Marianne, though initially resistant, eventually relented to her sister's gentle urging and agreed to venture out with them and Mrs. Jennings. However, she placed a firm condition on the excursion: they were to avoid social calls, limiting their outing strictly to a brief stop at Gray's in Sackville Street, where Elinor was managing the sale of some of her mother's antique jewelry.

As they approached the shop, Mrs. Jennings remembered a lingering duty to visit a neighbor down the street. Since she had no pressing business at Gray's, it was arranged that she would attend to this errand first, leaving her younger charges to conduct their trade while she returned promptly.

Inside, the atmosphere was stifling with activity. The counters were crowded, and the staff overwhelmed, forcing the Dashwood sisters to wait. They took a seat at the counter end that promised the swiftest turnover, hoping to catch the eye of the sole gentleman currently attending to a customer. This man, however, possessed a sharpness of vision and a fastidiousness of taste that rendered him entirely incapable of courtesy. For nearly twenty minutes, he scrutinized toothpick cases, debating their shapes, sizes, and ornamentations with an obsessive inventiveness, offering the sisters not a word, but merely three or four wide, blank stares. These glances, devoid of warmth, left a sting of profound insignificance upon Elinor, marking her as someone of negligible consequence despite her fashionable attire.

Marianne, shielded from the humiliation of seeing her sister's distress, remained oblivious to the scene, her mind withdrawn into a quiet interior space where she could ignore the world around her. Finally, the transaction concluded. The ivory, gold, and pearls were weighed and valued. With a final, self-satisfied look at the sisters—one that demanded rather than offered admiration—the gentleman donned his gloves with deliberate slowness and departed, radiating a smug, affected indifference.

Elinor wasted no time in resuming her business, but just as she neared completion, another gentleman stepped beside her. Turning, she met the gaze of her own brother, John Dashwood. The reunion was warm, though the setting—a bustling jewel shop—rendered their affection somewhat theatrical. John, surprisingly content to see them, treated his mother with due respect and inquiry.

"I wished greatly to call yesterday," John explained, "but we were detained by Harry's desire to see the wild beasts at Exeter Exchange. The remainder of the day was spent with Mrs. Ferrars. Harry was delighted." He mentioned his intention to visit them later that morning, noting the busy nature of arriving in town. "I am here to commission a seal for Fanny, but I hope to be introduced to Mrs. Jennings tomorrow. I've heard she is a woman of substantial means. And regarding the Middletons... you must introduce me to them. As relatives of my wife's mother, I intend to treat them with the highest respect. They are, I understand, excellent neighbors."

"Excellent indeed," Elinor replied, masking her inner unease. "Their attention to our comfort and friendship knows no bounds."

John beamed with relief at her praise. "It is exactly as it should be. People of large fortune, related to you, surely owe every civility to ensure your happiness. And yet, you are settled so comfortably in your cottage! Edward spoke so highly of it—a model establishment, he said, where you all flourish. It was a true delight to hear."

A flush of shame warmed Elinor's cheeks at her brother's casual assessment of their modesty, but she was relieved from further conversation by the arrival of Mrs. Jennings's maid, who summoned them to the carriage.

Downstairs, John accompanied them to the vehicle, where he was formally introduced to Mrs. Jennings. After promising to return the following day, he took his leave. True to his word, he visited again shortly after, bringing an apologetic letter from his sister-in-law, Fanny, claiming she was too engrossed in domestic matters to join them. Yet, Mrs. Jennings, quick-witted and perceptive, dismissed the excuse, reminding John that they were cousins and asserting her intent to visit Mrs. John Dashwood soon, bringing the girls along. John's demeanor toward them was polite, if slightly distant, and his civility toward Mrs. Jennings was exemplary. When Colonel Brandon entered, John's curiosity flared, his eyes searching for signs of wealth with a calculating intensity that suggested his politeness was contingent solely on financial status.

Half an hour later, John proposed a walk to Conduit Street to meet Sir John and Lady Middleton. The weather was fair, and Elinor accepted. Stepping outside, John's inquiries became immediate and probing.

"Who is Colonel Brandon?" he asked, his tone sharpening. "Is he a man of fortune?"

"Yes," Elinor confirmed, "he holds significant property in Dorsetshire."

"A most gentlemanlike man," John mused, turning to his sister with a conspiratorial glint. "And I must congratulate you, Elinor, on the prospect of a respectable establishment."

"My brother!" Elinor protested, amused and exasperated simultaneously. "What do you mean?"

"He appreciates you," John declared, his conviction absolute. "I watched him closely; I am certain of it. What is his annual income?"

"Estate estimates suggest roughly two thousand pounds a year."

"Two thousand!" John exclaimed, his voice rising to a crescendo of enthusiastic benevolence. "Elinor, I pray with all my soul that it were *double* that amount, for your sake!"

"You speak kindly, but I am firmly convinced Colonel Brandon harbors no thought of marrying me."

"Nonsense, Elinor! You are grievously mistaken. A trifling effort on your part would secure him. Perhaps he hesitates momentarily, perhaps his friends advise caution, or perhaps your fortune seems slight. But a lady's attentiveness, her encouragement, can overcome any obstacle. You should try for him. It is obvious there is no prior attachment on your side—in fact, the notion is absurd, the barriers insurmountable—and you possess too much sense not to recognize this. Colonel Brandon is the man; and I pledge no lack of civility to win him over to your family. It is a union destined for universal satisfaction. In short, it is a matter—" he lowered his voice to a dramatic whisper—"'that will be exceedingly welcome to *all parties*.' Though, I hasten to correct myself, I mean to say: your friends are genuinely eager to see you settled, especially Fanny, whose heart beats for your interests, and her mother, Mrs. Ferrars, who would be thrilled."

Elinor offered no response.

"It would be a delightful irony," John continued, "if Fanny and I were both securing matches simultaneously. And yet, it is not improbable."

Turning the subject abruptly, Elinor asked with resolve, "Is Mr. Edward Ferrars to be married?"

"Not definitively settled, yet there is talk," John admitted. "An excellent match, I assure you. Mrs. Ferrars, with generous spirit, will provide a settlement of a thousand pounds a year if the union proceeds. The lady is the Honorable Miss Morton, daughter of the late Lord Morton, possessing thirty thousand pounds. A connection of high quality, and I foresee no delay."

"And the expense?" Elinor pressed gently.

"The costs of establishing ourselves here are heavy," John confessed. "My father's bequest of the Stanhill effects to your mother meant we must replace the linens and china removed from Norland. Then, there is the enclosure of Norland Common, a draining project, and my recent purchase of East Kingham Farm. I felt compelled to acquire it, adjacent to my lands, to preserve the estate's integrity. It has cost me a fortune."

"You purchased it above its intrinsic value," Elinor noted softly.

"Why, I trust not! I could have sold it the next day for a profit. But the stock market was volatile; had I not held the funds in hand, I would have suffered a catastrophic loss. Other expenses, too, have arisen since moving to Norland. My father left the remaining effects to your mother, obliging us to replenish the household goods. Consider, after all these drains, how far from riches we remain, and how vital Mrs. Ferrars's kindness becomes."

"Certainly," Elinor agreed. "With her support, I hope you will achieve ease."

"One or two years may alter things significantly," John replied gravely. "Yet, much remains undone. Not a stone is laid for Fanny's greenhouse, and the flower garden exists only on paper."

"Where will the greenhouse stand?"

"On the knoll behind the house. The ancient walnuts fell to clear the path. It will be a striking feature, viewed from across the park, with gardens sloping gracefully below."

Elinor suppressed her criticism and pity, grateful that Marianne was absent to witness John's grandiose visions. Having exhausted his list of grievances, John's mood lifted. He turned to compliment Elinor on her friendship with Mrs. Jennings.

"She is a woman of immense value," John observed. "Her lifestyle speaks of ample income. This acquaintance has already benefited you and promises to prove advantageous in the long run. Her invitation to town is a gesture of high regard; when she passes, you will surely inherit her estate."

"Unlikely," Elinor countered. "She leaves only her jointure to her daughters."

"But few prudent women spend their entire income," John argued. "Whatever she saves, she can distribute. And do you not think it more probable she favors you over her daughters

Mrs. John Dashwood possessed such absolute faith in her husband's discernment that she deferred all social introductions until the following day, waiting upon both Mrs. Jennings and her daughters. Her trust was duly vindicated; she discovered that even the hostess of Berkeley Street, a woman of limited wit, held no unworthiness that precluded polite regard. As for Lady Middleton, Mrs. Dashwood found her to be a creature of exquisite charm. A reciprocal attraction bound them together, forged in the fires of a shared, cold-hearted self-interest. They mirrored one another in an insipid propriety of manner and a profound, mutual lack of genuine understanding.

Yet, the very qualities that endeared Mrs. John Dashwood to Lady Middleton repelled Mrs. Jennings. To the latter, she appeared merely as a slightly haughty woman of unfeeling address, who greeted her sisters-in-law without warmth or substance. Of the fifteen minutes spent in their parlor, seven and a half minutes were consumed in a silence so heavy it bordered on the absurd.

Elinor burned with a quiet curiosity to know if Edward was still in London, though she carefully refrained from asking. It would take little to dissuade Fanny from mentioning his name voluntarily; she would remain silent until she could confirm that his marriage to Miss Morton was finalized, or until Colonel Brandon's expectations were fully realized. Believing the lovers' attachment to be so intense that they required constant division in word and deed, Fanny withheld this intelligence. However, news soon flowed from another source. Lucy arrived promptly to solicit Elinor's sympathy regarding her inability to see Edward, who had recently returned to town with Mr. and Mrs. Dashwood. Dreading detection, Edward dared not visit Bartlett's Buildings. Though their mutual longing to reunite was palpable, circumstances forced them to content themselves with letter-writing.

Soon after, Edward confirmed his presence in town through two separate visits to Berkeley Street, leaving his calling-card upon the table each time. Upon returning from their morning engagements, the sisters found the evidence of his attentions. Elinor felt a peculiar blend of satisfaction at his arrival and relief at having missed him physically.

The Dashwoods were so enchanted by the Middletons that, despite their usual frugality, they resolved to treat them to a dinner. Soon after establishing their acquaintance, they invited them to dine at Harley Street, where they had secured a fine residence for three months. The sisters and Mrs. Jennings were included in the invitation, and John Dashwood diligently ensured Colonel Brandon's attendance. Always eager to be near the Miss Dashwoods, the Colonel accepted their enthusiastic civilities with some surprise, yet with evident pleasure. They expected to meet Mrs. Ferrars, though Elinor remained uncertain whether her sons would attend. Nevertheless, the prospect of encountering her heightened Elinor's interest; although she could now face Mrs. Ferrars without the fierce anxiety that once plagued her, viewing her with indifference regarding her own judgment, a vivid curiosity regarding the lady's appearance persisted.

This anticipation was soon amplified, far more powerfully than pleasantly, by the news that the Miss Steeles were also to be present. Having won Lady Middleton's favor through their assiduous flattery, Sir John readily invited them to spend a fortnight in Conduit Street. For the Miss Steeles, whose claims to notice as the nieces of their guardian were otherwise weak, this invitation proved fortuitous, arriving just days before the party. Lucy, who longed to scrutinize the Dashwood family's characters and understand her own precarious position, felt a thrill of happiness she had rarely known upon receiving Mrs. John Dashwood's card.

For Elinor, however, the news brought a sudden chill. Determining immediately that Edward, living with his mother, must be invited as part of her household, she faced the terrifying prospect of seeing him, for the first time since their separation, in the company of Lucy. She scarcely knew how she could endure it. Perhaps these fears were not wholly grounded in reason, nor in truth. Yet, they were assuaged not by her own recollections, but by Lucy's goodwill. Lucy, believing herself to be dealing a cruel blow, informed Elinor that Edward would certainly not be at Harley Street on Tuesday, hoping further to soothe her by suggesting that his absence stemmed from an overwhelming, concealed affection for her.

Tuesday finally arrived, destined to introduce the two young ladies to this formidable future mother-in-law. "Pity me, dear Miss Dashwood!" cried Lucy as they ascended the stairs together, for the Middletons had arrived immediately after Mrs. Jennings, and they all followed the footman simultaneously. "There is no one here save yourself who can comprehend my plight. I declare I cannot stand upright. Good heavens! In a moment I shall behold the person upon whom all my happiness depends—my mother!"

Elinor could have alleviated her friend's terror by suggesting that perhaps Miss Morton's mother awaited them rather than her own, but instead, she offered sincere, albeit hollow, condolences to Lucy's utter amazement. Lucy, though genuinely uneasy, hoped to inspire in Elinor an envy she could not suppress.

Mrs. Ferrars was a diminutive, slender woman, standing with an upright rigidity bordering on formality. Her aspect was grave, verging on sourness. Her complexion was sallow, her features small and devoid of beauty or natural expression. However, a fortunate contraction of her brow rescued her countenance from the disgrace of insipidity, imbuing it with the strong characteristics of pride and ill-nature. She spoke sparingly, proportioning her words to the number of her thoughts, and of those few syllables that escaped her lips, not one fell to the share of Miss Dashwood. Mrs. Ferrars eyed Elinor with a spirited determination to dislike her at all costs.

Mrs. Jennings leaned close and whispered urgently, "Extract everything from her, my dear. She will reveal anything if you simply ask. You see, I cannot abandon Mrs. Clarke."

Fortune smiled upon Mrs. Jennings's curiosity and Elinor's, for Miss Steele revealed everything without needing prompting. "I am so delighted to see you!" Miss Steele exclaimed, linking arms with Elinor familiarly. "I sought you above all things in the world." Lowering her voice, she added, "I trust Mrs. Jennings has heard the whole truth? Is she angry?"

"Not at all, I believe, with you."

"That is a relief! And Lady Middleton—is she furious?"

"I cannot imagine how she could be."

"Oh, I am monstrous glad! Good heavens, what a torment I've endured! I never saw Lucy in such a rage. She swore initially she would never trim a bonnet or perform any service for me again, so long as she lived. Yet now she has completely recovered, and we are friends as ever. I'm certain I wouldn't have guessed he preferred it if he hadn't mentioned it. My cousins have been so pestering me! I sometimes don't know where to look."

Her rambling drifted far from the thread of inquiry, compelling Elinor to steer the conversation back to its original focus. "Well, Miss Dashwood," she said triumphantly, "though people may whisper whatever they wish about Mr. Ferrars declaring he would not accept Lucy, I assure you it is untrue. It is a scandal for such malicious rumors to circulate. Whatever Lucy's private sentiments may be, it was not anyone else's business to settle it definitively."

"I have never heard anything hinting at this before," Elinor replied.

"Oh, have you? Well, it *was* said, clearly, by many. Miss Godby told Miss Sparks that no sensible person expected Mr. Ferrars to forsake a woman like Miss Morton, with thirty thousand pounds, for Lucy Steele, who possesses nothing. I received it straight from Miss Sparks. Furthermore, my cousin Richard admitted himself that he feared Mr. Ferrars would bolt when the moment arrived. When Edward vanished from us for three days, I was utterly bewildered. I truly believe Lucy considered the engagement hopeless; we departed your brother's house on Wednesday and saw nothing of him until Thursday evening, then Friday, then Saturday—I didn't even know what had become of him. Once Lucy attempted to write to him, but her spirits rebelled against that idea.

"Then, this morning, he appeared just as we returned from church, and everything spilled forth. He explained that he had been summoned to Harley Street on Wednesday, confronted by his mother and others, and declared before them all that he loved no one but Lucy and desired no one else. Distressed by these proceedings, he fled immediately upon leaving his mother's house, riding into the countryside to stay at an inn all through Thursday and Friday, attempting to compose his mind. After weighing matters thoroughly, he confessed to me that, now possessing no fortune and virtually nothing, it would be cruel to maintain the engagement. It would inevitably be a burden to her, for he had only two thousand pounds and no hope of more. If he entered holy orders, as he contemplated, he would secure nothing but a curacy, and how could they subsist on that? He could not bear the thought of her settling for less, so he implored her, if she shared his feelings, to terminate the matter instantly and allow him to navigate his fate alone. I heard him articulate this plainly.

"It was entirely for *her* sake, on *her* account, that he suggested stepping aside—not out of weariness for her, nor desire for Miss Morton. I swear he never uttered a word of fatigue. However, Lucy refused to listen to reason. She told him directly—with much sentimental talk of sweetness and love—that she had not the slightest intention of breaking off. She insisted that she could live with him on a trifle, and regardless of how little he possessed, she would gladly accept it all."

"And so?" Elinor prompted gently.

"So he became monstrously happy! They discussed their future for some time, agreeing that he would enter orders immediately and they would wait to marry until he secured a living. Just then, I could hear no further, for my cousin called from downstairs informing me that Mrs. Richardson had arrived in her carriage to transport one of us to Kensington Gardens. I was compelled to retreat to the room to inquire if Lucy wished to accompany them, but she did not care to leave Edward. Consequently, I hurried upstairs, donned a pair of silk stockings, and descended with the Richardsons."

"Do you mean to say you interrupted them?" Elinor asked, puzzled. "You were all in the same room, were you not?"

"No, indeed, not us. La! Miss Dashwood, do you suppose people engage in courtship when others are present? Oh, shame! To be sure, you must know better than that. (She laughed artificially.) No, no; they were secluded in the drawing-room, and all I heard was by standing at the door."

"How!" cried Elinor. "Have you repeated to me what you learned solely by eavesdropping? I am sorry I was unaware beforehand; for I certainly would not have permitted you to recount particulars of a conversation which you ought not to have known. How could you behave so unfairly toward your sister?"

"Oh, la! There is nothing improper in *that*. I merely stood at the door and heard what I could. And I am certain Lucy would treat me exactly the same; for a year or two ago, when Martha Sharpe and I shared many secrets, she never hesitated to hide in a closet or behind a chimney-piece to overhear us."

Elinor attempted to divert the conversation, but Miss Steele proved relentless, unable to be kept from the topics dominating her mind for more than a couple of moments. "Edward speaks of traveling to Oxford soon," she remarked. "But currently, he lodges at No.—, Pall Mall. What a disagreeable woman his mother is, isn't she? And your brother and sister were hardly kind! Nevertheless, I shall say nothing against them to *you*; and to be sure, they did send us home in their own carriage, which exceeded my expectations. As for me, I was terrified lest your sister request the huswives she gifted us a day or two prior, but nothing was said, and I took care to conceal mine. Edward has some business at Oxford, he says, so he must go there temporarily. Afterwards, as soon as he can locate a Bishop, he will be ordained. I wonder what curacy he will obtain! Good gracious! Two months had now elapsed since the Dashwood sisters took up residence in London, and Marianne's longing to return to the country grew by the hour. She breathed for the fresh air, the open liberty, and the tranquil silence of the countryside; in her imagination, nothing could compare to the peace of Barton. Elinor shared this desire deeply, though she tempered it with a sober awareness of the practical difficulties inherent in a journey of such length—a constraint Marianne, ever impulsive, refused to see. Yet, Elinor began to formulate a concrete plan, having already voiced their wishes to Lady Middleton, who, despite her warm hospitality, hesitated to release them from her care. Then, a new opportunity arose. The Palmers were preparing to move to Cleveland near Bristol around the end of March, coinciding with the Easter holidays, and Mrs. Jennings, along with the sisters, received a fervent invitation from Charlotte Palmer to accompany them. While this arrangement alone might have seemed insufficient to Elinor's discernment, it was bolstered by Mr. Palmer's own heartfelt entreaty. Having witnessed a marked softening in his demeanor toward the sisters since the news of Marianne's unhappiness reached him, Elinor accepted the proposal with genuine delight.

However, when she revealed her decision to Marianne, her friend's reaction was immediate and sharp. "Cleveland!" she exclaimed, visibly agitated. "No, I cannot go to Cleveland." Elinor, gentle and composed, tried to soothe her, pointing out that Cleveland lay outside the vicinity of Somersetshire, away from the specific landscape that haunted Marianne's memories. But Marianne remained resolute. "It is in Somersetshire," she insisted. "I cannot step foot in Somersetshire. Where I once hoped to live... No, Elinor, you cannot ask me to do this." Elinor wisely chose not to debate the morality of suppressing such deep-seated emotions. Instead, she worked to shift Marianne's perspective, framing the move as a strategic step toward a reunion with their mother. From Cleveland, merely a day's journey from Barton, their mother's servant could easily meet them, ensuring a swift return. With only a brief stay anticipated, they could be home in barely three weeks. Knowing the depth of Marianne's love for her mother, Elinor believed this pragmatic argument would ultimately outweigh Marianne's lingering attachment to the past.

Lady Jennings, far from growing weary of her charges, pressed them earnestly to join her again after their visit to Cleveland. Though Elinor appreciated the kindness, her plans remained unchanged. Once their mother agreed, arrangements were finalized, and Marianne found solace in calculating the remaining hours until she would once again walk the grounds of Barton. Meanwhile, the social dynamics shifted elsewhere. When the Colonel first visited Lady Jennings following the decision to depart, the elderly hostess lamented greatly over the absence of the Dashwood girls. "How forlorn we shall be, Colonel," she declared, "when they are gone! We shall sit and stare at one another like two dull cats." Perhaps hoping to stir his ambition to avoid this ennui, she soon found her hope realized. As Elinor moved to inspect a print, intending to copy it for a friend, the Colonel followed, engaging her in conversation with a distinct intensity. Elinor, sensing the weight of his gaze, turned pale and ceased her work, clearly distracted by his presence. Although Lady Jennings, honoring her own decorum, sat near the pianoforte where Marianne played to avoid overhearing, she could not miss the visible agitation in Elinor's countenance or the profound attention the Colonel commanded.

Later, during a pause in Marianne's music, fragments of the Colonel's speech drifted to Lady Jennings' ear. He seemed to apologize for the modesty of his house, a gesture that confirmed his hopes. "I am afraid it cannot take place very soon," he remarked calmly later, a phrase that startled Elinor and chilled Lady Jennings. Astonished by such hesitation regarding a union, Lady Jennings marveled at the Colonel's apparent lack of urgency. Yet, as they parted, Elinor, her voice trembling with emotion, assured him, "I shall always think myself very much obliged to you." Lady Jennings interpreted this as a sign of deep affection, wondering why the Colonel departed with such composure (*sang-froid*) without further declaration.

The truth, however, was far more complex. Standing by the window, the Colonel had approached the subject with profound compassion. "I have heard," he said, "of the injustice your friend Mr. Ferrars has suffered at the hands of his family. If I understand correctly, he has been entirely disowned for his steadfast devotion to a worthy young woman. Is this true?" Elinor confirmed it. Speaking with deep feeling, the Colonel condemned the cruelty of separating two souls bound by mutual attachment, warning that Mrs. Ferrars's actions might ruin her son. He expressed his high regard for Mr. Ferrars, noting that while the young man required time to truly know, he possessed qualities worthy of admiration. Crucially, the Colonel revealed a recent development: the living of Delaford, newly vacated, was intended for Mr. Ferrars. However, he confessed a limitation. "I am sorry to say that my patronage ends here," he admitted gently. "This is but a small rectory, yielding perhaps two hundred pounds a year. It may render Mr. Ferrars comfortable as a bachelor, but it cannot support a marriage. My influence extends no further than this point. Unless fortune intervenes, his marriage remains a distant dream."

These words, delivered with apparent resignation, caused a momentary shock to Elinor. Had the Colonel been offering his hand, she would have been overwhelmed; instead, he offered a livelihood that fell woefully short of enabling Mr. Ferrars to wed. Yet, beneath this surface of restraint lay a deeper sentiment. Elinor, acutely aware of the delicacy involved, felt a surge of gratitude for the Colonel's kindness in securing *any* future for Edward, especially when she understood that accepting the role of messenger would bring personal pain to Edward if the gift came from her. Nevertheless, the Colonel's insistence that she deliver the news was firm, driven by a sense of duty and a desire to spare Edward the discomfort of receiving aid directly from a friend.

As the conversation concluded, the Colonel returned to the topic of the house, explaining its deficiencies with regret. Elinor, however, dismissed the concerns regarding size with characteristic pragmatism. "The smallness of the house," she noted, "will be inconsequential in proportion to their family and income." In doing so, she inadvertently revealed a secret conviction: she viewed Mr. Ferrars's marriage as inevitable, assuming the living would be supplemented by other means. The Colonel, unaware of this confidence, was taken aback by her certainty, reiterating that the income was meager and unlikely to sustain a household for a gentleman of Edward's standing. "His marriage must remain a distant good," he repeated, his tone heavy with foreboding. "At least, I fear it cannot take place very soon." These final words, spoken in private, struck a discordant note for Lady Jennings, who misinterpreted them as a reflection of the Colonel's own doubts about his prospects. Yet, after recounting the exchange, the nature of Elinor's gratitude became clear. It was not born of romantic passion, but of a deep respect for the Colonel's benevolence and a profound thankfulness for his efforts to secure a future for her friend, regardless of the obstacles.

"Well, Miss Dashwood," Mrs. Jennings said, her eyes twinkling with a conspiratorial warmth the moment the gentleman had departed, "I needn't ask you what the Colonel has been whispering to you. Though, upon my honor, I *did* try to stay out of earshot, I couldn't help catching enough to understand exactly what business he was conducting. And I assure you, I have never been happier in my whole life. I wish you joy of it with all my heart!"

"Thank you, madam," Elinor replied, a gentle blush warming her cheeks. "It truly is a matter of great joy to me, and I feel Colonel Brandon's kindness acutely. Not many men would have acted as he has, and so few possess a heart so compassionate! I am at a loss for words to express my astonishment."

"My dear girl, you're being dreadfully modest," Mrs. Jennings chided gently. "I'm not at all astonished. In fact, I've been convinced for some time now that nothing else could possibly happen."

"You judge by your knowledge of his benevolent nature," Elinor observed, "but surely you could not foresee that the opportunity would arrive so swiftly."

"Oh, opportunity!" Mrs. Jennings repeated, laughing. "As to that, once a man sets his heart on something like this, somehow or other, he'll find a way to seize it. Well, my dear, I wish you joy again and again. If ever there was a pair destined for happiness, I daresay I shall soon know where to look for them."

"Do you mean to go to Delaford after them?" Elinor asked, offering a faint, tentative smile.

"Ay, my dear, that I do, indeed. And as for the house being a 'bad' one, I don't know what the Colonel is talking about. It's as fine a residence as I've ever laid eyes on."

"He mentioned it being out of repair," Elinor noted.

"Well, whose fault is that? Why doesn't he repair it? Who should do it but himself?"

Their conversation was abruptly halted by the arrival of a servant announcing the carriage at the door. Mrs. Jennings prepared immediately to depart, saying, "Well, my dear, I must be off before I've had half my talk out. But we can finish it all in the evening, for we shall be quite alone. I won't ask you to come with me; I suspect your mind is too full of this delightful news to care for company, and besides, you must long to tell your sister everything."

Marianne had already slipped out of the room before the discussion had begun.

"Certainly, ma'am, I shall tell Marianne," Elinor said. "But I shall not mention it to anyone else at present."

"Oh! Very well," Mrs. Jennings said, looking slightly disappointed. "So you wouldn't have me tell Lucy? For I intend to travel as far as Holborn today."

"No, ma'am, not even Lucy, if you please. A day's delay makes no difference. Until I have written to Mr. Ferrars, I believe it ought not to be mentioned to anyone. I shall do that directly. It is vital that no time be wasted with him, considering he will naturally have many matters concerning his ordination pressing upon him."

At first, Mrs. Jennings was utterly perplexed by this statement. Why write to Mr. Ferrars in such haste? Yet, after a moment's reflection, a brilliant idea struck her, and she exclaimed, "Oh, ho! Now I understand. Mr. Ferrars is to be the groom. Well, so much the better for him. Ay, to be sure, he must be ordained in readiness. And I am so glad to find things moving along so quickly between you. But, my dear, is this really in character? Surely the Colonel should write himself? After all, he is the proper person."

Elinor did not fully grasp the start of Mrs. Jennings's speech, nor did she deem it necessary to inquire further, replying only to its conclusion. "Colonel Brandon is so delicately constituted a man that he preferred someone else to announce his intentions to Mr. Ferrars."

"And so *you* are forced to do it," Mrs. Jennings remarked, her tone shifting to mock admiration. "Well, that is a peculiar sort of delicacy! However, I shall not disturb you, seeing how intent you are on writing. You know your own affairs best. So goodbye, my dear. I haven't heard anything to please me so well since Charlotte was brought to bed."

She hurried away, but returned almost instantly. "I've just been thinking of Betty's sister, my dear. I'd be delighted to secure her such a kind mistress. But whether she'd do for a lady's maid, I'm afraid I can't say. She's an excellent housemaid and works wonders at her needle. However, you'll think of all that at your leisure."

"Certainly, ma'am," Elinor replied, barely registering her words and far more eager to be alone than to master the subject, though her thoughts were already racing ahead.

Her entire focus was now on how to begin her note to Edward, how to phrase her gratitude. The unique circumstances surrounding them rendered what would have been simple for any other pair difficult for her; yet she feared equally speaking too much or too little. She sat deliberating over her paper, pen in hand, until the door opened and Edward himself entered.

He had encountered Mrs. Jennings at the threshold as he came to leave his farewell card. She, after apologizing for not returning personally, had insisted on his entry, stating that Miss Dashwood was upstairs and wished to speak with him on very particular business.

Elinor had been congratulating herself that, however difficult it might be to express herself properly by letter, it was preferable to delivering the news verbally, when her visitor burst in, forcing her into this supreme test. Her astonishment and confusion were profound. She had not seen him since his engagement became public knowledge, meaning not since he knew she was aware of it. This realization, coupled with the weight of what she needed to tell him, made her feel acutely uncomfortable for several minutes. Edward, too, looked deeply distressed. They sat down together in a state of promising embarrassment. Whether he had initially asked her pardon for his intrusion, he could not remember; but determining to err on the side of caution, he offered a formal apology as soon as he could speak.

"Mrs. Jennings told me," he said, "that you wished to speak with me. At least, I understood her so—or I certainly should not have intruded upon you in such a manner. Though at the same time, I should have been extremely sorry to leave London without seeing you and your sister; especially as it will most likely be some time—perhaps not probable that I shall soon have the pleasure of meeting you again. I depart for Oxford tomorrow."

"You would not have gone," Elinor said, regaining her composure and determined to overcome whatever she so dreaded as soon as possible, "without receiving our good wishes, even if we had not been able to give them in person. Mrs. Jennings was quite right. I have something of consequence to inform you of, which I was on the point of communicating by letter. I am charged with a most agreeable office." She breathed slightly faster as she spoke. "Colonel Brandon, who was here only ten minutes ago, has desired me to say that, understanding you mean to take holy orders, he takes great pleasure in offering you the living of Delaford, now just vacant. He only wishes it were more valuable. Allow me to congratulate you on having so respectable and well-judging a friend, and to join in his wish that the living—it yields about two hundred a year—were much more considerable, and such as might better enable you to... to establish all your views of happiness."

Whatever Edward felt, unable to voice it himself, no one else could articulate for him. His countenance betrayed the sheer astonishment such unexpected, unimagined information must evoke. But he uttered only two words: "Colonel Brandon!"

"Yes," Elinor continued, gathering resolution as the worst was over, "Colonel Brandon offers this as a testament to his concern for your plight—the cruel situation into which the unjustifiable conduct of your family has cast you—a concern which I am sure Marianne, myself, and all your friends share. Likewise, it serves as proof of his high esteem for your general character and his particular approval of your behavior on this occasion."

"Colonel Brandon gives *me* a living! Can it be possible?"

"The unkindness of your own relations has left you so shocked by finding friendship anywhere."

"No," he replied, a sudden consciousness dawning in his eyes, "not to find it in *you*. For I cannot be ignorant that to you, to your goodness, I owe it all. I feel it—I would express it if I could—but, as you well know, I am no orator."

"You are greatly mistaken," Elinor assured him. "I do assure you that you owe it entirely, at least almost entirely, to your own merit and Colonel Brandon's discernment of it. I have had no hand in it. I did not even know, until I understood his design, that the living was vacant; nor had it ever occurred to me that he might have had such a living under his control. As a friend of mine, of my family, he may, perhaps—in truth, I know he *does*—derive still greater pleasure in bestowing it. But, upon my word, you owe nothing to my solicitation."

Edward, buoyed by gratitude toward Colonel Brandon, hastened to share his joy with Lucy. By the time he arrived at Bartlett's Buildings, his elation was so profound that when Mrs. Jennings visited the following day with congratulations, Lucy could testify to a level of spirit she had never witnessed in him before. Her own happiness was equally assured, and she joined her aunt in the fervent hope that all three would soon reside comfortably together at Delaford Parsonage before Michaelmas. Lucy showed no reluctance in acknowledging the debt owed to Elinor; she spoke of their friendship with warm gratitude, declaring that no effort Elinor might make for their sake, past or future, would ever take her by surprise. She trusted implicitly in Elinor's capacity to value those she loved. Regarding Colonel Brandon, Lucy was ready to venerate him as a saint, genuinely anxious that his stipends be maximized and resolving, in secret, to exploit every convenience his household offered—from his servants and carriage to his livestock—at Delaford.

It had been over a week since John Dashwood had visited Berkeley Street, and with no further attention paid to his wife's illness save for a single inquiry, Elinor felt compelled to pay her a visit. This duty, however, clashed with her inclinations and lacked support from her companions. Marianne not only refused to go but urged Elinor not to attempt it, while Mrs. Jennings, despite offering her carriage, harbored such intense dislike for Mrs. John Dashwood that neither curiosity regarding the recent revelations nor a desire to champion Edward could sway her. Consequently, Elinor departed alone to face the inevitable tête-à-tête with a woman who held little favor among the sisters.

At the door, Mrs. Dashwood initially refused entry, but before the carriage could turn, Mr. Dashwood emerged. Expressing delight at meeting Elinor, he claimed he had intended to call on Berkeley Street anyway and, assuring her that Fanny would be delighted to see her, invited her inside. They ascended to the drawing-room, where it was empty. "Fanny is in her own room, I suppose," Mr. Dashwood remarked, stating he would speak to her shortly, as he was certain she would have no objection to seeing Elinor. He noted that both sisters had once been favorites, wondering why Marianne hadn't accompanied her. Elinor offered a polite excuse for her sister's absence.

"I am not sorry to see you alone," Mr. Dashwood replied, "for I have a good deal to say to you." He then asked if the news of Colonel Brandon granting the living of Delaford to Edward was true. Elinor confirmed it, detailing the arrangement. The revelation sent Mr. Dashwood into a frenzy of calculation. With livings fetching high prices, he mused that the previous incumbent, had he been old and likely to resign, could have secured fourteen hundred pounds. He chided Colonel Brandon for such "improvidence" in selling the presentation, suggesting Edward would merely hold the living until the purchaser reached maturity. When Elinor firmly contradicted this theory, recounting her direct involvement in conveying the offer from Colonel Brandon to Edward, he was forced to yield to her authority.

"It is truly astonishing!" he exclaimed, struggling to comprehend Colonel Brandon's motives. "To be of use to Mr. Ferrars?" Elinor suggested simply. "Then Edward is a very lucky man," John agreed, though he quickly turned to a request for discretion. "Do not mention this to Fanny," he warned, lowering his voice. Although he had broken the news to her and she bore it well, he feared further discussion would upset her. Elinor internally scoffed at the notion that Fanny could not compose herself under the addition of wealth to her brother, noting that neither she nor her child stood to lose anything.

John lowered his voice further, treating the subject with grave importance. "Mrs. Ferrars knows nothing about it," he insisted, urging concealment until the marriage took place. Elinor questioned the logic, arguing that a mother who had so ruthlessly discarded her son and influenced others to do the same could not possibly remain affected by his fortunes. Yet John dismissed her reasoning as founded on ignorance of human nature. "Mrs. Ferrars is one of the most affectionate mothers in the world," he declared, insisting that whenever Edward's unhappiness became known, she would feel as deeply as if she had never abandoned him. Every circumstance accelerating that dread union must be hidden from her.

Shifting the conversation, John broached the topic of Robert's proposed marriage to Miss Morton. Elinor, amused by his grave demeanor, gently observed that Miss Morton likely possessed no choice in the matter, implying that it mattered little to her whether she married Edward or Robert. John protested vigorously, asserting that Robert was now effectively the heir and that both brothers were agreeable. Silence fell briefly between them before John seized Elinor's hand, whispering with an air of awe that he had received definitive information from the "best authority"—Mrs. Ferrars's daughter—that the rejected match with Edward would have caused far less vexation than the current arrangement. He relished telling her this, believing it would gratify her vanity, and hinted that Elinor's prospects with Colonel Brandon were secure.

Elinor, however, found little solace in these words. While they might have flattered her self-importance, they only agitated her nerves. Relief came swiftly with the arrival of Mr. Robert Ferrars. John, remembering to inform Fanny of her sister's presence, departed to seek her out, leaving Elinor alone with Robert. She observed him with growing disdain as he enjoyed the unfair division of his mother's love and fortune, a result of his dissipated lifestyle rather than any merit.

Within moments, Robert launched into a tirade concerning Edward. Having heard of the living, he listened with eager inquisitiveness. Upon learning the details, Robert erupted in laughter, finding the idea of Edward, a clergyman in a humble parsonage, utterly ridiculous. He visualized Edward reading prayers in a white surplice and publishing banns for the most mundane couples, deeming it absurd. Elinor, maintaining a stony silence and unyielding gravity, could not suppress a look of pure contempt fixed upon him. It was a gaze well-placed, relieving her own feelings without revealing her judgment to him. Eventually, Robert's own sensibilities recalled him from folly to a semblance of wisdom.

"We may treat it as a joke," he finally conceded, recovering from his affected laughter, "but, upon my soul, it is a most serious business. Poor Edward! He is ruined forever." He lamented Edward's lack of social grace, attributing it to his upbringing, yet professed shock at the depth of Edward's misfortune. He recounted how, upon hearing the news from his mother, he had immediately vowed never to see Edward again if he married the country girl. He described her as an awkward, unsophisticated girl without style or beauty, perfectly suited to captivate a naive youth like Edward. He confessed that he had wished to dissuade the match but was too late, now left to watch Edward "starve" and shut himself out from decent society.

Just as Robert settled this grim conclusion with composure, Mrs. John Dashwood entered, abruptly ending the conversation. Though she rarely discussed matters outside her immediate family, Elinor noticed the visible disturbance in her countenance and an artificial cordiality in her behavior toward Elinor. She feigned concern over the Dashwoods' impending departure from town, hoping to see them more often, an affectation that seemed to delight her husband, who hovered over her with adoration.

A final brief encounter on Harley Street concluded the sisters' time in town. There, Elinor accepted her brother's cheerful congratulations regarding their progress toward Barton—a journey accomplished thus far without financial strain—and learned that Colonel Brandon would accompany them to Cleveland within the next day or two. This exchange, paired with a tentative, likely futile invitation from Fanny to visit Norland, and a fervent, private promise from John to pay her a speedy visit at Delaford, constituted all future plans for rural reunion.

It struck Elinor as amusing how resolutely her circle conspired to exile her to Delaford; a destination she now dreaded above all others. To her brother and Mrs. Jennings, it appeared as her destined home, and even Lucy, upon their parting, pressed her with urgent entreaties to visit. Yet, as the date approached, the reality of leaving London, however painful, began to take shape.

Early in April, the travelers departed from their respective lodgings in Hanover Square and Berkeley Street, converging by appointment on the roads. Their journey, prolonged slightly for Charlotte and her infant, promised to span several days, with Mr. Palmer and Colonel Brandon joining them near Cleveland shortly after their arrival.

For Marianne, whose time in London had offered scant comfort yet held so many hopes for Willoughby, the prospect of departure was a torment. She lingered over memories of a house where she had last clung to illusions now shattered forever, weeping as she imagined leaving it to a man engaged in new schemes from which she was eternally excluded. Conversely, Elinor felt a clear, unburdened relief. She possessed no lingering attachments that would cause regret, no creature from whom separation would be a grief. Freed from the subtle persecution of Lucy's "friendship" and grateful to whisk her sister away unseen by the newly married Willoughby, Elinor looked forward with genuine hope to the tranquility of Barton, confident it would heal Marianne's mind and fortify her own.

The journey proceeded smoothly. By the third morning, they crossed into Somersetshire—the land of cherished memories for Marianne and prohibition for others—and drove up to Cleveland.

The estate was a spacious, modern residence perched upon a sloping lawn, lacking a park but boasting extensive pleasure grounds. A winding gravel path, bordered by a mix of firs, mountain ashes, acacias, and towering Lombardy poplars, concealed the offices from view. Marianne entered with a heart swollen with emotion, barely eighty miles from Barton and a mere thirty from Combe Magna. Before five minutes had elapsed, having helped show off the accommodations to the housekeeper, she fled again, slipping through the awakening shrubberies to climb a distant hill. From a Grecian temple atop the rise, her gaze wandered southeast, resting fondly on the farthest ridges of the horizon, imagining Combe Magna visible from these summits.

In moments of exquisite, agonizing misery, she rejoiced in being at Cleveland. As she returned, savoring the luxury of country liberty and solitary wandering, she resolved to dedicate nearly every hour of her stay to such rambling excursions. Just in time to join the others for a tour of the immediate grounds, she spent the remainder of the morning lounging around the kitchen garden, admiring the blooms, and listening to the gardener's woes about blight, or dawdling through the greenhouse where lost favorites elicited Charlotte's laughter, before inspecting the poultry yard, where the dairy-maid's disappointments regarding hens, foxes, and dwindling broods provided further amusement.

The weather remained fine and dry initially, leading Marianne to neglect calculating for a change. However, a sudden, settled rain thwarted her plans after dinner. While a cool twilight might have tempted her, the heavy downpour convinced even her that outdoor exercise was impossible. The party was small; Mrs. Palmer tended to her child, Mrs. Jennings to her carpet work, and they discussed old acquaintances and Lady Middleton's affairs, speculating on whether Mr. Palmer and Colonel Brandon would travel past Reading. Edward was free. Lucy Steele's behavior in this affair, and the prosperity it crowned, stands as a testament to how unrelenting attention to self-interest, however obstructed, secures fortune with little cost but time and conscience. What followed is known. They spent several months in great happiness at Dawlish, where she entertained numerous relations and acquaintances, and he drafted plans for magnificent cottages. Settling in town, they received generous aid from their mother-in-law, maintained close ties with the Dashwoods (despite the inevitable jealousy between Fanny and Lucy), and lived in a harmony that belied their internal domestic squabbles.

It remains difficult to discern precisely what Edward did to forfeit the rights of the eldest son, or what Robert accomplished to inherit them. Yet, the arrangement was justified by its effects. Nothing in Robert's lifestyle or speech suggested regret over the disparity in their incomes, nor did it appear that Edward envied his brother's wealth. Judging Edward by his diligent discharge of clerical duties, his deepening attachment to his wife and home, and the regular cheerfulness of his spirits, he seemed content with his lot, free from any longing for exchange.

Elinor's marriage kept her intimately connected to her family, sparing the cottage at Barton from total obscurity, as her mother and sisters spent much of their time there. To see Marianne settled at the mansion-house was the darling object of Mrs. Dashwood, Edward, and Elinor. They each felt the weight of sorrow, honored their obligations, and agreed unanimously that Marianne should be the reward for all.

With such a confederacy working in her favor, with a knowledge of Colonel Brandon's goodness so intimate, and with a conviction of his enduring, fond attachment that finally burst upon her heart long after it had been obvious to everyone else, what could Marianne do?

Marianne Dashwood was born to an extraordinary fate. She was born to discover the falseness of her own youthful opinions and to counteract, through her actions, her most beloved maxims. Born to overcome an affection formed so late in life—at seventeen—that it sprang from no deeper sentiment than strong esteem and lively friendship, she voluntarily gave her hand to another! And that other was a man who had suffered no less than herself under the shadow of a former attachment, whom two years prior she had deemed too old to marry, and who still clung to the constitutional safeguard of a flannel waistcoat.

Yet, so it was. Instead of falling a victim to an irresistible passion, as she had once fondly flattered herself, instead of remaining forever with her mother to find solace in retirement and study as her calmer judgment later dictated, she found herself at nineteen submitting to new attachments. She entered new duties, a new home, becoming a wife, the mistress of a family, and the patroness of a village.

Colonel Brandon was now as happy as those who loved him best believed he deserved to be. In Marianne, he found consolation for every past affliction; her regard and society restored his mind to animation and his spirits to cheerfulness. That Marianne found her own happiness in forming his was the shared persuasion and delight of every observing friend. Marianne could never love by halves, and her whole heart, in time, became as devoted to her husband as it had once been to Willoughby.
