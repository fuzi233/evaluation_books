After the little junketings at Grosvenor Place and Greenwich—where Major Pendennis had eaten, bowed, and smiled with a conscientiousness that amounted to labour—his friendship for the Claverings ripened with the speed of a peach in a hothouse. He called daily; he hovered; he suggested, with the air of a man casually mentioning the weather, that Lady Clavering’s proper element was precisely those drawing-rooms where a great deal of money was admired in silence and a great deal of pedigree was discussed in whispers.

Would her ladyship not grace Gaunt House? There was to be a grand entertainment. Would she not condescend to a breakfast-ball at Viscount Marrowfat’s at Fulham, where “everybody” was expected—by which the Major meant the precise set who could make “everybody” feel like nobody? There was to be a Watteau quadrille, too; and Miss Amory, in such a piece of painted pastoral, would look—so the Major vowed—like a shepherdess with an income. He offered to conduct, to escort, to fetch, to carry, to be useful; and when a man has reached the age at which usefulness becomes his chief surviving romance, he is very ardent about it.

Sir Francis Clavering, in spite of his title and his present house and his present impudent air of belonging to them, was still received by the world with that cautious politeness which is only a shade warmer than the door being shut. Suspicious rumours clung to him as kitchen-smells cling to a coat—one may air it for an hour, but the faint savour remains and every one pretends not to notice it while taking care to stand a little aside. He was blackballed at two clubs running. In the House he attached himself with a kind of fatal instinct to the very men whom decent people avoid in a street at noon. The gentleman called Mr. Hotspur—who, in his professional capacity, could be civil to a chimney-sweep if the sweep held a vote—confessed to a loathing for Sir Francis which no necessity could sweeten.

“If that man’s wanted for a division,” Hotspur said, “ten to one he’s to be found in a hell. He was educated in the Fleet, and he hasn’t heard the end of Newgate yet. He’ll muddle away the Begum’s fortune at thimble-rig, be caught picking pockets, and finish aboard the hulks.”

Now if Hotspur, with such opinions, could still smile and shake hands for votes, why should not Major Pendennis have reasons of his own for smiling and shaking hands for dinners?

“He has a very good cellar and a very good cook,” the Major remarked with the calm of a philosopher who measures virtue by the state of a sauce. “As long as he’s silent he isn’t offensive, and he very seldom speaks. If he chooses to lose his money to blacklegs, what’s that to me? Don’t look too curiously into any man’s affairs, Pen, my boy. Every fellow has some cupboard in his house he wouldn’t like you and me to peep into. Why should we try, when the rest of the house is open to us? And a devilish good house too, as you and I know. If the man isn’t all one could wish, the women are excellent. The Begum is not over-refined, but as kind a woman as ever lived—and devilish clever too. And as for little Blanche—you rogue—you know my opinion. My belief is she’s sweet upon you, and would have you for the asking. But you’re growing such a great man I suppose you won’t be content under a Duke’s daughter. Hey, sir? Ask one of ’em. Try.”

Pen, who had lately found the world very obliging to him, allowed the notion to glitter for a moment—Miss Amory, clever and bright as a knife, might indeed be disposed to renew the old, brisk flirtation that had once skimmed along the banks of the Brawl like a dragonfly above water. Yet he was not disposed to be pinned down. He said so, with that fashionable fatigue young men cultivate as they do their whiskers.

“You are very happy, sir,” he told the Major, “and you get on very well alone, and so do I. With a wife at my side, I should lose my place in society. I don’t much fancy retiring into the country with a Mrs. Pendennis; or taking my wife into lodgings to be waited on by the servant-of-all-work. The period of my little illusions is over. You cured me of my first love—who certainly was a fool, and would have had a fool for her husband, and a sulky discontented husband too. We young fellows live fast, sir; and I feel as old at five-and-twenty as many of the old—” (here he caught the Major’s eye and amended himself) “as many of the old bachelors whom I see in the bow-window at Bays’s. Don’t look offended; I only mean I’m blasé about love matters, and I could no more fan myself into a flame for Miss Amory now than I could adore Lady Mirabel over again. I rather like old Mirabel for his infatuation, and think his passion the most respectable part of his life.”

“Sir Charles Mirabel was always a theatrical man, sir,” the Major said, bristling as if Pen had spoken lightly of the Ark of the Covenant. “He acted at Carlton House when he was page to the Prince. He has been mixed up with that sort of thing. He can afford to marry whom he chooses. And Lady Mirabel is a most respectable woman, received everywhere—everywhere, mind. The Duchess of Connaught receives her. Lady Rockminster receives her. It doesn’t become young fellows to speak lightly of people in that station. There’s not a more respectable woman in England than Lady Mirabel—and the old fogies, as you call ’em, at Bays’s are some of the first gentlemen in England, of whom you youngsters had best learn a little manners, a little breeding, and a little modesty.”

The Major began to suspect that Pen was getting pert; and as the Major’s own entire system depended on everybody else being properly respectful to everybody above them, Pen’s impertinence was, in a manner, a revolutionary pamphlet.

Pen, on his part, was amused. He studied his uncle as one tastes an old wine—finding odd flavours in it, and being entertained by the solemn way it insists upon its own importance.

“I am a youngster of fifteen years’ standing, sir,” he said, “and if you think we are disrespectful, you should see those of the present generation. A protégé of yours came to breakfast with me the other day. You told me to ask him, and I did it to please you. We had a day’s sights together, dined at the club, went to the play. He said the wine at the Polyanthus was not so good as Ellis’s at Richmond, smoked Warrington’s cavendish after breakfast, and when I gave him a sovereign as a farewell token, said he had plenty of them, but would take it to show he wasn’t proud.”

“Did he?—did you ask young Clavering?” the Major cried, appeased as if a key had turned in his temper. “Fine boy—rather wild—but a fine boy. Parents like that sort of attention, and you can’t do better than pay it to our worthy friends at Grosvenor Place. So you took him to the play and tipped him? That was right, sir—that was right.” And the Major, having had his doctrine honoured, departed in peace, thinking the world not quite lost and Pen still salvageable.

Young Frank Clavering, in the meantime, grew in years, stature, and obstinacy until he was too large for a governess and too noisy for a mother’s patience. With his father he was silent and sulky, keeping out of Sir Francis’s way as if the Baronet’s very glance smelt of stale cigars and worse debts. With his mother he roared and fought whenever appetite or whim was opposed; and in disputes with his governess over his book, he kicked that mild creature’s shins so fiercely that education was obliged to retire. He attempted once or twice to treat his sister Blanche in the same manner; but Miss Amory possessed the sort of spirit that does not argue—it acts. She boxed his ears so soundly that Master Frank learned, for the first time, the meaning of a boundary.

When the family came to London Sir Francis declared, with an oath of paternal wisdom, that “the little beggar had best be sent to school.” So the young heir of Clavering was packed off to the Reverend Otto Rose’s establishment at Twickenham—one of those preparatory temples where boys are introduced to Latin by means of comfort.

We will not follow Master Clavering through scholarship; it would be like watching a fat duck waddle towards water. The path to learning, for him, was smoothed like a well-buttered muffin. He advanced towards the fane in a carriage-and-four, so to speak, and might halt for refreshment whenever he pleased. Varnished boots shone on him at an age when other boys still suffered from honest leather; cambric handkerchiefs fluttered; lemon-coloured kid gloves were drawn on hands scarcely old enough to be ashamed of ink. At Mr. Rose’s they dressed for dinner; there were shawl dressing-gowns, fires in bedrooms, occasional horse-and-carriage exercise, and oil for the hair—so that even their heads were marinated in gentility. Corporal punishment was abolished: moral discipline was to do everything; and the boys were advanced so rapidly in many branches of learning that they acquired the art of drinking spirits and smoking cigars before they were old enough to be publicly ashamed of it. Young Frank stole his father’s Havannahs and conveyed them to school, or smoked them in the stables with astonishing precocity; and at ten years old he drank champagne as sturdily as any whiskered cornet of dragoons, with the additional accomplishment of making a face like a judge passing sentence on a lemon.

When the interesting youth came home for vacations, Major Pendennis was as civil to him as to the ladies, though the boy privately held “old Wigsby” in contempt; mimicked him behind his back as the polite Major bowed and smirked; and drew rude caricatures in which the Major’s wig, nose, and tie were represented with artless ferocity. Still, the Major was untiring. He urged Pen to take notice of the boy; to invite him to chambers; to give him a dinner at the club; to take him to Madame Tussaud’s, the Tower, the play; and to tip him at the end of the day, as if sovereigns were the natural dessert to amusements.

Arthur, good-natured and fond of children, went through the ceremonies one day. The boy breakfasted in the Temple, and made the most contemptuous remarks on the furniture, the crockery, and the tattered state of Warrington’s dressing-gown—observations delivered with the serene certainty of a young gentleman who has never had to pay for a chair, a plate, or a gown. He smoked a short pipe and recounted the history of a fight between Tuffy and Long Biggings at Rose’s, to the high entertainment of his hosts, who listened as men listen to a foreign language—catching a word here and there and pretending the rest is very fine.

As the Major predicted, Lady Clavering was deeply grateful for Arthur’s attention to the boy—more grateful than the boy himself, who took attentions as he took food: as a matter of course, and with an opinion on the quality. He very likely carried more sovereigns in his pocket than poor Pen, who nevertheless gave him one out of his slender stock.

The Major’s eyes—sharp by nature and sharpened by age—watched the boy and the family with a discretion which looked like delicacy and was really calculation. Without appearing rudely curious, he contrived, as an old neighbour and family friend, to discover Lady Clavering’s means, the disposition of her capital, and what the boy was to inherit. He set to work as diligently as if fortune were a sauce requiring exact reduction; and soon possessed a tolerably accurate knowledge of the Begum’s affairs. The daughter, it appeared, would have a slender provision. The bulk of the property would go to the son. Sir Francis cared for nobody, least of all his heir; the mother was dotingly fond of the child of her later days; the sister disliked him. Such, in round numbers, was the result of Major Pendennis’s gentle enquiries.

“Ah, my dear madam,” he would say, patting the boy’s head with a hand that had patted many a circumstance into a convenient shape, “this boy may wear a baron’s coronet one day at a coronation—if matters are managed rightly, and if Sir Francis would but play his cards well.”

At this the widow Amory heaved a deep sigh. “He plays only too much of his cards, Major, I’m afraid,” she said.

The Major admitted, with sympathetic gravity, that he had heard of Sir Francis’s unfortunate propensity. He pitied her—he seemed to pity her with such sense, such seasoned tenderness, that Lady Clavering, who had long been obliged to keep her troubles shut up like preserves, found herself eager to take down the jar and show him. Major Pendennis became her confidant, her house-friend; and as a mother, a wife, and—most tender of all offices—a capitalist, she consulted him.

He let her understand, with respectful commiseration, that he knew something of her first unlucky marriage, and even of her late husband’s person—having remembered him in Calcutta, in those days when she lived in seclusion with her father. The poor lady, with tears in her eyes—tears of shame more than sorrow—told her version.

Sent back as a child to India after two years at a European school, she had met Amory and, with the heedless courage of a girl whose head had been filled with novels and whose ears were unused to men’s voices, she had married him. She spoke of the calm nights on the passage out; of his wild stories and adventures; of how beautifully he talked in the dark, when the sea made that steady whispering sound against the ship’s side that persuades young hearts they are in a poem. Then the domestic truth arrived—loud, coarse, and persistent as a street-organ beneath a sick-room window.

“Oh, you don’t know how miserable that man made me,” she said, “or what a life I passed betwixt him and my father. Before I saw him I had never seen a man except my father’s clerks and native servants. You know we didn’t go into society in India on account of—” (here she stopped, and the Major bowed as if that single pause contained a whole suppressed language) “I was a wild romantic child—my head full of books. I listened to him. Well, I married him, and I was wretched from that day—wretched with my father—whose character you know, Major Pendennis, and I won’t speak of: but he wasn’t a good man, sir—neither to my poor mother nor to me—except that he left me his money—nor to no one else that I ever heard of. And as for Amory, he was almost worse. He was a spendthrift when my father was close. He drank dreadful, and was furious when in that way. He wasn’t a good or faithful husband to me, Major Pendennis, and if he’d died in the gaol before that trial, instead of afterwards, he’d have saved me a deal of shame and unhappiness since, sir.”

Then she confessed, with a candour that made the Major’s eyebrows lift slightly as if acknowledging an inconvenient truth: perhaps she would never have married again had she not been so anxious to change the horrid name. And her second marriage—she supposed he knew—had not made her happy.

“Ah, Major Pendennis,” she said, “I’ve got money, to be sure, and I’m a lady, and people fancy I’m very happy, but I ain’t. We all have our cares and griefs and troubles. Many’s the day I sit down to one of my grand dinners with an aching heart; and many a night I lay awake on my fine bed more unhappy than the maid that makes it. I’m not happy in my husband; I’m not in my daughter. She ain’t a good girl like that dear Laura Bell at Fairoaks. She’s cost me many a tear, though you don’t see ’em; and she sneers at her mother because I haven’t had learning and that. How should I? I was brought up amongst natives till I was twelve, and went back to India when I was fourteen. Ah, Major, I should have been a good woman if I’d had a good husband.”

Her voice broke; and she added that she must go upstairs and wipe her eyes, for they were red with crying, and Lady Rockminster was coming to drive in the Park. And when Lady Rockminster did arrive, there was not a trace of tears left—only Lady Clavering’s buoyant good humour and her lively assaults upon the king’s English, which she murdered with as much spirit as if it had insulted her first.

“Begad, she is not such a bad woman,” the Major thought. “Not refined, certainly, and calls Apollo ‘Apoller;’ but she has some heart, and I like that sort of thing—and a devilish deal of money too. Three stars in India Stock to her name. Which that young cub is to have—is he?” And he reflected, with a pious wishfulness, how pleasant it would be if some of those stars might be induced to shine in Miss Blanche’s name; and better still, in Mr. Arthur Pendennis’s.

Still pursuing his schemes—whatever they might presently declare themselves to be—the old negotiator used the privilege of age and intimacy to talk kindly, fatherly, and confidentially with Miss Blanche when he found her alone. He came so often at luncheon-time, and became so domesticated among them, that the ladies did not even hesitate to quarrel in his presence; and Lady Clavering’s loud tongue and brusque temper had many a battle with the Sylphide while the family friend sat by, serene as a cat near a dairy.

Blanche’s wit seldom failed to master these skirmishes; her words went in like little skewers, neat and stinging, and drove her mother, in a rage, from the field. “I am an old fellow,” the Major would say, “and I’ve nothing to do in life. I have my eyes open. I keep good counsel. I’m the friend of both of you; and if you choose to quarrel before me, I shan’t tell any one. But you are two good people, and I intend to make it up between you. I have between lots of people—husbands and wives, fathers and sons, daughters and mammas—before this. I like it. I’ve nothing else to do.”

One day he entered Lady Clavering’s drawing-room just as her ladyship quitted it in a towering indignation and swept past him up the stairs to her own apartments. She could not speak to him now, she said; she was too angry with that—that—that little, wicked—Anger choked the rest and carried it out of hearing.

“My dear, good Miss Amory,” the Major said, advancing into the drawing-room, “I see what is happening. You and mamma have been disagreeing. Mothers and daughters disagree in the best families. It was but last week I healed up a quarrel between Lady Clapperton and Lady Claudia. Lady Lear and her eldest daughter have not spoken for fourteen years. Kinder and more worthy people I never knew—admirable to everybody but each other. They can’t live together; they oughtn’t to live together; and I wish with all my soul, my dear creature, I could see you with an establishment of your own—for there is no woman in London who could conduct one better—with your own establishment, making your own home happy.”

“I am not very happy in this one,” said the Sylphide; “and the stupidity of mamma is enough to provoke a saint.”

“Precisely so. You are not suited. Your mother committed one fault in early life—or was it Nature, my dear, in your case?—she ought not to have educated you. You ought not to have been bred to become the refined and intellectual being you are, surrounded by those who have not your genius or refinement. Your place would be to lead in the most brilliant circles, not to follow. I have watched you, Miss Amory: you are ambitious; and your proper sphere is command. You ought to shine—and you never can in this house. I hope I shall see you in another and happier one some day, and the mistress of it.”

The Sylphide shrugged her lily shoulders with scornful grace. “Where is the Prince, and where is the palace, Major Pendennis? I am ready. But there is no romance in the world now—no real affection.”

“No, indeed,” said the Major, with a face so simple and sentimental that it deserved to be framed.

“Not that I know anything about it,” Blanche added, casting down her eyes, “except what I have read in novels.”

“Of course not,” the Major cried. “How should you, my dear young lady? And novels ain’t true, as you remark admirably, and there is no romance left in the world. Begad, I wish I was a young fellow like my nephew.”

“And what,” continued Miss Amory, musing, “what are the men we see at balls every night—dancing guardsmen, penniless Treasury clerks—boobies! If I had my brother’s fortune, I might have such an establishment as you promise me. But with my name and my little means, what am I to look to? A country parson; or a barrister in a street near Russell Square; or a captain in a dragoon regiment who will take lodgings for me, and come home from mess tipsy and smelling of smoke like Sir Francis Clavering. That is how we girls are destined to end life. O Major Pendennis, I am sick of London, and of balls, and of young dandies with their chin-tips, and of insolent great ladies who know us one day and cut us the next—and of the world altogether. I should like to leave it and go into a convent. I shall never find anybody to understand me. And I live here as much alone in my family and in the world as if I were in a cell locked up for ever. I wish there were Sisters of Charity here, and that I could be one and catch the plague and die of it. I wish to quit the world. I’m not very old; but I am tired—I have suffered so much—I’ve been so disillusionated—I’m weary, I’m weary—O that the Angel of Death would come and beckon me away!”

Translated into plain domestic prose, this meant: a few nights earlier, Lady Flamingo had cut Miss Amory and Lady Clavering; Miss Blanche had not been invited to Lady Drum’s ball; it was the end of the season and nobody had proposed; she had made no sensation at all—she, who was cleverer than any girl of the year. Dora with five thousand pounds, Flora with nothing, and Leonora with red hair were to be married, and nobody had come for Blanche.

“You judge wisely about the world and your position, my dear Miss Blanche,” the Major said. “Princes don’t marry nowadays, as you say—unless the Princess has a doosid deal of money in the funds, or is a lady of his own rank. The young folks of the great families marry into the great families; if they haven’t fortune, they have each other’s shoulders to push on in the world, which is pretty nearly as good. A girl with your fortune can scarcely hope for a great match—but a girl with your genius, your admirable tact, and fine manners, with a clever husband by her side, may make any place for herself. We are grown doosid republican. Talent ranks with birth and wealth now, begad; and a clever man with a clever wife may take any place they please.”
