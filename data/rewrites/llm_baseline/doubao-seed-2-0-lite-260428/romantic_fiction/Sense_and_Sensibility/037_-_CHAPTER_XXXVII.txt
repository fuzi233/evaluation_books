Two full weeks had barely slipped by before Charlotte Palmer was well enough that her mother, Mrs. Jennings, no longer felt the need to spend every waking hour at her son-in-law’s house. Content to drop by once or twice a day to coo over the new baby and fuss over Charlotte’s rest, she returned at last to her own home on Berkeley Street and her familiar, comfortable routines – much to the quiet delight of Elinor and Marianne Dashwood, who were glad to slip back into their easy, shared days with her.

It was on the third or fourth morning after this quiet return to normal that Mrs. Jennings came trundling back from her daily visit to the Palmers, her bonnet askew, her cheeks flushed with breathless excitement, and an air of bustling importance that could only mean she’d stumbled on the most extraordinary piece of gossip Elinor had ever heard. She barely paused to hang her pelisse by the door before she cried out, “Lord, my dear Miss Dashwood! Have you heard the news at all?”

“No ma’am,” Elinor said, looking up from her embroidery. “What has happened?”

“It’s so strange I can scarce credit it myself! Wait till I tell you every last bit. When I got to Mr. Palmer’s today, Charlotte was in a perfect fuss over the baby – certain he was deathly ill, he’d cried for an hour straight, and was covered head to toe in tiny pimples. I looked at him straight away and said ‘Lord, my dear, that’s nothing in the world but red gum,’ and the nurse said the very same. But Charlotte wouldn’t be satisfied, so they sent for Mr. Donavan the apothecary. Luckily he was just stopping by from Harley Street, so he popped right in, took one look at the baby, and confirmed we were all right – no harm done at all. The minute he was turning to leave, though, I don’t know what possessed me, but I asked him if there was any other news about. He smirked, and simpered, and suddenly looked all serious, like he was holding a great secret, and at last he leaned in and whispered, ‘Lest any unpleasant report about your young ladies’ acquaintance reach you unawares, I thought it best to say there is no great cause for alarm – Mrs. Dashwood is expected to do perfectly well.’”

“What?” Elinor sat bolt upright. “Is Fanny Dashwood ill?”

“That’s exactly what I said!” Mrs. Jennings exclaimed. “‘Lord, is Mrs. Dashwood ill?’ I cried, and that’s how it all came pouring out. The long and short of it is this: that Mr. Edward Ferrars, the young man I used to tease you about so often – and thank heavens nothing ever came of that, now it turns out – has been secretly engaged to my cousin Lucy for more than a twelvemonth! There now! Can you believe it? No one on earth knew a syllable of it but her sister Nancy! It’s not so odd they fell for each other, but to carry on for a whole year without a single soul suspecting? That’s the strangest part of all. I never once caught them together, else I’d have spotted it in a second.

They kept it all quiet, you see, for fear of Edward’s mother Mrs. Ferrars, who’s set on him making a grand, wealthy match. Not a word of it reached your brother or sister, not a hint, until this very morning. Poor Nancy, bless her, she’s good-hearted but as daft as a brush, and she thought to herself ‘Everyone is so fond of Lucy, they’ll never object to the match,’ so she trots straight over to Fanny, who was sitting alone at her carpet-work, not suspecting a thing – why, only five minutes before, she’d been telling your brother she planned to marry Edward off to some lord’s daughter or other, I forget her name. You can imagine what a blow it was to her pride! She fell into violent hysterics at once, screaming loud enough for your brother to hear all the way down in his dressing room, where he was planning a letter to his steward. He flew up the stairs, and what a terrible scene followed. Lucy turned up at the house that very minute, all unsuspecting, and Fanny flew at her like a fury, scolding her so harshly the poor girl fainted dead away. Nancy dropped to her knees sobbing, your brother paced the room in a panic, Fanny swore they’d not stay another minute under her roof, and your brother had to go down on his own knees to beg her let the girls stay long enough to pack their trunks. Then Fanny had another fit of hysterics, he was so frightened he sent for Mr. Donavan, and that’s when he walked in on a whole house in an uproar. The carriage was already at the door to take my poor cousins away, and they were just stepping in when he left – he says Lucy was so distraught she could barely walk, and Nancy was little better. I declare I have no patience for Fanny Dashwood! I hope with all my heart the two of them marry in spite of all her fuss. Lord, how wretched poor Edward will be when he hears of this! To have the woman he loves treated so scornfully! Everyone says he’s monstrous fond of her, and Mr. Donavan agrees. He’s gone back to Harley Street now to be on hand when they break the news to Mrs. Ferrars, who was sent for the second the girls left the house. She’ll no doubt have hysterics too, and I don’t pity her one bit. I can’t stand all this nonsense about money and grand connections. There’s no reason at all Edward and Lucy shouldn’t marry! Mrs. Ferrars can easily afford to settle enough on him, and Lucy, bless her, knows better than anyone how to stretch a penny. I swear, if she gave them five hundred a year, Lucy would make them look as smart as any couple on eight hundred a year. They could live so snug in a little cottage just like yours in Barton, maybe a touch bigger, with two maids and two menservants – why, I can even get them a housemaid, my Betty’s sister is out of work and she’d be perfect for them.”

When Mrs. Jennings finally paused for breath, Elinor had had time to steady her racing heart. She was relieved to see no one suspected the sharp, quiet ache under her skin – Mrs. Jennings had long stopped imagining Elinor was attached to Edward, and best of all, Marianne was out on a walk, so there was no need to hide her distress in front of her sister just yet. She answered calmly, as impartial as she could be, commenting on the absurdity of the scene the apothecary had described, while her mind whirled. She could barely tell what she truly expected of the situation, save that it would almost certainly end with Edward keeping his promise and marrying Lucy. She felt deep compassion for Edward, very little for Lucy even when she tried to summon it, and not an ounce of sympathy for Fanny or Mrs. Ferrars.

She knew immediately she must tell Marianne the truth at once, before anyone else brought the subject up to her unawares. It was a painful, heavy task: she would have to shatter what she knew Marianne still believed, that Edward might somehow one day be free for her, and risk making Marianne relive all the sharp agony of Willoughby’s betrayal, thinking the two men’s situations were the same. But there was no putting it off, so she went to find her sister at once.

She spoke gently, plainly, no dramatic flourishes, no dwelling on her own pain – only mentioning the months of quiet self-command she had practiced since Lucy first told her the secret, as an example Marianne might follow. But Marianne broke down the second she understood, crying so hard she could barely breathe, horrified that Elinor had carried this secret for four whole months, all while she was tending to Marianne through the worst of her grief for Willoughby, while Marianne had even snapped at her for seeming calm and happy.

“Four months?” Marianne gasped, clutching her hands. “You knew all that time? And you never said a word? While I moaned and wailed and made a whole household fuss over my broken heart, you carried this all alone?”

“I promised Lucy I would not tell a soul,” Elinor said softly. “I could not break that trust. And I could not bear to make you and Mama unhappy for no reason, when there was nothing any of you could do to help.”

Marianne was utterly humbled. She begged Elinor’s forgiveness, sobbing for being so selfish, so wrapped up in her own sorrows she never saw her sister was suffering too. She promised Elinor anything she asked – never to speak of the engagement with bitterness, never to snub Lucy when they met, to greet Edward with perfect kindness if chance ever brought them face to face, no matter how sharp the pain was. And she kept that promise beautifully: when Mrs. Jennings chattered on and on about the news for the rest of the afternoon, Marianne sat perfectly still, her face steady, agreeing politely every time she was spoken to, even when the older woman gushed about how fond Edward was of Lucy, and the pain in her throat felt like it might choke her.

The next morning brought a new trial. Their brother John Dashwood arrived, his face grave and solemn, as if the world had nearly ended.

“I suppose you have all heard,” he said, sitting down carefully, “of the shocking discovery that took place under our roof yesterday.”

They all nodded, silent.

“Your poor sister Fanny has suffered dreadfully,” he went on. “And Mrs. Ferrars too – it has been a scene of most complicated distress. But I am hopeful we may all weather the storm. Poor Fanny was in hysterics all day yesterday, but Mr. Donavan says there is no real danger, her constitution is strong. She says she shall never think well of anyone again, and who can blame her, after showing those Steele girls nothing but kindness and hospitality, to be rewarded with such secret deception? She swears now she wishes she had invited you two to stay with us instead of them.”

He paused to accept their quiet thanks, then continued. “Mrs. Ferrars was even more distraught when Fanny broke the news to her. She had been planning such an excellent, eligible match for Edward, settling him comfortably with Miss Morton, who has thirty thousand pounds of her own – the thought that he was secretly engaged to a girl with no fortune at all never once crossed her mind. She sent for Edward at once, and I was there to help reason with him. We told him if he would only break the engagement and marry Miss Morton, his mother would settle the Norfolk estate on him, clear of all land tax, bringing in a full thousand a year – and she offered to raise it to twelve hundred a year, when he would not bend. When he still refused, she swore she would give him nothing at all but the two thousand pounds he already had of his own, she would never see him again, she would block every possible path to advancement in any profession he chose to enter.”

Marianne gasped, clapping her hands together in indignation. “Gracious God! Can such cruelty be real?”

“Your surprise is most natural, Marianne,” her brother said, nodding. “Who could imagine such stubbornness, to turn down such generosity?”

Marianne bit her lip, remembering her promise, and held her tongue.

“All of our arguments did nothing,” John continued. “Edward said very little, but what he said was unshakable. He would not break his engagement, no matter the cost.”

“Why, then he’s acted like a perfectly honest man!” Mrs. Jennings blurted out, unable to stay quiet a moment longer. “Begging your pardon, Mr. Dashwood, but if he’d done any different, I’d think him a right scoundrel. Lucy is my cousin, and there’s not a sweeter, more deserving girl walking this earth.”

John Dashwood was surprised, but too polite and too keen to keep on the good side of a woman with Mrs. Jennings’s fortune to take offense. “I would never speak ill of any relation of yours, ma’am. Lucy Steele is no doubt a very deserving young woman, but this connection is simply impossible. We all wish her very happy. Mrs. Ferrars’s behavior, in this situation, is only what any conscientious, wealthy mother would do. Edward has chosen his own path, and I fear it will be a very hard one.”

“Where has he gone, the poor young man?” Mrs. Jennings asked.

“I am sorry to say, there was a complete, permanent rupture between him and his mother. He left her house yesterday, and we do not know where he has gone in town, nor would we dream of making inquiries. Only two thousand pounds to live on – imagine how dreadful that prospect is, for a man born to expect thousands a year! If not for his own folly, he would have had two thousand five hundred a year within three months, married to Miss Morton. We all feel for him, but there is nothing we can do to help.”

“Poor dear boy!” Mrs. Jennings cried. “He is more than welcome to a bed and every meal at my house, any time he likes. He has no business wasting his money on tavern dinners and rented lodgings.”

Elinor’s heart ached so sharply for Edward she could barely breathe, and she smiled faintly at Mrs. Jennings’s kindness, grateful for it even as John Dashwood went on.

“His mother has already decided to settle that Norfolk estate on his younger brother Robert, instead of Edward,” he said. “I left her this morning with her lawyer, sorting out the paperwork. Can there be anything more galling for a man, than to see his own younger brother given the property that should have been his, all because he chose to be stubborn? Poor Edward. I truly feel for him.”
