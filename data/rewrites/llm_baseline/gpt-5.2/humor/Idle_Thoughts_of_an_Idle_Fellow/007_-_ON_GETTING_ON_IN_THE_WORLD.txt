Getting on in the world is not, you might think, a subject for an idle fellow—one whose proper business is to sit somewhere green and unnecessary, and let other people bustle. Yet idleness has its own advantages. It gives a man time to listen.

I have a little place of my own, a sort of lean-to paradise at the edge of things: a patched arbor by the wayside, trellised with hops and whatever else will climb if you coax it with twine. From there I can look out upon the great high-road of Life as upon a market-street on Saturday—only with fewer apples and more desperation. I keep a kettle murmuring on a spirit-lamp, and for company I have a small pan in which onions are always meant to brown and never quite get there, because something is forever happening that I must lean forward to watch.

The procession never stops. The road does not merely carry people; it mills them, like grain between stones. Day and night you hear it—the quick, nervous tramp of innumerable feet, a sound like dried peas flung hard upon a tin tray. Some run, some walk, some drag a foot as if it has been borrowed and they intend to return it worn; but all hurry, all strain, all pant toward a horizon that retreats with the slyness of a waiter who has seen your hand go to your pocket.

If you listen closely—and an idle man can afford close listening—you catch separate lives in the noise, as you pick out flavours in a stew. Here goes an old man, his breath coming in little whistles through his teeth, like a kettle that has forgotten how to boil properly. There a timid girl is driven on by a matron with a face sharp enough to cut bread without a knife; the matron’s skirts snap like sailcloth, and her voice has the crack of a butcher’s cleaver on bone. Here is a studious youth, reading “How to Get On in the World,” and letting everyone pass him, because he is staring at the print as though the secret were hidden between the commas. He stumbles over ruts and other people’s heels without ever looking up—like a cook who follows a recipe so faithfully that he never notices the kitchen is on fire.

There is a bored-looking man, his hat at the correct angle of indifference, while a fashionably dressed woman jogs his elbow as if he were a perambulator and she the baby. There is a boy gazing back at a sunny village he will not see again; his longing makes a hollow in the road behind him, the way a ladle leaves a little crater in porridge. A broad-shouldered fellow strides with the calm rhythm of a man chopping wood: thud, thud—he will get there because his legs believe in it. And then, sidling as a rat at a larder door, a thin-faced stooping man shuffles and dodges, never quite in a straight line, yet always convinced he has found a shorter way.

One fellow keeps his eyes fixed on the ground, weaving from side to side as if the future were a coin he has dropped and he’s afraid someone will stand on it. A youth with a noble face hesitates, looking from the distant goal to the mud at his feet, as though he cannot decide whether to step over it or to write an ode about it. And then comes a fair girl whose dainty face wrinkles a little more with each step, as fresh pastry dries if left too long by the oven door; and after her a careworn man, and after him a hopeful lad, still smelling faintly of morning.

It is a motley throng, and there is no keeping them sorted, any more than you can persuade a pantry to remain tidy when there are children. Prince and beggar elbow one another. Sinner and saint jostle shoulder to shoulder. Butcher and baker and candlestick-maker—ploughboys and sailors—counsel in wig and gown, and the old clothes-man under his dingy crown of hat, soldier in scarlet and undertaker’s mute in streaming hat-band and worn cotton gloves. The musty scholar fumbles his faded leaves; the scented actor swings his seals and looks as if he has been dusted with perfumed flour.

A politician goes by, crying legislative panaceas in a voice practised for echo; a Cheap-Jack follows, holding up bottles and promises, each guaranteed to cure human ills and, failing that, to remove stains. There is the sleek capitalist, and the sinewy labourer; the man of science and the shoe-back; the poet and the water-rate collector; cabinet minister and ballet-dancer. A red-nosed publican roars the praises of his vats; a temperance lecturer lectures at fifty pounds a night with a zeal as well-paid as it is loud. Judge and swindler, priest and gambler. A jewelled duchess smiles like iced cake; a thin lodging-house keeper looks as if she has spent her life trying to make mutton behave; and behind them a wabbling, strutting thing, tawdry in paint and finery, a shop-window advertisement escaped into the street.

Cheek by cheek they struggle onward—screaming, cursing, praying, laughing, singing, moaning—making a racket that would turn milk sour. Their speed never slackens. There is no wayside rest, no halt by cool fountains, no pause beneath green shades. On through heat and crowd and dust—on, or be trampled down and lost. On with throbbing brain and tottering limbs, until a man’s heart grows sick and his eyes blur, and he makes a little gurgling groan that says to those behind: “Close up; there is room now.”

And yet, though it looks like misery and sounds like a factory at full tantrum, who can stand apart from it? Who watches the commotion and does not feel the old instinct to be in it—not merely to look on, but to put a shoulder in and try the weight of life for oneself?

Certainly not I. I began, you see, with talk of an arbor and a pipe of contentment and lotus-leaves of indolence, as though I were a philosopher in a dressing-gown. They are excellent metaphors, but they do not fit my figure. When there is anything like a row in the street, I am not the kind of man to stay indoors and practise resignation. I am rather like that Irishman who, seeing a crowd collecting, sent his little girl to ask whether there was going to be a fight—“because, if so, father would like to be in it.”

I love the fierce strife. I like to watch it. I like to hear of people getting on in it—battling their way bravely and fairly—that is, not slipping through by luck or trickery, as one might steal a pie cooling on a sill and call it enterprise. There is something in the struggle that stirs one’s old fighting blood, the same blood that thrilled, in schoolboy days, to tales of knights who fought against fearful odds. And fearful odds there are still, only the giants now wear respectable coats, and the dragons have learnt to keep accounts. The golden casket is not so easy to win as the story-books pretend.

In those pleasant romances Algernon takes one long last look at the ancestral hall, wipes a tear from his eye with a lace handkerchief, and goes away—returning three years later rolling in riches. Nobody tells you precisely what he did in the three years. That is a pity, because it would be the most exciting part; the rest is only furniture. Novelists linger for a dozen pages over a tea-party—how the cups were handed and what Miss So-and-So said to Mrs. This-and-That—yet they toss off a lifetime with: “He had become one of our merchant princes,” or, “He was now a great artist, with the world at his feet.” As though a man were put into an oven raw at chapter three and drawn out at chapter ten done to perfection, without any basting in between.

Why, there is more real life in one of Gilbert’s patter-songs than in half the biographical novels ever written. Gilbert will tell you step by step how an office-boy rose to be the ruler of the Queen’s navee; and how a briefless barrister became a great and good judge, ready to try a breach of promise of marriage. That is what one longs for: the petty details, the secret seasonings, the moments when the mixture refuses to thicken and you have to improvise. Show us the under-current of an ambitious man’s career—his struggles and failures, his hopes, his small victories, his splendid disappointments. It would sell like hot bread.

For wooing Fortune is quite as interesting as wooing any flesh-and-blood maiden, and in practice astonishingly similar. Fortune, as the ancients painted her, is very like a woman—not quite so unreasonable and inconsistent, but nearly so; and the pursuit obeys much the same ridiculous laws. Ben Jonson puts both into a couplet:

“Court a mistress, she denies you;
Let her alone, she will court you.”

A woman never thoroughly cares for her lover until he has ceased to care for her; and it is not until you have snapped your fingers in Fortune’s face and turned on your heel that she begins to smile upon you. But by that time you do not much care whether she smiles or frowns. Everything comes too late in this world. Good people say it is quite right and proper that it should be so, and that it proves ambition is wicked. Bosh! Good people are wrong. (They always are, in my opinion; we never agree on any point, not even about the weather.)

What would the world do without ambitious people? It would be as flabby as a Norfolk dumpling—wholesome perhaps, but with no spring in it. Ambitious people are the yeast; they are the leaven that raises the lump into bread. Without them the world would never get up at all. They are the busybodies who rise early, hammering, shouting, clattering the fire-irons, and making it generally impossible for the rest of the house to remain in bed. Wrong to be ambitious, forsooth! Wrong are the men who, with bent back and sweating brow, cut the smooth road over which humanity marches forward from generation to generation! Wrong for using the talents their Master entrusted to them—for toiling while others play!

Of course they are seeking reward. Man is not given that godlike unselfishness that thinks only of others’ good; he wants his own dinner as well as the neighbour’s. But in working for themselves they work for all. We are bound together like a net of onion-string: tug one end and every knot feels it. Each blow a man strikes in his own behalf helps to mould the universe. The stream, struggling onward, turns the mill-wheel; the coral insect, fashioning its tiny cell, joins continents; and the ambitious man, building a pedestal for himself, leaves a monument to posterity.

Alexander and Caesar fought for their own ends, but in doing so they put a belt of civilisation half round the earth. Stephenson invented the steam-engine to win a fortune; Shakespeare wrote his plays to keep a comfortable home for Mrs. Shakespeare and the little Shakespeares—no doubt with a decent supper on the table and a fire that did not sulk. Great things often begin as plain domestic needs: a roof, a meal, the wish to hear one’s own language spoken kindly at the hearth instead of shouted down by the iron noises outside.

Contented, unambitious people are all very well in their way. They form a neat, useful background for great portraits to be painted against; they make a respectable audience—if not particularly intelligent—for the active spirits of the age to perform before. I have no word against contented people so long as they keep quiet. But do not let them go strutting about, as they love to do, crying that they are the true models for the whole species. Why, they are the deadheads, the drones in the hive, the street crowds lounging and gaping at those who are working. And let them not imagine either—as they are also fond of doing—that they are very wise and philosophical, and that it is a very artful thing to be contented.

It may be true that “a contented mind is happy anywhere,” but so is a Jerusalem pony, and the consequence is that both are put anywhere and treated anyhow. “Oh, you needn’t bother about him,” people say; “he is very contented as he is, and it would be a pity to disturb him.” So your contented party is passed over and the discontented man gets the place, because he makes enough noise to be heard above the general din. If you are foolish enough to be contented, do not show it. Grumble with the rest. If you can do with a little, ask for a great deal; because if you don’t, you won’t get any.

In this world you must adopt the principle of a plaintiff in an action for damages: demand ten times more than you are ready to accept. If you could be satisfied with a hundred, begin by insisting on a thousand. If you start by suggesting a hundred, you will get ten—and someone will tell you to be grateful for the generosity of the age.

It was by not following this simple plan that poor Jean Jacques Rousseau came to grief. He fixed the summit of his earthly bliss at living in an orchard with an amiable woman and a cow, and he never attained even that. He got as far as the orchard, but the woman was not amiable, and she brought her mother with her, and there was no cow. Now if he had made up his mind to demand a large country estate, a houseful of angels, and a cattle-show, he might have lived to possess his kitchen garden and one head of live-stock—and possibly, by some rare accident, come across that rara avis: a really amiable woman.

And what a terribly dull affair life must be for contented people! How heavy the time must hang upon their hands, and what on earth do they occupy their thoughts with, supposing they have any? Reading the paper and smoking seems to be the intellectual food of most of them, to which the more energetic add playing the flute and talking about the next-door neighbour’s affairs. Their days are like cold potatoes: sustaining, perhaps, but not exciting.

They never know the sharp hunger of expectation, nor the stern delight of accomplished effort—the particular satisfaction of a thing at last done, as when a sauce that has sulked and separated all evening finally comes together with a triumphant gloss. To the ambitious man life is a brilliant game—a game that calls forth tact and energy and nerve, to be won in the long run by the quick eye and the steady hand, and yet having enough chance in it to keep the whole business piquant. He exults in it as the strong swimmer in heaving billows, the athlete in the wrestle, the soldier in battle. And if he is defeated he wins the grim joy of fighting; if he loses the race, he has at least had a run. Better to work and fail than to sleep one’s life away.

So—walk up, walk up, walk up! Walk up, ladies and gentlemen! Walk up, boys and girls! Show your skill and try your strength; brave your luck and prove your pluck. Walk up! The show is never closed, and the game is always going. The only genuine sport in all the fair, gentlemen—highly respectable and strictly moral—patronised by the nobility, clergy, and gentry. Established in the year one, gentlemen, and flourishing ever since—walk up!

Walk up, ladies and gentlemen, and take a hand. There are prizes for all and all can play. There is gold for the man and fame for the boy; rank for the maiden and pleasure for the fool. So walk up, ladies and gentlemen—walk up! All prizes and no blanks; for some few win, and as to the rest—why:

“The rapture of pursuing
Is the prize the vanquished gain.”
