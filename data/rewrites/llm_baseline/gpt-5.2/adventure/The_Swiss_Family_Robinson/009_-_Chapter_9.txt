Spring did not arrive on our island with ceremony. It came first as a hush: the wind that had worried our roof-branches fell still, the clouds tore open like wet cloth, and sunlight poured through in broad, honest beams. The rain stopped as if someone had shut a door. We stepped out of our winter refuge blinking, half-dazed, as though released from captivity; and what restored us most quickly was not only the green—new, tender verdure everywhere—but the sound. After months of drumming water and groaning trees, the world had changed its music. The air was full of small bright notes: birds calling in layers, one answer stitching to another, until the whole valley became a living chorale. Even the dogs paused, ears pricked, tasting the new season with their hearing.

It was impossible not to feel how much our minds depended on that gentler soundscape. The storm had been a kind of tyranny, loud enough to flatten thought; spring, by contrast, let each modest noise find its proper place—the flick of a leaf, the click of a beak, the soft scrape of our boots in damp sand. I wondered, as I listened, how people in crowded cities endured the ceaseless metallic clatter of wheels and hammers, and how quickly such noise must drive out the older, quieter memory of how a morning ought to sound.

Our plantations had taken heart. The seed we had put into the moist earth was already pushing up, green points pricking through the dark soil. Nature looked washed and newly varnished. But our own nest—our poor winter lodging—was the first thing that demanded attention. The wind had worried it, the rain had filled it with leaves, and the whole place looked like a bird’s home after a quarrel. We repaired it with haste, and within a few days it was again fit to shelter us.

Then my wife, who never let the needs of the table and the household drift far behind any adventure, begged that I would set her to work with the flax without delay. She spoke of linen as other people speak of treasure. Cloth, for her, was not merely a covering; it was comfort preserved, labour remembered, a kind of domestic history you could fold and store.

So I built a drying-oven as early as possible, and prepared it for her use. With more trouble than I expected, I manufactured a beetle-reel and a spinning-wheel. Soon she and Franz were absorbed in the sweet, steady labour—she drawing out the fibres with patient fingers, turning them into thread, and Franz, proud of his importance, reeling off the slender line as it came. The soft whirr of the wheel joined the spring birdsong, a homely counter-melody that made our camp feel less like a temporary accident and more like a place where years might gather.

Yet my mind kept turning toward Tentholm. We had stores there—our hard-won riches from the wreck—and the winter storms had been too violent for comfort. I feared damage. Fritz and I therefore set out to examine our outpost.

What we found made Falconhurst look almost untouched by comparison. The tent was blown flat, its canvas torn into rags; provisions were soaked through; and worst of all, two casks of powder were ruined beyond recovery. We spread everything we still hoped to save out in the sun, laying cloth, rope, and whatever would dry on stones and sand. The pinnace, thank Heaven, was safe; but our faithful tub-boat lay dashed to pieces like a broken shell. The sight of that wrecked little craft, which had served us so loyally, struck me harder than I expected. It made plain what we could not afford: another winter in fragile quarters.

On the way home Fritz proposed a bold remedy. We should hollow a cave in the rock, he said—a fortress for the rainy season. The difficulties seemed nearly insurmountable, and yet the thought took hold of me at once. If we could not hew a room, we might at least create a cellar: a cool, safe place for what was most precious and perishable.

A few days later we left Falconhurst with the cart loaded—spades, hammers, chisels, pickaxes, crowbars—our whole noisy arsenal of labour. On the smooth perpendicular face of the rock I drew, with chalk, the dimensions of the entrance we intended, and we began to batter away.

Six days of hard, incessant toil made scarcely any impression. I think Master Knips himself could hardly have squeezed into the hole we had gained. But we would not surrender. By the tenth day, as our blows kept time—iron on stone, stone answering with dull protest—we were rewarded. The rock changed. What had been stubborn became more yielding; the tool bit more deeply, and hope returned with every shower of grit.

It was then that Jack, who had been working with a hammer and crowbar, suddenly shouted with such triumph that the sound rang off the cliff like a bell.

“Gone, father! Fritz—my bar has gone through the mountain!”

“Run round and fetch it,” Fritz called, laughing. “Perhaps it’s dropped into Europe. Don’t lose a good crowbar.”

But Jack was not jesting; his eyes were bright with certainty. “It is through! I heard it crash down inside. Come—do come and see!”

We sprang to him. I thrust the handle of my hammer into the opening. It met no resistance. I could turn it as if in empty air. Fritz handed me a long pole; I tried the depth and felt nothing. A thin wall, then—only a thin wall between us and a vast hollow.

Joy made the boys’ arms redouble their strength. They battered, and piece by piece the rock gave way until the hole would admit us. I stepped near the aperture to look within—and instantly a rush of foul air struck my face. The world swam; my stomach lurched; I clutched the rock and cried out for the boys to stand back.

When I had recovered, I explained the danger. A cavern long sealed holds air that has been stagnant—vitiated—deadly to breathe. “Unless air is renewed,” I told them, “it becomes a trap. Fire may help awaken it.” We threw handfuls of blazing hay into the opening, hoping flame would purify and stir the air; but each bundle went out at once, snuffed as if pressed between wet palms.

I saw we needed something more forceful. I sent them to fetch a chest of signal-rockets from the wreck. Soon we were firing them—dozens of fiery serpents hissing into the dark. They whizzed and vanished at what seemed an immense distance. Some curved and spun, casting sudden light across a roof that glittered like a magician’s hall. In those brief illuminations the cavern looked alive with jewels, and the rockets themselves seemed like bright, wrathful dragons driving a malignant spirit from a beautiful palace.

We waited a little. Then I threw in lighted hay again. This time it burned clearly. The cave, so long sealed, could be entered.

While Fritz and I enlarged the opening, Jack sprang on his buffalo and thundered away toward Falconhurst to carry the astonishing news. The bridge timbers soon resounded again beneath Storm’s heavy gallop, and before long the rest of our family came up in the cart, faces eager, voices tumbling over one another.

Jack had packed every candle he could find. We lit them, took our arms—for prudence, even in wonder—and entered. I led, sounding the ground with a long pole, unwilling that a hidden chasm should swallow any of us in our first steps.

Silently we advanced. Even the dogs moved as if they feared to disturb something sacred. The cave widened around us into a world of glimmering crystal. The candles threw gold onto the walls; points of light multiplied and trembled like stars seen through water. Great pillars rose from the sandy floor like trees in a petrified forest; high above, branches turned to stone drooped in stalactites, each one catching flame-colour—red, green, violet—so that the air itself seemed strewn with rainbows.

The floor was hard, dry sand—so dry that I knew at once we might live here without dread of damp. I broke off a fragment from the glittering wall and put it to my tongue. The taste was unmistakable.

Rock-salt.

An unlimited supply of pure salt—no longer a rationed treasure but a gift spread before us like a tablecloth. My wife’s eyes shone at that discovery, for she understood immediately what it meant: meat preserved, fish cured, winter made less hungry; and with salt, even the simplest broth becomes more than warm water—it becomes comfort with a name.

Only one thing troubled my delight. Here and there lay large crystals that had fallen from the roof. If more should drop, the palace might become a death-trap. I examined the fallen masses and saw that the breaks were fresh. The rockets’ concussion, I concluded, had shaken them loose. To make sure nothing else hung ready to fall, we discharged our guns from the entrance and watched.

Nothing more came down. The roof held firm. Our magnificent shelter was safe.

We returned to Falconhurst full of plans. Now nothing was discussed but the new house—how it should be arranged, how we should live in it, what work might be done there, what stores kept there. Falconhurst, which had seemed so secure at first, shrank in our imagination. It would serve, we decided, as a summer residence; the cave should be our winter house and, if need arose, our impregnable castle.

Work began at once. We needed light and air, so we hewed a row of windows in the rock and fitted into them the window-cases we had saved from the officers’ cabins. We brought the door from Falconhurst and fixed it into the entrance. The opening in the trunk of the tree, by which we had first approached the rock face, I resolved to conceal with bark, so that wild beasts—or worse, human strangers—might be less likely to notice the place in our absence.

Inside, we divided the cave into four parts. In front was a large compartment where the door opened; this we subdivided into sitting, eating, and sleeping spaces. On the right we set our kitchen and workshop; on the left, the stables. Farther back, in the darker recesses, we placed our storehouse and powder magazine. Having endured one rainy season already, we planned with the memory of discomfort fresh upon our skin. We would not be smoke-dried again. We built a proper fireplace and chimney. We arranged the stable more sensibly. We left ample space in the workshop for the kind of labour that makes a household independent.

It was during our frequent journeys to Tentholm that we discovered unforeseen advantages. The shore there became a kind of generous market, arriving without coin and without advertisement. Great turtles came ashore to lay their eggs in the sand, and their rich flesh gave us many a sumptuous meal. When more than one appeared, we cut off their retreat to the sea and turned them on their backs, fastening them to a stake near the water’s edge with a cord through a hole in the shell. Kept thus, they remained in good condition for weeks, so we had fresh turtle whenever we wished. Lobsters, crabs, and mussels abounded as well, their shells clicking softly in the shallows like castanets when the tide moved them.

Then one morning, as we approached Tentholm, the sea itself performed a spectacle. Far out, the surface heaved and boiled as if something vast were stirring beneath. The morning sun struck the disturbed water, and flashes leapt across it like fire. Above the shimmering patch hovered hundreds of birds, screaming, plunging, rising again in harsh, frantic chorus. The whole scene was a moving storm of sound and light—a wild orchestra chasing its own feast.

The shining mass rolled on toward our bay, the birds following as if tethered by hunger. We hurried down.

As we neared, the truth shaped itself clearly. “A shoal of herrings,” I said.

At once questions flew at me. What made such a bank? Why did it move so? How could so many fish be gathered into one living body?

I explained as best I could: that a herring-bank is an immense multitude swimming together, extending over a great area and many fathoms deep; that ravenous fish follow and devour them from below, while birds strike from above; and that, pressed between enemies, the shoal makes for shallow water where the great fish cannot follow. “But there,” I told them, “they meet a third enemy—man. They may escape tooth and talon, but ingenuity is harder to flee. And yet, for all the millions caught, the roe of only a small number could replenish the sea again.”

Our fishery began at once. Jack and Fritz waded in with baskets and baled out fish as one bales water, hurling them up to us on shore. We worked with the brisk rhythm of necessity: gutting, rinsing, packing. And there, in the midst of the noise—birds still screaming, surf murmuring—we made our own domestic music: knives tapping on stone, salt pouring like dry rain, barrel staves creaking as we pressed the layers down.

We salted them properly: first a layer of salt, then herrings, then salt again, until the casks were full. The sharp clean scent of the salt rose into our faces; the fish gave off their cold sea-smell, bright and metallic. We closed the barrels carefully and rolled them into the cool vault at the back of our cave.

Good fortune did not stop there. A day after the herring shoal left, seals appeared, drawn by the refuse we had thrown into the sea. I doubted whether their flesh would suit our table, but we secured a score or two for their skins and fat. We drew off the skins with care for harness and clothing; we boiled down the fat for oil, which we stored in casks for tanning, soap-making, and lamps. Thus one harvest fed another: fish gave us seals, and seals promised light when the long evenings returned.

These labours delayed our work at Rock House for a time, but as soon as we could we returned to it with renewed vigour. I had noticed that beneath the salt crystals lay a kind of gypsum, which I knew could serve us as plaster. I broke off pieces, heated them strongly, reduced them to powder, and mixed it with water. The plaster came out smooth and white. With no better use at the moment, I plastered over some of the herring casks to make doubly sure that air was excluded; the remainder I left unsealed, intending to preserve their contents by smoking.

For the smoking we built a small hut of reeds and branches. We strung the herrings on lines across the roof, and on the floor we lit a great fire of brushwood and moss. Dense smoke rose and curled around the fish, wrapping them in a fragrant fog. Over several days the flesh darkened and tightened; when we handled it, it felt firm and well-cured, the smell rich and savoury, promising meals that would taste of summer even in bad weather.

About a month after the herrings, other shoals visited us. Jack saw them first and shouted that young whales were off the coast. We ran down and found the bay alive—not with whales, but with great fish: sturgeon, salmon, trout, all making for the mouth of Jackal River to ascend it and deposit their spawn among the stones. The river itself seemed to hum with their movement, water thick with muscular backs and quick fins. The birds’ cries returned, the surf kept its steady bass, and beneath it all the fish made a hidden rushing sound like wind through reeds.

Jack was in raptures. “Here are proper fish!” he cried. “None of your paltry fry. How do you preserve these—potted, salted, or smoked?”

“Not so fast,” I said. “First tell me how to catch them, and then I will tell you how to cook them.”

“Oh, I’ll catch them fast enough,” he answered, and darted off to Rock House.

He returned with his fishing apparatus: a bow and arrow and a ball of twine. He had fastened a barbed spike to the arrow-head and secured the arrow to the string. Armed with this, he crept to the river’s edge and took aim.

The arrow flew—and struck one of the largest fish in the side.

“Help, father, help!” Jack shouted at once, as the fish surged away, dragging arrow and line. “Help! Or he’ll pull me in!”

I ran to him. Together we held the cord, bracing our feet in the sand as the creature fought. The fish lashed water into foam, tugging with astonishing strength, but we did not let go. Slowly its struggle weakened. At last, exhausted and bleeding, it allowed us to draw it ashore.

It was a noble prize. Fritz and Ernest arrived just as we finished and looked at Jack with envy sharp enough to season a stew. Not to be outdone, they rushed off for weapons. Soon we were all engaged—Fritz with a harpoon, Ernest with rod and line, and I, like some improvised Neptune, with an iron trident that was in truth nothing more heroic than a pitchfork. Monster after monster came to land until the shore was strewn with shining bodies, their scales catching sunlight like coins.

Then Fritz harpooned a sturgeon eight feet long—so heavy we could not drag it ashore. We pulled together, straining until our arms shook, but the beast held stubbornly in the shallows.

“The buffalo!” my wife proposed at once—practical as ever, her mind always finding the hinge that would move the door.

Jack ran for Storm. We harnessed the buffalo to the harpoon rope, and soon the monstrous fish lay panting on the sand.

When we had taken as many fish as we could possibly use, we set about cleaning and preparing them. Here my wife became the true commander of the day. Knife-work, washing, sorting—each task had its order, its economy. Some fish we salted; some we dried as we had dried the herrings; some we treated like Mediterranean tunny, preserving them in oil, sealing flavour and richness into casks like a promise.

From the sturgeon roe I decided to make caviare. I removed the membranes, washed the roe in vinegar, salted it, pressed out the moisture drawn by the salt, and packed it into small barrels for our storehouse. In that moment the cave felt less like a mere shelter and more like an archive of tastes—foods carrying names from far countries, lodged now in our island life. I thought how easily such names disappear when no one speaks them, how a dish can vanish from memory simply because the word for it no longer passes from mouth to mouth. Here, at least, we kept the word alive with the work of our hands.

I also knew that the sturgeon’s bladder yields the best isinglass. Carefully collecting the air-bladders, I washed them and hung them to stiffen. Then I peeled off the outer membrane, cut the rest into strips—staples—placed them in an iron pot over the fire, and boiled them to the proper consistency. I strained the glue through clean cloth and spread it thin on a stone slab to dry. The transparent substance that resulted promised to serve as a substitute for glass in our window-frames—another small victory of necessity over lack.

In this climate, our kitchen garden demanded scarcely any attention. Seeds sprang up and flourished as if time itself had become generous. Peas, beans, wheat, barley, rye, and Indian corn seemed always on the verge of ripening; cucumbers, melons, and other vegetables grew luxuriantly. The success of our patch at Tentholm encouraged me to hope my experiment at Falconhurst had prospered too, and one morning we went to see.

As we passed the field from which we had dug the potatoes, we found it covered with barley, wheat, rye, and peas in profusion. I stopped, astonished.

“Where has this fine crop sprung from?” I asked.

“From the earth,” my wife replied, laughing—yet with the quiet pride of one whose labour often appears as if by magic because it is done without fuss. “Franz and I sowed the seed I brought from the wreck. The ground was already tilled by you and the boys. All we did was scatter.”

Delighted, I hurried to the maize plantation. It had grown well—and it was not unappreciated. A tremendous flock of feathered thieves rose as we approached. Among them Fritz saw a few ruffed grouse. Quick as thought he unhooded his eagle, sent it in chase, and sprang onto his onager to follow at a gallop. The noble bird marked the finest grouse, soared above it, and then stooped, striking it to the ground.

Fritz was close enough to spring through the bushes and save the grouse from death. He hooded the eagle again and returned triumphantly, prey in hand.

Jack, meanwhile, had not been idle. He slipped his pet Fangs among the quails still feeding in the field; and to my surprise the jackal secured a dozen, bringing them faithfully to his master’s feet.

We turned toward Falconhurst, hungry with honest labour. There my wife refreshed us with a drink so delicious and strange it deserves remembrance: she crushed the stems of young Indian corn, strained out the sweet green juice, and mixed it with water and the juice of sugar-cane. It tasted of spring itself—fresh, grassy sweetness, with a deeper honeyed note from the cane. While we drank, the world outside hummed gently, and the birds continued their bright argument in the trees.

The next day we prepared for an excursion. I wished to establish a sort of semi-civilized farm at some distance from Falconhurst, where we might place animals that had become too numerous for our limited supply of fodder. In the large cart—harnessed to the buffalo, the cow, and the ass—we loaded a dozen fowls, four young pigs, two couple of sheep, as many goats, and also a pair of hens with one cock grouse. Fritz led on his onager, and we forced a new track through woods and tall grasses toward Cape Disappointment.

At last we emerged onto a large plain covered with curious little bushes. Their branches and the ground around them were strewn with pure white flakes.

“Snow! Snow!” cried Franz. “Oh, mother, come down and play snowballs! This is jolly—much better than the ugly rain!”

His mistake was understandable; the flakes did resemble snow. Before I could speak, Fritz declared it must be a kind of dwarf cotton-tree. We approached and found he was right. Soft fine wool lay in pods still clinging to the bushes and scattered everywhere on the ground. We had discovered cotton. My wife’s delight was like a sudden warmth—another domestic future made possible. She gathered a great quantity into three capacious bags, and we resumed our journey.

Crossing the cotton-field, we climbed a pretty wooded hill. From its summit the view opened magnificently: luxuriant grass ran down the slope, dotted with shady trees; a sparkling brook gushed and sang as it descended; below lay rich green forest, and beyond it the sea, steady and shining. The place had everything—pasture, water, shade, shelter. It seemed made for our new farm, as if some careful elder had chosen it long ago and left it waiting.

We pitched our tent and built our fireplace. My wife set about preparing our repast—her hands always turning what the island offered into something that tasted like home—while Fritz and I chose a site for a shed. We found a group of trees so placed that their trunks could serve as posts. We carried tools there, then returned to supper and afterward lay down on beds my wife had prepared with the newly gathered cotton—softness after months of rough straw and damp leaves, as if the island itself had finally learned our needs.

The trees formed, by happy chance, a regular rectilinear figure, with the longest side facing the sea. I cut deep mortices in the trunks about ten feet from the ground, and again ten feet higher to form a second storey. Into these I set beams, creating the framework. For a roof I laid laths and overlaid them with bark stripped from a neighbouring tree, fastening it with acacia thorns. It would shed rain like a well-made cloak.

As I cleared scraps of bark and rubbish for fuel, a peculiar smell rose—clean, resinous, and bright, as though the air itself had been seasoned. I stooped and picked up pieces. To my surprise, some were from the terebinth tree, and the rest from the American fir. The goats, snuffling and routing through the same heap, made an important discovery of their own: pieces of cinnamon, fragrant and warm, a spice that seemed almost too civilised for our wild life.

I showed the boys what we had found. “From the fir,” I said, “we may obtain turpentine and tar. So we can prepare pitch for our yacht with tar and oil—and cart-grease with tar and fat. As for the terebinth, a gum issues from incisions in its bark, hardens in the sun, and becomes as transparent as amber. Burned, it gives a most delicious perfume; dissolved in spirits of wine, it forms a clear varnish.”

Thus, at the end of a day of building and finding, of hauling beasts and gathering cotton, the island offered us not only shelter and food, but scent—cinnamon on our fingers, resin in the air—like a reminder that even in exile, a household can keep its old arts alive: to cure, to preserve, to season; to listen for the right sounds; and to give names, carefully, to what sustains us.
