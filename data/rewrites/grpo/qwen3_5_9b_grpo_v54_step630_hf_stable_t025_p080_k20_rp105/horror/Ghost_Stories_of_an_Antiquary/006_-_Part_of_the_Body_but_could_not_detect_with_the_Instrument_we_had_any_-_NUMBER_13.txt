Viborg stands as a jewel among the towns of Jutland, a place where history clings to the cobblestones with a grip that feels almost supernatural. It is the seat of a bishopric, crowned by a cathedral that is handsome yet startlingly new, surrounded by a garden of deceptive charm and a lake that mirrors the sky with unsettling clarity. Storks wheel overhead, their cries echoing the ancient murders committed nearby; just across the water lies Hald, a picturesque haunt, and closer still, Finderup, the site where Marsk Stig slaughtered King Erik Glipping in 1286. When Erik's tomb was finally breached in the seventeenth century, fifty-six blows of iron maces were found scored into his skull, a grim testament to violence that seems to linger in the very air. Yet, I am not here to write a guidebook for tourists seeking the macabre.

My cousin, Mr. Anderson, arrived in Viborg not as a tourist, but as a scholar driven by a peculiar obsession with the Church history of Denmark. He sought the Rigsarkiv, hoping to unearth papers preserved from the great fire of 1726 that detailed the final, agonizing days of Roman Catholicism in the land. For his lodging, he chose the Golden Lion, an inn that survived the inferno which consumed much of the town's older architecture. It is a formidable red-brick structure, its façade imposing under the setting sun, though the courtyard behind reveals a jarring mix of black-and-white wood and plaster that speaks of a decayed grandeur.

Anderson was delighted by the old-fashioned atmosphere, promising himself a stay that would be both satisfactory and amusing. His mission was academic, yet his mind was already drifting toward the uncanny. He requested a room large enough to serve as both bedroom and study. The landlord, a man named Herr Kristensen, suggested he inspect the available quarters. The top floor was dismissed due to the climb, and the second offered no suitable dimensions. On the first floor, however, lay a choice of rooms.

The landlord favored Number 17, but Anderson rejected it for its lack of light, facing only a blank wall. He preferred Number 12 or 14, which overlooked the street. Number 12 was selected. It was a long, high room with three windows on one side, devoid of a fireplace but warmed by a handsome, ancient cast-iron stove depicting Abraham sacrificing Isaac, inscribed with "1 Bog Mose, Cap. 22." The only other decoration was a faded colored print of the town from around 1820.

As supper approached, Anderson examined the hotel's guest list, a large blackboard divided into columns. It was a mundane roster: an advocate, some German travelers, and baggage men from Copenhagen. The only anomaly was the conspicuous absence of Number 13. Anderson, a man of method, noted this omission with a frown. He had encountered this superstition often in Danish hotels, where the thirteenth room was simply skipped. He wondered if the taboo was so potent that no landlord dared to rent it, or if it was merely a polite fiction.

That evening, after unpacking and arranging his papers, Anderson decided to read before bed. He retrieved a book from his great-coat hanging near the dining room. The journey back to his room was short, yet when he reached the corridor, he found his door locked. A frantic movement echoed from within. Confused, he checked the number on the door he had just opened: it was 13. His actual room, Number 12, was to the left. He had been in bed for minutes, reading and extinguishing his candle, before the realization struck him: the blackboard listed no Number 13, yet the room physically existed. He felt a strange pang of regret, wishing he had claimed it, perhaps to prove to the landlord that a well-born Englishman could dwell there without ill fortune. He dismissed the thought, assuming it was a servant's quarters, and drifted into sleep, unaware that the room's nature was far more sinister than mere superstition.

The following day, Anderson immersed himself in the archives. The documents were a treasure trove, particularly correspondence regarding Bishop Jörgen Friis, the last Catholic bishop of the diocese. The letters revealed a scandalous tenant in the Bishop's house, a man named Mag Nicolas Francken, accused of practicing dark arts and selling his soul to the enemy. The Protestant leaders, led by Rasmus Nielsen, condemned Francken as a viper, while the Bishop defended his court's authority. Anderson noted the Bishop's boldness in demanding proof of Francken's crimes, yet he found no record of Francken's fate. A subsequent letter from the Bishop mentioned that Francken had been "suddenly removed," implying death, yet the circumstances remained a mystery.

Leaving the archives, Anderson walked with Herr Scavenius, the archivist, who confessed that the location of the Bishop's house was a puzzle, as the maps from 1560 were incomplete. Later, returning to the Golden Lion, Anderson's curiosity about Number 13 resurfaced. He paused before the door marked 13, hearing footsteps and a sharp, hissing breath inside. He retreated to his room, only to find it seemed smaller than before. His portmanteau, left by the porter, was gone, vanished as if by magic. He dismissed the idea of theft, attributing it to the clumsiness of the *stuepige*, the local maids.

That night, looking out his window, Anderson observed the shadows of his neighbors. Across the street, a figure in Number 13 leaned on the sill, illuminated by a flickering red lamp. The figure was tall and thin, draped in dark fabric. Suddenly, a distant step caused the figure to vanish instantly, the red light extinguished. Anderson, smoking a cigarette, placed the butt on the sill and went to bed.

Morning brought a rude awakening. The maid laughed at his demand to know where his portmanteau was, leaving him bewildered when he found it back in its original spot. Then, another shock: his cigarette butt was found on the sill of the middle window, not the right-hand one where he had smoked. He rushed to check the numbers again. He passed Number 14, then Number 12. There was no Number 13. Three errors in twelve hours drove a methodical man to the brink of madness. He decided to observe further, convinced he was witnessing a trick of the mind or a hallucination born of fatigue.

At supper, Anderson confronted the landlord about the missing number. Kristensen laughed it off, explaining that travelers simply refused to sleep in Number 13, fearing bad luck. He insisted the room did not exist. Anderson, however, remained uneasy. That evening, he invited the landlord to his room to smoke, hoping to verify the layout. As they sat, the lawyer in Number 14 began to dance wildly in his room, his shadow flailing against the opposite wall. Soon, a high, thin, and terrifying voice began to sing from the room next to the lawyer's—Number 13. The song was disjointed, a despairing moan that sent shivers down their spines.

The lawyer, Herr Jensen, burst into the room, furious and confused, claiming the noise was coming from his neighbor. Anderson and the landlord realized the horror: the singing was emanating from the non-existent Number 13. They armed themselves with sticks and umbrellas and marched down the corridor. The door to Number 13 was locked. Jensen pushed, but it held firm. Desperate, they called for the strongest servants. When the door finally gave way, it did not open into a room. Instead, the plaster wall of the corridor stared back, scarred by the crowbar's blow. Number 13 had ceased to exist.

In the silence that followed, broken only by a rooster's crow, the group stood frozen before the blank wall. The landlord, terrified, offered them a different room. The next morning, they returned to investigate. Servants, acting as carpenters, tore up the floorboards beneath the vanished room. They found no skeleton, no body of Mag Nicolas Francken. Instead, they uncovered a small copper box containing a vellum document with twenty lines of writing.

Anderson and Jensen, a paleographer, struggled to decipher the script. It was neither Latin nor Old Danish, but something ancient and alien. Anderson, shaken by the experience, refused to draw conclusions, though he knew the implications were dire. He recounted the tale to me months later in a wood near Upsala, his face grim. He spoke of Daniel Salthenius, a student who sold his soul to Satan, and scoffed at the boy's ignorance of the company he kept. Anderson would not elaborate on the document found in the copper box, leaving the mystery of Number 13 and the fate of Mag Nicolas Francken to haunt the silence between us.
