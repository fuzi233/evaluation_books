I can’t cite the exact source, but I’m 99% sure it was Shakespeare (or if not, some equally brainy, top-tier wordsmith) who pointed out that the exact second a chap is feeling chuffed to the gills, all warm and fuzzy about how life’s treating him, Fate creeps up behind him and whacks him over the skull with a length of lead plumbing pipe. The man was dead right, obviously. It’s always exactly that way for me. Take the whole daffy mess with Lady Malvern and her son Wilmot, for example. Two minutes before they turned up unannounced on my doorstep, I was literally patting myself on the back for how perfectly everything was going.

It was one of those heavenly spring mornings, I’d just clambered out of an ice-cold shower, and felt spry as a two-year-old racehorse. Even better: the night before, I’d absolutely asserted my authority over Jeeves. Properly put my foot down, showed him who the actual master of the flat was. Don’t get me wrong, I don’t usually mind the bloke having opinions—his suit judgment is so sound it’s basically national treasure level—but he’d been leaning on me lately like I was a doormat with a pulse. I’d almost mutinied when he tried to talk me out of my beloved cloth-topped boots, which I adored like a pair of long-lost brothers. Then when he tried to ban my new hat? That was the last straw. He wanted me to wear that stuffy Longacre model John Drew sported, but I’d set my whole heart on the dashing Country Gentleman style some famous stage chap rocked. After a rather heated, awkward scene, I bought the Country Gentleman, and strode around the rest of the evening feeling positively manly and independent.

So there I was, in the bathroom, rubbing my spine raw with a rough towel, humming a bit of a music hall number, and trying to guess what Jeeves had laid out for breakfast, when there was a tap at the door. I cut the singing off mid-note and cracked the door an inch.
“What ho out there?”
“Lady Malvern is waiting to see you in the sitting-room, sir,” Jeeves said.
“Eh? Pull yourself together, man,” I said, severely, because I don’t abide practical jokes before I’ve had my first sip of tea. “It’s barely ten o’clock. No one’s in my sitting-room. Who even is Lady Malvern?”
“Her ladyship mentioned she disembarked from an ocean liner at dawn this morning, sir.”

That tracks, I thought. I’d landed in New York a year prior, and they’d booted me off the boat before 8 a.m., bleary-eyed and still half-convinced I’d dreamed the last three days of crossing. I pulled on my dressing gown and told Jeeves to dig out my heather-mixture lounge suit, and while I dressed, I finally placed the name.
“Got her, Jeeves! She’s a crony of my Aunt Agatha. Met her at a Sunday lunch in London once. Total terror. Writes big heavy books. Did one on social conditions in India right after she went to the Durbar.”
“Indeed, sir. Pardon me, sir—but not that tie.”
“Eh? What’s wrong with this tie? It’s a cheerful pink! Perfectly nice!”
“Too ornate for the heather mixture lounge, sir. Unsuitable.”

The air went thick. This was a crisis. If I caved now, every bit of the authority I’d won the night before would be tossed straight in the bin. I set my jaw, knotted the pink tie right there in front of him, and said “This is the tie I’m wearing, Jeeves.”
“Very good, sir.”
You could practically see the man’s feelings hurt. But I was firm. I trotted into the sitting-room, ready to face the day.

“Ah! Mr. Wooster! You’ve never met my son Wilmot, I think? Motty darling, this is Mr. Wooster.”
Lady Malvern was a hearty, loud, overpowering tank of a woman—she wasn’t very tall, but she stretched nearly six feet wide, and swallowed my biggest armchair like it was a petit four. She had bright bulging eyes, a haystack of yellow hair, and a grin that showed about 57 front teeth. The minute she turned that grin on me, I felt like a ten-year-old kid dragged into the drawing room in scratchy Sunday clothes to greet a stranger. Definitely not the sort of surprise you want on an empty stomach.
Motty, the son, was 23, tall, thin, and so meek he looked like he’d apologize to a doorknob if he bumped into it. Same yellow hair as his mum, plastered flat and parted dead center, bulging dull grey eyes with pink rims, a chin that gave up the fight halfway down his face, and zero eyelashes. He was sucking the knob of his walking stick like it was a lollipop, staring at the wall.
“Awfully glad to see you!” I said, through gritted teeth. “Staying long in New York?”
“About a month. Your Aunt Agatha gave me your address, told me you’d be delighted to help us out.”
I blinked, and felt a flicker of relief. A year prior, Aunt Agatha had shipped me off to New York to rescue Cousin Gussie from a music hall actress, and I’d somehow messed it up so thoroughly that Gussie married her and joined the vaudeville circuit. I’d been convinced I was permanently banned from England, that Aunt Agatha would be waiting on the quay for me with a stuffed eelskin to whack me over the head. Her recommending her pals drop by was a great sign, right?
“Absolutely, anything I can do!” I said.
“Wonderful! I want you to put dear Motty up for a little while.”
“Put him up for my clubs? But he looks more of a home bird—”
“No no! I don’t want him joining clubs. I mean let him live here, in your flat, while I’m away.”
The words oozed out of her like honey, like she had no idea she’d just dropped a life sentence on me. I stared at Motty, who was still gnawing his stick, and was just about to tell her no dice, lady, call a hotel, when she steamrolled right over me. She was off on a cross-country tour to visit every prison on the route, to write a new companion book to her India tome, all about American social conditions. She couldn’t take Motty, because he got terribly train sick, and he was a vegetarian teetotaller who loved nothing more than curling up with a nice book. She’d be back in a month to pick him up. Then she grabbed Motty by the elbow, dragged him off to catch her midday train, and said she’d send him back to my flat that afternoon.

The second the door shut, I yelled for Jeeves.
“Jeeves! That woman just dumped that walking-stick-sucking excrescence on me for a whole month! What are we going to do?”
“Lord Pershore will be residing here this evening, sir. Breakfast is ready.”
He was still pouting about the tie! I could’ve sobbed into my bacon and eggs. I almost caved right then, told him he could burn the tie and the Country Gentleman hat for all I cared, but my Wooster pride wouldn’t let me. If Aunt Agatha got wind I’d kicked her pal’s son out, I was a dead man. There was nothing for it but to grin and bear it.

By midday, Motty’s luggage turned up, plus a massive parcel of “nice educational books” his mum had packed for him. I brightened up a bit—surely that stack of tomes would keep him quiet for the whole month. I stuck my Country Gentleman hat on my head, gave my pink tie a jaunty tweak, and headed out to lunch with the lads at the pub down the street. We browsed and sluiced and bantered till late, and I waltzed home that night having completely forgotten about Motty.
The parcel of books was still unopened, string and all, sitting on the coffee table. Rummy, I thought, till I heard a scrabbling thud outside my front door.
“Jeeves, go see what that is,” I said.
He came back two seconds later. “If you would be so kind as to help me carry him in, sir? His lordship is lying on the doormat.”
Sure enough, there was Motty, huddled on the mat, moaning softly.
“Great Scot—he’s not drunk, is he?” I yelped.
Jeeves nodded. Turns out the second his mum had boarded that train, Motty had hotfooted it to the nearest bar, drank the entire stock of whiskey they had, ate three steaks (no vegetarian in sight), and had the time of his life till he stumbled home and passed out on my doormat. We hauled him to bed, and I sat up staring at the fire, already sensing the next four weeks were going to be absolute chaos.

Next morning, I walked into his room expecting to find a wreck, and there he was, sitting up in bed, flipping through a silly adventure story, as chipper as a sparrow.
“What ho!” he yelled at me, and we went back and forth going what ho about seven times before I could get a word in edgewise.
“How’s the head?” I asked.
“Top-hole!” he said. “Your man Jeeves made me a magical dark drink, cured my headache in two seconds. Absolute genius, that bloke. And by the way—no more of that vegetarian teetotal rubbish. I drank too much last night, and I’m going to do it every night! This is the first time I’ve ever been allowed out of the house alone. I’ve spent my whole life cooped up in Much Middlefold, Shropshire, where the most exciting thing that happens is a choirboy gets caught sucking chocolate during the sermon. I’ve got four weeks in New York, and I’m going to make every single second count. Mum told me to keep my eyes open for impressions for her book, right?”

I tried sticking close to him for one night, to act as a responsible chaperone. Never again. The bloke is a force of nature. We went out for supper, and before I knew it, he was throwing soft-boiled eggs at the electric fan, dancing on top of the table, yelling so loud we got booted out by three different waiters. I barely escaped alive at 2 a.m., and swore off ever tagging along with him again. The next week, I kept running into him getting tossed out of every dive bar in the neighborhood, flying through the air towards the sidewalk like a human cannonball. I started losing weight, jumping at every sudden noise, waiting up every night to drag him in and tuck him into bed. And Jeeves? Still moping. Still refused to give me a single idea to get out of the mess, all because of that stupid hat and pink tie.

Then the cherry on top of the chaos sundae: Motty won a bull terrier named Rollo in a raffle, tied him to the sitting room table, and didn’t tell anyone. I got home one night, and Rollo lunged straight for my trouser leg before I could even flick the light on. I screamed, fell backwards into the hall, and Jeeves strolled in, calmed the beast down instantly with that weird Jeeves magic of his, till Rollo was rolling over on his back begging for belly rubs. But the second he spotted me again, he was back to trying to chew through my trousers.
I was at my wits’ end. I’d made a terrible mistake, letting Jeeves get that cross over a hat and a tie. But I had no idea that far funnier, far more unexpected chaos was still right around the corner.
