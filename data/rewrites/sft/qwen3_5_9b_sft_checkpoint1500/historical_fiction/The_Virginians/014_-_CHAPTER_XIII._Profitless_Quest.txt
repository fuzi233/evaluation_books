With every mile Harry Warrington traveled north toward Pennsylvania, the tales of the British rout grew louder, confirmed by a chorus of shaken voices. Two legendary regiments, veterans of Scotland and the Continent, had dissolved before an enemy that seemed to vanish into the mist. Their iron discipline, their famed valor, had crumbled against a handful of "savages" and scattered French infantry. The General, Braddock, had fought with a courage that bordered on the suicidal; four times his horse fell beneath him, twice he bled from wounds, and finally, a mortal blow ended his life three days later.

Those who spoke to Harry painted a harrowing scene: the crossing of the swollen river, the long, weary march through the wilderness, the futile frontal assault, and the artillery failing to clear the path. Then, the ambush. Fire erupted from behind every bush and tree, a murderous fusillade that tore the expedition apart, cutting down half the force before they could even regroup. Yet, Harry learned, not everyone perished. An aide-de-camp, a Virginia gentleman, lay at Dunbar's camp, ravaged by fever and exhaustion.

"But which one?" Harry asked, rushing toward the encampment, his heart pounding. "Is it George?"

It was not George. Stretched in a canvas tent, pale and burning with fever, was George Washington. The sight of Harry brought a sharp pain to Washington's brow, deeper than the malady itself. He shook his head, unable to offer news of his friend. He could not speak the full truth. For three days following the battle, his duty had bound him to the General. On that fatal ninth of July, he had seen George stride forward with orders from the chief, never to return. Only after Braddock's death had the aide managed to retrace his steps to the battlefield. There, amidst the horrors of the aftermath, the bodies lay stripped and mutilated. Washington had buried what he believed to be George's corpse, a task that had deepened his sickness with the agonizing weight of uncertainty.

"Ach, George!" Harry cried, his voice breaking. "If you truly loved him, you would find him, dead or living!"

Nothing satisfied the boy but the physical act of searching. With money borrowed, he hired guides and crossed the river at the point where the army had passed. He traversed the dreadful field, no longer haunted by the redcoats but teeming with vultures feasting on mangled flesh. It was here Harry saw the face of Death for the first time—not in the calm repose of his grandfather, sleeping with a sweet smile, but in the grotesque reality of rotting bodies. The horror of the mutilation turned his stomach; he could glean no sign of his brother from the silent woods or the festering remains.

Driven by desperation, Harry considered approaching the French fort alone, bearing a white flag. But his guides refused to accompany him past the river. "Keep your hair for your lady mother, my young gentleman," the guide warned grimly. "'Tis enough that she loses one son in this campaign." The warning hung heavy in the air: the French might show mercy, but the Indians would not.

When Harry returned to Dunbar's encampment, the fever claimed him. Delirium seized the boy, and he lay for days in a tent, then on a bed from which Washington had just risen convalescent. For several days, he drifted in and out of consciousness, unaware of his watchers. Poor Dempster, who had nursed him through previous illnesses, feared the worst—that the widow would lose both her children. Yet, slowly, the fever abated. The boy rallied, mounted his horse, and prepared to journey home. Both Mr. Washington and Dempster escorted him back to Castlewood.

Their arrival was met with a servant ahead to announce their presence. First came Mrs. Mountain and her small daughter, Fanny. They welcomed Harry with tears and embraces, but Mrs. Mountain offered not a nod to Mr. Washington. Little Fanny, however, caused the young officer to turn deadly pale. She approached him, hands clasped behind her back, and asked, "Why have you not brought George back too?"

Harry, overwhelmed by his sister-in-law's sobs and the warm caresses of his nurse, failed to hear the child's question. But as the carriage settled, the tension in the air became unbearable. Dempster was treated with warmth, but the atmosphere shifted when the topic of food arose. Mr. Washington, having ridden hard and counting on the hospitality of Castlewood, politely declined a meal, stating he needed only water for his horse and a drink for himself before departing.

"My mother won't see him, child," Mrs. Mountain whispered urgently to Harry. "She left this room when the Colonel's servant announced your coming. She said she felt she could not see Mr. Washington."

"Not see George? Why, he is like a son of the house!" Harry protested, confused. "He gave me his bed when I was ill!"

"She had best not see him," Mrs. Mountain insisted, her composure cracking. "I don't meddle in family matters, but... I cannot look upon him. Will you go to her?"

Harry took his friend's arm, apologized to the Colonel, and ascended the stairs. He moved quickly, averting his gaze from the door of his brother's empty room, which he dreaded to open. Instead, he found Madam Esmond waiting for him. She pulled him into a crushing embrace, leading him into the room where George had lain. A settee sat by the bed, and a book of Psalms lay open on the covers. Everything remained exactly as it had been when George departed.

"My poor child! How thin you've grown," she sobbed, smoothing his hair. "Never mind. A mother's care will heal you. 'Twas nobly done to seek your brother through sickness and danger. If others had been as faithful, he might be here now." Her voice rose, filled with a fierce, almost manic conviction. "Our hero will return! I know it! He is not dead. One so good, so brave, so gentle—he cannot be lost to us entirely."

When Harry pressed her for the basis of her faith, she revealed the source of her torment and hope: she had seen her father in dreams for two nights running. In visions, he had told her that her boy was a prisoner among the Indians.

Madam Esmond's grief, unlike Harry's, had not paralyzed her. It had animated her, lending her eyes a frantic eagerness and her countenance an air of righteous anger. Harry marveled at her transformation. When he urged her to descend and welcome George Washington, who had accompanied him, her excitement spiked dangerously.

"I should shudder at touching his hand!" she declared, recoiling as if from poison. "He took my son from me! I cannot sleep under the same roof with him!"

"He gave me his bed when I was ill, Mother!" Harry pleaded, bewildered. "If George lives, how does George Washington bear responsibility for his death? Please God, let it be only as you say."

"If your brother returns, it will not be through Mr. Washington's aid," she snapped. "He did not defend him on the field, nor did he bring him out."

"But he tended me!" Harry interjected, defending his friend. "He was still ill himself when he yielded his bed. Any other man would have thought only of self."

"A friend!" Madam Esmond sneered, the word dripping with venom. "Of all His Excellency's aides-de-camp, mine is the only one who returns unwounded. The brave and noble fall, but *he* survives! I entrusted my boy to him, the pride of my life, whom he would defend with his own blood—and he leaves my George in the forest, bringing me back himself! Oh, what a welcome I shall give!"

"No gentleman," Harry cried, standing his ground, "ever refused shelter under my grandfather's roof."

"Oh, no gentleman!" the little widow exclaimed, composing herself. "Let us go down, if you wish, son, and pay our respects to this one. Will you give me your arm?" She took hold of his slender wrist, supporting herself, and they descended the broad stairs together.

In the parlor, where the Colonel sat, Mrs. Mountain made a formal curtsy and placed her small, trembling hand in his. "I wish that our meeting had been happier, Colonel Washington," she said softly.

"You do not grieve more than I do that it is otherwise, madam," Washington replied, his voice steady despite the turmoil around him.

"I might have wished for the meeting to be spared," the widow continued, her black eyes piercing and unreadable. "That I might not have kept you from friends you are naturally anxious to see. Home, his nurse, and his mother, along with our kind Doctor Dempster, will soon restore him. 'Twas scarce necessary, Colonel, that you, burdened with so many military and domestic affairs, should turn physician as well."

"Harry was ill and weak, and I thought it my duty to ride by him," Washington stammered slightly.

"You yourself, sir, have endured the fatigues and dangers of this campaign in a most wonderful manner," Mrs. Mountain observed, curtsying again. "I wish to Heaven, madam, some one else had come back in my place!"
