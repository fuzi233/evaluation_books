When my visit to my brother Harry drew to its close, and my wife Theo and our young child had returned to our maternal home outside Richmond, I made haste to ride out to the Royal Governor’s country seat a few miles from Williamsburg, to confer with him on the open war preparations spreading not just across Virginia, but every colony we had word from.

Men whose names would soon be known the world over as founding figures of a new nation had just been named Virginia’s delegates to the upcoming General Congress in Philadelphia. In Massachusetts, Royal troops and colonial militiamen stood face to face, almost on the brink of open fighting; Maryland and Pennsylvania still held a reputation for far greater loyalty to the Crown, while the Carolinas and Georgia, it was said, counted staunch adherents to the mother country in the vast majority of their populations. No one imagined Virginia, proud of its ancient Cavalier lineage, would ever abandon its long tradition of loyalty. We had barely a handful of regular troops in the province, but crowds of loud, confident Loyalists swarmed the Governor’s household, all eager for the moment they could draw their swords and scatter the rascally rebels before them.

In every one of these assemblies, I was forced to hear endless harsh condemnations of my poor Harry. All agreed, not entirely without reason, that his wife Fanny had led him to adopt the strident anti-British opinions he had begun to declare openly of late. Worse, they claimed he was hopelessly infatuated with the gentleman of Mount Vernon, whose views Harry had always followed, and who was daily committing himself further to the desperate, dreadful path of resistance. “This is the man your Excellency favoured!” the Governor’s courtiers would cry. “The man you trusted, who enjoyed your hospitality over and over again!”
It was impossible to deny much of this was true, though what the hot-headed Loyalists called treachery was in fact proof of Mr. Washington and his compatriots’ long, earnest desire not to cast off their allegiance to the Crown, but to exhaust every last possible chance for reconciliation before risking the terrible alternative of full revolt and separation.
Let traitors arm, let villains draw the parricidal sword, they cried: they would stay faithful. England’s unconquerable power would crush the misguided, ungrateful provinces and drag them back to obedience. How loudly we cheered King George’s health after our banquets! We would die defending his rights, they vowed: we would invite a prince of his royal house to cross the Atlantic and rule his ancient Virginian dominions.

In recognition of my own and my excellent mother’s long record of loyalty, they insisted Harry’s delinquent conduct ought to be forgiven. Was it not still possible to win him over with the offer of a fine command? Would I not intercede with him, who everyone knew held such great influence over his choices?
Our Williamsburg councils swung wildly between extremes: one hour exalted and triumphant, the next seething with rage at the rebels, full of anxious hope for reinforcements from home, then sinking into doubt, distrust, and gloomy dread.
I promised I would speak to Harry. I wrote to him, I admit, with very little hope of success, repeating and reinforcing the same arguments I had laid out to him in our many long talks before. My mother also threw the full weight of her authority behind the cause, though I expected very little good to come of that. She assailed Harry, as was her habit, with carefully selected Scripture texts she believed backed her position, threatening him with divine punishment for disobeying the powers ordained by God. She pointed to my example as the elder son, and repeatedly hinted that he was entirely under his wife’s thumb — the very worst possible argument she could have chosen for that sort of debate. She never showed me the letter she sent him, likely knowing I would protest the ferocity of some of her phrasing, but she did let me read his reply, from which I could easily guess the full tenor of her case. To my great surprise, Harry had countered every one of her Biblical citations with a dozen of his own, in a neat, temperate, even elegant letter I had believed only his wife Fanny was capable of writing. It turned out he had enlisted the help of Mr. Belman, the New Richmond clergyman who had taken a firm Whig stance, and who was busy preaching and publishing anti-Tory sermons that routinely out-argued our own chaplain, Hagan.

My exhortations to Harry fared no better than our mother’s. He never replied to my letters. Badgered further by the Governor’s loyal circle, I made the imprudent mistake of writing to say I would visit him at Fanny’s Mount at the end of the week. When I arrived, however, only Fanny was at home. She greeted me with perfect cordiality, but informed me Harry had ridden far out towards the Blue Mountains to inspect a herd of fine horses, and would be gone indefinitely — she could not say when he would return.
I knew then there was no hope left.
“My dear,” I said to her, “as far as I can read the signs of the times, the powder train that has been laid these past years will soon have a match set to it. Harry is riding far away, and God alone knows what end he rides towards.”
“The Lord prosper the righteous cause, Sir George,” she said.
“Amen, with all my heart. You and he speak as Americans. I speak as an Englishman. Tell him from me that when anything in the course of nature befalls our mother, I have more than enough for myself and my family in England, and I shall cede every inch of our Virginian land to him entirely.”
“You do not mean that, George?” she cried, her eyes brightening. “Of course it is only right and fair — why should you, who are only older by an hour, have everything? A palace and lands in England, the plantation here, the family title, and children, and poor Harry nothing? But it is generous of you, all the same — handsome and proper, and I never expected it of you. You do not take after your mother in this, Sir George, not a bit. Give my love to sister Theo!” She turned her cheek to me to kiss, before I mounted my horse to ride away from her door. With a woman like Fanny at his side, how could I ever have hoped to turn my brother back to our side?

I returned to the Governor to report my total failure, and we agreed it was high time to arm in earnest, to prepare for the shock that was surely coming. His Excellency and his whole circle of officials were far more agitated than I thought necessary, savagely abusing the wicked Whigs at every turn and roaring “Old England for ever!” at the top of their lungs, all equally eager for the day they could meet their adversaries face to face and ride the rebel forces straight into the dust.
I left his lordship with the formal thanks of the Council, and a commission to do my utmost to raise a company of men in defence of the Crown. This was the origin of the corps later known as the Westmoreland Defenders, of which I had the honour to be named Colonel, tasked to lead them when they took the field. We announced the formation in the Government Gazette, and filled our officer ranks without difficulty — but the recruits, it must be said, were slow to enlist, and even quicker to desert. Our chaplain Hagan volunteered for the post at once. My mother sewed and presented our regimental colours, and travelled around the countryside drumming up volunteers, but the most zealous recruiter of all was my old tutor, little Mr. Dempster, who had fought on the Jacobite side in Scotland as a boy. He travelled south specifically to the Carolinas, to recruit among the descendants of his banished comrades who had worn the white cockade of Prince Charlie, and who still remained the most fiercely loyal to the Crown of all the colonies’ inhabitants.

Harry’s “horse buying expedition” did not stop at the Blue Mountains. He travelled on to Annapolis, then Baltimore, and then to Philadelphia, where the Second General Congress was in session, attended by the same Virginian delegates we had sent the year before. Meanwhile, every almanac of the time records what happened next: Lexington, the first shots of the war that would end in the colonies’ independence. The colonists still protested their loyalty to King George, but declared their determination to die free rather than submit. Twenty thousand armed militiamen assembled outside Boston, carrying arms and artillery they had seized from government stores. Mr. Arnold began the famous, doomed career for which he would be remembered, by staging a daring, almost burglar’s raid to capture two undefended royal forts. Three generals fresh out of London, with a huge reinforcement of regular troops, were sailing to lift General Gage out of his precarious position in Boston.
The two armies soon clashed, and the British high command launched their campaign of “conquest and pacification” in the colonies with the glorious blunder of Breed’s Hill. They fortified their position afterwards, too weakened to press for more “victories” over the rebels, and the two armies settled down to watch each other while Congress deliberated in Philadelphia over who should take command of the combined colonial forces. We all know now who that fortunate choice fell on. One of the Virginian regiments that marched north to join the new Commander-in-Chief was led by Henry Esmond Warrington, late a Captain in his Majesty’s service, and riding at his side was his brave little wife, whose courage we would hear many stories of in the years that followed. For my part, I was glad Fanny had left Virginia: if she had stayed after Harry’s departure, my mother would inevitably have marched over to her house to pick a fight, and I was thankful that particular terrible domestic clash of civil war was spared our family.

Nearly all the farmers and country folk around us flocked to join the new northern rebel army, and the whole colony was flattered that a Virginian gentleman had been chosen for the highest military post. The news of bloodshed at Lexington sent a thrill of wrath and fury through every province, and the colonists shouted denunciations of the cruel, wanton brutality of the British invaders. The invaders, of course, were only doing their duty, trying to recover government property seized by armed rebels — but no one stopped to weigh their words once they had made up their minds to be angry. The colonists had picked their side, and with a natural, fiery ardour, they were determined to test their strength against the braggart, domineering mother country. Breed’s Hill became a mythic mountain, visible to every man on the North American continent, with Liberty, Victory, and Glory blazing on its summit. The red-coated regulars, everyone saw, could be resisted by farmers and ploughmen. The famous British officers could be outgeneralled by doctors, lawyers, and ordinary civilians! Granted that Britons could conquer the whole world, their children across the Atlantic were just as capable of conquering Britons!
I do not know which of the two sides deserves the greater prize for bravery, or for vainglory. We laugh at our French neighbours for their boasting and gasconading, but for steady self-esteem and indomitable confidence in their own courage, greatness, and magnanimity, no one can match the Britons — except their children across the Atlantic.

The vast majority of people in our neighbourhood threw their lot in with the rebel cause, and the ranks of Sir George Warrington’s Westmoreland Defenders dwindled away to almost nothing, not just after the news of the northern battles, but because of the behaviour of our Royal Governor, whose conduct enraged none so much as his own loyal partisans across the colony. Everyone had agreed, over countless meetings and thousands of bumper toasts, that he would plant the King’s standard and summon every loyal gentleman to rally around it. I could name dozens of gentlemen now serving as smug officers of the United States government, whom I have heard hiccup out prayers that they might die wrapped in the folds of the royal flag, or roar challenges to the absent traitors who had run off to join the rebel army. But let bygones be bygones.
This much is public history, however: his lordship, our Scottish peer, the Sovereign’s representative in the Old Dominion, who had loudly invited every loyal subject to rally to the King’s standard, was the first man to stuff the flag in his pocket and flee to his ships, out of all reach of danger. He would not leave the safety of their decks, save to act like a pirate under cover of midnight to burn and destroy colonial property.
All us loyal gentlemen were left stranded on shore, fully committed to the royal cause, and placed in far greater danger than ever before, all thanks to the cowardice and cruelty of the man who should have been our leader.

It was the start of June, our orchards and gardens all blooming thick with summer blossoms. A week earlier, I had been in Williamsburg, exchanging compliments with his Excellency, devising plans for future moves to push back the rebellion, shaking his hand heartily at parting, with vincere aut mori — conquer or die — the last words on all our lips. Our little family was gathered at our Richmond house, talking daily over the latest news from the north, and the constant bickering between the colonial Assembly and his Excellency, when our chaplain Hagan came rushing into the parlour.
“Have you heard the news of the Governor?” he gasped.
“Has he dissolved the Assembly again? Put that scoundrel Patrick Henry in irons?” my mother asked.
“No such thing! His lordship, his lady, and all their family snuck out of the Palace in the dead of night. They are on board a man-of-war anchored off York, from which he sent a dispatch to the Assembly begging them to keep sitting, and announcing he only left Government House out of fear of the people’s fury.”

What was to become of the sheep, when their shepherd had run away? No entreaties were more pathetic than those from the members of the House of Assembly, who guaranteed the Governor full personal safety if he would only come ashore, and implored him to appear before them even just to pass routine bills and conduct necessary business. He refused. The man-of-war was now his seat of government, and he ordered the Assembly to come out to the ship to wait on him. That was “planting the King’s standard”, in very deed. The Governor had abandoned us, and the Assembly was forced to take over governing in his stead. Crowds of people, including dozens of enslaved people who had run away from local plantations, flocked out to join the fugitive Viceroy on his ships. He and his new raiding parties landed under cover of darkness to burn and seize property, matching the most lawless of the rebels in their eagerness for plunder. He not only invited runaway enslaved people to join his ranks, but sent envoys to Indigenous communities to beg them to take up arms on the Crown’s behalf. Whenever he did land, it was only to burn and destroy: and when the colonists resisted, as they did at Norfolk and Hampton, he would retreat straight back to the safety of his ships.

Even my mother, after this miserable flight of our supposed leader, was shocked by the state of affairs, and began to doubt the rebellion would be crushed quickly. Arming the enslaved population was, in her view, the most cowardly blow of all. Many of the loyal gentry were ruined, robbed of their only livelihoods. A score of our own enslaved hands from Richmond and Castlewood deserted to join the Governor’s fleet; not all of them, though some, were killed, and a couple were hanged by rebel forces for plunder they committed while serving in his lordship’s precious army.
But though her property was wantonly damaged, and the King’s chief representative in the colony had proven a complete imbecile, Madam Esmond had no intention of abandoning the cause of Royalty and Honour. My good mother was never more grandly dignified, loudly and enthusiastically loyal, than after she heard of the Governor’s lamentable defection. The local population, most of whom disagreed fiercely with her politics, listened to her speeches without unkindness. Her oddities were well known across the whole province, and I fear many young wags were in the habit of teasing her, drawing out her long stories about the Marquis, her father, and the splendours of the Esmond family. But along with her oddities, everyone remembered her charity and kindness, and many a “rebel”, as she called them, held a quiet soft spot for the pompous little Tory lady.

As for me, the Colonel of the Westmoreland Defenders, my command vanished entirely after our chief’s outrageous conduct — but I escaped very serious danger that would have befallen me and my family because I had the courage to confront Lord Dunmore on his own ship, after he had burned the stores at Hampton and issued his proclamation inviting the enslaved to rally to his standard. I remonstrated with him sharply over both decisions, begging him to return to Williamsburg, where hundreds, thousands of us, would defend him to the very last. I spoke so freely, and my behaviour made my contempt for his conduct so obvious, that his lordship flew into a rage, called me a rebel just like all the rest of them, and ordered me arrested on the spot. In my capacity as a militia officer, I demanded an immediate court martial, and turned to the two other officers who had witnessed our altercation, asking them to record every word that had passed between his lordship and me. These gentlemen shared my low opinion of the Governor’s conduct, and our interview ended with me walking back ashore entirely unguarded.
The story leaked out among the Whig gentry, and grew in the telling. I had spoken my mind manfully to the tyrannical Governor, it was said — no Whig could have spoken more bold, liberal sentiments. When riots broke out in Richmond, and most remaining Loyalists fled to the ships for fear for their lives, my mother’s property and house were never endangered, nor any member of our family insulted. We were still in that early stage of the conflict where many still fondly hoped reconciliation with England might be possible. “Ah! If all Tories were like you,” one leading Whig gentleman once said to me, “we and the government at home would soon come to terms again.”
This was all before the famous Fourth of July, and the Declaration that made any reconciliation impossible. Once parties grew far more rancorous, far less creditable motives were assigned to my conduct, and it was said I was a cunning old fox who had chosen to be a moderate liberal Tory only to hold onto my family estates no matter who won the war. That, I must say, remains the prevailing opinion of my humble conduct in very high circles back in England, where everyone has long assumed my actions through the unhappy conflict were guided purely by my profound regard for my own interests.
