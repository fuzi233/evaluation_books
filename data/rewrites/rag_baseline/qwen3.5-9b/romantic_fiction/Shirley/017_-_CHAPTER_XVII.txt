The procession was not forged in the fires of war, nor did it seek out enemies; yet, led by priests and officiated by women, the band played marches that stirred a martial longing in the hearts of those who listened. Miss Keeldar, among them, felt the rhythm ignite a spirit that yearned for conflict, though no battle loomed on the horizon. Old Helstone, turning by chance, caught her gaze; he laughed, and she laughed back, a shared moment of defiance against the mundane safety of their nation.

"There is no battle in prospect," Helstone declared, his voice cutting through the festive air. "Our country does not require our swords. No tyrant questions our liberty. We are merely taking a walk. Keep your hand on the reins, Captain, and slacken the fire of that spirit. It is unnecessary, and a pity to waste such energy."

"Take your own advice, Doctor," Shirley replied, her tone sharp with suppressed intensity. Turning to Caroline, she murmured, "I shall borrow from imagination what reality refuses to grant. We are not soldiers of bloodshed, unless we are soldiers of the Cross. Time has rolled back centuries, binding us on a pilgrimage to Palestine. Yet, that is too visionary. I crave a sterner dream. We are Lowlanders of Scotland, following a Covenanting captain into the hills to defy persecuting troopers. We know that battle may follow prayer, and if the worst comes, heaven must be our reward. We are ready to redden the peat-moss with our blood. That music stirs my soul; it awakens every dormant cell, making my heart beat not with its temperate daily pulse, but with a new, thrilling vigor. I almost long for danger—for a faith, a land, or at least a lover worth defending."

"Look, Shirley!" Caroline interrupted, pointing upward. "What is that red speck above Stilbro' Brow? Your sight is keener than mine. Turn your eagle eye to it."

Miss Keeldar scanned the horizon. "I see," she said, her expression tightening. "There is a line of red. Cavalry soldiers. They ride fast. Six of them. They will pass us... No, they have turned off to the right. They saw our procession and are avoiding it by making a circuit. Where are they going?"

"Perhaps they are only exercising their horses," someone suggested weakly.

"Perhaps so. We see them no more now."

Then Mr. Helstone spoke, directing the path forward. "We shall pass through Royd Lane, reaching Nunnely Common by a shortcut."

Into the straits of Royd Lane they defiled. The passage was agonizingly narrow, barely wide enough for two to walk abreast without stumbling into the ditches lining either side. By the time they reached the center, excitement rippled through the clerical commanders. Boultby's spectacles trembled; Helstone's Rehoboam glass seemed to vibrate with agitation. The curates nudged one another nervously, while Mr. Hall turned to the ladies with a strained smile.

"What is the matter?" the demand rang out.

Helstone pointed his staff toward the end of the lane. There, emerging from the mist, was another procession—an opposing force—headed by men in black robes, followed closely by the discordant sound of competing music.

"Is it our double?" Shirley whispered, her eyes widening. "Our manifold wraith? Here is a card turned up."

"If you wanted a battle, you are likely to get one—at least of looks," Caroline whispered back, a laugh bubbling up despite the tension.

"They shall not pass us!" the curates cried in unison, their voices rising in protest. "We will not give way!"

"Give way!" Helstone retorted sternly, spinning around to face them. "Who speaks of yielding? You, boys, mind what you are doing. The ladies, I know, will remain firm. I can trust them. Not a single churchwoman here will retreat before these folk, for the honor of the Establishment. What does Miss Keeldar say?"

"She asks what this is."

"It is the Dissenting and Methodist schools—the Baptists, Independents, and Wesleyans—united in an unholy alliance," Helstone explained, his voice dropping to a dangerous register. "They have deliberately entered this lane intending to obstruct our march and drive us back."

"Bad manners!" Shirley spat, her composure cracking. "And I hate bad manners. Of course, they must receive a lesson."

"A lesson in politeness," Mr. Hall suggested gently, ever the advocate for peace. "Not an example of rudeness."

Old Helstone moved forward, quickening his stride until he marched several yards ahead of his company. He had nearly reached the sable leaders of the opposition when the hostile commander-in-chief—a large, greasy man with black hair combed flat against his forehead—called a halt. The procession froze. He produced a hymn book, intoned a verse, set a tune, and they launched into the most dolorous of canticles.

Helstone signaled to his bands. With the full power of brass, they clashed out a defiant counterpoint. He commanded them to play "Rule, Britannia!" and ordered the children to join vocally. They responded with enthusiastic fervor, singing the anthem of empire until the enemy's psalm was drowned out and quelled. As far as noise was concerned, the Anglicans had won.

"Now, follow me!" Helstone exclaimed. "Not at a run, but at a firm, smart pace. Be steady, every child and woman. Keep together. Hold on by each other's skirts if necessary."

He strode on with a determined, deliberate gait, perfectly seconded by his scholars and teachers. Neither ran nor faltered; they marched with cool, solid impetus. Even the curates, trapped between the twin fires of Helstone and Miss Keeldar, were compelled to match their step. Both watched any deviation with lynx-eyed vigilance, ready to rebuke the slightest breach of order—one with a cane, the other with a parasol. The body of Dissenters was first amazed, then alarmed, then overwhelmed. Pressed back and borne down, they were eventually forced to turn tail, leaving the outlet of Royd Lane clear. Boultby suffered greatly in the onslaught, but Helstone and Malone, working in tandem, held him upright and brought him through the ordeal whole in limb, though sorely tried in wind.

The fat Dissenter who had led the hymn remained sitting in the ditch. A spirit merchant by trade and a leader of the Nonconformists, it was rumored he had drunk more water in that single afternoon than he had consumed in the preceding twelve months. Mr. Hall had ensured Caroline's safety, and Caroline had attended to him. Later, he and Miss Ainley exchanged quiet comments on the incident. Once the entire party had cleared the lane, Miss Keeldar and Mr. Helstone shook hands heartily. The curates began to exult, but Helstone soon placed a curb on their innocent spirits, remarking that they lacked the sense to know what to say and advising them to hold their tongues, reminding them that the management of such affairs was not their burden.

At half-past three, the procession turned back, regaining the starting place by four o'clock. Long lines of benches were arranged in the close-shorn fields surrounding the school. Children were seated, and huge baskets covered with white cloths, alongside great smoking tin vessels, were brought out. Before the distribution of provisions began, a brief grace was pronounced by Mr. Hall and sung by the children. Their young voices sounded melodious, even touching, in the open air. Large currant buns and hot, well-sweetened tea were administered with a spirit of generous liberality. On this day, no stinting was permitted; the rule for each child's allowance was that it should be about twice as much as they could possibly eat, leaving a reserve to be carried home for those whose age, sickness, or other impediments prevented attendance. Buns and beer circulated among the musicians and church-singers. Afterwards, the benches were removed, allowing everyone to unbend their spirits in licensed play.

A bell summoned the teachers, patrons, and patronesses to the schoolroom. Miss Keeldar, Miss Helstone, and many other ladies were already there, glancing over the arrangement of their separate trays and tables. Most of the female servants of the neighborhood, along with the wives of clerks, singers, and musicians, had been pressed into service as waiters. Each vied with the others in the smartness and daintiness of their dress, and many handsome forms were visible among the younger women. About half a dozen were cutting bread and butter, another half-dozen supplying hot water drawn from the rector's kitchen coppers. The profusion of flowers and evergreens decorating the white walls, the display of silver teapots and bright porcelain on the tables, and the active figures in blithe faces and gay dresses flitting about created a refreshing and lively spectacle. Everyone talked, not very loudly, but merrily, while canary birds sang shrilly in their high-hung cages.

As the rector's niece, Caroline took her place at one of the three first tables; Mrs. Boultby and Margaret Hall officiated at the others. At these tables, the *élite* of the company were to be entertained, for strict rules of equality were not more fashionable at Briarfield than anywhere else.
