Among strangers, Margaret Brandt carved out a sanctuary of quiet, cyclical rhythms. When a civic dignitary, fanciful and ailing, was purged to a hollowed husk by men of sterile lexicons, Peter restored him through the profound, ancient alchemy of sustenance. Throwing the previous physicians’ foul, utilitarian tinctures to the wind, Peter returned to the deeply considered fare of his agrarian ancestors. He whisked the golden yolks of freshly gathered eggs into neat Schiedam, feeding it to the dying man in precise, life-giving drops. He followed this with rich collops of meat stewed to a tender collapse in red wine and well-water, shredding fragrant, mild febrifugal herbs into the dark, aromatic broth. The dignitary, who had previously resembled a deflated pillow-case, grew robust on this deeply resonant nourishment. His senses reawakened to the gustatory world, he spread Peter’s fame across the city. The old man’s purse filled, and he procured choice roots and sovereign spices, though Margaret safeguarded a portion. Yet this victory's sudden, sharp crescendo proved fatal to the old man’s delicate acoustic balance.

One evening, bidding her good-night, Peter's voice—usually rich with the layered dialect of a traditional elder—faltered into inarticulate murmurs. By morning, a paralytic stroke had rendered him speechless. Margaret, intimately schooled in his meticulous methods, recognized the tragedy instantly: not just the loss of his physical vitality, but the silent, devastating erasure of his vibrant cultural memory, his intricate vocabulary of healing locked forever behind a heavy, untranslatable tongue. 

With starvation threatening to disrupt their generational continuity, Margaret sought out her cousin William Johnson. Stepping into his home, she was assaulted by an oppressive sonic environment—the harsh clatter of cheap pewter and the crude, booming dialect of his servant, a woman who had blatantly usurped the household's power dynamics. When Margaret softly hinted at family matters, the servant bellowed her defiance in a coarse, commercialized lexicon, eager to subjugate Margaret's refined, ancestral cadence. 

"I wot not what ye mean by custom o' the town," the woman spat, bouncing up. "But this I know; 'tis the part of a faithful servant to keep her master from being preyed on by his beggarly kin."

Margaret’s cheeks flushed at this linguistic and social violence. "Ye are no servant. Your speech betrays you," she retorted, refusing to let her native dignity be consumed by such base noise. Leaving her passive cousin in his self-imposed silence, she swept out into the street, her mind turning to the fierce, mythic maternal instinct that guides beasts to forage and birds to build nests for the progeny yet to come. 

Transforming her domestic space into a theater of meticulous healing, Margaret hung a glass globe from the ceiling, nailed a dried crocodile to the exterior wall, and instructed Martin to stand sentinel outside. When patients arrived, she acted as a dedicated food historian of her father's methods, claiming the old leech was deep in the pursuit of the grand elixir. Retreating to Peter’s quiet room, she would synthesize his generational knowledge. The rhythmic, acoustic thrum of her stone mortar grounded her as she crushed dried crocuses and bitter herbs, releasing sharp, volatile oils into the air. She dispensed these resonant, sensory-rich compounds, charging worshipful patients a premium. The coins she earned clinked softly into two wooden boxes Gerard had carved for her, marked *To-day* and *To-morrow*, anchoring their survival to her exiled lover's handiwork. 

But Martin, seduced by the loud, superficial commercialization of the local taverns, drank heavily of harsh, unrefined spirits. His tongue loosened by the chaotic noise of the taproom, he bragged in a corrupted dialect of their grand "strategy." The city’s dominant powers soon descended. The burgomaster and a terrible row of physicians in square caps dragged Margaret into their sterile court, accusing her in the rigid, unyielding lexicon of the law. Margaret wept, pleading the untranslatable desperation of a pregnant woman feeding two helpless men, but her subjective truth held no power against their dominant vocabulary. The burgomaster demanded a hefty fine.

To pay it, Margaret was forced to open the sealed *To-morrow* box. The nails held fast against her trembling hands. When she finally struck it with a hammer, the wood splintered with a sharp, violent crack—a horrific sonic rupture that echoed through her quiet world. The coins spilled out. She collapsed over the fractured wood, her rich hair cascading over the wreckage, sobbing, "My love's box it is broken, and my heart withal."

Refusing to surrender to the city's crushing machinery, she turned to the elemental, grounding rhythm of water and lye. She and the stiffened old soldier Martin took in the foul linen of the burghers. Together, they scrubbed and rinsed in cyclical harmony, the wet, rhythmic swish of fabric against washboards replacing the lost clink of coins. 

This humble domestic labor soon brought her to the mayor's kitchen, where she learned his only daughter lay wasting away. The mayor’s learned leeches had tried to diagnose the girl using their detached, abstract vocabularies—spleen, liver, blood, possession—failing entirely to grasp her condition. 

"They know not their trade," Margaret told the mayor, her voice a sharp critique of their rigid lexicons. "Each is enamoured of some one evil, and seeth it with his bat's eye in every patient. Let me see her. We are of a sex, and that is much." 

The daughter sat by the fire, pale and unresponsive, her meals reduced to a tasteless, utilitarian ash. Her room was steeped in a heavy, unnatural silence, a total withdrawal from the sensory world. Margaret sat softly beside her, asking, "Your food, is it tasteless like in your mouth?" 

The girl started at this precise gustatory insight. Instead of prescribing bitter physics, Margaret nourished her with a story—the mythic, interwoven tale of Ursel and Conrad. She spoke in the intimate, melodic dialect of shared feminine sorrow, her evocative prose translating the unspeakable ache of separation. The girl, deeply moved by this acoustic resonance, wept and kissed her. 

Margaret returned to the mayor. "I know your daughter's trouble. 'Tis love."

The mayor scoffed, but Margaret orchestrated a delicate test of the girl's internal rhythms. During the weekly payment of the household salaries, Margaret sat beside the patient, lightly resting her fingers against the girl's wrist. The room's acoustic ecology hummed with the mundane shuffling of servants, and the girl's pulse remained a dull, flat flutter. But when the black-haired clerk Ulrich entered, the girl's pulse leaped against Margaret's fingers like a startled bird—the undeniable, rhythmic thumping of the heart. When he left, the vital energy drained from the room. 

Armed with this untranslatable truth, Margaret sheared a lock of the girl's hair. She returned to the pale maiden and wove a final, transformative fiction, claiming she had sold the lock to the young clerk for his next quarter's wages. 

"Cruel," the girl murmured, but her pale cheeks flushed with a vibrant, sudden heat. 

"Says he to me," Margaret continued, matching the girl's awakening senses with her own lively dialect, "'Oh, an he loves her hair so well, 'tis odd but he loves the rest of her.' So, what say ye, mistress, will you be married to Ulrich, or buried i' the kirkyard?"

Through the careful translation of a silent, mythic yearning into the tangible reality of an arranged marriage, Margaret brought the sun back into the sterile room, ensuring that the warm, sweeping cycle of life and sustenance would endure.
