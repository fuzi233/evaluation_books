The morning sun rose over the island, casting a golden glow on the beach where the family gathered. While the breakfast preparations were underway, I turned my attention to the magnificent kangaroo skin, anxious to preserve it in its entirety as a testament to our survival. Once Fritz had organized our gear for the expedition to the wreck, I summoned Ernest and Jack to deliver final instructions. However, the moment breakfast concluded, the boys vanished. My wife, puzzled, surmised they had gone to gather the potatoes we desperately needed. Though I urged her to reprimand them for leaving without permission upon their return, the sight of Turk trotting alongside them reassured me that no harm would come to them. Reluctantly, I left my beloved wife alone with the youngest, Franz, offering her comfort with promises of our swift return laden with treasures from the shipwreck.

Our journey across the bridge at Jackal River was interrupted by a burst of laughter. Emerging from a concealed spot where they had been waiting, Jack and Ernest startled us with their mischievous prank. Their sudden appearance, clearly designed to mimic a rescue mission, convinced them they were finally joining us to the wreck. I immediately halted this delusion, unable to scold the merry rogues for their harmless frolic, especially since I needed to send a message to my wife. I instructed them to hurry home to relieve her anxiety, noting that while they were far enough away, they might as well collect some salt on the way. I tasked them with explaining that my work on the ship would consume a long time and that my wife must endure our absence for a night. I had intended to say this upon our initial departure, but fear of her objection had held me back; however, the boys' antics inadvertently gave me the opportunity to convey this news.

"Goodbye, boys! Take care of yourselves! We're off!" shouted Fritz as I joined him in the tub-boat, and we pushed off into the current. The water carried us briskly out of the bay, and soon we were moored securely alongside the shattered hull of the wreck. Clambering up the broken sides, we stood on the remnants of the deck and began to formulate our plans. My objective was clear: to construct a raft capable of transporting a vast array of heavy and bulky items that our small boat could not carry. We identified a series of empty water-casks as the ideal foundation. Sealing them tightly, we pushed them overboard and arranged twelve of them side by side in rows of three, binding them firmly together with spars. Upon this sturdy base, we laid a substantial floor of planks, reinforced by a low bulwark, creating a first-rate raft perfectly suited to our needs.

Fatigue prevented us from returning to land that evening. Having eaten only light refreshments from our wallets and worked without respite, we rejoiced in the knowledge that we were not expected home. We prepared an excellent supper from the ship's provisions and retired to sleep on spring mattresses—a genuine luxury after days spent in narrow hammocks.

The following morning, we vigorously began loading the raft and boat. We first cleared our own cabins, then moved to the captain's quarters, removing furniture, doors, window frames, and their associated hardware. We loaded the officers' chests and those of the carpenter and gunsmith, dividing the heavier contents into manageable portions. One large chest contained an assortment of fancy goods that resembled a jeweler's display: gold and silver watches, snuff-boxes, buckles, studs, chains, rings, and countless trinkets. Alongside a box of money, these items captivated our attention, though a case of common knives and forks proved far more practical than the silverware we had previously salvaged.

To my delight, we discovered a number of young fruit trees, meticulously packed and labeled with names familiar to European ears: apples, pears, chestnuts, oranges, almonds, peaches, apricots, plums, cherries, and vines. This cargo, originally destined for a distant colony, became our rich and nearly inexhaustible treasure trove. Among the supplies were ironmongery, plumber's tools, lead, paint, grindstones, cart wheels, and everything necessary for a smith's forge, including spades and plough-shares. We also found sacks of maize, peas, oats, and wheat, a hand-mill, and the numbered parts of a saw-mill, ready for assembly if we possessed the strength to erect it.

Overwhelmed by the sheer volume of wealth surrounding us, we struggled to decide what to take. Realizing that a single storm could destroy the ship and wipe out everything we left behind, we selected the most essential items, prioritizing the grain and fruit trees. Fishing lines, reels, cordage, harpoons, and a mariner's compass were added to the load. Fritz, remembering our encounter with the shark, positioned the harpoons with readiness, amusing himself by imagining he was a whaler flourishing his weapon. By early afternoon, both crafts were heavily laden, and we prepared to head for shore. The voyage began with anxiety due to the risk of accidents involving the raft in tow, but the calm sea and favorable wind allowed us to spread the sail, ensuring satisfactory progress.

Suddenly, Fritz asked for the telescope, pointing out a curious object floating at a distance. After handing it back, he urged me to examine it. I soon realized it was a turtle sleeping peacefully on the water, unaware of our approach. "Do, father, steer towards it!" he exclaimed. I complied, allowing him a closer look. Little did I suspect the chaos that would follow. With his back turned to me and the broad sail blocking my view, I missed his actions entirely. Suddenly, I felt a sharp shock, followed by the sensation of a line running through a reel. Before I could cry out, a second shock hit us, and the boat was rapidly dragged through the water.

"Fritz, what are you doing?" I cried. "You are sending us to the bottom!"

"I have him! Hurrah! I have him safe!" he shouted in eager excitement. To my astonishment, I saw that he had struck the tortoise with a harpoon; a rope was attached, and the creature was towing us away. Lowering the sail and grabbing my hatchet, I rushed forward to cut the line and release the turtle and harpoon immediately.

"Father! Wait!" the boy pleaded. "There is no danger just yet! I promise to cut the line myself the instant it is necessary! Let us catch this turtle if we possibly can."

"My dear boy, the turtle will be a terrible bargain if he upsets all our goods into the sea, even if he doesn't drown us first. For heaven's sake, be careful! I will wait a few minutes, but the instant there is danger, cut the line."

As the turtle began to head for the open sea, I hoisted the sail again. Finding the resistance too great, the creature turned landward, dragging us rapidly after it. The shoreline we approached was considerably to the left of our usual landing place, featuring a gradual shelf. At some distance from land, we grounded with a sharp shock but managed to avoid capsizing. The turtle was evidently exhausted from acting as a steam tug, hauling two heavily laden vessels at full speed. Its intention was clearly to reach land. I leaped into the water, waded up to it, and dispatched it with my axe. Despite its tenacity of life, it ceased struggling only after I severed its head from its body.

Not far from Falconhurst, Fritz signaled our approach by firing his gun and shouting in triumph. While we secured the boats and brought the turtle ashore, the entire family appeared in the distance, hastening eagerly toward us. Our new prize, along with the well-loaded boat and raft, sparked lively interest. My wife's primary relief, however, was seeing us return safely; our night's absence had disturbed her, and she was horrified by my description of our perilous chase in the wake of the fugitive turtle.

Anxious to unload some goods before nightfall, the boys ran to fetch the sledge. I, lacking an anchor, moored the boats using the heavy iron blocks we had brought. It required our combined strength to hoist the massive turtle onto the sledge; with the addition of the sapling fruit trees, it constituted a sufficient load. We hurried home, chatting merrily about our adventures. The first priority upon arrival was to prepare the turtle's flesh for supper. To my wife, this seemed a difficult task, but I turned the beast onto its back and quickly detached a portion of the meat from the breast with a hatchet by breaking the lower shell. I directed that it be cooked with a little salt, shell and all.

"But let me first cut away this disgusting green fat," my wife said, shuddering slightly. "See how it sticks all over the meat. No one could eat anything so nasty."

"Leave the fat, whatever you do!" I exclaimed. "Why, my dear, that is the very best part, the delight of the epicures. If there is too much, cut some off—it can be used as lard, and let the dogs make a supper of the refuse."

"And the handsome shell!" cried Fritz. "I should like to make a water-trough of that, to stand near the brook and stay full of clear water. How useful it would be!"

"That is a capital idea," I replied. "We can manage it easily if we find clay to create a firm foundation."

"Oh, as to clay," said Jack, "I have a grand lump of clay there under that root."

"Well done, my lad! When did you find it?"

"He found a bed of clay near the river this morning," his mother interjected, "and came home in such a mess I had to scrape his clothes and wash him thoroughly!"

"Well, mother, I can only tell you I should never in all my days have found the clay if I hadn't slipped and fallen amongst it."

"That I can well believe," she returned, "only, to hear your talk this morning, one would have thought your discovery was the result of very arduous search indeed."

"When you have finished discussing the clay and the turtle-shell," said Ernest, "I should like to show you some roots I found today. They are getting rather dry now. They look something like radishes, although the plant itself was almost a bush. I haven't dared to taste them, although our old sow was devouring them at a great rate."

"In that you did wisely, my boy. Swine eat many things injurious to men. Let me see your roots. How did you discover them?"

"I was rambling in the wood this morning and came upon the sow, very busy grubbing under a small bush and eating something ravenously. So I drove her away and found a number of these roots, which I brought for you to see."

"Indeed, Ernest," I exclaimed, examining the roots attentively, "I am inclined to believe you have made a brilliant discovery! If this proves to be the manioc root, as I expect, we might lose every other edible we possess and yet not starve. In the West Indies, cakes called cassava bread are made from it. Already having potatoes, we shall be very independent if we can succeed in preparing flour from these roots. Great care must be taken in the manufacture to express the juice, otherwise the flour may be injurious and even poisonous."

"If we can collect a sufficient quantity, we will attempt bread-making. I think I know how to set about it."

Seeing there was still time for another trip with the sledge, I went off with the elder boys, leaving Franz with his mother. We all looked forward with satisfaction to the princely supper awaiting us, for our day's work had been no light burden.

"I have been thinking about my turtle, father," said Fritz as we walked along, "is not the shell very valuable? Surely beautiful combs, boxes, and ornamental things are made of tortoise-shell, and if so, it seems a pity to use it for a water-trough."

"Your turtle, Fritz, is only fit for eating; its shell is worthless regarding ornament. Whereas the species whose shell is prized so much is unfit for food. Tortoiseshell is subjected to the action of heat; the outer layer peels off, leaving a beautifully marked, semi-transparent surface susceptible to a very high polish."

The sledge quickly received its second load from the raft: chests, four cart-wheels, the hand-mill, and various smaller articles. We returned to Falconhurst without delay.

My wife welcomed us joyfully, remarking that we had been regularly overworked during the last two days. "However, now you are come home to rest," she said, "and you little think what refreshment awaits you here in the shade. Come and see my cellar!" She smilingly exhibited a small cask half sunk in the ground, well sheltered with leaves and branches.

"Ah! You wonder where this came from," she continued. "Well, I found it myself on the sands today while you were all absent. Fancying it was wine of some sort, I got it up here on purpose to be ready for you. The boys are most anxious to know what sort of wine it will prove to be."

Using the simplest method, I inserted a straw into the vent-hole and immediately announced that in all my life I had never enjoyed a more delicious draught of canary sack. My wife was immensely pleased to find her exertions had not been in vain, and the boys pressed around me, armed with straws, begging for a taste. After so strongly expressing my own enjoyment, it seemed unreasonable to deny them, so I let them come in turns. But I was speedily obliged to call a halt; the rogues became so eager and excited that I had to reprove them for their greediness and warn them of the risk of intoxication.

Supper was more to the purpose. The turtle proved delicious and was heartily enjoyed, giving us the strength to haul the mattresses we had brought from the ship into our sleeping rooms. Refreshing slumbers closed the day.

The dogs were delighted when I descended the ladder, bounding to meet me; the cocks crowed and flapped their wings; two pretty kids gambolled around.
