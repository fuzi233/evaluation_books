Could Helen have guessed that as Pen's strength returned, his unfortunate fondness for little Fanny would also wake up? Though she never said a single word about the young girl after talking to the Major, and though she pretended to ignore Fanny completely, Mrs. Pendennis kept a very sharp eye on Master Arthur. Under the excuse of bad health, she hardly let him leave her side, and was especially worried that he shouldn't have to deal with any letters for the time being. Probably Arthur looked at his own mail with a bit of a tremble; probably, as he took them at the dinner table while feeling his mother watching him (even though her good nature seemed fixed on her teacup or her book), he waited every day to see a tiny handwriting he'd never seen before, and his heart raced as he opened envelopes addressed to him. Was he happier or more annoyed that, day after day, nothing happened? Was his mind relieved because there was no letter from Fanny? Although, surely, in matters like this, when Lovelace gets tired of Clarissa (or vice versa), it's best for both sides to break ties immediately and go their separate ways, living solitary lives; yet our self-love, pity, or sense of decency doesn't like such a sudden bankruptcy. Before announcing to the world that "Lovelace & Co." can't pay its debts, we try to make deals: we hold sad partner meetings; we delay closing the shutters and making the gloomy announcement of failure. It must happen eventually: but we pawn our jewels to keep things going a little longer. On the whole, I suspect Pen was rather annoyed that Fanny didn't send any protests. What! Could she leave him and never even glance back? Could she sink without holding out a little hand or crying, "Help, Arthur?" Well, well: not everyone who sets sail on that voyage goes down. A few drown when the ship sinks; but most are just dipped and scramble onto the shore. And anyone who knows A. Pendennis, Esquire, of the Upper Temple, can tell whether that gentleman belongs to the group likely to sink or to swim.

Although Pen was still too weak to walk half a mile, and couldn't be trusted to take a carriage ride alone without a nurse, Helen couldn't keep watch on Mr. Warrington forever, and had no power to stop him from going to London if business required it. In fact, if he had gone and stayed, perhaps the widow, for her own reasons, would have been glad; but she checked these selfish thoughts as soon as she admitted them. Remembering Warrington's deep respect and service, and his constant friendship for her boy, she welcomed him almost as a family member, treating him with her usual melancholy kindness and submissive acceptance. Yet, one morning when his affairs called him to town, she guessed what Warrington's mission was: he had gone to London to find news about Fanny for Pen.

Arthur had indeed spoken with his friend and told him more fully about his adventures with Fanny (which the reader already knows) and his feelings toward her. He was very grateful he had escaped the great danger, to which Warrington heartily agreed. He felt he had no major faults to blame himself for regarding his behavior, but if they had to part—which they must—he would be glad to say, "God bless her," and hope she remembered him kindly. In his talks with Warrington, he spoke with such gravity and emotion that George, who had strongly supported the separation, began to fear his friend wasn't as healed as he claimed. That if the two met again, all the danger and temptation might have to be fought once more. With what result? "It is hard to struggle, Arthur, and easy to fall," Warrington said. "And the best courage for us poor wretches is to flee from danger. I wouldn't be what I am now if I practiced what I preach."

"And what did you practice, George?" Pen asked eagerly. "I knew there was something. Tell us about it, Warrington."

"There was something that can't be mended, and that wrecked my whole fortune early," Warrington answered. "I said I'd tell you about it someday, Pen: and I will, but not now. Take the moral without the fable now, Pen, my boy; and if you want to see a man whose entire life has been ruined by an unlucky rock he hit as a boy—here he is, Arthur: and so I warn you."

We have seen how Mr. Huxter, writing home to his Clavering friends, mentioned a fashionable London club where he met a distinguished Irish officer who gave the information about Pendennis that the young surgeon sent to Clavering. This club was none other than the Back Kitchen, where the disciple of Saint Bartholomew usually met the General. The peculiarities of the General's brogue, appearance, disposition, and conversation greatly amused many young gentlemen who used the Back Kitchen for nightly entertainment. Huxter, possessing a fine natural talent for mimicry—whether of a favorite tragic or comic actor, a rooster on a dungheap, a corkscrew plunging into a bottle, or an Irish officer of gentle connections who offered himself as an object of imitation with too much eagerness—talked his talk and twanged his poor old longbow whenever drink, an audience, and an opportunity arose. He studied our friend the General with particular gusto, drawing the honest fellow out many nights. A bait consisting of sixpence worth of brandy-and-water was always enough to make the worthy old man swallow; and under the influence of this liquor, who was happier than he to tell stories of his daughter's triumphs and his own exploits in love, war, drink, and polite society? Thus, Huxter presented his friends with many pictures of Costigan: of Costigan fighting a jewel in the Phoenix, of Costigan's interview with the Duke of York, of Costigan at his son-in-law's table surrounded by the nobility of his country, and of Costigan, when crying drunk, lamenting his daughter's ingratitude and stating that his gray hairs were hastening to premature grave. Through these tales, Huxter brought many young fellows to the Back Kitchen, who drank the landlord's liquors while enjoying the General's quirks, so the host forgave many of the latter's foibles in exchange for the profit they brought. Not the highest position in life was this—certainly, or one we would wish for an old man if we revered him—but of this aged buffoon, it may be noted that he had no particular idea his condition wasn't high, and in his whiskied blood there was not a black drop, nor in his muddled brains a bitter feeling, against any mortal being. Even his child, his cruel Emily, he would have taken to his heart and forgiven with tears; and what more can one say of Christian charity than that a man is ready to forgive those who have done him every kindness, and with whom he is wrong in a dispute!

Some among the young men frequenting the Back Kitchen believed the Captain hid his lodgings to avoid debt collectors or desired privacy, living in some wonderful place. Neither the landlord, when questioned, nor Costigan in his most intoxicated moments would answer inquiries. There was no real secret, as we have seen, having entered his apartments more than once; but due to the vicissitudes of a long life, he had often resided in places requiring privacy where visitors' presence would bring him nothing but discomfort. Hence, wild legends sprang up. It was said he slept in a watch-box in the City, in a cab at a mews, or in the Duke of York's Column—the wildest theory coming from the facetious Huxter. For Huxter, when not silenced by "swells" and in the company of his own friends, was a very different fellow from the youth cowed by Pen's impertinence earlier; he was the life and soul of the circles he joined, whether around a festive board or a dissecting table.

One bright September morning, as Huxter enjoyed coffee at a Covent Garden stall after a delightful night dancing at Vauxhall, he spotted the General staggering down Henrietta Street, pursued by a crowd of hooting blackguard boys who had left their beds under the river arches early, prowling for breakfast and the day's strange livelihood. The poor old General wasn't in a condition where the sneers of these beggars affected him much: cabmen and watermen recognized him and commented; policemen glared after him, warning the boys off with looks of scorn and pity. What did the scorn of men or the jokes of ribald children matter to the General? He reeled along with glazed eyes, having just enough sense to know where he was bound and to follow his accustomed path home. He went to bed unaware of how he arrived, waking to find himself there, asking no questions. He continued this daily perilous voyage until, from his coffee-stall vantage point, Huxter saw him. To note his friend and pay his twopence (he had only eightpence left, otherwise he would have hired a cab from Vauxhall) was instantaneous work for the eager Huxter. Costigan dove down the alleys near Drury Lane Theatre, where gin-shops, oyster-shops, and theatrical wardrobes lay dormant behind closed shutters as the pink morning light touched their chimneys. Huxter followed through these courts until reaching Oldcastle Street, where the gate of Shepherd's Inn stood.
