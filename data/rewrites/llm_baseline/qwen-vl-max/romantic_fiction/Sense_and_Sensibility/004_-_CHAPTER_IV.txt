“What a shame,” said Marianne, with a gentle sigh, “that Edward has no real passion for drawing. It seems such a pity, when one considers how much beauty and feeling it can express.”

Elinor smiled softly. “No passion for drawing? Why do you say that? He may not draw himself, but he deeply appreciates the work of others. I assure you, he possesses a natural sense of taste—refined, sincere, and grounded in true understanding. Had he had the chance to study, I believe he could have become quite skilled. But he’s so cautious about his own judgment that he rarely speaks on art at all. Still, his instinct for what is pleasing, balanced and meaningful, is remarkably sound.”

Marianne hesitated, unwilling to offend her sister. She knew Elinor held Edward in high regard, and she didn’t want to appear unkind. Yet the quiet admiration Elinor described was far removed from the deep, rapturous joy Marianne believed true taste should inspire. Still, she admired her sister’s devotion—her willingness to see only goodness in Edward, even if it meant overlooking certain shortcomings.

“I hope,” Elinor continued gently, “you don’t think him lacking in general refinement. You mustn’t, for your kindness toward him speaks volumes. If you truly believed him deficient, I’m certain you wouldn’t treat him with such warmth.”

Marianne blinked, caught between truth and affection. She couldn’t lie to her sister, nor could she wound her feelings. After a pause, she said, “Don’t be offended, Elinor, if my praise isn’t as full or as detailed as yours. I haven’t known him as long or as closely as you have. I haven’t seen the subtle nuances of his mind, the delicate inclinations that shape his character. But I hold him in the highest esteem—his heart, his integrity, his sense of honor. I consider him everything that is noble and admirable.”

Elinor laughed lightly. “I’m certain he’d be pleased with such praise. I don’t know how one could speak more warmly than that.”

Marianne felt relief wash over her. She was glad her sister was satisfied. Then Elinor added, “Of his intelligence and moral strength, there can be no doubt—anyone who has spoken with him honestly would agree. His mind is well-cultivated, his love for literature profound, his imagination vivid, his observations keen and fair. His taste is refined, his understanding clear. As you grow to know him, his qualities only deepen. At first glance, he may seem reserved, perhaps even plain. But once you see the light in his eyes—so expressive, so thoughtful—and the softness of his expression, you begin to understand his true worth. Now, I find him genuinely handsome—or nearly so. What do you think, Marianne?”

“You’ll soon think him handsome,” replied Marianne with a tender smile, “if you don’t already. When you ask me to love him as a brother, I’ll see no flaw in his face, just as I now see none in his soul.”

Elinor started slightly. She had let her feelings slip, and now regretted it. She realized how deeply Edward had come to mean to her. She believed their affection was mutual, but she needed certainty before she could allow herself—or Marianne—to assume anything more. She knew her sister’s nature: where they wished, they hoped; where they hoped, they expected. It was dangerous to let imagination run ahead of reality.

She tried to clarify things gently. “I won’t deny,” she said, “that I value him greatly. I admire him. I care for him.”

At this, Marianne burst out, “Admire him? Care for him? Cold-hearted Elinor! Worse than cold-hearted—ashamed of your own heart! Say those words again, and I shall leave this room at once!”

Elinor laughed, unable to suppress her amusement. “Forgive me. I meant no insult. I simply spoke quietly because I didn’t wish to exaggerate. Believe my feelings are stronger than I’ve expressed. Believe them to be as deep as his merit and the hope—yes, the *hope*—of his affection for me might justify. But beyond that, you must not go. I am not certain of his regard. There are moments when I wonder whether he feels as I do. Until his feelings are clearly known, I prefer not to encourage myself into believing more than I know.”

She paused, then continued, “In truth, I feel little doubt in my heart that he favors me. But there are other matters—more serious ones. He is not independent. We don’t know exactly what kind of woman his mother is, but from what Fanny has hinted, we suspect her to be harsh and ambitious. And I strongly believe Edward knows that marrying someone without great wealth or rank would be difficult, perhaps impossible, under her influence.”

Marianne was stunned. Her imagination had painted a far brighter picture than reality allowed. “You’re not engaged to him?” she whispered. “But surely it will happen soon. Two good things will come from this delay. First, I shall keep you longer. Second, Edward will have time to develop a taste for drawing—your favorite art. Oh, if he could learn to draw from your genius, how happy it would make us all!”

Elinor had been honest. She could not share Marianne’s confidence. There were times when Edward seemed distant, weary, as though burdened by something unseen. That wasn’t indifference—but it wasn’t the warmth of love either. A mere suspicion of her affection might cause anxiety, but not the deep sadness that often clouded his face. The real source of his sorrow, Elinor suspected, was his dependence. He couldn’t freely give his heart when his future was tied to his mother’s ambitions.

She knew too well that Mrs. Ferrars neither made life easy for him nor offered any promise that he could build a home of his own unless he followed her strict plans. With that knowledge, how could she feel secure? She could not take for granted what her mother and sister still assumed. The longer she saw Edward, the more uncertain his feelings became. At times, she feared they were no more than friendship.

Yet even if his feelings were limited, they were enough to make his sister uneasy—and often, unpleasant. Once, she had boldly challenged Mrs. Dashwood, speaking openly of Edward’s expectations, of Mrs. Ferrars’s demand that both her sons marry well, and of the danger any young woman faced if she dared to “draw him in.” Her words were sharp, cutting through the silence like a blade.

Mrs. Dashwood could not pretend ignorance or calmness. She responded with quiet dignity, expressing her contempt, and left the room immediately. She resolved, regardless of cost or inconvenience, that her beloved Elinor would never again endure such insults under her roof.

Just then, a letter arrived from the post—a timely and welcome offer. It came from Sir John Middleton, a relation of hers, a gentleman of standing and means in Devonshire. He wrote with genuine kindness, offering a small cottage on very reasonable terms. Though modest, he promised to make any improvements necessary if she liked the location. He urged her to visit Barton Park, his estate, where she could judge for herself whether the cottage could be made comfortable.

The letter was written with such warmth and sincerity that it brought comfort to her heart—especially at a moment when she was grieving over the coldness of her own family. She needed no time to decide. The distance of Devonshire, which had once seemed an obstacle, now became its greatest advantage. Leaving Norland was no longer a sorrow—it was a necessity. To stay near that place while such a woman ruled it would be unbearable.

She immediately wrote to Sir John, thanking him and accepting his offer. Then she hurried to show both letters to her daughters, wanting their approval before sending her reply.

Elinor had always thought it wiser to live apart from Norland, rather than among familiar faces who might remember their past. So she did not oppose her mother’s decision to move to Devonshire. The cottage, as described, was simple and affordable—no grounds for objection. Though the idea of leaving Norland saddened her, she said nothing. She would not stand in the way of her mother’s peace.
