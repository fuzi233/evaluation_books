The long, gray evenings of winter melted away, replaced by the golden warmth of spring. Inside our study, the air hummed with the soft rhythm of weaving and plaiting, broken occasionally by the lively storytelling of the boys and Jenny. Outside, the rains had ceased, revealing a landscape bathed in sunlight. Stepping from our rocky sanctuary back into the open air, we marveled at how quickly the seasons could shift from confinement to freedom. The island, once a prison, had transformed into a thriving kingdom. Fields and orchards demanded labor, filling the lads with purpose, while Mother and Jenny oversaw the poultry yard and household chores with practiced ease.

Our empire stretched far beyond our immediate home. From Falconhurst to Woodlands, from Prospect Hill to the distant Shark and Whale Islands, every outpost required tending. Jack and Franz had been assigned the honor—and burden—of maintaining the coastal defense line. They spent the day reinforcing the flagstaff, scrubbing the cannons, and polishing the brass until it gleamed. By evening, as the rest of us strolled along the beach, enjoying the cooling sea breeze, the twins finished their tasks. They loaded the cannons and paddled out in their small boat, using a floating tub as a target. With a triumphant shout, they fired, shattering the dummy, before hauling their weapons back into position.

Almost immediately, the horizon erupted in response. Three distinct booms rolled across the water from the west. The beach fell silent. Was it a phantom? A trick of the ear? Or the unmistakable roar of a ship? Before anyone could speak, Jack and Franz dashed back to shore, paddling with frantic urgency. Their faces were pale with a mixture of terror and exhilaration.

"Hear you?" Jack gasped, clutching the sand. "Hear them? What do we do?"

"It must be a European ship!" Fritz cried, seizing his brother's hand, his eyes shining with hope. "Fatherland! Civilization!"

A wave of emotions swept over us—fear, relief, desperate longing. Were they allies or pirates? Friends or foes? The answer mattered more than life itself. Before we could formulate a strategy, the wind shifted, and the sky darkened. A terrible storm brewed, the waves rising into towering mountains that threatened to swallow everything in their path. For two days and two nights, the hurricane raged, drowning out all sound and rendering travel impossible.

When the sun finally broke through on the third day, leaving a calmer, receding sea, the boys urged me to test the waters again. Perhaps the ship had survived, or perhaps it had fled. Curiosity and necessity drove us to Shark Island. One cannon, then two, fired into the distance. Moments later, the echo returned. The stranger was still there.

We signaled with our flag—a sign of peace—and retreated to the mainland, where the entire family gathered in a frenzy of anticipation. Fritz and I, armed with muskets, cutlasses, and spyglasses, ventured out in our canoe to investigate. We circled the high cliffs, scanning the shoreline where the gunshots had originated. Then, hidden behind the curve of Cape Pug-Nose, we saw it: a brig-of-war anchored in a secluded cove, flying the English colors.

At first, a shadow of doubt crossed my mind. Why would a naval vessel seek such a remote, treacherous harbor? Could it be a mutiny? A crew of pirates hiding in plain sight? But then I saw the captain speaking with an officer, his demeanor calm and dignified. I peered closer at the decks; they were spotless, the brass polished to a mirror finish. Discipline and order reigned supreme. It was a lawful ship, not a pirate den. Satisfied, we slipped back unnoticed, returning to Rockburg to report our findings.

Jenny's heart leaped at the news of Englishmen nearby, but I counseled patience. We could not approach as beggars. Instead, we worked feverishly to restore our dignity. The yacht, usually a humble craft, was scrubbed until her wood shone like varnish. Her guns were burnished, and the English flag was hoisted high at her peak. We dressed in our finest clothes, discarding our rough workwear for tailored suits and hats that would command respect.

As dawn broke, the family assembled in silence, the air heavy with unspoken emotion. Fritz and Jack returned with baskets overflowing with fresh fruit, gifts to welcome our new neighbors. Under sail, the yacht glided toward the cove, a tiny speck against the vast blue ocean. To the surprise of the crew on the brig, we emerged from behind the cape, a pleasure yacht manned by a family of explorers.

Captain Littlestone, a man of straightforward kindness, welcomed us aboard. He listened intently as I recounted our story—the shipwreck, our survival, and most importantly, our rescue of Miss Montrose. Tears welled in the captain's eyes as he revealed the truth: he had sailed specifically to find traces of her. The disappearance of the *Dorcas*, carrying her father, Colonel Montrose, had been a tragedy for three years. Survivors from a lifeboat had guided him here, leading him to believe that Miss Montrose might be alive on this shore.

"I owe you a debt of gratitude," the captain said, taking Fritz's hand. "Finding her... it is a miracle."

Soon, the rest of our party joined us. Lunch was served, and the captain shared news of his own companions: an invalid gentleman, Mr. Wolston, and his family, who had traveled from Sydney hoping to recover their health in the tropics. Later that afternoon, we visited them in their shaded encampment. Mr. Wolston, frail but hopeful, was charmed by our hospitality. That evening, unable to return to the mainland due to the storm-tossed waters, we stayed on shore, sharing stories until the stars appeared.

Later that night, Father and Mother sat together by the fire, their voices low and serious. The proximity to civilization had sparked a difficult debate. Should we return to Europe? The journey would be perilous, and the island had become a paradise they had built with their own hands. Yet, the pull of home remained. Slowly, gently, the truth surfaced. Neither parent, nor child, harbored a burning desire to leave. The love they had forged in isolation outweighed the allure of the Old World. They wished to stay. They wished to call this island "New Switzerland" forever.

"We must decide who goes," Father said softly, breaking the silence. "Which sons are meant to remain, and which to return?"

Time, it seemed, was needed to reveal the answers. Days passed, filled with preparations and visits. The captain and his crew toured our estates—Falconhurst, the gardens, the workshops. They marveled at the ingenuity of our settlements, the beauty of our homes, and the harmony of our family. Mr. Wolston, encouraged by our stability, proposed settling permanently on the island alongside his family.

Finally, the moment of truth arrived. Sitting in the circle, the decisions were made.

"Fritz returns to England," Father announced, his voice steady. "He must reunite with his father, Colonel Montrose, and ensure Miss Montrose finds her way home safely."

"And I?" asked Ernst.

"You stay," Father smiled. "Mother and I will support your scientific pursuits here."

"What about Jack?" I asked, looking at my youngest son. "You always wanted to be a performer."

Jack laughed, a bright, infectious sound. "I'm staying. When Fritz leaves, I'll be the best rider and shooter in New Switzerland. And frankly, I'd rather avoid the strict discipline of European schools than risk getting caught up in them."

Franz nodded eagerly. "A school is wonderful, Father. Emulation drives progress. If I stay, I can learn from others, grow, and contribute to this new nation."

Even Jenny, though her heart ached for her father, nodded. "If all go to England, the cheers for New Switzerland would feel hollow. I choose to stay."

Father raised his glass, tears glistening in his eyes. "To New Switzerland! May she flourish, free, and happy!"

"To England! Success to those who return!" cried Fritz, raising his glass in a toast to his father and his homeland.

"To those who depart!" Jenny added with a playful twinkle, acknowledging her sacrifice for the sake of family unity.

"The choice is made," Father declared, his spirit lightening. "Captain Littlestone, will you aid us in sending Fritz and his sister home?"

The captain hesitated only briefly before nodding. "It is my divine mission to guide these families. Three passengers wish to disembark here, while three require passage to England. Fate has woven a perfect thread."

As the night deepened, the atmosphere shifted from uncertainty to profound peace. The anxiety that had plagued Father for years lifted. The future was no longer a mystery; it was a tapestry being woven with love and destiny. Preparations for the journey began in earnest. Gifts were packed—pearls, furs, spices, and treasures from the island—to ensure the travelers would arrive in England in comfort and style. Father entrusted them with private documents and the ledger of the lost ship, urging them to find justice for the dead sailors.

Before Fritz departed, Father handed him the journal he had written throughout their exile. "Write it down," he instructed. "Let it serve as a lesson for others. Show them the blessings of patience, the power of knowledge, and the sweetness of unity."
