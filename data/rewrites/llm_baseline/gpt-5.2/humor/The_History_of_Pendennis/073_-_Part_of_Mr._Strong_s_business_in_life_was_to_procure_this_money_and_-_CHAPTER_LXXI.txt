Dinner had been waiting with that patient, simmering resentment which roast fowls acquire when they are kept too long from their proper destiny. Arthur came in at last, not with the victorious air of a man who has conquered time, but with the look of one who has been pursued by it, and nearly taken. His face, usually as ready with a smile as a well-trained waiter with the sauce-boat, was pale and startled, as if some unseen hand had clapped a cold dish over his heart.

Lady Rockminster, who regarded punctuality as a moral virtue and other people’s punctuality as her personal property, opened fire at once.

“So! We condescend to dine with us at last!”

Laura, however, had the quick eye of a woman who can tell by the colour of a cheek whether the soup is too hot or the news too dreadful. She cut across her patroness’s cannonade with a softness that still carried authority, like a spoon laid firmly upon a rattling teacup.

“Arthur—what has happened? Are you ill?”

Arthur, who looked as if a single word might shatter him, said nothing for a moment, but seized a great glass of sherry and drank it as a man swallows courage—fast, without tasting, as though afraid it might escape him. The sherry, at least, did its honest work: it gave him a little colour, though not composure.

“I have heard the most extraordinary news,” he said, glancing at the servants, who were very respectfully deaf in the way servants are when a family secret is whispering at the table. “I will tell you afterwards.”

He was nervous all through the meal; his foot kept up a miserable little tattoo under the cloth, as if trying to drum the truth out of hiding.

“Don’t tramp and beat so with your feet under the table,” cried Lady Rockminster, whose ear for domestic sound was sharp when the sound annoyed her. “You have trodden on Fido, and upset his saucer. You see Mr. Warrington keeps his boots quiet.”

Mr. Warrington did keep his boots quiet; but the quiet had a pressure in it, like a closed oven-door with something dangerous inside.

At last dessert came—late, languid, interminable; the poor fruit looked as if it had been peeled with melancholy. Lady Rockminster surveyed the company as a general reviews a defeated army.

“This dinner has been exceedingly stupid,” she announced. “I suppose something has happened, and that you want to speak to Laura. I will go and have my nap. I am not sure that I shall have any tea—no. Good night, Mr. Warrington. You must come again, and when there is no business to talk about.”

With that she tossed up her head and swept out, dignity crackling around her like starched linen.

The gentlemen rose. Warrington was already shaping his farewell—his “Good night” had nearly reached his lips—when Arthur, with sudden urgency, held him.

“Pray, stay, George. You should hear my news too, and give me your counsel in this case. I hardly know how to act in it.”

Laura’s heart, which had been beating the whole dinner like a covered kettle, gave a little scalding leap.

“It’s something about Blanche, Arthur,” she said; and the blush that rose in her cheek startled herself, as if some long-silent dialect of feeling had spoken suddenly without permission.

“Yes—and the most extraordinary story,” Arthur answered. “When I left you to go to my uncle’s lodgings, I found his servant, Morgan—who has been with him so long—at the door. He said he and his master had parted that morning; that my uncle had quitted the house, and had gone to an hotel—this hotel. I asked for him when I came in; but he was gone out to dinner. Morgan then said he had something of the most important nature to communicate to me, and begged me to step into the house—his house it is now. It appears the scoundrel has saved money in my uncle’s service, and is now a capitalist, a millionaire, for what I know.”

Arthur’s lip curled as if the word “millionaire” had an aftertaste.

“Well, I went into the house; and what do you think he told me? This must be a secret between us all—at least if we can keep it, now that it is in possession of that villain. Blanche’s father is not dead. He has come to life again. The marriage between Clavering and the Begum is no marriage.”

The words fell, one after another, like knives dropped upon a plate.

“And Blanche, I suppose, is her grandfather’s heir,” Warrington said quietly, as if arranging the facts on a board for carving.

“Perhaps: but the child of what a father!” Arthur broke out. “Amory is an escaped convict—Clavering knows it; my uncle knows it—and it was with this piece of information held over Clavering in terrorem that the wretched old man got him to give up his borough to me.”

Laura’s hands clenched and unclenched in her lap, as if kneading an invisible dough of terror into something bearable.

“Blanche doesn’t know it,” she said, almost whispering. “Nor poor Lady Clavering?”

“No,” Arthur answered. “Blanche does not even know the history of her father. She knew that he and her mother had separated; and had heard as a child, from Bonner, her nurse, that Mr. Amory was drowned in New South Wales. He was there as a convict, not as a ship’s-captain, as the poor girl thought. Lady Clavering told me they were not happy, and that her husband was a bad character. She would tell me all, she said, some day; and I remember her saying, with tears in her eyes, that it was hard for a woman to be forced to own she was glad to hear her husband was dead: and that twice in her life she should have chosen so badly.”

He paused, the picture of those women—innocent, domestic, sitting with their needlework and their small daily noises—set against this brutal revelation, making the air in the room feel suddenly coarse, like flour cut with grit.

“What is to be done now?” he asked. “The man can’t show and claim his wife: death is probably over him if he discovers himself—return to transportation certainly. But the rascal has held the threat of discovery over Clavering for some time past, and has extorted money from him time after time.”

“It is our friend Colonel Altamont, of course,” Warrington said. “I see all now.”

Arthur went on, anger growing as naturally as yeast.

“If the rascal comes back, Morgan—who knows his secret—will use it over him; and having it in his possession, proposes to extort money from us all. The d——d rascal supposed I was cognisant of it,” Arthur cried, white with rage; “asked me if I would give him an annuity to keep it quiet; threatened me—me—as if I was trafficking with this wretched old Begum’s misfortune, and would extort a seat in Parliament out of that miserable Clavering. Good heavens! was my uncle mad, to tamper in such a conspiracy? Fancy our mother’s son, Laura, trading on such a treason!”

“I can’t fancy it, dear Arthur,” Laura said, and caught his hand and kissed it—as if the gesture could wipe off the very thought. Her touch was like bread broken and shared: simple, sustaining, unmistakably meant.

“No!” Warrington’s deep voice broke out; there was a tremor in it like a low note heard under a loud room. He looked at them—those two, so honest in their affection—then spoke, forcing himself into practicality as a cook forces a boil into order.

“No. Our boy can’t meddle with such a wretched intrigue as that. Arthur Pendennis can’t marry a convict’s daughter; and sit in Parliament as member for the hulks. You must wash your hands of the whole affair, Pen. You must break off. You must give no explanations of why and wherefore, but state that family reasons render a match impossible. It is better those poor women should fancy you false to your word than that they should know the truth. Besides, you can get from that dog Clavering— I can fetch that for you easily enough—an acknowledgment that the reasons you have given to him as head of the family are amply sufficient for breaking off the union. Don’t you think with me, Laura?”

He scarcely dared to look at her. A man can make brave noises in a noisy room; it is harder when the room is quiet enough to hear the shame in one’s own throat.

Arthur had started up, staring at him, eager, almost pleading to be told what to do. Warrington turned his head away. Laura rose too, went to Arthur, and once again took his hand and kissed it.

“She thinks so too—God bless her!” Warrington said, with a sound in his voice that was half gratitude, half loss.

But Laura did not think so. She grew very pale, and began speaking quickly, as if afraid that if she paused the courage would cool.

“Her father’s shame is not Blanche’s fault, dear Arthur, is it? Suppose you had been married—would you desert her because she had done no wrong? Are you not pledged to her? Would you leave her because she is in misfortune? And if she is unhappy, wouldn’t you console her? Our mother would, had she been here.”

And as she spoke she put her arms round Arthur, and hid her face on his breast, as if to shut out the brutal vocabulary the world had thrust into their gentle lives. Whatever language the scandal used—convict, hulks, bribery—hers rejected it and spoke only the old words that needed no translation: promise, pity, duty.

“Our mother is an angel with God,” Arthur sobbed. “And you are the dearest and best of women—the dearest, the dearest and the best. Teach me my duty. Pray for me that I may do it—pure heart. God bless you—God bless you, my sister!”

“Amen,” Warrington groaned, and covered his face with his hands. “She is right,” he murmured to himself. “She can’t do any wrong, I think—that girl.”

Laura looked up then, and smiled—radiant and quiet. Warrington would see that smile long afterwards, as one hears again in memory a particular sound in a particular room: the soft hush before the kettle sings, the familiar foot on a stair. It was domestic, and therefore holy.

She leaned her little fair hand on the table a moment, playing on it, as if the scene could be brought back to ordinary life by some simple household motion.

“And now, and now,” she said, looking between the two men—

“And what now?” Warrington asked.

“And now we will have some tea,” Laura said, with her smile.

It was the most English attempt at magic: to drown tragedy in a cup.

But before the kettle could even be imagined, a servant came with the message that Major Pendennis had returned to the hotel and was waiting to see his nephew.

Laura’s face changed. She gave Arthur one of those looks that manage to be both command and prayer—Behave yourself well; hold to the right; do your duty; be gentle, but firm—and then she withdrew to her room.

Warrington, who cared little for tea at any time, found himself grudging that cup with quite unreasonable bitterness. Why could not old Pendennis have arrived an hour later? Yet what is an hour, earlier or later, when the inevitable is already on the stair? The hour strikes all the same; the door shuts all the same; and when the brief warmth is gone you are alone with the cold.

Warrington went out. In the street, the hotel windows looked alike, each pane reflecting the same indifferent night.

“In which of those many windows does her light beam?” a man might ask himself, and then hate himself for asking it.

He strode to a neighbouring club and took refuge in the smoking-room, where men were talking loud—politics, opera-girls, horse-racing, the atrocious tyranny of committees. They made noise as if noise were a virtue; the air was thick with smoke and laughter, that commercialized counterfeit of feeling which men buy with brandy and approval. Warrington, carrying his private truth like a stone under his coat, entered the brawl, and even joined it; for it is one of the oddest things in life that we can be shouting a joke while some quieter self sits apart within, listening to another sound entirely—the small, honest sound of a girl’s voice saying, “And now we will have some tea.”

Arthur, walking through the hotel passages toward his uncle, felt anger rouse within him step by step. He was indignant to think that the old gentleman—whom he was about to see—had made him a tool and puppet, and had compromised his honour and good name. The corridor had its usual hotel noises—distant doors, a clink of tray, a cough—but in Arthur’s ears they seemed to accuse, like coins dropped upon a counter.

He found the Major by the fire, shrunken into his dressing-gown, peevish as an old man whose comforts have been mislaid by Providence.

The Major’s hand, when Arthur took it, was cold and shaky. He was coughing; he was grumbling; Frosch could not bring his dressing-gown or arrange his papers as that d——d impudent scoundrel of a Morgan.

“The confounded impudent scoundrel!” the Major cried, bemoaning himself with an injured pathos that had, as usual, one eye on his own inconvenience. “He was drunk last night and challenged me to fight him, Pen; and begad, at one time I was so excited I thought I should have driven a knife into him; and the infernal rascal has made ten thousand pound, I believe—and deserves to be hanged, and will be; but curse him, I wish he could have lasted out my time. He knew all my ways; and dammy, when I rang the bell, the confounded thief brought the thing I wanted—not like that stupid German lout.”

He shifted, coughed, and looked toward Arthur with the affectionate inspection of a man counting his own investments.

“And what sort of time have you had in the country? Been a good deal with Lady Rockminster? You can’t do better. She is one of the old school—vieille ecole, bonne ecole, hey? Dammy, they don’t make gentlemen and ladies now; and in fifty years you’ll hardly know one man from another. But they’ll last my time. I ain’t long for this business: I am getting very old, Pen, my boy; and gad, I was thinking to-day, as I was packing up my little library, there’s a bible amongst the books that belonged to my poor mother; I would like you to keep that, Pen. I was thinking, sir, that you would most likely open the box when it was your property, and the old fellow was laid under the sod, sir.”

He wagged his head over the fire and coughed again.

The sight of his age—his genuine fondness—did what it always did: it softened Arthur’s wrath into something more complicated. Compunction crept in, unwelcome and heavy. Arthur knew the announcement he must make would smash the Major’s favourite air-castle, that pretty, profitable fantasy the old man had been baking for years.

“Hey—hey—I’m off, sir,” the Major nodded, half joking, half trembling. “But I’d like to read a speech of yours in the Times before I go—‘Mr. Pendennis said, Unaccustomed as I am to public speaking’—hey, sir? hey, Arthur? Begad, you look dev’lish well and healthy, sir. I always said my brother Jack would bring the family right. You must go down into the west, and buy the old estate, sir. Nec tenui penna, hey? We’ll rise again, sir—rise again on the wing—and begad, I shouldn’t be surprised that you will be a Baronet before you die.”

The words smote Arthur like a hymn heard in a place where one is about to commit a sin.

“And it is I,” he thought, “that am going to fling down the poor old fellow’s air-castle. Well, it must be. Here goes.”

Aloud, slowly: “I—I went into your lodgings at Bury Street, though I did not find you—and I talked with Morgan, uncle.”

“Indeed!” The old gentleman’s cheek flushed despite himself. He muttered, “The cat’s out of the bag now, begad!”

“He told me a story, sir, which gave me the deepest surprise and pain,” Arthur said.

The Major tried to look unconcerned, and failed with the particular failure of old men who have been managing other people’s morals too long.

“What—that story about—about—What-d’-you-call-’em, hey?”

“About Miss Amory’s father—about Lady Clavering’s first husband, and who he is, and what.”

“Hem—a dev’lish awkward affair!” the Major said, rubbing his nose. “I—I’ve been aware of that—eh—confounded circumstance for some time.”

“He is all safe,” the Senior thought, relieved. “Gad! I should have liked to keep it from you altogether—and from those two poor women, who are as innocent as unborn babes in the transaction.”

“I wish I had known it sooner, or not at all,” Arthur said gloomily.

“Gad! I should have liked to keep it from you altogether,” the Major repeated aloud, a little too quickly; “and from those two poor women—”

“You are right. There is no reason why the two women should hear it; and I shall never tell them—though that villain, Morgan, perhaps may,” Arthur said. “He seems disposed to trade upon his secret, and has already proposed terms of ransom to me. I wish I had known of the matter earlier, sir. It is not a very pleasant thought to me that I am engaged to a convict’s daughter.”

“The very reason why I kept it from you—my dear boy. But Miss Amory is not a convict’s daughter, don’t you see?” the Major cried, eager now, warming to his own argument as if it were a glass of hot negus. “Miss Amory is the daughter of Lady Clavering, with fifty or sixty thousand pounds for a fortune; and her father-in-law, a Baronet and country gentleman, of high reputation, approves of the match, and gives up his seat in Parliament to his son-in-law. What can be more simple?”

“Is it true, sir?” Arthur asked.

“Begad, yes, it is true, of course it’s true. Amory’s dead. I tell you he is dead. The first sign of life he shows, he is dead. He can’t appear. We have him at a deadlock, like the fellow in the play—the ‘Critic,’ hey?—dev’lish amusing play, that ‘Critic.’ Monstrous witty man, Sheridan; and so was his son. By Gad, sir, when I was at the Cape, I remember—”

His voice wandered, as if he would lead Arthur away from the subject into safer colonial reminiscence, where convict-fathers were merely anecdotes and not daggers.

Arthur cut in sharply.

“If you had told me this tale sooner, I believe you would have spared me and yourself a great deal of pain and disappointment; and I should not have found myself tied to an engagement from which I can’t, in honour, recede.”

“No, begad, we’ve fixed you—and a man who’s fixed to a seat in Parliament, and a pretty girl, with a couple of thousand a year, is fixed to no bad thing, let me tell you,” the Major said, with that brisk, businesslike cheerfulness which, in some men, passes for conscience.

“Great Heavens, sir!” Arthur cried. “Are you blind? Can’t you see?”

“See what, young gentleman?” the other snapped.

“See, that rather than trade upon this secret of Amory’s,” Arthur cried out, “I would go and join my father-in-law at the hulks! See, that rather than take a seat in Parliament as a bribe from Clavering for silence, I would take the spoons off the table! See, that you have given me a felon’s daughter for a wife; doomed me to poverty and shame; cursed my career when it might have been—when it might have been so different but for you! Don’t you see that we have been playing a guilty game, and have been overreached;—that in offering to marry this poor girl, for the sake of her money, and the advancement she would bring, I was degrading myself, and prostituting my honour?”

“What in Heaven’s name do you mean, sir?” the Major cried, shocked less at the morality than at the disruption of his plan.

“I mean to say that there is a measure of baseness which I can’t pass,” Arthur said. “I have no other words for it, and am sorry if they hurt you. I have felt, for months past, that my conduct in this affair has been wicked, sordid, and worldly. I am rightly punished by the event; and having sold myself for money and a seat in Parliament, by losing both.”

“How do you mean that you lose either?” shrieked the old gentleman. “Who the devil’s to take your fortune or your seat away from you? By G—, Clavering shall give ’em to you. You shall have every shilling of eighty thousand pounds.”

“I’ll keep my promise to Miss Amory, sir,” Arthur said.

“And, begad, her parents shall keep theirs to you.”

“Not so, please God,” Arthur answered. “I have sinned, but Heaven help me, I will sin no more. I will let Clavering off from that bargain which was made without my knowledge. I will take no money with Blanche but that which was originally settled upon her; and I will try to make her happy. You have done it. You have brought this on me, sir. But you knew no better: and I forgive—”

The Major, at that, broke as suddenly as a hard crust cracked to show it was only air inside. All his toughness ran out of him.

“Arthur—in God’s name—in your father’s, who, by Heavens, was the proudest man alive, and had the honour of the family always at heart—in mine—for the sake of a poor broken-down old fellow, who has always been dev’lish fond of you—don’t fling this chance away—I pray you, I beg you, I implore you, my dear, dear boy, don’t fling this chance away. It’s the making of you. You’re sure to get on. You’ll be a Baronet; it’s three thousand a year: dammy, on my knees, there, I beg of you, don’t do this.”

And the old man actually sank on his knees, seized Arthur’s hand, and looked up piteously. It was dreadful to see: the shaking fingers, the wrinkled, quivering face, the watery eyes winking and spilling, the broken voice—this proud manager of other people reduced to begging like a discharged servant.

“Ah, sir,” Arthur groaned, “you have brought pain enough on me—spare me this. You have wished me to marry Blanche. I marry her. For God’s sake, sir, rise! I can’t bear it.”

“You—you mean to say that you will take her as a beggar, and be one yourself?” the Major said, rising with a cough that sounded like anger choking on its own fumes.

“I look at her as a person to whom a great calamity has befallen, and to whom I am promised,” Arthur answered. “She cannot help the misfortune; and as she had my word when she was prosperous, I shall not withdraw it now she is poor. I will not take Clavering’s seat, unless afterwards it should be given of his free will. I will not have a shilling more than her original fortune.”

The Major stared at him as if Arthur had begun speaking in some foreign provincial tongue—honour, duty, pity—words which, to the old man, had always required translation into income.

“Have the kindness to ring the bell,” he said at last, with a sudden coldness. “I have done my best, and said my say; and I’m a dev’lish old fellow. And—and—it don’t matter. And—and Shakspeare was right—and Cardinal Wolsey—begad—‘and had I but served my God as I’ve served you’—yes, on my knees, by Jove, to my own nephew—I mightn’t have been—Good night, sir. You needn’t trouble yourself to call again.”

Arthur took his hand. The Major left it in his grasp without pressure—passive, clammy, like something already withdrawing from life. His face looked older in the firelight, as if the fight and the loss had aged him in an hour.

The next day the Major kept his bed, and refused to see his nephew.
