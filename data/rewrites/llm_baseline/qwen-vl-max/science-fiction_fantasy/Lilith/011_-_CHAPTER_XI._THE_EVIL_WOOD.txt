I fell into a deep sleep, and when I awoke, the sun was rising over the edge of the world. I climbed to the highest point nearby and looked back: the hollow I had crossed under moonlight lay silent, empty—no trace of movement, no breath of life. Could it be that beneath this stillness, something terrible stirred? The land before me stretched wide and barren, a vast desert broken only by a distant patch of darker hue—perhaps a forest. No smoke curled from hidden fires, no dust marked the passage of beasts or men, no signs of cultivation broke the silence. The sky was clear, unblemished by cloud or haze. I descended and set off toward the shadowed shape on the horizon. If anything lived in this world, it would likely be there. Here, nothing could endure.

The plain proved to be a desolate riverbed, long since dried up. Rock formed its bed—flat in places, cracked and grooved by ancient currents; elsewhere, humped and jagged like broken teeth. Not a drop of water remained. Some channels bore dry moss, and a few rocks were speckled with lichens so hard they seemed carved from stone. The air, once filled with the song of flowing water, now held no sound at all—only silence, thick as death.

It took me the entire day to reach the dark patch. When I did, I found it was indeed a forest—but not a single trickle of water had crossed my path. Yet through the blistering noon, I heard it anyway: the rushing of rivers, the whisper of brooks. A phantom chorus echoed in my ears, so vivid I almost believed my eyes deceived me. The sun dipped below the treetops as I stepped into the woods. It cast long rays between massive, column-like trunks, painting the ground in shifting patterns of light and shadow. This was not the pine forest I had expected. These trees were strange—some familiar, others unlike any I had ever seen. One resembled a eucalyptus in bloom, but its flowers had a rigid calyx shaped like a skull, the lid lifting to reveal a froth-like bloom spilling over the rim. I lay beneath its broad, scimitar-shaped leaves, gazing into the depths of the forest.

Soon, the paths narrowed, the glades closed in. Night fell swiftly, sharp and cold. And what a night it was! How can I convey its eerie beauty, its haunting strangeness?

The tree above me rose tall before branching, its limbs bent low as if to enclose me. As twilight faded, the shapes of the foliage began to shift. In my wandering gaze, the clumps of leaves transformed—not imitated, but *suggested* other forms. A faint wind stirred, setting the branches swaying. Each twig, each leaf moved in rhythm with the branch, creating a dance of motion. Among them, I saw a pack of wolves straining at an invisible leash—greyhounds could not have fought harder to break free.

Another cluster of leaves became a stable full of horses, their heads and necks thrust forward from stalls. Their necks bobbed up and down, restless, then snapped sideways as the wind intensified. Some were bare skulls; one had skin stretched tight over bone. Another had lost its jaw, hanging loose, yet every now and then lifting high as though to ease a bit in its mouth.

Above them, perched on a branch, floated the figure of a woman—arms raised, commanding, imperious. Her form was unmistakable, though made of shadows and leaves. I stared, transfixed. The clarity of these visions unsettled me. What if they weren’t illusions at all? What if they were real, waiting only for my mind to believe?

Then darkness swallowed everything. The wind died. The shapes vanished. I slept.

But sleep brought no peace. Before dawn, I began to hear sounds—a distant rush, mingled with faint cries. They grew louder, closer. From every direction, noise surged into the wood. The place where I lay became the center of a storm. I dared not move. My breath slowed. I feared even the rustle of my cloak might betray me.

At last, the moon crested the trees and slipped into the forest. With her first light, the chaos erupted. A deafening roar filled the air. Shapes flickered in the dimness—figures battling, leaping, screaming. It was a war of the dead.

Skeletons clashed with phantoms. Swords passed through ghostly forms, leaving them trembling but unharmed. Maces shattered bones, yet the skeletons kept fighting—so long as two bones remained joined, they would not fall. Men and horses lay scattered, their bones crushed beneath foot and hoof. White, gaunt steeds charged through the fray. On misty horses and on foot, specters raged—unstoppable, unkillable. Weapons rang, hooves thundered, jaws gnashed. And through it all, voices screamed: curses, prayers, mockery, lies, truths twisted into weapons.

Every creed, every hatred, every lie that had ever fueled conflict echoed in the air. “Truth!” one roared, turning on his allies. “Truth!” he cried again, slashing wildly. Two fighters collapsed side by side, exhausted—then rose and fought anew. No one paused to help the fallen. No mercy. No end.

The moon shone until the sun rose. All night, I watched a woman glide above the battle—now here, now there, one arm outstretched, urging the fight, the other pressed to her side. Her eyes were dark, lifeless. I recognized her—the same figure I had seen in the leaves. She shouted: “Ye are men: slay one another!”

Then, just before sunrise, a breeze swept through the trees. A voice, calm and final, echoed: “Let the dead bury their dead.”

At once, silence. The combatants froze. When the sun rose, there was no sign of battle. Only scattered branches, brittle and dry. The forest stood quiet, still, untouched.

I rose and continued my journey. The morning air was still. No birds sang. No creature stirred. Not a moth, not a mouse, not a squirrel crossed my path. But I walked with caution, my eyes never lingering on any shape. For I knew—any moment, the forest might come alive again. The sounds of digging, of bones crashing, of whispers—still echoed in my mind. Perhaps ten thousand phantoms waited, not to appear, but to be *seen*. And I, too, might be the one who summoned them.

By mid-afternoon, I emerged from the woods. Before me lay another network of dry riverbeds—identical to the first. At first, I thought I had turned around, but soon realized it was another branch of the same ancient stream. I began crossing it, stepping into a wide channel just as the sun set. I sat down, waiting for the moon. Sleep came quickly. I lay on the moss, head resting, and instantly—I heard water. Streams rushing, springs murmuring, waterfalls singing. A soft, liquid melody wrapped around me, lulling me into dreamless rest.

When I woke, the sun was already climbing. The landscape stretched wide—rocky, wrinkled, covered in shadows that ran like stripes across the earth, like the hide of some great beast. As the sun rose, the shadows receded, as if the rocks were drinking in the darkness they had exhaled during the night.

Until now, I had loved my Arab mare and my books more than any living soul. But now, my heart burned for human presence. I longed for those beings of this strange world—the ones the raven had described so vaguely as being closest to my kind. With heavy steps and a hopeful heart, haunted by doubt whether I was even moving forward, I pressed on—north-west and by south.
