The Young Wife.

While Tito hurried across the bridge, the new steel armor hidden beneath his cloak, Romola paced the floor of the old library, her thoughts fixed on his return. Outside, the city had turned out to greet the French King and his nobility; almost every window had opened to witness the spectacle. Romola's had remained shut. It had been three months since her father's sudden death, a silence that had fallen over her household as abruptly as the event itself.

Earlier that evening, when the bell had tolled for the customary hour, her father Bardo had asked, "Is not Tito coming to write?" He had feared a refusal, yet Romola had seen the restless energy in his face. She had gently excused him, noting his obligation to a supper at the Cardinal's. Bardo, momentarily pacified by the hope of news regarding the library, had fallen silent. Then, flushing with a sudden urgency, he had commanded, "I must go on without him, Romola. Get the pen. He brings no new text, but I must speak of the New Platonists. I shall die, and nothing will be done."

Romola had stood ready with the pen, but the silence that followed was absolute. Her father never spoke again.

In the hours since, Romola had wrestled with a complex mixture of grief and a strange, quiet resignation. She told herself that her life with Tito would now be more perfect, a dream of undivided happiness finally realized. Yet, she acknowledged the subtle bitterness that had always lurked beneath the surface of their union. It was not Tito's fault; he remained gentle, tender, and endlessly accommodating. But the burden of her father's monotonous, exacting demands was a load only she could bear, and even she had found the patience required to sustain it eroding over the years. Tito, once unwearying in his novelty, now seemed to escape the suffocating atmosphere of their shared solitude with a charming gaiety that left Romola with a small, aching heartache. She convinced herself that their natures were simply different—his light and social, hers absorbing and domestic—and that her own inferiority lay in her inability to match his brilliance. To preserve her love, she had to suppress every rising impulse of pride or resentment, straining herself to subdue her nature to his.

Now, with her father gone, the weight of his legacy pressed heavily upon her. The library, the fruit of his lifetime of toil, was her sole sacramental obligation. It was safe from creditors thanks to Bernardo del Nero's advance, but the political winds had shifted violently. The Medici were expelled, and the famous collections in the Via Larga faced dispersal. The Florentine State, facing war and the need for French subsidies, would likely sell the manuscripts for cash. To Romola, these grave political upheavals mattered only insofar as they threatened the fulfillment of her father's wish. Her entire ardour, once directed toward her husband, was now concentrated on this singular duty.

Seeking to understand the forces shaping Florence, she had attended Fra Girolamo's sermon in the Duomo that morning. The fanatical preacher's voice had stirred something deep within her, a strange sympathy with the call for martyrdom that transcended her daily concerns. Yet, this emotion felt transient, lying outside the sanctuary of her true life. As she waited for Tito, she resolved to hide her growing interest in public affairs, fearing it might isolate her from him. She needed his return to restore the balance, to confirm that their union could still flow in one current.

When the great door finally creaked open, Romola hastened to the top of the stone steps. Tito appeared, and the sight of him brought a flush of joy to her face, making her seem more lovely than on the day of their wedding. He greeted her with a forced smile, attributing his lateness to fatigue. Inside, the fire crackled warmly, but Tito's demeanor was distant. He dismissed the library as too cold, preferring their private rooms, a remark that stung Romola deeply. She had expected solicitude, not indifference.

"I am not well, Romola," he admitted eventually, rubbing his face as if trying to shake off a shadow. "Do not be surprised if I am peevish."

Romola knelt beside him, caressing his hair, but her touch stopped abruptly when she felt something hard beneath his tunic. "What have you got under your clothes, Tito? Something as hard as iron?"

"It is chain-armour," he replied calmly. "I must wear it constantly."

"What threatens you?" she asked, terrified.

"Everyone is threatened who is not a rabid enemy of the Medici," Tito said, putting his hand on her neck to break the tension. "I am suspected of being an envoy. Men like Bernardo are protected by their families; I am a Greek with no one to avenge me."

Romola shuddered at the thought of him in danger, imagining the armor as a malignant shell replacing his sensitive humanity. "Will you not give it up?" she pleaded.

"If you do not mind my being stabbed or shot, why should I mind?" he teased, though a flicker of pain crossed his face.

Later, Tito recounted the day's events: the rain interrupting Luca Corsini's speech, the clumsy French welcome, and the chaotic display of armor and jewels at the Tornabuoni house. He spoke of the flattery and the food, eager to share the details of his day, yet his eyes remained distant.

Romola mentioned her visit to the Duomo and her reaction to Fra Girolamo's sermon. Tito reacted with playful warning, suggesting she had caught a touch of fanaticism. He described a wild-eyed prisoner who had run into the church to escape a French soldier, a man who had wept openly.

"I saw that man," Tito said suddenly, his voice dropping. "I was outside with Lorenzo Tornabuoni when he ran in. He escaped from a French soldier. Did you see him when you came out?"

"No," Romola answered softly. "Piero di Cosimo took him away. But you want rest, Tito? You feel ill?"

"Yes," he said, rising. The horrible sense that he must live in continual dread of what Baldassarre had said or done pressed upon him like a cold weight.
