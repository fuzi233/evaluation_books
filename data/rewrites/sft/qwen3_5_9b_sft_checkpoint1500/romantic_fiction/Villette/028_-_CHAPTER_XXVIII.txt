M. Paul Emanuel possessed an exquisite sensitivity regarding interruptions. To disturb his lessons, regardless of the cause, was considered by the faculty and students alike a transgression tantamount to a mortal sin for a young woman. Madame Beck, if compelled to traverse the classroom, would move with frantic caution, hugging her skirts and skirting the high platform as if navigating treacherous shoals. Rosine, the portress, whose hourly duty involved extracting pupils from the heart of the classes to attend music lessons elsewhere, often found herself rendered speechless by sheer terror when confronted with M. Paul's piercing gaze through his spectacles.

One morning, seated in the *carré* and working on embroidery begun by a pupil, I listened to the crescendo of voices rising in the adjacent class. A sturdy wall separated me from the gathering storm, and a glass door offered a quick escape to the courtyard. Thus, I observed the commotion with amused detachment. Poor Rosine was not so fortunate; having already risked crossing the threshold four times, she faced her fifth perilous mission: retrieving a student from M. Paul's wrath.

"Mon Dieu!" she cried, "What shall I become? Monsieur will surely kill me; he is so angry!"

Driven by desperation, she opened the door. "Mademoiselle La Malle au piano!" she shouted.

Before she could retreat, a voice boomed: "From this moment, the class is closed! The first to enter or exit shall be hanged—even if it be Madame Beck herself!"

Within ten minutes, Rosine returned, her slippers shuffling softly. "Mademoiselle," she whispered, "I cannot possibly return there again today. His glasses are dreadful. A messenger from the Athénée has arrived with an urgent summons for Monsieur. Madame Beck insists I charge you with delivering it."

"Myself?" I replied, though my heart beat faster. Curiosity mingled with apprehension. "Come, Rosine, bear your cross."

"I cannot!" she pleaded. "Five times today I have crossed him. Madame must hire a policeman."

"Bah!" I dismissed her fears. "What is the message?"

"It is precisely what he despises: an official inspector has arrived, and Monsieur *must* meet him immediately. He hates obligations."

I knew this well. The restive little man detested any spur or curb. Yet, I accepted the task, not without fear, but with a blend of curiosity and resolve. I opened the door, entered swiftly, and closed it silently. To linger or bustle would only aggravate the situation. Inside, he sat, his humor visibly dark. He had been teaching arithmetic, a dry subject that invariably agitated him; not a pupil remained unmoved by his stern tone. Bent over his desk, he refused to look up at the intrusion, a testament to his rigid will.

Approaching the *estrade*, I paused. Too short to peer over his desk, I ventured to look around. Twice I saw him clearly, his face resembling a sullen tiger. On the third glance, his "lunettes"—spectacles—caught my eye. Rosine was right; they radiated a terrifying, immutable dread, far worse than the unglazed fury of his own eyes. Realizing their utility for spotting criminals was nullified by his proximity, he removed them. Suddenly, standing eye-to-eye, the dynamic shifted.

To my relief, I felt no genuine terror. When he demanded the cord and gibbet mentioned in his threat, I offered him a needleful of embroidery thread with accommodating civility, handing it discreetly around the corner of his desk and attaching it to his chair back.

"What do you want?" he growled, teeth clenched, the sound vibrating within his chest.

"Monsieur," I began firmly, "I seek the impossible, unheard-of things." I delivered the message from the Athénée, exaggerating its urgency. Naturally, he refused to budge. "He would not leave," I realized, knowing that duty and self-interest demanded compliance. I waited in silence until he asked for further demands.

"Only Monsieur's answer for the commissionaire."

He waved his hand impatiently. Then, I stretched toward the *bonnet-grec* resting on the windowsill. He watched, a mix of pity and astonishment in his gaze. "If Miss Lucy meddles with my hat," he muttered, "she might just wear it, turn boy, and go to the Athénée in my stead."

Respectfully, I placed the hat on his desk, where its tassel seemed to nod ominously. "I'll write a note of apology—that will suffice!" he insisted, trying to evade departure.

Knowing an apology would not do, I gently nudged the bonnet toward his hand. It slid across the polished, varnished surface, dragging his steel-framed spectacles behind it. In a tragic twist of fate, they tumbled onto the *estrade*. A dozen times before, they had fallen safely, but this time, the clear lenses shattered into countless fragments.

Panic seized me. These glasses were precious, uniquely fitted to his peculiar vision, his "treasures." Trembling, I picked up the shards. My fear was eclipsed by profound sorrow. I avoided meeting his gaze for several seconds. Finally, he spoke.

"Là!" he exclaimed. "I am widowed of my glasses! Now I suspect Miss Lucy will admit the cord and gallows were fully deserved. She trembles at the thought of her doom. Ah, traitress! You intend to render me blind and helpless!"

I raised my eyes. Instead of anger or gloom, his face bloomed with a smile, reminiscent of the warmth I had seen at the Hôtel Crécy. He was not angry, nor grieved. Showing clemency despite the provocation, he was as patient as a saint. This accident, which I feared ruined my chances, became my greatest asset. Having caused him harm, he became pliant and gracious.

Railing gently at me as "a fierce woman, a terrible Englishwoman, a little wreck-ball," he confessed he dared not disobey one who demonstrated such dangerous prowess. Comparing me to the Great Emperor who smashed a vase to inspire awe, he crowned himself with the bonnet, took the broken glasses from my hand with a gesture of kindness, bowed, and departed for the Athénée in high spirits.

Yet, by evening, I found myself quarreling with M. Paul once more, a contradiction I could not prevent.

His customary evening ritual involved bursting unannounced into the silent hours of study, imposing a sudden despotism. Books were stowed, workbags produced, and in lieu of sleepy, pious lectures, he substituted grand tragedies read with ardent fervor. He used literature as a vessel for his own passionate outpourings, pruning any passage deemed unsuitable for a congregation of young girls, often improvising superior dialogue where cuts would have left voids.

That evening, the room was silent as nuns in retreat. We heard the sharp bell, the familiar tread, and the words "Voilà Monsieur!" Before the double doors could fully part, he stood amidst us. Two study tables were arranged with lamps overhead. Teachers sat at the ends, girls flanking them. As per custom, he offered a chair to Zélie St. Pierre, the senior mistress, who smiled broadly, displaying every tooth in a sharp, thin curve that failed to reach her eyes. However, M. Paul, capricious as ever, ignored her, citing his new glasses as an excuse for oversight. Passing Zélie, he moved to the other side of the table.

Before I could rise to clear the path, he whispered, "Do not move," and positioned himself between me and Miss Fanshawe, my usual neighbor. It was easy to comply, yet difficult to endure her proximity. I cleared my workspace, withdrawing to create space for him, leaving barely a yard between us. M. Paul, flint and tinder, ignited instantly.

"You do not wish me for a neighbor," he growled, "you assume airs of caste; you treat me as a pariah." Scowling, he declared, "Very well, I shall arrange matters!"

He ordered the girls to rise and file to the other table. Placing me at one extreme of the long bench, he meticulously brought me my basket, silk, and scissors, then seated himself at the opposite end. This absurd isolation drew no laughter; the atmosphere was too tense. I sat, mind on my work, undisturbed.

"Is this sufficient distance?" he demanded.

"The arbiter is Monsieur," I replied.

"You know it is not," he countered. "You created this vast void; I laid not a finger upon it."

With this, he began reading. He had selected a French translation of what he termed "a drama of William Shakespeare; the false god," dismissing the English as foolish pagans. The translation was inefficient, and I could not suppress the contempt some of its failures aroused. Though I could not speak, I could look. My glances were scrutinized by his vigilant "lunettes." Consequently, he abandoned his screen, his eyes blazing free, growing hotter at his northern exile than the sun under Cancer.

When the reading concluded, it was unclear if he would express his anger. He rose to depart, then returned briefly to retrieve his pencil-case. Breaking a lead against wood, recutting it, and pocketing it, he walked promptly to me. Standing behind me, while the others talked freely nearby, he inquired about my work.

"A watchguard," I answered. "For a gentleman—one of my friends."
