Miss Clack respectfully informs Mr. Franklin Blake that she has sent him the fifth installment of her modest account, and in doing so, expresses her deep sorrow at being unable to fully convey the gravity of Lady Verinder’s passing—especially under such distressing circumstances. She feels ill-equipped to expand upon the tragedy as she would wish, and thus has appended to her manuscript a selection of excerpts drawn from cherished writings in her possession, all of which touch upon this dreadful event. May these excerpts, she earnestly hopes, serve as a clarion call to her esteemed relative, Mr. Franklin Blake.

Mr. Franklin Blake returns his regards to Miss Clack and thanks her for the fifth chapter of her narrative. He acknowledges receipt of the accompanying excerpts and, without expressing personal disapproval of such literature, politely declines their inclusion in the final record. He maintains that no additional material is required to fulfill the purpose he has set for himself.

Miss Clack acknowledges the return of her excerpts and gently reminds Mr. Blake that, as a Christian woman, she cannot be offended by anything he may say or do. Her concern for Mr. Blake remains steadfast, and she offers, should illness ever overtake him, to lend him her collection of excerpts once more. Before proceeding with the concluding chapters of her story, she seeks clarification: may she now incorporate insights gained from later developments in the Moonstone mystery, to complete her humble contribution?

Mr. Franklin Blake regrets having to decline again. He reiterates the original instruction given when Miss Clack began her narrative: she is to confine herself strictly to her own observations and experiences as recorded in her diary. Any information derived from subsequent discoveries must be left to those who have witnessed them firsthand.

Miss Clack writes once more, regretting the need to disturb Mr. Blake further. Her excerpts have been returned, and her reflections on the Moonstone have been barred from inclusion. While she recognizes, in worldly terms, that she has been rebuffed, she insists—having learned perseverance through hardship—that she will not be deterred. She asks whether Mr. Blake’s restrictions extend to the letters they have exchanged. Since their correspondence now forms part of her narrative, she believes it only fair that she be allowed to present them unaltered, allowing the reader to judge their meaning for themselves.

Mr. Franklin Blake agrees, on condition that this communication marks the end of their exchange. He requests that Miss Clack regard his consent as final.

In response, Miss Clack states that, as a matter of Christian duty before their correspondence concludes, she must inform Mr. Blake that his last letter—clearly meant to wound—has failed in its intent. She urges him to retire to his private chamber and reflect on what kind of strength it takes to rise above insult, especially when one is weak and alone. If he can acknowledge the nobility of such resilience, she solemnly promises to return the full series of her excerpts to him.

No reply was ever received. Further comment is unnecessary.

(Signed) DRUSILLA CLACK
