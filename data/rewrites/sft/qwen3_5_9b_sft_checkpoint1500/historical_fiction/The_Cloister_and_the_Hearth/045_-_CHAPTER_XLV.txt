The room at the hosier's house, previously filled with the low murmur of a family gathering, fell abruptly silent as the mother entered. Her return carried a weight that silenced the men immediately seated around the table. With a gaze that pierced through the pretense of the gathering, she placed a single sheet of paper flat upon the wood. Though illiterate, she recited the contents of the letter from memory, tracing the ink with a finger that trembled slightly, feigning the ability to read the very words she had just heard spoken aloud. Then, her head snapped up, and a second, sharper scrutiny landed upon Cornelis and Sybrandt. Their eyes dropped instantly to the floor.

In that instant, the tempest which had been gathering within the household for weeks broke loose, drowning everything in its fury. Catherine, the matriarch, seemed to inflate before them like an enraged hen fluffing her plumage, her voice rising into a torrent of wisdom and nonsense, a chaotic mix of high-minded invectives and petty scolding that no man could possibly sustain in a single flow, let alone most women.

"I have harbored suspicions for years," she cried, her breath coming in ragged, rapid bursts, "that you kindled the fire between Gerard and your father, and set that old knave, Ghysbrecht, to stoke it. And now, here stands Gerard's own handwriting to confirm your treachery. You have driven your own flesh and blood across seas, stripping me of the darling who was the pride of my eye and the joy of my heart. Yet, you remain a perfect unit from stem to stern. When we were all lads, my other children brought comfort, but you were a plague—mischievous and cunning. It took a woman half a day merely to mend your garments, for why? Labor wears down cloth, but play tears it apart. Wisdom arrives with the beard, but it never reached you; you were still the last to retire and the last to rise. Why? Because honesty sleeps early, and industry wakes before dawn, whereas two idlers in a house breed a pair of failures. I have gazed upon your conduct and marveled at your origin; you bear neither your father's likeness nor mine—not even as a wasp resembles an ant. Surely you were swapped in the cradle, or perhaps a cuckoo bird deposited you upon my floor, for you possess neither our hands nor our hearts. Of all my offspring, none but you have mocked those whom God afflicted. While I turned my back, I heard you sneer at Giles for lacking stature, and at my flower-like daughter Kate for not being strong as a Flemish mare. After that, robbing a church would seem easy to you! In the sight of Him who created Kate and Giles, and in the sight of Mine, who suffered for them, you are nothing but paltry, unfeeling, treasonous curs!"

She paused, breathing heavily, then continued with relentless intensity. "No, I shall not stop, my daughter. The cup has been filled to the brim. It takes a mountain of stone to turn a mother's heart against the sons she has nurtured upon her knees. Many times I have closed my eyes and seen too much, biting back my tongue so that your father might not discover the truth and cast you forth like dogs. But now, the measure is full. And whence did you acquire this fortune? For the past month, you have rolled in wealth. You earned not a penny of it. May I never hear from another lip how you procured it! It began the night you stayed out so late, returning with a swollen head, Cornelis. Sloth and greed are a fatal pairing, my masters. Those who love money must either sweat or steal. If you plundered any poor soul, it must have been a woman, for I'd lay my life on it a man would drive you off with a bare hand. Be that as it may, it serves one purpose. It reveals how you would manage affairs if they ever passed into your keeping. I have watched you closely, my lads. Today, you squandered a groat between you, while I, who keep you all, good and bad, scarcely spend a groat per week. I abandon hope of seeing the shoes that might follow your coffins. This shop and this home shall never belong to you. Gerard is our heir—the poor Gerard whom you have exiled and done your utmost to destroy. From this hour, never address me as 'mother' again! But you have made him ten times dearer to me. My lost boy! Soon I shall behold him once more, hold him in my embrace, and seat him upon my knees. Yes, you may stare! You are too shrewd, yet not shrewd enough. You severed the stalk, but you left the seed—a seed that shall outgrow you and outlive you. Margaret Brandt is swift, and the child within her is Gerard's, and whatever belongs to Gerard is mine. I have prayed the saints for a son, and it shall be so—it must be. When I learned the truth, my bowels yearned over her unborn child as if it were my own. He is our heir. He will survive us. You will not; for a wicked heart within a rotting carcass is like the worm in the nut, swiftly bringing the body to dust. So, Kate, fetch down Gerard's bib and tucker from the drawer you know of. One day, we shall carry them to Sevenbergen. We will borrow Peter Buyskens' cart and go to comfort Gerard's wife beneath her burden. She is his wife. What power does Ghysbrecht Van Swieten hold? Can he stand between a couple and the altar, and sever those united by God and the priest? She is my daughter, and I am as proud of her as I am of you, Kate. As for you, keep clear of my sight for a time, for you are like the black dog that haunts my vision."

Cornelis and Sybrandt seized the subtle warning, slipping quietly from the room, their steps heavy with a mixture of remorse, impotence, and hatred. They avoided her gaze as diligently as ever. For many days thereafter, she said not a single word—neither kind nor cruel nor indifferent—to either of them. Her soul, finally, was free.
