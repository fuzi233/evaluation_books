To understand the architecture of Shirley's spirit, one must first comprehend the absolute isolation of her social universe. She demonstrated the sincerity of her desire for Caroline's company by relentlessly pursuing it; indeed, without this active pursuit, the connection would never have formed, for Miss Helstone was a woman constructed of hesitation, believing herself invisible to the world and incapable of amusement. To the heiress of Fieldhead—a brilliant, radiant creature seemingly untouched by the mundane constraints of society—the slow, reticent Miss Helstone appeared utterly unremarkable, a figure too detached from the vibrant currents of life to ever find welcome in her own quiet orbit.

Yet, Shirley was not immune to the gravitational pull of genial society. Within a month, she had mapped the landscape of local families, establishing cordial ties with the Misses Sykes, the Misses Pearson, and the formidable Misses Wynne of Walden Hall. Still, she found none among them truly congenial; she fraternized with none, using her own words to describe the transaction. Even if she had possessed the divine right of being Shirley Keeldar, Esquire, Lord of the Manor of Briarfield, there was not a single fair maiden in the parish or its neighbors whom she would have deemed worthy of becoming Lady Keeldar. She voiced this radical declaration to Mrs. Pryor, who received it with the stoic silence of a veteran educator, dismissing the notion gently. "Do not allow that habit of alluding to yourself as a gentleman to be confirmed," Mrs. Pryor advised, noting that such speech patterns would suggest an affectation of masculine manners to the uninformed ear.

Shirley never mocked her former governess; even the minor formalities and peculiarities of that lady commanded respect in her eyes. To ridicule quiet worth would be to reveal oneself as weak, a character devoid of substance. Thus, she accepted the reproof in silence. Standing near the window, she watched a grand cedar on her lawn, observing a bird perched on a lower bough. Soon, she began to chirrup, the sound clarifying until it transformed into a whistle, executed with such sweetness and technical dexterity that it cut through the air.

"My dear!" Mrs. Pryor exclaimed, breaking the spell.

"Was I whistling?" Shirley asked, startled. "I forgot. I beg your pardon, ma'am. I had resolved to take care not to whistle before you."

"But, Miss Keeldar, where did you learn to whistle? You must have acquired the habit since descending into Yorkshire. I never knew you guilty of it before."

"Oh! I learned to whistle a long while ago."

"Who taught you?"

"No one. I took it up by listening, and I had laid it down again. But lately, yesterday evening, as I was coming up our lane, I heard a gentleman whistling that very tune in the field on the other side of the hedge, and that reminded me."

"What gentleman was it?"

"We have only one gentleman in this region, ma'am, and that is Mr. Moore—at least, he is the only gentleman who is not gray-haired. My two venerable favorites, Mr. Helstone and Mr. Yorke, are indeed fine old beaus, infinitely superior to any of the stupid young ones."

Mrs. Pryor fell silent.

"You do not like Mr. Helstone, ma'am?"

"My dear, Mr. Helstone's office secures him from criticism."

"You generally contrive to leave the room when he is announced."

"Do you walk out this morning, my dear?"

"Yes, I shall go to the rectory, seek and find Caroline Helstone, and compel her to take some exercise. She shall have a breezy walk over Nunnely Common."

"If you go in that direction, my dear, have the goodness to remind Miss Helstone to wrap up well, as there is a fresh wind, and she appears to me to require care."

"You shall be minutely obeyed, Mrs. Pryor. Meantime, will you not accompany us yourself?"

"No, my love; I should be a restraint upon you. I am stout, and cannot walk so quickly as you would wish to do."

Shirley easily persuaded Caroline to join her. Once they were fairly out on the quiet road, traversing the extensive and solitary sweep of Nunnely Common, she effortlessly drew her into conversation. Having overcome the initial feelings of diffidence, Caroline soon found herself glad to talk with Miss Keeldar. The mere exchange of slight observations sufficed to define each other's essence. Shirley expressed her delight in the green expanse of the common turf, but preferred the heath on its ridges, for the heath reminded her of the moors she had witnessed while traveling on the Scottish borders. She recalled a specific district traversed one long afternoon during a sultry yet sunless summer day. They journeyed from noon until sunset over what seemed a boundless waste of deep heath, seeing nothing but wild sheep and hearing nothing but the cries of wild birds.

"I know how the heath would look on such a day," said Caroline. "Purple-black—a deeper shade of the sky-tint, and that would be livid."

"Yes, quite livid, with brassy edges to the clouds, and here and there a white gleam, more ghastly than the lurid tinge, which, as you looked at it, you momentarily expected would kindle into blinding lightning."

"Did it thunder?"

"It muttered distant peals, but the storm did not break till evening, after we had reached our inn—that inn being an isolated house at the foot of a range of mountains."

"Did you watch the clouds come down over the mountains?"

"I did. I stood at the window an hour watching them. The hills seemed rolled in a sullen mist, and when the rain fell in whitening sheets, suddenly they were blotted from the prospect; they were washed from the world."

"I have seen such storms in hilly districts in Yorkshire; and at their riotous climax, while the sky was all cataract, the earth all flood, I have remembered the Deluge."

"It is singularly reviving after such hurricanes to feel calm return, and from the opening clouds to receive a consolatory gleam, softly testifying that the sun is not quenched."

"Miss Keeldar, just stand still now, and look down at Nunnely dale and wood."

They both halted on the green brow of the common. They looked down on the deep valley robed in May raiment; on varied meads, some pearled with daisies, and some golden with king-cups. Today, all this young verdure smiled clear in sunlight; transparent emerald and amber gleams played over it. On Nunnwood—the sole remnant of antique British forest in a region whose lowlands were once all silvan chase, as its highlands were breast-deep heather—slept the shadow of a cloud; the distant hills were dappled, the horizon shaded and tinted like mother-of-pearl; silvery blues, soft purples, evanescent greens, and rose-shades melted into fleeces of white cloud, pure as azury snow, alluring the eye as with a remote glimpse of heaven's foundations. The air blowing on the brow was fresh, sweet, and bracing.

"Our England is a bonny island," said Shirley, "and Yorkshire is one of her bonniest nooks."

"You are a Yorkshire girl too?"

"I am—Yorkshire in blood and birth. Five generations of my race sleep under the aisles of Briarfield Church. I drew my first breath in the old black hall behind us."

Caroline presented her hand, which was accordingly taken and shaken. "We are compatriots," she said.

"Yes," agreed Shirley, with a grave nod.

"And that," asked Miss Keeldar, pointing to the forest—"that is Nunnwood?"

"It is."

"Were you ever there?"

"Many a time."

"In the heart of it?"

"Yes."

"What is it like?"

"It is like an encampment of forest sons of Anak. The trees are huge and old. When you stand at their roots, the summits seem in another region. The trunks remain still and firm as pillars, while the boughs sway to every breeze. In the deepest calm their leaves are never quite hushed, and in high wind a flood rushes, a sea thunders above you."

"Was it not one of Robin Hood's haunts?"

"Yes, and there are mementos of him still existing. To penetrate into Nunnwood, Miss Keeldar, is to go far back into the dim days of old. Can you see a break in the forest, about the centre?"

"Yes, distinctly."

"That break is a dell—a deep, hollow cup, lined with turf as green and short as the sod of this common. The very oldest of the trees, gnarled mighty oaks, crowd about the brink of this dell. In the bottom lie the ruins of a nunnery."

"We will go—you and I alone, Caroline—to that wood, early some fine summer morning, and spend a long day there. We can take pencils and sketch-books, and any interesting reading book we like; and of course we shall take something to eat. I have two little baskets, in which Mrs. Gill, my housekeeper, might pack our provisions, and we could each carry our own. It would not tire you too much to walk so far?"
