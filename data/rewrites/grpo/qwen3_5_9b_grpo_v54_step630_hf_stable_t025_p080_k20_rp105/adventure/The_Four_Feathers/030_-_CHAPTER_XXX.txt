The wind cut sharp and biting from the north, driving the camels into a frantic trot.

"Push them harder," Trench gritted out, his jaw clenched tight. "Idris could have lost us already."

"Even if he has," Feversham replied, though his voice lacked conviction, "it takes time to rally men, to fetch mounts, and it is already dark."

He spoke with forced hope, yet his gaze kept snapping back toward the distant, fading glow of Omdurman. The rhythmic tapping of drums had ceased, offering a grim sort of relief. Now, they moved through a silence so profound it felt heavy, a landscape where speed produced no sound. There would be no thunder of hooves to signal pursuit. Yet, at any moment, Ansar riders could be closing in within thirty paces. Feversham strained his neck, searching the black void for the glimmer of a white turban. Trench, however, refused to look back. He rode with a rigid, forward focus, though the terror gripping him was deeper than Feversham's. Trench did not dare turn his head; his eyes were locked on the horizon, yet his mind was trapped in the nightmare behind them—the narrow streets, the scattered fires, the frantic shadows of soldiers hunting the two prisoners who had fled the House of Stone.

When Feversham finally spoke, breaking Trench's trance, the other did not turn.

"What is it?"

"I no longer see the fires of Omdurman."

"The golden blot, eh? Very low down?" Trench murmured, his voice detached. Feversham didn't press for an explanation, nor could Trench have given one. The words surfaced suddenly, carrying a strange appropriateness: the vision that had been the final thing to haunt Feversham's eyes as he left Omdurman was now the last thing to vanish from his sight as the mission concluded. They fell silent until two figures emerged from the darkness at their camels' feet.

"Instanna!" Abou Fatma whispered urgently.

They halted, forcing the beasts to kneel.

"The new camels?" Abou Fatma asked. Two men vanished briefly, returning with four fresh mounts. While the saddles were stripped from the old camels and fitted onto the new ones, Feversham asked, "Are they good?"

"Anafi breed," Abou Fatma confirmed, glancing anxiously eastward. "Quick! Quick!"

"The arms?" Trench demanded, diving to the ground to scour the sand. "Where are they?"

"In a moment," Abou Fatma assured him, though Trench seemed unable to wait. He displayed more frantic energy handling the weapons than he had showing fear of capture.

"Ammunition?" he asked, his voice rising in feverish pitch.

"Yes, rifles, revolvers, ammunition."

Abou Fatma led them twenty yards into the tall desert grass. He knelt and dug into the soft earth with his bare hands.

"Here."

Trench threw himself beside him, scooping furiously with both hands, emitting an inhuman, whining sound like a hound at a scent. To Feversham, the scene was horrifying; Trench had sunk into an animal state, his movements jerky and desperate. Moments later, Trench's fingers brushed the trigger of a rifle, and he snapped back to humanity. Standing upright, he held the weapon as if it were a living thing. The rest of the arms were unearthed, the ammo distributed.

"Now," Trench breathed, a thrill of pure joy breaking through his exhaustion. "Now I don't care. Let them follow from Omdurman! One thing is certain: I shall never return there. Not even if they catch us." He stroked the rifle affectionately.

Two Arabs mounted the old camels and rode slowly back toward the city. Abou Fatma stayed with the fugitives. They mounted and pushed northeast. Less than fifteen minutes had passed since they halted.

All night they rode through halfa grass and mimosa trees, moving slowly until sunrise brought them to flat, barren ground dotted with small hills.

"Are the Effendis tired?" Abou Fatma asked. "We can eat here. Food is on the saddles."

"No," Feversham said. "We eat as we go."

Dates, bread, and water from a zamsheyeh formed their meal as they sat atop their camels. Freed from the dense grass, the beasts trotted at full speed. By sunset, they stopped for an hour, then rode again through the night and the following day, pushing their endurance and that of the animals to the limit. They climbed rocky heights, crossed valleys, and galloped across honey-colored sands. Yet, to each man, the pace felt agonizingly slow, like a funeral procession. A mountain would rise above the horizon at dawn, loom before them for the entire day, and never move a single inch closer. Occasionally, a farmer tending a patch of sorghum would make their hearts leap, forcing a wide detour; or a distant caravan would be spotted by Abou Fatma's keen eyes, sending them crouching behind rocks with rifles ready.

Ten miles from Abu Klea, a relay of fresh camels awaited them. They traveled west of the Nile, passing through the Ababdeh desert until a broad grey tract stretched across their path.

"The road from Berber to Merowi," Abou Fatma noted. "North of it, we turn east to the river. We cross tonight; if God wills, we'll be across by tomorrow evening."

"If God wills," Trench echoed, his fear growing as they neared safety. "If only He wills."

They were in caravan territory; daylight travel was no longer safe. They dismounted and hid behind a belt of shrubs on high ground, watching the road like specks moving below. They crossed in the dark and pushed hard toward the river through the night.

At dawn, Abou Fatma ordered another halt. They stood in a desolate, open expanse where the flatness offered no cover. Feversham and Trench scanned the horizon to their right. The Nile flowed somewhere in that direction, but it remained invisible.

"We must build a stone circle," Abou Fatma instructed. "Lie close to the ground inside. I will go to the river to check the boat and our friends. I'll return after dark."

They gathered stones quickly, building a low wall a foot high. Inside, Feversham and Trench lay prone with water skins and rifles at their sides.

"You have dates?" Abou Fatma asked.

"Yes."

"Do not stir until I return. I'll take your camels and bring fresh ones in the evening." With his companion, he rode off toward the river.

Trench and Feversham dug out the sand within the circle and lay still, watching the horizon through gaps in the stones. For both, this was perhaps the longest day of their lives. So near to safety, yet not safe. To Trench, it felt longer than his night in the House of Stone; to Feversham, longer than any day he had spent waiting in his rooms above St. James's Park six years prior. They were close to Berber, and the pursuit must be hot on their heels. Feversham wondered how he had ever found the courage to face Berber then. The sun burned pitilessly on their backs, offering no shade within the narrow stone ring. They spoke little.

As the sun dipped, friendly darkness gathered, and a cool wind rustled through the desert.

"Listen!" Trench whispered.

Both men strained their ears, hearing the soft padding of camels nearby. A low whistle summoned them from their shelter.

"We are here," Feversham said quietly.

"God be thanked!" Abou Fatma exclaimed. "Good news and bad. The boat is ready, our friends are waiting, and camels are prepared on the track by the riverbank to Abu Hamed. But your escape is known; the roads and ferries are watched. Before sunrise, we must strike inland from the eastern bank."

They crossed the river cautiously around one in the morning, sinking the boat on the far side. Camels waited, but the journey inland was slow due to thick boulders. That night, they hid again in a stone ring while the camels grazed on high ground. By the next night, they made good progress, reaching the groves of Abu Hamed in two days. After resting for twelve hours, they mounted a fresh relay and headed across the great Nubian Desert.

Today, a traveler could traverse the two hundred and forty miles of that waterless plain of black rock and yellow sand in comfort, sleeping in a berth, passing stations marked by tents and telegraph poles. But in those days, progression was a struggle. They kept east of the current railway, following a line of wells among the hills.

On the second night of this leg, Trench shook Feversham awake.

"Look," he pointed south. "There is no Southern Cross tonight." His voice broke with emotion. "For six years, every night of those six years, I have seen it. How often I lay awake watching those four slanting stars, wondering if the night would ever come when I wouldn't see them! I tell you, Feversham, this is the first moment I have truly dared to think we might escape."

Both men sat up, watching the southern sky with hearts full of thanksgiving. When sleep finally claimed them, it was fitful, punctuated by fears that the constellation would blaze low on the horizon again, followed by confident dreams of their freedom.

Seven days later, they reached Shof-el-Ain, a tiny well in a barren valley between featureless ridges. Here, they camped. They were in the land of the Amrab Arabs, and their peril had ended.

"We are safe!" Abou Fatma cried. "God is good! North to Assouan, west to Wadi Halfa—we are safe!" Spreading a cloth before the kneeling camels, he heaped dhurra before them. In his gratitude, he patted one of the animals, which immediately turned and snarled.

Trench reached out his hand to Feversham.

"Thank you," he said simply.

"No need," Feversham replied, refusing the handshake. "I served myself from start to finish."

"You've learned the churlishness of a camel," Trench laughed. "It carries you where you want, till it drops dead, and yet if you show gratitude, it bites. Hang it all, Feversham, there's my hand."

Feversham untied a knot in his ragged jibbeh and pulled out three white feathers—two small heron feathers and one large ostrich feather broken from a fan.

"Will you take yours back?"

"Yes."

"You know what to do with it."

"Yes. There shall be no delay."

Feversham carefully wrapped the remaining feathers in a corner of his jibbeh and tied them secure.

"We shake hands, then," he said. As their hands finally met, he added, "Tomorrow morning we part company."

"Part company, you and I—after a year in Omdurman, weeks of flight?" Trench exclaimed. "Why? Castleton is dead. You keep the feather he sent, but he is gone. You can do nothing with it. You must come home."

"Yes," Feversham answered, "but after you, certainly not with you. You go on to Assouan and Cairo. Friends will welcome you there. I shall not go with you."

Trench fell silent. He understood Feversham's reluctance; it would be easier for Feversham to tell his story to Ethne Eustace without his presence.

"I ought to tell you no one knows why you resigned your commission, or of the feathers we sent. We never spoke of it. We agreed never to speak, for the honor of the regiment. I can't tell you how glad I am that we all kept that agreement," Trench said softly.

"Perhaps you will see Durrance," Feversham suggested. "If you do, give him a message. Tell him that the next time he asks me to come and see him, whether in England or Wadi Halfa, I will accept the invitation."

"Which way will you go?"

"To Wadi Halfa," Feversham said, pointing west. "I'll take Abou Fatma and travel slowly and quietly down the Nile. The other Arab will guide you to Assouan."

They slept that night in security beside the well. The next morning, they parted. Trench was the first to ride off. As his camel rose, he bent down toward Feversham, who passed him the nose rein.

"Ramelton, that was the name? I shall not forget."

"Yes, Ramelton," Feversham said. "There's a ferry across Lough Swilly to Rathmullen. You must drive the twelve miles to Ramelton. But you may not find her there."

"If not there, I shall find her somewhere else. Make no mistake, Feversham, I shall find her."

Trench rode forward, alone with his Arab guide. More than once he turned to see Feversham still standing by the well, drawn to stop and ride back to that solitary figure, but he contented himself with a wave. Even that salute was not returned.

Feversham had neither thought nor eyes for his companion. His six years of hard probation had ended that morning, yet he felt a greater sense of loss and vacancy than joy. For six years, through trials and falterings, his mission had sustained him. Now, it seemed, there was nothing left to occupy his life. Ethne? She was surely married... and a bitterness of despair rose within him for that futile, unnecessary mistake made six years ago. He saw again the room in London overlooking the quiet trees of St. James's Park, heard the knock on the door, and took the telegram from his servant's hand.
