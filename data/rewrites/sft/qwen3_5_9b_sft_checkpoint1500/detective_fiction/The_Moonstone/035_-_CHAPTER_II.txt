My immediate priority now is to lay out whatever fragments of intelligence I hold regarding the Moonstone—or, to be precise, concerning the Indian conspiracy to abscond with the diamond. Though scant, these details bear a striking significance upon the events yet to unfold.

Approximately ten days following Miss Verinder's departure, a clerk entered my private study carrying a calling card. A gentleman awaited in the antechamber, wishing to confer with me. Upon examining the card, I noted a foreign designation that had since faded from my recollection. Beneath it, however, a line remained etched clearly in ink: *"Recommended by Mr. Septimus Luker."*

The sheer audacity of a man in Mr. Luker's station to presume authority over me struck me with sudden paralysis. For a heartbeat, I sat frozen, questioning the fidelity of my vision. The clerk, perceiving my confusion, offered his assessment of the visitor awaiting downstairs. "Sir, he is a most peculiar specimen," the clerk remarked. "His complexion is so deeply swarthy that the entire staff initially mistook him for an East Indian, or perhaps someone of similar extraction."

Connecting the clerk's observation with the inscription on the card, a hypothesis formed within me: the Moonstone itself may have been the catalyst for both Mr. Luker's endorsement and this stranger's intrusion. To the clerk's evident shock, I immediately granted an audience to the gentleman below.

To justify this lapse in professional decorum, driven purely by curiosity, I need only recall that no living soul in England possesses an intimacy with the saga of the Indian Diamond comparable to mine. I was entrusted with Colonel Herncastle's escape plans against assassination; I carried his periodic dispatches confirming his survival; I drafted his will, devising the trust to pass the stone to Miss Verinder; I convinced his executor to proceed under the belief that the jewel would enrich her family; and finally, I dissuaded Mr. Franklin Blake from scruple, guiding him to transport the gem to Lady Verinder's residence. If anyone holds a prescriptive right to the Moonstone and its entanglements, it must surely be me.

As soon as the mysterious client was ushered in, an internal certainty seized me: I stood before one of the three Indians—likely their leader. Though attired in impeccable European fashion, his tanned skin, lithe stature, and a gravity of demeanor marked with Eastern grace betrayed his origins to any discerning observer. I indicated a chair and requested the nature of his business.

First, offering a profound apology in flawless English for the disturbance, the Indian presented a small parcel wrapped in cloth of gold. Unwrapping this, then a layer of silk, he revealed a tiny box, exquisitely inlaid with jewels upon an ebony base, setting it upon my desk.

"I have come, sir," he stated, "to request a loan of funds. And I leave this casket as assurance that my debt shall be repaid."

I gestured toward his card. "And you seek an audience with me," I replied, "solely upon the recommendation of Mr. Luker?"

The Indian bowed low.

"Pray, why did Mr. Luker himself not advance the sum you require?"

"Mr. Luker informed me, sir, that he possessed no funds to lend."

"And therefore, he directed you to me?"

Again, the Indian pointed to the card. "It is written there," he said.

Brief, direct, and utterly purposeful! Had the Moonstone lain in my possession, this Oriental gentleman would have slain me without a second thought, I knew full well. Yet, barring that lethal risk, I must attest that he was the paragon of a client. He might not have cherished my life, but he observed a discipline absent in all my dealings with countrymen: he respected my time.

"I regret," I said, "your necessity to trouble me. Mr. Luker is entirely mistaken in sending you here. While I am, like others in my trade, trusted with capital, I neither lend to strangers nor accept such paltry securities."

Far from pleading my case, the Indian merely bowed again, reverting the casket to its double wrappings without a murmur of protest. Rising—the admirable assassin rising to depart the instant I had answered—he turned toward the door.

"Will your courtesy towards a stranger permit me one inquiry before I withdraw?" he asked.

I inclined my head. One question at parting! My average fare had been fifty.

"Suppose, sir, that circumstances and custom had allowed *you* to grant me the loan," he posed, "what period would have been deemed necessary and customary for *my* repayment?"

"By the standard practices of this nation," I answered, "you would be entitled to settle the principal within twelve months from the date of advancement."

The Indian bowed one final, deep reverence, and then, with a softness and silence akin to a stalking cat, he slipped from the room.

It was executed in a moment, a noiseless, supple motion that slightly jarred me. Once composure returned, a singular conclusion crystallized regarding this enigmatic visitor. His countenance, voice, and manner, while I held him captive, were mastered with absolute perfection, defying all scrutiny. Yet, he had afforded me a glimpse beneath his polished exterior. Until I spoke of the customary timeline for early repayment, he showed no sign of registering a single word I uttered. The instant I delivered that piece of information, he fixed me squarely in the eye. I deduced that his final query was not casual; it was engineered specifically to elicit that precise answer. The deeper I reflected on our exchange, the more shrewdly I suspected that the offer of the casket and the plea for a loan were mere theatrical preambles, designed solely to facilitate the departing interrogation.

Having confirmed the validity of this deduction, I sought to pierce further into the Indian's motives when a letter arrived, proving to be from no less a person than Mr. Septimus Luker himself. He implored my forgiveness in tones of sickening subservience, promising to elucidate affairs to my satisfaction should I consent to a personal meeting.

Thus, I committed another unprofessional sacrifice to mere curiosity, scheduling an appointment for the following day.

Mr. Luker, in every dimension, was a creature far inferior to the Indian—vulgar, hideous, cringing, and tedious—rendering him wholly unworthy of detailed chronicle here. The substance of his confession ran as follows:

The day prior to the Indian's visit, Mr. Luker had received an audience from that accomplished gentleman. Despite the visitor's European guise, Mr. Luker instantly recognized him as the chief of the three Indians who had previously haunted his estate, leaving him no recourse but to consult a magistrate. This revelation sent a tremor through him; he reasoned, naturally enough, that he must be in the company of the men who had blindfolded him, gagged him, and stolen his banker's receipt. Paralyzed by terror, he became convinced his end was nigh.

While the Indian maintained the mask of a perfect stranger, presenting the casket and making the identical application he later directed to me, Mr. Luker, seeking the swiftest liberation from his dread, immediately declared his inability to lend. Whereupon, the Indian inquired of the safest avenue for securing the needed funds. Mr. Luker, trembling, advised that a respectable solicitor was the optimal choice. When pressed to name an individual, Mr. Luker blurted out my name—the sole reason being that, in the throes of his panic, it was the first name that surfaced in his mind. "Perspiration poured off me like rain, sir," the wretch concluded. "I knew not what I uttered. I pray you review this, Mr. Bruff, in mercy for having been truly frightened out of my wits."

I pardoned the fellow gracefully, the quickest path to dismissing him. Before he departed, I detained him for a final probe. Had the Indian uttered anything notable at the moment of leaving Mr. Luker's domicile?

"Yes!" Mr. Luker stammered. "He put precisely the same question to me, at parting, which he asked you; receiving, of course, the identical answer."

"What did it signify?" Mr. Luker's explanation offered no aid toward solving the riddle. My own unassisted ingenuity, called upon next, proved woefully inadequate to grapple with the difficulty. That evening, I had a dinner engagement; I ascended to my chambers in a mood ill-suited to joy, little suspecting that the route to my dressing-gown and the path to truth were, on this fateful occasion, one and the same.
