The coroner, a man of ample frame and genial disposition, was determined to rise to the occasion. For one day—just one—he would be the center of national attention, and he meant to conduct himself with the dignity befitting such a moment. He had long appreciated the theatrical side of his role, and now that a mysterious death had occurred within his jurisdiction, he felt nothing short of triumphant. His ability to organize facts was matched by a gift for oratory, which could sway juries like puppets on strings. Occasionally, this eloquence veiled a questionable grasp of legal nuance, but today no one seemed to mind.

The inquest took place in a long, bare room appended to the hotel—a space originally intended for balls and concerts. Rows of reporters occupied the front seats, their pens poised, their eyes sharp. Behind the coroner’s table sat the witnesses, awaiting their turn. To either side, the jury stood in two neat lines, their hair carefully combed, their expressions stiffly neutral, as if they’d been cast from the same mold. The rest of the room was filled with townspeople who watched in hushed reverence as the proceedings began. The journalists exchanged murmurs, some confirming among themselves that the famed detective Trent was not yet present.

The first witness called was Mrs. Manderson. She stepped forward with quiet dignity, lifting her veil just before speaking. Her face was pale, but composed—no trace of hysteria, only a deep stillness that spoke of inner strength. There was something profoundly feminine about her, yet she carried herself with an unyielding resolve. She touched her eyes once or twice with a handkerchief, but her voice remained steady throughout.

She described how her husband had returned to their bedroom around his usual bedtime on Sunday night. Their rooms were connected by a door left open during the night, though both had separate entrances from the hall. Mr. Manderson had always favored simplicity—sleeping in a small, unadorned space. Mrs. Manderson had not been fully awake when he came up, but she stirred slightly when the light in his room switched on. She recalled speaking to him, though she couldn’t remember exactly what she said. She did recall that he had gone out for a moonlit drive and she had asked if it had been a good run, and what time it was. She had expected him to stay out late, so she was surprised when he told her it was half-past eleven.

“And did he explain why he hadn:’t gone?” the coroner asked gently.

“Yes,” she replied. “He said he had changed his mind. I remember it clearly because—” She paused, visibly flustered.

“Because?” prompted the coroner.

“Because my husband was not usually inclined to talk about business,” she said, raising her chin slightly. “He thought it wouldn’t interest me, and he rarely mentioned it. That’s why I found it unusual when he told me he had sent Mr. Marlowe to Southampton to retrieve important information from a man leaving for Paris the next day. He said Marlowe could do it easily—if there was no accident. Then he added that he had started in the car, but walked back home a mile or so, and felt better for it.”

“Did he say anything else?”

“Not that I remember. I was very sleepy. I fell asleep again shortly after. I only recall him turning off the light. That was the last time I saw him alive.”

She gave no further details, her tone flat and final. But the coroner wasn’t finished.

“Mrs. Manderson,” he said, his voice soft but firm, “I must ask a question that may be painful under these circumstances. Was there, over the past months, a breakdown in your relationship with your husband? Were you estranged?”

She straightened, color rising in her cheeks.

“If it is necessary,” she answered, her voice cold and clear, “then I will answer plainly. During the last few months, my husband’s behavior toward me caused me great sorrow and anxiety. He became distant, reserved, almost suspicious. I saw less of him than ever before. He preferred solitude. I cannot explain why. I tried to maintain my dignity while reaching out to him, but I never asked directly what troubled him. My pride kept me from doing so. I simply continued being the wife I had always been—so far as he would allow. And now I shall never know what lay between us.”

Her voice trembled slightly at the end. She lowered her veil and stood motionless.

One juror ventured a question, hesitantly.

“Was there ever any kind of… words… between you and your husband, ma’am?”

“Never,” she replied, the word spoken without emotion—but the silence that followed spoke volumes. Everyone sensed the depth of her restraint, and the absurdity of the question.

The coroner then asked if she knew of anything else that might have weighed heavily on her husband’s mind.

“No,” she said simply. “Nothing.”

Her testimony concluded, she withdrew quietly. The room turned its attention to Martin, the next witness.

It was then that Trent entered through the doorway, slipping into the crowd unnoticed. He didn’t look at Martin. Instead, his gaze fixed on a woman moving swiftly through the throng—Mrs. Manderson. His expression darkened. As he stepped aside, bowing slightly, she approached him.

“I wanted to ask you,” she said in a low, fragile voice, “if you would give me your arm for a little way. I can’t see my uncle near the door, and suddenly I feel faint… I’ll be better outside…”

“No,” she insisted when he began to suggest otherwise. “I must go to the house. Please.”

Her hand gripped his arm briefly—tight enough to pull him along, yet weak enough to lean upon him. With bowed head and slow steps, she moved toward the exit, guided by Trent. He walked beside her in silence, thoughts racing like storm-winds behind a calm mask. Every suspicion, every clue he had gathered, surged through his mind. But above all, the sensation of her cool fingers against his skin burned in his memory—both comforting and infuriating.

When they reached White Gables, she sank onto a couch in the morning room. Removing her veil, she looked up at him with genuine gratitude.

“I’m much better now,” she said. “A cup of tea will restore me completely. I’m sorry I took you away from something important.”

She smiled faintly. “I thought I could handle it, but those last questions… I didn’t expect them to shake me so. And all those men staring at the door…” She paused. “Thank you for helping me when I asked.”

Trent nodded, saying nothing. He left soon after, his hand still tingling from her touch.

The remaining testimonies—the servants’, the finder of the body, the police—added little new to the public record. But Bunner’s account, delivered with quiet conviction, became the highlight of the day. He repeated what he had told Trent: the risks taken by industrialists in America, the tensions between labor and capital, and his belief that Manderson had been targeted. The press captured every word, and the story spread across Britain and the United States with startling speed.

Public opinion paid no heed to the coroner’s subtle hint—offered in his closing remarks—that suicide could not be ruled out. He had pointed out that no weapon was found near the body, and that the medical evidence suggested the wound was not self-inflicted due to the lack of powder residue or close-range marks. Dr. Stock had testified that death occurred six to eight hours before discovery, caused by a bullet entering the left eye and shattering the base of the brain. The external wound showed no signs of the gun being pressed against the face, though it was technically possible the victim fired it himself at a slight distance.

Still, the doctor noted that bruises and scratches on the wrists and forearms were recent—clear signs of violence. The body had been found in a collapsed position, consistent with a fatal shot, but the injuries indicated a struggle.

The coroner emphasized Bunner’s testimony, not as proof, but as a point for consideration. “Can we say,” he asked the jury, “that the deceased was under threat? Was he more vulnerable than an ordinary man? And does the change in his behavior—his sudden anxiety—suggest that his final days were marked by fear?”

With that, he left the jury to deliberate.

And thus, the weight of the case shifted—not just to the facts, but to the shadows behind them.
