When Deronda carried his letter into the banking-house in the Schuster Strasse at Mainz and asked for Joseph Kalonymos, he was led through the public hush into an inner room where the air changed—less of street-dust, more of paper and sealing-wax, that particular scent of money handled honestly and often. Beyond the door the city’s clatter thinned to a dull percussion, as if the walls had been built not merely to guard accounts, but to spare the mind from being rasped raw by noise.

At a table sat the white-bearded man Deronda remembered from the synagogue at Frankfort: the same old felt hat on his head, the same intent posture, as if every letter he touched had a pulse. Open envelopes lay in ordered disorder before him. Beside his chair was a packed portmanteau with a wrap and overcoat laid across it—travel already folded into readiness.

Kalonymos rose when Deronda entered, but he did not advance or offer his hand. He looked sharply, his small eyes bright and hard as dark stones set in the yellowed hollows of his face, and in German he said, “Good! It is now you who seek me, young man.”

“Yes; I seek you with gratitude, as a friend of my grandfather’s,” Deronda answered, the German coming to him without struggle, that generous tongue which makes room even for the stranger’s accent. “And I am under an obligation to you for giving yourself much trouble on my account.”

Kalonymos’ gaze held him a moment longer, as if testing whether gratitude had weight or was merely a polished phrase. Then his manner shifted: he put out his hand, and the grip was sudden, warm, decided. “So you are no longer angry at being something more than an Englishman?”

“On the contrary. I thank you heartily for helping to save me from remaining in ignorance of my parentage, and for taking care of the chest that my grandfather left in trust for me.”

“Sit down, sit down,” Kalonymos said quickly, in an undertone that matched the room’s guarded stillness. He seated himself again and motioned to a chair close by.

Then, with deliberation, he laid aside his hat. Under it was a thick mass of white hair. He stroked and clutched his beard while he examined Deronda’s face, searching in it for a remembered contour, a line of will, a likeness to a dead friend that might justify long vigilance and trouble.

To Deronda, that scrutiny had a strange sweetness in it. It was as though the man’s hand on his beard and his eyes on Deronda’s brow were touching a live wire running back through years and continents, binding him to a hope formed before he had breathed. The room, the sealed letters, the packed luggage, the muffled city outside—all the ordinary apparatus of a life spent in transactions—seemed suddenly to serve a mythic office: a guardian at a threshold, keeping safe a name and a story against the world’s forgetfulness.

Kalonymos spoke in Hebrew, quoting from the liturgy with the cadence of a remembered chant, and in the quiet the syllables sounded like something older than walls: “As thy goodness has been great to the former generations, even so may it be to the latter.”

After a pause he began again in German, briskly, as if time were a strict creditor. “Young man, I rejoice that I was not yet set off again on my travels, and that you are come in time for me to see the image of my friend as he was in his youth—no longer perverted from the fellowship of your people—no longer shrinking in proud wrath from the touch of him who seemed to be claiming you as a Jew. You come with thankfulness yourself to claim the kindred and heritage that wicked contrivance would have robbed you of. You come with a willing soul to declare, ‘I am the grandson of Daniel Charisi.’ Is it not so?”

“Assuredly it is,” Deronda said. “But let me say that I should at no time have been inclined to treat a Jew with incivility simply because he was a Jew. You can understand that I shrank from saying to a stranger, ‘I know nothing of my mother.’”

“A sin, a sin!” Kalonymos said, lifting his hand, his eyes closing in disgust as if at a taste gone rancid. “A robbery of our people—as when our youths and maidens were reared for the Roman Edom. But it is frustrated. I have frustrated it.”

He leaned forward, and the beard he had been grasping seemed to steady the flow of his speech, anchoring memory so it could not drift. “When Daniel Charisi—may his Rock and his Redeemer guard him!—when Daniel Charisi was a stripling and I was a lad little above his shoulder, we made a solemn vow always to be friends. He said, ‘Let us bind ourselves with duty, as if we were sons of the same mother.’ That was his bent from first to last—as he said, to fortify his soul with bonds. It was a saying of his, ‘Let us bind love with duty; for duty is the love of law; and law is the nature of the Eternal.’ So we bound ourselves. And though we were much apart in our later life, the bond has never been broken. When he was dead, they sought to rob him; but they could not rob him of me. I rescued that remainder of him which he had prized and preserved for his offspring. And I have restored to him the offspring they had robbed him of. I will bring you the chest forthwith.”

He went out for a few minutes. Deronda, left alone, listened to the building: the faint scratch of a pen somewhere, the soft shutting of a drawer, a footfall that did not hurry, the controlled acoustics of a place where even sound was disciplined. Out in the street, beyond the thick windows, there was a harsher rhythm—wheels over stone, a distant vendor’s call—life made noisy by the need to sell itself at every corner. Here, in this quieter chamber, the past could speak without being drowned.

Kalonymos returned with a clerk who carried a chest and set it down on the floor. The clerk drew off a leather cover and left without a word. The chest was not large, but its ornamental bracers and handles of gilt iron gave it weight beyond its size. The wood was beautifully incised with Arabic lettering, the curved lines like a script of flames arrested—beauty pressed into usefulness, as if even ornament had been taught to keep faith.

“So!” Kalonymos said, resuming his seat.

“And here is the curious key,” he added, taking it from a small leathern bag. He held it out, but his look stayed on Deronda with that wary, admonitory suspicion age often turns on youth. “Bestow it carefully. I trust you are methodic and wary.”

“I shall be more careful of this than of any other property,” Deronda said, smiling as he put the key into his breast-pocket, feeling its cool metal settle there as if it had found the place prepared for it. “I never before possessed anything that was a sign to me of so much cherished hope and effort. And I shall never forget that the effort was partly yours. Have you time to tell me more of my grandfather? Or shall I be trespassing in staying longer?”

“Stay yet a while. In an hour and eighteen minutes I start for Trieste,” Kalonymos said, looking at his watch with exactness that made time itself seem an object he handled and arranged like letters. “And presently my sons will expect my attention. Will you let me make you known to them, so that they may have the pleasure of showing hospitality to my friend’s grandson? They dwell here in ease and luxury, though I choose to be a wanderer.”

“I shall be glad if you will commend me to their acquaintance for some future opportunity,” Deronda said. “There are pressing claims calling me to England—friends who may be much in need of my presence. I have been kept away from them too long by unexpected circumstances. But to know more of you and your family would be motive enough to bring me again to Mainz.”

“Good! Me you will hardly find, for I am beyond my threescore years and ten, and I am a wanderer, carrying my shroud with me. But my sons and their children dwell here in wealth and unity. The days are changed for us since Karl the Great fetched my ancestors from Italy to bring some tincture of knowledge to our rough German brethren. I and my contemporaries have had to fight for it too. Our youth fell on evil days; but this we have won; we increase our wealth in safety, and the learning of all Germany is fed and fattened by Jewish brains—though they keep not always their Jewish hearts.” He cut that last clause short, as if it left a bitter aftertaste. “Have you been left altogether ignorant of your people’s life, young man?”

“No,” said Deronda. “I have lately, before I had any true suspicion of my parentage, been led to study everything belonging to their history with more interest than any other subject. It turns out that I have been making myself ready to understand my grandfather a little.”

Kalonymos nodded, and Deronda felt he had struck the right path, the one that would lead back—without seeming to demand it—to what he most needed: not mere facts, but an image of the man whose thought had reached forward to him.

“So—you would perhaps have been such a man as he if your education had not hindered; for you are like him in features:—yet not altogether, young man.” Kalonymos’ eyes narrowed, measuring. “He had an iron will in his face: it braced up everybody about him. When he was quite young he had already got one deep upright line in his brow. I see none of that in you. Daniel Charisi used to say, ‘Better, a wrong will than a wavering; better a steadfast enemy than an uncertain friend; better a false belief than no belief at all.’ What he despised most was indifference. He had longer reasons than I can give you.”

“Yet his knowledge was not narrow?” Deronda asked, with that half-hidden thought that sometimes indecision is excused as breadth.

“Narrow? no.” Kalonymos shook his head, and a compassionate smile passed over his face, softening the sharpness. “From his childhood upward, he drank in learning as easily as the plant sucks up water. But he early took to medicine and theories about life and health. He traveled to many countries, and spent much of his substance in seeing and knowing. What he used to insist on was that the strength and wealth of mankind depended on the balance of separateness and communication, and he was bitterly against our people losing themselves among the Gentiles; ‘It’s no better,’ said he, ‘than the many sorts of grain going back from their variety into sameness.’”

As he spoke, Deronda seemed to feel that phrase on his tongue—grain, variety, sameness—and with it the sense of stored harvests and names for things that might vanish if one yielded too easily to the dominant speech of the world. A people could lose itself not only in marriage and custom, but in the very words by which it tasted bread, greeted morning, blessed the dead. To have one’s dialect thinned to an accent, one’s prayers turned into curiosities for others’ ears—there were power and violence in that, quieter than chains, but persistent.

Kalonymos went on, “He mingled all sorts of learning; and in that he was like our Arabic writers in the golden time. We studied together, but he went beyond me. Though we were bosom friends, and he poured himself out to me, we were as different as the inside and outside of the bowl.” His hand made a small shaping gesture, as if he could feel the vessel’s curve. “I stood up for two notions of my own: I took Charisi’s sayings as I took the shape of the trees: they were there, not to be disputed about. It came to the same thing in both of us; we were both faithful Jews, thankful not to be Gentiles.

“And since I was a ripe man, I have been what I am now, for all but age—loving to wander, loving transactions, loving to behold all things, and caring nothing about hardship. Charisi thought continually of our people’s future: he went with all his soul into that part of our religion: I, not. So we have freedom, I am content. Our people wandered before they were driven.” He paused, and through that pause Deronda heard again the controlled quiet of the room, as if even silence were a practiced craft. “Young man when I am in the East, I lie much on deck and watch the greater stars. The sight of them satisfies me. I know them as they rise, and hunger not to know more. Charisi was satisfied with no sight, but pieced it out with what had been before and what would come after. Yet we loved each other, and as he said, he bound our love with duty; we solemnly pledged ourselves to help and defend each other to the last. I have fulfilled my pledge.”

Kalonymos rose then, the movement decisive, like the closing of a ledger. Deronda rose also. The chest on the floor seemed to have altered the room’s meaning; it was no longer a place where letters were sorted, but a place where a life had been delivered into another’s hands.

“And in being faithful to him you have caused justice to be done to me,” Deronda said. “It would have been a robbery of me too that I should never have known of the inheritance he had prepared for me. I thank you with my whole soul.”

“Be worthy of him, young man.” Kalonymos’ voice sharpened, businesslike again, as if worthiness were a duty to be scheduled. “What is your vocation?”

The abruptness embarrassed Deronda. To call his law-reading a vocation felt like speaking a half-truth in a room where truth had been guarded at cost. “I cannot say that I have any.”

“Get one, get one. The Jew must be diligent.” Kalonymos put his hand on Deronda’s shoulder, the touch steadying, proprietary in the way of elders who believe they have earned the right to demand. He looked sharply into Deronda’s face. “You will call yourself a Jew and profess the faith of your fathers?”

Under that gaze Deronda grew slightly paler, not from fear, but from the weight of being asked to set his name aloud like a seal. “I shall call myself a Jew,” he said deliberately. “But I will not say that I shall profess to believe exactly as my fathers have believed. Our fathers themselves changed the horizon of their belief and learned of other races. But I think I can maintain my grandfather’s notion of separateness with communication. I hold that my first duty is to my own people, and if there is anything to be done toward restoring or perfecting their common life, I shall make that my vocation.”

As he spoke, it was as if the necessity of speech pressed a resolve into being—like dough that becomes coherent only under the hand’s demand. He felt, in the very forming of the words, that he had stepped into an identity that could not be translated into any other tongue without loss.

“Ah, you argue and you look forward—you are Daniel Charisi’s grandson,” Kalonymos said, and he added a benediction in Hebrew, the ancient sounds settling in the air with a tenderness that did not ask permission from modernity.

They parted. And almost as soon as Deronda was in London again, the aged man was once more on shipboard, with the sea’s vast hush under him and the cleaner, older music above—greeting the friendly stars without any eager curiosity, content to know their rising as one knows the return of a well-loved prayer.
