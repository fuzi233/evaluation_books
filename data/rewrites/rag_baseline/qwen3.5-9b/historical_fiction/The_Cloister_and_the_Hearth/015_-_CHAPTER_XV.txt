Where is the woman who cannot perform the role demanded of her? Where is she who refuses to play the part, yet fails to execute it with the necessary artifice to save the life of the man she adores? In moments of such catastrophic gravity, nature itself seems to conspire against the simplicity of the female spirit, forcing her to scatter dust in the eyes of Solomon, blinding reason with the grit of despair.

As the male figures withdrew, retreating into the shadows of their own rooms and descending the stairwell, Margaret rose from the bed. She moved with a fluid, ghostly grace, stripping away the layers of her disguise—the skirt, the petticoats, the stockings, the nightgown that had served as her shroud. She pushed the heavy chest, laden with linens and her own form, against the far wall until it stood flush with the timber. Then, she glided to the threshold, pressing her ear to the wood, listening for the echo of footsteps. Silence answered her; the sounds of the household had faded into the night.

Within that chest lay a deception so profound it defied detection by any eye unaccustomed to the mechanics of survival. A section of the floorboards above had been fractured, and when Gerard had requested repairs to restore the room's dignity, lacking proper materials, he had ingeniously cut away a precise aperture. This opening was sized exactly to accommodate Margaret's slender frame, allowing her to slide beneath the planks while the stolen wood sealed the gap behind her. The chest itself, appearing merely a foot deep, actually rested on the rafters two feet below the surface, creating a hollow tomb disguised as furniture.

Now, under the cover of absolute stillness, Margaret knelt and offered a silent prayer to the heavens. Rising, she crept to the edge of the hidden cavity and whispered, tenderly, "Gerard."

There was no response. She pressed her voice slightly higher, desperation creeping into her tone. "Gerard, all is safe. Thank Heaven! You may rise; but oh, be cautious!"

Silence hung heavy in the air. She placed her trembling hand upon his shoulder. "Gerard!"

Still nothing. Panic, cold and sharp, pierced her resolve. "Oh, what is this?" she cried, her fingers flying wildly across his face, searching his chest for a pulse. She gripped his shoulders, shaking him violently, lifting him as if he weighed nothing more than a bundle of rags. But he did not stir. He slid from her grasp, falling back not with the resistance of a living man, but with the final, weightless collapse of a corpse.

A primal dread seized her. The lid had been down. She had lain upon him. The intruders had occupied the room for some time. With the frenzied strength of a cornered animal, she tore him from the darkness of the chest and dragged him to the window. She flung the sash wide, inviting the cool, fragrant night air to rush in. She laid him there, exposed to the silver wash of the moonlight.

His complexion was the color of ash; his limbs were slack, utterly devoid of motion. She pressed her palm to his breast. Horror! It was as still as death itself. The horror of horrors washed over her: she had suffocated him with her own body. The human mind struggles to accept such a sudden, grotesque calamity. Gerard, who had barely managed to squeeze into that cramped confinement mere minutes prior, how could he already be dead?

She called out to him using every endearing name her heart could conjure, every phrase her tongue could frame. She kissed him, caressed him, coaxed him, imploring him to speak. There was no answer to words of love she had never dared utter before, nor ones she believed capable of escape. Trembling violently, she began to babble over his ashen features, words that were simultaneously terrible and pitiful.

"Oh, Gerard! I am so sorry you are dead. I am so sorry I have killed you. Forgive me for not letting the men take you; it would have been kinder than this. Oh, Gerard! I am very, very sorry for what I have done."

Suddenly, her composure shattered into madness. "No! No! Such things cannot be, or there is no God. It is monstrous! How can my Gerard be dead? How can I have killed my Gerard? I love him. Oh, God! You know how I love him. He does not. I never told him. If he knew my heart, he would speak to me; he would not be so deaf to his poor Margaret. Is it all a trick to make me cry out and betray him? But no! I love him too well for that. I'll choke myself first."

She seized her own throat, silencing the wild urge to scream in her terror and anguish. "If he would but say one word. Oh, Gerard! Don't die without a word. Have mercy on me and scold me, but speak to me! If you are angry with me, scold me! Curse me! I deserve it: the idiot who killed the man she loved better than herself. Ah, I am a murderess. The worst in all the world. Help! Help! I have murdered him. Ah! Ah! Ah! Ah! Ah!"

She tore at her hair, unleashing shrieks so wild, so piercing, they rang out like funeral knells, striking the ears of Dierich Brower and his men. Instantly, the group sprang to their feet, turning in unison, their faces illuminated by the shock of the sound.
