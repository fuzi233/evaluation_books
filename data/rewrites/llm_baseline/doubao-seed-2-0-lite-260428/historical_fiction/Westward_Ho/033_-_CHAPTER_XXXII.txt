It is done. The great Spanish Armada is vanquished.

The long, unceasing cosmic war waged since the dawn of time — the battle of light against dark, of Michael’s angels against Satan and his fiends — has for one brief, rare, judgement-day moment stepped out of the spiritual plane and into flesh and blood. Ordinary men did not need to puzzle out where they stood: they could strike a blow for good not just with prayers and pens, but with cannon shot and cold steel. The lines were drawn clean, the false grandeur of Spain’s vaunted imperial might crumpled like a punctured bladder at the first real, brave blow. All across the Protestant world, from England’s southern shores to the frozen Alpine valleys where generations of Vaudois shepherd saints lay buried, their bones the seed of coming religious freedom, a shout of holy joy went up that had not been heard for centuries. It was the birth cry of a new world: of free settlement across North America, Australia, and the Pacific, of free trade, of the age of discovery and science soon to come.

As the contemporary chronicler Van Meteran records, Elizabeth ordered a national public holiday on November 29, where every man, woman and child was enjoined to attend church to give public thanks for the deliverance. The queen herself rode through London in a triumphal procession, following the old Roman custom, her retinue of lords and civic officers lining her route, the streets hung with blue cloth, the defeated Spanish standards hanging from the rafters of St Paul’s Cathedral as she entered to give public praise to God and urge her people to the same. The crowds cheered so loud their voices shook the rafters, calling down long life on their queen and ruin on all her enemies.

Medals struck to mark the day bore the simple, mocking legend: *It came, it saw, it fled*. The remnants of the once-invincible Armada ran north like frightened deer, past Orkney and Shetland, past the coast of Norway where the descendants of the Vikings turned them away without food or fresh water, ever closer to the arctic midnight sun. Their stores ran out, they threw their dying horses overboard to lighten the load, the northern sea echoing with the animals’ screams. The duke of Medina Sidonia fled for home with 25 of his best, most seaworthy ships, leaving the rest of the battered fleet to fend for themselves. Gale after gale rose out of the Atlantic, and barely a handful of those desperate remaining vessels ever made it back to Spanish shores.

All this while, where were Amyas Leigh and his ship the *Vengeance*?

The main English fleet had turned south for home off the 57th parallel, their powder spent, their rations running thin, choosing to leave the scattered Spaniards to the mercy of the storm-tossed northern seas. But Amyas begged his superior, Lord High Admiral Howard, for leave to stay on the chase, and went to Sir John Hawkins to beg for whatever spare ammunition and provisions he could spare, promising to pay the full sum back out of his plunder if he survived, or out of his own family estate if he died, signing formal promissory notes that Hawkins, ever the practical man, stuffed into his pocket alongside his twine, thimbles, and pouch of tobacco. When that was done, he gathered his crew of Bideford men, reminded them again of the terrible wrong done to the Rose of Torridge by Don Guzman de Soto, the Spaniard they had hunted across the Atlantic for years, and asked them straight: “Men of Bideford, will you follow me still? There is plunder for those who want wealth, revenge for those who have lost kin to Spanish cruelty, and for all of us the honour of never quitting the chase as long as a Spanish flag flies in English seas.”

Every single man on board swore they would follow Amyas Leigh around the whole world.

The sixteen days of chase that followed were brutal and winding, a nonstop cat and mouse game that took them past the Outer Hebrides, where they skirmished with Skye islanders who first cursed them in a tongue no one on board knew, then embraced them as long-lost kin, past the wreck of a great Venetian galleon off Islay that gave the crew enough plunder to pay back all their arrears, down the west coast of Wales, through Cardigan Bay where the Santa Catharina (the great galleon carrying Don Guzman) ran aground for a time on the sunken pebble dyke of Sarn David, the legendary remains of the drowned Welsh kingdom. They fought four separate running skirmishes, Amyas refusing every chance to seize rich disabled prizes along the way, all to keep his single-eyed focus on the one man he meant to bring to justice.

By the sixteenth day, the two ships had worked their way down the Bristol Channel. The previous evening, the Welsh coast had loomed black against the glow of inland thunderstorms, and lightning had flickered all through the small hours under a soft south-west breeze. Dawn broke grey, drizzly, and windless, the sky a low roof of dingy inky cloud, the sea rolling in glassy, foam-flecked troughs, no land visible through the thick, clinging mist.

Amyas paced the soaked deck, his jaw tight with the fury of his months-long obsession. He knew the Spaniard could not escape, but every extra moment that dragged between him and his revenge felt like a knife twisted in his gut. The men sat hunched along the bulwarks, whistling pointlessly for wind, the sails slapping idly against the masts, the *Vengeance* rolling so far her yardarms dipped close to the water on each side.

“Watch those guns,” Amyas growled. “One of them’s going to break loose if we keep rolling like this.”

“We’ll watch the guns well enough,” old Yeo rumbled back, “if the Lord will only send us a wind to move this ship.”

“We’ll have wind enough before night,” Will Cary laughed, “and thunder to go with it.”

“So much the better,” Amyas snapped. “Let the heavens split if they want, so long as I get my work done first.”

“He can’t be far off, depend on it,” Cary said, squinting through the mist. “One break in the cloud, and we’ll spot his masts.”

“Probably to windward of us, the devil himself is fighting for him,” Amyas snarled, shaking the sword in his hand. “Sixteen days at his heels, and I haven’t run this through his black heart yet!”

The morning dragged on. Not a single gull crossed the glassy grey water. Amyas’s dark, brooding fury hung over the whole ship like a shroud. “God,” he cried out suddenly, unthinking, “let me just drive this blade through his accursed heart, and then — strike me dead, if You will!”

Parson John Brimblecombe went white. “Sir! What sort of a vow is that to make?”

“None of your business, parson,” Amyas grunted. “They’re piping dinner. Go below. I won’t be joining you.”

Brimblecombe trundled down to the mess, whispering to the rest of the crew that Amyas’s words boded nothing but ill luck. Only old Yeo shrugged it off. “What better end could a man ask for, than to be called home the second he’s finished his work? I say let the Don meet his reckoning, and if lightning takes me the second I see him safe on shore, I’ll die a happy man.”

Before anyone could answer, Amyas’s shout boomed across the deck: “There she is!”

The crew scrambled up the hatches as fast as the rolling deck would let them. The mist had torn open for a minute, a ragged patch of blue sky spilling a single shaft of golden sunlight straight down onto the Santa Catharina, rolling four or five miles to their east, her tall galleon masts rising clear above the water.

“Now we see her,” Cary said, “but where in God’s name are we? Where’s the land?”

“Tide’s running hard up the Channel right now, sir,” the master called back.

“Who cares about the tide?” Amyas said, his cold blue eyes devouring the distant Spanish galleon. “Can’t we get to her?”

“Not unless someone jumps over the side and shoves her,” Cary laughed, before he sobered at the look on Amyas’s face. “You’re not yourself, old friend. You haven’t been for three days now.”

“I’m not Amyas Leigh right now. I’m my family’s avenger,” Amyas said, passing a shaking hand over his brow. “When this is over, I’ll be the old merry fool you know again. I swear it.”

He pulled the sharpening stone out of his pocket and began running it up and down the edge of his sword, over and over, as if he meant to hone the blade to a supernatural sharpness. The patch of blue closed back over, the air grew thick and sultry, a low distant mutter of thunder rolling from the west. They had no boats big enough to tow the *Vengeance* through the calm, so there was nothing to do but wait.

By two in the afternoon, Yeo came up to him. “The tide’s been running east for two hours now. He’s trapped. Can’t escape us.”

“Safe as a fox in a snare. Not even Satan can pull him out of this,” Amyas snarled.

“But God can,” Brimblecombe said, quiet and earnest.

Amyas rounded on him, ready to snap, when a great thunderclap boomed directly over their heads, the sound rolling across the empty water. A black line of storm had appeared on the north-west horizon, cutting the sea clean off from the sky.

“Here comes the wind!” Amyas yelled. “Hard round with the yards!”

The sails filled with a roar, the *Vengeance* heeled over, and they surged forward before the rising gale, straight for the becalmed Santa Catharina. Thunder peal followed peal, lightning zigzagging down out of the black storm arch, and the rain hit a few minutes later, sharp as hail, lashing the sea into a white froth. Amyas refused to shorten sail, determined to reach the Spaniard before the squall blew itself out.

Ten minutes later, Yeo came sprinting back from the bow, his face as white as bone. “Land dead ahead! Port the helm, sir! For the love of God, port the helm right now!”

Amyas hauled the tiller over with all his strength, the fore-sail splitting with a crack like a cannon shot, and the *Vengeance* swung hard around. A hundred and fifty yards away, the Santa Catharina was already careening straight for the dark, cliff-bound mass of Lundy Island rising out of the storm, the jagged black fang of the Shutter rock sticking up out of the foaming breakers directly in her path. The clumsy galleon lurched, her helmsman fighting to turn her out of the trap, but her heavy hull refused to answer the tiller. For a heartbeat she hung half-buried in spray, then she pitched forward, and rushed headlong onto the waiting rock, to tear her hull apart on the jagged granite teeth of the Bristol Channel.
