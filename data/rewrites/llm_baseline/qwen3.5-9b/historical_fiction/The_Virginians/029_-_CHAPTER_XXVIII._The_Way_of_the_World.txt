After spending a few days at Tunbridge Wells, our young Virginian discovered himself to be the most significant figure in that cheerful, small resort. No nobleman commanded such intense curiosity. Even the Bishop of Salisbury received less deference than Harry. As he strolled through the town, heads turned to follow him; country folk paused in the marketplace just to stare. Inside the assembly rooms, matrons eagerly ushered him toward their daughters, finding ways to leave them alone together, where smiles were plentiful. Everyone seemed to know the exact acreage of his Virginia estate down to the penny, and the precise sum of his annual income. At every tea table, his gambling winnings were discussed and tallied with mathematical precision. One wonders at the depth of neighbors' knowledge regarding one another's private affairs!

So profound was the public interest in Harry that even his servant, Gumbo, was treated with favor. People smiled upon the lad, inviting him aside for ale and cold meat solely to extract news of his master. Under this regimen, Gumbo grew fat, becoming a prominent member of the local valet society, and began to fabricate tales of even greater magnitude than usual. No social gathering was considered complete without Mr. Warrington's presence. The young man was neither amused nor astonished by this sudden elevation; indeed, he bore his new honors with surprising composure. Having been raised at home with a healthy sense of self-worth, his current good fortune merely reinforced his natural satisfaction. Yet, he remained unboasted. He did not trumpet his victories or affect airs of superiority. Guided by a peculiar code of honor, he felt compelled to answer every summons—whether to a racecourse, a drinking bout, or a card match—for the sake of Old Virginia. He refused to shrink from any challenge.

His new acquaintances were eager to test him. Possessed of a steady mind, a skilled hand, a secure seat, and unflinching nerves, the representative of Old Virginia acquitted himself splendidly against the representatives of the Mother Country. Madame de Bernstein, who had exhausted her nightly supply of cards (and presumably rectified the misfortunes hinted at previously), was delighted by her nephew's rising fame. He had bested Jack Morris at shooting, defeated Mr. Scamper in a riding match, and held his own remarkably well against Captain Batts at tennis, despite having never attempted the sport before. He also faced the renowned gamblers, the Earls of Chesterfield and March, earning praise from both for his coolness, gallantry, and breeding. While he lacked brilliance at the books, his handwriting was competent enough to satisfy most fashionable circles, amusing the old lady with its naive simplicity.

Madame de Bernstein, a woman who preferred the world to the library, relished humor and possessed a taste for Molière and Fielding, yet she valued a lively game of cards above any novel. With fond pleasure, she supervised Harry's attire, scouring for fine laces for his ruffles and discovering a lovely diamond brooch for his cravat. Thus, he became the favorite of all her nephews and kin. Lady Maria, it seems, was only too happy to see her brother surpassed by the lad, though her brothers likely trembled with fear and envy upon hearing of Mr. Warrington's prodigious success and his rapid ascent in their wealthy aunt's affections.

Two weeks into his stay, Harry had become quite a personage. He knew all the good company in the place, and inevitably, he became acquainted with the bad as well. Was it his fault for accepting the world as he found it, or for drinking from that sweet, sparkling cup of pleasure that had been filled to the brim for him? His aunt enjoyed his triumphs and simply urged him to continue his enjoyment. She was neither a rigid moralist nor a particularly wholesome guide for youth. Should the Cattarina have sent him love letters, I suspect Aunt Bernstein would have encouraged him to accept them. However, Harry arrived from his colonial home carrying a reserve of modesty that he retained alongside his honest homespun linen. Libertinism was unknown in the sparsely populated regions from which he hailed; the vices of great cities were rarely practiced in the rough towns of America. Consequently, Harry blushed like a maiden at the bold talk of his new European associates. Even Aunt Bernstein's worldly jests astounded him, prompting the old woman to address him as "Joseph" or "simpleton." Yet, regardless of his innocence, the world credited him with the same depravity as everyone else. How could he possibly know that associating with the saucy Cattarina was improper? He had seen my Lord March driving her in his carriage. Harry perceived no harm in offering her his arm or parading openly with her on the public walks.

When she spotted a trinket in a toy shop, Harry, whose pockets were heavy with coin, delighted to buy her the coveted locket. The next day it was lace; the next, something else. There seemed no limit to Madame Cattarina's whims, until the young gentleman gently declined further requests with a joke and a laugh. He was shrewd enough and not reckless, though generous; he had no intention of buying diamond drops for the petulant lady's ears. But who was to credit him with this modesty? Old Bernstein insisted her nephew was playing the role of Don Juan, supplanting my Lord March. Her conviction grew stronger whenever poor Maria was present, delighting in stabbing the spinster's tender heart and reveling in her niece's painful silence and confusion.

"Why, my dear," the Baroness would say, "boys will be boys, and I do not wish Harry to be the first milksop in his family!" Sometimes the bread Maria ate at her aunt's expense tasted hard and indigestible. Oh, how skillfully some women know how to make food seem bitter!

Meanwhile, Mr. Wolfe continued his courtship of the lady of his heart at Westerham. Knowing the Colonel was fully occupied, Mr. Warrington did not expect to see much of him, despite liking the officer's conversation. This was distinct from the ribald chatter surrounding Harry. Mr. Wolfe never spoke of cards, horse pedigrees, hunting exploits, conquests, or the endless scandals of the age. It was indeed a difficult time. That old world was more dissolute than ours. An old king kept mistresses openly in his train, honored by the great. The nobility was often mad and reckless in the pursuit of pleasure, marked by a looseness of speech and action that history must record faithfully, without needing to shock honest readers with particulars.

Our young gentleman had stumbled upon some of the wildest elements of this crowd and found an old relative living right in the center of the chaos. Harry failed to notice how Colonel Wolfe deliberately avoided him, or how the Colonel's demeanor grew cold and distant when they occasionally met. He knew nothing of the stories circulating about him. Who knows the stories told of others? Who creates them? Who fathers these wonderful lies? Poor Harry was unaware of the reputation he was cultivating. While he rode, played, and frolicked, he was passing among respectable people as the most abandoned, profligate, and godless young man alive.

Alas, to think that the gentle, quiet lad we admired—the one so simple and easily pleased—should be viewed as a hardened profligate, a spendthrift, a confirmed gambler, and a frequent visitor to abandoned women! These rumors reached honest Colonel Lambert at Oakhurst. First came one bad tale, then another, until they swelled into a flood, filling the good man's heart with grief and anxiety. His family noticed the disturbance. Initially, Lambert refused to speak of it, dismissing his wife's affectionate inquiries. Mrs. Lambert feared a great misfortune: ruin, a dangerous mission, illness, disgrace, or death. Who can withstand an anxious woman or escape the interrogation of the marital pillow? Eventually, Lambert was forced to reveal part of what he knew.

The wife was as grieved and amazed as her husband. From the privacy of their bedroom, where the sorrow was briefly stifled beneath the pillows, the grief descended to the lower floors. Theo and Hester inherited the complaint, suffering terribly. Oh, kind, little, wounded hearts! At first, Hester turned red, flew into a rage, clenched her fists, and vowed she would believe not a word of the wicked stories. But eventually, she succumbed. Scandal almost always masters people, especially the good and innocent. Oh, the serpent they had nurtured by their fireside! Oh, the wretched, wretched boy! To imagine him walking with that horrid painted Frenchwoman, dangling diamond necklaces around her neck, and parading his shame before all society at the Wells!

After the three ladies wept over the tale and the father was deeply moved, they confided in the parish priest. In vain he preached his favorite sermon on scandal the following Sunday, denouncing humanity's tendency to assume the worst. We repent, we promise to stop—but when the next malicious story arises concerning our neighbor, we believe it anyway. So did those kind, wretched people of Oakhurst believe everything they heard about poor Harry Warrington.

Harry, meanwhile, was far too pleased with himself to realize how poorly his friends regarded him. He pursued an idle, pleasant, albeit unprofitable life, oblivious to the hubbub he created and the terrible reputation he held in the eyes of many good men.
