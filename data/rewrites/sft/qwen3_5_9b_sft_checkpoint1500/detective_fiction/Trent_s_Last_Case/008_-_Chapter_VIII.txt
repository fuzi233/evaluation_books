The coroner, acutely aware that for a single, fleeting day he stepped out of the shadows of provincial solicitorhood into the blinding glare of public scrutiny, resolved to play his part with theatrical gravity. A man of jovial temperament and substantial girth, he possessed a rare talent for marshaling facts, bolstered by a copious vocabulary that treated juries like malleable clay. Though his interpretations of evidentiary rules occasionally wavered under the weight of his own dramatic flair, the news of Manderson's mysterious death within his jurisdiction had elevated him to the status of England's most delighted coroner.

The proceedings unfolded in a cavernous, unfurnished chamber annexed to the hotel, originally conceived as a ballroom. A regiment of reporters sat entrenched in the front rows, their pencils poised like weapons, while witnesses were seated to one side of the presiding table. Flanking the coroner on either side sat the jury, a double row of men with plastered hair and a calculated, spurious ease of manner. An undistinguished public filled the remaining space, listening to the opening solemnities in an awed, hushed silence. Among the press, whispers circulated; those familiar with Trent by sight quietly assured the others that the detective was nowhere to be seen in the courtroom.

First to take the stand was the widow, whose identity was immediately confirmed. After a brief inquiry into the deceased's health and habits, the coroner steered her toward an account of the last occasion she had witnessed her husband alive. As she rose, lifting a heavy veil that concealed her features, a singular impression struck the room: an extreme pallor paired with unbroken composure. Yet, this was not the coldness of a heart of stone. Beneath the surface lay a vibrant, interesting femininity, masked by a formidable inner strength that was laboriously mastering the shock of her situation. She was not enigmatic, nor was she stoical; she was simply a woman of powerful character holding back a storm.

As she spoke, she occasionally dabbed her eyes with a handkerchief, but her voice remained low and clear throughout. Her husband, she testified, had ascended to his dressing-room at his customary hour for retirement on that Sunday night. The space connected directly to her own suite via a door typically left ajar. Manderson, known for his predilection for simple, unadorned surroundings, preferred sleeping in a small room. Although she had not been fully awake when he arrived, the sudden illumination of his lamp had roused her from a deep slumber. She recalled speaking to him, though the specifics of their conversation were blurred by drowsiness. She remembered asking if he had enjoyed his "moonlight run" in the car and, perhaps more urgently, what time it was. Having slept only briefly, she had expected him to be out later than usual.

"He told me it was half-past eleven," she recounted, "and that he had changed his mind about going for another drive."

"Did he offer a reason?" the coroner prompted.

"Yes," she replied, pausing as if gathering courage. "He explained why. I recall it distinctly because..." A flicker of confusion crossed her face.

"Because?" the coroner pressed gently.

"Because my husband rarely discussed his business affairs with me," she answered, her chin lifting with a faint, defiant resolve. "He seldom deigned to share details, considering them beneath my interest. Thus, I was startled when he mentioned sending Mr. Marlowe to Southampton to retrieve crucial information from a departing traveler bound for Paris. He noted that Mr. Marlowe could secure it easily, provided he met with no accident. He added that he had begun the journey in the car but had returned on foot for a mile or so, feeling considerably refreshed by the exercise."

"And further details?"

"No," she said, her voice dropping. "That was all I remember. I drifted back to sleep almost instantly. The last image I have is of my husband switching off his light. After that, I never saw him alive."

"Do you recall hearing anything unusual during the night?"

"Not a sound," she affirmed. "I did not wake until seven o'clock, when my maid served my tea. She had drawn the curtain to his door as per routine, and I assumed he was still there. He required vast amounts of sleep, often rising not until late. I ate breakfast in my sitting-room, and it was nearly ten before I learned that his body had been discovered." She lowered her head, waiting for dismissal.

However, the ordeal was not concluded.

"Mrs. Manderson," the coroner's voice softened, carrying a thread of steel nonetheless. "In these tragic circumstances, I must ask a painful question, yet it is my duty. Are you prepared to confirm that, for some time past, the relationship between you and your late husband lacked mutual affection and confidence? Did an estrangement exist between you?"

The lady straightened, color rising in her cheeks. "If necessity compels me to answer," she stated with chilling distinctness, "let there be no misunderstanding. For several months prior to his death, his attitude toward me caused me profound anxiety and sorrow. He had transformed; becoming increasingly reserved and seemingly mistrustful, he withdrew from me. I saw him far less than before, preferring solitude. I cannot explain this shift. I endeavored to counteract it, acting with every ounce of justice and dignity I could muster. Some invisible barrier separated us, something he refused to articulate. My own stubborn pride forbade me from pressing the subject in so many words; I contented myself with maintaining my usual demeanor, within the limits he permitted. Regrettably, I suspect I shall never know the nature of this barrier." Her voice trembled slightly at the end, and she pulled her veil down once more, standing tall and silent.

A juror, hesitating visibly, interjected, "Might I inquire, Madam, if there ever arose any sentiment, however fleeting, akin to what is termed 'love' between you and your husband?"

"Never," she responded, the word spoken with a flat, colorless tone. Yet, the air in the room grew heavy, sensing that a crude misjudgment of a woman like Mrs. Manderson had incurred severe consequences.

Turning to the wider inquiry, the coroner asked if she knew of any other matters plaguing her husband's mind recently. Mrs. Manderson knew of none. Satisfied, the coroner signaled the end of her testimony, and the veiled lady departed. The general attention, previously fixed on her, shifted eagerly toward Martin, whom the coroner had summoned next.

It was at this precise moment that Trent materialized in the doorway, edging into the crowded hall. His gaze did not seek Martin; instead, it locked onto the well-balanced figure approaching swiftly along a gap in the throng—the widow. His expression was somber. As he stepped aside with a slight bow, he heard Mrs. Manderson whisper his name. He followed her a few paces into the corridor.

"I wished to ask you," she said, her voice now weak and strangely fractured, "if you would lend me your arm for part of the walk home. I could not locate my uncle near the entrance, and suddenly, I felt quite faint... I shall feel better in the fresh air." Before Trent could suggest otherwise, she interjected sharply, "No, no! I cannot remain here—please, Mr. Trent!" Her hand clutched his arm momentarily, as if her frailty could somehow anchor him, dragging him away from the scene, before she leaned heavily upon him. Supported by his arm and with her head bowed, she walked slowly from the hotel, passing beneath the oaks toward White Gables.

Trent accompanied her in silence, his thoughts a whirlwind of insanity, dancing to a chorus of internal curses: *Fool! Fool!* Every piece of knowledge he possessed, every suspicion he harbored regarding this tragedy, raced through his mind. Yet, the lingering, unnerving pressure of her hand on his arm never faded from his consciousness, igniting a mixture of exaltation and bewilderment that enraged him. He continued to berate himself fiercely behind the mask of conventional solicitude he wore for the lady, attending her to the house where she sank onto a couch in the morning room.

Raising her veil, she thanked him gravely and frankly, her eyes reflecting genuine gratitude. She reported that she was much improved and that a cup of tea would perform miracles. Concerned that he might have missed something vital, she expressed shame for her collapse. "I am glad you did not hear me," she admitted softly when he explained the nature of her distress. "Of course, you will read everything in the reports. It shook me so terribly to speak of that," she added simply, "and the effort to avoid making an exhibition of myself drained me completely. And those staring men by the door! Thank you again for helping me when I asked.... I thought I might," she concluded with a weary, strange smile. Trent retreated, his hand still trembling from the cool, fleeting touch of her fingers.

Meanwhile, the testimonies of the servants and the discoverer of the body yielded nothing new to the press. The police officer, Mr. Bunner, offered evidence as colorless and cryptic as is typical at the inquest stage. To Mr. Bunner's evident satisfaction, his account generated the sensation of the day, effectively eclipsing the revealing domestic difficulties disclosed by the widow. He recounted to the court, in substance, what he had already told Trent.
