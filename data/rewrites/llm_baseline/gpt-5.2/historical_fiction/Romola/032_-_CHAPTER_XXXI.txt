“My Romola,” Tito said on the second morning after his speech in the Piazza, while the house was still cool and the courtyard sounds had not yet risen to their full day’s clatter, “I am to receive grand visitors to-day. The Milanese Count comes again, and the Seneschal de Beaucaire—the great favourite of the Cristianissimo. They will want to be shown the antiquities and the library, and there will be nothing but smiling ceremony with those rustling magnates whom we are not likely to see again. I know you do not care for it. Perhaps you had better give up your work to-day, and go to see your cousin Brigida.”

Romola heard, beneath the lightness, the particular wish he was careful not to shape into a command. She assented at once, because yielding in such small things had become one of her ways of loving: like setting a crust to soak for soup because he liked it that way, or seeing that the oil in the lamp was strained clear of bitterness. Yet when she came back in her hood and mantle, the buckle newly fastened at her throat, her glance went out beyond the threshold as if it could already see the city’s lungs expand.

“Oh, what a long breath Florence will take,” she said, “when the gates are flung open and the last Frenchman is walking out of them. Even you are getting tired, with all your patience, my Tito; confess it. Ah—your head is hot.”

He was bent over the desk, writing, the quick scratch of the pen a dry insect-sound in the hush. She laid her hand on his head as she had done many times, intending it for a parting caress—the touch that had been as homely between them as laying a cloth straight or tasting broth for salt. Often, at that touch, he would lift his face, lean back a little, and meet her eyes with that ready smile which made the house feel inhabited. But now her hand lay there, and he did not raise his head. It seemed to him as if he could no more lift it than if her palm had turned to lead.

He spoke lightly, his pen running on. “The French are as ready to go from Florence as the wasps to leave a ripe pear when they have just fastened on it.”

Romola’s senses, trained by love to mark the smallest changes—as one marks at table when a familiar loaf has been baked too long, or a jar has taken an alien taint—felt the absence of the accustomed movement and look. She drew away her hand.

“I am going, Tito.”

“Farewell, my sweet one. I must wait at home. Take Maso with you.”

Still he did not look up. She went out without adding more, her footfall swallowed by the stone passage, and the morning outside meeting her with its mingled voices: the splash of water flung from a pail, a vendor’s call rising and falling like a chant, the iron ring of a distant hammer. Very slight things make epochs in married life; and for the first time Romola admitted to herself—not in anger, but in a sudden, cold clearness—that Tito had changed, and that the change had turned towards her.

Was the reason in herself? She might have believed it, as wives so readily do, searching their own hearts as if a fault there must be the hidden worm. But there were those strange facts—the armour, the picture—standing in her memory like objects found out of place in a well-ordered pantry, suggesting hands at work when she had thought the room secure. The meaning of them was a sealed jar she could not open.

No sooner did Tito feel assured she was beyond sight than he laid down his pen and lifted his head with a relieved breath, as if he had been holding it under water. He looked about him with a kind of delighted safety—only parchment, only broken marble, harmless fragments that could not judge. Then came a sharp disgust with himself. He would have chosen, if he could, to be even more than usually kind. His whole nature leaned toward keeping Romola’s tenderness as a man keeps warmth in a house by shutting out draughts. Yet he could not master, in an instant, that involuntary shrinking from her which had risen by subtle links out of the very qualities in him that made him dread her pain.

He was about to take a step which he knew would rouse her deep indignation. He would have to endure displeasure, perhaps anger, before he could win forgiveness; and Tito could never find it easy to face anger. He was far from defiance: his instincts were those of the creature that smooths and slips away from blows. Scruples, sentimental and unprofitable, did not torment him; he had argued them down with swift skill whenever they threatened to swell. But freedom from scruple is not freedom from the dread of what is disagreeable. Unscrupulousness may rid you of much, but not of toothache, nor wounded vanity, nor the hollow sound of loneliness in the room when love has withdrawn; and Tito, tasting beforehand the bitterness of the coming collision, felt as if he were being driven to a desperate leap by something close behind—teeth and claw—while his own nature was that of a timid animal that would rather creep softly and never startle any one.

Long before, he had come to think—quietly, as one convinces oneself that stale bread must be used and not wasted—that this tenacious obedience to Bardo’s wishes about the library had become, under present difficulties, a piece of sentimental folly: it deprived himself and Romola of substantial advantages. That thought might have lain inert, like yeast kept too cool to rise, if the events of the last week had not pressed on him with a new motive and opened a rare opportunity. Not until his dread had been aggravated by seeing Baldassarre look more like his sane self—more like a man whose eyes might suddenly recognise and accuse—not until Tito had begun to feel that he might be compelled to flee from Florence, did he bring himself to resolve on using his legal right to sell the library before the great chance offered by French and Milanese bidders slipped away.

If he had to leave Florence, he did not mean to leave it as a destitute wanderer. He had grown used to agreeable conditions—the ease of well-served meals, the soft assurance of being greeted with regard, the comfort of money ready to quiet a difficulty as oil quiets the squeal of a hinge. He wanted to carry with him all means at hand for keeping life pleasant. Among the things he wished to carry was Romola; and not, if possible, any infamy. Success had sharpened his appetite for all pleasures that depend on an advantageous place among men, and it could look to him only like a hideous alternative to decamp under dishonour, even with diamonds hidden in a bag, and accept the life of an adventurer.

He could not make himself independent of the Florentines who courted him; still less could he make himself independent of Romola. She was the wife of his first love—he loved her still; she belonged to that furniture of life he shrank from losing, as one shrinks from coming home to a room stripped bare. He winced under her judgment. He could not measure how far revulsion might carry her. And that husband’s sense of power which tempts betrayals that a lover would never dare was not enough to counteract Tito’s uneasiness. This was the weight that had held his neck stiff beneath her hand: the light of her eyes would have brought too near the prospect of struggle.

But it was not to be helped. If they had to leave Florence, they must have money. Indeed, Tito could not arrange life at all to his mind without a considerable sum. And the very habit of arranging life to his mind had been the source of all his misdoing. He could have made any sacrifice that was not unpleasant.

The day went on. The rustling magnates came and went: the smell of damp woollen cloaks and perfumed leather drifted through passages; boots rang on stone; foreign voices rose and fell with the clink of metal as cases were opened and shut. The bargains were concluded. When Romola returned, nothing grave was spoken that night. Tito was even gayer than usual, chatty and sparkling, pouring out tales of what he had seen and heard during the French visit—the bright surfaces of it, the pageant and the phrases—like a man ladling sweet wine to cover the taste of a bitter herb in the cup.

Romola thought she discerned effort in his liveliness, and because she loved him she chose the kind interpretation: that he knew he had wounded her in the morning and was making amends. Yet inwardly she ached at that sign of growing distance—that there was an offence about which neither dared speak, as if their speech itself had begun to lose words for what was passing between them.

The next day Tito remained away from home until late at night. It became a marked day to Romola for another reason. Piero di Cosimo, stimulated to greater industry on her behalf by the fear that he might have been the cause of pain to her in the past week, sent home her father’s portrait.

The picture had the faint, pungent odour of oil and varnish newly laid—an odour that always seemed to Romola like something half nourishment, half wound, as if the very act of making likeness were both a keeping and a hurting. She propped it against the back of Bardo’s old chair, as one sets bread beside the place of the dead at a remembered feast, and sat looking at it until the afternoon light began to thicken towards dusk. Outside, Florence sounded as it had sounded for generations: a rhythm of voices and footsteps, an occasional burst of quarrel, the far-off peal of a bell, the living city’s pulse beating through stone. Yet through all ran that harsher undertone—the tramp of foreign soldiery, the rough laughter that did not belong to Florentine streets, the iron language of occupation. It was as if a familiar song were being drowned by a louder, coarser refrain.

She had been gazing for some time when the door opened behind her and Bernardo del Nero came in.

“It is you, godfather! How I wish you had come sooner—it is getting a little dusk,” Romola said, going towards him. She took him as if she could steady herself by his presence, as by a sturdy old table that had stood in the house through many rearrangements.

“I have just looked in to tell you good news, for I know Tito has not come yet,” Bernardo said. “The French king moves off to-morrow—not before it is high time. There has been another tussle between our people and his soldiers this morning. But there’s a chance now of the city getting into order once more and trade going on.”

“That is joyful,” Romola said, and in her voice there was the thirst of one who has heard too long the wrong sounds in her own streets. “But it is sudden, is it not? Tito seemed to think yesterday there was little prospect of the king’s going soon.”

“He has been well barked at—that’s the reason,” Bernardo said, smiling, with the ease of a man whose humour has survived many storms. “His own generals opened their throats pretty well, and at last our Signoria sent the mastiff of the city—Fra Girolamo. The Cristianissimo was frightened at that thunder, and has given the order to move. I’m afraid there’ll be small agreement among us when he’s gone; but at any rate all parties are agreed in being glad not to have Florence stifled with soldiery any longer. The Frate has barked this time to some purpose.”

As he spoke, the room seemed to Romola to listen with them—the old walls that had heard Latin disputes, Greek quotations, the rustle of pages turned by Bardo’s eager fingers. Florence too was a listener: a city that knew how much the nature of the sounds in its streets could soothe or scourge the mind.

Bernardo’s eyes fell on the picture. “Ah—what is this?” he said, as Romola clasped his arm and led him in front of it. “Let us see.”

He began to unwind his long scarf while she placed a seat for him, careful, as always, with these domestic ministries which were her silent language.

“Do you not want your spectacles, godfather?” she asked, anxious that he should see precisely what she saw, not merely some dim substitute.

“No, child, no,” Bernardo said, uncovering his grey head as he seated himself with firm erectness. “For seeing at this distance, my old eyes are perhaps better than your young ones. Old men’s eyes are like old men’s memories; they are strongest for things a long way off.”

He sat silent a while, and Romola watched his face as she watched the painting—seeking in him, too, the true likeness of her dead. At last she said, apologetically, as if defending a plain dish set before a guest when one had wished to offer something finer, “It is better than having no portrait. It is less like him now than the image I have in my mind; but then that might fade with the years.”

She rested her arm on the old man’s shoulder as she spoke, drawn towards him strongly by their common interest in the dead—the need to preserve what time and noise and new tongues wear away.

“I don’t know,” Bernardo said at last. “I almost think I see Bardo as he was when he was young better than that picture shows him to me as he was when he was old. Your father had a great deal of fire in his eyes when he was young. It was what I could never understand, that he, with his fiery spirit, which seemed much more impatient than mine, could hang over the books and live with shadows all his life. However—he had put his heart into that.”

He gave a slight shrug at the last words; and Romola heard in his voice something that met her own feeling. Her father’s hunger had been for a world held fast in words—words already slipping into silence as fewer mouths shaped them, as if the very dialects and learned tongues he loved were seeds drying out for want of earth.

“And he was disappointed to the last,” she said involuntarily.

Then, fearing her words might seem an accusation against Tito, she hurried on, as if to cover the sound: “If we could only see his longest, dearest wish fulfilled just to his mind!”

“Well, so we may,” Bernardo said kindly, rising and putting on his cap. “The times are cloudy now, but fish are caught by waiting. Who knows? When the wheel has turned often enough, I may be Gonfaloniere yet before I die; and no creditor can touch these things.”

He looked round as he spoke, taking in the room with the practised eye of one who knows how quickly a house can be emptied when power shifts. Then, turning to her, he patted her cheek. “And you need not be afraid of my dying; my ghost will claim nothing. I’ve taken care of that in my will.”

Romola seized the hand that rested against her cheek and pressed it to her lips in silence, as if she could draw courage from the salt of old friendship.

“Haven’t you been scolding your husband for keeping away from home so much lately?” Bernardo said, ready to turn from the grave subject as men do who know a woman’s heart is tender. “I see him everywhere but here.”

The flush spread over Romola’s neck and face. “He has been very much wanted; you know he speaks so well. I am glad to know that his value is understood.”

“You are contented then, Madonna Orgogliosa?” Bernardo said, smiling as he moved to the door.

“Assuredly.”

Poor Romola. There was one thing that would have made the pang of disappointment in her husband harder to bear: that any one should know he gave her cause for disappointment. If it was weakness, it was close to nobleness. The woman who willingly lifts the veil of her married life has profaned it—from a sanctuary into a vulgar place.
