The winter days drifted past for Romola with the same silent, relentless rhythm as the white ships passing a lone watcher on the shore. Each vessel carried a hidden cargo of impending change, though the movement was marked only by a uniform quietude. Tito's earlier warning had infused her curiosity regarding public affairs with a deepening dread, leading her to prefer ignorance over knowledge. While the menacing German Emperor had vanished and Florence's immediate political situation had eased, a profound distress lingered. Consequently, Romola's active duties showed little diminution, serving once again as a sanctuary for her troubled mind.

She hesitated to celebrate the relief that had arrived at the brink of disaster—a relief that seemingly validated the policies of the *Piagnoni*. Indeed, Francesco Valori, the fiery leader of the faction, had been elected Gonfaloniere at the start of the year and was eager to implement his brand of liberty within his brief two-month tenure. On the surface, this appeared to strengthen the party most devoted to freedom and to bolster the protection afforded to Savonarola. Yet, Romola remained acutely sensitive to any sign that suggested the present stability was merely an unconscious incubation of mixed seeds destined to sprout tragedy. By late February, near the height of Carnival, her premonition was confirmed: the Medici family had ceased their passive stance and were actively maneuvering to secure the election of Bernardo del Nero as the new Gonfaloniere.

On the final day of Carnival, between ten and eleven in the morning, Romola departed toward the Corso degli Albizzi, fulfilling a promise to meet her cousin Brigida. Together, they planned to leave the Via de' Bardi early in the afternoon to occupy a window reserved by Tito in the Piazza della Signoria. They awaited a spectacle of unprecedented nature, one that demanded the attention of every Florentine eye. The *Piagnoni* were enforcing their vision of Carnival with absolute rigor. Despite the efforts of Dolfo Spini and his associates to revive traditional masques and bawdy jests seasoned with indecency, such frivolities were deemed incompatible with a city that had proclaimed Christ its King.

Romola set forth with a languid demeanor, typical of anyone undertaking a long day of sightseeing solely to please a cherished child or friend. The day undoubtedly marked an epoch in the history of Carnival observance, yet this phase of reform failed to ignite her enthusiasm. Unaware of it, she stood on the precipice of another epoch in her own life—one where a different destiny would become visibly intertwined with hers. Choosing to traverse the vast Piazza alone, she sought a preliminary survey of the extraordinary sight unfolding before her.

Entering from the south, her gaze fell upon something monstrous and kaleidoscopic: a structure resembling a pyramid, or more accurately, a colossal fir-tree standing sixty feet high. Its branches widened into shelves as they descended, expanding until the base circled eighty yards. The square teemed with life. Slender youths clad in white garments, olive wreaths adorning their heads, moved to and fro around the pyramid's foundation, carrying baskets brimming with vibrant colors. Beside them, mature figures—some in monastic robes, others in the loose tunics and dark-red caps of artists—assisted, examined, or retreated to vantage points to take in the wondrous totality. Among a distant group standing on the marble steps of Orgagna's Loggia, Romola recognized Piero di Cosimo, whose posture betrayed a mixture of discontent and scorn.

As she approached, she paused to inspect the myriad objects arranged in graded rows from the base to the summit. There were tapestries and brocades of immodest design; paintings and sculptures suspected of inciting vice. Boards and tables for every conceivable game lay alongside playing cards, printing blocks, dice, and other gambling paraphernalia. Worldly music books and instruments of all kinds—lutes, drums, cymbals, trumpets—were displayed prominently. Masks and costumes from the old Carnival traditions hung nearby. Handbound copies of Ovid, Boccaccio, Petrarch, Pulci, and other works deemed vain or impure lined the shelves. Then came the implements of feminine vanity: pots of rouge, false hairpieces, mirrors, perfumes, powders, and sheer veils designed to invite lecherous glances. Finally, crowning the entire edifice, sat the unflattering effigy of a mythical Venetian merchant, rumored to have paid a heavy price for this collection of "marketable abominations." Soaring above him in grotesque ugliness was the symbolic figure of the ancient, debauched Carnival.

This arrangement served as the preparation for a novel form of bonfire: the Burning of Vanities. Concealed within the pyramid's hollow core was a substantial stockpile of dry fuel and gunpowder. At dusk, on this final day of the festival, the pile was to be ignited to the blare of trumpets, sending the detested remnants of the old Carnival tumbling into the flames amidst songs of reformist triumph.

Such a grand gesture could scarcely have been orchestrated without a unique organization initiated by Savonarola two years prior. The masses of Florentine boys and youths were no longer left to their natural inclinations toward street mischief and crude dissoluteness. Guided by Fra Domenico, Savonarola's lieutenant, these lads and striplings—the hope of Florence—were instructed to speak only pure words. Their zeal was directed toward an "Unseen Good" that would shame the lukewarmness of their elders, restricting their pleasures to those of an angelic nature: singing divine praises and walking in white robes. High benches erected against the walls of the Duomo were reserved for them, where they had learned to listen to Savonarola, who addressed them as the future glory of a city divinely appointed to execute God's work.

These fresh-faced troops were the primary agents of the regenerated merriment of the new Carnival, a sacred parody of the old. Where once there had been bonfires, there would now be a fire consuming impurity from the earth. Where symbolic processions had existed, there were now processions featuring white robes, red crosses, and olive wreaths—emblems of peace and innocent joy—with banners proclaiming the triumphs of goodness. Instead of dancing in rings beneath the open sky to the sound of choral voices singing licentious songs, there would be dancing in a circle involving monks and laity united in fraternal love and divine joy, accompanied by hymns.

Collections from street passersby were expected to exceed previous totals, not to fund lavish feasts, but to aid the hungry and needy. Furthermore, there was the collection of the *Anathema*, or the Vanities, destined for the great pyramidal bonfire. Regiments of youthful inquisitors patrolled the streets, urging households to surrender their forbidden items. Perhaps, after yielding the obvious vanities, the mistress of the house still possessed small, reddened balls imported from the Levant, meant to create a deceptive flush of artificial beauty on a pale cheek? If so, she was urged to bring them down and cast them into the basket of doom. Or perhaps she kept ringlets and coils of "dead hair"? Then she was to bring them to the street door, holding them in her hands rather than wearing them, publicly renouncing the *Anathema* that masked the signs of age with a ghastly mockery of youth. In return, she would hear fresh, young voices pronounce a blessing upon her and her home.

The beardless inquisitors, organized into little battalions, undoubtedly embraced this task with eagerness. Coercing people through shame or spiritual pressure into relinquishing possessions they might resent losing proved to be a form of piety easily adopted by the boyish mind. Even when obstinate sinners threatened whips or cudgels, the excitement persisted. Savonarola himself seemed aware of the difficulties inherent in training these children, balancing noble aspirations with an imperfect grasp of means, often resorting to supernatural constraints as the only sure hope. The Florentine youth had harbored vicious habits and foul tongues; initially, it seemed an unmitigated blessing when they shouted "*Viva Gesù!*" However, Savonarola eventually confessed from the pulpit that there was "too much shouting." He warned that the constant repetition of sacred words risked bringing them into contempt, ordering a cessation of the clamor until the next feast.

Nevertheless, the sight of the long stream of robed youthfulness, adorned with little red crosses and olive wreaths, proceeding to the Duomo at dawn to receive communion from Savonarola's hands was undeniably beautiful. Doubtless, many of these young souls stored away memories of hope and awe that would protect them from viewing their civic duties through a merely vulgar lens. No conscious obedience represents a step backward from lawlessness, and these boys evolved into the generation of men who fought bravely and endured greatly in the Republic's final struggle.

In the intervening hours between early communion and dinner, the youths engaged in their final rounds to collect alms and vanities. This was the reason Romola observed the slender white figures moving about the base of the great pyramid.

"What think you of this folly, Madonna Romola?" a brusque voice asked close to her ear.

"Your *Piagnoni* will make Hell a pleasant prospect for us if they continue to carry matters their own way on earth," the speaker continued. "It takes a cudgel to haul over the mountains to see painters like Lorenzo di Credi and young Baccio assisting in the burning of color out of life in this manner."
