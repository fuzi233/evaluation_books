My next obligation is to lay before you the scant but critical details regarding the Moonstone—or, more accurately, the Indian conspiracy to steal it. Though brief, these facts hold a peculiar significance, foreshadowing events yet to unfold.

Approximately a week after Miss Verinder departed, a clerk entered my private study, holding a calling card. A gentleman awaited in the lobby, desiring an audience.

I examined the card. The name was foreign, now faded from my memory, but beneath it, a line in English remained vividly etched in my mind: "Recommended by Mr. Septimus Luker."

The sheer audacity of a man in Mr. Luker's station to recommend anyone to *me* stunned me into silence. I stared at the paper, doubting my own eyes. The clerk, noticing my confusion, offered his assessment of the man downstairs.

"He is a striking figure, sir. His complexion is so dark we initially mistook him for an Indian or someone of similar origin."

Connecting the clerk's observation with the inscription on the card, I suspected the Moonstone was the catalyst for both Mr. Luker's endorsement and this stranger's visit. To my clerk's astonishment, I immediately granted an audience.

To justify this lapse in professional decorum driven by mere curiosity, I need only remind the reader that no living soul in England possesses a connection to the romance of the Indian Diamond as intimate as mine. I was entrusted with Colonel Herncastle's escape plans and his periodic letters confirming his survival. I drafted his will, bequeathing the Moonstone to Miss Verinder, and convinced his executor to act on the hope of a valuable acquisition. Furthermore, I allayed Mr. Franklin Blake's scruples, persuading him to transport the jewel to Lady Verinder's residence. If anyone holds a prescriptive right to the Moonstone and its entanglements, it is undoubtedly me.

When my mysterious client was ushered in, I felt an immediate certainty: I stood before one of the three Indians—likely the chief. He wore European attire, yet his swarthy skin, lithe frame, and grave, graceful politeness betrayed his Oriental heritage to any discerning eye.

I gestured to a chair and requested the nature of his business.

After apologizing in flawless English for the intrusion, the Indian produced a small parcel wrapped in cloth of gold. Beneath this and a second layer of silk lay a tiny casket, exquisitely inlaid with jewels upon an ebony base.

"I have come, sir," he said, "to request a loan. I leave this casket as assurance that my debt shall be repaid."

I pointed to his card. "And you seek me at Mr. Luker's recommendation?"

The Indian bowed.

"May I inquire why Mr. Luker himself did not advance the funds you require?"

"Mr. Luker informed me, sir, that he possessed no money to lend."

"And thus, he directed you to me?"

The Indian indicated the card again. "It is written there."

Concise and to the point! I knew that if the Moonstone had been in my possession, this Oriental gentleman would have murdered me without hesitation. Yet, barring that lethal risk, I must testify that he was the epitome of a client. He might not have valued my life, but he respected my time—a courtesy none of my countrymen had ever shown me in my years of practice.

"I regret," I said, "that you were compelled to trouble me. Mr. Luker is mistaken in sending you here. While I am trusted with funds to lend, as any practitioner of my trade, I never lend to strangers, nor on such security as you have presented."

Rather than attempting to sway me, the Indian merely bowed again, rewrapped his casket in its two coverings without protest, and rose. This admirable assassin prepared to depart the instant I had answered.

"Will your condescension toward a stranger permit one final question before I leave?" he asked.

I bowed in return. One question at parting! The average in my experience was fifty.

"Supposing, sir, it had been possible—and customary—for *you* to lend me the money," he inquired, "in what timeframe would it have been possible—and customary—for *me* to repay it?"

"According to standard practice in this country," I replied, "you would be entitled to repay, if you chose, within one year from the date of the loan."

The Indian made his final, lowest bow, then suddenly and softly exited the room.

He moved with a momentary, noiseless, supple grace that startled me. Once composed, I reached a singular conclusion regarding this incomprehensible visitor. His face, voice, and manner had been under such perfect control during our meeting that they defied scrutiny. Yet, he had offered me a glimpse beneath his smooth exterior. He showed no sign of retaining what I said until I mentioned the customary period for earliest repayment. At that precise moment, he looked me straight in the face. I inferred that his final question served a special purpose, and my answer held a specific interest for him. The more I reflected on our exchange, the more shrewdly I suspected the casket and the loan request were mere formalities designed to pave the way for that parting inquiry.

I had confirmed this theory when a letter arrived from none other than Mr. Septimus Luker himself. He begged my pardon in sickeningly servile terms, promising an explanation if I would grant him a personal interview.

Curiosity, that fatal flaw, compelled me to honor him with an appointment for the following day.

Mr. Luker was, in every respect, an inferior creature to the Indian—vulgar, ugly, cringing, and prosy—rendering him unworthy of detailed report. The substance of his confession was as follows:

The day prior to the Indian's visit, Mr. Luker had been summoned by that accomplished gentleman. Despite the visitor's European disguise, Mr. Luker instantly recognized him as the chief of the three Indians who had previously harassed him, leaving him no choice but to consult a magistrate. From this revelation, he naturally concluded he was in the company of the men who had blindfolded, gagged, and robbed him of his banker's receipt. Overcome by terror, he believed his last hour had arrived.

On his side, the Indian maintained the guise of a perfect stranger. He produced the casket and made the identical application he later used with me. Seeking the swiftest means to dismiss him, Mr. Luker immediately declared he had no money. The Indian then asked for the safest person to approach for a loan. In his panic, Mr. Luker suggested a respectable solicitor. When asked to name one, Mr. Luker mentioned me—the sole reason being that, in his extreme fright, my name was the first that came to mind. "The perspiration poured off me like rain, sir," the wretched man concluded. "I knew not what I spoke. I hope you will overlook it, Mr. Bruff, in consideration of my having been truly frightened out of my wits."

I excused him graciously, eager to release myself from his presence. Before he departed, I detained him for one inquiry: Had the Indian said anything notable when leaving Mr. Luker's house?

"Yes!" Mr. Luker confirmed. "The Indian asked precisely the same question of me at parting that he asked of you, receiving, of course, the identical answer."

What did it signify? Mr. Luker's explanation offered no aid in solving the puzzle. My own ingenuity proved insufficient to grapple with the difficulty. That evening, I had a dinner engagement; I ascended the stairs in a mood far from genial, little suspecting that the path to my dressing room and the path to discovery were, on this occasion, one and the same.
