CHAPTER XXXIX. “Before the wise, to stand—
Safe in every case!
If long you’ve suffered pain,
He knows where it lacks.
And praise you may expect;
For he sees where you hit.” —GOETHE: _West-östlicher Divan_

That very evening after Deronda’s visit to the little house in Chelsea—where the conversation had turned on Mirah’s public name—something momentous unfolded for him. But for those gathered there, the true consequence of that meeting came two days later.

At about four o’clock, wheels paused before the door, followed by a knock and ring so resonant it seemed to announce not just a visitor, but an event. In this quiet corner of London, where the most stirring sounds were often the muffin-man’s call, such a signal stirred the air with sudden importance. All the girls were at home, and the two rooms had been joined together to accommodate Kate’s latest drawing project—and a long stretch of embroidery, now taking center stage like a grand finale in their domestic artistry. It sprawled across the front room, a colorful tapestry of threads, with Mrs. Meyrick bent over one end, Mab in the middle, and Amy at the other. Mirah, whose sewing skills were more suited to patchwork than precision—her education in needlework having been neglected—sat on a camp-stool reading aloud to the group. She also served as Kate’s model for a vignette depicting a fair audience absorbed in the family tea-table’s literary offerings.

She was reciting Elia’s delightful essay, “The Praise of Chimney-Sweeps,” her voice clear and charming, drawing smiles from all at the mention of “innocent blackness,” when the knock and ring interrupted them. They looked up, startled.

“Dear me!” said Mrs. Meyrick. “Could it be Lady Mallinger? Is there a grand carriage, Amy?”

“No—only a hansom cab,” Amy replied. “It must be a gentleman.”

“The Prime Minister, I should think,” Kate said dryly. “Hans says even the greatest man in London can ride in a hansom.”

“Oh, oh, oh!” Mab cried. “Suppose it’s Lord Russell!”

Their faces lit with amusement when the old maid-servant rushed in, card in hand, and left the parlor door open. And there, bowing toward Mrs. Meyrick, stood a figure quite unlike any political leader—tall, commanding, even in his simple coat and kerseymere, with a massive face, fiery hair, and gold spectacles. From the card, Mrs. Meyrick read: _Julius Klesmer_.

Even if she’d been flustered, the “little mother” would have remained composed. But she quickly grasped the significance: the great musician had come to Mirah, not the other way around—a sign of genuine interest. Yet as he stepped inside, the small rooms seemed to shrink into closets. The cottage piano looked absurdly tiny, and the whole household felt suddenly insignificant, like mice in the Tuileries.

Klesmer’s presence alone evoked vast halls and crowded audiences. His glance swept the room as though he were surveying a concert hall, and indeed, he likely did—because we each carry our inner world within a familiar setting, and those who imagine larger stages often seem pompous to others. Klesmer was vain, yes—but no more so than many men of stature whose vanity bursts forth like a hidden blade. His gestures were natural, his bearing effortless. To appear diffident would have been the true affectation.

As he took in the scene, his sensitivity revealed itself—not just in observation, but in empathy. He remembered a modest home on the edge of Bohemia, and knew well the dignity found in humble lives. He addressed Mrs. Meyrick with deep respect.

“I hope I’m not overstepping,” he said. “Being nearby, I thought I’d save time by calling. Mr. Deronda mentioned that I might meet a young lady here—Miss Lapidoth.”

Though he’d seen Mirah the moment he entered, he glanced politely around at the three sisters as if uncertain which was the one in question.

“These are my daughters,” Mrs. Meyrick said, gesturing toward Mirah. “This is Miss Lapidoth.”

“Ah,” Klesmer replied, turning a radiant smile and deep bow toward her. Mirah, calm and unflustered, met his gaze with quiet joy. She liked him—felt sure he would scold her, like a great artist and a kind soul.

“You’ll not mind beginning our acquaintance with a song?” he asked, eager to skip the formalities.

“I’d be honored,” Mirah said. “It’s generous of you to listen.” She moved to the piano.

“Shall I accompany myself?” she asked.

“By all means,” Klesmer replied, seating himself where he could see her clearly. Mrs. Meyrick didn’t admit it, but she thought: *He’ll appreciate her singing more if he sees her.*

All hearts—except Mirah’s—beat fast. Klesmer sat with a stern, listening frown, and they dared only glance at him sideways. If he criticized, it would hurt them all. Still, they comforted themselves: Prince Camaralzaman, who’d heard the finest voices, had preferred Mirah’s. And she sang with such care—more at ease than usual.

She chose a powerful setting of words from Leopardi’s Ode to Italy:
“_O patria mia, vedo le mura e gli archi / E le colonne e i simulacri e l’erme / Torridi degli avi nostri._”
Recitative. Then: “_Ma la gloria—non vedo_”—a mournful melody, a rhythmic lament. Then, rising into triumph: “_Beatissimi voi / Che offriste il petto alle nemiche lance / Per amor di costei che al sol vi diede_”—a soft adoration. Then, bursting forth: “_Oh viva, oh viva: Beatissimi voi / Mentre nel mondo si favelli o scriva._”

When she finished, Klesmer sat silent for a moment. Then: “That is old Leo’s music.”

“Yes,” Mirah said softly. “He was my last teacher in Vienna—so fierce, yet so good.” She smiled wistfully. “He said my voice wasn’t meant for the stage. And he was right.”

“_Con_tinue,” Klesmer said, pursing his lips, shaking his fingers, muttering something unintelligible.

The three girls hated him—why say nothing? Mrs. Meyrick felt a pang. Mirah, focused only on obeying, assumed he wanted German next. She sang through Prince Radzivill’s settings of Gretchen’s songs from _Faust_, one after another, without pause.

When she finished, Klesmer rose, walked to the far end of the room, then returned to the piano. Mirah stood, hands crossed before her, waiting.

Then—suddenly—his brow softened, his eyes gleamed, and he reached out his hand. “Let us shake hands,” he said firmly. “You are a musician.”

Mab felt tears rise. The girls all fell in love with him.

Mrs. Meyrick exhaled deeply.

But instantly, the frown returned. He held out his hand again, palm up, and gently touched the back of Mirah’s with his fingertip. “Not for great tasks,” he said, lip protruding. “No high roofs. We are no skylarks. We must be modest.”

Mab’s admiration cooled. *As if Mirah had shown the least pride!*

Mirah waited in silence. Klesmer continued: “I wouldn’t advise or support your singing in any space larger than a private drawing-room. But there, you will do. And in London, that’s one of the best paths open. Lessons will follow. Will you sing at a private concert at my house on Wednesday?”

“Oh, I’d be grateful,” Mirah said, hands clasped devoutly. “I’d rather earn my bread that way than through anything more public. I’ll try to improve. What should I focus on?”

Klesmer muttered, fingers twitching, then spoke clearly: “I’ll introduce you to Astorga—he is the foster-father of good singing. He’ll guide you.”

Then, turning to Mrs. Meyrick: “Mrs. Klesmer will call before Wednesday, if you allow it.”

“We’ll consider it a great kindness,” Mrs. Meyrick said.

“You’ll sing for her,” Klesmer added, turning back to Mirah. “She is a true musician, and has more ears than most. Your singing will satisfy her. ‘Vor den Wissenden sich stellen.’ You know the rest?”

“‘Sicher ist’s in allen Fällen,’” Mirah replied promptly.

“Schön!” Klesmer said, extending his hand again. He had praised her in the most delicate way. The girls now admired him fully.

Then, fixing his gaze on Mab, he declared: “That young lady is musical, I see.”

She blushed, burned.

“Yes,” Mirah said, defending her. “And she has a touch.”

“Oh, please, Mirah—a scramble, not a touch,” Mab said, trembling. *What would he demand next?* This man—this terrible, knowing spirit—might make her sit at the piano. Her heart felt molten.

But instead, he smiled kindly. “Will she accompany Miss Lapidoth and hear the music on Wednesday?”

“There could hardly be a greater pleasure,” Mrs. Meyrick said. “She’ll be delighted.”

Klesmer bowed to the three sisters—more grandly than they’d ever been bowed to. The image was amusing: the small room, the immense sweep of his bow, the sisters like miniature figures, the Holbein portraits on the walls watching silently, as if witnessing a drama beyond their time.

Mrs. Meyrick slipped out with him, closing the door behind her.

He understood her. “She will do,” he said, nodding. “If she doesn’t overreach, and her voice holds, she can earn a living. I know that’s the point—Deronda told me. You’re looking after her. She seems a good girl.”

“She is an angel,” Mrs. Meyrick said warmly.

“No,” Klesmer replied, playfully. “She is a pretty Jewess. Let’s not give angels all the credit. But I think she’s found a guardian angel.” He bowed and left.

The four girls stared at each other in stunned silence until the door slammed. Then—explosion.

Mab clapped and danced wildly. Mrs. Meyrick kissed Mirah and blessed her. Amy exclaimed: “We can never get her a new dress before Wednesday!” Kate said: “Thank heaven my table isn’t knocked over!”

Mirah had quietly sat back on her stool, tears streaming down her cheeks.

“Now, now, Mab,” Mrs. Meyrick said. “Come sit and let’s talk.”

“Yes, let’s talk,” Mab agreed, returning to her seat, caressing her knees. “I feel large again. Hans said he’d come this afternoon. I wish he’d been here—though there’d have been no room for him. Mirah, why are you sad?”

“I’m too happy,” Mirah said. “Full of gratitude. And he was so kind.”

“Yes, at last,” Mab said sharply. “But he might have said something encouraging sooner. I thought him dreadfully ugly when he frowned and said only ‘_Con_tinue.’ I hated him—from the top of his head to the toe of his boot.”

“Nonsense,” Kate said. “He has a splendid profile.”

“_Now_, but not _then_,” Mab insisted. “I can’t bear people who bottle up their thoughts just to burst them out with a pop. It feels like they begrudge your happiness unless they make you miserable first. But I forgive him everything—though he invited me. Why did he pick me as the musical one? Was it because I have a bulging forehead and peep from under it like a newt under a stone?”

“It was how you listened,” Mrs. Meyrick said. “He sees everything through those magic spectacles.”

“What was that German quote, Mirah?” Amy asked. “You learned it so quickly.”

“Oh, that wasn’t learning,” Mirah said, smiling through her tears. “I repeated it for lessons. It means: it’s safer to perform—sing, or do anything—before those who truly understand.”

“That’s why you weren’t afraid,” Amy said.

“But now—we must talk about your dress for Wednesday.”

“I don’t need anything better than this black merino,” Mirah said, standing to show it. “Just white gloves and new _bottines.”_ She lifted her foot, still clad in the famous felt slipper.

“There comes Hans,” Mrs. Meyrick said. “Stand still—let’s hear what he thinks.”

Hans entered. Mirah turned toward him childlike. He knelt on a hassock to examine her.

“This would be a fine stage-dress for me,” she said pleadingly, “if I were playing a poor Jewess singing to fashionable Christians.”

“It would stand out,” Hans said thoughtfully. “Among the fashionable _chiffons_.”

“But you shouldn’t claim all the poverty,” Amy said. “There are poor Christians and rich Jews alike.”

“I didn’t mean harm,” Mirah said. “I’ve always thought about dresses for roles. I usually had plain ones.”

“That makes it questionable,” Hans said, suddenly conventional—just as he’d judged Deronda over the Berenice paintings. “It looks a bit theatrical. We mustn’t make you a _rôle_—not of a poor Jewess, nor of being a Jewess at all.”

“But it’s who I am,” Mirah said. “I’m not pretending. I’ll never be anything else. I always feel myself a Jewess.”

“But we don’t feel that,” Hans said, earnestly. “What does it matter whether a perfect woman is a Jewess?”

“That’s your kind way of praising me,” Mirah smiled. “I’ve never been praised so.”

“People don’t see me as British Christian,” Hans said, grinning. “They see me as an imperfectly handsome young man and an unpromising painter.”

“But the dress—the dress!” Amy insisted.

“Yes—is it settled?” Mirah asked, looking at Mrs. Meyrick, who glanced at her son.

“What do you think, Hans?”

“That dress won’t do,” Hans said firmly. “She’s not going to sit on ruins. Little mother, jump into a cab with her and go to Regent Street. Plenty of time to get a black silk dress—like ladies wear. She mustn’t be mistaken for charity. She has talents that make people owe her.”

“I think Mr. Deronda would want her to have a fine dress,” Mrs. Meyrick said.

“Of course he would,” Hans said sharply. “Take my word—any gentleman would.”

“I wish to do what Mr. Deronda would want,” Mirah said gravely.

Hans turned away, picked up one of Kate’s drawings.

“Shouldn’t you sketch Klesmer’s head, Hans?” Kate asked. “Have you seen him often?”

“Seen him!” Hans exclaimed, throwing back his head, seating himself at the piano, surveying the room as if it were an amphitheater, fingers poised above the keys.

Then, in a flash, he wheeled around, looked at Mirah, and said half-shyly: “Perhaps you don’t like this mimicry. Always stop me when you don’t.”

Mirah had been smiling—now she smiled more, but with something deeper. “Thank you. But you’ve never done anything I didn’t like. I hardly think he could, belonging to you,” she added, looking at Mrs. Meyrick.

And so Hans found nourishment for his hope. How could the rose resist when several bees, one after another, took its sweetness as proof of personal devotion?
