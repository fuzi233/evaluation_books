Archer stood frozen, the weight of old Catherine’s words pressing upon him. That Madame Olenska had rushed back from Washington at her grandmother’s summons was no surprise—but that she had chosen to stay, now that Mrs. Mingott had nearly recovered, defied easy explanation.

Money, he was certain, had not swayed her. He knew the meager sum her husband allotted her after their separation—hardly enough to sustain even the barest existence by Mingott standards, let alone support both herself and the ruined Medora Manson. Yet Archer refused to believe Ellen had accepted her grandmother’s generosity out of necessity. She had always been indifferent to wealth, reckless with it even, as only those accustomed to abundance could be. She lacked the Mingott obsession with appearances, a fact that had often drawn sighs from her aunts.

More telling was the silence between them. Months had passed since her allowance was cut, yet she had made no move to regain favor. If she had altered her course now, it was not for financial refuge.

No—he knew the reason.

That day on the ferry, she had pressed her face against his chest and whispered that they must part. There had been no artifice in her words, only the same desperate resolve that had driven his own struggle. She would not betray those who trusted them.

Yet in the days since her return, his silence must have spoken volumes. Had she sensed his resolve hardening? Had she feared her own weakness and chosen, at last, the path of least resistance?

When he had rung Mrs. Mingott’s bell earlier, he had believed his course was set. He had meant to speak with Ellen alone—or, failing that, to learn when she would return to Washington. He had planned to follow her, to board that train and go as far as she would let him. Japan, perhaps. He had imagined leaving May a note that would sever all other possibilities.

He had thought himself ready—even eager—for the leap. Yet when he learned that Ellen would stay, his first feeling had been relief.

Now, walking home through the cold streets, the weight of what lay ahead settled upon him. The path before him was not unknown—he had trodden it before, but as a free man, unbound by consequence. The game of discretion, the careful dance of lies and half-truths, had once been played with detached amusement.

Now, it sickened him.

This was the code of "protecting a woman’s honor," a script he knew by heart. But where he had once seen gallantry, he now saw only deception—a smiling, endless lie. A lie in every glance, every touch, every silence.

For a wife to deceive a husband was one thing—society expected little better. But for a man to deceive his wife? That was contemptible. Archer had always thought so. Yet here he stood, on the brink of becoming what he despised.

Ellen Olenska was no ordinary woman, and he was no ordinary man. Their love existed outside convention—did that not absolve them?

But then there was May. And duty. And honor.

At the corner of Fifth Avenue, he hesitated. Ahead loomed the dark bulk of the Beaufort mansion, its windows unlit save for one upstairs room. A carriage waited at the door—Mrs. Manson Mingott’s. The sight of it twisted something inside him.

Then the door opened.

Ellen emerged, silhouetted against the faint light from within. She turned to say a word to someone unseen before stepping into the night.

"Ellen," he called softly.

She startled, her hand tightening on her muff. Just then, two figures rounded the corner—Lefferts and young Chivers, their polished indifference grating against Archer’s raw nerves.

As they passed, he grasped her hand, the warmth of her skin searing through his gloves.

"I’ll see you now—we’ll be together," he said, the words tumbling out.

She exhaled. "Granny told you?"

He barely registered her reply. His mind raced. "Tomorrow—somewhere we can be alone."

She hesitated, then gave a brittle laugh. "In New York? Where?"

"The Art Museum. At half-past two."

She turned away without answer, slipping into the carriage. As it pulled away, he thought he saw her hand lift in farewell.

He stood there, hollowed by longing and doubt.

---

The museum was quiet, the halls empty but for the echoes of their steps. They wandered past forgotten relics—fragments of lives long gone, labeled *Use unknown*.

"It’s cruel," she murmured, "how little any of it matters in the end."

Archer watched her, the way the light caught the curve of her cheek beneath her veil. "Everything matters—if it concerns you."

She turned to him then, her eyes dark with unspoken fear. "Why did you bring me here?"

"Because I know why you stayed. You were afraid—afraid I’d follow you to Washington."

Her hands trembled in her muff. "Yes."

"And now?"

She exhaled. "This is better, isn’t it? We hurt fewer people this way."

"Better?" His voice roughened. "To meet in shadows? To lie?"

"You once said this was what you wanted."

"I was a fool." He stepped closer. "Tell me you don’t want more."

A tear slipped down her cheek, silver in the dim light. "I can’t stay. I won’t destroy their lives—not when they’ve helped rebuild mine."

He caught her wrist. "Then come to me once. Just once."

She looked at him as if he were both salvation and ruin. "And then go home?"

"Home?" The word was ash in his mouth.

"To my husband."

He released her as if burned.

The silence between them was a living thing.

At last, she whispered, "The day after tomorrow."

Then she was gone, leaving him alone with the weight of what they had done—and what they would yet do.

---

That evening, May found him by the fire, his thoughts adrift.

"I’ve just seen Ellen," she said, her voice bright with forced cheer. "We had such a good talk."

Archer stared at her, seeing the strain beneath her smile.

She touched his shoulder. "You’re very quiet."

He caught her hand, struck by the fragility of her fingers. "It’s nothing."

But it was everything.

And tomorrow would change it all.
