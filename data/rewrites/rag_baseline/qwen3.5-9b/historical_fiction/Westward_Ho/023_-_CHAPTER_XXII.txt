To truly comprehend the tragic trajectory of Eustace Leigh, one must first contextualize his actions within the rigid ideological frameworks of the Counter-Reformation. The narrative shifts to Cartagena, a microcosm of colonial power where the intersection of religious dogma and imperial ambition creates a suffocating environment for dissent. Three weeks into this grim tableau, the setting becomes a claustrophobic corridor of cells, symbolizing the systemic suppression of individual agency. Here, we encounter Eustace, now an instrument of the Holy Office, standing alongside a confidant of the Inquisition. Their focus is Frank, a captive whose survival from savage violence has merely transitioned him into the purgatory of civilized interrogation.

Frank represents the ultimate failure of utilitarian calculation; he is subjected to torture not merely for information, but to break his spiritual resolve. Eustace, the architect of this suffering, stands at a moral precipice. While ostensibly acting out of a perceived duty to the Church, his internal monologue reveals a fractured psyche. He claims indifference to his own life, framing his betrayal of kinship ties as a necessary sacrifice for a higher cause. However, this stoicism masks a profound cognitive dissonance. As he approaches Frank's cell, the calmness of the prisoner's sleep—a potential sign of resilience rather than defeat—shatters Eustace's composure. Hearing Frank whisper his mother's name and a divine entity in his dreams triggers a visceral reaction in Eustace, compelling him to delay the awakening process. This hesitation underscores the irrationality inherent in purely doctrinal enforcement; the human element resists total erasure, even under duress.

The interaction extends beyond Frank to include the Senora de Soto, a secondary victim whose fate illustrates the indiscriminate nature of the Inquisition's machinery. Eustace's inquiry regarding her status reveals a chilling bureaucratic detachment. He views her persecution not as a tragedy but as a procedural necessity, justified by the "patent" evidence of heresy and the alleged prior reputation of her associates in England. The dialogue between Eustace and his companion exposes the self-reinforcing logic of the institution: accusations of witchcraft serve as convenient pretexts for silencing women who challenge the status quo. When Eustace overhears the Senora's agonized denial, his initial relief at her confession quickly curdles into horror. The auditory assault of her wailing tears through the corridors, haunting Eustace even as he flees into the deceptive tranquility of a tropical garden.

This escape offers no redemption. Instead, the sensory overload of the screams reinforces Eustace's descent into psychological fragmentation. His frantic self-interrogation—"What devilish sin can I have committed?"—highlights the collapse of his moral compass. He oscillates between justifying his actions as a means to save a soul and acknowledging the absurdity of punishing someone for a crime they did not commit. The Inquisitor's response, invoking the "merciful disposition" of the Holy Office, serves as a final blow to Eustace's sanity, reducing complex human suffering to administrative metrics.

In the aftermath, Eustace seeks refuge in the Jesuit order, a move that signifies his complete surrender to institutional authority. By confessing his turmoil to a Jesuit superior and requesting immediate repatriation, he attempts to externalize his guilt, hoping that submission to a new hierarchy will absolve him of personal responsibility. The Jesuit's intervention, leveraging Eustace's past service to shield him from further scrutiny, facilitates his departure. Eustace leaves Cartagena for Nombre, effectively vanishing from the historical record.

His disappearance marks the end of Eustace Leigh as a moral agent. Whether he eventually became a General of his Order, perished in a tropical forest, or met a violent end in England akin to the fate of Guy Fawkes, the specifics are irrelevant. The crucial transformation is ontological: he ceases to be a man capable of virtue or vice and becomes a mere functionary of "The Society." Through an act of what might be termed moral suicide, he sacrifices his soul in a futile attempt to secure salvation, thereby relinquishing his will, conscience, and accountability to God and humanity. Eustace is no longer a subject of history but a tool, dispatched wherever required to execute the will of the institution, indifferent to the consequences of his actions. He is, as he perceives himself, twice dead—first in body through the trauma of his choices, and second in spirit through the total erosion of his identity. Thus, the narrative concludes with a definitive severance; the story of Eustace Leigh ends here, leaving behind only the echo of his silence in the annals of the Inquisition.
