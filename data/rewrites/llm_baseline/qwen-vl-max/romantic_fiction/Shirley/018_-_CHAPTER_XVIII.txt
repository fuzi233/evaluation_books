The evening was still and warm, thick with the quiet hum of summer. The sun dipped low, painting the sky in deep purples and golden hues—colors more fitting to an Indian twilight than an English one. These rich tones spilled across the hillsides, rooftops, tree trunks, winding lanes, and rolling pastures, turning everything into a dreamlike scene. Slowly, the two girls made their way down from the fields. By the time they reached the churchyard, the bells had fallen silent, and the congregation had gathered inside. The world outside seemed hushed, empty, and full of peace.

"How peaceful and calm it is!" Caroline said softly.

"And how stifling it will be in there," Shirley replied. "And what a long, tedious sermon Dr. Boultby will deliver! The curates will drone on with their rehearsed speeches. Honestly, I’d rather stay out here."

"But Uncle will be angry if we’re not present."

"I’ll take the blame," Shirley said, smiling. "He won’t devour me. I’ll miss his sharp remarks—always defending the church, always lashing out at dissenters. He won’t forget Royd Lane. And I’ll miss your chance to hear Mr. Hall’s honest, heartfelt sermon—full of that lovely Yorkshire charm. But I’m staying. Look—the gray church and the older graves glow under this crimson light. Nature is praying now. She kneels before those red hills, offering her evening worship. I see her prostrate on the steps of her great altar, begging for safe seas for sailors, for travelers in deserts, for lambs on moors, for young birds in trees. Caroline, I see her—and I’ll tell you what she looks like. She reminds me of Eve when Adam and she stood alone on earth."

"Not Milton’s Eve," Caroline said gently.

"Milton’s Eve? No, by the pure Mother of God, she isn’t! Cary, we’re alone—we can speak our minds. Milton was brilliant, but was he kind? His mind was clear—but what of his heart? He saw heaven, looked down upon hell. He saw Satan, Sin, and Death. Angels lined up before him, their shields flashing like stars. Devils marched past, dim and tarnished. Milton tried to picture the first woman—but he failed. He saw only a servant or a cook, perhaps Mrs. Gill making custards in summer heat, cooling the kitchen with roses and nasturtiums outside the window, preparing a cold feast for the rector—preserves and 'dulcet creams,' wondering which dish would best please, which order would delight without mixing flavors too harshly."

"That sounds quite charming," Caroline said.

"Let me remind him," Shirley continued, "that the first humans were Titans, and Eve was their mother. From her came Saturn, Hyperion, Oceanus. She bore Prometheus—"

"Pagan talk!" Caroline laughed. "What does any of that matter?"

"It matters because giants walked the earth then—giants who dared to reach for the heavens. The first woman’s heart, beating with life, held the courage to challenge God, the strength to endure endless bondage, the vitality to feed death through ages, the unbroken spirit and purity that, after centuries of suffering and sin, could bring forth a Savior. She was born of heaven. Her heart was the source of all nations’ blood. Her head wore the crown of creation—unshaken, noble, eternal."

"She ate an apple and was tricked by a serpent," Caroline said, shaking her head. "But you’ve mixed Scripture and myth so wildly, I can hardly follow. Still—you haven’t told me what you saw kneeling on those hills."

"I saw—still see—a woman-Titan. Her robe of blue air stretches to the edge of the heath where sheep graze. A veil as white as snow falls from her head to her feet, edged with lightning. Around her waist, a belt of purple like the horizon glows, and through it shines the evening star. Her eyes—I cannot paint them. They are clear, deep as lakes, raised in reverence, trembling with love and prayer. Her forehead is wide as a cloud, pale as the moon before nightfall. She rests her chest on Stilbro’ Moor, hands clasped beneath. In this posture, face to face with God, she speaks. That Eve is Jehovah’s daughter, just as Adam was His son."

"She’s vague and dreamy," Caroline said. "Come, Shirley—we should go in."

"No," Shirley insisted. "I’ll stay here with my mother Eve—Nature. I love her—this living, enduring force. Heaven may have faded from her brow when she fell, but all that’s beautiful on earth still shines within her. She draws me into her arms, shows me her soul. Hush, Caroline—if we’re both quiet, you’ll feel it too."

"I’ll humor you," she said. "But you’ll start talking again in ten minutes."

Miss Keeldar leaned against a tall headstone, captivated by the warmth of the evening. Her gaze fixed on the glowing west, and she drifted into a soft reverie. Caroline stepped aside, walking slowly beneath the rectory wall, lost in her own thoughts. Shirley had spoken of “mother”—a word that stirred something deep within Caroline. Not the vast, mystical figure of Shirley’s vision, but a gentle, human form—the image she carried of her own mother: unknown, unloved, yet never forgotten.

"Oh, that the day would come when she remembers me," she whispered. "That I might know her—and love her!"

Her childhood longing rose again. The wish that had kept her awake many nights, nearly extinguished by fear of illusion, flared bright once more. She dreamed of a happy day when her mother would send for her, look at her with tender eyes, and say in a sweet voice, “Caroline, my child, I have a home for you. You shall live with me. All the love you’ve missed since infancy—I’ve saved it for you. Come; let it comfort you now.”

A sound on the road broke her reverie. Shirley was pulled from her visions. They listened—horses clattered softly. Through the trees, they glimpsed flashes of scarlet, helmets gleaming, plumes fluttering. Six soldiers rode past in silence, orderly and deliberate.

"The same ones we saw earlier," Shirley whispered. "They’ve been waiting somewhere. They want to stay unnoticed, meeting at this quiet hour while people are in church. Didn’t I say we’d see strange things soon?"

Before the last hoofbeat faded, another sound pierced the stillness—a child’s loud cry. They turned. A man emerged from the church, carrying a small boy, about two years old, wailing fiercely. Two little girls, nine and ten, followed close behind. The fresh air and some flowers picked from a grave soon calmed the child. The man sat down, cradling the boy on his knee with gentle care. The girls took their places beside him.

"Good evening, William," Shirley said after a moment’s study. He had seen her before and waited to be noticed. Now he tipped his hat and grinned, pleased. He was rough-looking, weathered, not old but worn by hard work. His clothes were clean, his children’s attire unusually neat. It was William Farren, their familiar friend.

"You’re not going into church?" he asked, gazing at them with pride and a hint of shyness—not from fear of their status, but admiration for their grace and youth. With men like Moore or Helstone, William could be stubborn; with haughty women, defiant. But with kindness and fairness, he softened. His strong nature rejected rigidity in others. He had never liked Moore, though the gentleman respected him—unknowingly—even recommending him to Mr. Yorke. William carried a grudge over Moore’s sternness, unaware of the favor done. Recently, he often worked at Fieldhead. Miss Keeldar’s open-hearted ways delighted him. Caroline, whom he’d known since childhood, was his ideal of a lady. Her quiet manner, her step, her elegance moved something deep within him—like seeing rare flowers or a fine landscape. Both girls liked William; they loved lending him books, giving him plants, and found his company far richer than many higher-born but coarse people.

"Who was speaking when you came out?" Shirley asked.

"A gentleman ye hold in high regard, Miss Shirley—Mr. Donne."

"You seem to know me well, William. How did you guess my feelings for him?"

"Ay, Miss Shirley, sometimes there’s a bright spark in your eyes that gives you away. You look downright scornful when Mr. Donne is near."

"Do you like him yourself?"

"Me? I’m fed up with curates, and so’s my wife. They’ve no manners. They speak to poor folk as if they think us beneath them. Always boasting of their office. It’s a shame their office couldn’t make them better men—but it doesn’t. I hate pride."

"But you’re proud in your own way," Caroline said. "You’re what you call house-proud—everything must be tidy, handsome. Sometimes you look almost too proud to accept your wages. When you were out of work, you refused credit. For your children, I believe you’d rather starve than ask for help. When I offered you something, how hard it was to get you to take it!"

"Partly true, Miss Caroline. Any day I’d rather give than take—especially from someone like you. Look at us—ye’re a slight, young girl, and I’m a big, strong man—more than twice your age. It’s not right for me to take from you—to owe you. That day you came to our door and gave me five shillings—money I know you didn’t spare easily—well, I was furious. I thought it shameful that, willing and able to work, I should be in such need that a young girl—about the age of my eldest daughter—should feel the need to offer me her coin."

"You were angry with me?"

"Nearly. But I forgave you quickly. You meant well. Yes, I am proud—and so are you. But our pride is the right kind—what we call clean pride in Yorkshire. Mr. Malone and Mr. Donne know nothing of it. Their pride is dirty. I’ll teach my daughters to be as proud as Miss Shirley, and my sons as proud as myself. But I’d whip little Michael if I saw him showing signs of curate pride."

"What’s the difference, William?"

"You know the difference well enough—but you want me to explain. Mr. Malone and Mr. Donne are so proud they can’t do anything for themselves. We’re so proud we won even let anyone do anything for us. The curates can’t bear to speak kindly to those they think below them. We can’t bear to take an unkind word from those who think themselves above us."

"Now, William," Shirley said, "be humble enough to tell me honestly—how are you doing?"

"Miss Shirley, I’m very well. Since I began gardening with Mr. Yorке’s help, and since Mr. Hall helped my wife set up a little shop, I’ve nothing to complain of. My family has food and clothes. My pride makes me save a pound here and there for rainy days. I’d die before I’d go to the parish. Me and mine are content. But the neighbors—many are poor. I see much hardship."

"And so, there’s still unrest, I suppose?" Miss Keeldar asked.

"Of course—your word is right. Starving people cannot be satisfied. The country isn’t safe. I’ll say that much!"

"But what can be done? What more can I do?"

"Do? You’ve done what you can, poor young lady. You’ve given your money. If you could send Mr. Moore to Botany Bay, you might do better. People hate him."

"William, for shame!" Caroline exclaimed. "If people hate him, it’s their fault, not his. Mr. Moore hates no one. He only wants to do his duty and protect his rights. You’re wrong to speak so."

"I speak what I think. He has a cold, hard heart, that Moore."

"But," Shirley interjected, "if Moore were driven out and his mill destroyed, would people have more work?"

"Less," William said. "I know it, and they know it. Many honest boys are driven desperate, knowing no matter which way they turn, they can’t improve. And there are dishonest men ready to lead them to ruin—scoundrels who pretend to be the people’s friends, who know nothing of the people, and are as false as Lucifer. I’ve lived forty years, and I believe ‘the people’ will never have true friends except themselves—and a few good souls in different stations who care for all. Human nature, taken as a whole, is selfish. Only a few—exceptions like you two and me—can understand each other, be friends without servility or pride. Those who claim to be friends to lower classes for political gain are never to be trusted—they always try to use them. I won’t be patronized or misled. I’ve had offers lately—I saw them as treacherous, and I threw them back in their faces."

"You won’t tell us what offers?"

"I won’t. It wouldn’t help. It wouldn’t change anything. Let them deal with their own affairs."

"Ay, we’ll look after ourselves," said another voice. Joe Scott had stepped out of the church for fresh air.

"I’ll wager you will, Joe," William smiled.

"And I’ll wager my master," Joe replied. "Young ladies," he said, adopting a stiff tone, "you’d better go inside."

"Why?" Shirley asked, used to his rigid ways. Joe resented the fact that his master’s mill was, in effect, under female influence. He disliked the heiress’s visits to the counting-house.

"Because there’s nothing happening worth women’s concern."

"Indeed? There’s prayer and preaching in that church. Are we not concerned in that?"

"You weren’t present for either, ma'am, if I observed rightly. I meant politics. William was speaking of that."

"Well, then? Politics are our daily study, Joe. Do you know I read a newspaper every day—two on Sundays?"

"I imagine you read the marriages, murders, accidents—such things?"

"I read the leading articles, foreign news, market prices. In short, I read just what gentlemen read."

Joe looked as if she were chattering nonsense. He stayed silent.

"Joe," Miss Keeldar said, "I’ve never been sure whether you’re a Whig or a Tory. Which party holds your loyalty?"

"It’s difficult to explain where you’re sure not to be understood," he snapped. "As for being a Tory—I’d as soon be an old woman—or a young one, which is worse. Tories carry on wars and ruin trade. If I belong to any party—though parties are nonsense—I’m with the one most favorable to peace, and thus to commerce."

"So am I, Joe," Shirley said, enjoying teasing him. "Though I lean toward agriculture too—after all, I don’t want England under French rule, and while part of my income comes from Hollow’s Mill, more comes from the land around it. We shouldn’t harm farmers, Joe, do you agree?"

"The dew at this hour is unhealthy for ladies," Joe said.

"If you mean it for my sake, I assure you I’m immune to cold. I’d gladly watch the mill tonight with your musket, Joe."

Joe’s chin jutted forward farther than usual.

"But—back to my point," Shirley continued. "As a clothier, mill-owner, and farmer, I can’t shake off the idea that we manufacturers and businessmen sometimes grow too selfish, too blind to suffering, too eager for profit. Don’t you agree, Joe?"

"I cannot argue where I cannot be understood," he replied.

"Man of mystery! Your master argues with me sometimes. He’s not as stiff as you."

"Maybe not. We all have our ways."

"Joe, do you truly believe all wisdom lies in male heads?"

"I think women are tricky and stubborn. I respect the teachings in the second chapter of Paul’s letter to Timothy."

"What teachings?"

"'Let the woman learn in silence, with all subjection. I suffer not a woman to teach, nor to usurp authority over the man, but to be in silence. For Adam was first formed, then Eve.'"

"How does that relate?" Shirley asked. "It sounds like primogeniture. I’ll bring it up next time Mr. Yorke complains about it."

"And," Joe added, "Adam was not deceived, but the woman, being deceived, was in the transgression."

"More shame to Adam for sinning with his eyes open!" Caroline exclaimed. "To be honest, Joe, I’ve never felt comfortable with that chapter. It puzzles me."

"It’s very plain," Joe said. "He that runs may read."

"He may read it his way," Caroline said. "You allow private judgment, I suppose?"

"My certainty, yes! I allow it for every line of the holy Book."

"Women may exercise it too?"

"No. Women should take their husbands’ opinions—in politics and religion. It’s healthiest for them."

"Oh! oh!" both Shirley and Caroline exclaimed.

"Of course," Joe insisted. "No doubt about it."

"Consider yourself groaned down and shouted at for such foolishness," Miss Keeldar said. "You might as well say men should take their priests’ words without question. What value would such a religion have? It would be blind superstition."

"And what’s *your* interpretation, Miss Helstone, of these words?"

"Hem! I—well, I think Paul wrote it for a specific church, under special circumstances. And I suspect, if I could read Greek, I’d find many words mistranslated or misunderstood. With a little skill, one could twist it to say, 'Let the woman speak whenever she sees fit.' 'It is permitted to a woman to teach and lead, while the man should keep quiet.' And so on."

"That won’t wash, miss."

"I dare say it will. My ideas are dyed in stronger colors than yours, Joe. Mr. Scott, you’re dogmatic—always have been. I prefer William."

"Joe is fine in his own home," Shirley said. "I’ve seen him as quiet as a lamb. He’s one of the kindest husbands in Briarfield. He doesn’t preach to his wife."

"My wife is hard-working, plain. Time and trouble have taken all her vanity. But not you, young misses. You think you know so much—yet it’s only shallow vanities. I remember—perhaps a year ago—Miss Caroline coming into the counting-house while I was packing behind the desk. She didn’t see me, and brought a slate with a sum on it to the master. It was a simple problem—our Harry could’ve solved it in two minutes. She couldn’t. Mr. Moore had to show her. And even then, she didn’t understand."

"Nonsense, Joe!"

"No nonsense. And Miss Shirley—she pretends to listen intently when Mr. Moore talks business, as if she follows every word, as clear as a mirror. But all the while, she’s peeking out the window to see if the mare is calm, checking a stain on her riding skirt, glancing at the dust and cobwebs in our counting-house, thinking how dirty we are and how grand her ride over Nunnely Common will be. She hears nothing—no more than if he spoke Hebrew."
