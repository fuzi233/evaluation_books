Every soul who knows Bideford knows Bideford Bridge; it is the very navel, the center, and the soul around which the town has organized itself. Just as Edinburgh is defined by its castle, Rome by its Capitol, and Egypt by its pyramids, so Bideford is Bideford by virtue of its bridge. Yet few understand the occult powers that have animated this wondrous structure for five centuries, making it, according to Prince and Fuller, the chief wonder of Devon. It is an inspired bridge, a soul-saving bridge, an alms-giving bridge, an educational bridge, a sentient bridge, and lastly, a dinner-giving bridge. Few know how, when construction began half a mile upstream, invisible hands carried the stones downstream each night until Sir Richard Gurney, the parish parson, beheld a vision of an angel bidding him build where the materials had been transported, for there alone lay a sure foundation amid the shifting sands. Few know how Bishop Grandison of Exeter proclaimed indulgences and spiritual blessings to all who promoted the bridging of that dangerous ford, allowing them to "make the best of both worlds."

Few know, nor do I, that though the bridge's foundation rests on wool, it shakes at the slightest step of a horse; or that though it boasts twenty-three arches, one William Alford carried four bushels of salt water on his back across its entire length for a wager. The bridge is a veritable esquire, bearing arms of a ship and bridge on a plain field, owning lands and tenements in many parishes. With these holdings, it has founded charities, built schools, waged lawsuits, and, most importantly for our tale, provided yearly dinners, maintaining the finest cellar of wines in all Devon.

In the year 1583, all the notabilities of Bideford were invited to one such dinner, alongside Mr. St. Leger of Annery, brother of the Marshal of Munster and of Lady Grenville. He was a worthy and hospitable gentleman who, finding riches a snare, parted with them so freely that he prevented his children from falling into temptation. Between Mr. St. Leger and one of the bridge trustees arose an argument regarding whether a salmon caught below the bridge was superior to one caught above. To settle this weighty question by experiment, Mr. St. Leger vowed that, having received a good dinner from the bridge, he would give one in return. He offered a bet of five pounds that he could produce a salmon from the pool below Annery as firm and flaky as the Appledore one they had just consumed. In the fulness of his heart, he invited the entire company to dine with him at Annery three days later, each bringing a wife or daughter, and Don Guzman was included.

Thus, a mighty feast was held in the great hall at Annery, such as had seldom occurred since Judge Hankford feasted Edward the Fourth there. While guests ate their best and drank their worst, Rose Salterne and Don Guzman pretended not to see one another, watching each other all the more closely. Rose, however, had to be exceedingly careful; her father sat at the table, and directly opposite her were Messrs. William Cary and Arthur St. Leger, lieutenants in Her Majesty's Irish army, recently returned on furlough.

Rose and the Spaniard had not exchanged a word in six months, though they had met often. The Spaniard did not avoid her company, save in her father's house; instead, he carefully obeyed her by appearing unconscious of her presence beyond the stateliest salutations at entry and departure. Yet, whenever he was in her presence, he laid himself out to the utmost advantage, becoming more witty, eloquent, romantic, and full of wonderful tales than ever before. The cunning Don had found his first tactic foiled; he now employed a far more formidable one. First, Rose deserved severe punishment for daring to refuse the love of a Spanish nobleman. What greater punishment could he inflict than withdrawing the honor of his attentions and the sunshine of his smiles? There was conceit in that notion, but also cunning. None knew better than the Spaniard that women, like the world, value a man at his own price; the more he demands, the more they will give.

Now he would put a high price on himself and pique her pride, for she was too accustomed to worship to be won by flattering it. He might have paid attention to someone else, but he was too wise to employ such a coarse method, which might raise indignation or despair but would never bring a woman of worth to his feet. So, quietly and unobtrusively, he showed her he could do without her. She, poor fool, began to ask herself why? What hidden treasure or reserve force made him independent of her while she could not say the same of him? Did he possess a secret? How pleasant to know it! Some huge ambition? Some mysterious knowledge? Some capacity of love beyond the common? He must be greater, wiser, richer-hearted, and better-born. Ah, if his wealth would but supply her poverty! Step by step, she was being led to sue in forma pauperis to the very man she had spurned when he sued in like form to her. The temptation of possessing some mysterious private treasure, of being the priestess of a hidden sanctuary, and of thanking Heaven for not being like other women, became too much for Rose, as it is for most. For none knew better than the Spaniard how much more fond women are, by the very law of their sex, of worshipping than of being worshipped, and of obeying than of being obeyed; how their coyness and scorn are often masks hiding their consciousness of weakness, masks of which they themselves will often tire.

Rose was utterly tired of that mask as she sat at the Annery table that day. Don Guzman saw it in her uneasy, downcast looks. Thinking (conceited coxcomb) that she must be sufficiently punished, he stole glances at her, unashamed when he saw her drop her eyes upon meeting his. He noted her silence and abstraction increasing, and a blush stealing into her cheeks. So he pretended to be as downcast and abstracted as she was, continuing his glances until he found her looking at him to see if he was looking at her. Then he knew his prey was safe. He asked her with his eyes, "Do you forgive me?" and saw her stop dead in her talk, falter, drop her eyes, and raise them again after a minute in search of his, so he might repeat the pleasant question. Then what could she do but answer with all her face and every bend of her pretty neck, "And do you forgive me in turn?"

Whereupon Don Guzman broke out jubilant, like a nightingale on a bough, with story, jest, and repartee; he became the soul of the company and the most charming of all cavaliers. Poor Rose knew she was the cause of his sudden change of mood, blamed herself for what she had done, shuddered and blushed at her own delight, and longed for the feast to end so she might hurry home to hide herself alone with sweet fancies about a love whose reality she dared not face.

It was a beautiful sight, the great terrace at Annery that afternoon. Smart dames in gaudy dresses paraded in twos and threes before the stately house or looked down upon the park, with its old oaks, deer, and broad land-locked river spread out like a lake beneath, all bright in the glare of the midsummer sun. They listened obsequiously to the two great ladies who did the honors, Mrs. St. Leger the hostess, and her sister-in-law, fair Lady Grenville. All chatted, laughed, eyed each other's dresses, and gossiped about husbands and servants; only Rose Salterne kept apart, longing to get into a corner to laugh or cry, she knew not which.

"Our pretty Rose seems sad," said Lady Grenville, coming up to her. "Cheer up, child! We want you to come and sing to us."

Rose answered she knew not what and obeyed mechanically.

She took the lute and sat on a bench beneath the house while the rest grouped around her.

"What shall I sing?"

"Let us have your old song, 'Earl Haldan's Daughter.'"

Rose shrank from it. It was a loud, dashing ballad that chimed little with her thoughts; Frank had praised it in happier days long gone by. She thought of him and others, of her pride and carelessness, and the song seemed ominous. Yet, for that very reason, she dared not refuse, fearing suspicion where none existed, so she began per force.

"It was Earl Haldan's daughter, She look'd across the sea; She look'd
across the water, And long and loud laugh'd she; 'The locks of six
princesses Must be my marriage-fee, So hey bonny boat, and ho bonny
boat! Who comes a wooing me?'

"It was Earl Haldan's daughter, She walk'd along the sand; When she was
aware of a knight so fair, Come sailing to the land. His sails were all
of velvet, His mast of beaten gold, And 'hey bonny boat, and ho bonny
boat! Who saileth here so bold?'

"'The locks of five princesses I won beyond the sea; I shore their
golden tresses, To fringe a cloak for thee. One handful yet is wanting,
But one of all the tale; So hey bonny boat, and ho bonny boat! Furl up
thy velvet sail!'

"He leapt into the water, That rover young and bold; He gript Earl
Haldan's daughter, He shore her locks of gold; 'Go weep, go weep, proud
maiden, The tale is full to-day. Now hey bonny boat, and ho bonny boat!
Sail Westward-ho, and away!'"

As she ceased, a measured voice with a foreign accent thrilled through her.

"In the East, they say the nightingale sings to the rose; Devon, more
happy, has nightingale and rose in one."

"We have no nightingales in Devon, Don Guzman," said Lady Grenville; "but our little forest thrushes sing, as you hear, sweetly enough to content any ear. But what brings you away from the gentlemen so early?"

"These letters," said he, "which have just been put into my hand; and as they call me home to Spain, I was loath to lose a moment of that delightful company from which I must part so soon."

"To Spain?" asked half-a-dozen voices; for the Don was a general favorite.

"Yes, and thence to the Indies. My ransom has arrived, and with it the promise of an office. I am to be Governor of La Guayra in Caracas. Congratulate me on my promotion."

A mist was over Rose's eyes. The Spaniard's voice was hard and flippant. Did he care for her, after all? And if he did, was it nevertheless hopeless? How her cheeks glowed! Everybody must see it! Anything to turn away their attention from her, and in that nervous haste which makes people speak foolishly, she asked—

"And where is La Guayra?"

"Half round the world, on the coast of the Spanish Main. The loveliest place on earth, and the loveliest governor's house, in a forest of palms at the foot of a mountain eight thousand feet high: I shall only want a wife there to be in paradise."

"I don't doubt that you may persuade some fair lady of Seville to accompany you thither," said Lady Grenville.

"Thanks, gracious madam: but the truth is, that since I have had the bliss of knowing English ladies, I have begun to think that they are the only ones on earth worth wooing."

"A thousand thanks for the compliment; but I fear none of our free English maidens would like to submit to the guardianship of a duenna. Eh, Rose? how should you like to be kept under lock and key all day by an ugly old woman with a horn on her forehead?"

Poor Rose turned so scarlet that Lady Grenville knew her secret on the spot and would have tried to turn the conversation. But before she could speak, some burgher's wife blundered out a commonplace about the jealousy of Spanish husbands; and another, to make matters better, giggled out something more true than delicate about West Indian masters and fair slaves.

"Ladies," said Don Guzman, reddening, "believe me that these are but the calumnies of ignorance. If we be more jealous than other nations, it is because we love more passionately. If some of us abroad are profligate, it is because they, poor men, have no helpmate, which, like the amethyst, keeps its wearer pure. I could tell you stories, ladies, of the constancy and devotion of Spanish husbands, even in the Indies, as strange as ever romancer invented."

"Can you? Then we challenge you to give us one at least."

"I fear it would be too long, madam."

"The longer the more pleasant, senor. How can we spend an hour better this afternoon, while the gentlemen within are finishing their wine?"

Story-telling, in those old times when books were rarer than now, was a common amusement.
