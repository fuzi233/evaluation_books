I set down the manuscript, a heavy comfort settling over me as I realized my father had glimpsed that same mysterious realm. He had known Mr. Raven. Yet, a sudden, sharp memory pierced the relief: I had never learned why or how my father had died. The truth settled with chilling clarity—he must have followed Mr. Raven and never returned. A wave of shame washed over me, drowning my earlier fear. How could I have fled? What vast, wondrous truths about life and death, about realms beyond ordinary sight, might I have gathered by now? Surely the Ravens were kind souls; a single night in their house would have been harmless, perhaps enlightening. They were strange, yes, but their capacity for understanding was unique, their beauty profound. I had treated them as unworthy of trust, suspecting designs against me! The more I reflected on my cowardice, the more loathed I became. Why fear the dead? To share their holy silence was an honor I had spurned. What harm could that sleeping king, that lady with the wounded palm, possibly inflict? I fell into a longing for the sweet, stately stillness of their faces, and tears streamed down. Sobbing, I collapsed onto a couch and sank instantly into sleep.

As abruptly as I had fallen, I woke, sensing a voice calling me from within. The house stood silent, quiet as an empty church. Outside, a blackbird sang on the lawn. "I will go and tell them I am ashamed," I told myself, "and do whatever they wish!" Rising, I climbed straight to the garret.

The wooden chamber remained unchanged, the mirror dimly reflecting the room. It was nearly noon, the sun slightly higher than when I first arrived. I needed to raise the hood a fraction, adjusting the mirrors! If only I had been quick enough to witness Mr. Raven's adjustment himself!

I pulled the chains, allowing light to spill across the first mirror. Turning to the second, I saw the shapes of my former vision—distinct, yet trembling like a landscape disturbed by a gentle breeze. I reached out, touching the glass; it was solid, impermeable. Suspecting polarization, I shifted the mirrors again and again, altering their angles until, by a stroke of chance, they aligned perfectly. Suddenly, the mountains appeared blue, steady, and crystal clear. I stepped forward, and my feet landed softly among the heather.

I knew only that the path to the cottage led through a pine forest. I threaded through thickets and small fir woods, constantly thinking I recognized the terrain, but no forest materialized. Now the sun hung low, the air growing chilled by the approaching winter. Then, to my delight, I saw a tiny black figure approaching—a raven!

I hurried to meet him.

"I beg your pardon, sir, for my rudeness last night," I said, bowing low. "Will you take me with you now? I heartily confess I do not deserve it."

"Ah!" he replied, looking up. After a brief pause, "My wife does not expect you tonight," he said. "She regrets that we encouraged your stay last week."

"Take me to her so I may tell her how sorry I am," I pleaded humbly.

"It is of no use," he answered gently. "Your night had not yet come then, or you would not have left. It has not come now, and I cannot show you the way. Underneath their daisies, the dead were rejoicing—all lying among the roots of Heaven's flowers—at the thought of your joy when winter passes and the bird-filled mornings return. Before you departed, they shivered in their beds. When the spring of the universe arrives... well, that cannot be for ages, perhaps millennia, and I care not to calculate."

"Tell me one thing, I beg of you, Mr. Raven: is my father with you? Have you seen him since he left?"

"Yes; he sleeps soundly beside you. That was him you saw, his arm on the coverlet, his hand half-closed."

"Why did you not tell me? I could have been so close, and I didn't know!"

"And turn your back on him!" the raven corrected sharply.

"I would have lain down immediately if I had known!"

"I doubt it. Had you been ready to lie down, you would have known him—" Old Sir Up'ard "and your twice-great-grandfather both depart long ago. Your great-grandfather has been with us many years; I think he will soon stir. You saw him last night, though of course you did not recognize him."

"Because he is so much nearer to waking than you. No one who refuses to sleep can ever wake."

"I do not at all understand you!"

"You turned away, and chose not to understand!" I fell silent, fearing further words would drive him off.

"And my grandfather—is he also with you?" I asked.

"No; he remains in the Evil Wood, fighting the dead."

"Where is the Evil Wood, so I may find him?"

"You will not find it, but you will hardly miss it. It is the place where those who refuse to sleep rise at night to kill their dead and bury them."

"I cannot understand you!"

"Naturally not. Nor do I understand you; I cannot read your heart or your face. When my wife and I fail to understand our children, it is simply because there are not enough of them to comprehend. Only God understands foolishness."

"Then," I said, feeling exposed and utterly worthless, "will you kindly show me the nearest way home? I know there are many paths, for I have taken two already."

"There are indeed many ways."

"Please tell me how to recognize the nearest."

"I cannot," the raven answered. "You and I use the same words with entirely different meanings. Often, we cannot tell people what they *need* to know because they *want* to know something else, and thus misunderstand everything we say. Home is always so far away in the palm of your hand, and telling you how to reach it is futile. Yet you will get there; you *must* get there; you *have* to get there. Everyone who is not at home must go home. You thought you were at home when I found you; if that truly were your home, you could not have left. Nobody leaves home. And nobody ever exists or will exist at home without having journeyed there."

"An enigma stepping on another!" I exclaimed. "I did not come here to solve riddles."

"No; but you came, and found the riddles waiting for you! Indeed, you are yourself the only riddle. What you call riddles are truths, appearing puzzling only because you are not yet true."

"Worse and worse!" I cried.

"And you *MUST* answer these riddles!" he continued. "They will keep asking themselves until you understand yourself. The universe is a riddle trying to escape, and you are holding your door shut tight against it."

"Will you not, in pity, tell me what I must do? Where I must go?"

"How could I tell *your* duty, or the path to it?"

"If I am not to go home, at least direct me to some of my kind."

"I know of none. The beings most like you lie in that direction."

He pointed with his beak. All I could see was the setting sun, blinding and white.

"Well," I said bitterly, "I cannot help feeling ill-treated—plucked from my home, abandoned in a strange world, and denied instruction on where to go or what to do!"

"You forget," said the raven, "that when I offered you hospitality and you declined, you reached what you call 'home' safely. Now you have come of your own accord."

He turned and walked slowly away, his beak pointing downward. I stood dazed. It was true I had come of my own free will, but surely not without intent to make amends? My heart ached, hollow and empty, devoid of quest, purpose, hope, or desire. I watched him recede, wishing to follow, yet knowing it was useless.

Suddenly, he lunged at a patch of earth, driving the full weight of his body onto his bill, digging vigorously for moments. Then, flapping his wings, he threw back his head, and something shot from his bill, soaring high into the air. Just then, the sun dipped below the horizon, and the air plunged into deep dusk. Yet the object glowed with a soft, radiant light, pulsing toward me like a giant firefly, yellow and warm. It soared over my head. I turned and followed it.

Here I must interrupt my narrative to note the constant struggle to articulate what cannot be spoken with even approximate precision. The things recorded here, in their nature and the nature of the creatures involved, are so inexpressibly alien to any conceivable event in this world that I can present them only by describing, in the forms and languages of earthly life, the effects they had upon me—not the things themselves, but the feelings they awakened. Even this attempt is made with a continuous, abiding sense of failure. I find it impossible to convey more than a single phase of a multitudinously complex significance, or one concentric sphere of graduated embodiment. A single thing seems to be and mean many things simultaneously, possessing an uncertain identity at their hearts that keeps constantly altering their appearance.
