The talk of Helstone, lightly dropped by Mr. Lennox in the clear daylight of yesterday, worked its way into Mr. Bell’s waking thoughts and, once the candles were out, took full possession of his sleep. In his dream he was young again—not young in the vague way of wishes, but with the particular vigour of a man whose limbs remember how to spring and whose lungs drink air as if it were wine. He was tutor once more in the old college, and the long vacation had come round, and he was staying with his friend newly married—the proud husband, the happy Vicar of Helstone.

It was not the Helstone of maps, measured by miles, but the Helstone of sense, measured by what the heart can bear. They went out over the lanes where the hedge-rows still held last year’s sweetness; there were brooks that talked all at once—water chuckling over pebbles, reed-stems clicking together, a blackbird lifting the whole phrase of the morning into song. Those sounds swam along the buoyant air, and in the dream they were as necessary as breath. They leaped—impossibly—over the babbling water, and for whole, suspended days it seemed they hung in the air, as if gravity had been set aside for friendship and youth. Time and space had no authority; nothing existed except the feeling of being there.

Yet everything looked so exact. The trees were gorgeously heavy with autumn leaf, each bough offering its own tint of russet and gold. Warm odours came up, not as a general “country smell,” but in their separate, homely truths—the bruised mint under a boot-heel, the damp iron-sweetness of turned earth, the faint pepper of crushed fern, the honeyed breath of late flowers kept back by the churchyard wall. Indoors, too, the dream insisted on household particulars. The young wife moved through the rooms with that very mixture Mr. Bell remembered from life—a quick, half-irritated sense of her position as to wealth, and, beneath it, an honest pride in her handsome, devoted husband. She lifted lids, directed servants, smelt at a jug of milk as if her nose were judge and jury, and cut bread with a firmness that had more meaning than many speeches. A joint hissed faintly before the fire; some herb—sage, perhaps—gave out a green, clean bitterness when her fingers tore it. Even the small noises of the house were there: a spoon against a basin, the soft thud of a loaf set down, the stir of skirts in a passage. It was a world made credible by its sounds and scents.

So like life it was, that when he woke, his present life turned unreal, as if the true thing had been snatched away and this were only the thin, hard copy.

Where was he? In the close, handsomely furnished room of a London hotel, where carpets drank up footfalls and the air had that flat, over-warmed smell of city lodging—coal, beeswax, and yesterday’s extinguished lamp. The silence was not the country’s listening hush, full of distant water and birds, but the deadened quiet of thick walls meant to keep out other people’s lives. A carriage rolled somewhere far off with a rasping, iron-edged rumble, and then it was swallowed again. The noise did not belong to any season; it belonged only to commerce.

And those who had been near him an instant ago—those voices, those hands that had touched him with the ease of the familiar—where were they? Dead. Buried. Gone beyond any calling back, as far as earth’s “for evermore” could reach. He was an old man—so lately exultant in the full strength of manhood—and the utter loneliness of it came down on him like a weight on the chest. He got up hastily, as if movement could shake off grief, and dressed in a hurry, trying to forget what would not be forgotten, because it was not a thought but a loss.

By the time he reached Harley Street for breakfast, his face had settled into its ordinary lines; but the dream had left a soreness behind the eyes, and something in him kept listening for the country sounds that were not there. Even at table, the clink of china and the murmur of voices felt sharp and purposeless; there was no brook’s persuasion in them, no leaf’s soft answer.

Mr. Lennox had his papers ready, and his manner was what it always was—regulated, methodical, professional. He went through details with a clearness that would have been admirable if it had been about an estate, or a lease, or any matter in which words could repair what was wrong. But these words were only nets drawn tighter.

Mr. Bell sat and tried to attend. He could not. He saw, instead, Margaret’s eyes as she listened—how they widened at one statement, how her lips lost their colour at another. The lawyer’s facts fell one by one, like stones dropped into a well, and with each fall it seemed that some morsel of ground on which she had been standing gave way and disappeared. This, then that—each little hope extinguished with a relentless minuteness. Mr. Lennox’s voice, so trained to be even, softened almost in spite of itself as he drew nearer to the extinction of the last possibility.

It was not that Margaret had not foreseen the result. She had known what was likely—she had lived with knowledge as one lives with hunger, endured rather than cured. But to have every separate disappointment named, and proved, and placed in order—this was like being made to taste drop by drop the bitterness of something one had already swallowed. At last the composure that had held her gave way, and tears gathered and fell, quietly at first, then with that helplessness that comes when the heart has been pressed too long.

Mr. Lennox stopped reading. “I had better not go on,” he said, and the concern in his voice was real.

“It was a foolish proposal of mine.” He paused, then spoke again with an instinctive gentleness. “Lieutenant Hale—” and even that title, belonging to the service from which Frederick had been so harshly expelled, went through Margaret like a touch meant to soothe.

“Lieutenant Hale is happy now; more secure in fortune and future prospects than he could ever have been in the navy; and has, doubtless, adopted his wife’s country as his own.”

“That is it,” said Margaret. The words came out as if she had been holding them down. “It seems so selfish in me to regret it,” she added, trying to smile through tears that would not be reasoned with; “and yet he is lost to me, and I am so lonely.”

Mr. Lennox turned over his papers. A thought crossed him—quick, private, and unspoken—that he wished himself as rich and prosperous as he believed he would be some day; as if wealth could give him the right to comfort where now he could only offer legal statements and restrained pity. Mr. Bell blew his nose with unnecessary vigour, which was the only way he knew to make a noise in place of speech; otherwise he, too, kept silence.

In a minute or two Margaret had gathered herself again, as people do who have long practice in making their own hearts behave. She thanked Mr. Lennox very courteously for his trouble—more courteously, indeed, because she was aware that her tears might make him believe he had inflicted needless pain. Yet it was pain she would not have spared herself; it was the pain of certainty, and certainty, however cruel, had its own stern mercy.

Mr. Bell rose to go. He came up to her, fumbling with his gloves in that awkwardness which often attends real feeling in an old man who dislikes making a scene.

“Margaret!” said he. “I am going down to Helstone to-morrow, to look at the old place. Would you like to come with me? Or would it give you too much pain? Speak out—don’t be afraid.”

“Oh, Mr. Bell,” she said; and could say no more. But she took his old, gouty hand and kissed it, as if gratitude had no other language.

“Come, come; that’s enough,” he returned, reddening and half-angry at his own emotion. “I suppose your aunt Shaw will trust you with me. We’ll go to-morrow morning, and we shall get there about two o’clock, I fancy. We’ll take a snack, and order dinner at the little inn—the Lennard Arms, it used to be—and go and get an appetite in the forest. Can you stand it, Margaret? It will be a trial, I know, to both of us, but it will be a pleasure to me, at least. And there we’ll dine—it will be but doe-venison, if we can get it at all—and then I’ll take my nap, while you go out and see old friends. I’ll give you back safe and sound, barring railway accidents, and I’ll insure your life for a thousand pounds before starting, which may be some comfort to your relations; but otherwise, I’ll bring you back to Mrs. Shaw by lunch time on Friday. So, if you say yes, I’ll just go upstairs and propose it.”

His speech ran on with that bluff, half-jesting practicality behind which tenderness hid itself. Yet in the midst of it Margaret caught at the words as at a rope thrown to one who is drowning: Helstone. The very name seemed to carry with it not only sights and faces, but the remembered sound of her father’s voice in its own cadence, the dialect-tinge he had never quite lost, the country vowels that London did not speak. A place could hold a language in its hedges and lanes; it could keep safe, for a while, what the town wore away.

“It’s no use my trying to say how much I shall like it,” she said, tears still shining on her lashes.

“Well, then, prove your gratitude by keeping those fountains of yours dry for the next two days. If you don’t, I shall feel queer myself about the lachrymal ducts, and I don’t like that.”

“I won’t cry a drop,” said Margaret, winking hard to shake the tears away and forcing a smile into shape.

“There’s my good girl. Then we’ll go upstairs and settle it all.”

Margaret was in a state of almost trembling eagerness while Mr. Bell discussed his plan with Mrs. Shaw. Her aunt, startled at first, then doubtful and perplexed, shifted from objection to objection—not out of hardness, but from that anxious regard for propriety which, in her mind, stood guard like a butler at every door. Was it right? Was it proper? Would it look well? She could not settle it to her own satisfaction. Mr. Bell, rougher and more decisive, pressed his point with the kind of force that is less argument than momentum, as if to carry scruples along before they had time to fasten themselves.

In the end Mrs. Shaw yielded—more to Mr. Bell’s hearty insistence and Margaret’s quiet, hungry longing than to her own conviction. And even then she did not feel decision enough to approve until the thing was done and safely over in imagination: until she could picture Margaret returning unhurt and composed, and hear herself say, with the comfortable air of one who had foreseen it all, that she was sure it had been a very kind thought of Mr. Bell’s, and just what she herself had been wishing for Margaret—giving her the very change she required after all the anxious time she had had.
