VII. Mrs. Henry van der Luyden sat perfectly still, her pale blue eyes fixed on her cousin Mrs. Archer as she spoke. It was one of those moments when silence carried more weight than words—when the air in the room seemed to hold its breath. The Madison Avenue drawing-room, with its high ceilings and white walls, felt like a place untouched by time. The brocade armchairs had been freshly uncovered for the occasion, and delicate gauze still draped the ornate mantel ornaments and the carved frame of Gainsborough’s “Lady Angelica du Lac.” Opposite the painting hung Mrs. van der Luyden’s own portrait by Huntington—her face serene in black velvet and Venetian lace, a likeness so exact it could have passed for a twin sister to the woman now listening in quiet dignity.

She wore the same black velvet and lace today, though she rarely left her home except to receive guests. Her fair hair, unchanged by age, was parted in soft, overlapping waves across her forehead. Her straight nose, sharp yet gentle, framed eyes that were pale and thoughtful. She looked preserved—like a figure from another century, carefully kept in a world untouched by change. Newland Archer had always admired her grace, but he found her presence strangely distant, almost ethereal. She wasn’t harsh or cold, but her kindness felt reserved, as if held back behind a glass door. Unlike his mother’s stern, no-nonsense aunts, who declared their opinions without hesitation, Mrs. van der Luyden never said yes or no outright. Instead, her lips would curve into a faint, hesitant smile, and she’d murmur: “I shall first have to talk this over with my husband.”

It was a phrase repeated so often it had become ritual. Yet today, something shifted. As Mrs. Archer concluded her account of the incident involving Countess Olenska, Mrs. van der Luyden reached out—not toward her tea cup, nor her fan—but to the bell-rope beside her chair.

“I think,” she said quietly, “I should like Henry to hear what you’ve told me.”

A footman appeared instantly. “If Mr. van der Luyden has finished reading the newspaper,” she added, “please ask him to be kind enough to come.”

She said it with such calm authority that it sounded less like a request and more like a summons. Not because she was arrogant, but because years of tradition had made her husband’s actions sacred in the eyes of the world—and even in her own. He was not just her partner; he was part of a legacy, a pillar of society whose decisions carried weight beyond mere personal opinion.

Mrs. Archer and Newland exchanged glances. This was unusual. But before they could react, the double doors opened, and Mr. Henry van der Luyden stepped through—tall, slender, dressed in a dark frock coat, his hair faded silver, his features mirroring his wife’s in every detail. His eyes, pale grey instead of blue, held the same quiet intensity, the same sense of measured restraint.

He greeted Mrs. Archer with polite warmth, offered Newland low-voiced congratulations—words as carefully chosen as a diplomat’s—then settled into one of the brocade chairs with the ease of a man accustomed to being obeyed.

“I had just finished reading the Times,” he said, folding his long fingers together. “In town, my mornings are so full I find it more convenient to read after luncheon.”

“Ah, there’s much to be said for that,” Mrs. Archer replied. “Uncle Egmont used to say it was less agitating to wait until dinner.”

“Yes,” Mr. van der Luyden agreed slowly. “My father abhorred hurry. But now we live in constant rush.”

His wife interjected gently, “But I hope you *had* finished your reading, Henry?”

“Quite—quite,” he reassured her.

Then she turned to Mrs. Archer. “I should like Adeline to tell you—”

“Oh, it’s really Newland’s story,” his mother smiled, and began again the tale of the affront to Mrs. Lovell Mingott—how the Leffertses had refused to attend her dinner, how the Countess Olenska had been excluded, and how the whole affair threatened to shake the delicate balance of New York society.

“And of course,” Mrs. Archer concluded, “Augusta Welland and Mary Mingott both felt that, especially given Newland’s engagement, you two ought to know.”

Mr. van der Luyden exhaled slowly. A long silence followed. The clock on the mantelpiece ticked with a sound like a drumbeat. Archer watched them—their stillness, their shared understanding. They were not merely a couple; they were an institution. Two figures standing at the threshold between tradition and change, bound by duty and love, yet longing for peace in the quiet of Skuytercliff, where they might play Patience beneath the oaks and tend their gardens in silence.

At last, Mr. van der Luyden spoke. “You really believe this is due to Lawrence Lefferts?” he asked, turning to Newland.

“I’m certain of it, sir,” Archer said. “Larry’s been acting rather rashly lately—having some sort of trouble with the postmaster’s wife in his village. Whenever Gertrude suspects anything, he starts making noise about morality, about people not knowing who belongs where. He’s using Madame Olenska as a lightning rod—just like he’s done before.”

“The *Leffertses*!” Mrs. van der Luyden murmured.

“The *Leffertses*!” echoed Mrs. Archer.

“What would Uncle Egmont have said if Lawrence Lefferts dared pronounce on someone’s social standing? It shows how far Society has fallen.”

“We’ll hope it hasn’t quite come to that,” Mr. van der Luyden said firmly.

“Oh, if only you and Louisa went out more!” Mrs. Archer sighed.

She immediately regretted it. The van der Luydens were deeply sensitive to any suggestion that their seclusion was a flaw. They were the final arbiters of taste, the ultimate court of appeal. Everyone knew it. And yet they lived in near isolation, retreating to Skuytercliff whenever possible, declining invitations under the pretense of Mrs. van der Luyden’s health.

Newland stepped in. “Everyone knows what you and cousin Louisa represent. That’s why Mrs. Mingott felt she couldn’t let this pass without consulting you.”

The two glanced at each other—pale eyes meeting in silent communion.

“It is the principle I dislike,” Mr. van der Luyden said. “As long as a member of a respected family is supported by that family, it should be considered… final.”

“That seems right to me,” Mrs. van der Luyden said softly, as if revealing a truth long hidden.

“I had no idea,” Mr. van der Luyden continued, “that things had come to such a pass.” He paused, then added, “It occurs to me, my dear, that the Countess Olenska is already somewhat related—through Medora Manson’s first husband. At any rate, she will be when Newland marries.”

He turned to the young man. “Have you read this morning’s Times, Newland?”

“Yes, sir,” Archer replied, thinking of the papers he usually devoured with coffee.

The couple exchanged another look—long, deliberate, meaningful. Then a soft smile touched Mrs. van der Luyden’s lips. She understood. She approved.

Mr. van der Luyden turned to Mrs. Archer. “If Louisa’s health allowed her to dine out—I wish you would tell Mrs. Lovell Mingott—that she and I would have been happy to… er… fill the places of the Lawrence Leffertses at her dinner.”

He paused, letting the irony hang in the air. “As you know, this is impossible.”

Mrs. Archer nodded sympathetically.

“But Newland tells me he’s read the Times,” Mr. van der Luyden continued. “So he may have seen that Louisa’s cousin, the Duke of St. Austrey, arrives next week on the Russia. He’s coming to enter his sloop, the Guinevere, in the International Cup Race—and to do a little canvasback shooting at Trevenna.”

He paused again, then spoke with growing warmth. “Before taking him down to Maryland, we’re inviting a few friends to meet him here—just a small dinner, with a reception afterward. I’m sure Louisa will be delighted if Countess Olenska joins us among our guests.”

He rose, bowed stiffly but kindly to his cousin, and added, “I think I have Louisa’s authority to say she will personally deliver the invitation when she drives out shortly—with our cards, of course—with our cards.”

Mrs. Archer rose quickly, murmuring thanks.

Mrs. van der Luyden smiled warmly—like Esther pleading with Ahasuerus. But her husband raised a hand. “There is nothing to thank me for, dear Adeline. Nothing at all. This kind of thing must not happen in New York. It shall not—so long as I can help it.”

And with that, he guided his cousins to the door.

Two hours later, the news spread like wildfire. The great C-spring barouche—always seen gliding through the streets—had been spotted at old Mrs. Mingott’s house. A large square envelope had been delivered. That evening, at the Opera, Mr. Sillerton Jackson announced with satisfaction that it contained an invitation to the Countess Olenska for the van der Luydens’ dinner the following week—honoring the Duke of St. Austrey.

In the club box, a few younger men exchanged smiles. One glanced sideways at Lawrence Lefferts, who sat casually in the front row, twirling his moustache. As the soprano paused mid-aria, he declared with absolute certainty: “No one but Patti ought to attempt the Sonnambula.”
