The room was dim, illuminated only by the harsh, focused glow of a reading lamp whose shade was screwed down tight, casting a shadow over my lady's face. She sat hunched at the table, refusing to lift her gaze. Instead of meeting us with her customary directness, she stared obstinately at an open book, her posture rigid.

"Officer," she said, her voice steady despite the tension in the air, "does the inquiry you are conducting require to know beforehand if anyone currently residing in this house intends to depart?"

"It is of paramount importance, my lady," Sergeant Cuff replied.

"I must inform you," she continued, "that Miss Verinder has decided to travel to stay with her aunt, Mrs. Ablewhite, of Frizinghall. She has arranged to leave us at first light tomorrow morning."

Sergeant Cuff glanced at me. I stepped forward instinctively to speak to my mistress, but a sudden failure of courage seized me. My heart hammered against my ribs with a violence that would have made a man of twenty-five blush. I took a step back, swallowed my words, and remained silent.

"May I inquire," the Sergeant asked, turning his gaze back to my lady, "at what precise moment Miss Verinder informed you of her intention to visit her aunt?"

"Approximately an hour ago," my mistress answered.

Sergeant Cuff looked at me again. They say the hearts of the elderly are calloused, immune to sudden shocks. My own heart, however, was far from hardened; it thumped with a ferocity that would have belied my age if I had been five-and-twenty once more!

"I have no authority," the Sergeant said, "to command Miss Verinder's movements. What I can request is that you delay her departure until later in the day. I must proceed to Frizinghall myself tomorrow morning, and I shall return by two o'clock, or sooner. If Miss Verinder can be kept here until that hour, I wish to speak to her unexpectedly before she leaves."

My lady nodded and immediately turned to me. "Give the coachman my orders," she instructed. "The carriage is not to collect Miss Rachel until two o'clock."

"Have you further instructions?" she asked the Sergeant once the order was given.

"Only one thing, your ladyship," Cuff replied. "Should Miss Verinder be surprised by this change of plans, please do not attribute the delay to me."

My mistress lifted her head abruptly from her book, as if to protest, but checked herself with a visible effort. She cast a final glance at the open page and dismissed us with a sharp wave of her hand.

"That is a remarkable woman," Sergeant Cuff observed as we stepped out into the hall. "Without her self-command, the mystery that baffles you, Mr. Betteredge, would have been solved tonight."

Those words struck a chord deep within my stupid, aging brain. For a fleeting moment, I believe I lost my senses entirely. I grabbed the Sergeant by the collar of his coat and pinned him against the wall.

"Damn you!" I shouted. "Something is terribly wrong with Miss Rachel—and you have been concealing it from me this entire time!"

Sergeant Cuff looked up at me, pressed flat against the plaster, without shifting a muscle of his somber face.

"Ah," he said calmly, "you have finally guessed it."

My hand dropped from his collar, and my head sank onto my chest. Please remember, as an excuse for my outburst, that I had served this family for fifty years. Miss Rachel had climbed onto my knees and tugged at my whiskers countless times when she was a child. To me, despite her faults, she had always been the dearest, prettiest, and most virtuous young mistress an old servant could love. I begged Sergeant Cuff's pardon, though I fear I did so with watery eyes and in a manner far from dignified.

"Do not distress yourself, Mr. Betteredge," the Sergeant said, with a kindness I did not expect from him. "In my line of work, if we were quick to take offense, we would be worthless. If it brings you any comfort, grab me again. You lack the skill to do it properly, but I will overlook your clumsiness out of consideration for your feelings."

He curled the corners of his lips upward, and in his own bleak fashion, seemed to think he had delivered a fine joke.

I led him into my small sitting-room and closed the door.

"Tell me the truth, Sergeant," I demanded. "What do you suspect? It is no kindness to hide it from me now."

"I do not suspect," said Sergeant Cuff. "I know."

My bad temper began to rise again.

"Do you mean to tell me, in plain English," I pressed, "that Miss Rachel has stolen her own diamond?"

"Yes," the Sergeant confirmed. "That is precisely what I mean. Miss Verinder has been in secret possession of the Moonstone from the beginning to the end; and she has drawn Rosanna Spearman into her confidence because she calculated that we would suspect Rosanna of the theft. There is the entire case in a nutshell. Grab me again, Mr. Betteredge. If it vents your feelings, do so."

God help me! My feelings were not to be so easily relieved. "Give me your reasons!" was all I could manage to say.

"You shall hear my reasons tomorrow," the Sergeant replied. "If Miss Verinder refuses to postpone her visit to her aunt—which you will find she does—I shall be obliged to lay the whole case before your mistress tomorrow. Since I do not know the outcome, I request your presence to hear what passes on both sides. Let the matter rest for tonight. No, Mr. Betteredge, you shall not get another word on the subject of the Moonstone from me. Your table is set for supper. That is one of the many human frailties I always treat with tenderness. If you will ring the bell, I shall say grace: 'For what we are about to receive...'"

"I wish you a good appetite, Sergeant," I said. "My own appetite has vanished. I shall wait to see you served, and then I shall ask to be excused if I retire to try to regain my composure alone."

I watched him eat the finest provisions available, and I would not have been sorry if the food had choked him. At that moment, the head gardener, Mr. Begbie, entered with his weekly account. The Sergeant immediately turned his attention to roses and the comparative merits of grass versus gravel paths. I left them to their debate and exited with a heavy heart. This was the first trouble I could recall in many years that tobacco could not blow away, nor could *Robinson Crusoe* solve.

Restless and miserable, with nowhere specific to go, I paced the terrace, thinking in solitude. It mattered little what my thoughts were; I felt wretchedly old, worn out, and unfit for my station. For the first time in my life, I began to wonder when God would see fit to take me. Yet, despite this despair, I held fast to my belief in Miss Rachel. Even if Sergeant Cuff had been Solomon in all his glory and declared my young lady involved in a mean and guilty plot, my answer would have been simple: "You do not know her; I do."

My reverie was broken by Samuel, who brought me a written message from my mistress. Entering the house to fetch a light, Samuel remarked that the weather seemed to be changing. My troubled mind had prevented me from noticing it before, but now, with my attention roused, I heard the dogs pacing uneasily and the wind moaning low. Looking up, I saw the cloud racks darkening and rushing faster over a watery moon. Wild weather was coming—Samuel was right.

The message informed me that the magistrate at Frizinghall had written to remind my lady about the three Indians. Early in the coming week, the rogues were to be released to follow their own devices. If we had further questions for them, there was no time to lose. Having forgotten to mention this during her last conversation with Sergeant Cuff, my mistress now ordered me to supply the omission. The Indians had completely faded from my mind (and likely from yours as well). I saw little use in reopening that subject, yet I obeyed my orders immediately.

I found Sergeant Cuff and the gardener engaged in a heated argument over a bottle of Scotch whisky regarding the cultivation of roses. The Sergeant was so absorbed that he held up his hand to signal me not to interrupt when I entered. As far as I could discern, the debate centered on whether the white moss rose required budding onto the dog-rose to thrive. Mr. Begbie argued "Yes"; Sergeant Cuff argued "No." They appealed to me with the fervor of schoolboys. Knowing nothing of horticulture, I steered a middle course, much like Her Majesty's judges when the scales of justice hang even to a hair. "Gentlemen," I remarked, "there is much to be said on both sides." In the temporary lull created by this impartial sentence, I placed my lady's written message on the table before Sergeant Cuff.

By this time, I had nearly reached the point of hating the Sergeant. Yet, truth compels me to acknowledge that, regarding mental agility, he was a wonderful man.

Within half a minute of reading the message, he had recalled Superintendent Seegrave's report, isolated the section concerning the Indians, and was ready with his response. A certain great traveler, expert in Indian languages, had been mentioned in Mr. Seegrave's report, had he not? Very well. Did I know the gentleman's name and address? Very well again. Would I write them on the back of my lady's note? Much obliged. Sergeant Cuff would look up this gentleman upon his arrival in Frizinghall the following morning.

"Do you expect anything to come of it?" I asked. "Superintendent Seegrave found the Indians as innocent as a newborn babe."

"Superintendent Seegrave has been proven wrong in all his conclusions thus far," the Sergeant answered. "It may be worthwhile to discover tomorrow whether he was also wrong about the Indians." With that, he turned back to Mr. Begbie and resumed the argument exactly where it had left off. "This question between us concerns soils and seasons, patience and diligence, Mr. Gardener. Now, let me put it to you from another angle. You take your white moss rose..."

By then, I had closed the door behind me, shutting out the rest of the dispute.

In the passage, I encountered Penelope hovering near the stairs. I asked what she was waiting for.

She was awaiting her young lady's bell, summoned to assist with packing for the journey the next day. Further inquiry revealed that Miss Rachel had cited the house as unbearable and the presence of a policeman under the same roof as intolerable as her reason for wishing to leave. Upon being informed half an hour prior that her departure was delayed until two in the afternoon, she had erupted into a violent rage. My lady, present at the time, had severely rebuked her and then, appearing to have something reserved for her daughter's private ear, had sent Penelope out of the room. The girl was in wretched spirits about the altered atmosphere. "Nothing goes right, father; nothing is like what it used to be," she lamented. "I feel as if some dreadful misfortune is hanging over us all."

That was my feeling too. But I put on a brave face for my daughter. Just then, Miss Rachel's bell rang. Penelope ran up the back stairs to resume packing. I took the other route to the hall to check the barometer regarding the weather change.

As I approached the swing-door leading from the servants' offices into the hall, it was violently thrown open from the inside. Rosanna Spearman ran past me, her face twisted in a look of misery, one hand pressed hard over her heart as if clutching a sharp pain. "What is the matter, my girl?" I asked, stopping her. "Are you ill?"

"For God's sake, don't speak to me," she answered, twisting herself out of my grasp and running toward the servants' staircase. I called to the cook, who was within hearing, to look after the poor girl. Two others proved to be listening as well. Sergeant Cuff darted softly out of my room and asked what was wrong. I answered, "Nothing." Mr. Franklin, on the other side, pulled open the swing-door, beckoned me into the hall, and inquired if I had seen Rosanna Spearman.

"She has just passed me, sir," I replied, "with a very disturbed face and in a very odd manner."

"I am afraid I am innocently the cause of that disturbance, Betteredge," Mr. Franklin said.

"You, sir!"

"I cannot explain it," Mr. Franklin admitted. "But if the girl is indeed concerned in the loss of the Diamond, I truly believe she was on the point of confessing everything—to me, of all people in the world—not two minutes ago."

Looking toward the swing-door as he spoke, I fancied I saw it open slightly from the inner side. Was someone listening? The door fell shut before I could reach it. Peering through the crack immediately afterward, I thought I saw the tails of Sergeant Cuff's respectable black coat disappearing around the corner of the passage. He knew, as well as I did, that he could expect no further help from me now that I had discerned the true direction of his investigation. Under those circumstances, it was quite in his character to help himself, utilizing the underground routes.

Not entirely sure I had actually seen the Sergeant—and unwilling to stir up needless mischief where there was already plenty—I told Mr. Franklin that I believed one of the dogs had entered the house. Then I begged him to describe what had occurred between Rosanna and himself.

"Were you passing through the hall, sir?" I asked. "Did you meet her accidentally when she spoke to you?"

Mr. Franklin pointed to the billiard table.

"I was knocking the balls about," he said, "trying to distract myself from this miserable business of the Diamond. I happened to look up—and there stood Rosanna Spearman beside me, like a ghost!
