At the close of Queen Anne’s reign, Colonel Henry Esmond—once a loyal officer of her army—found himself entangled in ill-fated schemes to restore the Stuart line to the British throne. The nation, however, had chosen differently, and those who opposed the new Hanoverian rule were compelled to seek refuge abroad. Though Colonel Esmond regretted his part in the affair, the Crown proved merciful, and his friends soon secured his pardon.

Yet England held little charm for him now. The political strife had wearied him, and private sorrows weighed heavy upon his heart. So it was that he turned his gaze westward, to Virginia, where a vast estate—granted to his ancestors by King Charles I—awaited him. There he settled, raising his daughter and later her twin sons beneath the wide Virginian sky. His wife, the widow of his ill-fated kinsman, Viscount Castlewood (slain in a duel at the close of King William’s reign), had died years before, leaving him to the quiet companionship of his books and his thoughts.

He named his new home *Castlewood*, after the family seat in Hampshire, and in doing so, carried more than just a name across the Atlantic. The customs, the loyalties, even the prejudices of the old country took root in Virginian soil. Here, English kings were toasted with fervor, the Church of England held sway, and the gentry prided themselves on their pure bloodlines, looking down upon the Dutch traders of New York or the stern Puritans of New England. It was a land of contradictions—staunchly royalist, yet destined to lead the rebellion against the very Crown it revered.

Life in Virginia unfolded in a manner almost feudal. The great estates were self-sufficient kingdoms, worked by scores of enslaved laborers and indentured servants. The rivers teemed with fish, the forests with game, and the fields yielded tobacco, the lifeblood of the colony’s wealth. Ships laden with the fragrant leaves sailed for London, returning with fine English goods—silks, china, and the latest volumes for Colonel Esmond’s ever-growing library.

Hospitality was boundless. No traveler was turned away from a gentleman’s door, and visits between neighbors were conducted with the ceremoniousness of old-world courts. As for slavery—that question, which would one day tear the nation asunder, was then scarcely a whisper. To own slaves was as natural as owning horses, and the whip, in the eyes of men like Madam Esmond, was as necessary for one as the bridle was for the other.

Colonel Esmond himself was a man of quiet melancholy. Some sorrow, never spoken of, had shadowed his later years. He was kind, even indulgent, to those around him—especially the women of his household, whom he seldom contradicted—but his spirit had long since withdrawn from the world’s tumult. When his daughter assumed control of the estate, he relinquished it without protest. Books and solitude contented him. Company he entertained with dry wit, but their departures did not grieve him.

“My dear,” he once remarked to his daughter, “I shall not be sorry to go myself, and you—though the most devoted of daughters—will find consolation soon enough. At my age, romance is absurd. You, however, may indulge in it, if you choose.”

This was said with a faint smile, for the lady in question—Madam Esmond, as she would later be known—was the least romantic of creatures. Proud of her lineage, she filled her father’s later years with schemes to elevate the family’s standing. When the old wooden house no longer suited her ambitions, she persuaded him to build a grander mansion, fit for the descendants of Norman lords and, as she firmly believed, English royalty.

Her pride in her ancestry was boundless. The Colonel, amused, humored her, even painting two ludicrous heraldic trees tracing the Esmonds back to Charlemagne on one side and Queen Boadicea on the other—the latter depicted in a scandalously “ancient” costume that would have shocked any true antiquarian.

Upon the Colonel’s death, Madam Esmond ruled Castlewood with an iron hand. She brooked no slight to her dignity, quarreling with governors and neighbors alike over precedence. The colonial aristocracy, in her eyes, were upstarts compared to the true-born Esmonds. Her sons, George and Harry, were raised to revere the Crown—little dreaming that one day they would take up arms against it.

The twins, though identical in face, could not have been more different in temper. George, the elder by minutes, was bookish and gentle, while Harry was all fire and mischief—a born soldier, drilling the plantation’s enslaved children with martial fervor. Their grandfather had doted on George, whose quiet humor matched his own, while Harry’s boisterousness won him fewer confidences.

Yet it was George who surprised them all. When his enslaved playmate was sentenced to a whipping, the boy erupted in furious defiance, vowing to free the child when he came of age—a outburst that left his mother aghast and his grandfather quietly thoughtful.

“You let mamma rule you in everything, grandpapa,” George accused tearfully.

The old Colonel only laughed. “So I do. I am an old man, my dear, and I find it easier to sit than to stand.”

Thus life passed at Castlewood—a world of books and hunting, of feudal pride and coming strife. And when Colonel Esmond was laid to rest, it was with a funeral befitting a lord, though he himself would have scoffed at the pomp. The monument, paid for by his English stepson, bore the Esmond arms and a flattering epitaph—one of the few lies in which he had no part.
