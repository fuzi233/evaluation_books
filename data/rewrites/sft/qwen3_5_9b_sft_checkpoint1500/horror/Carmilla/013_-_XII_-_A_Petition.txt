XII. A Petition

"Then we are to lose Madame la Comtesse," I murmured, bowing low, though the air around me felt suddenly thin, as if the shadows had grown teeth. "I pray it is but for a few hours."

"It may be hours," the woman replied, her voice smooth as polished ice, "or it may stretch into weeks. Fate is a fickle mistress; it was unluckiest timing for him to speak to me thus. Do you truly know me?"

"I assure you, I do not."

"You shall know me," she said, a smile playing on lips that seemed too white, "but not yet. We are older, deeper friends than you suspect. I cannot unveil myself today. In three weeks, I shall pass your magnificent *schloss*, about which I have been making inquiries. Then I will look in upon you for an hour or two, to renew a friendship I cherish with a thousand sweet recollections. But now, a thunderbolt of news has struck me. I must depart immediately, traveling by a winding road nearly a hundred miles away, with all the speed of a ghost. My troubles multiply. Only the strict reserve I practice regarding my name holds back a singular request."

She paused, her eyes locking onto mine with an intensity that made the hair on my arms stand erect. "My poor child has not fully recovered her strength. Her horse stumbled beneath her during a hunt she attended; her nerves are still shattered by the shock. Our physician forbids any exertion. Thus, we arrived here by easy stages, barely six leagues a day. Now, however, I must journey day and night on a mission of life and death—a mission whose gravity I shall explain when we meet again, as I hope we shall, in a few weeks, without need for further concealment."

She continued her petition, yet the tone suggested she was bestowing a favor, not asking for one. Yet, in substance, it was far more audacious. She spoke as if granting me the burden of her daughter, rather than requesting it. To take charge of a stranger, especially one so young, was a heavy yoke. And yet, she had stripped away every argument against it, casting herself entirely upon my chivalry.

As if fate itself had intervened to seal my doom, my own niece stepped forward at that precise moment. Beseeching me in a whisper, she urged me to invite this new friend, Millarca, to stay. She had probed the stranger, and believed, if permitted, her mother would approve.

At any other time, I would have bidden her wait. Prudence demanded silence until their true natures were known. But I had no moment to think. The two women assailed me simultaneously. The Countess's grave authority, coupled with the young lady's breathtaking beauty and the electric fire of high birth, overwhelmed me. Overpowered, I yielded too easily, undertaking the care of the girl whom her mother named Millarca.

The Countess signaled to her daughter, who listened with solemn attention as her mother outlined the sudden summons and the arrangements I had made, claiming I was one of her earliest and most valued friends. I offered the polite speeches the situation required, only to realize, with a chill creeping up my spine, that I stood in a position I deeply disliked.

The gentleman in black returned, conducting the Countess from the room with a ceremony that marked her as a woman of immense, perhaps terrifying, power. Her countenance impressed me with the conviction that she was far more important than her modest title suggested.

Her final charge to me was absolute: "Make no attempt to learn more about me than you might already guess, until my return." She pointed to the distinguished host—the man in black—as someone who understood her reasons. "Here," she added, her voice dropping to a hiss, "neither I nor my daughter could safely remain longer than a day. I removed my mask imprudently an hour ago, and I fear you saw me. If I had discovered you witnessed me, I would have thrown myself on your honor to keep my secret for weeks. As it stands, I am satisfied you saw nothing. But if suspicion now lingers, or returns later, I commit myself, and my daughter, entirely to your honor. You will remind her, from time to time, lest she thoughtlessly discloses it."

She whispered urgently to her daughter, kissed her twice with frantic haste, and departed with the pale gentleman in black, vanishing into the throng.

"In the next room," Millarca said, her voice trembling slightly, "there is a window overlooking the hall door. I wish to see my mother leave, and to wave to her."

We complied, guiding her to the glass. Outside, a handsome, old-fashioned carriage waited, flanked by a retinue of couriers and footmen. There, standing in the mist, was the slim figure of the pale gentleman in black, holding a thick velvet cloak. He draped it over the Countess's shoulders, pulling the hood down over her face. She nodded to him, briefly touching his gloved hand. He bowed repeatedly as the doors closed, and the carriage rolled away.

"She is gone," Millarca sighed, turning back to us.

"She is gone," I echoed to myself, the words tasting like ash. For the first time, in those hurried moments following my consent, the sheer folly of my action crashed down upon me.

"She did not look up," Millarca said, her voice plaintive.

"The Countess likely took off her mask and did not care to show her face," I lied, trying to soothe the unease in my gut. "And she could not have known you were at the window."

Millarca sighed, looking at me with wide, innocent eyes. She was so beautiful that I relented instantly. Any regret I harbored for rejecting her warmth vanished, replaced by a desperate desire to atone for the unspoken rudeness of my reception.

Replacing her mask, Millarca joined my ward in persuading me to return to the grounds, where the concert was to resume. We agreed, walking along the terrace beneath the castle windows.

Millarca grew intimate with us quickly, delighting us with lively tales of the great people we encountered. I found her increasingly captivating. Her gossip, devoid of malice, was refreshingly diverting to one so long isolated from the great world. I imagined the vibrant life she would bring to our lonely evenings.

The ball dragged on until the morning sun breached the horizon. The Grand Duke danced with unwavering loyalty, and so, bound by etiquette, none of us dared retire.

Just as we emerged from a crowded salon, Millarca asked where she had seen me. I thought she stood beside me; she fancied she stood beside me. In truth, we had lost her.

Every effort to locate her proved futile. I feared she had mistaken others for her new friends in the confusion of a brief separation, wandering off into the vast, labyrinthine grounds.

"Now," I realized, the horror dawning with crushing force, "I recognize a new folly: undertaking the charge of a young lady without knowing her name. Fettered by my promises, and ignorant of the reasons for them, I cannot even direct my search by identifying her as the daughter of the Countess who departed mere hours ago."

Morning broke, bringing clear daylight, yet I continued my fruitless search. It was not until nearly two o'clock the following day that any news of my missing charge surfaced.

A servant knocked at my niece's door, reporting that a young lady, appearing in great distress, had begged him to locate General Baron Spielsdorf and his daughter, whom she claimed to have been entrusted to by her mother.

There could be no doubt, despite the slight inaccuracy, that our friend had resurfaced. And so she had. Oh, if only we had let her vanish forever!

She recounted a tale to explain her prolonged absence. Late in the evening, driven to despair by failing to find us, she had sought refuge in the housekeeper's bedroom, falling into a deep sleep. That slumber, however long, had hardly sufficed to restore her strength after the exhaustion of the ball.

That day, Millarca returned with us. I was, for all intents and purposes, too happy to secure such a charming companion for my dear girl, unaware that I had just invited a nightmare into our sanctuary.
