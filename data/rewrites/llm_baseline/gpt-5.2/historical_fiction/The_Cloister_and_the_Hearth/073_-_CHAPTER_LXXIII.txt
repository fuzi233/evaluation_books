A Catherine is no simple blessing in a house that is not her own.

She had scarce set foot over Margaret’s threshold before the place began to look as if it had been awaiting her all along: pewter took a cleaner shine under her eye; the hearth seemed to sweep itself for shame; the window-lattices admitted a whiter day. The very sound of the dwelling altered. Where before there had been the soft, homely music of a Dutch room—milk in the pail, a sparrow’s skitter on the sill, the hush of a woman moving carefully for love of her burden—now came the brisk clack and scrape of governance: stool-legs shifted, pots set down with decision, lids lifted and replaced as if each had a proper note to strike in the kitchen’s rough orchestra.

But this hearth-cricket that animated the house was no singer of content; it was a busy-body. And a busy-body, once possessed, sets to rights not only things that want it, but things that ought to be left to their own familiar crookedness. Catherine altered the place of every chair and table; perhaps she bettered the air of the room, perhaps she merely made it hers. Yet she could not rest while the live furniture remained.

When Margaret’s time drew close, Catherine—under a show of motherly prudence—treacherously enlisted Denys and Martin. The poor simple fellows, earnest as dogs that long to be useful, asked what they might do. Catherine told them they could make themselves comparatively serviceable by taking a little walk.

So far, fair.

But then, with her chin lifted and the dish-clout snapping like a banner, she intimated that if their promenade should chance to extend into the middle of next week, why, so much the better.

This did not ingratiate.

After that, the bearing of strong men under a weak woman’s yoke might have softened a she-bear robbed of sleep. Denys and Martin slipped out at daybreak, and stole in like thieves at night; and if chance caught them within doors, they went about like cats on a wall strewn with broken glass—eyes wide, shoulders drawn in, every movement cautious, as if a tongue might lash them from behind the dresser. Yet even this humility did not buy peace. Their very presence jarred on Catherine’s nerves, like an ill-timed loud voice in a room where one listens for a baby’s breath. Perhaps instinct whispered that depopulators have no business where multipliers are at work. A breastplate is no armour against a woman’s words; and Catherine ran infinite pins and needles of speech into them.

In brief, when Margaret came downstairs at last, pale but smiling with that subdued radiance which seems to rise in a woman from the mere fact of having endured and been spared, she found the kitchen swept of heroes.

Martin, old and stiff, had retreated no farther than the street—yet with the honours of war; for he had carried off his baggage, a stool, and sat on it in the open air. The sun lay on his bent shoulders; he looked like a man planted there to dry, and had taken root in the light.

Margaret saw him, and was glad he was warm; but she did not guess he had become a fixture in that luminary. Her first thought went elsewhere.

“Denys?” said she. “Good, kind Denys; he will be right pleased to see me about again.”

Catherine stood wiping a bowl with a vigour now in excess of need, as if the pottery had offended her. Without looking up she answered, “Gone to his friends in Burgundy. And high time. Hasn’t been anigh them this three years, by all accounts.”

Margaret’s face changed as quickly as milk clouds when a hand’s shadow passes over the pail.

“What—gone without bidding me farewell?” she asked; and her eyes lifted, tender and full, like violets in bloom after rain.

Catherine reddened. This turn of the matter pricked her, and for a moment the kitchen clatter seemed to falter, as if even the ladle listened. But she gave her head a little toss, and hardened her voice into practicality.

“Oh, you were asleep at the time. And I would not have you wakened.”

“Poor Denys,” said Margaret; and the dew gathered visibly on the violets.

Catherine saw it out of the corner of her eye, and without openly owning it, slipped away—suddenly—and lavished hospitality and tenderness upon the surviving depopulator, as if kindness might mend what conscience had nicked.

She went to Martin in the street, and made her voice honey.

“Come in, Martin,” said she. “Why, you’ll take cold sitting there like a stone. There’s broth within, good broth, with a bit of onion sweated soft, and parsley chopped fine as green threads; and bread to it, not new so as to stick, but yesterday’s, that drinks kindly. Come, I’ll set it by the fire for you.”

But Martin—old and stiff in more ways than one—shook his head.

“No, thank you, dame. I’ve got used to out o’ doors. And I love not changing and changing. I meddle wi’ nobody here; and nobody meddles wi’ me.”

“Oh, you nasty, cross old wretch!” screamed Catherine; and in a moment treacle turned to the sharpest vinegar. She flounced back into the house, and the door shut with that particular thud that makes every pan and trencher in a kitchen answer it in little echoes.

On calmer reflection she had a little cry. The sound was small—more a catching of breath than any sob—yet in such a room, where one listens always for the slightest stir of a child, even that seemed loud. Then she half reconciled herself to her own conduct by vowing to be so kind that Margaret should never miss her plagues of soldiers.

But the uneasiness remained—a grain of grit under the tongue.

So she dispersed all regrets by a process at once simple and sovereign.

She took and washed the child.

From head to foot she washed him in tepid water; not hot—no, Catherine would have scalded her own hand sooner than a baby’s skin—and not cold to shock his tender bones, but a temperature judged by the back of her wrist, and by long practice with water and flesh. She made a mild lather with good soap, the kind that smells of clean fat and ash, and she turned the little limbs carefully, as if each joint were a delicate hinge in a costly casket. The room filled with that particular odour of a well-kept home: warm water, soap, linen, and the faint sweet steam that rises from a child’s head like blessing made visible.

Heroes, and their wrongs, sank like dust in an ocean of soap and water.

Margaret could not keep still. She hovered round the fortunate performer as if the basin were an altar and Catherine its priest. She must have an apparent hand in the rite, if not a real one. She slipped a finger into the water—to pave the way for her boy, perhaps; for she could not have deceived herself so far as to imagine Catherine would allow her to choose the heat.

During the ablution she kneeled opposite little Gerard and prattled to him with amazing fluency; taking care, however, not to articulate like grown-up people—for how could a cherub be expected to understand their ridiculous pronunciation? The very cadence of her baby-talk was different from her speech to women: it fell in coos and soft clicks, the domestic dialect of love, unrecorded and untranslatable, which no learned lexicon has ever mastered.

Then, as Catherine lifted the tiny hand and washed between the fingers with her thumb, Margaret’s eyes fixed on something.

“I wish you could wash out that,” said she.

“What?” asked Catherine, still intent on her work. “Where?”

“What—have you not noticed? On his little finger.”

Catherine looked, and there indeed was a little brown mole.

“Eh, but this is wonderful!” she cried. “Nature, my lass, you’re strong—and meddlesome to boot. Hast noticed such a mark on some one else? Tell the truth, girl!”

Margaret stared, and for a moment her mouth parted as if the thought were a taste she had not expected.

“What—on him? Nay, mother, not I.”

“Well then he has,” said Catherine, with the triumph of one who has stored a hundred homely observations and brings them forth in season. “And on the very spot. And you never noticed that much. But, dear heart, I forgot; you han’t known him from child to man as I have. I’ve had him hundreds o’ times on my knees, the same as this, and washed him from top to toe in lukewarm water.”

She swelled with conscious superiority, not cruelly, but as women do who have held another’s whole life in their hands by sheer daily labour. Margaret looked up meekly, as at a woman beyond competition.

Catherine, from her dizzy height, did what made her differ from many busy-bodies: she reflected—if not deep, at least real.

“It is strange,” said she, “how things come round and about. Life is but a whirligig. Leastways, we poor women, our lives are all cut upon one pattern. Wasn’t I for washing out my Gerard’s mole in his young days? ‘Oh, fie! here’s a foul blot,’ quoth I; and scrubbed away at it I did till the poor wight cried; so then I thought ’twas time to give over. And now says you to me, ‘Mother,’ says you, ‘do try and wash you out o’ my Gerard’s finger,’ says you. Think on’t!”

“Wash it out?” cried Margaret. “I wouldn’t for all the world. Why, it is the sweetest bit in his little darling body.” Her voice softened into that private dialect again, the one that belongs to cradles and hearths and cannot be carried, whole, into any other tongue. “I’ll kiss it morn and night till he that owned it first comes back to us three. Oh, bless you, my jewel of gold and silver, for being marked like your own daddy, to comfort me.”

And she kissed little Gerard’s little mole.

But she could not stop there. Presently she had him sprawling on her lap, warm and damp and clean, and she kissed his back all over again and again—those round baby-shoulders, the soft spine, the dimples where the skin gathers like dough under a gentle finger. She seemed to worry him as a wolf a lamb; and the child, half laughing and half protesting in that thin piping language of infancy, answered with small squeaks and gurgles that filled the kitchen with a music no minstrel ever sold in a market.

Catherine looked on and smiled. She had seen a good many of these savage onslaughts in her day.

And this little sketch shows the tenor of Margaret’s life for several months. One or two small things occurred in that time which must be told; but I reserve them, since one string will serve for many glass beads.

Meanwhile, while her boy’s father was passing through fearful tempests of the soul, ending in the dead monastic calm, her life might fairly be summed in one great blissful word—Maternity.

You who know what lies in that word, enlarge the sketch: see the young mother nursing and washing, and dressing and undressing; see her stir a little pot with one hand while the other rocks the cradle with a foot; see her taste a broth—salt, a pinch more—then listen, always listen, to the room: to the crackle of turf, the scold of a kettle, the far-off wheel on the street, the blessed quiet between cries. In such quiet, a woman can think. In such quiet, memory has room to live. And in the very syllables she croons—those intimate household sounds—her identity dwells, safe from the heavy foreign words of men and markets.

Then, swifter than lightning, dart your eye into Italy, and see the cold cloister; monks passing like ghosts, eyes down, hands meekly crossed over bosoms dead to earthly feelings. There the sound is different: not the genial clatter of pots and the little noises of a growing child, but measured footsteps, a murmured Latin that belongs to all and therefore to none, doors that shut softly on human speech. One of these cowled ghosts is he, whose return—full of love, and youth, and joy—that radiant young mother awaits.

In the valley of Grindelwald the traveller has on one side the perpendicular Alps, all rock, ice, and everlasting snow, towering above the clouds and piercing to the sky; on the other hand little every-day slopes, green as emeralds and studded with cows and pretty cots and life; whereas those lofty neighbours stand leafless, lifeless, inhuman, sublime. Elsewhere sweet commonplaces of nature pass unnoticed; but fronting the grim Alps they soothe, and gently strike, the mind by contrast with their tremendous opposites.

Such, in their way, are the two halves of this story, rightly looked at: on the Italian side rugged adventure, strong passion, blasphemy, vice, penitence—pure ice, holy snow, soaring direct at heaven; on the Dutch side, all on a humble scale and womanish, but ever green—hearth, basin, bread, and the unrecorded tongue of love spoken over a child.

And as a pathway parts the ice towers of Grindelwald, aspiring to the sky, from its little sunny braes, so here is but a page between the Cloister and the Hearth.
