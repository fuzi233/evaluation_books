After leaving Vienna, and long before you reach Budapest, the Danube slips into a realm of profound solitude—a place where the river forgets its name and becomes something else entirely. Here, it splits into a thousand channels, none dominant, all whispering secrets as they snake through a vast, waterlogged plain. For miles on end, there is no shore, only a sea of willows—low, dense, and endless—bending under the wind like a living tide. On maps, this region is brushed with pale blue, fading toward the edges, labeled in bold, looping script: _Sümpfe_—marshes.

In flood, the waters rise so high that even the highest mounds are nearly submerged. But now, at mid-July, the river is neither full nor low—just restless. The willows sway in silvered waves, their undersides flashing like armor when the sun hits them, their tops trembling as if caught in some unseen breath. They never grow tall enough to be trees; they remain humble, rounded, soft—yet somehow, they move. Not just the branches, but the whole landscape seems to breathe, to pulse. The wind drives across them in gusts, sending ripples over the green field, turning it into a rolling sea of leaves. When the breeze shifts, the color changes—first deep green, then sudden white as the backs of the foliage catch the light. It’s as though the land itself is alive, dreaming.

The Danube, freed from the rigid banks of civilization, roams freely here. It carves new paths daily, tearing away sandbanks, swallowing islands whole, then spitting out others in their place. Whirlpools form without warning, rapids roar like beasts, and the current carries away entire clumps of willow as if they were nothing more than driftwood. Nothing lasts. No island remains unchanged for long. This is not a river—it’s a wild beast, growing, shifting, devouring.

We reached this part of the journey in our Canadian canoe, loaded with a gipsy tent, a frying pan, and dreams of the Black Sea. We had left Vienna at dawn, slipping past its sleeping streets while the sky bled crimson. By noon, we’d passed Fischeramend, Hainburg, Petronell—the old Roman city of Carnuntum—and finally, under the shadow of Thelsen, crossed into Hungary. The March joined us quietly from the left, and the frontier vanished behind us.

Twelve kilometers an hour carried us swiftly into Hungarian territory. The muddy water, thick with silt, signaled rising floodwaters. We scraped over shingle beds, spun helplessly in whirlpools, and were tossed like corks until Pressburg—Pozsóny—rose against the horizon. Then, with a surge, the canoe leapt forward, darted beneath the grey stone walls of the city, slipped past the sunken chain of the Fliegende Brücke ferry, turned sharply left, and plunged into the chaos beyond—the swamp, the islands, the endless willows.

And then, abruptly, the world changed.

It was like stepping from a film of cities into one of forests and lakes—without warning, without transition. Within minutes, there was no sign of human life. No boats, no huts, no smoke, no roofs. Just water, willows, and wind. A silence so complete it felt like being swallowed by nature itself. The isolation pressed down on us, heavy and real. We laughed, half-serious, that we must have needed a special passport to enter this hidden kingdom—one ruled not by men, but by water and wind and ancient spirits.

By late afternoon, the wind had grown fierce, beating us relentlessly. We searched for a place to camp. The islands shifted and blurred in the current, pulling us in and pushing us out. Willows snagged our hands as we tried to steady the canoe. Finally, a gust drove us sideways into a narrow backwater. With a spray of foam, we grounded the bow on yellow sand. Panting, we collapsed onto the beach—sheltered, hot, and drenched in sunlight. Above, the sky was a flawless blue. Around us, the willows danced, clapping their leaves like tiny hands, as if applauding our arrival.

“What a river,” I said, staring at the water.

“Won’t stand much nonsense now, will it?” my companion replied, dragging the canoe farther up the bank. He stretched out, already half-asleep.

I lay beside him, content. The elements surrounded us—wind, water, sun, sand—like a bath of pure sensation. I thought of the journey: from the Black Forest, where the Danube began as a timid stream, to this moment, where it roared like a god. And I thought of my friend—the Swede—quiet, practical, unimpressed by ghosts or legends. Yet even he could not deny the river’s power.

From the beginning, the Danube had seemed alive. Not merely flowing, but thinking. At night, it sang to the moon—a low, sibilant hum, like stones grinding beneath its surface. We knew its voices: the gurgle of whirlpools, the thunder of rapids, the constant rush beneath everything. When rain fell, it shouted. When the wind blew upstream, it laughed. It chattered when hills rose beside it, spoke with dignity through towns, whispered when the sun warmed its curves. It was never still. It played tricks—disappearing into the earth near Donaueschingen, reappearing on the other side of limestone hills, starting anew under another name. It refused to acknowledge tributaries, running alongside them for miles, ignoring their presence. Only after Passau did it yield—when the Inn arrived with such force that it couldn’t be ignored. Then the river fought, the canyon shook, and our canoe rode the waves like a child in a storm.

But now, after Pressburg, the Danube had changed. It grew serious. It was no longer a boy playing games. It was becoming something older, deeper. It split into three arms, each wide and powerful, converging only far downstream. There was no sign which path to take.

“I warn you,” said the Hungarian officer in Pressburg, “if you take a side channel, you may wake up forty miles from anywhere, stranded, with no food, no people. No fishermen. No help. The river is still rising. The wind will worsen.”

We didn’t fear the flood. But the idea of being trapped, high and dry, terrified us. So we stocked extra provisions. And indeed, the wind increased—steady, cold, relentless—until it became a gale.

We made camp earlier than planned. The sun was still hours from setting. I left my companion asleep and wandered the island.

It was small—less than an acre—a mere sandbar jutting into the river. The apex pointed toward the sunset, where the wind hurled spray like a curtain of fire. I stood there, watching the crimson flood roar down upon us, crashing against the bank as if trying to tear it apart. The water swirled past in two foaming streams, one on each side. The ground trembled. The willows bent and twisted, creating the illusion that the island itself was moving—drifting, breathing, alive.

Above, the river descended like a sliding hill, white with foam, leaping up in every direction. From the lower end, it looked dark, angry. Only the crests of the waves showed, streaked with foam, pushed forward by the wind. For a short stretch, it wound between islands, then vanished into the willows—swallowed whole, as if the trees were monstrous creatures gathering around a sacred spring.

It was a scene of eerie beauty. And yet, as I watched, something stirred within me—not fear, not exactly, but a quiet unease. A sense that we were not welcome here.

The river was powerful, yes—but that wasn’t it. The wind was wild, but I shared its joy. The flood was terrifying, but I admired its strength. No—this feeling came from the willows.

They were everywhere. Thick, endless, pressing close. They crowded the river, as if trying to smother it. Their ranks stretched mile after mile, silent, watchful, waiting. And in their number, in their stillness, in their endless motion, I sensed something unnatural. Something alien.

Mountains awe. Oceans terrify. Forests enchant. But these willows… they did not inspire wonder. They inspired dread. A slow, creeping dread. As if they were not plants, but beings—sentient, ancient, guarding something forbidden.

I tried to shake it off. I walked the island, collecting driftwood. The Swede climbed the bank, searching for fuel. “By Jove!” he called suddenly. I ran to see what he had found.

He was looking across the river, pointing.

“Good heavens—it’s a man’s body!” he cried.

A black shape rolled in the foam, flipping over and over. Twenty feet out, it lurched and turned—eyes reflecting the sunset, glowing yellow in the dimming light. Then it plunged beneath the surface.

“An otter!” we both said at once, laughing.

It was an otter—alive, hunting. But for a moment, it had looked like a drowned man. A real man. A dead man.

We turned back, arms full of wood. And then—another thing.

A boat. A small, flat-bottomed craft. And a man standing in it, steering with a long oar. He was on the opposite shore, moving fast, heading downstream. The slanted light distorted his shape. The water shimmered, refracting the sun. I couldn’t focus. But I saw him—upright, facing us, gesticulating wildly.

His voice came across the water—shouting, furious—but the wind tore the words apart. I couldn’t hear a single syllable.

Then I saw it—he was crossing himself. Making the sign of the Cross.

“He’s crossing himself!” I shouted.

The Swede shaded his eyes. “You’re right. He’s scared.”

The man disappeared quickly, melting into the willows where the sun struck them red and gold. Mist rose from the water. The air grew thick.

“What in the world is he doing here at dusk?” I asked, half to myself. “What did he mean by those signs? Was he warning us?”

“He saw our smoke,” the Swede said, laughing. “Thought we were spirits. These Hungarians believe in all sorts of nonsense. Remember the shopkeeper at Pressburg? She said no one ever lands here because it belongs to beings outside human law. Fairies, elementals—maybe demons. That peasant saw people on an island for the first time in his life. It scared him.”

His tone was light, but I noticed something—his usual calm was gone. His voice sounded strained. His laughter didn’t reach his eyes.

“If they had imagination,” I said, louder than necessary, trying to fill the silence, “they might populate this place with gods. The Romans built shrines here. Sacred groves. Elemental deities.”

I smiled. But inside, the unease remained. The willows watched. The river breathed. The wind whispered. And somewhere, deep in the marsh, something waited.

II. I woke abruptly, my body stiff against the coarse sand of the mattress, eyes fixed through the flaps of the tent as if drawn by some unseen force. The moon hung high and bright, casting silver across the canvas, and when I glanced at my watch—pinned there like a relic—I saw it was past midnight. Two hours had passed since I’d drifted into sleep. The Swede lay still beside me, breathing deep and even, oblivious to the storm that raged beyond our fragile shelter. The wind screamed through the trees, a constant wail that scraped at the edges of my mind. Something tugged at my chest—not fear exactly, but a cold knot of unease, as though the air itself had turned thick with warning.

I sat up slowly, heart thudding. The world outside seemed restless. The willows bent and twisted under the gale, their branches clawing at the sky, yet our little green camp remained untouched, cradled in the hollow like a secret. Still, the disquiet wouldn’t leave. I slipped out of the tent on hands and knees, careful not to disturb my companion. My skin prickled with anticipation—something strange was stirring.

Halfway out, I froze.

Across the clearing, the tops of the bushes were alive with motion. Not just the rustling leaves, but shapes—shadows woven into the foliage, shifting and coalescing beneath the moon’s pale gaze. I pulled back, hunkered down, staring. It couldn’t be real. Yet there they were: forms rising from the earth, taller than men, darker than night, moving with a rhythm that had nothing to do with wind or branch. They were bronze-hued, fluid, almost liquid in their movement, emerging from the roots of the willows and climbing upward in a continuous stream.

They weren’t human. Not by any measure I knew. Their limbs flowed into one another, merging and separating like smoke, forming a column that spiraled toward the heavens. Faces? I never saw them. Only the suggestion of features—deep-set eyes, broad brows—flickering in and out of existence. And yet, they moved with purpose, with grace, as if ascending to some ancient ritual no man had ever witnessed.

I wanted to shout. To wake the Swede. But something held me back—the thought that if he saw them too, I might lose what little grip I had on sanity. So I stayed, rooted, breath shallow, eyes wide. The longer I watched, the more certain I became: these were not illusions. They were real—though not of this world. They rose silently, unceasingly, each figure melting into the next, forming a living serpent of flesh and shadow that climbed into the dark sky and vanished without trace.

For a time, I felt no fear. Only awe. A profound, humbling reverence. It was as though the land itself had awakened, its hidden spirits stirred by our presence. This place—this wild, forgotten stretch of riverbank—was sacred, ancient, and we were trespassers. My mind flooded with myths: gods of the forest, guardians of the deep, beings who walked between worlds. These were not ghosts. They were power made visible.

Then, the wind struck—a sudden, brutal gust that slammed into my side, nearly throwing me off balance. It snapped me back. Reality reasserted itself, sharp and cold. *It must be a hallucination*, I told myself. *The moonlight, the fatigue, the isolation—it’s all playing tricks*. The brain, after all, could conjure such things. I began to move forward, testing the ground, seeking proof. My feet touched warm sand. The river roared nearby, real and raw. The wind tore at my hair. All senses confirmed I was awake.

Yet the figures remained—silent, majestic, undisturbed by my doubt.

And then, as suddenly as they appeared, they were gone.

The sky cleared. The bushes stood still. The silence returned—deeper now, heavier.

But the moment the vision vanished, fear came. Cold. Crushing. I turned, scanning the darkness, heart pounding. Where had they gone? Were they still watching? I saw no escape—no path, no shelter, no weapon. Helpless, I crawled back into the tent, pulling the flap shut behind me. I buried my face in the blankets, muffling the sound of the wind, shutting out the moonlit willows. But the image lingered—those towering, bronze forms rising into the stars—and with it, a terrible understanding:

This place was not empty.
It was *alive*.
And it remembered us.

III. As though to drive home the certainty that I had not been dreaming, I remember it took me a long time to drift back into sleep—sleep that was restless, fragmented, and never truly deep. Even then, only the surface of my being rested; beneath, something remained awake, alert, watchful. Then, abruptly, I jerked upright—not from wind or water, but from a slow, insidious pressure that had crept in while I slept. The sleeping part of me shrank away until it vanished entirely, leaving me sitting bolt upright, heart hammering, ears strained.

Outside, there was a sound—countless tiny taps, like rain on leaves, or dry sand skittering across canvas. It had been coming for some time, I realized now, first faintly audible through the haze of sleep. But now, fully awake, I felt the weight of it pressing down upon me. My breath came shallow and labored. A cold sweat broke out across my skin despite the heat of the night. Something was against the tent—pushing, pressing, not just from the sides but from above. Was it the wind? Rain? Spray from the river? Or something else?

I thought quickly—of broken branches, of the poplar tree swaying in the gale, its boughs threatening to snap and crush us. That must be it—the wind had torn a limb loose, still caught in the higher branches, trembling with every gust. Its leaves would brush the tent, tapping, pattering, like a thousand small feet. I grabbed the flap and burst outside, shouting for the Swede to follow.

But when I stood up, the air was clear. No branch hung over the tent. No rain fell. No spray clung to my clothes. Only the wind howled, and a pale, grey light seeped through the trees, touching the sand with a ghostly glow. Stars still crowded overhead, but the east was streaked with red. Hours had passed since I’d last stood here, watching those figures rise into the sky. Now the memory returned—not as a dream, but as something worse: a truth too terrible to face.

How tired I felt—the exhaustion of a night without rest, yet my nerves were electric, buzzing with dread. Sleep was impossible. The river had risen again, roaring through the darkness, sending fine mist across my skin. Yet nothing threatened. No sign of danger. Only the lingering sense of unease, deep in my chest, unexplained, unrelenting.

The Swede hadn’t stirred when I called. He remained still, wrapped in his blankets. I didn’t wake him. Instead, I scanned the camp: the overturned canoe, the yellow paddles—one, two—still lying beside it; the provision sack and extra lantern hanging from a low branch. And everywhere, the willows—endless, swaying, whispering in the wind. A bird sang its morning song. A flight of ducks cut through the twilight with a rush of wings. Sand stung my bare feet as I walked around the tent, then ventured deeper into the bush.

From there, I could see across the river—the distant shore, the endless sea of willow thickets stretching to the horizon, blurred and unreal in the dawn’s weak light. Again, that same nameless dread washed over me, cold and heavy. I wandered slowly, still trying to understand the sound—the infinite pattering, the pressure. It *had* to be the wind, I told myself. The wind blowing hot sand against the taut canvas. The wind pressing down on our fragile shelter. But even as I said it, I knew it wasn’t enough. My unease grew, deeper, heavier.

I crossed to the opposite bank, where the river had reshaped the shore overnight, carving away great mounds of sand. I dipped my hands and feet into the cool current, splashed water on my forehead. The sky was glowing now, the air crisp with the promise of day. On my way back, I passed beneath the very bushes where I had seen the figures rise—tall, shadowy, ascending into the air like smoke.

And then—suddenly—a wave of terror seized me. From the shadows, a large shape moved swiftly past. Someone walked by. Not an illusion. Not a trick of the wind. *Someone* had passed me. I staggered forward, thrown off balance by a sudden gust. Once I reached open ground, the fear lessened—but only slightly.

“The winds are walking,” I whispered, remembering how they sometimes moved like living things under the trees. But this was different. This fear was vast, ancient, unlike anything I’d ever known. It filled me with awe as much as dread, a strange mix of horror and wonder. And then, standing on a high point at the center of the island, I saw the river bathed in crimson sunrise—its beauty so overwhelming that a wild longing surged within me, almost making me cry out.

But the cry died in my throat.

Because as I turned from the river to look at the camp, I saw it—the horrifying truth.

Something had changed.

It wasn’t the angle of view. It wasn’t the light. It was the willows. They were closer. Much closer. Crowding the tent, pressing in from all sides. Unnaturally close. They had moved. During the night, they had crept nearer—silent, slow, deliberate—over the shifting sands, inch by inch.

Had the wind done this? Or had they moved on their own?

I recalled the pattering—the pressure—the weight on my chest. The feeling that something was watching. Was it the wind? Or was it them?

I swayed in the breeze, struggling to stay upright. For a moment, I felt like a tree myself—rooted, trembling, aware of something immense and hostile drawing near.

Then came the reaction—laughter. I wanted to laugh. The idea was absurd. Trees couldn’t move. They couldn’t think. They couldn’t *attack*. But the laughter wouldn’t come. Because if I could believe such things, then the real danger wasn’t physical—it was mental. It was in the mind. And if they could invade the mind… then they were already inside.

The sun rose fast, burning away the grey. I stood there longer than I meant to, afraid to descend into the thicket. When I finally moved, I did so quietly, cautiously. I circled the tent again, measuring distances—carefully pacing the space between the willows and the canvas, noting the shortest gap. I crawled into my blankets, silent.

The Swede slept on, undisturbed. I was grateful for that. If he had seen nothing, if he had no memory of the figures, the sounds, the pressure—if he simply believed it was all my imagination—then perhaps I could believe it too. With daylight, I could tell myself it was a nightmare, a fever dream, a hallucination born of fatigue and fear.

Nothing more disturbed me after that. I fell asleep almost immediately—exhausted, broken, yet still listening, still waiting, still terrified of hearing that sound again: the endless pattering, the creeping pressure, the whisper of willows closing in.

The sun hung high and unrelenting in the sky when my companion roused me from a deep, uneasy sleep, announcing that the porridge was ready and there was just time to bathe. The rich, sizzling scent of bacon drifted through the tent flap, sharp and comforting in the morning air.

“River’s still rising,” he said, voice low but steady. “Several islands out in midstream have vanished entirely. Ours is shrinking fast.”

“Any wood left?” I asked, rubbing my eyes.

“The wood and the island will finish at the same time—tomorrow,” he replied with a dry laugh. “But we’ve enough for now.”

I plunged into the water from the island’s tip, which had already changed shape during the night, and was swept downstream in an instant to the landing spot opposite the tent. The current was fierce, the water icy, and the banks raced past like scenery seen from a speeding train. Bathing under such conditions was both terrifying and exhilarating—a cleansing ritual, as if the terror of the previous night were evaporating from my mind. The sun beat down mercilessly; not a cloud marred the blue dome above. Yet the wind remained relentless, unyielding, as if nature itself refused to let us forget its power.

Then, suddenly, the full weight of his words struck me: *“Enough to last till tomorrow.”* He no longer intended to leave immediately. He’d changed his mind. The certainty of departure the night before had vanished. Why? What had shifted?

At breakfast, great chunks of the riverbank collapsed into the flood with thunderous splashes, sending sprays of water toward our frying pan. My companion talked endlessly about how the Vienna-Pesth steamers must struggle to find their way through such a swollen channel. But I found myself more preoccupied by the change in him. Something had altered since the evening before. His manner was different—tense, hesitant, almost fearful. A new wariness threaded through his movements, a subtle shift in tone, as though he were listening to something beyond sound, watching something unseen. I couldn’t name it then, but I felt it plainly: he was afraid.

He ate little, didn’t smoke his pipe—unheard of. The map lay open beside him, and he studied it repeatedly, tracing lines with a finger that trembled slightly.

“We’d better get off sharp in an hour,” I said, trying to draw him out gently, hoping to coax some truth from him.

His reply unsettled me: “Rather. If they’ll let us.”

“Who’ll let us? The elements?” I asked, forcing lightness into my voice.

“The powers of this place,” he answered, eyes fixed on the map. “The gods are here, if they’re anywhere at all.”

“The elements are the only true immortals,” I laughed, though my laughter sounded brittle even to myself. When he looked up, grave and unblinking, he said across the smoke:

“We’ll be lucky if we leave without further disaster.”

That was precisely what I feared. I braced myself for the direct question—it felt like stepping into a dentist’s chair, knowing the pain was inevitable. I had to ask.

“Further disaster? What’s happened?”

“The steering paddle’s gone,” he said quietly.

“The steering paddle?” I repeated, heart leaping. That was our rudder. Without it, navigating the Danube in flood was suicide. “But—”

“And there’s a tear in the bottom of the canoe,” he added, voice trembling faintly.

I stared, unable to speak. In the heat, on the scorched sand, a chill crept over me. I rose and followed him, who walked ahead with solemn silence, leading toward the tent on the other side of the fire. The canoe lay where I’d last seen it—upside down, ribs exposed. One paddle rested beside it. Only one.

“There’s only one,” he said, bending to pick it up. “And here’s the rent in the base-board.”

I opened my mouth to say I’d clearly seen two paddles earlier—but stopped. It would have been useless. I approached instead, drawn by dread.

A long, clean tear ran through the canoe’s hull, a narrow strip of wood neatly removed as if bitten away by a sharp tooth. The hole pierced through. Had we launched her without noticing it, we would have sunk quickly—two inches above water, helpless against the current. At first, the wet wood might swell and seal it, but once out in the stream, water would pour in relentlessly.

“There,” he murmured, half to himself, “an attempt to prepare a victim for sacrifice. Two victims, perhaps.” He leaned over, running his fingers along the slit.

I began to whistle—an unconscious habit when flustered—and deliberately ignored his words. I refused to believe them.

“It wasn’t there last night,” he said, straightening up, avoiding my gaze.

“We must’ve scraped it when we landed,” I said, stopping the whistling. “The stones are sharp.”

I froze. He turned and met my eyes. We both knew the lie. There were no stones.

“And then there’s this,” he said, handing me the paddle and pointing to the blade.

A cold dread spread through me as I took it. The blade had been meticulously scraped thin—so thin it would snap with the first stroke. Like someone had spent hours sanding it down, weakening it beyond use.

“One of us walked in our sleep,” I said weakly, “or maybe the wind blew sand against it for days.”

“Ah,” he said, turning away with a hollow laugh. “You can explain everything.”

“The same wind that carried the paddle near the bank and dropped it into the water,” I called after him, desperate to maintain logic.

“I see,” he shouted back, glancing at me before vanishing into the willows.

Alone again with these signs—evidence of deliberate action—I thought: *One of us did this. Not me.* Then came the second thought: neither of us could have done it. My companion—my trusted friend, seasoned traveler—could not have acted so knowingly. And the idea that he’d gone mad, that he was acting out some insane purpose, seemed absurd. Yet the truth remained: something had changed in him. He was nervous, watchful, afraid of things he wouldn’t name. He was waiting—for what? For a climax he expected soon.

I searched the tent and surroundings. The measurements of the night were unchanged. But now I noticed deep hollows in the sand—basin-shaped craters, some the size of tea cups, others like bowls. The wind, no doubt, had carved them. It had also lifted the paddle and tossed it toward the water. The tear in the canoe remained inexplicable—but maybe we’d snagged it on landing. Still, the shore showed no rocks. I clung to that theory, clinging to reason like a drowning man to driftwood. An explanation was necessary—not because it was true, but because without one, sanity unraveled.

I melted pitch and began patching the canoe. Soon, the Swede joined me, working with quiet focus. I pointed to the hollows.

“Yes,” he said. “They’re everywhere. But you can explain them, no doubt?”

“Wind,” I said firmly. “Like those little whirlwinds in cities—sand shifts easily.”

He said nothing. We worked in silence. I watched him covertly. He seemed to be listening—turning his head, staring into the bushes, scanning the sky, the river. Once, he held his hand to his ear for minutes. He said nothing. I asked nothing. And as he mended the canoe with the precision of a native craftsman, I was glad he didn’t mention the willows. Because if he had seen *that*, I could no longer deny it. The trees weren’t just trees anymore. They were something else.

After a long pause, he spoke.

“Queer thing,” he said abruptly, as if compelled to confess. “About that otter last night.”

I looked up sharply. I’d expected something else entirely.

“Shows how lonely it is,” I said. “Otters are shy.”

“I don’t mean that,” he cut in. “Do you think it really was an otter?”

“What else? In God’s name, what else?”

“You know,” he said slowly, “I saw it before you. At first, it seemed… much bigger than an otter.”

“The sunset magnified it,” I replied.

He stared blankly, as if thinking of something far away.

“It had yellow eyes,” he whispered.

“The sun,” I laughed, too loudly. “Next you’ll wonder if the man in the boat—”

I stopped. He was listening again, head turned toward the wind. His face—fearful, expectant—made me halt.

Five minutes later, he looked across the canoe, pitch smoking in his hand, expression grim.

“I *did* wonder,” he said slowly, “what that thing in the boat was. I thought then it wasn’t a man. It rose suddenly out of the water.”

I laughed again—boisterously, defiantly—but this time with irritation beneath.

“Look here,” I snapped. “This place is strange enough without inventing things! That boat was ordinary. The man was ordinary. They were going downstream. And that otter *was* an otter. Stop playing the fool!”

He looked at me steadily. No anger. Just sorrow. I found courage in his silence.

“And for Heaven’s sake,” I continued, “don’t pretend you hear things. It only scares me. There’s nothing but the river and this cursed wind.”

“You *fool!*” he said, voice low, broken. “You utter fool. That’s exactly how all victims talk. As if you don’t understand what I do!” He sneered, yet with resignation. “The best thing you can do is stay silent. Hold your mind firm. This weak pretense only makes the truth harder when it finally hits.”

My defiance collapsed. I knew he was right. I was the fool. He had seen it all along. He’d sensed the danger from the beginning. I had been blind, while he stood alert, aware. And I missed the meaning of his words: *There must be a victim. And we are it.*

From that moment, I stopped pretending. And from that moment, my fear grew—steadily, inexorably—toward its final peak.

“But you’re right about one thing,” he added, before the subject passed. “We’re wiser not to talk about it—or even think about it. Because what you *think* becomes words. And what you *say*… happens.”

That afternoon, while the canoe dried, we fished, tested the leak, gathered wood, and watched the flood rise. Driftwood surged past, and we tried to grab it with long willow branches. The island shrank visibly, banks collapsing with thunderous gulps. The weather stayed clear until four o’clock, then clouds gathered in the southwest, creeping slowly across the sky.

The easing of the wind brought relief. The constant roar had grated on our nerves. But when the wind died at five, the silence was worse. The river filled the air with a deep, monotonous murmur—three dull notes, like a funeral dirge. Unlike the wind, which sang in many tones, the river’s song was flat, oppressive, mournful. It sounded like doom.

Without sunlight, the landscape lost its cheer. The shadows deepened. The willows, already menacing, stood closer together. They moved oddly—rustling when no wind stirred, shaking from roots upward. Common things, when charged with horror, become more terrifying than anything strange. These bushes, huddled around us, took on grotesque forms—living, watching things. Their ordinariness hid their malice. The forces of this place were drawing near. Focusing on us. On our island.

I had slept in the early afternoon and regained some strength—but that only made me more sensitive to the haunting. I laughed at my fears, told myself they were physical, irrational. But they grew stronger. I dreaded the night like a child in a forest.

The canoe was covered with a waterproof sheet. The single paddle was tied securely to a tree. From five onward, I prepared dinner—my turn. We had potatoes, onions, bacon fat, leftover stew residue, black bread. The result was excellent. Then plum stew with sugar, strong tea with dried milk. Wood was stacked nearby. No wind meant easy work.

My companion sat idly, cleaning his pipe, offering advice—an off-duty privilege. He’d been quiet all afternoon, busy with repairs and gathering wood while I slept. No talk of dark things. Only remarks about the island’s shrinking size.

The pot began to bubble when I heard him call from the bank.

“Come and listen,” he said. “See what you make of it.” He cupped his ear, as he had done before.

“Now do you hear it?” he asked, watching me.

We stood together, listening. At first, only the river’s deep hum and the hissing surface. The willows were still. Then—a sound. Faint, rhythmic. Like a distant gong, suspended high in the sky, struck again and again. Muffled, musical, metallic. It came from the swamps across the river, shifting direction—sometimes overhead, sometimes under the water. Once, I thought it came from within me, as if echoing from another dimension.

“I’ve heard it all day,” he said. “While you slept, it circled the island. I hunted it, but never found it. Sometimes it was inside—like a sound from the fourth dimension.”

I listened hard, searching for a familiar echo. Nothing. It changed distance, growing near, then fading into void. Not ominous—yet it stirred unease. A feeling I couldn’t name.

“The wind in the sand-funnels,” I said, forcing an explanation. “Or the bushes rubbing.”

“It comes from the whole swamp,” he replied. “From everywhere. It comes from the willows.”

“But the wind has dropped,” I objected. “How can the willows make noise?”

His answer terrified me—because I’d feared it, and because I knew it was true.

“It’s *because* the wind dropped we hear it now. It was drowned before. It’s the cry, I believe, of the—”

I broke away, rushing back to the fire—the stew threatened to burn. I needed escape. I didn’t want to talk. I feared he’d bring up the gods, the forces, the sacrifice. I needed control. One more night. Who knew what it would bring?

“Come cut bread for the pot!” I called, stirring vigorously. That stew-pot was our anchor. Our sanity.

He came slowly, took the sack from the tree, fumbled inside, then emptied its contents onto the ground-sheet.

“Hurry!” I shouted. “It’s boiling!”

He burst into laughter—sharp, sudden, forced. Not joy. Hollow.

“There’s nothing here!” he roared. “No bread. They’ve taken it!”

I dropped the spoon and ran. Everything lay on the sheet—except the loaf.

The weight of fear crushed me. Then I laughed too. It was the only thing to do. The sound echoed between us—unnatural, released pressure. A safety valve. Then silence.

“How criminally stupid of me!” I cried, clinging to logic. “I forgot to buy bread in Pressburg. That woman distracted me. I must’ve left it on the counter—”

“The oatmeal’s less than this morning,” he interrupted.

Why bring that up? I thought angrily.

“There’s enough for tomorrow,” I said, stirring fiercely. “We’ll get more in Komorn or Gran. In twenty-four hours, we’ll be miles away.”

“I hope so—to God,” he muttered, putting things back in the sack. “Unless we’re claimed first… as victims for the sacrifice,” he added, laughing foolishly. He dragged the sack into the tent—“for safety,” I supposed. I heard him mumbling, indistinctly. I chose not to listen.

V. I had resolved to stay awake all night, to keep watch, to guard against whatever might come creeping from the dark—yet my body, worn thin by fear and fatigue, betrayed me. Sleep crept in like a thief, stealing over me with the quiet embrace of oblivion. That my companion also succumbed only hastened its arrival. At first he was restless, sitting up every few minutes, his voice hushed but urgent: “Did you hear that?” or “Something’s moving outside.” He tossed on his cork mattress, muttering about the tent shifting, the river rising, the island sinking beneath us. Each time, I rose to check—peering into the blackness, listening intently—and returned with reassurance: nothing stirred. Eventually, his movements slowed. His breathing deepened, then settled into a steady rhythm. Then came the snoring—low, rhythmic, almost soothing. It was the first time in my life I’d found such a sound comforting. That, I remember, was the last thought before I slipped into unconsciousness.

I woke abruptly, gasping. The blanket was pulled over my face, but something heavier pressed down upon me—not just fabric. For a moment, I thought my friend had rolled onto my bed in his sleep. I called out, sat up, and instantly felt it—the tent was _surrounded_. Outside, the soft, countless pattering returned, filling the night with an eerie, living dread. I shouted again, louder this time. No answer. And then I noticed: the snoring had stopped. The flap of the tent hung open. A cold certainty seized me—the Swede was gone.

I crawled out into the darkness, fumbling for the flap, pulling it shut with trembling hands. As I did, I realized with a jolt that he wasn’t inside. Not there. Not anywhere. Panic surged through me. I burst from the tent, stumbling into a wave of sound—a thick, humming drone that seemed to rise from the ground, the air, the sky itself. It was the same hum I’d heard before, but now it was wild, uncontrolled, as if a swarm of unseen insects filled the atmosphere, pressing in from every direction. My breath caught. The air felt thick, heavy, resisting my lungs. But my friend was out there. In danger. I could not hesitate.

Dawn was breaking—just a pale strip of light along the horizon, faint and ghostly. The sky was still, no wind stirring. I could barely make out the shapes of the willows, the river, the sandbars. I ran wildly across the island, calling his name, screaming anything that came to mind. But the trees swallowed my voice. The hum muffled everything. My shouts died within a few feet. I crashed through the underbrush, tripping, falling, scraping my face on thorns, tearing through branches like a madman.

Then, suddenly, I broke free at the island’s edge—and saw him.

A dark silhouette stood between the water and the sky. One foot already dipped into the river. He was about to jump.

I lunged forward, grabbing him around the waist, dragging him back with all my strength. He fought like a beast, thrashing, shouting words that twisted into the same cursed hum, ranting about “going _inside_ to Them,” “taking the way of the water and the wind”—phrases that made no sense, yet chilled me to the bone. I held on, refusing to let go, until finally, exhausted, he collapsed.

I dragged him into the tent, threw him onto the mattress, and held him down until the frenzy passed. Then, as suddenly as it began, it ended. The humming ceased. The pattering stopped. The silence was absolute.

He opened his eyes, turned his head toward me, and said—softly, like a child who’d just been saved—“My life, old man—it’s my life I owe you. But it’s all over now. They’ve found a victim in our place.”

And then he fell back, sank into sleep, snoring deeply, peacefully—as though nothing had happened. As though he hadn’t tried to drown himself moments before.

When the sun rose three hours later, I watched him wake naturally, without fear or memory. He rose, lit the fire, prepared breakfast. I followed him to the riverbank, anxious, watching closely. He dipped his head into the water, muttered something about how cold it was. “River’s falling at last,” he said. “Good.”

“The humming has stopped too,” I said.

He looked at me calmly, as though nothing unusual had occurred. “Everything has stopped,” he replied. “Because—” He paused, then smiled faintly. “Because they’ve found another victim.”

I forced a laugh. “Exactly,” I said. “That’s what I thought.”

He nodded. “I feel safe now. Truly.”

Then he turned and walked toward the bank, poking among the sandy hollows with a stick. I followed. He moved slowly, deliberately. Suddenly, he stopped. “Ah!” he exclaimed. The tone sent a shiver through me—familiar, terrible.

He pointed. Half in the water, half on the sand, lay a large dark shape. Twisted roots held it fast. A body. A man. Drowned. Recently. The current had carried it here—just as the fit had passed.

“We must give it a decent burial,” I said.

“I suppose so,” I replied, though I shivered. There was something about the corpse—its stillness, its emptiness—that made my blood run cold.

The Swede glanced at me sharply, his expression unreadable, then climbed down the bank. I followed more slowly. The river had stripped much of the clothing from the body. The neck and chest were bare.

Halfway down, he froze, raising a hand to stop me. But I stumbled, losing balance. We collided—me into him, him into the body.

At that instant, a sound erupted from the corpse—loud, sharp, pulsing. A chorus of humming, rising from the very skin, spreading outward like wings beating through the air. It swirled above us, rising into the sky, growing fainter, vanishing into the distance. It was as if we’d disturbed some invisible creatures, alive and hidden beneath the flesh.

We clutched each other, stunned. Then, the current shifted. The body turned. Slowly, it rotated, revealing the face—pale, dead, staring upward.

The Swede dropped to his knees, covering his eyes.

I rushed to his side.

And I saw.

The skin of the man’s chest and face—deeply indented with small, perfect hollows. Shaped exactly like the sand-funnels we’d found scattered across the island. Beautiful. Unnatural.

“Their mark,” the Swede whispered, voice broken. “Their awful mark.”

And then, the river took it.

The body turned once more, spun into the current, and was swept away—over and over, like an otter in the water—until it vanished into the distance, beyond sight, beyond reach.
