**Chapter 34: A Case of Domestic Persecution—A Remarkable Instance of Pious Perseverance in the Discharge of Religious Duties**

Having tasted the thrill of excitement and the intoxicating weight of power, Martin found himself loath to surrender either. Miss Helstone—the girl he had always dismissed as plain, whose face now haunted his waking hours and dreams alike—had once entered his orbit. The possibility that their paths might never cross again gnawed at him. Though he had been merely a schoolboy, he possessed an unusual spark; destiny seemed poised to forge him into something original. For a brief period, he had attempted to sand down his edges to fit the mold of conventional society, but the attempt had failed. An indelible, unique stamp remained upon him.

Currently, Martin sat idly at his desk in the grammar school, brainstorming ways to add another chapter to the romance already begun in his mind. He was unaware that countless life stories are condemned to remain unfinished after the opening pages. That Saturday half-holiday, he retreated to the woods with his book of fairy tales and the unwritten manuscript of his own imagination. Yet, Martin harbored a distinct, almost religious dread of Sunday approaching.

His parents, while officially distancing themselves from the established church, ensured that every Sunday their entire, blooming family filled the large pew at Briarfield Church. Theoretically, Mr. Yorke treated all denominations as equals. Mrs. Yorke, however, reserved special praise for the Moravians and Quakers, admiring the humility she perceived in them. Despite this theoretical openness, neither parent ever stepped foot in a conventicle. Martin disliked Sunday primarily because the morning service dragged on interminably and the sermons rarely appealed to his tastes.

However, this particular Saturday afternoon revealed a new allure in the impending day. A deep snowstorm had descended, so profound that Mrs. Yorke declared at breakfast that the children would be safer indoors. Instead of attending church, she proposed they sit silently in the back parlor for two hours while Rose and Martin took turns reading John Wesley's *Sermons*. Wesley, a figure of reform and agitation, held favor with both parents.

"Rose may do as she pleases," Martin remarked, not lifting his gaze from the book he habitually studied over his bread and milk.

"Rose will do exactly as she is told, and so will Martin," his mother countered firmly. "I am going to church."

Her son replied with the serene, unyielding quietude characteristic of a true Yorke—a man who knows his will and possesses the means to enforce it. He knew that if cornered, he might endure crushing pressure, but he would never capitulate unless absolutely forced.

"It is unfit weather," his father interjected.

There was no response. The youth continued to read intently, breaking his bread and sipping his milk methodically.

"Martin hates going to church, but he hates obedience even more," Mrs. Yorke observed.

"I suppose I am driven by pure perversity?"

"Yes, you are."

"Mother, *I* am not," Martin corrected sharply.

"Then what influences you?"

"A complex web of motives, the intricacies of which I would explain to you only if I were prepared to dismantle my own body to show its inner workings."

"Hear Martin! Hear him!" cried Mr. Yorke enthusiastically. "I must see this lad raised to the Bar. Nature intended him to live by his tongue. Hester, your third son must become a lawyer; he possesses the essential inventory—brass, self-confidence, and words. Words, words, words."

"Some bread, Rose, please," Martin requested, speaking with intense gravity, serenity, and phlegm.

The boy possessed a naturally low, plaintive voice; in his stubborn moods, it rose barely above a lady's whisper. The more rigid his obstinacy, the softer and sadder his tone became. He rang the bell and gently asked for his walking shoes.

"But, Martin," his father urged, "there is drift everywhere; a man could hardly wade through it. However, lad," he continued, noting that the boy rose as the church bell began to toll, "this is a situation where I would by no means deny the obdurate fellow his will. Go to church by all means. There is a pitiless wind, sharp frozen sleet, and deep snow underfoot. Go out into it, since you prefer it to a warm fireside."

Martin calmly donned his cloak, comforter, and cap, and deliberately walked out the door.

"My father has more sense than my mother," he pronounced aloud. "Women simply miss it! They drive nails into the flesh, believing they are hammering against insensate stone."

He arrived at the church early. He reasoned that if the weather frightened her—or if Mrs. Pryor objected to her leaving—he would be vexed. Yet, regardless of the tempest, hail, or ice, she *ought* to come. If she possessed a soul worthy of her striking eyes and features, she *would* come. She would arrive hoping to glimpse him, just as he was there hoping to glimpse her. She would seek a word regarding her confounded sweetheart, just as he sought another flavor of existence itself—not evaporated, but preserved in spirit. Adventure was to stagnation what champagne was to flat porter.

He looked around. The church was cold, silent, and nearly empty, save for one elderly woman. As the chimes faded and the single bell tolled slowly, more frail parishioners trickled in, taking humble stations in the free seating area. On such a wild morning, not one affluent family attended; not a single carriage party appeared. All the lined and cushioned pews remained vacant; only on the bare oak benches sat the gray-haired elders and feeble paupers.

"I'll scorn her if she doesn't come," Martin muttered to himself, shortly and savagely.

The rector's shovel hat passed the porch. Mr. Helstone and his clerk were in the vestry. The bells ceased, the reading desk was filled, the doors closed, and the service commenced. Empty stood the rectory pew; she was not there. Martin scorned her.

"Worthless thing! Vapid thing! Commonplace humbug! Like all other girls—weakly, selfish, shallow!" Thus ran his liturgy.

"She does not resemble our portrait. Her eyes lack size and expression; her nose is not straight, delicate, or Hellenic; her mouth lacks the charm I once believed it held, the charm I imagined could beguile me from my sullen moods. What is she? A scrap of paper, a doll, a toy, a *girl*, in short."

So absorbed was the young cynic that he forgot to rise from his knees at the appropriate moment, remaining in an exemplary posture of devotion until the litany ended and the first hymn was sung. Being caught off guard did nothing to soothe him. He sprang up, flushed with embarrassment, for he was as sensitive to ridicule as any girl. Matters worsened when the church door reopened and the aisles began to fill with the rhythmic *patter, patter, patter* of a hundred small feet. These were the Sunday scholars. According to Briarfield winter custom, the children had previously been kept near a warm stove, entering the church only moments before communion and sermon.

The little ones were settled first. Finally, when the boys and younger girls were arranged, and the organ swelled high as the choir and congregation rose to sing a spiritual anthem, a tall class of young women entered quietly, bringing up the rear. Their teacher, having guided them to their seats, moved toward the rectory pew. The French-gray cloak and small beaver bonnet were instantly recognizable to Martin; it was the exact costume his eyes had ache to spot. Miss Helstone had not allowed the storm to impede her journey. After all, she had come to church.

Perhaps Martin whispered his satisfaction into his hymn book; at any rate, he hid his face for two minutes. Whether satisfied or not, he had ample time to become angry with her again before the sermon concluded. She had never once looked his way; he had not been fortunate enough to catch a single glance.

"If," he thought, "she pays me no attention, if she demonstrates I am not in her thoughts, I shall form a worse, a meaner opinion of her than ever. It would be most despicable to come solely for the sake of those sheep-faced Sunday scholars, and not for my sake or that long-suffering skeleton Moore's."

The sermon ended, the benediction was pronounced, and the congregation dispersed. She had not approached him. Now, as Martin turned his face homeward, he felt the sleet biting sharp and the east wind chilling. His shortest route lay across several open fields—a dangerous path because it was untrodden. He did not care; he chose it.

Near the second stile rose a cluster of trees. Was an umbrella waiting there? Yes, an umbrella held with evident difficulty against the gale; behind it fluttered a French-gray cloak. Martin grinned as he toiled up the steep, encumbered field, which felt as difficult to traverse as a slope in the upper realms of Mount Etna.

An inimitable look settled on his face when, having gained the stile, he seated himself coolly upon it, thereby opening a conversation which, for his part, he was willing to prolong indefinitely.

"I think you had better strike a bargain. Exchange me for Mrs. Pryor."
