CHAPTER LXIII. Which accounts perhaps for Chapter LXI. (Don’t pretend you remembered what that one was about, we barely did.)

Major Pendennis, that seasoned professional meddler who’d built a side career of digging through gentry families’ dirty laundry for the benefit of his nephew Pen, had just finished compiling his Clavering family dossier and had to pause mid-sip of his Madeira to wheeze. The idea of foisting Miss Amory on Pen as a wife, he realised, came with two very alarming bonus fathers-in-law no guardian in their right mind would wish on a ward. The pair were such walking disasters they almost cancelled each other out, sure — but one of them was the fugitive convict Altamont, who’d hacked down a prison guard mid-transit and would be hoisted straight up the gallows the second he set foot within a British magistrate’s line of sight. Even if he stayed hidden, he could wave that secret over Sir Francis Clavering’s head forever, making the hapless Baronet his personal doormat for pocket change for the rest of time.

Worse, the Begum (Lady Clavering, the poor rich sucker who’d bailed her husband out of debt a dozen times already) was on track to burn through every last penny of her fortune on this irredeemable gambling addict. By the time the dust settled, Miss Amory wouldn’t bring Pen a fancy annual income and a safe seat in Parliament — she’d bring him just herself, and a family tree with a giant red scrawled note next to her last male relative’s name that read *sus. per coll.* (hang him, basically).

Scheming old Pendennis was already turning over a third, better plan in his head, and you’ll see how it all shook out if you stick through the very awkward conversation he ended up having with Sir Francis Clavering, Member for Clavering, a man whose greatest life achievement was avoiding all of his responsibilities.

It’s a universal truth: when a guy is drowning in debt, he vanishes from all his usual fancy haunts faster than a rat off a sinking ship. I’ve known a Pall Mall dandy who vanished from every club and park promenade, only to turn up pink-faced and chowing down on whitebait at an 18-penny ordinary in Billingsgate, covered in fish grease. I knew a wit so deep in debt he once sent me an address for a pub called the Fox Under The Hill, tucked down a dark, smelly Strand archway that looked like it was a front for pickpockets. These men might technically have a house, but they’re never in it; they might have an address for letters, but only a total fool would turn up there expecting to see them. Only their very most trusted cronies know their hiding spots.

Which is exactly what happened to Sir Francis Clavering after he’d had a screaming match with his wife and burned through the last of her goodwill. His butler Lightfoot complained to his mate Morgan that Clavering hadn’t been home before 3am in weeks, pretended to be fast asleep when he brought the morning water jug up, and snuck out the back door the second Lightfoot’s back was turned. He was also dodging his old mate Chevalier Strong, who kept yelling at him about unpaid bills — he was basically running from his own conscience, like the rest of us who try out a new coffee shop just to avoid texting our landlord back.

So the Major, who was determined to corner Clavering for his own little scheme, had to chase him for days like a guy hunting a feral cat that stole his sandwich. First he sent Clavering a very innocent-sounding dinner invite to Greenwich, to “meet a few friends” — the friends were just him, but Clavering got suspicious, bailed last minute, and left the Major to eat an entire platter of whitebait alone while seagulls stared at him through the window. Next he wrote asking for ten minutes of Clavering’s time, arranged a meeting *precisely* 4 o’clock the next day at Bays’s club (the Baronet underlined the “precisely” three times, like that would make it less obvious he was going to ghost) — but 4 o’clock rolled around, and no Clavering was anywhere to be found. The man avoided Pendennis like he owed him 200 quid, not zero.

The very same day Strong had publicly blessed Clavering’s face then cursed him behind his back, Clavering had already broken the sworn, Bible-backed oath he’d made to his wife’s advisors to never sign another bill. He’d scribbled his fancy baronet signature on a bit of stamped paper, handed it to his mate Mr. Moss Abrams, and told him to date it two months prior, so he could pretend he’d forgotten all about it when he did his last debt settlement with the Begum. Strong heard all the tea over a cigar at Santiago’s tobacco shop, the back parlor where every bill discounter and minor aristocrat hung out to swap gossip.

“He’s at it again,” Santiago told him, wiping ash off his counter. “Moss dragged him in here ten minutes ago, sent my boy out for a stamp. That’s a 50 pound bill, mark my words. He’ll be drinking the profits in a gambling den before sunset.” A man who signs six-month “promise to pay” slips for pocket change should know the whole of London’s shadiest characters know his signature by heart, at this point.

Santiago’s shop was two steps from St James’s Street, just down the road from the Major’s Bury Street lodgings. The Major was strolling daintily home when he saw Strong striding towards him, mustachios flapping, reeking of cigar smoke. “Ugh, these young cads and their smoke,” the Major thought. “Every fellow with a mustache and a cigar is a low-class type — oh, it’s Mr. Strong. Hope you’re well?” He gave him a stuffy dignified bow, and was about to fumble his key in the lock to escape, when Strong stopped him and held out a hand. The Major shook it with exactly two very ungracious, cold fingers.

“Still in town, Mr. Strong? Hope you’re well,” the Major said, in the tone of a king addressing a street sweeper, since he’d spent 60 years in fancy company and assumed speaking to a non-noble was doing them a favour.

“My news is bad, sir,” Strong grumbled. “It’s about the Tunbridge Wells crew. Clavering’s at his old tricks again.”

“Good heavens, do come in!” the Major said, suddenly very interested, and dragged him up to his drawing room. Strong ranted for ten full minutes about Clavering’s total lack of loyalty. “That man swore on his knees to his wife, rang the bell for a Bible, cursed his own soul to hell if he ever signed another bill — and he’s at it again! There’s even a bill with both our names on it due next week, sir. He begged me not to mention it, I let him, and half the money was mine, for heaven’s sake! I don’t care if they chase me for it, I’m used to it — but it’s Lady Clavering that makes me mad. That sweet woman bailed him out of debtor’s prison a dozen times, and he’s throwing it all away on bill discounters, bare-knuckle boxers, any random rascal that’ll slip him a fiver. Would you believe he’s even been taking money from Altamont? You know who I mean.”

The Major’s face went perfectly blank. “Altamont? That tipsy fellow who turned up to Clavering’s dinner once? Who even is that, Mr. Strong?”

Strong stared at him, so surprised he almost dropped his cigar. “If you don’t know, I sure don’t.”

“To be frank,” the Major said, purring, “I have my suspicions — mind, just suspicions — that our friend Clavering is exactly as loose a fish as any man in London, and he’s definitely got a few skeletons in his closet he doesn’t want anyone digging up. This Altamont fellow almost certainly knows one of those skeletons, and he’s milking Clavering for every penny over it. Half the best families in England are paying hush money like that, these days. Their private business isn’t mine. I’m only involved for Lady Clavering and her daughter’s sake, they’re charming creatures. But say, is this Altamont in town, then?”

Strong stood up, grabbed his hat, completely offended by the Major’s slippery, patronizing act. “I don’t think I’m obliged to tell you where he is, Major Pendennis,” he huffed.

The Major’s whole demeanour flipped instantly, from haughty to chuckling, matey know-it-all. “Ah, Captain Strong, you’re being clever too, and right you are! Walls have ears, my friend, no use firing off your mouth about secrets that don’t do anyone any good. I’m the same — I won’t know a single thing I don’t have good reason to know, and that’s what any sensible man does. And we’re totally on the same page that Clavering needs to be reined in, before he ruins his whole family.”

“Of course,” Strong said, drily, still not buying the act.

“Wonderful! Delighted we agree. So glad I ran into you today. Good evening, Morgan, show Captain Strong out!”

Strong left more confused than he’d arrived, and the Major’s valet Morgan was equally perplexed — he’d been lingering by the door for the whole second half of the conversation, eavesdropping like the professional he was, and half of what he’d heard made zero sense.

The next time he ran into Lightfoot at the valets’ secret gossip club, Morgan asked, “Who’s this Altamont bloke? You know what he and Strong are up to?”

“Strong does Clavering’s odd jobs, signs his bills for him, the usual kite-flying racket that needs three people to work,” Lightfoot shrugged. “Altamont won a pile of money betting on the Derby. I just wish Clavering could get a big windfall so he pays me the 14 quid he owes me.”

“You think the Begum will pay his debts again?” Morgan said. “Find that out for me, and I’ll slip you a nice bonus, mate.”

Major Pendennis joked all the time that Morgan was way richer than he was, and he wasn’t wrong. The quiet, sneaky valet had spent years hoarding cash, schmoozing with other fancy families’ valets to learn their masters’ secrets. When Pen inherited his fortune, Morgan had even sidled up to him and offered to lend him 50 or 100 quid if he was short before his rents came in, like a tiny, very polite loan shark. Pen thought it was the funniest joke he’d ever heard, the idea of Morgan the valet being a capitalist, and turned him down laughing before telling his uncle the story.

The Major laughed so hard he had to sit down. “That rascal’s been getting rich for decades. He owns the house I’m renting right now, for God’s sake! He bought up all of Clavering’s old bills after he found out the Begum was bailing him out, and made a killing. Sly as a fox, that Morgan.”

The day after Strong’s visit, the Major cornered Morgan to ask him about the Clavering bills. “I hear you’re investing money these days, Morgan,” he said.

“Blast that Mr. Arthur for blabbing,” Morgan thought. Out loud he said, “Thank you, sir, no complaints about my post.”

“Good man, glad you’re doing well. Don’t go and sink your savings into a public house, though, that’s a fool’s game.”
*Public house?* Morgan thought. *Me? This old fool! I’ll be sitting in Parliament before I die, mark my words.* “No, sir, no plans for that. My savings are all put out safely, sir.”
“You do a little bit of bill discounting, eh?”
“Very little, sir — if you’ll pardon my asking, sir, do you think Sir Francis Clavering’s paper is still any good? Will Lady Clavering pay them off anymore, sir?”
“You’ve already been buying his bills, have you?”
“A little bit, sir,” Morgan said, dropping his eyes all demure. “Got access to the family news through your good self, sir, thought I might as well make a profit as anyone else. I’d be ever so grateful if you could tell me if the Begum will pay out again.”

The Major was so shocked he almost dropped his teacup — he felt like he’d just found out his cat was secretly a millionaire. But he respected success, even when it was under his own nose, and suddenly found himself a little bit intimidated by his valet. “Well, Morgan, I shan’t ask how much you’ve made. If Lady Clavering asks me what to do, I’ll tell her not to pay another penny — though I fear she’s too soft, and will anyway. I heard there’s a new bill out with that Mr. Moss Abrams, for 50 quid. Can you find out the details, and tell me where I can corner Clavering?”

“Of course, sir, right away,” Morgan said, bowing and slipping out of the room like the quiet shadow he was.

The next morning Morgan came back with the intel: the bill was for 50 pounds, and Clavering would be camped in the back parlour of the Wheel of Fortune Tavern at 1 o’clock sharp that day.

The Major showed up right on time, pushed open the door of the back parlour, and found Sir Francis slouched in a Windsor chair, surrounded by overflowing spittoons, prints of bare-knuckle boxers and trotting racehorses, and a beer-stained copy of Bell’s Life in London that reeked of old tobacco. He looked up, saw the Major, and groaned.

“So it’s you, old boy?” he said, disappointed, because he’d been expecting Moss Abrams to show up with his cash.

“Good day, Sir Francis,” the Major said, smiling like a shark. “I wanted a word, so I followed you here.”

Clavering’s face immediately turned sulky. “What’s your problem, Pendennis? Why are you hounding me all over London, sticking your nose in my business? I never borrowed a penny from you! I never did you any harm! If my wife has got a problem with me, she can send her lawyers. I don’t want you meddling, and I won’t have it.”
