In the month of May, Durrance turned his gaze from Wadi Halfa and began to look longingly toward home. But far to the south—five hundred miles beyond his frontier, across the vast Nubian desert and the treacherous Belly of Stones—events were unfolding that would shape his fate, though he knew nothing of them.

On a desolate stretch of track between Berber and Suakin lay the wells of Obak, buried deep in shifting sands. To the east, a narrow strip of trees marked the edge of the dunes, beyond which rose a barren plain of granite hills. To the west, the desert stretched for fifty-eight waterless miles to Mahobey and Berber on the Nile—a flat, unbroken expanse where even a tuft of grass seemed a distant tree, and a pile of stones loomed like a mountain. In this May, no place could have been more forsaken.

The sun blazed from dawn until dusk with a heat that seared the soul. At night, icy winds swept across the land, sculpting the sand into towering pyramids only to level them again, carving valleys and burying slopes. The landscape changed constantly, as if the earth itself breathed and dreamed. Vultures circled overhead, and sand-grouse skittered through the silence. Scattered among the mounds were bleached camel bones—remnants of caravans long gone—and the crumbling frame of a hut built from bent branches, once home to wandering Arabs who had herded goats and lived off the land.

Now, only the wind and the sky remained. Silence pressed down like a shroud, making the place feel alive with unseen purpose.

Yet one man lingered here, hidden and alone. Each morning at sunrise, he led two swift Bisharin camels from the shelter of the trees to the well. He watered them, then sat quietly by the mouth of the well for three hours, gazing toward the dunes and Berber. Then he returned the camels to their shade, fed them carefully with dhoura laid out on cloth, and vanished for the rest of the day. For five mornings, no one came near him.

But on the sixth, as he was about to return to his hiding place, he saw figures rise suddenly against the horizon—a man and a donkey silhouetted atop a sand crest.

The Arab by the well glanced first at the animal. Its grey coat caught his eye, and he half rose. But then he looked at the rider. Though the man’s jellab covered his face, his bare legs stood out starkly against the sand—black as ebony. A negro.

The Arab sat back, unmoved, waiting. He did not flinch when the man stepped close, boots crunching over the loose grains.

"Salam aleikum," the negro said, placing a long spear, a short one, and a hide shield upon the ground before sitting beside him.

"Aleikum es salam," the Arab replied, offering no more than a nod.

"It is Abou Fatma?" the negro asked.

The Arab nodded.

"Two days ago," the man continued, "a Bisharin named Moussa Fedil stopped me in the market at Berber. Seeing I was hungry, he gave me food. After I ate, he told me to bring this donkey to you at Obak."

Abou Fatma studied the animal casually, as though noticing it for the first time.

"Tayeeb," he said. "The donkey is mine." And he sat still, as if the matter were settled.

But the negro stayed.

"I am to meet Moussa Fedil again in three days’ time, in Berber’s market. Give me a token so he may know I’ve delivered the message and reward me."

Abou Fatma drew his knife from his belt, picked up a stick from the ground, and notched it three times at each end.

"This will be your sign," he said, handing it over.

The negro tied the stick securely into his wrap, removed the water-skin from the donkey, filled it at the well, slung it over his shoulder, and gathered his weapons.

Abou Fatma watched him climb the slope, disappear over the crest, and vanish into the distance.

Then he rose quickly, his movements sharp and urgent.

Six days earlier, Harry Feversham had left Obak, riding this very donkey across fifty-eight miles of barren desert to reach the Nile. Now, Abou Fatma drove the beast down into the grove of trees, tethered it to a sapling, and examined its shoulders.

There, beneath the skin, was a tiny incision—neatly stitched shut with fine thread. Abou Fatma cut the stitches open, parted the wound, and pulled out a small package no bigger than a postage stamp. It was a goat’s bladder, sealed tightly. Inside, wrapped in thin paper, was a note written in Arabic and folded into a tight square.

Abou Fatma read it—not because he was a scholar, but because he had served Gordon, and under him had learned to read.

The letter said:

*“The houses of old Berber are gone. A new town rises with wide streets. There is no longer any mark by which I can find Yusef’s house among the ruins. Yusef himself no longer sells rock-salt in the bazaar. Yet wait for me another week.”*

The writer was Harry Feversham.

Disguised in the patched jubbeh of the Dervishes, his skin stained and weathered, his hair frizzed and matted like seaweed, Feversham wandered through the new town of Berber, searching for a man who might not exist. To the south, separated by a mile of desert, lay the old town—the warren of ruined homes where Abou Fatma had once slept and hidden letters. Walls had been torn down, roofs carried away, leaving only skeletal frames standing in silent rows. The courts were indistinguishable now, all crumbling into ruin. Foxes had already begun to dig burrows beneath the foundations.

Feversham had planned to stay just one night. He meant to slip into Berber after dark, spend the night, and leave before dawn. But now he had to move openly among crowds, pretending to have business, afraid to speak lest his voice betray him. Every passing word, every name shouted in the street, sent a jolt through him. Was there a mention of Yusef? Could he find him?

Despair clung to him like shadow. Fear never left. Yet from these torments, something else grew—madness, yes, but also a fierce, fanatical resolve. He believed now, as the Dervishes believed in their dark faith, that he *must* succeed. That the world could not allow such injustice—to burden the weakest man with the greatest trial, then destroy him for trying.

Three days after leaving Abou Fatma, Feversham stood on a slope, looking toward Berber. Before him stretched the endless sand, rising to meet the dark walls of the town. Beyond, the Nile bank shimmered with palm trees, green and inviting.

Inside those walls, the Dervishes gathered—hundreds of them, fanatics sworn to death and devotion. To enter was madness. To survive, impossible.

Was it right, he wondered, to even try?

He stood there, paralyzed. The mud walls loomed, sinister and watchful. The palms, once a welcome sight, now mocked him—green promises leading only to slaughter. His strength drained away. If he were caught… oh, surely he would be. He imagined the faces around him—wild, furious eyes. Would they mutilate him? Cut off his hands? His tongue?

He shivered, though the sun burned hot. The loneliness of the desert pressed upon him. His knees trembled.

He turned and ran—sprinting blindly across the empty waste, heart pounding, breath ragged. No one chased him. He was alone, utterly alone, fleeing from a fate he could not escape.

But only a few yards into his flight, he stopped.

What was this? Had four years of preparation meant nothing? Had he trained in language, endured hardship, lived in bazaars—all for nothing? Was he still the coward who had sent in his papers?

The quiet confidence he’d shown Lieutenant Sutch at the Criterion grill-room—was that just arrogance? A lie he told himself?

And Ethne… what of her?

He collapsed onto the sand, pulled his coat over his head, and lay still—just another brown patch in the vast emptiness. He closed his eyes and let his mind drift across oceans and continents.

For a moment, he was back in Donegal. Summer night whispered through an open doorway. Music drifted from a nearby room, where people danced. He saw the three feathers falling slowly to the floor. He saw Ethne’s face—pale, troubled.

If he could do this—if he could endure what lay ahead—he might one day see that face free of sorrow.

And then came the words she had spoken: *"I should have no doubt that you and I would see much of one another afterwards."*

As the sun sank low, Feversham rose. He walked steadily toward Berber, passed through the gates, and stepped into the heart of danger.
