October fifteenth marked the ceremonial opening of Fifth Avenue, a date when shutter windows flung wide, carpets unrolled in vibrant hues, and curtains swelled in three layers of fabric to welcome the world. By November first, this domestic ritual concluded, leaving society free to survey itself, taking inventory of the year's progress. By the middle of November, the season roared into full bloom: opera houses unveiled new stars, theaters offered fresh attractions, dinner invitations piled high, and dance cards filled with promises. And precisely at this juncture, Mrs. Archer invariably declared that New York had undergone a profound transformation.

Watching from the elevated, detached vantage point of a non-participant, aided by Mr. Sillerton Jackson and Miss Sophy, Mrs. Archer meticulously traced every fissure in the city's facade and every wild weed sprouting between the orderly rows of social convention. It had long been an amusement of Archer's youth to await this annual prophecy from his mother, listening as she cataloged the minute signs of decay that his casual eye had missed. To Mrs. Archer, New York never evolved without deteriorating; in this conviction, Miss Sophy Jackson wholeheartedly agreed.

Mr. Sillerton Jackson, acting as a man of the world, suspended judgment and listened with amused neutrality to the ladies' laments. Yet even he could not deny that New York had changed. And Newland Archer, in the winter of his second year of marriage, was compelled to concede that if the city had not altered, it was certainly in the throes of change.

These observations were typically raised at Mrs. Archer's Thanksgiving dinner. On the occasion when she was formally tasked with gratitude for the year's blessings, her custom was to conduct a mournful, albeit not embittered, audit of her world, wondering what exactly warranted thanks. Certainly, not the state of society; if it could be termed existence, it resembled a spectacle worthy of biblical curses—and indeed, everyone understood the implication when Reverend Dr. Ashmore selected Jeremiah (Chapter II, Verse 25) as his Thanksgiving text. Dr. Ashmore, the newly appointed Rector of St. Matthew's, had been chosen specifically for his "advanced" views; his sermons were deemed bold in thought and innovative in language. When he denounced fashionable society, he invariably spoke of its "trend." To Mrs. Archer, it was both terrifying and mesmerizing to feel herself part of a community trending downward.

"There is no doubt," she remarked, treating the concept of a "trend" as tangible and measurable, akin to a hairline fracture in a foundation wall, "that Dr. Ashmore is correct. There *is* a marked trend."

"It was peculiar, however, to address it during Thanksgiving," Miss Jackson observed. Her hostess replied dryly, "Oh, he intends for us to give thanks for what remains."

Archer, accustomed to smiling at his mother's annual prophecies, found himself unable to do so this year. As he listened to the litany of changes, he acknowledged that the "trend" was undeniable.

"The extravagance in dress—" Miss Jackson began. "Mr. Jackson took me to the inaugural night of the Opera, and I can assure you that Jane Merry's gown was the only one I recognized from last year. Even then, the front panel had been altered. Yet I am certain she acquired it from Worth only two years prior, for my seamstress invariably alters her client's Paris creations before they are worn."

"Ah, Jane Merry belongs to *our* circle," Mrs. Archer sighed, as if it were not a privilege to belong to an era where ladies displayed their Parisian garments immediately upon exiting Customs, rather than allowing them to mature under lock and key, as Mrs. Archer's contemporaries practiced.

"Yes; she is one of the few. In my youth," Miss Jackson countered, "it was deemed vulgar to dress in the latest fashions. Amy Sillerton has often told me that in Boston, the rule was to store Paris dresses for two seasons. Old Mrs. Baxter Pennilow, who executed everything with grandeur, imported twelve dresses annually—two velvet, two satin, two silk, and six of poplin and the finest cashmere. It was a standing order. As she was ill for two years before her death, forty-eight Worth dresses were discovered untouched in tissue paper. Consequently, when the girls ceased mourning, they wore the first batch at the Symphony concerts without appearing ahead of fashion."

"Ah, well, Boston is more conservative than New York; but I always consider it prudent for a lady to set aside her French dresses for one season," Mrs. Archer conceded.

"It was Beaufort who initiated this new fashion by ordering his wife to don her new attire the moment it arrived: I must say, at times, it requires all of Regina's distinction not to resemble..." Miss Jackson glanced around the table, catching Janey's widening eyes, and retreated into an unintelligible murmur.

"Like her rivals," Mr. Sillerton Jackson supplied, delivering the remark with the air of a polished epigram.

"Oh—" the ladies murmured. Mrs. Archer added, partly to divert her daughter's attention from prohibited subjects, "Poor Regina! Her Thanksgiving has not been particularly cheerful, I fear. Have you heard the rumors concerning Beaufort's investments, Sillerton?"

Mr. Jackson nodded casually. Everyone had heard the rumors, and he scorned to corroborate a tale already public property.

A somber silence descended upon the gathering. No one truly admired Beaufort, and it was almost pleasant to speculate on the worst aspects of his private life; yet the notion that he had brought financial disgrace upon his wife's family was too shocking to enjoy, even by his adversaries. New York's social sphere tolerated hypocrisy in personal relations, but in matters of commerce, it demanded absolute, transparent integrity. It had been many years since a prominent banker had failed so discreditably, yet everyone remembered the social annihilation visited upon families following the last such collapse. It would be the same with the Beauforts, regardless of his influence and her popularity; not all the united strength of the Dallas connections would save poor Regina if the reports of her husband's illicit speculation proved true.

Conversation drifted to less ominous topics, yet everything touched upon seemed to confirm Mrs. Archer's perception of an accelerating decline.

"Of course, Newland, I know you allow dear May to attend Mrs. Struthers's Sunday evenings—" she began. May interjected cheerfully, "Oh, you know, everyone attends Mrs. Struthers's now; she was even invited to Granny's last reception."

Thus, Archer reflected, New York managed its transitions: conspiring to ignore them until they were complete, and then, in good faith, assuming they had occurred in a previous age. There was always a traitor within the citadel; and once that individual (usually female) surrendered the keys, what purpose was there in feigning that the fortress remained impregnable? Having once tasted Mrs. Struthers's effortless Sunday hospitality, visitors were unlikely to retreat home to remember that her champagne was, in reality, shoe polish.

"I know, dear, I know," Mrs. Archer sighed. "Such things must endure, I suppose, as long as *amusement* is what draws people outward; but I have never quite forgiven your cousin, Madame Olenska, for being the first to countenance Mrs. Struthers."

A sudden flush rose to young Mrs. Archer's cheeks, surprising her husband as much as the other guests. "Oh, *Ellen*—" she murmured, employing the same accusing yet deprecating tone her parents might have used regarding "the Blenkers."

It was the note the family adopted whenever the Countess Olenska's name arose, ever since she had astonished and inconvenienced them by stubbornly refusing her husband's advances. Yet on May's lips, it sparked reflection, causing Archer to regard her with a sense of strangeness that occasionally surfaced when she most mirrored her environment.

His mother, with less sensitivity to atmospheric nuances than usual, persisted: "I have always believed that individuals like the Countess Olenska, who have resided in aristocratic societies, ought to assist us in maintaining our social distinctions, rather than disregarding them."

May's blush remained vivid, seemingly carrying significance beyond mere acknowledgment of Madame Olenska's social indiscretion.

"I have no doubt we all appear alike to foreigners," Miss Jackson remarked sharply.

"I do not believe Ellen cares for society; but no one knows precisely what she does care for," May continued, as if groping for a non-committal statement.

"Ah, well—" Mrs. Archer sighed again.

Everyone knew that the Countess Olenska was no longer in favor with her family. Even her devoted champion, elderly Mrs. Manson Mingott, had failed to defend her granddaughter's refusal to return to her husband. The Mingotts had not voiced their disapproval openly; their sense of solidarity was too strong. They had simply, as Mrs. Welland phrased it, "allowed poor Ellen to find her own level"—and that, mortifyingly and incomprehensibly, lay in the dim recesses where the Blenkers prevailed, and where "people who write" celebrated their chaotic rituals. It was unbelievable, yet factual, that Ellen, despite her opportunities and privileges, had become merely "Bohemian." This fact reinforced the argument that she had made a fatal error in not returning to Count Olenski. After all, a young woman's proper place was beneath her husband's roof, especially when she had departed under circumstances that... well, if one cared to investigate further...
