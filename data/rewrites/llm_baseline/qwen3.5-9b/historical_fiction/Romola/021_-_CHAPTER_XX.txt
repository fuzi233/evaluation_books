The final week of the Carnival had arrived, transforming the streets of Florence into a cacophony of noise and color. Masqued processions wound through the city, their chants echoing the new traditions introduced by Lorenzo the Magnificent. In the Piazza, under a pale, frosty sky, the *rigoletto*—a lively round dance—was performed with vigor. Yet, beneath this festive veneer lurked a darker undercurrent. Practical jokes ranged from harmless confetti to malicious projectiles; indeed, stones were the weapon of choice for the city's unruly youth. These boys and striplings, a formidable force in Florentine crowds, had grown as loud and chaotic as cicadas during the height of Carnival. Their immemorial custom was to block passersby with poles, demanding tribute in the form of supper or bonfire contributions, before concluding their revelry with the standing entertainment of stone-throwing. This sport was rarely monotonous; the resulting injuries varied wildly, and death was not uncommon. Thus, the pleasures of the Carnival were checkered, a grotesque tapestry of grossness and barbarity that a truthful painter would struggle to depict without turning the canvas to the wall. Only for the sake of history, to justify later reforms born of ignorance, could such scenes be justified.

Yet, amidst the chaos, there remained a more innocent, picturesque merriment, fueled by the quick animal spirits and sensitive hearts of the Florentines. Unlike the heavy stupor of the north or the stealthy ferocity of the south, where brawls often ended in daggers, the Florentine spirit retained a certain wild vitality.

High morning broke, but the carnival spirits lingered, lounging and recounting the night's jests. Among them walked Tito Melema at a brisk pace toward the Via de' Bardi. Beside him strode young Bernardo Dovizi—the same man who would later appear in Raphael's portrait as the keen-eyed Cardinal da Bibbiena. Though they moved through the throngs of the Por' Santa Maria, their conversation bore no relation to the sights and sounds surrounding them. Instead, they spoke animatedly, gesturing and smiling, yet casting frequent, wary glances around them. At the turn toward the Lung' Arno, leading to the Ponte Rubaconte, Tito's attention sharpened. In a rapid survey of the crowd, he spotted someone he desperately wished to avoid recognizing at that precise moment.

His mind was consumed with anticipation. Today marked a unique occasion in his life: his betrothal, scheduled for the evening. The arrangement had come suddenly. Preparations for the marriage had been underway for some time, primarily Tito pouring his florins into furnishing rooms in Bardo's dwelling, which had previously been sparsely equipped. Originally, both the betrothal and the wedding were to be deferred until after Easter, once Tito's year of probation, demanded by Bernardo del Nero, was complete. However, an urgent proposal arrived inviting Tito to accompany Cardinal Giovanni to Rome. There, his superior knowledge of Greek would assist Bernardo Dovizi in organizing a library—an opportunity clearly aligned with Tito's ascent. Unable to decline such a clear path to advancement, Tito had urgently petitioned that the betrothal occur before his departure. With the engagement formalized, the delay before the actual marriage upon his return would be minimal, and the pain of separation mitigated by the binding nature of their pledge. Legally, the exchange of rings and signing of mutual contracts constituted more than half of a marriage, leaving only the nuptial benediction to finalize it.

Romola's feelings had mirrored Tito's desire, and the elders' consent had been secured. Now, amidst frantic preparations for his imminent departure, Tito hastened to snatch a morning visit from Romola. He needed to exchange final words before their union in the evening. Recognition was unlikely to bring pleasure today, especially from Tessa.

It was unmistakably Tessa whom Tito had glimpsed moving with a timid, forlorn expression toward the bend in the Lung' Arno. As he continued his discussion with Bernardo Dovizi, an uncomfortable undercurrent of awareness settled over him. Tessa had seen him; she would certainly follow. Escape along the direct route by the Arno and over the Ponte Rubaconte was impossible. Yet, she dared not approach while he was accompanied, and Tito would maintain Dovizi's presence until they reached Bardo's door.

Quickening his pace, Tito spun new topics of conversation, but the sensation of being pursued intensified. Irritating, perhaps even more so because a flicker of tenderness and pity for the poor girl made his resolve to evade her silently feel like an unpalatable compromise. Undeterred, he guided his companion to the door, skillfully delivering his "goodbye" without turning his face in a direction where importunate blue eyes might catch him. Ascending the stone steps, he attempted to banish unpleasant thoughts, convincing himself that Tessa might not have seen him, or, if she had, might not have followed.

However, human perception operates independently of will. Before Tito could fully dismiss the notion, he knew—with absolute certainty—that a small figure in a white hood stood near the doorway. He sensed the truth before he felt the firm grasp laid upon his arm. It was not a timid touch, but a desperate clutch. Tessa, startled by his swift departure, had surged forward with a final, bold effort.

When Tito halted and turned, fear etched her features. She dreaded the consequence of her audacity.

"Tessa!" Tito exclaimed, his voice sharper than she had ever heard it. "Why are you here? You must not follow me—you must not loiter at doorways waiting for me."

Her blue eyes widened with unshed tears, and she remained silent. Tito feared ridicule more than anything else; the image of a high-street nobleman in the Via de' Bardi accompanied by a peasant girl staring pathetically at him was unbearable. Bernardo del Nero, or some other dangerous figure, could appear at any moment. Even absent the day of his betrothal, the scene would be awkward. Yet, driving Tessa away with harsh words was impossible.

That cursed folly regarding the *cerretano* resurfaced, buried for months only to bloom now as an unseasonable weed of vexation. He could not speak cruelly, so he spoke hurriedly instead.

"Tessa, I cannot—must not talk to you here. I will go to the bridge and wait for you. Follow me slowly."

He turned and walked rapidly toward the Ponte Rubaconte, leaning against the wall of a quaint house overlooking the approach Tessa would take. To see her advance, her round face pale and sorrowful since their parting at the Nunziata, would have softened even a harder heart. Fortunately, the bridge was seldom crowded, and at that moment, it was nearly empty.

As soon as she approached, Tito spoke. "Now, Tessa, I have very little time. Do not cry. Why did you follow me this morning? Never do so again."

"I thought," Tessa whispered, fighting back a sob that threatened to break loose at the sharpness of his tone, "I thought you wouldn't be so long before you came to take care of me again. And the *patrigno* beats me, and I can't bear it any longer. Always when I come for a holiday I walk about to find you, but I can't. Oh, please don't send me away from you again! It has been so long, and I cry so now because you never come to me. I can't help it; the days are so long, and I don't mind the goats or the kids... I can't—"

Sobs tore through her quickly, great tears streaming down her face. Tito realized he could not simply reject her. Sending her away was necessary, yes, but telling her truths that would plunge her into hopeless grief was impossible. He saw deeper troubles looming, but the immediate pressure of the moment outweighed distant consequences.

"Tessa, my little one," he said, adopting his old, caressing manner, "you must not cry. Endure the cross *patrigno* a little longer. I will return to you. But I am going now to Rome—a very long way off. I shall return in a few weeks, and then I promise to come and see you. Promise me to be good and wait for me."

It was the familiar voice; the sound alone was half sufficient to soothe Tessa. She looked up with trusting eyes, still shimmering with tears, sobbing despite her utmost efforts to comply.

"Promise me, my Tessa," he urged gently.

"Yes," she whispered.

"But you won't be long?"

"No, not long. But I must *go now*. Remember what I told you, Tessa. No one must know you ever see me, or you will lose me forever. Go straight home when I leave you, and never follow me again. Wait for me to come. Good-bye, my little Tessa: I *will* come."

There was no alternative. He had to turn and depart without looking back to witness her reaction, for he had no time to spare. Once he rounded the corner into the Via de' Benci, where he could no longer see the bridge, he knew Tessa would obey. She was too trusting and compliant not to do exactly as instructed.

For that day, the difficulty was resolved. Yet, this resurgence of Tessa's presence, occurring at a moment when he could not deceive her, left an unpleasant mark on his memory. Tito's mind, however, was currently saturated with the hopeful glow of his first love, intertwined with ambitions that promised a bright future.
