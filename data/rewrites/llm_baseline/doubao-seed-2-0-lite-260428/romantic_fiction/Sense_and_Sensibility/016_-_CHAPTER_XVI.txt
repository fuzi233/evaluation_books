Marianne Dashwood would have deemed herself unforgivably selfish if she’d managed to sleep a single sound hour the first night after Willoughby rode away. To wake the next morning well-rested, unburdened by grief, would have felt like a betrayal of every tender promise they’d whispered to each other beneath Barton’s ancient oak trees – too shameful for her to ever face her mother or sisters. As it happened, she had no chance to feel that guilt at all: she lay wide awake till dawn, sobbing through the majority of the dark hours, her pillows crumpled and soaked with tears. She rose at sunrise with a throbbing headache, too raw to speak, too sick at heart to swallow a single bite of food, and waved off every gentle attempt at comfort her family offered. No one could soothe a grief as fierce and fiery as hers.

Once breakfast was cleared away, she slipped out alone, wandering the lanes of Allenham for hours, lingering at every spot that held a memory of Willoughby: the fallen log where they’d shared a picnic of wild strawberries, the stone wall where he’d sketched her as she laughed, the cottage garden where he’d plucked her a red rose that still retained its scent pressed between the pages of her favourite poetry book. She cried almost nonstop till midday, replaying every word he’d said the morning he mounted his horse to leave.

That evening, she sank into the same soft, sharp indulgence of grief. She played every song on the pianoforte that they’d ever sung together, every duet where their voices had tangled perfectly, till her fingers ached, and she sat staring at the handwritten sheet music he’d copied out for her, tears dripping onto the ink, until her heart felt so heavy there was no more space for sadness to settle. This quiet torture became her daily ritual: hours at the piano, half-singing, half-sobbing, her voice breaking completely when the memory of his laugh played in her head. She only read the books they’d pored over side by side, turning to every marked, dog-eared page, and letting the contrast between their happy past and her empty present twist her heart all over again.

Such wild, unregulated pain could not burn bright forever. After a few days, it softened into a quieter, constant melancholy, but her long solitary walks, her silent hours sitting by the open window staring down the lane Willoughby had taken, still sparked sudden, loud bursts of weeping that shook her whole body.

No letter arrived from Willoughby, and Marianne never once asked for one. Mrs Dashwood was surprised by the silence, but Elinor’s gentle worry bloomed into sharp unease. Her mother, though, was ever-ready with a romantic, convenient explanation that soothed her own mind completely. “You forget, Elinor,” she said, “Sir John fetches and drops off all our post himself. We already agreed their engagement must be a secret for now – how could they possibly exchange letters without Sir John fumbling through them and spilling the news to every servant in the shire?”

Elinor could not argue that logic, and tried to cling to it to calm her own fears. But there was one simple, straightforward way to settle all the mystery at once, and she could not stop herself from suggesting it. “Why do you not just ask Marianne if she and Willoughby are engaged?” she said. “From you, her dearest, most indulgent mother, the question could never give offence. She has never hidden anything from you, not once.”

“I would never do such a thing for the world,” Mrs Dashwood protested. “If by some chance they are not yet engaged, how much pain that enquiry would inflict! Even if they are, it would be cruel to force her to reveal a secret they have chosen to keep private. I would never earn back her trust if I pressured her into a confession she is not ready to give. I know my Marianne – she loves me too well, and I will be the very first person she tells the moment the time is right. I will never force the confidence of someone I love, least of all my own child.”

Elinor thought that delicacy was overstrained, given how young Marianne was, and tried to argue further, but to no avail. Common prudence, practical care, all common sense was drowned out in Mrs Dashwood’s soft, romantic respect for her daughter’s feelings.

For days, no one in the family dared speak Willoughby’s name in front of Marianne, though Sir John and Mrs Jennings had no such tact: their teasing jibes about her pining for her handsome suitor only added fresh pain to her already raw days. Until one evening, when Mrs Dashwood picked up a volume of Shakespeare by accident, and exclaimed, “Marianne, we never finished *Hamlet*, did we? Our dear Willoughby left before we could get to the final act. We will tuck it away, so when he comes back… though it may be months, perhaps, before he returns.”

“Months?” Marianne cried, her head flying up, her eyes bright with shocked disbelief. “No – not months. It will only be weeks.”

Mrs Dashwood apologized immediately for her careless words, but Elinor felt a quiet spark of relief. Marianne’s outburst made it clear: she had absolute faith in Willoughby, absolute certainty he intended to come back for her.

A week after Willoughby had ridden away, Elinor finally succeeded in persuading Marianne to join her and Margaret on their daily walk, instead of slipping off alone as she had done every day before. She had been deliberately avoiding all company on her rambles: if her sisters planned to walk the downs, she snuck off to the narrow lanes, if they spoke of heading to the valley, she climbed the hills alone, vanishing before they could call her over. But Elinor refused to let her shut herself away any longer, and at last Marianne gave in.

They walked along the valley road in near silence: Marianne’s thoughts were too tangled, too tied up in Willoughby to make small talk, and Elinor was content to have pulled her sister out of the cottage, making no further demands. Beyond the mouth of the valley, the land opened up, rich but less wild, and they found themselves on the long stretch of highway they had travelled the very first day they arrived at Barton. They stopped there, a spot they had never reached on any of their prior walks, to gaze out at the view of their cottage far off in the distance.

Off down the road, they soon spotted a figure on horseback, riding fast towards them. In minutes they could see he was a gentleman – and Marianne’s face lit up so bright it outshone the sun. “It’s him! It is, truly!” she cried, and started running towards him at a full sprint.

“Marianne, no,” Elinor called after her, hurrying to catch up. “You are mistaken. That is not Willoughby. He is not tall enough, he does not move with Willoughby’s easy, bright grace.”

“He is! He is!” Marianne insisted, not slowing down. “His coat, his horse, his smile – I knew he would come back soon!”

Elinor hurried to keep pace with her, half-afraid the stranger would notice Marianne’s wild excitement and think her foolish, and they were soon only thirty yards away. Marianne glanced up at the rider again, and the colour drained straight from her face. Her heart plummeted, and she spun on her heel to rush back the way she’d come, when two familiar voices called her name, and a third – almost as familiar to her as Willoughby’s – begged her to stop.

She turned around, stunned, to see Edward Ferrars.

He was the only person in the whole world who could have not been Willoughby and not made her burst into fresh tears of disappointment right there on the road. He was the only one who could draw a smile from her, even through her damp eyes. She brushed her tears away, grinning to greet him, and for a little while her own heartache faded, replaced by joy for Elinor.

He dismounted, handed his horse off to his servant, and walked back with them towards Barton Cottage, for he had ridden there that day expressly to visit them. The whole family welcomed him warmly, but none more so than Marianne, who greeted him with far more open warmth than even Elinor herself. To Marianne’s eyes, though, something was very wrong. Edward was supposed to be in love with Elinor – so why was he so stiff? He looked flustered, hardly seemed pleased to see them at all, no bright smile, no rapturous greeting. He barely spoke unless someone asked him a direct question, and he did not give Elinor a single special look, not a single gentle gesture a lover would offer. Marianne watched him, more and more confused, until she almost felt a flicker of dislike for Edward. How dull his manners were, compared to Willoughby’s bright, open warmth.

After the first flurry of greetings, Marianne asked if he had ridden straight from London. No, he told her, he had been in Devonshire for a full fortnight.

“A fortnight?” Marianne repeated, shocked. He had been in the same county as Elinor for two whole weeks, and had not come to see her till now? He looked uncomfortable as he explained he had been staying with friends near Plymouth.

“Did you visit Sussex recently?” Elinor asked him, softly.

“I was at Norland a month ago,” he replied.

“Oh, dear Norland!” Marianne exclaimed. “How does it look?”

“Dear Norland,” Elinor said, smiling a little, “no doubt it looks as it always does this time of year. The woods and paths are carpeted thick with fallen dead leaves.”

“Oh!” Marianne cried, her eyes glistening. “I used to feel such wild joy watching those leaves fall! I would walk for miles, laughing as the wind sent them swirling all around me. No one there cares for that now, though. They see leaves as a nuisance, sweep them up as fast as they can, and hide them from sight.”

“Not everyone shares your passion for dead leaves, Marianne,” Elinor said, gently.

“No, my feelings are rarely understood,” Marianne murmured, drifting off into a daydream for a moment, before she shook herself and pointed out the view to Edward. “Look, there is Barton Valley. Stare at those hills – have you ever seen anything so beautiful? To the left, tucked in the woods, you can see the edge of Barton Park. And beyond that furthest grand hill, that is our little cottage.”

“It is a pretty place,” Edward said. “Though those low meadows down there must be horribly muddy in the winter.”

“Mud?” Marianne said, bewildered, under her breath. “How can you look at all this beauty and only think of mud?”

“Are your neighbours pleasant here?” he asked, ignoring her shock. “Are the Middletons good company?”

“Not at all,” Marianne said immediately. “We could not have chosen worse people to live near.”

“Marianne!” Elinor cried, horrified. “How can you say that? They are a most respectable family, and they have been nothing but kind to us. Have you forgotten all the happy afternoons we have spent at their home?”

“No,” Marianne mumbled quietly. “I have not forgotten all the painful afternoons, either.”

Elinor chose not to scold her further, and turned her attention back to Edward, making an effort to keep the conversation flowing, talking about their cottage, its little comforts, drawing only short, polite remarks from him. His cold reserve stung her deeply, and she felt half angry, half hurt. But she was determined to treat him as she had always treated him, to ignore his strange distance and not let him see how much he had wounded her that day.
