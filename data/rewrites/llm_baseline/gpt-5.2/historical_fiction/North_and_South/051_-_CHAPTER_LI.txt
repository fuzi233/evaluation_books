It was one of those London evenings when the heat lies over the squares like a lid, and even the plane-trees seem to hoard their breath. The house held its own small weather: a hush of shut blinds, the faint medicinal coolness of shaded passages, and—wherever Dixon had lately been—an undercurrent of lavender-water and starched linen, as if cleanliness were the only defence against summer.

Edith swept into Margaret’s bed-room in her first impatience of dressing, and then, for the second time, returned fully prepared for dinner, as though she could not bear to be alone with her own arrangements. At first the room was empty. When she came again, Dixon was there, laying Margaret’s gown across the bed with the careful, almost reverent smoothing of a woman who had handled fine fabric in many households and knew how much authority hid in a well-set fold. Margaret herself was nowhere.

Edith began to hover and pull at trifles the way some people worry a stitch.

“Oh, Dixon! not those horrid blue flowers with that dead gold-coloured gown. What taste! Wait—wait; I will bring you some pomegranate blossoms.”

“It’s not dead gold, ma’am,” returned Dixon, stiff with offence, without ceasing to pin and arrange. “It’s straw-colour. And blue always goes with straw-colour.”

But Edith had already gone, and was back before Dixon’s protest could gather itself into a sermon, carrying the scarlet cluster as if she had plucked it from a hotter country. The blossoms seemed to throw off a bitter, green-tinged perfume of broken stem, sharp as a knife on rind.

“There!” Edith cried, holding them up against the dress with the triumph of one who believes colour is argument. “Now—where is Miss Hale? I can’t think,” she went on, as she adjusted the garniture, “how my aunt allowed her to get into such rambling habits in Milton! I’m always expecting to hear of her having met with something horrible among those wretched places she pokes herself into. I should never dare go down some of those streets without a servant. They’re not fit for ladies.”

Dixon, still smarting under the slight to her “taste,” answered shortly, as if clipping her words to save them from further insult.

“It’s no wonder to my mind, when I hear ladies talk such a deal about ladies—and when they’re such fearful, delicate, dainty ladies too—I say it’s no wonder to me that there are no longer any saints on earth—”

“Oh, Margaret! here you are!” Edith broke in, for at that moment Margaret came in—her face warmed, as if she had been out under the brazen sky or had climbed too quickly. There was a little disarray about her, not of dress but of spirit, as though she had been called back from thought. “I have been so wanting you. How your cheeks are flushed with the heat, poor child! But only think what that tiresome Henry has done—really he exceeds brother-in-law’s limits. Just when my party was made up so beautifully—fitted in so precisely for Mr. Colthurst—there has Henry come, with an apology it is true, and making use of your name for an excuse, and asked me if he may bring that Mr. Thornton of Milton—your tenant, you know—who is in London about some law business. It will spoil my number, quite.”

The name, dropped so airily into the room, went through Margaret like a sudden bell heard close at hand. The very stillness behind Edith’s chattering seemed to fill with it. Dixon paused in her pinning as if listening, though her eyes stayed on the gown.

“I don’t mind dinner. I don’t want any,” said Margaret, and her voice came low, as if the heat had dried it. “Dixon can get me a cup of tea here, and I will be in the drawing-room by the time you come up. I shall really be glad to lie down.”

“No, no! that will never do. You do look wretchedly white, to be sure; but that is just the heat, and we can’t do without you possibly. Those flowers a little lower, Dixon. They look glorious flames, Margaret, in your black hair. You know we planned you to talk about Milton to Mr. Colthurst. Oh! to be sure! and this man comes from Milton. I believe it will be capital after all. Mr. Colthurst can pump him well on all the subjects he is interested in, and it will be great fun to trace out your experiences, and this Mr. Thornton’s wisdom, in Mr. Colthurst’s next speech in the House. Really, I think it is a happy hit of Henry’s. I asked him if he was a man one would be ashamed of; and he replied, ‘Not if you’ve any sense in you, my little sister.’ So I suppose he is able to sound his h’s, which is not a common Darkshire accomplishment—eh, Margaret?”

Margaret stood still while Dixon’s practised fingers settled the pomegranate blossoms into her hair. The scent rose up at each touch—red fruitiness without sweetness, a memory of bruised rind—and in that sharpness there was something that steadied her. She made herself ask, evenly:

“Mr. Lennox did not say why Mr. Thornton was come up to town? Was it law business connected with the property?”

“Oh! he’s failed, or something of the kind, that Henry told you of that day you had such a headache—what was it? There, that’s capital, Dixon. Miss Hale does us credit, does she not? I wish I was as tall as a queen, and as brown as a gipsy, Margaret.”

“But about Mr. Thornton?”

“Oh! I really have such a terrible head for law business. Henry will like nothing better than to tell you all about it. I know the impression he made upon me was, that Mr. Thornton is very badly off, and a very respectable man, and that I’m to be very civil to him; and as I did not know how, I came to you to ask you to help me. And now come down with me, and rest on the sofa for a quarter of an hour.”

Downstairs the house had begun to sound as if it were putting on its public voice: the soft thud of a door eased to, the discreet clink of glass, the murmur of servants in the corridor, and over it all the distant, steady rumble of London—wheels on paving-stones, the far cry of a street-seller, the faint metallic ring of a horse’s shoe striking curb. The city’s noise did not come to them as Milton’s did, close and insistent; here it was a backdrop, like sea heard from behind a wall, and it lent to every word spoken in drawing-room tones a kind of artificial importance.

Henry Lennox came early, as the privileged brother-in-law may. Margaret, with the pomegranate red burning at her temples, began at once, as soon as they were near enough to speak without Edith’s fluttering interruptions, to ask what she needed to know.

“He came up about this sub-letting the property—Marlborough Mills, and the house and premises adjoining, I mean,” said Mr. Lennox. “He is unable to keep it on; and there are deeds and leases to be looked over and agreements to be drawn up. I hope Edith will receive him properly; but she was rather put out, as I could see, by the liberty I had taken in begging for an invitation for him. But I thought you would like to have some attention shown him: and one would be particularly scrupulous in paying every respect to a man who is going down in the world.”

He had dropped his voice for Margaret’s ear, and the words “going down” were spoken with a delicacy that yet carried the faint triumph of the prosperous over the fallen. But before Margaret could answer, he sprang up, for their guest had entered.

Mr. Thornton came in with that self-command that seems at once a shield and a proclamation. It was more than a year since Margaret had seen him. He was still distinguished by his height and the easy power of his carriage; but his face had altered as stone alters under weather—sharper about the mouth, lined where there had been smoothness, with a look of being used to bracing the will against disappointment. Yet there was a composure upon him, something quiet and indestructible, that made the talk of his changed position seem to those who watched him like talk of an accident that could not touch the core of the man.

He looked round the room at once and saw Margaret. He had caught her intent listening posture while Mr. Lennox spoke; and he came up to her with the perfectly regulated manner of an old friend. At his first calm words a vivid colour flashed into Margaret’s cheeks, and did not leave them again all evening.

She did not say much. She asked him, quietly, almost coldly, questions about Milton and their old acquaintances—questions that seemed to him merely the necessary civilities. Others came in—people more intimate with the house than he—and he fell back, as if he had no claim to any nearer place. From time to time he and Mr. Lennox talked together with the constrained politeness of men who have measured each other.

“You think Miss Hale looking well,” said Mr. Lennox, watching him with the curiosity of one who likes to read effect. “Milton didn’t agree with her, I imagine; for when she first came to London, I thought I had never seen any one so much changed. To-night she is looking radiant. But she is much stronger. Last autumn she was fatigued with a walk of a couple of miles. On Friday evening we walked up to Hampstead and back. Yet on Saturday she looked as well as she does now.”

“We!” The syllable dropped into Mr. Thornton’s mind like a pebble into deep water, making circles he could not stop.

At dinner Mr. Colthurst—clever, quick-eyed, already gaining that authority in the House which comes not only from speaking but from being listened to—caught on to a remark Mr. Thornton made, and asked Edith who he was. Edith, rather to her surprise, found that Mr. Thornton’s name was not wholly unknown to him; the “Indeed!” with which he received it was charged with more meaning than she had expected.

Edith’s dinner went off well. Henry was in good humour, and his dry, caustic wit showed to advantage. Mr. Thornton and Mr. Colthurst found one or two mutual subjects, which they could only touch upon then, reserving them for the more confidential after-dinner talk that men take as naturally as wine. Margaret, with the pomegranate blossoms against her dark hair, looked beautiful; and if she leaned back in her chair and spoke little, Edith was not vexed, for the talk flowed without her, and she could enjoy her own success.

Margaret watched Mr. Thornton’s face. He never looked at her; so she could study him unobserved and mark what the short span of time and the heavy press of events had done. Only when Mr. Lennox threw out some unexpected mot, Mr. Thornton’s face flashed into the old look of intense enjoyment: the merry brightness returned to his eyes, the lips parted to suggest the brilliant smile of former days; and for an instant his glance, by instinct, sought Margaret’s, as though he wanted her sympathy as he used to. But when their eyes met, his whole countenance changed; the light went out as quickly as it had come, leaving him grave and anxious once more; and after that he resolutely avoided even looking near her again through the rest of dinner.

In the drawing-room afterward there were only two other ladies besides their own party; and these were engaged by Mrs. Shaw and Edith. Margaret took up some work and let her fingers move, though she hardly knew what she did. The sound of conversation arranged itself in layers: Edith’s clear little ripple, Mrs. Shaw’s heavier cadence, the thin rustle of silk as someone shifted in a chair. Under it, the city continued its distant chorus, the steady tide of wheels and far voices. It struck Margaret, with a sudden ache, how different this sound-world was from Milton’s—no loom-throb, no harsh factory bell—yet here too there was a kind of machinery, only made of manners and ambition.

The gentlemen came up. Mr. Colthurst and Mr. Thornton were in close conversation as they entered, as if the room had to make space for the current of their thoughts. Mr. Lennox sauntered to Margaret and spoke low.

“I really think Edith owes me thanks for my contribution to her party. You’ve no idea what an agreeable, sensible fellow this tenant of yours is. He has been the very man to give Colthurst all the facts he wanted coaching in. I can’t conceive how he contrived to mismanage his affairs.”

“With his powers and opportunities you would have succeeded,” said Margaret.

He did not quite relish her tone, though it expressed a thought that had crossed his own mind. He was silent; and their attention was caught by a swell in the talk near the fire-place, where Mr. Colthurst and Mr. Thornton stood.

“I assure you, I heard it spoken of with great interest—curiosity as to its result, perhaps I should rather say. I heard your name frequently mentioned during my short stay in the neighbourhood.”

They lost some words in the shifting of other voices, and then Mr. Thornton’s was distinct again, deep and controlled.

“I have not the elements for popularity—if they spoke of me in that way, they were mistaken. I fall slowly into new projects; and I find it difficult to let myself be known, even by those whom I desire to know, and with whom I would fain have no reserve. Yet, even with all these drawbacks, I felt that I was on the right path, and that, starting from a kind of friendship with one, I was becoming acquainted with many. The advantages were mutual: we were both unconsciously and consciously teaching each other.”

“You say ‘were.’ I trust you are intending to pursue the same course?”

“I must stop Colthurst,” said Henry Lennox, hastily; and by an abrupt yet apt question he turned the stream, as if he could, by sheer social dexterity, dam up the shame of failure. But it was only for a moment. As soon as the new subject had run its little course and dried up, Mr. Thornton returned to the point with the quiet courage of a man who would rather name his loss than have it guessed at.

“I have been unsuccessful in business,” he said, “and have had to give up my position as a master. I am on the look-out for a situation in Milton, where I may meet with employment under some one who will be willing to let me go along my own way in such matters as these. I can depend upon myself for having no go-ahead theories that I would rashly bring into practice. My only wish is to have the opportunity of cultivating some intercourse with the hands beyond the mere ‘cash nexus.’ But it might be the point Archimedes sought from which to move the earth, to judge by the importance attached to it by some of our manufacturers, who shake their heads and look grave as soon as I name the one or two experiments that I should like to try.”

“You call them ‘experiments,’ I notice,” said Mr. Colthurst, and there was a delicate increase of respect in his manner, as if he valued caution more than rhetoric.

“Because I believe them to be such. I am not sure of the consequences that may result from them. But I am sure they ought to be tried. I have arrived at the conviction that no mere institutions, however wise, and however much thought may have been required to organise and arrange them, can attach class to class as they should be attached, unless the working out of such institutions bring the individuals of the different classes into actual personal contact. Such intercourse is the very breath of life. A working man can hardly be made to feel and know how much his employer may have laboured in his study at plans for the benefit of his workpeople. A complete plan emerges like a piece of machinery, apparently fitted for every emergency. But the hands accept it as they do machinery, without understanding the intense mental labour and forethought required to bring it to such perfection. But I would take an idea, the working out of which would necessitate personal intercourse; it might not go well at first, but at every hitch interest would be felt by an increasing number of men, and at last its success in working come to be desired by all, as all had borne a part in the formation of the plan; and even then I am sure that it would lose its vitality, cease to be living, as soon as it was no longer carried on by that sort of common interest which invariably makes people find means and ways of seeing each other, and becoming acquainted with each other’s characters and persons, and even tricks of temper and modes of speech. We should understand each other better, and I’ll venture to say we should like each other more.”

“And you think they may prevent recurrence of strikes?” asked Mr. Colthurst.

“Not at all. My utmost expectation only goes as far as this—that they may render strikes not the bitter, venomous sources of hatred they have hitherto been. A more hopeful man might imagine that a closer and more genial intercourse between classes might do away with strikes. But I am not a hopeful man.”

As he spoke, Margaret heard, behind the ease of his phrases, the other sound she remembered from Milton: not the hiss of steam now, but the inward harshness of effort, the way a man must keep his voice even when everything in him strains. And she thought, too, of how much of that struggle could never be translated into the fine language of a drawing-room or the clean arguments of Parliament—how “cash nexus” itself, tossed out as a term, hid the daily hunger and pride and anger that had given it its meaning. Words, she felt, could be a kind of coin as well; and those who minted them ruled what could be said.

Suddenly, as if a new thought had struck him—something simple and human breaking through his careful discourse—Mr. Thornton crossed the room to where Margaret sat. He began without preface, as if he knew she had been listening all the while.

“Miss Hale, I had a round-robin from some of my men—I suspect in Higgins’ handwriting—stating their wish to work for me, if ever I was in a position to employ men again on my own behalf. That was good, wasn’t it?”

“Yes. Just right. I am glad of it,” said Margaret.

She looked up straight into his face with her speaking eyes; then, under the force of his glance, dropped her own. The room seemed very quiet for a moment, as if the air itself listened; and in that quiet she could hear the faint crackle of coal in the grate, the delicate tapping of someone’s fan, the distant London wheels making their constant undertone—sound upon sound, indifferent to private hearts.

He gazed at her for a minute, as if he did not know exactly what he was about, or what right he had to stand there. Then he sighed; and, saying, “I knew you would like it,” he turned away. After that he did not speak to her again until he said a formal “good night.”

When at last Mr. Lennox took his departure, Margaret followed him into the hall, her cheeks still stained with heat and something deeper. The pomegranate blossoms had begun to droop a little, their scarlet less triumphant, but their scent clung.

“Can I speak to you to-morrow?” she asked, with a blush she could not repress and a hesitation that betrayed how bitter it was to ask help of him. “I want your help about—something.”

“Certainly. I will come at whatever time you name. You cannot give me a greater pleasure than by making me of any use.” His voice warmed as if he tasted victory in the offer. “At eleven? Very well.”

His eye brightened with exultation as he went away. It seemed to him that she was learning, at last, to depend upon him; and that any day now might give him certainty—without which he had resolved he would never offer himself to her again.
