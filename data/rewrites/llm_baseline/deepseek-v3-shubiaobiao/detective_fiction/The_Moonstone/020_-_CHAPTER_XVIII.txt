The air was thick with tension as I descended the front steps, only to collide with Sergeant Cuff. Pride warred within me—after our recent clashes, I had no wish to betray curiosity about his investigations. Yet curiosity won, as it so often does. My dignity abandoned me entirely.

“Any news from Frizinghall?” I demanded.

The Sergeant’s expression remained unreadable. “I’ve seen the Indians,” he replied. “And I’ve uncovered Rosanna’s purchase in town last Thursday.” His voice lowered. “The Indians will be released next Wednesday. Make no mistake, Mr. Betteredge—they came for the Moonstone. Their plans were disrupted by the events of Wednesday night, but if *we* fail to recover the jewel, rest assured *they* will not.”

A chill crawled down my spine. At that moment, Mr. Franklin returned from his walk. Unlike me, he mastered his curiosity, passing us in silence. I, however, had already sacrificed my pride—I might as well reap the benefits.

“Enough of the Indians,” I pressed. “What of Rosanna?”

Cuff’s frown deepened. “The mystery thickens. She visited Maltby’s linen drapery in Frizinghall—bought nothing but long cloth, enough for a nightgown, and of a very particular quality.”

“Whose nightgown?” I interrupted.

“Her own, naturally.” His tone was patient, as if explaining to a child. “Between midnight and three on Thursday, she must have slipped into Miss Verinder’s room to conceal the Diamond. Returning, her nightgown brushed the wet paint. She couldn’t remove the stain, nor destroy the garment without raising suspicion. Hence, the replacement.”

I bristled. “What proof is there it was hers?”

“The simplicity of the fabric,” he countered. “A lady’s nightgown would require lace, frills—far more effort than a servant could manage overnight. No, Mr. Betteredge, the question isn’t *what* she did, but *why* she hid the stained gown instead of burning it. The answer lies at the Shivering Sand.”

“And how do you propose to find it?”

A ghost of a smile flickered across his face. “That, I keep to myself.”

(For the curious, I’ll reveal what he withheld: armed with a search warrant, Cuff suspected Rosanna carried some record of the hiding place. A seasoned officer, he knew such precautions were common among thieves.)

“Enough speculation,” he said briskly. “Where is Joyce?”

As if summoned by the hour, the clock struck two, and the carriage rolled into view—bound for Frizinghall, bearing Miss Rachel to her aunt’s.

“One matter at a time,” Cuff said, halting me. “Miss Verinder first.”

The rain still loomed, so the closed carriage had been prepared. With a gesture, Cuff beckoned Samuel from his perch.

“A friend of mine waits among the trees,” he murmured. “He’ll join you in the rumble. Keep silent, and shut your eyes—unless you wish trouble.”

The implication struck like a blow: Rachel was to be watched. A spy in her own carriage! I could have bitten off my tongue for engaging with Cuff at all.

Lady Verinder emerged first, a silent sentinel on the steps, arms folded beneath her cloak. Then Rachel descended—a vision in yellow, her dark beauty accentuated by the tight-fitting jacket, straw hat, and veil. But her eyes burned with a feverish intensity, her lips pale and unsmiling.

She kissed her mother hastily. “Try to forgive me,” she whispered, then yanked down her veil with such force it tore. In a flash, she was in the carriage, as if fleeing a storm.

Cuff moved swiftly, blocking her path. “A word, miss,” he said, hand on the door. “Your departure hinders the Diamond’s recovery. The choice is yours.”

Rachel’s answer was a cold command: “Drive on, James!”

The door slammed. Mr. Franklin lunged forward. “Goodbye, Rachel,” he called, offering his hand.

“Drive on!” she cried again, ignoring him entirely. Franklin recoiled, stunned. The coachman hesitated, looking to Lady Verinder, who stood rigid. With a fraught gesture, she signaled the carriage onward, then retreated inside.

Franklin found his voice. “Aunt, you were right,” he shouted after her. “Thank you—let me go.”

Lady Verinder turned, her face a tempest of emotions. “See me before you leave,” she managed, her voice breaking, then vanished upstairs.

Franklin faced me, eyes glistening. “Get me to the train, Betteredge. Quickly.”

Rachel had undone him completely. Need I say more of his devotion?

Left alone with Cuff, I glared as he hummed *The Last Rose of Summer*, watching the carriage disappear down the drive.

“There’s a time for whistling,” I snapped, “and this isn’t it.”

The carriage reappeared briefly through the trees—Samuel in the rumble, and beside him, another figure.

“All right,” Cuff murmured. He turned, all business. “Now, Mr. Betteredge, we proceed without mercy. Starting with Rosanna. Where is Joyce?”

Joyce, when found, was trembling. He’d lost Rosanna nearly an hour prior, too cautious in his surveillance.

Cuff dismissed him with icy calm. “Your talents are wasted here, Mr. Joyce. Good morning.”

Rosanna’s disappearance left me reeling. Cuff, however, was unmoved.

“She won’t escape,” he assured me. “As long as Miss Verinder is watched, Rosanna will lead us to her. They’ll meet in Frizinghall now, not here. The investigation shifts—sooner than expected.” He paused. “Gather the servants again.”

Despite myself, the old detective fever surged. I gripped his arm. “What now?”

Cuff stared into the distance, as if addressing the heavens. “If this man understood roses,” he mused, “he’d be the most perfect creature alive.”

Then, briskly: “Rosanna has two choices—Frizinghall directly, or the Shivering Sand first. We must learn who last saw her.”

The kitchenmaid Nancy provided the clue: Rosanna had handed a letter to the butcher’s man, insisting it be posted in Frizinghall despite the detour.

“The hiding place is in that letter,” Cuff concluded. “I must see its address. If it’s as I suspect, Mrs. Yolland will have another visitor come Monday.”

As we prepared the pony-chaise, a fresh revelation awaited us in the stable yard—but that, as they say, is another chapter.
