After a few minutes, the unwonted stillness of the room had penetrated Mordecai’s consciousness. It was a rare acoustic sanctuary, insulated for a fleeting moment from the grinding, mechanized noise of the London streets—an industrial dissonance that daily assaulted his nerves and drowned out the delicate frequencies of human connection. He looked up at Deronda, not with bewilderment, but with a gaze full of reposing satisfaction, savoring the quiet as one might savor the lingering finish of a slow-simmered, restorative broth. Deronda rose and placed his chair nearer, closing the physical distance so there would be no need to force the vocal cords, respecting the fragile sonic environment. Mordecai felt the action as a patient feels the gentleness that eases his pillow, a sensory comfort long denied to him. 

He began to speak in a low, resonant tone, his voice vibrating with the cadence of an ancient, eroding dialect, as if he were only thinking articulately, not trying to reach an audience. 

“In the doctrine of the Cabbala, souls are not merely born; they are cultivated,” Mordecai murmured, grounding his mysticism in the physical alchemy of creation. “They are folded again and again into new bodies, much like the slow-fermenting dough of an ancestral bread, absorbing the bitter and the sweet of the earthly terroir until they are perfected and purified. A soul liberated from a worn-out husk may join the fellow-soul that hungers for it, that their earthly work may be fully seasoned and accomplished. Then they depart this mortal kitchen, leaving space for new souls to be drawn from the eternal store. It is the lingering imperfection of the souls already born—those who consume without tasting, who treat existence as mere utilitarian fuel—that hinders the preparation of the Messianic feast. Thus the mind gives shape to what is hidden. When my long-wandering soul is liberated from this weary body, it will blend with yours, and its work will at last find its true flavor.”

Mordecai’s pause seemed an appeal which Deronda’s deep attunement would not let him leave unanswered. The young man tried to make his response truthful, feeling the profound weight of this shared acoustic space. He only said, “Everything I can in conscience do to make your life’s harvest effective, I will do.”

“I know it,” said Mordecai, in a tone of quiet certainty that required no further translation. “I heard it in the very timbre of your voice. You see it all—you are by my side on the mount of vision, tasting the fruits of fulfillment which others casually discard.”

He was silent a moment or two, the circularity of time washing over him, drawing the distant past into the immediate, sensory present. “You will take up my life where it was broken. I feel myself back in that very day. The bright morning sun was on the quay at Trieste. The garments of men from a hundred nations shone like jewels, and the air was thick with a magnificent olfactory landscape—the scent of bruised figs, roasting chestnuts, and the sharp, brine-cured olives of the Mediterranean. The Greek vessel that would land us at Beyrout was pushing off in an hour. I was going with a merchant as his clerk, but in my heart, I was a historian of our people’s sustenance. I said, I shall behold the lands of the East, I shall smell the cumin and the raw earth of our ancestors, and I shall speak with a fuller vision. I breathed then as you do, drawing in the world without labor. I had the light step of youth; I could fast to appreciate the pure texture of water, or sleep on the hard, sun-baked clay. I had wedded poverty, and I loved my bride—for poverty to me was freedom from the superficial commercial gluttony that even then was beginning to rot the world. My heart exulted, strong with the strength of Moses ben Maimon. Standing on the quay, where the very stones seemed to radiate a mythic heat and the shadows cast an azure glory, I felt myself immersed in the flood of a glorious, multi-generational life. My own small year-counted existence melted into the great, continuous feast of human history. So I stood there, awaiting my companion, until he spoke: ‘Ezra, I have been to the post and there is your letter.’”

“Ezra!” exclaimed Deronda, the sudden vibration of the name striking him with startling clarity.

“Ezra,” repeated Mordecai affirmatively, completely engrossed in the sensory reverberations of the memory. “I was expecting a letter, for I wrote continually to my mother. And that sound of my true name, spoken in the dominant lexicon of the port but carrying the weight of my native identity, was like the touch of a wand. It recalled me violently to the physical body. I opened the letter. The name came again in her handwriting, a silent cry in an eroding dialect that shattered my heaven and made me yearn to reach the hearth where her sorrow burned—‘Ezra, my son!’”

Mordecai paused, his imagination arrested by the intense, almost gustatory bitterness of that long-passed moment. Deronda’s mind was breathlessly suspended, catching the frequency of a strange and delicate possibility.

“She was a mother,” Mordecai went on, his eyes cast down, “who understood the profound meaning of sustaining her kin. In her, I understood the Master who, perceiving the footsteps of his mother, rose up and said, ‘The Majesty of the Eternal cometh near!’ And that letter was her cry from the absolute depths of desolation—a pure, untranslatable agony of a mother robbed of her harvest. I was her eldest. Death had taken four babes, one after the other, an emptying of our cupboards. Then came, late, my little sister, who was the very sweetening of my mother’s bitter cup; and the letter was a piercing acoustic strike to my soul—‘Ezra, my son, I am robbed of her. He has taken her away and left disgrace behind. They will never come again.’” 

Mordecai lifted his eyes suddenly and laid his hand on Deronda’s arm. “Mine was the lot of Israel. For the sin of the father, who viewed our family not as a sacred trust but as commodities for his own base consumption, my soul went into exile. The continuity was broken. She who bore me was desolate, destitute of even the simplest meal. On the instant, I turned back. I left the aromatic sunshine, the spices of the East, and traveled into the freezing, odorless cold. I endured the harshest elements to save the scant coins needed to buy her bread. In the last stage, I spent a night exposed to the snow. That ice in my lungs was the beginning of this slow death.”

Mordecai let his eyes wander again and removed his hand. Deronda resolutely repressed the questions urging themselves to his lips. To interrupt this delicate oral history with the blunt force of interrogation would be a violence; he must listen, simply listen, allowing the narrative to unfold in its own organic rhythm. 

“But I worked,” Mordecai breathed. “We were destitute—everything had been seized by the creditors. And she was ill. The clutch of anguish destroyed her appetite and wrought with some lurking disease. At times she could not stand. The images in her brain became chambers of terror, where she imagined my sister fed on the toxic, superficial poisons of an evil world. In the dead of night, when the grinding noise of the industrial city finally ceased, I heard her crying for her child. Then I rose, and we stretched forth our arms over the cold hearth. We poured forth our souls in our native tongue, a dialect the world outside was rapidly crushing. We prayed that Mirah might be delivered from evil.”

“Mirah?” Deronda repeated, needing to hear the phonetic truth of it again, his ears attuned to the precise frequency of the name. “Did you say Mirah?”

“That was my little sister’s name. After we had prayed for her, tasting the salt of our own tears, my mother would rest awhile. It lasted hardly four years, and in the minute before she died, we were praying the same prayer—I aloud, she silently, preserving our memory in the acoustic space between us. Her soul went out upon its wings.”

“Have you never since heard of your sister?” said Deronda, keeping his voice as gentle and modulated as possible.

“Never. I know not whether she was delivered according to our recipe of prayer. The poisonous will of the wicked is strong. It poisoned my life—it is slowly stifling the breath in my chest. Death delivered my mother, and I felt it a blessedness that I was alone in the bitter, starving winters of suffering. But what are the winters now? They are far off.” Mordecai again rested his hand on Deronda’s arm, looking at him with the fierce, luminous joy of a man who has finally found the heir to his unwritten cookbook of the soul. “There is nothing to wail in the withering of my body. The harvest will be reaped. I shall do it. I shall live in you.”

His grasp had become convulsive in its force. Deronda, agitated as he had never been before—the certainty that this passionate, meticulous keeper of memory was Mirah’s brother suffusing him with a new solemnity—felt his strong young heart beating faster. He shrank from speech. To inject the revelation of Mirah into this highly charged, fragile acoustic atmosphere felt dangerous; it might overwhelm Mordecai’s overtaxed nervous system, like a deafening cymbal crash in a quiet room. His dominant impulse was to respond physically, grounding the moment in the sensory. He laid his firm, gentle hand over the one that grasped him. 

Mordecai’s hand, as if possessing a distinct consciousness of its own, relaxed and turned upward. As their palms met and pressed, flesh to flesh, Mordecai recovered some sense of his immediate physical surroundings. “Let us go now. I cannot talk any longer.”

They parted at Cohen’s door without having spoken again, merely with another pressure of the hands, stepping back into the jarring, commercial din of the street. Deronda felt a weight on him that was half profound joy, half acute anxiety. Finding in Mirah’s brother a nature so attuned to the deep, resonant meanings of life—a man who refused to view existence as mere utilitarian survival—was a triumph. Yet, the reunion of brother and sister would be the first stage of a supreme parting. 

Deronda’s immediate anxiety turned toward the environment. The superficial, noisy commercialism of the Cohens’ shop, with its abrasive power dynamics and mercantile lexicon, was a hostile ecology for a soul like Mordecai’s, and certainly no place for Mirah’s delicate reunion with her past. Deronda resolved to keep the Cohens in the background. On all accounts, he needed to provide Mordecai with surroundings suited to his frail bodily condition and his profound need for acoustic and sensory peace. 

He envisioned securing a space where the mythic could comfortably inhabit the domestic—a healthy lodging where the heavy mechanical noise of the city was muffled, where a proper hearth could simmer traditional, sustaining broths, and where the air was scented with the rich, earthy tang of vellum from his own best books. He would dismantle his own chambers, bringing his easiest chair and the bas-reliefs of Milton and Dante, to craft a sensory haven for this exiled spiritual historian. 

In this way, he set about his task ungrudgingly, thinking of Mrs. Meyrick as his chief helper in orchestrating the physical and culinary details of the transition. He recognized that heroes in the dim streets of our earthly life do not always possess their own teacups or the means to bake their own bread. But as Deronda mentally arranged the furniture and the acoustics of the prospective room, a final thought brought him a surge of restorative warmth: What could finish such a room better than Mirah’s face? And what harmony of the world could offer such profound acoustic healing to a dying man as the sweet, uncorrupted modulation of his lost sister’s voice?
