The letters of flame scrawled across the church wall had asked, with an earnest and curious fervor, why no mass was being held for the Duke amidst this time of trouble. Many had witnessed this supernatural protestation before it slowly faded, leaving the crowd staring in stunned silence. Ultimately, the city council, unwilling to lag behind divine power in their loyalty to a temporal ruler, generously funded the required services. From this transaction, our old friend the Curé derived a handsome sum in hard coin—a fact he certainly appreciated given his discerning palate for wealth. Yet, I shall not detain you further with this trivial miracle.

Denys pleaded for his weapons. "Do not dishonor him as you break his heart?"

"Swear then, upon the cross of thy sword, to remain in the service of the bastard until the sedition is crushed." Yielding to necessity, he swore three solemn vows, reclaiming both his arms and his liberty.

The troops paused at "The Three Fish," where Marion, spotting him, exclaimed, "I am ruined! Who would have thought to see you again?" Seeing his somber countenance and noting that he appeared more pained than amused by her bluntness, she inquired what troubled him. He confided in her, and she adopted a bright, optimistic outlook. Gerard, she reasoned, was too handsome and well-mannered to suffer harm; moreover, the women would always support him. Things must eventually resolve themselves; if fortune smiled, he would join the companies bound for Rome, and if ill, he would return home, perhaps arriving before his friend, for he had fighting to do in Flanders by all accounts. She produced her gold pieces, steadfastly refusing to accept a single coin despite his repeated urging. Denys found her honesty refreshing, convincing him slightly of the honor of that world where his poor friend wandered alone.

Small detachments of foot soldiers, reduced to pairs or trios, were already straggling toward Flanders, many penniless, passing from town to town under the strict orders of bailiffs demanding food and lodging from innkeepers. Anthony of Burgundy overtook numerous stragglers, gathering them beneath his banner until he marched into Flanders at the head of six hundred men. Upon crossing the frontier, he was joined by his brother Baldwyn, bringing men, arms, and provisions. Anthony organized his forces and marched through several towns in full battle array, encountering not impediments but great acclamations. Such unbridled loyalty drew comments that were hardly gracious.

"This rebellion of ours is a joke," growled a soldier named Simon, who had chosen to be Denys's companion.

Denys remained silent, yet he made a private vow to Saint Mars to slay Anthony of Burgundy if the rebellion, having already cost him Gerard, proved to be anything less than genuine.

That afternoon, the vanguard spotted a heavily fortified town. A whisper rippled through the small army identifying it as a disaffected stronghold. However, when they drew near, the great gate stood wide open, and the flanking towers were guarded by but a single sentinel apiece. The advancing force, lulled by carelessness, broke formation. Within a furlong, however, the drawbridge across the moat rose slowly and groaning until it stood vertical against the fort. The precise moment it settled into this warlike posture, the portcullis crashed down, and the towers and curtains bristled with lances and crossbows.

A grim hum swept through the bastard's front rank and echoed to the rear.

"Halt!" cried Denys. The command traveled down the line, and they stopped. "Herald to the gate!" A pursuivant spurred forward, halted twenty yards from the entrance, raised his bugle, and blew a summons. A towering figure in brazen armor emerged over the gate. Fiery words, inaudible yet clearly understood, passed between him and the herald. The herald responded with a single, piercing, threatening note, galloped back with war in his eyes, and returned. The bastard stepped out to meet him, and within two seconds, he wheeled in his saddle and shouted, "Pioneers, to the van!" Instantly, hedges were leveled, the force cleared the field, and encamped just outside the range of the walls. Mounted officers scattered south, east, and west toward friendly towns, gathering catapults, palisades, mantelets, raw hides, tar barrels, carpenters, provisions, and every material necessary for a siege.

This bright prospect greatly cheered a despondent soldier. At the first clanging of the portcullis, his eyes lit up and his temples flushed. When the herald returned with battle in his gaze, the soldier realized it instantly. For the first time in many days, he cried, "Courage, everyone, the devil is dead." If the great warrior heard, he surely would have grinned.

The besiegers encamped a furlong from the walls and laid out roads. They kept their pikemen in camp, ready for assault, while sending forward their sappers, pioneers, catapultiers, and crossbowmen. These engineers opened the siege by filling the moat, mining, and breaching the walls. Since much of this labor had to be performed under heavy fire from arrows, quarrels, bolts, stones, and rocks, these artisans required a hundred eyes. They acted in concert with a vigilance, individual intelligence, daring, and skill that made a siege not merely a military endeavor, but a spectacle of interest, if not amusement, for observers.

Their first task was to advance their carpenters behind rolling mantelets to erect a high, strong stockade on the very edge of the moat. Some lives were lost, but not many; a strong contingent of crossbowmen, including Denys, rolled their mantelets forward and showered the workers with arrows whenever a besieged man showed his nose or exposed a loophole commanding their position. Protected by this condensed fire, they soon raised a high palisade shielding them from missiles fired from the pierced masonry.

Yet the besieged anticipated this stratagem. At night, they extended boards or wooden penthouses atop their curtains. These curtains featured square holes near the summit designed to receive supporting beams—the true defense of medieval forts. From these positions, the defenders delivered missiles with far greater freedom and variety of range than the besiegers could achieve through the oblique, immovable loopholes of the curtain or the sloping crenelets of the higher towers. In response, the besiegers brought up mangonels, hurling huge stones at these woodworks and battering them to ruins. Simultaneously, they constructed a triangular wooden tower as high as the curtain, keeping it ready for use just out of effective shot.

To the besieged, this was a terrifying sight. Wooden towers had captured many towns. They began mining beneath the section of the moat where the tower loomed, making other preparations for a fierce reception. The besiegers also mined at another point, aiming to undermine the square barbican and bring it crashing down. Throughout this operation, Denys remained behind his mantelet alongside another arbalestrier, protecting the workmen and delivering excellent shots. Eventually, these deeds earned him the esteem of an unseen archer who periodically sent a winged compliment quivering into his mantelet. One arrow struck within an inch of the narrow slit through which Denys was currently squinting.

"Peste!" cried Denys. "You shoot well, my friend. Come forth and receive my congratulations! Shall merit such as thine hide its head? Comrade, it is one of those cursed Englishmen, wielding his half-ell shaft. I will not die until I have had a shot at London Wall."

On the side of the besieged, a figure quickly attracted great attention by promenading under fire. It was a tall knight, clad in complete brass, carrying a light but prodigiously long lance, with which he directed the movements of the defenders. Whenever disaster befell the besiegers, this tall knight and his long lance were almost certain to be implicated.

"Young reader," you might ask, "why did not Denys shoot him?" Denys did shoot him; every day of his life, other arbalestriers shot him, archers shot him. Everyone shot him. He was there to be shot, apparently. The abomination was that he did not mind being shot. Worse still, he became so demoralized by the constant barrage that he seemed unaware when he was hit. He paced his battlements under fire, like a sturdy skipper walking his deck in a suit of Flushing, calmly oblivious to the April rains falling upon his woollen armor. Finally, the besiegers grew spiteful and refused to waste any more good steel on him, cursing him and his impenetrable coat of mail. He took those missiles like the rest.

Gunpowder has since spoiled war. War has always been detrimental to the solid interests of mankind, but in ancient times, it painted well, sang divinely, and furnished Iliads. Yet, invisible butchery under a pall of smoke a furlong thick leaves no one the better for it. A poet with his notebook may repeat, "Suave etiam belli certamina magna tueri"; but the sentiment is hollow and savors of a cuckoo. One cannot "view" anything but a horrid row. He did not say, "Suave etiam ingentem caliginem tueri per campos instructam."

The Middle Ages managed better.

This siege was a small affair, yet a writer or minstrel could witness it and earn an honest penny by singing it; thus, so far, the sport was reasonable and served an end.
