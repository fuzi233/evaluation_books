The following day, Romola joined the general excitement sweeping through Florence regarding the imminent departure of the French forces. Beyond the shared civic relief, she harbored a vague, almost superstitious hope that the specific anxieties plaguing her regarding Tito—burdens that had arrived alongside the foreign guests—might dissipate with them. Though the French had occupied the city for barely eleven days, Romola felt a depth of acute unhappiness in that span she had never previously known. Since their arrival, Tito had donned a kind of hateful armor; while she could not logically discern why their exit should cure his fear, the terrifying image of a prisoner seizing him, as depicted in Piero's sketch, haunted her thoughts whenever she considered the cause. Yet, simply by their absence, she hoped to shed something deeply intertwined with her own suffering.

Wrapped in her mantle, she stood beneath the upper loggia, watching for glimpses of the troops and the royal entourage crossing the bridges toward the Porta San Piero, the gate facing Siena and Rome. Even after the gates were sealed, she lingered, eager to feel the building vibrate with the great tolling of the bells signaling the evacuation. Dusk had fallen by the time she descended into the library. Lighting her lamp, she resolved to quell the agitation that had rendered her idle all day and return to her work of copying the catalogue. Tito had departed early, and she did not anticipate his return immediately. Once he arrived, she planned to retreat to the charming salon adorned with dancing nymphs and birds—a refuge she had claimed every evening since he deemed the library too chilly and gloomy.

To her astonishment, she had not worked long before Tito entered. Her immediate thought was of how desolate he must feel in the vast darkness of the room, illuminated only by a solitary oil lamp at the far end, with the fire nearly extinguished. She rushed toward him. "Tito, dearest, I did not know you would return so soon," she said, her voice tinged with nervousness as she raised her white arms to undo his *becchetto*.

"Am I not welcome?" he replied, offering one of his brightest smiles. He embraced her but playfully held his head back, preventing her from kissing him immediately.

"Tito!" she exclaimed, the word vibrating with affectionate reproach. He responded with a fond kiss, stroking her hair in his customary manner, seemingly unconcerned with removing his cloak. Romola quivered with delight. All the turmoil of the day had sharpened her sensitivity to the return of this familiar tenderness. *"It will come back,"* she whispered to herself. *"The old happiness will perhaps return. He is like himself again."*

In truth, Tito was exerting immense effort to appear as his former self; his heart hammered with anxiety. "If I had expected you so soon," Romola said, finally helping him remove his wrappings, "I would have prepared a small celebration for this joyous ringing of the bells. I did not intend to remain in the library upon your return."

"Never mind, sweet," he dismissed casually. "Forget the fire. Come, sit down."

A low stool stood beside his chair, Romola's usual perch during their conversations. Resting her arm on his knee as she once did on her father's, she gazed upward at him. He had never yet noticed the portrait looming nearby, nor had she brought it up, contemplating it with growing intensity.

"I have enjoyed the sound of the bells for the first time, Tito," she began. "I loved being shaken and deafened by them; I fancied myself a Bacchante possessed by divine rage. Do the people seem joyful tonight?"

"Joyful in a sour, pious fashion," Tito shrugged. "Truth be told, those left behind in Florence have little cause for joy. The most logical grounds for gladness would be to have fled the city entirely."

He struck the desired note effortlessly, without apparent premeditation. Speaking with no emphasis, yet maintaining a gravity that prompted anxious concern from Romola, he continued, "Why, Tito? Are there fresh troubles?"

"No need for new ones, my Romola. Three powerful factions exist in the city, all poised to tear each other apart. Should the Frate's party prove strong enough to silence the others—as seems likely—life will be as pleasant and amusing as a funeral. They are already simmering with plans for a Great Council; if established, the man who sings sacred Lauds the loudest will be the most eligible for office. Furthermore, the city will be drained by the massive subsidy paid to the French King and the war to reclaim Pisa. Without the rule of fanatics, the prospect remains dismal. On balance, Florence will be a delightful haunt for those who spend their evenings in crypts lashing themselves, but for everyone else, the exiles fare better. Personally, I have given serious consideration to leaving Florence, my Romola."

She recoiled. "Tito, how could we leave? Surely you do not think I could abandon it—at least, not yet, not for a long while."

Her voice grew cold and trembling; finding speech difficult, she sensed he must know the depths of her attachment. "That is merely a fabrication of your own imagination, my sweet one. Your secluded existence has led you to place undue weight on trivial matters. Remember, before our marriage, I expressed a wish to reside elsewhere than in Florence. Had you traveled more, seen more people, you would understand what I mean when I say the Florentines remind me of their cutting spring winds. I prefer those who approach life less eagerly. It would benefit your Romola to witness a new life, to dip her feet in the soft waters of forgetfulness."

He leaned forward, kissing her brow and resting his hand on her hair, yet she felt his caress no more than if he had touched a mask. Agitated by the chasm widening between their minds, she remained unaware of his physical contact.

"Tito, it is not because I imagine Florence to be the most pleasant place in the world that I refuse to depart. It is because—and because—we must fulfill my father's wish. My godfather is elderly, seventy-one years old; we cannot leave him alone."

"It is precisely those superstitions clinging to your mind like dimming clouds that constitute a major reason why I wish we were two hundred leagues distant. I am compelled to protect you against your own will. If those dear eyes, so tender in their gaze, perceive falsely, I must see for them and save my wife from wasting her life on impracticable dreams."

Romola sat silent and motionless. She could not ignore the trajectory of his words: he sought to convince her that the library could be deposited in a monastery or otherwise disposed of to sever their ties to Florence. Determined never to submit her mind to his judgment on this matter of filial duty, she prepared internally to endure whatever pain resistance required. However, this resolve remained latent in these initial moments, crushed by the heart-rending realization that she and Tito were now openly divided in their desires.

He welcomed her silence. Despite fearing the strength of her feelings, it was impossible for him, confined within the narrowness characteristic of clever but unimpassioned men, not to overestimate the persuasiveness of his own logic. His actions did not appear sinister to himself, nor did his imagination allow him to foresee exactly how they appeared to Romola. Continuing in a gentle, admonishing tone, he said, "You know, dearest—you whose clear judgment has always been evident—that the idea of isolating a collection of books and antiquities, attaching a single name to them forever, offers no valid, substantial good for its object. Moreover, it is liable to defeat in a thousand ways. Consider the fate of the Medici collections! For my part, I deem it even blameworthy to entertain such petty notions of appropriation. Why should anyone be reasonably glad that Florence possesses the benefits of learned research and taste more than any other city? I comprehend your sentiment regarding the wishes of the dead, but wisdom imposes limits on such sentiments, lest lives be wasted in futile devotion—much like praising deaf gods eternally. You dedicated your life to your father while he lived; why demand more of yourself now?"

"Because it was a trust," Romola replied, her voice low but distinct. "He trusted me, he trusted you, Tito. I did not expect you to feel differently, but I did expect you to recognize that."

"Yes, dearest, naturally I would feel so concerning your father's actual welfare or happiness. But there is no question of that now. If we believed in purgatory, I would be as anxious as you to ensure masses were said; and if I believed it could pain your father to see his library preserved and utilized in a manner contrary to his intent, I would share your strict views. However, a little philosophy should teach us to free ourselves from these air-woven fetters that mortals bind around themselves, spending their lives in misery under the mere imagination of weight. Your mind, which seizes ideas so readily, my Romola, is capable of distinguishing between substantial good and these brain-wrought fantasies. Ask yourself, dearest, what possible good these books and antiquities offer, stored together under your father's name in Florence, compared to if they were dispersed or carried elsewhere? Indeed, is not the very dispersion of such treasures among hands that know how to value them a means of extending their utility? This rivalry among Italian cities is petty and illiberal. The loss of Constantinople was a gain for the entire civilized world."
