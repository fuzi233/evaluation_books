The sermon was already afoot when Margaret crossed the threshold of Saint Laurens.

The great church was more promise than completion: side aisles roofed and cool as cellars, but the broad middle still open to the sky, the chancel raw, the very ribs of arches standing up like bones with here and there a smear of whitewash. Only one window wore glass; the rest were jagged mouths bitten out of the walls. Yet on that summer afternoon the unfinished work had a kind of homely grandeur, as if the building, like a loaf not yet cooled, still gave off its own warmth.

Sunlight came in not as a single clean beam, but broken and sifted through those rough openings, falling in slanted sheets that turned dust into gold. It split the dim aisles with bright rivers, flashed on the pale pillars, and laid the central space with a patchwork of light and shadow—cool shade like water in a stone trough, hot light like amber held up to a flame. Overhead, from west to east, the blue sky vaulted the nave so near it seemed you could almost hear it, an airy bowl set above the crowd. Beneath, the women’s white caps spread like a field of linen in harvest-time; and though the mid-aisle was crammed, the multitude kept still, held as if by a hand upon the shoulder.

It was not the sight alone that stilled them. The preacher’s voice—mellow, gentle, earnest—moved through the half-built space and made it whole. In that mingled hush, Margaret became aware of every thread in the church’s sound: the soft breath of many bodies, the faint creak of timber somewhere aloft, a far pigeon’s coo swallowed by stone. The voice rode above it all like a steady chant above a hearth-fire, soothing without asking leave.

She slipped behind a pillar in the north aisle. There she could hardly seize a word, yet the cadence itself was enough; a devotional languor stole over her, as if warm oil were poured on the troubled water in her heart and smoothed it. Her eyelids drooped; her cheek leaned to the cool lime of the pillar; all things grew soft and dreamlike. It felt good—simply good—to be there where sound was ordered and gentle, not jangled and shouted as in streets and markets.

Presently she saw a lady forsake an excellent place opposite, fleeing the sun that poured straight upon her cap from the one glazed window. Margaret moved, softly but swiftly, and took the place. Now she stood by a pillar of the south aisle, no more than fifty feet from the pulpit, a little behind the preacher’s flank. Here the words came clear.

And then her attention was snared by something absurdly lively upon the wall: the shadow of a man’s head and shoulders bobbing up and down with such busy persistence that she had ado not to smile. It was no buffoonery; it was labour. She looked behind her, through the window, and saw Jorian Ketel the sexton digging in the churchyard, lifting and driving his spade with the patient rhythm of one who knows the weight of earth. Each thrust made its own dull thud, muffled by stone and sermon, like a potter’s hand tamping clay.

As she watched that honest motion, a certain tone in the preacher’s voice dropped upon her ear—and her very body answered it. It struck her so distinctly she felt it, as one feels the sudden sharpness of salt on the tongue or the first bite of hot spice; her hand flew to her bosom at the shock of the thrill.

She turned at once and looked toward the pulpit.

But his back was to her; nothing of him visible but the tonsure, pale as a shorn field. She sighed. The bare crown contradicted the voice in a way she could not name. Yet she leaned forward a little, eyes downcast, unwilling to lose that accent that had touched some hidden place in her like a remembered lullaby.

It did not come again—not in that same single unmistakable note. Still the voice grew strangely upon her. As the friar warmed, it rose and fell; it gathered and flowed; and in its turns it woke faint echoes of a thousand small memories—days when the world had seemed secure, and evenings when familiar speech had held its own flavour on the air. She would not look up, fearing to break the spell, as one might spoil a broth by lifting the lid too often, letting all its goodness fly.

Then, in the midst of an eloquent period, the preacher stopped.

The sudden absence of sound was like a snapped string. Margaret almost sighed aloud, as if music had been cut off mid-note. Was the sermon ended? She glanced about: no one stirred. But faces began to turn—many faces—toward her. She felt their attention like heat upon skin.

She looked sharply behind her. There was nothing—only the pillar, only the crowded stillness.

Startled countenances near her fixed on the pulpit. She followed their gaze; and there, above, was a face like a staring corpse. The friar’s eyes—naturally large and made larger by the hollows of his cheeks—stood out dilated beyond nature, glaring her way from a bloodless visage.

Margaret shrank and whipped her head about in terror, persuaded there must be some dreadful thing beside or behind her that drew such a look. There was nothing. She was but the outermost figure of the listening crowd, with bare stone at her back.

At once the church broke into commotion. People rose all over the building and craned forward. Hundreds of eyes flashed from the friar to Margaret, from Margaret back to the friar. The turning to and fro of so many white caps made a broad rustle, like wind worrying a field of dry grain. Then nervous women shrieked; men began to buzz; the ordered soundscape—the very calm that had been like balm—was torn and ragged in an instant.

Seeing herself so marked, Margaret slid behind the pillar, terrified, and stole one hurried glance at the preacher. Brief as it was, it caught something in that stricken face that made her shiver.

She went faint. She sank upon a heap of chips the workmen had left, and hid her face in her hands.

The sermon began again. She heard the sound of it, the lift and fall, but the meaning would not enter. Her thoughts whirled like flour in a draught; nothing would settle. Only one thing fixed itself, hard and sharp: that she had seen, stamped upon that prodigy-struck face, a look whose name she could not escape.

Recognition.

The friar’s discourse, after wavering, ended in a strain of exalted—almost feverish—eloquence, that carried the crowd with it and blurred, for many, the memory of that ghastly pause and stare. When at last they poured out, she mingled hastily with them, keeping her head down, and passed through the door into the common daylight.

Most went homeward in groups, their talk already turning to supper and the day’s close. Margaret did not follow. At the threshold she turned aside into the churchyard, where the air was warmer, and the earth smell rose clean and plain, as it does where spades have been at work.

She went to Peter’s grave.

Poor as she was, she had given him a slab and a headstone. She sat down upon the slab and kissed it, the stone tasting of sun and grit. Then she drew her apron over her head so none might know her by her hair.

“Father,” she whispered, “thou hast often heard me say I am wading in deep waters; but now I begin to think God only knows the bottom of them. I’ll follow that friar round the world, but I’ll see him at arm’s length. And he shall tell me why he looked toward me like a dead man wakened—and not a soul behind me. Oh, father; you often praised me here: speak a word for me there. For I am wading in deep waters.”

Her father’s tomb commanded a side view of the church door. Upon that tomb she sat, face hidden, listening to the yard’s small honest noises—the distant tap of tool on soil, a bird’s quick note, the low murmur of dispersing feet—lying in wait for the holy preacher.
