When Fra Luca fell silent, Tito remained standing beside him, paralyzed by indecision. It was only after the crowd of pilgrims had thinned and the friar rose to walk slowly toward the church of Santa Felicita that Tito followed, turning his steps down the Via de' Bardi.

"If this monk is truly Florentine," he murmured to himself, "and if he intends to stay in Florence, then everything must be revealed." He sensed a new crisis had arrived, yet he was not so visibly shaken that he could not proceed with his scheduled visit to Bardo to apologize for his recent absence. Tito's gift for concealment was rapidly evolving from a passive trait into an active weapon. It was conceivable—perhaps inevitable—that he could now accept the changed circumstances openly and confess Baldassarre's existence. However, doing so would cast a harsh retroactive light on his initial silence, framing it as a calculated equivocation designed to evade a recognized claim. Furthermore, it would expose his quiet settlement of funds and investments, actions taken while his benefactor's fate remained unverified. For the moment, prudence dictated acting as if nothing had occurred. He would suspend decisive thought; the night offered ample time for meditation, and no one would know the exact hour he received the letter.

He ascended to the second-floor room where Romola and her father sat amidst parchment and marble, isolated from the street's holiday bustle just as they were on ordinary days. Tito entered with a countenance only slightly brighter than usual, masking a regret at his lateness—a regret requiring no proof, since he had sacrificed the spectacle of the Corso to express it. He then endeavored to inject extra animation into the evening conversation, though his consciousness operated like a complex machine, depositing thoughts entirely distinct from the flow of speech. By the time he descended the stone stairs and emerged from the grim door into the starlight, his mind had genuinely reached a new stage in forming a purpose.

The following day, once freed from his professorial duties, he turned up the Via del Cocomero toward the convent of San Marco, his purpose now fully crystallized. He intended to ascertain from Fra Luca precisely what he suspected of the truth and on what grounds; furthermore, he needed to know how long the friar planned to remain at San Marco. With this fuller knowledge, he hoped to craft a statement that would spare him the necessity of leaving Florence. Tito had never before had occasion to fabricate an ingenious lie, yet the opportunity had arrived—the specific circumstance that always breeds tacit falsehood. His ingenuity was ready. He had convinced himself that he was not bound to search for Baldassarre. He had once declared that upon receiving a fair assurance of his father's existence and whereabouts, he would unhesitatingly pursue him. But why, after all, was he bound to go? What, upon close inspection, was the ultimate end of life but to extract the maximum sum of pleasure? Was not his own blooming existence a promise of incomparably greater joy—not merely for himself, but for others—than the withered, wintry life of a man past the age of keen enjoyment, whose ideas had stiffened into barren rigidity? Those ideas had been sown in the fresh soil of Tito's mind; they were lively germs there. That was the proper order of things—the order of nature, which treats maturity as merely a nesting ground for youth. Baldassarre had done his work, had drawn his fill of life; Tito declared it was his turn now.

The prospect loomed vague yet enticing: "I think they are taking me to Antioch." Here was a vista! After a long voyage, to spend months, perhaps years, in a search for which there was no guarantee it would not prove vain, and to leave behind at the starting point a life of distinction and love. And if he found anything, he would encounter the old, exacting companionship known by rote. Certainly, the gems—and therefore the florins—were, in a narrow legal sense, Baldassarre's. But in that larger, more radically natural view where the world belongs to youth and strength, they belonged to whoever could extract the most pleasure from them. He was conscious that this was not the sentiment engendered by the complicated play of human feelings in society. The men around him would expect him to immediately apply those florins to his benefactor's rescue. But what was the sentiment of society? A mere tangle of anomalous traditions and opinions, which no wise man would take as a guide, except so far as his own comfort was concerned. Did he care for the florins save perhaps for Romola's sake? He would give them up readily enough. It was the joy due to him, hovering close to his lips, which he felt he was not bound to thrust away and thus travel on, thirsting. Any maxims requiring a man to fling away the good needed to make existence sweet were merely the lining of human selfishness turned outward; they were crafted by men who desired others to sacrifice themselves for their sake. He would rather that Baldassarre should not suffer; he liked no one to suffer. But could any philosophy prove to him that he was bound to care for another's suffering more than his own? To do so, he must have loved Baldassarre devotedly, and he did not. Was that his fault? Gratitude, seen closely, made no valid claim. His father's life would have been dreary without him; were we convicted of a debt to men for the pleasures they give themselves?

Once he began to explain away Baldassarre's claim, Tito's thought showed itself as active as a virulent acid, eating its rapid way through all the tissues of sentiment. His mind was destitute of that dread erroneously decried as nothing higher than a man's animal care for his own skin: the awe of Divine Nemesis felt by religious pagans, and, though taking a more positive form under Christianity, still felt by the mass of mankind simply as a vague fear of anything called wrongdoing. Such terror of the unseen was so far above mere sensual cowardice that it would annihilate it; it was the initial recognition of a moral law restraining desire, checking the hard, bold scrutiny of imperfect thought into obligations that could never be proved to possess sanctity in the absence of feeling. "It is good," sang the old Eumenides in Aeschylus, "that fear should sit as the guardian of the soul, forcing it into wisdom—it is good that men should carry a threatening shadow in their hearts under the full sunshine; else, how should they learn to revere the right?" That guardianship might become needless, but only when all outward law has become needless—only when duty and love have united in one stream to make a common force.

As Tito entered the outer cloister of San Marco and inquired for Fra Luca, there was no shadowy presentiment in his mind; he felt himself too cultured and skeptical for such fears. He had been nurtured in contempt for the tales of priests whose impudent lives were proverbial, and in erudite familiarity with disputes concerning the Chief Good, which he considered ultimately a matter of taste. Yet fear was a strong element in Tito's nature—the fear of what he believed or saw was likely to rob him of pleasure. He harbored a definite fear that Fra Luca might be the means of driving him from Florence.

"Fra Luca? Ah, he has gone to Fiesole—to the Dominican monastery there. He was taken on a litter in the cool of the morning. The poor Brother is very ill. Could you leave a message for him?"

This answer came from a *fra converso*, or lay brother, whose accent plainly marked him as a raw countryman, and whose dull glance implied no curiosity.

"Thanks; my business can wait."

Tito turned away with a sense of relief. "This friar is not likely to live," he said to himself. "I saw he was worn to a shadow. And at Fiesole there will be nothing to recall me to his mind. Besides, if he should return, my explanation will serve as well then as now. But I wish I knew what it was about his face that recalled to me."
