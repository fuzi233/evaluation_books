"On earth is known to none / The smile that is not sister to a tear." — Elliott.

Margaret and her father walked home together. The night was fine, the streets immaculate, and clad in her pretty white silk dress, kilted up to her knee like the gown of Leezie Lindsay in the ballad, she moved forward with her father, eager to dance along with the crisp, refreshing air.

"I rather think Thornton is not entirely at ease regarding this strike," Margaret observed. "He seemed very anxious tonight."

"I should not be surprised," Mr. Hale replied. "Yet he spoke with his customary composure to the others when they proposed various solutions just before we departed."

"So he did after dinner as well. It takes a great deal to disturb his cool demeanor; however, his face betrays anxiety."

"I would be anxious in his place," Mr. Hale admitted. "He must perceive the growing fury and barely suppressed hatred within his workforce, who view him as the 'hard man' described in Scripture—not necessarily unjust, but unfeeling. He stands firm on his rights with a clarity that seems unnatural given our own petty claims in the sight of the Almighty. I am glad you noticed his worry. Recalling Boucher's half-mad words and actions makes me unable to conceive how calmly Mr. Thornton could have spoken."

"In the first instance, I am not as convinced as you are of Mr. Boucher's total despair. In that moment, he was undoubtedly distressed. However, there is often a mysterious reservoir of funds within these Unions. Furthermore, based on what you said, it appears Mr. Boucher is of a passionate, demonstrative nature, giving free rein to every emotion he feels."

"Oh, Papa!"

"Well! My only wish is that you render justice to Mr. Thornton, whom I suspect possesses a nature diametrically opposed to Mr. Boucher's—a man far too proud to display his feelings. That is precisely the character I expected you to admire, Margaret."

"So I do—I should; yet I do not feel entirely certain of the existence of those hidden emotions. He is a man of immense strength of character and unusual intellect, considering the limited advantages he has enjoyed."

"Not so few," Mr. Hale corrected gently. "He has lived a practical life from a very young age, constantly called upon to exercise judgment and self-control. Such experiences develop a specific facet of the intellect. Granted, he lacks some historical knowledge that provides the truest foundation for predicting the future; but he recognizes this deficiency, and that awareness is significant. You are becoming somewhat prejudiced against Mr. Thornton, Margaret."

"He is the first manufacturer—the first person engaged in trade—I have ever had the opportunity to study closely, Papa. He is my first olive; let me make a face while I swallow it. I know he is good-hearted, and eventually, I shall like the kind. I think I am already beginning to do so. I was deeply interested by the conversation among the gentlemen earlier, though I understood only fragments of it. I felt quite uncomfortable when Miss Thornton came to escort me to the other end of the room, assuring me I would be uneasy being the sole lady among so many men. I had never considered it until then, so engrossed was I in listening; besides, the ladies were so dull, Papa—oh, so dull! Yet, I suppose it was clever. It reminded me of our old game where each of us had to incorporate a set number of nouns into a sentence."

"What do you mean, child?" Mr. Hale asked.

"Why, they selected nouns signifying objects indicative of wealth—housekeepers, under-gardeners, the extent of glass, valuable lace, diamonds, and similar items—and each fashioned her speech to include them in the most pleasing, seemingly accidental manner possible."

"You will be just as proud of your single servant once you acquire her, if everything Mrs. Thornton says about her proves true."

"To be sure, I shall. I felt like a profound hypocrite tonight, sitting there in my white silk gown with idle hands, remembering all the diligent, thorough housework performed today. They treated me as a fine lady, I'm sure."

"Even I was foolish enough to think you looked like a lady, my dear," Mr. Hale said, smiling quietly.

However, smiles vanished instantly, replaced by pale, trembling expressions when they beheld Dixon's face as she opened the door.

"Oh, Master!—Oh, Miss Margaret! Thank God you are here! Dr. Donaldson is here. The servant next door fetched him because the charwoman has gone home. She is better now; but, oh, Sir! I thought she would have died an hour ago!"

Mr. Hale seized Margaret's arm to steady himself from collapsing. He gazed at her face, noting an expression of surprise and extreme sorrow, yet lacking the paralyzing agony of terror that constricted his own unprepared heart. She knew more than he did, yet she listened with a hopeless look of awed apprehension.

"Oh! I should not have left her—wicked daughter that I am!" Margaret moaned, supporting her trembling father's hurried steps upstairs.

Dr. Donaldson awaited them on the landing. "She is improving now," he whispered. "The opiate has taken effect. The spasms were severe; no wonder they frightened your maid, but she will rally this time."

"This time!" Mr. Hale cried. "Let me go to her!"

Half an hour prior, Mr. Hale had been a middle-aged man; now his vision blurred, his senses wavered, and his walk tottered as if he were seventy years old. Dr. Donaldson took his arm and guided him into the bedroom. Margaret followed closely.

There lay her mother, bearing an unmistakable look upon her face. Perhaps she was better now; she slept, but Death had claimed her signature, and it was evident he would soon return to take full possession. Mr. Hale stared at her for some time in silence. Then he began to tremble violently. Turning away from Dr. Donaldson's anxious ministrations, he groped for the door, unable to see it despite the several candles burning brightly in the sudden panic. He staggered into the drawing-room and searched for a chair. Dr. Donaldson wheeled one toward him and seated him.

He felt his pulse. "Speak to him, Miss Hale. We must rouse him."

"Papa!" Margaret cried, her voice wild with pain. "Papa! Speak to me!"

Speculation returned to his eyes, and he made a tremendous effort. "Margaret, did you know of this? Oh, it was cruel of you!"

"No, Sir, it was not cruel!" Dr. Donaldson replied with swift decisiveness. "Miss Hale acted under my instructions. There may have been a mistake, but it was not cruel. Your wife will be a different creature tomorrow, I trust. She suffered spasms, as I anticipated, though I did not inform Miss Hale of my fears. She has taken the opiate I brought; she will have a long, restorative sleep, and tomorrow that look which has alarmed you so greatly will have passed."

"But not the disease?"

Dr. Donaldson glanced at Margaret. Her bowed head and face raised in silent plea revealed that this keen observer of human nature believed the whole truth should be spoken.

"Not the disease. With all our vaunted skill, we cannot touch the disease. We can only delay its progress and alleviate the pain it causes. Be a man, Sir—a Christian. Have faith in the immortality of the soul, which no pain, no mortal disease, can assail or touch!"

But the only response he received was the choked words, "You have never been married, Dr. Donaldson; you don't know what it is," accompanied by deep, manly sobs that echoed through the stillness of the night like heavy pulses of agony.

Margaret knelt beside him, caressing him with tearful tenderness. No one, not even Dr. Donaldson, knew how the time passed. Mr. Hale was the first to dare address the necessities of the present moment.

"What must we do?" he asked. "Tell us both. Margaret is my staff—my right hand."

Dr. Donaldson offered his clear, sensible directions. There was no cause for fear tonight—nay, perhaps even peace for tomorrow and many days thereafter. Yet, there was no enduring hope of recovery. He advised Mr. Hale to retire to bed, leaving only one person to watch the slumber, which he hoped would remain undisturbed. He promised to return early in the morning. With a warm, kindly handshake, he departed.

They exchanged few words; exhaustion from their terror rendered them incapable of more than deciding on immediate action. Mr. Hale was resolved to stay awake through the night, and all Margaret could achieve was persuading him to rest on the drawing-room sofa. Dixon stoutly and bluntly refused to go to bed. As for Margaret, it was simply impossible for her to leave her mother, regardless of what doctors worldwide might say about "husbanding resources" or requiring "only one watcher."

Thus, Dixon sat, staring, blinking, drooping, and jerking herself upright again, finally surrendering the battle and falling into a fair snore. Margaret had removed her gown, tossing it aside with impatient disgust, and donned her dressing-gown. She felt as though she could never sleep again; her entire senses were acutely vital, endowed with double keenness solely for the purpose of vigilance. Every sight, sound—even every thought—touched some nerve to the very quick.
