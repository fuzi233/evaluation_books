All things pass—even the private martyrdoms of shy young men who have sentenced themselves to their own company and then complain of the cooking. When winter came, I took to work; there is no stew so thick as labour, and it will swallow a great deal of self-pity if you keep it on the boil. My “apartment,” with its grudging little fire and two candles that behaved as though wax were an expensive virtue, grew almost homelike. I bought paper, pens, and ink, as a man lays in flour, salt, and fat when he means to bake miracles. Great or small, what more can a writer do? He is the saucepan; whether any spirit consents to simmer inside him is another matter.

London—seasoned with a million voices, full of grave and gay, its romances and mysteries, its pathos and its humour—lay ready to my hand like a market that never closes. It asked only the right kind of listening and the right kind of noticing. Yet, in the manner of most beginners, I never thought to make my stories out of what I actually knew. I wrote instead of cottage maidens and bucolic yeomen; I laid my scenes in windmills, among mountains, or in moated granges—anything, in short, that did not require me to describe the smell of paraffin on a stair, or the particular creak a banister makes when it has been worried by five lodgers and a landlady with short breath.

A trail of gentle melancholy lay over my efforts like a dusting of icing sugar on a cake that has no cake beneath. Sentiment was more popular then than it is now, and I followed fashion as a cook follows a recipe when he has not yet dared to taste. Generally speaking, to be a heroine of mine was fatal. However cheerfully her hair might curl—curly hair, I still maintain, is the hallmark of vitality—and however many indications of robust health I crammed into chapter one (“dancing eyes,” “colour that came and went,” “ringing laughter,” “fawn-like agility”), she was tolerably certain, poor girl, to be served up cold by chapter ten. Snowdrops and early primroses (my botany I had whipped up out of a useful little book, “Our Garden Favourites, Illustrated”) sprouted everywhere as though forced under glass; and if the story happened to be near the coast, the sea-breezes would select that particular churchyard—irreverently—as their favourite playground. Years later a white-haired man would come leading little children by the hand, and he would tell them the tale all over again, which must have been a singularly gloomy form of nursery entertainment, like putting wormwood in the jam.

Now and then, for variety, I would kill the gentleman instead. It was of no consequence, so he himself declared in his last soliloquy; it was “better so.” He never got snowdrops; his grave, marked by a rude cross, lay, as a rule, in some exceptionally unfrequented portion of the African veldt or amid burning sands. For these endings I had to make a pilgrimage to the British Museum Reading Room, as one visits a spice-merchant when one has run out of all flavours at home.

Dismal little fledgelings! Again and again I shoved them out of the nest; again and again they fluttered back, soiled, crumpled, singed at the corners by my own despair. Yet one person had admired them, cried over them—myself. It is astonishing what tears a man can spare when he has no audience and no handkerchief.

I tried every method. Sometimes I sent them out with a curt business note of the take-it-or-leave-it order, as though I were delivering a joint to a customer who had already paid. At other times I attached pathetic appeals for consideration, like parsley over a plate that needs meat. Sometimes I gave them a price—five guineas—and requested the cheque should be crossed; at other times, in a burst of entrepreneurial genius, I offered my “first contribution” for nothing, the sample packet, sent gratis, one trial surely sufficient. Now I wrote sarcastically and enclosed, besides the stamped envelope for return, a brutally penned rejection note I had composed on the editor’s behalf—thus saving him time, an act for which he showed no gratitude. Or I wrote frankly, explaining that I was a beginner and asking to be told my faults—if any.

Not one found a resting place for its feet. A month, a week, a couple of days, they would remain away from me and then return, like dogs that have been kicked but still believe in the hearth. I never lost a single one. I wished I had. It would have varied the monotony.

I hated the poor little slavey who would burst into the room with the morning’s post, holding out my envelopes between apron-hidden thumb and finger as though they were soiled rags. Her chronic sniff I translated into contempt; and if, flying down the stairs at the postman’s knock, I snatched the letters from his hands, it seemed to me he smiled—as if he had heard the same song of rejection in ten thousand keys. Tearing open the envelopes, I would curse the manuscripts, abuse them, fling them into the fire; but before they were more than scorched I would rescue them, smooth them, reread them, like a cook pulling a fallen cutlet out of the ashes and insisting it is still “quite all right.” The editor himself could never have seen them; it was impossible. Some jealous underling had done this thing. I had sent them to the wrong paper. They had arrived at an inopportune moment. Their triumph would come. Rewriting the first and last sheets—my favourite sort of stirring—I would send them out again with fresh hope.

Meanwhile, believing that the would-be happy warrior must shine in camp as well as in the field, I sought to fit myself for the social side of life. Smoking and drinking were the twin sins I found most difficulty in acquiring. I do not claim mental superiority so much as confess bodily frailty. The spirit was always willing; the flesh, like underdone pastry, refused to rise to the occasion.

At school, fired by emulation, I had occasionally essayed a cigarette. The result had been distinctly unsatisfactory. After two or three attempts I abandoned further endeavour for the time being, excusing myself with a sanctimonious air that smoking was bad for growing boys; attempting to delude myself before sturdier contemporaries by striking a pose of moral disapproval; yet in my heart knowing myself a young hypocrite, dressing physical cowardice in the robes of virtue—a form of seasoning human nature is fond of sprinkling over its failures.

So, too, I had tasted the red wine that stood year in, year out, decanted on our sideboard, like a family relic no one dared admit was useless. The true inwardness of St. Paul’s prescription became plain to me; those who drink “under doctor’s orders,” regarding liquor purely as medicine, seemed reasonable. I had noticed grown men making wry faces over my mother’s claret and concluded that a taste for strong drink was an accomplishment less easily acquired than is generally supposed. The lack of it in a young man could be no disgrace—so I postponed effort, and called postponement prudence.

But now, a gentleman at large, my education could no longer be delayed. To the artist in particular training—severe training—was considered indispensable. Fashion has shifted a little since; but a quarter of a century ago a genius who did not smoke and drink (and more than was good for him) would have been dismissed without further evidence as an impostor. About the genius I was hopeful, but never certain. The smoking and drinking, at least, could be achieved by perseverance. I set to work methodically, conscientiously, as though learning a trade.

Smoking, experience taught me, was best practised on Saturday nights, Sunday offering the opportunity to walk off the consequences in the open air, under a sky that at least did not reek of other men’s breath. Patience and determination were crowned with success: I learned to smoke a cigarette to all appearance as though I were enjoying it. Lesser men might have stopped there—outward success, inner nausea; but attainment of the highest has always been with me a motive force. The cigarette conquered, I next attacked the cigar.

My first cigar I remember well; most men do, though some remember it with pride and others with pallor. It was at a smoking concert in the Islington Drill Hall, to which Minikin had invited me. Not feeling sure whether my growing dizziness was due solely to the cigar or in part to the hot, overcrowded room (air there was not, only a heated mixture of smoke and humanity), I made my excuses and slipped out. I found myself in a small courtyard, divided from a neighbouring garden by a low wall. The cause of my trouble was clearly the cigar. Inclination urged me to remove it from my mouth and see how far I could throw it—an experiment I felt would have a moral value. Conscience urged perseverance.

It occurred to me that if I climbed onto the wall and could walk along it from end to end, there would be no excuse for my not heeding the counsels of perfection. If, on the contrary, the wall proved not wide enough for my steps, then Providence itself would have pronounced against the cigar, and I might drop it without shame and make my way home to bed. I attained the wall with some difficulty and began my ordeal. Two yards further I found myself lying across it, my legs hanging down one side, my head overhanging the other—like a man trying to straddle two worlds, neither of which wants him. The position proving suitable to my requirements, I maintained it, and in that dumb pause the city’s noises came to me oddly sorted: a cart rumbling in the street like far-off thunder; a sudden burst of laughter from the hall; a dog barking at nothing in particular; and over all the constant London hum, as if the town itself were one vast kettle never quite taken off the boil.

Inclination seized its chance and urged me then and there to take a solemn vow never to smoke again. I am proud to write that through that hour of temptation I remained firm; strengthening myself by whispering: “Never despair. What others can do, so can you. Is not all victory won through suffering?” Thus did I cling to my cigar as though it were a moral principle, and to my wall as though it were a career.

A liking for drink I found, if possible, even more difficult to achieve. Spirits I almost despaired of. Once, confusing bottles, I drank some hair oil in mistake for whisky and found it decidedly less nauseous—an observation which I mention for the benefit of moralists who maintain that everything in life is arranged for our improvement. Twice a week I forced myself to swallow a glass of beer, standing over myself insisting on draining it to the bitter dregs. Afterwards, as reward and as antidote, I treated myself to chocolates to take the taste out of my mouth; comforting myself that it was for my good, that there would come a day when I should really like it, and be grateful to myself for my severity. I was training my palate as one trains a dog: by punishment and bribes.

In other and more sensible directions I sought to progress. Gradually I was overcoming my shyness. It was a slow process. I found the best plan was not to mind being shy, to accept it as part of my temperament, and with others laugh at it. The coldness of an indifferent world does harden a too sensitive skin. The gradual rubbings of existence rounded off my many corners. I became possible to my fellow creatures, and they to me; and I began to take pleasure in their company, even when it arrived accompanied by boots on stairs and opinions at full volume.

By directing me to a particular house in Nelson Square, Fate did me a kindness. It was a leaky-roofed menagerie, and I flatter myself it was an interesting one. We were thrown together not by affinity but by rent, which is the great match-maker of London. Our landlady, Mrs. Peedles, slept in the basement with the slavey and had been an actress in Charles Keane’s company at the old Princess’s. There, it is true, she had played only insignificant parts; but London, she explained, was even then a poor judge of art, hampered by prejudices. Besides, an actor-manager is always hampered by a wife—we understood. But previously, in the Provinces, there had been a career of glory: Juliet, Amy Robsart, Mrs. Haller in “The Stranger”—almost the entire roll of the “Legitimates.” Should we show any sign of doubt, proof was produced: handbills a yard long, rich in exclamation marks, as if punctuation could make genius undeniable—“On Tuesday Evening! By Special Desire!!! Blessington’s Theatre! In the Meadow, adjoining the Falcon Arms!”—“On Saturday! Under the Patronage of Col. Sir William and the Officers of the 74th!!!! In the Corn Exchange!” Maybe she would convince us further by running through a passage or two—the sleep-walking scene from “Macbeth,” or Ophelia’s entrance in the fourth act? It would be no trouble; her memory was excellent. We assured her hurriedly of our perfect faith, as a man agrees with a cook who has the carving-knife in her hand.

Listening to her, it was difficult—as she herself would frankly admit—to imagine her the once “arch Miss Lucretia Barry;” looking at her, to remember there had been an evening when she had been “the cynosure of every eye.” One had to fortify oneself with perusal of underlined extracts from ancient journals, much thumbed and creased, which she thoughtfully lent for the purpose. Since those days Fate had woven round her a mantle of depression. She was now a faded, watery-eyed little woman, prone on the slightest provocation to sit down suddenly on the nearest chair and at once commence a history of her troubles. Quite unconscious of this failing, it was an idea of hers that she was an exceptionally cheerful person.

“But there, fretting’s no good. We must grin and bear things in this world,” she would conclude, wiping her eyes on her apron. “It’s better to laugh than to cry, I always say.” And to prove it was no idle sentiment, she would laugh there and then upon the spot, the sound a little thin—like a spoon clinking in an empty cup.

Much stair-climbing had bestowed upon her a shortness of breath which no amount of panting in her resting moments was able to make good. Her life, when you listened to it, was a concert of effort: footfalls, wheezes, doors, bells. In that house bells were more than furniture; they were the reigning dynasty. They ruled Mrs. Peedles absolutely. She lived listening for them. Conversation with her was merely filling in time while waiting for interruptions; and even while she spoke her head would cock slightly, as if one ear belonged to you and the other to the house.

Once our second-floor front—a kindly young man—attempted to improve her breathing.

“You don’t know ’ow to breathe,” he explained. “You don’t swallow it, you only gargle with it. Take a good draught and shut your mouth; don’t be frightened of it; don’t let it out again till it’s done something: that’s what it’s ’ere for.”

He stood over her with his handkerchief pressed against her mouth to assist the process, as though air were a medicine to be administered by force. It was of no use.

“There don’t seem any room for it inside me,” she explained, and this, in a sense, was true: there was too much past packed into her.

A bottle of whisky fell into my hands that Christmas-time, a present from a commercial traveller in the way of business. Not liking whisky myself, it was no sacrifice to reserve it for the occasional comfort of Mrs. Peedles when, breathless, hands to her side, she would sink upon the chair nearest my door. Her poor washed-out face would lighten at the suggestion, as if I had offered her not drink but a brief return to the old footlights.

“Ah, well,” she would reply, “I don’t mind if I do. It’s a poor heart that never rejoices.”

Then, her tongue unloosed, she would sit there and tell me stories of my predecessors—young men lodgers who, like myself, had taken her bed-sitting-room—and of the woes and misfortunes that had overtaken them. I gathered that a more unlucky house could scarcely have been selected. A former tenant of my room, of whom I strangely reminded her, had written poetry on my very table. He was now in Portland doing five years for forgery. Mrs. Peedles appeared to regard the two accomplishments as merely different expressions of the same art, as if rhymes naturally led to cheques. Another of “her young men,” as she affectionately called us, had been of studious ambition; his career up to a point seemed brilliant.

“What he mightn’t have been,” according to Mrs. Peedles, there was practically no saying; what he happened to be at the moment of conversation was an inmate of the Hanwell lunatic asylum.

“I’ve always noticed it,” Mrs. Peedles would explain; “it’s always the most deserving, those that try hardest, to whom trouble comes. I’m sure I don’t know why.”

I was glad, on the whole, when that bottle of whisky was finished. A second might have driven me to suicide. Even pity can be over-fed.

There was no Mr. Peedles—at least not for Mrs. Peedles, though as an individual he continued to exist somewhere in London, like a bad smell that has found another room. He had been “general utility” at the Princess’s—a fine figure of a man in his day, I was given to understand, but easily led away, especially by minxes. Mrs. Peedles spoke bitterly of general utilities as people of not much use.

For working days she had one dress and one cap, both black and void of ostentation; but on Sundays and holidays she would appear metamorphosed. She had preserved the bulk of her stage wardrobe, even to paste-decked shoes and tinsel jewellery. Shapeless in classic garb as Hermia, or bulgy in brocade and velvet as Lady Teazle, she would receive her few visitors on Sunday evenings—discarded puppets like herself—who talked of gayer nights before their wires had been cut; or, her glory hidden under a mackintosh, she would pay her few calls. Perhaps it was the unusual excitement that brought colour into her furrowed cheeks and straightened and darkened her eyebrows, at other times so unobtrusive. Be that as it may, the change was remarkable; only the thin grey hair and the work-worn hands remained for identification. Nor was the transformation merely surface. Mrs. Peedles hung on her hook behind the kitchen door, dingy, limp, discarded; out of the wardrobe with the silks and satins was lifted down and put on—like an undergarment—Miss Lucretia Barry, somewhat aged, somewhat withered, but still distinctly “arch.”

In the room next to mine lived a law-writer and his wife. They were very old and miserably poor. It was none of their doing. Despite copy-book maxims, there is such a thing as ill-luck—persistent, monotonous—that gradually wears away the power of resistance as water wears down a stone. I learned their history; it was hopelessly simple, hopelessly uninstructive. He had been a schoolmaster, she a pupil-teacher. They married young and for a while the world smiled. Then came illness, attacking them both—not a melodrama, no moral to be extracted, merely bad drains leading to typhoid fever. They started again, saddled by debt; after years of effort they cleared themselves, only to fall once more, this time in helping a friend. Nor was it folly: the man had helped them in their trouble; they could hardly do otherwise without becoming ungrateful. And so on—commonplace, trivial, the sort of story that newspapers never print because it has no villain except circumstance.

Now, listless, patient, hard-working, they had reached an animal-like indifference to their fate. They were content so long as they could obtain the bare necessities; passive when these were not forthcoming. Their interest in life had narrowed to the one luxury of the poor—an occasional glass of beer or spirits. Often days would go by without his getting any work; then they would more or less starve. Law documents are generally given out to such men in the evening, to be returned finished next morning. Waking in the night, I would hear through the thin wooden partition the even scratching of his pen—steady, tireless, like a small insect in a cupboard. London’s great roar softened at night, but that sound remained: labour without audience, words without ownership.

Thus, cheek by jowl, we worked: I on my side of the screen, he on his—youth and age, hope and realisation. The house itself seemed to keep time: the scrape of his pen; the occasional distant bell; the creak of a stair; the drip of a tap in the kitchen like a metronome for poverty. Sound was never merely sound in Nelson Square; it was climate. It pressed upon the nerves, it sweetened or soured the temper, it could make courage feel like a thing borrowed and due to be returned.

Out of that old man my fears fashioned a vision of the future. Past his door I would slink on tiptoe, dread meeting him on the stairs. Had he not once said to himself, “The world’s mine oyster”? Had not the voices of night proclaimed him king also? Might I not be but an idle dreamer, mistaking desire for power? Would not the world prove stronger than I? At such times I saw my life laid out before me like a cheap weekly bill of fare: the clerkship at thirty shillings a week, rising by slow instalments to one hundred and fifty a year; the four-roomed house at Brixton; the girl-wife, pretty perhaps, but soon sinking into the slatternly woman; the squalling children; the endless scraping and eking, the thinning of all flavours. How could I, unaided, expect to raise myself from the ruck? Was not that the more likely picture?

Our second-floor front was a young fellow in the commercial line. Jarman was Young London personified—blatant yet kind-hearted; aggressively self-assertive, generous to a fault; cunning, yet at the same time frank; shrewd, cheery, and full of pluck. “Never say die” was his motto, and anything less dead would be difficult to imagine. All day long he was noisy; all night long he snored. He woke with a start, bathed like a porpoise, sang while dressing, roared for his boots, whistled during breakfast. His entrance and exit were always accompanied by an orchestration of banging doors and shouted directions concerning his meals as he plunged up and down the stairs, the clatter of brooms and pails flying before him. His departure left behind it the suggestion that the house was now to let; it came almost as a shock to meet a human being on the landing afterward, as though the building had been emptied by a gale.

Sometimes he would march into my room carrying his own supper-tray arranged for two. At first I resented the familiarity; I would hint at my desire to be alone, explain that I was busy.

“You fire away, Shakespeare Redivivus,” he would reply. “Don’t delay the tragedy. Why should London wait? I’ll keep quiet.”

But Jarman’s notion of keeping quiet was to retire into a corner and enact a tragedy of his own in a hoarse whisper, with gesture large enough to be seen from the Strand.

“Ah, ah!” I would hear him muttering. “I ’ave killed ’er good old father; I ’ave falsely accused ’er young man of all the crimes that I ’ave myself committed; I ’ave robbed ’er of ’er ancestral estates. Yet she loves me not! It is streeange!”

Then, changing his bass to a shrill falsetto: “It is a cold and dismal night: the snow falls fast. I will leave me ’at and umbrella be’ind the door and go out for a walk with the chee-ild. Aha! who is this? ’E also ’as forgotten ’is umbrella. Ah, now I know ’im in the pitch dark by ’is cigarette! Villain, murderer, silly josser! it is you!”

Then, with lightning change of voice and gesture: “Mary, I love yer!” “Sir Jasper Murgatroyd, let me avail myself of this opportunity to tell you what I think of you—” “No, no; the ’ouses close in ’alf an hour; there is not tee-ime. Fly with me instead!” “Never! Un’and me!” “’Ear me! Ah, what ’ave I done? I ’ave slipped upon a piece of orange peel and broke me ’ead! If you will kindly ask them to turn off the snow and give me a little moonlight, I will confess all.”

After such an entertainment, it was (much to Jarman’s surprise) impossible to catch again the thread of my own work. My tragedy, such as it was, curdled in the pot. I would abandon literature and listen instead to Jarman’s talk, which was always interesting. It was generally about himself, it is true, but on that account it was no less attractive. His love affairs—numerous as public-houses—formed his chief topic. There was no reserve about him; his life contained no secret chambers. What he “told her straight,” what she “up and said to him” was for any one within earshot. So far his search after the ideal had met with ill success.

“Girls,” he would say, “they’re all alike, till you know ’em. So long as they’re trying to palm themselves off on yer, they’ll persuade you there isn’t such another article in all the market. When they’ve got yer order—ah, then yer find out what they’re really made of. And you take it from me, ’Omer Junior, most of ’em are put together cheap. Bah! it sickens me sometimes to read the way you paper-stainers talk about ’em—angels, goddesses, fairies! They’ve just been getting at yer. You’re giving ’em just the price they’re asking without examining the article. Girls ain’t a special make, like what you seem to think ’em. We’re all turned out of the same old slop shop.”

“Not that I say, mind yer,” he would add, generous even in cynicism, “that there are none of the right sort. They’re to be ’ad—real good ’uns. All I say is, taking ’em at their own valuation ain’t the way to do business with ’em.”

What he was on the lookout for—by his own description—was a really first-class article, not something from which the paint would come off almost before you got it home.

“They’re to be found,” he would affirm, cheerfully as a man whistling against London’s din, “but you’ve got to look for ’em. They’re not the sort that advertises.”
