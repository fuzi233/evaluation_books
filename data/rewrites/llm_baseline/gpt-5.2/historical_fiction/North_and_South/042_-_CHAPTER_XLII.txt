The blow had fallen too heavily to spend itself in any violent show. Margaret lay as if the life in her had been stunned—eyes closed, breath coming so low that it seemed borrowed, answering only when she must, and then in a whisper that sounded like a voice remembered rather than a voice spoken. It was not that tears were denied her by pride; it was that grief had seized on her like a frost, and held her dumb.

Mr. Bell sat by the fire, his broad hands idle in his lap, his thoughts chafing against the walls of necessity. He dared not leave her, and yet there were messages to send, arrangements at Oxford that waited on him. The plan he had shaped on his way to Milton—carrying her back with him into quieter courts and older stones—was now as impossible as asking a fainting bird to fly against a gale. Even the mere imagining of what she must pass through made him wince.

Dixon hovered, sobbing hospitality made into duty, and insisted—first with her hands, then with her voice—that Mr. Bell must eat. She had prepared a dinner downstairs, and there was something in her notion that proper food, properly administered, was an act of allegiance to the living, even in a house newly claimed by death. But he would not go.

At last he allowed a plate to be brought up. In other times he had been a man who could tell, with irritating accuracy, what herb had been overused, what butter had been too much clarified, what sauce had lost its edge by waiting. Now he put the morsel into his mouth, and it might have been planed wood for all the pleasure it gave him. The devilled chicken—so carefully pricked with cayenne, so conscientiously browned—had no heat for him, no savour, no meaning.

He tried, because one tries, to make it serve Margaret. He minced the fowl smaller, as if tenderness could be manufactured by the knife; he shook over salt and pepper, as if the tongue could be coaxed back to the world by pungency. Dixon, receiving his directions with the humility of a servant who would gladly take orders from any one that might save her young mistress, attempted to feed Margaret. The girl’s head moved a little—no, only no; not with petulance, but with a quiet refusal that said food would do her harm by reminding her she still had a body to be cared for.

Mr. Bell sighed—a great, old, helpless sound; then, stiff from travel and from sitting too long, he rose and followed Dixon out.

“I cannot leave her,” he said, stopping in the passage as if the distance between himself and the drawing-room were already treachery. “I must write to Oxford, to have my preparations made without me. And she must have a woman with her. Someone to talk her into a good fit of crying. Can’t Mrs. Lennox come?”

Dixon wiped her eyes with her apron, steadied her voice with an effort that was almost soldierly, and told him Mrs. Lennox was too near her confinement to undertake a journey.

“Then we must have Mrs. Shaw,” cried Mr. Bell, impatience breaking through his kindness. “She’s in England again, is she not?”

“Yes, sir; she’s come back,” said Dixon, reluctant as a dog guarding its wounded. “But I don’t think she’ll like to leave Mrs. Lennox at such an interesting time.”

“Interesting time be—” Mr. Bell checked himself with a cough, but his indignation went on speaking in his eyes. “She could be content enough to be at Venice or Naples, or some of those Popish places, at the last ‘interesting time,’ which happened in Corfu, I believe. And what does that little prosperous woman’s interesting time signify compared with that poor creature in there—homeless, helpless, friendless Margaret—lying as still as if the sofa were an altar-tomb and she the stone image upon it? Mrs. Shaw shall come. See that a room is ready by to-morrow night. I’ll take care she comes.”

So he wrote—no petition, no coaxing, but a command, sharp enough to pierce Mrs. Shaw’s indolence and her habit of arranging her sympathies at a distance. She received it with tears, declaring, as she dabbed at the corners of her eyes, that it was so like one of the dear general’s letters when he expected a fit of gout, that she should preserve it as a relic. Indeed, if Mr. Bell had offered her any choice—if he had urged instead of ordered—she might have remained in Harley Street, feeling herself very kind, and sending messages of tender regret. But being told she must come, she found energy enough to allow herself to be packed by her maid, boxes shut and corded with brisk authority.

Edith, all cap-strings and shawl and tears, came to the head of the stairs as Captain Lennox led Mrs. Shaw down to the carriage.

“Don’t forget, mamma—Margaret must come and live with us. Sholto goes to Oxford on Wednesday; you must send word by Mr. Bell when we are to expect you. And if you want Sholto, he can go on to Milton from Oxford. Don’t forget, mamma—you are to bring back Margaret.”

She went back into the drawing-room, where Mr. Henry Lennox sat with a new Review, cutting open the pages with deliberate neatness. Without looking up, he said, in the tone of a man who offers assistance because it is his due to offer it—

“If you don’t like Sholto to be so long absent, Edith, I hope you will let me go down to Milton and give what aid I can.”

“Oh, thank you,” she answered readily. “I dare say old Mr. Bell will do everything, and perhaps more help won’t be needed. Only one doesn’t expect much savoir-faire from a resident Fellow. Dear, darling Margaret! Won’t it be nice to have her here again? You were both great allies years ago.”

“Were we?” he asked indifferently, as if the printed column had more claim on him than memory.

“Well, perhaps not—I forget. I was so full of Sholto. But doesn’t it fall out well that if my uncle was to die it should be just now, when we’re come home and settled in the old house, quite ready to receive Margaret? Poor thing! what a change it will be from Milton. I’ll have new chintz for her bedroom—make it bright and fresh and cheerful.”

In that same spirit of kindly planning—kindly, and yet touching grief as one touches a bruise—Mrs. Shaw travelled to Milton, sometimes dreading the first meeting, more often thinking how quickly she might remove Margaret from what she always termed, with a shudder, “that horrid place,” back to the smooth upholstery and well-muffled street-sounds of Harley Street.

“Oh dear!” she said to her maid, as the train drew nearer and the air thickened. “Look at those chimneys! My poor sister Hale! I don’t think I could have rested at Naples if I had known it was like this. I must have come and fetched her and Margaret away.”

And though she had always thought Mr. Hale a weak man, she never judged him so weak as when she saw, with her own eyes, what he had chosen in exchange for Helstone—green silence for this perpetual breath of smoke; the natural music of birds and wind for a place where the very air seemed to throb with iron.

Margaret remained as she had been: white, motionless, without tears. They had told her her aunt was coming; she had not shown surprise or pleasure, dislike or gratitude. Mr. Bell, whose appetite had returned with the irrepressible selfishness of the living body, tried again to tempt her with something delicate—sweetbreads stewed with oysters, rich with a sea-scent, the sauce thickened carefully, and the whole intended to insinuate comfort into a failing frame. But Margaret shook her head with the same quiet obstinacy, as if nourishment were a foreign language she could not bear to hear.

Mr. Bell ate them himself, partly to spare Dixon’s feelings, partly because one must go on chewing something when the mind can do nothing else but grind sorrow.

It was Margaret, however, who first heard the cab stop at the door that brought Mrs. Shaw from the station. The sound itself—wheels ceasing, a door shutting, the faint stir of voices—broke into her stillness like a stone dropped into deep water. Her eyelids quivered; a little colour came and went in her lips, as if life tried once more to claim her.

Mr. Bell went down to meet Mrs. Shaw. When they returned upstairs, Margaret was standing—trying to steady her dizziness by mere force of will. She saw her aunt; she went forward, and the arms held out to her were not the polished embrace of a fashionable woman so much as the sudden shelter of blood and likeness. And there, on her aunt’s shoulder, she found at last the passionate relief of tears.

It was not only sorrow for the dead that melted then. It was the remembered tenderness of years; it was the soft compulsion of relationship; it was that inexplicable family resemblance in look and gesture, in tone and even in the way a hand was laid upon a sleeve—so like her mother in that instant that Margaret’s numbed heart gave way as ice breaks under a spring sun.

Mr. Bell stole out, grateful as any man is to be spared the sight of a grief he cannot mend. He went down into the study, ordered a fire, and tried to occupy himself by taking books down from the shelves. But each volume was a trap for memory. A name written within, a dog-eared page, a quotation marked—everything was Hale speaking again, in that quiet voice of his that had so often softened argument into persuasion. It was a change of labour from watching Margaret; it was no change of thought.

He was glad—almost startled into pleasure—to catch the sound of Mr. Thornton’s voice at the door.

Dixon, however, was at her most ungracious. The arrival of Mrs. Shaw’s maid had summoned in her mind whole processions of former grandeur—Beresford blood, ancient coaches, “station” (as she called it with satisfaction) from which her young mistress had been thrust down. In the kitchen she had been skilfully drawing out from the maid every circumstance of consequence connected with Harley Street, for the edification of Martha and the strengthening of her own self-importance. Those visions made her inclined to be supercilious toward any Milton man. She stood in awe of Mr. Thornton, but she was as curt as she dared.

He could see none of the inmates of the house that night.

It was uncomfortable to have her statement contradicted by Mr. Bell opening the study-door and calling out—

“Thornton! Is that you? Come in for a minute. I want to speak to you.”

So Mr. Thornton entered the study; and Dixon withdrew, to reinstate herself among pots and plates by repeating, with prodigious embellishment, the tale of Sir John Beresford’s coach and six when he was High Sheriff.

Mr. Bell, once Mr. Thornton was inside, looked at him with the vagueness of a mind overfull.

“I don’t know what I wanted to say to you after all,” he confessed. “Only it’s dull enough to sit in a room where everything speaks of a dead friend. Yet Margaret and her aunt must have the drawing-room to themselves.”

“Is Mrs—Is her aunt come?” asked Mr. Thornton; and the break in his voice betrayed more than the question.

“Come? Yes. Maid and all. One would think she might have come alone at such a time. And now I must turn out and find my way to the Clarendon.”

“You must not go to the Clarendon,” said Mr. Thornton quickly. “We have five or six empty bedrooms at home.”

“Well aired?” asked Mr. Bell, old habits of comfort asserting themselves in the midst of trouble.

“I think you may trust my mother for that.”

“Then I’ll run upstairs, wish that wan girl good-night, make my bow to her aunt, and go off with you straight.”

He was longer upstairs than Mr. Thornton expected. Mr. Thornton had business—workmen’s accounts, plans to decide, the heavy daily management that waited for no man’s private anguish—and he had only snatched time to run up to Crampton and ask how Miss Hale did. He paced, listening to the fire, to the small creaks of the room, to the distant under-note of Milton itself: that continual, restless hum which never quite ceased, and which some men took as the pulse of prosperity, while others felt it as a kind of perpetual intrusion, a forcing of the world’s harsh lexicon into the ear until a man forgot what natural quiet could be.

At length Mr. Bell came down, and they set out together through the night.

“I was kept by those women,” said Mr. Bell, with an impatience half comic, half bitter. “Mrs. Shaw is anxious to get home—on account of her daughter, she says—and wants Margaret to go off at once. Now she is no more fit for travelling than I am for flying. Besides, Margaret says—very justly—that she has friends she must see, people to wish good-bye to; and then her aunt plagued her about old claims, and whether she was forgetful of old friends. And Margaret burst out crying, and said she should be glad enough to go from a place where she had suffered so much. Now I must return to Oxford to-morrow, and I don’t know which way to throw in my voice.”

He paused as if asking counsel. Mr. Thornton did not answer. A phrase had fastened on him and would not loosen.

Where she had suffered so much.

So that was how she would remember Milton: not as the place where she had been seen, where she had been loved in secret and in pain; not as the ground on which every mile walked toward her door had been a kind of feast to him, richer than any banquet because anticipation seasoned it, because each step both brought him nearer and carried him away, making his mind rehearse some grace in her, some keen, pungent quality in her speech that stung and delighted together. To him, even its bitterness had been precious—an austere spice that made the sweetness sharper when it came. And she called it suffering.

The thought made the streets seem emptier than they were. The factories’ night-sounds—distant clanks, a hiss, a monotonous beat like a great heart made of metal—pressed upon his nerves. There are silences that heal, and silences that wound; but there are also noises that deaden feeling until grief has no room to move. Milton’s sound never asked permission of the soul.

They reached Marlborough Mills. In the dining-room Mrs. Thornton and Fanny were sitting. Fanny was in a flutter of small exultation while the maid held up glossy materials—one silk after another—to judge their effect by candle-light. The room was bright; the fabrics caught the flame, and threw back sheen, shadow, and the whispering rustle of money spent for ornament.

Mrs. Thornton made an effort to sympathise, but neither taste nor dress was within her proper dominions. She heartily wished Fanny had accepted her brother’s offer of having the wedding-clothes provided by a first-rate London dressmaker, without endless troublesome discussions and waverings born of Fanny’s desire to choose and superintend everything herself. Mr. Thornton, for his part, was only too willing to show his grateful approval of any sensible man who could be captivated by Fanny’s second-rate airs and graces, by giving her ample means for providing herself with the finery which rivalled—if it did not exceed—the lover in her estimation.

When Mr. Thornton and Mr. Bell entered, Fanny blushed, simpered, and fluttered over the signs of her occupation. It might have drawn attention from most; it drew none from Mr. Bell. If he thought of her and her satins at all, it was by contrast with the pale sorrow he had left behind—Margaret sitting motionless with bent head and folded hands in a room so still you might almost have believed the rush in your own ears was made by the spirits of the dead lingering near the beloved. For when he had first gone upstairs, Mrs. Shaw had fallen asleep on the sofa, and no sound had broken that solemn hush.

Mrs. Thornton gave Mr. Bell her formal welcome. She was never so gracious as when receiving her son’s friends in her son’s house; the more unexpected they were, the more honour to her admirable housekeeping.

“How is Miss Hale?” she asked.

“About as broken down by this last stroke as she can be.”

“I am sure it is very well for her that she has such a friend as you.”

“I wish I were her only friend, madam,” said Mr. Bell, with blunt feeling. “It sounds brutal; but here have I been displaced and turned out of my post of comforter by a fine lady aunt; and there are cousins and what not claiming her in London as if she were a lap-dog belonging to them. And she is too weak and miserable to have a will of her own.”

“She must indeed be weak,” said Mrs. Thornton, with an implied meaning her son understood.

“But where,” she continued, “have these relations been all this time that Miss Hale has appeared almost friendless, and has certainly had a good deal of anxiety to bear?”

Yet she did not care enough for the answer to wait. She left to make household arrangements, her mind already travelling over linen, sheets, fires laid, a room made ready—comforts she could command, and therefore trusted.

“They have been living abroad,” said Mr. Bell to Mr. Thornton when they were alone. “They have some claim, in justice. The aunt brought her up; she and the cousin have been like sisters. What vexes me is—I wanted to take her for a child of my own, and I am jealous of those who don’t seem to value their right. Now it would be different if Frederick claimed her.”

“Frederick!” exclaimed Mr. Thornton; and the name fell upon him like a blow in the dark. “Who is he? What right—” He stopped short, as if he had almost spoken too much.

“Frederick,” repeated Mr. Bell, surprised. “Why, don’t you know? He’s her brother. Have you not heard—”

“I never heard his name before. Where is he? Who is he?”

“Surely I told you, when the family first came to Milton—the son concerned in that mutiny.”

“I never heard of him till this moment,” said Mr. Thornton, and his voice grew hard with the effort of restraint. “Where does he live?”

“In Spain. Liable to be arrested the instant he sets foot on English ground. Poor fellow—he will grieve at not being able to attend his father’s funeral. We must be content with Captain Lennox; I know no other relation.”

“I hope I may be allowed to go?” said Mr. Thornton.

“Certainly; thankfully. You’re a good fellow, after all, Thornton. Hale liked you. He spoke of you only the other day at Oxford. He regretted he had seen so little of you lately. I am obliged to you for wishing to show him respect.”

“But about Frederick,” pursued Mr. Thornton, the same idea gnawing in him. “Does he never come to England?”

“Never.”

“He was not over here about the time of Mrs. Hale’s death?”

“No. Why, I was here then. I hadn’t seen Hale for years and years; and, if you remember, I came—No, it was some time after that I came. But poor Frederick was not here. What made you think he was?”

“I saw a young man walking with Miss Hale one day,” replied Mr. Thornton, “and I think it was about that time.”

“Oh, that would be this young Lennox—the Captain’s brother. He’s a lawyer. They were in constant correspondence with him, and Mr. Hale thought he would come down.”

Mr. Bell wheeled round suddenly, shutting one eye, the other sharpening itself into keen scrutiny of Mr. Thornton’s face.

“Do you know,” he said, “that I once fancied you had a little tenderness for Margaret?”

There was no answer. No change of countenance.

“And so did poor Hale. Not at first—not till I put it into his head.”

“I admired Miss Hale,” said Mr. Thornton at length, as if choosing the safest word as one chooses the safest dish when one’s appetite is watched. “Every one must. She is a beautiful creature.”

“Is that all!” cried Mr. Bell, anger flaring. “You can speak of her in that measured way—simply as a ‘beautiful creature’—something to catch the eye. I did hope you had nobleness enough to pay her the homage of the heart. Though I believe—in fact I know—she would have rejected you, still to have loved her without return would have lifted you higher than all those, be they who they may, that have ever known her to love. ‘Beautiful creature’ indeed! Do you speak of her as you would of a horse or dog?”

Mr. Thornton’s eyes glowed like embers.

“Mr. Bell,” he said, very low, “before you speak so, you should remember that all men are not as free to express what they feel as you are. Let us talk of something else.”

For every word Mr. Bell had spoken had struck his heart like a trumpet-call; yet he would not be forced into any confession. He was not a mocking-bird of praise, to be tried, to be set to out-sing another man’s extolling. He turned abruptly to matters that could be held between them without laying bare the quick.

“What is that heap of brick and mortar we came upon in the yard?” asked Mr. Bell, catching at the change. “Any repairs wanted?”

“No; none, thank you.”

“Are you building on your own account? If you are, I’m obliged to you.”

“I’m building a dining-room—for the men, I mean—the hands.”

“I thought you were hard to please if this room wasn’t good enough for you, a bachelor.”

“I’ve got acquainted with a strange kind of chap,” said Mr. Thornton, speaking now as if it were easier to confess practical matters than feeling. “And I put one or two children in whom he is interested to school. So, as I happened to be passing near his house one day, I went in about some trifling payment; and I saw such a miserable black frizzle of a dinner—a greasy cinder of meat—as first set me thinking. But it was not till provisions grew so high this winter that I bethought me how, by buying wholesale and cooking a quantity together, money might be saved and comfort gained.

“So I spoke to my friend—or my enemy—the man I told you of; and he found fault with every detail of my plan; and in consequence I laid it aside, as impracticable, and also because if I forced it I should be interfering with the independence of my men. Then suddenly this Higgins came to me and graciously signified his approval of a scheme so nearly the same as mine that I might have claimed it; and more, the approval of several fellow-workmen to whom he had spoken.

“I was a little riled, I confess, and thought of throwing the whole thing overboard. But it seemed childish to relinquish what I had once thought wise merely because I did not get the honour due to the originator. So I coolly took the part assigned me—something like steward to a club. I buy the provisions wholesale, and provide a fitting matron or cook.”

Mr. Bell’s humour returned for a moment, as if the mention of meals—even these plain, workmen’s meals—gave his mind something homely to hold.

“I hope you give satisfaction in your new capacity. Are you a good judge of potatoes and onions? But I suppose Mrs. Thornton assists you in your marketing.”

“Not a bit,” replied Mr. Thornton. “She disapproves of the whole plan, and now we never mention it to each other. But I manage pretty well—get stocks from Liverpool; butcher’s meat from our own family butcher. And I can assure you, the hot dinners the matron turns out are not to be despised.”

“Do you taste each dish as it goes in, in virtue of your office? I hope you have a white wand.”

“At first I was scrupulous,” said Mr. Thornton, “confined myself to purchasing; and even then I rather obeyed the men’s orders conveyed through the housekeeper than my own judgment. One time the beef was too large, another the mutton not fat enough. I think they saw how careful I was to leave them free, not to intrude my ideas; so one day two or three—Higgins among them—asked me if I would not come in and take a snack. It was a busy day; but I saw they would be hurt if I didn’t meet them half-way. So I went in; and I never made a better dinner in my life.

“I told them—my next neighbours, I mean, for I’m no speech-maker—how much I enjoyed it; and for some time, whenever that especial dinner recurred in their dietary, I was sure to be met with, ‘Master, there’s hot-pot for dinner to-day; win yo’ come in?’ If they had not asked me, I would no more have intruded on them than I’d have gone to the mess at barracks without invitation.”

Mr. Bell laughed a little, though the sound had no mirth in it.

“I should think you were rather a restraint on your hosts’ conversation. They can’t abuse masters while you’re there. I expect they take it out on non-hot-pot days.”

“Hitherto we’ve steered clear of vexed questions,” said Mr. Thornton. “But if any of the old disputes come up again, I shall speak out next hot-pot day. You are hardly acquainted with our Darkshire fellows, for all you’re a Darkshire man yourself. They’ve such a sense of humour—such a racy mode of expression. I am getting really to know some of them now, and they talk pretty freely before me.”

“Nothing like eating for equalising men,” said Mr. Bell. “Dying is nothing to it. The philosopher dies sententiously—the pharisee ostentatiously—the simple-hearted humbly—the poor idiot blindly, as the sparrow falls to the ground; but philosopher and idiot, publican and pharisee, all eat after the same fashion—given an equally good digestion. There’s theory for theory for you!”

“Indeed I have no theory,” replied Mr. Thornton shortly. “I hate theories.”

“I beg your pardon,” said Mr. Bell, with a sudden impulse of generosity that sought an outlet. “To show my penitence, will you accept a ten-pound note toward your marketing and give the poor fellows a feast?”

“Thank you; but I’d rather not,” answered Mr. Thornton. “They pay me rent for the oven and cooking-places at the back of the mill, and will have to pay more for the new dining-room. I don’t want it to fall into a charity. I don’t want donations. Once let in the principle, and people will go talking, and spoil the simplicity of the whole thing.”

“People will talk about any new plan,” said Mr. Bell. “You can’t help that.”

“My enemies—if I have any—may make a philanthropic fuss about this dinner-scheme,” returned Mr. Thornton. “But you are a friend, and I expect you will pay my experiment the respect of silence. It is but a new broom as yet, and sweeps clean enough. But by-and-by we shall meet with stumbling-blocks, no doubt.”
