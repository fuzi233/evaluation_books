Late that evening, a knock at my lodgings announced the arrival of Mr. Bruff. The lawyer's demeanor had undergone a profound transformation; the usual confidence and vigor that characterized his visits were absent, replaced by a somber, defeated air. He extended his hand to me, a gesture he had never before performed in silence, his grip heavy with unspoken weight.

"Are you returning to Hampstead?" I asked, seeking any thread of conversation.

"I have just departed Hampstead," he replied, his voice low. "Mr. Franklin, I know you have finally grasped the truth. Yet, if I could have anticipated the terrible price exacted for that knowledge, I would have preferred to leave you in ignorance."

"You have seen Rachel?"

"I returned her to Portland Place after bringing her here; it was impossible to allow her to travel alone in the carriage. I cannot hold you accountable for the shock this unfortunate meeting has inflicted upon her, considering you witnessed her in my home with my permission. My duty now is to prevent a recurrence of such distress. She is young, possessed of a resolute spirit, and with time and rest, she will recover. I require only your assurance that you will do nothing to impede her healing. Can I rely on your promise to make no further attempts to see her without my explicit sanction?"

"After the suffering she has endured, and the suffering I have borne," I said, "you may rely on my word."

"I have your promise?"

"You have my promise."

Relief washed over Mr. Bruff's features. He removed his hat and drew his chair closer. "That is settled. Now, regarding your future. In my view, the extraordinary turn events have taken leads to a singular conclusion. First, we are certain Rachel told you the entire truth as plainly as words allow. Second, though we acknowledge a dreadful error exists somewhere, we cannot blame her for believing you guilty based on the evidence of her senses, supported by circumstances that, on their face, condemn you."

I interjected immediately. "I do not blame Rachel. I only regret she could not force herself to speak more plainly to me at the time."

"You might as well regret that Rachel is not someone else," Mr. Bruff countered. "Even then, I doubt if a girl of any delicacy, whose heart was set on marrying you, could bring herself to accuse you openly of theft. It was not in Rachel's nature. In a matter entirely different from yours, yet placing her in a position not unlike hers toward you, I know she was influenced by a similar motive. Furthermore, as she told me on our way to town, if she *had* spoken plainly, she would no more have believed your denial then than she does now. What answer can you offer? None. Admit it, Mr. Franklin. My view of the case was proven wrong, but my advice remains valid. We shall waste our time and exhaust our minds if we attempt to unravel this frightful complication from the beginning. Let us resolutely close our minds to last year at Lady Verinder's country house and focus on what we *can* discover in the future, rather than what we *cannot* uncover in the past."

"Surely you forget," I argued, "that the entire matter is essentially of the past, so far as I am concerned?"

"Answer me this," Mr. Bruff retorted sharply. "Is the Moonstone at the bottom of all the mischief, or is it not?"

"It is—of course."

"Very well. What do we believe was done with the Moonstone when it was taken to London?"

"It was pledged to Mr. Luker."

"We know you are not the person who pledged it. Do we know who did?"

"No."

"Where do we believe the Moonstone is now?"

"Deposited in the keeping of Mr. Luker's bankers."

"Exactly. Observe. We are already in June. Towards the end of the month, a year will have elapsed since the jewel was pledged. There is a distinct possibility that the person who pawned it will be prepared to redeem it when the year expires. If he redeems it, Mr. Luker must, according to his own terms, retrieve the diamond from the bankers. Under these circumstances, I propose setting a watch at the bank as the month draws to a close to discover who claims the Moonstone. Do you see the logic?"

I admitted, albeit reluctantly, that the idea was novel.

"It is Mr. Murthwaite's idea as much as mine," Mr. Bruff noted. "It might never have occurred to me without our conversation some time ago. If Mr. Murthwaite is correct, the Indians will likely be watching the bank at the end of the month too, and something serious may ensue. What happens does not concern us directly, except as it helps us identify the mysterious individual who pawned the Diamond. That person, you may be certain, is responsible for your current predicament; only they can restore your standing in Rachel's eyes."

"I cannot deny," I said, "that your proposed plan meets the difficulty with daring ingenuity. But—"

"But you have an objection?"

"Yes. My objection is that your proposal obliges us to wait."

"Granted. As I calculate, it requires a fortnight—more or less. Is that so long?"

"It is a lifetime, Mr. Bruff, in my situation. My existence is unendurable unless I take immediate action to clear my name."

"Well, I understand. Have you considered what you can do?"

"I have thought of consulting Sergeant Cuff."

"He has retired from the police. It is useless to expect his aid."

"I know where to find him; I can but try."

"Try," Mr. Bruff said after a moment's pause. "The case has assumed such an extraordinary aspect since Sergeant Cuff's time that you *may* revive his interest. Try, and let me hear the result. Meanwhile, if you make no discoveries before the end of the month, am I free to attempt the bank vigil on my own?"

"Certainly," I answered, "unless I relieve you of the necessity of trying the experiment in the interim."

Mr. Bruff smiled and picked up his hat. "Tell Sergeant Cuff," he added, "that *I* say the discovery of the truth depends on identifying the person who pawned the Diamond. Let me hear what the Sergeant's experience says to that."

We parted ways.

Early the next morning, I set out for Dorking, the small town where Sergeant Cuff had retired, as Betteredge had indicated. Inquiring at the hotel, I received directions to his cottage. It was approached by a quiet by-road, situated snugly within its own garden plot, protected by brick walls and a high hedge. The gate, adorned with painted trellis work, was locked. After ringing the bell, I peered through the lattice and saw Cuff's favorite flowers blooming everywhere, clustering over his door and looking in at his windows. Far from the crimes and mysteries of the great city, the illustrious thief-taker was living out his final years in peaceful seclusion, smothered in roses.

A decent elderly woman opened the gate, instantly annihilating all the hopes I had built on securing Cuff's assistance. He had departed only the previous day on a journey to Ireland.

"Has he gone there on business?" I asked.

The woman smiled. "He has only one business now, sir," she replied. "And that is roses. A great man's gardener in Ireland has discovered something new in cultivating roses, and Mr. Cuff is away to investigate it."

"Do you know when he will return?"

"It is quite uncertain, sir. Mr. Cuff said he would return directly, or be away for some time, depending on whether he finds the discovery worthless or worthy of study. If you have a message for him, I will ensure he receives it."

I handed her my card, having first penciled on it: "I have something to say about the Moonstone. Let me hear from you as soon as you return." With that, there was nothing left but to submit to circumstances and return to London.

In the irritable state of my mind at this time, the failure of my journey to Dorking merely aggravated my restless impulse to act. On the day of my return, I resolved that the next morning would see me make a new effort to force my way from darkness to light.

What form should this next experiment take?

If Betteredge had been present during my deliberations, he would undoubtedly have declared that my German side was currently dominant. To speak seriously, my German training may have been partially responsible for the labyrinth of useless speculations in which I now entangled myself. For most of the night, I sat smoking, constructing theories, each more improbable than the last. When sleep finally claimed me, my waking fancies pursued me in dreams. I rose the next morning with "Objective-Subjective" and "Subjective-Objective" inextricably tangled in my mind. I began the day intended for practical action by doubting whether I had any philosophical right to consider any thing, including the Diamond, as existing at all.

How long I might have remained lost in the mist of my own metaphysics is impossible to say. As events proved, accident came to my rescue. Happening to wear the same coat I had worn during my interview with Rachel, I searched one pocket and found a crumpled piece of paper: Betteredge's forgotten letter.

It seemed hard on my good old friend to leave him without a reply. I went to my writing table and read the letter again. A letter containing nothing of slight importance is not always easy to answer. Betteredge's correspondence fell into this category. Mr. Candy's assistant, Ezra Jennings, had told his master that he had seen me; consequently, Mr. Candy wished to see me and say something when I was next near Frizinghall. What could I possibly say in response that would be worth the paper? I sat idly drawing likenesses from memory of Mr. Candy's remarkable assistant on the sheet I had vowed to dedicate to Betteredge—until it suddenly occurred to me that the irrepressible Ezra Jennings was getting in my way again. I threw a dozen portraits of the man with the piebald hair into the waste-paper basket and, there and then, wrote my answer to Betteredge. It was a perfectly commonplace letter, but it had one excellent effect on me: the effort of writing a few sentences in plain English completely cleared my mind of the cloudy nonsense that had filled it since the previous day.

Devoting myself once more to elucidating the impenetrable puzzle of my own position, I attempted to meet the difficulty by investigating it from a plainly practical standpoint. The events of the memorable night remaining unintelligible, I looked back to search my memory of the earlier hours of the birthday for any incident that might serve as a clue.

Had anything happened while Rachel and I finished painting the door? Later, when I rode to Frizinghall? Afterwards, when I returned with Godfrey Ablewhite and his sisters? Or later still, when I placed the Moonstone in Rachel's hands? Or finally, when the company assembled around the dinner table? My memory disposed of these questions readily until I reached the last. Looking back at the social event of the birthday dinner, I found myself brought to a standstill. I was not even capable of accurately remembering the number of guests who had sat at the same table as me.

To feel myself completely at fault here, and to conclude that the incidents of the dinner especially repaid investigation, formed part of the same mental process. I believe others in a similar situation would have reasoned as I did. When the pursuit of our own interests makes us objects of inquiry to ourselves, we are naturally suspicious of what we do not know. Once in possession of the names of those present at the dinner, I resolved—as a means to enrich the deficient resources of my own memory—to appeal to the memories of the other guests. I would write down all they could recall of the social events of the birthday and test the results against what happened afterwards when the company left.

This last and newest of my many contemplated experiments in the art of inquiry—which Betteredge would probably attribute to the clear-headed, or French, side of me being uppermost for the moment—may fairly claim record here on its own merits. Unlikely as it may seem, I had now actually groped my way to the root of the matter. All I needed was a hint to guide me in the right direction at the start. Before another day had passed, that hint was given to me by one of the company present at the birthday feast!

With the plan of proceeding now in view, it was first necessary to possess the complete list of guests. This I could easily obtain from Gabriel Betteredge. I determined to return to Yorkshire that day and begin my contemplated investigation the next morning.

It was just too late to start by the train leaving London before noon. There was no alternative but to wait nearly three hours for the departure of the next train. Was there anything I could do in London to usefully occupy this interval?

My thoughts returned obstinately to the birthday dinner.

Though I had forgotten the numbers and, in many cases, the names of the guests, I remembered readily that by far the larger proportion came from Frizinghall or its neighborhood. But the larger proportion was not all. Some few of us were not regular residents in the country. I myself was one of the few. Mr. Murthwaite was another. Godfrey Ablewhite was a third. Mr. Bruff—no: I recalled that business had prevented him from attending. Had any ladies been present whose usual residence was in London? I could only remember Miss Clack as falling into this category. However, here were three guests at least whom it was clearly advisable for me to see before leaving town. I drove off immediately to Mr. Bruff's office, not knowing the addresses of the persons I sought, thinking it probable he might put me in their way.

Mr. Bruff proved too busy to give me more than a minute of his valuable time. In that minute, however, he contrived to dispose, in the most discouraging manner, of all the questions I had to ask.
