“Turn back to your own truth, which holds: the more perfect a thing is, the more keenly it feels joy—and every grief.”

When Deronda rounded the staircase and nearly collided with Gwendolen and Grandcourt that afternoon, his whole being thrumming with the weight of the second, life-altering interview he’d just finished with his mother, the Princess Halm-Eberstein. Barely two hours after he’d said his final goodbye to her, he learned she’d already left the Genoa hotel, and technically, every last obligation of his journey had been fulfilled. He could have caught the next train straight to Mainz, delivered the letter from Joseph Kalonymos, and claimed the ancient family chest that held the entire legacy of his hidden heritage. But a tangled mess of feeling, too raw and new to untangle into neat, logical reasons, held him fast in place.

Long after the Princess had gone, he sat motionless, bowed under the soft, heavy weight of retrospection. Every sharp, revelatory moment of their conversation replayed in his memory, bright and burning, as if he’d not just learned his true origins that day, but lived them. Alone in his room, he let himself weep, with a tenderness sharper than any woman’s, over the strange, lonely life of the mother who was so bound to him by blood, and yet a stranger he’d only just met. The whole world felt shifted under his feet: new ties he’d never guessed at had rebalanced every old hope and fear, woven him into a belonging he’d never dared to imagine. It was as if he’d wandered all his life thinking he belonged to one small band of exiles, only to wake at dawn and see the tents of his own kith and kin stretched out far across the horizon. He could almost feel the ghost of his grandfather’s presence beside him, the same fiery passions, the same quiet beloved dreams, stirring awake deep in his own chest. Through every beat of that fevered, loving meditation, Mordecai and Mirah were there too, silent, steady, as if they were holding his hands across the miles in perfect, unspoken sympathy.

That was the soft, responsive core of Daniel Deronda, beneath the calm, self-restrained reserve he’d built up over years of quiet, lonely childhood. When the hotel’s dinner bell pealed insistently to remind him of the hour, he pulled out the railway guide to look up the next train to Mainz, made a half-hearted move to pack his case—and then did nothing at all. His thoughts pulled him one way, to Mainz and the family chest, to London and the two people who were the deepest loves of his life. But another, stronger pull anchored him to Genoa, the same quiet, stubborn urge that makes you linger after a goodbye you already know will be the last, overshadowed by a premonition of grief too big to name.

He never said out loud to himself, *I will stay tonight, because it is Friday, and I want to go to the evening synagogue service, and besides— I might see Gwendolen and Grandcourt again*. Instead, he sat idle, his mind drifting first to the little Genoese synagogue, picturing the faces there no different than they would have been in his grandfather’s day, listening to the ancient Spanish-Hebrew liturgy that had outlasted centuries of wandering, like a hardy weed whose seeds blow across every far shore to tie even the loneliest exile back to home. Then his thoughts drifted to Gwendolen, too, recalling the fear and unspoken despair he’d seen in her eyes the last time they’d parted, and the quiet certainty that it would be cruel to slip away without even a word, ignoring Grandcourt’s obvious hatred of him, to let her know he still cared.

He pushed back his departure, ate his whole dinner without tasting a single bite, then hurried out to find the synagogue. On his way past the porter’s desk, he asked if Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt were still staying there, and what their room number was. The porter gave him the number, and added offhand that the couple had gone out for an evening row on the bay.

That news tangled with the thoughts he carried from the synagogue, where he’d watched the small congregation of dark-eyed, weathered Italian Jews wrap their tallit around their shoulders, pray with a familiar, unselfconscious warmth that made him smile, and think that his grandfather, whom the Princess had described as such an exceptional, unorthodox Jew, must have been exactly like Mordecai—one of those rare, fiery souls whose far-reaching dreams feed the whole rest of the world, without which ordinary life would shrink down to nothing smaller than the narrow instincts of insects. That quiet, restless ache mingled with his constant, anxious care for Gwendolen, and pushed him to leave the synagogue early, and head straight for the quay, his old favorite spot to wander, hoping he’d be there in time to see the Grandcourts’ boat come back in. He resolved then, that if he saw them, he would walk straight over to greet them, openly, and ignore every petty glare Grandcourt might send his way.

The sun had sunk low behind a bank of heavy cloud, leaving only faint streaks of honeyed gold to kiss the tops of the choppy waves, whipped by a sharp, brisk wind. Deronda wandered slowly along the waterfront, and noticed the crowd of onlookers clustering tight, all their eyes fixed on a rowboat speeding toward shore, two men straining at the oars. Above the babble of half a dozen different languages, Deronda didn’t waste time calling out for news. He pushed his way to the front of the crowd, watching, his chest tight. People held up telescopes, yelling out wild guesses: one man shouted that it was the English milord who’d gone out sailing, drowned. Another swore the limp figure draped in the bottom of the boat was miladi. A French tourist who had no telescope joked loudly that the English husband had probably rowed his wife out to drown her, as was their national custom— a quip an English skipper immediately dismissed as utter nonsense, declaring plainly that the still, reclining figure was most definitely a woman.

Deronda’s blood ran cold. Every possible, terrible scenario flashed through his mind in the space of a single oar-stroke, wondering if the woman they were hauling out of the waves was Gwendolen. Soon there was no doubt left. The boat bumped against the shore, and he saw her, half pushing herself up on trembling hands, swaddled in heavy tarpaulins and thick pea jackets, pale as a corpse pulled from the grave, shivering so hard her teeth must be chattering, her wet dark hair streaming down her shoulders, a wild, dazed horror in her eyes as if she’d woken in a world where divine judgment was about to fall, and every stranger around her had come to drag her away.

The first rower jumped to shore soaking wet and ran off to get help, the crowd of sailors blocking Deronda’s path so he could only stand and watch as the rough, bronzed fishermen lifted Gwendolen tenderly, carefully, out of the boat, her wet clothes clinging heavy to her legs, making her weak limbs even harder to move. Then her wild, wandering eyes landed right on Deronda, standing straight in front of her, and she immediately tried to stretch her arms out to him, as if she’d been searching for him the whole time, calling out in a muffled, shaking voice: “It’s come. It’s come! He’s dead.”

“Hush, hush,” Deronda said, his voice steady, authoritative, soft as he stepped forward. “Calm yourself, you’re safe now.” He turned to the men holding her. “I’m a relation of her husband’s. Get her back to the Hotel *Italia* as fast as you can. I will take care of every last arrangement.”

He stayed behind a moment to hear from the remaining boatman: Grandcourt had gone over the side in the sudden gust of wind, and sunk before they could reach him, his empty sailing boat still drifting out on the waves. He and his friend had heard Gwendolen scream, rowed over as fast as they could, and pulled her straight out of the water when she’d jumped in after him, before she could do herself any real harm.

Deronda hurried back to the hotel at once, to make sure the best doctor in the city was called immediately. Once he’d confirmed that, he sent two telegrams: one to Sir Hugo, begging him to come to Genoa as soon as possible, and one to the rectory where Gwendolen’s uncle Mr. Gascoigne lived, so someone could break the news gently to her mother. A long-forgotten memory of Gwendolen’s, from a time she’d been half mad with grief and ready to confess everything, had popped into his head unbidden: she’d said once, that the only comfort she’d ever craved in the worst moments of her life, was her mother’s presence.
