**“Messire Bailiff,”** interjected Denys, **“do you lock up every witness, then?”**

Flattered by the title, the alderman grew talkative. **“In cases of blood, we hold all who might flee justice. Women are fickle creatures—today they swear truth, tomorrow they recant. This one wept for betraying five men to the gallows. Had they been women, we might have trusted her to return. But no—she stays.”** He adjusted his spectacles. **“Besides, she was one of them.”**

Denys and Gerard protested in unison.

**“Spare your outrage,”** said the alderman dryly. **“Here it is, plain as day.”** He tapped a parchment. **“‘Jean Hardy confessed that the girl Manon lured victims for Georges Vipont, her lover—hanged last month. Since his death, she has raged against the band, blaming them for his capture. It is his belief she betrayed them out of spite.’”**

**“His belief?”** Gerard’s voice sharpened. **“What weight has the word of a thief, desperate for revenge? Is a criminal not a liar by trade? And here, with motive to damn her?”** He stepped forward, eyes burning. **“Sir, can you not see the difference between those hardened men and this broken girl? Would you hang her beside them? She risked her life for ours!”**

The alderman shifted, discomfort flickering across his face. Gerard dropped to his knees. **“I beg you—spare her! Denys and I owe her our lives. Must we watch her die and then cut our own throats at her feet?”**

The alderman huffed, torn between duty and pity. **“Enough! Daniel—fetch the town clerk.”** When the clerk arrived, the alderman muttered, **“This boy’s heart is set on saving her. Can we bend the law?”**

The clerk, a stickler, shook his head. **“She was no accomplice. Had she been, we might have pardoned her testimony.”**

**“She did better than an accomplice!”** Gerard cried. **“She stopped the crime and ran to expose it!”**

The alderman waved him off. **“Law is law.”** Yet after a heated debate—and spurred by the clerk’s stubbornness—he relented. Manon was freed on bond, with Denys depositing gold as surety.

As they left the courthouse, two processions clogged the street: prisoners bound for the gallows, and a silent train bearing bones to consecrated ground. The crowds jeered as the cart of condemned men jostled against the mourners’ bier—a grim collision of crime and consequence.

Gerard, solemn, followed the dead to their burial. After the rites, he sought a mass for their souls, haggling with the priest over the fee—until they discovered a shared cunning in piety and thrift. Over wine, the priest shared a tale of his own: how he’d once outwitted his flock by charging burial fees in advance, lest they cheat the Church by dying on the gallows. **“Greed against greed,”** he chuckled. **“They hanged fewer men after that.”**

As dusk fell, Gerard returned to the inn, where Manon wept in her chamber. The landlord, sensing profit in her notoriety, kept her safe. Outside, the town buzzed with the day’s drama—justice served, mercy granted, and the dead at last laid to rest.

But in the churchyard, a skull rolled loose from a disturbed pillar—the defiant bones of a nobleman who’d demanded an upright burial. **“No base man shall pass over my stomach!”** his descendant laughed, kicking the relic aside. The priest sighed. **“Pride survives even death.”**

And so the day ended—with the living weighed by their deeds, the dead by their dust, and the threads of fate tangled yet unbroken.
