There are those who believe that the deepest tragedy lies not in Prometheus bound *after* he has stolen the celestial fire, but *before*. Before he secures the torch in his staff to bring it to mortals, he is thrust by the brute forces of rigid systems into a solitude of despised ideas. Fastened by the crushing weight of poverty and sickness, he exists in a lonely realm where many pass by, yet none truly look.

"Second sight" is a banner flying over contested terrain. Yet, it is undeniable knowledge that some souls possess yearnings and visions that materialize as images with prophetic power. To them, the deeds they intend to perform rise before them in complete form, casting a coercive shadow; the events they crave or fear grow rapidly from a tiny seed, fed by countless impressions. These individuals are not necessarily less logical or saner than the ordinary calculators of commerce; indeed, their natures may harbor manifold openings, like the hundred gates of Thebes, allowing a greater and more varied influx than through a single, guarded door. Of course, there exist abject specimens of the visionary, as there is a tiny mammal you might trap inside the finger of your glove. This small relative of the elephant causes no harm, but what great mental or social archetype remains entirely free from specimens whose insignificance is both ugly and harmful? One hesitates to consider everything the word "patriot" encompasses, or the jostling that might occur at the final judgment for those who claimed authorship, carrying volumes in their hands or on carts.

This defense of inevitable kinship serves to introduce facts concerning Mordecai. A figure had bitten itself into Daniel Deronda's mind as a new, compelling question, though the interest felt was nothing more than a vague, expectant suspension. The consumptive-looking Jew, seemingly a fervid scholar earning his bread through quiet handicraft, fit into none of Deronda's anticipations.

For Mordecai, however, the effect of their meeting was profound. For many winters, as his physical life ebbed and spiritual loneliness widened, his entire passion had concentrated on finding a young ear into which he could pour his mind as a testament. He sought a soul kindred enough to accept the spiritual product of his brief, painful life as a mission to be executed. Remarkably, the hopefulness that often serves as a beneficent illusion for consumptive patients was in Mordecai wholly diverted from the prospect of bodily recovery. Instead, it flowed entirely into this yearning for transmission. The desperate panting of his desire, rising from overwhelming discouragement, had hardened into a hope, which in turn became a confident belief. Rather than checking his resolve, the clear conception of his hastening decline intensified his expectant faith in a prophecy destined to fulfill itself in a brief span.

Several years had passed since Mordecai first began measuring men with a keen, searching glance, looking for a possibility that gradually sharpened into a distinct concept. At first, this distinctness was achieved chiefly through contrast: he sought a man who differed from himself. Tracing the reasons for his rebuffs and the hindrances that beset him, he imagined a counterpart who would possess all the elements necessary for sympathy but embodied them differently. This man must be a Jew, intellectually cultured and morally fervid—a nature ready to be filled from Mordecai's—but his face and frame must be beautiful and strong. He must be accustomed to the refinements of social life, his voice flowing with a full and easy current, his circumstances free from sordid need. He must glorify the possibilities of the Jew, not sit and wonder as Mordecai did, bearing the stamp of his people amidst the signs of poverty and failing breath. Sensitive to physical characteristics, Mordecai, both abroad and in England, studied pictures as well as people. In idle hours, he lingered in the National Gallery seeking paintings that might feed his hopefulness with grave and noble types of the human form, suitable to men of his own race. Yet, he returned in disappointment. The instances scattered thinly across European galleries, where the fortune or selection of the chief masters had gifted art with a face at once young, grand, and beautiful, failed to meet his standard. If any melancholy existed in these portraits, it was not a feeble passivity, but entered into a foreshadowed capability of heroism.

Observant persons may recall his emaciated figure and dark eyes deep in their sockets as he stood before a picture that touched him to new or habitual meditation. He commonly wore a cloth cap with black fur around it, which no painter would have asked him to remove. Spectators likely viewed him as an odd-looking Jew, perhaps extracting money from the gallery, and Mordecai, when he looked at them, was perfectly aware of the impression he made. Experience had rendered him morbidly alive to the effect of poverty and other physical disadvantages in cheapening ideas, unless those ideas were akin to Peter the Hermit's alarm bells for the rabble. However, he was too sane and generous to attribute his spiritual banishment solely to the excusable prejudices of others; certain incapacities of his own had sealed the sentence of exclusion. Hence, his imagination constructed another man—one who would be something more ample than the "second soul" bestowed, according to Cabalist notions, to aid the insufficient first. This imagined man would be a blooming human life, ready to incorporate all that was worthiest in an existence whose visible, palpable part was burning itself fast away. His inward need for this conception of an expanded, prolonged self was reflected as an outward necessity. The thoughts of his heart, that ancient phrase best shadows the truth, seemed too precious, too closely interwoven with the growth of things to lack a further destiny. As the more beautiful, stronger, and more executive self took shape in his mind, he loved it beforehand with an affection half-identifying, half-contemplative and grateful.

Mordecai's mind worked so constantly in images that his coherent trains of thought often resembled the significant dreams attributed to sleepers by waking persons in their most inventive moments. Nay, they often resembled genuine dreams in their way of breaking off the passage from the known to the unknown. For a long while, he habitually thought of the Being answering to his need as one distantly approaching or turning his back, darkly painted against a golden sky. The reason for the golden sky lay in one of Mordecai's habits. He was keenly alive to some poetic aspects of London, and a favorite resort, when strength and leisure allowed, was some bridge, especially about sunrise or sunset. Even when bending over watch-wheels and trinkets or seated in a small upper room looking out on dingy bricks and cracked windows, his imagination spontaneously planted him on a spot with a far-stretching scene. His thoughts traveled in wide spaces, and whenever possible, he tried to experience the influences of a large sky. Leaning on the parapet of Blackfriars' Bridge and gazing meditatively, the breadth and calm of the river, with its long vista half-hazy, half-luminous, along with the grand, dim masses of tall buildings marking world-commerce, and the oncoming of boats and barges from the distant silence into sound and color, entered his mood. They blended indistinguishably with his thinking, acting like a fine symphony that bears up our spiritual wings. Thus, the figure representing Mordecai's longing was mentally seen darkened by the excess of light in the aerial background. But in the inevitable progress of his imagination toward fuller detail, he ceased to see the figure with its back turned. It began to advance, and a face became discernible. The attributes of youth, beauty, refinement, Jewish birth, and noble gravity turned into hardly individual but typical form and color, gathered from his memories of faces seen among the Jews of Holland and Bohemia, and from the paintings that revived those memories.

Reverently, it must be said of this mature spiritual need that it was akin to the boy's and girl's picturing of the future beloved. Yet, the stirrings of such young desire are feeble compared with the passionate current of an ideal life straining to embody itself, made intense by resistance to imminent dissolution. The visionary form became a companion and auditor, keeping a place not only in the waking imagination but in those lighter dreams of slumber where it is truest to say, "I sleep, but my heart waketh"—when the disturbing, trivial story of yesterday is charged with the impassioned purpose of years.

Of late, the urgency of irremediable time, measured by the gradual choking of life, had turned Mordecai's trust into an agitated watch for a fulfillment that must be at hand. Was the bell on the verge of tolling, the sentence about to be executed? The Deliverer's footsteps must be near—the Deliverer who was to rescue Mordecai's spiritual travail from oblivion and grant it an abiding-place in the best heritage of his people. An insane exaggeration of his own value, even if his ideas were as true and precious as those of Columbus or Newton, many would count this yearning as the sublimer part for a man to declare: "If not I, then another," holding cheap the meaning of his own life. But the fuller nature desires to be an agent, to create, not merely to look on. Strong love hungers to bless, not merely to behold blessing. While there is warmth enough in the sun to feed an energetic life, there will still be men who feel, "I am lord of this moment's change, and will charge it with my soul."
