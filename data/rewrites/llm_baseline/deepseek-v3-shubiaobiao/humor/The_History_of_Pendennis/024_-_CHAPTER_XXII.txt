The Major’s letter, naturally, sent Doctor Portman galloping to Fairoaks with the grim enthusiasm of a man delivering bad news. There’s a peculiar energy in the step of a person bearing unpleasant tidings—as if the sooner the blow is struck, the sooner the healing can begin. And so the good doctor, after reading the missive aloud to his wife and daughter (accompanied by a symphony of condemnations about young Pen’s descent into ruin), marched off to break the widow’s heart—or so he assumed.

But Helen Pendennis, it turned out, had already read Pen’s letter. And instead of collapsing into despair, she felt an odd sense of relief. The storm she had long feared had finally broken, and her boy—her reckless, foolish, beloved boy—was coming home to her, repentant and tender. What more could she want?

The Rector’s lecture on duty, discipline, and the sacred importance of a university degree bounced off her like raindrops off a duck’s back. What good was a degree if it made Pen miserable? Why must he be thrust into a den of temptation, far from his mother’s care? As for his debts—well, of course they would be paid. Wasn’t his father’s money his own?

Meanwhile, a certain domestic awkwardness had arisen between Pen and his adoptive sister, Laura. Once upon a time, their affectionate greetings had included a fraternal kiss—a habit of childhood now abruptly abandoned. The girl he had left behind had transformed into a young lady of sixteen, tall and slender, who greeted him with a curtsey, a proffered hand, and a blush that spread precisely over the spot his lips had once claimed.

Now, describing female beauty is a hazardous business (and, frankly, not one I care for—virtue being infinitely more attractive than a pretty face), but Laura Bell at sixteen was undeniably striking. Pale, with a faint rosy tint that flared under emotion, she had eyes so large and luminous that critics accused her of deploying them like weapons. Her lashes, long and dark, provided a modest veil, though some insisted even this was calculated for effect. Her mouth might have been deemed too wide by the finicky, but her smile was irresistible, revealing perfect teeth, and her voice was so soft it seemed to hum like a melody.

Laura had not been blind to Pen’s recent failings. His letters home had grown rare and terse. His vacations were spent in grand houses, not with his mother. His talk had become cynical, his manners careless. He dismissed church, avoided walks, and lounged in dressing gowns while Helen fretted. And then there was the Fotheringay scandal—an actress! A drunken father! The very thought made Laura’s blood boil.

Yet the moment she heard Pen was in trouble, her anger evaporated. Debts? A trifle! A failed degree? The examiners must have been prejudiced. She rallied to his defense with a fervor that left Doctor Portman baffled and Helen tearfully grateful.

That night, as the two women lay awake, Laura hatched a plan. For years, Helen had supported her as if she were a charity case. Laura would now repay her—with interest. The next day, they would withdraw her modest savings (a sum Helen had prudently set aside) and send it to Pen. No more debts, no more suffering. Helen, overwhelmed, could only embrace her and weep.

When Pen finally arrived—traveling by coach to save money, shivering atop it like a penitent—he found Fairoaks waiting, warm and forgiving. No reproaches, no lectures. Just love.

But peace did not settle easily on Pen. He sold his horse, curtailed his indulgences, and moped about like a ghost. He avoided Dr. Portman, attended church dutifully, and scribbled gloomy verses. Laura, watching him, suggested—with some hesitation—that he return to Oxbridge. Not for glory, but for redemption.

And so, with a sigh, he went. A plucked man is a pitiable creature at university—a ghost among the living. Pen kept to himself, studied grimly, and emerged with his degree. But London, when he visited, offered no comfort. His uncle received him coldly.

Back at Fairoaks, the days dragged. Helen grew paler, Laura quieter. Pen, trapped between guilt and inertia, wrote tragedies and dreamed of escape. The house, once lively, now echoed with silence.

And so the prodigal returned—not to feasting, but to the quiet ache of home. The fatted calf had been killed, but the taste was bittersweet.
