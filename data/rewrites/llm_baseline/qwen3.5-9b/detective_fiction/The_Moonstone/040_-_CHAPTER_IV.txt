I possess no words to describe my own internal state.

My impression is that the shock delivered to me completely suspended both my capacity for thought and my faculty of feeling. Indeed, I could not possibly recall what I was doing when Betteredge joined me upon the shore. It is solely on his testimony that I know I laughed when he inquired what was the matter, and that, handing him the nightshirt, I commanded him to solve the riddle for himself.

Regarding whatever conversation transpired between us on the beach, I retain absolutely no memory whatsoever. The first location where I can now perceive myself clearly is the plantation of firs. Betteredge and I were walking back toward the house together; Betteredge was assuring me that I would be able to face the ordeal, and that he likewise would, once we had consumed a glass of grog.

The scene shifts from the plantation to Betteredge's small sitting-room. My previous resolve never to enter Rachel's house has vanished. I feel a profound gratitude for the coolness, shade, and silence of this room. I drink the grog—a perfect novelty to me at that hour—which my faithful old friend mixes with icy water drawn from the well. Under any other circumstances, such a drink would merely stupefy me. However, in my current condition, it sharpens my nerves. I begin "to face it," as Betteredge predicted. And Betteredge, on his part, begins to "face it" as well.

The portrait I am now painting of myself will, I suspect, appear exceedingly strange to the discerning reader. Placed in a situation entirely without parallel, what is my first recourse? Do I isolate myself from all human contact? Do I devote my mind to analyzing the abominable impossibility that nonetheless confronts me as an undeniable fact? Do I hasten back to London by the earliest train to consult the highest authorities and initiate a thorough investigation immediately? No. I accept the shelter of a house I had sworn never to degrade myself by entering again; and I sit, consuming spirits and water in the company of an elderly servant, at ten o'clock in the morning. Is this the behavior expected of a man in my terrible predicament? I can only answer that the sight of old Betteredge's familiar face was an inexpressible comfort to me, and that the consumption of his grog aided me, as I believe nothing else could have, in the state of total physical and mental prostration into which I had fallen. I offer this sole excuse for my actions; and I can only admire that unvarying preservation of dignity and strict logical consistency of conduct that distinguishes every man and woman who reads these lines, in every emergency of their lives from cradle to grave.

"Now, Mr. Franklin, there is one thing certain, at any rate," said Betteredge, tossing the nightshirt onto the table between us and pointing to it as if it were a living creature capable of hearing him. "_He_ is a liar, to start with."

This comforting perspective was not the one that presented itself to my mind.

"I am as innocent of any knowledge regarding the taking of the Diamond as you are," I replied. "Yet there is the witness against me! The paint on the nightshirt and the name embroidered upon it are facts."

Betteredge lifted my glass and pressed it persuasively into my hand.

"Facts?" he repeated. "Take another drop of grog, Mr. Franklin, and you will overcome the weakness of believing in facts! Foul play, sir!" he continued, lowering his voice confidentially. "That is how I interpret the riddle. Foul play somewhere—and you and I must uncover it. Was there nothing else in the tin case when you reached inside it?"

The question instantly recalled the letter in my pocket. I withdrew it and unfolded it. It was a lengthy document, written in close script. I scanned impatiently for the signature at the conclusion. "Rosanna Spearman."

As I read the name, a sudden illumination struck my mind, and a fresh suspicion arose from this new light.

"Stop!" I exclaimed. "Rosanna Spearman came to my aunt from a reformatory? Rosanna Spearman was once a thief?"

"There is no denying that, Mr. Franklin. So what of it now, pray tell?"

"So what of it now? How do we know she may not have stolen the Diamond after all? How do we know she may not have deliberately smeared my nightshirt with the paint?"

Betteredge laid his hand upon my arm, halting me before I could utter further words.

"You will be cleared of this, Mr. Franklin, beyond all shadow of a doubt. But I trust you will not be cleared in _that_ manner. Read what the letter says, sir. Out of respect for the girl's memory, read what it says."

I sensed the earnestness with which he spoke—felt it as a friendly rebuke. "You shall form your own judgment on her letter," I said. "I will read it aloud."

I began—and read these lines:

"Sir—I have something to confess to you. A confession that entails much misery may sometimes be expressed in very few words. This confession requires but three words. I love you."

The letter slipped from my grasp. I looked at Betteredge. "By Heaven," I said, "what does this mean?"

He seemed to recoil from answering the question.

"You and Limping Lucy were alone together this morning, sir," he said. "Did she say nothing concerning Rosanna Spearman?"

"She never even mentioned Rosanna Spearman's name."

"Please return to the letter, Mr. Franklin. I tell you plainly, I cannot bring myself to distress you further after what you have endured. Let her speak for herself, sir. And finish your grog. For your own sake, finish your grog."

I resumed the reading of the letter.

"It would be deeply disgraceful for me to reveal this to you if I were a living woman when you read it. I shall be dead and gone, sir, by the time you discover my letter. It is this certainty that gives me boldness. Not even my grave will remain to testify to my existence. I may own the truth—with the quicksand waiting to conceal me the moment the words are written.

"Furthermore, you will find your nightshirt in my hiding-place, bearing the smear of paint upon it; and you will wonder how it came to be hidden by me, and why I remained silent during my lifetime? I have but one reason to offer. I committed these strange acts because I loved you.

"I shall not burden you with details of my own life prior to your arrival at my lady's house. Lady Verinder rescued me from a reformatory. I had entered the reformatory from prison. I was imprisoned because I was a thief. I became a thief because my mother descended into the streets when I was a mere child. My mother went to the streets because the gentleman who was my father abandoned her. There is no need to recount such a commonplace tale at length. It is frequently told in the newspapers.

"Lady Verinder was very kind to me, and Mr. Betteredge was very kind to me. Those two, along with the matron at the reformatory, are the only good souls I have ever encountered in my entire life. I might have managed to survive in my station—not happily, perhaps—but I might have survived, had you not come calling. I do not blame _you_, sir. It is my fault—entirely my fault.

"Do you recall when you emerged from among the sandhills that morning, seeking Mr. Betteredge? You appeared like a prince in a fairy-tale. You were like a lover in a dream. You were the most adorable human creature I had ever beheld. Something akin to the happy life I had never known leaped within me the instant I set eyes upon you. Do not laugh at this if you can help it. Oh, if only I could make you comprehend how serious it is to _me!_

"I returned to the house and wrote your name and mine in my work-box, drawing a true lovers' knot beneath them. Then, some devil—or rather, some good angel—whispered to me, 'Go and look in the mirror.' The mirror revealed—never mind what. I was too foolish to heed the warning. I proceeded to grow fonder and fonder of you, as though I were a lady of your own rank and the most beautiful creation your eyes had ever rested upon. I strove—Oh, dear, how I strove—to gain your attention. If you knew how I used to weep at night with the misery and humiliation of your never deigning to acknowledge me, you might perhaps pity me and grant me a glance now and then to sustain me.

"It would have been a merciful glance, perhaps, had you known how I detested Miss Rachel. I believe I discovered you were in love with her before you realized it yourself. She used to present you with roses to wear in your buttonhole. Ah, Mr. Franklin, you wore _my_ roses far more often than either you or she imagined! The only solace I possessed at that time was placing my rose secretly in your glass of water in lieu of hers—and then discarding her rose.

"If she had truly been as lovely as you perceived her, I might have endured it better. No; I believe I should have become even more spiteful toward her. Imagine you dressed Miss Rachel in a servant's attire and removed her ornaments? I do not know the point of my writing thus. It cannot be denied that she possessed a poor figure; she was too slender.
