Young Harry Warrington's sudden rebellion caught Madame de Bernstein completely off guard, leaving her with no alternative response but the prompt, fiery eruption of anger that marked the end of the previous scene. As she stormed from the room, darting two piercing glares at Lady Fanny and her mother, Lady Maria remained oblivious, her attention fixed entirely on her tambour-frame. She barely lifted her head from the embroidery to watch her aunt retreat or to register the sharp, silent exchanges passing between the mother-in-law and her daughter.

"So, despite all warnings, you have yielded, madam?" the maternal gaze seemed to inquire.

"Yield to what?" asked Lady Fanny, her eyes wide with feigned confusion. Yet, innocence was a mask she wore poorly. She lacked the practiced composure of Maria, who, if guilty, would have known exactly how to craft a visage capable of deflecting blame. Whatever truth lay behind Fanny's eyes, her mother stared at her with a glare intense enough to peel flesh, wishing she could pluck them from their sockets.

However, Lady Castlewood possessed no immediate means to extract them, unlike the barbarous monsters described in *King Lear*. When her ladyship intended such violence, she first embraced her daughter with a simpering affection, only to deliver the blow in privacy.

"So you have decided against accompanying the old lady to Tunbridge Wells?" Lady Castlewood asked, masking her displeasure with a perfectly bred smile.

"And who could blame our cousin?" interjected My Lord, breaking the silence. "A young man requires more than idle holidays. He must blend utility with pleasure; perhaps visit the fiddlers and pump-rooms of Tunbridge or Bath later. Mr. Warrington must learn to manage a great American estate; let him observe how ours are conducted here. Will has already shown him the kennels and stables, and the customary games—I believe you play them well enough, cousin. After the harvest, we shall introduce him to English fowling and shooting; in winter, we will take him hunting. Although relations with our kinswoman in Virginia have grown cold, the blood remains the same. Before sending our cousin back to his mother, let us demonstrate what constitutes the life of an English gentleman at home. I have pressed him to stay recently, partly to sport with him, but chiefly to read with him."

Lord Castlewood spoke with such unfeigned candor that his wife, half-brother, and sister stood bewildered, unsure of his intent. These three often conspired in quiet corners, murmuring against the head of the household. When he adopted this open tone, however, his meaning became a riddle unsolvable for months. It was the unwritten convention that Lord Castlewood harbored a noble desire to cultivate domestic affections and educate his young relative, taking a genuine fancy to the lad and wishing Harry to remain under his roof for some time.

"What scheme does Castlewood pursue now?" William whispered to his mother and sister as they slipped down the corridor. "Wait! By George, I have it!"

"What, William?"

"He intends to lure him into gambling, to win back the Virginia estate."

"But the lad possesses no debt to pay if he loses," remarked the mother.

"If my brother has no design in view, may I..."

"Hush! Of course he has a plan. What is it?"

"It cannot be Maria—she is nearly as old as Harry's mother," mused Mr. William.

"Nonsense! With her aged features, sandy hair, and freckled complexion! Impossible!" cried Lady Fanny, exhaling a sigh.

"Of course, your ladyship fancied the Iroquois, too!" laughed the mother.

"I trust I understand my station and duties better than that, madam. Even if I had felt a spark for him, that would not compel me to wed him. You have taught me as much."

"My Lady Fanny!"

"I am certain you married our father without loving him. You have confessed this a thousand times!"

"And if you did not love him prior to the wedding, you certainly did not fall in love afterward," broke in Mr. William, laughing. "Fan and I recall how our honored parents used to quarrel. Did we not, Fan? And little Esmond kept the peace."

"Do not dredge up those dreadful, vulgar scenes, William!" cried the mother. "When your father drank too freely, he became a madman; his conduct must serve as a warning to you, sir, who share this terrible habit."

"I am sure, madam, you found little happiness in marrying the man you did not love, and your ladyship's title has brought scant fortune compared to many trade widows." Lady Fanny whined. "Consider the grocer's widow from London Bridge who moved into our square at Kensington. Her daughters possess three gowns where I have one. While they are attended by merely a man and two maids, I know they feast and drink a thousand times better than we do, subsisting on scraps of cold meat and flanked by our great, flagrant, insolent, lazy lackeys!"

"He! He! Glad I dine at the Palace rather than at home!" chuckled Mr. Will. Through his aunt's influence with Count Puffendorff, Groom of the Royal Powder-Closet, he held a minor court position as Deputy Powder.

"Why should I not find contentment without a title save my own?" continued Lady Frances. "Many do. I dare say they are even happier in America."

"Yes—with a mother-in-law who is a perfect Turk and Tartar, as I hear, with Indian war-whoops echoing around you, and the constant peril of losing your scalp or being devoured by a wild beast whenever you attend church."

"I would not step foot in church," declared Lady Fanny.

"You would go with anyone who asked you, Fan!" roared Mr. Will. "And so would old Maria, and so would any woman, that is the fact." He laughed at his own wit.

"Pray, good friends, what causes this merriment?" asked Madame Bernstein, peering into the gallery from the tapestried door.

Will explained that his mother and sister were embroiled in a dispute—a novelty only to outsiders, given Madame Bernstein's familiarity with their bickering—because Fanny desired to marry their cousin, the "wild Indian," and the Countess forbade it. Fanny immediately protested this fabrication. Since her mother first ordered her to avoid speaking to the young gentleman, she had exchanged fewer than two words with him. She knew her place and feared neither scalping nor being eaten by bears.

Madame Bernstein appeared perplexed. "If he is not staying for you, for whom is he staying?" she asked. "In the homes to which he has been led, you ensured no woman was presented to him unless she was a fright or confined to a nursery. I suspect the boy is too proud to fall in love with a dairymaid, Will."

"Hmph! That is a matter of taste, ma'am," shrugged Mr. William.

"The taste of Mr. William Esmond, as you say; but not of yonder boy. An Esmond, nurtured by his grandfather, would not seek a courtesan in the kitchen."

"Well, ma'am, every man to his taste. A fellow might fare worse than my brother's servants' hall. Besides Fan, there is only the maidens or old Maria to choose from."

"Maria! Impossible!" Yet, as the words left her lips, a sudden thought struck Madame Bernstein. This elderly Calypso might have captivated her young Telemachus. She recalled instances of young men infatuated by older women. She remembered Harry Warrington's recent, unexplained absences, which she had attributed to his love for field sports, and how often Maria Esmond vanished simultaneously. Whispers in garden temples, casual hand-squeezes in twilight corridors, and lingering glances on the stairs suggested a lively romance, an intimate knowledge of the world, and perhaps considerable personal experience. These possibilities danced in her mind, contradicting her initial dismissal of the idea as impossible.

"Impossible, ma'am! I don't know," Will persisted. "My mother warned Fan off."

"Oh, your mother did warn Fanny?"

"Certainly, my dear Baroness!"

"Did she not? Did she not pinch Fanny's arm black-and-blue? Did they not fight over it?"

"Nonsense, William! For shame!" cried both implicated ladies in unison.

"Now, since we have heard how rich he is, perhaps it is sour grapes. And since he has been barred from the young bird, perhaps he is hunting the old one."

"William, how can you speak of Lady Suffolk to your aunt?"

A grin spread across the young gentleman's face. "Because Lady Suffolk was a special favorite at Court? Well, others have succeeded her."

"Sir!" cried Madame Bernstein, sensing offense.

"They have, I say. Who, pray, is Lady Yarmouth now? Did not old Lady Suffolk fall in love with George Berkeley and marry him when she was advanced in years? Nay, ma'am, if I remember aright—and we hear much town-talk at our table—Harry Estridge went mad over you when you were rising twenty; he would have changed your name a third time had you permitted it."

This allusion to a notorious affair from her later years did not anger Madame Bernstein as her previous remark about her favor at George II's court might have. Instead, it put her in a cheerful mood.
