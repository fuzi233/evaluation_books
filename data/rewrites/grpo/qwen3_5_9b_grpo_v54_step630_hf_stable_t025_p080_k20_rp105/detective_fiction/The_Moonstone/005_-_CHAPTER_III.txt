The dilemma of how to commence this narrative properly has plagued me, leading to two distinct failures. First, I scratched my head until it ached, yielding nothing but confusion. Second, I consulted my daughter, Penelope, which resulted in a proposal so novel it nearly derailed the story entirely.

Penelope's suggestion was that I chronicle the events day by day, commencing with the specific date we received word that Mr. Franklin Blake was arriving. She argued that anchoring memory to such a rigid schedule would force the past to reveal itself. The only hurdle was retrieving the dates themselves, a task Penelope offered to solve by consulting her diary—a habit instilled during her schooling and maintained ever since. However, when I countered with the idea that she should narrate the tale directly from her journal, she reacted with a fierce glare and a flushed countenance. "My journal is for my own private eye," she declared, "and no living soul shall ever know its contents but myself." When I pressed her on this secrecy, she simply dismissed me with a sharp "Fiddlesticks!" to which I replied, "Sweethearts."

Accepting her initial plan, I must record that on a Wednesday morning, the twenty-fourth of May, 1848, I was summoned to my lady's private sitting room.

"Gabriel," my lady said, "there is news that will astonish you. Franklin Blake has returned from abroad. He has been residing with his father in London and is arriving tomorrow to stay until next month, in time for Rachel's birthday."

Had I possessed a hat in my hand, nothing short of profound respect would have stopped me from hurling it against the ceiling. I had not seen Mr. Franklin since his boyhood, when he lived within these walls. As I recalled him, he was the finest boy ever known for spinning tops or breaking windows. Miss Rachel, who was present, offered a starkly different recollection. "She" remembered him as the most atrocious tyrant who ever tormented a doll and the harshest driver of an exhausted child in string harness that England could produce. "I burn with indignation, and I ache with fatigue," Miss Rachel summarized, "whenever I think of Franklin Blake."

You may wonder how Mr. Franklin managed to vanish from his homeland for so many years, growing from a boy into a man abroad. The answer lies in his father's unfortunate status as the next heir to a Dukedom, a claim he could not legally prove.

Here is the brief history: My lady's eldest sister wed the celebrated Mr. Blake, a man renowned equally for his immense wealth and his protracted legal battle. For years, he besieged the nation's tribunals, attempting to displace the current Duke and secure the title for himself. He drained the pockets of countless lawyers and tore apart the lives of innocent bystanders in his quest. Yet, his wife and two of his three children died before the courts finally rejected his claims. When the dust settled and the rightful Duke remained in possession, Mr. Blake concluded that the only way to balance the scales was to deny his country the honor of educating his son. "How can I trust my native institutions?" he asked, "after the manner in which they have treated *me*?" Compounded by a deep-seated dislike for all boys, including his own, the decision was inevitable. Master Franklin was removed from England and sent to institutions in Germany, a land his father trusted. Meanwhile, Mr. Blake remained in England, continuing his parliamentary career and publishing an unfinished treatise on the Duke's legitimacy that remains incomplete to this day.

Thank heavens for that explanation! We need trouble our heads no longer about Mr. Blake, the senior. Let the Dukedom handle him; let us focus on the Diamond.

The Diamond, however, brings us back to Mr. Franklin, the innocent instrument by which that cursed jewel entered our home.

Our dear boy did not forget us. He corresponded periodically, writing to my lady, Miss Rachel, and myself. Prior to his departure, he had borrowed from me a ball of string, a four-bladed knife, and seven shillings and sixpence—a coin of a color I have never seen again and never hope to witness. His letters mostly concerned further borrowings. Through my lady, I learned of his progress abroad. After mastering the German institutions, he turned to France, then Italy, becoming something of a universal genius among them. He wrote, painted, sang, played, and composed, though I suspect he borrowed the inspiration for all these arts just as he had borrowed from me. Upon reaching adulthood, his mother's fortune of seven hundred pounds a year fell to him, only to vanish through him as quickly as water through a sieve. The more money he possessed, the more he craved; there was a hole in Mr. Franklin's pocket that no needle could stitch. Yet, wherever he went, his lively, easy demeanor made him welcome. He lived everywhere, his address often listed as "Post Office, Europe—to be left till called for." He resolved twice to return to England, but twice some unmentionable woman stood in his path. His third attempt, as you know, succeeded. On Thursday, the twenty-fifth of May, we were to behold for the first time the man he had become. He was of good blood, possessed high courage, and was twenty-five years old by our reckoning. Thus, you now know as much of Mr. Franklin Blake as I did before his arrival.

The Thursday was a summer day of exquisite beauty. My lady and Miss Rachel, not expecting Mr. Franklin until dinner, drove out to lunch with friends in the neighborhood.

Once they departed, I inspected the bedroom prepared for our guest, ensuring everything was in order. As the butler of my lady's household and, at my own request, the steward of the late Sir John's cellar, I felt it my duty to fetch some of our famous Latour claret. I set the bottles in the warm summer air to remove the chill before dinner. Deciding that what is beneficial for old wine is equally good for old age, I took my beehive chair to sit in the back court. Just as I settled in, a sound like the soft beating of a drum reached my ears from the terrace in front of the house.

Rounding the terrace, I discovered three mahogany-skinned Indians dressed in white linen frocks and trousers, gazing upward at the house. Closer inspection revealed they carried small hand-drums slung across their chests. Behind them stood a delicate, light-haired English boy carrying a bag. I initially judged them to be strolling conjurers, with the boy serving as their apprentice carrying their tools. One of the three, who spoke English and displayed manners of remarkable elegance, confirmed my assessment. He politely requested permission to perform his tricks in the presence of the lady of the house.

Now, I am not a sour old man. I generally favor amusement and am the last to distrust someone merely because their skin tone differs slightly from my own. However, even the best of us have weaknesses. My particular flaw, when I know a valuable plate-basket is left unattended on a pantry table, is to be instantly reminded of its vulnerability by the sight of a stranger whose manners surpass my own. Consequently, I informed the Indian that the lady was away and warned him and his party to leave the premises. He returned a beautiful bow, and they departed. On my side, I returned to my beehive chair, settled on the sunny side of the court, and, if the truth must be admitted, drifted into a state bordering on sleep.

I was abruptly roused by my daughter Penelope running out at me as if the house were ablaze. What did she want? She demanded the immediate arrest of the three Indian jugglers. Her reasoning was that they knew Mr. Franklin was coming from London and intended to commit some mischief against him.

The mention of Mr. Franklin's name snapped me awake. I opened my eyes and urged Penelope to explain.

It transpired that Penelope had just visited the lodge, where she had chatted with the lodge-keeper's daughter. The two girls had witnessed the Indians leaving after I had warned them off, followed by the little boy. Suspecting the boy was being mistreated by the foreigners—though for reasons I could not discern, perhaps simply because he was pretty and delicate—the girls had slipped along the inner side of the hedge separating us from the road to watch the proceedings from the outside.

Their observations revealed extraordinary tricks. First, they scanned the road in both directions to ensure they were alone. Then, all three turned to stare hard at our house. They began to jabber and dispute in their own language, looking at one another with expressions of doubt. Finally, they all turned to the little English boy as if expecting him to assist them. The chief Indian, who spoke English, then addressed the boy: "Hold out your hand."

Upon hearing these dreadful words, Penelope confessed she did not know what prevented her heart from flying straight out of her body. I privately suspected it might have been her stays. "You make my flesh creep," I remarked. (*Nota bene:* Women appreciate such little compliments.)

When the Indian commanded, "Hold out your hand," the boy recoiled, shook his head, and refused. The Indian then asked kindly if he would prefer to be sent back to London, left where they found him sleeping in an empty basket in a market—a hungry, ragged, and forsaken child. This seemingly ended the difficulty. Reluctantly, the boy held out his hand. Immediately, the Indian produced a bottle from his bosom and poured a black substance, resembling ink, into the palm of the boy's hand. Touching the boy's head and making signs in the air, the Indian said, "Look." The boy became rigid, standing like a statue gazing into the ink pooled in his hand.

At this point, it appeared to me to be mere juggling accompanied by a foolish waste of ink. I was beginning to drift back to sleep when Penelope's next words stirred me again.

The Indians scanned the road once more. Then the chief Indian instructed the boy: "See the English gentleman from foreign parts."

"I see him," the boy replied.

"Is it on the road to this house, and on no other, that the English gentleman will travel today?" the Indian asked.

"It is on the road to this house, and on no other, that the English gentleman will travel today," the boy answered.

The Indian paused, then asked a second question: "Has the English gentleman got It about him?"

"Yes," the boy answered, also after a brief pause.

A third and final question followed: "Will the English gentleman come here, as he has promised to come, at the close of day?"

"I can't tell," the boy said.

"Why not?" the Indian asked.

"I am tired. The mist rises in my head, and puzzles me. I can see no more today."

With that, the interrogation ended. The chief Indian spoke to his companions in their own tongue, pointing to the boy and then toward the town where they were lodged. After making further signs on the boy's head and blowing on his forehead to wake him with a start, they all proceeded toward the town, disappearing from the girls' sight.

Most things possess a moral if one looks for it. What was the moral here?

As I saw it: First, the chief juggler had overheard discussions of Mr. Franklin's arrival among the servants and saw an opportunity for profit. Second, he and his men intended to linger until they saw my lady drive home, then return to foretell Mr. Franklin's arrival via magic. Third, Penelope had heard them rehearse their hocus-pocus like actors practicing a play. Fourth, I should keep a vigilant eye on the plate-basket that evening. Fifth, Penelope should calm down and allow me, her father, to doze in the sun.

This seemed the sensible course. Knowing the ways of young women, you will not be surprised to learn Penelope would not accept this view. To her, the moral was grave. She fixated on the Indian's third question: "Has the English gentleman got It about him?" "Oh, father!" she cried, clasping her hands, "do not joke about this. What does 'It' mean?"

"We shall ask Mr. Franklin, my dear," I said, "if you can wait until he arrives." I winked to indicate I was joking. Penelope took it entirely seriously. Her earnestness amused me. "What on earth should Mr. Franklin know about it?" I inquired. "Ask him," she insisted. "And see whether *he* thinks it a laughing matter, too." With that final remark, she left me.

Once she was gone, I decided I must indeed ask Mr. Franklin, primarily to ease Penelope's mind. What occurred between us when I did so later that day will be detailed in its proper place. However, I must warn you here, before we proceed further, that you will find not a ghost of a joke in our conversation regarding the jugglers. To my great surprise, Mr. Franklin, like Penelope, took the matter very seriously. How seriously, you will understand, when I tell you that, in his opinion, "It" referred to the Moonstone.
