CHAPTER III. The Barber’s Shop.

“To be honest,” said the young stranger to Nello, as they stepped away from the jumbled carts and mules, “I’m not sorry to leave behind that patron of mine and fall into the hands of someone with a more civil tongue and a less mysterious trade. Is it common in Florence for a wandering peddler of broken glass and rags to speak of running a shop where he sells lutes and swords?”

“Common? Not at all,” replied Nello, whose talk flowed like water from an overfilled vessel—no small measure could contain it. “Bratti is no ordinary man. He has a philosophy, and lives by it—more than I can say for any philosopher I’ve had the honour to shave. Bratti believes in squeezing every drop of pleasure out of life—that is, the pleasure of hard bargaining—and plans to conclude his earthly dealings with a final bargain: a swift passage through purgatory by handing over his winnings to the Church when his time comes. He even made his will on the cheapest terms a notary would accept. But I’ve often told him, ‘Bratti, your deal is lame, and you’re on the weak side of it. Doesn’t it sadden you to gaze upon the paintings of Paradise? You’ll never haggle there over rags or rusty nails—the saints and angels need neither pins nor tinder. And unless you strike a deal with Saint Bartholomew, who carries his skin around rather inconveniently, I see little chance of trading second-hand clothes.’ But God forgive me,” added Nello, crossing himself, “such jesting is ill-suited to a morning when Lorenzo lies dead and the Muses are tearing their hair—always a sorrowful sight for a barber. And you yourself, sir, must be under some shadow; for when a man of your bearing and speech takes shelter in such a poor lodging, it suggests misfortune has overtaken him.”

“What Lorenzo do you mean?” asked the stranger, so absorbed in the mention of death that he seemed to overlook the subtle question that followed.

“What Lorenzo? There’s only one Lorenzo whose passing could send the Mercato into chaos, make the Duomo’s lantern leap in despair, and stir the lions of the Republic to devour each other. I mean Lorenzo de’ Medici, the Pericles of our Athens—if I may make such a comparison before a Greek.”

“Why not?” laughed the other. “I doubt Athens, even in Pericles’ time, could have produced so learned a barber.”

“Yes, yes,” said Nello swiftly, “I knew I wouldn’t be mistaken—else my shaving of the venerable Demetrio Calcondila would have been in vain. But forgive me—I’m astonished: your Italian surpasses even his, though he’s lived here forty years. It’s better even than Marullo’s, who might be said to have wedded the Italic Muse in more ways than one, since he married our learned and lovely Alessandra Scala.”

“You’ll find it easier to believe if I tell you I come from a Greek lineage planted in Italian soil longer than the mulberry trees that thrive so well here. I was born in Bari, and raised by an Italian—so, in truth, I am a Greek, much as your peaches are Persian. The Greek blood was subdued in me, I suppose, until long travel and residence in the land of gods and heroes re-dyed me. And now, to confess something personal: this Greek heritage, along with a few ancient gems I carry, is all the fortune shipwreck left me. But—when towers fall, it’s bad news for those who build small nests—the death of your Pericles makes me wish I’d turned toward Rome instead. I would have, but for a misleading Minerva in the form of an Augustinian monk. ‘At Rome,’ he said, ‘you’ll vanish among hungry scholars; but in Florence, every corner is touched by Lorenzo’s patronage. Florence is the finest market in Italy for goods like yours.’”

“_Gnaffè_, and so it shall remain,” said Nello. “Lorenzo was not the only patron of learning in our city—heaven forbid! Just because he was a large melon, does that mean every Florentine is a pumpkin? We have Bernardo Rucellai, Alamanno Rinuccini, and many others. And if you seek knowledge, I, Nello, am your man. It feels like a thousand years since I last served a scholar of your calibre. First, let us attend to your hair. That beard, young man, must go—were it as dear to you as your dream nymph. In Florence, we don’t care for men whose noses hang over cascades of hair. But remember: once shaved, you’ll have crossed the Rubicon. If you regret it later and let it grow back after resisting the razor, your mouth will soon resemble not Messer Angelo’s divine lips, but a dark cavern fringed with bristling thorns.”

“A dire prophecy,” said the Greek, “especially if Florentine maidens are as fair as the little Tessa I stole a kiss from this morning.”

“Tessa? She’s a rough-handed country girl. You’ll win favour with ladies who carry no scent of mule-stables. But to do so, you must not look like a _sgherro_, a man of ill repute. You must appear a courtier, a polished scholar—like our Pietro Crinito—someone who sins among well-bred, well-fed folk, not one who swallows cheap wine in a roadside tavern.”

“With all my heart,” said the stranger. “If the Florentine Graces demand it, I’ll sacrifice my beard—but—”

“Yes, yes,” interrupted Nello. “I know what you’d say. It’s the _bella zazzera_—the hyacinthine locks you won’t part with. But there’s no need. A little trimming—ecco!—and you’ll look not unlike the illustrious Prince Pico di Mirandola in his prime. And here we are, just in time, at the Piazza San Giovanni and the door of my shop. But you hesitate—I see: naturally, you want to admire our wonder of the world, our Duomo, Santa Maria del Fiore. Very well, a quick glance—but I beg you wait till you’re shaved. My art stirs me to the very edge of the razor. Ah, then, come this way.”

The lively barber took the stranger’s arm and led him to a point on the south side of the piazza, where he could see the vast dark shell of the dome, the slender grace of Giotto’s campanile, and the quaint octagon of San Giovanni before them, its unique bronze gates still gleaming faintly with original gilding. The inlaid marbles were fresher then—pink, white, and purple—than they are now, weathered by four centuries into the rich ochre of aged meerschaum. The cathedral’s façade stood proudly, not yet shrouded in faded stucco, but adorned with the grand promise of half-finished marble inlays and sculpted niches Giotto had designed a hundred and fifty years earlier. As the campanile rose in harmonious colour and form into the clear April air, it seemed a living symbol: human life, too, must someday shape itself into harmony with such pure, soaring beauty.

But the Greek did not seem moved. His eyes lifted upward, yet there was a hint of scorn on his lip. When they fell again, they swept the scene with a cool, assessing gaze that nettled Nello’s Florentine pride.

“Well, my fine young man,” he said, impatiently, “you treat our Cathedral as if you were the Angel Gabriel fresh from Paradise. Tell me—have you seen finer work than Giotto’s tower? Or a dome that wouldn’t look like a mushroom beside Brunelleschi’s? Or marbles more beautiful or skillfully wrought than these our Signoria brought from distant quarries—at a price that could buy a dukedom? Come now—have you seen anything to match them?”

“If you asked me with a scimitar at my throat, Turkish style—or even your own razor,” replied the young Greek, smiling, and moving toward the Baptistery gates, “I might confess. But with my neck free of danger, I must say your buildings smack too much of Christian barbarism for my taste. I feel a shudder at what lies within—smoked Madonnas, fleshless saints in mosaic staring down idiotic rebuke from the apse, skeletons draped in skins, pierced with arrows, stretched on gridirons; monks and women with heads twisted in eternal lamentation. I’ve seen enough of such wry-necked favourites of heaven in Constantinople. But what is this bronze door, rough with imagery? These women seem shaped by a different spirit from those starved saints I mentioned—these high-relief faces suggest a mind within them, not just an index to perpetual spasms and colic.”

“Yes, yes,” said Nello, with quiet triumph. “We’ll show you soon enough that Florentine art is far from barbarous. These gates, my fine young man, were cast half a century ago by our Lorenzo Ghiberti, who was barely as old as you are.”

“Ah, I recall,” said the stranger, turning away as if his curiosity were satisfied. “I’ve heard your Tuscan sculptors and painters have studied the antique. But with monks for models and tales of mad hermits and martyrs for subjects, even Olympus itself would be of little help.”

“I understand,” said Nello, shrugging meaningfully as they walked. “You think as Michele Marullo does—yes, even as Angelo Poliziano, despite his canonry, when he relaxes in my shop after lectures and speaks of the gods waking from long sleep, making woods and streams alive again. But he mocks Roman scholars who wish to make us all speak Latin: ‘My ears,’ he says, ‘are already raw from the barbarisms of the learned, and if the common people begin to speak Latin, I’d rather have been in Florence the day they beat every kettle in the city because the bells weren’t enough to appease the saints.’ Ah, Messer Greco, if you wish to taste Florentine scholarship, visit my shop—it is the focus of our intellect, the navel of the earth—as my great predecessor Burchiello claimed of his shop, though on the lighter ground that his street, Calimara, was the city’s centre. And here we are at the sign of ‘Apollo and the Razor.’ Apollo, you see, bestows the razor upon Triptolemus, the first reaper of beards, the sublime _Anonimo_, whose hidden identity is marked by a shadowy hand.”

“I see you’ve already had customers, Sandro,” Nello continued, addressing a solemn, dark-eyed youth who stepped aside for them. “Now clear the way for this gentleman. Prepare the finest-scented lather—he has a learned and handsome chin.”

“You have a pleasant little adytum here,” said the stranger, glancing through a latticed screen that separated the shop from a room of equal size, which opened onto a smaller walled courtyard where a few bay trees and laurels surrounded a stone Hermes. “I suppose your circle of _eruditi_ meets there?”

“There, and no less in my shop,” said Nello, leading the way into the inner room, furnished with benches, a table holding an open manuscript and a printed book in capitals, a lute, a few oil sketches, and a model or two of hands and ancient masks. “For my shop is no less a haunt of the Muses—you’ll see when you feel the sudden clarity of thought and serene inspiration that follows a clean chin. Ah! You can play that lute, I see. I have some skill myself, though serenatas are useless when daylight reveals a face like mine—no fresher than an apple that’s endured winter. But look at this sketch—it’s Piero di Cosimo’s fancy, a strange painter who claims he saw it by staring at a mouldy wall.”

The sketch showed three masks: a drunken laughing Satyr, a sorrowing Magdalen, and between them, the rigid, cold face of a Stoic. They rested obliquely on a child’s lap, whose cherubic features looked up with a divine promise, the kind painters had begun to give to the Christ Child.

“A symbolic image,” said the young Greek, touching the lute to draw a soft murmur. “Perhaps the child is the Golden Age—needing neither worship nor philosophy. And the Golden Age returns as long as men are born infants, not arriving in cassocks or fur-lined robes. Or perhaps the child stands for Epicurus’ wise philosophy—free from the gross, the sad, and the severe.”

“Ah! Everyone interprets that picture differently,” said Nello. “Ask Piero himself, and he’ll say his works are an appendix Messer Domeneddio has appended to the universe. If anyone doubts their meaning, they should ask Holy Church. He was asked to paint a full version, but he covers his ears and shakes his head—the idea is gone. A peculiar creature, our Piero. But now—everything is ready for your initiation into the mysteries of the razor.”

“Mysteries indeed,” continued the barber, spirits rising at the prospect of a long discourse, as he wrapped the young Greek in the shroud-like shaving cloth. “Mysteries of Minerva and the Graces. I gather the finest thoughts from men, because I catch them in the moment after shaving. (Ah! You flinch slightly—yes, the lather tickles the edges of the nose.) That’s why a barber’s shop is fit for wit and learning. Consider a druggist’s shop: there’s a dull gathering at ‘The Moor,’ which pretends to rival mine. What inspiration can one draw from the smell of foul vegetable decoctions?—not to mention the doctor of physic waiting at the door like a giant spider in fur and scarlet, or even seated on a bony horse, inspecting saliva. (Raise your chin slightly—look at the angel blowing the trumpet above you. I painted it expressly to guide my clients’ chins.) Besides, your druggist, who gathers herbs and brews potions, is biased—he’s poisoned people by system and must defend it. But a barber can remain impartial. The only thing he defends is the razor—unless he’s an author. That was Burchiello’s flaw—he was a poet, and thus had a bias toward his own verses. I avoided that. I saw early that authorship narrows the mind, while the art of the razor demands affection for all chins. Ecco, Messer! Your chin and lip are as clear as a maiden’s. Now fix your mind on a knotty question—ask whether Virgil should be spelled with an _i_ or an _e_, and see if you don’t feel a new clarity. Only—keep your choice to yourself until you’re wealthy, for the _e_ has the stronger following in Florence. Ah! I see a spark of sharper wit in your eye. Niccolò Macchiavelli himself, keen enough to discern _il pelo nell’ uovo_, and fond of fine shaving—though his beard is barely two years old—says that as soon as hairs begin to grow, he feels a certain coarseness creeping over his mind.”

“Let me look at myself,” said the stranger, laughing. “This intellectual clarity may be blocked by uncertainty about my appearance.”

“Behold yourself in this mirror,” said Nello. “It’s a Venetian mirror from Murano—the true _nosce teipsum_, as I call it. Compared to steel or silver mirrors, it is pure darkness. See how careful shaving preserves the human outline of your lower face, instead of blurring it into the visage of a bearded owl or a Barbary ape. I’ve seen men whose beards invaded their cheeks so thoroughly that one might pity them as victims of a brutal punishment worthy of Dante’s Inferno—had they not strutted with strange pride in their wild hairiness.”

“It seems,” said the Greek, still gazing into the mirror, “that you’ve taken some of my capital with your razor—I mean, a year or two of age, which might have earned me greater credit for my learning. Under a patron whose eyesight has dimmed, I’ll look dangerously like an eighteen-year-old girl in boy’s clothing.”

“Not at all,” said Nello, clipping the excessive curls. “Your proportions are not those of a maiden. And your age? I remember seeing Angelo Poliziano begin his Latin lectures with a younger beard than yours. Between us, his youthful ugliness matched his precocious learning. But you—no, no, your age is not a problem. Yet, between us, let me whisper: being Greek—even Apulian Greek—is not in your favour. Some of our scholars claim Greek learning is a degenerate plant until transplanted into Italian minds. With so many superior minds now flourishing, native teachers are seen as mere propagators of decay. Ecco! Your curls are now properly proportioned to neck and shoulders. Rise, Messer, and let me free you from this cloth. _Gnaffè_! I almost advise you to keep your faded jerkin and hose a while longer—they give you the air of a fallen prince.”

“But the real question,” said the young Greek, leaning against the high back of a chair, meeting Nello’s admiring gaze with one of quiet inquiry, “is where I should carry this princely air to rise from my fallen state. If Florentine patrons share this scholarly disdain for Greeks, I see no hope for refuge here, as you suggested earlier.”

“_Pian piano_—not so fast,” said Nello, tucking his thumbs into his belt and nodding to Sandro to restore order. “I won’t hide that there’s prejudice against Greeks among us. Though, as a barber unbound by authorship, I hold no prejudices myself, I must admit Greeks aren’t always as refined as you. Their learning often appears unruly, their Italian thick with barbaric accents—making conversation no more pleasant than a German in a drunken state. And then—we Florentines value speech freely. We believe the tongue, so skilled in flattery and promise, was made partly for such purposes; truth is a riddle for eyes and wit to solve, not for the tongue to betray. Still, we draw limits—beyond which we call deceit treachery. But it’s said Greeks begin their honesty where we end ours. Since the old Furies slept, your Christian Greek has such a light conscience he’d use his father’s corpse as a stepping-stone.”

A flush crept across the stranger’s face—a natural reaction of resentment. Nello, ever good-natured, hastened to apologize.

“Do not take offence, _bel giovane_. I only repeat what I hear in my shop—as you see, my eloquence is merely the cream skimmed from my clients’ talk. Heaven forbid I should lose my impartiality by holding opinions. And regarding this scholarly hostility,” added Nello, with a mocking grimace, “the truth is, you are heretics, Messer. Jealousy has nothing to do with it. If you simply changed your view on leaven and adjusted your Doxology, our Italian scholars would wait a thousand years to yield their chairs to you. Yes, yes—it’s mostly religious scruple, and partly the authority of a great classic—Juvenal, isn’t it? He, I gather, felt as strongly about the swarm of Greeks as our Messer Angelo, who loves quoting a passage about their incorrigible impudence—_audacia perdita_.”

“Pooh!” said the Greek, recovering his composure. “That passage is a compliment.”

“‘Ingenium velox, audacia perdita, sermo Promptus, et Isaeo torrentior.’”

“A rapid mind and fluent speech may excuse a bit of boldness.”

“Indeed,” said Nello. “And since you know Latin literature as well as Greek, you won’t fall into Giovanni Argiropulo’s mistake—running headlong into Cicero and calling him nearly a pumpkin-head. Let me give you one piece of advice, young man—trust a barber who has shaved the finest chins and kept his eyes and ears open for twenty years: oil your tongue well when speaking of ancient Latin writers, and give it an extra dip for modern ones. A wise Greek may win favour here—see our excellent Demetrio, loved by many, not hated beyond reason even by the most renowned scholars.”

“I see the wisdom of your counsel clearly,” said the Greek, his bright smile lighting up his fine face, “so I’ll ask for a little more. Who, for instance, would be the most likely patron for me? Is there a son of Lorenzo who inherits his tastes? Or another wealthy Florentine especially fond of ancient gems? I possess a fine Cleopatra carved in sardonyx, and several other intaglios and cameos—curious and beautiful—worthy of a prince’s cabinet. Happily, I secured them inside my doublet before setting sail. Moreover, I’d like to raise a small sum from this ring,” he said, drawing it out and replacing it, “if you could recommend me to an honest trader.”
