The first thing John Jasper registered when he came to was the sharp, acrid tang of burnt sago jelly and Mrs Tope’s sniffling breath fanning over his cheek. Mr and Mrs Tope, summoned by his unlooked-for visitor, had dragged him from the floor to his favourite armchair, and that visitor—angular, wooden-faced Grewgious—sat bolt upright on a hard-backed oak chair, hands planted square on his knees, watching every flicker of Jasper’s eyelids like a man auditing a unforgiving ledger.

“There now, sir, you’ve come round fine,” Mrs Tope blubbered, dabbing at her eyes with her apron hem. “You were fit to drop, no wonder at all, running yourself ragged like that.”

“A man cannot have his rest broken, his mind cruelly tormented, and his body overtaxed by fatigue without being thoroughly worn out,” Grewgious intoned, as if reciting a line he’d memorised word for word.

“I fear I gave you all a fright,” Jasper mumbled faintly, as they helped him settle deeper into the chair.

“Not at all, I thank you,” Grewgious replied, stony as ever. “You are too considerate.”
“Not at all, I thank you,” he repeated before Jasper could speak.

“You will take some wine now, sir,” Mrs Tope cut in, bustling off towards the scullery. “And that jelly I had set aside for you at noon, which you turned your nose up at, though I warned you what would come of it, not even a crumb of breakfast in you! And a wing of roast fowl that’s been kept warm at the fire so many times I’ve lost count. It’ll all be on the table in five minutes, and this good gentleman will surely stop to see you eat it.”

Grewgious huffed a snort that could have meant anything: yes, no, utter indifference, and Mrs Tope, already half-way out the door, didn’t linger to puzzle it over.

“Won’t you take a bite with me?” Jasper asked, as the cloth was spread across his table.
“Couldn’t get a morsel down my throat, I thank you,” Grewgious said, fixed in his upright pose, no flicker of expression on his face, every line of him a polite, unyielding protest against casual conversation, as if pre-emptively turning down any invitation to chat about the weather or the cathedral bells.

Jasper ate and drank like a man fighting to fend off a total collapse, his movements fast, unthinking, as if he didn’t taste a single bite. He was not eating for pleasure, it was clear: he was fortifying himself, shoring up his strength against any further fainting spells, any crack in his carefully composed mask.

When he pushed his plate away minutes later, he sat staring into the empty fire grate for several long minutes before he spoke. “Do you know, I find crumbs of comfort in the news you brought me? The news that Edwin has broken off his engagement to your ward, never told a soul, not even me?”

“_Do_ you?” Grewgious said, the unspoken follow-up hanging thick in the air: I don’t, I thank you.

“I was gutted by it at first, of course. A piece of news that blows apart every last plan I’d built for my dear boy. But after I’d time to turn it over in my head… there’s hope there, isn’t there? If Edwin knew he’d have to face endless awkward questions, to explain to every soul in Cloisterham why the wedding was off, wouldn’t it make sense he’d run off to avoid it? Vanish rather than be the seven days’ wonder for every gossiping fool in the close to poke and pry at?”

“Such things do happen,” Grewgious allowed, slow, after a moment of pondering. “I’ve read of folk vanishing for years, just to escape the nuisance of explaining themselves.”

“Exactly!” Jasper leaned forward, voice brightening, eager now, as if he was clinging to this new line of reasoning like a lifeline. “I never had the faintest idea Edwin was hiding anything from me, least of all something so critical as calling off his marriage. How could I ever have imagined he’d leave of his own accord, unannounced, cruel to his friends, to me? But now that I know the engagement is off? It makes his disappearance make sense. He wouldn’t hurt his Rosa by vanishing out of the blue, not when he’s just parted from her. Even to me—he knew you were coming to tell me the news! He knew exactly what conclusions I’d draw, that I’d assume he ran off rather than face the mess. It takes the edge off the cruelty to both of us, don’t you see?”

Grewgious could not find fault with the logic.

“I’ve had terrible dark fears, these past days,” Jasper went on, hands clasped tight, eyes glistening. “But this news you brought me? It gives me hope. I can believe it now—Edwin’s gone off of his own free will, he’s alive, he’s well, he’ll turn up soon.”

It was at that exact moment that Mr Crisparkle stepped through the door. Jasper repeated his hopeful theory word for word, and the good Minor Canon, already wracked with guilt over his pupil Neville Landless, who stood accused of hurting Edwin, drank the reasoning in. It fit too well: Edwin had been weighed down by the secret of the broken engagement, it would absolutely explain a sudden, impulsive flight.

“I told Sapsea as much when we called on him,” Jasper said, earnest as could be. “There was no quarrel between the boys the last time they met at my house, you know. Their first meeting was ugly, I was so afraid Neville’s wild temper would hurt my Edwin, I wrote all my dark forebodings down in my diary and showed you the entry. I want you all to know—this new hope has softened even my old distrust of Neville. I won’t hold my old grudges, not if Edwin’s alive out there somewhere.”

Crisparkle was shamed to his core. Jasper was laying all his cards on the table, and he’d been hiding two critical facts: Neville had flown into a second violent rage at Edwin, and he’d been flamingly jealous of him over Rosa Bud. He was certain Neville was innocent, but the circumstantial evidence was piling higher and higher against him, and he’d been holding back those details for fear of making things worse. But seeing Jasper’s frankness, he could not hold back any longer. He confessed everything to Grewgious, the full truth of Neville’s hot temper, his jealousy over Rosa.

Jasper paled a little at the news, but shook his head resolutely. “I will cling to my hope. Until we find proof my boy is dead, I will not believe harm came to him.”

Crisparkle left the house that night more troubled than ever, his mind so full of worry for Neville that he did not notice where his feet were carrying him until he heard the low rush of falling water, close enough to make his ears ring. He had walked all the way to Cloisterham Weir, two miles upstream from where the two boys had gone to watch the Christmas Eve storm.

“How did I get here?” he thought, stopping short. “Why did I come here?”

The starlight was thin and cold, the weir hissing and roaring as it always did on a frosty night. A half-remembered line from his old poetry books drifted unbidden into his head, about airy tongues that syllable men’s names, and he swatted at the air as if he could brush the words away. No one had searched this far upstream. The tide had been running strong down towards the sea on Christmas Eve, so any body that fell in would have been carried far away from the weir. But the place felt haunted, heavy, like something was hidden there. He strained his eyes, peered at the wooden timbers and posts, saw nothing out of the ordinary, and resolved he would come back at first light.

The weir ran through his dreams all night, and he was back at sunrise, the bright frosty light laying every last detail of the structure bare. He scanned the timbers for minutes, ready to leave, when a tiny glint caught his eye, something stationary, not rippling and falling with the rest of the water. He tore off his coat, his boots, plunged into the icy river, swam for the spot.

Tucked in the cracks of the weir’s heavy timbers, caught fast by its chain, was a gold pocket watch. Engraved on the back, in tiny neat script, the initials: E.D.

He swam back to the bank, then dove again, and again, and again, searching every dark hole in the river bed, until his fingers were numb and he could not bear the cold a moment longer. He found no body, only a single shirt pin stuck in the mud and ooze at the bottom.

He brought the two finds back to Cloisterham, fetched Neville Landless, and went straight to the mayor. Jasper was sent for, the watch and pin were identified as Edwin’s, and the whole town of Cloisterham exploded in a frenzy of gossip against Neville. The rumors spread like wildfire: he was a violent savage, he’d whipped natives to death back in his old homeland, he’d threatened to murder Mr Crisparkle half a dozen times, he was a walking danger to every soul in the close. The hard, sharp facts stacked up against him too: he’d openly threatened Edwin, he’d carried a weapon that night, he’d been seen leaving town at dawn, he had traces of blood on his clothes, he’d burned all his papers the afternoon Edwin vanished. The jeweller confirmed he’d wound Edwin’s watch at 2:20 that same afternoon, and it had run down long before anyone fished it from the weir, meaning it had been tossed in the hours right after Edwin vanished.

No trace of Edwin’s body ever turned up, though, and eventually the authorities had to release Neville for lack of proof. But the town had already turned its back on him. The Dean told Crisparkle flat out that Neville could not stay in his house, not in the cloister, sanctuary or no sanctuary. The Minor Canon protested that he knew Neville was innocent, but the Dean waved him off, warning him not to say so out loud, not to be a partisan, to keep his heart warm and his head cool and hold that “judicious middle course” that suited the clergy.

So Neville Landless was gone, run out of Cloisterham, a permanent blight on his name.

It was only then that John Jasper quietly returned to his place in the cathedral choir. Haggard, red-eyed, all that false sanguine hope drained out of him, he looked like a man who’d been gutted from inside. A day or two later, after the service, he was unrobing in the vestry, when he pulled his diary out of his coat pocket, flipped to a fresh page, and handed it wordlessly to Mr Crisparkle to read. The ink was fresh, dark, the writing sharp and jagged, like he’d carved the words into the page with his pen:
“My dear boy is murdered. The discovery of the watch and shirt-pin convinces me he was killed that Christmas Eve, and his jewellery was stripped from him to stop anyone identifying his body. All the foolish, delusive hopes I built around his broken engagement? I throw them to the winds. They are dead, the second I laid eyes on that watch half-buried in the weir timbers. I swear this oath, and I write it here in black and white, for all time: I will never discuss this mystery with any soul, not until I hold the full clue to it in my hand. I will never slacken my search. I will track the murderer of my dear dead boy to the ends of the earth. And I will devote every last breath of my life to his destruction.”
