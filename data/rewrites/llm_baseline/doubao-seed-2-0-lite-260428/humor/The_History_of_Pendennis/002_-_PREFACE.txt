Look, let’s get this straight from the start: the two years’ worth of scribbling that’s landed in your hands today is never going to win awards for fancy, polished, perfect art. That’s a given, and I’m not pretending otherwise. But it’s got one huge advantage over all those over-labored, pretentious tomes: it’s unapologetically honest.

When a writer’s yammering directly at you, page after page, no fancy middlemen, he’s bound to slip into being frank, just blurting out whatever’s bouncing around his head no filter. Flipping back through these pages now, I spot a dozen typos, half a dozen lines I banged out in a huff at 2 a.m that I’d give a pound to take back. This whole thing is just a long, rambling chat between you and me, no more, no less. Of course there will be dull bits! Of course the story will drag sometimes! Anyone who nags at you for hours is going to accidentally let slip their dumb obsessions, petty vanities, and weird little quirks eventually, right?

You don’t judge your best mate after two years of hanging out only on that one stupid drunk speech he made at your cousin’s wedding, right? You judge him on the whole of him – is he a stand-up bloke? Does he mostly tell the truth? Is he trying not to lie to you? Or is he a phony hack putting on a whole dramatic act to make you clap and buy him a pint, pulling cheap little tricks to make you like him? I’m not daft, I know I’ve gotten far more readers than I ever could have daydreamed about, and I’m not about to yell at you that you’re forbidden to think my art is garbage, or that you’re not allowed to nod off halfway through a chapter. All I ask? Believe that I’m trying my best to tell you the truth. If there’s no truth in these pages, there’s nothing at all.

For all you excitement addicts who only pick up books for the drama, let me tell you a secret: this one had a completely different, wildly chaotic original plot that I tossed in the bin. You were going to get the most blood-pumping thrill ride imaginable, and me and my publisher were going to get so rich we’d both buy new top hats. What’s more exciting than a roguish St Giles ruffian (who was secretly very noble, of course) getting regular secret visits from a posh Belgravia heiress? Contrasts! Slang mixed with fancy aristocratic banter! Fist fights! Narrow escapes! *Murder!* Heck, up until 9 o’clock this very morning, poor old Colonel Altamont was all lined up to be hanged – he was already standing on the trapdoor, halfway out the jail window – before I felt too sorry for him and called the whole execution off.

I scrapped the entire “super exciting murder book” plan (my publishers were so polite about ditching their big payday, bless them) because I quickly realized I had no clue what I was writing about. I’ve never once been best mates with a convict! I don’t know how actual jailbirds and street ruffians talk! I couldn’t go compete with M. Eugène Sue and all his gritty crime stories, that would be like me trying to run the Grand National with no pants on, I’d faceplant in the mud before the first hurdle. And if I *did* write a real, unfiltered rascal, he’d be so hideously awful you’d hurl the book straight across the room. If an artist can’t paint a villain fairly, full warts and all, he’s got no right to show him off at all.

Heck, we can’t even draw normal, regular educated men as they really are these days! This book is about one of those guys, no better and no worse than most of the posh blokes you meet at dinner parties – but I’m not allowed to show you all his real dumb foibles, his selfish little habits, all the messy stuff he gets up to. Ever since the author of *Tom Jones* died, no fiction writer in this country has been allowed to draw a full, unfiltered, actual whole MAN. We all have to drape them in fancy polite cloth, stick a fake conventional little simper on their faces. Society won’t let us show the unvarnished real thing in art! A whole crowd of very proper ladies wrote me angry notes and cancelled their subscriptions, all because I wrote a young man who struggled with temptation, who had the passions to feel the pull of bad choices, and the decency to fight and overcome them. You’ll never hear the unvarnished truth about what really goes on in the clubs, the colleges, the army mess halls – what your sons actually talk about when they think no grown-ups are listening. I tried to be a little more frank than the average prissy Victorian novel here, I swear I had no bad intentions, and I promise it won’t do any of you a bit of harm.

Truth doesn’t always taste like fancy sugarplums, but it’s always better than the fake, cloying nonsense everyone else is peddling – whether it’s a stuffy learned man on a stage giving you a lecture, or a worn-out old storyteller wrapping up his work to say farewell to all his kind readers.

KENSINGTON, Nov. 26th, 1850.
