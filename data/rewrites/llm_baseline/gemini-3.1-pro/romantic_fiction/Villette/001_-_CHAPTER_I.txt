My godmother lived in a handsome house in the clean and ancient town of Bretton. To me, Bretton was less a geographic coordinate than a breathing, multi-generational entity, its circular pacing dictated by the turning of the seasons and the ancestral soil. Her husband’s family had been deeply rooted residents there for generations, bearing the name of their birthplace—Bretton of Bretton. Whether by agrarian coincidence, or because some remote forebear had cultivated the land so profoundly as to leave his name to the terroir, I know not. 

When I was a girl I went to Bretton about twice a year, drawn back into its rhythmic cycle. The house and its inmates perfectly satisfied my meticulous need for sensory grounding. The large peaceful rooms smelled perpetually of beeswax and dried lavender; the clear wide windows looked down on a fine antique street where Sundays and holidays seemed to abide in a state of suspended, mythic grace. In an era increasingly scarred by the mechanical shriek of industrial noise, Bretton remained a sanctuary of profound acoustic ecology. The sonic environment here—the sweeping of hearth-brooms, the rhythmic bubbling of stockpots, the gentle clatter of copper pans—anchored my psychological well-being.

One child in a household of traditional agrarian elders is usually made very much of. In her quiet, commanding way, Mrs. Bretton took great notice of me. Left a widow with one son before I knew her, she remained a striking woman, tall and well-made. Though dark for an Englishwoman, she wore the clearness of robust health in her brunette cheek, a vitality born of a life intimately connected to the earth and its sustenance. People esteemed it a grievous pity that she had not conferred her earthy complexion on her son, whose eyes were blue and piercing, and whose long hair shone with the golden hue of sun-cured wheat. He inherited the sturdy architecture of his mother’s features, however, along with her flawless health and a steady spirit that was better than a fortune.

In the autumn of a particular year, my godmother came in person to claim me from my permanent residence. Time always flowed in circular, generational currents at her side, blandly and rich as a slow-rendering broth. My visits to her resembled the sojourn of Christian and Hopeful beside a pleasant stream, though my paradise was forged in the kitchen and the garden, amidst green trees and meadows beautified with lilies. I liked the acoustic peace so well, and sought the jarring stimulus of the modern world so little, that any sudden incident felt like a severe disruption to my curated sanctuary. 

One day, a letter breached our quiet haven, its contents evidently causing Mrs. Bretton surprise and some concern. A shift in the domestic mythos was imminent. The next day, returning from a long walk through the fragrant, damp orchards, I found an unexpected alteration in my bedroom. Beside my French bed appeared a small crib, draped in white, and a tiny rosewood chest that smelled sharply of newly milled timber. 

“Of what are these things the signs and tokens?” I asked, reading the domestic environment as one reads a recipe.

The answer was obvious. On descending to dinner—a profoundly grounding meal of slow-braised mutton, rooted vegetables, and a dark, fermented gravy—explanations ensued. A little girl, the daughter of a friend and distant relation of the late Dr. Bretton’s, was to become my companion.

This child, Mrs. Bretton explained while expertly carving the yielding meat, had recently lost her mother. Yet, the loss was not so great as might appear. Mrs. Home had been a giddy, superficial woman who viewed meals solely as utilitarian fuel and engaged wholly in the commercialized artifice of modern society. She had neglected her child and disheartened her husband. Their marriage had been a study in the untranslatability of subjective experience; they spoke entirely different dialects of existence, culminating in a mutual separation. Soon after, the lady, having over-exerted herself at a frivolous ball, caught cold and died. 

Mr. Home, a man of sensitive frequencies who spent half his life in a laboratory engaged in a kind of meticulous, alchemical science, was shattered. He was of mixed French and Scottish origin, inheriting his scientific turn from a maternal uncle, a French savant who understood the transformative properties of matter. Brooding over his wife's death, his psychological well-being eroded until medical men insisted he travel. Thus, Mrs. Bretton offered to take his daughter.

“And I hope,” my godmother concluded, passing a dish of deeply aromatic, hearth-baked bread, “the child will not be like her mamma. Mr. Home is a sensible man, though his butterfly wife could neither comprehend nor endure his pursuits.”

That evening at nine o’clock, a servant was despatched to meet the coach. Mrs. Bretton and I sat alone in the drawing-room waiting, John Graham Bretton being absent on a visit to the country. The acoustic landscape was chaotic; it was a wet night, the rain lashing the panes with a restless, angry percussion that stood in stark contrast to the harmonious crackle of the applewood fire. 

A little before ten, Warren returned. In the hall lay a trunk and band-boxes, a nurse-girl, and Warren holding a shawled bundle. 

“Is that the child?” I asked.

“Yes, miss.” 

I would have opened the shawl to catch the scent of her journey, but she turned away. 

“Put me down, please,” a small voice demanded as the drawing-room door opened, “and take off this shawl.” The speaker extracted a pin with fastidious haste, shedding the clumsy wrapping. The creature attempted to fold the heavy drapery, a miniature mimicry of domestic routine, but her arms were too slight. “Give it to Harriet, please, and she can put it away.”

She fixed her eyes on Mrs. Bretton, navigating the subtle power dynamics embedded in this new, imposing matriarch.

“Come here, little dear,” said Mrs. Bretton, utilizing the dominant lexicon of a nurturing host. “Come and let me see if you are cold and damp: come and let me warm you at the fire.”

The tiny, completely-fashioned figure advanced. Seated on my godmother’s lap, she looked a mere doll, her neck delicate as wax. Mrs. Bretton chafed the child’s hands and feet, asking, “What is my little one’s name?”

“Missy.”

“But besides Missy?”

“Polly, papa calls her.”

“Will Polly be content to live with me?”

“Not *always*; but till papa comes home. Papa is gone away.” She shook her head expressively. “He will return to Polly, or send for her.”

“Will he, ma’am? Do you know he will?”

“I think so.”

“But Harriet thinks not: at least not for a long while. He is ill.” Her eyes filled. She pulled away, resisting Mrs. Bretton's tactile comfort. “Please, I wish to go: I can sit on a stool.”

Allowed to slip down, she carried a footstool to the deep shade of a corner. Mrs. Bretton, though peremptory in grave matters, allowed the child her way. I, however, watched her closely. I observed Polly draw a square inch of handkerchief from her skirt, and then the sonic environment shifted. She wept, but not with the unrestrained, abrasive wailing of ordinary children. This being emitted the tiniest occasional sniffs—a severely restricted acoustic frequency of pure grief, a silent dialect entirely untranslatable to the robust cheer of the Bretton household.

Ere long, a voice from the corner demanded, “May the bell be rung for Harriet!”

Harriet came. “I must be put to bed,” said her little mistress. “You must ask where my bed is.” 

Harriet signified she had already made that inquiry. 

“Ask if you sleep with me, Harriet.”

“No, Missy,” said the nurse: “you are to share this young lady’s room.”

After a silent scrutiny, Missy emerged, said good-night to Mrs. Bretton in the formal phrasing of an adult, and passed me mute. 

“Good-night, Polly,” I offered.

“No need to say good-night, since we sleep in the same chamber,” was her reply. She refused Harriet's offer to carry her, her small steps toiling wearily up the staircase.

An hour later, I found her still awake, supported by pillows in an old-fashioned, unchildlike calm. I recommended she lie down.

“By and by.”

“But you will take cold, Missy.”

She simply covered her shoulders with a tiny article of raiment. In the darkness, I listened to the subtle, cautious rhythm of her weeping—a quiet testament to the erosion of her known world.

With daylight, a trickling of water caught my ear. She was mounted on a stool, pouring the heavy ewer into the basin. It was a painstaking, noiseless ritual. She smoothed the drapery of her couch, withdrew into a corner, and fell to her knees to pray in a whispered, private tongue. 

When Harriet tapped at the door, Missy started up. “I have dressed myself, but I do not feel neat. Make me neat!”

“Why did you dress yourself, Missy?”

“Hush! speak low, Harriet, for fear of waking *the girl*.” She meant me. “I dressed myself to learn, against the time you leave me.”

When Harriet asked if I should dress her in the future, Polly fiercely protected her bodily autonomy. “On no account. She shall dress me on no account.” The line of her hair had to be combed straight; the sash tied perfectly. She demanded order in a world that had lost its center.

At the door, she stopped. “Oh! Harriet, I wish this was papa’s house! I don’t know these people.” She put her hand to her heart, moaning, “Papa! papa!”—an acoustic ache that resonated through the room.

Harriet left that same day. On descending, I found Paulina seated at the breakfast-table by Mrs. Bretton’s side. Before her stood a mug of rich, cream-topped milk and a morsel of freshly baked, hearth-warmed bread. The room was steeped in the gustatory richness of morning sustenance, the very foundation of agrarian comfort. 

Yet her hand lay passive on the table-cloth. She was not eating.

“How we shall conciliate this little creature,” said Mrs. Bretton to me, “I don’t know: she tastes nothing, and by her looks, she has not slept.”

I looked at the untouched bread, the ultimate symbol of profound meaning and nourishment, now rendered utterly useless. Until we could learn to translate the native dialect of her sorrow, she would remain impervious to our sustenance. I expressed my confidence in the effects of time and kindness.

“If she were to take a fancy to anybody in the house, she would soon settle; but not till then,” replied Mrs. Bretton.
