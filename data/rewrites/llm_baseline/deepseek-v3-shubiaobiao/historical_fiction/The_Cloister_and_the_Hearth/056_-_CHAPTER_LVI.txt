Gerard found a modest lodging on the Tiber’s western bank and set out each day in search of work, carrying samples of his lettering to every shop that might employ him. But the booksellers met him with cold indifference.

"Your hand is too bold," one remarked.

"Your ink too dark," sniffed another.

The common refrain was: "Latin is no longer in demand. Can you write Greek?"

"Not as well as Latin," Gerard admitted.

"Then you will starve in Rome."

Undeterred, he borrowed a fine Greek manuscript—costly, but necessary—and spent the next fortnight mastering the script. By day he practiced; by evening, he sought patrons. When at last he presented a Greek sample finer than any in the shops, the traders shrugged. "Greek and Latin alike gather dust. The city overflows with manuscripts from across Europe. You should have come last year."

With a heavy heart, Gerard bought a psaltery, hoping music might sustain him. His landlady, a warm-hearted Siennese, often paused to chat. One day, over a shared meal, she eyed him keenly.

"Your spirits are dashed. What troubles you?"

He confessed his struggles.

"Ah, those sly traders!" she scoffed. "They have scribes in their pay—men they underpay while charging nobles double for their work. No wonder they turn you away. You write *too well*."

"How do you know?"

"Because you leave your door unlocked, unlike that miser Pietro. Women are curious, you know." She winked. "Your skill would shame their shoddy wares."

Gerard sighed. "You judge them too harshly, kind as you are."

"Child, Romans are a cunning breed. I? I am Siennese, thanks be to the Virgin!"

He lamented leaving Augsburg.

"Augsburg?" She sniffed. "What is that compared to Rome? Never heard of it." Then, brightening, she added, "But take heart! The great lords here are mad for books—trading lands and palaces for parchment to hoard in chests. Why, even the Pope and Cardinal Bassarion could keep a dozen scribes busy. I’ll ask Teresa—she hears court gossip."

Days later, she brought names of five nobles bitten by the "writing spider." Gerard crafted fresh samples, left them at each door, and waited.

No reply came.

As his purse dwindled, he skipped meals, filling time with music. His landlady soon noticed.

"Shame on you!" she scolded. "Do you think hunger conquers misfortune? *Patienza!* Eat—I’ll feed you for the love of heaven."

"Nay, I’ll not take charity while I’ve a coin left."

"Foolish boy! Leave cooking to women. Your mother would say the same."

Her kindness stirred him. "You remind me of her."

Her face softened. "Then heed me better."

---

One neighbor intrigued him—Pietro, a Florentine painter who locked his door like a miser. The landlady scoffed. "He brought money and an unfinished picture. When buyers offered fair sums, he scorned them. Now he’s sold all but his bed—a chest, if the keyhole tells true."

Gerard pitied him. One day, hearing groans, he knocked.

A surly voice bade him enter.

Inside, a gaunt young man coiled on a chest like a wary serpent, his eyes burning. A single painting leaned face to the wall.

"Forgive my intrusion," Gerard said. "I am your neighbor—an artist too."

"A painter?" Pietro brightened, waving him to the chest. "Sit!"

"Not a painter. A scribe—a caligrapher."

Pietro’s enthusiasm dimmed. "An artist *thinks* and paints his thoughts. A scribe merely copies."

"Yet a scribe preserves the wisdom of ages—things no brush could capture."

Pietro shrugged. "Better a poor painter than a fine scribe in Rome."

Just then, footsteps approached.

"The landlady," Gerard said. "Perhaps she’ll let me dine here."

She brought his meal, expressionless, and left.

Gerard hesitated. "I eat poorly alone. Share this with me?"

Pietro’s eyes flashed. "You offer a stranger your food?"

"There’s enough."

With sudden violence, Pietro hurled the dish out the window.

"Let that teach you to pity an artist you do not know!"

Gerard flushed, torn between fury and pity. At last, coldly: "You had a right to refuse—not to waste my share. Pride is fair, but justice is better."

Pietro stared, then sighed. "I misjudged you. Forgive me. The world has soured me. Leave me now."

As Gerard turned to go, a thought struck him. "Since you stole my dinner, I claim a look at your hidden painting."

Pietro tensed. "I vowed to stab the next man who mocked it."

"Then I’ll hold my tongue."

Reluctantly, Pietro turned the canvas—a Virgin and Child soaring above a vast landscape.

Gerard studied it in silence, stepping close, then back.

"Well?" Pietro snapped at last.

Gerard drew his sword.

Pietro flinched. "What—?"

"To keep your dagger at bay while I speak." He kept the blade leveled, eyes flicking between man and painting.

"First, your colors lack Flemish depth. But your *vision*—Mother of Heaven! You’ve flung your soul onto this panel. The drapery could flow freer, the figures being airborne. But this landscape!" He marveled. "Each distance perfect, shrinking to the horizon. And you set your saints aloft, untouched by the world below. In Flanders, they’d tear this from your hands!"

Pietro’s defiance crumpled. He seized Gerard in a fervent embrace.

"They called it a madman’s dream!"

"Then they are fools," Gerard said. "Your work is immortal. Now, monster, will you sup with me?"

Pietro laughed through tears. "Gladly!"

"Then I’ll order food. You keep your picture company."

As Gerard left, Pietro clasped the canvas. "Child of my heart," he whispered, "may you outlive us both."
