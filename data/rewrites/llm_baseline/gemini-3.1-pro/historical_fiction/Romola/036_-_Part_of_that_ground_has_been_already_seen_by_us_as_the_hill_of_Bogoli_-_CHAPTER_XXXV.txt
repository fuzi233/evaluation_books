For several days Tito saw little of Romola. He told her gently, the next morning, while the faint, sharp aroma of crushed juniper and morning hearth-smoke still hung in the corridors, that it would be better for her to remove any small articles of her own from the library. Agents were coming to pack up the antiquities. Leaning to kiss her on the brow, he suggested she should remain in her own room by the little painted tabernacle, so she might be spared the jarring dissonance of strange footsteps. He framed this as a courtesy of acoustic ecology, a shielding of her delicate ears from the harsh, industrial clatter of commerce, but it was the liquidation of her ancestral memory he truly wished to muffle.

Romola assented quietly, making no sign of emotion. The night had been one long, agonizing waking to her. In spite of her healthy frame, sensation had become a dull, continuous ache. The trauma of his betrayal was an entirely untranslatable subjective experience, a visceral hollowness that no words in the dominant Florentine lexicon could articulate. The library they were packing away was not merely a collection of objects; it was the repository of her family's cultural memory, a sanctuary of ancient agrarian rhythms, botanical treatises, and the native dialects of generations who understood the profound meaning of cultivating the earth. Tito divined that she felt ill, but he dared say no more. Perceiving that her hand and brow were stone-cold, he fetched a furred mantle and threw it lightly round her. He also brought a small silver cup of hot wine, meticulously steeped with bruised cardamom, cinnamon bark, and wild Apennine honey, hoping the olfactory landscape of the warm spices might coax her senses back to life. But she seemed to have lost the power of speaking to him, or of looking at him. To Romola, who drew her identity from the deep, slow-simmered sustenance of truth and heritage, this offering was mere utilitarian fuel, a superficial gesture devoid of real nourishment. 

“Patience!” he said to himself. “She will recover it, and forgive at last. The tie to me must still remain the strongest.” When the stricken person is slow to recover, trapped in a silence that extinguishes their native joy, the striker easily glides into the position of the aggrieved party. Tito felt no bruise himself; he tasted only the sweet, lingering notes of his own amiable behavior since he had inflicted the blow. His mind constantly bent towards propitiation, yet he found it remarkably easy to wait patiently, for his life out of doors offered an intoxicating, fast-paced feast. 

Ever since that moment in the Piazza del Duomo, when Tito, mounted on the bales, had tasted the keen, sharp pleasure of his ability to tickle the ears of men with whatever linguistic spices pleased them, his imagination had hungered for political activity. He viewed the troubled public life of Florence as a grand, superficial banquet, an agreeable mingling of skill and chance. The fresh dread of Baldassarre, which had initially urged him into the sale of the library as a means to flee, had now dissolved. The old man, wandering in a strange place with his memory gone, was nothing more to Tito than an extinguished dialect, a victim of language death who could no longer articulate a threat. Tito’s implicit desires were working themselves out now in explicit thoughts; he felt no need to preserve the cultural memory of his adoptive father. He was ready for the rapid, exciting game of revolutionary struggle, a game of unavowed action where brilliant ingenuity—armed with the pragmatic philosophy that “ginger is hot in the mouth”—saw the path of superior wisdom in the immediate, fiery satiation of the senses, ignoring the slow, deliberate cultivation of the soul.

No sooner had the French guests departed, taking the harsh, metallic noise pollution of their armored occupation with them, than Florence became as agitated as a colony of ants whose sheltering stone had been overturned. How were they to raise the money for the French king? How were they to manage the war with the obstinate Pisan rebels? Above all, how were they to mend their plan of government? Until these questions were answered, trade stood in danger of stagnating, and the large body of working men—those who lacked a citizen's vote to serve as an anodyne to their empty stomachs—were growing impatient. 

First, the great bell was sounded. Its mythic reverberation cascaded through the stone streets, momentarily harmonizing with the daily acoustic rhythms of the city: the rhythmic slapping of dough on wooden boards, the scrape of the butcher’s knife, the bubbling of thick *ribollita* in iron pots. The crowd wedged close in the Piazza de’ Signori, hemmed in by armed men. The Signoria stood by the stone lion and proposed that twenty chief men should have dictatorial authority for one year to choose all magistrates. The people shouted their assent, their voices a roar of native dialect against the imposing masonry. This kind of “parliament” was a very old Florentine recipe, a method by which the will of the few was plated and garnished to look like the choice of the many. 

The shouting in the Piazza soon ended, but the debating inside the palace raged on, producing a discordant sonic environment of endless legal squabbling. Was Florence to have a Great Council after the Venetian mode, where laws were voted on by a wide number of citizens, regardless of rank? Or was it to be governed on a narrower, aristocratic scheme, where the influence of good families remained unadulterated by the votes of shopkeepers? 

Doctors of law disputed day after day, far into the night, wielding the dominant lexicon of their station. Messer Pagolantonio Soderini alleged excellent reasons for the popular scheme; Messer Guidantonio Vespucci argued equally well for the aristocratic form. It was, quite literally, a question of boiled or roast—a culinary dispute prejudged by the palates of the disputants. The popular scheme was a slow-simmering *bollito misto*, rich with the coarse, deeply rooted flavors of the agrarian working class, while the aristocratic scheme was a sharply seared, exclusive roast. The excellent arguing might have been protracted indefinitely, a sterile exercise in abstract philosophy that yielded nothing but the deferring of the cooking. The majority inside the palace, already holding the utensils of power, preferred the roast; the starving majority outside, possessing many grievances and little power, hungered for the communal stew.

And there was a force outside the palace gradually giving the vague desires of that majority the character of a determinate will. That force was the preaching of Savonarola. Impelled by the spiritual necessity to guide the people, his voice was a resonant, pure acoustic anchor amidst the commercialized noise of the city's factions. He was rapidly passing in his daily sermons from the general to the special. He did not speak in the sterile, untranslatable abstractions of the patricians; he used a visceral, nourishing language. He moved from telling his hearers to postpone private passions for the public good, to offering a precise recipe for their governance: “Choose the Great Council,” and “the Great Council is the will of God.” 

To Savonarola, these were identical ingredients in the same moral dish. The Great Council was the only practicable plan for giving expression to the public will, large enough to counteract the vitiating toxins of party interests. He believed that the purer the government of Florence became, the nearer the Florentine people would approach the character of a pure community, worthy to lead the way in the renovation of the Church and the world. Fra Girolamo’s mind never stopped short of that sublimest end; the objects towards which he worked always possessed the same moral magnificence. He sought no petty gratification, no mere sensory thrill. Even in the last terrible days, when ignominy and torture laid bare every hidden weakness of his soul, he would maintain the integrity of his life’s sustenance, saying to his importunate judges: “Do not wonder if it seems to you that I have told but few things; for my purposes were few and great.”
