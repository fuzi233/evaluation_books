Tito's sudden journey to Rome, which cleared the path for Romola's departure, had been decided at a dinner merely the previous evening. Tito had left for that meal buoyed by pleasant anticipations: delicate meats, choice wines, and a distinguished assembly. The venue was the *Selva* or *Orto de' Rucellai*, the Rucellai Gardens, hosted by Bernardo Rucellai, a quintessential Florentine magnate.

The family name itself holds a symbolically apt meaning. Properly interpreted, it evokes a small lichen, commonly known as *orcella* or *roccella*, which clings to the rocks of Greek islands and the Canaries. Soaking up abundant sunlight, this plant releases, under specific conditions, a reddish-purple dye highly prized by mankind. By importing the secret of this dye, *oricello*, from the East to Florence, a merchant living nearly a century prior to Bernardo amassed immense wealth and secured the surname Oricellari—or *Roccellari*. On the Tuscan tongue, this swiftly evolved into Rucellai. Our Bernardo stood out vividly against this purple backdrop. He had elevated the family status by marrying the sister of Lorenzo de' Medici, hosting a wedding so magnificent it remained unmatched in Florentine history. For these merits, plus others, he was dispatched on embassies to France and Venice, elected Gonfaloniere, constructed a splendid palace, and completed the black-and-white marble façade of Santa Maria Novella. He cultivated a garden of rare trees, transforming it into classic ground by hosting the Platonic Academy after Lorenzo's death. A learned author of a new topographical treatise on ancient Rome, a collector of antiquities, and a master of pure Latin, the simplest biography of Bernardo reads like a laudatory epitaph. One might imagine the Greek and Roman Muses tearing their hair in despair at Nature's audacity to pack such a multitude of virtues into a single set of organs.

Bernardo's invitation to Tito arrived via Lorenzo Tornabuoni, delivered with such gravity that it implied a political purpose, regardless of the era's distracting public crises. Indeed, Tito was certain that party aims were to be advanced alongside the flavors of stewed fish and aged Greek wine. Bernardo was not merely influential; he was one of the Elect Twenty currently steering Florence. This assurance lifted Tito's spirits as he approached the Via della Scala, where the classical garden lay. Without it, he might have feared the company would be as dull as distinguished, recalling previous evenings in the Rucellai grounds where he had been forced to endure entire philosophical systems—from the origins of the universe to their final maturation in a speaker's tractate.

It was a dark evening. Only when Tito crossed the intermittent glow of a lamp hung before an image of the Virgin did his silhouette become distinct enough for recognition. To any observer watching his passage from light to light, the tall, graceful figure with his mantle folded around him appeared constantly trailed by a starkly different form: a thickset, elderly man in a serge tunic and felt hat. Their union might have seemed accidental, given the crowd on the streets. Yet, when Tito halted at the gate of the Rucellai gardens, the figure behind him stopped as well.

The *sportello*, the smaller gate door, was held ajar by a servant distracted by another task, failing to close it after the last arrival. Tito stepped inside quickly, handing his name to the servant and moving past the evergreen bushes, which shimmered like metal in the torchlight. The follower entered immediately.

"Your name?" asked the servant.

"Baldassarre Calvo," came the instant reply.

"You are not a guest; all guests have passed."

"I belong to Tito Melema, who has just entered. I am to wait in the gardens."

The servant hesitated. "I was ordered to admit only guests. Are you a servant of Messer Tito?"

"No, friend, I am not a servant; I am a scholar."

There are those to whom one needs only say, "I am a buffalo," in a tone of quiet confidence, and they yield. The porter stepped aside instantly. Baldassarre entered, hearing the door click shut and chain fastened behind him as he vanished among the glowing shrubs.

Those steady, firm answers signaled a profound transformation in Baldassarre since their last confrontation, when the dagger had snapped in two. The change had manifested dramatically. Just as Tito's shadow passed the hovel on his way home, Baldassarre sat in the state of post-passion tremors familiar to anyone prone to violent outbursts—a condition where physical paralysis coexists with extraordinary mental clarity, as if the dissipation of excited passion clears away a mist of fire, leaving pure lucidity behind. Unable to rise immediately, his limbs numb and hands shaking, he sat enveloped not by the usual dimness, but by crystal-clear images of the past. He relived a continuous sequence of a life that seemed a long apprenticeship for bitterness.

For several minutes, so absorbed was he in these visions that he failed to realize he was witnessing them as memories. Then, as that sudden clarity traveled across the distance and settled on the recent scene, he anchored himself in the present. He recalled Monna Lisa and Tessa. Ah! *He* was the mysterious husband; the one with another wife in the Via de' Bardi. It was time to retrieve the broken dagger and vanish, leaving no trace. Hiding his weakness was the only semblance of power left to him. Leaning forward, he grasped the dagger's fragments, then turned to the open book beside him.

It was a fine, large manuscript—an unusual volume of Pausanias. Moonlight bathed the page, illuminating the large capital letters at the top: **ΜΕΣΣΕΝΙΚΑ. ΚΒ′**. In former days, he knew Pausanias intimately; yet an hour or two prior, staring at that page had yielded no more meaning than black weather-stains on a wall. Now, however, the letters were magical signs conjuring a world. The beam of moonlight striking the text resurrected Messenia and its struggle against Spartan oppression.

Snatching up the book, he realized the light was too faint for further reading. It mattered not; he knew the chapter. He read inwardly. He visualized the stoning of the traitor Aristocrates—cast out by a whole people to lie unburied, replaced by a pillar inscribed with verses declaring how Time had restored justice to the unjust. Words arose within him, stirring infinite vibrations of memory. He forgot his age; he could almost shout. The light had returned, mother of knowledge and joy! In that exultation, his limbs regained strength. He sprang up with the broken dagger and the book, stepping out beneath the broad moonlight.

The air was biting and frosty, yet Baldassarre felt no chill, only the glow of conscious power. He paced the open spaces of the high ground, gazing down upon the domed and towered city sleeping darkly beneath its mountain guardians, the pale gleam of the river, and the valley fading toward snowy peaks. He felt himself master of it all. That sense of mental empire available to everyone in moments of exceptional clarity was amplified for Baldassarre by the long days and nights when memory had been little more than the awareness of loss. The city, once a weary labyrinth, was now malleable material for his designs. His mind scanned its affairs with flashing conjecture; he was once again a man who knew cities, his vision instructed by vast experience, savoring the keen delight of holding everything within the grasp of language.

Names! Images! His mind raced through this wealth without pause, like an inheritor entering a great estate. Yet, amidst this rushing eagerness, one dark deity presided in the innermost cell of his consciousness—one seemingly forgotten only while his sacrifice was being prepared. Once the initial triumph of recovered certainty had played out, his thoughts centered on Tito. That fair, slippery viper could not escape him now. Thanks to struggling justice, the heart devoid of tenderness possessed sensitive, selfish fibers vulnerable to the sharp point of anguish. The soul that bowed to no right now bowed to the great lord of mortals: Pain. He could penetrate every secret of Tito's life. Some he already knew, and the failure of the broken dagger, seeming like frustration, marked the true beginning of his achievement.

Doubtless, that sudden rage had shattered the obstruction stifling his soul. Twice before, partial returns of memory occurred due to sudden excitement: once defending himself from an enraged dog, once scrambling up a rock to escape crashing waves. Yes, but if this time, as then, the light faded and the dreary blank returned! This time the light was stronger, steadier. Yet, what guarantee existed that before dawn the dark fog would not engulf him again? Even fear felt like the onset of weakness. Alarmed that this excited vigil on the hill might drain his remaining strength, he searched anxiously for shelter, finally nestling against a heap of warm garden straw until sleep claimed him.

When he awoke, it was daylight. The first moments brought strange bewilderment: a man with a double identity. Had he woken to the life of dim-sighted sensibilities, akin to the sad inheritance of fallen greatness, or to the life of recovered power? Surely the latter, for the night's events flooded back: the recognition of the page in Pausanias, the surging return of facts and names, the sudden, wide prospect that had granted him such a moment as the Maenad experiences in glorious amazement upon waking atop a mountain
