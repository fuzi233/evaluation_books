Martin Yorke had tasted the thrill of power and found it intoxicating. Once he’d known what it was to command attention, to be the center of a quiet storm of emotion, he could not bear the thought of returning to silence. Miss Helstone—once dismissed as plain, now haunting his thoughts like a shadow at dusk—had slipped into his world, and though she was gone, her presence lingered in every breath he drew. He sat at his desk in the grammar school, restless, his mind weaving new threads into the story he’d begun in secret: a tale not of kings or knights, but of himself and this girl whose eyes held more depth than any fairy legend ever promised.

On Saturday afternoons, he wandered the woods with a book of old tales in one hand and dreams in the other. But today, even the forest seemed dull compared to the possibility ahead—Sunday. For Martin, Sunday meant church, long sermons, and enforced stillness. Yet something stirred within him. Perhaps it was hope. Or pride. Or simply the need to see her again.

The morning broke under a sky heavy with snow. By breakfast, the drifts were deep enough that Mrs. Yorke declared no child would venture out. Instead, they were to spend two hours in the back parlour, reading from John Wesley’s sermons—her favorite, for his fire and reformist spirit. “Rose will do as she pleases,” Martin said, without looking up from his bread and milk, his voice low and calm as always.

“Rose will do as she is told,” replied his mother firmly. “And so will you.”

“I am going to church,” Martin said simply, the words falling like stones into silence.

His father frowned. “It’s not fit weather.”
No reply. Martin continued eating, slow and deliberate, as if chewing not only his meal but the very idea of disobedience.

“She hates church,” Mrs. Yorke muttered. “But she hates obedience more.”

“I suppose I’m just being stubborn?” Martin asked, half-serious.

“Yes,” she said. “You are.”

“Mother, I am not.”

“Then by what are you driven?”

“By a tangle of feelings so complicated, I couldn’t explain them any more than I could turn myself inside out and show you my bones.”

“Hear him!” Mr. Yorke laughed. “This boy is born for the bar. Hesther, our third son must become a lawyer—he has all the tools: courage, wit, and an endless supply of words.”

“Some bread, Rose, please,” Martin requested, as though nothing had been said. His tone was soft, almost sorrowful, yet unshakable. He rang the bell, then quietly asked for his walking shoes.

“Martin,” his father urged, “the snow is knee-deep. No man could walk through it.”

But Martin rose as the church bell began to toll. “If he wants to go,” said Mr. Yorke, “let him go. There’s a bitter wind and freezing sleet. Let him walk into it if he prefers it to a warm hearth.”

Martin donned his cloak and cap, stepped out without a word, and vanished into the storm.

As he walked, he whispered to himself: “If the weather keeps her away—or if Mrs. Pryor forbids her to come—I’ll be angry. But she *should* come. If she has any strength, any worth, she *will*. She’ll come to see me, just as I’ve come to see her. Not for duty, not for piety—but because we both know there’s something alive in this meeting. Adventure is life’s true flavor. Without it, everything tastes flat.”

He arrived early. The church stood cold and empty, save for one elderly woman. Slowly, others trickled in—old men, frail women, those who never missed a service, no matter how harsh the winter. The pews of the wealthy remained vacant. Only the humble wooden benches held the faithful.

Martin scanned the room. Where was she?

When the rector entered, when the service began, still she did not appear. He clenched his jaw. “She won’t come,” he muttered. “Worthless. Weak. Just another girl—plain, selfish, shallow. Her eyes aren’t bright, her nose isn’t fine, her mouth doesn’t hold the magic I imagined. She’s just a doll. A toy.”

So lost in his thoughts, he failed to rise during the litany. When the hymn began, he was still on his knees—caught in a moment of mock devotion. He jumped up, flushed with embarrassment. Worse, the church door opened again—the Sunday scholars poured in, their little feet patter-pattering across the stone floor.

They settled, then came the older girls—tall, quiet, serious. And among them, she stepped in. The French-gray cloak, the small beaver bonnet—his heart leapt. She had come.

Martin hid his face behind his hymn book for a moment, then straightened. She had not looked at him. Not once.

“If she ignores me,” he thought, “if she pretends I don’t exist… then I’ll despise her even more. To come here for those children, and not for me? Or for Moore? That would be the worst kind of failure.”

The sermon ended. The congregation dispersed. Still, she had not approached him.

Now, walking home, the wind bit through his coat. He chose a shortcut across the fields—a path untouched by foot, treacherous under snow. As he climbed toward the second stile, he saw movement beneath a tree—an umbrella struggling against the gale, and beside it, the familiar gray cloak.

He smiled. Then stopped. Sat on the stile, cool and composed.

“You should make a deal,” he said. “Trade me for Mrs. Pryor.”

“I wasn’t sure you’d come this way,” she answered. “But I took the chance. There’s no peace in church or churchyard—too many eyes.”

“Will you trade? Give Mrs. Pryor to my mother, and put me in her place?”

“What nonsense! Why bring her into this?”

“You call her ‘mamma,’ don’t you?”

“She *is* my mamma.”

“Impossible. Such a careless, indifferent mother. I’d be ten times better. You may laugh. I don’t mind. Your teeth—most people hate ugly teeth, but yours are lovely—like pearls, even, well-shaped and perfect.”

“Martin, what is this?” she said, startled. “I thought Yorkes didn’t give compliments.”

“They haven’t, until now. I feel called to change the family tradition. I’m tired of being like my ancestors—four generations of stern, solemn men. All named Hiram or Samuel or John. They were exactly like my father. Before them, there was Godfrey. We have his portrait—it hangs in Moore’s room. He looks like me. Long dark curls, fine clothes. Handsome. Different. I believe he was different. And I intend to be too.”

“You’re not handsome, Martin.”

“No,” he said, “but wait. I mean to become so. Starting today. I’ll cultivate myself. Polish myself. Watch.”

“You’re strange, Martin. And I doubt you’ll ever be handsome.”

“I’ll try. But let’s return to Mrs. Pryor. How can she allow her daughter to walk out in this storm?”

“She worried,” she admitted. “But I insisted. I *had* to come.”

“To see me?”

“Exactly. I feared the snow would keep you away. But when I saw you alone in your pew… I was relieved.”

“I came to fulfill my duty,” he said, “and set an example.”

“And you were obstinate?”

“I like obstinacy. I’d love to see you stubborn. I’d train you, if I could.”

“Don’t think you’ll ever be handsome,” she said, laughing.

“I’ll try.”

“Tell me about Mr. Moore,” she said quickly.

“That,” he replied, “is why you came.”

“Come on—just tell me.”

“Hang him—he’s no worse. But still imprisoned. Starved. They want to declare him mad. Horsfall treats him like a beast. You saw how thin he was.”

“You were good to him the other day.”

“What day? I’m always good. A model.”

“When will you be good again?”

“I see where you’re going. But you won’t wheedle me. I’m not a cat’s-paw.”

“It must be done. It’s right. Necessary.”

“You encroach. I helped before—not because I was asked, but because I wanted to.”

“And you’ll help again.”

“I won’t. It was too much trouble. I prefer ease.”

“Mr. Moore wishes to see me. I wish to see him.”

“I dare say,” he said coolly.

“It’s unfair of your mother to shut out his friends.”

“Tell her so.”

“His own family.”

“Go blow her up.”

“You know that would do nothing. But I’ll persist. I’ll see him. With or without help.”

“Good,” Martin said. “Nothing like self-reliance.”

“I have no time to argue. You’re provoking. Goodbye.”

She turned and left, shutting the umbrella against the wind.

Martin watched her go. Then smiled.

“She’s not vapid,” he murmured. “Not shallow. She’s strong. I’ll watch her—see how far she’ll go to get what she wants. If the storm were fire, she’d walk through it to speak to Moore for five minutes. I’ve had a good morning. The delays, the anger—they made the moment sweeter. She expected me to yield at once. She won’t win so easily. She’ll come again. And again. I’d like to see her angry. To see her cry. I want to know how far she’ll go. What she’ll risk. What she truly feels.”

He turned toward home, appetite stirring. “Won’t I enjoy that goose?” he said aloud. “And whoever gets the biggest slice of apple pie—Matthew or I—we’ll have to fight for it.”
