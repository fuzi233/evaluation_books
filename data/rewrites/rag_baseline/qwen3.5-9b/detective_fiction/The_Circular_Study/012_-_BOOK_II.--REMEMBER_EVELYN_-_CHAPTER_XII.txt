Mr. Gryce possessed a talent for finesse, a subtle art he deployed with practiced ease. He had declared his intent to trail Mr. Adams, and indeed, he followed, boarding the identical train and claiming the seat directly behind the young man. From this vantage point, he observed the subject with a growing, unsettling fascination. This youth intrigued him in a manner entirely unforeseen, stirring something within Gryce's own conscience that forced him to confront the weight of his years—a burden he felt ill-equipped to bear so soon after concluding an intricate and arduous pursuit.

Seated at a respectful distance, Gryce watched the metamorphosis unfolding on the prisoner's handsome countenance. He noted the profound calm that now settled those features, which had mere hours prior contorted in the deepest agony. Where did this fortitude originate? What reservoir of strength allowed this young man to sit so stoically beneath the scrutinizing gaze of men whose approval he had desperately sought only an hour before? Was it that courage blooms exclusively from the soil of despair? Or was the boy so consumed by his internal misery that he remained oblivious to the shadow it cast upon the world? His brow furrowed in brooding contemplation, his eyes vacant and distant, suggesting a mind utterly withdrawn from the immediate surroundings. Into what abysses of remorse had he plummeted? No one could say, certainly not this veteran detective, seasoned by decades of contact with criminals, some of whom shared this young man's cultivated appearance and social standing.

At the Brooklyn station, Gryce reconnected with his charge, who barely lifted his head as the older man approached. Within an hour, they stood before the imposing doors of Police Headquarters, where the formal interrogation of Mr. Adams commenced. The suspect made no attempt to evade the proceedings; rather, he appeared eager to speak, driven by a heavy burden he longed to shed. Yet, the confession that emerged bore no resemblance to the grim anticipation of the officers. He did not admit to murdering his brother, but instead confessed to being the incidental catalyst of that tragedy.

"My name is Cadwalader, not Adams," he began, his voice steady despite the gravity of his admission. "My father, a Scot by birth, became a naturalized citizen of Pennsylvania, settling in a hamlet named Montgomery when he was a young husband. He had two children then; one perished in infancy, while the other was my brother, Felix. It was under the name of Adams that your authorities have summoned me today to explain his violent death. I am the fruit of a subsequent union, entered into by my father several years after he departed Montgomery. Born in Harrisburg, I knew nothing of that city, for my father left shortly after I reached my third year. My earliest memories are not of this land, but of vastly different scenes. Having lost my mother while I was still an infant, I was dispatched to the Old World, whence my father hailed, returning only this very year. Upon my arrival, I found my father dying and my brother, Felix, a grown man possessing considerable wealth—a fortune I had assumed he intended to share with me. But once my father was laid to rest, Felix..." (a visible tremor seized his throat as he struggled to utter the name) "...Felix descended upon New York, leaving me adrift without settled hopes or any tangible means to construct a future or launch a career."

"While wandering aimlessly, I stumbled upon the town where my father had spent his youth. Hunting down his old acquaintances, I encountered in the residence of a fellow emigrant from Scotland a young lady" (his voice fractured, the accent thickening with emotion as he whispered the beloved name) "...in whom I quickly became enamored, to the extent that I desired nothing less than to wed her. Yet, I possessed no money, no profession, no home to offer her, and, as I was compelled to acknowledge, no prospects whatsoever. Still, I could not abandon the hope of making her my wife. Thus, I wrote to my brother, Felix Cadwalader—or, as he preferred to be known in later years for reasons entirely detached from his sudden demise, Felix Adams—and requested that he settle a specific sum upon me, enabling me to marry her with a measure of self-respect. My sole response was a repetition of the vague promises he had previously dangled before me. But youth is inherently hopeful, bordering on audacious, and I resolved to claim her without further negotiation, banking on the belief that her beauty and endearing nature would compel him, at first sight, to grant the definitive concession he had so stubbornly withheld."

"This I did. The fault for which I harbor the greatest regret is that I entered this alliance without disclosing the truth to either her or her father. They perceived me as well-off, perhaps wealthy. While Mr. Poindexter is undoubtedly a man of means, I am certain that had he known I possessed nothing but the clothes on my back and a trifling amount of pocket change, he would have halted the marriage in its tracks, for he is a man of immense ambition and regards his daughter as worthy of a millionaire's devotion—as she truly is."

"Felix, whom you must pardon if I display no affection for, given how strange a creature he proved to be, was notified of my marriage. However, he chose neither to witness the ceremony nor to forbid it. Consequently, the event was conducted quietly, with strangers serving as witnesses, within a hotel parlor. Armed with both vague hopes and certain, gnawing fears, I prepared to introduce my young bride to my brother. Hardened as he was by years of bachelorhood, I reasoned that he could not remain entirely impervious to feminine charm, failing to recognize my wife as a woman deserving of every consideration."

"But I had calculated without my host. Two days after the ceremony that bound us together, when I escorted her to the house that has since become so tragically infamous, I discovered that my brother had revealed to me but a single facet of his nature, and the least obstinate of his many sides."

"As brilliant as steel, he was equally hard. Not only did he profess himself unmoved by my wife's manifold charms, but he also declared himself totally unsympathetic to follies such as love and marriage, dismissing them as the fruits of unoccupied minds and a pastime wholly unworthy of men boasting of our talents and attainments. Then, he turned his back upon us. Overcome by an anger bordering on frenzy, I launched into an abuse for which he was so unprepared that he crouched like a man under blows. Losing his self-control moment by moment, he eventually snatched up a dagger lying nearby and, crying out, 'You want my money? Well, then, take it!' drove the blade into his own heart with one desperate, fatal thrust."

"I fear I shall not be believed, but that is the story of this crime, gentlemen."
