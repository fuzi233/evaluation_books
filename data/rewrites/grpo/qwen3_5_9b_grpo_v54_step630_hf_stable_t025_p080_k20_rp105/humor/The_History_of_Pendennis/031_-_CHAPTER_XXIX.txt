The reader is invited to leave the quiet woods and the whispering sea-shores of the west, to trade the gossip of Clavering and the sleepy rhythm of Fairoaks for the roar of London. Arthur Pendennis rides the 'Alacrity' coach into the night, a vessel hurtling him toward the city where he intends to face the world and carve out his fortune. As the wheels spin through the darkness, the young man's mind races with schemes of prudence, success, and fame. He knows he has outgrown the boy who walked this road a year ago; his first failure has stripped away his naivety, leaving behind a sharper courage and a higher opinion of his own potential. A hundred eager fantasies keep him awake, fueled by the belief that his mishaps have aged him into wisdom.

His thoughts drift with tender reverence to his mother, who blesses him as he departs. He prays to the stars above her that she remains awake, sending purer prayers for his safety than he can muster himself. To him, her love is a talisman, a shield against the perils of the city. He also thinks of Laura, whose affection he has been denied because he feels unworthy. With a mix of shame and relief, he acknowledges her superior nature, comparing himself to a prodigal who flees a church he once loved, not out of hate, but because he feels he has no right to enter such a holy space.

These reflections keep him awake until the nipping dawn of an October morning. When the coach stops at the familiar breakfasting house at B——, Pen wakes refreshed. The sun breaks through, the horn blows, and the milestones fly by. The road grows busier, the coach rattles into London, and a thrill runs through the young man. "Here is my place," he thinks, "here begins my battle." From the roof of the coach, he gazes down at the vast city with the longing desire of a soldier on the eve of a campaign.

On the journey, he has struck up a conversation with a cheery fellow-passenger in a shabby cloak, a reporter named Mr. Doolan who talks endlessly of literary giants like Tom Campbell and Sydney Smith. Pointing out Mr. Hurtle, the famous reviewer, Doolan confirms Pen's admiration. Pen calculates wildly that if he can match the earnings of the novelist Wagg, he might make five thousand a year.

Upon arriving at the Gloster Coffee-house, his first sight is that of his old friend Harry Foker, prancing down Arlington Street in white kid gloves and sporting a tuft on his chin. Foker, accompanied by a knock-kneed cab-boy, waves a whip in greeting. Excited to be in London and flush with a hundred pounds, Pen invites Doolan to dine at the Covent Garden Coffee-house, where he stays. He dresses in his best, orders a modest pint of sherry to show economy, and heads to the theatre.

The lights, music, and gaiety charm the country boy. He laughs at jokes and applauds songs, delighting the bored regulars in the boxes. After the first act, he struts through the lobbies like a man of high fashion. He is joined by Foker, who introduces him to Grandy Tiptoff and Lord Colchicum, a venerable peer who has chased pleasure since the French Revolution. They rush into a private box where sits Miss Blenkinsop, the eminent actress, and her father, the "veteran" Blenkinsop, snoozing in a wig.

While Pen admires Miss Blenkinsop's beauty, believing her to sing like a nightingale, the men in the box discuss Mrs. Leary, an actress in a hussar jacket, and declare her singing out of tune. When the actor Hoppus appears, the gentlemen grow bored, but Miss Blenkinsop insists that Lord Colchicum gave her a bouquet. Pen blushes as the peer mentions introducing him to the Megatherium Club.

Suddenly, the conversation turns to "Mirabel." Harry Foker laughs, revealing that Mirabel is Miss Fotheringay, Pen's first love, who married Sir Charles Mirabel the previous year. Pen looks out and sees her again, wondering how he could have ever loved her.

Thus, on his very first night in London, Arthur Pendennis finds himself entangled with a club, a heavy-acting father, and a dashing society of jovial blades. Lord Colchicum, despite his age, is indefatigable in his pursuit of enjoyment, hosting sumptuous banquets for dramatic professors from all nations. The next day, Pen pays his entrance fee to the Club, spending a third of his hundred pounds with immense satisfaction. He writes a letter to Fairoaks, imagining the comfort of the club after his work, and visits his uncle's lodgings in Bury Street. Finding the Major absent, he leaves his card.

When the Major returns in November, he discovers a note from Arthur stating that he has joined the Upper Temple to study for the bar. Intrigued and slightly concerned, the Major sends his valet, Morgan, to investigate. Morgan reports that Arthur lives in a "shy" place on the third floor of Lamb Court, sharing rooms with Mr. Warrington. The Major, initially dismissive of the grim surroundings, decides to visit.

He navigates the dismal alleys and archways of the Temple until he reaches Lamb Court. The atmosphere is bleak, lit by feeble oil lamps, filled with the sounds of clerks and the sight of wigmakers' shops. Shuddering at the thought of his nephew living in such filth, the Major climbs the damp, black stairs to the third floor. He finds the rooms open and smoky.

An Irish charwoman lets him in. Inside, a great voice demands beer. Seated on a table is Mr. Warrington, unshorn and smoking a pipe, while Pen sits nearby with a cigar. The Major, choking on the smoke, introduces himself. Warrington, a younger son of Sir Miles Warrington of Suffolk, is a rough diamond: dressed in a ragged shooting jacket, drinking beer like a coal-heaver, yet possessing a frank, gentlemanly demeanor. He cooks mutton chops on a gridiron in the room, teaching Pen the "first principles of law, cooking, and morality" simultaneously.

During their meal, the Major learns that Pen has been to Lady Whiston's party and met an "old flame," Lady Mirabel. The Major, in his simple philosophy, advises his nephew to abandon youthful follies. He argues that a woman with money is a much easier profession than the law, urging Pen to aim for Parliament, a good carriage, and a ladylike wife rather than vegetating at Fairoaks. Pen listens, wondering what his mother and Laura would think of this pragmatic advice.

After the Major leaves, Warrington emerges from his bedroom, now dressed neatly, and shows off his ragged but cozy quarters. He contrasts his own Spartan existence—sleeping on straw, reading Plato, and cooking his own food—with Pen's "dandy" room, complete with curtains, shiny boots, and prints of opera dancers. The Major watches in amusement as Warrington prepares another meal, marveling at the eccentricity of his nephew's new life in the heart of London's legal underworld.
