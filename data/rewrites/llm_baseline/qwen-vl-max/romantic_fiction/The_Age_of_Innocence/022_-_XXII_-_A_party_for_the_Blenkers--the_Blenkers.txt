XXII. "A party for the Blenkers—the Blenkers?" Mr. Welland set down his knife and fork, his eyes widening with disbelief as he stared across the luncheon table at his wife. She adjusted her gold-rimmed spectacles and read aloud in a voice laced with theatrical irony: "Professor and Mrs. Emerson Sillerton request the pleasure of Mr. and Mrs. Welland’s company at the meeting of the Wednesday Afternoon Club on August 25th at 3 o’clock punctually. To meet Mrs. and the Misses Blenker. Red Gables, Catherine Street. R.S.V.P."

"Good heavens—" Mr. Welland gasped, as if the absurdity of it required a second glance to fully register.

"Poor Amy Sillerton," Mrs. Welland sighed, lowering her voice. "You never know what her husband will do next. I suppose he's just discovered the Blenkers."

Professor Emerson Sillerton was a persistent thorn in the side of Newport society—unyielding, unremovable, rooted in a family tree both venerable and respected. He had every advantage: a father who was Sillerton Jackson’s uncle, a mother from Boston’s Pennilow lineage, wealth, breeding, and social standing. Yet nothing—nothing at all—compelled him to become an archaeologist, or even a professor, or to winter in Newport, or to host gatherings that flouted tradition. But if he must break from custom, why, then, had he married Amy Dagonet? A woman who deserved more than eccentricity, who had funds enough to maintain her own carriage, who ought to have been spared such a life.

No one among the Mingott circle could fathom how Amy had endured so quietly—a house filled with long-haired scholars and short-haired intellectuals, trips to Yucatan tombs instead of Parisian salons. Still, they were fixed in their ways, unaware of their strangeness. And each year, thanks to the Sillerton-Pennilow-Dagonet connection, every family along the Cliffs drew lots to send a reluctant representative to their dreary garden parties.

"It’s a wonder," Mrs. Welland mused, "that they didn’t pick Cup Race day! Do you remember two years ago, when they hosted a party for a Black man on Julia Mingott’s *thé dansant*? Luckily this time there’s nothing else scheduled—at least, some of us will have to go."

Mr. Welland exhaled heavily. "'Some of us,' my dear—more than one? Three o'clock is terribly inconvenient. I must be back by half-past three for my drops. Bencomb’s treatment requires consistency; otherwise, it’s useless. And if I join you later, I’ll miss my drive." He laid his cutlery down again, a flush creeping up his finely lined cheek.

"There’s no reason for you to go at all," Mrs. Welland said cheerfully, though the tone had grown automatic over years of managing expectations. "I need to leave cards at the other end of Bellevue Avenue. I’ll drop in around half-past three and stay long enough to show poor Amy we haven’t slighted her." She glanced hesitantly toward her daughter. "And if Newland has plans, perhaps May can take you out with the ponies—try their new russet harness?"

It was a sacred principle in the Welland household that every hour should be “provided for.” The very idea of idle time—especially for those who disliked whist or solitaire—was a horror to Mrs. Welland, like the specter of unemployment haunting a philanthropist. Another tenet: parents must never visibly interfere in their married children’s lives. Balancing these ideals meant constant ingenuity, leaving not a moment of her own time unaccounted for.

"Of course I’ll go with Papa," May said gently, reminding her husband of his lack of planning. "Newland will surely find something to do."

It pained Mrs. Welland that her son-in-law showed so little foresight. Already during the fortnight he’d spent under her roof, whenever she asked what he planned for the afternoon, he’d replied with a peculiar nonchalance: “Oh, I think I’ll just save it this time,” or once, after she and May had gone on overdue calls, confessed he’d lain all afternoon beneath a rock on the beach below the house.

“Newland never seems to look ahead,” she once whispered to May.

“No,” May answered serenely. “But you see, it doesn’t matter. When there’s nothing particular to do, he reads.”

“Ah, yes—like his father!” Mrs. Welland conceded, as if accepting a familial quirk. From then on, the subject of Newland’s idle hours was dropped.

Yet as the date of the Sillerton gathering neared, May began to show quiet concern for her husband’s comfort. She suggested a tennis match at the Chiverses’ or a sail on Julius Beaufort’s cutter—anything to make up for her temporary absence. “I’ll be back by six, dear. Papa never drives later than that—” and she wasn’t truly reassured until Archer mentioned hiring a run-about to drive up the island and visit a stud-farm to look at a second horse for her brougham.

They’d been searching for this horse for weeks. The suggestion pleased May so much she glanced at her mother as if to say: *See? He knows how to plan his days like any of us.*

The idea had formed in Archer’s mind the moment the Sillerton invitation surfaced. He’d kept it to himself, as if the plan carried a secret urgency. But he’d already arranged the run-about with a pair of old livery-stable trotters still capable of eighteen miles on flat roads. At two o’clock, he abandoned the luncheon table abruptly and leapt into the light carriage.

The day was perfect. A northern breeze pushed soft clouds across a sky of deep blue, with sunlight glinting off the sea. Bellevue Avenue lay empty at that hour. After dropping the stable boy at Mill Street, Archer turned onto the Old Beach Road and drove across Eastman’s Beach.

He felt the familiar thrill of half-holidays at school—the anticipation of going somewhere unknown. Keeping the horses at a steady pace, he aimed to reach the stud-farm beyond Paradise Rocks before three, so he might examine the horse—and test it if promising—before having four golden hours left to fill.

As soon as he heard about the Sillerton party, he’d known the Marchioness Manson would likely come with the Blenkers. Perhaps Madame Olenska would spend the day with her grandmother again. At the very least, the Blenker house would probably be empty—offering him a chance, without impropriety, to satisfy a strange curiosity.

He wasn’t certain he wanted to see the Countess Olenska again. But ever since he’d glimpsed her from the path above the bay, he’d felt an irrational, indefinable longing—to see the place she lived, to trace the imagined path of her figure through the summer-house, just as he’d watched her real self move beneath the trees.

The desire was constant, restless, like a sick man’s sudden craving for a forgotten taste. He couldn’t imagine what it might lead to. He didn’t wish to speak to her, nor hear her voice. He only knew that if he could carry away the image of the land she walked upon—the way the sea and sky framed it—the rest of the world might feel less hollow.

When he arrived at the stud-farm, a quick glance told him the horse wasn’t right. Still, he took a turn behind it, pretending patience. By three o’clock, he snapped the reins and turned toward Portsmouth.

The wind had died. A faint haze on the horizon signaled fog waiting to creep up the Saconnet with the tide. But around him, fields and woods shimmered in golden light. He passed grey-shingled farmhouses nestled in orchards, hayfields, groves of oak, villages with white steeples piercing the fading sky. Finally, after asking directions from men working in a field, he turned down a narrow lane flanked by high banks of goldenrod and brambles.

At the end, the river sparkled blue. To the left, amid a cluster of oaks and maples, stood a long, dilapidated house—white paint peeling from its clapboards. On the roadside, facing the gate, stood one of the open sheds where New Englanders stored tools and visitors tied their teams.

Archer jumped down, led the horses into the shed, and tied them to a post. Then he approached the house.

The lawn had reverted to a hayfield. To the left, an overgrown box-garden burst with dahlias and rust-colored roses, encircling a ghostly summer-house of weathered trellis, once white, crowned by a wooden Cupid missing his bow and arrow but still aiming uselessly.

Archer leaned against the gate. No one was visible. Not a sound came from the open windows. A grizzled Newfoundland slept before the door—just as ineffective a guardian as the arrowless Cupid.

It was odd to think this silent ruin belonged to the turbulent Blenkers. Yet he was certain he was right.

For a long while, he stood there, soaking in the scene, slowly surrendering to its quiet spell. Then, roused by the passage of time, he hesitated—should he linger, or go?

He suddenly wished to see inside the house—to picture the room where Madame Olenska sat. Nothing stopped him from walking up and ringing the bell. If she were away, as he suspected, he could introduce himself and ask permission to enter briefly to write a message.

Instead, he crossed the lawn and stepped into the box-garden.

As he entered, he caught sight of something bright in the summer-house. A pink parasol.

It pulled him like a magnet. It had to be hers.

He stepped inside, sat on the wobbly bench, picked up the silken umbrella, and studied its carved handle—made of rare wood that released a sweet, aromatic scent. He lifted it to his lips.

Then—rustling of skirts against the bushes.

He froze, hands clasped around the handle, eyes lowered, letting the sound draw closer.

He’d always known this might happen…

“Oh, Mr. Archer!” a loud, youthful voice exclaimed.

He looked up.

Before him stood the youngest and largest of the Blenker girls—blonde, rumpled, in bedraggled muslin. A red blotch on her cheek hinted she’d been sleeping. Her half-awake eyes blinked at him, hospitable but confused.

“Gracious—where did you drop from? I must’ve been fast asleep in the hammock. Everyone else went to Newport. Did you ring?”

Archer’s confusion matched hers.

“I—no—that is, I was about to. I came up the island to see about a horse, and I thought I’d stop by to see if Mrs. Blenker or your guests were here. But the house seemed empty—so I sat down to wait.”

Miss Blenker shook off sleep, her gaze sharpening. “The house *is* empty. Mother’s not here. Neither is the Marchioness. Just me.”

Her expression softened with mild reproach. “Didn’t you know Professor and Mrs. Sillerton are giving a garden party for us this afternoon? It’s too bad I couldn’t go—I’ve had a sore throat, and mother didn’t want me driving home tonight. Isn’t it dreadful? Of course,” she added brightly, “I wouldn’t have minded nearly as much if I’d known you were coming.”

A hint of clumsy flirtation stirred in her. Archer found strength to interrupt: “But Madame Olenska—has she gone to Newport too?”

Miss Blenker looked surprised. “Madame Olenska? Didn’t you know she’d been called away?”

“Called away?” he echoed.

“Oh, my best parasol! I lent it to Katie—because it matched her ribbons—and careless thing dropped it here. We Blenkers are all like that… real Bohemians!” She reclaimed the sunshade with a strong hand, unfurled it, and held it aloft like a banner.

“Yes, Ellen was called away yesterday. She lets us call her Ellen, you know. A telegram came from Boston—she said she might be gone two days. I *love* the way she does her hair, don’t you?”

Archer stared past her, as if she were transparent.

All he saw was the trumpery pink parasol shielding her giggling head.

After a pause, he ventured: “Do you happen to know why Madame Olenska went to Boston? I hope it wasn’t bad news?”

Miss Blenker dismissed the idea with cheerful disbelief. “Oh, I don’t believe so. She didn’t tell us what was in the telegram. I think she didn’t want the Marchioness to know. She’s so romantic-looking, isn’t she? Doesn’t she remind you of Mrs. Scott-Siddons reading ‘Lady Geraldine’s Courtship’? Have you ever heard her?”

Archer’s thoughts raced. His entire future unfolded before him—an endless emptiness, and within it, the shrinking figure of a man to whom nothing would ever happen.

He glanced around—overgrown garden, crumbling house, dusk gathering beneath the oaks.

It had seemed the perfect place to find her. But she was far away. And even the parasol wasn’t hers.

He frowned, hesitated.

“You don’t know, I suppose—well, I’m going to Boston tomorrow. If I could manage to see her…”

He sensed Miss Blenker losing interest, though her smile remained.

“Oh, of course! How lovely of you! She’s staying at the Parker House—it must be awful there in this weather.”

From then on, Archer barely registered their conversation. He only remembered stubbornly refusing her plea to wait for the family’s return and share tea.

Eventually, with his hostess still beside him, he stepped out from beneath the wooden Cupid, unhitched his horses, and drove off.

At the lane’s turn, he saw Miss Blenker standing at the gate, waving the pink parasol.
