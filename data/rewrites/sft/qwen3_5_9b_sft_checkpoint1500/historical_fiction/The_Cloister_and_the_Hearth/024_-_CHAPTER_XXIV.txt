Gerard encountered a small party resting by the roadside. Two servants in somber attire leaned idly against a horse, chatting, while their master, a knight clad in a silk doublet and a vibrant green jerkin, lay prone in the dust like a sluggish lizard. His velvet cloak, a blazing yellow, was draped neatly over the horse's flank.

"Is anything amiss?" Gerard asked.

"Not that I know of," replied one servant. "But your master lies as if dead. Are you not ashamed to let him sprawl there?"

"The bare earth is the finest remedy for his malady," the other scoffed. "If you become sober in bed, you wake with a splitting headache, but rising from hard ground makes you jump up like a lark in spring. Does not Ulric speak truth, boy?"

"He speaks plainly, young sir," Ulric agreed warmly.

"What!" Gerard exclaimed, "Is the gentleman drunk?"

The servants erupted in a rough laughter at his naivety. Suddenly, Ulric halted, scanning Gerard with piercing intensity. "Who are you, and where born, that you know not the Count is always drunk at this hour?" Gerard instantly felt the weight of suspicion.

"I am a stranger," Gerard stammered, "but a truthful man who loves learning; therefore I ask questions, not out of prying."

"If you be a true man," Ulric said shrewdly, "give us a trinket for the knowledge we have shared."

Gerard looked confused but forced a smile. "A trinket you shall receive, whatever my lean purse can spare, if you tell me why you have taken his cloak from the man and placed it on the beast."

Inspired by the prospect of payment, the servants immediately proposed two theories. First, that if the Count regained consciousness and found his horse sweating in the chill while his cloak lay discarded, he would certainly curse and perhaps lash out. Second, more philosophically, that a horse is a simpleton requiring warmth and care, whereas a master, constantly filled with good beer, possesses an internal heat sufficient to ward off the cold, rendering the cloak a mere vanity. Both men passionately embraced their respective arguments until their voices rose so high that the green-clad sot began to growl in his sleep.

Their debate quickly soured. Hans, holding the bridle, cuffed Ulric soundly; Ulric retaliated with equal fervor. They exchanged blows over the horse's mane, battering the poor animal until it reared backward and stepped heavily onto the green lord's foot. The knight, like a frog stung by a spear, leaped up howling, clutching his ankle and drawing his sword. Terrified, the servants released the reins. The horse galloped away, the men pursued in panic, and the enraged knight chased them down the lane, his sword drawn and his body bouncing wildly from hedge to hedge.

"In which hurry," Gerard muttered, turning his back on the chaos, he walked calmly south. He was relieved to have saved the four tin farthings he had intended to offer as a bribe, yet his heart remained heavy at their drunken folly.

The sun was nearing its zenith, dipping low in the western sky. Gerard, who had hoped in vain for an inn, grew increasingly uneasy. Black clouds gathered overhead, and he quickened his pace to a run. Rain fell in torrents, soaking the bewildered traveler and seemingly extinguishing the fading sunlight.

Gerard trudged on, dark, wet, and lost in an unfamiliar region. "Fool," he muttered to himself, "to leave Margaret."

Darkness thickened as he entered a vast wood. Huge branches blocked the narrow path, and the benighted stranger groped through what felt like an endless, ink-black cavern with a rugged floor. He stumbled repeatedly, his limbs shivering, his stomach empty, and his heart faint. Suddenly, wolves rose from their dens, bayed around the trees, and filled the air with their harsh cries.

Gerard's hair bristled, but he gripped his cudgel, preparing to sell his life dearly. With no wind, his heightened senses caught the patter of light feet over fallen leaves and the rustle of low branches as creatures glided swiftly past.

Amidst the sea of darkness, a great fiery star appeared close to the ground. He hailed it as his patron saint, shouting, "CANDLE! A CANDLE!" and attempting to run toward it. However, the terrain was too treacherous, and the light proved distant. Finally, deep within the forest, he spotted a house with lit windows and voices rising inside. Looking for a signboard, he found none. "Not an inn, after all!" he sighed. "Yet, what Christian would turn a dog out into this wood tonight?"

Making for the door, he opened it slowly and peered inside timidly. Abruptly, he recoiled as if slapped, returning to the rain and darkness. Inside lay a large, low room dominated by a huge round clay stove reaching to the ceiling. Wet clothes hung everywhere—some on lines, others on rustic poles. These garments, soaked with the day's rain and stained by a life of labor, emitted rank vapors and indescribable odors.

In one corner sat a large traveling family, bringing with them the peculiar, sickly smell of neglected children. Garlic permeated the air, filling every space. With windows closed and an intense heat radiating from the central furnace, the breath of at least forty persons created a stifling atmosphere. They had just finished supper.

Like many artists, Gerard possessed sensitive organs, and the potent effluvia struck him with dismay. Yet, the relentless rain outside and the temptation of the light and fire drew him back again and again, like a moth to a flame. Upon closer inspection, he realized the various smells did not fully blend. An odor of family prevailed in two corners; stewed rustic foods reigned supreme in the center; and garlic, emanating from a noisy group by the window, occupied the smallest orbital space. The scent of the reeking rustic foods, however, drifted farthest—a flavor reminiscent of ancient goats or the ancestors of all foxes, dried by Nebuchadnezzar.

Creeping into a corner near the door, Gerard found himself besieged. Though the solid fetors isolated him slightly, the circulating heat and reeking vapors made the walls drip. He felt as though a cold snake wound about his legs, his head turning to a heavy lump of lead. Next, he felt the urge to choke, to sweetly slumber, and to die, all in one motion. Within an ace of swooning, he recovered with a deep sense of disgust and discouragement, resolving to return to Holland at first light. Gathering a modicum of courage, he approached a man steeped in garlic and asked if this was truly an inn.

"Whence come you, who know not 'The Star of the Forest'?" the man replied.

"I am a stranger; in my country, inns always have signs."

"Droll country yours! What need of a sign to a public-house, a place known to every soul?"

Too tired for argument, Gerard asked where he could find the landlord. This display of ignorance sparked the native's contempt. He pointed to a middle-aged woman seated opposite the oven and signaled to his companions, who fell silent as the news spread, their eyes turning upon Gerard like pivots following a zoo exhibit.

The landlady sat on a chair slightly elevated above the others, between two bundles of feathers. She was diligently plucking downy plumes from quills, filling a second bundle ankle-deep with the debris and contributing a stuffy, malaria-like aroma to the room. Gerard asked if he could have something to eat.

She opened her eyes in astonishment. "Supper is over an hour ago."

"But I had none, good dame."

"Is that my fault? You were welcome to your share."

"But I was benighted, a stranger, and sore delayed against my will."

"What have I to do with that? Everyone knows 'The Star of the Forest' serves from six to eight. Come before six, you sup well; come before eight, as Heaven pleases; come after eight, you get a clean bed and a horn of milk at dawn."

Gerard looked blank. "May I go to bed, then, dame?" he asked sulkily. "For it is ill sitting up wet and fasting, and the proverb says, 'He sups who sleeps.'"

"The beds have not yet arrived," she replied. "You will sleep when the rest do. Inns are not built for one."

It was Gerard's turn to be astounded. "The beds were not come! What, in Heaven's name, did she mean?" Afraid to question her further, sensing the zoological scrutiny of the room, he leaned against the wall and sighed.

A ripple of amusement went through the watchful company. "So this is Germany," Gerard thought, "and Germany is a great country by Holland. Small nations for me." He consoled himself, knowing it would be his last and first night in the land.

His reverie broke as an elbow drove into his ribs. Turning sharply, he saw a woman in the corner beckoning. Approaching gingerly, surprised and irresolute, he stopped beside her. "Hold the child," she said in a hearty voice. In a moment, she plumped the infant into Gerard's arms.

He stood transfixed, his hands feeling like jelly of lead, sudden horror widening his features.

At this expression, the lynx-eyed assembly laughed loudly and for a long time.

"Never heed them," the woman said cheerfully.
