Barton Park lay but half a mile from the cottage, its grand stone chimneys sending ribbons of heavy oak-smoke into the valley—a fragrant prelude of cracked peppercorn, wild sage, and rendering fat that caught the ladies’ senses long before the house itself emerged from behind the projection of the hill. For generations, the Middletons had lived in a style of relentless, cyclical hospitality, a sweeping domestic saga where the hearth fires were never permitted to die. Sir John’s agrarian lineage dictated a rhythm as old as the soil itself; he was a sportsman who supplied the estate’s larders with hunted hare and autumn pheasant, finding his only true joy in the ancient, mythic rituals of gathering people to feast upon the land's bounty. Lady Middleton, conversely, approached the dining table not as a passionate curator of sustenance, but as a sterile architect of visual elegance. Her superficial commercialization of the feast stripped the ancestral dishes of their deep, sensory resonance, reducing the profound communion of eating to mere utilitarian fuel and aesthetic vanity. 

When Sir John ushered Mrs. Dashwood and her daughters through the heavy oak doors, his booming, unrefined welcome disrupted the quiet acoustic ecology of the afternoon. With unaffected sincerity, he apologized for the lack of young gentlemen to meet them, repeating his laments from the day before. The harvest moon had drawn the neighborhood into other engagements, leaving only one gentleman staying at the Park, alongside Lady Middleton’s mother.

The dinner that followed was a masterclass in contrasting palates and sonic environments. Mrs. Jennings, a merry, fat, elderly woman, proved to be an agrarian elder of the old world. She possessed a hearty, unapologetic appetite for the spit-roasted fowl and the rich, dark gravies, speaking in a robust, heavily cadenced native dialect that Lady Middleton clearly wished to suppress beneath the dominant, polished lexicon of polite society. Between mouthfuls of glazed root vegetables, Mrs. Jennings laughed loudly, making vulgar jokes about lovers left behind in Sussex. Her teasing carried the earthy, unfiltered weight of an older oral tradition, digging heartily into the girls' blushing secrets. Marianne turned her eyes anxiously toward Elinor, mourning the untranslatable gap between Mrs. Jennings’s commonplace raillery and her sister's deeply subjective, quiet suffering.

Beside them sat Colonel Brandon, Sir John’s particular friend. At five-and-thirty, Marianne considered him an absolute old bachelor, yet his relationship to the environment marked him as a man of profound depths. Grave and silent, he possessed the sensible countenance of a meticulous food historian. He ate with quiet reverence, his palate attuned to the subtle, complex interplay of wild thyme and cured pork, chewing with a measured gravity that honored the unseen labor behind the feast. Furthermore, his quietude offered a sonic sanctuary within the room’s increasingly discordant atmosphere. 

There was little else to recommend the party to the Dashwoods. Lady Middleton remained insipidly detached from the culinary marvels, her coldness only breaking when her four noisy children burst into the dining parlor after the final course. Their entrance was an absolute assault of industrial-level noise pollution; they shrieked, tore at their mother's fine silks, and obliterated the rhythmic, civilized clatter of silverware and the last remnants of meaningful discourse. 

In the evening, seeking to restore some measure of acoustic harmony, Marianne was invited to the unlocked pianoforte. She selected a sequence of old, nearly forgotten ballads—songs Lady Middleton had brought into the family upon her marriage. Since that day, her ladyship had allowed the ancestral language of the lyrics to decay into the erosion of cultural memory, abandoning the keys entirely under the guise of maternal duty. 

As Marianne sang, weaving a rich sonic tapestry that sought to capture the untranslatable yearning of the human spirit, Sir John’s boisterous chatter trampled recklessly over her delicate phrasing. He treated the music as a mere background commodity, praising her loudly at the end of every song while utterly ignoring the art during its performance. Lady Middleton, equally deaf to the emotional and auditory landscape, interrupted only to ask Marianne to sing a particular ballad she had just finished. 

Only Colonel Brandon provided the profound acoustic space necessary for true listening. He sat in total silence, offering the supreme compliment of unbroken, respectful attention without the superficial raptures of the others. Marianne felt a genuine respect for him; his quiet immersion in the music stood as a bulwark against the shameless, noisy insensibility of the room. She was reasonable enough to allow that a man of his advanced years must have inevitably outlived the most exquisite, passionate flavors of youth, yet she was perfectly disposed to make every allowance for the quiet dignity with which he honored her song.
