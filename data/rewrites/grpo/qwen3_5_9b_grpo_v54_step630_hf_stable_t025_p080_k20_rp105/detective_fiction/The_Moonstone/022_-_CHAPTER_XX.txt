The servants were already in a frenzy when we arrived, the news having spread like wildfire through the corridors. As we approached my mistress's door, it flew open from within, and she emerged, flanked by Mr. Franklin who struggled in vain to steady her. She was utterly undone by the horror of the discovery, her composure shattered.

"You are answerable for this!" she shrieked, pointing a trembling finger at the Sergeant. "Gabriel! Give that wretch his money—and let me be rid of his sight!"

The Sergeant was the only one among us capable of holding his ground, the only one who had not lost his wits.

"I am no more responsible for this calamity, my lady, than you are," he replied, his voice calm yet unyielding. "If, within half an hour, you still insist upon my departure, I shall accept your dismissal, but I will not accept your money."

His words were delivered with a respectful firmness that cut through her hysteria. She allowed Mr. Franklin to guide her back inside, and as the door shut them away, the Sergeant turned his observant gaze upon the female servants. While the others trembled in fear, he noted that Penelope was weeping. "When your father has changed his wet clothes," he instructed her gently, "come and speak to us in your father's room."

Before the half-hour elapsed, I had donned my dry garments and provided the Sergeant with the change of clothes he required. Penelope entered, and I felt a profound surge of pride in my role as her guardian. I seated her on my knee, clasped her close, and prayed for her safety. She buried her face in my bosom, her arms tight around my neck, and we waited in a heavy silence. It was clear to me that the poor dead girl's actions stemmed from a tragedy involving both my daughter and me. The Sergeant moved to the window, gazing out into the garden. I felt compelled to thank him for his consideration of our situation, and I did so sincerely.

Those of high station possess a luxury denied to the lower classes: the freedom to indulge their emotions. Necessity, which spares the wealthy, shows no mercy to us; we learn to suppress our feelings and endure our duties with patient stoicism. I make no complaint of this, merely observe it. Soon, Penelope and I were prepared to face the Sergeant.

When asked if she knew why her fellow-servant had taken her own life, my daughter answered, as one might expect, that it was out of love for Mr. Franklin Blake. When questioned further on whether she had shared this belief with anyone else, she replied, "I have not mentioned it, for Rosanna's sake." I felt it necessary to add a word. "And for Mr. Franklin's sake, my dear," I said. "If Rosanna died for love of him, it was without his knowledge and without his fault. Let him leave the house today, if he chooses, without the added pain of knowing the truth." The Sergeant nodded, agreeing wholeheartedly, and fell silent, seemingly comparing Penelope's confession with a private deduction of his own.

At the end of the allotted time, my mistress's bell rang.

On my way to answer it, I encountered Mr. Franklin emerging from his aunt's sitting-room. He informed me that her ladyship was ready to receive the Sergeant in my presence, as before, but added that he wished to speak with the Sergeant privately first. Returning to my room, he paused to examine the railway timetable in the hall.

"Are you truly going to leave us, sir?" I asked. "Miss Rachel will surely recover if you simply give her time?"

"She will recover," Mr. Franklin answered, "once she hears that I have departed and that she will see me no more."

I initially interpreted his tone as resentment over my young lady's treatment of him, but I was mistaken. My mistress had observed since the police's arrival that even the mention of his name ignited Miss Rachel's temper. Having loved his cousin too deeply to admit it to himself until the truth forced itself upon him, he had resolved to leave when she drove off to her aunt's. With eyes hardened by a cruel realization, he had made the only decision a man of spirit could make: to depart.

He addressed the Sergeant in my presence, stating that her ladyship was willing to acknowledge her hasty words. He asked if the Sergeant would, under these circumstances, accept his fee and leave the matter of the Diamond as it currently stood.

"No, sir," the Sergeant replied. "My fee is earned by performing my duty. I decline to accept it until that duty is fulfilled."

"I do not understand you," Mr. Franklin said.

"I shall explain, sir," the Sergeant responded. "When I arrived, I undertook to shed light on the disappearance of the Diamond. I am now ready to honor that pledge. Once I have presented the case to Lady Verinder as it stands and advised her plainly on the course of action to recover the Moonstone, the responsibility will shift entirely to her. Only then, after she decides whether to allow me to proceed further, will I consider my task complete and accept my fee."

In those words, the Sergeant reminded us that even within the Detective Police, a man possesses a reputation to protect.

His logic was undeniably sound, leaving no room for argument. As I rose to escort him to my lady's room, he asked if Mr. Franklin wished to attend. "Not unless Lady Verinder desires it," Mr. Franklin replied. He leaned in close to whisper to me as I followed the Sergeant out, "I know what that man is going to say about Rachel; and I am too fond of her to hear it and lose my temper. Leave me alone."

I left him there, desolate and leaning against the window sill, his face hidden in his hands, while Penelope peered through the doorway, longing to offer comfort. In his position, I would have summoned her immediately; there is great solace in confiding in another, for nine times out of ten, the listener takes your side. Perhaps, when my back was turned, he did call her in? If so, I can attest that my daughter would stop at nothing to comfort Mr. Franklin Blake.

Meanwhile, the Sergeant and I proceeded to my lady's room.

In our previous conference, she had been reluctant to look up from the book lying on the table. This time, however, there was a marked improvement. She met the Sergeant's gaze with a steadiness that mirrored his own. The family spirit shone in every line of her face, and I knew that the Sergeant would find a formidable opponent, for a woman of her mettle, strung up to hear the worst, was no easy match.
