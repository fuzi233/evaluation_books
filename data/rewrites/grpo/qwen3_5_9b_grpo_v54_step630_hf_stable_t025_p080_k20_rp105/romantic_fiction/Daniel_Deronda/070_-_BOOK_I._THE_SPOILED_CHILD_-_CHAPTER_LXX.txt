In the intricate tapestry of human life, the seasons do not march in rigid succession as they once did in mythic ages. Instead, they blend seamlessly; fruit hangs beside blossoms, and the sickle cuts the grain while seeds are scattered in the same breath. One hand tends the green cluster while another crushes the grapes. In every life, harvest and spring are eternally intertwined until the Great Gatherer calls us home and sows us anew in His invisible fields.

Among the profound gifts of love, none shines brighter than the certainty that by binding our lives to another's, we become guardians of their happiness. We can weave comfort into the fabric of their hardship and turn the bitter memories of past suffering into fountains of joy. Deronda's love for Mirah was steeped in this sacred protectiveness. Even as an infant, she had stumbled among thorns, and the first time he saw her face, it appeared to him as a young image of utter despair.

But now, she glowed like a delicate flower with dark tips, bathed in the warm sunlight of contentment. She viewed every potential grief as merely a shadow within the radiant life she shared with Deronda—a life she could name only "good." He watched the sober gladness that illuminated her movements and the peaceful stillness of her resting poses with a delight so deep he whispered to himself that saving her from pain was sufficient personal joy for him. She knew nothing of Hans's struggle or Gwendolen's sharp pang. Since Deronda had revealed his hidden love for her, she easily interpreted Gwendolen's anxious concern as a grateful dependence on Deronda's kindness, a feeling she herself had known. All of Deronda's distant references to Mrs. Grandcourt only confirmed this view. Mirah was ready to believe he had been a rescuing angel to many, and the only marvel was that she alone possessed the bliss of standing constantly by his side.

Thus, when the bridal veil fell around Mirah, it hid no trembling doubt, only a thrilling awe at accepting a great gift that demanded great uses. Under the velvet canopy stood a bride and groom of such nobility that their people would wisely wish them offspring; no lips were more truthful in touching the marriage wine, nor did the blessing gather a stronger promise of fulfillment than in the integrity of their mutual pledge. Naturally, they wed according to the Jewish rite. And since no religion demands that a feast invite only the highest ranks of acquaintance, few will be offended to learn that among the guests at Deronda's modest wedding feast sat the entire Cohen family, save for the baby who continued to nurse its teething troubles intelligently at home. How could Mordecai bear to exclude those friends of his adversity from sharing in his joy?

Mrs. Meyrick fully understood this sentiment and had completely reconciled herself to meeting the Jewish pawnbroker. She arrived with her three daughters, all of whom enjoyed the consciousness that Mirah's marriage to Deronda crowned a romance destined to remain a sweet memory for them. Which of them—mother or daughters—had not played a generous part in it, giving their best feelings and actions to the girl who needed them? If Hans could have been present, it would have been better, but Mab had already noted that men must suffer for being inconvenient. She mused that if she, Kate, and Amy had all fallen in love with Mr. Deronda, perhaps they would have been ridiculous, but as women, they were not so foolish.

The Meyricks were rewarded for conquering their prejudices by hearing a speech from Mr. Cohen, a rare occurrence among speeches that did not follow the usual pattern. Jacob ate beyond his years and contributed several small, whinnying laughs as a free accompaniment to his father's words—not irreverently, but from a lively sense that his family was distinguishing itself. Meanwhile, Adelaide Rebekah, clad in a new Sabbath frock, maintained a grave air of responsibility throughout.

Mordecai's brilliant eyes, sunken deep in their large sockets, surveyed the scene with the cherishing benignity of a spirit already lifted into an aloofness that nullified only selfish requirements, leaving pure sympathy alive. Yet, continually, after his gaze traveled over the others, it returned to dwell on Deronda with a fresh gleam of trusting affection.

The wedding feast was humble, yet Mirah was not without splendid gifts. As soon as the betrothal became known, friends devised graceful tokens. Sir Hugo and Lady Mallinger had taken care to provide a complete equipment for Eastern travel, along with a precious locket inscribed: *"To the bride of our dear Daniel Deronda all blessings. H. and L. M."* The Klesmers sent a perfect watch, also accompanied by a pretty inscription.

But something far more precious than gold and gems arrived for Deronda from the neighborhood of Diplow on the morning of his marriage. It was a letter containing these words:

"Do not think of me sorrowfully on your wedding-day. I have remembered your words—that I may live to be one of the best of women, who make others glad that they were born. I do not yet see how that can be, but you know better than I. If it ever comes true, it will be because you helped me. I only thought of myself, and I made you grieve. It hurts me now to think of your grief. You must not grieve any more for me. It is better—it shall be better with me because I have known you.

Preparations for the departure of all three to the East began immediately. Deronda could not deny Ezra's wish that they set out on the voyage forthwith, so that he might accompany them rather than detaining them to watch over him. He held no belief that Ezra's life would last through the journey, for symptoms indicated that the final stage of his malady had set in. Yet Ezra himself had declared, "Never mind where I die, so that I am with you."

He did not set out with them. One morning early, he told Deronda, "Do not quit me today. I shall die before it is ended."

He chose to be dressed and sit upright in his easy chair as usual, with Deronda and Mirah on either side of him. For several hours, he remained unusually silent, making no effort to speak, but occasionally looking at them with eyes full of restful meaning, as if to assure them that while this remnant of breathing time was difficult, he felt an ocean of peace beneath him.

It was not until late in the afternoon, as the light began to fall, that he took hold of each of their hands. Looking at Deronda, he said, "Death is coming to me as the divine kiss which is both parting and reunion—which takes me from your bodily eyes and gives me full presence in your soul. Where thou goest, Daniel, I shall go. Is it not begun? Have I not breathed my soul into you? We shall live together."

He paused, and Deronda waited, thinking there might be another word for him. But slowly and with effort, Ezra pressed on their hands, raised himself, and uttered in Hebrew the confession of the Divine Unity, a prayer long spoken on the lips of the dying Israelite.

He sank gently back into his chair and spoke no more. But it was some hours before he ceased to breathe, with Mirah's and Deronda's arms wrapped around him.

"Nothing is here for tears, nothing to wail
Or knock the breast; no weakness, no contempt,
Dispraise or blame; nothing but well and fair,
And what may quiet us in a death so noble."
