As I ventured further west, I found myself beset by the ignominy of traversing the unforgiving terrain between Plymouth and the northern reaches of Devon. To reach my destination without succumbing to the perils of the journey, I was compelled to navigate the treacherous domain of a powerful and elusive potentate, known colloquially as the King of the Gubbings. His reputation for ferocity and cunning had earned him notoriety throughout the realm, having vanquished the forces of Her Majesty Queen Elizabeth on multiple occasions. The whispered epithets that trailed in his wake spoke volumes about the esteem in which he was held – or rather, the terror he inspired.

It is said that the term 'Gubbings' originates from the discarded remnants of fish, a humble commodity hardly worthy of note. And yet, the inhabitants of this foreboding land were acutely aware of the pejorative connotations associated with this designation. My erudite friend, Mr. Joseph Maynard, had posited that the name might be derived from the rugged terrain of the 'montes gibberosos', but I suspect that such etymological gymnastics are little more than a flight of fancy, a desperate attempt to impose order upon the chaos that lies at the heart of this enigmatic region.

As I delved deeper into the annals of history, I chanced upon an account of an England that lay beyond the Welsh border, a realm of untamed wilderness and unyielding heathens. The Gubbings land, with its stark beauty and lawless inhabitants, seemed an affront to the very fabric of civilization. Nestled in the shadow of Dartmoor, this Scythian outpost was a bastion of paganism, its people living in a state of communal anarchy, exempt from the authority of bishop, archdeacon, or any other earthly power. Theirs was a language that defied comprehension, a cacophonous jumble of sounds and gestures that only added to the mystique surrounding this reclusive tribe.

Their existence was a testament to the enduring power of ignorance, a stubborn refusal to submit to the trappings of luxury and the corrupting influence of power. They lived by their wits, plundering the moors and eking out a meager existence on the fringes of society. Theirs was a world governed by a code of conduct that defied rational understanding, a labyrinthine hierarchy of kinship and loyalty that brooked no outsiders.
