While the virtuous Bishop of Cambray, in his recent romance concerning Calypso, lamented the sorrow of the sorceress at Ulysses' departure, I recall no mention of the grief suffered by Calypso's lady's-maid upon bidding farewell to Odysseus's own gentleman. Yet, surely, the menials must have wept together within the kitchen precincts while their masters embraced wildly in the drawing-room; they must have clustered around each other in the fore-cabin as their principals shattered their hearts in the grand salon. When the final bell rang and Ulysses's mate cried out, "Now! Any one for shore!" both Calypso and her attendant must have stepped onto the same plank, hearts pounding and eyes streaming. Standing on the quay, they would have waved pocket-handkerchiefs—of vastly different textures and values—to their friends aboard the departing vessel, while crowds on land and in the ship's bows shouted *hip, hip, hurrah* (or whatever the ancient Greek equivalent might be). The essential truth remains: if Calypso could not console herself, neither could her maid. They traversed the same plank of grief, feeling the identical pang of separation. Upon returning home, they might not employ handkerchiefs of equal quality, but the tears shed in her marble halls were undoubtedly as salty and abundant as those poured forth in the servants' quarters.

Not only did Harry Warrington depart Castlewood a captive to love, but Gumbo also left the premises prey to the same delightful passion. His wit, accomplishments, good humor, and skills in dancing, cooking, and music had endeared him to the entire female domestic circle. While some men might have harbored jealousy, the ladies were entirely his. In those days in England, there existed no such prejudice against Black men as exists today among white-skinned peoples. Their condition was certainly not one of equality, yet they commanded a certain tolerant sympathy from all, and from women, a kindness far more generous. When Ledyard and Parke in Blackmansland were persecuted by the men, did they not find Black women pitiful and kind toward them? Women are invariably kind toward our sex. What mental infirmities do they not cherish? What moral deformities, what lepers, what idiots, what dull drivellers, what misshapen monsters (speaking figuratively) do they not fondle and cuddle? Gumbo was treated by the women with the same warmth afforded to many individuals far superior to himself; it was merely the men in the servants' hall who rejoiced at the Virginian lad's departure. I would dearly like to witness his farewell. I imagine Molly, the housemaid, slipping to the terrace gardens in the grey dawn to gather a wistful bouquet. I picture Betty, the kitchenmaid, snipping a thick lock of her chestnut ringlets to exchange for a woolly token from young Gumbo's head. Naturally, he claimed to be of *regum progenies*, a descendant of Ashantee kings. Given the vast numbers of hereditary bondsmen currently inhabiting Caffraria, Connaught, and elsewhere, there must once have been countless such potent sovereigns in those lands to account for the descendants now extant.

On the morning appointed for Madame de Bernstein's departure, the numerous domestics of Castlewood crowded the doors and passages. Some sought a final glimpse of her ladyship's men and the fascinating Gumbo; others intended to say goodbye to her ladyship's maid; all aimed to intercept the Baroness and her nephew for parting fees, a custom of the era involving the liberal distribution of gratuities among household staff. Both the Baroness and her nephew showered gold upon the livery-clad society, the gentlemen in black and ruffles, and the swarm of female attendants. Castlewood was the home of the Baroness's youth. As for her honest Harry, who had not only lived at free expense but had won horses and money—or promises thereof—from his cousin and the unfortunate chaplain—he was naturally generous and felt compelled not to stint his benevolence at this critical moment. "My mother, I know," he reasoned, "will wish me to be liberal to all the retainers of the Esmond family." Thus, he scattered his gold pieces right and left, acting as if he were as wealthy as Gumbo had proclaimed. No one approached him without receiving a share of his bounty. From the major-domo to the shoe-black, Mr. Harry offered a peace-offering to them all. He gave tokens to the stern housekeeper in her still-room and the feeble old porter in his lodge. When a man loves one woman in a family, it is astonishing how fond he becomes of everyone connected with it. He ingratiates himself with the maids; he is bland with the butler; he interests himself in the footman; he runs errands for the daughters; he offers advice and loans to the young son at college; he pets little dogs he would otherwise kick; he smiles at old stories that would induce yawns from anyone else but Papa; he drinks sweet port wine he would otherwise curse the steward for serving; he endures the cantankerous old maiden aunt; he keeps time as darling little Fanny plays the piano; and he smiles when wicked, lively little Bobby spills coffee over his shirt.

For a brief period, according to the customs of that age, Harry Warrington had bestowed these artless testimonies of affection upon the Castlewood family regarding one of them. Cousin Will should have reclaimed his money and welcome, or secured as much of Harry's own funds as the lad could spare. Nevertheless, though a lover, the lad was shrewd, keen, fond of sport and fair play, and a discerning judge of a good horse. Having gambled for and won all the money Will possessed, alongside a collection of Mr. Esmond's valuable autographs, Harry was delighted to win Will's brown horse—the very quadruped that had nearly thrown him into the water on his first evening at Castlewood. Having watched the horse perform often, amidst all his passion and romance, he was not sorry to possess such a sound, swift, well-bred hunter and roadster. After gazing at the stars shining over his mistress's window and extinguishing her candle, he retired to his dormitory. There, no doubt, he reflected on his Maria and his horse with youthful satisfaction, imagining the sweetness of riding one pillioned on the other, touring the entire island with a pair of white arms encircling his waist. He fell asleep ruminating on these thoughts, meditating a million blessings on his Maria, in whose company he was to luxuriate for at least another week.

Early in the morning, poor Chaplain Sampson sent his little black mare via his groom, footman, and gardener, who wept and bestowed numerous kisses upon the beast's white nose as they handed it over to Gumbo. Both Gumbo and his master were moved by the fellow's sensitivity; the Negro servant expressed his sympathy through tears, while Harry produced two guineas, astonishing and swiftly comforting the chaplain's boy. Then Gumbo and the former groom led the beast to the stable, instructed to bring him round with Mr. William's horse after breakfast, at the hour the Baroness's carriages were scheduled.

So courteous was he to his aunt, or so grateful for her departure, that the master of the house appeared at the morning meal to bid his guests farewell. The ladies and the chaplain were present—the only family member absent being Will, who left a note for his cousin stating, in exceedingly poor spelling, that he was obliged to travel to Salisbury Races that morning but had left the horse his cousin had won the previous night. Tom, Mr. Will's groom, would deliver it to Mr. Warrington's servant. Will's absence did not hinder the remainder of the party from sharing tea amicably. Soon, the carriages rolled into the courtyard, servants packed them with the Baroness's multiplied luggage, and the moment of departure arrived.

A large open landau carried the stout Baroness and her niece, flanked by two men-servants mounting the box with pistols and blunderbusses ready for potential highwaymen. Another carriage held their ladyships' maids, and a third guarded the trunks, vast and numerous as they were, yet insignificant compared to the enormous baggage train accompanying a lady of the present time. Mr. Warrington's modest valises were placed in this second carriage under the maid's guardianship, and Mr. Gumbo proposed to ride by the window for the majority of the journey.

My Lord, accompanied by his stepmother and Lady Fanny, escorted their kinswoman to the carriage steps, bidding her farewell with many dutiful embraces. Her Lady Maria followed in a riding-dress, which Harry Warrington deemed the most becoming costume in the world. A host of servants stood around, begging Heaven bless her ladyship. The Baroness's departure was known throughout the village, and scores of locals waited under the trees outside the gates, cheering and waving their hats as the ponderous vehicles departed.

Gumbo had gone ahead to fetch Mr. Warrington's horses while My Lord, with his arm under his young guest's, paced up and down the court. "I hear you are carrying away some of our horses out of Castlewood?" My Lord asked.

Harry blushed. "A gentleman cannot refuse a fair game at cards," he replied. "I never wanted to play, nor would have wagered money had not my cousin William forced me. As for the chaplain, it went to my heart to win from him, but he was as eager as my cousin."
