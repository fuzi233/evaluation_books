The morning air was thick with the rich, heavy scent of woodsmoke and sizzling bear-fat as François crouched over the cast-iron skillet. He turned pieces of salted pork with meticulous care, the crackling hiss of the meat creating a small, rhythmic sanctuary against the vast silence of the frost. 

“Eh? Wot I say? I spik true w’en I say dat Buck two devils.” This was François’s speech when he discovered Spitz missing and Buck covered with wounds. He drew the great dog toward the warmth of the cooking fire, and by its flickering light pointed out the damage. 

“Dat Spitz fight lak hell,” said Perrault, inhaling the savory, curing smoke as he surveyed the gaping rips and cuts on Buck’s shoulders. 

“An’ dat Buck fight lak two hells,” François answered, handing Perrault a tin plate of the rendered meat. “An’ now we make good time. No more Spitz, no more trouble, sure.”

While Perrault packed the camp outfit, wrapping the remaining provisions in heavily waxed canvas to preserve their pungent, earthy aromas, François proceeded to harness the dogs. Buck trotted up to the place Spitz would have occupied as leader. But François, operating within the established lexicon of camp hierarchy, brought Sol-leks to the coveted position. To François, Sol-leks was the best lead-dog left, a physical translation of order. 

Buck sprang upon Sol-leks in a fury, driving him back and standing in his place. 

“Eh? eh?” François cried, slapping his thighs gleefully. “Look at dat Buck. Heem keel dat Spitz, heem t’ink to take de job.” 

“Go ’way, Chook!” he cried, attempting to impose his verbal dominance. But Buck refused to budge. He spoke an older, untranslatable dialect of physical assertion. François took Buck by the scruff of the neck, and though the dog growled a low, resonant warning from deep within his chest, dragged him to one side and replaced Sol-leks. The old dog did not like it, showing plainly that he understood the dangerous acoustic frequency of Buck’s wrath. 

When François turned his back, Buck again displaced Sol-leks. François was angry. “Now, by Gar, I feex you!” he cried, returning with a heavy club. 

Buck remembered the man in the red sweater. He retreated slowly, honoring the crude, industrial power of the club, but he did not submit. He circled just beyond its range, snarling with bitterness, his growls vibrating through the crystalline air. François went about his work, calling to Buck when he was ready to put him in his old place in front of Dave. Buck retreated. After an hour of this circular, rhythmic pacing around the camp, François threw down the club. Buck wanted, not to escape a beating, but to claim the leadership. It was a birthright, a title in an ancestral language he was finally remembering how to speak. 

Perrault looked at his watch and swore, his French-Canadian curses dissolving impotently into the pristine acoustic ecology of the wild. They were beaten. François went to Sol-leks, unfastened his traces, and called to Buck. Buck laughed, as dogs laugh, and trotted in, swinging around into position at the head of the team. 

At a bound, Buck took up the duties of leadership. Where judgment was required, he showed himself the superior of Spitz. But it was in giving the law and restoring the sonic harmony of the pack that Buck truly excelled. The chaotic, unruly yelping that had plagued the team during Spitz’s final days was swiftly silenced. Pike, the malingerer, was shaken repeatedly for loafing; the sour-tempered Joe was smothered beneath Buck’s superior weight until his defensive snapping gave way to a submissive whine. The general tone of the team picked up immediately. They recovered their old-time solidarity, their breathing and the rhythmic crunch of their paws upon the snow merging into a single, unified pulse. 

At the Rink Rapids, two native huskies, Teek and Koona, were added to the traces. Buck broke them in with a physical fluency that took François’s breath away. “Nevaire such a dog as dat Buck!” he cried. 

They were ahead of the record, the trail a well-packed ribbon of ice. The temperature held steady at fifty below zero. Across Marsh, Tagish, and Bennett, they flew so fast that the runners hummed a continuous, high-pitched note against the ice, a perfect, frictionless song of travel. On the last night of the second week, they topped White Pass and dropped down the sea slope to Skagway. 

Here, the pure, harmonic landscape of the wild was violently interrupted. Skagway was a festering wound of modern industrial noise pollution—the clatter of shipping, the shouting of transient crowds, the superficial commercialization of a gold rush that stripped the land of its dignity. For three days, Perrault and François strutted through this cacophony, deluged with invitations to drink. Then came official orders. François wept over Buck, throwing his arms around his neck, and passed out of his life for good. 

A Scotch half-breed took charge of the team. He was a man of quiet, meticulous routine, deeply attuned to the agrarian rhythms of survival. In company with a dozen other dog-teams, they started back over the weary trail to Dawson, dragging the heavy mail train. It was grueling toil, but the half-breed operated the camp with a deep reverence for domestic ritual, understanding that sustenance was the profound anchor of their existence. 

Every night, before any man rested, the dogs were tended to. The half-breed was a careful steward of their food, preparing the day's singular meal with the gravity of a food historian preserving an ancient rite. He sourced rich, oily King salmon, sun-cured and heavy with umami. He would thaw the stiff fish near the roaring fire, allowing the intense, smoky aromas of the fat to bloom into the freezing air before distributing it. To Buck, this was the defining feature of the day. The consumption of the rich flesh was not merely utilitarian fueling; it was a communion, a sensory grounding in the physical world after the exhausting, repetitive labor of the traces. 

Best of all, he loved to lie near the cooking fire, hind legs crouched, eyes blinking dreamily at the flames, digesting the rich fats. As the rhythmic crackle of the burning pine enveloped him, time would collapse upon itself. The modern mail train faded, and the interwoven timelines of his bloodline merged. The flames of the half-breed’s fire became the flames of another era. 

Through the smoke, Buck would see a different man squatting by the coals. This man was shorter of leg and longer of arm, covered in matted hair. He did not speak the dominant lexicon of Skagway’s gold-seekers, but communicated in a forgotten, untranslatable dialect of grunts and clicks. Buck watched this ancestral elder roast marrow-rich bones directly on the embers, the olfactory landscape thick with the smell of charred meat and raw earth. This hairy man lived in perpetual acoustic vigilance, listening to the crashing of unseen beasts in the undergrowth. And dreaming there by the Yukon bank, Buck’s hair would rise along his spine, deeply connected to this multi-generational saga of survival, until the half-breed cook shouted, “Hey, you Buck, wake up!” The ancient world would vanish, and the smell of the King salmon would return.

It was a hard trip. They were short of weight and in poor condition when they made Dawson, only to be loaded with letters for the outside two days later. It snowed constantly, creating a soft, sound-dampening trail that increased the friction on the runners. 

The heavy labor began to exact a terrible toll, most of all on Dave. Something inside him had ruptured. He became morose, making his nest the moment camp was pitched, where the Scotch half-breed would meticulously bring him his portion of fish. In the traces, Dave would suddenly cry out, a sharp, untranslatable vocalization of inner agony that pierced the quiet rhythm of the team. 

The drivers grew deeply concerned. They discussed his case nightly over their own meals of roasted caribou and black tea, their voices murmuring in the acoustic sanctuary of the firelight. They pressed and prodded Dave's body, trying to read the physical text of his pain, but could locate no broken bones. The subjective experience of his failing body was utterly untranslatable. 

By the time Cassiar Bar was reached, Dave was falling repeatedly. The half-breed called a halt, gently removing Dave from the traces and moving Sol-leks into his place. He intended to let Dave run free behind the sled to heal. But Dave resented the demotion. The language of the trace, the profound, driving purpose of the work, was the only dialect he knew. Stripped of his position, he was stripped of his cultural memory, his very identity. He floundered in the soft snow alongside the beaten trail, whimpering broken-heartedly. He attacked Sol-leks, striving to leap back inside his traces, desperate to reclaim his voice in the team. 

The half-breed tried to drive him away with the whip, but Dave paid no heed to the stinging lash, and the man lacked the heart to strike harder. When the sled churned by, Dave fell, howling lugubriously. With the last remnant of his strength, he staggered behind until the train stopped again. 

When the driver returned to start the dogs, they swung out with remarkable lack of exertion, then stopped in surprise. The sled had not moved. Dave had bitten through both of Sol-leks’s heavy leather traces, tasting the salty, sweat-cured hide, and was standing directly in front of the sled in his proper place. He pleaded with his eyes to remain. 

The driver was perplexed. Around the evening fire, as the scent of curing meat wafted through the dark, the comrades discussed the profound tragedy of a dog breaking its heart from being denied its life's work. They understood the subtle power dynamics of the soul—that to deny Dave his place was to erase him entirely. They held it a mercy that he should die in the traces, speaking his native language to the end. 

So he was harnessed in again. Proudly, he pulled as of old, though he cried out involuntarily from the bite of his inward hurt. Several times he fell and was dragged, but he held out till camp was reached. 

Morning found him too weak to travel. At harness-up time, he tried to crawl to his driver. He wormed his way forward slowly toward where the harnesses were being put on his mates, dragging his body with a sort of hitching movement. His strength left him, and he lay gasping in the snow, yearning toward the team. 

The sleds churned along the trail. The dogs could hear Dave mournfully howling, his voice a fading, untranslatable echo of grief against the vast acoustic canvas of the wild, until they passed out of sight behind a belt of river timber. 

The train was halted. The Scotch half-breed slowly retraced his steps to the camp they had left. The men ceased talking. The deep, eternal silence of the snow was abruptly shattered by a single revolver-shot—a violent, industrial punctuation that forever ended Dave's song. The man came back hurriedly. The whips snapped, the sleds moved, but Buck knew, and every dog knew, the profound loss that had taken place behind the trees.
