"My heart revolts within me, and two voices
Make themselves audible within my bosom."

Upon returning home, Margaret discovered two letters resting on the table. One was a brief note for her mother; the other, bearing foreign postmarks and possessing a thin, silvery quality that rustled softly as she lifted it, was clearly from Aunt Shaw. As Margaret began to examine the letter, her father entered abruptly.

"So your mother is tired and has retired early!" he remarked, his tone shifting instantly to concern. "I fear such a thundery day was hardly conducive to the doctor's visit. What did he say? Dixon mentioned he spoke with you regarding her condition."

Margaret hesitated. Her father's expression grew grave and anxious. "Does he not consider her seriously ill?"

"Not at present," Margaret replied. "He believes she requires care. He was very kind and promised to return to observe how his medicines take effect."

"Only care?" Mr. Hale pressed, his voice rising slightly. "Did he not recommend a change of air? Did he not suggest this smoky town is harming her, Margaret?"

"No," she answered gravely. "He said nothing of the sort. He seemed merely anxious."

"Doctors possess that anxious manner; it is part of their profession," he dismissed, though his agitation betrayed him. Margaret perceived, in her father's nervous demeanor, that the initial shadow of danger had already taken root in his mind. Despite his attempts to minimize the situation, he could not shift his focus to other matters. Throughout the evening, he returned to the subject repeatedly, refusing to entertain even the slightest unfavorable thought, a behavior that filled Margaret with profound sadness.

"This letter is from Aunt Shaw, Papa," she said, breaking the silence. "She has arrived in Naples but found it too hot, so she has secured apartments in Sorrento. However, I suspect she does not truly enjoy Italy."

"He did not mention anything regarding diet, did he?"

"It was to be nourishing and easily digestible. Mother's appetite seems quite good, I believe."

"Yes! And that makes it all the stranger that he thought to speak of diet at all."

"I asked him, Papa." After a pause, Margaret continued, "Aunt Shaw mentions she has sent me some coral ornaments. But," she added with a half-smile, "she fears the Milton dissenters will not appreciate them. She seems to hold all her notions of Dissenters based solely on the Quakers, does she not?"

"If you ever hear or suspect your mother desires anything, ensure you inform me immediately. I am terrified she does not always tell me what she wishes. Please, attend to that girl Mrs. Thornton named. If we possessed a competent house-servant, Dixon could remain constantly with her, and I guarantee we would soon restore her to our midst if rest alone can achieve it. She has been exhausted lately due to the heat and the difficulty of securing help. A little repose will set her right entirely—eh, Margaret?"

"I hope so," Margaret replied, but her tone was so laden with sorrow that her father noticed immediately. He pinched her cheek gently.

"Come; if you appear so pale, I must apply a touch of rouge. Take care of yourself, child, or you will require the doctor next."

Yet he remained unsettled throughout the evening. He moved restlessly back and forth on the balls of his feet, peering into the bedroom to check if his wife was still asleep. Margaret's heart ached at his agitation—his desperate attempt to suppress and strangle the terrifying fear looming in the dark recesses of his soul.

Finally, he returned, somewhat comforted.

"She is awake now, Margaret. She smiled warmly upon seeing me standing by her. It was her old smile. She stated she feels refreshed and ready for tea. Where is the note for her? She wishes to see it. I shall read it aloud while you prepare the tea."

The note contained a formal invitation from Mrs. Thornton, extending an invitation to Mr., Mrs., and Miss Hale to dinner on the twenty-first. Margaret was surprised to find an acceptance being considered, given the grim probabilities she had learned that day. Nevertheless, the prospect of her husband and daughter attending captivated Mrs. Hale's imagination even before Margaret had read the contents. It represented an event to break the monotony of the invalid's existence, and she clung to the idea with fretful persistence whenever Margaret attempted to object.

"Nay, Margaret: if she wishes it, I am certain we shall both go willingly. She would never express such a desire unless she genuinely felt stronger—truly better than we believed, eh, Margaret?" Mr. Hale asked anxiously as Margaret prepared to draft the acceptance note the following day.

"Eh, Margaret?" he questioned, making a nervous gesture with his hands. It seemed cruel to deny him the comfort he craved. Moreover, his passionate refusal to acknowledge the existence of fear nearly inspired hope within Margaret herself.

"I do believe she is better since last night," she said. "Her eyes appear brighter, and her complexion clearer."

"God bless you," her father said earnestly. "But is it true? Yesterday was so sultry that everyone felt unwell. It was a most unfortunate day for Mr. Donaldson to visit."

Thus, he departed to his daily duties, which had recently expanded to include preparing lectures for the working people at a neighboring Lyceum. He had selected Ecclesiastical Architecture as his topic, a subject more aligned with his own tastes and knowledge than with the specific interests of his audience or the nature of the institution. The Lyceum, burdened by debt, was only too eager to secure a free course from an educated and accomplished man like Mr. Hale, regardless of the subject matter.

"Well, mother," Mr. Thornton asked that evening, "who have accepted your invitations for the twenty-first?"

"Fanny, where are the notes?" Mrs. Thornton replied, listing the responses. "The Slicksons accept, Collingbrooks accept, Stephenses accept, Browns decline. The Hales—father and daughter are coming; mother is too great an invalid. The Macphersons come, along with Mr. Horsfall and Mr. Young. I was considering asking the Porters since the Browns cannot attend."

"Very good. Do you know, I am genuinely afraid Mrs. Hale is far from well, based on what Dr. Donaldson said."

"It is strange they would accept a dinner invitation if she were so ill," Fanny observed.

"I did not say 'very ill,'" her brother replied sharply. "I only said 'far from well.' They may not realize it either." Then, suddenly, he remembered that from Dr. Donaldson's account, Margaret must be fully aware of the exact state of affairs.

"Very probably they are quite conscious of what you said yesterday, John—of the immense advantage it would be for them, for Mr. Hale specifically, to be introduced to individuals like the Stephenses and the Collingbrooks."

"I am certain that motive would not influence them. No! I believe I understand the situation now."

"John!" Fanny laughed in her small, weak, nervous manner. "How you profess to understand these Hales, and how you never allow that we can comprehend them. Are they really so vastly different from most people one encounters?"

She did not intend to vex him, yet if she had, she could not have succeeded more thoroughly. He chafed in silence, refusing to deign a reply.

"They do not seem to me out of the ordinary," Mrs. Thornton remarked. "He appears a worthy enough man, though perhaps too simple for trade—which is likely why he became a clergyman first and now a teacher. She is a bit of a fine lady, with her invalidism. As for the girl—she is the only one who puzzles me when I think of her, which I do not often do. She seems to have a great notion of giving herself airs, and I cannot fathom why. I could almost fancy she thinks herself too good for our company at times. And yet, they are not wealthy; from all I hear, they have never been."

"And she is not accomplished, mamma. She cannot play."

"Go on, Fanny. What else does she require to bring her up to your standard?"

"Nay! John," his mother interjected, "that speech of Fanny's caused no harm. I myself heard Miss Hale admit she could not play. If you would leave us alone, we might perhaps learn to like her and recognize her merits."

"I am sure I never could!" murmured Fanny, shielded by her mother. Mr. Thornton heard but chose not to respond. He paced up and down the dining room, wishing his mother would order candles so he could begin reading or writing, thereby halting the conversation. Yet he never considered interfering in the minor domestic regulations Mrs. Thornton observed, born of her habitual remembrance of past economies.

"Mother," he said, stopping and speaking the truth with brave earnestness, "I wish you would like Miss Hale."

"Why," she asked, startled by his tender yet serious manner. "Are you not thinking of marrying her? A girl without a penny."

"She would never have me," he said with a short laugh.

"No, I do not think she would," his mother answered. "She laughed in my face when I praised her for speaking out something Mr. Bell had said in your favor. I liked the girl for her frankness, as it assured me she had no thoughts of you; and the next minute she vexed me by seeming to think... Well, never mind! Only you are right in saying she holds herself too high to consider you! The saucy jade! I should like to know where she would find a better match!"

"Well, as I am just as convinced of the truth of what you have been saying as you can be, and as I have no thought or expectation of ever asking her to be my wife, you may believe me henceforth that I am entirely disinterested in speaking of her. I foresee trouble for that girl—perhaps a lack of maternal care—and I only wish you to be ready to be a friend to her should she need one. Now, Fanny," he continued, "I trust you have sufficient delicacy to understand that it is as great an injury to Miss Hale as to me—in fact, she would deem it a greater injury—to suppose that I have any reason, beyond what I have stated, for begging you and my mother to show her every kindly attention."

"I cannot forgive her pride," his mother declared. "I will befriend her if necessary, for your sake, John. I would befriend Jezebel herself if you asked me. But this girl, who turns up her nose at us all—who turns up her nose at you..."

"Nay, mother; I have never yet placed myself, and I mean never to place myself, within reach of her contempt."

"Contempt, indeed!" (One of Mrs. Thornton's expressive snorts.) "Do not speak of Miss Hale, John, if I am to be kind to her. When I am with her, I do not know whether I like or dislike her most; but when I think of her and hear you talk of her, I hate her. I can see she has given herself airs to you as if you had told me so yourself."

"And if she has," he said, pausing for a moment before continuing, "I am not a lad to be cowed by a proud look from a woman, nor to care for her misunderstanding me and my position. I can laugh at it!"

"To be sure! And at her too, with her fine notions and haughty tosses!"

"I only wonder why you talk so much about her, then," Fanny said, expressing her fatigue with the subject.

"Well!" her brother replied, with a shade of bitterness. "Suppose we find a more agreeable topic. How about a strike, by way of something pleasant to discuss?"

"Have the hands actually turned out?" Mrs. Thornton asked with vivid interest.

"Hamper's men are actually out. Mine are working out their week, fearing prosecution for breach of contract. I would have had every one of them arrested and punished for leaving before their time expired."

"The legal expenses would exceed the value of the hands themselves—a set of ungrateful naughts!" his mother commented.

"To be sure. But I would have shown them how to keep my word and how I expect them to keep theirs. They know me by now. Slickson's men are off; he is unlikely to spend money getting them punished. We are facing a turn-out, mother."

"I hope there are not many orders in hand?"

"Of course there are. They know that well enough, though they do not quite understand everything, although they think they do."

"What do you mean, John?"

Candles had been lit, and Fanny had resumed her interminable piece of worsted work, yawning periodically. She threw herself back in her chair from time to time to gaze into the void, thinking of nothing at her ease.

"Why," he explained, "the Americans are flooding the general market with their yarns, so our only chance lies in producing at a lower rate. If we cannot, we must close our shops immediately, and both hands and masters will be forced to wander. Yet these fools revert to the prices paid three years ago—nay, some of their leaders quote Dickinson's price now—though they know as well as we do that, with fines extorted from their wages, which no honorable man would demand, the real rate of wage paid at Dickinson's is less than at ours. Upon my word, mother, I wish the old Combination Laws were in force. It is too bad to discover that fools—ignorant, wayward men like these—by uniting their silly heads, are to rule over the fortunes of those who bring all the wisdom that knowledge, experience, and often painful thought and anxiety can provide. The next step will indeed be, and we have almost reached it now, that we shall have to go and stand hat in hand, humbly asking the secretary of the Spinner's Union to furnish us with labor at their own price. That is what they want—they, who lack the sense to see that if we do not receive a fair share of the profits to compensate us for our wear and tear here in England, we can simply move to another country
