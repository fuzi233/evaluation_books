Martin's plan required absolute secrecy for the day. Consequently, he found no desire for breakfast. Just as schooltime approached, a sharp pain seized his heart, making it prudent to abandon the journey to the grammar school with Mark. Instead, he retreated to his father's armchair by the fireside, taking up his morning paper. With Mark off to Mr. Summer's class, and Matthew and Mr. Yorke withdrawn to the counting-house, Martin faced four remaining tasks. First, he had to consume the breakfast he had skipped—a necessity for a boy of fifteen whose energy depended on food. Second, third, and fourth, he must successfully remove his mother, Miss Moore, and Mrs. Horsfall from the premises before four o'clock.

The immediate priority was hunger. Martin knew the route to the larder and took it. The servants were eating in the kitchen behind locked doors; his mother and Miss Moore were outside on the lawn, complaining about those same doors. Safe within the larder, Martin made a fastidious selection. Determined that his delayed meal should be *recherché*, he rejected his usual bland diet of bread and milk. From a shelf lined with straw, he chose three rosy apples. He selected an apricot puff and a damson tart from a dish of pastry. While he ignored the plain household bread, he favored some currant tea-cakes, choosing one. Using his clasp-knife, he appropriated a wing of fowl, a slice of ham, and a piece of cold custard-pudding. Satisfied, he marched out into the hall.

He was halfway across—just three steps from safety in the back parlour—when the front door swung open and Matthew appeared. It would have been preferable if the Old Gentleman, in full demonic regalia, had shown up. Matthew, the skeptic and scoffer, had already dismissed Martin's claim of heart pain, muttering phrases like "shamming Abraham" that caused Martin mental spasms. The sight of the stolen feast—apples, tarts, cakes, fowl, ham, and pudding—confirmed Matthew's suspicion of Martin's cunning.

Martin froze for a heartbeat, then regained his composure. Possessing the true insight of elite souls, he instantly recognized how this mishap could serve his goals. He realized that a confrontation with Matthew would inevitably drive his mother into a hysterical fit. Following the principle that calm follows the storm, a morning of hysteria would surely lead to an afternoon of bed rest, solving his second task perfectly.

The collision occurred immediately in the hall. Dry laughter, an insulting sneer, and a contemptuous taunt met with a nonchalant but cutting retort. They clashed violently. Servants rushed in, followed by Mrs. Yorke and Miss Moore; no woman could pull them apart. Mr. Yorke was summoned and declared, "Sons, one of you must leave my roof if this recurs. I will have no Cain and Abel strife here."

Martin allowed himself to be led away. Though physically slight and the youngest, he remained cool, even smiling, pleased that the hardest part of his labor was complete. For a brief moment, he flagged, remarking, "It is not worth bothering myself for that Caroline." Yet, fifteen minutes later, he returned to the dining room, staring at a head with disheveled hair and eyes filled with despair. "Yes," he said, "I made her sob, shudder, almost faint. I'll see her smile before I'm done; besides, I intend to outwit all these women."

Directly after dinner, Mrs. Yorke retired to her chamber, exactly as predicted. Now for Hortense. She was comfortably seated in the back parlour mending stockings when Martin, feigning illness and stretching lazily on the sofa, began a conversation about Sarah, the maid at the Hollow. Through winding dialogue, he revealed that Sarah supposedly had three suitors—Frederic Murgatroyd, Jeremiah Pighills, and John-of-Mally's—and that Miss Mann confirmed she often entertained them with the best food the house offered.

There was no need for more. Hortense could not remain another hour without rushing to investigate these nefarious activities personally. Mrs. Horsfall remained. Master of the situation, Martin retrieved a bunch of keys from his mother's workbasket, unlocked the sideboard cupboard, and produced a black bottle and a small glass. Placing them on the table, he swiftly ascended the stairs, tapped on Mr. Moore's door, and waited. When the nurse opened it, he invited her to the back parlour for refreshment, assuring her she would not be disturbed as the family was out. He watched her enter, secured the door, and knew she was safely detained.

The hard work was done; now came the pleasure. Snatching his cap, he headed for the woods. It was barely half-past three. The morning had been fine, but the sky now looked dark. Snow began to fall, the wind biting cold. The wood looked dismal, the ancient tree grim, yet Martin found charm in its spectral appearance. He paced back and forth as the flakes intensified and the wind shifted from a moan to a howl.

"She is late," he muttered, scanning the narrow track. "I wonder what I wish to see her for? She isn't coming for me. But I hold power over her, and I want her to come so I may wield it." As time dragged on, his mood hardened. "If she fails to come," he resolved, "I shall hate and scorn her."

At four o'clock, the distant church bell tolled. Then, a step—quick, light, almost inaudible amidst the rustling leaves—checked his impatience. The wind blew fiercely, the snowstorm thickening into a bewildering white chaos, yet she advanced, undismayed.

"Well, Martin," she breathed eagerly, "how is he?"

"It is curious how she thinks of *him*," Martin reflected internally. "The blinding snow and bitter cold mean nothing to her; yet she is merely a 'chitty-faced creature,' as my mother says. I could wish I had a cloak to wrap around her." Lost in thought, he forgot to answer Miss Helstone until she pressed, "You have seen him?"

"No."

"Oh! You promised you would."

"I intend to do better than that. Didn't I say *I* don't care to see him?"

"But now it will be so long before I learn anything certain. I am sick of waiting. Martin, *please* see him. Give him Caroline Helstone's regards. Tell him she wishes to know how he is and if anything can be done for his comfort."

"I won't."

"You are changed. You were so friendly last night."

"Come, we must not stand in this wood; it is too cold."

"But before I go, promise me to return tomorrow with news."

"No such thing. I am too delicate to make and keep appointments in winter. If you knew the pain in my chest this morning, how I went without breakfast, and how I was knocked down, you'd feel the impropriety of dragging me here in the snow. Come, I say."

"Are you really delicate, Martin?"

"Don't I look so?"

"You have rosy cheeks."

"That's hectic. Will you come—or won't you?"

"Where?"

"With me. I was a fool not to bring a cloak. I would have made you cozy."

"You are going home; my nearest road lies in the opposite direction."

"Put your arm through mine; I'll take care of you."

"But the wall—the hedge—it's hard climbing, and you are too slender and young to help without hurting yourself."

"You shall go through the gate."

"But—"

"But, but—will you trust me or not?"

She looked into his face. "I think I will. Anything rather than return as anxious as I came."

"I can't answer for that. But this I promise: be ruled by me, and you shall see Moore yourself."

"See him myself?"

"Yourself."

"But, dear Martin, does he know?"

"Ah! I'm dear now. No, he doesn't know."

"And your mother and the others?"

"All is right."

Caroline fell into a long silence, walking on with her guide toward Briarmains.

"Have you made up your mind?" he asked.

She was silent.

"Decide; we are just on the spot. I *won't* see him—that I tell you—except to announce your arrival."

"Martin, you are a strange boy, and this is a strange step; but all I feel *is* and *has been*, for a long time, strange. I will see him."

"Having said that, you will neither hesitate nor retract?"

"No."

"Then here we are. Do not fear passing the parlour window; no one will see you. My father and Matthew are at the mill, Mark is at school, the servants are in the back kitchen, Miss Moore is at the cottage, my mother is in bed, and Mrs. Horsfall is in paradise. Observe—I need not ring. I open the door; the hall is empty, the staircase quiet; so is the gallery. The whole house and all its inhabitants are under a spell, which I will not break until you are gone."

"Martin, I trust you."
