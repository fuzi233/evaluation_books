A singular purpose drove Romola forward, a resolve that had coalesced from the scattered anxieties of the afternoon into a single, urgent necessity. Ignoring the encroaching dusk and refusing the slow, cautious protection of Maso, she crossed the bridge where the river churned black beneath the fading crimson of the sky. She took the shortest path to the Old Palace, accepting the risk of encountering her husband as a trivial price to pay for the need to speak. She did not calculate the odds; she simply had to unload the weight pressing upon her soul. Whether she passed unnoticed through the pillared court or climbed the broad stairs remained a blur; her only certainty was the desperate urgency of her request to an usher. She gave her name, demanding a private audience with the Gonfaloniere.

She was not long left alone amidst the silent frescoes and the flickering new candles. The door swung open, and Bernardo del Nero entered, his white hair held high and proud above his silk lucco.

"Romola, my child, what is this?" he asked, his voice tight with anxious surprise as he shut the door against the corridor.

She uncovered her head and moved toward him in silence. He placed a hand on her shoulder, holding her at a respectful distance to study her features. Her face was ravaged by exhaustion and prolonged turmoil, her hair tumbling in disarray, yet her eyes burned with a fierce excitement that seemed to have conquered her physical frailty.

"What has he done?" Bernardo demanded abruptly. "Speak, child; cast aside your pride. I am your father."

"It is not about myself—nothing concerns me personally," Romola replied quickly. "Dearest godfather, it concerns you. I have heard whispers—some I dare not utter. But you are in peril within these walls; you are in danger everywhere. Fanatics seek to harm you, and there are traitors among you. Trust no one. If you place your trust, you will be betrayed."

Bernardo smiled faintly.

"Have you driven yourself into such a frenzy, my poor child," he said, lifting a hand to stroke her hair gently, "to repeat such ancient truths to an old man like me?"

"Oh, no, no! These are not old truths," Romola insisted, clutching her hands together with painful force as if to stifle the words she could not yet speak. "These are fresh horrors I know, but cannot reveal. Dearest godfather, you know I am not foolish. I would not disturb you without cause. Is it too late to warn you against anyone, every single one who appears to serve your interests? Is it too late to say, 'Retreat to your villa and hide in the countryside once these final three days of office end'? Oh God! Perhaps it is too late! And if harm comes to you, it will feel as though I have caused it!"

The final words tore from her involuntarily, a long-suppressed emotion finding spasmodic release. Yet, she was startled by her own outburst and fell silent.

"I mean," she added, her voice trembling, "I know nothing definite. I only know what fills me with dread."

"Poor child!" Bernardo said, regarding her with quiet intensity for a moment. Then he spoke softly, "Go, Romola—return home and rest. These fears may merely be the monstrous shadows of something small and harmless. Even traitors require motive; the rats run only where they smell the cheese, and we do not yet know which direction the scent drifts."

He paused, turning his gaze away with an air of abstract resignation, until, with a slow shrug, he continued,

"As for warnings, they avail me nothing, child. I enter no conspiracies, yet I never abandon my colors. If I march alongside obstinate men who rush toward guns and pikes, I must share the consequences. Let us say no more of that. I have not many years left in my sack for them to steal. Go, child; return home and rest."

He touched her head again with affection, and she could not resist clinging to his arm, pressing her forehead against his shoulder. His caress felt like the final remnant of that youthful filial bond, which now appeared so bright to her even in its distress, for those troubles were untainted by malice.

"Is silence best, my Romola?" the old man asked.

"Yes, now; but I cannot tell if it will always be so," she answered hesitantly, lifting her head with a pleading gaze.

"Well, you have a father's ear while I draw breath," he said, lifting the black drapery to fold it tenderly around her head, adding, "and a father's home; remember that." Opening the door, he urged, "Hasten away. You look like a black ghost; you will find safety enough."

That night, when Romola finally succumbed to sleep, it was deep and profound. Agitation had reached its limit; she needed to gather strength before she could endure further suffering. In defiance of her rigid habits, she slept well past sunrise.

Her awakening came to the sound of gunfire. Piero de' Medici, backed by thirteen hundred men, stood before the gate facing Rome.

Romola learned this from Maso, accompanied by many circumstantial details of dubious reliability. A countryman had arrived before dawn to alarm the Signoria; otherwise, the city would have fallen by surprise. His master was absent, having been summoned to the Palazzo long ago. Romola sent the old man out again to gather news while she ascended to the loggia repeatedly, straining to discern any sign of the dreaded entry or its repulsion. Maso reported that the great Piazza teemed with armed men and that many leading citizens, suspected of favoring the Medici, had been summoned and detained. Some of the populace seemed indifferent to Piero's arrival, with rumors even suggesting the Signoria itself had invited him; yet, regardless of the truth, they offered him a grim welcome, and soldiers from Pisa were moving against him.

In her recollection of those morning hours, few external events stood out clearly above the tumultuous waves of retrospect and anticipation. She knew she had walked to the Badia at the appointed time despite the street alarms, and she knew she had waited there in vain. However, the scene she witnessed upon leaving the church, standing on the steps while the doors closed behind her for the afternoon recess, returned to her with the vivid clarity of a remembered dream.

There was a distinct shift in the faces and tones of the armed and unarmed crowds pausing or hurrying through the streets. The guns fired again, but this time the sound provoked only laughter. She soon understood the cause of the change. Piero de' Medici and his horsemen had turned their backs on Florence, galloping as fast as possible along the road to Siena. She learned this from a substantial shopkeeper, a Piagnone who had not yet laid down his pike.

"It is true," he concluded, with a bitter emphasis. "Piero is gone, but those left behind who were privy to his coming remain—we all know that; and if the new Signoria fulfills its duty, we shall soon know who they are."

The words struck Romola like a sharp spasm, but the evil they foreshadowed was not yet immediate. As she entered her home once more, her most pressing anxiety shifted to the possibility that she had lost sight of Baldassarre for a long while.
