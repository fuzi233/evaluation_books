The words repeated to Romola by countless lips echoed as a single, piercing cry, cutting through the haze of her recent days: *'Pray for me, for God has withdrawn from me the spirit of prophecy.'* These were the final utterances of Savonarola, spoken as he took leave of the friars of San Marco who had witnessed his signing of the confession. They struck her anew, shattering any remaining certainty she held regarding how a man, fully possessed of his faculties, looked back upon a life of such intensity. Those initial doubts were swiftly drowned out by even more heartbreaking tidings.

May nineteenth had arrived, and with the sun of that day, the two Papal Commissaries had entered Florence. Charged with concluding Savonarola's trial, they were welcomed amidst a clamor of voices demanding the brother's death. The common cry had shifted to a singular, venomous conviction: *'It is the Frate's deception that has brought all our misfortune; let him be burned, and all shall be right.'*

The following day, the accounts were grim. Fresh tortures were inflicted upon the shattered frame of the man who had once inspired such fervor. At the mere sight of the brutal instruments, Savonarola, convulsed with agitation, fell to his knees. In brief, passionate bursts, he retracted his confession. He declared that he had lied when he denied his prophetic gifts, asserting that if he were to suffer, it would be for the truth alone—"The things that I have spoken, I had them from God."

Yet the torment continued. Under the lash, the question returned: *Why had you uttered those retracting words?* In those times, men were not demons, yet the only path to release from agony lay in admitting guilt. The answer tore from him: *"I said it that I might seem good; tear me no more, I will tell you the truth."*

Florentine assessors presided over this renewed trial, and the news of his twofold retraction spread quickly through the city. It filled Romola with a paralyzing uncertainty. A sudden flash of insight pierced her despair: *There will come a moment when he speaks.* She envisioned a time when fear of falsehood would be his sole companion, when he stood before death itself, lifted above the mob, gazing upon them for the last time. Then, they could not stop him from speaking a final, decisive word. *I will be there,* she resolved.

Three days later, on the twenty-third of May, 1498, a long, narrow platform stretched across the great Piazza della Signoria, reaching from the Palazzo Vecchio toward the Tetta de' Pisani. Where a grove of fuel had once stood, there now remained a single, massive heap piled high on the circular area at the platform's end. Rising above this pyre was a gibbet with three halters; though one arm had been severed to avoid the appearance of a cross, the two remaining limbs still cast a shadow that made many viewers uneasy.

Upon the marble terrace of the Palazzo stood three distinct tribunals. Near the door sat the Bishop, prepared to strip Fra Girolamo and his two brethren of their religious orders. Beside them, the Papal Commissaries awaited to pronounce them heretics and schismatics, handing them over to the secular arm. Closest to the Marzocco statue, at the corner where the platform began, sat the Gonfaloniere and the Eight, ready to pass the sentence of death.

Once again, the Piazza was packed with expectant faces. A great fire was destined to be kindled. For most of the crowd pressing around the gibbet, the anticipation was fueled by ferocious hatred or a morbid curiosity to witness barbarity. Yet, scattered among them on the wide pavements, rooftops, and windows, lingered others. Even in the midst of bitter grief and enduring insults from the hypocritical "Piagnoni," they harbored a fading hope that God might intervene, manifesting their beloved prophet as His servant. And there were others, like Romola, who trembled with an eager desperation for that final instant when Savonarola might cry out, *"O people, I was innocent of deceit."*

Romola stood at a window on the north side of the Piazza, far removed from the marble terraces where the judges sat. Nearby, watching the scene with equal painful doubt, was a young Florentine of twenty-two, Jacopo Nardi. History would later honor him as one of the few to write of Fra Girolamo with simple, unvarnished truth, driven by a reverence for his eminence. Earlier, seeing the struggle within Romola—a shuddering horror warring with a desperate yearning to see the end—he had gently urged her, *"Madonna, there is no need for you to look at these cruel things. I will tell you when he comes out of the Palazzo. Trust to me; I know what you would see."*

Romola had covered her face, but the hootings that rendered the hideous scene vivid in her mind could not be silenced. Finally, an arm touched hers, and she heard the whispered signal, *"He comes."* She turned toward the Palace.

She saw Savonarola led forth in his Dominican garb. Standing before the Bishop, he was stripped of the black mantle, the white scapulary, and the long tunic until he stood in a close woolen under-tunic that betrayed no sacred office, no rank. He had been degraded, cut off from the Church Militant. The baser elements of the multitude delighted in such degradations, understanding them as the ultimate satire. A fresh, triumphant hoot rang out as the three degraded brethren moved toward the tribunal of the Papal Commissaries. To the crowd, did the prophet not look like a schismatic and a heretic now? It was easy to believe in the damnable state of a man standing stripped and humiliated.

They passed the third tribunal, that of the Florentine officials who would pronounce the sentence. From afar, Romola could discern the odious figure of Dolfo Spini, clad in the heavy black lucco, sitting among the Eight. The three figures, robed in close white garments, trudged along the platform amidst yells and grating tones of insult.

*"Cover your eyes, Madonna,"* Jacopo Nardi warned. *"Fra Girolamo will be the last."*

Before long, she was forced to uncover them again. Savonarola was there. He was no longer distant; he had mounted the steps, and she could see him looking round upon the multitude.

In that same moment, however, expectation died. All she saw was what he saw: torches waving to kindle the fuel beneath his dead body, faces glaring with a light far worse than any human eye could bear. All she heard was what *he* heard: gross jests, taunts, and curses. The moment had passed. Her face was covered again, and she knew only that Savonarola's voice had passed into eternal silence.

On the evening of May 22nd, 1509, five individuals, whose histories we have followed, were seated in a handsome upper room opening onto a loggia. This loggia overlooked the Borgo Pinti and, at its right corner, gazed down toward the city gates and the solemn heights leading to Fiesole beyond.

At one end of the room stood an archway leading to a narrow inner recess, barely large enough to be called a chamber. Light filtered from above, illuminating a small altar draped in fine white linen. Above it hung a picture, discernible from their distance only as the small, full-length portrait of a Dominican Brother. It was shaded from the overhead light by overhanging branches and wreaths of flowers, and beneath it, fresh tapers burned unlit. Yet, the decoration of the altar and its recess appeared incomplete. Part of the floor was strewn with a jumble of flowers and green boughs, and seated among them was a delicate, blue-eyed girl of thirteen. She tossed her long, light-brown hair from her eyes as she selected blooms for the wreaths she was weaving, or she looked up at her mother's work with a manner of instruction.

For her mother, Tessa, was not particularly adept at weaving flowers or any other craft. Though her fingers had not grown more dexterous with the passing years, they had certainly become plumper. She worked slowly, turning her head frequently, and sought Ninna's (the girl's) opinion with much deference, for Tessa never ceased to be astonished by the wisdom of her children. She still wore her countrywoman's gown, only broader than the old one, adorned with a silver pin in her rough, curly brown hair and the memorable necklace with its red cord, ending mysteriously at her bosom. Her rounded face wore a look of childish contentment that was even more perfect than in her youth; everyone in the world was so good, Tessa believed—even Monna Brigida never found fault with her anymore, sleeping most of the time, an amiable habit shared by all, which Tessa loved to emulate.

Monna Brigida slept at this moment in a straight-backed armchair, a couple of yards away. Her hair, parted backward beneath her black hood, possessed a soft whiteness unlike snow, simply the lovely whiteness of aged hair. Her chin rested upon her bosom, and her hands lay upon the elbow of her chair.
