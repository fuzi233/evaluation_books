In the autumn of 1588, the tranquil town of Bideford, nestled along the western coast of England, was abuzz with an air of jubilation. November's crisp mornings had brought an unseasonable stillness, but the daily routine was disrupted by the persistent ringing of Bideford church's bells, now two hours past their usual time. The cacophony echoed through the streets, where merchants and sailors, adorned in their finest attire, mingled with the town's elite. Garlands and tapestries adorned the windows, while the ships in the harbor proudly displayed their colors, their cannons booming out a celebratory fanfare. Sir Richard Grenville's residence, a hub of activity, resonated with the sounds of feasting and merriment, as grooms and serving-men scurried about, attending to the needs of the numerous guests. Amidst the chaos, the aristocracy of North Devon gathered in the churchyard, their stately demeanor a testament to the region's storied heritage. Prominent among them was the Countess of Bath, accompanied by Sir Richard, as the Earl of Bourchier remained in London attending to the Queen. The Fortescue family, with their various branches from Umberleigh, Clovelly, Wear, Buckland, and other locales, mingled with the Coles of Slade, the Stukelys of Affton, the St. Legers of Annery, and the Coffins of Portledge, as well as the Coplestones from Eggesford, a full thirty miles away. The patriarchal figure of Sir John Chichester of Ralegh led the procession, followed by his eight daughters and three sons, including one who would later become a renowned Lord Deputy and Baron of Belfast, having valiantly fought in Ireland. As they entered the church, the townspeople, eager to catch a glimpse of the esteemed guests, were aided by the benevolent Sir Richard, who graciously assisted the overwhelmed churchwardens in arranging the seating according to social hierarchy. Amidst the whispers and shuffling, the rector took his place at the pulpit, ready to deliver the thanksgiving service in honor of a recent naval victory.
