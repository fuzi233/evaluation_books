When Margaret awoke, her head rested upon Catherine’s arm, and the faces of Gerard’s family—those who had not turned against him—gathered around her with rough but earnest kindness. Giles, loud and hearty, bellowed reassurance. “Gerard’s alive and well, or he’d never have penned such a letter—long as a sermon and fairer than any I’ve seen!”

Margaret’s voice trembled. “Aye, he was alive when he wrote it. But who knows what has befallen him since? Oh, why did he leave Holland to wander among strangers fierce as lions? And why did I not send him away sooner, rather than see him torn from his own flesh and blood?” She turned to Catherine, her eyes brimming with remorse. “Forgive me, you who are his mother.”

She tried weakly to slip from the chair to her knees, but Catherine held her fast, pulling her into an embrace instead. With a sigh, Margaret surrendered, pressing the letter into Eli’s hands. “Take it. I can read no more—and I would not leave this comfort.” She nestled closer, her other arm winding around Catherine’s neck.

Eli passed the letter to Richart. “Your eyes are younger than mine. Read it.”

Richart took the parchment, marveling at the script. “Never have I seen such fine writing—like needlework, yet clear as day. What a clerk he’d have made in Amsterdam instead of tramping the world!”

As Richart prepared to read, Kate and her mother exchanged a glance, then drew out their hidden sewing—tiny caps, half-finished, delicate as monks’ cowls.

When I came to myself, I was in the litter, my merchant friend holding my hand. I babbled nonsense, then shuddered in silence. He pressed wine to my lips—

Catherine interrupted. “Bless him!”

Eli hushed her.

—and when I told him what had happened, he examined my leg. The ankle was swollen, my hose barely stayed up for lack of points. “Sir,” I said, “I shall burden you now, and my psaltery is broken—no more music for our journey.” But he only patted my cheek and bade me fret not. He propped my leg on a pillow, tending me like a father.

Carried in the litter, I saw Lombardy’s plains burst into view—a land of milk and honey! Sloping fields, rivers laughing through meadows, orchards in bloom though it be winter. Vines, olives, cattle, and goats abound. The oxen wear linen about their necks; the women, straw hats embroidered with silk flowers. We crossed a river by chained ferry—a marvel! An old woman eyed a young girl, who hid behind her crucifix, fearing the evil eye.

Venice at last! A city of wonders—canals like liquid gold, palaces on piles, the great Piazza San Marco. But from my window, I see only plain streets and houses with oiled paper for glass. So I turn inward and share what I have learned in my wanderings.

The Germans are frank but love their drink too well. They say, *He who cannot swill and gorge is no good servant.* Yet their women are chaste and faithful—unlike our Dutch lasses, who command their men.

The French are fair-spoken but not always true. Still, they have courtesy in their blood. “Pray be seated,” they say, and a napkin on a bench reserves your place. Yet they flatter women and despise them—unless she rules them.

The Italians are subtle and polished. They judge a man by his bearing, not his garb. Here, even a cardinal might arm his guest with a fork to hold meat while cutting—as if God’s own hands were not fit! Their women bleach their hair, defying nature. Their laws are strict, their punishments bloody. And their trade in sin is state-sanctioned—twenty thousand courtesans honored for drawing strangers to Venice.

Yet they are wise in small things: roses forced to bloom in winter, fountains that dance like living things. And in London and Lübeck, water flows tame through pipes—a wonder!

Today I saw St. Mark’s relics—his very body in a chest of brass, his Gospel written by his hand. I touched his ring! And I saw the Virgin’s chair, her portrait by St. Luke, now faded.

But Venice’s darker side cannot be ignored. Three traitors’ heads rot on a slab, bought by the state for two thousand ducats each. A gallows of alabaster stands ready for the Doge himself, should he betray the republic. And on a wall, a fresco depicts four brothers who poisoned one another for land—all dead in a single afternoon, their souls lost, their estate passed to strangers.

As the saying goes:

Richart finished reading, and silence fell. Denys, ever the jester, feigned offense. “*My foul mouth?* Surely the clerk misread!”

Margaret blushed. The others hid smiles behind their sewing.

Denys crossed his arms. “Well? Do I speak foully?”

No one answered.

With a sigh, he gestured for Richart to continue. But the letter was done—and so was Gerard’s time in Venice. Tomorrow, he would sail for Rome.
