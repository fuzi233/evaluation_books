After a spirited and unyielding resistance on Mrs. Ferrars's part—a defense so fierce and steadfast that it shielded her from the dreaded accusation of being "too amiable"—Edward was finally granted an audience. He was once more pronounced her son.

The Ferrars household had recently undergone a turbulent fluctuation in its composition. For years, Mrs. Ferrars had been blessed with two sons; yet the recent annihilation of Edward had deprived her of one, and the prior loss of Robert had left her bereft of both for a fortnight. Now, through Edward's resuscitation, she possessed one again.

Yet, despite this restoration, Edward did not feel his existence secure until he revealed his current engagement. He feared that the mere publication of his intention to marry Miss Dashwood might shock his constitution, hastening his demise as swiftly as his previous crisis. With apprehensive caution, he disclosed the news, only to be met with an unexpected calmness. Mrs. Ferrars initially attempted to dissuade him, marshaling every argument in her arsenal. She urged him toward Miss Morton, a woman of higher rank and greater fortune, emphasizing that Miss Morton was the daughter of a nobleman with thirty thousand pounds, whereas Miss Dashwood was merely the daughter of a private gentleman with a modest three thousand. When she realized that, while acknowledging the truth of her assertions, Edward was unmoved by them, she deemed it wiser, based on past experience, to submit. After an ungracious delay born of her own dignity, designed to prevent any suspicion of goodwill, she issued her decree of consent to the marriage of Edward and Elinor.

The next matter to be settled was the augmentation of their income. Here, it became plain that although Edward was now her only son, he was by no means her eldest. While Robert was inevitably endowed with a thousand pounds a year, no objection was raised against Edward taking holy orders for a mere two hundred and fifty at the utmost. Beyond the ten thousand pounds already given with Fanny, nothing further was promised, either for the present or the future. This was, however, precisely what Edward and Elinor desired, and more than they had expected. Indeed, Mrs. Ferrars herself, through her shuffling excuses, seemed the only one surprised that she did not offer more.

Secured with an income sufficient for their needs, they awaited only the completion of the Parsonage, which Colonel Brandon was eagerly improving to accommodate Elinor. After enduring the usual thousand disappointments and delays caused by the unaccountable dilatoriness of the workmen, Elinor broke through her first firm resolution to postpone marriage until everything was perfect. The ceremony took place in Barton church early in the autumn.

Their first month of wedded life was spent at the Mansion-house with their friend, allowing them to superintend the progress of the Parsonage and direct every detail from afar. Mrs. Jennings's prophecies, though somewhat jumbled, were largely fulfilled; by Michaelmas, she was able to visit her new daughter-in-law and found in Elinor and her husband one of the happiest couples in the world. They lacked for nothing but the marriage of Colonel Brandon and Marianne, and perhaps better pasturage for their cows.

They were visited on their first settling by nearly all their relations and friends. Mrs. Ferrars came to inspect the happiness she had authorized with some shame, and even the Dashwoods undertook a journey from Sussex to pay their respects.

"I will not say that I am disappointed, my dear sister," said John, as they walked together one morning before the gates of Delaford House. "That would be saying too much, for certainly you have been one of the most fortunate young women in the world, as it is. But, I confess, it would give me great pleasure to call Colonel Brandon brother. His property here, his place, his house, everything is in such respectable and excellent condition! And his woods—I have not seen such timber anywhere in Dorsetshire as there is now standing in Delaford Hanger! And though, perhaps, Marianne may not seem exactly the person to attract him, yet I think it would altogether be advisable for you to have them stay frequently with you. As Colonel Brandon seems so much at home, nobody can tell what may happen; for when people are much thrown together and see little of anyone else, opportunities arise. You may as well give her a chance; you understand me."

Though Mrs. Ferrars did come to see them, treating them with a make-believe of decent affection, they were never insulted by her real favor or preference. That was reserved for the folly of Robert and the cunning of his wife, who earned it within months. The selfish sagacity of the latter, which had initially drawn Robert into his scrape, became the principal instrument of his deliverance. Her respectful humility, assiduous attentions, and endless flatteries, seizing the smallest opening for their exercise, reconciled Mrs. Ferrars to his choice and re-established him completely in her favor.

The entire course of Lucy's behavior in the affair, and the prosperity that crowned it, stands as an encouraging instance of what earnest, unceasing attention to self-interest can achieve, even when progress appears obstructed. It secures every advantage of fortune with no sacrifice but that of time and conscience. When Robert first sought her acquaintance in Bartlett's Buildings, it was with the view imputed to him by his brother: merely to persuade her to break her engagement. Expecting that one or two interviews would settle the matter, he erred in his assumption. Though Lucy soon gave him hopes that his eloquence would convince her in time, another visit, another conversation, was always required to produce this conviction. Doubts lingered in her mind upon parting, removable only by another half-hour of discourse. Thus, his attendance was secured, and the rest followed in course. Instead of speaking of Edward, they gradually spoke only of Robert—a subject on which he had always more to say, and in which she soon betrayed an interest equal to his own. It became speedily evident to both that he had entirely supplanted his brother. He was proud of his conquest, proud of tricking Edward, and very proud of marrying privately without his mother's consent.

What immediately followed is known. They passed some months in great happiness at Dawlish, where she had many relations and old acquaintances to court, and he drew several plans for magnificent cottages. Returning to town, they procured the forgiveness of Mrs. Ferrars by the simple expedient of asking it, an action adopted at Lucy's instigation. The forgiveness, at first, as was reasonable, comprehended only Robert. Lucy, who had owed her mother no duty and therefore could have transgressed none, remained unpardoned for some weeks longer. But perseverance in humility, in self-condemnation for Robert's offense, and in gratitude for the unkindness she was treated with, eventually procured her the haughty notice that overcame her by its graciousness. This led, by rapid degrees, to the highest state of affection and influence. Lucy became as necessary to Mrs. Ferrars as either Robert or Fanny. While Edward was never cordially forgiven for having once intended to marry her, and Elinor, though superior in fortune and birth, was spoken of as an intruder, *she* was in everything considered, and always openly acknowledged, as a favorite child. They settled in town, received very liberal assistance from Mrs. Ferrars, and were on the best terms imaginable with the Dashwoods. Setting aside the jealousies and ill-will continually subsisting between Fanny and Lucy, in which their husbands of course took a part, as well as the frequent domestic disagreements between Robert and Lucy themselves, nothing could exceed the harmony in which they all lived together.

What Edward had done to forfeit the right of the eldest son might have puzzled many to find out; and what Robert had done to succeed to it might have puzzled them still more. It was an arrangement, however, justified in its effects, if not in its cause. Nothing ever appeared in Robert's style of living or talking to give suspicion that he regretted the extent of his income, as either leaving his brother too little or bringing himself too much. If Edward might be judged from the ready discharge of his duties, from an increasing attachment to his wife and home, and from the regular cheerfulness of his spirits, he might be supposed no less contented with his lot, no less free from every wish of exchange.

Elinor's marriage divided her as little from her family as could well be contrived without rendering the cottage at Barton entirely useless, for her mother and sisters spent much more than half their time with her. Mrs. Dashwood acted on motives of policy as well as pleasure in the frequency of her visits to Delaford; for her wish of bringing Marianne and Colonel Brandon together was hardly less earnest, though rather more liberal, than what John had expressed. It was now her darling object. Precious as the company of her daughter was to her, she desired nothing so much as to give up its constant enjoyment to her valued friend. To see Marianne settled at the mansion-house was equally the wish of Edward and Elinor. They each felt his sorrows, and their own obligations, and Marianne, by general consent, was to be the reward of all.

With such a confederacy against her—with a knowledge so intimate of his goodness—with a conviction of his fond attachment to herself, which at last, though long after it was observable to everybody else, burst upon her—what could she do?

Marianne Dashwood was born to an extraordinary fate. She was born to discover the falsehood of her own opinions and to counteract, by her conduct, her most favorite maxims. She was born to overcome an affection formed so late in life as at seventeen, with no sentiment superior to strong esteem and lively friendship, voluntarily to give her hand to another! And *that* other, a man who had suffered no less than herself under the event of a former attachment, whom, two years before, she had considered too old to be married, and who still sought the constitutional safeguard of a flannel waistcoat!

But so it was. Instead of falling a sacrifice to an irresistible passion, as once she had fondly flattered herself with expecting, instead of remaining forever with her mother and finding her only pleasures in retirement and study as she had later determined in her more calm and sober judgment, she found herself at nineteen submitting to new attachments, entering on new duties, placed in a new home, a wife, the mistress of a family, and the patroness of a village.

Colonel Brandon was now as happy as all those who best loved him believed he deserved to be. In Marianne he was consoled for every past affliction; her regard and her society restored his mind to animation and his spirits to cheerfulness. That Marianne found her own happiness in forming his was equally the persuasion and delight of each observing friend. Marianne could never love by halves, and her whole heart became, in time, as much devoted to her husband as it had once been to Willoughby.

Willoughby could not hear of her marriage without a pang, and his punishment was soon afterwards complete in the voluntary forgiveness of Mrs. Smith. By stating his marriage with a woman of character as the source of her clemency, she gave him reason for believing that had he behaved with honor toward Marianne, he might at once have been happy and rich. That his repentance of misconduct, which thus brought its own punishment, was sincere, need not be doubted; nor that he long thought of Colonel Brandon with envy and of Marianne with regret. But that he was forever inconsolable, that he fled from society, or contracted a habitual gloom of temper, or died of a broken heart, must not be depended on—for he did neither. He lived to exert and frequently to enjoy himself. His wife was not always out of humor, nor his home always uncomfortable; and in his breed of horses and dogs, and in sporting of every kind, he found no inconsiderable degree of domestic felicity.

For Marianne, however, in spite of his incivility in surviving her loss, he always retained that decided regard which interested him in everything that befell her, making her his secret standard of perfection in woman. Many a rising beauty would be slighted by him in after-days as bearing no comparison with Mrs. Brandon.

Mrs. Dashwood was prudent enough to remain at the cottage without attempting a removal to Delaford. Fortunately for Sir John and Mrs. Jennings, when Marianne was taken from them, Margaret had reached an age highly suitable for dancing, and not very ineligible for being supposed to have a lover.

Between Barton and Delaford, there was that constant communication which strong family affection would naturally dictate. Among the merits and the happiness of Elinor and Marianne, let it not be ranked as the least considerable that, though sisters and living almost within sight of each other, they could live without disagreement between themselves or producing coolness between their husbands.
