"Good morning," I murmured, sinking back into the duvet with a sigh of profound contentment. "Just the perfect blend of Earl Grey and silence."

Across the room, Jeeves moved with the silent, fluid efficiency of a man who treats domestic chaos as a minor inconvenience. He adjusted the silk cravat of a phantom guest, polished a brass knob until it screamed in protest, and arranged the day's attire with a precision that bordered on the artistic. Watching him, I couldn't help but wonder: what would happen if the fellow ever decided to pack his bags and leave me stranded?

In London, the prospect was terrifying. Here in New York, it was merely annoying. Back home, there were constant, low-level assassination attempts on his person. Young Reggie Foljambe had once offered him double my salary, a move that smelled distinctly of treachery. Then there was Alistair Bingham-Reeves, whose own valet was rumored to possess the ability to press trousers sideways without wrinkling them; whenever he visited, he'd stare at Jeeves with a predatory, glittering hunger that made my blood run cold. These were bally pirates, plain and simple.

The truth of the matter is that Jeeves is undeniably competent. You can spot it in the microscopic details, like the exact angle at which he shoves a stud into a shirt collar. I rely on him absolutely during crises, and he never fails. Furthermore, he extends his loyalty to my friends, even when they are knee-deep in financial bouillon.

Take the peculiar case of Bicky and his uncle, the Hard-Boiled Egg.

It occurred shortly after my return from America. Late one night, as Jeeves poured my final drink, he announced, "Mr. Bickersteth called to see you this evening, sir, while you were out."

"Oh?" I asked, swirling the amber liquid.

"Twice, sir. He appeared a trifle agitated."

"What, pipped?"

"He gave that impression, sir."

I took a sip. I felt a pang for Bicky, though I admitted privately that I was relieved to have a topic of conversation that wasn't my new facial hair. Things had been strained between us lately. I had decided, rightly or wrongly, to cultivate a moustache, a decision that had cut Jeeves to the quick. He could not abide the thing at any price, and I had lived in an atmosphere of his disapproval until I was thoroughly fed up. While Jeeves's judgment on costumes was impeccable, editing my face felt like an overreach. One cannot expect a valet to stake a claim on one's upper lip without offering a bit of bulldog defiance.

"He said he would call again later, sir."

"Something must be up, Jeeves."

"Yes, sir."

I twirled the moustache thoughtfully. Since it clearly distressed Jeeves, I discarded it.

"I see by the paper, sir, that Mr. Bickersteth's uncle is arriving on the *Carmantic*."

"Yes?"

"His Grace the Duke of Chiswick, sir."

News to me, that Bicky's uncle held a dukedom. How little one knows about one's pals! We had met at a jamboree in Washington Square, shortly after my arrival in New York. Homesick and seeking camaraderie, I had bonded with Bicky upon discovering he was an Oxford contemporary. He was a frightful chump, so we naturally drifted together. During a quiet smoke in a corner free of pretentious artists, he endeared himself further with an extraordinary imitation of a bull-terrier chasing a cat up a tree. Though we became close, my knowledge of him remained superficial: he was perpetually hard up, sustained only by monthly remittances from his uncle.

"If the Duke of Chiswick is his uncle," I said, "why doesn't he hold a title? Why isn't he Lord What-Not?"

"Mr. Bickersteth is the son of his Grace's late sister, sir, who married Captain Rollo Bickersteth of the Coldstream Guards."

Jeeves knows everything.

"Is Mr. Bickersteth's father dead, too?"

"Yes, sir."

"Left any money?"

"No, sir."

Now I understood why poor Bicky was constantly on the rocks. To the casual observer, having a duke for an uncle sounds like a masterstroke. The trouble with old Chiswick was that, despite owning half of London and five northern counties, he was notoriously the most prudent spender in England. He was, in American parlance, a hard-boiled egg. Without inheritance, dependent solely on extracting funds from such a miser, Bicky was in a precarious position. Yet this didn't explain why Bicky was hunting me down; he was a man who never borrowed money, claiming he kept his pals by refusing to bite anyone's ear on principle.

Just then, the doorbell rang. Jeeves glided out to answer it.

"Yes, sir. Mr. Wooster has just returned," I heard him announce.

Bicky trickled in, looking thoroughly sorry for himself.

"Halloa, Bicky!" I said. "Jeeves told me you've been trying to reach me. Bring another glass, and let the revels commence. What's the trouble?"

"I'm in a hole, Bertie. I want your advice."

"Say on, old lad!"

"My uncle's turning up tomorrow, Bertie."

"So Jeeves told me."

"The Duke of Chiswick, you know."

"So Jeeves told me."

Bicky seemed surprised.

"Jeeves seems to know everything."

"Rather rummily, that's exactly what I was thinking just now myself."

"Well, I wish," said Bicky gloomily, "that he knew a way to get me out of the hole I'm in."

Jeeves shimmered in with the glass, placing it competently on the table.

"Mr. Bickersteth is in a bit of a hole, Jeeves," I said, "and wants you to rally round."

"Very good, sir."

Bicky looked dubious.

"Well, of course, you know, Bertie, this thing is by way of being a bit private and all that."

"I shouldn't worry about that, old top. I bet Jeeves knows all about it already. Don't you, Jeeves?"

"Yes, sir."

"Eh!" said Bicky, rattled.

"I am open to correction, sir, but is not your dilemma due to the fact that you are at a loss to explain to His Grace why you are in New York instead of in Colorado?"

Bicky rocked like jelly in a gale.

"How the deuce do you know anything about it?"

"I chanced to meet His Grace's butler before we left England. He informed me that he happened to overhear His Grace speaking to you on the matter, sir, as he passed the library door."

Bicky gave a hollow laugh.

"Well, as everybody seems to know all about it, there's no need to try to keep it dark. The old boy turfed me out, Bertie, because he said I was a brainless nincompoop. The plan was to send me to some blighted locality named Colorado to learn farming or ranching. I hated the idea. Riding horses and pursuing cows? I detest horses; they bite. I opposed the scheme, yet I needed that remittance."

"I get you absolutely, dear boy."

"When I reached New York, it seemed a decent place, so I cabled my uncle, claiming I'd found a sound business wheeze in the city and wished to abandon the ranch idea. He wrote back approvingly, assuming I was thriving. I never dreamed he would actually come out here. What on earth am I to do?"

"Jeeves," I said, "what on earth is Mr. Bickersteth to do?"

"You see," said Bicky, "he wired that he was coming to stay with me—to save hotel bills, presumably. I've always given him the impression I live in style. I can't have him at my boarding-house."

"Thought of anything, Jeeves?" I asked.

"To what extent, sir, if the question is not a delicate one, are you prepared to assist Mr. Bickersteth?"

"I'll do anything I can for you, of course, Bicky, old man."

"Then, if I might make the suggestion, sir, you might lend Mr. Bickersteth this flat. He could convince His Grace he owns it. With your permission, I could convey the notion that I am in Mr. Bickersteth's employ, not yours. You would reside here temporarily as Mr. Bickersteth's guest. His Grace would occupy the second spare bedroom. I fancy this answers satisfactorily, sir."

Bicky ceased rocking and stared at Jeeves in awe.

"I would advocate dispatching a wireless message to His Grace on board the vessel, notifying him of the change of address. Mr. Bickersteth could meet His Grace at the dock and proceed directly here. Will that meet the situation, sir?"

"Absolutely."

"Thank you, sir."
