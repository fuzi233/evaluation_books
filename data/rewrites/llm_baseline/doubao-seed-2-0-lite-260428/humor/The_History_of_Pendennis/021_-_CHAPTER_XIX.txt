Our boy Pen could not have been more relieved when his chaperone, Major Pendennis, waved him off for the London coach two days after they arrived at Oxbridge. The Major, for his part, was even more over the moon to be done with his three-month long nephew-sitting gig.

Think about it: three whole months of his precious, carefully curated selfish bachelor time, wasted on keeping a teenage boy out of trouble! Has any self-absorbed Major ever made so grand a sacrifice? Show me ten men you know who’d give up their easy chair, their dinner parties, their grouse shoots and gossiping sessions over post-prandial port to chaperone a snotty teen, and I’ll eat my top hat. We’ll all happily lay down our lives for honour, but handing over our daily comforts? That’s a trial almost no modern man can survive. I won’t speak ill of ladies, of course—they’ve been trained from day one to put other people first, God love ’em—but most of us gents will have a pretty sad looking credit line for self-sacrifice on our final heavenly balance sheet. All that to say: I fully side with the Major on this one. He earned his holiday, and he bolted straight from London to Cheltenham, then bounced between the fanciest nearby country houses that still had good shooting and even better dinner guests, no boring nephew required.

Foker and Pen saw him off on the coach, and Foker even made the driver promise to take extra special care of the old gent tucked in the back. The Major was chuffed to bits that Pen already had a friend like Foker to introduce him to the absolute best, most fashionable university set.

Now, we’ve already dragged out one little stretch of Pen’s life for roughly a quarter of the word count most three-volume serial novels reserve, so rest assured we’re not going to document every single boring minute of his university career from here on out. For one thing, most teenage lads’ daily lives are far too messy to print. Be honest, reader—would you want all your dumb college hijinks written up for strangers to gawk at? I thought not. As long as our honour’s still intact, we’re all happy enough to keep the rest buried. The world’s full of imperfect men, after all—go count the spotless saints in your own acquaintance, I’ll wait. I’ve got a huge social circle and I can’t find ten, so don’t pretend you can.

Pen started his first term fairly keen, turning up to classics and math lectures on the regular… until he very quickly realized he hated exact sciences with every fibre of his being. What’s more, a handful of scruffy lads who didn’t even own trouser straps to cover their thick, cheap work boots and holey stockings were absolutely destroying him in problem sets, and his ego could not take that public humiliation. He stopped showing up to math lectures entirely, and wrote to his mum to announce he was dedicating himself exclusively to fancy Greek and Roman poetry from here on out.

Mrs. Pendennis was over the moon. Her darling boy should chase whatever learning made him happy! The only thing she begged was that he didn’t stay up too late studying and give himself a brain fever, like all those poor tragic student stories she’d heard about that died halfway through their degrees. Pen promised her he wouldn’t burn the midnight oil, and shockingly, he actually kept that promise far better than he kept most of his other casual pledges to her.

Pretty soon, Pen realized the classics lectures were even more of a dud than the math ones. The other students were too slow, the tutor Mr. Buck was less sharp than a fifth-former at Pen’s old Grey Friars school, and the man had about as much understanding of the actual poetry in Aeschylus as Pen’s bed-maker Mrs. Binge. Pen could read and understand a whole passage of a play in ten minutes, while the rest of the class stumbled over the lines for an hour, and he got so bored he bailed on those lectures too. Private self-study was the only way to learn properly, he wrote to his mum, and she nodded along happily—she knew about as much Homer as she did algebra, so she had zero doubts her brilliant boy would top every exam he sat.

Pen didn’t come home for Christmas, which disappointed his mum and little Laura to bits, who’d been begging him to help build a giant snow fort like he’d done three winters before. But he’d been invited to Lady Agnes Foker’s estate at Logwood, where they were doing private theatricals and hosting a huge fancy Christmas party full of the exact high-society people the Major had insisted he befriend. He finally turned up home for the last three weeks of the vacation, loaded down with new fancy clothes, and Laura noticed he’d picked up a very grown-up, decisive swagger that hadn’t been there before.

He skipped coming home for Easter too, and when he showed up for the long summer vacation, he had even more fancy gear: bright shooting jackets with ridiculous giant buttons, velvet waistcoats for dinner, embroidered silk cravats, fancy imported linen, and a stunning silver-mounted dressing case full of little rings and trinkets. He’d dumped his late dad’s giant, heavy old fob-watch, the one his mum had given him before college with a whole solemn speech about respecting his father’s memory and using time wisely, calling it “about as useful as a warming pan with a clock face” and shoving it in a drawer full of out-of-fashion primrose gloves and discarded old cravats. Laura thought it was horribly disrespectful, and cried for three days when Pen swapped his old gentle horse Rebecca out for a faster, fancier new model that cost a fortune.

He also turned up with a huge box of fancy imported cigars with ridiculous names like Colorados and Telescopios, and started smoking them everywhere—even the conservatory, where the fumes were doing terrible things to his mum’s rosebushes. He told her he was working on a prize poem, and couldn’t possibly compose without a cigar in hand, quoting Lord Byron’s old lines about how smoking was mandatory for great writers. The soft old soul caved immediately, and one day when she found him “hard at work” on the sofa (actually reading a racy new popular novel, for “light literature research”) she didn’t even disturb him—she went to his bedroom, fetched his cigar case and a box of matches, walked back, stuck the cigar in his mouth, and lit it for him. Pen laughed, kissed her hand, and joked “Dear old mum, if I told you to burn the house down, I do believe you’d do it.” And he was probably right.

On top of his English light novels, he brought back stacks of racy French books from Oxbridge, and the first time Helen flipped through one by Paul de Kock her eyes nearly popped out of her head. Pen insisted this was all critical language study, just as important as reading Moliere or Swift, and Helen sighed and let him keep them—though both her and Pen, the self-appointed moral authority, strictly banned Laura from ever touching the stack. A young lady’s education didn’t require that sort of thing, obviously.

Over that long vacation, Pen drank through every last bottle of his dad’s old carefully laid-in claret, the same collection he’d once bragged didn’t have a single headache in the whole hogshead. Once it was gone, he wrote off to the London wine merchants old Doctor Portman had recommended before he went to college, ordering a huge new shipment of port and sherry. The rector had warned him that Oxbridge local wine sellers charged a fortune for terrible plonk, and told him to stock up from a respectable London firm if he wanted to host wine parties for his mates. The bill that arrived at Fairoaks made poor Helen nearly faint, but Pen laughed it off, said it was totally normal, every gentleman drank claret and champagne these days, and Helen paid up. She and Laura could pinch pennies and eat simple meals while Pen was back at college, after all—why spoil his few precious weeks at home?

Pen’s allowance was absurdly generous, far higher than the allowances of most lads whose families were twice as rich as the Pendennises. Years before he died, Pen’s dad had started putting aside cash for what he called the A.E.F., or Arthur’s Education Fund, and Helen had added to it every year after he passed. Major Pendennis had insisted he give Pen a huge, fat allowance: no stinting a boy, he argued. If Pen entered university living like a gentleman, he’d make all the right friends and pick up all the right gentlemanly habits, and be perfectly prepared to fight his way through the real world later. It was an excellent argument, all the better for the fact the Major didn’t have to pay a single penny of it.

So Pen waltzed around Oxbridge looking like he was the heir to a huge aristocratic estate, with his easy swagger, free-spending ways, good singing voice, and habit of buying rounds for all his mates. He rode well to hounds, turned up to meets in full pink hunting gear, and while no one of his hobbies was outrageously expensive on its own, he did all of them. He collected rare books with fancy gilded bindings, bought fancy Rembrandt etchings and Wilkie prints to decorate his rooms, wore ridiculous amounts of jewellery, and once people swore he even wore rings over his kid gloves. (He still denies this, but we all did stupid stuff when we were 19.) He took perfumed baths, he claimed, only to wash off the stench of the awful working-class lads he’d had the misfortune to sit next to in dining hall.

In his second year, when Miss Fotheringay was the biggest star in London and prints of her were sold on every street corner, Pen hung a giant framed print of her over his desk, and regaled all his mates with the tragic, dramatic story of how madly, passionately he’d once loved her. He showed them the terrible emo love poems he’d written for her, heaved big dramatic sighs and rolled his eyes recalling his broken heart, and the boys ate it all up. Nothing makes you more popular at university than having a reputation for a grand, tragic romantic past. Soon freshmen were pointing him out on the street, whispering that he’d almost killed himself over the actress, that he’d fought a duel with a baronet for her honour. He’d swagger around campus at 2pm surrounded by his crew, tipping his hat to the Proctors like they were rival heads of state, and bragging to his mates about how ugly the tourist ladies gawking at the university were. People were proud to be seen walking with Pendennis of Boniface, like they were strolling down Pall Mall with a duke.

He was the undisputed campus trendsetter. The second he bought a crimson satin cravat, two dozen lads turned up to lectures the next week wearing the exact same one. The local Jewish jeweler sold two full gross of the little fancy pin Pen picked out one afternoon, all to copycat undergrads.

If you sat down and did the math, you’d realize that Pen dabbled in every single expensive hobby that existed at the time—unlike his rich mates, who were all one-note: Lord Paddington only cared about mirrors and fancy dinners, Guttlebury hated riding and didn’t care what he wore, Snaffle never read anything except the Racing Calendar. Pen collected rare books, bought fancy art, rode horses, hosted dinner parties, bought fancy clothes, smoked imported cigars, and threw huge wine parties for his mates. By the end of his second year, his stack of unpaid bills was so tall you could use it as a side table, and Pen hadn’t even bothered to do the math yet. He was far too busy being the coolest man on Oxbridge campus to worry about something as boring as money.
