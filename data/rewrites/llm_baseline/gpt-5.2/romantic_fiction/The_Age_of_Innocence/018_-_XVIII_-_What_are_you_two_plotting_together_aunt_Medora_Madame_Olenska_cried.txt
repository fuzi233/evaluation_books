“What are you two plotting together, aunt Medora?” Madame Olenska called as she swept into the room.

She had dressed as if for a ball—yet not in the dutiful New York way of being prettified into obedience. Her gown seemed spun from the palest flame: candlelight caught in silk and lace, gliding as she moved, a soft glitter that turned the drawing-room air warmer, scented faintly of orris and the crushed green bite of violets. Her hair was heaped into a chignon that looked both disciplined and about to break free, and she carried herself with the lifted chin of a woman accustomed to entering rooms where envy had teeth.

Mrs. Manson Mingott—Medora to her intimates, and never quite intimate with anyone because she was always in love with some new idea—rose with theatrical satisfaction and pointed toward the flowers on a table.

“We were saying, my dear, that here was something beautiful to surprise you with.”

Madame Olenska halted.

Newland Archer, standing a little apart near the hearth, saw the change pass over her not as color but as a whitening, a bright, cold flash—anger like summer lightning that lights the whole landscape without warming it. She looked at the bouquet as if it were a thing that had been set down alive.

“Ah,” she cried, in a sharp voice he had never heard from her—too high, too naked. “Who is ridiculous enough to send me a bouquet? Why a bouquet? And why tonight of all nights? I am not going to a ball; I am not a girl engaged to be married. But some people are always ridiculous.”

The room, which had been quiet with the domestic hush of thick carpets and banked coals, suddenly seemed full of sound: the faint ticking of a clock, the little crackle of fire, the distant scrape of wheels out in the street. Even her words had a different ring—metallic, ringing off the mirrors. Newland, who had grown up in houses where anger was muffled like a cough behind a napkin, felt the shock of it as a kind of illicit vividness.

She turned, opened the door, and called: “Nastasia!”

The handmaiden appeared at once, as if the house had been listening for her name. Madame Olenska spoke in Italian—slowly, deliberately, with that precision that made the language feel like something you could taste: clean vowels, a blade’s edge to the consonants, chosen so that Newland would understand.

“Here—throw this into the dustbin!”

Nastasia stared, protesting with her eyes; the bouquet’s scent—rose and hyacinth and something spicy—had already begun to fill the room more boldly, as if insisting on its own innocence.

“But no—it’s not the fault of the poor flowers,” Madame Olenska added at once, with a quick recoil from her own violence. “Tell the boy to carry them to the house three doors away, the house of Mr. Winsett, the dark gentleman who dined here. His wife is ill—they may give her pleasure… The boy is out, you say? Then, my dear one, run yourself; here, put my cloak over you and fly. I want the thing out of the house immediately! And, as you live, don’t say they come from me!”

She flung her velvet opera cloak over Nastasia’s shoulders—velvet that held the faint warmth of her own body and the perfume that clung to her skin like a secret—and shut the drawing-room door sharply, sealing the flowers out as if their very fragrance could betray her.

For a moment her breathing rose under the lace at her throat, and Newland thought she might cry; instead she laughed—one of those laughs that was not merriment but release—and looked from the Marchioness to him with a sudden, challenging light.

“And you two—have you made friends!”

“It’s for Mr. Archer to say, darling,” Mrs. Manson returned, pleased with herself and with the scene. “He has waited patiently while you were dressing.”

“Yes—I gave you time enough: my hair wouldn’t go,” Madame Olenska said, lifting her hand to the heavy curls as if they had been a living creature resisting her. Then, briskly: “But that reminds me: I see Dr. Carver is gone, and you’ll be late at the Blenkers’. Mr. Archer, will you put my aunt in the carriage?”

Newland found himself in the hall amid the heap of overshoes, shawls, and tippets—a clutter of wool and fur, smelling of damp and cold air and camphor, the ordinary armor New York women wore against weather and comment. He helped Mrs. Manson into it all while Madame Olenska, already impatient for the next movement, stood on the threshold and called after them:

“Mind, the carriage is to be back for me at ten!”

Then she returned to the drawing-room, and when Newland came back he found her at the mantel-glass, looking at herself with an unembarrassed scrutiny. It was not usual in New York to hear a lady call her servant “my dear one,” or to send her out wrapped in her own opera cloak. The speed with which feeling became action here—no smoothing, no waiting for propriety to cool it—gave Newland a startled pleasure, as if he had stepped into a climate where the air itself carried more oxygen.

She did not turn when he approached. Their eyes met in the mirror for the briefest second—two reflections held in the same frame—then she went to the sofa and dropped into her corner, exhaling as if she had come in from a run.

“There’s time for a cigarette.”

He handed her the box. The little ritual—paper, tobacco, flame—had its own domestic intimacy, like cutting bread at a table. He struck a spill and held it; as the flame flared and warmed her face, the room filled with that first sweet-bitter smell that seemed to lay a veil over everything.

She glanced at him through the first ring of smoke and smiled, her temper already transmuted into wit.

“What do you think of me in a temper?”

He hesitated only a moment. The clock ticked; the fire shifted; somewhere outside, a cab rattled over the cobbles and then faded, leaving the street quiet again.

“It makes me understand what your aunt has been saying about you.”

“I knew she’d been talking about me. Well?”

“She said you were used to all kinds of things—splendours and amusements and excitements—that we could never hope to give you here.”

Madame Olenska smiled faintly, smoke curling at her lips like a soft guard.

“Medora is incorrigibly romantic. It has made up to her for so many things!”

Newland, feeling the edge of time already—this being their last evening here by this fire, this room with its muffled city-silence outside—pushed further.

“Is your aunt’s romanticism always consistent with accuracy?”

“You mean: does she speak the truth?” She considered, as if weighing spices by scent alone. “Well, I’ll tell you: in almost everything she says, there’s something true and something untrue. But why do you ask? What has she been telling you?”

He looked into the coals. The heat breathed; the room’s faint odors—wax, old upholstery, tobacco—seemed suddenly precious, unrepeatable.

“She says—she pretends that Count Olenski has asked her to persuade you to go back to him.”

Madame Olenska did not start. He had noticed before her strange lack of surprise, as if life had ceased long ago to astonish her and only continued to wound or delight.

“You knew, then?” he burst out.

She was silent so long the ash fell from her cigarette. She brushed it to the floor with an absent movement.

“She has hinted about a letter: poor darling! Medora’s hints—”

“Is it at your husband’s request that she has arrived here suddenly?”

Again that pause, that inward listening.

“There again: one can’t tell. She told me she had had a ‘spiritual summons,’ whatever that is, from Dr. Carver. I’m afraid she’s going to marry Dr. Carver… poor Medora, there’s always some one she wants to marry. But perhaps the people in Cuba just got tired of her! I think she was with them as a sort of paid companion. Really, I don’t know why she came.”

“But you do believe she has a letter from your husband?”

Madame Olenska sat with her gaze lowered, as if she were reading not the present but a script long familiar.

“After all, it was to be expected.”

Newland rose and leaned against the chimney-piece, restless. The street outside seemed too quiet, as though it were holding its breath to hear what would be decided in this room. He imagined the sudden jingle of harness, the return of her carriage, time coming back for her.

“You know that your aunt believes you will go back?”

Madame Olenska lifted her head quickly. A deep blush rose over her face and down her neck and shoulders—painful, rare, as if it burned her.

“Many cruel things have been believed of me,” she said.

“Oh, Ellen—forgive me; I’m a fool and a brute!”

She smiled a little, gentler now, and, with the instinct that made her turn away from her own pain, she shifted the subject as if moving a lamp to soften a harsh corner.

“You are horribly nervous; you have your own troubles. I know you think the Wellands are unreasonable about your marriage, and of course I agree with you. In Europe people don’t understand our long American engagements; I suppose they are not as calm as we are.”

She gave the “we” a faint emphasis—ironic, almost tender in its irony. He felt it and dared not touch it.

“Yes,” he said abruptly, following her lead because he could not bear to lose her in a fog of politeness. “I went south to ask May to marry me after Easter. There’s no reason why we shouldn’t be married then.”

“And May adores you—and yet you couldn’t convince her? I thought her too intelligent to be the slave of such absurd superstitions.”

“She is too intelligent—she’s not their slave.”

Madame Olenska looked at him steadily.

“Well, then—I don’t understand.”

He reddened and went on, the words crowding like people at a door.

“We had a frank talk—almost the first. She thinks my impatience a bad sign.”

“Merciful heavens—a bad sign?”

“She thinks it means that I can’t trust myself to go on caring for her. She thinks, in short, I want to marry her at once to get away from some one that I—care for more.”

Madame Olenska’s face held curiosity, not jealousy. It was one of the ways she was unlike the women he knew—she could look at feeling without instantly turning it into a weapon.

“But if she thinks that—why isn’t she in a hurry too?”

“Because she’s not like that: she’s so much nobler. She insists all the more on the long engagement, to give me time—”

“Time to give her up for the other woman?”

“If I want to.”

Madame Olenska leaned toward the fire and stared into it. Outside, Newland heard at last the approaching trot—hoofbeats on frozen ground, the faint chiming of harness bells, sound traveling through the night like fate drawing nearer.

“That is noble,” she said, and her voice broke slightly.

“Yes. But it’s ridiculous.”

“Ridiculous? Because you don’t care for any one else?”

“Because I don’t mean to marry any one else.”

“Ah.”

Silence. The horses came nearer, then slowed.

At length she looked up and asked, almost clinically: “This other woman—does she love you?”

“Oh, there’s no other woman; I mean, the person that May was thinking of is—was never—”

“Then, why, after all, are you in such haste?”

“There’s your carriage,” he said.

She half rose and looked about, absently. Her fan and gloves lay beside her on the sofa; she picked them up as if they belonged to another woman and must be carried out for the sake of decorum.

“Yes; I suppose I must be going.”

“You’re going to Mrs. Struthers’s?”

“Yes.” She smiled. “I must go where I am invited, or I should be too lonely. Why not come with me?”

He could not let her go—not yet, not like this, with everything still unsaid and the house already filling with departure. Ignoring her question, he stood rigidly, eyes fixed on the hand that held her gloves and fan, as if he might will her fingers to open.

“May guessed the truth,” he said. “There is another woman—but not the one she thinks.”

Madame Olenska did not move. The fan’s sticks were motionless between her fingers.

He sat down beside her, and, taking her hand, unclasped it softly. Gloves and fan dropped to the sofa between them with a small, decisive sound.

She started up, pulling away, crossing to the other side of the hearth as if the fire itself were a border.

“Ah, don’t make love to me! Too many people have done that,” she said, frowning.

He changed color. It was the bitterest thing she could have said, because it pushed him into the common crowd of men who had used her beauty as a door to their own appetites.

“I have never made love to you,” he said, “and I never shall. But you are the woman I would have married if it had been possible for either of us.”

“Possible for either of us?” She stared, honestly astonished. “And you say that—when it’s you who’ve made it impossible?”

He stared back as if the room had suddenly gone dark.

“I’ve made it impossible—?”

“You, you, you!” she cried, her lip trembling like a child’s at the edge of tears. “Isn’t it you who made me give up divorcing—give it up because you showed me how selfish and wicked it was, how one must sacrifice one’s self to preserve the dignity of marriage… and to spare one’s family the publicity, the scandal? And because my family was going to be your family—for May’s sake and for yours—I did what you told me, what you proved to me that I ought to do. Ah,” she broke off with a sudden laugh, “I’ve made no secret of having done it for you!”

She sank back into the sofa, crouching among the festive folds of her dress like a masquerader struck down mid-dance. Newland stood by the fireplace, unable to move, as if the words had nailed him to the spot.

“Good God,” he groaned. “When I thought—”

“You thought?” The blush rose again, scorching her skin. She sat upright, rigid with a dignity that looked like armor hammered on while still hot. “I do ask you.”

“Well, then: there were things in that letter you asked me to read—”

“My husband’s letter?”

“Yes.”

“I had nothing to fear from that letter: absolutely nothing! All I feared was to bring notoriety, scandal, on the family—on you and May.”

He bowed his face in his hands. The silence that followed was heavy, final; it pressed on him like a stone lid. In his mind the future stretched out and not one day in it seemed capable of lifting that weight.

“At least I loved you—” he forced out.

From the other side of the hearth he heard a faint, stifled crying, small and helpless as a child’s. The sound—so unguarded in a house where even grief was usually upholstered—went through him like a knife.

“Ellen!” He crossed to her. “What madness! Why are you crying? Nothing’s done that can’t be undone. I’m still free, and you’re going to be.”

He took her in his arms. Her face was wet against his lips like a flower after rain—alive, fragile, real. In that closeness every argument shriveled instantly, as if words were only ghosts and touch the sunrise that destroyed them. She returned his kiss wholly; and for a moment it seemed to him absurd that they had stood arguing across the room, as though distance itself could be maintained by reason.

Then he felt her stiffen. She put her hands against him—not harshly, but with a firmness that made him understand she was drawing on some reservoir deeper than passion.

“Ah, my poor Newland—I suppose this had to be. But it doesn’t in the least alter things,” she said, looking down at him from the hearth as if she were already removed to a higher, colder place.

“It alters the whole of life for me.”

“No, no—it mustn’t, it can’t. You’re engaged to May Welland; and I’m married.”

He stood too, flushed, suddenly reckless with resolve.

“Nonsense! It’s too late for that sort of thing. We’ve no right to lie to other people or to ourselves. We won’t talk of your marriage; but do you see me marrying May after this?”

She leaned her thin elbows on the mantelpiece. Her profile was doubled in the glass behind her, as if the room were making a second, quieter Ellen who watched and remembered. One lock had loosened from the chignon and lay along her neck; the effect was not pretty but human, and it made her look haggard, almost old.

“I don’t see you,” she said at last, “putting that question to May. Do you?”

He shrugged, trying to make courage out of defiance.

“It’s too late to do anything else.”

“You say that because it’s the easiest thing to say at this moment—not because it’s true. In reality it’s too late to do anything but what we’d both decided on.”

“Ah, I don’t understand you!”

She smiled, pitifully; it pinched instead of smoothing her face.

“You don’t understand because you haven’t yet guessed how you’ve changed things for me: oh, from the first—long before I knew all you’d done.”

“All I’d done?”

“Yes. I was perfectly unconscious at first that people here were shy of me—that they thought I was a dreadful sort of person. It seems they had even refused to meet me at dinner. I found that out afterward; and how you’d made your mother go with you to the van der Luydens’; and how you’d insisted on announcing your engagement at the Beaufort ball, so that I might have two families to stand by me instead of one—”

At that, despite everything, he broke into a laugh—short, incredulous, almost angry with the scale of what she called small.

“Just imagine,” she went on, “how stupid and unobservant I was! I knew nothing of all this till Granny blurted it out one day. New York simply meant peace and freedom to me: it was coming home. And I was so happy at being among my own people that every one I met seemed kind and good, and glad to see me. But from the very beginning,” she continued, in a low even voice, “I felt there was no one as kind as you; no one who gave me reasons that I understood for doing what at first seemed so hard and—unnecessary. The very good people didn’t convince me; I felt they’d never been tempted. But you knew; you understood; you had felt the world outside tugging at one with all its golden hands—and yet you hated the things it asks of one; you hated happiness bought by disloyalty and cruelty and indifference. That was what I’d never known before—and it’s better than anything I’ve known.”

Each word fell into him like something molten—burning not because it was cruel but because it was true. He sat bowed, staring at the hearth rug, at the small tip of satin shoe that showed beneath her hem as if that detail were all he could bear to look at.

Suddenly he knelt and kissed the shoe.

She bent over him, laid her hands on his shoulders, and looked down with eyes so deep he could not move under their gaze.

“Ah, don’t let us undo what you’ve done!” she cried. “I can’t go back now to that other way of thinking. I can’t love you unless I give you up.”

His arms strained toward her, but she drew away. They faced each other, divided now by the distance her words created—distance not of feet but of a moral language he could hear and yet could not speak.

Abruptly his anger surged up, wild and ugly, seeking something it could strike.

“And Beaufort? Is he to replace me?”

He expected her anger in return—expected it and would have welcomed it, for it would have been something simpler than this quiet firmness. But she only went a shade paler. Her arms hung down; her head bent slightly, as it did when she pondered something, listening inwardly.

“He’s waiting for you now at Mrs. Struthers’s; why don’t you go to him?” Archer sneered.

She turned and rang the bell.

“I shall not go out this evening; tell the carriage to go and fetch the Signora Marchesa,” she said to the maid when she came.

When the door closed again he still looked at her with bitter eyes, ashamed and unable to stop.

“Why this sacrifice? Since you tell me that you’re lonely I’ve no right to keep you from your friends.”

She smiled faintly through wet lashes.

“I shan’t be lonely now. I was lonely; I was afraid. But the emptiness and the darkness are gone; when I turn back into myself now I’m like a child going at night into a room where there’s always a light.”

Her tone wrapped her again in that soft inaccessibility, as if she lived in a country whose dialect he could almost understand and yet could never speak without an accent that betrayed him.

“I don’t understand you!” he groaned.

“Yet you understand May!”

He reddened at the hit and kept his eyes fixed on her, as if only looking could hold her there.

“May is ready to give me up.”

“What! Three days after you’ve entreated her on your knees to hasten your marriage?”

“She’s refused; that gives me the right—”

“Ah, you’ve taught me what an ugly word that is,” she said quietly.

He turned away, weariness flooding him. He felt as if he had been climbing for hours up a sheer face of rock, hands bleeding, breath torn; and now, just as he reached what should have been the top, his hold had broken and he was falling back into darkness. If he could only have taken her again—if he could have drowned her reasons in warmth and closeness—perhaps he might have swept them away. But something in her look and bearing held him off, and his own awe of her sincerity enforced the distance.

At last he began to plead again, stumbling over his desperation.

“If we do this now it will be worse afterward—worse for every one—”

“No—no—no!” she cried, almost screaming, as if his insistence frightened her.

At that moment the bell rang—long, insistent, the sound traveling through the house with a shiver. No carriage had stopped at the door. They stood motionless, staring at each other, startled by the intrusion of that sharp mechanical summons into their strained, breathing silence.

Nastasia crossed the hall; the outer door opened; then she came in with a telegram and handed it to Madame Olenska.

“The lady was very happy at the flowers,” Nastasia said, smoothing her apron with the placid satisfaction of someone reporting that a dish had been well received. “She thought it was her signor marito who had sent them, and she cried a little and said it was a folly.”

Madame Olenska smiled, took the yellow envelope, and went to the lamp. The paper crackled as she tore it open. When Nastasia had withdrawn, she held the telegram out to Newland.

It was dated from St. Augustine and addressed to the Countess Olenska. He read:

“Granny’s telegram successful. Papa and Mamma agree marriage after Easter. Am telegraphing Newland. Am too happy for words and love you dearly. Your grateful May.”

Half an hour later Newland unlocked his own front door. The house smelled of extinguished lamps, of closed rooms and furniture wax—his mother’s world, muffled and sure. On the hall-table, among notes and letters, lay a similar yellow envelope.

Inside was May’s message:

“Parents consent wedding Tuesday after Easter at twelve Grace Church eight bridesmaids please see Rector so happy love May.”

Newland crumpled the telegram as if the violence of his hand could erase the words. Then he pulled out a small pocket diary, turned the pages with trembling fingers, searching for dates as though time were an enemy he might outmaneuver. He did not find at once what he wanted. He jammed the telegram into his pocket and went upstairs.

A light showed under the door of the little hall-room that served Janey as dressing-room and boudoir. He rapped impatiently.

The door opened. His sister stood there in her immemorial purple flannel dressing-gown, her hair “on pins,” her face pale with the hope of bad news—as if misfortune would justify her vigilance.

“Newland! I hope there’s no bad news in that telegram? I waited on purpose, in case—”

He did not answer. Nothing of his correspondence was safe from Janey’s anxious devotion.

“Look here—what day is Easter this year?”

She stared, shocked at such unchristian ignorance.

“Easter? Newland! Why, of course, the first week in April. Why?”

“The first week?” He bent again over his diary, calculating rapidly under his breath. “The first week, did you say?”

He threw back his head and laughed—long, uncontrollable, as if the sound tore itself out of him because silence would have killed him.

“For mercy’s sake what’s the matter?” Janey whispered, frightened now. “Do hush, or you’ll wake Mamma.”

“Nothing’s the matter,” he said, still laughing. “Nothing’s the matter, except that I’m going to be married in a month.”

Janey flung herself upon his neck and pressed him to her purple flannel breast.

“Oh Newland, how wonderful! I’m so glad! But, dearest—why do you keep on laughing?”
