The days that followed brought no word or sign from Willoughby—no letter, no visit, no token of his presence. To Elinor, this silence was not a cause for regret, but rather a quiet confirmation of what she had already feared: that the affection once so freely given had faded, perhaps vanished entirely. She watched her sister closely, sensing the weight of unspoken sorrow pressing down upon Marianne like a shadow.

At last, an invitation arrived for a gathering hosted by Lady Middleton—a social event to which Mrs. Jennings could not attend due to her youngest daughter’s illness. Marianne prepared for the evening with little care, her movements slow and listless, as if she were merely going through the motions of dressing, not expecting joy or comfort from the occasion. Her appearance reflected her inner state: pale, withdrawn, untouched by the usual glow of anticipation. After tea, she sat motionless by the drawing-room fire, lost in thought, oblivious even to Elinor’s presence beside her. It wasn’t until Lady Middleton’s carriage was announced at the door that Marianne stirred—startled, as though she had forgotten the world beyond her own mind.

They arrived at the party on time, stepping out of their carriage into a throng of carriages and guests. The house was brilliantly lit, crowded with people, and stiflingly warm. After offering their polite curtsies to Lady Middleton, they were left to navigate the crush of conversation and heat. Eventually, Lady Middleton seated herself at a game of Cassino, and since Marianne showed no desire to mingle, Elinor managed to secure two chairs nearby. They settled quietly, observing the scene around them.

It was then that Elinor saw him—Willoughby—standing just a few yards away, engaged in earnest conversation with a young woman whose fashionable attire and poised manner marked her as someone of high society. He glanced up and caught Elinor’s eye. He bowed briefly, but made no attempt to approach or speak. His attention remained fixed on the woman beside him, though he could not have failed to notice Marianne sitting only a few feet away.

Elinor turned to her sister, hoping to gauge whether she had seen him. At that moment, Marianne looked up—and froze. A sudden flush swept across her face, her eyes brightening with hope. She rose instinctively, reaching out her hand as if to call him to her. But Elinor seized her arm gently yet firmly.

“Good heavens!” Marianne whispered, trembling. “He is here—he is here! Why doesn’t he look at me? Why won’t he speak?”

“Be still,” Elinor urged, her voice low and urgent. “Do not betray your feelings. Perhaps he has not seen you yet.”

But Elinor knew better. She saw the way his gaze flickered toward them, then quickly away. And when he finally turned again, it was too late. Marianne stood, breathless, calling out his name in a tone thick with emotion, her hand extended.

He stepped forward—not to her, but to Elinor. Addressing her with forced calm, he asked after Mrs. Dashwood, inquired how long they had been in town—his words clipped, hurried, avoiding any direct contact with Marianne. He spoke more to Elinor than to her sister, as though deliberately shielding himself from her gaze.

Elinor was stunned. She could not respond. But Marianne, unable to contain herself, burst forth. “Good God, Willoughby! What does this mean? Have you not received my letters? Will you not shake hands with me?”

He took her hand, but only for a second—his grip fleeting, almost painful. His face betrayed a struggle; his composure wavered. Then, after a pause, he regained control.

“I called at Berkeley Street last Tuesday,” he said steadily. “I was disappointed not to find you at home. I trust my card reached you?”

Marianne gasped. “Have you not received my notes? There must be some terrible mistake! Tell me—what is wrong? For heaven’s sake, tell me!”

He did not answer. His color deepened. He looked flustered, uneasy. But then, catching sight of the young woman he had been speaking with earlier, he straightened himself, composed his features, and replied coolly, “Yes, I received word of your arrival. Thank you for sending it.” With that, he bowed slightly and walked away.

Marianne sank into her chair, her face ashen, her body trembling. Elinor rushed to her side, fetching lavender water to revive her. As soon as she could speak, Marianne clutched at Elinor’s sleeve. “Go to him,” she pleaded. “Force him to come back. I must see him—must speak to him now. I cannot bear this silence. Something dreadful has happened—some terrible misunderstanding. Oh, go to him this instant!”

“How can I?” Elinor whispered. “No, dearest Marianne, you must wait. This is not the place. We will speak tomorrow—when we can be alone.”

But Marianne would not be calmed. She muttered broken phrases under her breath—words of grief, despair, disbelief—each one cutting deeper than the last. Soon, Elinor saw Willoughby leave the room, heading toward the staircase. She told Marianne he was gone, urging her to remain still, to endure until the next day.

But Marianne could not. “Take us home,” she begged. “I cannot stay another minute. I am too miserable.”

Lady Middleton, mid-game, paused without hesitation. Upon hearing Marianne was unwell, she immediately handed off her cards and arranged for the carriage. Within moments, they were on their way back to Berkeley Street.

Not a word passed between them during the journey. Marianne sat silent, her face drawn, her spirit crushed. Only when they reached their rooms and Mrs. Jennings had not yet returned did Elinor dare to ease her sister into bed. Hartshorn brought a slight return of color, and slowly, Marianne drifted into sleep, asking only to be left alone.

Alone in the quiet room, Elinor sat and reflected. She no longer doubted that a serious connection had once existed between Willoughby and Marianne. And now, the coldness of his behavior left no room for denial—he had moved on. Not out of malice, perhaps, but certainly out of indifference. That he had once cared deeply, she could still believe. But that he no longer did—that was undeniable.

And yet, there had been something in his expression—something troubled, ashamed—even as he avoided her sister’s eyes. It suggested guilt, not cruelty. He had not played with her affections lightly; he had changed, perhaps, under pressure or convenience, but not without regret.

For Marianne, the pain was unbearable. The cruel meeting, the unanswered questions, the silence—it all pointed toward a final, irrevocable end. Elinor grieved for her sister, knowing the depth of her suffering. Yet her own heart, though touched by Edward’s absence, held a steadier resolve. She could still honor him, still cherish the bond they shared, even if fate kept them apart. But for Marianne—whose love had been so pure, so passionate—this rejection felt like a wound that might never heal.
