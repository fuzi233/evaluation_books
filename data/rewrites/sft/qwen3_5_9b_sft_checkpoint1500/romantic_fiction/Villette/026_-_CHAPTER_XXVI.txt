My life from this point forward lacked variety; yet I ventured out frequently, fully sanctioned by Madame Beck, who, I noted, held a high esteem for the circle I kept. That capable director had never treated me with anything less than genuine respect, and when she observed that I received invitations from a country estate and a grand hotel, her respect deepened into a form of distinction.

She was not, however, obsequious. Madame Beck possessed a formidable intellect and a keen grasp of worldly mechanics. There was a measured logic to her relentless pursuit of self-interest, and a calm deliberation in her tightest grips for profit. Without exposing herself to the scorn of a flatterer, she displayed a shrewd tact in noting that those associated with her institution should frequent companions who elevated the spirit, rather than those who diminished it. She rarely praised me or my acquaintances directly. However, on one occasion, seated comfortably in the garden bathed in sunlight, a cup of coffee at her side and the *Gazette* in hand, I approached her to request leave for the evening. With a gracious nod, she remarked: "Yes, yes, my dear friend. I grant you leave willingly and freely. Your service in my house has always been admirable, filled with zeal and discretion; you have every right to enjoy yourself. Go wherever you wish. As for your choice of company, I am content; it is wise, dignified, and praiseworthy." Then she closed her lips and returned to her newspaper.

It is not for the reader to take this triviality too seriously, yet around this time, the triple-wrapped packet containing five letters vanished temporarily from my desk. My initial reaction was a blank, paralyzing dismay. Yet, within moments, I rallied my spirits. "Patience!" I whispered to myself. "Say nothing. Wait peacefully. They will return." And they did return. They had merely paid a short visit to Madame Beck's chamber; having passed their scrutiny, they returned duly and truly. I found them all intact the following day.

I wondered what she thought of my correspondence. What estimation did she form of Dr. John Bretton's epistolary powers? How did those pithy, sound, and occasionally original opinions, presented without pretense in a flowing, spirited style, appear to her? How did she perceive that genial, half-humorous vein which brought me such delight? What did she make of the sparse, kind words scattered throughout, not densely like diamonds in Sinbad's valley, but sparingly, lying in unfabled beds? Oh, Madame Beck! How did these things seem to you?

I believe the five letters found favor in Madame Beck's eyes. One day, after she had "borrowed" them from me (speaking of so suave a little woman, one ought to employ suave terms), I caught her examining me with a steady, contemplative gaze. She looked puzzled, yet not malevolent. It was during that brief interval between lessons when the pupils played in the courtyard for fifteen minutes; she and I remained alone in the first class. When our eyes met, her thoughts spilled partially onto her lips.

"There is," she said, "something remarkably notable in the English character."

"How, Madame?"

She gave a small laugh, repeating the word "how" in English.

"I cannot tell you 'how,' but, finally, the English have ideas of their own regarding friendship, love, and everything. But at least, it is not necessary to supervise them," she added, rising and trotting away like a compact little pony.

"Then I hope," I murmured to myself, "you will graciously refrain from reading my letters in the future."

Alas! Something surged into my eyes, blinding me utterly, obscuring the classroom, the garden, and the bright winter sun. As I recalled that those letters, such as she had read, would never reach me again, I realized I had seen the last of them. That noble river, whose banks I had sojourned by, whose waves trickled a few reviving drops to my lips, was turning to another course. It was leaving my little hut and barren field behind, pouring its wealth of waters far away. The change was right, just, natural; no word could alter it. Yet I loved my Rhine, my Nile; I had almost worshipped my Ganges, and I grieved that the grand tide should roll estranged, vanishing like a false mirage. Though stoical, I was not quite a Stoic. Tears streamed rapidly down my hands and across my desk. I wept one hot, brief shower.

Soon, however, I told myself, "The Hope I am mourning suffered greatly and made me suffer much; it did not die until it was time. Following an agony so lingering, death ought to be welcome."

Welcome I endeavored to make it. Long pain had made patience a habit. In the end, I closed the eyes of the dead, covered the face, and composed the limbs with great calm.

Nevertheless, the letters must be put away, out of sight. Those who have undergone bereavement always jealously gather and lock away mementos; it is unbearable to be stabbed to the heart at every moment by the sharp revival of regret.

On a vacant holiday afternoon (Thursday), intending to dispose of my treasure, I went to retrieve it. This time, however, a strong impulse of displeasure seized me. The packet was there, but the ribbon securing it had been untied and retied. Other symptoms indicated that my drawer had been visited.

This was too far. Madame Beck herself was the soul of discretion, possessing a brain as sound and judgment as profound as ever equipped a human head. For her to know the contents of my casket was unpleasant, though bearable. But the idea that she had ventured to communicate this information to others—that she might have amused herself with a companion over documents most sacred to me—shocked me cruelly. Yet, seeing reason to fear this, I even guessed her confidant. Her kinsman, M. Paul Emanuel, had spent the previous evening with her. She was accustomed to consulting him and discussing matters with him that she broached to no one else. That very morning, in class, that gentleman had bestowed upon me a glance he seemed to have borrowed from Vashti, the actress. I had not then comprehended that blue, yet lurid flash in his angry eye, but I read its meaning now. *He*, I believed, was not inclined to view what concerned me fairly, nor to judge me with tolerance or candor. I had always found him severe and suspicious. The thought that these letters, mere friendly missives, had fallen once, and might fall again, into his hands, jarred my very soul.

What should I do to prevent this? In what corner of this strange house could I find security or secrecy? Where could a key be a safeguard, or a padlock a barrier?

The attic (*grenier*)? No, I disliked the *grenier*. Besides, most boxes and drawers there were moldering and unlocked. Rats gnawed through the decayed wood, and mice nested amongst the litter. My dear letters (though "Ichabod" was written on their covers) might be consumed by vermin; certainly, the ink would soon fade into oblivion due to damp. No; the *grenier* would not do—but where then?

While pondering this dilemma, I sat on the dormitory window-seat. It was a fine frosty afternoon; the winter sun, already setting, gleamed pale on the tops of the garden shrubs in the "defended alley." One great old pear-tree—the nun's pear-tree—stood up like a tall, dryad skeleton, grey, gaunt, and stripped. A thought struck me—one of those queer, fantastic thoughts that sometimes strike solitary souls. I put on my bonnet, cloak, and furs, and went out into the city.

Bending my steps toward the old, historical quarter of the town, whose hoary and overshadowed precincts I always sought by instinct in melancholy moods, I wandered street by street. After crossing a half-deserted square, I found myself before a sort of broker's shop; an ancient place, full of ancient things. What I sought was a metal box that could be soldered, or a thick glass jar or bottle that could be stoppered or sealed hermetically. Amongst miscellaneous heaps, I found and purchased the latter article.

I then rolled my letters, wrapped them in oiled silk, bound them with twine, and placed them in the bottle. I instructed the old Jew broker to stopper, seal, and make it air-tight. While obeying my directions, he glanced at me now and then suspiciously from under his frost-white eyelashes. I believe he thought some evil deed was on hand. In all this, I felt a dreary something—not pleasure, but a sad, lonely satisfaction. The impulse under which I acted, the mood controlling me, were similar to those that had induced me to visit the confessional. With quick walking, I regained the pensionnat just as darkness fell, in time for dinner.

At seven o'clock the moon rose. At half-past seven, when the pupils and teachers were studying, and Madame Beck was with her mother and children in the dining hall, when the half-boarders had gone home, and Rosine had left the vestibule, and all was still—I shawled myself, took the sealed jar, and stole out through the first-class door, into the *berceau*, and thence into the "defended alley."
