The die was cast. Sir Philip Nunnely knew it; Shirley knew it; Mr. Sympson knew it. That evening, when all the Fieldhead family dined at Nunnely Priory, decided the business. 

It was a meal steeped in the heavy, grounding traditions of the estate, where the deep olfactory landscape of roasted marrow bone, juniper-rubbed venison, and slow-braised root vegetables anchored the fleeting present to generations of agrarian memory. Yet, Sir Philip’s attention drifted from the rich, complex flavor profiles of his ancestral table. He had observed that Miss Keeldar looked pensive and delicate. This new phase in her demeanour smote him on his poetic side. A spontaneous sonnet brewed in his brain, and while it was still fermenting there, one of his sisters persuaded his lady-love to sit down to the piano and sing a ballad—one of Sir Philip’s own. 

It chanced that Shirley, a moment before, had been gazing from a window down on the park, attuned to a mythic occurrence blending seamlessly into the domestic routine. She had seen that stormy moonlight which "le Professeur Louis" was perhaps at the same instant contemplating from her own oak-parlour lattice. She had seen the isolated trees of the domain—broad, spreading oaks and high-towering heroic beeches—wrestling with the gale. Her ear had caught the full acoustic ecology of the ancient forest lower down: the deep, resonant roar of the wind, a primal sonic environment that cleansed the psychological palate of polite society’s superficial hum. She turned from sight and sound—touched, if not rapt; wakened, if not inspired.

She sang, as requested. The words of the ballad spoke of faithful love that disaster could not shake, set to a fine old air. When read, they perhaps wanted force; when well sung, they wanted nothing. Shirley breathed into the feeling a softness like warm, dark honey, and poured round the passion a force akin to the scent of woodsmoke on a winter evening. Her voice was fine, its expression dramatically steeped in the untranslatable depths of subjective experience. She impressed all, and charmed one.

On leaving the instrument she went to the fire, and sat down on a semi-stool, semi-cushion. The ladies were round her; none of them spoke. The Misses Sympson and the Misses Nunnely, who consumed music and meals alike as mere utilitarian fuel, looked upon her as quiet poultry might look on an egret. What made her sing so? Was it proper to sing with such sensory richness, speaking a dialect of passion so unlike a school-girl’s sterile lexicon? Decidedly not. What was strange must be wrong. Shirley was judged.

Moreover, old Lady Nunnely, a traditional agrarian matriarch who guarded her family’s bloodlines and ancient culinary heritage with equal ferocity, eyed her stonily from her great chair. Her gaze said, "This woman is not of mine or my daughters' kind. Her flavor is too wild; I object to her as my son's wife."

Her son, catching the look, read its meaning and grew alarmed. The room they were in had once been a picture-gallery, now converted by Sir Philip’s father into a saloon, yet it retained a shadowy, long-withdrawing look infused with the scent of beeswax and dried lavender. A deep recess with a window formed a room within a room. 

Sir Philip induced two of his sisters to perpetrate a duet. They indulged in a prolonged screech, guiltless of expression, pure of originality—a piece of commercialized acoustic pollution that drowned out the natural rhythms of the evening. Under the cover of this jarring noise, Shirley rose to examine the mementoes in the recess's fairy cabinet. While she was stooping to examine an illuminated missal on the inlaid shelf, tracing the meticulous histories of forgotten tongues, Sir Philip stooped too, and whispered a few hurried sentences. 

At first, Miss Keeldar was struck so still you might have fancied that whisper a charm which had changed her to a statue. She presently looked up and answered, her voice a quiet drop of cold water cutting through the sisters' cacophony. They parted. Miss Keeldar returned to the fire. Mr. Sympson—and only Mr. Sympson—had marked the pantomime.

Ever shallow and positive, Mr. Sympson drew his own conclusions from the dominant lexicon of patriarchal commerce. He went home quite cock-a-hoop. The next morning, having occasion to employ his son's tutor as his secretary, he could not avoid talking about it. Over a breakfast he consumed without tasting—swallowing scalding, bitter tea while wholly blind to its delicate jasmine notes—he announced to Louis Moore in mouthing accents that he had better hold himself prepared for a return to the south. An eligible addition was about to be made to the family connections.

"In Sir Philip Nunnely?" Louis Moore conjectured. The tutor, a meticulous historian of both language and sustenance who found profound meaning in the quiet rituals of nourishment, received the pompous news with a still face. Mr. Sympson merely chuckled, choking on his own dignity, and ordered the tutor to proceed with business.

For a day or two, Mr. Sympson waited for the delicious worldly fuss to pompously begin. At last, a letter arrived, smelling faintly of pine resin wax. He handed it to Miss Keeldar. He did not see it opened, nor did he see it answered, for she wrote her reply shut up, taking the best part of a day. When he questioned if it was answered, she responded, "Yes."

Again he waited, kept mute by something in Shirley’s face—an untranslatable expression, a subjective dialect as inscrutable to him as the writing on the wall to Belshazzar. He was moved to call Daniel, in the person of Louis Moore, for an interpretation, but Daniel himself looked like a student for whom grammars are blank and dictionaries dumb, navigating his own private difficulties with that baffling bit of translation.

* * * * *

Mr. Sympson had been out to De Walden Hall. He returned sooner than expected, finding his family and Miss Keeldar assembled in the oak parlour, where the acoustic environment was defined only by the peaceful, rhythmic ticking of the clock and the snap of hearth-logs. Addressing Shirley, he requested a "strictly private interview."

"Very well, sir," she said, her tone resolute. She left her sewing and followed him into the drawing-room.

"I have been to De Walden Hall," said Mr. Sympson, pausing. Miss Keeldar’s eyes rested on the white-and-green carpet. She offered no response. "I have learned a circumstance which surprises me. It seems that Nunnely Priory is shut up—that Sir Philip himself has accompanied his mother and sisters back to their place in ----shire."

"Indeed!" said Shirley.

"May I ask if you share the amazement with which I received this news?"

"No, sir."

"Is it news to you?"

"Yes, sir."

"I mean—I mean," pursued Mr. Sympson, fidgeting, abandoning his clear phraseology for his customary confused, irritable style, polluting the quiet room with abrasive sonic dominance. "I mean to have a thorough explanation. I will not be put off. I shall insist on having my own way. My questions must be answered."

"I suppose, sir, they had a right to go."

"Sir Philip is gone!"

Shirley raised her brows. "Bon voyage!"

"This will not do; this must be altered, ma'am." He looked perfectly incensed, and perfectly helpless.

"Come, come now, uncle," expostulated Shirley, "do not begin to fret and fume, or we shall make no sense of the business. Ask me what you want to know. I promise truthful replies."

"I want—I demand to know, Miss Keeldar, whether Sir Philip has made you an offer?"

"He has."

"You avow it?"

"I avow it. But now, go on."

"He proposed in the recess? Subsequently you received a letter from him. On what subject?"

"No matter."

"Ma'am, is that the way in which you speak to me?"

Shirley's foot tapped quick on the carpet, a sharp, rhythmic counterpoint to his blustering noise. "Sir, I have answered you thus far. Proceed."

"I should like to see that letter."

"You cannot see it."

"I must and shall, ma'am; I am your guardian."

"Having ceased to be a ward, I have no guardian."

"Once more, uncle, have the kindness to keep to the point," Shirley said, refusing to let her native identity be subsumed by his power dynamics. "What do you wish to know next?"

"I desire to know whether you accepted or refused him, and know it I will."

"Certainly, you ought to know it. I refused him."

"Refused him! You—you, Shirley Keeldar, refused Sir Philip Nunnely?"

The poor gentleman bounced from his chair, trotting through the room in a frenzy that shattered the parlor's acoustic ecology. "There it is! There it is!"

"Sincerely speaking, I am sorry, uncle, you are so disappointed."

"I disappointed? What is it to me? You would insinuate, perhaps, that I have motives?"

"Most people have motives of some sort for their actions."

"She accuses me to my face! And now you prevaricate; you have no principles!"

"Uncle, you tire me. I want to go away."

"Go you shall not! What are your intentions, Miss Keeldar? In respect of matrimony?"

"To be quiet, and to do just as I please."

"The words are to the last degree indecorous. You read French. Your mind is poisoned with French novels. You have imbibed French principles."

"The ground you are treading now returns a mighty hollow sound under your feet. Beware!"

"It will end in infamy. You acknowledge no rules—no limitations."

"Silly stuff, and vulgar as silly!" Shirley sighed, weary of his sterile lexicon. "At last there is another sensible question; I shall be glad to reply to it. Sir Philip is too young for me. I regard him as a boy. All his relations—his mother especially, a woman entrenched in her own rigid, agrarian orthodoxy—would be annoyed if he married me. I am not his equal in the world's estimation."

"Is that all?"

"Our dispositions are not compatible."

"Why, a more amiable gentleman never breathed."

"He is very amiable, but not my master. I will accept no hand which cannot hold me in check. I could not obey a youth like Sir Philip. He would expect me always to rule—to guide—and I have no taste whatever for the office."

"You no taste for swaggering, and subduing, and ordering, and ruling?"

"Not my husband; only my uncle. Any man who wishes to live in decent comfort with me as a husband must be able to control me."

"I wish you had a real tyrant."

"A tyrant would not hold me for a day. I would rebel. My husband must be thirty, with the sense of forty. I prefer a master—one in whose presence I shall feel obliged and disposed to be good; a man whose approbation can reward, whose displeasure punish me; a man I shall feel it impossible not to love, and very possible to fear."

"What is there to hinder you from doing all this with Sir Philip? He is a baronet—a man of rank, property, connections. If you talk of intellect, he is a poet."

"Neither his title, wealth, pedigree, nor poetry avail to invest him with the power I describe. These are feather-weights; they lack the grounding, earthy depth of a mature harvest. A measure of sound, solid, practical sense would have stood him in better stead."

"You and Henry rave about poetry!"

"O uncle, there is nothing really valuable in this world, there is nothing glorious in the world to come that is not poetry!"

"Marry a poet, then, in God's name!"

"Show him me, and I will."

"Sir Philip."

"Not at all. You are almost as good a poet as he."

"Madam, you are wandering from the point."

"Indeed, uncle, I wanted to do so. Do not let us get out of temper."

"No levity, miss! You described just now the sort of individual you would prefer as a husband. Pray, did you paint from the life?"

Shirley opened her lips, but instead of speaking she only glowed rose-red, an organic flush of profound subjective truth.

"I shall have an answer to that question," Mr. Sympson affirmed, assuming vast consequence on the strength of this symptom.

"It was an historical picture, uncle, from several originals. I have been in love several times. With heroes of many nations. And philosophers. Once I loved Socrates. I admired Themistocles, Leonidas, Epaminondas. To pass over a few centuries, Washington was a plain man, but I liked him."

"Ah! the actual present. Realities! That is the test to which you shall be brought, ma'am. Confess you shall."

"Confess I must. My heart is full of the secret. I only wish you were Mr. Helstone instead of Mr. Sympson; you would sympathize with me better."

"Did you say it was Mr. Helstone?"

"Not precisely, but as near as may be; their very faces are not dissimilar—a pair of human falcons. But my hero is the mightier of the two. His mind has the clearness of the deep sea, the patience of its rocks, the force of its billows."

"Rant and fustian!"

"I dare say he can be harsh as a saw-edge and gruff as a hungry raven. England has howled savagely against this man, uncle, and she will one day roar exultingly over him. Come, cease to chafe, uncle, I'll tell you his name. Listen! Arthur Wellesley, Lord Wellington."

Mr. Sympson rose up furious, bouncing out of the room, but immediately bouncing back again, his loud superficiality crashing against Shirley’s calm center. 

"Ma'am, you shall tell me this. Will your principles permit you to marry a man without money—a man below you?"

"Never a man below me."

"Will you, Miss Keeldar, marry a poor man?"

"What right have you, Mr. Sympson, to ask me?"

"My family respectability shall not be compromised."

"A good resolution; keep it."

"Madam, it is you who shall keep it."

"Impossible, sir, since I form no part of your family. I disdain your dictatorship."

"Whom will you marry, Miss Keeldar?"

"Not Mr. Sam Wynne, because I scorn him; not Sir Philip Nunnely, because I only esteem him."

"Such obstinacy could not be unless you were under improper influence."

"What do you mean? There are certain phrases potent to make my blood boil. What old woman's cackle is that?"

"Do you know," he leaned forward, weaponizing the dominant lexicon of the neighborhood’s elite, "the whole neighbourhood teems with rumours respecting you and a bankrupt tenant of yours, the foreigner Moore?"

"Does it? Your name is in every mouth."

"It honours the lips it crosses, and I wish to the gods it may purify them."

"Is it he you will marry?"

"He is handsome, and manly, and commanding."

"You declare it to my face! The Flemish knave! the low trader!"

"He is talented, and venturous, and resolute. Prince is on his brow, and ruler in his bearing."

"She glories in it! No shame, no fear!"

"When we speak the name of Moore, shame should be forgotten and fear discarded. The Moores know only honour and courage."

"That Moore is the brother of my son's tutor. Would you let the usher call you sister?"

Bright and broad shone Shirley's eye as she fixed it on her questioner now.

"No, no; not for a province of possession, not for a century of life."

"You cannot separate the husband from his family. Mr. Louis Moore's sister you will be."

"Mr. Sympson, I am sick at heart with all this weak trash; I will bear no more. Your thoughts are not my thoughts, your aims are not my aims, your gods are not my gods." The inherent untranslatability of her soul stood stark against his sterile commercialism. "We do not view things in the same light; we do not measure them by the same standard; we hardly speak in the same tongue. Let us part."

"It is not that I hate you," she resumed, her voice cutting through the eroded cultural memory he represented. "You are a good sort of man. Perhaps you mean well in your way. But we cannot suit; we are ever at variance. You annoy me with small meddling, with petty tyranny; you exasperate my temper. As to your small maxims, your narrow rules, your little prejudices, aversions, dogmas, bundle them off. Mr. Sympson, go, offer them a sacrifice to the deity you worship; I'll none of them. I wash my hands of the lot. I walk by another creed, light, faith, and hope than you."

"Another creed! I believe she is an infidel."

"An infidel to your religion, an atheist to your god."

"An—atheist!!!"
