As soon as the last of their visitors had gone down the stair and the street-door shut with its thin, sharp clap, Margaret was already flying up again, snatching bonnet and shawl with the haste of one who runs not toward a diversion but toward a duty that has begun to taste like love. She meant to see how Bessy Higgins bore the day, and to sit with her as long as the household clock would allow before dinner called her back.

Milton’s streets received her with their usual press—close-built brick, jostling shoulders, children’s cries, the perpetual shuffle of feet on stone. Yet the very narrowness had altered since she had learned to care for one life that was lived here. The place no longer seemed merely a passage of grim commerce; it had become a map in which one door mattered more than a hundred shop-fronts, and one upstairs window could hold a whole human world. Above all, there was the sound: the town’s ceaseless undertone, as if the air itself were always being worked—wheels, carts, voices bouncing off walls, and, far behind it, the mill’s dry, persistent thunder. She felt that noise in the bones, and understood, with a new unwilling reverence, what it must be to have it for one’s lullaby and one’s waking bell.

Mary Higgins had made what preparation she could. She had scraped and rough-stoned a wide patch in the middle of the flagged floor until it looked paler, raw, and newly worried, while under the chairs and along the walls the stones lay dark with old unwashed grease and soot, as if the dirt had chosen those safe borders to brood in. Though the day was hot—hot enough that the very window-light seemed thick—there was a great fire burning in the grate, so that the room breathed like an oven. Margaret, pausing a moment in the doorway, thought first with a kind of dismay of Mrs. Hale upstairs and the cool cloths she needed; then she guessed, imperfectly, what Mary meant by it—coal flung on lavishly as a sign of welcome, warmth offered as if warmth could stand in for all the gentler comforts a poor house cannot afford.

Bessy lay on her short sofa under the window, the thin daylight falling on her face and making it seem more transparent than yesterday. She had grown weaker in the space of one night; even her waiting had tired her. Mary said, in her slatternly, half-defensive tone, that Bessy had kept lifting herself up, again and again, to see if it were Miss Hale coming along the street.

Now that Margaret was there, seated close, Bessy sank back with a child’s content, speaking little at first—only looking, looking, as if Margaret’s face were a picture she could rest on. Her hand wandered to Margaret’s sleeve, her bonnet-ribbon, the edge of her shawl, stroking the textures with a wonder that was not envy so much as hungry curiosity, like a palate tasting something it has heard of but never known.

“I never knew why folk i’ th’ Bible cared for soft raiment afore,” Bessy said at last. Her voice was thin, but the words came with a homely certainty. “But it must be nice, to go dressed as yo’ do. It’s different fro’ common. Most fine folk tire my eyes out wi’ their colours; but some how yours rest me. Where did ye get this frock?”

“In London,” Margaret answered, smiling in spite of herself.

“London!” Bessy’s eyes opened wider. “Have yo’ been in London?”

“Yes. I lived there for some years. But my home was in a forest—in the country.”

“Tell me about it,” Bessy whispered, and the longing in her made the small room feel smaller. “I like to hear speak o’ th’ country, and trees, and such like things.”

She lay quite still now, as if arranging her body to receive a draught of coolness by the ear. Her hands folded over her breast. She shut her eyes, not to refuse the world but to make room for another one.

Margaret had not spoken of Helstone—of that name that, in her own thoughts, had become as tender and unhandled as a pressed flower—except in passing. It came to her most vividly at night, when the town’s noises went on without her having to answer them, and memory could steal back to green shade and birds. Yet here, with Bessy’s starving attention, her heart opened as if it had been waiting for precisely such a listener—one to whom “forest” was not a pretty word but a promise.

“Oh, Bessy,” she said, and her voice softened, thickened with pictures, “I loved the home we have left so dearly. I wish you could see it. I cannot tell you half its beauty. There are great trees standing all about it, with their branches stretching long and level, making a deep shade of rest even at noonday. And yet—though every leaf may seem still—there is a continual rushing sound of movement all around, not close at hand. It is as if the forest were breathing in some language too large for one ear to catch at once.

“Then sometimes the turf is as soft and fine as velvet; and sometimes quite lush with the perpetual moisture of a little hidden brook near by, always tinkling—never loud enough to trouble you, only enough to make silence feel alive. And then, in other parts, there are billowy ferns—whole stretches of fern; some in the green shadow; some with long streaks of golden sunlight lying on them—just like the sea.”

“I have never seen th’ sea,” Bessy murmured, as if that was an omission the world ought to be ashamed of. “But go on.”

“Then here and there,” Margaret continued, carried forward by Bessy’s eagerness, “there are wide commons, high up, as if above the very tops of the trees—”

“I am glad o’ that,” Bessy broke in quickly. “I felt smothered like down below. When I have gone for an out, I’ve always wanted to get high up and see far away, and take a deep breath o’ fulness in that air. I get smothered enough in Milton; and I think the sound yo’ speak of among th’ trees, going on for ever and ever, would send me dazed; it’s that made my head ache so i’ th’ mill. Now on these commons, I reckon, there is but little noise?”

“No,” said Margaret. “Nothing but, here and there, a lark high in the air. Sometimes I used to hear a farmer speaking sharp and loud to his servants; but it was so far away that it only reminded me pleasantly that other people were hard at work somewhere in the distance, while I sat on the heather and did nothing.”

Bessy’s lips quivered with something between a smile and a pain.

“I used to think once,” she said slowly, “that if I could have a day o’ doing nothing, to rest me—a day in some quiet place like that yo’ speak on—it would maybe set me up. But now I’ve had many days o’ idleness, and I’m just as weary ’o them as I was o’ my work. Sometimes I’m so tired out I think I cannot enjoy heaven without a piece o’ rest first. I’m rather afeard o’ going straight there without getting a good sleep i’ th’ grave to set me up.”

“Don’t be afraid, Bessy,” Margaret said, and laid her hand over the girl’s hand—so slight beneath it, so hot and dry, as if fever had drunk away all the natural moisture. “God can give you more perfect rest than even idleness on earth, or the dead sleep of the grave.”

Bessy moved uneasily, turning her head as if the very words had stirred a draught through some sore place.

“I wish father would not speak to me as he does,” she said at length, voice sharpening with a sudden edge. “He means well, as I telled yo’ yesterday, and tell yo’ again and again. But yo’ see—though I don’t believe him a bit by day, yet by night—when I’m in a fever, half asleep and half awake—it comes back upon me—oh! so bad!”

She drew breath, and it rasped, thin and catching.

“And I think, if this should be th’ end o’ all, and if all I’ve been born for is just to work my heart and my life away, and to sicken i’ this dree place, wi’ them mill-noises in my ears for ever—until I could scream out for them to stop, and let me have a little piece o’ quiet—and wi’ th’ fluff filling my lungs, until I thirst to death for one long deep breath o’ the clear air yo’ speak on—and my mother gone, and I never able to tell her again how I loved her, and o’ all my troubles—”

Her voice rose, and with it her whole frail body, as if something fierce had leapt up inside her like flame finding draught.

“I think if this life is th’ end, and there’s no God to wipe away all tears from all eyes—yo’ wench, yo’!” She sat upright, and clutched Margaret’s hand with a violence out of all proportion to her weakness, almost as if she meant to hold on by force to anything solid. “I could go mad, and kill yo, I could.”

It was not a threat so much as a cry of terror given teeth. Immediately after, the strength broke. She fell back, spent, panting, her eyes half shut, her face damp with the effort.

Margaret slid down from her chair and knelt by the sofa, close enough that Bessy might feel her steadiness.

“Bessy,” she said, very low, “we have a Father in Heaven.”

“I know it! I know it,” Bessy moaned, turning her head uneasily from side to side as if she wanted to hide from her own words. “I’m very wicked. I’ve spoken very wickedly. Oh! don’t be frightened by me and never come again. I would not harm a hair o’ your head.”

Then she opened her eyes and looked at Margaret with a kind of earnest pride, as if she would set right, at once, what her fever had disordered.

“And,” she added, “I believe, perhaps, more than yo’ do o’ what’s to come. I read th’ book o’ Revelations until I know it off by heart, and I never doubt when I’m waking, and in my senses, of all th’ glory I’m to come to.”

“Don’t let us talk of what fancies come into your head when you are feverish,” Margaret answered, smoothing the coverlet where it was rucked, as one does to quiet both cloth and mind. “I would rather hear something about what you used to do when you were well.”

Bessy’s gaze drifted toward the grate, where the fire made the air tremble.

“I think I was well when mother died,” she said, after a pause. “But I have never been rightly strong sin’ somewhere about that time. I began to work in a carding-room soon after, and th’ fluff got into my lungs, and poisoned me.”

“Fluff?” Margaret asked; the word was so small for so great a ruin.

“Fluff,” repeated Bessy, as if naming an enemy by its proper name gave a kind of mastery. “Little bits, as fly off fro’ th’ cotton when they’re carding it, and fill th’ air till it looks all fine white dust. They say it winds rounds th’ lungs, and tightens ’em up. Anyhow, there’s many a one as works in a carding-room, that falls into a waste, coughing and spitting blood, because they’re just poisoned by th’ fluff.”

Margaret could not help but imagine it—those pale fibres, light as thistledown, drifting into mouths, into throats, into the warm dark of the chest, where they did not dissolve like flour in broth but clung and gathered. She thought of a kitchen sieve shaken too hard, of meal making its own little fog—only this was a fog a body could never sweep away. And outside, the town’s noises went on, each clatter and whirr a proof of work, of profit, of the world’s indifference.

“But can’t it be helped?” Margaret asked.

“I dunno,” Bessy said, with the weary practicality of the poor. “Some folk have a great wheel at one end o’ their carding-rooms to make a draught, and carry off th’ dust; but that wheel costs a deal o’ money—five or six hundred pounds, maybe—and brings in no profit; so it’s but a few o’ th’ masters as will put ’em up. And I’ve heard tell o’ men who didn’t like working in places where there was a wheel, because they said as how it made ’em hungry, at after they’d been long used to swallowing fluff, to go without it, and that their wages ought to be raised if they were to work in such places. So between masters and men th’ wheels fall through. I know I wish there’d been a wheel in our place, though.”

The thought struck Margaret with a bitter irony: that human lungs should be the cheapest filters, that habit should turn even poison into something a man might claim as part of his due. The very language of it—profit, wages, cost—seemed to swallow up the simple fact of breath.

“Did not your father know about it?” Margaret asked.

“Yes,” said Bessy, and there was tenderness in the word, folded in with her complaint. “And he was sorry. But our factory were a good one on the whole; and a steady likely set o’ people; and father was afeard o’ letting me go to a strange place. For though yo’ would na think it now, many a one then used to call me a gradely lass enough.”

A faint, brief colour came into her cheeks at the remembered praise, as if a ghost of her old self had walked through.

“And I did na like to be reckoned nesh and soft. And Mary’s schooling were to be kept up, mother said, and father he were always liking to buy books, and go to lectures o’ one kind and another—all which took money—so I just worked on till I shall ne’er get th’ whirr out o’ my ears, or th’ fluff out o’ my throat i’ this world. That’s all.”

“The whirr out of your ears”—Margaret heard, behind the phrase, the tyranny of sound: noise that does not merely fill the day but lodges in the mind, like a tune you never chose and cannot stop humming. It seemed to her a cruelty almost worse than hunger, to be pursued by a din even in illness, even in memory.

“How old are you?” she asked softly.

“Nineteen, come July.”

“And I too am nineteen,” Margaret said.

For a moment she could not speak after that; the contrast between them lay across her mind like a shadow that would not be shifted. She thought of her own girlhood—rooms that could be kept clean, gardens, books, the gentle silence of trees—and of Bessy’s—fluff and whirr, flags that could never be fully washed, and air that came to her already used. The thought rose in her throat and made it hard to swallow.

“About Mary,” Bessy said suddenly, rousing herself with a purpose that gave her voice new steadiness. “I wanted to ask yo’ to be a friend to her. She’s seventeen, but she’s th’ last on us. And I don’t want her to go to th’ mill, and yet I dunno what she’s fit for.”

Margaret’s glance, unwilling but honest, swept the room: the gritty middle patch Mary had scoured; the untouched corners; the sooty walls; the very way disorder had settled as if it belonged. She felt the weight of what Bessy asked—love trying to purchase a future with no coin but hope.

“She could not do—” Margaret began, and stopped. Then she said, carefully, not wishing to wound. “She could hardly undertake a servant’s place, could she? We have an old faithful servant, almost a friend, who wants help, but who is very particular; and it would not be right to plague her with giving her any assistance that would really be an annoyance and an irritation.”

“No, I see,” Bessy answered, without resentment. She had asked, and the truth had come; she accepted it as she accepted the heat of the fire, the narrowness of the room. “I reckon yo’re right. Our Mary’s a good wench; but who has she had to teach her what to do about a house? No mother, and me at th’ mill till I was good for nothing but scolding her for doing badly what I didn’t know how to do a bit. But I wish she could ha’ lived wi’ yo’, for all that.”

“But even though she may not be exactly fitted to come and live with us as a servant—and I don’t know about that—I will always try to be a friend to her for your sake, Bessy,” Margaret said quickly, leaning closer as if to make the promise warmer than the room’s fierce heat. “And now I must go. I will come again as soon as I can; but if it should not be to-morrow, or the next day, or even a week or a fortnight hence, don’t think I’ve forgotten you. I may be busy.”

“I’ll know yo’ won’t forget me again,” Bessy said, and for a moment she sounded almost like a child being soothed. “I’ll not mistrust yo’ no more. But remember—in a week or a fortnight I may be dead and buried!”

“I’ll come again as soon as I can, Bessy,” Margaret replied, and squeezed her hand hard, as if by pressure she could force life back into it. “But you’ll let me know if you get worse.”

“Ay, that will I,” Bessy said, returning the pressure as much as her strength allowed.

From that day forward Mrs. Hale became more and more a suffering invalid. The year was turning round toward the anniversary of Edith’s marriage, and when Margaret looked back upon that circle of months, the heap of troubles seemed so high she wondered—quietly, without vanity—how they had been borne at all. If she could have foreseen them, she thought, she would have shrunk away as from a bitter draught. Yet each day, taken alone, had been endurable, and sometimes, like a bright herb unexpectedly bruised between the fingers, some small, keen enjoyment had risen fragrant in the very midst of sorrow.

A year ago—when she had first returned to Helstone and first become silently conscious of the querulousness in her mother’s temper—she would have groaned at the thought of a long illness borne in a strange place: a desolate, noisy town where even silence seemed manufactured, where the air was thick, the streets restless, and comforts diminished on every side. But now, with the increase of serious and just ground for complaint, a new kind of patience had sprung up in Mrs. Hale. She grew gentle and quiet in intense bodily suffering, almost in proportion as she had been restless and depressed when there had been no real cause for grief; as if pain, once undeniable, steadied what mere discontent had fretted.

Mr. Hale, meanwhile, was in that stage of apprehension which, in men of his kind, takes the shape of wilful blindness. Margaret’s expressed anxiety irritated him more than she had ever known.

“Indeed, Margaret, you are growing fanciful!” he said, with a forced brightness that did not ring true. “God knows I should be the first to take the alarm if your mother were really ill. We always saw when she had her headaches at Helstone, even without her telling us. She looks quite pale and white when she is ill; and now she has a bright healthy colour in her cheeks, just as she used to have when I first knew her.”

“But papa,” Margaret said, hesitating—because to contradict him was like stepping on a weak plank above deep water—“do you know I think that is the flush of pain.”

“Nonsense, Margaret. I tell you, you are too fanciful. You are the person not well, I think. Send for the doctor to-morrow for yourself; and then, if it will make your mind easier, he can see your mother.”

“Thank you, dear papa. It will make me happier, indeed,” Margaret answered; and she went up to kiss him.

But he put her away—gently, yet unmistakably—like one pushing aside a thought that threatens to become a fear. The motion hurt her more than harshness would have done, because it came from love struggling against truth.

He began to walk uneasily up and down the room. Margaret heard the boards answer his steps with their dry creak; even this small domestic sound seemed part of the town’s larger unrest, as if in Milton nothing could be entirely still.

“Poor Maria!” he said half to himself. “I wish one could do right without sacrificing others. I shall hate this town, and myself too, if she—” He broke off, then turned with sudden questioning. “Pray, Margaret, does your mother often talk to you of the old places: of Helstone, I mean.”

“No, papa,” Margaret said sadly.

“Then, you see, she can’t be fretting after them, eh? It has always been a comfort to me to think that your mother was so simple and open that I knew every little grievance she had. She never would conceal anything seriously affecting her health from me: would she, eh, Margaret? I am quite sure she would not. So don’t let me hear of these foolish morbid ideas. Come, give me a kiss, and run off to bed.”

Margaret obeyed. Yet long after her slow, languid undressing was finished—long after she lay down and tried to command her thoughts into order—she heard him still pacing, up and down, up and down, as Edith and she had once laughingly called it, “racooning.” The sound went on in the darkness, a troubled footfall marking time, as if he could walk away from dread by wearing a path into the floor.
