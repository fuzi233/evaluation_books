Far westward, where Devon and Cornwall clasp hands above the Atlantic, the land breaks suddenly into hidden combes—green cuts in the high table—each opening by a stone throat to the sea. All day they look like peace itself: oak-woods packed rich and brown inland, furze nearer the salt, then short turf, then the black iron cliffs shouldering out into the rollers in jagged castles and spires. In the bottom of each combe lies a strip of meadow, fat as a well-fed cow; a trout stream runs it like a bright seam, winding and doubling as though it cannot make up its mind to quit the hills; and beside it stands the gray mill, with water humming and sparkling round the wet wheel, a sound as old as the place—steady, consoling, domestic.

Yet the same valley that smells of bruised fern and warm hay by day ends at night in a mouth of teeth. At the cove’s edge the rock lies up in shark-tooth ridges, black and sharp, with here and there a pink thread of shell-sand caught in the cracks, and white foam lacing everything as the surge breathes in long, parallel lines toward the west. To landward, soft, sown, and sheltered; to seaward, a waste of roller and reef that gives nothing to the fisherman but danger, and to the shipwrecked nothing at all.

Only one of those mouths will take a boat with any mercy, because a long wall of rock breaks the Atlantic’s shoulders and makes a small lee. That mouth is Marsland—Lucy Passmore’s place, the White Witch’s place; and to Marsland, as Sir Richard Grenville guessed, the Jesuits had bent their secret steps.

But before their coming, two women were already there under an October moon: Rose Salterne, heated with fear and longing, and Lucy Passmore herself, solid as a boundary-stone, sharp as a thorn, and bustling with that kind of courage which is half anger and half mother-wit.

“You be safe enough here to-night, miss,” Lucy said, as if she had hired the very darkness to do her bidding. “My old man’s snoring sound abed; and there’s no other soul sets foot here o’ nights—save it be mermaids now and then. Goodness, Father—where’s our boat? It ought to be up here on the pebbles.”

Rose, drawing her cloak closer against the damp that smelled of kelp and cold iron, pointed down the beach. The boat lay upon a strip of sand nearer the sea, dark as a beetle in moonshine.

“Oh, the lazy old villain!” Lucy scolded, and her voice—high, brisk, all edges—cut clean through the hush of the cove. “He’s been round the rocks after pollock, and never taken the trouble to hale her up. I’ll trounce him for it when I get home. I only hope he’s made her fast, that’s all! He’s more plague to me than ever my money will be. O deary me!”

Down she went, skirts gathered, and Rose after her, stumbling on the round shingle that rattled like poured beans. The sea lay strangely still; no roller broke within the little wall of rock. Even the gulls, which by day screamed above the cliffs like quarrelling fishwives, were asleep on the ledges. It was one of those autumn silences which are not empty at all, but so full of minute sound—drips, far trickles, the faint sifting of sand—that the ear feels it like a pressure.

“Iss, ’tis fast, sure enough,” Lucy muttered, stooping to the rope. “And the oars aboard too! Well, I never! Oh, the lazy thief, to leave they here to be stole! I’ll just sit in the boat, dear, and watch mun, while you go down to the say; for you must be all alone to yourself, you know, or you’ll see nothing. There’s the looking-glass. Now go, and dip your head three times, and mind you don’t look to land or sea before you’ve said the words, and looked upon the glass. Be quick—it’s just upon midnight.”

She coiled her ample body in the stern-sheets like a broody hen in a basket, and Rose went faltering down the last strip of sand between two rock walls. On her left the cliff rose sheer and threw a deep shade; on her right the lower rock took the moon full in the face, and the sea-weed hung from it in purple festoons, wet and glossy, smelling rankly of salt and life. Those streaming fronds shaded cracks and crevices fit, in a girl’s fancy, for anything that had never been christened.

The breeze had died. The cove held its breath. Rose, stripped and shivering, stood a moment with the mirror cold against her palm and the sea’s cold smell in her mouth—metallic and clean, like a knife newly whetted. She listened for some proof that she was not the only living thing in that hollow. A faint bleat, as of a new-dropped lamb, came thinly from above; she started and stared up. Then a wail from the cliffs, like a child in pain, and another answering from the opposite rock. Snipe passing in the dark; an otter calling to her brood—common enough if you had been born to the place and knew its tongues. But Rose did not know the cliff-language; she knew only that the sounds found her heart and pinched it.

Lucy had warned her: fear nothing. If the rite were done duly, no harm could come.

Rose waded in.

The water bit like teeth; she gasped, and the sound seemed loud as a shout in that hush. She went as far as she dared, mirror held up, and then stopped as if a hand had caught her. A ring of pale fire ran round her waist. Each limb, each moving finger, each trembling knee, was bathed in lambent light. The sea itself, stirred by her, kindled into innumerable sparks; little globes of living flame swam and broke and drifted away, and the clear water below her shone as though star-dust had been shaken into it. She could see every shell crawling on the sand at her feet, every rock-fish darting in and out of the crannies and staring up with broad bright eyes. The great brown oar-weed, half seen in the glimmer, waved like long hands beckoning her down into its cold bowers.

For one instant she would have fled—not from witchcraft, but from the simple terror of being made so bright in such a dark place, as if the sea itself had chosen her for its lantern. But she had gone too far to retreat without confessing herself a coward to Lucy, and—worse—to her own superstition.

Hastily she dipped her head three times. Water ran in icy threads down her spine; her teeth clicked. She hurried back to the marge and, through her dripping locks, lifted the mirror to the moonlight and whispered the charm, careful, as she had been told, not to glance to land or sea until the last word was said:

“A maiden pure, here I stand,
Neither on sea, nor yet on land;
Angels watch me on either hand.
If you be landsman, come down the strand;
If you be sailor, come up the sand;
If you be angel, come from the sky,
Look in my glass, and pass me by;
Look in my glass, and go from the shore;
Leave me, but love me for evermore.”

She had scarcely finished; her eyes strained into the mirror, where nothing showed but the sparkle of drops shaken from her own tresses, when the cove’s silence split at once—pebbles rattling under hurried feet, horses slipping and striking sparks from stone.

She darted into a cavern in the higher rock, where sea-weed lay wet as soaked rope and barnacles roughened the walls. There she dragged on her clothes with hands that could scarcely obey her, and crouched, holding her breath, while the men came straight to the boat.

Four of them. Two had leaped from their horses and turned the beasts loose to scramble away; the other two ran the boat down with quick, practised pushes.

Then, out of the stern-sheets, rose Lucy Passmore like a fat ghost in a nightcap, and shrieked in her shrillest treble, loud enough to startle the sleeping gulls:

“Eh! ye villains, ye roogs, what do ye want staling poor folks’ boats by night like this?”

The whole party recoiled, and one, in sheer panic at the sudden apparition, turned to run up the beach shouting, “’Tis a marmaiden—a marmaiden asleep in Willy Passmore’s boat!”

“I wish ’twere any sich good luck,” Rose heard a man whimper—Will Passmore himself. “’Tis my wife, oh dear!” and he cowered as if expecting a blow.

He received it duly. Lucy, leaping out, cuffed him soundly and thundered that he should go home to bed; then planted herself before the strangers with all the belligerence of a goose defending a pond.

Rose, peeping from her crevice, saw what Lucy had seen: among the men was that ungainly Welsh gentleman whose calling she had guessed too clearly already; and Lucy, loyal in her fierce, rude way, held “Popish skulkers” in honest horror—unless paid to tolerate them.

In vain Father Parsons stormed; in vain Campian pleaded with gentle voice and scholar’s Latin; in vain Mr. Leigh’s groom swore and fidgeted and tried to look brave. Lucy stood with hands on hips and eyes like coals.

“No,” she cried. “As I am an honest woman and loyal! This is why you left the boat down to the shoore, you old traitor, you, is it? To help off sich noxious trade as this out of the hands of her majesty’s quorum and rotulorum? Eh? Stand back, cowards! Will you strike a woman?”

The speech, as usual, was invitation rather than warning; for she seized an oar and swung it round and round with a hiss through the air, the wood whistling in the moonlight. The sound of it—clean, circular, merciless—made even armed men step back. At last she brought it down across Father Parsons’s shins with such a crack that he retreated howling, hopping on one foot on the shingle.

“Lucy, Lucy!” shrieked her husband in a Devon falsetto, “be you mazed? Be you mazed, lass? They promised me two gold nobles before I’d lend them the boot!”

“Tu?” Lucy flung at him, the syllable full of scorn as if it were a stone. “And do yu call yourself a man?”

“Tu nobles! tu nobles!” he squealed again, hopping about just out of oar’s reach.

“Tu? And would you sell your soul under ten?”

“Oh, if that is it,” cried Campian, between a laugh and a wince, “give her ten, give her ten, brother Pars—Morgans, I mean; and take care of your shins, Offa Cerbero, you know—Oh, virago! Furens quid faemina possit! Certainly she is some Lamia, some Gorgon, some—”

“Take that, for your Lamys and Gorgons to an honest woman!”

The oar flashed again. Campian’s thin legs were cut from under him; he went down in the shingle, and Lucy, in a fury half comic and half terrible, mounted astride his trunk and cried, breathless:

“Ten nobles, or I’ll kep ye here till morning!”

Ten nobles clinked into her palm at last. The sound of coins—heavy, satisfying, unmistakably real—seemed to thicken the air and bring the whole strange business back from sorcery into bargain. Lucy pocketed them with the swift neatness of a housewife tucking away spice, and in the same breath ordered the boat run down and done with it.

They thrust it into the water. It passed close by Rose’s hiding-place, so near that she could smell the tar and wet wood, and hear water gurgle under the keel. Her heart hammered so loudly she feared it would betray her. Lucy followed them close—not solely for her fee, Rose guessed, but to hold the night in her own hands.

And Lucy, shrewd as a ferret, had seen the dark cavern by the water’s edge and guessed what it sheltered. As the boat moved, she planted her ample body directly across the cavern mouth, grumbling loud enough to cover any movement within—at her husband, at the strangers, and most of all at Mr. Leigh’s groom. To him she prophesied Launceston gaol and the gallows with a relish that made the serving-man’s teeth chatter. He dared not answer her. He dodged and pleaded and kept one of the priests between himself and her, as if the very line of her eye could blight him again.

The boat was launched; the men shoved off. Rose, in the dark behind Lucy’s skirts, could hear the oars bite, the low creak of rowlocks, the faint splash that travelled out into the still cove like a whispered secret.

Then a new sound came—a faint halloo from the beach, and after it the clatter of a horse plunging down the pebbles. In another minute a rider swept along the sand and pulled up so hard that the horse sat back on its haunches. The man dropped rather than leaped from the saddle.

The serving-man, heartened by something he understood at last—steel against steel—drew and sprang upon the newcomer, then recoiled as if struck.

“God forgive me, it’s Mr. Eustace! Oh, dear sir, I took you for one of Sir Richard’s men! Oh, sir, you’re hurt!”

“A scratch—a scratch!” Eustace Leigh almost moaned; and his voice, usually so smooth, was broken, raw, as if the night air had rasped it. “Help me into the boat, Jack. Gentlemen, I must with you.”

“Not with us, surely, my dear son—vagabonds upon the face of the earth?” said Campian, pity softening even his scholar’s jest.

“With you, for ever. All is over here. Whither God and the cause lead—” He staggered toward the boat.

As he passed the cavern, the moonlight struck his face, and Rose saw it—ghastly, bleeding, half bound up with a handkerchief that could not hide the convulsions of rage, shame, and despair twisting it out of all its usual beauty. His eyes glared wildly round; and once, straight into the dark where she crouched.

Their gazes met.

The look was so keen, so dreadful, that Rose, forgetting herself utterly, was on the very edge of shrieking aloud. The night, the charm, the phosphorescent sea—all of it seemed suddenly childish beside that human glance.

“He has overlooked me!” she said within herself, shuddering, remembering his threat.

“Who has wounded you?” asked Campian.

“My cousin—Amyas—and taken the letter!”

“The devil take him, then!” cried Parsons, stamping in fury on the sand.

“Ay, curse him—you may! I dare not!” Eustace panted. “He saved me—sent me here!” And with a groan he tried again to enter the boat.

Lucy Passmore, all bluster a moment before, melted at the sight of blood as any kitchen-woman might soften when a child burns its hand. Her woman’s heart broke through her loyalty and her greed.

“Oh, my dear young gentleman,” she cried, “you must not goo forth with a grane wound like to that. Do ye let me just bind mun up—do ye now!” and she came forward, already fumbling as if for clean linen.

Eustace thrust her back with a violence that was half shame.

“No! better bear it. I deserve it—devils! I deserve it! On board, or we shall all be lost—William Cary is close behind me!”

At that name, the air itself seemed to tighten. The boat was thrust out faster than ever it had moved, oars striking harder, the little splashes suddenly sounding loud as pistol-pricks. In moments it was round the rocks and out of sight.

Only just in time; for, scarcely had the darkness swallowed it when the rattle of horsehoofs came again above.

“That rascal of Mr. Leigh’s will catch it now, the Popish villain!” Lucy said aloud, with a sharp relish that was as much fear as malice. Then, dropping her voice, she bent toward the cavern. “You lie still there, dear life, and settle your sperrits. You’m so safe as ever was rabbit to burrow. I’ll see what happens, if I die for it!”

She squeezed herself up through a cleft to a higher ledge, her skirts scraping rock, and from that perch she could spy down into the meadow where the stream ran—a silver thread, noisy now in Rose’s ears, every little rush and chuckle sounding too clear in the night.

“There mun is!” Lucy whispered fiercely, watching. “In the meadow, trying to catch the horses! There comes Mr. Cary! Goodness, Father, how a rid’th! He’s over wall already! Ron, Jack! ron then! A’ll get to the river! No, a wain’t! Goodness, Father! There’s Mr. Cary cotched mun! A’s down, a’s down!”

“Is he dead?” Rose asked below, hugging her knees, sick with dread.

“Iss, fegs, dead as nits!—No, a bain’t! A’s up now. Suspose he was hit wi’ the flat. Whatever is Mr. Cary tu? Telling wi’ mun, a bit. Oh dear, dear, dear!”

“Has he killed him?” Rose whispered.

“No, fegs, no! Kecking mun, kecking mun, so hard as ever was futeball! Goodness, Father, who did ever? If a haven’t kecked mun right into river, and got on mun’s horse and rod away!”

Down Lucy came again, breathless with the tale, her face shining in moonlight with sweat and excitement.

“And now then, my dear life, us be better to goo hoom and get you sommat warm. You’m mortal cold, I rackon, by now.” She spoke briskly, as if warmth could be summoned by naming it: a fire of furze that crackled clean instead of smoking, a mug of ale with spice if she had it, bread thick-cut, butter salty and firm—anything solid to drive the sea out of a girl’s bones. “I was cruel fear’d for ye; but I kept mun off clever, didn’t I, now?”

“I wish—I wish I had not seen Mr. Leigh’s face!” Rose said. The words tasted bitter as brine.

“Iss, dreadful, weren’t it, poor young soul; a sad night for his poor mother!”

“Lucy, I can’t get his face out of my mind. I’m sure he overlooked me.”

“Oh then! who ever heard the like o’ that?” Lucy answered, half laughing to drive the fear away. “When young gentlemen do overlook young ladies, ’tain’t thikketheor aways, I knoo. Never you think on it.”

“But I can’t help thinking of it,” Rose said, and the stubborn tremble in her voice was worse than tears. The mirror’s cold edge seemed still to press her palm though she no longer held it. “Stop. Shall we go home yet? Where’s that servant?”

“Never mind; he wain’t see us, here under the hill.” Lucy peered into the dark as if she could bully it into giving up its secrets. “I’d much sooner to know where my old man was. I’ve a sort of a forecasting in my inwards, like, as I always has when aught’s gwain to happen—as though I shuldn’t zee mun again, like, I have, miss. Well—he was a bedient old soul, after all, he was.” Then, with sudden practical indignation: “Goodness, Father! and all this while us have forgot the very thing us come about! Who did you see?”

“Only that face,” Rose answered, shivering as the memory seized her again.

“Not in the glass, maid? Say then—not in the glass?”

“Would to heaven it had been! Lucy, what if he were the man I was fated to—”

“He?” Lucy barked, contempt returning as a shield. “Why, he’s a praste—a Popish praste—that can’t marry if he would, poor wratch.”

“He is none; and I have cause enough to know it!”

And because there was no other soul in the world to whom she could pour such a story at that hour, Rose—still damp, still shaken, still smelling the sea’s coldness on her skin—told Lucy everything of yesterday’s meeting: the threat, the uncanny sense of being seen through and through, the weight of another’s will pressing on her like a hand.

Lucy listened with the greedy patience of one who takes in a tale as she might take in a stew—tasting for what is meat, what is bone, what is spice, and what might poison.

“He’s a pretty wooer!” she said at last, with a snort. “Be a brave maid, then—be a brave maid—and never terrify yourself with his unlucky face. It’s because there was none here worthy of ye, that ye seed none in glass. Maybe he’s to be a foreigner, from over seas, and that’s why his sperit was so long a coming. A duke, or a prince to the least, I’ll warrant, he’ll be, that carries off the Rose of Bideford.”

But flattery, like sugar on bitter herbs, could not change the taste. Rose could not wipe that fierce face from her eyes. The combe’s sounds—the mill-hum she knew in childhood, the stream’s bright chatter, the soft drag of weed on rock, and, worst of all, the remembered whistle of Lucy’s oar—went with her like a strange music, half natural and half bewitched.

She reached home safely and crept to bed undiscovered. And when morning came, as morning would, it found her laid up with something very like a fever—born of cold sea-water, terror, and excitement. The phantom only grew stronger in her mind. She used all her woman’s tact and self-restraint to keep from betraying, by look or exclamation, what she had dared that night in Marsland cove, where the sea had burned around her like fire and men’s feet had sounded like fate upon the stones.

After a fortnight’s weakness she recovered and returned to Bideford. But before she came there, Amyas was far across the seas on his way to Milford Haven, as shall be told in the chapters that follow.
