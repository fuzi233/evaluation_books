Until the rival ships lunged at us in the mouth of the gulf, I had worn my own importance like a loose cloak—useful for warmth, not for show. A man trained to the priesthood learns early to keep his stomach quiet and his mind quieter: the Gods turn the wheel; we are only spokes. But those enemy galleys did not fight like men guarding a frontier. They fought like men hunting a single quarry, snapping oars, burning pitch, dying gladly, if only I might be taken living. That kind of appetite in an enemy is the surest seasoning a fate can have. Even the dullest palate reads it.

Phorenice, it seemed, had scouts as keen as her enemies were hungry. The arm of sea that leads to Atlantis is a twisting thing, narrow as a river where old fire-mountains have spilled themselves into stone, broad as a lake where the shores blur into heat-haze. A galley must follow every bend, and every bend costs time; but a runner with strong lungs can cut across flats and reeking swamps, pick his way between smoking hills, and—if he does not get taken by a fire-stream, a sudden flood, or some wandering beast—reach the capital far ahead of any ship.

Phorenice was no miser with messengers. They said later she flung twenty couriers from the headland like seed on the wind, each with his own route and his own prayers. Seven came in clean and whole, and three more were known to have survived. At the time we knew none of it. We drove on thinking ourselves unheralded.

The slaves on the row-banks were, for the most part, European savages: thick-bodied, raw-skinned men with old fear baked into them. The stink of them hung under the deck-planks and seeped up through every seam—sour sweat, rancid fat, the sweetish taint of unwashed sores. Add to it the wind that carried fine grit from an erupting mountain—ash like flour but sharp as ground glass—and what could have been a clean, bright voyage became a choking, gritty mouthful you could not spit out. We were eager to be done with it.

And if I am to confess a weakness without dishonour, I had something else burning in me besides impatience: curiosity, vast and very human, about how Atlantis would take me. A formal prisoner? A quiet nobody, to be summoned later? Some ceremonial welcome I could rehearse in my head, like a litany? I guessed at many shapes for my reception, but the true one never came to me.

At daybreak one morning, the sacred mountain showed its peak, a permanent bruise of fire on the horizon behind the city. The boatswains’ whips cracked harder, and the men on the benches—those offensive, panting bodies—gave us a last, ragged spurt. The wind was dead against us; no sail could be trusted. But the oars bit, and with each throb the city lifted itself higher from the water: first the pyramid-tips like stone teeth, then the taller towers, then the long white line of the harbour walls.

Twenty years is enough for a man to forget the exact feel of his childhood street under bare feet, yet the sight of Atlantis brought it all back as if the stones had remembered me. I picked out the temple where I had first been admitted to manhood, the pyramid where I had been given the lesser mysteries; and then, when lesser things sharpened into their proper outlines, I saw the house where my father and mother had reared me. My eyes blurred, and for a moment the city swam like a reflection.

We lay to, as law demands, outside the harbour’s white walls. The row-slaves sagged over their oar-looms and sobbed softly, their breath making small wet noises in the heat. Most captains must wait here while the port-captain measures his dignity by another man’s impatience. But this time there was no waiting at all.

A signal rang from the two entrance castles. The chain that hung between them dropped with a metallic slither, and a ten-oared boat shot out as though it had been held in a clenched fist and released. It came alongside so fast the wake slapped our hull.

“That should be Dason’s galley?” the port-captain called.

“It was,” Tob answered. He had the helmsman’s stance—feet planted, shoulders easy, eyes awake as a hunting dog’s.

“Oh, I saw Dason’s head on your beak,” said the port-captain. “You were Tatho’s captain?”

“And am still. Tatho’s fleet was sent by Dason and his friends to the sea-floor. We took this stinking galley to finish the voyage in, seeing it was the only craft left afloat.”

The port-captain’s eyes travelled over those of us on the after-deck. His gaze had the quick, nervous darting of a man who lives too close to punishment.

“I fear, captain, you’ll have but a dangerous reception. I do not see my Lord Deucalion. Or does he come with some other navy? Gods, captain—if you have let him get killed whilst under your charge, the Empress will have the skin torn slowly off you living.”

“What with Phorenice and Tatho both so curious for his welfare,” Tob said dryly, “my Lord Deucalion seems but a dangerous passenger. But I shall save my hide this voyage.”

He jerked his thumb at me. “He’s there to put in a word for me himself.”

The port-captain stared as if he had been told that a hearth-stone spoke. Then his face rearranged itself into ceremony. He bowed until his spine looked in danger.

“I trust my lord, in his infinite strength, will pardon my sin in not knowing him by his nobleness before. But truth to tell, I had looked to see my lord more suitably apparelled.”

“Pish,” I said. “If I choose to dress simply, I cannot object to being mistaken for a simple man. It is not my pleasure to advertise my quality by gauds. If you think amends are due, I pray of your charity that this inquisition may end.”

He became a machine of bows. “I am the humblest of my lord’s servants. It will be my exceeding honour to pilot my lord’s galley into the berth appointed.”

His boat leapt ahead; our slaves heaved the oars back into stroke. Tob stood by the helm with a dry smile as the harbour mouth widened before us.

“Well,” I said, indulging his mood, “what is it?”

“I’m thinking,” Tob said, “that my Lord Deucalion will remember me only as a very rude fellow when he steps ashore amongst all this fine gentility.”

“You don’t think anything of the kind.”

“Then I must prove my refinement,” he said, “and not contradict.”

He took my hand in his huge, hard fist—callused like old leather—and pressed it once, a sailor’s pledge, plain as salt. “By the Gods, Deucalion, you may be a great prince, but I’ve only known you as a man. You’re the finest fighter of beasts and men that walks this world to-day, and I love you for it. That spear-stroke of yours on the lizard is a thing the singers in the taverns shall make chaunts about.”

We passed between the entrance castles. Soldiers on the parapets blew trumpets in welcome, and the sound struck the stone and came back doubled, a bright brassy ringing that made the air feel crowded. The port-captain had run up my banner to his masthead, as though it had been waiting folded for this very hour, and across the wide basin of the harbour I heard musicians answering—pipes and drums—while welcoming fires sent up pale smoke that smelled of resin and sweet oil. Even in sunlight the flame-glow made a trembling veil above the square.

We were taken—no choice given—straight to the royal quay and made fast to posts and rings of gold, sacred to the reigning house. The rope in my hands felt suddenly like a poor thing: hemp against sanctity.

“If Dason could only have foreseen this honour,” Tob muttered with his grisly humour, “I’m sure he’d have laid in a silken warp to make fast on the bollards instead of mere plebeian hemp. I’m sure there’d be a frown on Dason’s head this minute, if the sun hadn’t scorched it stiff. My Lord Deucalion, will you pick your way with niceness over this common ship and tread on the genteel carpet they’ve spread for you yonder?”

The port-captain heard him and went white with horror, as if Tob’s words were a knife drawn in a temple. I felt, with a small inward sigh, how quickly colonial freedom curdles into offence in the air of a court. Atlantis had always loved ceremony, but this Atlantis wore it like armour.

A pavilion of crimson cloth had been erected to shelter me until the Empress should arrive. I stepped into it because refusing would have been a kind of childishness. But my complaisance ended there.

Three chamberlains, dazzling with goldwork and stiff with practice, presented changes of raiment—robes heavy with embroidery, sashes studded, ornaments that clinked and flashed. They would have had me dressed like a sacred bird for a festival.

I refused curtly. When one of them stammered that it was the Empress’s taste, I asked him plainly if he had any definite command from her mightiness on this paltry matter.

He had none.

“Then hear me,” I said. “Phorenice commanded Deucalion, the man, to attend before her, and sent no word concerning his outer casing. This dress suits my temper. It keeps the heat and wind from my skin, and—more than can be said for many things in this city, I suspect—it is clean. It seems to me, sirs, that your interfering savours somewhat of impertinence.”

With one accord they drew their swords and pushed the hilts toward me, faces set in a fearful sincerity.

“It would be a favour,” said their spokesman, “if the great Lord Deucalion would take his vengeance now, instead of delivering us to the tormentors hereafter.”

“Poof,” I said. “The matter is forgotten. You make too much of a little.”

But their gesture showed me a new texture to the air of Atlantis: fear. Not the fear of battle, which is honest and smells of sweat and iron, but the fear of instruments and rooms where screams are counted like coin. A city where men offer their throats over the cut of a coat is a city changed.

There was a bath in the pavilion. I took it gladly, though someone had laced the water with scent—some syrupy flower-sweetness that clung to the nostrils and made the clean water feel half-spoiled, as if a cook had scattered perfume over good broth. Still, it washed away salt and ash-grit. I sat afterward and waited, outwardly composed.

The chamberlains stood like statues. I offered no small talk; they did not break my calm. Outside, the music blared—pipes shrilling, drums thudding—while the welcoming fires sent their odours in waves: resin, oil, something like burned cedar. Over all, the sun hammered on the scarlet roof so that the cloth glowed hot, and the air inside tasted of warmed dye.

Some say the great should gather dignity by making lesser folk wait. I have always thought that trick paltry. Phorenice seemed to share my distaste. As she later told me, the moment Tob’s galley was reported moored to the royal quay, she left the palace. The procession was already marshalled—bedecked, aligned, rehearsed—waiting only for its chief ornament. When she mounted, the trumpets ordered the advance.

From the pavilion doorway I watched the head of the concourse emerge from the broad street. First came soldiery in ordered lines, crossing the square to salute, then ranging to the far side to make space. Then the Mariners’ Guild—men who smelled of tar and rope even at a distance—then more soldiers. After them the merchants, the tanners, the spear-makers, and all the acknowledged Guilds, wearing deliberate finery as though each trade had dressed itself for its own legend. Some walked; some rode beasts trained to carry them with dull patience.

And then the spectacle split open and showed its two wonders.

From the shadow between the houses swung a beast so vast it made the square seem suddenly smaller: a bull mammoth, huge even among its kind. I had hunted mammoths when they raided villages and trampled cornlands—had seen them shaggy and monstrous in wild ground, hotter in rage than cave-tiger or cave-bear, the most dangerous of beasts that dispute dominion with man save only a few greater lizards. I had killed them, yes, but always at risk and always with men around me.

This one walked like a disciplined slave. On its back sat a half-castle of gold stamped with the outstretched hand and decked with silver snakes. Its tusks were gilded; flowers were tangled into its hairy neck; and it moved in procession as if pageantry were its natural pasture. Savage power held in a leash—there was no clearer emblem of a ruler’s will.

And at the same moment, as if the mammoth were merely her platform, came the greater wonder: Phorenice.

At first the beast held my eye by sheer uncouth hugeness. But the woman within the golden half-castle drew my gaze away and kept it. When the crowd shouted at her approach, I rose and waited at the pavilion’s porch until the mammoth halted in the centre of the square. Then I walked out across the bare pavement toward her.

“On your knees, my lord,” whispered one chamberlain behind me, terror in his breath.

“At least with bent head,” urged another.

I had my own measure for what a man owes to his own self-respect. I went with head erect, giving her gaze for gaze.

She was summing me. I was doing the same. Those few seconds showed me a kind of woman I had not imagined living. I had always said women had no influence over me; that is true in the crude way men mean it. But I had watched their power over others as carefully as I watched a river’s current, for it shapes policy all the same. Still, this woman baffled me. I could read not one syllable of her thoughts.

Her body was slight, supple, beautifully made; her figure rather small. Her face was a perfect book of cleverness—and yet she was fair beyond belief. Her hair was a lovely ruddiness, cut short in the new fashion and bunching on her shoulders. And her eyes—no tint can hold them. They were like deep water that reflects sky one moment and shows black depth the next.

She nodded slightly as I drew near. When I did the obeisance permitted to my rank, she spoke in a voice clear enough that all near could drink the words.

“Never put forehead on the ground again on my behalf so long as I rule in Atlantis. For others it is fitting—once, twice, or several times, according to rank and station—for I am Empress and they are beneath me. But you are Deucalion, my lord. Till to-day I knew you only from pictures drawn with tongues. I have seen you now and judged for myself. And so I decree: Deucalion is above all other men in Atlantis. If there is one who does not render him obedience, that man is enemy also of Phorenice and shall feel her anger.”

A sign from her, and a stair was brought. She called to me. I mounted and sat beside her in the golden half-castle under the canopy of royal snakes. Behind us a girl attendant fanned with perfumed feathers. The scent was heavy—sweet, almost cloying—like honey left too long in the sun. At Phorenice’s word the mammoth turned, bearing us back toward the royal pyramid by the street we had come. Soldiers and guilds fell into order before and behind. I noted, with a soldier’s eye, heavily armed troops marching close on either flank of the mammoth, a living wall.

Phorenice turned to me with a smile that was sharp at the edges.

“You piqued me at first,” she said.

“Your Majesty overwhelms me with so much notice.”

“You looked at my steed before you looked at me. A woman finds it hard to forgive a slight like that.”

“I envied you the greatest of your conquests,” I said, “and do still. I have fought mammoths and killed at times, but I never dared even to think of taking one alive and bringing it into tameness.”

“You speak boldly,” she said, still smiling, “and yet you can turn a pretty compliment. Faugh, Deucalion—the way these people fawn on me gives me a nausea. I am not of the same clay as they are, I know; but because I am the daughter of Gods they must feed me on the pap of insincerity.”

So Tatho had been right. The swineherd fiction held; it had been swallowed whole and digested into doctrine. If she wished to live inside the story she had made, it was not my place to tear the cloth. Rightly or wrongly, I was her servant.

“I have been pining long enough for stronger meat than they can give,” she went on. “At last I have sent for you. I have been at some pains to procure my tongue-pictures of you, Deucalion, and though you do not know me yet, I may say I knew you with thoroughness even before we met. I can admire a man with a mind great enough to forego silly gauds of clothes, or excesses of feasts, or the pamperings of women.”

She glanced down at her own silks and glittering jewels, as if amused by her own contradiction.

“We women like to carry colours upon our persons, but that is a different matter. And so I sent for you here to be my minister and bear with me the burden of ruling.”

“There should be better men in broad Atlantis.”

“There are not,” she said, and the certainty in her tone made it sound like an inventory already taken. “I know them all by heart. They are enamoured of my poor person; they weary me with empty phrases and importunities. Though they cry service, their own advancement and their own treasuries come first. So I have sent for you, Deucalion—the one strong man in all the world. You at least will not sigh to be my lover?”

She watched me from the corner of her eyes, measuring, weighing.

“The Empress is my mistress,” I said. “I will be an honest minister to her. With Phorenice the woman, it is likely I shall have little enough to do. Besides, I am not the sort that sports with this toy they call love.”

“And yet you are a personable man enough,” she said thoughtfully. “That still further proves your strength, Deucalion. You at least will not lose your head through weak infatuation for my poor looks and graces.”

She turned her head slightly. “Ylga, fan not so violently.”

Our talk broke then, and I let my eyes roam as we moved through the chief street of the fairest city in the world. I had been gone a score of years and expected to see change as one expects a grown child’s face to have lengthened. In public buildings, Atlantis had indeed grown: new temples, new pyramids, new palaces, statues everywhere, stone and bronze and gold catching sun so that the street seemed sprinkled with hard light.

Yet the sharper shock was not the splendour but what crouched beside it.

The throngs lining the street showed gaunt bodies and hungry faces. Here and there stood men and women naked as European savages, but without even the savage’s fierce pride—only a dulled shame, or no shame at all, as if hunger had eaten that too. Even the traders, wearing trumpery gauds to ape the fashion of display, had a hunted look: eyes too quick, mouths tight, as though the word “safety” had slipped out of their language and left no synonym behind. The city’s music rolled over them—pipes and drums and trumpets—but beneath it I caught other sounds: a thin coughing, the moan of a child, the restless scuff of too many bare feet on hot stone. A city’s health lives in its noises as much as in its walls; this one had a fever-hum under the ceremonial clangour.

Phorenice followed the direction of my gaze.

“The season has been unhealthy of recent months,” she said. “These lower people will not build fine houses to adorn my city, and because they choose to live on in squalid, unsightly kennels, there have been calentures and other sicknesses amongst them, which make them disinclined for work. And then, too, for the moment, earning is not easy. Trade is nearly stopped this last half-year, since the rebels have been hammering so lustily at my city gates.”

I was startled clean out of decorum.

“Rebels!” I cried. “Who are hammering at the gates of Atlantis? Is the city in a state of siege?”

“Of their condescension,” Phorenice said lightly, “they are giving us holiday to-day, and so my welcome to you comes undisturbed. If they were fighting, your ears would have told you. They are noisy enough in all their efforts.”

As she said it, the street’s festival-sounds struck me newly: trumpets, drums, cheering—so much chosen noise laid over the quieter, truer sounds of want. It was like putting a bright sauce over meat that had begun to turn.

“My spies say they are making ready new engines for use against the walls,” she went on, “which you may sally out to-morrow and break if it gives you amusement. But for to-day, Deucalion, I have you, and you have me, and there is peace round us, and some prettiness of display. If you ask for more I will give it you.”

“I did not know of this rebellion,” I said, forcing my voice back into its proper harness. “But as Your Majesty has made me your minister, it is well I should know at once its scope. This is a matter we should be serious upon.”

“And do you think I cannot take it seriously also?” she snapped, and for the first time a crack showed in the smooth glaze of her amusement.

“Ylga,” she said to the girl behind us, “set loose my dress at the shoulder.”

The attendant’s hands went to the jewelled clasp. She obeyed, but with ill grace that showed in the stiffness of her fingers, in the way she did not quite hide her reluctance. Phorenice stripped down the fabric herself, baring the pure skin beneath and showing, just below the curve of the left breast, a bandage of bloodstained linen.

“There is a guarantee of my seriousness yesterday, at any rate,” she said, looking at me sidelong. “The arrow struck on a rib and that saved me. If it had struck between, Deucalion would have been standing beside my funeral pyre to-day instead of riding on this pretty steed of mine which he admires so much.”

Her voice took on mockery again, but the blood on the linen was not mockery, and the smell of it—faint iron under perfume—cut through the sweet oils like truth.

“Your eye seems to feast itself most on the mammoth, Deucalion. Ah, poor me. I am not one of your shaggy creatures, and so it seems I shall never be able to catch your regard.”

She turned her head back. “Ylga, you may link my dress up again with its clasp. My Lord Deucalion has seen wounds before, and there is nothing else here to interest him.”
