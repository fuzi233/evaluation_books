For several days, Tito saw little of Romola. The morning after the library’s fate was decided, he approached her gently, telling her it would be best if she removed any personal belongings from the room, as agents were soon to arrive to pack up the antiquities. Leaning forward, he kissed her brow and suggested she remain in her own chamber—where the small painted tabernacle stood, where she now sat—so that she might avoid the commotion of strangers’ footsteps. She agreed quietly, showing no sign of distress. Yet the night had been one long vigil for her; though her body remained strong, sensation had dulled into a persistent ache, as though she were numb and bruised from within.

Tito sensed her illness but dared not press further. When he noticed her hand and forehead were cold as stone, he fetched a soft fur-lined mantle and draped it lightly over her shoulders. Each time he returned, the scene remained much the same: he offered small acts of kindness or tender words, hoping to draw her back, but she seemed unable to speak, or even meet his gaze. “Patience,” he murmured to himself. “She will recover. She will forgive. Our bond must still be stronger than all this.”

It is easy for one who strikes to shift into the role of the wronged when the wounded remains silent and still. The striker feels no pain, only the weight of his own restraint, and begins to believe his forbearance a virtue. But Tito was not prone to such self-pity. His nature inclined toward reconciliation, and he would have endured much just to feel Romola’s hand rest once more upon his head—as it had done that first morning, when he had recoiled from her eyes.

Yet patience came easier now, for life beyond the walls of their home stirred with new purpose. A course of action shaped by deep character often traces its beginning to a single moment—a spark that ignites a flame. For Tito, that moment had come in the Piazza del Duomo, when mounted on bales, he tasted a sharp pleasure in knowing he could charm men with mere words. From then on, his mind turned increasingly toward political life—toward the kind of influence Florence’s turbulent times might demand.

But fear of Baldassarre had risen at the same instant, like a rock blocking his path. That dread had driven him to sell the library—an act meant to prepare for flight should necessity arise—just as Florence began to stir anew in his imagination. Now, however, that fear was nearly gone. He would still wear his armor, guard his wits, and ready himself for whatever trials might come—but he no longer felt compelled to leave Florence behind. His father had refused the offer of reconciliation, forcing Tito into defiance. And an old man, far from home, memory fading, was too weak to resist.

Now, Tito’s quiet desires unfolded into clear resolve. As the fervor of youth softened, life took on the shape of a game—part skill, part chance, and deeply engaging. And Florence promised a game both swift and thrilling: one of revolution, faction, and hidden maneuvering. It was a world where brilliance could thrive without burden—where loyalty to truth was replaced by loyalty to advantage, and where the only creed needed was that “ginger is hot in the mouth.”

No sooner had the French guests departed than Florence trembled with unrest—like an ant colony freed from shadow, scrambling to rebuild its nest. Questions flooded the air: How would they pay the king? How would they fight the stubborn rebels of Pisa? Above all, how could they reshape their government so that magistrates were chosen wisely, laws passed fairly?

Until these were answered, trade faltered, and the vast number of workers—those not counted citizens, denied even a vote—grew restless. Something had to be done.

So the great bell rang out, summoning the people to a parliament in the Piazza de’ Signori. The crowd gathered thickly, hemmed in by armed guards at every exit. Then the Signoria—the Gonfaloniere and eight Priors—appeared before the stone lion on the platform of the Old Palace. They proposed that twenty leading citizens be granted dictatorial power for one year: authority to appoint all magistrates and restructure the state. The people roared their approval, declaring themselves the electors of the Twenty.

This form of “parliament” was ancient Florentine tradition—a way to make the will of the few appear the choice of the many. The shouting died quickly, but debate raged inside the palace. Should Florence adopt a Great Council, Venetian-style, where all officials and laws were chosen by a broad assembly of qualified citizens—regardless of rank or party? Or should governance remain narrower, preserving the influence of noble families, diluted less by shopkeepers and artisans?

Doctors of law argued day and night. Messer Pagolantonio Soderini championed the popular model with eloquent reason. Messer Guidantonio Vespucci countered with equal strength for a more aristocratic order. It was a debate over boiled versus roasted meat—already decided by taste, with arguments merely prolonging the delay.

Inside the palace, those in power favored Vespucci’s caution. Change should be measured. Outside, the masses, aware of their lack of voice and their grievances, feared change less—and desired it more.

And there was a growing force outside the palace shaping these vague desires into something firm and decisive: the preaching of Savonarola.

Driven by spiritual duty and aided by politicians who could not pass laws without his support, Fra Girolamo moved swiftly in his sermons—from general exhortations to abandon private passions for the public good—to specific commands: “Choose the Great Council!” “The Great Council is God’s will!”

To Savonarola, these were not separate truths—they were one. The Great Council alone could give voice to the people in a way powerful enough to overcome partisan corruption. It made honest governance possible. And the purer Florence’s government became—the safer it was from men who profited from moral decay—the closer the city would come to becoming a true community, worthy of guiding the Church and the world toward renewal.

Savonarola’s vision never stopped at politics. Every effort served a higher end, grand and moral. He harbored no petty grudges, sought no personal gain. Even in his final hours—when shame, torture, and terror laid bare every secret of his soul—he told his tormentors: “Do not wonder that I have spoken so little. My purposes were few—and great.”[1]
