Dawn broke bright over our island camp, and my four boys and I hauled our bamboo-laden cart out at first light, bound for the half-planted avenue of fruit saplings we’d laid out months prior. We left the buffalo behind: the gash in his nostril still had not fully scabbed over, and I wanted no risk of reopening it before he was fit for hard work.

We could not have arrived a minute later. The last unseasonal gale had torn through the grove overnight: half our young trees lay flat on the mud, roots half ripped out, the rest bowed so far over their crowns nearly brushed the ground, with only a handful still standing straight. We knelt in the muck, dug deep around each toppled root, and drove stout bamboo stakes deep into the earth, lashing every wobbly sapling firm to its new support with tough, broad island fibre.

“Papa,” small Franz said, passing me a fresh length of twine as I worked, “are these wild trees, or tame?”
“These are the fiercest, most ferocious trees in the whole forest,” Jack cackled, not missing a chance to tease his little brother. “We’re tying them up so they don’t run off. Soon we’ll loosen the ropes a little, and they’ll trot after us like pets, dropping peaches and pears right at our feet! We’ll even put a nose ring through their trunks, just like the buffalo!”
“That’s silly,” Franz said, frowning very seriously. “I know wild trees grow in the woods, the sour little crabapples, and tame ones grow in gardens, like the pear and peach trees we had back in Switzerland. So which are these?”

“Neither fully wild, nor naturally tame,” I told him, wiping mud off my hands. “They’re grafted trees. No European fruit tree will grow good, sweet fruit on its own, not until you graft it.” When he blinked, confused at the new word, I hurried to explain: “Grafting means you snip a small living twig from a good fruit tree, and tuck it into a little notch, or eye, cut into the branch of a hardier wild tree. The two grow together, and the branch that sprouts bears fruit from the twig you brought, not the sour wild stock. If we took a twig from a fine sweet Ribston pippin apple tree, and tucked it into a wild crabapple’s notch, in two years that branch would hang heavy with perfect, juicy apples.”

“But if no good fruit trees grow on their own where we live,” Ernest put in, curious as always, “where did the very first slips of good fruit come from?”
“From far away,” I smiled. “Only the cold, temperate parts of the world require that trick. South of us, in warm tropical lands, luscious fruit grows wild all on its own, no tending needed. Back when the Romans invaded Britain, all they found growing wild was sour crabapples, hazelnuts, and brambles. They brought grafting slips from across the empire, and suddenly Britain had sweet apples, filberts, raspberries. Even our own Switzerland! Not a single one of our native fruit trees grew there naturally. Cherries? They come from Cerasus, a land in old Asia Minor, brought to Europe by the Roman general Lucullus 70 years before the birth of Christ. Walnuts came from Persia, grapes are even older—you remember the Bible tells us Noah planted the very first vineyard after the flood.”

We talked as we worked, until the last wobbly sapling was lashed tight to its bamboo stake, and by the time we trundled back to Falconhurst, our appetites were so ravenous even the fussiest gourmet would have stared. My wife nearly gasped at the way we fell on the plates of corned beef and buttery boiled palm cabbage she set out, and one by one we collapsed back on our mats to digest, brainstorming our next round of big camp projects.

“I have one very specific request,” she said, once we were all full. “That treehouse nest up in the great banyan is perfect, I wouldn’t change a thing about it. But I am sick of scaling that rickety wobbly rope ladder every single time to get up there. Can we build proper stairs instead?”

I thought hard for a minute. “Building stairs bolted to the outside of the trunk would be impossible, but—what if we built them inside the trunk? I swear that massive old banyan is hollow, at least part of the way. Didn’t you tell me a few days ago you saw honeybees buzzing in and out of a small hole halfway up?”
“I saw them!” Franz piped up. “I climbed up to look, and one flew right in my face and stung my cheek. I almost cried, but I didn’t.”
“Brave boy,” I said. “If there’s enough room for an entire swarm of bees inside that trunk, it might be hollow for almost its whole height, just like the willow trees back home that grow thick and strong through their bark even when their whole inner core rots away.”

Jack was already on his feet before I finished speaking, scrambling up the lower branches to tap at the trunk with his knuckles, the rest of the boys hot on his heels, peeking in the bee hole and rapping at the wood to listen for the hollow thud. They forgot all about the bees living inside that trunk—until a low angry buzz swelled out of the hole, and a whole swarm of infuriated insects burst out to chase them. They swarmed the boys, stinging their faces, hands, necks, tangling in their hair, and the four of them came scrambling back to me yelling and swatting. It took us ten minutes to wave the bees off, and Jack, who’d been the first one to lean over the hole, looked the worst: his face was puffed up so round and swollen he could barely see, and we had to slather cool damp mud over all their stings to dull the throbbing pain.

While they recovered, I got to work. I carved a perfect little beehive out of a large hollow calabash gourd: I flattened the bottom, cut an arched little doorway in the front, and wove a thatched straw roof over the top to keep the rain and hot sun out. We couldn’t do anything else that day, the bees were still too riled up, so after full dark fell, when every last bee had crawled back inside the trunk, Fritz and I crept over and sealed every single crack and hole in the wood tight with wet clay, trapping them safely inside.

We were up before sunrise the next day. I pushed a hollow reed cane through the clay plug over the main bee hole, lit my pipe full of strong tobacco, and blew thick billowing smoke down into the trunk. The roar of angry buzzing inside was deafening at first, then it softened to a low murmur, and after I’d smoked through two full pipes, it went dead quiet. The bees were completely stupefied.

“Fritz, quick, chisel and hammer,” I called. We cut a small removable door into the trunk right next to the old bee hole, propped it open, and carefully scooped every cluster of drowsy bees we could find out into the waiting gourd hive. Then we scraped every last drop of golden honey and beeswax out of their old nest, stowing all of it in a sealed cask I’d set aside. To make sure the bees didn’t try to wander back to their old hollow once they woke up, I set a small smouldering pile of tobacco leaves on a ledge nailed inside the trunk, so the whole cavity reeked of acrid smoke. It worked perfectly: when the bees woke up, they buzzed in circles around the smoky trunk for ten minutes, then finally settled back into their clean, sweet new gourd hive, and by sunset they were quiet and working as normal. That night we strained all the honey out of the comb, poured it into earthen jars, melted down the leftover wax, and pressed it clean through a canvas sack to store for candle making.

The next morning we started the big project: hollowing out the entire banyan trunk for the spiral staircase. We cut a proper man-sized doorway right at the base, just big enough to fit the spare heavy oak door we’d salvaged from the wrecked captain’s cabin, hung it on its hinges, then scraped out all the crumbly rotten inner wood until we could stand at the bottom of the trunk and look straight up through the open hollow all the way to the sky. We set a thick young sapling dead in the centre as a central axle, cut notches all up its length to hold the steps, matching notches in the inner wall of the trunk, then cut each step smooth from planks salvaged off the wreck, nailing every one firm into place. We spiralled the stairs up and up, cutting small glass-paned windows into the trunk wall every ten feet to let in light and fresh air, running smooth handrails along the central axle and the outer trunk wall the whole way. It took us a full month of steady, easy work to finish the whole flight, all the way up to the floor of our treehouse nest.

We didn’t waste a minute of that month on idle work. We tended the whole camp: the goats and the sheep both had frisky new baby kids and lambs, and we tied tiny little cast brass bells we’d fished off the wreck’s cargo hold around their necks, so we could hear them tinkle through the trees if they wandered off. Juno our mastiff had a litter of fluffy new puppies; we kept two, and Jack tucked his tiny adopted jackal cub Fangs right in with the puppies, and Juno accepted the strange little wild pup as her own without a fuss. Our buffalo’s nose wound healed completely, and we broke him to saddle and pack: we started by tying sacks and bundles to his hide girth to get him used to carrying weight, then let our little monkey Knips ride on his back to get him used to the feel of a passenger, before the boys climbed on to ride him bareback through the fields. Fritz trained his pet eagle to hunt: he left bits of raw small game on the buffalo’s horns and the back of our great tame bustard, until the eagle learned to swoop down and strike prey on the wing, perfectly obeying Fritz’s whistle, and we kept him far away from the poultry yard so he never got a taste for our tame chickens. Ernest wove a tiny little rush basket with straps for Knips the monkey, and trained him to scamper up tall coconut palms to pick ripe fruit and stuff it in the basket to bring back down. Even my attempt to make new, better candles worked out: we mixed half beeswax, half waxy candleberry sap, split our bamboo moulds down the middle to slide the finished candles out easily, and tested two types of wick: the karata fibre my wife spun, and long thin splints of flammable native Antilles wood. The wood wicks burned bright and steady, even if they flared a little, and beat the dim sputtering karata wicks by a mile—no more wasting our soft cotton neck sweatbands for wicks. I even made the whole family a set of waterproof boots: I moulded clay over sand-filled socks to make a perfect foot-shaped form, brushed on layer after layer of gummy caoutchouc rubber, nailed a tough slab of buffalo hide to the sole, and coated that in rubber too, making boots that kept out every drop of mud and rain.

The last big convenience we added that month was a gravity-fed water system. We built a small low dam across Jackal River a ways upstream, raised the water level just high enough that it would flow downhill all the way to Falconhurst, then strung hollow bamboo pipes straight from the new reservoir to a huge empty tortoise shell we set right outside our front door, to use as a trough. No more hauling heavy buckets of water from the river every single day. We celebrated the grand opening by dumping a full sack of dirty potatoes straight into the flowing trough to wash them clean.

Then one morning, halfway through adding the final smooth handrail to the top of our spiral stairs, we heard it: a terrible, unearthly roar, echoing through the forest, so loud and strange none of us could place it. Jack yelped it was a lion, Fritz guessed a gorilla, and Ernest said, very reasonably, that it sounded most like a hyena. “Whatever it is,” I said, grabbing my rifle, “everyone up the stairs to the treehouse right now, I’ll lock the base door. We sent the dogs out to patrol the pastures, they’ll warn us if it comes closer.”

Fritz and I crept back down the stairs a few minutes later, guns cocked, to scout the treeline. The roar boomed out again right beside us, and Fritz immediately dropped his rifle, snorting so hard he nearly doubled over laughing. The weird sound was hee-haw, hee-haw, hee-haw: our old stray jackass, who’d wandered off weeks before, braying at the top of his lungs. And he wasn’t alone. Trotting right behind him, slim, graceful, and the colour of pale sand, was a wild onager—the rare wild Asiatic ass no European had ever tamed on this side of the world.

I sent Fritz quietly back to the house for a length of strong rope, a handful of oats, and a pinch of salt, while I split a bamboo stalk halfway down to make a set of soft pincers for the onager’s nose. We tied one end of the rope to a thick tree, made a tight running noose at the other, and crept out to hide in the brush. The old tame jackass saw the oats in Fritz’s hand and trotted right over to nibble from his palm, and the shy onager, seeing her new friend unafraid, stepped forward to sniff at the salt in his other hand. Fritz flicked the noose out in one smooth motion, and it settled right around her neck. She reared back so hard the noose pulled tight, and she stumbled to the ground half choked. I ran forward immediately, loosened the rope, replaced it with a soft leather halter, slipped the bamboo pincers over her nose, and tied her secure between two strong trees while she recovered. She lashed out with her hooves, snorting and stamping for an hour before she finally tired and went still, quivering. By dusk, I was able to lead her gently to the stables we’d built under the banyan tree, and tie her up right next to her old new jackass friend. We had a new addition to our island family, and not a single one of us could wait to break her to saddle.
