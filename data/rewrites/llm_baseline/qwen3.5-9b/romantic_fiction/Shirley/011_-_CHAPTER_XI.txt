Caroline refused to yield easily. Deep within her, a girl's innate strength stirred, and she drew upon it. It is a truth that men and women fight hardest when they stand alone—without witnesses, counselors, or confidants; unencouraged, unadvised, and unpitied.

Miss Helstone found herself exactly in this plight. Her suffering was her only spur, and because it was so acute and piercing, it sharpened her resolve. Determined to conquer a pain that threatened to destroy her, she threw herself into every task with renewed vigor. She was never seen busier, more studious, or more active. She took long walks in every kind of weather, venturing into solitary paths. Each evening she returned pale and worn, yet strangely unfatigued. As soon as she removed her bonnet and shawl, instead of resting, she would begin pacing her room. At times, she would not sit until she was literally faint. She claimed she did this to tire herself thoroughly so she might sleep soundly at night. Yet, if that was her goal, it remained unachieved. When others slept, she tossed restlessly on her pillow or sat in the darkness at the foot of her couch, seemingly forgetting the need for repose. Often, unhappy girl, she wept—a crying born of an intolerable despair that swept over her, smashing down her defenses and reducing her to childlike helplessness.

In moments of such prostration, temptation assailed her. Weak whispers urged her to write to Robert, confessing her unhappiness caused by the prohibition against seeing him and Hortense. She feared he would withdraw his friendship—and perhaps his love—and forget her entirely. She begged him to remember her, to write occasionally. She actually penned one or two such letters, but she never sent them. Shame and common sense held her back.

Finally, the strain of her current existence reached a breaking point. She felt she could endure it no longer; she must find a change, or her heart and mind would collapse under the pressure. She longed to leave Briarfield, to flee to some distant land. Alongside this desire grew a deeper, secret, anxious yearning: to discover and know her mother. But this hope was tangled with doubt and dread. *If* she knew her mother, could she love her? There was cause for hesitation. In her entire life, Caroline had never heard her mother praised. Anyone who mentioned her did so with cold detachment. Her uncle seemed to harbor a tacit antipathy toward his sister-in-law. Even an old servant, who had worked briefly for Mrs. James Helstone after her marriage, spoke of her former mistress with chilling reserve—calling her "queer" or claiming she simply didn't understand her. These words were ice to Caroline's heart; they suggested it might be better never to know her parent than to know her and dislike her.

However, one plan emerged that offered a glimmer of relief: becoming a governess. She could do nothing else. A small incident finally gave her the courage to reveal this intention to her uncle.

Her long, late walks invariably led her near the Hollow. Whether she wandered along the dreary edges of Stilbro' Moor or across the sunlit expanse of Nunnely Common, her return path was always calculated to pass close to the mill. She rarely entered the den itself, but she visited its threshold at twilight almost as regularly as the stars rose over the hills. Her favorite spot was a specific stile beneath an ancient thorn. From there, she could survey the cottage, the mill, the dew-laden garden, and the still, deep dam. Most importantly, she could see the familiar window of the counting-house. At a fixed hour, a sudden, bright ray of the well-known lamp would shoot forth from its panes.

Her purpose was to watch for this light; her reward was catching it. Sometimes it sparkled brightly in clear air, sometimes shimmered dimly through mist, and at other times flashed brokenly between slanting lines of rain—for she came in all weathers.

On nights when the light failed to appear, she knew Robert was away, and she departed doubly sorrowful. Conversely, its kindling made her elated, as if she saw in it the promise of indefinite hope. If, while she gazed, a shadow bent between the light and the lattice, her heart leaped. That eclipse was Robert; she had seen him. Returning home comforted, she carried a clearer vision of his face, a sharper memory of his voice, his smile, his bearing. Blended with these impressions was a sweet persuasion that, if she could reach him, his heart might still welcome her. Perhaps at this very moment he would be willing to extend his hand and draw her close, sheltering her as he once did. That night, though she might weep as usual, she fancied her tears less scalding; the pillow seemed softer, the ache in her temples less severe.

The shortest route from the Hollow to the rectory wound past a certain mansion—the old, tenantless dwelling known as Fieldhead. Though vacant for ten years due to the proprietor's absence, it was no ruin. Mr. Yorke had ensured it was kept in good repair, and an old gardener and his wife resided there, tending the grounds and maintaining the house.

If Fieldhead lacked many merits as a structure, it certainly deserved the title of picturesque. Its regular architecture, combined with the gray and mossy patina of time, gave it a rightful claim to the epithet. The old latticed windows, the stone porch, the walls, the roof, and the chimneys were rich in artistic detail. The trees behind were fine, bold, and spreading; the cedar on the lawn in front was majestic; and the granite urns on the garden wall and the fretted arch of the gateway were delights for any artist's eye.

One mild May evening, as the moon rose, Caroline, feeling weary yet unwilling to return to a home filled only with beds of thorns and nights of grief, sat on the mossy ground near the gate. She gazed toward the cedar and the mansion. It was a still night—calm, dewy, and cloudless. The western gables reflected the clear amber of the horizon; the oaks behind were black, the cedar even darker. Beneath its dense, raven branches, a patch of sky opened, grave and blue, full of the moon, which looked down solemnly and gently upon Caroline from beneath that somber canopy.

She found this night and prospect mournfully lovely. She wished for happiness, for inner peace, wondering if Providence held no pity for her, offering no help or consolation. Memories of happy lover's trysts from old ballads surfaced; she imagined such a meeting in such a scene would be blissful. *Where was Robert now?* she asked herself. Not at the Hollow; she had waited long for his lamp and seen none. She questioned internally whether she and Moore were ever destined to meet and speak again.

Suddenly, the door within the stone porch of the hall opened, and two men emerged—one elderly and white-headed, the other young, dark-haired, and tall. They crossed the lawn, exited through a portal in the garden wall, and disappeared as they passed across the road, over the stile, and down into the fields. Robert Moore had passed before her, accompanied by his friend Mr. Yorke. Neither had seen her.

The apparition was fleeting—seen barely before vanishing—but its electric passage kindled her veins and stirred her soul. It found her despairing; it left her desperate. Two vastly different states.

"Oh, had he but been alone! Had he but seen me!" she cried. "He would have said something. He would have given me his hand. He *does*, he *must*, love me a little. He would have shown some token of affection. In his eye, on his lips, I should have read comfort; but the chance is lost. The wind, the shadow of a cloud, does not pass more silently, more emptily than he. I have been mocked, and Heaven is cruel!"

Thus, in the utter sickness of longing and disappointment, she returned home.

The next morning at breakfast, appearing white-cheeked and miserable as one who had encountered a ghost, she asked her uncle, "Do you object, Uncle, to my inquiring for a situation in a family?"

Her uncle, oblivious to the turmoil his niece had endured and continued to endure, scarcely believed his ears.

"What whim now?" he asked. "Are you bewitched? What can you mean?"

"I am not well, and I need a change," she replied.

He examined her closely. He discovered she had indeed experienced a transformation. Without his awareness, the rose had shrunk and faded to a mere snowdrop; bloom vanished, flesh wasted. She sat before him drooping, colorless, and thin. Save for the soft expression in her brown eyes, the delicate lines of her features, and the abundant flow of her hair, she no longer possessed a claim to being pretty.

"What on earth is the matter with you?" he demanded. "What is wrong? How are you ailing?"

No answer came, only the filling of her brown eyes and the trembling of her faintly tinted lips.

"Look out for a situation, indeed! For what situation are you fit? What have you been doing with yourself? You are not well."

"I should be well if I went from home."
