At the corner of a long, straight street in the far East End—brick upon brick like a ledger ruled by a joyless clerk—there stands a house that has the indecency to remember. The other houses merely endure. This one insists.

It draws me as a smell draws you: not the clean smell of bread, but the sour steam that escapes a pot when you lift the lid too late. I will be walking with no more intention than a spoon has ambition, and then, without any vote being taken, I find my feet hurrying me through the city’s din—through those thoroughfares where the naphtha lamps flare like cheap stage-lighting, and faces shine with that patient, lead-coloured resolve London polishes onto people as a cook polishes pewter with salt and vinegar. I pass into dimmer streets where blinds swell and shrink with monstrous shadows; into narrow, noisome lanes where gutters simmer with children, and every open doorway pours out riot as though some unseen hand keeps topping it up.

And always the sound comes first. Wheels gnash. Hooves strike. A distant quarrel rises and falls like a kettle trying to decide whether it will boil. A barrel organ begins a tune, and is murdered midway by laughter. Then, as I near my corner, the noises thin, not because the world grows gentler, but because my memory grows louder.

There the house waits—larger than its neighbours, older by a generation of soot. It was built when this street was still a country lane edging marshes; when a man could hear reeds hissing and the sea muttering to itself, instead of iron shrieking and commerce congratulating its own voice. Even now, if you stand close and refuse to be hurried, you can catch beneath the modern racket a quieter under-sound, like the last trace of a dialect on a tongue that has been bullied into speaking properly.

Two sides of it are encompassed by what was once a garden, though calling it that now is like calling dripping tea-leaves a banquet. It is a barren patch of stones and dust where clothes hang in perpetual surrender, flapping and snapping like flags for an army that has mislaid its cause. About the door remain the bones of a porch; the stucco has fallen away and left the place’s naked insincerity frankly on show—an old hypocrite in its shirt-sleeves.

Sometimes I drift here by day. In daylight, the house plays at not knowing me. Slatternly women gossip round the area gates. A cry goes up, hoarse and wailing—“Coals—any coals—three and sixpence a sack—co-o-o-als!”—chanted with the chronic melancholy that comes of calling into ears that have learned to shut like purses. The sun, unpitying, makes every stain confess. The house, ashamed of its own shabbiness, turns its face away and will not see me as I pass.

Not until Night—merciful to the ugly and therefore, in London, very busy—draws her veil across the street’s sordid features does the old place soften. Then, like some fond nurse remembered after years, it opens wide its arms to welcome me. The life it now shelters quiets for a time behind its walls. The “King of Prussia” opposite lets its last flare die out. And the house begins to talk.

It talks not in sentences, but in footsteps and creaks, in draughts that stir old smells from cracks and corners: damp brick, worn wood, cheap paraffin, stale beer blown across from the public-house, and—faintly, stubbornly—something domestic that refuses extinction, like toast once made on a grudging fire. The floors are thick with the ghost-pressure of feet. The walls are full of voices. I tell you, a violin long played upon learns a music of its own; and a house long lived in—long argued in, long wept in, long laughed in—learns a speech that has no dictionary.

Then into the silent street come the well-remembered footsteps; in and out the creaking gate pass, not seeing me, the well-remembered faces. We talk of them as two cronies turn the torn leaves of an old album where faded portraits, in forgotten fashions, lean forward to whisper. And we do it with that peculiar tenderness old things have: a smile, a sigh, and many an “Ah me,” and “Dear, dear,” as though the dead were merely late to supper.

A bent, worn man comes towards us with quick, impatient steps, yet every fifty yards or so he stops, leaning heavily on his high Malacca cane, as if even his impatience must pause to catch its breath.

“It is a handsome face, is it not?” I ask, gazing at him as the shadow frames it.

“Aye,” answers the old House, “handsome enough. Handsomer still before you and I knew it—before mean care furrowed it with fretful lines. Care is like bad salt: it draws the moisture out of everything and leaves it hard.”

“I never could make out,” the house continues, musingly, “whom you took after; for they were a handsome pair, your father and your mother—though Lord! what a couple of children!”

“Children!” I say, startled. For my father must have been past five and thirty before the house could have known him, and my mother’s face is close to mine in the darkness, so that I see the grey hairs mingling with the bonny brown.

“Children,” repeats the old House, irritably. Old folk—whether built of brick or bone—do not care to have their judgments questioned. “The most helpless pair of children I ever set eyes upon. Who but a child would conceive the notion of repairing his fortune by becoming a solicitor at thirty-eight? Or, having conceived such a notion, would select the outskirts of Poplar as a likely centre in which to put up his door-plate?”

“It was considered a rising neighbourhood,” I reply, a little resentful—because no son likes to hear the family wisdom criticised, even when, deep down, he suspects the critic is right. “All sorts and conditions of men, whose affairs were in connection with the sea, would, it was thought, come to reside hereabout, so as to be near the new docks; and had they, it is not unreasonable to suppose they would have quarrelled and disputed with one another, much to the advantage of a cute solicitor convenient to their hand.”

“Stuff and nonsense,” retorts the old House shortly. “Why, the mere smell of the place would have kept a sensible man away.” And then—if a brick façade can twist itself, it does; the grim face before me turns goblin. “And he, of all men, as ‘the cute solicitor,’ giving advice to shady clients eager to get out of trouble by the shortest way! Can you fancy it? He who for two years starved himself, living on five shillings a week—before you came to London, when he was here alone. Even your mother knew nothing of it till years afterwards—so that no man should be a penny the poorer for having trusted his good name. Do you think the crew of chandlers and brokers, dock-hustlers and freight-wreckers would have found him useful, even had they settled here?”

I have no answer; nor does the old House wait for one. It never did have patience with human slowness—having observed, for decades, the leisurely manner in which people ruin their own lives.

“And your mother!” it goes on. “Would any but a child have taken that soft-tongued wanton to her bosom, and not have seen through acting so transparent? Would any but the veriest child—never ought to have been let out alone—have thought to dree her weird in such folly? Children! Poor babies they were, both of them.”

“Tell me,” I say—for at such times all my stock of common sense cannot convince me the old House is mere clay and mortar. From its walls so full of voices, from its floors so thick with footsteps, surely it has learned to live. “Tell me—was there any truth in the story?”

“Truth!” snaps the old House. “Just truth enough to plant a lie upon; and Lord knows not much ground is needed for that weed. I saw what I saw, and I know what I know. Your mother had a good man, and your father a true wife—but it was the old story: a man’s way is not a woman’s way, and a woman’s way is not a man’s way, so there lives ever doubt between them.”

“But they came together in the end,” I say, remembering.

“Aye, in the end,” answers the House. “That is when you begin to understand, you men and women—when you come to the end.”

A small face peeps shyly at me through the railings—the grave face of an angel that has not been washed as recently as angels are supposed to be. The moment I turn my head it darts back and disappears, as if modesty were a game of hide-and-seek and Heaven played by London rules.

“What has become of her?” I ask.

“She? Oh, she’s well enough,” replies the House. “Lives close here. You must have passed the shop. You might have seen her had you looked in. She weighs fourteen stone, about; and has nine children living. She would be pleased to see you.”

“Thank you,” I say, with a laugh that is not wholly a laugh. “I do not think I will call.”

Yet I still hear, absurdly, the pit-pat of her tiny feet dying down the long street—sound lingering where sight fails, like a half-remembered phrase in a tongue you spoke before you learned the proper one.

The faces thicken round me. A large, looming, rubicund visage smiles kindly, and brings back into my heart that odd old mixture: instinctive liking held in check by conscientious disapproval, as though affection were a stew that must not be allowed to boil over respectability.

I turn from it, and see a massive, clean-shaven face with the ugliest mouth and the loveliest eyes I ever have known in a man.

“Was he as bad, do you think, as they said?” I ask my ancient friend.

“Shouldn’t wonder,” the old House answers. “I never knew a worse—nor a better.”

The wind whisks that face aside and shows me a little old woman hobbling nimbly by aid of a stick. Three corkscrew curls on each side of her head bob with every step, like punctuation marks dancing to an unheard tune. As she draws near, making the most alarming grimaces, I hear her whisper, as though confiding to herself some fascinating secret: “I’d like to skin ’em. I’d like to skin ’em all. I’d like to skin ’em all alive!”

It is a fiendish sentiment; and yet I only laugh, for memory has a strange palate and can find sweetness in the sharpest things. The little old lady, with one final contortion surpassing all dreams, limps beyond my ken.

Then, as though choosing contrasts, follows a fair, laughing face. I saw it in life only a few hours ago—at least, not it, but the poor daub Evil has painted over it, hating the sweetness underneath. I stand gazing, wishing it were among the dead who change not. And as I stand, there drifts back from the shadows that other face again—the wicked mouth and the tender eyes—so that once more I am helpless between the two I loved so well: he from whom I learned my first steps in manhood, she from whom I caught my first glimpse of the beauty and the mystery of woman.

Again the cry rises from my heart, as if it were an old chorus the house will always make me sing: Whose fault was it—yours or hers?

And again I hear his mocking laugh: “Whose fault? God made us.”

Thinking of her, and of the love I bore her—which was the love of a young pilgrim to a saint—it comes into my blood to hate him. But when I look into his eyes and see the pain that lives there, my pity grows stronger than my misery, and I can only echo his words: “God made us.”

Merry faces and sad; fair faces and foul; they ride upon the wind. Yet the centre round which they circle remains always the same: a little lad with golden curls more suitable to a girl than to a boy, with shy, awkward ways and a silent tongue, and a grave, old-fashioned face. He stands there as the kernel of the whole business—small, quiet, not understanding the language life speaks in, yet obliged to live by it all the same, like a child handed a foreign menu and told to order for the table.

Turning from him to my old brick friend, I ask, softly: “Would he know me, could he see me, do you think?”

“How should he?” answers the old House. “You’re so different to what he would expect. Would you recognise your own ghost, think you?”

“It is sad to think he would not recognise me,” I say.

“It might be sadder if he did,” grumbles the House.

We are both silent awhile. I know what the House is thinking; it always thinks the same thing when it has worked itself into tenderness.

Soon it speaks, as I expected. “You—writer of stories—why don’t you write a book about him? There is something that you know.”

It is the old House’s favourite theme. I never visit but it suggests it, like an elderly relative who cannot meet you without remarking that you look pale and ought to eat more.

“But he has done nothing,” I object.

“He has lived,” answers the old House. “Is not that enough?”

“Aye, but only in London, in these prosaic modern times,” I persist. “How of such can one make a story that shall interest the people?”

The old House waxes impatient. “‘The people!’” it retorts. “What are you all but children in a dim-lit room, waiting until one by one you are called out to sleep? And one mounts upon a stool and tells a tale to the others gathered round. Who shall say what will please them, what will not?”

So I leave it and go home with musing footsteps through softly breathing streets. Even the city, when it pauses between its louder hours, has a kind of hush that might be called peace if it were not so full of distant machinery clearing its throat. I ponder the house’s words. Is it only an old nurse thinking all the world must be interested in her nursling, or is there wisdom in this counsel?

Then, to my guidance or misguidance, comes the thought of a certain small section of the Public who often of an evening commands of me a story; and who, when I have told her of dreadful giants and gallant youths who slay them, of woodcutter’s sons rescuing maidens from Ogre-guarded castles; of princesses the most beautiful in the world, of princes with magic swords—still unsatisfied creeps closer, saying, “Now tell me a real story,” and, for my comprehending, adds: “You know—about a little girl who lived in a big house with her father and mother, and who was sometimes naughty, you know.”

So perhaps among the many there may be some who, for a moment, will turn aside from tales of haughty Heroes ruffling it in Court and Camp, to listen to the story of a very ordinary lad who lived with very ordinary folk in a modern London street, and who grew up to be a very ordinary sort of man—loving a little and grieving a little, helping a few and harming a few, struggling and failing and hoping.

If any such there be, let them come round me.

But let none grow indignant as they listen, saying, “This rascal tells us but a humdrum story, where nothing is as it should be,” for I warn all beforehand: I tell but of things that I have seen. My villains, I fear, are but poor sinners, not altogether bad; and my good men but sorry saints. My princes do not always slay their dragons; alas, sometimes the dragon eats the prince. The wicked fairies often prove more powerful than the good. The magic thread leads sometimes wrong, and even the hero is not always brave and true.

So let those come round me only who will be content to hear but their own story told by another—saying as they listen, “So dreamt I. Ah, yes, that is true, I remember.”
