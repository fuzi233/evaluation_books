The storm had passed, leaving in its wake a sea of shattered timbers and broken men. The great Armada, that pride of Spain, lay scattered upon the waters like the bones of a fallen giant. The children of light had prevailed against the children of darkness, and the reckoning had come at last.

Amyas Leigh stood upon the deck of the *Vengeance*, his eyes fixed upon the fleeing Spaniard—*La Sta. Catharina*—the ship that carried his brother’s murderer. Sixteen days he had pursued her, sixteen days of salt and wind and wrath, and now, at last, the end was near.

The sky hung low and sullen, the air thick with the promise of another storm. The men moved about the deck in silence, their faces grim. They knew what drove their captain, knew the oath that burned in his heart.

“She cannot escape us now,” muttered Yeo, squinting through the spray. “The tide has turned against her.”

Amyas did not answer. His hand rested on the hilt of his sword, his knuckles white.

A cry went up as the clouds parted for a moment, revealing the Spaniard—a dark shape against the gray, her sails torn, her hull battered by the chase. She was limping, wounded, but still defiant.

“There she is,” Amyas said, his voice low and terrible. “And here we are.”

The wind shifted, howling like a thing possessed, driving them onward. The *Vengeance* surged forward, closing the distance. The men readied themselves, pikes and muskets in hand, eyes fixed on their prey.

Then the storm broke anew. Rain lashed the decks, lightning split the sky, and the sea rose in great black swells. The Spaniard, desperate, tried to turn—but too late.

Ahead, through the chaos, loomed the jagged teeth of the rocks—Lundy Island, the Shutter, waiting like a wolf in the mist.

“She’s done for,” Yeo said, crossing himself.

Amyas watched, motionless, as the great galleon struck. A shuddering crack echoed across the water as her hull splintered against the stone. Waves swallowed her, timbers snapping like twigs, men screaming as they were dashed into the froth.

For a long moment, Amyas stood there, the wind whipping his hair, the salt stinging his eyes. Then, slowly, he drew his sword.

He raised it high—not in triumph, but in something darker, something that tasted of ashes. Then, with a great cry, he hurled it into the sea.

The blade flashed once before the waves took it, sinking into the depths where the bones of his enemy would lie forever.

And with it, something in Amyas sank too.

The vengeance was done.

But the cost?

Only the sea knew.
