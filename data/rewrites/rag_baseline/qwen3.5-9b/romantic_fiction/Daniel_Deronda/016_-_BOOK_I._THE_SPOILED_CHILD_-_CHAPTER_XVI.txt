The universe of human existence mirrors the cosmos: every individual possesses a visible trajectory charted by the stars and an invisible history woven through the dark ether. While astronomers deduce the path of a wandering planet with cold precision, mapping every arc against the backdrop of space, the chronicler of human souls must navigate far deeper currents. Their task requires threading the hidden pathways of emotion and cognition that culminate in every decisive act, illuminating those moments of agonizing intensity where suffering transforms into action—much like the cry of Prometheus, whose bound torment radiates a ferocity surpassing the very sea and sky he summons and the god he defies.

For Daniel Deronda, destiny had carved a singular, searing epoch into his youth. It was a day drenched in July sunlight, where large pink roses shed their final petals onto a grassy courtyard hemmed in by a Gothic cloister. Picture the scene: a boy of thirteen lying prone upon the cool grass within the shade, his curly head resting on his folded arms above an open book, while his tutor, similarly engrossed, sat on a camp-stool beneath the shelter of the arcade. In his hands lay Sismondi's *History of the Italian Republics*; the lad possessed a voracious hunger for history, desperate to comprehend how time had been filled since the Great Flood and how civilization persisted through its dull, stagnant intervals. Suddenly, he lowered his left arm and glanced at his tutor, his voice ringing out in the purest tones of boyhood.

"Mr. Fraser, how was it that popes and cardinals always had so many nephews?"

His tutor, an able young Scot serving as Sir Hugo Mallinger's secretary, roused himself reluctantly from his studies in political economy. With the clear, emphatic cadence characteristic of Scottish speech—which renders a truth doubly potent—he replied, "Their own children were called nephews."

"Why?" asked Deronda.

"It was simply for the propriety of the matter," the tutor explained, his tone sharpening slightly. "As you know very well, priests cannot marry, and therefore the children were illegitimate."

Mr. Fraser thrust out his lower lip, emphasizing the final word with a hint of impatience at being interrupted, before returning his gaze to his text. Yet, as if stung by a sudden insect, Deronda sprang upright into a sitting position, his back turned sharply toward the tutor.

He had always addressed Sir Hugo Mallinger as his uncle. When the question of his parents had once arisen, the baronet had responded with a vague dismissal: "You lost your father and mother when you were quite a little one; that is why I take care of you." At that time, straining to pierce the haze of early twilight, Daniel retained only a dim sensation of being kissed profusely, enveloped in thin, clouded, scented drapery, until his fingers brushed against something hard that caused pain, triggering a burst of tears. All other memories were confined to the little world in which he currently resided. At that age, he cared little to learn more, for his affection for Sir Hugo was so profound that he felt no sorrow for the loss of unknown parents. Life remained delightfully radiant for the lad, guided by an uncle who was perpetually indulgent and cheerful—a magnificent figure in the bright noon of life, whom Daniel deemed absolutely perfect. Sir Hugo's domain was one of England's finest seats, a place that was simultaneously historical, romantic, and intimately domestic: a picturesque architectural extension rising from an abbey, retaining remnants of its ancient monastic core.

Diplow lay in another county, a comparatively landless estate acquired by the family from a wealthy Restoration-era lawyer on the maternal side, a woman noted for wearing the powdered wig of the era. By contrast, the Mallingers held the grant of Monk's Topping dating back to Henry VIII, possessing ancestral rights to the neighboring lands of King's Topping. Their lineage traced directly to a certain Hugues le Malingre, who arrived with William the Conqueror—and seemingly inherited a pallid complexion that had fortunately been corrected in his descendants.

Two rows of descendants, direct and collateral, stared down from the gallery overlooking the cloisters as Daniel walked below: men in armor with pointed beards and arched brows, pinched ladies trapped in hoops and ruffs with faces obscured by fabric; solemn figures in black velvet with padded hips, and pale, frightened women clutching small boys by the hand; smiling politicians sporting magnificent wigs, and ladies of the prize-animal variety, with rosebud mouths and heavy-lidded eyes, rendered in the style of Lely. Then came generations whose features were revised and embellished in the tastes of Kneller, followed by refined iterations of the family type during the eras of Reynolds and Romney, until the line terminated with Sir Hugo and his younger brother, Henleigh. This latter brother had married Miss Grandcourt, adopting her surname alongside her vast estates, thereby forging a union between two equally ancient houses. He impaled the three Saracen heads and three bezants of his own coat of arms with the tower and argent falcons of hers, effectively merging their highest advantages in the prospects of Henleigh Mallinger Grandcourt—a man who, coincidentally, had become more of an acquaintance to the narrative observer than either Sir Hugo or his nephew, Daniel Deronda.

In Sir Hugo's youthful portrait, executed by Sir Thomas Lawrence with a rolled collar and high cravat, the artist captured the agreeable alacrity of expression and sanguine temperament evident in the original subject. However, Lawrence went beyond mere fidelity, slightly elongating the nose, which was in reality shorter than one might expect in a Mallinger. Fortunately, the distinctive nasal profile of the family reappeared in his younger brother, manifesting in all its refined regularity in his nephew, Mallinger Grandcourt. Yet, in the nephew Daniel Deronda, the myriad family faces displayed on the gallery walls found no reflection. Nevertheless, he was handsomer than any of them. At thirteen, he might have served as the ideal model for any painter seeking to capture the essence of the most memorable of boys; one could scarcely meet his gaze fully without believing that humanity had achieved nobility in times past and might achieve even greater heights in the future. The finest childlike faces possess this consecrating power, causing us to shudder anew at the world's grossness and basely crafted sorrows, fearing they might intrude here and defile such purity.

But amidst the rose-petal-strewn grass at that precise moment, Daniel Deronda was encountering those very griefs for the first time. A novel idea had invaded his mind, altering the landscape of his habitual happiness as surely as a sudden threat clouds the sky and changes the mood of carefree voyagers. He sat motionless with his back to the tutor, his face reflecting a rapid internal metamorphosis. The deep blush that had risen upon his initial start-up gradually faded, yet his features retained an indescribable look of subdued activity, often accompanying a fresh mental survey of familiar realities.

Having never associated with other boys, his mind exhibited a unique synthesis of childish ignorance and startling knowledge, a trait more commonly observed in bright girls. Having devoured Shakespeare alongside extensive histories, he could have discoursed with the wisdom of a bookish child regarding men born out of wedlock, who were deemed unfortunate due to the disadvantages necessitating heroic efforts to attain equality with their legally born brothers. Yet, he had never linked such knowledge to his own station, which had been so effortless that it never warranted contemplation—until this moment. With the magic of swift comparison, the possibility had darted into his consciousness: that here lay the secret of his own birth, and that the man he called uncle was, in truth, his father.

Some children, even younger than Daniel, recognize the first arrival of anxiety as an ominous, irremovable guest in their tender lives upon discovering that their parents, whom they imagined capable of purchasing anything, were actually poor and drowning in financial distress. Daniel felt the presence of a new guest arriving with an enigmatic, veiled face, carrying dimly conjectured, dreaded revelations. The ardor he had poured into the imaginary worlds of his books suddenly surged toward his own history, expending its pictorial energy there to explain what he knew and represent the unknown. The uncle he loved so dearly assumed the aspect of a father harboring secrets about him—a father who had committed a wrong, yes, a grievous wrong. And what had become of his mother, for whom he must have been taken away? Secrets surrounding which he, Daniel, could never inquire; for speaking or listening to such new thoughts felt akin to falling flakes of fire burning his imagination. Those who have known an impassioned childhood understand this dread of utterance regarding any shame connected to one's parents.

This impetuous influx of new images seized him with the force of absolute fact, leaving him no immediate capacity to reflect that he might be trembling at a mere fiction of his own making. The terrible sense of collision between a powerful rush of feeling and the terror of its betrayal finally found release in big, slow tears that fell without restraint until the voice of Mr. Fraser broke the silence: "Daniel, do you see that you are sitting on the bent pages of your book?"

Daniel immediately shifted the book without turning around, held it before him for an instant, then rose with it and walked away into the open grounds, where he could dry his tears unseen. Once the initial shock of suggestion had passed, he remembered that he possessed no certainty regarding how things truly had been. He realized he had been constructing conjectures about his own history, much as he had often fabricated tales about Pericles or Columbus simply to fill the blanks before they became famous.
