CHAPTER LXVIII. Romola’s Awakening

Romola stirred in her boat, drifting through dreams that melted into deep sleep, then back again into vivid visions—until at last she awoke with arms outstretched, as if reaching for something lost. She found herself not in the dim court of the Bargello, where flickering tapers had once seemed to burn brighter and brighter until darkness vanished beneath their glow, but under a sky washed clean by dawn. Her eyes opened slowly to the soft light of morning.

The boat rested quietly in a sheltered cove. To her right, the Mediterranean stretched like a flawless sapphire, calm and unbroken. To her left, nestled in the embrace of mountains, lay a scene repeated countless times along the shores of this most beautiful sea—a valley shaped like a crescent, cradled between gentle slopes clothed in trees and shadow, rising toward rocky peaks. Here and there, paths wound upward through the greenery, leading to a cluster of buildings perched precariously on the hillside, as though they had scrambled up in haste to claim a place where a tall bell tower might rise above the village below, visible from afar as a mark of beauty and faith.

The morning sun, newly risen, touched only the western edge of the valley, casting golden rays across its curve while the rest remained wrapped in cool, dewy silence. The water itself seemed still, as if it too had come to rest. A warmth crept over Romola’s body, tender and gentle, like a breath of kindness. She lay motionless, not watching the landscape, but simply feeling it—the quiet, the peace, the softness of existence. In youth, there are moments when waking brings no thought, no memory, only a blissful suspension of desire. For a few minutes, the past slipped away like smoke; her childhood dreams returned, and she felt she could stay here forever, not because she wished to, but because she simply *was*.

Then came the awareness: she was in the boat, the one that had carried her through the night. It had not brought her to death, but to life—cradling her in its quiet sway like a lullaby. Though she had faced despair at dusk, now she welcomed the dawn. She was alive. She was in sunlight, not in the unknown dark beyond. Could she stay? No sound from Florence would reach her. But already, behind the veil of golden haze, the city’s towers and domes loomed faintly, separated by a river and ringed by hills.

She sat up, trying to resist the flood of thoughts that surged with the question: how far had she drifted? Why should it matter? This was a hidden place, safe from the world. Simple villagers lived here—they would not harm her. For now, she could rest. Later, she would find bread and milk. Then she would settle into the green hush and let time pause.

She turned to gaze once more at the valley, hoping to reclaim that first sense of peace. But scarcely had she settled into contemplation when a cry tore through the stillness—not a single cry, but long and piercing, growing louder and more desperate with each passing second.

Romola knew instantly: a child in distress, abandoned, pleading for help.

She sprang to her feet, one foot already on the gunwale, ready to leap onto the shore. But she paused, listening. Perhaps the mother was near. Perhaps the cry would stop. But it did not. It grew more unbearable, echoing against the silence, and drew her forward with an invisible force. She stepped ashore and walked without knowing which way to go, drawn by the sound.

It came from a rough garden plot to her right, where a half-ruined hut stood among weeds and overgrown crops. She climbed over a broken stone fence and made her way across patches of neglected corn and wild greens. The air changed as she moved away from the sea—thicker, heavier, as if some unseen poison lingered in the greenness that had seemed so inviting.

Now she could see the doorway of the hut. The cry stabbed through her like pain. She stepped inside.

The dim interior held three bodies lying on straw. A man, tall and gaunt. A girl, perhaps eight years old. And a young woman, her long black hair tangled and grasped by a living child—the child whose cry had led Romola here.

Her experience with death and disease made her act swiftly. She lifted the child, pressing it close to her chest, trying to soothe it. Yet her eyes never left the dead. Their features bore the sharp lines of a distant race, their clothing unfamiliar—Spanish or Portuguese Jews, perhaps, cast ashore by pirates who stole their goods and left them to die. Such things happened often. The Inquisition drove them from their homes; greed cast them into the sea; superstition refused them mercy even when they were drowned.

“But surely,” Romola thought, “there must be someone in the village—some woman whose heart will not deny care to this helpless child—if the mother truly is dead.”

She knelt beside the woman. The body looked stronger than the others, less wasted. Romola reached to touch her chest, lifting the yellow woolen cloth that covered her breast—and froze. Purple spots marred the skin. The plague.

A sudden dread gripped her. If the villagers knew, they might recoil from her, from the child, from anything associated with death. But she had money. She could offer it for goat’s milk. She rose, holding the child tightly, and set off toward the village, her mind focused on comforting the infant and finding someone kind.

As she crossed a field, she noticed something strange: patches of corn left untouched, overripe and golden. Apples and figs lay rotting on the ground. There were grasslands nearby, yet no cows, no sheep, no goats. The silence deepened, becoming ominous.

She hurried toward the cluster of houses, seeking life. She picked two figs and fed small pieces to the child, who began to quiet. Still, the fear grew. The air felt thick, tainted.

She entered between two rows of dwellings. It was long past the hour when people should be stirring. Yet not a soul moved. Every door stood open, revealing empty rooms. One house showed a man lying dead, his clothes still on, his head resting on a spade handle, a clay jug in his hand—as if he had dropped everything in sudden collapse.

Romola shuddered. Was this a village of the unburied?

She wanted to listen—but the child cried again, demanding attention. Then she saw a figure crawling from a house, sinking slowly against the wall. She rushed forward.

It was a young woman, fever-ridden, clutching a pitcher. She did not flinch at Romola’s approach. Only one thought filled her: “Water! Get me water!”

Romola took the pitcher gently. “You shall have water. Can you point to the well?”

The woman raised a trembling hand toward the far end of the street.

Romola ran, carrying both pitcher and child. The baby grew calmer with each piece of sweet fruit. As she neared the well, she glanced up at the slope below the church—where she glimpsed a cow and two goats. She hoped to return there after fetching water.

But she was not aware that someone else approached the well from the opposite side.

To the youth of fifteen who saw her, Romola was a vision. Her grey robe flowed like water, her hair catching the sun, the child on her arm with jet-black eyes and olive skin. She carried a pitcher—like the Virgin bringing water to the sick.

“She is the Holy Mother,” he whispered. “Come to care for us.”

He dropped his own vessel and fled.

Romola, now aware of the fleeing figure, continued. She filled her pitcher halfway and hurried back. Inside the house, she found salted meat and flour—no signs of poverty. She placed the child on the floor and gave the woman water. The woman drank eagerly, then closed her eyes, leaning back as if surrendering to relief.

When she opened them again, she murmured, “Who are you?”

“I came over the sea,” Romola said. “This morning. Are all the people dead?”

“I think all who are not dead are ill. My father and sister lie upstairs. No one to bury them. Soon I shall die.”

“Not so,” Romola replied. “I am here to help. I know the pestilence. I am not afraid. But there must be others still healthy. I saw a boy running toward the mountain.”

“I do not know. When the plague came, many fled. They took the cattle. Give me more water.”

Romola decided to follow the boy’s path. She needed to find those who were well, to win their help. She promised to return, lifted the child again, and began climbing the slope.

It was hard going. The heat pressed on her. Hunger and thirst gnawed. At a fork in the path, she paused, considering whether to wait near the cow, which someone might soon milk.

Then she saw him—peering through the branches, five yards away. A broad face, a black priest’s robe, a bucket in hand. He watched her silently.

Romola spoke gently. “I came over the sea. I am hungry. So is the child. Will you give us milk?”

The priest had just heard from the acolyte that the Madonna had appeared—tall as a cypress, with a halo around her head, coming to fetch water. He had doubted. But now, seeing her, hearing her voice—soft, refined, otherworldly—he hesitated. Was this a miracle? Or a woman sent by God?

He dared not run. He dared not advance.

“Come down,” Romola said after a moment. “Do not fear. Fear rather to deny food to the hungry.”

Then the boughs parted. The priest stepped forward, bucket in hand, eyes wide with awe. He sat beside the cow, nervously drew milk, and offered it in a brass cup.

Romola gave it to the child, then drank some herself. The priest watched, his fear shifting into something deeper—an instinctive obedience.

When she smiled at the child, sucking its milky lips, and said, “Give us more, good father,” he obeyed.

Then she looked at him, her voice firm yet kind: “Now tell me, father, how this pestilence came. Why did your people die without sacraments? Why were they left unburied? I came over the sea to help the living—and you will help them now.”

He told her the story. Three families of Jews, twenty souls, had been cast ashore weeks before, some already sick. The villagers refused them shelter except in a distant hovel. When they died, some threw the bodies into the sea. But a storm brought them back. Panic seized the villagers. They dug a grave, buried the dead—but then the plague struck the Christians. Most fled, taking their animals and supplies. The priest stayed, praying. He kept the boy Jacopo with him—but he feared the disease and dared not descend.

“You will fear no longer,” Romola said. “Come down with me. We will find the living, bury the dead. I have walked through plague-stricken lands for months. See—I am strong.”

She turned to the boy hiding behind a bush. “Jacopo,” she called, smiling. “Come. You will carry the child for me. Your arms are strong. I am tired.”

The boy stepped forward, as if pulled by invisible threads. The priest followed.

“Now we will take the milk,” Romola said, “and see who needs it.”

Together they descended. That morning, hope returned to the valley.

Fewer than twenty lived. But all were comforted. Most survived. The dead were buried.

Days passed. Weeks. Months. The men began to dig and sow again. Women smiled at Romola as they carried water jars to the well. The Hebrew child, now named Benedetto, toddled about, baptized in the mountain church.

Romola took a cottage abandoned by its owner, a little apart from the village. On a bed of clean straw—so soft and pure—she rested through the daylight hours, cared for by a widow who had lost her husband to the plague.

Each day, the priest, Jacopo, and the survivors visited her cottage. They brought honey, cakes, eggs, polenta—offerings of gratitude.

They never forgot what they saw: the fair-haired lady with golden hair and brown eyes full of kindness, lying weary after her labors, while the little black-haired Benedetto crawled beside her, claiming every morsel brought to her. And she always shared, saying, “If you love me, love Benedetto.”

Legends grew in that valley. Of the blessed lady who came over the sea, who healed, who buried, who saved. Not a miracle, perhaps—but a truth remembered: that once, in a time of sorrow, a woman came and loved the dying, and the forgotten, and the forsaken.
