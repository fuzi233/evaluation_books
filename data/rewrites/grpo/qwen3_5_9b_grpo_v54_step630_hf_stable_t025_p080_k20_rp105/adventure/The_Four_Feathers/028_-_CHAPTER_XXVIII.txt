For three days, Feversham drifted in a feverish haze, his mind a chaotic storm. Trench was his anchor, fetching water from the Nile, sharing his meager rations, and standing guard alongside Ibrahim in the shadow of the House of Stone. Three nights passed in this vigil, a grim rhythm of survival. But on the fourth morning, the fog lifted. Feversham woke to the stark reality of the present, seeing Trench's face bent over him, clear and real. At first, the sight triggered a phantom terror, a nightmare memory from boyhood where shadows loom too large. He waved a weak arm to dismiss the illusion, but as his gaze swept the enclosure—the hard blue sky, the trampled earth, the chained figures of his fellow prisoners—the truth settled in with crushing weight. He grabbed Trench, clutching him as if fearing he would vanish, and then, slowly, a smile broke through.

"I am in the prison at Omdurman," he whispered, the words tasting like salvation. "Actually in the prison! This is Umm Hagar, the House of Stone. It seems too good to be true."

He slumped back against the wall, the tension draining from his frame. To Trench, the tone sounded almost ironic, a mask of indifference worn by a man who had survived too much. Yet Trench knew the difference. This was not the pose of a hardened cynic; it was the raw, genuine relief of a man who had finally reached his destination. The peril of Dongola was over. He had found Trench. He was in Omdurman. It was a place of death, yes, but it was also the longed-for goal, the place where his purpose could finally be fulfilled. He might have been hanging on a gibbet miles down the Nile, vultures circling his shoulders, his life's work undone. Instead, he was within the walls of the House of Stone.

"You have been here a long while," Feversham said.

"Three years."

Feversham scanned the zareeba. "Three years of it," he murmured. "I was afraid I might not find you alive."

Trench nodded. "The nights are the worst. It's a wonder any man survives a week, yet I have lived through a thousand." Even to him, the feat seemed impossible. "A thousand nights in the House of Stone!" he exclaimed.

"But we may go down to the Nile by daytime," Feversham urged, rising with sudden alarm at the sight of the thorn fence. "Surely we are allowed so much liberty. An Arab at Wadi Halfa told me so."

"And it is true," Trench confirmed, pointing to the earthen bowl of water at his side. "I filled that at the Nile this morning."

"I must go," Feversham insisted, lifting himself up. "I must go this morning." His voice rose with excitement, prompting Trench to whisper urgently, "Hush. There are many prisoners here, and among them many tale-bearers."

Feversham sank back, not just from weakness but in obedience. "But they cannot understand what we say," he argued, his voice losing its edge.

"They can see we talk earnestly. Idris would know within the hour; the Khalifa before sunset. Heavier fetters, the courbatch—we cannot speak. Lie still. You are weak, and I am tired. We will sleep, and later we will go to the Nile."

Trench lay down beside him and was instantly asleep. Feversham watched him, noting the deep lines etched into Trench's face by three years of suffering. It was not until noon that Feversham stirred.

"There is no one to bring you food?" he asked.

"Yes," Feversham replied, his voice steady. "A boy should come. He should bring news as well."

They waited until the gate of the zareeba swung open. The enclosure instantly transformed into a cage of wild beasts. Gaolers distributed the *aseeda*, a moist cake of dhurra that barely sustained life. The strong fought the weak for scraps; groups battled for dominance. Men gaunt as skeletons hopped and leaped within the limits of their chains, falling in stolid despair if they missed a bite. Thirty gaolers, armed with rhinoceros-hide courbatches, watched impassively, their weapons forgotten in the chaos of hunger.

Amidst the food-bearers, a young boy hid behind the crowd, looking around with irresolution. Before long, he was spotted. Knocked down, his food snatched, he screamed. The sound drew Idris-es-Saier himself to the scene.

"For whom do you come?" Idris demanded, shoving the prisoners aside.

"For Joseppi, the Greek," the boy answered. Idris pointed to the corner where Feversham lay. The boy approached, holding out empty hands to explain his lack of provisions. Squatting by Feversham's side, he began to speak, then loosened a gazelle skin from his waist and let it fall. Inside was a chicken. Feversham and Trench ate, breakfasted, and dined on that single bird.

An hour later, they were permitted to leave the zareeba and walk to the Nile. They moved slowly, halting frequently. At the water's edge, where prisoners unloaded dhows and paddled in the mud, the air was thick with shouting men. The gaolers were visible but out of earshot.

"Yes, we can talk here," Trench said. "Why have you come?"

"I was captured in the desert, on the Arbain road," Feversham admitted slowly.

"Yes, masquerading as a lunatic musician with a zither. I know. But you were captured by your own deliberate wish. You came to join me in Omdurman. I know."

"How do you know?"

"You told me. During the last three days you have told me much. Very much," Trench continued, his voice low. "You came to join me because five years ago I sent you a white feather."

"And was that all I told you?" Feversham asked anxiously.

"No," Trench replied, dragging out the word. He sat up, turning his face away so Feversham could not see his expression. "No, that was not all. You spoke of a girl, the same girl you mentioned when Willoughby, Durrance, and I dined with you in London. I know her name now—her Christian name. She was with you when the feathers came. I had not thought of that possibility. She gave you a fourth feather to add to our three. I am sorry."

A silence stretched between them, heavy with unspoken history. Then Feversham spoke slowly.

"For my part, I am not sorry. I am not sorry she was present. I think, on the whole, that I am rather glad. She gave me the fourth feather, it is true, but I am glad of that as well. Without her presence, without that fourth feather snapping from her fan, I might have given up there and then. Who knows? I doubt if I could have stood up to the three long years in Suakin. I used to see you and Durrance and Willoughby, men who had once been my friends, going about the work I was meant to do. You can't think how the mere routine of a regiment, which I cursed heartily, appealed as something desirable. I could so easily have run away. I could have slipped onto a boat and gone back to Suez. And the chance for which I waited never came—for three years."

"You saw us?" Trench asked. "And you gave no sign?"

"How would you have taken it if I had?" Trench remained silent. "No, I saw you, but I was careful that you should not see me. I doubt if I could have endured it without the recollection of that night at Ramelton, without the feel of the fourth feather to keep the memory actual and recent. I should never have gone down from Obak into Berber. I should certainly never have joined you in Omdurman."

Trench turned quickly to his companion. "She would be glad to hear you say that," he said. "I have no doubt she is sorry about her fourth feather, sorry as I am about the other three."

"There is no reason that she should be, or that you either should be. I don't blame you, or her," Feversham said, falling silent as he looked toward the river. The air was shrill with cries; the shore was thronged with Arabs and negroes in robes of blue, yellow, and dirty brown. The work of unloading went on busily. Across the river, the palm trees of Khartum stood against the cloudless sky, and the sun moved westward. In a few hours, the horrors of the House of Stone would begin. But for now, both men thought of the elms by the Lennon River, of a hall with an open door echoing to a waltz, where a girl and a man stood with three white feathers fallen upon the floor. One man recollected the scene; the other imagined it. To both, the picture was equally vivid. Feversham smiled.

"Perhaps she has now seen Willoughby; perhaps she has now taken his feather."

Trench held out his hand. "I will take mine back now."

Feversham shook his head. "No, not yet."

Suddenly, Trench's face lit up. A hope that had struggled in his hopeless breast for three days and nights, a hope he had repressed for fear it might be false, sprang to life.

"Not yet—then you *have* a plan for our escape," Trench said, anxiety returning to Feversham's face.

"I said nothing of it," Feversham pleaded. "Tell me that! When I was delirious in the prison there, I said nothing of it. I breathed no word of it? I told you of the four feathers, I told you of Ethne, but of the plan for your escape I said nothing."

"Not a single word. So that I myself was in doubt, and did not dare to believe," Feversham said, his anxiety dying away. He had spoken with his hand trembling on Trench's arm, his voice shaking with alarm.

"You see, if I spoke of that in the House of Stone," Feversham exclaimed, "I might have spoken of it in Dongola. For in Dongola as well as in Omdurman I was delirious. But I didn't, you say—not here, at all events. So perhaps not there either. I was afraid that I should—how I was afraid! There was a woman in Dongola who spoke some English—very little, but enough. She had been in the 'Kauneesa' of Khartum when Gordon ruled there. She was sent to question me. I had unhappy times in Dongola."

Trench interrupted in a low voice. "I know. You told me things which made me shiver," he said, catching Feversham's arm and pulling back the loose sleeve. Feversham's scarred wrists confirmed the tale.

"Well, I felt myself getting light-headed there," Feversham continued. "I made up my mind that of your escape I must let no hint slip. So I tried to think of something else with all my might, when I was going off my head." He laughed softly to himself. "That was why you heard me talk of Ethne," he explained.

Trench sat nursing his knees, looking straight ahead. He had dared now to give his hopes their way.

"So it's true," he said in a quiet, wondering voice. "There will be a morning when we shall not drag ourselves out of the House of Stone. There will be nights when we shall sleep in beds, actually in beds. There will be—" He stopped, with a shy air like a man on the brink of a confession. "There will be—something more," he said lamely, then stood up.

"We have sat here too long. Let us go forward."

They moved a hundred yards nearer to the river and sat down again.

"You have more than a hope. You have a plan of escape?" Trench asked eagerly.

"More than a plan," Feversham returned. "The preparations are made. There are camels waiting in the desert ten miles west of Omdurman."

"Now?" Trench exclaimed. "Now?"

"Yes, man, now. There are rifles and ammunition buried near the camels, provisions and water kept in readiness. We travel by Metemneh, where fresh camels wait, from Metemneh to Berber. There we cross the Nile; camels are waiting for us five miles from Berber. From Berber we ride over the Kokreb pass to Suakin."

"When?" Trench cried. "Oh, when, when?"

"When I have strength enough to sit a horse for ten miles, and a camel for a week," Feversham answered. "How soon will that be? Not long, Trench, I promise you not long," he said, rising.

"As you get up," he continued, "glance round. You will see a man in a blue linen dress, loitering between us and the gaol. As we came past him, he made me a sign. I did not return it. I shall return it on the day when we escape."

"He will wait?"

"For a month. We must manage on one night during that month to escape from the House of Stone. We can signal him to bring help. A passage might be made in one night through that wall; the stones are loosely built."

They walked a little farther to the water's edge. Amid the crowd, they spoke again of their escape, with the air of men amused by the chaos around them.

"There is a better way than breaking through the wall," Trench said, laughing as he pointed to a prisoner with a great load upon his back who had fallen face-down in the water. Encumbered by his fetters, the man was vainly struggling to lift himself. "There is a better way. You have money?"
