To any soul who truly loves the glittering machinery of London society—the exclusive clubs, the opera stalls, the carriage drives along Rotten Row, and the whispered intrigues behind the scenes of the great houses—let them admit that Major Pendennis was a man of staggering, almost tragic generosity. In May, he surrendered it all. He traded the vibrant chaos of town life for a quiet, dusty country cottage. He exchanged the thrill of a bow from a Marquis for the silence of a parson's study; he swapped the roar of the Opera House for the hum of a single candle flickering over a modest table. He gave up the "select" circles, the secret festivals, and the endless stream of invitations to lock himself away with a grieving widow, a son who had barely learned to shave, a curate who sighed too often, and a ten-year-old girl.

And what a sacrifice it was! Few outside his immediate circle realized the depth of his renunciation. Down in the country, his letters arrived, franked and heavy with the news of the season. To Helen, they were merely polite notes from distant cousins; to young Pen, they were portals to a universe of grandeur. The Major would hold up a letter, his face etched with a melancholy pride, and sigh over the name at the top. "Look at that," he'd murmur, "Lady Grampound Bowser! Or the Duchess of St. Albans!" Young Pen, wide-eyed and respectful, would stare at the superscripts, listening with bated breath to the tales of the fashionably wicked and the nobly virtuous.

The old Major's mind was a vast library of gossip, a repository of scandal and virtue alike. With the eloquence of a man recounting his own battles, he poured these stories into Pen's eager ears. "You must start your genealogical studies immediately, my boy," he would advise, pacing the terrace or settling over a glass of wine. "Read Debrett every day. Don't worry about the history; focus on the alliances. I once knew a young man, Mr. Suckling, who ruined his chances because he didn't know who sat next to whom at a dinner. He insulted Admiral Bowser without realizing the Admiral's wife was sitting beside him and her husband's son was across the table. A terrible mistake, I assure you."

These lectures, though tedious to the bewildered Mrs. Pendennis, were the lifeblood of young Pen's existence. The Major, a man of honor with eyes that were perpetually "open," viewed the world through a lens of practical morality. He believed that virtue lay not in abstract ideals, but in social survival. "A gentleman on his estate must set an example," he preached, insisting that Pen should attend church, not just for piety, but for the sheer necessity of appearing respectable to the local yokels. "You are the first man in the parish now that the Claverings are gone. Play your cards right, and you might even represent the town."

Pen, naturally pliable and awestruck by his uncle's authority, swallowed these doctrines whole. He found Captain Costigan, his intended father-in-law, increasingly repulsive—a drunken, unshaven brute who smelled of punch and trouble. Under the Major's gentle prodding, Pen admitted that Emily, his beloved, lacked accomplishment and possessed only a "very good average intellect." When pressed further, Pen confessed he had received but three trite letters from her. The Major nodded sagely, offering a humble apology for his intrusive questioning, all the while ensuring Pen understood that he was doing it for the sake of his nephew's future honor.

Back at Chatteris, the atmosphere shifted palpably. Emily, sensing the storm clouds gathering with the arrival of the Major, grew anxious. "I suppose you mean to leave me now?" she fretted. "He'll carry you off!" Yet, when Pen visited, surrounded by the Captain's boisterous hospitality and the sharp tongue of Miss Rouncy, he stood firm. In the presence of his father-in-law's trophies and the Captain's dirty embrace, Pen vowed that no other woman could ever compare to Emily. He fled Chatteris with a mixture of relief and guilt, muttering internally about the sacrifices he was making for a girl who remained, to his mind, cold and mistrustful.

However, the Major's surveillance was relentless. Through his faithful valet, Mr. Morgan, a London professional of impeccable discretion, the Major constructed a dossier on Pen and his love interest. Morgan, a man of the streets, scoured the local inns and theaters, extracting secrets from waiters and ostlers. He discovered that Captain Costigan was a man of questionable finances and habits, frequently drunk and often in debt, banned from the officers' mess by Colonel Swallowtail. Yet, when it came to Emily, the picture changed. The theater staff and the local community spoke highly of her father's protective nature. Despite his drinking, Captain Costigan ensured Emily never ventured out alone. Morgan noted that when officers like Sir Derby Oaks or the dashing Mr. Foker came to take fencing lessons, Emily usually retreated to her lodging or stayed close to her father, rarely engaging with the suitors directly.

Doctor Portman, the vicar, shared the Major's growing disquiet. While the Major lamented that Pen's attachment to an actress was a product of "romantic notions" bred by women, the Doctor argued for a pragmatic approach. "If I were his guardian," Portman mused, "I would command him to break it off. A virtuous attachment to a lady of his rank and fortune would secure his future." But the Major, cynical and experienced, countered that trying to force Pen to abandon his infatuation would only drive him deeper into it. "Time is what we lack," the Major observed. "We must do our best with what we have."

Seeing no other way forward, the two elderly gentlemen hatched a plan. On a day when Pen was expected to dine at home, Portman and Pendennis drove to Chatteris. Like jolly bachelors escaping the confines of propriety, they dined at the George Inn and proceeded straight to the theater to witness *Hamlet*. The stage was familiar ground; both gentlemen had seen Miss Fotheringay perform countless times, yet tonight they watched with fresh intensity.

As Ophelia swept her curtsy to the sparse audience, the Major leaned forward, squinting through his opera-glass. "Gad," he muttered, his voice tight, "the young rascal hasn't made a bad choice." The Doctor, clapping his hands in genuine appreciation, agreed. "She is a clever actress, Major, endowed with considerable personal attractions."

"But that young officer in the box," the Major pointed out, gesturing to a dragoon who was applauding with fervor. "He looks quite taken with her, doesn't he?"

"She certainly seems sweet upon him," the Doctor conceded.

The Major closed his glasses, pocketing them with a decisive click. "Very well," he said, standing up. "Enough observation for tonight." Nor did he linger for the afterpiece. He walked home toward the George, his mind already turning to the strategy ahead, leaving the Doctor to return to his duties at the Deanery. The game, the Major realized, required patience, but the pieces were moving exactly as he had anticipated.
