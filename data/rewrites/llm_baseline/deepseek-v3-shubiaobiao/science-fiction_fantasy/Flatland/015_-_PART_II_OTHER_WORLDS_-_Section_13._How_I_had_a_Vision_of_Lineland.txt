On the eve of the year 2000, as the Long Vacation began, I retired to bed with an unsolved geometric puzzle lingering in my thoughts. That night, I dreamed.

Before me stretched a world unlike any I had known—a single, infinite Straight Line, teeming with tiny, luminous points and slender Lines darting back and forth in perfect unison. A ceaseless chirping filled the air, rising and falling with their motion. When they halted, silence swallowed them whole.

I approached one of the longer Lines, assuming it to be a Woman, and spoke. No reply came. Twice more I tried, but only when I positioned myself directly in her path—blocking her movement—did she respond.

*"I am no Woman,"* the Line declared. *"I am the Monarch of this world. But you—who are you to trespass upon Lineland?"*

Startled, I apologized for my intrusion and begged the King to describe his realm. Yet every question I posed seemed to baffle him, for he could not fathom that I knew nothing of his Straight Line. To him, it was the entirety of existence—beyond it lay not emptiness, but *nothingness*.

His subjects—men as Lines, women as Points—lived bound to that single dimension. Their vision extended no further than a Point, their movement confined to forward and back. None could pass another, for the Line was their universe, and they filled it completely. Neighbors remained neighbors until death, as fixed as stars in the heavens.

I marveled at their cheerfulness, for such existence seemed a prison to me. Curiosity overcame discretion, and I asked how marriages could thrive when no two souls could draw near.

*"Absurd!"* the King cried. *"Do you think love requires proximity? Our unions are forged by harmony, not touch."*

He explained: Each man possessed two voices—a bass and a tenor—while women carried soprano and contralto. Once a week, all Linelanders swayed in a great rhythmic dance. At its peak, every voice rang out, and across vast distances, destined lovers found one another by sound alone. A perfect triad—one man and two women—united in harmony, their union birthing three children to preserve the balance of sexes.

*"Few succeed at first,"* he admitted. *"But time refines discord into unison. Nature wills it so."*

I dared not argue further. The King’s fury was plain, and his world, though narrow, was immutable. To him, my questions were not ignorance but heresy.

And as I awoke, I wondered—how many dimensions, how many truths, lie beyond *our* grasp?
