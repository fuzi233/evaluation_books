With Miss Clack’s account now closed, it falls to me—Matthew Bruff, solicitor, long-time legal advisor to the Verinder family—to take up the pen, for two very good reasons.

First, I hold the missing pieces to two critical puzzles no one else has yet laid bare: Rachel Verinder’s private motive for calling off her marriage to Godfrey Ablewhite, and the even better hidden motive that made Godfrey abandon all claim to her hand without a single fight. I was at the root of both. Second, by pure luck (or ill luck, I have never quite decided which) I found myself personally tangled in the mystery of the Indian Diamond at this exact point in events. I hosted an unmistakeable distinguished Oriental stranger—indisputably the chief of the three jugglers—at my own office for an interview, and met the celebrated traveller Mr. Murthwaite the very next day for a conversation about the Moonstone that would weigh heavily on all that followed. That is my full claim to a place in these pages.

The story of the broken engagement comes first in time, so it opens my narrative. Tracing the full chain of events back, you will find the starting point a most unexpected one: the deathbed of my old friend and client, the late Sir John Verinder.

Sir John had his full share of harmless, amiable human weaknesses. Chief among them, for our purposes, was an unconquerable reluctance, so long as he enjoyed good health, to face the bother of making a will. Lady Verinder hounded him about it, I hounded him about it, and he admitted we were right—right up until the day a fatal illness laid him low. Only then did he send for me.

His instructions were the simplest I had received in forty years of practice. He was dozing when I stepped into his room, roused himself long enough to wave a lazy hand at the pens and paper I laid out. “Everything to my wife,” he said, and immediately rolled over to go back to sleep.

I had to wake him again to confirm: absolutely all his property, land and capital alike, no strings attached, to Lady Verinder? “Yes,” he grumbled, “why can’t you say it as short as I do? Let me sleep.”

Now, in most cases, I would have pushed a client to reconsider a will that gave such unreserved control over every asset to his spouse. But I knew Lady Verinder was not just a good wife worthy of that trust—she was the rare woman in my experience, less than one in a thousand, sharp enough to administer a large estate perfectly fairly. Ten minutes later the will was drawn, signed, witnessed, and Sir John was finishing his interrupted nap.

Lady Verinder justified every bit of that confidence. Barely two weeks after her husband’s funeral, she sent for me to make her own will. Her plans for Rachel were so thoroughly sensible I barely needed to offer a single piece of advice: all I did was shape her instructions into proper legal form, securing their daughter’s future with every possible safeguard. That will sat locked in my fireproof office safe for years, undisturbed, until the summer of 1848.

That was the year the doctors gave poor Lady Verinder her death sentence. I was the first person she told, and she immediately asked to review her will with me. Rachel’s provisions were perfect and untouchable, but a handful of small legacies to distant relatives had shifted over the years, so we added a few codicils, then I drafted a second full, clean will to avoid the messy repetition and confusion of tacking extra pages onto the original. Miss Clack, kind soul, has already described the signing of that second will, which changed nothing at all about Rachel’s financial interests. The only new clauses were around the appointment of a guardian, added on my advice. As soon as Lady Verinder was laid to rest, the will was sent to my proctor to go through the standard probate process at Doctors’ Commons.

Three weeks later, the first odd, under-the-surface warning landed. I popped into my proctor’s office one afternoon, and he greeted me with a strange, tense look. “Got news for you,” he said. “Guess what I heard at Doctors’ Commons this morning? Someone has already pulled Lady Verinder’s will and read the whole thing.”

That was astonishing. There was nothing in that will to contest, no person alive I could think of who had the slightest reason to study it. (For the few readers who do not know, any person can pay a shilling fee to examine any probate will filed at the Commons, no questions asked.)

I asked who had requested it. “Mr. Smalley, of Skipp and Smalley,” he said. “The will hadn’t even been copied into the great folio registers yet, so the clerk had to let him see the original. He pored over every line, scribbled notes in his pocketbook. Any idea what he wanted?”

I shook my head. “I’ll find out before the day’s out.” I headed straight back to my office.

If any other solicitor’s firm had been involved, I might have struggled to pry the information loose. But Skipp and Smalley owed their steady side income to me: my top common-law clerk was Smalley’s own brother, and for years I had passed all the small, low-value cases I didn’t want to handle over to their firm. My patronage paid their rent. I sent my clerk over to his brother’s office with a simple message: Mr. Bruff’s compliments, and he would be glad to know why they had felt the need to examine Lady Verinder’s will.

Half an hour later, Smalley showed up at my office, his brother trailing behind him. He admitted he had acted on a client’s instructions, and argued it would be a breach of professional confidence to name that client. We had a sharp, undignified row. I was in the wrong, of course, but I was angry and suspicious, and I threatened to cut off all my patronage to their firm on the spot if he didn’t talk. He gave in immediately, resigned to the loss of his client’s business over the loss of mine.

The name he gave me was Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

That was all I needed to hear. Let me lay out the facts here, so you know exactly as much as I did in that moment: Rachel Verinder had no right to sell, mortgage, or touch a penny of the capital left to her, land or cash. She only held a life interest in the property. She and any future husband would get the two fine houses (in London and Yorkshire) and a very comfortable annual income, and nothing more. Lady Verinder and I had built that arrangement specifically to shield Rachel from being preyed on by some greedy, unscrupulous man who wanted to drain her of every last penny.

It was plain as day what Godfrey had been up to. The man I had always written off as a smooth, sanctimonious impostor had proven my worst suspicions right: he had been counting on getting his hands on the full capital sum, not just the income, and he had just learned there was no possible way to do it.

I was stuck in a terrible quandary. Less than a week before I had been grieved almost to distress to hear Rachel was engaged to him, certain she was throwing herself away. Should I tell her the truth immediately, while she was still deep in mourning for her mother, and risk hurting her? Or stay silent, and let her marry a man who would make her miserable for life?

The answer depended entirely on how desperate Godfrey was for cash. If he only wanted a comfortable living, the income from Rachel’s estate would be more than enough for him, and he would still go through with the wedding. If he needed a huge lump sum, and fast, to get himself out of some scrape, the will I had helped draw would protect her perfectly, no drama required.

I headed for Brighton the very next day, where Rachel and Mrs. Ablewhite were staying. The moment I saw her, my hesitation vanished. I knew I had to tell her the full truth. We went for a long walk along the front, and I laid out every last detail: how Godfrey’s lawyer had pried open her mother’s will, what his obvious mercenary motive was, all of it.

She did not make a sound as I spoke. Her hand tightened around my arm until her knuckles went white, her face went paler and paler, but she did not interrupt, did not cry, did not fly into a rage. We walked nearly a full mile in silence after I finished, while she turned it all over in her head, cut off completely from the world around her. I knew her well enough not to disturb her. Unlike most young women, who would immediately run to a friend to chatter over every detail, Rachel’s first instinct was to lock the world out and think things through on her own. It was a fine quality in a man, and a lonely, misunderstood one in a woman—but for her, it was proof she would never do anything petty or unworthy, no matter how suspicious the circumstances around the stolen Moonstone looked.

At last she looked up at me, a faint, fragile echo of her old bright smile on her face. “I owe you so much more than I can say,” she said. “If you hear any talk of my marriage back in London, contradict every rumour at once, on my authority. The engagement is over.”

I warned her that breaking it off would be harder than she thought, that she would attract cruel misconstruction if she did not give people a clear reason why. I offered her the sharp, practical solicitor’s advice: call Godfrey to a private interview, lay out that you know he was marrying you for your money, tell him he can agree to a quiet, no-scandal break or you will make his mercenary motive public, and refer him straight to me if he tries to argue.

But she refused, gentle but firm. She could not, she said, bring herself to call the man who had once been her intended a mean, deceitful fraud to his face. To accuse him that way would be to admit she had let herself trust a scoundrel, and the shame of it would be unendurable for her. She would simply tell him she had thought it over, and decided they would both be happier apart. No explanation, no accusations, no fight.

“Have you forgotten the Moonstone?” she said, when I protested that the whole world would misjudge her. “I already braved everyone’s whispers and gossip over that, for my own private reasons. I can brave this too.”

I could not argue with that. I left her, uneasy, with her promise to write to me the second anything happened, and headed back to London.

The very evening I got home, before I could possibly receive any letter from her, old Mr. Ablewhite turned up at my office. He was furious. He told me Godfrey had already received his dismissal, and accepted it, that very same day.

That one tiny fact told me everything. Godfrey had no fight in him at all. He was in desperate, urgent need of a huge sum of lump sum cash, and Rachel’s will made it impossible for him to get it. The comfortable annual income he would have gotten from marrying her was not enough to save him from whatever ruin was breathing down his neck. That was the only explanation for a man to give up a marriage that would have kept him in luxury for the rest of his life.

Old Mr. Ablewhite demanded to know what on earth had made Rachel behave so strangely, and of course I could not tell him a word. Irritated by me, and already angry at his son for the mess, he let his temper slip completely. I knew, as he stormed out of my office, that the merciless old man would be on the first train to Brighton the next morning, and Rachel would soon have a whole new set of troubles to face—all while the dark, unsolved shadow of the stolen Moonstone hung heavy over every last one of them.
