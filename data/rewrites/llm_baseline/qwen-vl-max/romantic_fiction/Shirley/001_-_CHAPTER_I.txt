It was not long ago that the north of England began to see a steady stream of young curates—men fresh from Oxford and Cambridge, eager for duty, full of zeal, and scattered thickly across the hills like seeds blown by the wind. Every parish seemed to have one or more, and their presence promised hope for renewal in quiet country towns. But we are not speaking of today. We go back—far back—to the early years of the nineteenth century, when the world was still shaped by simpler rules, and the air hung heavy with the scent of damp wool, woodsmoke, and old stone.

This is no tale of romance, reader. If you expect passion, drama, or moonlit confessions beneath willow trees, you may as well close this book now. This story is not spun from dreams, but from the solid, unyielding threads of everyday life. It is as plain and unadorned as a Yorkshire morning—cold, clear, and without pretense. The first course on our table will not be roses or wine, but lentils and vinegar; unleavened bread and bitter herbs. It may not please the fancy, but it will feed the soul.

In those days—1811–12—the shower of curates had not yet fallen. They were rare, almost precious. There was no Pastoral Aid Society, no fund to send young men into the parishes. No one thought of sending a man of twenty-two to serve beside an aging rector unless he could afford him. And so, the churches stood with few hands to tend them, and fewer hearts to warm them.

Yet even then, there were curates—three in particular, scattered across twenty miles of West Riding countryside, like stars in a sparse sky. These were not men of great learning or deep piety, nor were they saints. They were young, restless, and full of talk. Their names: Mr. Donne, curate of Whinbury; Mr. Malone, of Briarfield; and Mr. Sweeting, of Nunnely. And though each lived in his own modest lodging, they saw little reason to stay apart.

On this day, they gathered at Mr. Donne’s house—a neat garden cottage just outside Whinbury, rented from John Gale, a small clothier. Mrs. Gale, who ran the household with a sharp eye and sharper tongue, served dinner with a look that said she would rather have been alone. But hospitality was part of the bargain, and so she allowed them in.

The three sat at the table, eating roast beef and Yorkshire pudding, drinking flat beer, and arguing over trifles. They were all young—no older than twenty-five—and filled with energy that had nowhere else to go. Their vicars wished they would visit the sick, teach the children, or preach with sincerity. But these men preferred something far less noble: the endless round of dinners, teas, and suppers among themselves.

They moved between lodgings like clockwork—Malone to Donne, Donne to Sweeting, Sweeting to Malone—regardless of weather or time. Snow, rain, mud, wind—it mattered not. They came and went, staying longer than welcome, leaving behind disorder and grumbling landladies. Mrs. Gale, Mrs. Hogg, and Mrs. Whipp all complained alike: “It’s just for naught but to give folk trouble.” By “folk,” they meant themselves.

And indeed, they did. Mrs. Gale especially resented the way they treated her. She worked hard, cooked for them, cleaned their rooms, and yet they spoke of her as if she were beneath them. One of them, Mr. Malone, particularly offended her. He was tall, broad-shouldered, and carried himself with a kind of pride that felt foreign in such a place. His speech was thick with Irish brogue, and his manner rough. When he demanded, “Cut it, woman,” she nearly struck him—not out of anger, but because the tone grated against every fiber of her Yorkshire upbringing.

Still, they ate heartily. The beef was tough, the beer flat, but they consumed it all. A dish of vegetables vanished quickly, followed by cheese and a spice cake—so swiftly that Abraham, Mrs. Gale’s six-year-old son, burst into tears when he saw the empty plate. “I wanted a piece!” he cried. But no one heard him.

After dinner, they drank wine—plain, ordinary wine—and turned to argument. Not politics, not literature, not even theology in any serious sense. No, their debate was about the smallest points of church ritual—whether a surplice should be worn indoors, how many candles ought to burn during communion, whether the clerk should bow before the bishop. To outsiders, it seemed trivial. To them, it was everything.

Mr. Malone, having drunk two glasses while the others had only one, grew bold. He laughed loudly, mocked his companions, and teased them relentlessly. He joked about Mr. Donne’s thin frame and odd clothes—especially the chocolate-colored coat he wore in rain, which looked threadbare and absurd. He ridiculed Mr. Sweeting’s height, his flute-playing, and the way he always spoke of his sisters as if they were angels.

Sweeting took it lightly, smiling through it all. Donne, however, bristled. He responded with stiff dignity, trying to hold onto what little self-respect he had left. But soon, the teasing became too much. They turned on Malone, asking how many boys had shouted “Irish Peter!” after him that day (his name was Peter Augustus Malone), mocking his pronunciation—“vele” instead of “veil,” “firrum” for “firm”—and questioning whether Irish clergymen really carried pistols and shillelahs on pastoral visits.

Malone flared up. He rose, stamped his foot, and declared he would not stand for such insults. He called them “Saxons,” “snobs,” and worse. They retorted that he came from a conquered land, that Ireland was a nation of beggars and rebels. The room erupted. Glasses rattled. Voices rose. It sounded like a quarrel that might end in violence.

But it didn’t. Because this was not the first time such a scene had occurred. Mrs. Gale and her husband sat quietly in the kitchen, listening to the noise, knowing full well that no real harm would come of it. These young men fought like brothers, not enemies. They would argue fiercely tonight, but tomorrow they’d be laughing together again.

Then came a knock at the door.

A man stepped inside—Mr. Helstone, middle-aged, dressed in black, with a stern face and a hat perched high on his head. He walked straight through the kitchen, opened the parlour door, and paused—listening.

“What!” he said, once inside. “Has the miracle of Pentecost returned? Is this the sound of seventeen tongues? Parthians, Medes, Elamites—I hear them all! The whole house is alive with noise!”

He surveyed the three curates, arms folded, eyes sharp. He was short but upright, with a hawk-like gaze and a voice that dropped low, deep, and hollow, as if carved from stone.

“You call yourselves apostles?” he asked. “No. You are Babylonian masons—nothing more. This is not the gift of tongues, but the confusion of them.”

Mr. Donne tried to apologize. “We were only talking, sir—settling the Dissenters.”

“Oh, settling the Dissenters?” Mr. Helstone replied, coldly. “It sounded more like settling each other. You make more noise than Moses Barraclough and his Methodists down the lane. And I know whose fault it is—yours, Malone.”

“Mine, sir?”

“Yes, yours. Before you arrived, the others were quiet. Now, chaos. You bring your Dublin ways here—your loudness, your arrogance. Such conduct brings shame not only upon yourselves, but upon the Church itself.”

There was a silence. Then Mr. Helstone continued, his tone firm but measured.

“I met Supplehough earlier—going to preach at Milldean. He baptized sixteen adults in one day. Barraclough draws crowds with his fiery sermons. And here you sit, drinking wine and quarreling like women. No wonder the people turn away from us.”

He paused, then said, “I came to see you, Malone. I have a task for you.”

“What is it?” Malone asked, annoyed.

“There is no funeral. But there is danger. Moore is alone at Hollow’s Mill. He sent his workers to Stilbro’, and only two women remain. The path is clear. Someone might take advantage.”

“I don’t care for Moore,” Malone said.

“You do not fear him either,” Mr. Helstone replied. “But there is a chance of trouble. Moore expects new machinery tonight. Scott and a few men are going to fetch it. But someone must be there—someone to witness if anything happens.”

“I won起 to go,” Malone said.

“Then you are afraid.”

“No, sir. But Moore is strange. I wouldn’t go for his company.”

“And yet you will go—for duty. For the sake of peace.”

Malone hesitated. Then nodded.

“Very well. I’ll go.”

“Good. And take your weapons. The pistols I gave you. Your blackthorn.”

Malone reached for them.

As he did, Mr. Sweeting spoke up. “Sir, I heard something the other day—about Mike Hartley.”

“The Antinomian weaver?”

“Yes. He’s drunk often, and when he is, he goes to Nunnely vicarage to tell Mr. Hall how terrible his sermons are. But he’s also a Jacobin—violent, angry. He talks of regicide. And lately, he’s said he wants Moore to be made an example.”

“A sacrifice?” Mr. Helstone asked.

“Yes. He called Moore the smartest mill-owner in Yorkshire—and said that makes him the perfect offering.”

Mr. Helstone frowned. “Mike Hartley is not right in the head. Or perhaps he is. Either way, he is dangerous.”

“But he sees visions,” Sweeting added.

“He does. Last Friday, he came to my door, breathless, describing one. In Nunnely Park—he saw a figure walking among the trees, cloaked in red, holding a sword. And he said the figure pointed toward Hollow’s Mill.”

A silence fell.

Mr. Helstone looked at Malone. “You go. Not for Moore’s sake—but for the sake of order. For the sake of peace.”

Malone nodded again.

And so, with pistols in hand and a mind full of doubt, he left the house—into the dark, wet night, toward the mill where shadows waited.
