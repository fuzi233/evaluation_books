I went down to the railway station with Gabriel Betteredge at my side; and I carried, like two contraband articles against my own peace, Rosanna Spearman’s letter in my pocket and the nightgown—folded tight, as if linen could be made to hold its tongue—in a little bag for Mr. Bruff’s inspection before I slept.

We left the house in a silence so absolute that it seemed to have weight. For the first time, in all my knowledge of him, Betteredge walked with me and offered no homely proverb, no comfortable commonplace—nothing. The only sound was the country morning itself: damp leaves whispering where the hedge met the road, a lark turning its small music over and over as if it were kneading it into the air, and—further off—the faint, mechanical insistence of wheels and iron that told of the line. I noticed it the more because the human voice was absent; and I thought, with a bitterness I could not dismiss, how easily a man’s inward life is altered when the world around him changes its tune.

I had questions that burned my mouth. Once we were clear of the lodge gates I forced speech between us.

“Before I go to London,” I said, “I have two questions to ask you. They relate to myself—and I believe they will surprise you.”

“If they’ll only put that poor creature’s letter out of my head, Mr. Franklin,” he answered, “they may surprise me till kingdom come. Begin, sir.”

“My first question is this. Was I drunk on the night of Rachel’s birthday?”

“You drunk!” Betteredge stopped short, as if I had accused the sun of rising in the west. “Why, it’s the great defect of your character, Mr. Franklin, that you only drink with your dinner, and never touch a drop afterwards!”

“But the birthday was a special occasion. I might have abandoned my habits.”

He considered, searching his memory with that slow, honest precision of his—like a cook who tastes again, for certainty, what he has already judged.

“You did go out of your habits,” he said at last. “And I’ll tell you how. You looked wretchedly ill. We persuaded you to take a drop of brandy-and-water to cheer you.”

“I am not used to it. It is quite possible—”

“Wait a bit. I knew you weren’t used too. I poured you out half a wineglass of our fifty-year-old Cognac—real noble liquor, sir—and (more shame to me!) I drowned it in near a tumbler-full of cold water. A child couldn’t have got drunk on it—let alone a grown man.”

He spoke of that Cognac as people speak of old harvests, as if time had been stored in it and could be wasted by careless dilution; and his very indignation helped me. I could trust his palate and his memory. If Betteredge said no man could be made drunk on it, then drunkenness was not my refuge.

I tried the second question, the one I had kept back like the sharpest knife.

“Before I was sent abroad, Betteredge, you saw a great deal of me when I was a boy. Tell me plainly—do you remember anything strange of me after I had gone to bed? Did you ever find me walking in my sleep?”

He stopped again, looked at me steadily, nodded once as if he had caught the scent of my thought, and resumed his walk.

“I see your drift now,” he said. “You’re trying to account for how you got the paint on your nightgown without knowing it. It won’t do, sir. You’re miles away from the truth. Walk in your sleep? You never did such a thing in your life.”

I knew, even as he spoke, that he must be right. My life had never been solitary enough for such a habit to escape notice. At home, abroad—always there had been eyes enough, voices enough, rooms shared, servants passing, friends close at hand. Yet still, with a kind of miserable obstinacy that was not reason but mere self-defence, I clung to those two explanations—drunkenness or sleep-walking—because they were the only ones that allowed me to remain, in my own sight, the man I believed myself to be.

Betteredge, who could read a man’s stubbornness in his shoulders, broke both my props with a bluntness that was almost mercy.

“Let’s try it another way, sir. Keep your own opinion and see where it takes you. If we’re to believe the nightgown—which I don’t for one—you not only smeared the paint off the door without knowing it, but you also took the Diamond without knowing it. Is that right so far?”

“Quite right. Go on.”

“Very good. Say you were drunk, or asleep on your feet, when you took the jewel. That accounts for that night and the next morning. But how does it account for what’s happened since? The Diamond’s been taken to London since then. The Diamond’s been pledged to Mr. Luker since then. Did you do those things without knowing it too? Were you drunk when I saw you off in the pony-chaise that Saturday evening? Did you walk in your sleep to Mr. Luker’s when the train had brought you up? Excuse me saying it, Mr. Franklin, but this business has upset you. You’re not fit yet to judge for yourself. The sooner you lay your head alongside Mr. Bruff’s head, the sooner you’ll see your way out of the dead-lock you’ve got into.”

We reached the station with barely time. I gave him my London address, told him to write if needful, and promised to send him any news I could.

Just as I turned to bid him goodbye, my eye caught the book-and-newspaper stall. There, like an odd figure repeated by a dream that will not be shaken off, stood Mr. Candy’s assistant again—the man with the piebald hair. He was speaking to the stall-keeper. He looked up; our eyes met in the same instant. Ezra Jennings lifted his hat to me. I returned the salute and got into a carriage as the train began to move.

It was a relief—ridiculous as it was—to fasten on anything that seemed, personally, of no importance. The rattle of the wheels, the iron singing under us, the chorus of stations swallowed and flung behind, all made a kind of travelling music; and in that music my mind kept catching, again and again, on the absurdity that I should see the man twice in one day. Yet even that thought had its use: it diverted me from the more dreadful repetition—Rachel’s face, Rachel’s silence, and the white linen with the smear of paint that looked, to my fancy, like a stain that had soaked through to the bone.

I reached London too late to find Mr. Bruff in chambers. I drove to his private house at Hampstead and disturbed him dozing in his dining-room—his favourite pug on his lap, a bottle of wine at his elbow, the air warm with that comfortable domestic odour of a well-kept room: polished wood, cloth, old paper, and the faint, civilised sharpness of wine waiting to be poured.

I told my story. When I had finished, he showed me what he thought, not by words at first, but by what he did. He ordered lights and strong tea into his study; and he sent a message to the ladies of his family that we were not to be disturbed on any pretence.

Then he examined the nightgown, as if it were an exhibit laid out on a table in court; and after that he read Rosanna Spearman’s letter through from beginning to end. Only when the paper was folded up again did he speak.

“Franklin Blake,” he said, “this is a very serious matter—in more respects than one. In my opinion it concerns Rachel quite as nearly as it concerns you. Her extraordinary conduct is no mystery now. She believes you have stolen the Diamond.”

The words, cool from his lips, struck like a hand on a bruise. I had shrunk from arriving at that conclusion by my own reasoning; but the conclusion had been waiting for me all the same. My resolve to see Rachel rested, in truth, on that ground.

“The first step in this investigation,” Mr. Bruff went on, “is to appeal to Rachel. She has been silent from motives which I, who know her character, can readily understand. But it is impossible to submit to that silence any longer. She must be persuaded to tell us—or she must be forced to tell us—on what grounds she bases her belief that you took the Moonstone. The chances are that the whole case, serious as it seems now, will tumble to pieces if we can only break through Rachel’s inveterate reserve and make her speak.”

“That is comforting for me,” I said; and the bitterness broke through. “I should like to know—”

“You would like to know how I justify it,” he interposed. “I can tell you in two minutes. Understand, in the first place, I look at this from a lawyer’s point of view. It is a question of evidence. Very well. The evidence breaks down at the outset on one important point.”

“On what point?”

“You shall hear. I admit the name-mark proves the nightgown to be yours. I admit the paint-mark proves the nightgown made the smear on Rachel’s door. But what evidence is there to prove that you are the person who wore it on the night when the Diamond was lost?”

The objection—simple, plain, deadly—fell exactly where my own mind had felt the weakness, without daring to seize it. Mr. Bruff took up Rosanna’s letter again.

“I can understand,” he continued, “that this confession is distressing to you. I can understand you may hesitate to analyse it impartially. I am not in your position. I can bring my professional experience to bear on it as on any document.

“Without alluding to the woman’s career as a thief, I will merely remark that her own letter proves her an adept at deception; and I argue from that that I am justified in suspecting she has not told the whole truth. I won’t start any theory yet; I will only say this. If Rachel has suspected you on the evidence of the nightgown only, the chances are ninety-nine to a hundred that Rosanna Spearman showed it to her. In that case, we have Rosanna’s own admission that she was jealous of Rachel; her own confession that she changed the roses; her own acknowledgment that she saw a glimpse of hope for herself in the prospect of a quarrel between Rachel and you.

“I don’t stop to ask who took the Moonstone—as a means to her end, Rosanna Spearman would have taken fifty Moonstones. I only say that the disappearance of the jewel gave this reclaimed thief, who was in love with you, the opportunity of setting you and Rachel at variance for the rest of your lives. She had not decided on destroying herself then, remember; and having the opportunity, I distinctly assert that it was in her character, and in her position, to take it. What do you say to that?”

“Some such suspicion crossed my mind,” I admitted, “as soon as I opened the letter.”

“Exactly. And then you pitied the poor creature, and couldn’t find it in your heart to suspect her. Does you credit, my dear sir—does you credit!”

“But suppose it turns out I did wear the nightgown? What then?”

“I don’t see how the fact can be proved,” he answered. “But assuming the proof possible, the vindication of your innocence would be no easy matter. We won’t go into that now. Let us wait and see whether Rachel hasn’t suspected you on the evidence of the nightgown only.”

“Good God—how coolly you talk of Rachel suspecting me!” I burst out. “What right has she to suspect me, on any evidence, of being a thief?”

“A very sensible question,” said Mr. Bruff. “Rather hotly put—but worth considering. What puzzles you puzzles me. Search your memory and tell me this. Did anything happen while you were staying at the house—not to shake Rachel’s belief in your honour, of course—but to shake her belief, no matter with how little reason, in your principles generally?”

The question opened, like a trap-door, on something I had forgotten in the worse confusion that followed. I started to my feet, the blood rushing to my face.

There had been an incident—wretched enough at the time, and now more wretched for the uses it might have been put to. I told him.

I had been foolish enough—straitened for money, as I so often was—to accept a loan in Paris from the keeper of a small restaurant, a man who knew my tastes and my face as well as he knew his own kitchen. I could remember the place as one remembers a dish: the steam of stock in the back, the sharp scent of vinegar and chopped herbs, the crust of bread always warm as if it had just been broken, the talk in a quick dialect that made even quarrelling sound like singing. A time had been fixed for repayment; and when the time came I found myself, like thousands of honest men, unable to keep my engagement. I sent him a bill. My name was unfortunately too well known on such papers; he failed to negotiate it. His affairs fell into disorder; bankruptcy stared him in the face; and a relative of his—a French lawyer—came to England to find me and insist on payment.

He was violent; and he took the wrong way with me. We quarrelled loudly. My aunt and Rachel were in the next room. Lady Verinder came in and insisted on knowing what it meant. The Frenchman produced his credentials and declared me responsible for the ruin of a poor man who had trusted to my honour. My aunt paid him on the spot and sent him away. She knew me too well to take his view, but she was shocked at my carelessness—and justly angry that I had placed myself in a position which, but for her, might have become disgraceful.

Whether Rachel heard, or her mother told her, I could not say. Rachel took her own romantic, high-flown view. I was “heartless,” “dishonourable,” without “principle”—there was “no knowing what I might do next.” The breach lasted the whole of the following day. The day after, I made my peace and thought no more of it.

Had she remembered it, at the critical moment when her faith in me was assailed again, and far more terribly?

Mr. Bruff answered at once, gravely.

“It would have its effect on her mind. And I wish, for your sake, it had not happened. Still, we have discovered there was a predisposing influence against you. That clears one uncertainty out of the way. I see nothing more we can do now. Our next step must be the step that takes us to Rachel.”

He rose and walked thoughtfully up and down the room, the carpet taking his footfall into itself, the kettle in the next room beginning to murmur as if the house, too, had a voice to contribute. Twice I was on the point of telling him I had resolved to see Rachel myself; twice I held back, unwilling to thrust my own impatience upon his age and his habits of caution.

“The grand difficulty,” he resumed, “is how to make her show her whole mind without reserve. Have you any suggestions?”

“I have made up my mind,” I said, “to speak to Rachel myself.”

“You!” He stopped and stared as if I had proposed to conduct the case by throwing dice. “You, of all the people in the world!”

He checked himself, took another turn, and then his face altered with that quick professional honesty which can change its plan when a new chance appears.

“Wait a little,” he said. “In cases of this extraordinary kind, the rash way is sometimes the best way.”

He weighed it for a minute. Then he decided.

“Nothing venture, nothing have. You have a chance in your favour which I don’t possess—and you shall be the first to try the experiment.”

“A chance in my favour?” I repeated.

For the first time since my arrival, his face softened into a smile.

“This is how it stands,” he said. “I tell you fairly I don’t trust your discretion, and I don’t trust your temper. But I do trust in Rachel still preserving, in some remote little corner of her heart, a certain perverse weakness for you. Touch that—and trust the consequences for the fullest disclosures that can flow from a woman’s lips. The question is—how are you to see her?”

“She has been a guest of yours,” I answered. “May I suggest—if nothing is said about me beforehand—that I might see her here?”

“Cool!” said Mr. Bruff; and with that single comment he began pacing again.

“In plain English,” he resumed, “my house is to be turned into a trap to catch Rachel, with a bait to tempt her, in the shape of an invitation from my wife and daughters. If you were anybody else but Franklin Blake, and if this matter was one atom less serious than it is, I should refuse point-blank. As things are, I firmly believe Rachel will live to thank me for turning traitor to her in my old age. Consider me your accomplice. Rachel shall be asked to spend the day here; and you shall receive due notice.”

“When? Tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow won’t give time to get her answer. Say the day after.”

“How shall I hear from you?”

“Stay at home all the morning and expect me to call.”

I thanked him with all the gratitude I felt—gratitude made fierce by fear. I declined his kind invitation to sleep at Hampstead and returned to my lodgings.

Of the next day I can only say it was the longest day of my life. Innocent as I knew myself, certain as I was that the vile imputation must sooner or later be cleared away, there was still in me a sense of self-abasement which made me shrink from every friend’s face. We hear, commonly from superficial observers, that guilt can look like innocence. I believe the truer axiom is that innocence can look like guilt. I caused myself to be denied to every caller, and only ventured out when night had muffled the streets and softened their harsh, incessant London sound—the clatter and shout and rumble that seemed, in those hours, to grind every private thought into the same public dust.

The next morning Mr. Bruff surprised me at the breakfast-table. He placed a large key in my hand and announced, with the dry candour of a man who dislikes his own stratagems even when they serve justice, that he felt ashamed of himself for the first time in his life.

“Is she coming?” I asked.

“She is coming today—to lunch, and to spend the afternoon with my wife and my girls.”

“Are Mrs. Bruff and your daughters in the secret?”

“Inevitably. But women, as you may have observed, have no principles. My family don’t feel my pangs of conscience. The end being to bring you and Rachel together again, my wife and daughters pass over the means employed as composedly as if they were Jesuits.”

“I am infinitely obliged to them. What is this key?”

“The key of the gate in my back-garden wall. Be there at three this afternoon. Let yourself in; make your way through by the conservatory door. Cross the small drawing-room, and open the door in front of you into the music-room. There you will find Rachel—and find her alone.”

“How can I thank you?”

“I’ll tell you how. Don’t blame me for what happens afterwards.”

With that he left me.

The hours that remained were as long as my vigil the day before. To occupy myself I opened my letters. One was from Betteredge. I tore it open, hungry for any word from home; and found, to my disappointment, an apology warning me to expect no news of importance.

In the next sentence the everlasting Ezra Jennings appeared again—like a refrain returning in spite of the singer. He had stopped Betteredge on the way out of the station and asked who I was. On being told, he mentioned having seen me to his master, Mr. Candy. Mr. Candy, hearing it, had driven over himself to Betteredge to express regret at our missing each other. He had a reason, he said, for particularly wishing to speak with me; and when I was next near Frizinghall, he begged I would let him know.

Apart from Betteredge’s affectionate philosophy, that was the substance. The faithful old man confessed, in his own way, that he had written “mainly for the pleasure of writing” to me.

I crumpled the letter and thrust it into my pocket. Even the odd recurrence of Ezra Jennings could not hold my mind then. Everything in me flowed towards the coming interview with Rachel as surely as all kitchen smells find their way, sooner or later, to the one open door.

As Hampstead church struck three, I fitted Mr. Bruff’s key into the lock of the gate in the garden wall.

When I first stepped into the garden, and turned to secure the door behind me, I felt—shameful as it was—a guilty doubtfulness about what might happen next. I looked furtively to either side, suspecting an unseen witness, some lurking eye, some ear eager for scandal.

But there was no one. The walks lay empty. The only observers were the birds shifting in the shrubs and the bees busy in their small commerce with the flowers. Their humming made a soft, steady undertone, as if the place had its own harmless industry and could not understand the harsher traffic of human motives.

I crossed the garden, entered the conservatory, and passed through the small drawing-room. As my hand went to the door opposite I heard a few plaintive chords on the piano within—idly struck, tentative, like a voice trying its own note before it speaks. She had often touched the instrument so when I stayed at her mother’s—half music, half thought.

I was obliged to wait, to steady myself. Past and present rose together, side by side, and the contrast shook me as no accusation had done. After a minute I forced my courage into place, like a man setting a dish before guests though his hands tremble.

I opened the door.
