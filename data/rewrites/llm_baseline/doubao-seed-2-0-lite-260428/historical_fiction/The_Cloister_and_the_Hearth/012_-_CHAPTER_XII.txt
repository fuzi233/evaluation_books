Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, burgomaster of Tergou, kept the iron key to Gerard Eliassoen’s cell tucked deep in the fur-lined pouch at his belt, and bided his time until the town steeple tolled ten full chimes before he made his way to the prison stairs. “A little hunger softens even the stiffest-necked lad,” he’d muttered to his trusted armed servant Dierich Brower, certain a half-day on nothing but cold cell air would have broken the young scholar’s stubborn resolve.
He crept up the winding damp stone steps, paused to press his ear to the oak cell door, and heard no sound at all: no huffed complaints, no shuffling of feet, no plea for water. A grim, satisfied smirk tugged at his jowly, weathered face. “By now he will be as down-hearted as Albert Koestein was,” he thought, recalling the man who’d once dared contest Ghysbrecht’s land claims, and broken in that very same cell.
He turned the key in the lock, swung the door open, and stood stock-still.
The cell was empty.
For a long breath he did not move at all, his heavy wool cloak still swirled half-around his legs, then a violent tremor wracked his frame so hard his silver signet ring clattered against the door latch. Dierich, alarmed, pressed close to peer over his shoulder, and gaped at the sight before him: no Gerard, only a frayed rope braided from torn blanket strands dangling from the outer window ledge, and the iron bar that had once barred the window wrenched clean out of its mortar. He yelped in loud surprise, but his master paid him no heed at all. Ghysbrecht flung himself on his knees before the heavy iron-bound oaken chest that sat in the cell’s far corner, his quivering hands patting every inch of its surface wildly, as if refusing to trust his eyes that the secret lock he’d guarded for twenty years had been sprung, and the chest gaped wide, completely empty.
“Master, what ails you?” Dierich asked, bewildered. “The lad’s not in that box, look—see the rogue’s cunning? He pried the bar free and climbed out—”
“GONE. GONE. GONE,” Ghysbrecht gasped, his pale lips working but barely forming sound.
“Gone? Holy saints, the man’s planet-struck!”
“STOP THIEF!” Ghysbrecht shrieked suddenly, spinning to grab Dierich by the collar of his doublet and shaking him so hard his wool cap tumbled off his head. “D’ye stand there gawping while your master is robbed? Run! Fly! A hundred crowns to the man who brings it back to me! No—no, it’s too late. Oh, fool, fool that I was, to leave that chest in the same room with him. No soul ever found its secret spring before. None ever would have, but him. It was fated. Lost! All lost!”
Age and his chronic wheezing caught up with his short-lived frenzy then, and he sank heavily down onto the edge of the empty chest, muttering the word “lost” over and over under his breath.
“What is lost, master?” Dierich asked, half-sympathetic, half-curious.
The sharp, eager tone of the question cut through Ghysbrecht’s haze of despair, and his natural cunning snapped back to him. He darted a sidelong, furtive glance at his servant, like a fox caught mid-sneak near a henhouse, and stammered out a lie. “The town’s official record parchments. That is what I lost.”
“Oh, is that all?” Dierich huffed, relieved.
“Is that not enough?” Ghysbrecht snapped, regaining his bluster. “What will the burghers say? What will they do to me? A hundred crowns, mind you, for every last scrap of parchment that was in this chest. If even one is missing, you get nothing.”
“Then the crowns are as good as mine,” Dierich grinned. “Wherever Gerard Eliassoen is, those scuffed sheepskins you value so high will be too. I’ll round up four constables this instant, march to his father the hosier’s house, and seize him for the theft.”
“Theft!” Ghysbrecht’s eyes lit up, the perfect excuse no one would dare question. “Aye, theft! That’s the charge. Drag him down to the lowest dungeon, the one where the toads creep and the rats breed. That man will never see daylight again. It is his own fault, for prying where he had no business. Be quick, before he has time to talk, before he can tell anyone what he found.”
Less than half an hour later, Dierich and his four constables were pounding on the door of the hosier’s cottage, and demanded the boy Gerard be handed over. Gerard’s mother Catherine, white-faced and trembling, cried out that the boy would break her heart, that no one had seen him since he was taken to the prison.
Dierich softened his tone into a smooth, patronizing lie to calm her. “Nay, dame, it is nothing but a foolish prank of youth. He ran off with a handful of old town parchments for a lark, no doubt. The burgomaster is only anxious to get them back, that is all. The lad will get no worse than a sharp reprimand, you may rest easy.”
Catherine bought the story entirely, but Gerard’s lame little sister Kate, sharpened by years of loving and protecting her brother, saw the hungry, greedy glint in Dierich’s eyes, and her suspicion spiked higher still when he flew into a violent, frustrated rage the moment he confirmed Gerard had not been home all night, was nowhere in the house.
“Come on then,” he snarled at his men. “We are wasting time. I will hunt that boy down if he is still breathing above ground.”
As he turned to leave, Kate signaled to him that she wished to speak in private. He sent his constables ahead out the gate, and waited for her by the lintel.
“Hush,” she whispered, glancing back over her shoulder to make sure her mother could not hear. “Gerard has fled Tergou for good. I saw him at the foot of the old haunted tower last night. He swore once they locked him in that cell, he would never set foot in this town again. He is many leagues across the border by now. The burgomaster got his wish: he imprisoned the boy, drove him from his birthplace. What need have you now to drag our good name through the mud for a handful of worthless old skins?”
Dierich was too mortified at the loss of his hundred crowns to pause and see the sense in her words. “Worthless trash?” he snapped back. “Then likely enough the little thief tossed them straight in the nearest brook or pigsty before he left, eh? The sheepskins you scorn are worth more to me than the skin of any fool in Tergou.” He turned on his heel and stormed off, chasing the false scent that Gerard had fled Holland entirely.
Kate hurried back inside, pulled her hunchback brother Giles aside, and whispered her secret. “I lied to Dirk. Gerard is no leagues away. He has gone to Sevenbergen, to the girl he loves. The men chasing him mean him harm, not just a scolding, and I am lame, I cannot run there to warn him. Giles, dear Giles, you are strong. Will you not go for me?”
Giles grumbled that he hated to travel on foot, but when she pressed him if he loved Gerard, who had always been kindest to him of all their siblings, he puffed out his broad chest. “Aye, I like him best of the lot. I will borrow Peter Buyskens’ mule. I will ride out the west gate first, so all the gossips think I am heading for the northern market, and not turn the mule’s head south for Sevenbergen until I am well clear of the town walls.”
Kate made him repeat her warning message over and over, word for word, until he had it memorized perfectly, then saw him mount the mule and set off down the road. When he was out of sight, she sank to her knees beside the cottage hearth, and prayed long and earnestly for her brother’s safety, until the last of the peat embers burned low.
