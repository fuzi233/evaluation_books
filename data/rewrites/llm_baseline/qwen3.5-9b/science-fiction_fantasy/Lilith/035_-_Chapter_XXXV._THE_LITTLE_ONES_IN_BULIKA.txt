The dawn broke cool and clear over the vast, emerald plain, painting a picture of bold intent against the azure sky. Our departure was a spectacle of movement and life; we intended to traverse the distance during the morning hours, pause for rest in the afternoon, and resume our march under the cover of darkness. By following this rhythm—traveling at night, resting by day—we aimed to slip into the city gates just as the first light of morning touched the horizon, arriving unseen until discovery was inevitable.

It seemed the entire forest population had decided to accompany us. A vanguard of birds soared ahead, convinced in their innocence that they led the procession, while swarms of butterflies, wasps, and dragonflies danced around our heads. Behind us trailed a host of four-legged creatures. Yet, as night fell, most of these terrestrial followers deserted us, save for the avian and insect companions who remained loyal, fluttering alongside us all the way to the city's threshold.

We halted for a deep, undisturbed sleep through the afternoon. Though this marked our first true military march, fatigue was unknown to us. That night, driven by the chill air, we pressed forward with greater speed. Many of the children drifted off to sleep directly on the backs of their mounts, awakening in the morning refreshed and vigorous; not a single rider tumbled from their perch. Some rode shaggy, lumbering bears that somehow maintained a pace equal to the mighty elephants. Others were mounted on various species of deer, eager to race the entire journey—a feat I had to restrain them from attempting. Perched atop the hay-stacks of the elephants, unable to see the massive beasts beneath them, the riders chatted incessantly with their invisible mounts. One little voice, drawing hay from a trough, cooed affectionately to his "dear beast": *"Nosy dear, I am digging you out of the mountain and shall soon descend to you. Be patient; I'm coming! Very soon now you'll send up your trunk to look for me, and then we'll kiss like good elephants!"*

That same night erupted a cacophony of trumpeting elephants, neighing horses, and imitating children, echoing far across the silent plains. Uncertain of the city's proximity, I quickly silenced the uproar to avoid alerting our enemies.

Suddenly, one morning, the sun and the city seemed to rise simultaneously. To the children, the fortress walls appeared merely as a colossal mass of rock. However, when I explained that within those stones lay countless nests, a wave of apprehension—and genuine fear—for the first time in their brief lives swept through their hearts. The place looked ominous; how could they expect to find mothers amidst such gloom? Nevertheless, they marched on bravely, buoyed by their faith in Lona and in me, though I felt unworthy of such trust.

We passed through the resonant archway. Never before had such a rhythmic drumming of hooves and padding of paws echoed upon ancient stone. The horses startled at the reverberation of their own footsteps; some hesitated, others plunged wildly and spun about, but they were soon calmed and resumed their course. The Little Ones shivered, falling into a silence as profound as death. The three girls clutched tighter to the infants they carried. While the bears and butterflies showed no fear, every other creature manifested dread. Upon the woman's face sat a dark anxiety, mirrored in my own soul. The entire army rested on my shoulders and weighed heavily on my conscience; I had led these innocents straight into the shadow of danger.

Yet, I was sustained by the vision of the coming Kingdom of the Little Ones—a realm where the bad giants would serve as slaves and the animals as devoted friends. Alas, the dreamer had failed to learn obedience. Untrusting and obstinate, I had placed myself at the head of this army of innocents, myself nothing more than a slave, much like the kings I had left behind who acted solely according to their whims. Beside me rode Lona, a child indeed, and therefore truly free—calm, silent, watchful, and utterly fearless.

We were nearing the heart of the city before any inhabitant noticed our arrival. Then, windows began to open, and sleepy heads peered out. Initially, every face wore a blank stare of bewildered astonishment, which instantly curdled into consternation upon spotting the animals. Despite their terror, seeing that our invaders were almost exclusively children prompted the women to rush into the streets, followed closely by the men. For a time, however, they kept to the periphery of the buildings, daring not approach the beasts.

At length, a boy of about five, consumed by thoughts of his mother, spotted a woman in the crowd whose features captivated him. From his antelope mount, he threw himself onto her, clinging to her neck. She returned his embrace warmly, but a man's hand swiftly came over her shoulder, seizing the child by the throat. Instantly, a girl thrust her sharp spear into the man's arm. He let out a savage howl and, stabbed repeatedly by others, fled screaming.

"They are just bad giants!" Lona declared, her eyes flashing as she drove her horse against a particularly tall man who, spurred by latent aggression, barred her path with a club. Daring not withstand the impact, he slunk aside and collapsed moments later, struck down by several stones. Another huge figure, dodging my charger, stepped abruptly between me and the boy riding behind. The boy commanded him to address the King; the giant responded by striking the boy's small horse on the head with a hammer, sending it crashing to the ground. Before the brute could deliver another blow, an elephant from behind trampled him flat, keeping him down until hundreds of feet had passed over him and our army had moved on.

But at the sight of the women, dismay clouded Lona's face. Hardly one possessed a pleasant appearance. Could her darlings possibly find mothers among such figures?

Hardly had we halted in the central square when two girls rode up in anxious haste, reporting that two boys had been snatched away by some women. We turned immediately and discovered that the woman we had befriended had vanished with her baby. Simultaneously, a white leopardess bounded down a narrow lane leading from the square toward the palace. The Little Ones recalled the earlier battle with the two leopardesses in the forest; some looked terrified, and their ranks began to waver, but they remembered my command and stood firm.

Stopping to observe, we witnessed a small boy named Odu, renowned for his speed and courage, leap from his shambling bear. Having heard me speak of the white leopardess's kindness, he ran to meet her. To avoid knocking him down, the leopardess pulled herself up so abruptly that she rolled over and over; when she regained her footing, she found the child securely on her back. Who could doubt the power of a people witnessing an enemy's urchin astride a creature they lived in daily terror of? Confident this display would influence the whole army, we rode onward.

As we stopped at the house designated by our guides, a scream pierced the air. I sprang down and pounded on the door. My horse nudged me away, turned, and began battering the entrance with his heels. Just then, little Odu arrived on the leopardess's back. Seeing her, he froze, trembling violently. But she too had heard the cry; forgetting the child on her back, she threw herself against the door. The boy was dashed against the wood and fell unconscious. Before I could reach him, Lona caught him in her arms. As he regained consciousness, she placed him back on his bear, which continued to follow faithfully.

When the leopardess slammed against the door for the third time, it gave way, and she darted inside. We followed, but she had already vanished. Springing up the stairs and searching the house thoroughly, we found no one. Descending again, we spotted a door beneath the staircase that led into a labyrinth of excavations. We ventured in briefly before encountering the leopardess carrying the missing child across her back. He informed us that the woman he believed to be his mother had thrown him into a hole, intending to give him to the leopardess. Fortunately, the leopardess was kind and had retrieved him.

Following leads to locate the second boy, we entered the adjacent house more easily, only to discover, with heavy hearts, that we were too late: one of the savages had just slain the little captive. This news, however, consoled Lona, for she had feared he would grow into a bad giant; death had spared him that fate. The leopardess springed upon the murderer, gripped him by the throat, and dragged him into the street, trailing Lona behind like a cat clutching a large rat.

"Let us leave this horrible place," Lona said. "There are no mothers here! This people is not worth saving."

The leopardess dropped her burden and charged into the densest parts of the crowd. Slaves screamed and scattered, tumbling over one another in heaps. Returning to the main army, we found them exactly as we had left them: standing in perfect order, ready for action. Yet, unease settled deeply within me. The Princess gave no sign, and her intentions remained a mystery. Vigilance was required throughout the night.
