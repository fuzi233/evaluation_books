There is a peculiar chill that settles over a collector when the boundary between commerce and the supernatural begins to blur. Some years ago, I recounted an adventure involving a friend named Dennistoun, whose quest for antiquities for Cambridge led him into dealings with the uncanny. That tale did not remain secret within the university circles; it reached the ears of a colleague, Mr. Williams, who presided over the art museum at another institution. For a man dedicated to the acquisition of English topographical drawings, Dennistoun's experience served as a grim warning, yet it also offered a strange comfort: Williams needed not ancient manuscripts, a domain belonging to the Shelburnian Library, but merely the expansion of his existing collection of prints and engravings. Even in this seemingly mundane sphere, however, darkness lurked.

Mr. Williams relied heavily on the services of Mr. J. W. Britnell, a London dealer whose catalogs were the bible of his trade. While Britnell offered a vast array of items, Williams purchased sparingly, seeking only to fill specific gaps in his ranks rather than acquiring rarities. In February of a recent year, a catalog from Britnell arrived on Williams's desk, accompanied by a typewritten note regarding Item No. 978.

*"No. 978. Unknown. Interesting mezzotint: View of a manor-house, early part of the century. 15 by 10 inches; black frame. £2 2s."*

The description was unremarkable, and the price, steep for such a common object, raised an eyebrow. Nevertheless, Williams sent a postcard requesting the item "on approval," alongside other sketches from the same volume. The parcel arrived a day later, via the Saturday afternoon post, and was delivered to Williams's collegiate rooms by the attendant.

Upon opening the package at tea-time, accompanied by a friend whom we shall call Professor Binks, Williams examined the mezzotint. It was a mediocre print, depicting a modest manor house of the previous century, featuring three rows of sashed windows, rusticated stonework, and a central portico. Trees flanked the sides, and a broad lawn stretched before the facade. Inscribed on the narrow margin was *A. W. F. sculpsit*. To Williams, it appeared the work of an amateur, puzzling him as to why Britnell valued it at two guineas. Turning the frame over, he discovered a paper label where half the text had been torn away. Only fragments remained: *"ngley Hall"* and *"ssex."*

"It must be Somethingley Hall, either in Sussex or Essex," Williams murmured, reaching for a gazetteer. "Half the name is missing. If the vowel before 'ng' had survived, identification would be simple."

Binks, a fellow golfer, leaned forward. "What's this place, Williams?"

"Just trying to locate it," Williams replied, consulting the book. "Britnell wants two guineas for it. It's a wretched engraving, lacking any figures to give it life."

"The lighting is surprisingly effective," Binks noted, peering closely at the glass. "I thought I saw a figure, perhaps just on the edge."

Williams squinted. "Yes, there it is. Hardly more than a black blot on the extreme edge—the head of a man or woman, muffled, back turned, gazing at the house."

Williams, who had not noticed it initially, felt a prickle of unease. "Though it's better than I thought, I cannot spend museum funds on a picture of a place I do not know."

As dusk fell, Williams lit candles and prepared tea. The conversation drifted to golf, the universal solvent of academic stress, until Binks departed. Alone, Williams devoted hours to identifying the location. "Guestingley" to "Langley"—the gazetteer offered no index of terminations, frustrating his search.

By seven o'clock, Hall time, Williams joined his colleagues in the dining hall. Words of golf strategy were exchanged freely, though beneath the surface lay a mounting anxiety. Later, in the common room, Williams inadvertently passed the mezzotint to a mild-mannered acquaintance, describing its provenance. The visitor, casually examining the print, declared, "It is a very good piece of work... The light is admirably managed, and the figure, though grotesque, is somehow very impressive."

By midnight, the visitors had dispersed. Williams, clearing his desk, extinguished his lamp. As he blew out the flame, the mezzotint, lying face-upward, caught his eye. A sudden, visceral dread seized him. If he had remained in the dark, he feared a fit, but he struck a match and stared.

Impossible. Absolutely certain.

Where no figure had existed at five o'clock, now stood a creature crawling on all fours toward the manor. It was draped in a strange black garment bearing a stark white cross on its back.

Williams's reaction was immediate. He grabbed the frame, carried it to a second set of rooms, and locked it securely in a drawer. He barred the doors of both chambers and retired to bed, drafting a formal account of the phenomenon. Sleep eluded him briefly, but the knowledge that his companion from the previous night had seen the same apparition offered a sliver of sanity. Two tasks demanded attention in the morning: verify the image with a witness and finally identify the house.

He summoned his neighbor, Nisbet, for breakfast. The meal was a typical Sunday affair, filled with banter on tennis and cricket, masking Williams's disturbed state. When the pipe smoke cleared, Williams revealed the truth. Trembling with excitement, he produced the picture from the drawer.

"Now, Nisbet, describe exactly what you see," Williams urged.

Nisbet, careful and precise, described a country house viewed by moonlight. "The moon is waning, clouds drift overhead," Nisbet confirmed. "Three rows of windows..."

"But the figures?" Williams pressed.

"There are none," Nisbet stated firmly. "But—"

"What!" Williams exclaimed. "No figure on the grass?"

"Not a thing."

"You'll swear to that?"

"Certainly." Nisbet paused. "However, one window on the ground floor, to the left of the door, is open."

Williams rushed to inspect the print. It was undeniable. The window was ajar. "He must have gotten in," Williams whispered, his voice tight. "Two guineas is not an exorbitant sum after all."

They drafted statements, confirming the discrepancy. Nisbet suggested photography to preserve evidence before the image vanished again. "It looks like a tragedy unfolding," Nisbet observed. "Has it happened, or is it coming?"

Williams proposed a plan: take the print to Senior Fellow Green, who might recognize the estate, and consult Garwood, the previous night's visitor, regarding the figure. Before departing, Williams entrusted the print to Nisbet, instructing him to guard it like a hawk.

Returning shortly after, Williams brought Garwood. The former student recalled the figure clearly positioned away from the edge, noting the white mark on the back, though uncertain if it was a cross. A joint statement was signed, and Nisbet captured the scene.

"We shall watch it all day," Williams decided, locking the door and barring the entrance. "Between my sighting last night and this morning, time was sufficient for many things, yet the creature entered the house. The open window suggests it remains inside."

They waited until five o'clock. Upon arrival, they found the door unlatched—a minor annoyance attributed to the skip's early routine. Inside, the picture leaned against a stack of books. But the atmosphere had shifted.

Standing beside them, staring at the frame with undisguised horror, was Mr. Filcher, Williams's chief servant. Filcher, a man of rigid etiquette, sat on a chair, transfixed. Startled by their presence, he rose abruptly, apologizing profusely.

"I ask your pardon, sir," Filcher stammered. "Of course I do not set up my opinion against yours, but it ain't the picture I'd hang where my little girl could see it, sir."

"Why not, Robert?" Williams asked gently.

"No, sir. Why, the poor child, I recall once seeing a Door Bible with horrors half as bad, and we sat up three nights afterward... If she catches sight of this skeleton here, carrying off the poor baby, she'll be in a taking. You know how children are. Nervous with a little thing and all. It doesn't seem a right picture to be laying about, sir."

Filcher bowed and retreated, leaving the three gentlemen to circle the frame. The scene had transformed again. The moon still shone, the clouds drifted, but the window that had been open was now shuttered tight. The figure, previously crawling, now stood erect, moving swiftly with long strides toward the manor. Behind it, the moon cast a shadow. Its black drapery obscured most of its features, revealing only a pale, dome-like forehead and a scattering of hair. The head was bent low, arms tightly clasped around an object that appeared to be a child—whether living or dead was impossible to discern. Yet the legs were horrifyingly thin, bony things that seemed to strain against the fabric.

For hours, they sat in silence, watching by turns. From five to seven, the tableau held static, frozen in time. Finally, they agreed it was safe to depart, promising to return after Hall.
