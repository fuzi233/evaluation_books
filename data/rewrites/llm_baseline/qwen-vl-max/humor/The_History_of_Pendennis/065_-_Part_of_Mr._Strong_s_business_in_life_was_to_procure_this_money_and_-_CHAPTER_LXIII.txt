The more Major Pendennis learned about the Clavering family—through Strong, and through his own discreet inquiries as a friend of the household—the more he began to wonder if he’d ever really wanted to help Arthur at all. The idea of marrying Arthur to Lady Clavering’s daughter was beginning to look less like a romantic solution and more like a financial disaster wrapped in lace and embroidery. After all, what kind of father-in-law would you want? One who’d been convicted of murder (though conveniently vanished), and another whose name had a footnote like “sus. per coll.” after it, as though he’d been erased from history by a particularly spiteful clerk?

Now, Amory might be a charming rogue, but if he ever stepped foot back under British law, he’d probably end up on the gallows with a crowd cheering. And Altamont? Well, Altamont was a man who seemed to live in a perpetual state of near-discovery, always one step ahead of justice—and just far enough behind decency to make anyone with half a mind think twice before introducing him into polite society.

And yet, even if the Begum were to pay off Clavering’s debts ten times over, she’d still be left with nothing but an empty purse and a husband who couldn’t stop signing his name to bills like a man possessed. Her heirs would inherit a title and a scandal, not a fortune. And Miss Amory? She’d bring her husband not a seat in Parliament, but a pedigree so tarnished it looked like someone had tried to scrub out the last male ancestor with sandpaper.

But then again, there was another option—something the old schemer hadn’t quite ruled out yet. Something that might come to light if one happened to overhear a conversation between Major Pendennis and the honourable Baronet himself, Member for Clavering.

When a man gets into financial trouble, he doesn’t just disappear—he *vanishes*. Like a puff of smoke from a cigar, or a bad decision from a man’s memory. I once knew a gentleman of fashion, known for his fine horses and his impeccable waistcoats, who vanished from the clubs and parks and turned up one day, perfectly cheerful, at an eighteen-penny ordinary in Billingsgate. Another fellow—very clever, very witty, and possibly a literary man (though some critics would say that alone is proof of guilt)—sent me his address while on the run: “The Fox under the Hill,” tucked away in a dark archway in the Strand. He was hiding, yes—but also dining, drinking, and laughing, as though nothing had happened.

Such men have homes, but they’re never home. They have addresses, but only fools go there hoping to find them. Only a few loyal souls know where to look. So when Sir Francis Clavering started arguing with his wife and losing money faster than a gambler on a bad night, finding him became impossible. “Ever since I got him for my book—fourteen pounds—he don’t come home till three o’clock,” Mr. Lightfoot told Morgan. “Pretends to be asleep when I bring his water in the morning, and dodges out when I’m downstairs.” Then he announced he’d be moving to Lady Clavering’s house as butler and marrying the old woman—clearly a man who believed in loyalty, even if he didn’t practice it.

In much the same way, after his quarrel with Strong, the Baronet avoided him like a man avoiding a tax collector—or conscience. He fled to hidden corners, changed his habits, and hoped no one would notice. But the truth is, when you’ve done something shameful, you don’t just move—you hide. And sometimes, you hide so well that even your own valet thinks you’re dead.

So when Major Pendennis decided he must speak with this elusive representative of the House of Commons, it took more than one attempt. He invited him to dinner at Greenwich—“a few friends,” he said, as though the entire gathering would consist of him, a plate of whitebait, and a silent waiter. The Baronet accepted, then failed to appear. The Major ate alone, which was hardly a tragedy, but it did leave him feeling slightly absurd.

Another time, the Major wrote a note: “Ten minutes’ talk?” The Baronet replied instantly: “Four o’clock tomorrow, Bays’s. Precisely.” He underlined “precisely” like a man afraid of being late. But when four o’clock came, so did the inevitable silence. No Clavering. Not even a ghost.

It was clear: this was not a man to be found. It was a man to be hunted.

Just before sunset, on the day when Strong had given the Chevalier a blessing in front of him and a curse behind, Sir Francis Clavering—having sworn on his knees to his wife’s lawyers that he would accept no more bills—signed a piece of stamped paper anyway. Moss Abrams, his trusted friend and accomplice, carried it off, promising it would be “done” by someone with connections. And Strong heard about it—right in the back parlour of Mr. Santiago’s cigar shop, where the Chevalier spent most evenings pretending to read poetry while actually listening to gossip.

“He’s at it again,” Mr. Santiago told Strong. “Clavering and Abrams were in my parlour. Moss sent my boy for a stamp. Must’ve been fifty pounds. The Baronet told him to date it two months back. He’ll pretend it’s an old bill, the one he forgot during the settlement. I bet they’ll give him more money now.”

Ah, the joys of being a man whose signature is passed around like a novelty at a party. When you’re constantly writing “I promise to pay” on pieces of paper, you become famous—not in the good way, but in the way people whisper about you in dark corners.

Mr. Santiago’s shop was just off St. James’s Street, near where Major Pendennis lived. That evening, as the Major walked toward his lodgings with the dignity of a man who has never lost a fight, Strong strode past him, glowing with rage and smelling faintly of Havana.

“Confound these young men,” thought the Major. “They poison everything with their smoke. Here comes one with mustachios and a cigar. Every man with a cigar and mustachios is a low fellow. Oh! It’s Mr. Strong. I hope you are well?”

He bowed with the grace of a man who believes he’s doing a favour by speaking to someone who isn’t a duke.

Strong stopped. “Bad news, sir,” he said. “About our friends at Tunbridge Wells. Clavering’s at it again.”

“Indeed!” said the Major, suddenly interested. “Come in. Come into my lodging.”

They entered. The Major sat. Strong unburdened himself. “No promises bind him. Remember when we met with Lady Clavering’s lawyer? He wouldn’t settle for ‘my word’—he had to swear on a Bible, ring the bell for it, and threaten perdition. Now he’s signed another bill. And will sign more. He’ll deceive anyone—his wife, his child, his best friend. There’s a bill due next week—mine and his.”

“I thought we paid all.”

“No,” Strong said, blushing. “He asked me not to mention it. I had half the money ready. But they’ll come down on me. I don’t care—I’m used to it. It’s Lady Clavering I feel for. That good-natured woman, who’s paid him out of prison a dozen times, ruined by his heartlessness. A bunch of rogues get his money. And he throws honest men over. Would you believe it? He took money from Altamont.”

“Altamont?” asked the Major, feigning ignorance. “That singular man who came tipsy once to Sir Francis’s house?”

“I don’t know who he is,” said Strong, suspiciously.

“Ah,” said the Major, “I suppose—I mean, I only suppose—that in our friend Clavering’s life—well, let’s say he’s a rather loose fish. And such men often have secrets. Perhaps this Altamont knows something about Clavering. Has leverage. Gets paid for silence. I know men of the best families who pay through the nose that way. But it’s none of my business. I dine with a man, I don’t pry into his past. I’m interested in Lady Clavering’s sake. In her daughter’s. She’s a charming creature. When she asked me to look into things, I did. And I’ll do it again, if I can be useful. But—by the way—this Mr. Altamont. Do you know him? Is he in town?”

“I don’t know that I’m called upon to know where he is,” said Strong, rising sharply and grabbing his hat.

The Major’s tone shifted instantly from condescension to warm camaraderie. “Ah, Captain Strong, you’re cautious. Quite right. We don’t know what ears walls may have. As a man of the world, and an old soldier—a distinguished soldier, I’ve been told—you know better than to waste your fire. You may have your ideas; I may put two and two together. But there are things a man shouldn’t know. Things I won’t know until I have reason. And I believe that’s your maxim too.”

“Certainly,” said Strong, dryly.

“Delighted to hear it. Delighted that an old brother soldier agrees with me. And how lucky this meeting is! Good evening. Thank you. Morgan, show the door to Captain Strong.”

Strong left, baffled. And Morgan, standing silently by the door, equally baffled. For Morgan had heard nearly all of it—and understood almost none.

Later, Morgan asked Lightfoot: “Who is this Altamont? Know anything about him and Strong?”

“Strong’s his man of business,” said Lightfoot. “Draws the Governor’s bills, indosses ’em, does odd jobs. Altamont’s in it too. Kite-flying takes three men to start. Altamont bet on the Derby and won. I wish the Governor could win something. I’d like my book paid.”

“Do you think Lady Clavering will pay again?” Morgan asked.

“Find out,” said Morgan. “And I’ll make it worth your while.”

Morgan was rich. Not because he was a nobleman, but because he was a valet with a head for numbers. Over the past year, he’d quietly accumulated a tidy sum—fifty or a hundred pounds—by buying up Sir Francis Clavering’s bills, knowing full well that Lady Clavering would pay them. He’d learned the trade from other valets, who whispered secrets like “This man’s wife pays his debts” or “That one’s bankrupt but still lives like a king.”

When Arthur Pendennis came into his inheritance, Morgan approached him with a request: “I’ve a little sum—perhaps fifty or a hundred pounds. Could you help me invest it? The gentlemen in the Temple know about affairs and business. I’d be most grateful.”

Arthur laughed. “I know nothing about money.”

Morgan, unfazed, said, “Then I shall be most grateful anyway. And if you ever need a little cash before your rents come in, perhaps you’ll remember your uncle’s faithful servant has some money he’d like to lend.”

Arthur nearly snapped back—*Borrow from my uncle’s valet?*—but caught himself. The joke was too good. And maybe Morgan wasn’t joking at all. So he declined politely and told his uncle.

The Major chuckled. “I always said Morgan was richer than I am. He bought the house in Bury Street—where I’m a lodger—and made a fortune trading in Clavering’s paper. He’s a dev’lish sly fellow. And a dev’lish rich one. Many a nobleman would love such a valet.”

On the day of Strong’s visit, the Major remembered Morgan’s wealth and decided to tease him.

“I hear you’ve got money to invest, Morgan,” he said.

“It’s Mr. Arthur who’s been telling,” Morgan thought.

“I’m glad my place is such a good one.”

“Thank you, sir,” Morgan replied. “I’ve no complaints.”

“You’re a good fellow. And I’m glad you’re doing well. Just don’t go starting a public-house.”

A public-house? Morgan thought. Me? The old fool. If I were ten years younger, I’d set up in Parliament before I died. “No, sir. I’ve got my savings safely invested.”

“You do a little discounting, eh?”

“Yes, sir. A very little.”

“Might I ask—about Sir Francis Clavering’s paper? Do you think he’s any good? Will Lady Clavering pay anymore?”

“What? You’ve already done something?”

“A little, sir. Having some information, and acquaintance with the family through your kindness, I laid my money on. Bought his bills—many with his name and Mr. Strong’s. And I know about the blow-up in Grosvenor Place. So if you could tell me whether Lady Clavering will pay again…”

The Major stared. He’d never imagined his valet was a financier. But then again, he admired men who made money. Even if they did it under his nose.

“Well, Morgan,” he said, “I can’t ask how rich you are. The richer, the better. And if I can help you, I will. But frankly—if Lady Clavering asks me whether to pay more, I’d advise against it. Though I fear she will. And you know Sir Francis is back to his reckless ways?”

“At his old games, sir. Can’t stop him.”

“Mr. Strong mentioned Moss Abrams held a bill. Do you know him? The amount?”

“Don’t know the bill. Know Abrams well.”

“I wish you’d find out. And tell me where I can see Sir Francis.”

“Thank you, sir. Yes, sir. I will.”

Morgan left. The Major sat, stunned.

Next morning, Morgan returned: “I saw Abrams. The bill’s for fifty pounds. And Sir Francis will be at the Wheel of Fortune Tavern at one o’clock.”

At one o’clock, the Baronet sat in the tavern’s back room, surrounded by spittoons, boxing prints, and the lingering smell of tobacco. He was reading Bell’s Life in London, stained with beer.

And then in walked the Major.

“So it’s you, old boy?” said Clavering, thinking it was Abrams.

“How do you do, Sir Francis? I wanted to see you. Followed you here.”

Clavering’s face fell.

Now that he had his man, the Major went straight to work.

“I know,” he said, “who you’re here for. And why.”

“It ain’t your business,” Clavering muttered. “Why are you following me? Meddling? I’ve never done you harm. Never borrowed your money. I don’t choose to be domineered over. If Lady Clavering wants to talk, let it be through lawyers. I’d rather not have you.”

The Major smiled. “You’re not going to escape me, my dear Sir Francis. Not today.”
