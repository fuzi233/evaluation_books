The invisible architecture of habit sustained them; the frivolous chatter of the ballroom erupted automatically from their lips, and the performance of joy remained etched permanently upon their features. Consequently, no soul within Lennon House that evening suspected that a swift, catastrophic severance had fractured the bond between them. Harry Feversham watched Ethne laugh and converse as though burdened by no cares, perpetually startled by her vitality, failing to recognize that he wore the same mask of forced gaiety. When she swept past him, the light, rhythmic cadence of her feet nearly convinced him that her heart beat in time with the dance. It appeared she could command the flush of color upon her cheeks with mere will. Thus, they both donned brave visages, honoring her silent bid. They even danced together. Yet, throughout, Ethne remained acutely conscious of the immense load of pain and humiliation she carried, a weight destined soon to crush her spirit, while Feversham felt the four feathers burning fiercely against his breast. It astounded him that the entire assembly remained ignorant of their shared disgrace. He never approached a partner without the chilling intuition that she would turn upon him, spitting the contemptuous epithet that hung ready on her tongue. Yet, he felt no terror regarding this possibility. He would scarcely have cared had the word been spoken aloud. He had already lost Ethne. He watched her, scanning the crowd in vain for a fitting counterpart, knowing full well he would find none. There were women, certainly—pretty, graceful, even beautiful—but Ethne stood apart, defined by the unique character of her radiance. The broad forehead, the perfect arch of her eyebrows, the great steady, clear grey eyes, the full red lips capable of dimpling into tenderness or shutting level with iron resolve, and the royal grace of her carriage marked her out to Feversham's thinking, a distinction that would hold true in any company. He watched her with a despairing amazement that he had ever possessed a chance of owning her.

Only once did her endurance falter, and then merely for a fleeting second. She was dancing with Feversham, and as she gazed toward the windows, she perceived that the daylight beyond the blinds was turning pale and cold. "Look!" she whispered, and Feversham suddenly felt the totality of her weight pressing upon his arms. Her face drained of color, growing tired and ash-grey. Her eyes squeezed shut tightly before opening again. He feared she would faint. "The morning at last!" she exclaimed, and then, in a voice as weary as her countenance, "I wonder whether it is right that one should suffer so much pain." "Hush!" Feversham breathed. "Courage! A few minutes more—only a very few!" He halted and stood before her until her strength returned. "Thank you!" she said gratefully, and the bright wheel of the dance caught them in its spokes once more. It was peculiar that he should be exhaling courage while she thanked him for aid; yet the irony of this brief, queer reversal of their positions escaped both of them. Ethne was too exhausted by the strain of those final hours, and Feversham, having learned from that singular failure of her endurance, from the drawn aspect of her face and the profound depths of pain in her eyes, realized how deeply he had wounded her. He no longer muttered, "I have lost her"; he ceased to think of his loss entirely. He heard her words, "I wonder whether it is right that one should suffer so much pain." He felt they would ring down the world with him, persistent in his ears, spoken with the very accent of her voice. He was certain he would hear them at the end, rising above the clamor of any who might stand beside him when he died, hearing in them his ultimate condemnation. For it was not right.

The ball concluded shortly thereafter. The last carriage departed, and those remaining in the house retreated to the smoking-room or ascended to their bedrooms, segregated by gender. Feversham, however, lingered in the hall alongside Ethne. She understood the necessity. "There is no need," she said, standing with her back to him as she kindled a candle, "I have told my father. I told him everything." Feversham bowed his head in acquiescence. "Still, I must wait and see him," he stated. Ethne did not object, though she turned to look at him quickly, her brows drawn in a frown of perplexity. To await her father under such circumstances seemed to demand a certain fortitude. Indeed, she herself felt a flicker of apprehension as she heard the study door open and Dermod's footsteps echoing on the floor. Dermod strode straight up to Harry Feversham, appearing for once truly aged, and stood there staring into Feversham's face with a muddled, bewildered expression. Twice he opened his mouth to speak, but no words emerged. In the end, he turned to the table, lighting his own candle and Feversham's. Then he turned back toward Feversham, moving somewhat quickly—so quickly that Ethne stepped forward as if to interpose herself; yet he did nothing more than stare at Feversham again, for a prolonged duration. Finally, he lifted his candle. "Well—" he said, and stopped. He snuffed the wick with the scissors and began again. "Well—" he said, and paused once more. Clearly, his candle had offered no illumination for suitable expressions. He stared into the flame now instead of into Feversham's face, for an equal length of time. He could conceive of nothing whatsoever to say, yet he was acutely conscious that something must be uttered. In the end, he said lamely: "If you want any whiskey, stamp twice on the floor with your foot. The servants understand." Thereupon, he walked heavily up the stairs. The old man's forbearance was perhaps not the least component of Harry Feversham's punishment.

It was broad daylight when Ethne was finally alone within her room. She drew up the blinds and flung the windows wide. The cool, fresh air of the morning struck her like a draught of spring water. She looked out upon a world as yet unilluminated by vibrant colors, finding therein an image of her days to come. The dark, tall trees loomed black; the winding paths appeared a singular, dead white; the very lawns were dull and grey, though the dew lay upon them like a network of frost. It was a noisy world, however, despite its aspect of quiet. For the blackbirds were calling from the branches and the grass, and down beneath the overhanging trees, the Lennon flowed in music between its banks. Ethne drew back from the window. She had much to accomplish that morning before sleep claimed her. For she designed, with her natural thoroughness, to bring an immediate end to all her associations with Harry Feversham. She wished that from the moment she next awoke, she might never encounter a single thing capable of recalling him to her memory. And with a sort of stubborn persistence, she proceeded with the work. But she changed her mind. In the very process of gathering together the gifts he had bestowed upon her, her resolve shifted. For each gift she beheld carried its own history, and the days preceding this miserable night, which had darkened her happiness, returned one by one slowly to her as she looked upon them. She determined to retain one item that had belonged to Harry Feversham—a small thing, a thing of negligible value. Initially, she chose a penknife, which he had once lent her and which she had forgotten to return. But the next instant, she dropped it and withdrew rather hurriedly. For she was, after all, an Irish girl, and though she did not subscribe to superstitions generally, where such matters were concerned, she preferred to err on the side of caution. She selected his photograph in the end and locked it away in a drawer. She gathered the remainder of his presents together, packed them carefully into a box, fastened it, addressed it, and carried it down to the hall so the servants might dispatch it in the morning. Then, returning to her room, she took his letters, making a small pile of them on the hearth, and set them alight. They took some time to consume, but she waited, sitting upright in her armchair while the flame crept from sheet to sheet, discoloring the paper, blackening the writing like a stream of ink, and leaving in the end only flakes of ashes like feathers, and white flakes like white feathers.

The last sparks were barely extinguished when she heard a cautious step on the gravel beneath her window. It was broad daylight, yet her candle still burned on the table at her side, and with a quick, instinctive movement, she reached out her arm and snuffed the light. Then she sat very still and rigid, listening. For a while, she heard only the blackbirds calling from the trees in the garden and the throbbing music of the river. Afterward, she heard the footsteps again, cautiously retreating; and in spite of her will, in spite of her formal disposal of the letters and the presents, she was mastered all at once—not by pain or humiliation, but by an overpowering sense of loneliness. She seemed to be seated high upon an empty world of ruins. She rose quickly from her chair, and her eyes fell upon a violin case. With a sigh of relief, she opened it, and a short while later, one or two of the guests sleeping in the house chanced to wake and heard floating down the corridors the music of a violin played very lovingly and low.
