XII. In the old-fashioned world of New York, dinner was served at seven, and the custom of after-dinner calls—though mocked by some in Newland Archer’s circle—still held sway across much of society. As he walked up Fifth Avenue from Waverley Place, the wide street lay quiet and nearly empty, save for a cluster of carriages drawn up before the Reggie Chiverses’ house, where a dinner was being given for the Duke. Occasionally, an elderly gentleman wrapped in a heavy overcoat and muffler would appear, climb the steps of a brownstone, and vanish into a gas-lit foyer. Passing through Washington Square, Archer noticed old Mr. du Lac visiting his cousins, the Dagonets; further along, near West Tenth Street, he saw Mr. Skipworth, a partner in his own firm, clearly on his way to call on the Miss Lannings. A little higher up, Beaufort emerged from his home, silhouetted sharply against the glow of his doorway, descended to his private brougham, and drove off into the night toward a destination that seemed secretive, perhaps even scandalous. It wasn’t Opera night, nor was anyone hosting a soirée—so Beaufort’s outing must have been something more private, more intimate.

Archer couldn’t help but connect it with a small house beyond Lexington Avenue, recently spruced up with bright window curtains and flower boxes. The canary-yellow brougham of Miss Fanny Ring often waited there, suggesting a certain romantic intrigue. Beyond the polished, predictable sphere of Mrs. Archer’s social world lay another realm—the uncharted territory of artists, musicians, and “people who wrote.” These individuals had never sought inclusion in the rigid structure of high society. Though they might be considered odd or unpredictable, they were generally respectable in their own way. They preferred solitude, keeping to themselves and their craft.

Once, Medora Manson had attempted to create a literary salon, but it fizzled out quickly—no one in the literary world wanted to attend. Others tried too, most notably the Blenkers: a loud, intense mother and her three flamboyant daughters, all mimicking her mannerisms. There, one could meet Edwin Booth, Patti, William Winter, George Rignold, magazine editors, and critics. But Mrs. Archer and her friends viewed such people with cautious unease. They were strange, uncertain, carrying secrets in their pasts that no one quite understood. Literature and art were respected in their circles—Mrs. Archer often lamented how much richer society had once been when it included figures like Washington Irving, Fitz-Greene Halleck, and the poet of *The Culprit Fay*. Those men had been gentlemen. But the new generation of writers—unknown, unconventional, often linked to the stage or opera—defied the old standards. Their appearance, their lifestyles, their very hair made them impossible to judge by the old rules.

“Back when I was young,” Mrs. Archer used to say, “we knew everyone between the Battery and Canal Street. Only those we knew had carriages. You could always place someone. Now? No one is what they seem. And I’d rather not try.”

Only old Catherine Mingott, with her lack of moral judgment and her almost vulgar disregard for fine distinctions, might have bridged that gap. But she never read a book or admired a painting—music mattered only because it reminded her of gala nights at the Italiens during her glory days at the Tuileries. Perhaps Beaufort, bold and daring as she was, could have brought about a fusion—but his grand mansion and silk-stockinged footmen stood in the way of anything informal. Besides, he was as illiterate as Mrs. Mingott and saw “fellows who wrote” as mere entertainers, hired to amuse the rich. No one wealthy enough to challenge this view ever had.

Newland Archer had known all this since childhood. He accepted it as part of the world he lived in. Yet he also knew other places—where painters, poets, novelists, scientists, and great actors were as admired as dukes. He often dreamed of living in rooms filled with conversation from Mérimée, Thackeray, Browning, or William Morris. But such dreams were impossible in New York. They unsettled him just to think of.

He knew many of these artists and writers—he met them at the Century Club or at the emerging musical and theatrical societies. He enjoyed them there, but found them tedious at the Blenkers’, where they were paraded like exotic curiosities among fervent, dowdy women. Even after stimulating conversations with Ned Winsett, he always left feeling that both worlds were narrow—and that the only way to expand either was to reach a point where they naturally merged.

This thought returned to him now as he approached Madame Olenska’s door, wondering what kind of society she had lived in, suffered in, and perhaps—secretly—enjoyed. He remembered her amusement when she told him how her grandmother Mingott and the Wellands objected to her living in a “Bohemian” quarter full of “people who wrote.” It wasn’t the danger they feared—it was the poverty. But she hadn’t understood that nuance; she assumed they thought literature itself was compromising. She herself had no such fear. Her drawing-room, where books usually belonged only in libraries, was scattered with novels by Paul Bourget, Huysmans, and the Goncourt brothers—names unfamiliar yet intriguing to Archer.

As he neared her door, he felt again the peculiar way she turned his values upside down. To truly help her now, he had to imagine a life utterly different from his own.

Nastasia opened the door with a mysterious smile. On the hall bench lay a sable-lined overcoat, a folded opera hat of dull silk bearing a gold monogram—J.B.—and a white silk muffler. There was no mistaking whose belongings they were: Julius Beaufort’s.

Archer felt a surge of anger—so strong he nearly scribbled a note on his card and walked away. Then he remembered: in his letter to Madame Olenska, he had held back from saying he wished to speak privately. So if she received others, it was his own discretion that had left her open to visitors. He stepped inside, determined to make Beaufort feel unwelcome—and to stay longer than him.

Beaufort leaned against the mantelshelf, draped with antique embroidery held by brass candelabras filled with yellow church candles. His chest thrust forward, shoulders braced, one large patent-leather shoe planted firmly. He smiled down at Madame Olenska, who sat half-reclined on a sofa angled toward the fireplace. Behind her, a table overflowed with flowers—orchids and azaleas, clearly gifts from Beaufort’s greenhouses. Her head rested on one hand, her sleeve wide open, leaving her arm bare to the elbow.

Evening guests typically wore “simple dinner dresses”—tight, whalebone-silk gowns with lace ruffles at the neck and tight sleeves flaring slightly to reveal a bracelet or velvet band. But Madame Olenska ignored tradition. She wore a long red velvet robe trimmed with glossy black fur around her chin and down the front. Archer recalled seeing a portrait by Carolus Duran in Paris—a bold, sheath-like gown with fur framing the face. It was oddly defiant to wear fur indoors in winter, especially with a muffled throat and bare arms. Yet the effect was striking.

“Lord love us—three whole days at Skuytercliff!” Beaufort boomed, sneering. “You’d better take your furs and a hot-water bottle.”

“Why? Is the house so cold?” she asked, holding out her left hand to Archer in a gesture that subtly invited a kiss.

“No,” Beaufort replied, nodding casually to the young man. “But the missus is.”

She smiled faintly. “I thought her so kind. She came herself to invite me. Granny says I must go.”

“Granny would, of course,” Beaufort said. “And I say it’s a shame you’re missing the oyster supper I’ve planned for you at Delmonico’s next Sunday—with Campanini, Scalchi, and a lot of jolly people.”

She hesitated, glancing between them. “Ah—that does tempt me! Except for the other evening at Mrs. Struthers’s, I haven’t met a single artist since I’ve been here.”

“What kind of artists?” Archer asked boldly. “I know a few painters—very good fellows—I could bring if you’d allow me.”

“Painters?” Beaufort scoffed. “Are there painters in New York?”—as if their existence depended solely on whether he bought their work.

Madame Olenska turned to Archer, her eyes soft. “That would be charming. But I was thinking of dramatic artists—singers, actors, musicians. My husband’s house was always full of them.”

She said “my husband” without hesitation, almost wistfully, as if recalling lost pleasures.

Archer looked at her, puzzled. Was it lightness—or deception—that let her speak so freely of the past while risking everything to escape it?

“I do think,” she continued, addressing both men, “that the *imprévu* adds to one’s enjoyment. It’s perhaps a mistake to see the same people every day.”

“It’s confoundedly dull,” Beaufort grumbled. “New York is dying of dullness. And when I try to liven it up, you go back on me. Come—think better of it! Sunday is your last chance. Campanini leaves next week for Baltimore and Philadelphia. I’ve got a private room, a Steinway, and they’ll sing all night.”

“How delicious! May I think it over and write to you tomorrow morning?”

Her tone was polite, but carried the faintest hint of dismissal. Beaufort sensed it. Unused to being sent away, he stared at her, a stubborn line forming between his brows.

“Why not now?”

“It’s too serious a question to decide at this hour.”

“Do you call it late?” she countered coolly.

“Yes,” he said. “Because I still have business to discuss with Mr. Archer.”

“Ah,” Beaufort snapped.

There was no appeal. With a slight shrug, he regained composure, took her hand, kissed it with practiced ease, and called out from the doorway: “I say, Newland—if you can persuade the Countess to stay in town, you’re included in the supper.” Then he left, his heavy footsteps echoing down the hall.

For a moment, Archer thought Letterblair must have warned her of his coming. But her next words proved otherwise.

“You know painters, then? You live in their milieu?”

Her eyes sparkled with interest.

“Oh, not exactly,” he said. “I don’t think any art has a true milieu here. They’re more like a thinly settled outpost.”

“But you care for such things?”

“Immensely. When I’m in Paris or London, I never miss an exhibition. I try to keep up.”

She looked down at the tip of her satin boot peeking from beneath her robes. “I used to care immensely too. My life was full of such things. But now… I want to try not to.”

“You want to try not to?”

“Yes. I want to cast off my old life. To become just like everyone else here.”

Archer reddened. “You’ll never be like everyone else.”

She raised her eyebrows slightly. “Ah, don’t say that. If you knew how much I hate being different!”

Her face darkened, like a tragic mask. She leaned forward, clasping her knee, gazing into distant shadows. “I want to get away from it all.”

He cleared his throat. “I know. Mr. Letterblair told me.”

“Ah?”

“That’s why I’ve come. He asked me to—well, you see, I’m in the firm.”

She looked surprised, then her eyes brightened. “You mean you can manage it for me? I can talk to you instead of Mr. Letterblair? Oh, that will be so much easier!”

Her tone touched him. His confidence swelled. He realized she had mentioned business to Beaufort only to dismiss him—and to have driven Beaufort away felt like a small victory.

“I’m here to talk about it,” he repeated.

She sat silent, her head resting on her arm. Her face looked pale, dimmed by the deep red of her dress. Suddenly, she seemed fragile, almost pitiful.

“Now we’re coming to hard facts,” he thought, recognizing the same instinctive recoil he’d criticized in his mother and her generation. How little practice he had in dealing with such situations! The language itself felt foreign—like something from fiction or the theater. Facing what was to come, he felt as awkward as a boy.

After a pause, she spoke with sudden intensity: “I want to be free. I want to wipe out the past.”

“I understand.”

Her face warmed. “Then you’ll help me?”

“First—perhaps I should know more.”

She seemed surprised. “You know about my husband—my life with him?”

He nodded.

“Well—then—what more is there? In this country, are such things tolerated? I’m a Protestant—our church doesn’t forbid divorce in such cases.”

“Certainly not.”

They fell silent again. The specter of Count Olenski’s letter loomed between them. It filled only half a page—just as Archer had described it to Letterblair: a vague, angry accusation. But how much truth lay behind it? Only his wife could know.

“I’ve reviewed the papers you gave to Mr. Letterblair,” Archer said at length. “Well—can there be anything more abominable?”

“No.”

She shifted slightly, shielding her eyes with her hand. “Of course you know,” he continued, “that if your husband chooses to fight the case—as he threatens to—”

“Yes?”

“He can say things—things that might be… disagreeable to you. Say them publicly. They’d spread, and harm you—even if—”

“If—?”

“I mean: no matter how unfounded.”

She paused. So long that Archer studied the shape of her other hand, resting on her knee—the rings on her fourth and fifth fingers, none of which was a wedding band.

“What harm could such accusations do me here—even if he made them public?”

He almost cried out: “My poor child—far more harm than anywhere else!” Instead, he echoed Mr. Letterblair’s voice: “New York society is a very small world compared to the one you’ve known. And it’s ruled, despite appearances, by a few people with… rather old-fashioned ideas.”

She said nothing.

“Our ideas about marriage and divorce are particularly old-fashioned,” he went on. “Our laws favor divorce—our customs don’t.”

“Never?”

“Well—not if the woman, however injured, however innocent, has any appearance against her. If she’s exposed herself to offensive insinuations…”

She lowered her head slightly. He waited, hoping for outrage, a denial. Nothing came.

A tiny clock ticked beside her. A log cracked, sending sparks upward. The room seemed to hold its breath with him.

“Yes,” she murmured. “That’s what my family tells me.”

He winced. “It’s not unnatural—”

“Our family,” she corrected gently. “For you’ll soon be my cousin.”

“I hope so.”

“And you take their view?”

He rose, wandered across the room, stared blankly at a painting, then returned to her side. How could he say: *Yes—if what your husband hints is true, or if you can’t disprove it?*

“Sincerely,” she interjected.

He looked into the fire. “Sincerely, then—what would you gain that would compensate for the certainty of terrible talk?”

“But my freedom—is that nothing?”

At that moment, it flashed across him: the charge in the letter was true. She hoped to marry the man involved. How could he tell her that, if she meant to do that, the law of the state would stop her? The mere suspicion made him impatient.

“But aren’t you already free?” he said. “Who can touch you? Mr. Letterblair says the financial matter is settled—”

“Oh, yes,” she said indifferently.

“Well, then—is it worth risking pain and disgrace? Think of the newspapers—their cruelty! It’s all stupid and narrow and unjust—but one can’t change society.”

“No,” she whispered, her voice faint and broken.

He felt a pang of remorse. “The individual is often sacrificed,” he said, pouring out stock phrases, eager to cover the silence. “People cling to conventions—to protect families, children. It’s not personal. It’s collective.”

“It’s my duty,” he added, “to show you how those who love you see this. The Mingotts, the Wellands, the van der Luydens—all your friends. If I didn’t show you honestly how they judge such matters, wouldn’t that be unfair?”

He spoke urgently, pleading with her, desperate to avoid the abyss of her silence.

“No,” she said slowly. “It wouldn’t be fair.”

The fire had died to ash. One lamp gurgled softly. She rose, wound it up, and returned to the hearth—without sitting. Standing was a signal: there was nothing more to say.

Archer stood too.

“Very well,” she said abruptly. “I will do what you wish.”

Blood rushed to his forehead. Caught off guard, he grasped her hands clumsily. “I—I do want to help you.”

“You do help me. Good night, my cousin.”

He bent and kissed her hands—cold, lifeless. She pulled away. He turned, found his coat and hat under the dim gaslight, and stepped out into the winter night, bursting with words he could not speak.
