Viborg stands proudly among the towns of Jutland, holding a distinguished rank. It serves as a seat of a bishopric, boasting a magnificent cathedral that is nearly new, a delightful garden, a lake of surpassing beauty, and a multitude of storks. Nearby lies Hald, often cited as one of Denmark's prettiest locales, and close by is Finderup, the site where Marsk Stig slew King Erik Glipping on St. Cecilia's Day in 1286. Sixty-six blows of square-headed iron maces were later etched onto Erik's skull when his tomb was disturbed in the seventeenth century. Yet, I am not penning a travel guide.

While Viborg offers excellent accommodations—Preisler's and the Phoenix meet every standard of comfort—my cousin, the narrator of these tales, chose the Golden Lion for his first visit. He has not returned since, and the following account may elucidate why.

The Golden Lion is a rare survivor of the catastrophic fire of 1726, which levelled the cathedral, the parish church, the town hall, and countless other historic structures. It is a formidable edifice of red brick, featuring corbie steps atop its gables and a Latin inscription above the entrance. However, the courtyard accessible to the omnibus is constructed of black-and-white timber and plaster.

As twilight deepened, casting the golden light of the setting sun across the imposing façade, my cousin, Mr. Anderson, approached the door. He was charmed by the antiquity of the establishment and envisioned a thoroughly satisfying, albeit eerie, stay.

Anderson's journey to Viborg was not driven by mundane commerce. He was engaged in scholarly research into Danish ecclesiastical history, specifically seeking documents preserved from the fires regarding the final days of Roman Catholicism in the region. He intended to spend several weeks in the Rigsarkiv, studying and transcribing these archives, and he sought a suite large enough to serve as both bedroom and study.

Upon presenting his request to the landlord, a lengthy deliberation ensued. The landlord, contemplating the layout, suggested that Anderson inspect the larger chambers and select his own quarters. The attic floor was deemed impractical due to the exertion of climbing stairs after a day's labor. The second floor lacked a room meeting Anderson's precise dimensional needs. Thus, attention turned to the first floor, where two or three spacious options presented themselves.

Though the landlord strongly advocated for Room 17, Anderson noted that its windows overlooked only a blank wall, promising a gloomy afternoon. He preferred Rooms 12 or 14, which faced the street and offered a brighter vista, accepting the trade-off of increased street noise. Ultimately, Room 12 was chosen. It shared the characteristics of its neighbors: three windows aligned on one side, a fair height, and an unusual length. While devoid of a fireplace, it featured a handsome, aged cast-iron stove adorned with a depiction of Abraham sacrificing Isaac and the biblical citation "1 Bog Mose, Cap. 22" inscribed above. Aside from an old colored print of the town dating to circa 1820, the room held no other notable features.

Approaching supper time, Anderson, fresh from his ablutions, paused before descending the stairs to review the roster of fellow lodgers. As is customary in Denmark, guests' names were displayed on a large blackboard, organized into columns and lines, with room numbers marking the start of each entry. The list was unremarkable save for an advocate, a German traveler, and several baggage carriers from Copenhagen. The sole anomaly that warranted reflection was the conspicuous absence of Number 13 from the list. Having observed this omission repeatedly throughout his travels, Anderson wondered if a pervasive superstition rendered the leasing of such a room impossible. He resolved to inquire of the landlord whether professional peers often encountered refusals to occupy the thirteenth chamber.

He recounted nothing of the supper itself to me, though the evening, spent unpacking and organizing his belongings, passed without incident. Around eleven o'clock, Anderson decided to retire. For many, reading a few pages prior to sleep is a near-necessary ritual. He recalled that the specific volume he had been engrossed in aboard the train, and which alone suited his current mood, resided in the pocket of his greatcoat, hanging on a peg outside the dining hall.

Securing the book took but a moment. Since the corridors were not dark, returning to his door should have been straightforward. So, he believed. Yet, upon reaching the destination and grasping the handle, the door stubbornly refused to yield. From within, he discerned the sound of a hasty movement. Realizing instantly that he had mistaken the door, he hesitated: was his room to the right or left? He glanced at the numberplate affixed to the jamb. It read "13." His actual room lay to the left.

Before retiring, he read his accustomed three or four pages, extinguished his candle, and turned over. Then, a realization struck him: whereas the hotel's register omitted Number 13, a physical door bearing that number undeniably existed within the house. A pang of regret washed over him. Had he chosen it, he might have served the landlord a kindness, proving to the world that a well-born English gentleman could reside comfortably therein for weeks. It was likely, however, utilized as a servant's chamber or similar utility, perhaps lacking the dimensions or quality of his own suite. He surveyed the room in the dim glow of the streetlamp; it appeared strangely contracted in length and unnaturally lofty compared to daylight impressions. Sleep, he decided, outweighed such vague musings, and he succumbed to rest.

The following day, Anderson assaulted the Rigsarkiv with vigor. He was received with warmth, and access to the archives was facilitated with alacrity. The documents awaiting him exceeded his expectations in both quantity and intrigue. Beyond official records, a substantial collection of correspondence pertained to Bishop Jörgen Friis, the final Roman Catholic incumbent of the see. These letters revealed numerous amusing and intimate particulars of private life and individual character. Frequent mention was made of a house owned by the Bishop but uninhabited by him; its tenant was described as a scandal and a stumbling block to the reformers. He was branded a disgrace to the city, accused of practicing secret and malevolent arts, and of having sold his soul to the enemy. This behavior was characterized as symptomatic of the gross corruption and superstition inherent in the Babylonish Church, prompting accusations that a viper and blood-sucking *Troldmand* (wizard) was being patronized by the prelate. Bishop Friis responded boldly to these reproaches, protesting his abhorrence of occult practices and demanding that his antagonists produce evidence before the appropriate spiritual courts to prove Mag Nicolas Francken's guilt.

Anderson had scarcely perused the subsequent letter from Protestant leader Rasmus Nielsen before the archives closed. Its tenor indicated that Christians were no longer bound by Papal decrees and that the Bishop's Court was ill-suited to adjudicate such a grave matter.

Exiting the office, Anderson was escorted by Herr Scavenius, the senior archivist. Though broadly knowledgeable, Scavenius admitted he was not a specialist in Reformation-era documents. He expressed keen interest in Anderson's findings and eagerly anticipated the future publication of Anderson's work. Regarding the Bishop's vacant residence, Scavenius confessed it was a profound puzzle. Despite meticulous study of old Viborg's topography, the crucial section of the 1560 terrier detailing the Bishop's properties had vanished from the archive. "Never mind," Scavenius remarked optimistically, "perhaps I shall eventually solve this riddle."

After exercising, Anderson returned to the Golden Lion for supper, a game of patience, and bed. En route to his chamber, he realized he had neglected to query the landlord regarding the missing Number 13 and needed to verify its physical existence before raising the topic. The decision was swift. The door bore its number plainly, and activity within suggested ongoing work; footsteps and voices drifted from inside. As he halted to confirm the digit, the sounds ceased abruptly, replaced by a sharp, hissing breath indicative of intense excitement. Proceeding to his own room, he marveled anew at how diminished it appeared compared to his initial selection. A slight disappointment, yet manageable; if the space proved insufficient, relocation was easily arranged.

He sought a pocket-handkerchief from his portmanteau, which the porter had ostensibly placed on a trestle stool at the far end of the room. Curiously, the trunk was nowhere to be seen. Officious servants had undoubtedly relocated it, presumably to the wardrobe, yet it remained absent. Dismissing the notion of theft, as such incidents were rare in Denmark, he attributed the disappearance to human error. He resolved not to disturb the staff, deciding to retrieve the item in the morning. Moving to the right-hand window, he gazed out onto the silent street. Opposite stood a tall, blank-walled building. The darkness was absolute, save for the shadow cast by his own form and those of his neighbors. To the left, the shadow of the occupant of Number 11 flickered as he paced in shirtsleeves, occasionally brushing his hair or changing into a nightgown. To the right, the silhouette of Number 13 leaned elbow-deep on the sill, peering outward. This figure appeared tall and slender, or perhaps female, draped in some fabric that obscured the head, illuminated by a faint, erratic red light suggestive of a flickering lampshade. A distinct pulsation of dull crimson danced upon the opposite wall. Craning forward, Anderson glimpsed only a fold of pale material on the sill before a distant footstep in the street
