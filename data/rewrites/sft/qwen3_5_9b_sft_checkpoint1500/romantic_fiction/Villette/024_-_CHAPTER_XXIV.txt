To those who dwell in seclusion, within the walls of schools or guarded retreats, the sudden and prolonged erasure from the memories of friends living in the free world is a common hazard. Without apparent cause, and often following a cluster of lively circumstances that should have spurred further contact, communication halts abruptly. A stony silence descends; the frequent letters cease, the periodic visits stop, and the tokens of remembrance vanish into oblivion. An unbroken, unexplained blank remains.

For the hermit, this void stretches interminably, while outside, the lives of friends whirl in the vortex of activity. For the isolated, time drags so heavily that clocks seem frozen and hours limp like weary travelers resting at milestones. Yet, in that same interval, the world outside teems with events, panting with haste for those left behind. A sensible hermit learns to swallow his thoughts and lock his emotions away during these winters of inward solitude. He accepts his role as a dormouse, curling into a tight ball against the drift of life which soon buries him in ice, safe for the season.

Perhaps, eventually, the snow-sepulcher opens. Spring returns with softness, and the sun warms the frozen heart. The budding hedges and singing streams call for a gentle resurrection. Or perhaps not; perhaps the frost penetrates the core and never thaws, leaving only bones for a crow to peck from the wall. Even then, it is all right. One must know from the start that death awaits, and "as well soon as syne" goes everyone.

Following that eventful evening at the theatre, seven weeks passed for me as blank as seven sheets of unwritten paper. No word, no visit, no token marked the pages.

By the middle of this desolate time, I fancied some disaster had befallen my friends at La Terrasse. For the solitary, the midpoint of a blank is a clouded point where nerves ache with strained expectancy. Doubts, previously repelled, gather into a mass and roll back with vindictive force. Night becomes unkind; sleep refuses agreement with nature. Strange starts and struggles harass the couch, joined by a sinister band of bad dreams headed by horror of calamity and a sick dread of total desertion. Poor wretch! I did my best to endure, yet remained pale and wasting despite my efforts.

Towards the end of these seven weeks, I finally admitted what I had jealously excluded for six: the conviction that these silences were inevitable. The result of circumstances, the fiat of fate, a part of my lot. Above all, a matter whose origin must never be questioned, nor whose painful sequence murmured against. I did not blame myself for suffering; I thanked God for a truer sense of justice than to indulge in self-accusation. Regarding others, my reason knew them blameless, and my heart acknowledged it, but the road was rough and heavy, and I longed for better days.

I tried various expedients to sustain existence: elaborate lace-work, studying German, and devouring the driest, thickest books in the library. In all, I was as orthodox as I could be. Yet, was there error somewhere? Likely. The result felt as if I had gnawed a file to satisfy hunger or drunk brine to quench thirst.

My hour of torment was the post-hour. I knew it too well, trying vainly to cheat myself of that knowledge. I dreaded the rack of expectation and the sick collapse of disappointment that followed the well-recognized ring. Animals kept in cages, starving on the verge of famine, await their food as I awaited a letter. Oh, to speak truth and drop the false calm that longs to sustain oneself beyond nature's endurance—in those seven weeks, I suffered bitter fears, strange inward trials, miserable defections of hope, and intolerable encroachments of despair. Despair crept so near that her breath seemed to pass right through me, a baleful air penetrating deep until the heart paused under unspeakable oppression. The beloved letter would not come; it was all sweetness in life I had to look for.

In extremity, I resorted again and again to the little packet in the case—the five letters. How splendid that month seemed whose skies had beheld the rising of these five stars! I visited them only at night, buying a wax taper and matches to light them, for I dared not ask for a candle in the kitchen. At study-hours, I stole up to the dormitory and feasted on my crust from the Barmecide's loaf. It did not nourish me; I pined on it, growing as thin as a shadow, yet not physically ill.

One evening, reading somewhat late, I felt the power to read slipping away. The letters, from incessant perusal, were losing all sap and significance; my gold was withering to dust before my eyes. Sorrowing over this disillusion, suddenly a quick, tripping foot ran up the stairs. I knew Ginevra Fanshawe's step. She had dined in town and was returning to replace her shawl and other items in the wardrobe.

Yes, she came in, dressed in bright silk, her shawl falling from her shoulders, her curls half-uncurled in the damp of night, drooping careless and heavy upon her neck. I had hardly time to recasket my treasures and lock them up when she was at my side, her humour seemingly poor.

"It has been a stupid evening: they are stupid people," she began.

"Who? Mrs. Cholmondeley? I thought you always found her house charming?"

"I have not been to Mrs. Cholmondeley's."

"Indeed! Have you made new acquaintance?"

"My uncle de Bassompierre is come."

"Your uncle de Bassompierre! Are you not glad?—I thought he was a favourite."

"You thought wrong: the man is odious; I hate him."

"Because he is a foreigner? or for what other reason of equal weight?"

"He is not a foreigner. The man is English enough, goodness knows; and had an English name till three or four years ago. But his mother was a foreigner, a de Bassompierre, and some of her family are dead and have left him estates, a title, and this name. He is quite a great man now."

"Do you hate him for that reason?"

"Don't I know what mamma says about him? He is not my own uncle, but married mamma's sister. Mamma detests him; she says he killed aunt Ginevra with unkindness: he looks like a bear. Such a dismal evening!" she continued. "I'll go no more to his big hotel. Fancy me walking into a room alone, and a great man fifty years old coming forward, and after a few minutes' conversation actually turning his back upon me, and then abruptly going out of the room. Such odd ways! I daresay his conscience smote him, for they all say at home I am the picture of aunt Ginevra. Mamma often declares the likeness is quite ridiculous."

"Were you the only visitor?"

"The only visitor? Yes; then there was missy, my cousin: little spoiled, pampered thing."

"M. de Bassompierre has a daughter?"

"Yes, yes: don't tease one with questions. Oh, dear! I am so tired."

She yawned. Throwing herself without ceremony onto my bed, she added, "It seems Mademoiselle was nearly crushed to a jelly in a hubbub at the theatre some weeks ago."

"Ah! indeed. And they live at a large hotel in the Rue Crécy?"

"Justement. How do *you* know?"

"I have been there."

"Oh, you have? Really! You go everywhere in these days. I suppose Mother Bretton took you. She and Esculapius have the *entrée* of the de Bassompierre apartments: it seems 'my son John' attended missy on the occasion of her accident—Accident? Bah! All affectation! I don't think she was squeezed more than she richly deserves for her airs. And now there is quite an intimacy struck up: I heard something about 'auld lang syne,' and what not. Oh, how stupid they all were!"

"*All!* You said you were the only visitor."

"Did I? You see one forgets to particularize an old woman and her boy."

"Dr. and Mrs. Bretton were at M. de Bassompierre's this evening?"

"Ay, ay! as large as life; and missy played the hostess. What a conceited doll it is!"

Soured and listless, Miss Fanshawe was beginning to disclose the causes of her prostrate condition. There had been a retrenchment of incense, a diversion or total withholding of homage and attention; coquetry had failed of effect, vanity had undergone mortification. She lay fuming in the vapours.

"Is Miss de Bassompierre quite well now?" I asked.

"As well as you or I, no doubt; but she is an affected little thing, and gave herself invalid airs to attract medical notice. And to see the old dowager making her recline on a couch, and 'my son John' prohibiting excitement, etcetera—faugh! the scene was quite sickening."

"It would not have been so if the object of attention had been changed: if you had taken Miss de Bassompierre's place."
