The scent of damp earth and decaying leaves clung to the air, a morbid perfume that accompanied the fragile threads of life in the House of Stone. Three more days, Trench had whispered in a fleeting moment of lucidity, his voice barely audible above the cacophony of suffering that filled the prison's cramped corridors. But on the following morning, his usually robust frame betrayed him, wracked by a fever that crept in like a thief in the night, leaving behind a trail of desolation.

As the sun climbed higher, Trench's condition worsened, and Feversham found himself shouldering the burden of caring for his friend. He had witnessed countless nights of the House of Stone's oppressive weight bearing down on the prisoners, yet he couldn't shake the feeling that fate had chosen this precise moment to strike Trench down, as if the mere promise of freedom had proven too great a strain on his exhausted body.

"It is nothing," Feversham insisted, trying to reassure Ibrahim, who stood by, his eyes clouded with concern. But deep within, Feversham harbored a different truth: Will the men with the camels wait? Each day, as he ventured to the Nile, he caught sight of Abou Fatma in his blue robe, stationed at his post, awaiting a response that never came. The silence hung heavy, a palpable entity that weighed upon Feversham's heart.

Together, Feversham and Ibrahim tended to Trench, coaxing him through the long, arduous hours with cooling draughts brewed from tamarinds, dates, and roots. The boy who brought the provisions each day – a young, quiet soul with an air of quiet devotion – was sent to fetch more nourishment for the starving family of Idris, and Trench's chains were removed, a small mercy in the midst of his suffering. His body was wrapped in camel-cloths, anointed with butter, and fed vegetable marrow soaked in salt water. The fever raged on, its grip tightening like a vice, until Ibrahim declared, on the seventh day, "This is the last. Tonight he will die."

But Feversham refused to accept the inevitable. "No," he said, his voice laced with a fierce determination. "That is impossible. 'In his own parish,' he said, 'beneath the trees he knew.'
