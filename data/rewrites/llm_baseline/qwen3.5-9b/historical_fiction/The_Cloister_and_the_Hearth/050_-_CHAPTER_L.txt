Amidst strangers, Margaret Brandt found a comparative happiness. Soon, however, a new and unforeseen source of joy emerged. A civic dignitary, plagued by illness and prone to fanciful notions, consulted doctor after doctor until he teetered on the brink of death. Desperate, he summoned Peter. Upon arrival, Peter discovered the patient reduced to nothing by excessive bleeding and purging. With decisive action, Peter threw a battalion of bottles out the window, leaving the casement open; he whisked egg yolks in neat Schiedam, administering them in measured doses, followed by meat stewed in red wine and water, seasoned with mild febrifuge herbs that caused no harm. Eventually, the patient recovered mobility, appearing somewhere between a man and a pillowcase. Being a voluble dignitary, he spread Peter's fame through every street. Thus, the artist, who had long deserved a reputation in vain, gained one rapidly by sheer luck.

Things appeared bright. The old man's pride was finally cheered, and his purse began to swell. Yet, he squandered much of his earnings on sovereign herbs and choice drugs, intending to invest them all in such remedies. Margaret, however, intercepted a portion. The victory came too late; the happy excitement proved fatal.

One evening, as he bid her goodnight, his voice sounded strangely inarticulate. By the next morning, he was found speechless, barely conscious. Margaret, who had served as her father's attentive pupil for years, immediately recognized the signs of a paralytic stroke. Trusting no one, she fetched a physician—one of those who had not been obstructed by Peter and thus had not harmed the previous dignitary. The doctor cheerfully confirmed her diagnosis and proposed bleeding the patient. Margaret refused. "He was always against bleeding," she stated firmly, "especially the elderly."

Peter survived, but he was never the same man again. His memory deteriorated significantly, rendering him unfit to prescribe. Several patients, eager to be cured by the new doctor exclusively, waited for his recovery. Misery stared Margaret in the face. Resolved to seek counsel and comfort, she turned to her cousin, William Johnson, from whom she had previously kept aloof due to pride and poverty.

She found him and his servant in the same room, neither improved by liquor. Mastering her surprise, she greeted them and announced she had come to discuss a family matter, glancing quietly at the servant as a subtle hint. The woman took the cue, but not as expected.

"Oh, you can speak before me, can she not, my old man?"

At this familiarity, Margaret flushed deeply. "I cry you mercy, mistress. I knew not my cousin had adopted the customs of this town. Well, I must take a fitter opportunity," she said, rising to depart.

"I wot not what ye mean by custom o' the town," the woman retorted, springing up. "But this I know: 'tis the duty of a faithful servant to keep her master from being preyed upon by his beggarly kin."

"You are too modest, mistress," Margaret replied sharply. "Ye are no servant. Your speech betrays you. 'Tis not till the ape hath climbed the tree that she shows her tail so plainly. Nay, there sits the servant; God help him! While this remains true, fear not that his kin will ever be so poor in spirit as to come where the likes of you can flout their charity." Casting a look of mute reproach at her cousin for remaining passive and silent throughout the exchange, Margaret turned and exited haughtily, refusing to shed a single tear until she reached home. There, she faced the grim reality: two men to lodge and feed, plus another mouth arriving into the world.

Yet, this last addition, though the most helpless, was their greatest ally. Nature was strong within Margaret Brandt—the same instinct that drives beasts, birds, and insects to cunningly provide food and shelter for their future progeny. Stimulated by this primal force, she sat and brooded, thinking intensely on how to preempt destitution. Even with five gold pieces remaining, she foresaw starvation approaching with inevitable steps.

When women deviate from custom to think with male intensity, they achieve results just as effective as men. Margaret rose, ordered Martin to move Peter to another room, tidied her own space meticulously, polished a glass globe, and suspended it from the ceiling. She dusted a crocodile figure and nailed it to the exterior wall. After instructing Martin, she assigned him to act as a lounging sentinel at the street door, telling passersby that a great, aged, and learned alchemist resided within, who occasionally amused himself during recreation by curing mortal diseases.

Patients soon arrived, demanding to see the leech. "That is impossible," Margaret declared. "He is deep in study, seeking the grand elixir; not even princes may speak with him. You must describe your symptoms and return in two hours." And oh, the mysterious power of suggestion! When they returned, the drug or draught was invariably ready. For worshipping patients, she would carefully examine their faces, check pulses and skins, and listen to their tales. Retreating softly to Peter's room, she would ponder how her father, whose methods she had long observed, would treat the case. She would then scribble an illegible note filled with cabalistic letters, bring it down reverently, and present it to the patient. "Can you read that?" she would ask. Usually, the response was either, "I am no reader," or, with admiration, "Nay, mistress, I understand naught of it."

"Ay, but I can. 'Tis sovereign. Consider yourself cured!" If she possessed the necessary materials—and she was too economical not to favor medicines from her own stock—she would sometimes allow the patient to watch her compound them, anxiously consulting the sacred prescription lest Great Science suffer in her hands. Thus, she sent them away relieved of cash but burdened with medicine, their minds filled with faith and humbugged to their hearts' content. *Populus vult decipi* (the people want to be deceived). Once they departed, she retrieved two small boxes Gerard had crafted for her. On one she wrote "Today" and on the other "Tomorrow." She placed smaller coins in the "Today" box and larger ones in the "Tomorrow" box, alongside whatever gold pieces survived the journey from Sevenbergen and the costs of housekeeping in a foreign land. In this manner, she met current expenses while saving for the rainy days she anticipated, mixing drugs with simples and vice with virtue. Conscience pricked her sorely regarding this last practice. After each day's performance, she knelt and prayed God to forgive her "for the sake of her child." Yet, lo and behold, reports of cures continued to pour in; consequently, her conscience began to harden.

Martin Wittenhaagen had recently become a dead weight on her hands. Like many who had endured great hardships, he had stiffened abruptly. Though less supple, he remained as strong as ever and could have carried the doctor herself around Rotterdam at his own pace. Instead, he carried her slops.

In this new enterprise, Martin displayed the qualities of a soldier: unreasoning obedience, punctuality, accuracy, dispatch, and drunkenness. Falling among "good fellows," the blackguards plied him with Schiedam; he babbled and bragged.

Doctor Margaret had risen very high in his estimation. All this brandishing of a crocodile as a standard, ambushing a dotard, disposing of slops, and exchanging good money struck him not as science, but as something far superior: Strategy. He boasted in his cups and before mixed companies how "me and my General we are a biting of the burghers."

When this revelation had time to ferment through the city, his General, Doctor Margaret, received a summons from the constables. They took her, trembling and begging her subordinate machines to forgive her, before the burgomaster. Standing by his side were real physicians—a terrible row—in long robes and square caps, accusing her of practicing unlawfully on the bodies of the Duke's subjects. Initially, she was too terrified to speak. A novice, the mere name of "Law" paralyzed her. But questioned closely, yet not as harshly as if she were ugly, she told the truth. She had long been her father's pupil and merely followed his system, noting that she had cured many. "And it is not for myself, truly, sirs," she pleaded, "but I have two poor, helpless, honest men at home upon my hands. How else can I keep them? Ah, good sirs, let a poor girl make her bread honestly; you hinder them not from making it idly and shamefully. Oh, sirs, ye are husbands, ye are fathers; ye cannot but see I have reason to work and provide as best I may." Ere these words left her lips, she wished she could recall them, standing with one hand upon her heart and the other covering her face, hiding it but not the tears trickling beneath. Unfortunately, her appeal went to the wrong address. Perhaps a female bailiff might have yielded to such arguments, permitting her to practice medicine and break the law until her child was weaned.

"What have we to do with that," said the burgomaster, "save and except that if thou pledgest thyself to break the law no more, I will remit the imprisonment and exact but the fine?"

Upon this, Doctor Margaret clasped her hands and vowed most penitently never, never, never to cure body or beast again. Dismissed with the constables to pay the fine
