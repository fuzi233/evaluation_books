The ground was damp when I awoke, the river swelling in its ancient bed, hungry to reclaim the valley. Soon it would thunder over the precipice, split into twin torrents—one to drown the orchard, the other to scour the den of monsters—and between them, the Evil Wood would stand isolated. I had to return to those who sent me.

At the cliff’s edge, I walked between the branching waters, my path now familiar even if the river surged behind me. I paused at Mara’s cottage, hoping to find her, but the door yawned empty, bread and water untouched on the table. Silence pooled thick in the air. I called out, but no answer came.

Further on, an old man sat weeping in the sand.

"Why do you weep?" I asked.

"They deny me death," he said, voice cracked like drought-stricken earth. "The Lady of the House refuses me. Plead for me, stranger."

"She refuses none who are ready," I said.

"Ready?" He scoffed. "You’re too young to understand."

"Age isn’t the measure," I replied. "You must *want* to live before you can die rightly. Go to Mara’s cottage. Eat her bread, drink her water. Wait for her. She’ll teach you."

He wept harder, deaf to counsel. Mara would find him.

The moon had not yet risen when I passed the lair of the horde. Only stillness answered—until I crossed. Then, a distant roar of water, a shriek at my back. I didn’t turn.

Cold seeped into my bones as I reached the House of Death, a darkness so thick it clung like frost. But Eve’s candle glowed in the window, a beacon. The door stood open. Inside, emptiness.

Loneliness swallowed me. Thousands slept nearby, yet I was utterly alone. They lived; I was dead—but not dead enough to bridge the gulf. The silence gnawed.

Then—movement. A whisper not of sound but of the air itself trembling. The coffin-door creaked open.

Lona’s face emerged, pale as moonlit marble, eyes closed yet fixed on me.

"Come, king," she murmured, voice like wind through dry grass. "I can’t rest without you. The river waits."

"My love!" I cried, gathering her icy form. "I thought you lost."

"Carry me back," she sighed. "The air hurts."

I laid her on her couch. "Sleep. I’ll be here when you wake."

She was gone before I finished. I collapsed onto my own bed, aching for the cold to still my heart.

Then—light. Adam stood over me.

"You didn’t wait," he chided gently.

"My child couldn’t sleep," I said.

"She never woke," he corrected. "You held her dream."

I tried to rise, but my limbs were stone.

"Rest," Eve’s voice floated from the dark. "You’re home."

Mara brought bread and wine. Adam breathed warmth into my chest.

"Your mother lies beside you," he said, candlelight brushing her face.

"She’s younger," I whispered.

"All things return," he said. "Even Lilith begins to fade."

They dressed me in the white shroud. The cold no longer bit.

Around me, the sleepers’ faces glowed faint in the dark. Then—I was among them.

And knew nothing more.
