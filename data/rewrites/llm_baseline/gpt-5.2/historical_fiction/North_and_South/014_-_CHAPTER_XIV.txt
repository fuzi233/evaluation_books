It soothed Margaret, in those days, to find her mother drawing her nearer with a tenderness she had not known since childhood. Mrs. Hale began to lean on her as on a companion—almost a friend admitted behind the curtain of small daily feelings; and Margaret, who had so often watched Dixon occupy that place, answered every summons to sympathy, even when the matter was some delicate discomfort of the room, a draught that set a candle wavering, a teacup that tasted faintly of soap. She listened, and made much of what her mother made much of, the way one might stoop to lift a pin that another cannot bear to see trodden underfoot.

So it happened that, without knowing she had earned it, she found herself paid in the very coin her heart had long desired.

One evening, when Mr. Hale was out, the house settling into its hush between tea-things cleared away and the lamps properly turned, Mrs. Hale began to speak of Frederick.

The name, which Margaret had carried like a covered dish—fragrant, forbidden, too precious to unveil at the wrong moment—was brought out of its hiding-place with a suddenness that made her breath catch. All the while she had hungered for knowledge, she had never dared ask; the more she wanted it, the more her voice failed her.

“Oh, Margaret,” Mrs. Hale said, shifting as if the sheets themselves remembered a storm, “it was so windy last night! It came howling down the chimney in our room. I could not sleep. I never can when it is like that. I got into a wakeful habit when poor Frederick was at sea; and now, even if I don’t waken all at once, I dream of him in some storm, with great, clear, glass-green walls of waves on either side his ship—higher than her very masts—curling over with that cruel white foam, like a gigantic crested serpent. It is an old dream, but it always comes back on windy nights, till I am thankful to waken, sitting straight up in bed with my terror.”

Outside, Milton’s night had its own soundscape: the far-off, steady thunder of industry, a pulse under the nearer noises of the street. The wind worrying at the chimney-pot did not sound like Helstone’s wind through leaves; it had a harsher edge, as if it scraped soot and grit along the brick. Mrs. Hale’s nerves—trained long ago to the sea’s imagined roar—took the town’s clang and hiss as if they were warnings.

“Poor Frederick! He is on land now, so wind can do him no harm. Though I did think it might shake down some of those tall chimneys.”

Margaret, leaning close, caught the faint scent of her mother’s lavender-water and the warm linen; and with it, suddenly, the remembered smell of letters that had travelled—paper that had slept in salt air and been handled by hands that had held ropes.

“Where is Frederick now, mamma? Our letters are directed to the care of Messrs. Barbour, at Cadiz. I know that; but where is he himself?”

Mrs. Hale knit her brows, as if trying to steady a name that slid away. “I can’t remember the place. But he is not called Hale; you must remember that, Margaret. Notice the F. D. in every corner of the letters. He has taken the name of Dickenson. I wanted him to have been called Beresford—to which he had a kind of right—but your father thought he had better not. He might be recognised, you know, if he were called by my name.”

Margaret’s timidity gave way before this opening. “Mamma,” she said softly, “I was at Aunt Shaw’s when it all happened; and I suppose I was not old enough to be told plainly. But I should like to know now—if I may—if it does not give you too much pain to speak of it.”

“Pain! No.” Mrs. Hale’s cheek flushed with a quick proud heat. “Yet it is pain to think I may never see my darling boy again. Or else he did right, Margaret. They may say what they like, but I have his own letters to show, and I’ll believe him—though he is my son—sooner than any court-martial on earth. Go to my little japan cabinet, dear; in the second left-hand drawer you will find a packet of letters.”

Margaret rose at once. The cabinet—black and shining, with its small painted birds and flowers—had always seemed to her as much a shrine as a piece of furniture. When she drew out the drawer, it came with a soft rasp of wood on wood; within lay the packet, tied with a silken string, and the paper edges yellowed as if by sun and salt. The smell rose at once: a faint briny odour, like dried seaweed on stone, mingled with the dust of years—an intimate scent, as if one could taste distance.

She carried them back as carefully as if bearing something that might spill.

Mrs. Hale’s hands trembled as she untied the string. The silk slid through her fingers with a little whisper. She peered at dates, lips moving, and then, in a hurry of anxious love, began to speak over the very words Margaret was trying to take in.

“You see,” she said, pressing the first sheet into Margaret’s hand, “how from the first he disliked Captain Reid. He was second lieutenant then—on the Orion—when Frederick went out the first time. Poor little fellow—how well he looked in his midshipman’s dress, with his dirk in his hand, cutting open all the newspapers with it, as if it were a paper-knife! But Mr. Reid—as he was then—took a dislike to Frederick from the beginning.”

She shifted to another letter. “And then—stay!—these are the letters from the Russell, when Frederick was appointed to her and found his old enemy Captain Reid in command. He did mean to bear it patiently. Look—this is the letter. Just read it, Margaret. Where is it he says—Stop—‘my father may rely upon me, that I will bear with all proper patience everything that one officer and gentleman can take from another. But from my former knowledge of my present captain, I confess I look forward with apprehension to a long course of tyranny on board the Russell.’ You see, he promises—he promises!”

Margaret read slowly. The ink had paled; parts were blurred, as if some spray had once kissed the page and left its mark. Even in that faintness the voice came through—a young man trying to set down restraint like a rule of seamanship, trying to measure his own temper as carefully as a course.

Mrs. Hale hurried on, unable to stop the tide of memory. “Is that the letter where he speaks of the captain’s impatience with the men, for not going through the manœuvres as quickly as the Avenger? You see, he says the Russell had many new hands; while the Avenger had been nearly three years on the station, with nothing to do but keep slavers off and work her men, till they ran up and down the rigging like rats or monkeys.”

As Margaret made out the lines, she could feel the ship in them: the cramped deck, the tarred ropes, the hot iron smell of fittings warmed by sun, the rank breath of many bodies in close quarters. It might be, she thought, that the letter exaggerated—written too close to the sting of anger, with the heat of the scene still in his blood. Yet the picture it painted was sharp as a knife.

Some sailors, Frederick wrote, had been aloft in the main-topsail rigging. Captain Reid ordered them to race down, threatening the hindmost with the cat. The man farthest on the spar, seeing he could not pass, and dreading the flogging like a shame worse than pain, threw himself desperately down to catch a rope lower. He missed. He struck the deck senseless, and lived only a few hours after. The crew’s indignation boiled; and Margaret could almost hear it—the sudden hush after the thud, then the muttered surge, the dangerous animal sound of men restrained.

Mrs. Hale pressed her lips together, as if tasting bitterness. “But we did not receive this letter till long, long after we heard of the mutiny. Poor Fred! I dare say it was a comfort to him to write it, even though he could not have known how to send it.”

She sat up a little straighter, as if bracing herself against what came next. In the pause the house made its own noises: a distant door shut; somewhere a kettle-lid clicked as if a servant were settling the last things for the night; and beyond all, the town’s ceaseless industrial breath—a reminder that there are powers that never sleep, that grind on regardless of one family’s grief.

“And then,” Mrs. Hale went on, “we saw a report in the papers—long before Fred’s letter reached us—of an atrocious mutiny on board the Russell, and that the mutineers had remained in possession of the ship, which had gone off, it was supposed, to be a pirate; and that Captain Reid was sent adrift in a boat with some men—officers, or something—whose names were all given, for they were picked up by a West Indian steamer.”

Her voice trembled as she came to the list, that brutal catalogue of the saved. “Oh, Margaret! how your father and I turned sick over that list when there was no name of Frederick Hale. We thought it must be a mistake. Poor Fred was such a fine fellow—only perhaps too passionate; and we hoped the name of Carr, which was in the list, was a misprint for Hale. Newspapers are so careless.”

Margaret imagined the newspaper itself: the cheap ink, the coarse paper, the words set in hard black—dominant, public, and careless. How quickly a name—misspelt, omitted, replaced—could become a verdict. How easily a living person could be translated into a label and then lost in it.

“And towards post-time the next day,” Mrs. Hale continued, “papa set off to walk to Southampton for the papers; and I could not stop at home, so I went to meet him. He was very late—much later than I thought—and I sat down under the hedge to wait. He came at last, his arms hanging loose, his head sunk, walking heavily, as if every step was a labour. Margaret, I see him now.”

“Don’t go on, mamma,” Margaret said, her heart tightening. She slid closer, and kissed her mother’s hand, which felt thin and hot. “I can understand.”

“No, you can’t. No one can who did not see him then.” Mrs. Hale’s eyes shone, fixed on something far away. “I could hardly lift myself to go and meet him—everything reeled around me. And when I got to him, he did not speak, or seem surprised to see me there—more than three miles from home, beside the Oldham beech-tree. But he put my arm in his, and kept stroking my hand, as if he wanted to soothe me to be very quiet under some great heavy blow.”

Her hand, remembering, twitched faintly in Margaret’s grasp.

“And when I trembled so all over I could not speak, he took me in his arms, and stooped his head on mine, and began to shake and cry in a strange muffled groaning voice, till I—out of fright—stood quite still, only begging him to tell me what he had heard. And then, with his hand jerking, as if someone else moved it against his will, he gave me a wicked newspaper to read—calling our Frederick a ‘traitor of the blackest dye,’ ‘a base, ungrateful disgrace to his profession.’ Oh! I cannot tell what bad words they did not use.”

The words themselves seemed to scrape the air, like grit on teeth.

“I took the paper,” Mrs. Hale said, her breath quickening, “and as soon as I had read—I tore it up to little bits. I tore it—I believe, Margaret, I tore it with my teeth. I did not cry. I could not. My cheeks were as hot as fire, and my very eyes burnt. Your father looked grave at me. I said it was a lie, and so it was.”

She drew one of Frederick’s letters close, as if it were a warm loaf taken from the oven—proof of honest labour against the coldness of print. “Months after, this letter came, and you see what provocation Frederick had. It was not for himself, or his own injuries, he rebelled; but he would speak his mind to Captain Reid, and so it went on from bad to worse; and you see, most of the sailors stuck by Frederick.”

Margaret held the paper and felt, with a kind of helpless clarity, how power moved: how one man’s voice, because it had a captain’s title, could become law; how another’s, because it was the voice of a younger officer, might be called insolence; and how the newspaper—using its own proud dialect—could coin a story that would stick to a family name like soot.

After a pause Mrs. Hale spoke again, her strength spent, her tone low and shaking. “I think, Margaret… I am glad of it. I am prouder of Frederick standing up against injustice than if he had been simply a good officer.”

“I am sure I am,” Margaret answered, with a steadiness that surprised herself. “Loyalty and obedience to wisdom and justice are fine; but it is finer to defy arbitrary power—unjustly and cruelly used—not on behalf of ourselves, but on behalf of others more helpless.”

Mrs. Hale’s proud look faltered into yearning. “For all that, I wish I could see Frederick once more—just once. He was my first baby, Margaret.”

She spoke as if apologising for the hunger of it, as if a mother’s longing might wrong the child beside her. But Margaret felt no such jealousy; she was already turning the desire over in her mind, as one turns a problem of household management—how to make a little go far, how to contrive warmth where there is none.

“It is six or seven years ago,” she said. “Would they still prosecute him, mother? If he came and stood his trial—what would be the punishment? Surely he might bring evidence of his provocation.”

“It would do no good,” Mrs. Hale replied, and the plainness of the words fell like a heavy lid. “Some of the sailors who accompanied Frederick were taken, and there was a court-martial held on them on board the Amicia. I believed all they said in their defence—poor fellows—because it just agreed with Frederick’s story. But it was of no use—”

For the first time that evening she cried. The tears came slowly, as if drawn from deep reserves; they shone on her lashes and slid down without sound.

And yet something in Margaret—some stern necessity of knowing the whole—drove her to ask what she dreaded.

“What happened to them, mamma?”

“They were hung at the yard-arm,” said Mrs. Hale, solemnly; and the sentence seemed to ring in the room like a struck bell, harsh and final. “And the worst was that the court, in condemning them to death, said they had suffered themselves to be led astray from their duty by their superior officers.”

Silence followed—thick, airless. The house noises continued their small indifferent music; far off, Milton’s engines kept up their iron chant. Margaret listened to that industrial roar and thought how it drowned out so much—how a town could make one kind of sound constant and call it prosperity, while other sounds—water, wind in trees, a dialect spoken softly at a hearth—grew faint, as if the world had decided they were unnecessary.

“And Frederick was in South America for several years, was he not?” she asked at length, to steady the talk into facts.

“Yes. And now he is in Spain. At Cadiz, or somewhere near it.” Mrs. Hale’s voice dropped. “If he comes to England he will be hung. I shall never see his face again—for if he comes to England he will be hung.”

There was nothing to be done with such a truth—not folded up neatly like linen, not sweetened like bitter tea. Mrs. Hale turned her face to the wall and lay still in a despair that seemed older than the day’s sorrow, as if it had been simmering all these years and now, uncovered, filled the room with its heavy, inescapable smell. She drew her hand out of Margaret’s with a small impatient movement, not unkind, only craving solitude with the image of her son.

When Mr. Hale came in, his step and voice stirring the air, Margaret slipped away. She went out as one leaves a room where something has burned too long and too darkly, oppressed with gloom, and seeing no promise of brightness on any side of the horizon.
