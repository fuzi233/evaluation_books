“And is not Margaret—” Edith began, whispering as though the very curtains might take offence at money spoken aloud. She had drawn her husband’s head down to her, standing on tiptoe with a conspirator’s tremor, “—the heiress?”

It was late; the lamps in their Oxford lodging had been lowered, and the house—tired with the solemn journey and the day’s constrained sorrow—settled into that thin night-silence in which every sound is magnified: a coal shifting in the grate, a distant footstep on boards, the soft sigh of wind against the sash.

Captain Lennox, roused from his half-doze, looked blankly at her; his mind, trained to winds and charts, had never kept a ledger of College Fellows’ fortunes.

“If I ever heard it, I’ve forgotten it,” he said. “And what can a Fellow of a small College leave? Not much, I should think. Your cousin never cost him wine, did she? Two hundred and fifty a year is something ridiculous to make a romance out of.”

Edith dropped down from her tiptoes with the faintest air of a heroine whose chapter has been mislaid. A whole little castle of imagination—Margaret poor, Margaret noble in poverty, Margaret to be consoled by Edith’s lavish pity—collapsed into common brick.

But Edith’s nature could not keep long to disappointment; it sprang back like a child’s india-rubber ball. A week after, she came in upon her husband with a rustle and a low, theatrical curtsey, her eyes bright with the relish of being right.

“I am right, and you are wrong, most noble Captain. Margaret has had a lawyer’s letter. She is residuary legatee—the legacies are about two thousand pounds, and the remainder about forty thousand, at the present value of property in Milton.”

“Forty thousand!” He sat up now. “And how does she take it?”

“Oh! as if she’d known it all her life—only she had no idea it was so much. She looks very white, and says she’s afraid of it; but that’s nonsense, you know, and will soon go off. I left mamma pouring congratulations down her throat, and stole away to tell you.”

After that, by a sort of family instinct, it was taken for granted that Mr. Henry Lennox must become, as it were, the steward of this unexpected abundance. Margaret’s ignorance of business was complete; the names of deeds and settlements, the very sound of legal phrases, had to her the same meaning as a foreign tongue heard too quickly—heavy syllables, authority without comprehension. So Henry chose out her attorney; he brought her papers, smoothed them, pointed to where her hand must go, and was never so well pleased as when he could translate the mysteries of property into tidy clauses and orderly consequences.

Edith watched those long consultations with the mischievous satisfaction of one who believes life exists chiefly to arrange itself into pleasing pairs.

“Henry,” she said one day, archly, “do you know what I hope and expect all these conversations with Margaret will end in?”

“I don’t,” he answered, colouring in spite of himself; and then, with the stiffness of a man who feels himself observed, “and I desire you not to tell me.”

“Oh, very well! Then I need not tell Sholto not to ask Mr. Montagu so often to the house.”

“Just as you choose,” he said, forcing an indifferent tone. “What you are thinking of may or may not happen; but this time, before I commit myself, I will see my ground clear. Ask whom you please. And—” he added, with a sudden flash of irritation at her meddling—“it may not be very civil, Edith, but if you meddle in it you will mar it. She has been very farouche with me for a long time, and is only just beginning to thaw a little from her Zenobia ways. She has the making of a Cleopatra in her, if only she were a little more pagan.”

“For my part,” said Edith, with a small malicious pleasure, “I am very glad she is a Christian. I know so very few!”

There was no Spain for Margaret that autumn. She held to the hope till it frayed into threads—some lucky turn, some summons that should call Frederick to Paris, whither she might go with an excuse, and a convoy, and the whole terrible sweetness of seeing him safely. But the days moved on with their ordinary punctuality, and nothing came. Instead of Cadiz and orange-scented nights, she had to content herself with Cromer.

Aunt Shaw and the Lennoxes were bound thither; they had wished Margaret to accompany them from the first, and with their kind, languid temper they made no eager effort to forward her own separate wish. Perhaps Cromer was, in one sense, the best. Her body needed bracing, and her mind needed a place where it might fall silent without being chidden into cheerfulness.

The hope that had most persistently haunted her—quietly, like a sound below hearing—was that Mr. Bell would have told Mr. Thornton the simple facts which had gone before that miserable accident in the station-yard: what had driven her, what had tempted her, why she had done as she did. Whatever judgement Mr. Thornton now held—however altered from what he had once believed—she had longed that it should be founded on truth. It would have been a kind of rest, a small clean hearth in the middle of so much cold misunderstanding.

But Mr. Bell was dead; and with him went the one possible channel for that plain, honourable explanation. Time had slid too far; the story could not be spoken now without seeming to be an excuse, a bid for pity, a stirring of ashes that ought to lie. She must submit, like many another, to be misread. She could reason herself into accepting that this, too, was a common lot; yet her heart ached no less with the craving that some day—years hence, if need be, but before he died—he might know how near she had been to falling, and yet how she had stood.

She told herself she did not need to hear that he understood, if only she could be sure that he would. But that, too, was vain, like so many wishes that rise warmly within us and then are dashed down by the mere weight of circumstance. When she had schooled herself to the conviction that there was no help to be looked for here, she turned, with all her heart and strength, to the life immediately before her, and resolved to strive to make the best of it.

At Cromer she sat long upon the beach. The sea there was not a picture; it was an occupation, a continual labour. Wave after wave came in, combed itself into foam, and worried the pebbly shore with a patient, perpetual insistence, as if it would grind every stone into a smoother truth. If she looked farther out, there was the broad heave and glitter that met the sky, as if the world’s edge were always being renewed. And through it all ran sound—the long inhaling and exhaling of water, the sudden hard clatter of pebbles sucked back, the hush that followed, the returning rush. It was an “eternal psalm,” not in words that could be translated into any dominion of speech, but in that older language which enters the nerves and settles the heart without asking permission.

She heard it, and yet was hardly conscious of hearing; it came to her like breath comes—necessary, unnoticed till it fails. In Milton, sound had been a tyranny: iron ringing on iron, wheels screaming on stone, a ceaseless commercial clamour that pressed upon the mind as a hand upon the mouth. Here the sound did not command; it accompanied. It did not thrust itself into her thoughts; it washed round them, and in that washing soothed without arguing, eased without explaining. She began to feel—dimly at first—that the very kind of noise a place breeds enters into the kind of person one is allowed to become.

Often she sat on the ground, her hands clasped round her knees, while her aunt Shaw did her small shoppings in the little town, and Edith and Captain Lennox rode far and wide along the shore and inland lanes. Nurses sauntered past with their charges; the children’s voices came and went like little flutes, and the women glanced at Margaret, whispering their wonder at the young lady who could look so long at nothing but water.

When they gathered at dinner-time, Margaret’s silence was so deep and absently sustained that Edith declared her “moped,” and with great satisfaction hailed her husband’s proposal that Mr. Henry Lennox should be invited down for a week, on his return from Scotland in October.

Yet those hours were not lost. The sea’s uncommercial music—so unlike the jangling engines and shouted reckonings of Milton—made room in her for a new ordering of memory. Events took their right places, not as she had suffered them in the moment, but as they had grown from causes, and as they might bear upon her future. Anyone with the perception to read a face could have seen something altering in hers: a quietness, not of indifference, but of settled strength; light, not of gaiety, but of a mind that has found where to stand.

Henry Lennox was struck by it at once. The first evening after his arrival, she left the room; he watched her go with an attention too practised to be merely casual.

“The sea has done Miss Hale an immense deal of good, I should fancy,” he said.

“That’s the bonnet I got her!” cried Edith, triumphantly. “I knew it would suit her the moment I saw it.”

“I beg your pardon,” said Henry, in the half-contemptuous, half-indulgent tone he used to Edith’s pretty certainties. “But I believe I know the difference between the charms of a dress and the charms of a woman. No mere bonnet would have made Miss Hale’s eyes so lustrous and yet so soft, or her lips so ripe and red—and her face altogether so full of peace and light.” Then, lowering his voice, as if naming Helstone were a thing to be touched reverently: “She is like, and yet more—like the Margaret Hale of Helstone.”

From that time the clever, ambitious man bent all his powers to gaining her. He loved her beauty; but he loved, too, the latent sweep in her mind, which he believed could be turned—gently, skilfully—towards the objects he had set his heart on. Her fortune was part of the completeness of her position; he did not pretend to himself that it was nothing, and he saw clearly what rise it would immediately enable him, the poor barrister, to take. Later he would earn such success, such honours, as would enable him to repay—handsomely—the first advance which he should owe to her.

He had been to Milton on business connected with her property on his return from Scotland; and with the quick eye of a lawyer trained to weigh contingencies, he had seen additional value accruing year by year to the lands and tenements she owned in that thriving, increasing town. He was glad, too, to find that the present relation between them—client and adviser—was gradually superseding the recollection of that unlucky, mismanaged day at Helstone. In this guise he had unusual opportunities of intimate intercourse besides those which arose naturally from the family connection.

Margaret, for her part, was only too willing to listen while he spoke of Milton. He had seen none of the people she most cared for; yet the mere name of the place, the mere calling up of its streets and air, stirred her more than Edith’s lively chatter or her aunt’s languid comments could stir her. It had been the tone with Mrs. Shaw and Edith to speak of Milton with dislike and contempt—just such feelings as Margaret was ashamed to remember she herself had once expressed when she first went to live there, shrinking from its smoke as from a stain.

But Henry Lennox almost exceeded Margaret in his appreciation of Milton and its inhabitants. Their energy, their power, their indomitable courage in struggling and fighting—their lurid vividness of existence—captivated and arrested him. He could not tire of describing it: the town’s unceasing pulse, the hard-driven days, the sense of lives measured in hours and output, in contracts and markets. Yet he had not, until Margaret pointed it out with candid pain even amid her gratification, perceived how selfish and material were too many of the ends proposed as the reward of all that mighty endeavour—how the very words men used for success had been narrowed till they could name only gain. It was, she told him, the tainting sin in much that was noble.

Still, when other subjects fell flat upon her, and her answers grew brief, Henry discovered that any enquiry into some Darkshire peculiarity of character, any mention of Milton’s temper and speech, called back the light into her eyes and the glow into her cheek. It was as if that harsh, unmusical town had left in her an inner hearing—trained by suffering to catch meanings beneath rough sounds—and as if, for all her criticism of its noise and greed, she could not help honouring the force of its living.

When they returned to town, Margaret fulfilled one of her seaside resolves and took her life into her own hands.

Before Cromer she had been as docile to her aunt’s laws as if she were still the frightened child who cried herself to sleep that first night in the Harley Street nursery. But the solemn hours by the sea had taught her that she must one day answer for her own life and what she had done with it. She tried, steadily and humbly, to settle that most difficult problem for a woman—how much is to be merged in obedience to authority, and how much may be set apart for freedom in working.

Mrs. Shaw was good-tempered; Edith had inherited that charming domestic quality; Margaret herself had probably the worst temper of the three, for her quick perceptions and over-lively imagination made her hasty, and her early isolation from sympathy had made her proud. Yet there was in her an indescribable childlike sweetness of heart, which made her manners, even in her rare wilful moods, irresistible once. Now, chastened even by what the world called her good fortune, she charmed her reluctant aunt into acquiescence with her will. She gained, quietly and firmly, the acknowledgment of her right to follow her own ideas of duty.

“Only don’t be strong-minded,” pleaded Edith. “Mamma wants you to have a footman of your own; and I’m sure you’re very welcome, for they’re great plagues. Only to please me, darling, don’t go and have a strong mind; it’s the only thing I ask. Footman or no footman, don’t be strong-minded.”

“Don’t be afraid, Edith. I’ll faint on your hands at the servants’ dinner-time the very first opportunity; and then, what with Sholto playing with the fire and the baby crying, you’ll begin to wish for a strong-minded woman, equal to any emergency.”

“And you’ll not grow too good to joke and be merry?”

“Not I. I shall be merrier than I have ever been, now I have got my own way.”

“And you’ll not go a figure, but let me buy your dresses for you?”

“Indeed I mean to buy them for myself. You shall come with me if you like; but no one can please me but myself.”

“Oh! I was afraid you’d dress in brown and dust-colour, not to show the dirt you’ll pick up in all those places. I’m glad you’re going to keep one or two vanities, just by way of specimens of the old Adam.”

“I am going to be just the same, Edith, if you and my aunt could but fancy so. Only, as I have neither husband nor child to give me natural duties, I must make myself some—in addition to ordering my gowns.”

In the little family conclave—Edith, her mother, and Captain Lennox—it was decided that perhaps all these plans of Margaret’s would only secure her the more for Henry Lennox. They kept her out of the way of other friends who might have offered eligible sons or brothers; and it was agreed, too, that she never seemed to take much pleasure in any society but Henry’s, out of their own family circle. Other admirers, attracted by her appearance or by the rumour of her fortune, were swept aside by her unconscious, smiling disdain into the paths frequented by beauties less fastidious or heiresses with a heavier weight of gold.

So Henry and Margaret grew, slowly, into closer intimacy; but neither he nor she were people to brook the slightest notice of their proceedings. Outside, London made its usual noises—wheels, calls, the endless bargaining of lives—while within, something quieter and more fateful was being kneaded and shaped, as silently as bread rises in a covered bowl: fed by time, by habit, by what was spoken and what remained untranslatable, lying under the common words they used.
