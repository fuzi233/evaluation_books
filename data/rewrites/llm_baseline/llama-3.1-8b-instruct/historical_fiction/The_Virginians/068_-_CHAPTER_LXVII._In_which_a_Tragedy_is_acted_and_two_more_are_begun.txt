As the seasons passed, Colonel James Wolfe returned to England from his victorious campaign in America, bringing with him a reputation forged in the crucible of battle. It was a few weeks before Harry Warrington, now a seasoned officer, joined his new regiment, where Wolfe had risen to prominence following his gallant service at Cape Breton. Harry's respect and admiration for his commander were genuine, and he hastened to pay his respects upon Wolfe's return.

The eyes of the nation were upon Wolfe, and whispers of his potential greatness spread far and wide. In the aftermath of the recent French wars, several illustrious generals had fallen short of expectation. The Duke of Marlborough's legacy was tarnished, and doubts surrounded Lord George Sackville's military acumen, further diminished by his calamitous performance at Minden. The public craved a hero, and the government sought a leader who could satiate their thirst for glory. As fate would have it, Wolfe's duties kept him in London, where he maintained a close friendship with fellow officer Mr. Lambert, who rejoiced at his protégé's good fortune.

When away from duty, Harry would often engage Wolfe in animated discussions about the campaigns of the past year. Wolfe spoke candidly of his experiences, revealing a straightforward, unassuming nature that earned him the admiration of all who knew him. His frankness was reminiscent of Nelson's later example, and though some of the high-born gentlemen at St. James's might scoff, Harry and their circle of friends saw him as a paragon of honor and integrity.

Wolfe's interests extended far beyond the battlefield. He was an ardent lover of music and poetry, and his romantic spirit was evident in his words and deeds. Even in the midst of war, he confessed that he would rather have penned Gray's Elegy than won a triumph. It was little wonder, then, that he and George, who shared similar tastes and passions, grew close. Their conversations, filled with sentiment and emotion, would have been a treasure trove of insight had they been recorded accurately.

In one of his letters, Warrington wrote fondly of Wolfe, describing a shared topic that had captivated them both: the pursuit of honor and glory. "He was a man consumed by ambition," Warrington noted, "yet his simplicity and bravery shone like a beacon in the darkness."
