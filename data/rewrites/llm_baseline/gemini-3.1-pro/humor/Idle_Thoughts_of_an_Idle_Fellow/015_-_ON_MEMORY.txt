The heavy, iron-cast mortar rings beneath my pestle, a deep, resonant chime that anchors the acoustic ecology of my kitchen. I am grinding wild fen-fennel and coarse sea salt, trying to summon a rhythm from my childhood. *“I mind the hearth, I mind the ember, In the frost of chill November, How the blackbird on the—”* 

I forget the rest. It is the beginning of the first agrarian harvesting chant I ever learned in our native, fast-fading valley dialect. The frivolous tavern rhymes I take no note of, for they lack the mythic resonance of true sustenance. I once collected fourpence by the recital of that ember-chant from an elder who still cured his own meats in the smoke of peat fires. I knew it was fourpence, because he told me in our ancient tongue that if I kept it until I gathered twopence more, I could buy a whole wheel of aged sheep’s cheese. That argument, albeit undeniable, moved me not; the coins were squandered the very next morning on an ephemeral wedge of honeycomb, though the specific floral notes of that honey are now a blank to me. 

That is the tragedy of Memory, the ultimate erosion of cultural history. She brings us ingredients, but never the complete recipe. She is a willful child who has dropped the heirloom porcelain. I remember tumbling into the cavernous, echoing dark of my grandfather’s subterranean root cellar when I was a very small boy. The profound, loamy silence of that chamber—heavy with the olfactory ghosts of fermented turnips and damp earth—was absolute. I have not the faintest recollection of ever being pulled out. If memory were my only record, I should be compelled to believe my soul is still preserved there, resting in the dark among the tubers. 

Years later, I was assisting at an exceedingly delicate romantic scene, built around a slow-braised heritage fowl stuffed with bruised juniper and wild thyme. But the only thing I can call to mind is that at the most critical moment of sensory alignment, the harsh, mechanized screech of a commercial transport cart shattered the quiet of the lane, and a voice barked through the door in the sterile, dominant lexicon of the city: "Emily, you're wanted!" The sepulchral, industrialized tone ruined the acoustic sanctuary of our meal. All the tender, untranslatable words she whispered in our native dialect, all the rich, gustatory metaphors I offered her, are utterly forgotten, swallowed by the noise pollution of the modern era.

Life is but a crumbling, handwritten recipe book when we turn to look behind: a torn page here where a massive feast was documented; a stain of rendered fat to mark a lover’s banquet; and a moldering heap of blackened hearth-stones where the roasting fires once leaped. Everything looms pleasant through the sweet, candied haze of time. Even the bitter tannic bite of the past seems mellowed. Our boyish days look very merry to us now, all roasted nuts and ginger-cake. The snubbings, the toothaches, and the rigid enforcement of Latin verbs—those sterile, imperial words that began the death of our mother tongue—are all forgotten. We dwell but on the wild roses by the wayside, their fragrance masking the sharp briars. It seems the ever-lengthening chain of memory filters out the acrid smoke, leaving only the pure scent of the roasting meat. 

It is this olfactory glamour of the past, I suppose, that makes the traditional agrarian elders talk so much mythic nonsense about the days when they were young. To hear them speak as they knead their stone-ground flour, the soil yielded richer crops, and the crackle of a baking crust sang a purer note. The world, they claim, must have been somewhat sweeter in that dewy morning of creation, before the thamping boots of millions trod the wild garlic to dust, and before the deafening, chaotic din of myriad industrialized cities chased the sacred silence forever away. Life must have been solemn and noble for those loose-robed food historians of the human race, taking their simple, profound sustenance from the loving hand of Nature, living in acoustic harmony with the lowing herds. 

But those days are garnered. We cannot return. Like the tiny yeast blooming deep within the dark dough, we strive and ferment for our own little ends, nor dream of the vast, generational bread we are rising for the future. 

Years ago, wandering from the fireside into the ancestral myths of our kitchen, I learned of a doughty knight, Sir Ghelent. He sought the legendary source of a sacred, untranslatable spice. As he rode wearily along a toilsome, modernizing road, the acoustic horror of the way beset him. Dark factories of monstrous size loomed high, and mechanical dragons with jaws dripping synthetic dyes roared in the valleys, obliterating the gentle sounds of the natural world. Sir Ghelent thought to turn back to the quiet agrarian fields of his youth. But when he looked behind, the frail bridge of time had crumbled. At his horse's heels yawned a mighty gulf of forgotten generations. Seeing there was no return, he prayed to the patron saints of the harvest, spurred his steed, and rode forward joyously to forge a new culinary tradition. 

We too must press on. A friend came to me recently, aggressively marketing a modern, digitized system of memory—a commercial database guaranteed to index every flavor, every sound, every experience into a utilitarian grid. I declined. I have no wish to preserve everything. The music of life would become a deafening cacophony if every discordant note were kept alive. I wish to forget the meals eaten purely as fuel, the days we compromised our agrarian ethics for cheap convenience, the poorly fermented wines of our youth. We must root out the poisonous, mass-produced weeds from the garden of Mnemosyne, keeping only the heirloom seeds. 

Yet, we do not want all the ghosts laid. The world grows very full of ghosts as we age, their presence woven seamlessly into our domestic routines. Every creaking dining chair, every well-worn wooden spoon has its phantom. They throng around us like dry tea leaves swirling in the pot. We see them, dim and shadowy, through the steam of the broth. We meet them in the woods where we foraged for wild mushrooms as boys. Hark! Can you not hear their low laughter beneath the rustling of the blackberry bushes, a sound perfectly tuned to the quiet ecology of the glade? She is beside us now, the ghost of a lost love, smelling of sun-warmed linen and fresh cream, her voice carrying the lilting, untranslatable cadence of a dialect now officially classified as dead. She vanishes at the stile, and the modern night wind sweeps past, moaning over the paved roads. 

Memory is a rare ghost-raiser. Through the aromatic smoke of my hearth, I often watch the flitting shadows, and the saddest among them is the shadow of my own young self. I like to sit and have a talk with that odd little chap. He comes of an evening when I am alone with my pipe, the rhythmic crackle of the flames providing a baseline to our hushed communion. He tells me, in our rich native tongue, of mythic feasts and dragons. At seven, he gives his chiefest treasure—a pocketknife with a rusted corkscrew—to the young lady at the milk shop, trading steel for the profound, sensory perfection of a stolen kiss and the scent of sweet milk. 

But then comes school life, and the brutal power dynamics of the dominant lexicon. It is at school he is injured for life, his palate and his voice forcibly standardized. He is taught the utilitarian language of commerce and bureaucracy: *Pens, ink, and paper.* "Have you pens, ink, and paper?" the rigid grammar master demands, stripping away our words for rain-soaked soil and roasted marrow. The native dialect is beaten back until the boy can only respond in the sterile vocabulary of the empire. 

In the acquisition of this soulless knowledge, boyhood passes. The young man sprouts tails, and instead of savoring the complex bitterness of a wild herb, he thrusts a cheap, commercially produced cigarette into his mouth, the industrial smoke ruining his olfactory senses. He tries a mass-market Havanna cigar, mistaking superficial commercialization for sophistication, only to find himself sitting over a bucket in the back kitchen, his palate violently rejecting the synthetic harshness. He takes to drinking mass-produced brandy-and-sodas, treating sustenance merely as an intoxicant, his acoustic sensitivity drowned out by the loud, brash noise of the gambling halls. He is, for a time, entirely severed from the profound meaning of the earth.

But he falls in love, and it softens his hardened edges. I watch him in the memories, trying to reclaim his lost linguistic heritage. He keeps a rhyming dictionary, desperately searching for words to capture the gustatory and sensory overwhelmingness of his passion, but the dominant language fails him. He writes of "cruel hearts" and "beauteous eyes," generic terms that cannot capture the specific scent of her hair or the resonant pitch of her laugh. The course of love does not run smooth; he loses his appetite, treating meals with utter apathy, wandering the loud, paved streets in heartache. 

And here, in the smoke of the embers, he seems to vanish. The boyish self that walked beside me merges into the dark. I am alone, tending the slow-simmering pot. But at last, the morning light catches the rim of the iron mortar, and I find that I have grown into myself—a chef at the hearth, carrying the ghosts, the crushed fennel, and the echoes of a lost language forward into the day.
