Struggles against one's innate nature, those fierce battles waged with the strong, original bent of the heart, may appear futile and barren. Yet, in the long run, they yield a harvest. However slight the gain, they steer action and conduct toward the path Reason approves, even if Feeling, too often, rebels. They alter the general tenor of a life, making it more regulated, more equable, quieter on the surface—and it is only on the surface that the common gaze falls. As for what lies beneath, leave that to God. A fellow man, weak as you and unfit to judge you, may be barred from that inner sanctum; take it to your Maker. Show Him the secrets of the spirit He bestowed. Ask Him how to bear the pains He has ordained. Kneel in His presence and pray with faith for light in darkness, for strength in pitiful weakness, for patience in extreme need. Surely, at some hour—though perhaps not yours—the waiting waters will stir. In some shape, though perhaps not the form you dreamed of, for which your heart bled, the healing herald will descend. The lame, the blind, the mute, and the possessed will be led to bathe. "Herald, come quickly!" For thousands lie round the pool, weeping and despairing, watching it stagnate through slow years. Long are the "times" of Heaven; the orbits of angelic messengers seem vast to mortal vision. They may encompass ages; the cycle of one departure and return may embrace unnumbered generations. Dust, kindling into brief suffering life and passing back to dust through pain, may perish from memory again and again. To how many maimed and mourning millions is the first and sole angel visitor, whom the East calls Azrael?

I attempted to rise the following morning, but while dressing and intermittently sipping cold water from the carafe on my washstand—intending to brace the trembling weakness that made dressing so arduous—Mrs. Bretton burst in. "Here is an absurdity!" was her morning greeting. "Not so," she retorted, seizing me immediately in her own brusque, energetic fashion—that same manner I once delighted to see applied to her son, and which he vigorously resisted. Within two minutes, she had consigned me captive to the French bed. "There you shall lie until afternoon," she declared. "My boy left orders before he went out that this should happen, and I can assure you my son is master and must be obeyed. Breakfast will follow shortly."

Shortly thereafter, she brought that meal, serving it with her own active hands, refusing to delegate the task to servants. She seated herself on the bed while I ate. Not everyone, even among our most respected friends and esteemed acquaintances, is someone we wish near us, someone we want to watch, to wait upon, to approach with the intimacy of a nurse to a patient. Not every friend possesses an eye that acts as a light in a sickroom, a presence that offers solace. Yet, all this was Mrs. Bretton to me; all this she had always been. Food or drink never tasted as good as when it came through her hands. I cannot recall an instance where her entry into a room failed to make that room cheerier. Our natures hold strange predilections and antipathies. There are people from whom we secretly shrink, whom we would personally avoid, though reason admits they are good souls; conversely, there are others with evident faults of temper, beside whom we live content, as if the air surrounding them were beneficial. My godmother's lively black eye, her clear brunette cheek, her warm and prompt hand, her self-reliant mood, and her decisive bearing were all beneficial to me, like the atmosphere of some salubrious climate. Her son used to call her "the old lady"; it filled me with pleasant wonder to observe how the alacrity and power of twenty-five still breathed from her and surrounded her.

"I would bring my work here," she said, removing the empty teacup from my hand, "and sit with you all day, if that overbearing John Graham had not placed his veto on such a plan. 'Now, mamma,' he said when he departed, 'take notice, you are not to disturb your goddaughter with gossip.' He particularly instructed me to remain close to my own quarters and spare you my fine company. He believes, Lucy, that you have suffered a nervous fever, judging by your appearance—is that so?"

I replied that I was uncertain exactly what my ailment had been, but that I had certainly suffered greatly, especially mentally. Furthermore, on this subject, I deemed it inadvisable to dwell. The details of what I had endured belonged to a period of my existence in which I never anticipated my godmother would share. Into what new realm would such a confidence have led that hale, serene nature! The difference between her and me might be likened to that between a stately ship cruising safely on smooth seas, with a full complement of crew, a captain who is gay, brave, venturesome, and provident; and the lifeboat, which lies dry and solitary in an old, dark boathouse most days of the year, setting sail only when the billows run high in rough weather, when clouds meet water, when danger and death divide the rule of the great deep. No, the "Louisa Bretton" was never out of harbor on such a night, in such a scene: her crew could not conceive it. So the half-drowned lifeboat man keeps his counsel and spins no yarns.

She left me, and I lay in bed content; it was kind of Graham to remember me before departing. My day was lonely, but the prospect of the coming evening shortened and cheered it. Moreover, I felt weak, and rest seemed welcome. After the morning hours had passed—those hours that always bring, even to the necessarily idle, a sense of business to be done, of tasks awaiting fulfillment, a vague impression of obligation to be employed—when this stirring time faded and the silent descent of afternoon hushed the housemaids' footsteps on the stairs and within the chambers, I drifted into a dreamy mood, not unpleasant. My small, calm room seemed somehow like a cave in the sea. There was no color about it save for white and pale green, suggestive of foam and deep water; the blanched cornice was adorned with shell-shaped ornaments, and white moldings resembling dolphins graced the ceiling angles. Even that single touch of color visible in the red satin pincushion bore an affinity to coral; even that dark, shining glass might have mirrored a mermaid. When I closed my eyes, I heard a gale, subsiding at last, bearing upon the house-front like a settling swell upon a rock base. I heard it drawn and withdrawn far, far off, like a tide receding from a shore of the upper world—a world so high above that the rush of its largest waves, the crash of its fiercest breakers, could sound down in this submarine home only as murmurs and a lullaby.

Amidst these dreams came evening, and then Martha brought a light. With her assistance, I was quickly dressed. Stronger now than in the morning, I made my way down to the blue salon unassisted. Dr. John, it appeared, had concluded his rounds of professional calls earlier than usual; his figure was the first object to meet my eyes as I entered the parlor. He stood in the window-recess opposite the door, reading the dense print of a newspaper by the dim light of closing day. The fire burned clear, but the lamp stood unlit on the table, and tea had not yet been served. As for Mrs. Bretton, my active godmother—who, I later learned, had been outdoors all day—lay half-reclined in her deep-cushioned chair, completely lost in a nap. Seeing me, her son approached. I noted that he tread carefully, mindful not to wake the sleeper; he also spoke softly. His mellow voice never possessed sharpness; modulated as it was at that moment, it was calculated rather to soothe than to startle slumber.

"This is a quiet little château," he observed, inviting me to sit near the casement. "I do not know if you have noticed it in your walks: although, indeed, from the *chaussée* it is invisible; just a mile beyond the Porte de Crécy, you turn down a lane which soon becomes an avenue, leading you on, through meadow and shade, straight to the door of this house. It is not a modern place, but built somewhat in the old style of the Basse-Ville. It is rather a manor than a château; they call it 'La Terrasse' because its front rises from a broad turfed walk, whence steps lead down a grassy slope to the avenue. See yonder! The moon rises; she looks well through the tree trunks."

Where, indeed, does the moon not look well? What scene, confined or expansive, does her orb not hallow? Rosy or fiery, she mounted now above a nearby bank; even as we watched her flushed ascent, she cleared to gold, and in very brief space, floated up stainless into a now calm sky. Did moonlight soften or sadden Dr. Bretton? Did it touch him with romance? I think it did. Though not of a sighing mood, he sighed while watching it; sighed quietly to himself. No need to ponder the cause or course of that sigh; I knew it was awakened by beauty; I knew it pursued Ginevra. Knowing this, the idea pressed upon me that it was in some sort my duty to speak the name he meditated.
