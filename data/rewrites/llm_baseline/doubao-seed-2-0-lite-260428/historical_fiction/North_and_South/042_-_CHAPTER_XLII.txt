“When some beloved voice that was to you
Both sound and sweetness, faileth suddenly,
And silence, against which you dare not cry,
Aches round you like a strong disease and new,—
What hope? what help? what music will undo
That silence to your sense?”

The blow of her father’s sudden death had been so sharp Margaret sank into a total, numb prostration, no wracking sobs, no word of grief to offer relief. She lay motionless on the drawing-room sofa, eyes half-shut, never speaking save when directly addressed, and then only in the faintest of whispers.

Mr. Bell was at his wits’ end. He dared not leave her side, nor could he persuade her to travel back to Oxford with him as he had originally planned on his journey north to Milton; her exhaustion was so complete even the shortest carriage ride would have been agony, before he even considered the grim, unspoken funeral arrangements awaiting them. He pulled a chair close to the fire, sat staring at the flames, and turned over every possible plan in his head. Margaret lay still beside him, so quiet he could barely catch the soft catch of her breath. He would not even step downstairs for the dinner Dixon had prepared for him, the housemaid red-eyed and fussing over him in her sorrowful hospitality, so he sent a plate up to the drawing-room. Normally a man careful and particular over every subtle shade of flavour in his food, the devilled chicken now tasted like dry sawdust to him. He minced a portion of the fowl, seasoned it heavily with pepper and salt, and bade Dixon try to feed it to Margaret—but the faint, languid shake of her head made it plain any attempt to swallow food now would only choke her, not nourish her.

Mr. Bell heaved a great, heavy sigh, hauled his stout, travel-stiff limbs out of his chair, and followed Dixon out into the hall. “I cannot leave her. I shall write back to Oxford tonight, tell them to make all the necessary arrangements for the funeral there, they can proceed without me. Is there no woman who can come be with her? Not Mrs. Lennox? I’ll write at once and insist she come—this girl needs a friend of her own sex, if only to coax her into a good, healing cry.”

Dixon was already crying enough for two people, but she wiped her eyes steady enough to tell him Mrs. Lennox was months deep in her confinement, and could not possibly bear the strain of a journey. “Then we must send for Mrs. Shaw, her aunt, who came back to England not long ago, has she not?”

“Indeed she has, sir,” Dixon replied, “but I doubt she’ll wish to leave Mrs. Lennox at such an interesting time.” She had no great desire for a stranger to enter her household and steal any part of the tender, sovereign care she lavished on Margaret.

“Interesting time be—” Mr. Bell cut himself off with a loud cough before he swore outright. “She was perfectly happy gallivanting off in Venice and Naples, those Popish places, for Mrs. Hale’s last interesting time, if I recollect correctly, in Corfu? And what does that silly little prosperous woman’s lying-in signify, compared to that poor, helpless, homeless, friendless child lying as still on that sofa as if it were an altar tomb and she the stone statue atop it? I tell you, Mrs. Shaw will come. Get a room and all she could possibly want ready for her by tomorrow night. I shall see to it she comes.”

He wrote that very night a letter so sharp and unapologetically commanding that Mrs. Shaw later declared it was exactly the sort of note her late husband the General used to send when he was gearing up for a severe gout attack, and she would treasure it all her days. If he had softened his request, framed it as a favour she might refuse, she might well have delayed even with all her sincere sympathy for Margaret; it was that unceremonious, sharp order that spurred her to shake off her natural inertia and let her maid pack all her trunks immediately.

Edith, swaddled in cap and shawl and teary, stood at the top of the stairs as Captain Lennox led her mother down to the waiting carriage. “Don’t forget, mamma—Margaret must come back to live with us. Sholto will go to Oxford on Wednesday, and you must send word by Mr. Bell telling him when we are to expect the two of you. And if you need Sholto at all, he can travel straight on from Oxford down to Milton. Don’t forget, mamma—you are to bring Margaret home with you.” She wandered back into the drawing-room, where Henry Lennox sat cutting the pages of a new review. Without lifting his head he said, “If you would rather not have Sholto gone so long while you are unwell, Edith, I can go down to Milton in his place and offer whatever help I can.”

“Oh thank you, dear,” Edith replied, wiping her eyes, “but I am sure good old Mr. Bell is doing all that can be done already, and a resident Oxford fellow has not the slightest bit of savoir-faire for these sorts of troubles, more help might only get in the way. Won’t it be lovely to have Margaret with us again? You two were such great allies, years ago.”

“Were we?” he asked, indifferent, feigning deep interest in a passage he had just reached in the review.

“Well, perhaps not—I have forgotten, I was so wrapped up in Sholto then. But does it not fall perfectly that poor Uncle Hale died just now, when we have only just come home and settled into the old Harley Street house, perfectly ready to receive her? What a relief it will be for her to escape that grim Milton smoke! I shall buy new bright chintz for her bedroom, make it all fresh and cheerful to raise her spirits a little.”

Mrs. Shaw travelled north in that same warm, fussy spirit of kindness, dreading the moment she first met Margaret, but spending far more time planning how quickly she could whisk her niece away from “that horrid smoky place” back to all the gentle comforts of London. “Oh dear!” she said to her maid as their carriage rumbled past mile after mile of factory chimneys belching black smoke. “Look at those! My poor sister Hale! I do not think I could ever have rested easy in Naples if I had known what a dreadful place she was living in! I would have come fetched her and Margaret away years ago.” Privately, she had always thought her brother-in-law a little soft-headed, but never half so foolish as she thought him now, to have traded the sunlit green lanes of Helstone for this grimy, industrial wilderness.

Margaret had not moved from her state of pale, motionless, tearless quiet. They told her her aunt Shaw was coming, but she showed no surprise, no pleasure, not a flicker of dislike. Mr. Bell, whose appetite had returned now that he had a plan in motion, happily ate all the sweetbreads stewed with oysters Dixon had prepared for Margaret after she refused to take a single bite, just as stubbornly quiet as she had been the day before.

Margaret was the first to hear the hackney carriage pull up outside the house. Her eyelids fluttered, her pale lips flushed pink and trembled. Mr. Bell went downstairs to meet Mrs. Shaw, and by the time they climbed back up to the drawing room Margaret was on her feet, swaying a little, trying to steady her dizzy, grief-heavy body. The moment she saw her aunt, she stumbled forward into her open arms, and at last the long pent-up tears burst out, hot and healing, on her aunt’s shoulder. All the quiet years of shared family tenderness, the familiar, inexplicable likeness in tone, gesture, and the set of her aunt’s face that reminded her so sharply of her dead mother, melted her numbed heart completely, and the silence that had ached around her for days broke at last in sobs.

Mr. Bell slipped quietly out of the room, down into the late Mr. Hale’s little study, ordered a fire lit, and pulled books off the shelves to distract himself. But every volume brought back some memory of his dead old friend, a change of scene if not a change of thought, and he was glad when he heard Mr. Thornton’s voice at the front door asking after Miss Hale. Dixon was dismissing him with no little curt sharpness; with the arrival of Mrs. Shaw’s lady’s maid, she had begun to dwell fondly on visions of the old Beresford family grandeur, the noble “station” Margaret had been ousted from and was now, please God, returning to. She had been gossiping with the maid, pumping her for every story of the grand Harley Street household to tell to the other maids, and felt far too grand now to be deferential to any common Milton mill-owner. Even though she had always stood in some awe of Mr. Thornton, she was as sharp as she dared be telling him no one could see anyone in the house that night—until Mr. Bell opened the study door and called out, “Thornton! Is that you? Come in a minute, I want to speak to you.”

Dixon had no choice but to retreat back to the kitchen, where she restored her own ruffled dignity by telling the other maids a wild, grand story of Sir John Beresford driving a coach and six when he was high sheriff of the county.

“I do not know what I wanted to say to you, after all,” Mr. Bell said, as Thornton stepped inside. “It’s dismal enough sitting in a room where every single object speaks of a dead friend. But Margaret and her aunt need the drawing room all to themselves. I cannot very well stay here overnight, I was about to book a room at the Clarendon inn.”

“Nonsense, you will not go to the Clarendon. We have five or six spare bedrooms at our house, all well aired, you can trust my mother to see to that.”

“Then I shall just pop back upstairs to say good night to that poor wan girl, pay my respects to her aunt, and come straight off with you.”

Mr. Bell stayed upstairs longer than Thornton expected. Thornton had barely spared the time to run over from the mill to check on Margaret, all his work still piling up waiting for him. As they walked the dark Milton streets back to the Thornton house, Bell said, “Mrs. Shaw is desperate to get back to London, for her daughter’s sake, and wants to drag Margaret off with her at once. But the girl is no more fit to travel than I am to fly. And then she says, very fairly, that there are people here in Milton she must see, people she has to say goodbye to, and her aunt keeps badgering her about old friends, asking if she’s forgotten all the people who loved her. And she burst out crying just now, saying she would be glad to leave the place where she had suffered so much. I have to go back to Oxford tomorrow, and I do not know which side to throw my vote in on.”

He paused, waiting for an answer, but Thornton said nothing at all. His own mind was echoing those words over and over: *Where she had suffered so much*. All those eighteen months in Milton—so unspeakably precious to him, every bit of its bitterness sweeter than any other part of his life—would be remembered by her as nothing but pain. The death of his own mother, the loss of any person dear to him, could never poison the memory of those days when he could walk two pleasant miles every evening to her door, every step bringing him closer to her sweet presence, every moment with her revealing some new soft grace or sharp, bright, delightful quirk of her character. No matter what other misfortunes had befallen him, he would never call those days suffering. They had been a time of pure, unrivalled luxury, even with all their stings and humiliations, compared to the empty, grey poverty of a future with no hope, no chance of seeing her again every day.

Back at the Thornton house, Fanny was in the dining room, fluttering with quiet little glee as her maid held up one glossy silk after another to the candlelight, testing the colours for her upcoming wedding gowns. Her mother tried hard to sympathise, but could not care less about fabrics or frills, and heartily wished Fanny had taken her brother’s offer to have all her wedding clothes made by a top London dressmaker, instead of fussing endlessly over every little detail herself. Mr. Thornton was only too happy to show his approval of the man who had married Fanny, a sensible fellow who appreciated her little airs and graces, so he gave her unlimited funds to buy every bit of finery her heart desired, finery that rivalled even her love for her fiancé in her estimation.

When her brother and Mr. Bell walked in, Fanny blushed and simpered, fussed over hiding the silk scraps strewn across the table, though Mr. Bell barely noticed her at all. If he spared her a single thought, it was only to compare her, in all her trivial, bright happiness, to the pale, quiet grief he had left back at the Hales’ house, Margaret sitting bent-headed with folded hands in a room so still you could almost hear the faint rustle of the dead man’s spirit lingering close beside his grieving daughter.

Mrs. Thornton gave Mr. Bell her formal, stately, hospitable welcome; she was never more gracious than when she received her son’s friends in her own home, and the more unexpected the guest, the more it proved her housekeeping was always perfectly prepared for any emergency. “How is Miss Hale?” she asked.

“Broke almost completely by this last blow.”

“It is a very great mercy for her that she has such a devoted friend as you.”

“I wish I were her only friend, madam,” Bell replied gruffly. “Doubtless it sounds brutal, but here I have been displaced from my post as her comforter by a fine lady aunt, and there are cousins and relations in London all laying claim to her as if she were a lapdog left to them in a will. The poor girl is too weak and miserable to have a will of her own right now.”

“She must indeed be very weak,” Mrs. Thornton said, with a quiet, pointed implication her son understood perfectly. “But where have all these relations been this past year and more, when Miss Hale was so nearly friendless, bearing so much sorrow and anxiety?” She did not care enough for the answer to wait for it, and left the room to arrange the guest’s supper.

“They have been living abroad,” Bell said to Thornton. “The aunt did bring her up, she and Edith have been like sisters, so their claim is real. The thing that vexes me is that I wanted to take her as my own ward, and I am jealous of these people who do not seem to half value the precious privilege of loving her. It would be different if Frederick came back to claim her, of course.”

“Frederick!” Thornton exclaimed, startled. “Who is that? What right—” He cut himself off mid-sentence, checking his outburst.

“Frederick Hale, Margaret’s brother! Surely you know about him? I thought I told you long ago, when the Hales first came to Milton, that their eldest son was mixed up in a naval mutiny, and cannot set foot on English soil without being arrested.”

“I have never heard his name before. Where does he live?”

“Spain, as far as I know. The poor fellow will be heartbroken he cannot come home for his father’s funeral. We shall have to make do with Captain Lennox as the closest male relation.”

“I hope I may be allowed to attend the service, to pay my respects.”

“Of course you may! Hale liked you, you know, he spoke of you just a few days ago when I met him in Oxford. He regretted he had not seen more of you lately. I am very grateful you wish to honour him.”

“But about Frederick,” Thornton pressed. “He never came back to England secretly, did he?”

“Never. Good heavens, no.”

“Was he not here, around the time Mrs. Hale died? I saw a young man walking with Miss Hale one day, around that very time.”

“Oh that would be young Henry Lennox, the Captain’s brother, the lawyer. They corresponded with him regularly, and Mr. Hale told me once he was expecting him down for a visit.” Bell turned, squinting one eye shut to scrutinise Thornton’s face closely. “Do you know, I once fancied you had a little soft spot for Margaret?”

Thornton said nothing, did not change his expression.

“And poor Hale thought so too, though it never occurred to him till I put the idea in his head.”

“I admire Miss Hale,” Thornton said, steady as a rock, pushed to defence by Bell’s prodding. “Everyone must admire her. She is a beautiful creature.”

“Is that all? You speak of her in that cold, measured way, as if she is nothing but a pretty thing to catch the eye! I had hoped you had enough nobleness in you to give her the homage of a true heart. Even if she would have rejected you, I know she would have—loving her without return would have lifted you higher than all those people who ever knew her to love back. ‘Beautiful creature’ indeed! You would not speak of a horse or a dog that way, would you?”

Thornton’s eyes glowed like hot red embers. “Mr. Bell, before you speak so, remember not all men are free to say every single thing they feel out loud the way you are. Let us speak of something else.” His heart had leaped at every word Bell said, he knew he would value this old Oxford fellow’s understanding for the rest of his life, but he would not be badgered into gushing praise of Margaret like some cheap parrot reciting verses. He turned the conversation to the business they had between them, as landlord and tenant. “What is that heap of bricks and mortar I saw in the mill yard just now? Repairs needed?”

“No, no repairs. I am building a new dining hall for the mill hands.”

“I thought as a bachelor you would have no use for a second dining room big enough for a dozen families.”

“I got to know a very strange chap, Higgins the weaver, some time back. I put a couple of children he was fond of into school, and one day I stopped by his cottage to pay him a small sum of money he was owed, and saw his family eating the most miserable, cinder-greasy, burnt dinner I have ever seen. That got me thinking.
