The air in Mrs. Meecher's boarding house tasted of stale tobacco and the specific, cloying sweetness of cheap perfume. Sally looked down the long table, her expression one of manufactured contentment. The shock, sudden and violent, had evaporated, leaving her in a state of startling, unnatural calm. Every time she had anticipated this encounter, knowing it was inevitable, a phantom panic had seized her chest. Now, standing in the reality of it, the fear held no purchase. The chaotic energy of the night had drained her completely, rendering her immune to any surge of violent emotion.

"Hullo, Sally!" Gerald's voice emerged, thick and slurred.

He stood swaying, one hand gripping the doorframe for balance. He wore no collar, his sleeves rolled up, the scent of cheap liquor clinging to him. His face was pale and bloated, surrounded by a halo of sodden disreputability.

Sally said nothing. Exhaustion weighed her down, pushing her into a second phase of fatigue—a numb, hyper-alert stillness. She regarded him with the detached coolness reserved for strangers, observing the wreckage of a man she once knew.

"Hullo?" Gerald tried again.

"What do you want?" Sally asked.

"I heard your voice," he stammered, a foolish grin plastered on his features. "Saw the door open. Thought I'd come in."

"What do you want?"

The weak smile fractured. A single tear tracked down his cheek; his intoxication had curdled into maudlin despair.

"Sally... S-Sally... I'm very miserable." The syllables tripped over each other. "Heard your voice. Saw the door open. Thought I'd come in."

Something flickered at the edge of Sally's consciousness. She felt a phantom familiarity, as though she had already endured this script. Then memory clicked into place. This was simply Mr. Reginald Cracknell, resurrected.

"I think you had better go to bed, Gerald," she said, her tone steady and unyielding. Neither his shameless misery nor his ragged appearance stirred a ripple within her.

"What's the use? Can't sleep. No good. Couldn't sleep. Sally, you don't know how worried I am. I see what a fool I've been."

Sally made a sharp gesture, anticipating a belated apology for his treatment of her. She did not wish to listen to Gerald weeping for the life he had attempted to destroy. Yet, the weight pressing on his soul was elsewhere.

"I was a fool ever to try writing plays," he continued, his voice dropping to a soft, self-pitying murmur. "Got a winner first time, but can't repeat. It's no good. Ought to have stuck to newspaper work. I'm good at that. Shall have to go back to it. Had another frost to-night. No good trying any more. Shall have to go back to the old grind, damn it."

He wept softly, consumed by pity for his own tragic circumstances.

"Very miserable," he whispered.

He stepped forward, lurched, and retreated to the solid anchor of the door. For a fleeting instant, Sally's artificial composure pierced with a stab of contempt. It vanished instantly, replaced by her armor of indifference.

"Go to bed, Gerald," she commanded. "You'll feel better in the morning."

Perhaps a fragment of that warning penetrated Gerald's muddled logic. He winced, his demeanor sinking into a deeper melancholy.

"May not be alive in the morning," he declared solemnly. "Good mind to end it all. End it all!" He made a sweeping gesture, cutting it short as he clutched the door handle.

Sally had no patience for melodrama.

"Oh, go to bed," she said, her impatience rising. The frozen indifference that had gripped her began to thaw, replaced by a swelling resentment—not only against Gerald for degrading himself, but against herself for ever finding glamour in such a man. It humiliated her to recall how completely his personality had once mastered hers. Under the sting of this humiliation, she felt hard and pitiless. Dimly, she sensed a shift within her. Usually, the sight of suffering triggered immediate sympathy; yet Gerald's lamentations about returning to drudgery elicited none. The sufferer noted this, commenting silently on her lack of response.

"You're very unsymp... unsympathetic," he complained.

"I'm sorry," Sally replied. She walked briskly to the door and pushed it open. Gerald, still clutching the frame, moved out into the passage, attached to the handle like a man whose foundation had suddenly liquefied. He released the metal and shuffled uncertainly across the hall. Seeing his own door ajar, he staggered through the threshold. Sally watched him reach his destination safely before turning into her bedroom, intending to terminate the disturbing night with sleep.

Almost immediately, she abandoned the plan. Sleep was impossible. A feverish restlessness seized her. Donning a kimono, she went to the kitchen to check if her supplies permitted a glass of hot milk.

She had just recalled presenting the last of the milk to a sandy cat with a purposeful eye, which had slipped through the window earlier that morning to share breakfast, when a muffled crash shattered the silence.

She listened intently. The sound seemed to emanate from across the passage. Hurrying to the door, she opened it. Instantly, from the apartment opposite erupted a perfect fusillade of crashes, each one seemingly louder and more appalling than the last.

There is something about sudden, loud noises in the stillness of the night that shatters even the most rigid detachment. Moments prior, Gerald's threat of violence had left her unmoved; now, her mind leapt back to his words, and apprehension succeeded indifference. Undeniably, Gerald was in an irresponsible mood, capable of almost anything under its influence. Listening in the doorway, Sally felt a momentary spike of panic.

A brief silence followed the explosion, but as she hesitated, the noise burst forth again. This time, so loud and compelling, that Sally hesitated no longer. She ran across the passage and pounded on the door.

Whatever devastation had transpired inside, Gerald had survived it. A moment later, a dragging footstep sounded, and the door swung open. Gerald stood on the threshold, the weak smile back on his face.

"Hullo, Sally!"

Seeing him, disreputable and clearly unscathed, Sally's brief alarm died, replaced by the familiar feeling of impatient resentment. Beyond her other grievances, he had apparently frightened her unnecessarily.

"Whatever was all that noise?" she demanded.

"Noise?" Gerald considered the point, mouth agape.

"Yes, noise," Sally snapped.

"I've been cleaning house," Gerald said with the owl-like gravity of a man barely conscious of his own instability.

Sally pushed past him. The apartment was an almost exact replica of her own, evidently decorated by Elsa Doland with painstaking, niggly feminine care. Amateur interior decoration had always been Elsa's hobby. Even in the unpromising confines of her boarding-house room, she had contrived a certain daintiness that Sally, lacking such talent, had always envied. Elsa's mind ran toward small, fragile ornaments; she feared no over-furnishing. Pictures jostled one another on the walls; china of every description cluttered little tables; a profusion of lamps with parti-colored glass shades cast fractured light; plates lined the shelves.

One might say the plates were ranged and the pictures jostled, but it would be more accurate to reverse the statement: they had jostled and been ranged. Only by guesswork could Sally reconstruct the scene before Gerald began his "cleaning." She entered briskly but halted short, appalled by the majestic ruin awaiting her gaze. A shellburst in a sitting-room could scarcely have wrought more havoc.

The psychology of a man of weak character, intoxicated and thwarted by ambition, defies easy plowing; his moods shift with bewildering speed. Ten minutes prior, Gerald had been gripped by clammy self-pity; now, that phase seemed to have returned. Yet, in the interim, a brief but adequate spasm of near-Berserk fury must have occurred. What caused it, or why it expended itself so abruptly, Sally lacked the psychological tools to explain; ocular evidence confirmed only that it had happened. A heavy niblick, flung petulantly—or remorsefully—into a corner, indicated the instrument of destruction.

Bleak chaos reigned everywhere. The floor was littered with every conceivable shape and size of broken glass and china. Fragments of pictures, looking as if chewed by a prehistoric beast, lay amidst heaps of shattered statuettes and vases. As Sally moved slowly into the room after her involuntary pause, china crackled beneath her feet. Surveying the stripped walls with a wondering eye, she turned to Gerald for an explanation.

Gerald had subsided onto an occasional table, weeping softly once more. It had struck him again that he had been terribly, terribly mistreated.

"Well!" Sally gasped. "You've certainly made a good job of it!"

A sharp crack echoed as the occasional table, never designed to bear heavy weights, gave way in a splintering flurry of broken legs under the pressure of the homeowner. Sally's mood underwent an abrupt transformation. Few situations in life hold equal potentialities for both tragedy and farce, and it was the ludicrous side of this drama that appealed to Sally at this moment. Her sense of humor was tickled. If she could have analyzed her feelings, she would have found herself mocking—the feeble sentimental Sally who had once conceived the absurd

Darkness approached with a hesitant apology, reluctant to extinguish the perfection of the summer day. Beyond the treeline, a faint afterglow persisted, while a new moon hung above the barn like a silver sickle. Sally emerged from the house, bowing three solemn times for luck, then stood on the gravel, inhaling the evening's sweetness until life itself tasted good. The shadows, initially shy, now committed to their work with ruthless efficiency. The sky unified into a deep, matte blue, speckled with quiet stars, rendering the state road leading to Patchogue and Babylon indistinguishable from the earth.

Lights flickered on in the cottages across the meadow. From the kennels, a single, sleepy bark drifted out. The small, white, woolly dog that had trotted at Sally's heels halted abruptly, emitting a sharp, questioning squeak. The silence was so profound that Ginger's footsteps, pounding back from the village where he had procured provisions, newspapers, and wool for Sally's sweater, echoed long before he turned the gate. Invisible though he was, Sally caught the sound and felt that familiar, cozy thrill of happiness that had defined her evenings for a year.

"Ginger," she called. "What ho!"

The woolly dog responded with another significant squeak, scurrying down the drive to investigate, only to receive a cold greeting. Ginger, despite his professed love for canines, had never managed to extend affection to Toto. He had protested vehemently when Sally, distressed by Mrs. Meecher's lethargy regarding her pet, begged him to offer hospitality and country air to the invalid.

"It's wonderful what you've done for Toto, angel," Sally said as Ginger approached, evading the animal's enthusiastic leaps with icy indifference. "He's a different dog."

"Bit of luck for him," Ginger replied. "All through my tenure at Mrs. Meecher's, I never saw him move faster than a stately walk. Now he runs about constantly."

"The blighter had been overeating since birth," Ginger diagnosed. "That was the sole error. A little judicious dieting fixed him. We'll be able to ship him back next week."

"I shall quite miss him."

"I nearly missed him this morning—with a shoe," Ginger noted casually. "He was perched on the kitchen table wolfing bacon, and I took steps."

"My cave-man!" Sally murmured. "I always suspected a frightfully brutal streak in you. Ginger, what an evening!"

"Good Lord!" Ginger exclaimed suddenly as they stepped into the kitchen's light. "Now what?" He paused, scrutinizing her intently. "Do you know you're looking prettier than when I headed down to the village?"

Sally hugged his arm gently. "Beloved!" she said. "Did you secure the chops?"

Ginger froze, horror washing over him. "Oh, my aunt! I completely forgot them!"

"Oh, Ginger, you are an old chump," Sally laughed. "Well, you'll have to undergo a little judicious dieting yourself, like Toto."

"I say, I'm most awfully sorry. I got the wool."

"If you think I'm going to eat wool..."

"Isn't there anything else in the house?"

"Vegetables and fruit."

"Fine! But, naturally, if you desire chops..."

"Not at all. I'm spiritual. Besides, people claim vegetables are beneficial for blood pressure or something similar. Did you forget the mail?"

"Absolutely not! I was on top of it like a knife. Two letters from fellows seeking Airedale puppies."

"No! Ginger, we are progressing!"

"Pretty bloated," Ginger agreed complacently. "Pretty bloated. If things continue buzzing along like this, we'll manage to acquire that two-seater. There was a letter for you. Here it is."

"It's from Fillmore," Sally observed, inspecting the envelope as they entered the kitchen. "About time, too. No word from him for months."

She seated herself and unfolded the letter. Ginger hauled himself onto the table, wriggling into a comfortable posture to scan his evening paper. After skimming the sporting section, he lowered the sheet, allowing his gaze to rest on Sally's bent head with a sense of total contentment. Though a husband of nearly a year, Ginger still wandered a magical realm in a state of dazed disbelief, unable to fully grasp that such bliss existed. In his experience, he had witnessed many distant prospects that failed under scrutiny, except for this arrangement of marriage. With Sally as his partner, matrimony appeared to be one of the rare endeavors devoid of any catch. His honest eyes gleamed as he watched her.

Sally broke into a fit of giggles. "Ginger, look at this!"

He leaned forward, taking the slip of paper she offered. Bold letters declared: POPP'S OUTSTANDING SUCCULENT----APPETIZING----NUTRITIOUS. (JUST SAY "POP!" A CHILD CAN DO IT.)

Ginger regarded the cipher with a puzzled furrow. "What is it?" he asked.

"It's Fillmore."

"How do you mean?" Sally gurgled. "Fillmore and Gladys have launched a small restaurant in Pittsburgh."

"A restaurant!" Shock colored Ginger's voice. Although he knew the managerial career of his brother-in-law, the modern Napoleon, had ended in a spectacular crash, he had never lost his reverence for what he deemed a mastermind. For Fillmore Nicholas, the Man of Destiny, to descend to operating a restaurant—and a diminutive one at that—seemed almost indecent. Sally, however, utterly lacking in proper reverence for her brother's grandeur, was delighted.

"It's the most splendid idea," she enthused. "It truly appears Fillmore is finally destined for greatness. They evidently started small, merely crafting pork-pies..."

"Why Popp?" Ginger interrupted, voicing a question that perplexed him deeply.

"Just a trade name, silly. Gladys is a marvelous cook; she makes the pies while Fillmore sells them. They did so well they've expanded into a proper restaurant, which is also successful. Listen to this." Sally gurgled again and turned the page. "...sound financial footing. In fact, our success has been so instantaneous that I have decided to launch out on a really big scale. It is Big Ideas that lead to Big Business. I am contemplating a vast extension of this venture, and in a very short time, I shall organize branches in New York, Chicago, Detroit, and all the major cities, each managed separately and featuring, alongside standard cuisine, these Popp's Outstanding Pork-pies. Once established, I shall sail for England to introduce Popp's Pork-pies there..."

"Isn't he a little wonder!"

"Dashed brainy chap. Always said so."

"I must admit, I grew uneasy reading that. I've seen so many of Fillmore's Big Ideas. That's his pattern: he finds something promising, then overextends and bursts. However, it's fine now that he has Gladys managing him. She added a postscript. Just four words, yet oh, how comforting to a sister's heart. 'Yes, I don't think!' is what she writes, and I haven't read anything more cheering. Thank heavens she has poor dear Fillmore well in hand."

"Pork-pies!" Ginger mused, as the pangs of genuine hunger assailed his interior. "I wish he'd send us one of those outstanding little chaps. I could certainly use it."

Sally rose and tousled his red hair. "Poor old Ginger! I knew you couldn't handle it. Come on, it's a lovely night; let's stroll to the village and feast at the inn. We're going to be millionaires before we know it, so we can easily afford it."
