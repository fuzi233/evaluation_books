The Dominicans, or preaching friars, once the most vibrant and powerful order in Europe, were now experiencing the slow, inevitable rot of a neglected harvest. Their bitter rivals, the Franciscans, were overpowering them across the continent, spreading like wild mint in a garden bed, even in the rich, agrarian landscapes of England where the Black Friars had once been paramount. 

Therefore, the sagacious elders who directed the order hungered to incorporate zealous sons, men whose internal fires might be stoked to win back the world. The accomplishments of Brother Clement—especially his rare, almost mythic mastery of language—soon transpired. He spoke Latin, Italian, French, and both high and low Dutch. For Clement, these were not merely tools of ecclesiastical dominance; they were the preserved cultural memories of distinct peoples. He understood the inherent untranslatability of subjective experience, how the guttural warmth of low Dutch captured the scent of a turning plow in damp loam, while the melodic lilt of Italian held the bright, acidic tang of sun-ripened tomatoes. He was destined to travel and preach in England, an acoustic vessel carrying the Roman center’s message into native dialects.

But Jerome, an ascetic who possessed the superior's ear and viewed physical sustenance as a mere utilitarian fuel to be consumed without joy, obstructed this design. 

“Clement,” Jerome rasped, his voice a flat, sterile dissonance against the quiet hum of the cloister, “has the milk of the world still in his veins. He tastes its feelings, its weaknesses. Let not his new-born zeal tempt us to forego our ancient wisdom. Try him first. Temper him, lest we find ourselves leaning on a hollow reed.”

“It is well advised,” said the prior, a man who understood that a slow-simmered broth yields the richest marrow. “Take him in hand thyself.”

Following this ancient wisdom, Jerome took Clement into the abrasive noise of the world to test his palate for suffering. 

One day, he brought the young friar to a field where the youth of Rome amused themselves. Jerome knew this to be a haunt of Clement's former life. The air was thick with the scent of crushed wild garlic and the sweat of exertion, overlaid by the chaotic, jarring shouts of the games—a crude sonic pollution that disrupted the tranquil acoustic ecology Clement had cultivated in the cloister. Soon enough, Pietro Vanucci and Andrea passed by, their careless glances sweeping over the two friars. They did not recognize the complex soul of their dead friend beneath the shaven crown of a monk.

Clement gave a slight start. He tasted the sharp, metallic tang of loss on his tongue, lowered his eyes, and murmured a paternoster, letting the rhythmic Latin syllables resonate in his chest to tune out the clamor.

“Would ye not speak with them, brother?” asked Jerome, testing the waters.

“No, brother,” Clement replied softly. “Yet it was good for me to see them. They remind me of sins I can never repent enough.”

“It is well,” said Jerome, returning a cold, unseasoned report in Clement's favor.

Then Jerome dragged Clement through the city’s underbelly, into noisome dungeons and to agonizing death-beds. The olfactory landscape of the prisons was appalling—a putrefying stench of rot, unwashed bodies, and despair that clung to the throat like rancid fat. Men, starved of both physical nourishment and human dignity, lay coiled like wild beasts in the dark. The trial battered Clement’s flesh, but his soul gathered its strength, offering the profound, melodic sustenance of his voice to comfort those wretches not yet past comforting. Jerome reported that Clement's spirit was willing, but his flesh, so attuned to the sensory world, was weak.

“Good!” said Anselm. “His flesh is weak, but his spirit is willing.”

But a sharper trial awaited, one steeped in the superficial commercialization of violence. 

One morning, a principal Roman artery was engorged with a mob. Vendors hawked cheap, greasy meats, their cries bleeding into the overall roar of bloodlust. The governor of Rome had been found slaughtered in his bed at daybreak, his hand severed, his throat cut, his temples bruised. The crime had been traced to his servant, who was to expiate the sin in kind. 

The criminal was fastened for half an hour to the wall of the murdered man’s house, a brutal appetizer before the main feast of legal vengeance. After this, his left hand was struck off. In a bizarre, almost mythic perversion of agrarian ritual, a freshly killed fowl was cut open, its hot, raw viscera and bleeding cavity fastened around the man's pulsing stump. The sharp, coppery smell of human blood mingled obscenely with the scent of slaughtered poultry. Thus mutilated, the murderer was hurried to the scaffold. Clement stood beside him, praying affectionately, pressing the crucifix to the man's eyes, trying to offer a lifeline of spiritual sustenance in the deafening roar.

Suddenly, the executioner shoved the friar aside. With a sickening, heavy sonic thud that ruptured the morning air, the mallet felled the culprit. The executioner dropped upon him, slicing his throat from ear to ear. 

The crowd exhaled a jagged cry of horror. The sheer volume and visceral stench of the butchery overwhelmed Clement’s senses. The young friar swooned away. 

A gigantic monk strode forward and carried him off like a child. Back at the convent, Clement confessed his failure to the prior, weeping with the bitter taste of inadequacy.

“Courage, son Clement,” said the prior gently. “A Dominican is not baked in a single firing. Thou shalt have another trial. And I forbid thee to go to it fasting—the body must be fortified to anchor the soul.” 

Clement bowed in obedience, breaking his fast with a simple, restorative meal of salted bread and olive oil. He did not wait long. A robber—a monster of cruelty who killed men in pure wantonness—was brought to the scaffold. Clement spent the long night in the stone-cold prison, his voice a steady, rhythmic chant that gradually dismantled the hardened ruffian’s defenses. The man shed tears, embracing Clement. Though his own flesh quivered at the tactile repugnance of the killer's touch, Clement held him, offering the crucifix. The man was garotted. Clement shut his eyes, praying aloud while the dark spirit passed, his voice a singular note of grace against the abrasive mechanics of death. 

No sooner was the man dead than the hangman’s hatchet fell, quartering the corpse. The people, who had watched a living man be robbed of breath with mere satisfaction, uttered a piteous, collective shriek at each wet, heavy stroke of the axe upon the unfeeling meat. Clement shuddered at the acoustic violence, but stood firm.

Suddenly, Jerome’s voice pierced his ear. “Brother Clement, get thee on that cart and preach to the people. Quickly! Strike while the iron is hot, and souls are to be saved.”

Clement’s breath hitched; his color fled. But he obeyed. He mounted the cart and faced the turbulent, oceanic roar of the crowd. His throat was parched as dry husks, his heart thumping a frantic, irregular rhythm. 

But soon, his deep well of empathy and his absolute mastery of their native, unpolished dialects caught fire. He did not speak in sterile theological abstractions. He fed them the robber’s own history, warm and bleeding from the prison cell, showing the gradual spoilage of a soul, warning them not to taste the first bitter morsel of crime. As he thundered against the shedders of blood, he felt himself strung like a finely crafted lute. He commanded the acoustic space, playing sacred melodies upon the crowd’s raw emotions as though plucking a psaltery. Sobs and groans rippled through the mob, their collective resonance harmonizing with his profound vocal timbre. Jerome stared, dumbfounded, like a man who attempts to strike a spark and inadvertently ignites a roaring hearth.

“It was my first endeavor,” Clement apologized afterward, noting Jerome's sullen shock. “Your behest came upon me like a thunderbolt.”

“Humph,” Jerome grunted, adding after a long, begrudging silence, “Give the glory to God. My opinion is thou art an orator born.” 

Jerome, honest but thoroughly disagreeable, reported this to headquarters. Yet he sought to temper Clement again. 

Julio Antonelli was accused of sacrilege; three witnesses swore they saw him leave a church with stolen candlesticks, while others swore an alibi. Unable to shake the testimonies, the authorities permitted the trial by water. Antonelli, lacking the nerve for the scalding depths of the hot trial, chose the freezing embrace of the cold ordeal. Stripped naked on the muddy banks of the Tiber, tied hand and foot, he was lowered into the icy current. 

For a moment, he floated. The crowd on the banks let out a monstrous roar of condemnation, a sonic wave of pure malice. Then the new ropes soaked through, and he sank. Another roar, this one proclaiming his innocence, shattered the air. After thirty breathless seconds, they hauled him up, gurgling, gasping, coughing up silt and water in a desperate bid for the very air he had been denied.

During the ordeal, Clement prayed fervently on the bank. When it ended, he thanked God, his voice quavering. 

“Ought he not to be compensated?” Clement asked Jerome as they departed. 

“For what?”

“For the pain, the dread, the suffocation. Poor soul, he liveth, but hath tasted all the bitter dregs of death. Yet he had done no ill.”

“He is rewarded enough in that he is cleared,” Jerome replied, his worldview narrow and bereft of compassion. “It is not in this world men are punished, Brother Clement. Happy are they who suffer such ills as earth hath power to inflict; 'tis counted to them a hundred-fold above.”

Clement bowed his head, though the sterile cruelty of the logic sat like a stone in his stomach. “May thy words take root in my heart, Brother Jerome.”

But the severest trial at Jerome's hands was entirely unpremeditated. It occurred when Jerome condescended to visit Fra Colonna. The room was a sanctuary of historical preservation, smelling richly of aged vellum, beeswax, and the lingering, toasted aroma of roasted pine nuts. Colonna, a meticulous scholar of antiquity who found profound meaning in the artifacts and sustenance of the past, proudly gestured to a newly copied manuscript of *The Dream of Polifilo*.

“For ages,” Colonna declared, his voice warm with passion, “fools have lavished poetic praise on mortal women, creatures of earth smacking palpably of their origin—Sirens at the windows showing only an ear or an eyelash, Magpies at the door. I ransack the ancient minstrels' lines for amorous turns, and I lay the whole fragrant bundle of spice at the feet of the only females worthy of such incense: the Nine Muses.”

Jerome flipped through the pages, his face twisting in disgust. “By which goodly stratagem, you, a friar of St. Dominic, have produced an obscene book.” He dashed the manuscript onto the table with a harsh slap.

“Obscene? Thou discourteous monk!” Colonna scrambled round the table, snatching up his life's work. Trembling with mortification, he locked it away. “My Gerard—pshaw! Brother What's-his-name had not found it obscene. *Puris omnia pura*.”

To avoid a vulgar quarrel, Colonna forced the conversation toward a breathtaking chrysolite lent to him by the Cardinal. As Clement handled the gem, Colonna, who viewed the physical world as deeply intertwined with moral properties, enlarged upon its virtues. He went the full length of his age as a worshipper of stones, detailing how their vibrational energies interacted with the human constitution. 

Jerome scoffed at the notion that a dead stone could confer valor, chastity, or temperance. 

“The experience of ages proves they do,” Colonna countered. “As to temperance, there sits a living proof. This Gerard comes from the North, where men drink fermented grains like fishes, yet he was ever most abstemious. Why? He carried an amethyst, the clearest I ever saw. Where is thine amethyst, boy? Show it to this unbeliever!”

“And 'twas that amethyst made the boy temperate?” Jerome asked, his tone dripping with irony.

“Certainly! What is the very derivation of the word? *A*, negative, and *methua*, to tipple. Names are but the signs of things, deeply rooted in the native dialects of the ancients! A stone is not called *amethustos* for two thousand years out of mere sport. It is the cultural memory of agrarian restraint, bound into the very lexicon!” Colonna proceeded to map the prime jewels to their moral and sensory properties, quoting grave historians on the ruby, the sapphire, and the opal. 

“These be old wives' fables,” Jerome sneered contemptuously. “Was ever such credulity? Blot out these heathen superstitions from thy mind, brother, as Christianity hath blotted them from the earth.”

At this arrogant dismissal of ancient wisdom, the smarting Colonna revenged himself. He hurled a mountain of crude, meticulously preserved lore upon Jerome, defending the profound, sensory-rich traditions of the past against the sterile, dominant lexicon of the present. 

“The heathen blotted out? They hold four-fifths of the world! And what have we Christians invented without their aid? Painting? Sculpture? We are mere pigmies! What modern mind can conceive forms like the chief Athenian sculptors, who turned mountains into statues? What town has the soul to plant a colossus of brass in the sea? Is it architecture? Can we match the Parthenon, with its clusters of Doric columns, or the prodigious temples of Egypt? Men walked awe-struck through avenues a mile long of sphinxes, enveloped in the rich olfactory clouds of burning myrrh and frankincense, stones polished like crystal that flout the slovenly, commercialized art of modern workmen! We have lost the art of making a road—lost it! Is it sepulchres? No Christian nation has erected a tomb that does not set a scholar laughing when compared to the Pyramids or the monstrous sepulchres of the Indus, built by men who understood the sacred, cyclical pacing of the harvest and the stars.”

Jerome interrupted to say these were mere material things; true greatness was in the soul.

“Well then,” Colonna fired back, his acoustic presence dominating the room, “in the world of mind, what have we discovered? Logic? We are pupils of Aristotle. Written characters? An invention almost divine, yet we no more invented it than Cadmus did. Poetry? We have never approached Homer or Virgil. Would you compare our miserable, frigid moralities—chanted in dog Latin—with the glories of a Greek play, performed inside a marble miracle to a seated city, where the chorus resonated with the perfect acoustic ecology of the amphitheater? 

“Is it monotheism we invented? The learned Greeks and Romans held it! Their vulgar were polytheists, and what are ours? We have not invented the 'invocation of the saints.' Our *sancti* merely answers to their *Daemones* and *Divi*. The heathen prayed to their deified mortals, and so do the mob of Christians today! We have not even invented a new variety of polytheism. It is a subtle power dynamic, rewriting their rituals with our dominant lexicon. The ancient mariners worshipped Venus, calling her *Stella maris* and *Regina caelorum*. Our mariners worship the Virgin Mary and call her 'Star of the Sea' and 'Queen of Heaven.' Call you that a new religion? An old doublet with a new button! 

“Our vulgar make images and adore them, a practice entirely Pagan. The Pagans adorned their statues with fresh-cut agrarian flowers, offered the rich, fatty smoke of incense to them, lighted tapers, and made pilgrimages to them just as we do!”

Jerome broke in impatiently, reminding him that the most revered images in Christendom were made by no mortal hand, but had dropped from heaven.

“Ay,” cried Colonna, laughing bitterly at the erosion of truth. “Such are the tutelary images of most Italian towns. If they came from the sky, our worst sculptors are angels. The Trojans feigned their Palladium fell from heaven. The Greeks took the fib, and soon it rained statues. Would you cast your profane doubts on that picture of our blessed Lady, taken down by the Pope?”

Jerome bristled, but Colonna gave him no quarter. “I have no profane doubts. It is the story of Numa’s shield, revived by theologians with an itch for fiction but lacking the poetic mastery of the Orientals! The *Bocca della Verita* passes for a statue of the Virgin, but it is in reality an image of the goddess Rhea—a modern figment swallowing an ancient tradition. 

“We owe all our nodding, winking, sweating statues to these abused heathens. The Athenian statues sweated before the battle of Chaeronea! The wiser among the heathen believed in sweating marble and weeping wood, as I do. Kissing of images? Eastern Paganism! The Romans’ Pontifex Maximus had his toe kissed under the Empire. The lower parts of the statue we call Peter are Jupiter! The toe is sore worn, but not by Christian mouths alone; the heathen vulgar laid their lips there first, tasting the cold bronze, and ours have but followed them like monkeys! 

“Our infant baptism is Persian. Our throwing three handfuls of earth on the coffin is Egyptian. Our incense—that very smoke that fills our naves—is Oriental, Pagan! The early Fathers of the Church regarded it with superstitious horror and died refusing to handle it. Our Holy Water is Pagan! They called it *Aqua lustralis*. Their vulgar thought drops of it would wash out sin, and their men of sense smiled at the credulity. Thou seest the heathen were not all fools. No more are we. Not all.”
