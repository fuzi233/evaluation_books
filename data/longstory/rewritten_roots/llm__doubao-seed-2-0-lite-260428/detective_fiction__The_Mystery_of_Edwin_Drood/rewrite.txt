Ancient, solid, square, grey—there stands the tower of the old English cathedral I know like the back of my own hand. But it has no right to be here. No rusted iron spike jabs up into the sky between my line of sight and it, not in the real landscape I’m half-clawing my way back to. What is that spike, then? Who drove it there?
Maybe the Sultan ordered it, for impaling every last horde of Turkish robbers, strung up one after another. The dream’s warped logic fits perfect: cymbals clash, the Sultan rides past in a procession a mile long, ten thousand scimitars flash in the sun, thirty thousand dancing girls scatter rose petals in his path. White elephants draped in silks of every bright hue follow, endless, attended by a thousand servants. But still, that impossible cathedral tower looms in the background, and no writhing impaled figure is skewered on that grim spike.
Wait a second. Is that spike nothing more than the rusted iron tip on the top post of a broken, tumbled-over old bedstead? The thought draws a low, drowsy laugh out of the man as he floats half-aware, piecing his scattered wits back together.
Shaking from head to toe, he pushes himself up on trembling arms, and blinks at the room around him. It’s the smallest, meanest, airless hovel imaginable. Ragged scraps of curtain strung over the window filter pale early dawn light in from a filthy, rat-infested backyard court. He’s still fully clothed, splayed across a lumpy, stained bed that’s collapsed under its own rotting frame. Next to him, also fully dressed and laid across the bed rather than lengthwise, are a Chinaman, a Lascar sailor, and a gaunt, haggard old woman. The two men are dead to the world, sunk in a deep opium stupor. The woman is hunched over a crudely made pipe, blowing on it to get the spark going. Her bony hand cups the tiny red flame, that little glow the only lamp in the dim morning, lighting up her gaunt, yellowed face for him.
“Another hit?” she whispers, her voice rough and rattling, full of petty irritation. “Want another?”
He presses a palm to his throbbing forehead, staring around disoriented.
“You’ve already smoked five of them since you stumbled in at midnight,” she complains, droning on like she’s said the same lines a hundred times before. “Oh lord, my poor head’s killing me. Those two came in right after you. Business is so slow these days! No Chinamen hanging round the docks, hardly any Lascars, no ships coming into port, everyone says. Here, this one’s all ready for you, love. You’ll be a good soul and remember the market price is sky high right now, won’t you? Three and six a thimbleful! And don’t forget, nobody but me (well, Jack Chinaman on the other side of the court, but he can’t mix it half as good) knows the real secret to the blend. You’ll pay me what it’s worth, yeah, deary?”
She pauses to blow hard on the pipe, bubbling the dregs, and sucks in a deep drag herself.
“Oh my lungs, my poor weak lungs! This one’s nearly done for you, love. My hand’s shaking so bad I think it’s gonna fall off! I saw you coming round, I said to myself, ‘I’ll have another all ready for him, he’ll remember how high opium prices are right now, pay me fair.’ Look, I make all my pipes out of old penny ink bottles, see? Stick a little mouthpiece in the top, this little horn spoon scoops my mix right out of the thimble. Got so drunk on gin for sixteen years straight I was practically dead, before I took to this. This stuff don’t hurt me, not really. Cures the hunger better than bread and dripping, too, love.”
She shoves the nearly spent pipe into his hand, then collapses back onto the mattress, rolling face down.
He staggers to his feet, sets the pipe down hard on the cold hearthstone, and yanks back the ragged curtain, staring at his three slumbering companions with deep revulsion. He notices the old woman’s face, eaten away by years of opium, has taken on the exact same sharp cheekbones, narrowed eyes, sallow yellow skin as the Chinaman snoring next to her. The Chinaman suddenly convulses, fighting some invisible demon or god in his opium dream, snarling so loud it echoes off the walls. The Lascar laughs a wild, empty laugh, drool running down his chin. The old hostess is still, face buried in the bedclothes.
“What sort of visions is she seeing?” he muses, leaning over to tilt her face up towards him. “Dreams of more butcher shops, more pubs, running up tabs no one will pay? Dreams of more hideous customers, this rotting bedstead put back upright, this filthy court swept clean? Even high as she gets, she can’t ever dream of anything better than that, can she?”
He presses his ear to her mouth to catch her mumbles. Nothing but nonsense. Unintelligible.
Watching the tiny, spasmodic twitches rippling across her face and limbs, like jagged lightning flashes across a dark night sky, he feels a strange, contagious restlessness seize him. He stumbles over to a gaunt, broken armchair pulled up by the hearth—left there exactly for people coming down off their opium high, he figures—and grips the armrests tight until he fights off that mad, itching urge to mimic her twitching.
Then he strides back to the bed, grabs the snoring Chinaman by the throat with both hands, and yanks him violently over onto his back. The Chinaman claws at his wrists, gasping, fighting for air, jabbering in his native tongue.
“What are you saying?” he snarls, pausing, waiting for a coherent word. Nothing. Unintelligible.
He loosens his grip slowly, frowning as he listens to the man’s garbled, nonsensical ravings, then turns on the Lascar, dragging him right off the bed onto the dirty floor. The Lascar jolts half-awake, eyes wild, thrashing his arms like he’s fighting an unseen attacker, drawing a phantom knife out of thin air. Then he spots the real knife, tucked safely in the old woman’s dress: she’s jolted awake too, holding the blade, calming the panicking sailor until they both slump back drowsily onto the floor side by side. They chitter and snap at each other for a minute more, but not a single word that makes any sense comes out.
“Unintelligible,” he mutters again, nodding grimly, a cold, dark smile tugging at his mouth. He lays a few silver coins down on the rickety table, grabs his hat, stumbles his way down the broken, creaky stairs, mumbles a good morning to the half-asleep doorkeeper curled up in a rat-infested hutch under the staircase, and steps out into the fresh open air.
That same afternoon, that same massive grey square cathedral tower rises up in front of him, the jaded, travel-worn man. The bells are ringing for daily vesper service, and he walks so fast towards the open cathedral doors it looks like he’s being pulled there, not just heading to worship. The choirboys are scrambling into their smudged white surplices when he bursts in among them, pulls a spare surplice over his own clothes, and falls right into the procession filing into the sanctuary. The sacristan clanks the iron-barred gates shut, locking the chancel off from the rest of the church. Everyone in the procession hurries to their places, bows their heads, and the first low, intoned words echo up into the vaulted arches, rolling like distant thunder: “WHEN THE WICKED MAN—”

If we accept the jackass as the universal marker of smug, unearned conceit—admittedly a convention far harsher than the average beast deserves—then no finer specimen of the breed exists in all of Cloisterham than Mr. Thomas Sapsea, auctioneer.

Sapsea dresses to copy the Dean so closely that strangers have regularly bowed to him by mistake, and once a passerby stopped him on the high street to greet him as My Lord, convinced he was the Bishop, arrived unannounced without his chaplain. Sapsea has dined out on that story for years, just as he preens over his deep rolling voice and his grand dramatic sense of style. He even experimented with soft intoning mid-auction, standing at his podium like it was a church pulpit, to sell parcels of land, and wraps every public sale with a slow, sweeping gesture that looks for all the world like he’s bestowing a benediction on the crowd of brokers below. The real Dean, a quiet, modest gentleman, could never hope to compete.

The local consensus is nearly unanimous, even among people who privately think Sapsea is a dunderhead: he is an unqualified credit to Cloisterham. He is portentous, unshakably dull, speaks with a deliberate roll to every syllable, walks with a swaying gait, and waves his hands with such grave, slow ceremony mid-conversation that you half expect him to offer to confirm you on the spot. Closer to sixty than fifty, with a soft, bulging stomach and permanent horizontal creases worn deep into his waistcoat, widely reputed to be rich, reliable for the “respectable” vote at every local election, and fully convinced he is the only person who has grown in wisdom since infancy, how could he not be a credit to the town?

His premises sit on the High Street, directly across from the Nuns’ House. The building dates back to roughly the same era as the old convent, patched and half-heartedly modernized by generations of residents who gradually decided fresh air and sunlight were preferable to the constant threat of plague. Over his front door hangs a half-life-size wooden carving of Sapsea’s own father, posed in a curly judge’s wig and auctioneer’s toga, hammer raised mid-sale. The whole town has cooed over the “chastity of the concept” and the remarkably natural curve of the little figure’s pinky finger.

Sapsea slumps in his dim ground-floor parlor one crisp autumn evening, a bottle of port wine set out on the table before a crackling early fire. The room opens first onto a paved back yard, then a railed-off patch of unkempt garden. His three favorite possessions sit arranged facing him: his own oil portrait, an eight-day clock he swears never deviates even a second from perfect time, and a barometer he insists dictates the weather rather than the other way around. By his elbow rest a writing desk and loose sheets of paper. He glances down at a scrap of manuscript, reads it to himself with lofty solemnity, then paces the room slowly, thumbs tucked into his waistcoat armholes, reciting it from memory. Only one word drifts out loud, soft and reverent: “Ethelinda.”

Three clean wineglasses sit arranged on a tray. His serving maid pokes her head in to announce “Mr. Jasper is here, sir,” and Sapsea waves grandly to show him in, plucking two of the three glasses off the tray to set by his place.

“Delighted to welcome you to my humble home, sir,” he rumbles, greeting Jasper like he’s granting a audience to royalty. “I congratulate myself on the honor.”

“The honor is entirely mine,” Jasper smiles, sinking into the armchair opposite the fire. “I have wanted to make your acquaintance for a very long time. Everyone in Cloisterham speaks of your great knowledge of the world.”

“Kind of you to say so, young man.” Sapsea pauses to fill both their glasses, then raises his own in a resonant toast left over from his childhood, during the Napoleonic scares: *“When the French come over, May we meet them at Dover!”*

Jasper nods, lifting his glass. “You know the world far better than anyone else cooped up in this tiny town, that much is obvious. I barely step foot outside the close, and it feels like the whole of Cloisterham is all there is to me.”

“Foreign countries never needed to come find me, young man,” Sapsea chuckles, stretching his legs out to toast his heels by the fire. “They came to me, in the way of business. Lay my eyes on a French clock I’ve never seen before? I tap it and say *Paris*. Spot a set of unfamiliar china cups? *Pekin, Nankin, Canton*. I’ve laid my finger on an Esquimaux hunting spear before now, at an estate sale, and named it on the spot for half a pint of sherry. You don’t need to travel when the whole world turns up on your auction block.”

“Remarkable,” Jasper says, feigning wide-eyed fascination. “Most remarkable. That’s exactly what I wanted to speak to you about, when we arranged this visit: the monument for the late Mrs. Sapsea.”

“Quite right, sir.” Sapsea refills their glasses, tucking the decanter back safely on the sideboard before he pulls out his scrap of manuscript. “Before I ask for your opinion as a man of taste on this little inscription—one that cost me no small amount of brow-furrowing to write—you ought to understand what a singular woman Ethelinda was. She died just three quarters of a year ago, you know.”

Jasper tries to smother a yawn behind his wineglass, dropping the screen quickly to wear a look of respectful sympathy, watery eyes still crinkled from the held-back gape.

“Half a dozen years back, when I had expanded my mind to the point I knew I needed another smaller mind to be absorbed into it, I set about looking for a wife,” Sapsea drones on, voice dropping to gravestone solemnity. “Miss Brobity ran the other girls’ school down town, parallel to the Nuns’ House across the way. The whole town said she showed up to every one of my weekday half-holiday auctions, hung on every word of my sales patter. Folks even said you could trace my style of dictation in her students’ copybooks. There was a nasty, untrue rumor once that some idiot parent objected to it out loud, but who in their right mind would dare lay themselves open to the finger of scorn?”

Jasper shakes his head, mock-earnest. Sapsea, half lost in his own grandiloquence, accidentally tops up Jasper’s already full glass, then fills his own empty one to the brim.

“Ethelinda’s whole being was steeped in reverence for great Mind, you see,” he goes on. “When I proposed to her, she was so overawed she could only gasp two words: ‘O Thou!’ meaning me. She never got any further. I sold her school privately, we married, and to the very last day of her life—she suffered from a sluggish liver, poor thing—she never found a phrase grand enough to properly praise my intellect. Always just ‘O Thou!’”

Jasper’s eyes have drifted shut. He jolts them open abruptly, in perfect time with Sapsea’s deepening tone, and intones “Ah!” like he is hanging on every word.

“So here I am, a solitary mourner, wasting my evening conversation on the desert air,” Sapsea sighs, sipping his port. “I sometimes wonder, what if she’d married a man not quite so far above her? If she hadn’t had to look up quite so high, might that have stimulated her liver a little? But no doubt it was all for the best.”

“I suppose it must have been,” Jasper agrees, slumping like he’s fallen into a very dark mood.

“Man proposes, Heaven disposes, as they say.” Sapsea passes him the sheet of paper. “Now, the monument’s had plenty of time to set and cure, so tell me what you think of the inscription.”

Jasper reads:
> Reverential Wife of
> Whose Knowledge of the World,
> Though somewhat extensive,
> Never brought him acquainted with
> More capable of
> And ask thyself the Question,
> If Not,

“Admirable!” Jasper says, handing the scrap back. “Striking, unforgettable, perfect.”

Sapsea preens, inclining his head like he is accepting a well-earned tribute, just as the maid appears again at the door. “Durdles is come, sir.”

Sapsea plucks the third waiting wineglass off the tray and fills it brim full. “Show him in.”

Durdles trundles in, stonemason by trade, the color of chiseled limestone from the top of his dusty old russet hat to the laced boots caked in grave dirt. He is the most famous man in Cloisterham, a sort of licensed town vagabond. Everyone says he is a master stonemason—though no one has seen him do an honest day’s work in years—and everyone knows he is a spectacular sot. He knows every cranny of the cathedral crypt better than the Dean, better than the canons, maybe better than the skeletons buried down there. Legend says he first learned every hidden passage by sneaking down there to sleep off the effects of his drinking binges, back when he held the rough repairs contract for the cathedral grounds. He speaks almost entirely in the third person, like he is a character in a local folk tale: “Durdles come upon the old knight last winter, pickaxe straight through his oak coffin. Old chap looked up at Durdles with his crumbly open eyes like he was saying ‘Is that you, Durdles? I’ve been waiting for you a devil of a time!’ then turned straight to dust.” He carries a two-foot rule in his pocket at all times, a mason’s hammer almost always in his hand, and taps every wall, every buttress, every flagstone around the cathedral until he hears a hollow thud, and calls out to the verger Tope: “New old ’un buried in here!” and Tope reports it to the Dean as a formal new discovery. He lives in a half-finished hovel at the edge of town, the path to his door ankle-deep in stone chips, lined with half-carved urns, broken tombstones and draped marble fragments, his two apprentices chipping stone from dawn till dusk like living emblems of Time and Death.

He downs the glass of port Sapsea shoves at him in one go, then pulls out his two-foot rule and measures the length of Sapsea’s precious inscription line by line, stone grit coating the edge of the paper. “Fits exactly to an eighth of an inch,” he says, not a flicker of emotion on his face. “Your servant, Mr. Jasper. Hope you’re well.”

“Durdles,” Jasper greets him. “Heard you’ve been under the weather.”

“Got a touch of Tombatism,” Durdles grunts. “Comes of being down in the crypt before sunrise on a winter morning, walking among the old ’uns all day. Not rheumatism. Tombatism.”

“It must be bitterly cold down there,” Jasper shivers, authentically.

“Cold for you up in the chancel with all the live folks breathing warm air? You can imagine what it’s like for Durdles, down in the damp earth, breathing the dead breath of hundreds of folks who’ve been gone a thousand years.” Durdles shifts his weight, squinting at Sapsea. “You want this inscription on the monument right away? Then give me the key.”

“Man alive, it’s supposed to go on the outside of the memorial!” Sapsea blinks, confused.

“Durdles don’t do half work,” the stonemason growls. “Always checks his work all around, inside and out, to make sure it does him credit. Everyone in Cloisterham knows Durdles knows his own work better than anyone.”

Sapsea huffs, goes to his wall safe, fumbles out a large iron tomb key. Durdles slips his two-foot rule into the side pocket of his coarse flannel trousers, then opens the huge lined breast pocket sewn into his work coat to tuck the key away.

“Good lord, Durdles,” Jasper laughs. “You’re all pockets!”

“Got weight in ’em too,” Durdles says, pulling out two more huge iron keys, clinking them together. “All to Durdles’s monuments. Keep the keys for all my own work, don’t use ’em much.”

“Funny thing, Durdles,” Jasper says, weighing the keys in his palm idly. “I’ve been meaning to ask you for ages. The local boys sometimes call you Stony Durdles. Is that short for Tony? Or Stephen? Or after your trade?”

But Durdles is a gruff old thing, half foggy from drink, fiercely protective of his dignity. He shoves his two spare keys back into his pockets and buttons them tight, picks up his crumpled dinner bundle of cold bread and meat from the chair, tucks Sapsea’s new key deep inside the bundle like he plans to eat it for supper, and trundles for the door without a word of answer.

Sapsea suggests a game of backgammon after he leaves, and the two men play deep into the night, their conversation seasoned with Sapsea’s endless, meandering pearls of local wisdom, until they finish off a simple supper of cold roast beef and salad. The old auctioneer is only just getting started on his lectures by the time Jasper announces he must head home. He promises to return soon to hear more of Sapsea’s valuable insight, and steps out into the dark Cloisterham night, the great shadow of the cathedral looming over the street behind him, the faint, cold smell of damp crypt stone clinging to his coat.

Moonlight bleached the worn stone of Cloisterham’s Close as John Jasper turned toward home, when he pulled up short. Propped against the iron graveyard railing that separated the churchyard from the ruins of the old cloister arches was Stony Durdles, dinner bundle slung over his shoulder, entirely unperturbed as a ragged, hideous little boy pelted him with rocks like he was a living fairground target.

Some stones glanced off his coat, others missed entirely, and Durdles didn’t so much as flinch. The boy, meanwhile, tooted a shrill, triumphant blast through the jagged half-tooth gap in his front teeth every time his aim landed, and yelped “Mulled agin!” every time he missed, before squaring up for a meaner, more precise throw.
“What do you think you’re doing to that man?” Jasper stepped out of the shadow, into the moonlit open.
“Making a cock-shy of him,” the boy cackled, tensing to back away.
“Hand me those stones in your fist.”
“Yeah, I’ll shove ’em down your throat if you lay a hand on me,” he snarled, already edging out of reach. “I’ll knock your eye out, watch it!”
“Little devil. What harm did he ever do you?”
“He won’t go home.”
“What’s that to you?”
“He pays me a ‘apenny to pelt him there if I catch him out after ten,” the boy explained, then launched into a savage, half-stumbling chant, his tattered boots catching on the uneven cobblestones:
*Widdy widdy wen!
I—ket—ches—Im—out—ar—ter—ten,
Widdy widdy wy!
Then—E—don’t—go—then—I—shy—
Widdy Widdy Wake-cock warning!*
He finished with a wild swing of his arm, launching one last stone straight at Durdles. It was clear the ditty was an agreed-upon warning, giving Durdles fair chance to either dodge the next round of fire or drag himself home before the pelting picked up for good.

Jasper jerked his head to beckon the boy after him—coaxing or dragging the wild thing was clearly a lost cause—and crossed to the railing where Durdles stood, lost in deep, sodden thought.
“You know this… this child?” Jasper fumbled for a word that fit the feral little creature.
“Deputy,” Durdles nodded.
“Is that his name?”
“Deputy,” Durdles confirmed.
“I’m man-servant up at the Travellers’ Twopenny in Gas Works Garding,” the boy piped up, drifting a little closer. “All us man-servants at them lodging houses is called Deputy. When the place is full and all the tramps are abed, I come out for my ‘elth.” He backed off a step, lined up his aim again, and resumed the chant before Jasper cut him off.
“Hold your hand! Don’t throw while I’m standing this close to him, or I’ll make you regret it. C’mon, Durdles, I’ll walk you home tonight. Let me carry that bundle for you.”
“Not on your life,” Durdles adjusted the bundle against his shoulder, swaying slightly. “I was just musing here, you know, sir—surrounded by all my own work, like some famous author. That one there’s your brother-in-law,” he nodded at a cold white sarcophagus glowing in the moonlight, “that’s Mrs. Sapsea over there, her monument. The late incumbent’s broken column, that’s his. That vase there? Belonged to the late Assessed Taxes official. And that weathered headstone? The old muffin baker, everybody loved him. All my work, every last bit, perfectly safe here. No need to talk about the common folks buried under turf and brambles, they’re forgotten fast enough as it is.”
“Deputy’s trailing right behind us,” Jasper glanced over his shoulder. “Is he going to follow us all the way?”
The pair’s dynamic was a capricious, well-rehearsed thing: when Durdles turned slowly, swaying like a man half-drunk, Deputy darted a wide circle out into the road and stood his ground on the defensive.
“You never yelled that Widdy Warning before you started pelting tonight,” Durdles said, suddenly remembering some old, imagined slight.
“Yer lie, I did,” Deputy snapped, his version of polite contradiction.
“Own brother to Peter the Wild Boy,” Durdles turned back to Jasper, already forgetting the argument entirely. “But I gave him a purpose in life.”
“To throw stones at you?” Jasper suggested.
“That’s exactly it, sir,” Durdles grinned, perfectly pleased. “I took him in hand, gave him that one clear job. What was he before? Nothing but a destroyer, stoning every cat, dog, pig, and window in town, winding up in Cloisterham jail every other week. Now he earns an honest halfpenny a day, three penn’orth a week, no trouble.”
“Surprised no one’s competed for the job.”
“Plenty try, but he stones ’em all off. I don’t know what you’d even call this scheme of mine,” Durdles ruminated, his movements slow and heavy. “Some sort of national education program, is it?”
“I’d say not,” Jasper said.
“Me too. Let’s not bother naming it, then.”
Jasper glanced back again. “He’s still trailing. Do we have to let him?”
“Short cut’s round the back past the Travellers’ Twopenny, we’ll drop him there,” Durdles told him.
So the three trundled on, Deputy bringing up the rear at a safe distance, stoning every wall, post, and pillar along the empty lane to shatter the quiet of the night.
“Any new finds down in the crypt, Durdles?” Jasper asked.
“Old finds, you mean,” Durdles rumbled. “That place don’t do novelty. The seventh pillar on the left, down the broken steps of that little old underground chapel? I’ve been digging around, and I’m pretty sure there’s an old cleric with a crook under it. The passages and doors down there are so narrow, them old crook-wielding bishops must have bumped into each other’s mitres every other time they crossed paths.”
Jasper looked over his companion, caked head to toe in old mortar, lime, and stone grit, and felt a strange, creeping fascination with his weird, half-wild life. “Yours is a curious sort of existence.”
“Yours is different,” Durdles grunted, no clue to whether he’d taken that as a compliment or an insult.
“Well, we both live in this old, cold, unchanging place,” Jasper laughed. “But your work around the cathedral is full of far more mystery than mine. Truth be told, I’ve been thinking of asking you to take me on as a sort of free apprentice. Let me tag along sometimes, show me all these odd hidden nooks you spend your days in.”
“Sure, fine,” Durdles nodded vaguely. “Everybody knows where to find Durdles when they want him.” It wasn’t strictly true, but it was close enough—Durdles was always somewhere, loitering among stone and graves.
“What impresses me most,” Jasper pushed on, “is how you can tell exactly where every person is buried, no map needed. Wait, let me hold that bundle, it’s slipping.”
Durdles had stopped short, looking around for a ledge to set his dinner bundle down on. “Fish my hammer out of that, will you? I’ll show you.”
Clink, clink—Jasper handed him the heavy iron tool.
“Look here. You pitch your notes for your choir, right, Mr. Jasper?”
“Right.”
“I sound for mine. I tap.” He rapped the pavement hard, and Deputy darted back a few extra steps, half-convinced the hammer was meant for his skull. “Tap, tap, tap. Solid. Tap some more. Still solid. Then—holloa! Hollow! Tap again, careful. Solid inside the hollow! Tap, tap, tap—there it is. The old coffin crumbled away inside a stone vault, right under the ground you’d never notice.”
“Astonishing.”
“I don’t just do that on cobblestones,” Durdles pulled out his two-foot measuring rule, and Deputy crept closer, suddenly convinced buried treasure was about to turn up that would make him rich, and that he could watch the hanged men swing for stealing it. “If this hammer’s a wall, six feet thick—six feet behind that wall? That’s where Mrs. Sapsea’s buried. I tap that wall, and I can feel her there. Sure enough, my men found leftover rubble six feet in, right where I said it would be.”
“Must be a gift, that kind of accuracy,” Jasper said.
“I wouldn’t take it as a gift if you gave it to me,” Durdles snapped, offended. “I grubbed that knowledge out of the stone myself, dug for it until I pulled it up by the roots, when it didn’t want to come out. Hey you, Deputy!”
“Widdy!” the boy yelped, darting back.
“Catch this halfpenny. And don’t let me see you again tonight once we get to your lodging house.”
“Warning!” Deputy yelled, catching the coin, clearly agreeing to the terms.
They crossed what used to be the monastery’s old vineyard, stepping into the narrow back lane where the Travellers’ Twopenny leaned, a warped two-story wooden hovel that twisted and sagged as crookedly as the morals of its vagrant tenants. The last scraps of its lattice porch and front fence were half gone, stripped by lodgers to burn for roadside campfires over the years. Tattered red rags hung in the windows, lit from behind by guttering rush lights, glowing murky red through the dirty cloth.
As they drew near, a lantern slung over the door blared the lodging house’s name. Half a dozen other feral ragged boys burst out of the shadows, drawn by Deputy’s scent like vultures to carrion, and immediately started stoning both him and each other.
“Stop, you little brutes!” Jasper snapped. “Let us pass!”
The only reply was a chorus of yells and flying rocks, and Durdles observed, dry, that none of these boys had a proper purpose the way Deputy did, before leading the way down the lane.
At the corner, Jasper fumed and glanced back. All was silent for a heartbeat, then a stone whizzed straight past his hat, followed by a distant yell of “Wake-Cock! Warning!” and a crowing cackle that sounded like it came from some devil-hatched rooster, taunting him from the dark. He turned the corner to safety, and walked Durdles the rest of the way home, where the old stonemason stumbled through the litter of his stone yard like he was about to pitch headfirst into one of the half-finished empty tombs lined up along his walls.

Jasper took a different route back to his gatehouse, let himself in soft with his key, and found his fire still burning low. He pulled a strange, peculiar pipe out of a locked press, filled the bowl not with tobacco, and carefully prodded and adjusted the contents with a tiny metal pick, before climbing a short inner staircase that led to two small rooms. One was his own bedroom. The other belonged to his nephew, Edwin Drood. Both had lights lit.
Edwin lay fast asleep, his face calm and untroubled. Jasper stood over him for a long, long minute, holding his unlit pipe in one hand, his gaze fixed, deep and unblinking, on the boy’s sleeping face. Then he drew back, silent as a shadow, and tiptoed to his own room. He lit his pipe, and sank into the thick, strange, dark visions that the smoke conjured up for him, deep into midnight.

The Reverend Septimus Crisparkle—Septimus, because six baby boy Crisparkles before him had faded out one by one at birth, like six weak rushlights snuffed the second they were lit—ducked his head through the thin morning ice crust near Cloisterham Weir that day, and the bracing cold left him glowing, his whole frame tingling with energy. To work the last of the kinks out of his muscles, he was now shadowboxing before his pier glass, sharp as any trained pugilist: feinting, dodging, throwing straight punches straight from the shoulder, his open, radiant face brimming with innocent good humour, soft benevolence practically beaming off the laced leather of his gloves.

Breakfast was barely underway. Mrs. Crisparkle, his widowed mother (not his wife, as strangers sometimes assumed), had only just come downstairs to wait for the tea urn. At that exact moment, Septimus paused mid-combination, cupped her pretty old face between his two gloved palms, and kissed her tenderly before bouncing back to his stance, throwing a punishing left counter and smashing a hard right cross into empty air.
“I’ve said it every morning of your life, Sept,” his mother teased, leaning against the doorframe to watch. “One of these days you’ll smash the glass, or burst a blood vessel.”
“Neither, please God, Ma dear. Look at this!”
He launched into one final, brisk round: dodging imaginary blows, landing half a dozen light, precise taps, and finished by catching the frill of her lavender-ribboned cap in what boxers call a “Chancery hold”—so light his gloves didn’t stir a single ribbon thread. He released her magnanimously, shoving his gloves into a drawer just as the parlourmaid stepped in, and feigning deep, contemplative focus on the view out the front window.
Once the urn was on the table and the two of them were alone again, it was a gentle, charming sight to see the old lady stand to recite the Lord’s Prayer aloud, while Septimus, a respected Minor Canon pushing forty, bowed his head to listen just as reverently as he had when he was four years old, tiny and clutching her apron string. There was no prettier sight in Cloisterham than his mother at that age: bright-eyed, trim, cheerful and calm, her dress as neat and daintily coloured as a china shepherdess figurine made to sit on a mantelpiece. The good Minor Canon thought that every breakfast, and her only consistent thought about him could be summed up in two words she muttered to herself so often: “My Sept!”

Their breakfast nook in Minor Canon Corner could not have been quieter, tucked in the permanent soft shadow of the great cathedral. The caw of rooks, the faint echo of rare passersby, the cathedral bell’s chime, the distant rumble of the organ, all made the little pocket of old red brick walls, ivy-choked stone, latticed windows, and ancient monk-planted fruit trees in the walled garden feel more peaceful than total silence. Centuries of warlords rampaging, serfs drudging, powerful lash-wielding monks rising and falling had all washed over the spot, leaving nothing behind but this soft, settled air of tranquility, the kind of gentle, forgiving mood that settles over you when you hear an old sad story you know has already ended.

Septimus, digging heartily into his ham and eggs, gestured to the letter his mother had just set down on the tablecloth. “What does it say, Ma?”
Mrs. Crisparkle was fiercely proud of her sharp, unassisted vision that let her read fine print without spectacles. Septimus was equally determined to let her enjoy that pride unchallenged, so he’d long pretended his own eyes were too weak to read handwriting without a pair of absurdly huge, heavy-rimmed glasses that squashed his nose and made his eyes water. He fumbled them on now, squinting dramatically at the letter.
“It’s from Mr. Honeythunder, of course,” his mother said, folding her arms. Septimus squinted harder at the opening line.
“‘Haven of Philanthropy, Chief Offices, London, Wednesday. Dear Madam, I write in the—’ What’s the missing word here?”
“In the chair,” his mother said, amused.
Septimus whipped the glasses off to stare at her, bewildered. “Why on earth would he write *in* a chair?”
“Bless me, Sept, you missed the whole context! Give it back here.” His mother took it, reading clearly and precisely: “‘I write from the chair, to which I shall probably be confined for some hours. We have a meeting of our Convened Chief Composite Committee of Central and District Philanthropists here at our Head Haven, and it is their unanimous pleasure that I take the chair. Not to lose a day’s post, I take the opportunity of a long report being read, denouncing a public miscreant, to get our little affair of business off my mind. I have spoken with my two wards, Neville and Helena Landless, on the subject of their defective education, and they give in to the plan proposed; as I should have taken good care they did, whether they liked it or not.’”
Septimus set down his knife and fork, rubbing his ear in quiet exasperation. “It is the most extraordinary thing that these philanthropists are always denouncing someone. And it’s another extraordinary thing they’re never short of miscreants to yell about. They’re always dragging their fellow creatures by the scruff of the neck and bumping them into the paths of peace, like it or not. Pardon my interrupting you, Ma.”
Mrs. Crisparkle kept reading: “‘Therefore, dear Madam, you will please prepare your son, the Rev. Mr. Septimus, to expect Neville as an inmate to be read with, on Monday next. On the same day Helena will accompany him to Cloisterham, to take up her quarters at the Nuns’ House, the establishment recommended by yourself and son jointly. The terms in both cases are understood to be exactly as stated to me in writing by yourself. With compliments to the Rev. Mr. Septimus, I am, Dear Madam, Your affectionate brother (In Philanthropy), LUKE HONEYTHUNDER.’”
Septimus rubbed his ear a little more. “Well, Ma, we’ll make it work. We have the spare room, I have time to teach him, and I’m happy to do it. Though I confess I’m relieved he’s not coming to live here himself. Is he a very large man, Ma?”
“I’d call him a large man, my dear,” she said after a little hesitation, “but his voice is so much larger than he is. Larger than anybody.”
Septimus huffed a quiet “Hah!” and went back to his breakfast, but his fancy Souchong tea, his ham, his toast, all tasted suddenly bland.

The pair agreed the first priority was to make the two young Landless wards feel at ease, and what better way than a small friendly dinner? They’d invite Edwin Drood, Jasper’s young cheerful nephew who was visiting, and of course Jasper himself, then Miss Twinkleton from the Nuns’ House and the young ward Rosa who lived there, for eight total—exactly the capacity of their dining table, no extra fuss for Mrs. Crisparkle. They called on Miss Twinkleton that afternoon to arrange Helena’s lodging, the invitations were accepted, and word was sent to London that Neville and Helena should arrive in time for the dinner.

There was no railway that stopped in Cloisterham in those days; old Mr. Sapsea swore there never should be. The nearest rail line dumped its passengers miles away, and the only way into the town was a rickety short omnibus that trundled down a back stable lane that had borne a faded “Beware of the Dog” sign for decades. Septimus was waiting there when the overloaded little vehicle lumbered up, its roof stacked high with luggage like an overburdened baby elephant. Seated on the box was a massive broad-shouldered man, elbows squared, who had squashed the poor driver into a tiny sliver of seat space, glowering out at the road.
“IS THIS CLOISTERHAM?” he roared, so loud the rooks in the nearby trees took off squawking.
“Aye,” the driver said, rubbing his aching ribs as he tossed the reins to the ostler. “And I’ve never been so glad to see it in my life.”
“Tell your master to widen that box seat,” the man bellowed back. “He’s morally, and ought to be legally, obliged to provide for the comfort of his fellow man.”
The driver prodded his sore ribs, looking furious. “You were sitting on me, that’s what you were.”
“Take this card, my friend,” the man said, shoving a card at him. “Be a member of our Society.”
“What do I get out of that?” the driver asked, refusing the card.
“Brotherhood!” he thundered.
“Thank you very much,” the driver said, climbing down slowly. “My mother was content with me as I am, and so am I. I don’t want no random brothers.”
“But you *must* have them,” he boomed, climbing down after him. “Whether you like it or not. I am your brother.”
“Now see here—” the driver started, ready to snap, when Septimus stepped in to calm him down.
“Joe, Joe, don’t get riled, lad,” he said, and when the driver touched his hat peaceably, he turned to the big man. “Mr. Honeythunder? I’m Septimus Crisparkle.”
“Reverend Mr. Septimus? Glad to see you. Neville and Helena are inside the omnibus.” He looked Septimus up and down, obvious disappointment on his face, twisting a double eyeglass round and round by its ribbon like he was roasting it over a fire. “Hah! I expected you’d be far older, sir.”
“Hope you won’t be disappointed I’m not,” Septimus said, good humoured.
“Eh? Joke? I never see a joke. Jokes are wasted on me.” He waved the two young people out of the omnibus. “Helena! Neville! Come meet Mr. Crisparkle.”
They were a strikingly handsome pair, dark-skinned, lithe, near identical in face, Helena almost gipsy in her vivid features. There was something untamed about them both, an air of hunter and huntress, but also of prey—quick-eyed, half shy, half defiant, a faint, constant restlessness in their posture like they were coiled to either crouch or leap. They stared wide-eyed at the cathedral ruins as they walked through Cloisterham’s old streets, like two wild exotic captives brought in from some far tropical colony. Honeythunder stomped right down the middle of the road, shoving passersby out of his way as he ranted about his grand new philanthropist scheme to lock every unemployed person in the UK in jail and force them to become do-gooders on pain of extermination.

Dinner was a total, miserable disaster. Honeythunder blocked all the serving paths, made the footman pass plates over his head, and turned the whole meal into a public committee meeting, yelling at everyone at once as if the assembled guests were a faceless crowd, and kept pretending Septimus was some corrupt villain he was cross-examining, even when Septimus hadn’t said a single word. The whole party sat frozen, no one able to hold a normal conversation, until the first hints of moonlight came through the window and it was nearly time for Honeythunder to catch his return omnibus.

The whole house moved at top speed to hustle him out: his coffee was served a full hour before he asked for it, Septimus checked his watch every two minutes, the four young people swore the cathedral clock had struck three-quarters when it had only just struck one, Miss Twinkleton lied that the omnibus stop was twenty five minutes’ walk away when it was five. They all bundled him into his overcoat, pushed him out the door into the dark like they were smuggling a fugitive to safety, and Septimus and Neville walked him to the omnibus, fretting loudly that he’d catch his death of cold, shoving him inside the vehicle and shutting the door firmly a full half hour before it was even due to depart. Then they turned and hurried back down the lane, to the soft, quiet peace of Minor Canon Corner.

Moonlight bleached the cobblestones of Cloisterham as Neville Landless fell into step beside the Minor Canon Crisparkle, turning back toward the latter’s cottage after Mr. Honeythunder’s blustery departure. “I know hardly anything of that gentleman, sir,” he said plainly.

“Hardly anything of your guardian?” Crisparkle echoed, surprised.

“Almost nothing at all,” Neville confirmed. “You’ll recall my sister Helena and I came here from Ceylon? We lived with our stepfather there, after our mother died when we were small. Our life was wretched. She named him our guardian on her deathbed, and the man was a miser who grudged us every scrap of food, every stitch of clothing he gave us. When he dropped dead a little while back, he handed us straight over to Honeythunder—no reason I can guess, save that the man was some old acquaintance whose name he kept seeing in the papers.”

“Lately then?” Crisparkle asked.

“Only a matter of weeks. He was not just greedy, he was cruel. It’s a mercy he died when he did, or I’d have killed him.”

Crisparkle stopped dead on the path, staring at his new pupil in open consternation.

“I shock you, sir?” Neville’s tone softened fast, turning submissive.

“You shock me unspeakably.”

Neville hung his head for a few paces before lifting it again. “You never saw him beat your sister. I saw him beat mine, again and again. I never forgot it.”

“Nothing,” Crisparkle said, his own indignation softening his severity despite himself, “not even the sight of a beloved sister suffering dastardly cruelty, could justify the sort of violent language you just used.”

“I apologize, sir. I take the words back. But let me set you straight on one thing: Helena would have let him tear her limb from limb before she ever let him see a single tear fall.”

Crisparkle could not doubt it, looking at the fire in the young man’s face. “I hope you will not think me strange,” Neville added, hesitating, “to ask you to hear me out, to defend my character before you’ve even properly had the chance to form an opinion of it?”

“Defend? You are not on trial, Mr. Landless.”

“I feel as though I should be, if you knew the half of me,” Neville said, sullen and disappointed when Crisparkle suggested he’d rather judge him in his own time. The tone pricked the minor canon’s conscience—he could see he was about to shut down the vulnerable trust of a boy warped by a lifetime of abuse, before he could guide him. He spotted the glimmer of his own cottage lights ahead, and stopped.

“Let us take another turn around the path,” he said, smiling. “I did not mean to check you. I invite your full confidence.”

“You’ve invited it since the moment I stepped through your door,” Neville said, relieved. “The truth is, Helena and I showed up here fully intending to snub you, to pick a fight, and run off again. We’ve never liked a single person we’ve ever been placed with, so we made up our minds not to like you either. But this house, your kindness, the quiet of Cloisterham under the moon after Honeythunder left… all of it made me want to open up. And Helena is far better than me, you know—better than I am by as much as the Cathedral tower is taller than the slum chimneys below it.”

Crisparkle privately reserved judgment on that point, and let him go on.

“I grew up from my first memories suppressing a boiling, bitter hatred. It’s made me secretive, quick to vengeance. Beaten down by brute force my whole life, I learned to lie and scrape just to get by. I was starved of education, of freedom, of every small, ordinary joy most children take for granted. I don’t even have a name for all the soft, good instincts you’ve no doubt nurtured in other young men. Worse, I grew up surrounded by abject, servile local dependents back in Ceylon—sometimes I fear there’s a drop of that tigerish wildness in their blood running in my veins too.”

He went on to recount their four childhood escapes from the stepfather’s house, when seven-year-old Helena had planned every flight, disguised herself as a boy, and tried to bite off her own hair when Neville lost the pocketknife she’d meant to use to cut it short. “She never broke, not once,” Neville finished. “Even when they caught us and beat us half to death. I only ask that you bear with me, and make allowances.”

“You have my word,” Crisparkle said, offering his hand. “I won’t load you down with sermons. But I will ask you to remember: the only way I can help you is with your full cooperation, and that means seeking help from above, too.”

Neville shook his hand. “I’ll try my part.”

Before they went in, Crisparkle stopped him again. “You spoke just now as if you knew for certain Helena feels exactly as you do about me. You’ve not spoken a word to her since we met, you know. Honeythunder monopolized every moment of the introductions. Are you sure you don’t speak for her without warrant?”

Neville smiled, proud. “You don’t know what understanding twins share, sir. You don’t need to speak a word, barely even exchange a look, to know exactly what the other is thinking. She not only feels exactly what I described, she knows full well I’m here taking this chance to talk to you for both of us.”

Crisparkle stared, half-incredulous, at his steady, unshaken face, and said no more. Neville hesitated for a moment, color rising in his cheeks. “One last question, sir. There’s a Mr. Edwin Drood here, visiting his uncle Jasper? Does he read with you? Is Miss Rosa Bud his relation too?”

Crisparkle explained the pair’s long-standing childhood betrothal, and Neville’s scowl deepened. “Oh, that’s it, then. Now I understand that air of his, like he owns her.” The comment was half-muttered to himself, and Crisparkle did not press on it, as if he’d accidentally overheard a private line of thought in a letter.

They stepped inside to the drawing room, where John Jasper sat at the piano accompanying Rosa Bud as she sang. Jasper played without sheet music, his eyes fixed unblinkingly on the girl’s lips, his hands soft on the keys to cue her back to the right note every time she strayed. Helena Landless stood close beside Rosa, one arm looped loosely around her, her own gaze fixed hard on Jasper. Neville and Helena exchanged a single, almost invisible nod across the room, the silent twin understanding Neville had spoken of flashing between them so fast Crisparkle almost thought he’d imagined it. Neville leaned against the piano opposite the singer, Edwin Drood fussed over unfurling and refurling Miss Twinkleton’s fan, and Crisparkle settled into a chair beside the china shepherdess figurine by the mantel.

The song was a slow, mournful ballad of parting, Rosa’s fresh young voice soft and plaintive. But as Jasper’s eyes never left her mouth, whispering each note to her like a secret only he could hear, the girl’s voice wavered. All at once, she clapped her hands over her eyes, burst into terrified sobs, and shrieked: “I can’t bear this! I’m frightened! Take me away!”

Helena moved faster than anyone else, laying the limp, shaking little girl down on the sofa as gently as if she weighed nothing at all. She knelt one knee beside her, one hand light over Rosa’s trembling mouth, holding up her other hand to quiet the rest of the room. “It’s nothing, it’s over,” she said. “Don’t speak to her for one minute, and she’ll be right.”

Jasper’s hands had lifted clear off the piano keys the second Rosa screamed, and they hovered there poised, frozen, as if he was waiting for the room to go quiet so he could resume. He did not even turn his head, even as everyone else moved to crowd around Rosa to soothe her.

“Poor little Pussy’s just got stage fright,” Edwin laughed, forcing a light tone. “You work her too hard, Jack, that’s all. You’re such a strict teacher, no wonder she’s scared of you.”

“No wonder,” Helena repeated sharply.

“Ah, Miss Landless,” Jasper said, finally turning his head over his shoulder, a thin, cold smile on his face. “Thank you so much for defending my character.” He went back to making silent, air-played gestures over the keys while they led Rosa to the open window for fresh air, and by the time she was calm enough to come back to the fire, he was gone.

“Jack’s run off, Pussy,” Edwin told her. “I’m half afraid he was hurt to be called the monster who made you scream.” Rosa said nothing, and shivered, as if the open window had left her chilled to the bone.

Miss Twinkleton, the headmistress of the Nuns’ House boarding school, soon declared it far too late for her and her charges to be out wandering the streets. Neville and Edwin gallantly volunteered to walk the whole group back, and the iron gate of the Nuns’ House clicked shut behind the ladies minutes later.

All the other boarders had already retired for the night. Only Mrs. Tisher waited up to show Helena her new bedroom, which was connected straight to Rosa’s quarters, and left the two of them alone almost immediately.

“Thank goodness I don’t have to face meeting everyone in the dark by myself,” Helena sighed, sinking down on the edge of Rosa’s bed.

“The other girls are all very kind,” Rosa assured her, fidgeting with the edge of her nightgown. “At least I can promise you that.”

“I can promise you you’ll be my friend, can’t I?” Helena laughed, her dark, fiery eyes roaming over Rosa’s small, soft face as she tucked a strand of hair behind her ear.

“Of course! But it feels so silly,” Rosa pouted. “I’m such a tiny little thing, and you’re so strong and tall and brave. I feel like I could vanish right next to you.”

“I’m the one who’s rough and uneducated,” Helena said. “I’ve no accomplishments, no manners, I feel out of place here. But there’s a fascination about you, I couldn’t help but talk to you the second I saw you.”

“Pity Eddy doesn’t feel that,” Rosa said, half-teasing, half-sulky. “We’re such a ridiculous pair, he and I. We’re always bickering. We both know how silly our betrothal is, and we can’t stop fighting about it.”

Helena’s gaze was steady, serious, on her face. “You said your music master is Mr. Jasper. Edwin’s uncle.”

Rosa flinched so hard she nearly fell off the bed. “Ugh.” She buried her face in her hands, shaking.

“You are afraid of him.”

Rosa dropped to her knees on the floor at Helena’s feet, clinging to her skirt. “Don’t say his name! Please don’t. He haunts me like a ghost. I feel like he can walk through walls, no matter how hard I try to hide. He never says a word about loving me, never threatens me at all. But his eyes follow my hands when I play, follow my lips when I sing. Every note he plays for me, it feels like he’s whispering that he’s chasing me, that I can’t tell anyone. Even when he zones out in that horrible dreamy trance he gets sometimes, he’s still right there next to me, worse than ever. Tonight, when he stared at my mouth the whole time I was singing, it felt like he kissed me without even touching me. I couldn’t bear it. That’s why I screamed. Eddy adores him, he’d never believe a bad word about him. But you said you weren’t afraid of him, no matter what, and that’s why I could tell you.”

Helena pulled the shaking little girl tight against her, her dark hair falling over Rosa like a protective curtain. A low, smoldering fire glinted in her eyes, directed toward the dark door of the bedroom. Whoever John Jasper was, whatever secret he thought he’d hidden in the shadows, someone was now watching him. He would do well to look to himself.

The two young men linger long enough to watch their charges vanish through the Nuns’ House courtyard, then turn to find the brazen doorplate glinting like a sharp, disapproving eye, as if the frock-coated old beau with his monocle trapped on the street side of the metal is glowering at them for loitering. They glance at each other, then down the moon-silvered length of Cloisterham’s quiet thoroughfare, and fall into step slowly, unspoken friction thrumming between them.

“Do you stay here long, Mr. Drood?” Neville asks, his tone sharper than he intends.
“Not this trip,” Edwin answers, dismissive. “I’m back to London tomorrow. But I’ll pop in off and on till next Midsummer, then I’m saying goodbye to Cloisterham, and England too, for a good long stretch.”
“Off abroad?”
“Going to rouse Egypt a little,” Edwin says, condescending. “No reading, mind you—working. Engineering. My father was a partner at the firm I’m joining, left my small patrimony tied up as part of their capital. I’m on their payroll till I come of age, then I step into my modest share of the concern. Jack—you met him at dinner—is my guardian and trustee till then.”
“Mr. Crisparkle told me of your other fine fortune too.”
Edwin’s brow lifts, sharp and unpolite. “What do you mean, my other good fortune?”
Neville had phrased the line with a furtive, watchful edge, that strange air of being both hunter and hunted that clings to him even now. The two stop dead, and trade a heated, wary look.
“I hope I gave no offence,” Neville says, flushing. “I only innocently referenced your betrothal.”
“Good God!” Edwin snaps, picking up his pace. “Every gossiping soul in this mouldy old town won’t leave it be. I half expect some tavern to hang a sign with my face under *The Betrothed’s Head*—or Pussy Rosa’s. One or the other.”
“I’m not to blame for Mr. Crisparkle mentioning it openly to me,” Neville begins.
“No, that’s true enough,” Edwin allows.
“But I chose to bring it up to you,” Neville goes on, “because I assumed you’d be impossibly proud of it.”

Two quiet, unspoken currents of anger are feeding this fight now, invisible to both men. Neville is already half lost to quiet, smitten admiration for Little Rosebud, and seethes that Edwin—far less worthy of her than anyone he’s ever met—treats his prize like a trivial afterthought. Edwin, for his part, has already been struck by Helena Landless’s fierce, unyielding grace, and burns that her ragged, unpolished brother dares talk down to him like he’s some foolish green boy who doesn’t know his own luck.
Edwin scoffs. “I don’t reckon people gush most loudly about the things they’re proudest of, or want every stranger chattering about them. I live a busy life, after all—you, as the well-read newcomer, can correct me if I’m wrong, I’m sure you know better.”

They’re both savage now. Neville’s rage is out in the open, raw and unhidden; Edwin hides his own behind half-hummed snatches of popular tunes, and fake stops to gawk at “picturesque moonlit effects” as if he hasn’t even noticed they’re fighting.
“It’s hardly civil of you,” Neville bursts out at last, “to sneer at a stranger who came here with none of your advantages, trying to make up for lost time. I wasn’t raised in that ‘busy life’ of yours, you see. My ideas of politeness were formed among heathens.”
“Then the best civility anyone can learn, no matter where they grew up,” Edwin retorts, “is to mind their own business. Set me that example, and I’ll follow it faithfully.”
“You take far too much upon yourself!” Neville snaps, fists clenched. “Back where I come from, you’d be called to account for that kind of arrogance.”
“By who, exactly?” Edwin stops dead, staring at him with open disdain.

A heavy, unexpected hand lands on Edwin’s shoulder. Jasper steps out from the shadowed side of the lane, between the two of them, as if he’d been strolling past the Nuns’ House too and caught their raised voices mid-rant.
“Ned, Ned, Ned,” he chides, soft but firm. “None of this. I don’t like it. You two have been shouting loud enough to wake the cathedral tombs. Remember, my boy, you’re almost the host here tonight. You belong to this town, represent it to our new guest. Mr. Neville is a stranger, and hospitality demands you show him respect. And you, Mr. Neville,” he adds, resting his other hand on Neville’s inner shoulder, so he walks sandwiched neatly between the two young men, a hand on each, “you must pardon me, but I beg you to rein in your temper too. What is there even to fight over? Let’s just say there’s nothing amiss, and we can forget the whole thing. We’re all three on good terms, aren’t we?”

After a long, silent standoff where both young men fight to be the last to speak, Edwin speaks first. “No hard feelings from me, Jack.”
“Nor from me,” Neville concedes, though his tone is far less easy, far more tight. “Though if Mr. Drood knew everything I’ve left behind me back home, he might understand how sharp-edged words cut deeper for me than they do for others.”
“Ah, now, let’s not put conditions on our good mood,” Jasper soothes, steering them toward his gatehouse cottage only a few yards away. “That sounds far too much like a remonstrance, and that’s hardly generous. Frankly, no anger on Ned’s side—frankly, no anger on yours, Mr. Neville?”
“None at all, Mr. Jasper.” It still rings a little stiff, a little forced.
“Perfect! My bachelor rooms are right there, the heater’s on the fire, the wine and glasses are all laid out, and it’s barely a stone’s throw from Minor Canon Corner. Ned’s off to London tomorrow, after all. We’ll drag Mr. Neville in with us for a proper stirrup-cup.”
“With all my heart,” Edwin says, grinning.
“And with all mine,” Neville says, though he’d far rather be anywhere else. He can feel his temper slipping out of his grip, and Edwin’s lazy, unshakable cool only stokes the fire hotter.

Jasper hums the refrain of a raucous drinking song as he leads them inside. When he lights the lamp next to the hearth fire, the first thing they all see is Edwin’s portrait of Rosa, hanging over the mantel. It’s a disastrous thing to put between two men already at each other’s throats—it drags their fight back to the exact subject that sparked it. Both glance at it consciously, neither speaking. Jasper, who seems to have missed the root of their argument entirely, points it out cheerfully.
“You recognise that painting, Mr. Neville?” He shades the lamp to throw full light on the canvas.
“I recognise it. It doesn’t do the original any favours.”
“Oh, you’re too harsh! Ned painted it, gave it to me as a gift.”
“I’m sorry to hear that, Mr. Drood,” Neville says, sincerely apologetic. “If I’d known I was speaking to the artist—”
“Joke, man, just a joke,” Edwin cuts in, yawning so wide his jaw cracks. “Just messing around with Pussy’s little quirks. I’ll paint her properly, serious and proper, one of these days, if she’s a good girl.”
He leans back in his chair, lacing his fingers behind his head to prop it up, that lazy, patronizing air thick enough to cut. Neville burns with indignation so obvious it’s as bright as the firelight. Jasper glances between the two, a tiny, unreadable smile on his face, then turns his back to stir a jug of mulled wine at the hearth, lingering over the mixing far longer than he needs to.
“I suppose, Mr. Neville,” Edwin says, already picking a new fight, “if you were going to paint the girl you loved—”
“I can’t paint,” Neville snaps, cutting him off.
“That’s your misfortune, not your fault. You would if you could. But if you could, I expect you’d turn her into Juno, Minerva, Diana, Venus—all four goddesses rolled into one, wouldn’t you?”
“I’ve no lady love, so I can’t say.”
“If I ever tried my hand,” Edwin crows, boyish and boastful now, “at a serious portrait of your sister Helena—mind you, dead serious—you’d be blown away by what I could do.”
“My sister would never consent to sit for you,” Neville shoots back. “Since that’s never going to happen, I’ll never get to see what you’re capable of. I’ll survive the loss.”

Rosa Bud had no living relative she knew of anywhere in the world, and from the age of seven, the Nuns’ House had been her only home, the soft, strict Miss Twinkleton her only mother figure. Her clearest memory of her real mother was of a pretty, slight girl not much older than Rosa herself, carried home unconscious and drowned in her father’s arms after a pleasure-day accident. Every fold of her crumpled summer dress, every splash of its pastel print, even the long soaking strands of hair tangled with half-crushed wildflower petals that had clung to her when they laid the small still body on the bed, were etched indelibly into Rosa’s mind. Just as sharp was the image of her young father’s wild, screaming grief, and the quiet, broken-hearted death that claimed him exactly one year after that terrible day.

Their long-standing betrothal grew out of that period of his mourning. His dearest college friend, Mr. Drood, had also been left a widower in his youth, and the two men had made a gentle, earnest promise that their two motherless children, Rosa and Edwin, would grow up to love and marry each other. Then Drood too walked the quiet road that all mortal pilgrims take, sooner or later, leaving the two young orphans bound by a wish neither had asked for, but which everyone around them treated as fixed fate.

The soft hush of pity that had wrapped little Rosa when she first arrived in Cloisterham never faded entirely. It shifted color as she grew older: golden in the summer afternoons, roseate at tea time, pale blue at dusk, always gilding her with a soft, gentle light. Everyone at the school doted on her, fussed over her, competed quietly to be the one to slip her an extra jam tart, to invite her home for the holidays, to write her the longest letter when term was out. Those soft, gentle rivalries even carried a faint, sharp edge of unspoken bitterness now and then. Small wonder, then, if the medieval nuns who once lived under that same old roof hid far fiercer, harder fights under their veils and rosaries.

So Rosa grew into an amiable, giddy, a little willful, endlessly winning little thing—spoiled in the sense that she expected kindness from every soul she met, but never spoiled enough to be indifferent to the kindness she got. She carried an entire well of unspent affection deep inside her, that had brightened every corner of the Nuns’ House for years, but its depths had never been stirred. No one knew what changes would sweep over her light, careless little head the day that well finally overflowed.

No one could say how the story of the young men’s brawl the night before—of Neville Landless supposedly attacking Edwin Drood—got into the school before breakfast. It spread faster than the birds of the air could carry news, faster than the morning breeze drifting through the open casement windows, as if the baker had kneaded it straight into the loaves, the milkman had watered it down and delivered it as part of his usual adulteration, the maids beating dust out of the doormats had shaken it loose into the street air. By the time Miss Twinkleton had finished dressing (or, as she liked to put it for the benefit of fanciful parents, “sacrificing to the Graces”), every gable in the old building hummed with the rumor, passed straight to her by her second-in-command Mrs. Tisher.

At first, it was only that Neville Landless had thrown a bottle at Edwin Drood. Then it was a knife. Then a fork. By the time the cook had heard the tale, he’d hurled all three, plus a full crystal decanter, no warning at all. Everyone demanded to know why. The story went that Neville had dared to say he admired Miss Rosa Bud, and Edwin Drood had snapped back that he had no right to even look at her, which set him off. Poor Rosa stuffed her fingers in her ears, scurried to a dark corner, and begged no one tell her any more. But Helena Landless marched straight up to Miss Twinkleton, demanded leave to go fetch her brother and get the real truth from Mr. Crisparkle the minor canon, and made it plain she’d go permission or no.

When she returned, she had a long private word with Twinkleton first to filter out anything too unseemly for the rest of the girls to hear, then sought Rosa alone. She told her only the broad strokes of the fight, emphasizing how badly Edwin had provoked her brother, brushing past the fact that Edwin had been treating their long-planned engagement so carelessly it was making everyone around him uneasy. She passed on Neville’s earnest apology, begging Rosa to forgive him, for her sake, and then dropped the subject entirely.

It fell to Miss Twinkleton to calm the rest of the school. She swept into what she grandly called “the apartment allotted to study” (the rest of the world just called it the schoolroom), intoned “Ladies!” and every girl stood at attention, Mrs. Tisher positioning herself behind her like Queen Elizabeth’s loyal companion at Tilbury Fort. Twinkleton launched into a meandering, pompous speech: she quoted Shakespeare, the Swan of Avon, who painted Rumour as a monster with a hundred chattering tongues, and explained that the tiny, trivial scuffle that had happened a good hundred miles from their peaceful walls had been grotesquely exaggerated. All the girls were to put the uncongenial subject completely out of their heads and return to their lessons. She handed out detentions left and right for snickering, but the gossip refused to die: at dinner, one girl snuck a paper mustache out of her desk, pretended to hurl a water jug at her friend, who pulled a serving spoon to “defend herself,” and both of them landed in extra trouble.

Rosa could not stop turning the fight over in her head. A heavy, uneasy feeling clung to her that she was somehow the root of it, the cause or the consequence, bound up in the false, awkward position her betrothal had put her in. She could not even talk it through with Helena, who made a point of avoiding the subject, it was far too raw for her. Right then, of all times, they announced her guardian, Mr. Grewgious, had arrived to see her.

Grewgious was a man selected entirely for his perfect, unbreakable integrity—no one could have picked him for his social graces. He was a dry, sandy-looking man, who looked as if you could toss him straight into a grinding mill and he would emerge as fine high-dried snuff. His thin, matted yellow hair looked less like hair than a mangy old discarded fur tippet, so unflattering it could not possibly be a wig, for no man would ever voluntarily choose to wear such a thing. The few lines on his face were cut deep, hard curves that looked more carved than grown, and the faint notches in his forehead looked as if Nature had gotten halfway to softening them into some hint of sensibility, then thrown down her chisel in exasperation and said “I cannot be bothered finishing this one. He was clearly deeply flustered to be waiting in Miss Twinkleton’s private parlor, half-convinced she was about to quiz him on some unknown lesson he would fail. He stammered out greetings to Rosa, and when Twinkleton offered to leave the room, he insisted she not go out of her way, so she simply wheeled her desk over to the far corner window and pretended to work. He ticked through his checklist one by one: first, was Rosa well and happy? She said she was, perfectly. Next, pounds, shillings and pence: was her allowance enough? She laughed at the very idea of being in debt, she never wanted for a single thing. Then he got to his final, most important note, labeled *Marriage*. He pulled his chair a little closer, admitted he felt like a stiff old bear with a cramp, clumsily stumbled into a fancy youthful dance, which made Rosa laugh out loud. He confirmed that Edwin was due back for Christmas, and that the wedding scheduled for then would mean they would need to give Miss Twinkleton formal notice of Rosa’s departure by mid-year. He joked that he was far too angular, clumsy and awkward to give Rosa away at the altar, and suggested the school’s dancing master could do it with far more grace than he ever could.

He pulled out a certified copy of her father’s last will next. Rosa looked up fast, and begged him not to give the second copy, meant for Edwin, to Jasper, his uncle and trustee. She wanted it delivered straight to Edwin himself, no middle man. Grewgious blinked, agreed immediately, and then admitted he had no experience of young love at all: he was the only child of far too old parents, and he was fairly certain he had been born old himself. While every other person seemed to enter the world a soft, living bud, he was born a dry chip of wood, and had never known anything else.

He laid out the full terms of the inheritance plainly: Rosa had a 250 pound a year annuity, plus the saved interest and extra holdings that added up to a lump sum of more than 1700 pounds, enough to pay for all her wedding preparations. Tucked behind the oldest, gabled stretch of Holborn—where 300-year-old houses still press their weathered faces to the street, as if lingering to grieve the long-dried Old Bourne stream they once overlooked—lies Staple Inn: two lopsided, quiet quadrangles sealed off from the city’s roar. When new mayor Thomas Sapsea finds the self-congratulatory contemplation of his own profundity growing a little stale on a damp Cloisterham evening, he is wont to take a slow airing through the Cathedral Close. He ambles past the churchyard with the swagger of a landed proprietor, warm with the soft, self-satisfied glow of a benevolent landlord who has been unusually generous to a most deserving tenant: his late wife, whose monumental inscription he wrote and unveiled to great local fanfare. He loves to spot a stray stranger peering through the iron railings, likely pausing to read his handiwork on the stone markers. If he ever sees a visitor hurrying away from the churchyard, he is fully convinced they are exactly as his monument directs: “retiring with a blush,” humbled by the grandeur of his tribute.

His status as mayor has inflated his ego tenfold. He is fully certain he invented the brilliant observation that the entire framework of society would collapse entirely without mayors—plenty of them, all duly solemn, all duly self-important. Mayors have been knighted, after all, for trotting up to London with flowery addresses, firing off grandiloquent nonsense straight into the laps of the crown. Why shouldn’t Sir Thomas Sapsea be next? He has long since decided he is the very salt of the local earth.

His friendship with choirmaster John Jasper has deepened considerably since that first evening of port wine, epitaph debates, backgammon and roast beef. Jasper has hosted him at the gatehouse too, sat at his piano to play for him long enough to tickle his ears—figuratively, of course—for hours. What Sapsea likes most about the young man is that he is always eager to learn from the wisdom of his elders, and is sound, sir, sound to the very core. He proved it that night, Sapsea will tell anyone who listens, by playing none of the frivolous foreign ditties favoured by national enemies, but only rousing old loyalist George III era ballads, exhorting all brave British boys to smash every other island, continent, peninsula, isthmus and promontory on the map, and sweep every sea clean of verminous foreign folk. It was quite clear Providence had made a terrible mistake making the nation of oak hearts so small, and all those other useless countries so large.

This particular damp evening, Sapsea is strolling past the churchyard, hands behind his back, still on the lookout for that blushing retiring stranger, when he turns a corner and stumbles straight into the Dean, deep in conversation with verger Tope and Jasper. Verger Tope snorts with perfectly dutiful, respectful amusement.

“I have no intention at all of becoming an author or archaeologist, sir,” Jasper replies smoothly. “It is nothing but a silly whim of mine. And even that whim is more Mr. Sapsea’s fault than mine.”

“How so, Mr. Mayor?” the Dean says, nodding good-naturedly at him. “What are you responsible for here?”

There is hardly a soul stirring in these old nooks after dark. Ask a hundred Cloisterham residents at midday if they believe in ghosts, and every one of them will say no. Offer them a choice after dark between cutting through these eerie, silent precincts, or taking the long, detoured route along the busy high street, and ninety-nine of them will pick the long way. It is not just local superstition about the headless lady, or the ghost of a mother with a rope around her neck that haunts the cloisters. It is the basic, universal human instinct: the living do not like lingering too long in places thick with the dust of the dead. These were not the only signs of dispersal. Boxes that stayed locked in storage most of the year appeared in every bedroom, and a wild, dramatic frenzy of packing ensued, entirely disproportionate to the small amount of clothing actually being folded. The old lawyer’s unshakable, clear view of right and wrong in the matter of his engagement to Rosa could not be laughed off or brushed aside. If not for that dinner, and the small engagement ring tucked inside the breast pocket of his coat, he would have drifted all the way to his wedding day without a single real pause for thought, assuming everything would sort itself out if he just left it alone. It’s right that we’re sad, but how much better to be sad now than to be stuck miserable together, and angry at each other for the rest of our lives.”

They sat down on the half-ruined stone steps overlooking the river, and Rosa laid out exactly how she saw their relationship: Edwin had liked her, thought she was a nice little thing, gotten used to the idea of marriage to her, accepted it as an inevitable fact of life without ever stopping to question if he loved her properly. She had slowly, gradually started to think the same thing, stuck as she was at the school with no other distractions, until Grewgious had come to visit her and gently but firmly impressed on her how careful they ought to be with their inherited engagement, and she resolved to speak to Edwin the very first chance they got to be alone. Edwin admitted Grewgious had spoken to him too, back in London. His hand drifted to the ring in his breast pocket, then paused: if he was going to give it back to Grewgious, what was the point in upsetting Rosa by showing it to her?

“The girls will be so dreadfully disappointed,” Rosa said, laughing through the last of her tears, dewdrops glistening on her eyelashes. “They’ve all been looking forward to our wedding for ages, poor pets.”

“Ah, but I fear the news will hit Jack worse than anyone,” Edwin said, suddenly jolting at the thought. “I never considered Jack!”

Rosa’s head snapped towards him, her eyes wide and intent, a look that vanished as quickly as a flash of lightning, leaving her confused, her breath coming fast. She looked down at the ground instantly, as if she would do anything to take that reaction back.

“You don’t doubt this will come as a terrible shock to Jack, do you, Rosa?”

“Why should I?” she said evasively, hurrying her words. “I never thought about it. He hardly seems to have anything to do with our engagement.”

“My dear child! How could someone as completely wrapped up in someone else as Jack is in me, Mrs. Tope’s phrasing, not be completely stunned by a full, sudden shift like this in my life?” Edwin said, thinking aloud. “How on earth am I going to break it to him? I’m dining with him tomorrow and the next day, Christmas Eve and Christmas Day, and I can’t ruin his holidays. He fusses over every little trivial thing about me, he’ll be completely upset. Wait—you said your guardian promised to come down if you wrote to ask him? Would you mind if he’s the one to tell Jack? The other trustee, that makes perfect sense. He can lay out all the details gently, far better than we could. I’m not a coward, Rosa, but to tell you the truth, I’m a little afraid of Jack’s reaction. I saw him have one of his fits once, this wild paroxysm, and I’m worried a shock like this, coming straight from me, might trigger it.”

“Edwin, you are not afraid of him!” Rosa cried, her face going white, her hands clasped tight.

“Easy there, sister Rosa, what do you see from that turret of yours?” Edwin laughed, steadying her. “I’m not literally afraid of the dear old fellow, I just don’t want to catch him off guard. Grewgious is so steady, he’ll talk Jack’s thoughts back into order in no time.”

Rosa calmed, clearly relieved that Grewgious would be the one to break the news to Jasper, acting as a buffer between the man who was her music master and this awkward, sharp secret. Edwin’s hand closed around the little ring case again, and once more he decided to say nothing of it. The beautiful stones were nothing more than a memento of broken, childish hopes, a cruel little reminder that all human plans were brittle dust. He would give it back to Grewgious when he arrived, and it would go back into its old dark cabinet, alongside old letters and abandoned vows, until the day it might one day resurface and repeat its sad old round.

The frosty day faded as they walked along the river, planning their new separate paths: Edwin would head off to his engineering work in Egypt earlier than planned, Rosa would stay on at the Nuns’ House at least as long as Helena remained. They agreed everyone would be told that they remained the very best of friends, no hard feelings at all. All the while, both held back small secrets of their own: Rosa planned to ask Grewgious to arrange for her to stop taking music lessons from Jasper immediately, and Edwin found himself idly, curiously wondering if he would ever get to know Neville Landless’s sister Helena better.

The sun dipped low and painted the old stone cathedral walls crimson as they made their way back towards the Nuns’ House. Dark seaweed washed up at their feet at the river’s edge, and rooks circled overhead, their hoarse cries like dark splashes in the fading dusk.

“I’ll tell Jack I’m leaving the country soon, once Grewgious has had the talk with him,” Edwin said quietly. “I’ll be gone before they meet, it will be better that way, don’t you think?”

“Yes.”

“We know we did the right thing, Rosa?”

“Yes.”

“We know we’re better off this way, even now?”

“And we will be far, far better off, much happier, later on.”

The old tender affection for the bond they were letting go lingered between them, so they slowed their steps, prolonging their final parting. When they reached the stand of elms near the cathedral where they had sat together on Edwin’s last visit, they stopped by unspoken consent. Rosa lifted her face to his, something she had never done when they were formally engaged.

“God bless you, dear! Good-bye!”

“God bless you, dear! Good-bye!”

They kissed each other, warm and fervent.

“Now take me home, Eddy,” Rosa said. “I want to go and be by myself for a little while.”

“Don’t look back, Rosa,” Edwin cautioned her, as he tucked her arm through his. “Didn’t you see Jack? He was standing under the trees. He watched us say goodbye. Poor fellow, he has no idea we’ve called the whole thing off. This is going to hurt him so badly.”

Rosa hurried her pace, not stopping once until they passed under the gatehouse arch and back onto the main street. “Is he following us? Can you look without making it obvious? Is he behind?”

“No. Wait—yes, he is,” Edwin said. “He just stepped out under the gateway. The dear, overprotective old fellow just wants to make sure you get in safe. I’m afraid he’s going to be bitterly disappointed when he finds out.”

Rosa grabbed the rusty old bell handle and pulled hard, the hoarse clanging echoing inside the Nuns’ House. Before the door swung open to let her in, she turned and gave Edwin one last, wide, wondering look, as if she was screaming at him to understand, to see the danger he was blind to, before she vanished inside and the heavy oak door clicked shut between them.

Christmas Eve settles over Cloisterham like frost on old stone. A handful of unfamiliar faces drift through the streets, alongside half-strangers who grew up here and left, returning after years away to find the city unnaturally shrunken, as if it has never properly washed in all their time gone. For these wanderers, the cathedral clock’s chime and the rooks cawing from the tower are the exact voices of their nursery years. Some, dying far across the world, will lie on their sickbeds and imagine their floors are strewn with the Close’s fallen autumn elm leaves, the faint rustle and sharp green scent of their earliest years rising up as their lives near their end, the start and the final breath almost touching.

Christmas markers are everywhere. Red berries glint in the windows of Minor Canon Corner; Mr. and Mrs. Tope tuck sprigs of holly delicately into the cathedral stalls’ carvings and sconces, as if tucking them into the Dean and Chapter’s buttonholes. The shops are overflowing with currants, raisins, spices, candied peel, and thick moist sugar. An uncharacteristic air of cheerful chaos hangs over the town: a giant bunch of mistletoe hangs above the greengrocer’s doorway, and a sad, lopsided little Twelfth Cake topped with a crudely made Harlequin sits in the pastry shop window, raffled off for a shilling a ticket—so shabby it hardly deserves the name Twelfth Cake, more a Forty-Eighth Cake. Public amusements are laid on too: the famous waxworks that once “profoundly impressed the Emperor of China” are showing for one week only in the empty premises of the bankrupt former livery stable keeper, and a new grand comic Christmas pantomime is running at the theatre, advertised by a life-sized poster of the clown Signor Jacksonini that reads “How do you do to-morrow?” and looks almost as miserable as it is big.

Cloisterham is buzzing—save for the High School and Miss Twinkleton’s girls’ academy. The High School students are all home for the holidays, every single one of them secretly in love with one of Miss Twinkleton’s young ladies, who have no idea they are adored. Only the academy’s maids flit around the windows now, noticeably more giddy and playful without the students there to balance the dynamic.

Three men are due to meet at the gatehouse for dinner tonight. The whole of the quiet Christmas Eve stretches out before them, each going through the hours alone, none guessing how the night will change all their lives forever.

---
Neville Landless has been given full holiday leave from his studies by Mr. Crisparkle, but he sits in his quiet room, reading and writing with sharp, fixed focus, until two hours past noon. Then he sets to clearing every scrap of paper off his desk, arranging his books, tearing up and burning every stray note, every unfinished draft, every messy scribble. He tidies every drawer until no loose scrap is left that is not directly related to his school work. When he is done, he pulls out a new knapsack he bought on the High Street the day before, packs a change of sturdy clothes, thick socks, and heavy walking boots for a long hiking trip. Next he picks up a heavy iron-shod walking stick he bought the same day, swings it, tests its weight, sets it down on the window seat next to the knapsack. All his preparations are complete.

He dresses to go out, is already halfway down the stairs, and runs straight into Mr. Crisparkle coming out of his bedroom on the same landing. Neville remembers he forgot his stick, turns back to grab it, and when he reappears, Crisparkle laughs, takes the heavy stick right out of his hand, and shakes his head. “You picked this for its weight, didn’t you? Much too heavy, Neville, far too heavy for a walking stick.”

“For resting on during long walks, sir?” Neville asks.

“Resting on it? You don’t rest on a walking stick, you balance with it. It’s far too heavy for that.”

“I’ll get used to it with practice. I’ve never lived somewhere people go on long walking trips, you know,” Neville says, and Crisparkle nods, offers to take him out on a multi-mile hike together once the holidays are over. He asks if Neville will be back before dinner, and Neville says he doesn’t think so, as they are eating early that night. Crisparkle waves him off with a bright, unforced show of total trust.

Neville walks straight to the Nuns’ House, waits at the outer gate without stepping a foot over the threshold—he has given his word not to risk running into Rosa, and he honors that promise. Helena is waiting for him, and the two walk fast out of the town, up into the quiet inland countryside. Neville admits he is sick of being a burden to everyone around, that the terrible, ugly fight he is still waging against his own impulsive temper will never calm down if he stays in Cloisterham right now. He has talked it all through with Crisparkle, he says, and gotten full permission to go on a solitary walking tour for a fortnight, leaving tomorrow early enough that he will be out of the town, out of earshot of the cathedral bells, before the Christmas morning service starts. Helena agrees the plan is thoughtful and healthy, an honest effort to master himself, and encourages him. When he hands her the heavy ironwood walking stick, she remarks it is far too heavy for a casual walk, just as Crisparkle did.

As dusk falls and the first town lights flicker on in the distance, Neville’s mood plummets. “I wish I didn’t have to go to this dinner tonight,” he says, glum. Helena tries to reassure him it will be over in an hour, there will only be one tiny awkward moment at most. But Neville shakes his head. “I don’t like the feel of it. There’s a terrible dead, heavy weight hanging in the air.” He says almost nothing else the rest of the walk back, kisses Helena goodbye at the Nuns’ House gate, and she stands there watching him pace back and forth past the gatehouse not once but twice, too reluctant to step inside, until the cathedral bell chimes the quarter hour, and he turns and hurries in, up the postern stair.

---
Edwin Drood has had a lonely, quiet day too. He cried alone in his room the night before, because the engagement he took for granted his whole life has dissolved into nothing, leaving a hollow spot he had no idea he would feel. He thinks constantly of Rosa, braver and sharper than he ever guessed she was, and feels stupid for wasting years treating their betrothal like a given inheritance instead of something he should have cherished and earned. He cannot stop replaying the strange, sharp, questioning look Rosa gave him when they parted at the gate, the one that felt like she was seeing right through the cheerful mask he wore, and he cannot figure out what it meant.

He is waiting now for Mr. Grewgious to arrive, before he leaves Cloisterham for good, heading out to his work in Egypt. He drifts through every old familiar spot, saying silent goodbyes, knowing he may well never see them again. When his watch stops ticking, he pops into the jeweller’s shop to get it rewound and reset. The jeweller tries to sell him a dainty wedding bracelet, perfect for a small, pretty young bride, then a heavy gentleman’s signet ring that most men buy as a wedding keepsake, with the date of their marriage engraved on the inside. When Edwin says he only ever wears his father’s old watch, chain, and a simple shirt pin, the jeweller laughs and says John Jasper stopped by a few days ago for a new watch glass, and mentioned he already has a full mental inventory of every piece of jewellery Edwin owns, that he would never need to buy him a gift of that sort. Edwin leaves the shop smiling, thinking dear old Jack notices every tiny trivial detail about him, even a lopsided crease in his cravat.

He wanders through the Monks’ Vineyard as full dark falls, and after half an hour of pacing back and forth he spots a haggard, gaunt old woman huddled on the ground by a little-used wicket gate, staring blankly ahead of her, unblinking. He stops to check if she is sick, or lost, or cold, and she slowly drags her gaze up to him, and starts shaking. He recognizes the look on her face instantly: it is the same dazed, drugged expression he saw on Jack’s face the night he came home from that London opium den.

The woman wheezes that her lungs are bad, that she came all the way from London looking for something she never found, and all she needs is three and sixpence to get the fare back home. “If you give me three and sixpence,” she rasps, “I’ll tell you something. Be glad your name isn’t Ned. Ned’s a bad name to have right now. It’s a threatened name. A dangerous name.” She leans in, her finger shaking in front of his face, and whispers that the man named Ned is so cursed, he should live for all eternity just to outrun the harm coming to find him. Then she tugs her rags around her and slips off toward the travellers’ lodging house, before he can ask her any more questions.

Edwin’s skin crawls. Only Jack ever calls him Ned. He tells himself it is nothing but a silly coincidence, that he will not mention it tonight, that he will laugh it off with Jack tomorrow. But the woman’s words cling to him: they hum in the rising wind, ripple in the dark river, echo in the cathedral chime that jolts him right out of his skin when he turns under the gatehouse arch. He too walks up the postern stair.

---
John Jasper has had by far the calmest, brightest day of the three. No music lessons over the holidays, his only obligation is the cathedral services. He spends the morning making his dinner arrangements, ordering every one of Edwin’s favourite little luxuries from the bakers, the butchers, the wine merchant, telling them his nephew will be leaving soon, and deserves to be spoiled while he can. He stops by to see Mr. Sapsea on his rounds, and tells the mayor that Neville and Edwin are coming over to dinner tonight to patch up their quarrel. Sapsea snorts, declares Neville’s foreign features are “Un-English,” and once he has labeled something Un-English, he believes it is damned forever.

That afternoon, Jasper sings at the cathedral service better than anyone has ever heard him. The anthem’s difficult, fast passages are perfect, his timing flawless, his tone richer and more steady than it has ever been. Even though a thick, heavy black silk scarf is slung loose around his neck, over both his choir robes and his everyday clothes, his composure is so noticeable Mr. Crisparkle stops him after Vespers to congratulate him. “You outdid yourself today, Jasper. Perfect self-command, no forced notes, no rough edges—you look wonderfully well.”

Jasper smiles and says he has been trying a new medicine for his old bouts of low, dark mood, that it works wonderfully. He tells Crisparkle he has decided he will burn his current diary at the end of the year, rid himself of all the gloomy, overwrought, morbid thoughts he has been fixated on, start his new diary with a clear, calm head. Crisparkle is overjoyed, claps him on the shoulder, and says this is far better news than he ever dared hope for, and the two men part at the steps of Crisparkle’s front door.

Jasper walks the rest of the way back to the gatehouse, where his two guests have just arrived. The three of them are about to sit down to the Christmas Eve dinner that none of them will forget—if two of them ever live to remember it.

Neville Landless had set out long before the first grey tint touched the Cloisterham spires, walking at such a sharp, unrelenting pace that when the city church bells began pealing for morning service, he was eight miles clear of the cloisters. He’d left home with nothing but a dry crust of bread in his pocket, and his stomach gnawed at him so sharply he ducked into the next roadside tavern he spotted to get a hot meal.

Visitors asking for breakfast were such a rarity at the sign of The Tilted Wagon—save for horses and cattle, who got all the hay and trough-water they could want—that it took the flustered landlady a full half hour to drag tea, toast, and bacon out of her smoky, sodden kitchen. Neville waited perched on a splintered chair in the sanded front parlour, watching damp fagot smoke curl down the chimney, and wondering how many hours it would take that sneezy, sputtering fire to ever warm any other guest who stumbled in after him. The whole establishment was as damp and run-down as its hillside position promised: puddles of hoof-mud caked the doorstep, the landlady slapped a squalling, half-socked baby against her hip in the bar, a hard green cheese sat mouldering on a shelf next to a rusted knife and a rotting tablecloth in a dented iron tray, the pale, crumbly bread looked like it was shedding tears over its own stale fate, half-washed family linen was draped over every chair back, and every mug on the table was chipped, smudged, and stained with years of ale. Hardly the advertised good entertainment for man and beast. Neville didn’t complain. He ate as fast as he could, rested longer than he needed to, and set off again down the road.

A quarter mile further on, he hesitated at a fork: stick to the main high road, or take a rutted cart track winding between two overgrown hedgerows, cutting up the slope of a windswept heath to rejoin the road further on. He picked the track. The rise was steep, the mud clung thick to his boots, and he was hefting his heavy walking stick to pull himself up the deepest ruts when he heard other footsteps behind him.

They were gaining fast. He stepped back against the high bank to let them pass, but only four of the strangers strode on ahead. Four more slowed immediately, loitering ten feet behind him, and the half-dozen remaining in the party turned and bolted back down the track at a run. He glanced between the men in front and the men behind, and every one of them stared directly back at him. He kept walking. The four ahead kept looking over their shoulders. The four behind kept closing the gap. When they all stepped out onto the open heath slope, no matter which way Neville veered, the ring of eight held fast. There was no doubt left: he was trapped.

He stopped dead. Every last man stopped too.
“Why are you herding me like this?” he called out. “Are you a pack of thieves?”
“Don’t answer him,” someone hissed from the crowd, too quiet for him to tell who spoke. “Keep quiet.”
“Quiet?” Neville’s temper snapped. “I won’t be penned in between four men in front and four behind. I’m getting past the lot of you.” He hefted his stick, broke into a run straight for the group ahead. The biggest, strongest man in the group darted sideways to meet him, locked his arms around Neville, and they both went rolling into the heather. Neville’s heavy stick cracked down hard against the man’s shoulder before they hit the ground.
“Let him be,” the man grunted, spitting blood as they tussled, faces smearing red. “Fair play. He’s half my size, and he’s got that stick fighting weight. I can handle him.” He pushed his knee off Neville’s chest, climbed to his feet, and wiped blood from his jaw. “C’mon, two of you, take his arms.”
They grabbed Neville at once, pinning his arms to his sides.
“For the record, Mr. Landless,” the big man said, spitting another mouthful of blood onto the grass, “we’re not thieves. You’d know that if you stopped to think about where you’re from. We wouldn’t have touched you if you hadn’t started swinging. We’re taking you back up to the high road where there’s plenty of help if you actually do run into robbers. Someone wipe his face, that blood’s trickling all down his shirt.”

When the mud and blood was scrubbed off his cheeks, Neville recognised the speaker: Joe, the driver of the Cloisterham omnibus, who he’d only met once, the day he first arrived in town.
“Best not talk for now, Mr. Landless,” Joe warned him. “You got a friend waiting up on the high road—we sent the rest of the party ahead that way. You’ll meet him soon enough, best say nothing till then.” Someone slung Neville’s walking stick over their shoulder, and the group set off.

Utterly bewildered, Neville stumbled between his two guards like he was in a dream, until they broke out onto the main road and found the rest of the group clustered around Mr. Crisparkle the Minor Canon, and John Jasper. His guards released him immediately out of respect for the clergyman.
“What on earth is this? I feel like I’ve lost my mind,” Neville gasped, as the crowd pressed in around him.
“Where is my nephew?” Jasper screamed, wild-eyed.
“Your nephew?” Neville stared, confused. “Why are you asking me?”
“Because you were the last person with him,” Jasper snapped. “And Edwin Drood is gone. He can’t be found.”
“Gone?” Neville blanched.
“Steady, Neville.” Crisparkle put a hand on his shoulder. “Calm your thoughts. This is very serious. You left Jasper’s house with Edwin last night, correct?”
“Yes.”
“At what hour?”
“Midnight?” Neville glanced dazedly at Jasper.
“Correct,” Crisparkle nodded. “You walked down to the river together?”
“To check how hard the wind was blowing, yes. We stayed there ten minutes, no more, then walked all the way back to your house, and he said goodnight to me at your door.”
“Did he say he was going back down to the river after that?”
“No. He said he was heading straight home.”

The bystanders exchanged dark glances. Jasper, who had been staring at Neville unblinking this whole time, spoke in a low, sharp, suspicious voice.
“What are those dark stains on his coat?”
Every eye locked on the smears of blood on Neville’s sleeves.
“And the same stains on this stick!” Jasper snatched the walking stick out of the nearest man’s hand. “I know that stick. He carried it with him last night. What does this mean?”
“For God’s sake, Neville, explain it,” Crisparkle urged him.
“That man there,” Neville pointed at Joe the omnibus driver, “and I fought for that stick ten minutes ago up on the heath. You can see the same blood all over his face and shirt. What was I supposed to think when eight strangers jumped me out of nowhere and refused to say a word about why? Of course I fought back.”
The men who had apprehended him nodded, confirming the scuffle, but even their faces stayed dark, suspicious, staring at the dried blood smears the cold wind had already set into Neville’s clothes.
“We have to go back to Cloisterham, Neville,” Crisparkle said. “You’ll come back willingly to clear your name, won’t you?”
“Of course, sir.”
“Mr. Landless will walk right at my side,” Crisparkle told the crowd. “Come on.”

They set off back toward the city. Jasper walked on Neville’s other side, never leaving that spot, not speaking a word even when Crisparkle repeated his questions and Neville repeated his answers, even when they tossed out gentle theories about what might have happened. He kept his face fixed, stony, unblinking, until they walked into Mayor Sapsea’s parlour.
Crisparkle explained the situation, their voluntary statement to the mayor, and Jasper finally broke his silence. He declared he trusted Sapsea’s sharp, authoritative judgement completely. There was no reason Edwin would run off out of the blue—unless Sapsea could think of one, in which case he would defer. There was no reason he would have accidentally fallen in the river and drowned in the dark, unless Sapsea thought that likely, and he would defer again. He was doing all he could not to let horrible, ugly suspicions take root, unless Sapsea agreed those suspicions were unavoidable, given that Edwin’s last companion before vanishing had a long-running, public fight with him not two days prior. His own mind was too clouded with grief to be trusted, but Sapsea’s was perfectly clear.

Sapsea puffed out his chest, declared the case “darker than any properly English case ought to be”, rambled on for ten minutes about how taking another man’s life was the worst theft a person could possibly commit, and almost signed an arrest warrant to throw Neville Landless in jail on grave suspicion—until Crisparkle exploded in indignant protest, swearing he would keep Neville in his own house, and produce him the second he was called for, no exceptions.
Sapsea agreed. He ordered the entire river dragged, every foot of the banks searched, warnings and pleas sent to every town within a hundred miles and all over London, and placards posted all over the city begging Edwin Drood, if he had run off for some unknown private reason, to have pity on his grieving uncle and send word that he was still alive. All the orders were put into action that same hour.

It was impossible to say who looked more broken by shock and horror: Neville, or Jasper. The only difference was that Jasper was forced to stay active, working the search parties, while Neville was confined to Crisparkle’s house, passive, stunned.
By dawn the next day, men were already out on the river, dragging the water with nets and poles, wading the banks in jackboots, accompanied by dogs and spades and ropes. They searched all that day, and all the next, lanterns bobbing on the river long after dark, watchers posted at every remote creek and tidal causeway out near the sea, cresset torches flaring over dark water. No trace of Edwin Drood turned up anywhere.
Jasper worked every hour of it, unshaven, caked in dried mud, clothes torn half to rags, until he stumbled home at the end of the second night and collapsed into his armchair, exhausted. He had barely let his head fall back when Mr. Grewgious stepped through the door.
“Terrible news,” Grewgious said, smoothing his balding head with one hand, staring into the fire.
Jasper lifted his heavy, red-rimmed eyes, then dropped them again.
“How is your ward Rosa?” he asked, his voice thin and tired.
“Poor child. You can imagine how distraught she is.”
“Have you seen Neville’s sister Helena?” Jasper mumbled.
“Whose?” Grewgious’s voice was sharp, cold, his eyes sliding slowly from the fire to Jasper’s face. Jasper, too worn out to bristle, blinked.
“The young man everyone suspects.”
“Do you suspect him?” Grewgious asked.
“I don’t know what to think. I can’t make up my mind.”
“Neither do I,” Grewgious said. “But you called him the suspected man, so I thought you’d already decided. I just left Miss Landless. She’s ferociously defiant, completely, unshakably convinced her brother is innocent. But I didn’t come here to talk about her. I came to talk about my ward. I have news that will shock you. It shocked me.”
Jasper’s eyes snapped a little wider, sitting straighter in his chair.
“What is it?”
“Of course, I ought to have seen it sooner,” Grewgious said slowly, still staring at the fire, opening and closing his palms over the flames. “I’m such a stiff, practical old man I took everything for granted, never thought to ask.”
“What is it?” Jasper demanded again.
“Edwin, my ward Rosa, the two of them—they’ve been betrothed for years, due to be married soon—but they quietly realised, on their own, that they’d be far happier as brother and sister than as husband and wife. No fights, no bad blood, just a quiet, kind, sensible agreement to dissolve the engagement for good. They met the night before Edwin vanished to talk it through, and they parted as friends. Edwin decided he didn’t want to break the news to you himself, because he knew how deeply attached you were to the idea of the marriage, how disappointed you’d be. He left it for me to tell you, once he was gone. He’s gone, now.”
Jasper’s face went white as chalk, then grey, cold sweat beading up on his forehead like drops of frozen steel. His hands clamped so hard around the arms of the chair his knuckles cracked.
“They said goodbye properly that night, no tears that weren’t kind, no hard feelings,” Grewgious finished. “That was the secret he never got to tell you.”
A high, terrible shriek tore out of Jasper’s throat. He clutched at his hair, reeled backward, and crumpled in a heap on the floor, nothing but a tangled mass of torn, mud-caked clothes. Grewgious didn’t even flinch. He kept opening and closing his palms over the fire, looking down at the heap at his feet, his own quiet, sharp suspicions coiling tight in his chest.

The first thing John Jasper registered when he came to was the sharp, acrid tang of burnt sago jelly and Mrs Tope’s sniffling breath fanning over his cheek. Mr and Mrs Tope, summoned by his unlooked-for visitor, had dragged him from the floor to his favourite armchair, and that visitor—angular, wooden-faced Grewgious—sat bolt upright on a hard-backed oak chair, hands planted square on his knees, watching every flicker of Jasper’s eyelids like a man auditing a unforgiving ledger.

“There now, sir, you’ve come round fine,” Mrs Tope blubbered, dabbing at her eyes with her apron hem. “You were fit to drop, no wonder at all, running yourself ragged like that.”

“A man cannot have his rest broken, his mind cruelly tormented, and his body overtaxed by fatigue without being thoroughly worn out,” Grewgious intoned, as if reciting a line he’d memorised word for word.

“I fear I gave you all a fright,” Jasper mumbled faintly, as they helped him settle deeper into the chair.

“Not at all, I thank you,” Grewgious replied, stony as ever. “You are too considerate.”
“Not at all, I thank you,” he repeated before Jasper could speak.

“You will take some wine now, sir,” Mrs Tope cut in, bustling off towards the scullery. “And that jelly I had set aside for you at noon, which you turned your nose up at, though I warned you what would come of it, not even a crumb of breakfast in you! And a wing of roast fowl that’s been kept warm at the fire so many times I’ve lost count. It’ll all be on the table in five minutes, and this good gentleman will surely stop to see you eat it.”

Grewgious huffed a snort that could have meant anything: yes, no, utter indifference, and Mrs Tope, already half-way out the door, didn’t linger to puzzle it over.

“Won’t you take a bite with me?” Jasper asked, as the cloth was spread across his table.
“Couldn’t get a morsel down my throat, I thank you,” Grewgious said, fixed in his upright pose, no flicker of expression on his face, every line of him a polite, unyielding protest against casual conversation, as if pre-emptively turning down any invitation to chat about the weather or the cathedral bells.

Jasper ate and drank like a man fighting to fend off a total collapse, his movements fast, unthinking, as if he didn’t taste a single bite. He was not eating for pleasure, it was clear: he was fortifying himself, shoring up his strength against any further fainting spells, any crack in his carefully composed mask.

When he pushed his plate away minutes later, he sat staring into the empty fire grate for several long minutes before he spoke. “Do you know, I find crumbs of comfort in the news you brought me? The news that Edwin has broken off his engagement to your ward, never told a soul, not even me?”

“_Do_ you?” Grewgious said, the unspoken follow-up hanging thick in the air: I don’t, I thank you.

“I was gutted by it at first, of course. A piece of news that blows apart every last plan I’d built for my dear boy. But after I’d time to turn it over in my head… there’s hope there, isn’t there? If Edwin knew he’d have to face endless awkward questions, to explain to every soul in Cloisterham why the wedding was off, wouldn’t it make sense he’d run off to avoid it? Vanish rather than be the seven days’ wonder for every gossiping fool in the close to poke and pry at?”

“Such things do happen,” Grewgious allowed, slow, after a moment of pondering. “I’ve read of folk vanishing for years, just to escape the nuisance of explaining themselves.”

“Exactly!” Jasper leaned forward, voice brightening, eager now, as if he was clinging to this new line of reasoning like a lifeline. “I never had the faintest idea Edwin was hiding anything from me, least of all something so critical as calling off his marriage. How could I ever have imagined he’d leave of his own accord, unannounced, cruel to his friends, to me? But now that I know the engagement is off? It makes his disappearance make sense. He wouldn’t hurt his Rosa by vanishing out of the blue, not when he’s just parted from her. Even to me—he knew you were coming to tell me the news! He knew exactly what conclusions I’d draw, that I’d assume he ran off rather than face the mess. It takes the edge off the cruelty to both of us, don’t you see?”

Grewgious could not find fault with the logic.

“I’ve had terrible dark fears, these past days,” Jasper went on, hands clasped tight, eyes glistening. “But this news you brought me? It gives me hope. I can believe it now—Edwin’s gone off of his own free will, he’s alive, he’s well, he’ll turn up soon.”

It was at that exact moment that Mr Crisparkle stepped through the door. Jasper repeated his hopeful theory word for word, and the good Minor Canon, already wracked with guilt over his pupil Neville Landless, who stood accused of hurting Edwin, drank the reasoning in. It fit too well: Edwin had been weighed down by the secret of the broken engagement, it would absolutely explain a sudden, impulsive flight.

“I told Sapsea as much when we called on him,” Jasper said, earnest as could be. “There was no quarrel between the boys the last time they met at my house, you know. Their first meeting was ugly, I was so afraid Neville’s wild temper would hurt my Edwin, I wrote all my dark forebodings down in my diary and showed you the entry. I want you all to know—this new hope has softened even my old distrust of Neville. I won’t hold my old grudges, not if Edwin’s alive out there somewhere.”

Crisparkle was shamed to his core. Jasper was laying all his cards on the table, and he’d been hiding two critical facts: Neville had flown into a second violent rage at Edwin, and he’d been flamingly jealous of him over Rosa Bud. He was certain Neville was innocent, but the circumstantial evidence was piling higher and higher against him, and he’d been holding back those details for fear of making things worse. But seeing Jasper’s frankness, he could not hold back any longer. He confessed everything to Grewgious, the full truth of Neville’s hot temper, his jealousy over Rosa.

Jasper paled a little at the news, but shook his head resolutely. “I will cling to my hope. Until we find proof my boy is dead, I will not believe harm came to him.”

Crisparkle left the house that night more troubled than ever, his mind so full of worry for Neville that he did not notice where his feet were carrying him until he heard the low rush of falling water, close enough to make his ears ring. He had walked all the way to Cloisterham Weir, two miles upstream from where the two boys had gone to watch the Christmas Eve storm.

“How did I get here?” he thought, stopping short. “Why did I come here?”

The starlight was thin and cold, the weir hissing and roaring as it always did on a frosty night. A half-remembered line from his old poetry books drifted unbidden into his head, about airy tongues that syllable men’s names, and he swatted at the air as if he could brush the words away. No one had searched this far upstream. The tide had been running strong down towards the sea on Christmas Eve, so any body that fell in would have been carried far away from the weir. But the place felt haunted, heavy, like something was hidden there. He strained his eyes, peered at the wooden timbers and posts, saw nothing out of the ordinary, and resolved he would come back at first light.

The weir ran through his dreams all night, and he was back at sunrise, the bright frosty light laying every last detail of the structure bare. He scanned the timbers for minutes, ready to leave, when a tiny glint caught his eye, something stationary, not rippling and falling with the rest of the water. He tore off his coat, his boots, plunged into the icy river, swam for the spot.

Tucked in the cracks of the weir’s heavy timbers, caught fast by its chain, was a gold pocket watch. Engraved on the back, in tiny neat script, the initials: E.D.

He swam back to the bank, then dove again, and again, and again, searching every dark hole in the river bed, until his fingers were numb and he could not bear the cold a moment longer. He found no body, only a single shirt pin stuck in the mud and ooze at the bottom.

He brought the two finds back to Cloisterham, fetched Neville Landless, and went straight to the mayor. Jasper was sent for, the watch and pin were identified as Edwin’s, and the whole town of Cloisterham exploded in a frenzy of gossip against Neville. The rumors spread like wildfire: he was a violent savage, he’d whipped natives to death back in his old homeland, he’d threatened to murder Mr Crisparkle half a dozen times, he was a walking danger to every soul in the close. The hard, sharp facts stacked up against him too: he’d openly threatened Edwin, he’d carried a weapon that night, he’d been seen leaving town at dawn, he had traces of blood on his clothes, he’d burned all his papers the afternoon Edwin vanished. The jeweller confirmed he’d wound Edwin’s watch at 2:20 that same afternoon, and it had run down long before anyone fished it from the weir, meaning it had been tossed in the hours right after Edwin vanished.

No trace of Edwin’s body ever turned up, though, and eventually the authorities had to release Neville for lack of proof. But the town had already turned its back on him. The Dean told Crisparkle flat out that Neville could not stay in his house, not in the cloister, sanctuary or no sanctuary. The Minor Canon protested that he knew Neville was innocent, but the Dean waved him off, warning him not to say so out loud, not to be a partisan, to keep his heart warm and his head cool and hold that “judicious middle course” that suited the clergy.

So Neville Landless was gone, run out of Cloisterham, a permanent blight on his name.

It was only then that John Jasper quietly returned to his place in the cathedral choir. Haggard, red-eyed, all that false sanguine hope drained out of him, he looked like a man who’d been gutted from inside. A day or two later, after the service, he was unrobing in the vestry, when he pulled his diary out of his coat pocket, flipped to a fresh page, and handed it wordlessly to Mr Crisparkle to read. The ink was fresh, dark, the writing sharp and jagged, like he’d carved the words into the page with his pen:
“My dear boy is murdered. The discovery of the watch and shirt-pin convinces me he was killed that Christmas Eve, and his jewellery was stripped from him to stop anyone identifying his body. All the foolish, delusive hopes I built around his broken engagement? I throw them to the winds. They are dead, the second I laid eyes on that watch half-buried in the weir timbers. I swear this oath, and I write it here in black and white, for all time: I will never discuss this mystery with any soul, not until I hold the full clue to it in my hand. I will never slacken my search. I will track the murderer of my dear dead boy to the ends of the earth. And I will devote every last breath of my life to his destruction.”

Six full months had slipped by when the Minor Canon, Mr. Crisparkle, found himself cooling his heels in a waiting room at the London headquarters of the Haven of Philanthropy, waiting for an audience with the formidable Mr. Honeythunder.

Back in his university days, when he’d been active in amateur athletics, Crisparkle had known dozens of bare-knuckle pugilists, even attended a few of their gloved exhibition matches. Now he found himself struck by a peculiar parallel: the back of every professed professional philanthropist milling through those halls was shaped almost exactly like the back of a professional boxer’s skull. Every organ tied to a compulsion to “pitch into” other people was overdeveloped, swollen to a degree he’d only ever seen among the fighting set. The men sweeping past him all carried the same swagger, the obvious readiness to pick a fight with any random novice who dared cross their path, that he remembered well from the old fight circles. They were already making arrangements for a big moral rally out in the countryside, the backers loudly touting this or that heavyweight public speaker as a sure bet to land this line or that zinger on stage, exactly the same way sporting pub owners would bet on a prizefighter’s knock-out hits, the proposed rally resolutions treated like scheduled rounds in the ring. The event’s celebrated platform tactician, in his plain black suit, was the spitting image of old Frosty-faced Fogo, a legendary fight referee he’d known years prior who used to rope off the fighting ring and supervise the match start. Only three key differences separated the “philanthropy professors” from the old bare-knuckle pugilists: first, the philanthropists were dreadfully out of shape, far too fleshy, their faces and figures stuffed full of what fight experts called “suet pudding” fat. Second, the philanthropists had none of the boxers’ natural good temper, and used far fouler language. Third, their “fighting code” was entirely unregulated: they didn’t just press their opponent to the ropes, they bored him until he teetered on the edge of total madness. They hit men when they were down, hit them anywhere, kicked them, stamped on them, gouged them, and mauled them behind their backs without a shred of mercy. The old Noble Art of boxing was far nobler than this brand of professional philanthropy.

Crisparkle was so lost turning these similarities and differences over in his head, watching the endless stream of people come and go, all seemingly on errands designed to snatch something hostilely from someone else and never give anything back, that his name was called twice before he heard it. When he finally responded, a shabby, drastically underpaid clerk — a stipendiary philanthropist so poorly treated he could not have fared worse if he’d worked for an avowed enemy of humanity — led him straight to Mr. Honeythunder’s private office.

“Sir,” Honeythunder boomed, his voice as thunderous as a schoolmaster barking orders at a pupil he already suspected of every crime imaginable, “sit down.”

Crisparkle took the offered seat. Honeythunder finished signing off on a few hundred out of thousands of circulars, which ordered thousands of cash-poor families to “step forward, stump up immediately, become a philanthropist, or go straight to the Devil”. Another equally shabby, dismally paid clerk gathered the stack of circulars into a wicker basket and trundled away.

“Now, Mr. Crisparkle,” Honeythunder said, swiveling his chair half around to face him, squaring his arms with his hands planted firm on his knees, brows knitted like he was about to get this over with fast, “we hold very different views, you and I, sir, on the sanctity of human life.”

“Do we?” the Minor Canon replied mildly.

“We do, sir.”

“Might I ask,” Crisparkle said, “what your views on the subject are?”

“That human life is a sacred thing, sir.”

“Then might I ask,” the Minor Canon pressed on, “what you suppose my views on the subject are?”

“By George, sir!” the philanthropist snapped, squaring his arms even tighter as he glowered at Crisparkle. “They’re best known to you.”

“Here is a man — a young man, mark you — swept off the face of the earth by an act of brutal violence. What do you call that?” Honeythunder said, as if the victim’s youth made the crime infinitely worse, and he could have borne the loss of an old man with no trouble at all.

“Murder,” Crisparkle said.

“I’m surprised to hear you admit even that much, sir,” Honeythunder sneered in his most offensive tone, “and I’ll be frank, I never expected it.” He leaned forward, heavy and menacing.

“I don’t sit here, sir,” the philanthropist roared, raising his voice to a full yell, “to be browbeaten.”

“Murder!” Honeythunder boomed, in a loud, performative reverie, folding his arms the same way he would on stage, nodding in feigned abhorrence after every short punchy phrase. “Bloodshed! Abel! Cain! I strike no bargains with Cain. I recoil with a shudder from any red hand that is offered to me.”

Instead of leaping out of his chair and cheering himself hoarse, as the rest of the philanthropist’s crowd of followers would have done in a public meeting, Crisparkle only shifted the quiet cross of his legs and said, “Do go on with that explanation when you get to it.”

Crisparkle stood, a little flush rising in his cheeks but perfectly in control of himself.

“I may view my clerical duties through a lens that says my first obligation is to the needy, the suffering, the desolate, and the oppressed,” Crisparkle said. “And since I have no interest in making empty performative professions of piety, I won’t argue that point further. He was a good man, a steady, manly fellow, and so humble there was no trace of self-importance in him, no more vanity than the schoolboy who used to stand guard at the wicket on his old school’s breezy playing fields. “Those are strong words, sir!” the philanthropist gasped.

“I should hope so,” Crisparkle said. “Good morning.”

Not long after the quiet cathedral town was torn by the mystery of Edwin Drood’s disappearance, a stranger drifted into Cloisterham, cutting a distinctly odd figure against its weathered stone streets. His hair was a shock of thick, unruly pure white, made all the more striking by his pair of sharp, jet-black eyebrows. Buttoned tight into a blue surtout, buff waistcoat, and neat grey trousers, he carried a faint whiff of military bearing, but he was quick to dismiss the idea the moment he checked into the Crozier — the town’s staid, perpetually understaffed traditional inn — with nothing but a single portmanteau.

Leaning back against the empty coffee-room fireplace while he waited for his fried sole, veal cutlet, and pint of sherry, he announced to the lone waiter (who doubled as the entirety of the Crozier’s available audience that slow afternoon) that he was a man of independent means, a perfect idle dog with no work to tie him down, and he’d half a mind to rent lodgings in the picturesque old city for a month or two, before settling there for good.

His oversize white head bobbed as he shook out his mane of hair the way a Newfoundland dog might shake water off before curling up by the fire. “I suppose, waiter,” he said, “a decent little place for a single fellow like me isn’t hard to find around here, eh?”

The waiter had no doubt of it.
“Something old,” the gentleman pressed. “Take my hat off that peg a moment, will you? No, no, I don’t need to wear it — look inside. What name do you see written there?”
The waiter squinted and read off “Datchery.”
“Now you know who I am: Dick Datchery. Hang it back up. What I’m after is something odd, out of the way, venerable, architectural, and gloriously inconvenient.”

The waiter chuckled, modestly confident the town had no shortage of inconvenient homes, but scratched his head at the “architectural” part. “Mr. Tope’s the man to ask about that sort of thing, sir — he’s the cathedral verger. His missus used to have spare lodgings to let, for years, but no one ever took them. Her little window sign vanished so long ago it’s basically local legend now, probably blew down one stormy day and never got put back up.”

“I’ll call on Mrs. Tope right after dinner,” Datchery decided.
The Crozier was tucked down a quiet lane so out of the way, and the waiter’s directions so absurdly specific, that Datchery was hopelessly lost within ten minutes of stepping out. He wandered in circles around the cathedral tower, convinced Mrs. Tope’s place had to be right next to it, half-playing a game of “hot and cold” with himself — warm and close to the mark whenever he caught a glimpse of the spire, cold and wandering off course the moment it slipped out of view.

He was thoroughly “cold” when he stumbled on a patch of disused burial ground, where a ragged, feral little local boy was stoning a half-lame sheep through the railings, cackling that he was going to break its other three legs and bring it down. Datchery barked at him to stop, and after a quick, snappish back and forth, offered the urchin a shilling to lead him straight to the Topes’ door. The boy, who went only by the name Deputy, darted ahead, then stopped a safe distance from an arched passage to point.
“Yonder winder and door? That’s Jarsper’s,” he said, the name John Jasper dripping with unease. “Ain’t going no closer. He’d lift me right off my legs, bust my braces, and choke me before I could blink. Wait till I peg a good flint at the back of his old head one of these days, see how he likes it.” He then pointed out the low door two steps down on the other side of the arch, marked with Mr. Tope’s name on an oval brass plate. When Datchery handed over the shilling, he explained he had no smaller change, so Deputy owed him a sixpence’s worth of favor for later. Deputy took the coin in a flash, danced a taunting little demon dance from a safe distance to make sure Datchery couldn’t change his mind, then vanished across the green.

The verger’s lodgings were exactly what Datchery had been hoping for. The thick ancient stone walls felt like a cool, sunken dungeon, the rooms dug right out of the masonry rather than built into it, with groined ceilings and small windows sunk deep into the stone. The Topes lived on the floor above, and used a separate little side stair that opened straight out onto the cathedral precincts, meaning Datchery would have total, undisturbed privacy all to himself. The rent was dirt cheap, every draft and creak as quaintly inconvenient as he could have wished. He agreed to move in the very next evening, only asking if he could use John Jasper — whose gatehouse sat on the opposite side of the arch, sharing an upper stair with the Topes — as a reference.

Mrs. Tope fussed that poor, sweet Mr. Jasper was still half out of his mind with grief over his missing nephew Edwin, and would happily vouch for them. Datchery pretended to have only the faintest muddled memory of the Drood disappearance, stumbling over facts so badly that Mrs. Tope had to correct every single detail, and laughing it off by saying he was far too lazy a single idle fellow to keep track of every missing person case the papers babbled about these days.

When he sent his card up to Jasper, he was invited to climb the postern staircase, and found Jasper already in the company of the town’s pompous mayor, Mr. Sapsea. Datchery played the deferential, fawning stranger perfectly, bowing so low to Sapsea that the mayor preened, immediately assuming he was retired from the army, or the navy, or even foreign diplomacy. Datchery teased him gently, feeding his ego at every turn, until Sapsea was utterly charmed by the polite new settler who seemed to recognize his importance.

On their walk out past the cathedral, Sapsea bragged that the law was not just suspicious of whoever had harmed young Edwin Drood — they had all but certain proof, and the “long, strong arm of the law” would reach out and strike the culprit down soon enough. He led Datchery to pause by the churchyard, stopping right in front of his much-celebrated self-written epitaph for his late wife, boasting that tourists regularly stopped to copy it down. Datchery feigned utter rapture over the inscription, and was just pulling his notebook out to transcribe the whole thing when Durdles, the ragged local stonemason, lumbered over, followed close on his heels by Deputy, who was yelling about three pennies’ unpaid wages.

Datchery struck a quick deal with Durdles: he’d come visit him at his odd hours some evening, and bring enough liquor for both of them, if he was welcome to look over the old mason’s work. He also reminded Deputy that he still owed him that favor, to show him around wherever he pleased whenever he asked. The two men parted on excellent terms outside Sapsea’s grand home, Datchery still carrying his hat under his arm, letting his wild white hair blow in the evening breeze.

Later that night, back in the Crozier’s coffee-room, Datchery stood grinning at his reflection in the gaslit mirror over the mantel, shaking out his thick mane of white hair with a quiet huff.
“For a supposedly idle single fellow, who lives a quiet, uneventful life on his own means,” he muttered to his own reflection, “I’ve had myself one remarkably busy afternoon.”

Once more Miss Twinkleton’s valedictory address has wrapped, sticky with spilt white wine and crumbs of pound cake trodden into the Nuns’ House entry rug, and all the young boarders have scattered to their summer homes. Helena Landless is gone, off to stand by her brother Neville’s uncertain fortunes, leaving pretty Rosa Bud entirely alone.

Cloisterham glows so bright under the summer sun that the ancient cathedral walls and weathered monastery ruins look almost translucent, as if a soft inner light seeps out of their stone rather than the light falling on them from above. The gardens around the close blush heavy with ripening plums and apples, and the golden cornfields stretch out beyond the city, where dust-caked hay tramps loiter on cool doorsteps mending tattered shoes, their unused sickles swathed in straw slung over their shoulders. The local constables eye them with open suspicion, prodding them back onto the shimmering high roads to fry in the dust where they belong.

On one sweltering afternoon, right after the final cathedral service, the High Street side of the Nuns’ House falls deep into grateful shade, save for the west-facing garden opening through the boughs of old fruit trees. A breathless maid finds Rosa, and sends a jolt of terror through her: Mr. Jasper is at the gate, asking to see her.

He could not have picked a more perfect moment to catch her off guard. Helena is gone. Mrs. Tisher is away on leave. Miss Twinkleton, in her private civilian mode, has carted herself and a veal pie off to a picnic miles out of town. “Oh why did you tell him I was home!” Rosa wails, wringing her hands. The maid can only answer that Jasper never asked. He said he knew full well she was in, and would wait until she saw him.

Half crazed with desperation, Rosa decides she will meet him in the garden, not the parlor. To be shut inside a room alone with him is unthinkable. Every window in the house looks out over the garden, though. She can be seen, heard, scream in the open air, and run for the porch before he can trap her.

She has not spoken to him since the night Edwin vanished, save for the brief, terrible moment at the mayor’s inquest, where he stood watching her like a brooding shadow, posing as the grieving uncle bent on avenging his lost nephew. She hangs her garden hat on her arm and steps outside. The second she spots him leaning against the weathered stone sun-dial, that old, horrible, paralyzing feeling that he is pulling her toward him against her will wraps around her bones. She cannot turn back. She sinks onto the bench beside the dial, head bowed, unable to lift her eyes from the grass at her feet, and sees that he is clad head to toe in deep mourning, the same as herself.

He reaches first for her hand, and she yanks it away before he can touch her. She can feel his eyes boring into the top of her head.
“I have waited a long time,” he says low, “to be summoned back to my duty near you.”
“Duty, sir?” she manages, after several fumbling attempts to speak.
“The duty of teaching you, serving you as your faithful music master.”
“I have stopped my studies.”
“Discontinued, not abandoned. Your guardian told me you paused out of the shock we have all suffered. When will you resume?”
“Never, sir.”
“Never? You could not have refused me more firmly if you had loved my boy.”
“I did love him!” Rosa flairs, anger flaring hot in her chest.
“Ah, but not quite in the right way, was it? Not the way he should have loved you, the way any man in his place would have loved you, completely and possessively.”

She shrinks further into the bench. The polite line about abandoning her lessons, he suggests, was just a polite way of telling him she wants nothing more to do with him. Rosa snaps back, sharp and brave for a moment, that the politeness was her guardian’s, not hers: she told him flat out she would never take another lesson, and she will not change her mind. She feels his hungry, gloating gaze on her, drinking in the fire in her face, and her courage drains away as quickly as it rose, leaving her shaky, ashamed, and afraid, just as she was the night he sat beside her at the piano.

“I will not question you anymore,” he says. “I will confess something instead.”
“I do not wish to hear you, sir!” Rosa jumps to her feet. He stretches out a hand to stop her, and she stumbles back onto the bench to avoid his touch.
“Sometimes you must act against your own wishes,” he murmurs. “If you run now, you will do more harm than you can ever undo.”

When she demands to know what harm, he stalls, reminding her she is the one breaking her own rule of no questions. He leans his weight back against the sun-dial, his face darkening so much it looks like he is pressing a black mark right into the face of the sun-warmed stone. Rosa freezes mid-flight, horrified, as he tells her he knows full well every window in the house faces their spot. He will not touch her, not come an inch closer. Any passerby looking out will only see a former music master chatting politely with his old pupil, two people sharing the grief of losing Edwin. She sinks back onto the bench, frozen.

“Even when Edwin was engaged to you, I loved you madly,” he hisses, the words pouring out of him like poison. “Even when I pushed him to be more ardent to you, even when he gave me that portrait of your face that I pretended to hang on my wall for his sake, I worshipped it in torment for years. I loved you madly through every tedious day, every sleepless night, wandering through visions of paradises and hells with your face clutched to my chest.”

Rosa wants to run, but she is rooted to the spot. She snaps back, furious, calling him a liar, a monster who made her life miserable for months, who forced her to hide his harassment from Edwin so his sweet, trusting heart would not break. Jasper smiles, wild admiration glowing in his dark eyes, and tells her she is more beautiful when she is angry than she is at peace. He does not even ask for her love. He will take her hatred, her rage, her scorn. All of it is enough.

When she tries to bolt for the porch, he gestures after her, and his voice drops to a snarl. Run, and he will make the harm he promised real. Stay, and he will explain. Trembling so hard she can barely breathe, Rosa stops.

He tells her the police inquiries have named Neville Landless as Edwin’s rival. Jasper has spent six long months weaving a net of circumstantial evidence tight around Neville, a net sharp enough to hang him for the murder, whether Neville did it or not. He leans his chin on his hands, propped on the sun-dial, his face looking soft and idle to anyone glancing out a window, as if he is telling her a silly, lighthearted story. Helena Landless is her dearest friend, he says. Does she want her best friend to spend the rest of her life knowing her brother was hanged as a murderer, her name dragged through the mud in every tavern in the shire? All Rosa has to do is give him hope, favour, agree to be his, and he will throw every last scrap of that carefully built net into the dust. He will betray his vow to avenge Edwin, throw away six months of work, crush his own grieving heart, if only she will let him have her, even if she hates him for it.

The spell breaks. Rosa flies toward the porch, but Jasper is at her side in two steps, whispering right in her ear, calm as can be. No one must hear a word of what he said, not Crisparkle, not her guardian, not the maids. If she breathes a single word of their conversation to anyone, the net closes on Neville that very day. He will never let her go. No man will ever come between them. He will pursue her to the ends of the earth, to the death.

He tips his hat to the waiting maid at the gate and strolls away, as calm and still as the stone statue of old Mr. Sapsea standing across the High Street, no trace of the raving madness he just bared to her in the garden.

Rosa makes it halfway up the stairs before she faints dead away. The maids carry her to her bed, clucking over her, saying it is the coming thunderstorm, the sweltering, thick hot air that has made all their knees shake all day, that knocked the poor pretty dear off her feet. Not a single one of them knows the terrible, permanent shadow that has just fallen across the sun-dial, and over every day Rosa has left to live.

The second Rosa jolted back to full consciousness, the full weight of Jasper’s threat hung over her like a damp, smothering shroud. It had not left her even in her faint: it had chased her through unconsciousness, sharp and vivid as the sun-dial in the garden where he had laid his ravenous, twisted soul bare for her.

Frozen with fright, she had no clue what to do next—save one unshakable, screaming thought: she had to run from this terrible man.

But where? How? She had never breathed her fear of Jasper to anyone but Helena. If she went straight to the Landless girl now, laid out every vile word he had said, it would trigger the total ruin he swore to bring down on Helena’s brother Neville. The longer she turned Jasper’s snarls over in her mind, the more crippling her fear became: one wrong move, one slip, and his malevolence would destroy the innocent boy she had sworn to Helena she believed was innocent of killing Edwin.

For six months, a half-formed, unspoken suspicion had tossed and turned in Rosa’s mind, rising to the surface then sinking back out of sight, never quite solid enough to name. Jasper’s obsession with his nephew when he was alive, his endless, public crusade to prove Edwin had been murdered—everyone in Cloisterham ate it up. No one dared imagine the grieving, devoted uncle could be the hand that struck the blow. Rosa had asked herself a hundred times if she was just wicked, inventing a monstrosity no one else could see. She tried to logic it away: even if Jasper wanted her for himself, would he parade a six-month-long investigation for “vengeance” right in front of everyone? Would he lean on that sun-dial and threaten to “sweep even Edwin away” from her, if he had already done the deed?

But no matter how many times she picked the thought apart, she arrived at the same conclusion: this man was a monster no normal standard could measure. She had to run.

She could not turn to Helena. She could not risk Neville. The only safe port was her guardian, Mr. Grewgious.

The old, crawling feeling that the high walls of the Nuns’ House could not keep Jasper out, that he could follow her like a ghost, wrap her in a spell, was so strong she could not reason it away. When she glanced at the garden sun-dial through her window as she dressed, she went cold all over, as if he had infused the stone itself with his awful hunger.

She scribbled a hasty note to Miss Twinkleton, saying she had sudden urgent business with her guardian and begged the good woman not to worry, stuffed a handful of useless trinkets into a tiny palm-sized bag, left the note propped on her dressing table, and slipped out the gate. It was the first time she had ever stepped into Cloisterham High Street alone. She hurried straight for the omnibus stop, and the coach was just pulling away when she called out to the driver.

“Stop, please, Joe! I have to go to London right now.”

A minute later she was rumbling toward the railway station. Joe fussed over her, tucking her safely into a first class carriage, passing her tiny bag in after her as if it weighed a hundred pounds and might crush her if she so much as touched it. When he promised to deliver her message of safety to Miss Twinkleton, he added a quiet, unspoken thought to himself: he would not mind getting a bit of that sweetness for himself.

At first, the rush of running made Rosa feel brave. The sharp, indignant thought that Jasper’s declaration of love had soiled her, that only the honest, steady protection of her guardian could wipe that stain away, held her up. But as the evening darkened, and the sprawling, gritty mass of London loomed closer, doubts nipped at her: was this a wild, foolish move? What if Grewgious was not home? What would she do, alone, in that vast, strange city?

The train pulled into the London terminus as the gas lamps flickered on through the hot summer haze. Rosa rattled through the endless, shabby streets in a hired cab, the barrel organs and chapel bells echoing hollowly off brick walls, the shuffling of hundreds of feet on hot stone sounding like no comfort at all, until she pulled up at the locked, heavy gate of Staple Inn. The watchman let her and her tiny bag in, pointed her toward Grewgious’s staircase. At ten o’clock sharp, she tapped on his door.

Grewgious looked up from his seat by the open window, and his face went white. “Good Heaven!” he breathed, for a split second mistaking her for her long-dead mother. Rosa fell onto his neck sobbing.

“My child, my child! What on earth has happened? Who brought you here?”

“No one. I came alone. Poor, poor Eddy. His uncle has made love to me. I cannot bear it. I shudder at the very sight of him, and I have come to you to protect me, and all of us, from him, if you will.”

Grewgious exploded to his feet in a rush of uncharacteristic fire, thundering:
*“Damn him!
Confound his politics!
Frustrate his knavish tricks!
On Thee his hopes to fix?
Damn him again!”*
He paced wildly around the room, red-faced, half mad with loyal rage, before he stopped to mop his brow. “Forgive me, my dear—you’ll be glad to hear I feel much better now. Don’t tell me any more just yet, or I might burst out with worse. First, you need food, and rest.”

He got down on one knee to help her untie her hat, his awkward angular movements soft with true chivalry—the sort no one would ever have expected from the gruff old bachelor lawyer. He teased her gently about the tiny bag, which could barely hold a day’s seed for a canary, fussed that he would get her every meal under the sun, then ran hatless across to Furnival’s Inn to give his orders that the head chambermaid should spare no expense to get her everything she might have forgotten to pack.

Over tea, to calm her frayed nerves, he told her the silly, soft story of his clerk Bazzard: the discontented would-be playwright who had written a grand tragedy called *The Thorn of Anxiety* that no theatre would touch, who resented his master deeply because Grewgious could not write a play even if he were sentenced to hang for it. Rosa laughed, her first real smile in months, as Grewgious rambled about the circle of unpublished tragic poets who dedicated their unproduced work to each other in flowery, panegyric notes, while poor Bazzard’s boss had never once gotten a play dedicated to him.

When Rosa was fully calm, she laid out every last detail of her encounter with Jasper for him, lingering on every line that touched Helena and Neville Landless. Grewgious smoothed his balding head slowly, his face grave as he processed every word. “Well narrated,” he said at last, gesturing to the dark windows across the road, “that’s where the Landless siblings live, over yonder. We’ll sleep on the question of you visiting Helena tomorrow.”

He tucked her hand in his, carried her tiny useless bag on his arm, and walked her across the street to Furnival’s Inn, his stately awkward gait as if he were leading a minuet. He handed her over to the unlimited-expense head chambermaid, promised her he would be across the road at ten the next morning for breakfast, and smiled when Rosa told him she felt completely safe here.

“Safe from him?” he said, patting her hand. “This inn is locked behind iron bars, watched every hour of the night by the gatekeepers, and I live right over the way. If you need anyone to run to me at any hour after dark, tell the porter—he’ll get a whole crown for the trouble.”

Long after Rosa had gone up to her bright, comfortable room, Grewgious paced up and down outside the iron gates of Furnival’s Inn for nearly an hour, peering between the bars every few minutes, as if he had tucked a fragile dove high in its roost, and was standing guard to make sure no hawk slipped through to tear it apart.

No shadow, no unaccounted creak on the stairs, no distant echo of a familiar heavy tread disturbed Rosa through the night. The tired little dove who had fled Cloisterham in a panic the evening before woke at dawn fully rested, the tight knot of dread in her chest loosened just a little.

When the mantel clock struck ten, Mr. Grewgious’s door opened to admit the Minor Canon, Mr. Crisparkle, who had all but hurled himself straight from the banks of the Cloisterham river onto the earliest morning train.
“Miss Twinkleton was in such a state of flustered wonder when she turned up at our house with your note, Rosa, that I volunteered to come up at once to set her mind at rest,” he explained warmly, sitting down beside her. “At first I wished you had come straight to me. But now? I think it was far wiser of you to run to your guardian first.”
“I did think of you,” Rosa assured him, “but Minor Canon Corner sits so close to the Cloisterham danger I was running from—”
“Of course. It was the most natural choice you could have made.”
“I’ve already told Mr. Crisparkle every word you shared with me last night, my dear,” Grewgious put in, his dry tone softening. “I’d have penned the full account to him at once even if he hadn’t turned up out of the blue, and it was no small kindness of him to make the trip, when he’d only just settled back home after last night’s troubles.”
Rosa leaned forward, anxious. “Have the two of you worked out what to do for Helena and Neville?”
Crisparkle spread his hands, helplessly rueful. “I’m completely at a standstill. If a man as sharp and deliberate as Mr. Grewgious, who’s had a full night to turn the problem over, is still undecided, what hope have I?”

At that moment, Grewgious’s housekeeper rapped gently and stepped inside, announcing a gentleman below who wished a word with a Mr. Crisparkle, if one happened to be in the house. Rosa recoiled half a step, pressing back against her guardian’s side.
“Is he a dark man?” she whispered.
“Not at all, miss. More a weathered brown, brown hair, bright blue eyes.”
Rosa relaxed, just barely.
“Might be well to see him, reverend sir,” Grewgious advised, tapping his fingertips together in that cautious, deliberate way of his. “When you’re tangled in a difficulty, you never know what crack a way out might come through. I make it a business rule never to bolt a single door that might lead to a solution—though I could tell you a whole anecdote to illustrate that, if it weren’t too soon.”
Crisparkle nodded. “Show him in, please.”

The stranger stepped through the door, handsome and sun-browned, apologizing with easy grace for interrupting their meeting, turned straight to Crisparkle, and smiled. “Do you remember me? Who am I?”
“You’re the gentleman I spotted smoking under the Staple Inn trees not ten minutes ago,” Crisparkle said, frowning in faint confusion.
“True enough. But who else?”
Crisparkle stared at that sun-weathered face, and slowly, dimly, the ghost of a scrawny, towheaded schoolboy rose up in his memory. The stranger saw the recognition spark, and laughed. “What are you missing for breakfast this morning? You’re out of apricot jam, remember?”
“Wait—*Tartar*!” Crisparkle cried, shooting to his feet.
The two men shook hands so hard their knuckles whitened, then did the uncharacteristic thing for two reserved Englishmen of their class: they laid their hands on each other’s shoulders, grinning like fools, and stared straight into each other’s faces.
“My old fag!” Crisparkle said, voice thick.
“My old best master,” Tartar corrected him. “The one who never once picked on the smallest boy in the house.”
“You saved me from drowning, when I slipped under the river ice that winter!”
“And then you went and turned yourself into the strongest swimmer in the whole school after that,” Tartar chuckled.
“Good Lord, it’s been decades!” Crisparkle said.
“Amen to that.” They shook hands all over again, until Crisparkle’s eyes glistened. “You two have to hear this. This boy was the smallest junior in the whole school, dove straight through the ice after me, hauled his six-foot senior up by the hair, and struck out for the shore like he was born in the water.”
“Couldn’t very well let you sink, when you were the only one who ever stood up for me,” Tartar said, grinning.

Grewgious stepped forward, hand outstretched, his whole face alight with uncharacteristic warmth. “Sir, it’s an honour. I’m proud to make your acquaintance. I do hope you never caught your death of cold that day, or swallowed any more river water than you had to. How have you fared all these years?” He seemed half distracted by his own enthusiasm, but his good intentions were impossible to miss.
Rosa found herself thinking, for half a second, If only someone this brave and quick had been there for my poor mother, when she needed someone to pull her out of danger too.

Grewgious suddenly bounced twice on his heels, the way he did when a brilliant idea had just fully clicked in his head. “I—*I think* I’ve got it. The perfect solution.” He trotted across the room and back, while the rest of them stared, half convinced he’d had a sudden spell of indigestion. “Mr. Tartar—am I right that you rent the very top set of chambers in Staple Inn, the one with the wide window box full of flowers?”
“That’s correct, sir.”
“Tick that off.” Grewgious tapped his left thumb against his right. “And do you happen to know the name of the young man renting the top set directly on the other side of your party wall?”
“Landless. Neville Landless.”
“Tick that off. You’ve spoken to him, then?”
“A little. He struck me as lonely, and short on money, so a couple days back I asked if he’d mind if I strung my flower boxes across the shared wall, so my whole rooftop garden spills over onto his windowsill too.”
Grewgious gestured for them all to sit down, beaming like he’d just cracked a riddle no one else could solve. “I’ve got the full plan. Let me lay it out.
“We can’t have Rosa traipsing directly to Neville and Helena’s rooms right now. That local man we all know—the one with the bad habit of sneaking and spying around every corner, who means the pair of them harm—he almost certainly has watchers posted by their door, reporting every person who comes and goes. But no one is paying attention to *your* chambers, Mr. Tartar. No spy in their right mind is watching the random sailor’s flat two doors over.
“Rosa can go with you and Mr. Crisparkle up to your rooftop garden. She can wave to Helena through the flower boxes, call her over, talk to her for as long as she likes, no one the wiser. She can tell Helena everything that’s been threatened, all the things she’s too afraid to say anywhere the wrong person might overhear. No one will suspect a thing.”

Tartar nodded at once, grinning. “My chambers, my whole garden, all of it is yours to use for as long as you need it. No questions asked.”
Rosa blushed, twisting her fingers in her skirt. “I’m afraid I’m being terribly rude, turning up unannounced and taking over your whole home like this.”
“Miss Rosa,” Tartar said, sincere, “my little flat has never had a greater honour than to host you, for even a single minute.”
Rosa fumbled for Grewgious’s arm, too flustered to meet Tartar’s eye. “Should I go get my hat on, then?”

By the time she came back, Crisparkle had filled Tartar in on the whole tangled story of Neville and Helena, of the missing Edwin Drood, of the man they all knew was hiding in the shadows waiting to do them harm. Tartar offered Rosa his arm as they set off for Staple Inn, Crisparkle walking ahead to scout the path.
Rosa’s chest ached as they walked. Poor, poor Eddy, she thought. Where are you, right now?
She glanced up at Tartar’s sun-browned, steady hand on her arm. It hadn’t been half so strong, all those years ago, when he’d hauled Crisparkle out of the river—but it must have been just as steady, just as unshakable, back when he was a scrawny boy. He told her he’d spent ten years at sea, sailing all over the world, and when she asked if he’d ever go back, he shook his head. “Never again.”
Rosa found herself half daydreaming, imagining the schoolgirls back at Miss Twinkleton’s gawking if they saw her walking across the London streets on the arm of a broad-shouldered ex-sailor, who could lift her right off the ground and carry her out of any danger, for miles, without stopping. She was just thinking that his bright blue eyes looked like they were used to staring down gales and oncoming storms, never flinching, when she realized he was looking down at her, soft and curious.
She didn’t quite remember how they climbed the stairs up to his rooftop, how she stepped over the threshold to his garden full of climbing roses and jasmine, that hung over the wall to Helena’s windows like something out of a fairy tale, blooming bright and warm far above all the dark, scheming streets below. May that little patch of safety bloom for them all, for ever.

Mr. Tartar’s London chambers were the neatest, cleanest, most perfectly ordered set of rooms ever to grace a city rooftop. The floors were scrubbed so bright you’d swear London’s infamous soot had packed up and fled the island for good. Every inch of brass he owned – doorknobs, hooks, telescope fittings – was polished until it glowed like a mirror. Not a single smudge, speck, or stray splash of rain marred a single one of his possessions, large or small. His sitting room could have been lifted straight from an admiral’s cabin, his bathroom smelled of fresh milk and shone like a dairy, his bedroom lined top to bottom with custom lockers and drawers resembled a perfectly organized seedsman’s shop, and his slim cot sat centered in the room so evenly balanced it looked like it was breathing.
Every single thing Tartar owned had a designated nook: his nautical charts, his books, his hairbrushes, his boots, his case of spirits, his telescopes, all slotted exactly where they belonged, easy to grab without wasting a second of rummaging. The tiny collection of sea souvenirs he’d brought home from years of voyages – stuffed exotic birds, dried tropical fish, coral fragments, odd shell specimens – each had its own perfect display spot, no dust allowed. A fresh coat of varnish seemed to be kept on hand at all times, ready to erase a stray fingerprint the second it showed up. No Royal Navy man o’ war was ever kept half so spotless. That bright summer day, a sailor-rigged canvas awning shaded his tiny rooftop flower garden, and the whole setup felt so authentically nautical you could half believe the whole building would haul anchor the second Tartar blew the speaking trumpet slung by his door, yelling for the crew to heave up the anchor and set sail.
Tartar himself fit the vibe perfectly: easy, sunburnt, amused by his own obsession with tidiness, clearly proud of his little ship of a home without being stuffy about it. Rosa, treated like the First Lady of the Admiralty during the tour, thought it was utterly charming to watch him laugh at his own little storage tricks before he politely excused himself, leaving her free to wander the garden as its queen.
That was when a voice called out from the flower beds: “Helena! Helena Landless! Are you there?”
“Who calls? Rosa?” A second dark, handsome face peeked out through the rose bushes.
“Yes, my dearest!”
Rosa blushed so hard she thought she must be dreaming, when Helena asked how on earth she’d turned up on her doorstep out of nowhere. She hurried to explain the whole story – how she’d run into Mr. Crisparkle, how it was Tartar who’d once saved the minor canon’s life by dragging him out of the Thames when he was drowning, not the other way around. Helena’s dark eyes went soft when she heard that, then sharp, serious.
“Neville is in his room right now,” she said quietly, glancing back toward the house. “The sun’s too bright out this way, so he’s reading indoors. We can’t let him know you’re this close yet. Not while that wretch John Jasper is still shadowing him, trying to isolate him from every single friend he has, to wear him down grain by grain until no one will believe a word he says. Ask Mr. Crisparkle what he thinks: should we wait for Jasper to make his next move, or try to find out what he’s already plotting behind all their backs?”
Crisparkle was stumped for an answer, so he trotted over to consult Mr. Grewgious across the square, who didn’t hesitate for a second: “If you can sneak up on a brigand or a wild beast before it ambushes you, that’s the smart move. And John Jasper is both, rolled into one man.”
When Helena heard that, she nodded slowly, working through her idea. “We can count on Mr. Tartar’s help, right?”
Rosa stammered shyly that she was sure they could, darting off to ask, and came back moments later bright-faced, to say Tartar was already ready to do whatever they asked. Helena laid out her plan clearly: have Tartar visit Neville openly, every day, loiter outside his door, be seen chatting with him by anyone who was watching. If Jasper was really monitoring Neville’s every movement, he’d surely try to threaten or warn Tartar off, to get him to stay away from the man he’d framed for Edwin’s disappearance. The second he made that move, they’d have proof of his malice, plain as day.
Rosa was so flustered running back and forth between the two men and Helena that she barely knew which direction she was facing, when they heard Neville stirring inside the house. They had to part fast.
“I can’t go back to Miss Twinkleton’s,” Rosa blurted out suddenly. “Not after that horrifying interview with Jasper. I can never step foot there again.” She paused, then shrugged a little. “I don’t know where I’ll end up, but my guardian will make sure I’m safe. You’ll pass my sympathy on to Neville, won’t you? Tell him not to hate me?”
Helena kissed her goodbye, and a tanned, sea-calloused hand reached through the rose bushes to help Rosa step away safely, out of sight. The spread Tartar laid out for them back in his admiral’s cabin, pulled from hidden lockers with the twist of a knob, was like something out of a fairy tale: crisp macaroons, bright tropical jellies, spiced liqueurs, all pulled out in an instant. But time didn’t slow down, and soon enough Rosa was walking back to Furnival’s Inn on Grewgious’s arm.
“Now what do we do with you, my dear?” Grewgious asked gently. Rosa had no answer, beyond a silly passing fantasy of hiding in a top-floor fireproof apartment in Furnival’s Inn forever. Grewgious laughed, and had a better plan: they would rent a furnished London lodging for a month, invite Miss Twinkleton to come stay and act as her chaperone while they untangled the mess with Jasper.
Grewgious’s method of looking at rentals was famously useless: he’d stare at the house with the “furnished apartments to let” sign from across the street, then trudge around to stare at the back of it, then leave without ever knocking. Eventually he remembered Mrs. Billickin, the widowed distant cousin of his clerk Mr. Bazzard, who’d once asked him to send her clients – a woman so aggressively, unapologetically candid, she’d never lie about a single flaw in her property. Her brass doorplate, in huge uncompromising capital letters, read *BILLICKIN*, with no indication of gender or marital status. She drifted out of her back parlour looking half-fainted, as if she’d been woken mid-swoon just to greet them.
“I am as well as I hever ham,” she gasped, pressing a hand to her chest when Grewgious explained they wanted a month’s lodging for his ward and an elderly chaperone. “I will not deceive you. I do have apartments available.”
She wasted no time laying out all the flaws first, no softening the blow: the top floor bedrooms had wobbly floors, gas pipes run overhead instead of under the joists, the slates rattled loose in a gale, and the ceiling leaked rain often enough that you could wake up soaked through in a bad storm. Then she showed them the first and second floors, which were far sturdier, even if she warned them the stairs were so steep no one could ever pretend they were on ground level. The two floors were perfect, exactly what they needed.
When Grewgious handed her the rental agreement to sign, though, she balked. “No sir, you’ll excuse the Christian name,” she said firmly. “As long as everyone on this street thinks Billickin is some big, burly man hidden inside the house, no thief will dare break in. If I sign my full name and let everyone know I’m a single widow living here alone, I’d be in danger.” Grewgious didn’t argue, and the signature BILLICKIN was scrawled across the bottom of the page.
They walked back out onto the street, planning to move in two days’ time when Miss Twinkleton could arrive, and spotted Mr. Tartar pacing back and forth in front of Furnival’s Inn. He smiled when he saw them, and stepped forward.
“I thought I’d wait around,” he said, nodding toward the river. “The tide’s perfect, the weather’s gorgeous. I’ve got my own boat tied up at Temple Stairs. Would the two of you like to come up the river for a sail?”
Grewgious admitted he hadn’t been up the Thames for decades. Rosa said she’d never been at all.

More than half a year had slipped by since John Jasper had wordlessly showed the Minor Canon Mr. Crisparkle the final, grim entry scrawled in his diary about Edwin Drood. The two men crossed paths daily under the cathedral’s vaulted stone roof, but never once did a single word pass between them referencing the missing nephew. It was impossible their shared silence meant shared disinterest: Jasper, relentless in his crusade to brand Neville Landless a murderer and hound him to ruin, and Crisparkle, Neville’s unshakable, fierce defender, must each have spent every encounter turning over the other’s hidden designs in his head, wondering what unspoken secret the other clung to. But neither dared break the unspoken truce.

Crisparkle, a man constitutionally incapable of duplicity, made no secret of the fact he would have welcomed a frank, open discussion of the case at any time. But Jasper’s resolve to say nothing was unbreakable. Impassive, sullen, solitary, fixated on a single idea and the unshakable dark purpose that clung to it like a shadow, he moved through the town entirely disconnected from the people around him. Even his work as choir master, which forced him to move in perfect mechanical unison with the rest of the singers, never touched his cold, isolated inner world — a detail he had admitted openly to Edwin, long before the boy vanished.

No one could doubt Jasper had heard of Rosa’s sudden, secret flight from Cloisterham, and guessed exactly why she had run. But Crisparkle could never work out if he thought he had merely terrified her into silence, or if he feared she had told anyone — Crisparkle himself, for example — every last unsettling detail of that last interview they had shared. The terrible, unvoiced suspicion that Jasper himself was behind Edwin’s disappearance had never fully taken root in Crisparkle’s mind. Neville and Helena never spoke of it, even if it haunted their private thoughts. Mr. Grewgious made no secret of his ferocious, unforgiving dislike of the choir master, but he never offered a single hint as to its source — never mentioned that dark night he’d warmed his hands by the gatehouse fire, staring down at the heap of torn, mud-caked clothes heaped on the stone floor.

The whole drowsy, slow-moving town of Cloisterham had long since split evenly on its opinion of the Drood case, dismissed half a year prior by the local magistrates: half the town was convinced Neville had murdered Edwin in a jealous, unprovoked rage, the other half certain Edwin had faked his own disappearance for some selfish, hidden end. Then the whole town would yawn, note that the grief-stricken Jasper was still tireless in his hunt for answers and revenge, and doze right back off again. That was the state of things, the day the cathedral doors clicked shut for the night, and Jasper set off for London on a short leave from his choir duties.

He took the exact same train Rosa had taken weeks prior, and arrived in the capital on a sweltering, dust-choked summer evening. His entire luggage fit in the palm of his hand, and he walked straight to a new, strange budget hotel tucked in a little square behind Aldersgate Street, right by the General Post Office — one of those new half-hotel, half-boarding house ventures that advertised fixed, fair prices for bed, board, and boots blacked, no ridiculous hidden charges for trivial amenities. He ate a sparse, unappetizing meal, then tramped eastward through the stale, smog-thick streets until he reached his destination: a rotting, miserable courtyard, the worst of a dozen such slum courts clustered in that part of the city.

He climbed a splintered, broken staircase, pushed open a rickety door, and peered into a dark, airless room.
“Are you alone here?”

“Alone, deary,” a croaking, ragged voice answered. “Worse luck for me, better luck for you. Come in, come in — can’t see a thing till I light a match, but I’d swear I know the sound of your voice. We’re old mates, aren’t we?”

“Light your match and find out.”

She fumbled for ages, her hands shaking so bad she could barely close her fingers around the matchbox, her racking cough so violent she complained the matches kept jumping out of her grasp like live things. When she finally got the candle lit, the cough seized her, and she sat rocking back and forth, gasping that her lungs were worn thin as cabbage netting, until the fit passed. Then she squinted at him, and her eyes went wide.
“Why, it’s you! I thought you was dead and buried, deary. Never thought I’d see you again.”

They bantered a little, her teasing him about the black mourning band on his sleeve, asking what relative he’d lost. He gave short, sharp answers, barely speaking, as he kicked off his shoes, loosened his cravat, and flopped across the foot of her squalid straw mattress, his head propped lazily on his left hand. She set about prepping his opium pipe, babbling about how he’d been a shy new customer the first time he’d come, so high on the fumes he’d sing like a little bird half the night.

He took the pipe, drew slow, deep drags, and soon his eyes went distant, far away. He rambled about a dangerous journey he’d run through his head hundreds of thousands of times, over and over in that very room — a hazardous path over bottomless abysses, where one single misstep would mean total, irreversible ruin. He lurched forward, pointing at the bare floor as if staring down into some endless dark void, cackling that he’d run through that journey so many times, across what felt like eons of imagined time, that when he finally did it for real, it had been over in a single heartbeat, so fast it barely felt like it had happened at all.

He laughed maniacally, wild yelping laughs, as he thought of his “fellow traveler” who’d made that terrible journey right alongside him, and never once saw the road under his feet, never guessed a single thing about what was happening. Then he froze, staring at nothing, his face contorted with a look of raw, unhinged shock, pointing at a tiny, sordid, miserable thing only he could see, screaming that it was real, that the whole journey was over. Then the pipe slipped from his fingers, and he collapsed back onto the bed, as limp and unconscious as a log.

The old opium woman prodded him a few times, confirmed he was too far under to wake for hours, and muttered to herself that he’d once called her and two of her regulars “unintelligible” when he’d thought she was too far gone to hear him. “Don’t you be too sure, pretty boy,” she croaked, her cat-like eyes fixed unblinkingly on his face. She’d slipped a little extra of her own special mixture into his opium that night to make him lose his tongue, and he’d spilled every dark, secret thing he’d been hiding.

She sat by his bed all night, watching the guttering candles burn down to blackened stubs, until the first grey light of dawn leaked through the cracked window. When Jasper woke, he was shivering, cold and disoriented, fumbled his clothes back on, paid her, and stumbled out the door. She did not climb back into bed to sleep. She had been chasing him for six months, ever since he’d vanished from her den right after he’d made that “journey” he ranted about. She followed him at a distance, found out he was staying at the little hotel behind Aldersgate, tricked the staff into telling her he was heading back to Cloisterham on the six o’clock evening omnibus.

She took the same bus, and arrived in Cloisterham just after dark, waiting on the high street until Jasper stumbled past her, vanishing through the old stone cathedral archway. She hurried after him, and ran straight into Mr. Datchery, the odd, white-haired, good-natured gentleman who loitered by the gate all day, watching everyone pass. He laughed and told her Jasper lived up the little staircase beside the gate, that his name was John Jasper, and he was the cathedral’s choir master. When she wheedled him for three and sixpence, claiming she needed it to buy medicine for her bad cough, and admitted the medicine was opium, Mr. Datchery’s whole face shifted. He stared at her, sharp and alert, every trace of his lazy, easygoing air vanishing in an instant.
