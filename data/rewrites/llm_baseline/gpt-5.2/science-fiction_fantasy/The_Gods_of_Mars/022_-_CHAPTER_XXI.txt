Yersted’s whispered knowledge left no room for the luxuries of debate or the slow rituals of council. Dawn would bring Tars Tarkas and the full, furious weight of an assault; I had to be inside the Temple of Issus before that first pale spill of light, as quietly as a knife sliding between ribs. From within, I told myself, I could take my Princess from those perfumed walls—take her and run—because at my back, at last, stood the fighting strength of Helium in a single gathered body.

When Carthoris came to me with the others, we began at once—trip after trip—ferrying our men through the submerged passage, moving them like grain in careful measures through a narrow mill. The water swallowed sound in a thick, muffling way; only the small, intimate noises remained—the scrape of harness, the softened click of metal against stone, the controlled breathing of thousands trying to become one silent creature. Five thousand we brought through, seasoned warriors all, the hard red men who could read a battlefield the way an elder reads a field’s soil by rubbing it between finger and thumb.

Carthoris alone knew the hidden ways. We could not split and strike the temple at multiple points, though every instinct urged it; instead, we would be a single thrust, aimed at the heart. He was about to lead us from the pool chamber into the corridor when an officer touched my arm and pointed down at the black surface where the submarine floated.

At first I mistook the agitation for pursuit—a great body rising beneath us, another boat coming up like a predatory fish. But the motion had a different character: the water itself was lifting, steady as dough swelling in a warm bowl. It rose without drama, without hurry, yet with terrible certainty. It kissed the edge of stone, crept higher, and the pool began to lose its boundaries.

For a breath I did not comprehend. Then Carthoris’s face changed, as if he’d tasted something rancid.

“Haste!” he snapped. His voice rang in the chamber and bounced back harshly from the dome. “If we delay, we are lost. The pumps—Omean’s pumps—have been stopped. They mean to drown us. We must reach the upper pits before the flood takes them. Come!”

“Lead,” I said. “We follow.”

He sprang into the corridor. The men moved in column of twos, disciplined as if marching through a palace hall instead of into rising water. Each company went only on its dwar’s command, a chain of obedience holding firm against the animal urge to run.

Yet the water was already ankle deep before the last company left the chamber. I saw nervousness ripple through the ranks, and I understood it. These people lived with water as one lives with a spice—rare, precious, measured. To be forced into it in such bulk, to feel it tug at greaves and wrap around calves with cold insistence, was like being plunged into something alien and alive. Still they did not break. They tightened their jaws and marched, and their steadiness tasted, to me, like salt—bitter and admirable.

I was last out. By the time I entered the corridor the water had climbed to my knees. The passage floor lay level with the chamber’s, offering no rise for many yards, and the flood took advantage of every inch. We moved as fast as the narrow tunnel allowed, but the tide moved faster, gaining confidence as it gained depth. Behind me the blackness carried the sound of water in a way that made it feel nearer than sight: a thick slosh, an urgent suck, the soft boom of displaced air. It was a soundscape of drowning—no birds, no wind, no living world—only the industrial, deliberate noise of a system turned against men.

As the passage rose higher toward the dome’s apex, I realized the geometry of our doom: the space narrowed, so the water would climb faster. It was as if an unseen hand were pouring the same measure into a funnel. I could see it in my mind the way a food historian sees the loss of a recipe—once the vessel changes, everything changes; the same ingredients become a different fate.

Long before the rear could hope to gain the upper pits, I knew the water would overtake us. Half our force—maybe more—would be swallowed.

Then, as my eyes searched for anything, any flaw in the trap, I saw a diverging corridor to my right, angling upward more steeply than the main way. The water now swirled at my waist. The men directly ahead of me began to look back, their faces bright with fear, and fear is a contagion. If they stampeded they would crush their fellows, jam the passage, and turn the tunnel into a corked bottle where the flood would finish its work.

I threw my voice down the corridor, forcing command into it as one forces heat into an oven that must not fail.

“Call back the last twenty-five utans! Here—this way rises. Turn back and follow me!”

The order ran forward from dwar to dwar, and obedience—blessed obedience—flowed back. Nearer thirty utans turned, nearly three thousand men, and pushed into the teeth of the flood toward the branch I had found. They came splashing, struggling, lifting knees high as the current tugged. Their breath and the slap of water made a harsh chorus in the stone, a battered drumbeat of survival.

As the first dwar reached the opening, I caught his arm.

“Listen for my commands,” I told him. “Do not venture into the open. Do not leave the pits for the temple proper until I come up with you—unless you learn I died before I could reach you.”

He saluted, face tight, and led his men upward.

The line kept coming. The water rose to my chest. Men stumbled and went under. I grabbed them by harness or arm and heaved them upright, but the flood was a hungry cookpot boiling over—too much for one pair of hands. Some were pulled beneath and vanished into the churn without so much as a cry, like grains dropping through a crack in the floor.

Gur Tus, the dwar of the Tenth, took his stand beside me. A hard-eyed, valorous soldier, he planted himself as if he could physically hold back panic. Together we shouted, shoved, hauled, kept bodies moving, kept the line from breaking. When Djor Kantos, son of Kantos Kan—padwar of the Fifth—reached us, he joined without hesitation, and with three of us at the mouth of that branch corridor, not another man was lost among the hundreds who still had to pass.

By the time the last utan surged past, the water was at our necks. The tunnel’s air tasted of cold stone and fear; it carried the metallic tang of weapons half-submerged. Gur Tus and I clasped hands, and Djor Kantos braced against the wall. We held our ground until the last pair of soldiers vanished up the incline.

Then we followed, half swimming, half clawing. Within a hundred yards the corridor lifted us above the waterline. I dragged in air as if it were broth after starvation. We continued rapidly up the steep grade, hoping it would bring us to the upper pits beneath the temple.

But fate had layered its traps like courses in a cruel banquet.

A cry—thin, frantic—came from far ahead.

“Fire!”

Then came terror, and dwar voices trying to shape chaos into order. The news ran back like a sour wind: they had fired the pits ahead. Chemical combustion—hellish, deliberate. We were caught between flame and flood, smoke rolling toward us in a black, choking wave.

“Help, John Carter! We are suffocating!”

The smoke hit and the world became a throatful of ash. Eyes streamed; lungs burned; men stumbled blind into one another. The sound changed again—no longer the slap of water but the crackle and hungry roar of burning chemicals, and over it the ragged coughing of thousands. Noise here wasn’t life; it was injury, a weapon. Whoever controlled the pits understood what modern engines and clever murderers always understand: that to assault the senses is to assault the will.

There was nothing to do but find another way. I seized the first gallery that led out and up, away from the densest smoke. Again I stood aside, forcing order into a torrent of men running for breath. Two thousand passed in a rush. Then the stream thinned and stopped.

I could not be certain the last had escaped the fire’s reach. The thought of one man—any man—left behind to die alone in that choking heat was intolerable. So I ran back up the gallery toward the glow.

It was hot, stifling work. The air had weight; it pressed on the tongue like burnt oil. The corridor ahead shuddered with dull light, and the crackle grew louder until it seemed to fill my skull. At last I reached a point where the fire lit the tunnel enough for me to see: no Heliumite lay between me and the conflagration. Whatever was in that seething chemical furnace, none could pass through and live to tell it.

Satisfied—if such a word can ever sit beside such a sight—I turned and ran back.

And found my escape gone.

Across the mouth of the corridor through which my men had fled stood a massive steel grating. It had dropped from above with the finality of a lid slammed on a pot. I hurled myself against it; it did not tremble. I shook it; it answered only with cold, dead resistance.

So. Our movements had been watched. The fleet’s attack the day before, the stopping of Omean’s pumps at the exact moment to flood our tunnel, the firing of the one corridor we’d chosen—none of it was accident. Invisible eyes, invisible hands. And now this gate, locking me alone between flood and flame.

In that choking dimness I cursed myself. I had walked my men into a place built for ambush, for surprise, for unseen cruelty. Better, perhaps, to have kept our force intact and struck openly from the valley side, trusting skill and numbers to break through. Here, in the pits, every corridor was a sentence written in a language of traps, and the First Born were fluent.

Smoke pushed me back. I could hear the water behind, surging through darkness. Worse than the smoke was the black: with my men had gone the last torch, and these upper ways did not glow with phosphorescent rock like the lower levels. Still, that very absence told me I was near the temple above; the air had changed, thinner, less wet, carrying faint hints of perfumed life beyond stone.

At last my boots met water. It lapped at my feet, then climbed. Behind me the smoke thickened, turning each breath into a struggle. My eyes burned. My throat felt flayed.

There was one choice left: choose the death that would not suffocate me like an animal in a chimney. I moved down into the water until it closed about my body, and then I swam forward through utter blackness.

The instinct to live does not depart just because the mind has decided death is certain. I swam slowly, listening to my own breath, feeling for the moment my head would strike the roof and the corridor would become a submerged coffin. But before the water reached the ceiling, my outstretched hand hit stone—a blank wall.

Confused, I felt around. The wall was real. I had reached the main corridor. And above the waterline there was still a space where air lived.

I turned and swam along the main corridor in the direction Carthoris and the head of the column had gone earlier. Stroke after stroke, hope returned with each cold pull of water. If I was moving toward higher ground, the water ahead could not be deeper than it was here. Soon—soon—I would feel floor beneath my feet again.

Then my head struck rock overhead.

A dip. One of those sudden drops Martian tunnels sometimes take, plunging to a lower level before rising again. Somewhere beyond, the passage climbed, but how far did it run under water? How long could a man swim in blackness with lungs already bruised by smoke?

There was a single forlorn hope. I filled my lungs with the last clean air available and dove.

The water was icy, inky, numbing. I swam forward through darkness so complete it seemed to erase time. Again and again I rose, reaching up, only to find rock above—no air. My lungs began to scream; the body’s demand became a violent, singular command. Panic fluttered at the edges of thought, but there was no retreat. I knew I could not endure to swim back to where I’d last breathed.

Death came close—so close it felt like a cold mouth on my forehead.

One more effort. One more desperate, burning push.

I rose—and my mouth broke into air.

It rushed into me like life itself, cool and miraculous, and for a moment I could only cling to it, shaking. A few strokes more and my feet found stone. Then I was wading, then running, water streaming from me, lungs dragging greedily at every breath as I raced along the corridor hunting for an opening into the temple.

If I could not win Dejah Thoris back, then I would at least carve vengeance from the heart of this place. No other life would satisfy me but the life of the fiend who had made Barsoom suffer.

Sooner than I expected, a sudden incline rose at my right, ending in a doorway. The corridor continued beyond, perhaps to other entrances, but I could not afford delay or careful choice. Any point was as good as another when the enemy watched from shadows.

I ran up the short, steep way and pushed the portal.

It swung inward. Before it could betray me with a slow creak long enough for unseen guards to respond, I slipped through and drew it after me.

Though dawn had not yet come, the chamber was brilliantly lit. The air was warm, scented—oils and faint incense, the heavy sweetness of luxury that has never known hunger. Hangings draped the walls; furniture gleamed richly, soft as if meant to cradle lives that had never marched through floodwater. It felt like the domestic heart of a predator: a room made tender so the cruelty beyond could be forgotten.

A single figure lay prone on a low couch at the far side, apparently asleep.

From the sumptuousness I judged it a living-room of some priestess—perhaps Issus herself. The thought sent a sharp heat through my veins. If fortune had delivered the goddess into my hands alone and unguarded, I could take her as hostage, and with her throat under my blade force obedience to every demand.

I crossed the floor softly, each step measured, listening—listening for any sound that did not belong: the scrape of a hidden latch, the shift of a curtain, the whisper of breath that meant an unseen guard. The room’s acoustics were gentle, muffling, built for privacy, for secrets. Even my heartbeat sounded indecently loud.

Halfway across, the figure stirred.

As I sprang, she rose and faced me.

For an instant terror flooded her features—then startled disbelief, then hope as bright as the first taste of water after thirst, then thanksgiving so raw it broke her composure.

My heart hammered. My arms opened of their own will. Words surged up in me like a torrent and choked in my throat. Tears blurred the chamber’s lamplight into halos.

And then I held her—held her warmth, her living breath, the unmistakable shape of her—once more.

Dejah Thoris. Princess of Helium.
