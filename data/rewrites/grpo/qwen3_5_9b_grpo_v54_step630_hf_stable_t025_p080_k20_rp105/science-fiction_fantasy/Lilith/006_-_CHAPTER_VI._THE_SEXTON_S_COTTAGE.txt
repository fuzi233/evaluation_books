The landscape shifted beneath our feet, transforming from the jagged, dry expanse of the moor into a realm where the air itself felt ancient and heavy. In the distance, shrouded by a pall of gray clouds that seemed to swallow the setting sun, stood a solitary cottage. The wind here carried a chill that suggested not merely cold weather, but the perpetual night of a forgotten season.

"Here we are at last!" the raven called out, his voice echoing with a weariness that belied his flight. "What a journey! In half the time, I could have flown to Paradise to visit my cousin—the one who never returned to Noah. Oh, dear! It feels almost like winter."

"Winter?" I protested, startled by the sudden shift in tone. "It seems to me only half a day has passed since we left home."

"That is because you are bound by the gravity of your world," the raven replied, his form shifting slightly as if adjusting to a different physics. "You cannot simply pull up the plumb-line of gravity and let the world spin beneath you. Here, however, is my wife's house. She is kind enough to let me reside here, calling it the sexton's cottage."

"But where is the churchyard?" I asked, scanning the flat, desolate heath. "Where are the graves you tend?"

The raven stretched his neck, holding his beak horizontally. He slowly rotated it, pointing first north, then south, east, and west, offering no verbal answer. I followed the direction of his beak with my eyes, and the revelation struck me with the force of a physical blow: there was no church, no stone markers, no distinct boundary. The entire heath was the cemetery. Wherever the dreary wind swept, it marked a burial ground. The raven was the sexton of everything he surveyed, the lord of all that had been laid aside. I stood within the burial ground of the universe itself, bounded only by the low, starless horizon. I had left behind the cycle of spring, summer, and autumn, arriving instead at a winter that slept eternally in a winding sheet of ice. Yet, as the day unfolded in this strange region, I realized the seasons were not linear here. Winter slumbered through the night; Spring awoke with a childlike smile at dawn; Summer blazed in midday glory; and Autumn crept in with the fading afternoon, dying only at the first breath of the ghostly night.

As we approached the cottage, the clouded sun plummeted down the western slope, sinking just as we reached the threshold. An instant later, a coldness seized me, tangible and suffocating, like the grasp of an icy death. I stumbled across the door, fighting to shut it against the gale that swelled on the moor. Inside, silence fell instantly.

A single candle flickered on a rough wooden table. My first sight was what I assumed to be a coffin lid propped against the wall, but it swung open—it was a door—and a woman stepped through. She was clad entirely in white, as pure as fresh snow, yet her face, though pale, radiated a warmth that snow could never possess. Her features were flawless, but it was her eyes that arrested my breath. All the vitality of her being was concentrated there, glowing with an inner light. It was as if approaching death illuminated her countenance, yet those eyes held the fierce, dark life of a nation. Within each pupil, a whole night-sky was condensed; stars flashed in the blackness, framed by an iris of eternal twilight. What such eyes truly *were*, only God could know; they seemed to pour directly from His own essence. Her face might have been a primeval perfection, but those living eyes were a continuous creation.

"Here is Mr. Vane, wife!" the raven announced.

"He is welcome," she answered. Her voice was low, rich, and gentle, resonant with treasures of immortal sound.

I stared, unable to speak.

"I knew you would be glad to see him," the raven added.

The woman remained by the door, refusing to advance further.

"Will he sleep?" she asked softly.

"I fear not," the raven replied. "He is neither weary nor burdened."

"Why, then, have you brought him?"

"I fear it may prove precipitate," he admitted.

"I do not quite understand," I said, a vague foreboding tightening my chest, though a desperate hope for escape lingered. "Surely a man must perform a day's work first!"

I looked into the woman's white face, my heart fluttering wildly. She met my gaze in silence.

"Let me return home first," I insisted, "and come back after I have found, made, invented, or discovered something!"

"He has not yet learned that the day begins with sleep!" the woman said, turning to her husband. "Tell him he must rest before he can accomplish anything."

"Men," the raven countered, "think so highly of having done things that they fall asleep upon them. They cannot even empty an egg without turning into the shell and lying down!"

His words pulled my gaze away from the woman. Suddenly, the raven vanished, replaced by the Librarian—the same slender, elderly man in a rusty black coat, broad-bodied and long-tailed. I had only seen his back before; now, for the first time, I saw his face. It was gaunt, revealing the sharp shape of bones beneath the skin, a testament to the skulls his former profession had made familiar. Yet, in truth, I had never seen a face so vivid, a look so keen and friendly. His pale blue eyes held a haze, as if they had wept much, yet they burned with intelligence.

"You knew I was not a raven!" he said, smiling.

"I knew you were Mr. Raven," I replied, "but somehow, I thought you a bird as well."

"What made you think me a bird?"

"You looked like one, and I saw you digging worms from the earth with your beak."

"And then?"

"Tossing them in the air."

"And then?"

"They grew into butterflies and flew away."

"Did you ever see a raven do that? I told you I was a sexton!"

"Does a sexton toss worms into the air and turn them into butterflies?"

"Yes."

"I never saw one do that!"

"You saw me do it!—But I remain the Librarian in your house, for I was never dismissed, nor did I ever relinquish the office. Now, I am the Librarian here as well."

"But you just told me you were the sexton here!"

"So I am. It is much the same profession. Except you are a true sexton, books are merely dead bodies to you, and a library is nothing but a catacomb!"

"You bewilder me!"

"That is perfectly fine!"

For a moment, he stood silent. The woman, motionless as a statue, stood guard by the coffin-door.

"Upon occasion," the sexton said finally, "it is more convenient to present one's bird-self to the front. Everyone, as you should know, possesses a beast-self, a bird-self, a stupid fish-self, yes, and even a creeping serpent-self—all of which require significant effort to crush. In truth, one also has a tree-self and a crystal-self, and countless others, all needing to find harmony. You can discern the nature of a man by observing which creature most frequently emerges to the front."

He turned to his wife, and I studied him more closely. He stood taller than usual, more erect. Like his wife, his face was very pale, his nose elegantly framing a beak that had retreated within it. His lips were thin and colorless, yet their curves were beautiful, quivering with a shadowy smile that blended humor, love, and pity.

"We are in need of food and drink, wife," he said. "We have traveled a long way."

"You know, husband," she answered, "we can give only to those who ask."

She turned her unchanging, radiant face toward me.

"Please give me something to eat, Mrs. Raven," I said, my voice trembling slightly. "And something—whatever you will—to quench my thirst."

"Your thirst must be greater before you can receive what will quench it," she replied. "But whatever I can give, I will gladly offer."

She moved to a cupboard in the wall, retrieved bread and wine, and placed them on the table.

We sat down to a perfect meal. As I ate, the bread and wine seemed to penetrate deeper than mere hunger and thirst could explain. Anxiety and discomfort dissolved, replaced by a profound sense of expectation.

I grew drowsy, and for the first time, I felt truly weary.

"I have earned neither food nor sleep, Mrs. Raven," I said. "Yet you have given me the former freely, and now I hope you will grant me the latter, for I sorely need it."

"Sleep is too precious a thing to be earned," the sexton interjected. "It must be given and accepted, for it is a necessity. However, it would be perilous to use this house merely as a halfway hostelry—for a night's rest only."

A wild-looking black cat leaped onto his knee. He patted it gently, as one soothes a child to sleep, yet the gesture seemed to me like patting down the sod upon a grave, lovingly, with an inward lullaby.

"Here is one of Mara's kittens!" he told his wife. "Will you give it a bit and send it out? She may need it!"

The woman took the kitten gently, offered it a scrap of bread, and exited with it, closing the door softly behind her.

"How then am I to make use of your hospitality?" I asked.

"By accepting it to the full," he answered.

"I do not understand."

"In this house, no one wakes of their own accord."

"Why?"

"Because no one anywhere ever wakes of their own accord. You cannot wake yourself any more than you can create yourself."

"Then perhaps you or Mrs. Raven would kindly call me!" I pleaded, the vague foreboding returning with renewed intensity.

"We cannot."

"How dare I then go to sleep?" I cried.

"If you wish to possess the rest of this house, you must not trouble yourself with waking. You must go to sleep heartily, altogether, and outright." My soul sank within me.

The sexton sat, gazing steadily into my face. His eyes seemed to whisper, *Will you not trust me?* I met his gaze and answered,

"I will."

"Then come," he said. "I will show you your couch."

As we rose, the woman entered. She took up the candle, turned toward the inner door, and led the way. I followed close behind her, and the sexton trailed in her wake.
