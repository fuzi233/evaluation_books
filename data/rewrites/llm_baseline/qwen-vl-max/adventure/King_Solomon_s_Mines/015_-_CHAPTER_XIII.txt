The three enemy columns advanced slowly, without haste or panic, like shadows creeping across the plain. When they were about five hundred yards away, the central force halted at the base of a narrow strip of open ground that stretched up into the hillside—a natural corridor leading toward our position. This was no accident. The pause allowed the flanking divisions to circle around us, forming a trap. Our own line curved like a horseshoe, its two ends pointing toward the town of Loo, and now the enemy intended to strike from all sides at once.

“Oh, for a Gatling!” groaned Good, his eyes fixed on the dense ranks below. “I’d clear that field in twenty minutes.”

“We don’t have one,” Sir Henry replied calmly, “so let’s not waste time wishing. But how about you, Quatermain? Try a shot—see if you can hit that tall fellow who seems to be in command. Two to one you miss, and I’ll pay you an even sovereign—honestly—if we ever get out of this alive.”

That stung me. I loaded my express rifle with a solid ball, waited until the officer stepped forward ten yards from his men—just far enough to get a better view, accompanied only by a single orderly—and then lay down, resting the rifle on a rock. The weapon was sighted for only three hundred and fifty yards, so I aimed halfway down the man’s neck, calculating the bullet would strike him in the chest. He stood perfectly still, giving me every chance. But something—excitement, wind, distance—I don’t know what—made the shot go wrong. I thought I had him dead center. Instead, when the smoke cleared, he stood unharmed, while his orderly lay sprawled three paces to the left, motionless.

The officer turned sharply and bolted back toward his troops, clearly terrified.

“Bravo, Quatermain!” shouted Good. “You scared him!”

It made me furious. There’s nothing worse than missing in public when your reputation is on the line. And I’m not proud of what happened next. Without thinking, I raised the rifle again, tracked the fleeing figure, and fired the second barrel.

He went down instantly—arms thrown upward, face smacking the earth. This time, there was no mistake. And though I should have been ashamed, I confess I felt a strange satisfaction. Not because I killed him—but because I hadn’t failed again.

The soldiers below erupted into cheers. They saw it as a sign—the magic of the white man working. The general’s own regiment broke ranks and fell back in confusion.

Sir Henry and Good picked up their rifles and began firing. Good worked his Winchester repeater with fierce efficiency, sending bullets into the mass before him. I took a few more shots, and together we managed to bring down six or eight men before they closed the distance.

Then came the sound we dreaded—an ominous roar from our right, followed by another from the left. The other divisions had joined the attack.

At the noise, the main force surged forward, advancing up the grassy ridge in slow trot, chanting a deep, rhythmic war song. We kept up a steady fire—Ignosi joining in whenever he could—but it was like throwing stones at a tidal wave. Men fell, but the tide kept coming.

They reached the foot of the hill, driving through our pickets placed among the rocks. Then they slowed—climbing uphill required effort, and they conserved breath. Our first line held at mid-slope, the second fifty yards behind, the third at the edge of the plateau.

Now they charged, shouting:
“*Twala! Twala! Chiele! Chiele!*”
(Twala! Twala! Smite! Smite!)

Our men answered:
“*Ignosi! Ignosi! Chiele! Chiele!*”

They were close now. *Tollas*—throwing knives—flew back and forth like deadly rain. Then, with a deafening cry, the battle met.

A sea of bodies surged and twisted. Warriors fell like leaves in autumn. But soon the weight of numbers told. Our first line bent backward, merging into the second. That line held fiercely, but it too gave way, driven up the slope. Within twenty minutes, the third line was engaged.

By then, the attackers were tired, bloodied, and thinning. Breaking through that final wall of spears proved impossible.

For a time, the lines swayed like waves—neither side gaining ground. Sir Henry watched, eyes blazing, then without a word dashed into the thick of it, followed by Good. I stayed where I was.

The enemy soldiers caught sight of him—a tall figure plunging into chaos—and roared:

“*Nanzia Incubu! Nanzia Unkungunklovo!*”
(Here is the Elephant!)
“*Chiele! Chiele!*”

From that moment, the tide turned. Inch by inch, the attackers were pushed back down the hill. By the time they retreated to their reserves, they were in disarray.

Just then, a messenger arrived: the left flank had been beaten back.

I started to breathe easier—until I saw our right-wing defenders being driven across the plain, chased by swarms of enemy soldiers. They’d broken through there.

Ignosi, standing beside me, read the situation instantly. He barked an order. The reserve regiment—the Greys—spread out in formation. Another command echoed down the line. Before I knew it, I was swept into a charge.

I ducked behind Ignosi’s broad back and stumbled forward, doing my best to look like I belonged. We plunged through scattered groups of our own men, who reformed behind us.

Then—chaos.

All I remember is the thunder of shields colliding, and suddenly a monstrous figure lunging at me, eyes bulging, spear glistening with blood.

But I didn’t falter. I threw myself flat just as he reached me. He couldn’t stop—he crashed over me, headfirst into the dirt. Before he could rise, I rose—and shot him from behind with my revolver.

Moments later, someone knocked me down. I blacked out.

When I woke, I was back at the koppie. Good knelt beside me, holding water in a gourd.

“How do you feel?” he asked, voice tight with concern.

I sat up, shook myself. “Pretty well. Thank you.”

“Thank God,” he whispered. “I thought you were gone when they carried you in.”

“I’m not done yet,” I said. “Just got a blow to the head. How did it go?”

“They’re repulsed on all sides—for now. Losses are terrible. We’ve lost two thousand. They must have lost three times that.” He pointed across the plain. “Look at that.”

Lines of men marched in fours. In the center of each group, borne on a hide tray with corner loops, lay wounded soldiers. Medicine men examined them quickly. If the wound wasn’t fatal, the man was taken away. If it was hopeless—then came the knife.

One doctor would pretend to examine, then swiftly slit an artery. The man died quietly, painlessly. It was done often—especially when the wound was in the torso. The broad Kukuana spears left wounds that couldn’t heal. Most victims were already unconscious. Others didn’t seem to notice the cut.

It was horrifying. I’ve never seen anything more chilling than watching those brave men put to rest by their own healers—except once, when I saw Swazis bury their wounded alive after a battle.

We turned away from the scene and walked to the other side of the koppie. Sir Henry stood there, axe still in hand, talking with Ignosi, Infadoos, and several chiefs.

“Thank God, here you are, Quatermain!” Sir Henry said. “I can’t make out what Ignosi wants. We beat off the attack, but Twala’s bringing in reinforcements. He’s planning to surround us—starve us out.”

“That’s bad,” I said.

“Yes. Especially since Infadoos says the spring has run dry.”

“It is true, my lord,” Infadoos confirmed. “The water fails. Before nightfall, we’ll all be thirsty. Listen, Macumazahn. You are wise. You’ve seen many wars. Tell us—what shall we do? Twala has brought fresh men to replace the fallen. But he has learned. The hawk did not expect the heron to be ready. But our beak pierced his breast. He fears to strike again. He will wait. He will coil around us like a snake around a buck. He will fight the fight of ‘sit down.’”

“I hear you,” I said.

“So, Macumazahn,” Infadoos continued, “we have no water. Little food. We must choose: starve like a lion in his den, try to break northward, or—” He gestured toward the enemy mass—“charge straight into Twala’s heart. Incubu, the great warrior—today he fought like a buffalo in a net. His axe sent Twala’s men down like corn before hail. Incubu says ‘Charge.’ But the Elephant always charges. Now—what says Macumazahn, the wily old fox who loves to bite from behind? The last word is Ignosi’s—king’s right to command war. But let us hear you, O Macumazahn, who watches by night. And him—the one with the transparent eye.”

“What say you, Ignosi?” I asked.

“Nay, my father,” Ignosi replied, dressed now in full war regalia, looking every inch a king. “Speak. Let me, who am but a child in wisdom beside you, listen.”

After consulting briefly with Good and Sir Henry, I gave my opinion: trapped, with failing water, our best hope was to attack—not wait. We must strike now—before wounds stiffened, before fear took hold. Otherwise, some captains might betray us to Twala.

My words were received with respect—something rare among these people. But the decision rested with Ignosi.

After a long silence, he spoke.

“Incubu, Macumazahn, Bougwan—brave white men, my friends. Infadoos, my uncle, chiefs—my heart is set. Today I strike at Twala. I stake my life, and yours, on this blow. Listen—this is how I will strike.”

He gestured at the curve of the hill and the tongue of green land running toward us.

“You see how the hill bends like a half-moon, and the plain stretches like a green tongue within it?”

“We see,” I said.

“Good. It is midday. The men eat and rest. When the sun turns westward, let your regiment, my uncle, advance with one other down the green tongue. Twala will see it and hurl his forces to crush it. But the space is narrow—only one regiment can attack at a time. So they will be destroyed one by one. All eyes will be fixed on this battle—like none ever seen.”

“And with you, my uncle,” Ignosi added, “will go Incubu, so that when Twala sees his axe flashing in the front rank of the Greys, his heart may falter.”

“And I,” Ignosi continued, “will follow with the second regiment—so that if you fall, a king remains. With me will go Macumazahn, the wise.”

“It is well, O king,” Infadoos said, calm as stone—though he was leading his men into certain death. These people feared no end when duty called.

“And while Twala’s army watches that fight,” Ignosi said, “one-third of our remaining men—about six thousand—will creep along the right horn of the hill and fall upon Twala’s left flank. Another third will move along the left horn and strike his right flank. When I see both horns ready to toss Twala, then I will charge with the rest—straight into his face. If fortune favors us, the day is ours. Before Night drives her black oxen from mountain to mountain, we shall sit in peace at Loo.”

He paused. “Now, let us eat and prepare. Infadoos, see that the plan is carried out. And stay—let my white father Bougwan go with the right horn. His shining eye will give courage to the captains.”

The plan was set in motion swiftly. Within an hour, rations were eaten, units formed, orders given. Eighteen thousand men stood ready—except for a guard left with the wounded.

Then Good approached Sir Henry and me.

“Good-bye, you fellows,” he said. “I’m off with the right wing. Came to shake hands—just in case we don’t meet again.”

We shook hands in silence. No words needed. There was emotion—Anglo-Saxon restraint barely holding it back.

“It’s a queer business,” Sir Henry said, voice trembling slightly. “I confess I don’t expect to see tomorrow’s sun. From what I understand, the Greys—where I’m going—are to fight until wiped out, so the wings can slip around unnoticed. Well—so be it. At least it will be a man’s death. Good-bye, old friend. God bless you. I hope you live to claim the diamonds. But if you do—take my advice: never deal with Pretenders again.”

In a flash, Good gripped our hands, then vanished into the ranks.

Infadoos led Sir Henry forward—into the front of the Greys. And with heavy heart, I followed Ignosi to my place in the second attacking regiment.
