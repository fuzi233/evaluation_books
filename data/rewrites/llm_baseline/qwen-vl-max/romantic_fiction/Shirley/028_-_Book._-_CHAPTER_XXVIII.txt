Shirley seemed to be in one of her most cheerful moods the morning after her evening with Sir Philip, and her spirits were evident as she descended for breakfast.

"Who would like to take a walk with me?" she asked brightly. "Isabella and Gertrude, will you join me?"

Such an invitation from Miss Keeldar to her cousins was so rare that they hesitated before accepting. But when their mother gave her approval, they quickly fetched their bonnets, and the three set off together.

Though they often walked separately, these young women rarely spent much time in each other’s company. Shirley preferred few companions among ladies—indeed, her truest affections were reserved for Mrs. Pryor and Caroline Helstone. She was polite, kind, even attentive toward her cousins, but conversation between them usually remained light and brief. Yet on this particular morning, she managed to captivate them with a warmth and liveliness that transformed even the most ordinary topics into something vivid and engaging. Her words sparkled with life, as if every sentence carried its own quiet fire.

What could have stirred such joy in her? There was no reason in the world around her. The day was dull—a fading autumn afternoon, overcast and grey. The paths through the woods were damp, the air heavy, the sky clouded. Yet inside Shirley’s heart seemed to glow with the sunlit brilliance of Italy, and in her eyes, clear and calm as English skies, danced a warmth that no season could dim.

As they neared Fieldhead on their return, Shirley paused to speak with her foreman, John. A delay of perhaps twenty minutes separated her from her cousins. During that time, she had spoken with him, then lingered by the gate, lost in thought. A call to luncheon brought her back into the house. She declined the meal and went upstairs.

“Isn’t Shirley coming to lunch?” Isabella asked. “She said she was hungry.”

An hour passed. Still, she did not appear. One of her cousins went to check on her. She found Shirley sitting at the foot of the bed, her head resting on her hand, her face pale, her expression thoughtful—almost sorrowful.

“You’re not unwell?” the cousin asked.

“A little sick,” Shirley replied quietly.

It was clear she was far changed from the woman who had walked beside them just hours earlier.

This shift—explained only by those three words—had no visible cause. It had come swiftly, in less than ten minutes, and it settled upon her like a shadow that refused to lift. At dinner, she spoke as usual, joined in conversation, stayed through the evening. When questioned again about her health, she insisted she was perfectly recovered. “Only a momentary faintness,” she said, brushing it aside. “Nothing worth thinking about.” Yet everyone sensed the difference—something deeper, quieter, more profound.

The next day, and the days after, that subtle change lingered. A stillness took hold of Shirley—her gaze, her movements, even her voice carried a new weight. The transformation wasn’t dramatic enough to warrant constant inquiry, but it was undeniable. It clung to her like a storm-cloud no wind could scatter. Soon, it became clear that drawing attention to it only troubled her. At first, she withdrew from conversation; if pressed, she met the concern with sharp pride. “Am I ill?” she’d ask, almost challenging. “I am not.”

“Is anything troubling you? Has something happened?”

She scoffed. “What do you mean by ‘spirits’? I have no spirits—black or white, blue or grey—to affect.”

“There must be something wrong. You’ve changed.”

“I suppose I have a right to change when I please. I know I’m plainer now. If I choose to grow less attractive, why should anyone fret?”

“There must be a reason. What is it?”

She turned away sharply. “Please leave me alone.”

Then she would try—desperately—to seem bright again. But when she failed, she would berate herself in private. Alone, she muttered harsh words: “Fool! Coward!” “Poltroon! If you must tremble, do it where no one sees!” She scolded herself: “How dare you show weakness? How dare you betray your fears? Shake them off. Rise above them. If you can’t, hide them.”

And hide them she did. She resumed her usual cheerfulness in public. But when exhaustion overwhelmed her, she fled—not to her room, where she refused to brood, but out into the wild open spaces, riding Zoë, her mare, across the moors. Long rides of half a day became her habit. Her uncle disapproved, but dared not protest. Even in her healthiest moments, Shirley’s temper was formidable; now, with her face thinner, her eyes hollowed, there was something in her silence and intensity that both moved and alarmed.

To strangers who commented on her altered appearance—unaware of the inner struggle—she repeated only one thing:

“I am perfectly well. Not a single ailment.”

And indeed, she must have been strong, to endure the weather she faced. Rain or shine, calm or gale, she rode daily over Stilbro' Moor, Tartar galloping beside her, relentless and tireless.

Twice, three times, curious eyes—those ever-watchful eyes of gossip—noticed that instead of turning back at Rushedge, the highest ridge of the moor, she continued all the way to the town. Observers soon learned she stopped at the home of Mr. Pearson Hall, a solicitor related to the vicar of Nunnely. For generations, his family had served the Keeldars as agents. Rumours began to spread. Some said Shirley was entangled in business matters involving Hollow's Mill—perhaps she had lost money and was forced to mortgage land. Others whispered she was to marry, and settlements were being arranged.

* * * * *

In the schoolroom, Mr. Moore waited for Henry Sympson to finish preparing his lesson.

"Henry, make haste. The afternoon is slipping away."

"Is it, sir?"

"Certainly. Are you nearly ready?"

"No."

"Not nearly ready?"

"I haven’t done a line."

Mr. Moore looked up. The boy’s tone held a strange note.

"The lesson isn’t difficult, Henry. If it is, bring it to me. We’ll work through it together."

"Mr. Moore, I can’t study."

"My boy, you’re unwell."

"Sir, I’m no worse physically than usual—but my heart is full."

"Close the book. Come here, Harry. Sit by the fire."

Harry limped forward. His tutor placed him in a chair. His lips trembled, his eyes filled with tears. He laid down his crutch, bowed his head, and wept.

"This pain isn’t physical, you say, Harry? You carry a grief. Tell me what it is."

"Sir, I’ve never known grief like this. I wish it could be lifted somehow—I can barely bear it."

"Perhaps talking will help. What caused it? Whom does it concern?"

"It’s Shirley, sir. It’s about Shirley."

"Does it? You think she’s changed?"

"Everyone who knows her thinks so—except perhaps you, Mr. Moore."

"Not seriously. I see only slight changes—ones that might fade in weeks. And she says she’s well. That counts for something."

"That’s exactly it. As long as she said she was well, I believed her. When I felt sad in her absence, I’d find joy again in her presence. Now… now…"

"Now, Harry? Has she said anything to you? You two were together in the garden this morning. I saw her speaking, and you listening. If Miss Keeldar has told you she’s ill—and asked you to keep it secret—don’t obey her. For her sake, tell everything. Speak, my boy."

"She say she’s ill? I believe, sir, if she were dying, she’d smile and say, 'Nothing ails me.'"

"What have you learned, then? What new truth?"

"I’ve learned she’s just made her will."

"Made her will?"

They sat in silence.

"She told me," Henry said after a pause, "quite calmly, as if it were nothing ominous—though I felt it was. She said I was the only person besides her solicitor, Pearson Hall, Mr. Helstone, and Mr. Yorke who knew about it. And to me, she wanted to explain its terms."

"Go on, Harry."

"'Because,' she said, looking down at me with her beautiful eyes—oh, they are beautiful, Mr. Moore! I love them! I love her! She is my star! Heaven must not claim her! She belongs here, in this world. Shirley is not an angel—she’s a woman, and she should live among men. Seraphs shall not have her! Mr. Moore, if one of the sons of God, with wings wide as the sky and bright as the sea, saw how fair she was and came to claim her, I would stand against him—even though I’m only a boy and crippled!'"

"Henry Sympson, go on when I tell you."

"'Because,' she said, 'if I died before you, Harry, and made no will, all my property would pass to you. But I don’t intend that—though your father would like it. But you,' she said, 'will inherit your father’s estate—larger than Fieldhead. Your sisters will get nothing, so I’ve left them some money, though I don’t love them both as much as I love one lock of your hair.' She called me her 'darling,' let me kiss her. Then she told me she’d left Caroline Helstone a sum too. This manor house—with its books and furniture—she’s given to me, because she doesn-’t want to take the old family seat from her own blood. And the rest—about twelve thousand pounds, beyond the legacies—she’s left not to me, since I’m already rich, but to a good man who would use it wisely. A man gentle and brave, strong and merciful—one who may not claim piety, but who has true faith. He visits the fatherless and widows in distress, keeps himself pure from the world. Then she asked, 'Do you approve, Harry?' I couldn’t answer. My tears choked me—as they do now.'"

Mr. Moore let the boy gather himself. Then he asked, "What else did she say?"

"After I agreed, she said I was generous, and proud of me. 'Now,' she added, 'if anything happens, you’ll know what to tell Malice when she whispers lies—suggesting I wronged you, that I didn’t love you. You’ll know I _did_ love you, Harry—more than any sister ever could. My treasure.' Mr. Moore, when I remember her voice, her look—my heart beats as if it would break. She may go to heaven before me—if God commands it, she must. But the rest of my life—short as it will be, and I’m glad of that now—will be a swift, purposeful journey along the path her footsteps have marked. I thought to lie in the Keeldar vault before her. If not, lay my coffin beside hers."

Moore answered with quiet gravity, a stark contrast to the boy’s passion.

"You’re both mistaken—you harm each other. Youth, once touched by fear, imagines darkness will last forever. Its first sorrow feels like a lifetime. What else did she say?"

"We discussed a few family matters."

"I’d like to hear them."

"But, Mr. Moore, you’re smiling. I couldn’t smile seeing Shirley like that."

"My boy, I’m neither poetic nor sentimental. I see things clearly. You don’t yet. Tell me these points."

"Just that she asked if I considered myself more Keeldar or Sympson. I said, 'Keeldar to the core of my heart and marrow of my bones.' She said she was glad—for besides her, I was the only Keeldar left in England. Then we agreed on a few things."

"Well?"

"If I inherit my father’s estate and her house, I’m to take the name Keeldar and make Fieldhead my home. Henry Shirley Keeldar—I said I’d be called that, and I will. Her name and her manor are ancient. Sympson and Sympson Grove are of yesterday."

"Come, neither of you are going to heaven yet. I have high hopes for you both—proud, ambitious souls. Now, what conclusion do you draw from all this?"

"That Shirley thinks she’s going to die."

"She mentioned her health?"

"Not once. But I swear she’s wasting. Her hands are thin, her cheeks hollow."

"Does she complain to your mother or sisters?"

"Never. She laughs at them when they ask. Mr. Moore, she’s extraordinary—so fair, so girlish—yet not masculine, not fierce—but towering above help and sympathy."

"Do you know where she is now, Henry? In the house—or riding?"

"Surely not out, sir. It’s raining hard."

"True. But that doesn’t mean she isn’t riding over Rushedge. Lately, weather has never stopped her."

"You remember, sir, how stormy it was last Wednesday? So wild she wouldn’t let Zoë be saddled. Yet she walked nearly to Nunnely, facing the gale on foot. I asked her if she feared cold. 'Not I,' she said. 'It would be too much luck. I don’t know, Harry, but the best thing that could happen to me would be to catch a fever and pass on like any Christian.' She’s reckless, sir."

"Reckless indeed. Go and find her. If you can, speak to her privately and ask her to come here for a moment."

"Yes, sir."

He grabbed his crutch and hurried away.

"Harry!"

He returned.

"Don’t deliver it formally. Say it as you once would have said a simple summons to the schoolroom."

"I understand, sir. She’ll be more likely to come."

"And, Harry—"

"Yes, sir?"

"I’ll call you when I need you. Until then, you’re excused from lessons."

He left. Moore stood alone, rising from his desk.

"I can be cool and aloof with Henry," he murmured. "I can mock his fears, look down on his passion from my height. To him, I can pretend they’re both children. But can I keep that role with her? I’ve known moments when I nearly lost it—when confusion and submission threatened to crush me. My tongue faltered. I almost let the mask fall. I stood before her—no longer master, but something else. I pray I never play such a fool. It’s good for Sir Philip Nunnely to blush when he meets her eye. He may submit. He may even tremble when he touches her hand—without shame. But if one of her farmers showed such feeling, he’d need a straitjacket. So far, I’ve held myself. She’s sat near me, and I’ve not shaken—only my desk. I’ve met her looks and smiles like a tutor, as I am. I’ve never touched her hand—never faced that test. I am not her servant, nor her serf. But I am poor, and I must guard my dignity. What did she mean by those cold people who turn flesh to marble? It pleased me—though I won’t admit why. I never examine her words or face. If I did, I’d forget reason and believe in romance. A secret ecstasy flares through me sometimes. I won’t encourage it. I won’t remember it. I resolve to keep my right to say, as Paul did, 'I am not mad, but speak truth and soberness.'"

He paused, listening.

"Will she come? How will she receive the message? Naively? With scorn? Like a child or a queen? Both exist in her nature."

"If she comes, what shall I say? How justify my request? Should I apologize? I could humbly. But would that place us rightly? I must remain the teacher—otherwise—"

A door opened.

Miss Keeldar entered. She had been sewing when Henry found her. She brought her work with her. She had not ridden today. She wore her indoor dress and silk apron. Not a warrior from the fields, but a quiet domestic figure from the hearth. Moore had her at an advantage. He should have addressed her solemnly, with rigid formality. Perhaps he would have—had she looked defiant. But her expression held none of that. A soft, youthful shyness shaded her eyes, tinged her cheeks. He stood silent.

She stopped halfway between the door and his desk.

"Did you want me, sir?" she asked.

"I ventured, Miss Keeldar, to send for you—to request a brief interview."

She waited, needle in hand.

"Well, sir," she said without lifting her eyes, "what about?"

"Sit down first. The matter I wish to discuss is serious. I may not have the right to raise it. I may need to apologize. But the liberty I’ve taken comes from a conversation with Henry. He is distressed about your health—your friends are too. I wish to speak of your health."

"I am quite well," she said simply.

"Yet you’ve changed."

"That concerns only myself. We all change."

"Will you sit?"

She did.

"Formerly, Miss Keeldar, I had some influence over you. Do I now? May I assume you do not consider my words presumptuous?"

"Let me read French, Mr. Moore—or even Latin. Let us end this talk about health."

"No. It’s time for discussion."

"Discuss away—but don’t choose me as your subject. I am healthy."

"Do you not think it wrong to repeat what is fundamentally untrue?"

"I say I am well. No cough, no pain, no fever."

"Is that the whole truth?"

"The direct truth."

Moore studied her closely.

"I see no sign of disease," he said. "But why are you changed?"

"_Am_ I changed?"

"We’ll test it."

"How?"

"Do you sleep as you used to?"

"No—but not because I’m ill."

"Do you eat as you once did?"

"No—but not because I’m ill."

"You remember this ring on my watch-chain? It belonged to my mother. Too small for my little finger. You’ve often taken it playfully. It fit your forefinger. Try it now."

She allowed the test. The ring slipped from her slender hand. Moore picked it up and fastened it back. A flush crept across his brow. Shirley said again, "It’s not because I’m ill."

"Not only have you lost sleep, appetite, and flesh," Moore continued, "but your spirit is low. There’s a nervous tension in your eyes, a restlessness in your manner. These were not yours before."

"Mr. Moore, let us stop here. You’re right—I am nervous. Now, talk of something else. Look at this rain—steady, pouring!"

"_You_ nervous? Yes. And if Miss Keeldar is nervous, it’s not without cause. Let me reach it. Let me look closer. The illness is not physical. I suspected that. It began suddenly. I know the day. I noticed the change. Your pain is mental."

"Not at all. Just nervous. Please—dismiss the topic."

"When it’s exhausted. Not before. Nervous fears should be shared, so they may vanish. I wish I could persuade you to speak freely. I believe confession, in your case, would be half-cure."

"No," she said abruptly. "I wish it were true—but I fear it’s not."

She paused, then leaned her head on her hand, elbow on the table. Moore felt he had finally gained ground. She was serious. Her willingness to acknowledge unease implied a surrender of sorts. She could no longer deny that something stirred within her.

He waited. Once, his lips moved to speak—but he held back. Shirley raised her eyes to his. Had he shown emotion, she might have retreated. But he remained calm, steady, trustworthy.

"I’d rather tell _you_ than my aunt, or my cousins, or my uncle," she said. "They’d make such a fuss. I dread that—the alarm, the commotion, the _éclat_. Honestly, I never liked being the center of a little domestic storm. You can handle a shock—can’t you?"

"A great one, if needed."

Not a muscle twitched. Yet his heart beat fiercely in his chest. What would she reveal? Was disaster already done?

"Had I thought it right to come to you," she continued, "I would never have kept it hidden. I’d have told you at once, and asked your advice."

"Why wasn’t it right to come to me?"

She looked at him—then smiled faintly.

"Because I didn’t know how."
