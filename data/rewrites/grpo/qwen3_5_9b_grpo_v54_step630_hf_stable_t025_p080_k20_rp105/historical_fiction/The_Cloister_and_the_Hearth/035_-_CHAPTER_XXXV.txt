Gerard opened his eyes to find Denys leaning over him, his face etched with a quiet worry. "It is you for sleeping!" Denys exclaimed, glancing toward the window where the sun hung high in the sky. "'Tis well past noon."

"It was a blessed slumber," Gerard replied, his voice thick with lingering awe. "Methinks Heaven itself granted it to me, drawing a veil between my spirit and that dreadful nightmare. To sit here, alive and breathing, feels like a miracle. The terror of the vision still clings to me."

"Ay, lad," Denys said gently, patting his shoulder. "That is the only wise way to view such things once the dust has settled; they are but shadows, fleeting and false. Eat your breakfast, and the memory will fade. Besides, I promised to escort you to the town by midday to meet with his Worship."

Gerard dipped a piece of coarse rye bread into a cup of red wine and consumed it quickly to banish the last tremors of his dream. He then walked with Denys back over the site of the recent skirmish, shuddering at the echoes of violence, before they set out again. As they traveled, Gerard kept peering anxiously through the hedgerows, expecting ambushes that never came, until they finally reached the small Burgundian town.

Denys led him straight to "The White Hart."

"No fear of cut-throats here," Denys assured him, ushering him inside. "I have known this landlord for years; he is a burgess and likely serves as bailiff. It was here I intended to rest last night, but we lost time, and darkness overtook us—and..."

"And you saw a woman at the door," Gerard interrupted, his eyes lighting up. "She proved wiser than a Jeanneton, telling us there was no danger."

"Why, what saved our lives if not that woman? She risked her own safety to do it."

"That is true, Denys," Gerard said, his heart swelling with gratitude. "Though I hold little regard for women in general, I long to thank this poor soul and reward her handsomely, even if I must part with every coin in my purse to do so. Do you not agree?"

"Parbleu," Denys nodded vigorously.

"Where might we find her?"

"Perhaps the alderman can tell us. We must seek him out first."

The alderman received them with a countenance so singular and inexplicable that it seemed almost theatrical. Yet, after a brief pause for reflection, a grim smile softened his features. He proceeded to interrogate Gerard in detail, noting every answer with a quill. Once finished, he declared that they must remain in the town until the thieves were tried, warning them that failure to appear would result in fines and imprisonment. They stared at him, bewildered by the severity.

"However," the alderman continued, "it will not be long; the culprits were caught red-handed." He added casually, "And you know, in any case, you could not leave this place this week."

Denys stared at the remark, confused, while Gerard smiled inwardly at the old gentleman's simplicity. To him, the idea that a provincial town of Burgundy held any attraction strong enough to detain him from Rome and Margaret seemed a delightful delusion.

Now, he turned his attention to the matter nearest both their hearts.

"Your Worship," Gerard said earnestly, "we cannot find our benefactress within the town."

"Nay, but who is your benefactress?" the alderman asked.

"Who? Why, the good girl who came to you by night and saved our lives at the peril of her own existence," Gerard explained, his voice trembling with emotion. "Oh, sir, our hearts burn within us to thank and bless her. Where is she?"
