Arthur Pendennis, ever the model of candour and open-heartedness—especially when it came to his pal Warrington—was oddly tight-lipped about one particular episode from his recent trip to Tunbridge Wells. He chatted freely about bumping into his old tutor Smirke, his wife’s latest antics, the architectural splendour of his Anglo-Norman church, and his dramatic departure from Clapham to Rome. But when Warrington asked about Blanche? Suddenly Arthur became a master of evasion.

“Oh, she’s a nice little thing,” he’d say, scratching his head like a man who’d just forgotten the name of a distant cousin. “Clever, too. With proper guidance, she might even make a decent wife someday. But no—no marriage plans for me. My romantic days are over. I’m perfectly content with my current state of affairs.” And so forth.

Meanwhile, in the postbox at Lamb Court, Temple, arrived a steady stream of tiny satin envelopes, sealed with an elegant cipher that looked suspiciously like a fancy doodle by someone who’d spent too long practicing calligraphy after a bottle of sherry. If Warrington had been half as curious as he was judgmental, he might’ve noticed these letters were addressed to someone whose initials were B.A. — which, if you’re into cryptic romance, could stand for “Blanche Amory” or “Best Advice.” Either way, Mr. Pen was clearly corresponding with a lady of mystery.

And how did he reply? Oh, with the full panoply of gallant letter-writing: witty banter, gossip from town, clever quips, and—most dangerously—little verses. Poems! Yes, the very same love poems that later graced the pages of *Roseleaves*, that delightful annual edited by Lady Violet Lebas and illustrated with portraits of noblewomen by the legendary Pinkney. These weren’t written in some poetic fever dream—they were first sent to Blanche via post, likely wrapped around a cracker and delivered by a nervous boy with a bad case of hiccups.

“Poetry’s all well and good,” said old Major Pendennis, catching his nephew scribbling one of these tender effusions at the Club while waiting for dinner. “But mind, Pen, don’t go writing anything that’ll come back to haunt you. Letters can be dangerous things. I never wrote a single line that could incriminate me. And believe me, I’ve had experience with women.”

The Major, now slightly more garrulous than dignified, proceeded to share a series of cautionary tales—some of which were probably true, others possibly embellished by years of wine and regret. Like the time young Spoony wrote overly passionate verses to widow Naylor, only to have her brother, Colonel Flint, show up unannounced and demand he marry her immediately—resulting in a union with a woman old enough to be his grandmother. Or the time Louisa Salter, having won Sir John Bird’s heart, had her letters discovered by Hopwood of the Blues, leading to a sudden withdrawal and eventual marriage to Miss Stickney of Lyme Regis—a woman so plain she once caused a pigeon to fly away mid-coo.

Pen laughed politely, blushing slightly—perhaps not because of his uncle’s advice, but because he already knew what he was doing wrong. In fact, he was being *too* careful. His letters to Blanche were carefully worded, polite, and utterly safe. No confessions. No declarations. Just charming nonsense that could be read as flirtation or friendship, depending on your mood.

“I remember the lesson I learned in Lady Mirabel’s affair,” Arthur said, feigning humility. “I’m not going to be caught again, Uncle.”

Old Pendennis puffed out his chest like a satisfied hen. “Good lad! Progress! You’re becoming a man of the world!”

If Warrington had been consulted, he would’ve scoffed. “You know what?” he’d say, “the boy’s clumsy, honest letters are better than this polished deceit. Real love doesn’t hide behind euphemisms. A coward—or a knave—uses subterfuge. But Pen? He’s guilty. So he keeps quiet.”

Meanwhile, Captain Strong—once the sturdy pillar of Shepherd’s Inn—was facing a crisis of his own. After Altamont left for his foreign tour, Sir Francis Clavering retired to the country, leaving Strong alone to face the music. When Altamont offered him a loan before departing, Strong refused—on principle. A noble gesture, yes, but one that soon came back to bite him in the posterior.

He regretted it instantly. Why refuse kindness? Why reject water from a friendly hand just because it was slightly muddy? Strong’s conscience gnawed at him. Had he taken Altamont’s money, it might’ve ended up in a gambling den anyway. Instead, it stayed in Altamont’s pocket—and Strong’s financial situation began to resemble a sinking ship.

Rumours spread among tradesmen: “Strong and Clavering have split. Strong’s paper is worthless.” The once-trusted Captain found himself besieged. Tailors, bootmakers, pastry cooks—all descended upon Shepherd’s Inn, bills in hand, faces like thunder. One particularly persistent pastry cook even brought his entire staff to hold a daily levee on the stairs.

Strong, broke and cornered, retreated into his chambers—the Englishman’s castle, as they say. He barred both doors, peeked through the letter-box curtain like a fox in a burrow, and watched as duns banged their fists and cursed in vain. It was comedy gold: furious clerks, grim-faced collectors, all thwarted by a single locked door.

But when the enemies couldn’t reach him, they waited. And waited. And eventually, the Chevalier grew lonely.

Luckily, he wasn’t entirely alone. He had allies. Huxter and Miss Fanny Bolton were his secret lifelines. When creditors lurked outside, Fanny’s sisters would let out a special cry—like a confused alpine yodel—alerting the garrison. When Fanny and Huxter arrived, they sang the same tune at the door, and the trapdoor opened. Supplies came in: bread, porter, moral support. Strong would emerge, smile, and spend a pleasant evening surrounded by loyal friends.

And then there was the escape route. As previously mentioned, Strong’s kitchen window was close to Costigan’s room next door. A leaden pipe connected the two buildings. One day, Strong joked to Altamont, “If things get bad, I can climb across.” Altamont laughed. “Don’t tell Costigan. He’s got enough duns already.”

But when the siege intensified, Strong used the pipe. One afternoon, he burst into Costigan’s room like a pirate raiding a tea party. “Enemy on the staircase!” he announced, jolly as ever. “I’m taking the scenic route!”

While Marks’s men waited in the hall, Strong dined at the Albion, saw the play, and returned at midnight—much to the astonishment of Mrs. Bolton and Fanny, who hadn’t seen him leave.

For weeks, Strong held out. But morale dipped. Clavering, the supposed partner, remained silent. Letter after letter went unanswered. Not even a five-pound note—which, at the time, would’ve been a fortune—came through. Strong was ready to give up. “I was thinking of ending it all,” he confessed later. “Another week, and I’d have done it.”

Then—miracle of miracles—Altamont dropped from heaven.

“No, not literally,” said Altamont. “I came from Baden-Baden. Had a lucky month.”

“Well, sir,” said Strong, eyes shining, “he paid off every bill. Like a man. Like a gentleman.”

“And I shall buy a bottle of claret for the company,” said Altamont, blushing slightly. “Waiter! A magnum! Let’s drink to good fellows standing by each other. That’s my sentiment, Pendennis—even if I don’t like your name.”

“No?” asked Arthur.

Strong kicked Altamont under the table.

“Why?” asked Arthur.

Altamont, flushed and excited, raised his glass. “Because he was a gentleman. And that’s all that matters. We’re all gentlemen.”

They met at Richmond, where Pendennis joined them for dinner. Both Strong and Altamont were in high spirits—drunk, perhaps, but full of stories. Strong recounted his siege with theatrical flair: the sheriff’s officers’ complaints, Fanny’s signals, Costigan’s horrified reaction when the Chevalier burst in, and finally, Altamont’s divine rescue.

“It’s nothing,” said Altamont modestly. “Just luck. I won at Baden-Baden. I’ve got a system. I’ll make everyone rich. I’ll take Strong with me. He’ll play for me. I trust him more than myself—or Bloundell-Bloundell, or that Italian who ‘stands in.’”

As he emptied his second bottle, Altamont launched into a detailed explanation of his “infallible” gambling system. It involved coolness, capital, and a wheel that never lied. He planned to form a bank, invest everything, and win big. “I’ll put in for Fanny,” he declared suddenly. “She lent Ned two pounds! Didn’t she, Ned?”

“She did,” said Strong proudly.

“Let’s drink to her!”

Arthur pledged the toast with genuine warmth.

Later, Arthur told his uncle about Altamont.

“Altamont?” said the Major. “There’s Lord Westport’s son.”

“No,” said Arthur, laughing. “The guy who came in tipsy to Clavering’s dining room. Said he didn’t like my name, though he thought I was a good fellow.”

“I don’t know any Altamont,” said the Major firmly. “And I suggest you avoid him.”

Arthur chuckled. “He’s quitting the country to make his fortune with a gambling system. He’s partnering with Bloundell, and bringing Strong along as aide-de-camp. What ties Strong and Clavering together, I wonder?”

“Ah,” said the Major, lowering his voice. “I suspect Clavering has secrets. And if he doesn’t, well—that’s a lesson: keep yourself straight, boy. Don’t give anyone power over you.”

Arthur smiled. “But you’ve got power over Clavering, haven’t you? Otherwise, why would he give me that parliamentary seat?”

“Clavering thinks he’s unfit for Parliament,” said the Major. “And he’s right. He could give it to anyone. Why should you be squeamish when the highest nobles do it without blinking?”

Arthur accepted the logic—not because he believed it, but because he wanted to. Because sometimes we justify our choices not because they’re right, but because we like them. And that’s human.

At Tunbridge, Arthur regaled Miss Blanche with the tale of Strong’s imprisonment and Altamont’s heroic rescue. He praised Fanny’s generosity and Altamont’s admiration for her.

Miss Blanche, naturally, was intrigued—and mildly jealous.

Arthur, in their sweet rural drives and moonlit walks, couldn’t resist sharing the story. He described it with his usual satirical twist: how women recover from first loves easily, how emotions shift like fashion trends. Poor Fanny, he said, was proof: she’d loved deeply, suffered terribly, and now was happily engaged to Mr. Huxter—whom Arthur described as “a man of no charm, no wit, no looks, but immense loyalty.”

“You wicked creature,” said Blanche. “Are you jealous of Huxter?”

Arthur’s cheeks flushed—though he denied it.

“Not at all,” he said. “But I wish Fanny had ended up with someone better. I hate stories that end with a man like Huxter. Is life a compromise? Is love a surrender? Should Cupid’s quest end with a man who smells of tobacco and medicine?”

“Perhaps,” said Blanche, “you’re just afraid you’re not enough.”

Arthur sighed. “I don’t think I have much heart. But I made my move at eighteen. I lit my lamp and searched for Cupid. What did I find? A vulgar dancing-woman. I failed. But at least I failed before marriage.”

“Merci du choix,” said Blanche, curtseying.

“Look,” said Arthur, taking her hand, “at least I don’t flatter. I don’t lie. I don’t pretend to be a hero. I don’t claim you’re the most beautiful, the greatest poetess, or the tallest woman in England—like that giantess we saw at the fair.”

“But I think you’re very good-looking,” said Blanche.

“Thank you,” said Arthur.

“I think you sing beautifully. I know you’re clever. I hope you’re kind. And I believe you’ll be companionable.”

And so they sat, under the stars, two people who knew the truth of love: it’s messy, uncertain, and often ends with someone smelling of tobacco. But still, they chose to stay.
