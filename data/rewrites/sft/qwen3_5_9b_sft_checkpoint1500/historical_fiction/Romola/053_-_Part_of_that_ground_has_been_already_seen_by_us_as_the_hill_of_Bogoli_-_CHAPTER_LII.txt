To Romola, the chaotic whirlwind of the Carnival offered little more than a shadowy duty: the care of her cousin Brigida, whose frailty and surrender to age required a gentle, steady hand. Yet, beneath this domestic quietude, the coming Lenten season loomed like a storm cloud, charging her mind with a feverish intensity.

Bernardo del Nero had been elected Gonfaloniere. Through immense effort, the Medicean faction had secured a fragile victory, a triumph that deepened Romola's foreboding. She sensed a shadowy machinery at work, a scheme brewing in the silence of the next two months that threatened to bloom either into catastrophic success or ruinous betrayal. With every gray dawn that crept into her chamber, the same chilling dread returned, a ghost that clung to her as she traversed the cobblestones en route to the early sermon in the Duomo. There, however, amidst the throng, the cold shiver faded, replaced by the adrenaline of battle—the same way soldiers cease to fear death when the clash of steel begins.

Inside the vast cathedral, Romola stood within a tempest of passion that echoed far beyond Florence's walls. Savonarola preached the final series of Lenten sermons permitted before exile, aware that excommunication hung like a sword above his head. He had crossed the Rubicon, ready to defy it. His words were a terrible mirror, reflecting the rot of the Church without sugar-coating or euphemisms. With growing conviction, he heralded the advent of renovation—a time of universal uprising against corruption. His gaze flickered between two futures: sometimes he envisioned himself as the trumpet of a glorious revolution, shaking the corpse of the Church into new life; at others, he saw only the path to martyrdom, viewing this mortal coil merely as a watch until the dawn after death.

Even the minds of the skeptical, like Machiavelli, who read the Friar's character through a lens devoid of nobility, could not ignore the gravity of his stance. But for Romola, whose kinship with his fervor convinced her of his genuine magnanimity, the crisis felt intimately personal. It infused her daily labors with a transcendent glow. Famine still gnawed at Florence, distress grew weary of bearing, and plague hovered on the horizon. Walking among the sick, the starving, and the murmuring crowds, Romola found solace not in pity alone, but in the belief of a heroism striving toward sublime ends. Her acts of compassion, though feeble like dew upon weeds, served as preparation for a harvest yet unseen.

Yet, the majestic music of the Friar's voice contained dissonant notes. From those initial days of radiant hope—when he preached peace, charity, and the coming of a French deliverer—a sharp turn had occurred. Political intrigue proved too stubborn to forget; the dream of French salvation had collapsed, turning into a wound. Hostility, both petty and monumental, now besieged the Prophet with renewed vigor.

Consequently, the spirit of contention seeped into his sermons. Urged by the clamor of the crowd, he shifted from denying the desire for miracles to asserting that Divine power would soon validate his prophecy through a sign. As his moods swayed rapidly between visions of triumphant good and the grim reality of prevailing evil, his threats against tyrants and corrupt clergy gathered momentum, fueled by personal exasperation and indignant zeal. In the career of a great orator yielding to the heat of the moment, the internal war between selfish and unselfish emotions is laid bare, written in fire.

While these jarring tones troubled Romola, they resonated profoundly with another listener: Baldassarre. Having discovered the Frate's return, he attended frequently, drinking in the threats of a voice that sounded like justice itself. He watched Romola with a predator's patience, hoping for a shift in circumstances—or perhaps a softening in her spirit—that would justify an interview. Twice their eyes had met in the nave; once, his dark gaze had fixed upon hers. She wished to avoid him, yet she awaited his reappearance like a harbinger of doom. Whatever revelation he held was now a secondary worry compared to the crushing weight of the present.

As the stirring Lent drew to a close, April neared its end, and nothing materialized to fulfill Romola's premonitions. Rumors swirled from Rome regarding Piero de' Medici, but Bernardo's tenure was nearing expiration. It was only on the twenty-seventh, as she ventured out on her errands of mercy, that fate intervened. A messenger from Camilla Rucellai, one of Florence's foremost "seers," demanded her immediate presence on matters of the highest urgency.

Romola recoiled. She despised the shrill volubility of these illuminated women, and Camilla specifically terrified her due to rumors of revelations hostile to Bernardo. Her instinct screamed refusal, a desire to block out knowledge that would only heavy her burdens. Yet, habit had forged a chain around her impulses; she had learned to obey external claims rather than follow her own rebellious heart. Reproaching herself for cowardice, she complied and walked straight to Camilla.

She found the nervous, grey-haired woman in a chamber dressed like a convent cell. Thin fingers gripped Romola as she sat, and a loud whisper struck her like a blow, causing a physical shrinking that made sitting impossible. Camilla possessed a vision, revealed to her by Romola's Angel: secrets concerning her godfather, Bernardo, which, if exposed, could save the Republic. As she spoke, her voice climbed in pitch, urging Romola to obey her Angel and sever ties with the enemy of God.

Romola's nature was massive and slow-moving, usually calm and self-possessed, but she held a constitutional hatred for the shallow excitability of women like Camilla, whose faculties seemed entirely consumed by fantasy. Before Camilla finished, Romola sprang up, attempting to wrench her arm free. It was useless. The prophetess held on like a crab, and Romola's resistance only spurred her into a shrill declaration of further visions to corroborate the first. Christ Himself had appeared, commanding her to send orders to certain officials to cast Bernardo del Nero from the window of the Palazzo Vecchio. Even Fra Girolamo, she claimed, knew of it and dared not deny its divine origin.

"And since then," Camilla cried, her treble voice straining upward, wild eyes fixed on Romola's face, "the Blessed Infant has placed a wafer of sweetness on my tongue, token of His pleasure!"

"Let me go!" Romola roared, her voice deep with fury. "God grant you are mad! Else, you are detestably wicked!"

Her violent struggle finally broke Camilla's grip. Wrenching free, Romola fled the room, rushing down the street without pause until she reached the church of the Badia. She slipped behind the curtain under the ancient stone arch, finding a sanctuary shut off from the noise and haste of the world, a place where thoughts of eternal peace persisted amidst turmoil.

Kneeling on the step before Filippino Lippi's serene Virgin, visiting Saint Bernard, she waited for the inner tumult to subside. The thought that pressed hardest on her was Camilla's claim to know Savonarola's countenance of her wicked folly. Romola did not believe the Friar had sanctioned the murder of Bernardo as a divine command; she was certain there was falsehood or error in that charge. Savonarola had grown severe in his views on resisting malcontents, yet strict law and order were the bedrock of his politics. If he truly foresaw the fatal effects of such visions, and distrusted spirit-seeing women so deeply, why did he not publicly denounce these revelations that cast darkness over the Supreme Will?

The answer tore at her with painful clarity: he was fettered internally by the consciousness that such revelations were inseparable from his own visions, and externally by the fear of raising a cry against himself, even among his own party, as a suppressor of Divine inspiration shared with his confidential seer, Fra Salvestro.

Kneeling with her face buried, Romola endured a sickening moment. The enthusiasm that had been her only energy now seemed bound up with vain dreams and willful blindness. Her mind drifted back to the sturdy worldly sense, the dignified prudence, and the untheoretic virtues of her godfather, who might be treated like Agag for holding a more restricted form of government and refusing to forget old ties to the banished Medici. But with this thought arose the presentiment of a plot to restore the Medici. Then, again, she felt the popular party was half-justified in its suspicion. To keep Florence pure and avert vicious rule was a sacred cause, and the Frate was right there. But at this moment, her assent was given unwillingly. Her heart recoiled from a righteousness allied to so much narrowness, a judgment that measured men by superficial symbols rather than the humanity within.

Affection and respect clung to her godfather, linking her to the memories of her father, who also opposed the division of men into sheep and goats based on political or religious markers. Ideas, no matter how powerful, remain weak agents unless dissolved in the solvent of feeling. The great struggle of thought is always foreshadowed in the struggle of the affections, seeking justification for love and hope.
