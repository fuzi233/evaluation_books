It was only when enemy war galleys ambushed us at the gulf’s narrow mouth, fighting like feral beasts just to drag me into their custody, that I finally grasped how high my stakes ran as a new recruit for Empress Phorenice. Up to that point I’d barely spared my own significance a second thought. But that carefully laid trap, that unhinged ferocity to get their hands on me? Even the most blind fool could read the writing on the waves: I was meant to be the linchpin of Atlantis’s fate.
If enemies had been watching for our arrival, Phorenice’s scouts had beaten them to the punch. Perched high on the coastal mountain ridgeline, they spotted our galley first, then bolted overland for the capital. That twisting arm of the sea leading to Atlantis’s gates narrows to river width in spots, choked by old volcanic lava flows, then yawns open for half a day’s sail into glassy lake basins where far shores blur into haze. A courier who knew the safe paths across the flats, reeking swamps, and smoking volcanic hills could outrun even the fastest, most brutally whipped oar galley—if he avoided lava streams, roving predators, and flash floods.
Phorenice didn’t skimp on speed. She sent twenty runners to that headland overlooking the gulf mouth, each taking a separate overland route, ordered to prioritize speed over every last precaution. Seven of them made it back to the capital completely unharmed, three more confirmed to have survived their perils, meaning the entire city knew we were coming long before we sighted the sacred peak. We, though, had no clue we were expected, chugging along desperate to end the voyage as fast as possible. The rowing slaves were mostly savage captives from Europe, their unwashed stench thick enough to choke on, and a constant fine grit pouring off an erupting inland volcano bit at our eyes and throats the whole way. Even with all my priest-learned stoicism, plain human curiosity burned hot in me: what kind of reception would I get? Formal arrest and a public trial for defying the old mad priesthood of the Yucatan colony? Quiet anonymity, followed by a quiet palace summons a few weeks later? I never could have guessed the reality.
Dawn broke one morning, and there it was: the sacred mountain’s jagged peak, rimmed with the eternal glow of its summit fires, rising straight behind Atlantis. The boatswains’ whips cracked twice as loud, driving the gasping slaves to heave on their oars for a final sprint. The wind was dead against us, no sails could be raised, but we cut through the water fast, the mountain’s slopes growing longer, until the tips of the city’s hundred pyramids and the spires of its tallest towers began to peek over the horizon, bobbing like they were floating on the sunlit water. It had been twenty years since I’d last seen Atlantis, and my throat tightened. Those oar strokes didn’t just move the galley forward—they were pulling me home.
I picked out landmarks one by one as the city grew clear: the temple where I’d been initiated into manhood, the pyramid’s inner chamber where I’d been sworn to the lesser mysteries, then the small house where my father and mother had raised me, and my eyes blurred with old, soft memories. We pulled up outside the harbour’s white sea walls as the law required, the slaves slumping over their oars, heaving for air. Usually you’d wait hours for the port captain to drag himself out just to prove his dignity, but today a ten-oared launch was already waiting, the harbour boom chain dropped, and it shot out to meet us the second we hove to.
“That’s Dason’s captured galley, right?” the captain called up.
“Right,” Tob yelled back. “We saw his head nailed to the bowsprit. Tatho’s fleet sent all our other ships to the bottom, this stinking hulk was the only one left floating.”
The port captain’s eyes swept over us on the afterdeck, his face going pale. “Where’s Lord Deucalion? If you let him get killed under your charge, the Empress will flay you alive inch by inch.”
Tob jerked his thumb at me, grinning. “The man’s right here. He’ll save my hide himself.”
The captain stared, then dropped to a deep ceremonial bow. “My lord, forgive me for not recognizing you at once—truth be told, I expected you in royal robes, not rough sea clothes.”
“Spare me the formalities,” I said. “I don’t parade my rank with shiny baubles. Just get us into the harbour, will you?”
He all but grovelled, leading us in, the castle guards at the harbour mouth blowing trumpets to announce our arrival. He’d run my royal banner up his launch’s masthead, and by the time we glided across the harbour basin, we could hear music blaring from the quays, see the warm flicker of welcome fires dancing in the sunlight. I was half awestruck—this whole huge city, the Empress herself, making this much fuss over a regular returning subject?
We moored to the royal quay’s gold rings, sacred only to the imperial house, and Tob cackled: “If Dason saw us tying up with plain hemp rope instead of silk, his head would roll right off that bowsprit for real.” He squeezed my hand in his huge calloused fist, all gruff sincerity. “You’re the best fighter this world’s ever seen, Deucalion. That spear throw of yours through the giant lizard? Tavern bards are going to sing of that for a hundred years.”
Three gorgeously robed chamberlains escorted me to a crimson silk pavilion set up right on the quay, then laid out piles of bejewelled robes, insisting I change before the Empress arrived. I flat refused. When one stammered that it was what Phorenice wanted, I asked him point blank if he had a written direct order from her about my clothes. He had to admit he didn’t. “She summoned Deucalion the man, not some glittering popinjay,” I told him. The three of them immediately drew their swords and held the hilts out to me, begging me to kill them now instead of turning them over to the imperial torturers for the insult. I waved them off, but that small moment told me everything: this new Atlantis was a place where men feared torment over nothing more than a misstep about a coat.
I bathed quickly in the pavilion’s over-scented tub, then sat waiting. Phorenice didn’t make me play the petty waiting game that most rulers use to puff up their dignity. The second our galley tied to the quay, she left the palace, the whole grand procession already assembled and ready to march. I watched from the pavilion door as the regiments of soldiers marched out of the broad main street, lined the square, followed by the Mariners’ Guild, the merchant guilds, tanners, spear-makers, all parading in their finest, some riding tamed great beasts that would have torn them to pieces in the wild.
Then the crowds parted, and the first wonder rolled into view: a giant bull mammoth, shaggy as a storm cloud, its tusks gilded, its neck draped in flowers, carrying a gilded half-castle on its back carved with the imperial outstretched hand and silver snake emblems. I’d hunted mammoths all my life, killed dozens that raided village cornfields—they were the fiercest wild thing on the continent short of the giant lizards. And here this one was, tame as a house dog, plodding along like it was born to march in parades.
And then I saw the Empress.
She was small, lithe, perfectly formed, her fiery red hair cut in the new short fashion, bunching soft on her shoulders. Her face was sharp, clever, and breathtakingly fair, but her eyes—no man could plumb the depth of those eyes. She was studying me just as hard as I was studying her. The chamberlains hissed behind me to kneel, to bow my head at the very least, but I walked straight across the marble square, head held high, gaze meeting hers unflinchingly.
She smiled when I got close. “Others kneel to me, because they are all beneath me. But you are Deucalion. I decree this day: no man in Atlantis is above you. Any who disobeys you disobeys me, and will feel my wrath.”
A stair was wheeled over, I climbed up to sit beside her on the golden half-castle, and the mammoth turned, carrying us back toward the royal pyramid. The whole procession fell in around us, heavy-armed troops flanking the giant beast on both sides. Phorenice laughed, soft. “You slighted me at first. You looked at my mammoth before you looked at me. A woman never forgives that, usually.”
“I wasn’t slighting you,” I said. “I was envying you the greatest conquest any man or woman ever made. I’ve fought mammoths, killed them, never once dared to try to tame one.”
“Your flattery’s not hollow, that’s a relief,” she said, her tone turning sharp. “Everyone else around me fawns till I’m sick of it. They all want to be my lover, or stuff their own treasuries full. I sent for you because you’re the only strong man left in the world who doesn’t care about my looks, my title, or my favours. I need you as my chief minister, to rule this empire with me.”
I told her straight I’d be her honest minister, and that I had no interest in the silly game of love. She smiled, approving, and that’s when I noticed it: the gorgeous palace spires, the new temples, all that imperial grandeur, right next to gaunt, hungry-faced crowds, half-naked paupers, even the rich traders looking over their shoulders like they were waiting for a knife in the back.
“Rebels,” Phorenice said, following my gaze. “They’re besieging Atlantis, pounding at the gates, building new siege engines to break the walls. They gave us a holiday today for your welcome. Tomorrow, if you want, you can lead the sally and break their engines for sport.”
I was startled. I’d had no idea the city was under siege. Then she pulled down the shoulder of her silk robe, showing a bloodstained linen bandage just below her left breast. “An arrow got me yesterday,” she said, grinning a little. “If it had hit an inch to the right, you’d be riding to my funeral pyre today instead of this parade. That’s how serious I am about those rebels, Deucalion.”
The girl behind us, Ylga, fanned her robes closed again. My welcome to Atlantis, and to the service of Empress Phorenice, was complete.
