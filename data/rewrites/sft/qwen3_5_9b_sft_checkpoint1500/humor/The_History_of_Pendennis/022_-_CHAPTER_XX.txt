Just prior to Mr. Foker's departure from Oxford, a gentleman arrived at Boniface College bearing the distinguished name of Horace Bloundell. Though he hailed from the ancient Suffolk lineage of Bloundell-Bloundell Hall—a title the undergraduates affectionately shortened to "Bloundell-Bloundellshire"—his entry into Oxford was far from smooth. Having previously clashed with the authorities at Cambridge, where he had been expelled, Bloundell had been rejected by St. George's and several other institutions. Yet, owing to his pedigree and the fact that Dr. Donne, the Master of Boniface, was also a Suffolk native and possibly a distant relative, Bloundell found a welcoming home at Boniface.

Behind this social maneuvering lay a pressing financial imperative: a family living was waiting for someone to claim it. Originally designated for Bloundell's third brother, the position fell vacant when that brother succumbed to illness while serving in a dragoon regiment. Seizing the moment, Bloundell abandoned his crimson pantaloons and sable shako for the sober black coat and white cravat of the clergyman. Although his initial plans at Cambridge had stumbled, the former soldier proved resilient, undeterred by failure, and determined to secure his clerical future elsewhere.

By Pen's second year, the atmosphere within the college had shifted. Major Pendennis visited his nephew briefly, introducing him to a motley assembly of university peers: the gentle Lord Plinlimmon, the open-hearted Magnus Charters, the sly Harland, the intrepid Ringwood—who earned the nickname "Rupert" due to the bravery displayed in his blunders—and Broadbent, a Bristol dissenter known as "Barebones" for his republican zeal. Finally, there was Mr. Bloundell, who had quickly ascended to the select circle of the university.

While the Major found delight in the company surrounding Pen, save for one individual who affected the airs of a worldly man, the morning following the party revealed a friction between uncle and nephew regarding these guests. Over breakfast, the Major recounted anecdotes with the gusto of a seasoned raconteur, receiving honest sympathy and boisterous laughter from the lads. However, Mr. Bloundell wore a mask of scorn, occasionally betraying familiarity with the stories. On one occasion, he even interrupted the Major to correct a detail, citing a version he claimed was authentic based on conversations at the club. The students marveled at this boldness, watching as the Major, with a melancholy grace, graciously accepted the correction. Subsequently, Bloundell openly disparaged "Old Pen," labeling him a notorious bore and a heavy eater at Gaunt House.

"The sympathies of men are usually reciprocal," the Major observed later, noting that if a friend accused someone of slandering him, it was a safe bet that the slanderer held a misliking for the accuser as well. "We cannot explain why, but generally, if we detest Dr. Fell, he detests us."

Regarding Pen's recent dining exploits, the Major praised the carving and the hospitality, noting Lord Plinlimmon's amiability and Lord Magnus's temporary republicanism. Yet, he drew a firm line at Mr. Bloundell. "That young man, Bloundell, strikes me as entirely ineligible. A man may possess a splendid coat of arms and descend from Richard the Lionheart's Blondel, yet remain a tiger, my boy. I suspect he deserted his regiment in bad odor. Look at the jewelry; 'Scamp' is written plainly across his countenance."

"Oh, bless my soul, sir!" Pen laughed. "Bloundell is the most popular fellow in the university. We elected him to the Barmecides immediately! He comes from an excellent family, the Suffolk Bloundells."

"A good pedigree does not inoculate against vice," the Major countered, chipping his egg. "Avoid him. By the way, your dinner was exquisite, though perhaps slightly too lavish. You need not worry about the extra costs; you have a sufficient income."

Poor Pen, however, remained oblivious to the warning. The Major scarcely realized how frequently these extravagant meals occurred, fueled by Pen's desire to project an air of sophistication. To the young mind, a deep knowledge of wines and cuisine signaled a mature, worldly gentleman. Pen, aspiring to be the "Admirable Crichton" of dining, wasted little effort learning the arts of gastronomy, trusting blindly in the college cook's judgment. Soon, his reputation as a gourmet vanished, replaced by the sight of creditors gathering outside his door, and the unfortunate cook falling victim to Pen's blind trust.

Despite the Major's counsel, Pen could not resist Bloundell's allure. Young men are naturally drawn to those who seem to possess a "knowledge of life"—a rogue or a lover of adventure who has tales to tell. It is a dangerous trait, yet it persists. Even today, countless youths admit to reading *Don Juan*, unaware of the corruption it seeds. Bloundell alone was sufficient to lower the moral tone of the college, rendering the students unruly and ungenteel. While the dignitaries of St. George's remained unmoved by Bloundell's flashy charms, others like Broadbent dubbed him "Captain Macheath," predicting a hanging. Even the cautious Foker, speaking with characteristic ambiguity, advised Pen to avoid betting against Bloundell at cards, hinting that the Captain played with a reckless hand and limited funds.

Pen, driven by an insatiable appetite for pleasure, embraced the lifestyle wholeheartedly. He championed the maxim of "seeing life," quoting Terence and Shakespeare to justify his hedonism. He seemed poised to become a complete *roué* if the pace continued.

One evening, after a supper party where a game of *vingt-et-un* had been played, the group departed. As they removed their caps, Bloundell, displaying a practiced hand, produced a pair of dice from his waistcoat pocket. He placed them into a green wine glass, gave the glass a theatrical swirl, and rolled the ivory cubes onto the table, calling "seven" the main. He repeated the ritual, mesmerizing the onlookers. Pen, who had only ever played backgammon at home, watched with wide eyes.

Bloundell, possessing a robust voice, began to sing the chorus from *Robert the Devil*. Pen, buoyed by his recent winnings, joined in enthusiastically. Before long, the entire party sat around the table engaged in the game of hazard. The green glass circulated, and eventually, Pen shattered it after throwing six mains.

From that night forward, Pen plunged into the delights of gaming with feverish intensity. Bloundell would visit Pen's rooms post-breakfast, and time seemed to dissolve as the dice rattled. They hosted clandestine parties behind closed doors, employing a felt-lined box designed to silence the telltale roll of the bones, shielding them from the sharp ears of the tutors. On one occasion, they narrowly escaped detection by Mr. Buck, who, overhearing "Two to one on the caster" through an open window, burst in to find them engrossed in Homer. Pen, maintaining a grave demeanor, explained he was coaching his friends on the state of the River Scamander, asking gravely if it was navigable.

Yet, the lessons extended beyond gambling. Captain Macheath, a master of fabrication, spun tales of garrison conquests and Parisian extravaganzas that captivated Pen. He boasted of waking in his chambers to find twelve thousand francs under his pillow and a boot stuffed with Napoleons. Pen, entranced, believed these fantasies were facts, sighing that such wealth would solve all his troubles.

Furthermore, Macheath dismantled Pen's romantic illusions regarding Miss Fotheringay. When Pen confessed his devotion, Macheath scoffed, revealing that Miss Fotheringay was a notorious actress of questionable character, surrounded by admirers who spent lavishly on her. "Marry her?" Macheath sneered, comparing her favorably to the most infamous stage divas. "She hasn't a shred of character." Pen, a man of candid disposition who believed everything he was told, was devastated. How could his pure flame be directed toward an impostor? He found it impossible to doubt the stories of a man who spoke so poorly of women.

During an Easter vacation, Pen and Bloundell traveled to London, staying at a hotel in Covent Garden where Bloundell maintained a "tick." They frequented a military club, where Pen mingled with gallant youths sporting spurs and mustaches, drinking pale ale and beating the town by night. It was here, amidst the roar of theaters and singing houses, that Pen encountered the Major inadvertently. Sitting in the boxes at the Museum Theatre, Pen watched the performance of *The Fotheringay*, which was currently triumphant in London.

Though he had not seen her since their separation at Chatteris, Pen recognized the actress instantly. A sudden, feeblish thrill ran through him as the applause thundered and Ophelia entered with her familiar curtsey. For a moment, jealousy flared; he felt as though the entire house scrutinized him. He fled to the stage door, hoping to catch a glimpse, but she slipped past unnoticed. Returning the next night, he stayed quietly for the afterpiece, and on the final night, he attended *Don Giovanni* alongside the celebrated dancer Taglioni.
