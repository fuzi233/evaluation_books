*"Her wit values itself so highly, that to her all matter else seems weak."* — *Much Ado About Nothing.*

Gwendolen's arrival in the neighborhood proved exactly what her uncle had anticipated. From the imposing Brackenshaw Castle down to the cozy Firs at Wanchester, where Mr. Quallon the banker maintained a notably generous household, she was greeted with open admiration. Even among those ladies whose hearts were not entirely won by her charms, there existed a practical comfort in hosting a new, striking figure; for hostesses who entertain frequently must assemble their gatherings with the strategic acumen of ministers forming a cabinet, prioritizing balance over pure affection. Consequently, securing Gwendolen as a guest required no difficult diplomacy regarding disagreeable personalities, for Mrs. Davilow always presented a quiet, picturesque silhouette as a chaperone, and Mr. Gascoigne was universally sought after for his own considerable merits.

Among the households where Gwendolen was tolerated rather than loved, yet still invited, was Quetcham Hall. Her first summons there led to a grand dinner party that served as a de facto general introduction to the local society. In a select gathering of thirty, carefully balanced across ages, it was impossible to omit any visitable family. Yet, no youthful figure present could compare to hers. As she glided through the long suites of rooms, illuminated by light and blooming flowers, she appeared initially as a slender shape drifting in white drapery, becoming increasingly defined and radiant with each passing doorway. She had never experienced such a promenade before, and she felt a surge of exultation that it suited her perfectly; anyone observing her for the first time might reasonably assume that long galleries and serving staff had always been the backdrop of her existence. Meanwhile, her cousin Anna, who possessed a far greater familiarity with such formalities, felt nearly as awkward as a rabbit suddenly dropped into a brightly lit clearing.

"Who is that walking with Gascoigne?" asked the Archdeacon, abandoning a discussion of military maneuvers on which, as a clergyman, he was naturally consulted. Simultaneously, his son—a promising young scholar who had recently proposed "not less elegant than ingenious" corrections to Greek texts—remarked, "By George! Who is that girl with the awfully well-set head and jolly figure?"

However, to a mind of general benevolence that wished everyone to appear their best, it was somewhat exasperating to witness how Gwendolen eclipsed all others. Even the handsome Miss Lawe, identified as the daughter of Lady Lawe, seemed to shrink, appearing suddenly broad, heavy, and inanimate beside her. Similarly, Miss Arrowpoint, unfortunately clad in white as well, instantly resembled a *carte-de-visite* where one suspected the price had been inflated solely for the length of the skirt. Since Miss Arrowpoint was generally cherished for her amiable, unpretentious manner of wearing her fortune and for acting as a softening buffer for her mother's eccentricities, Gwendolen's overwhelming display of social importance seemed strangely unfitting.

"She is not actually so beautiful if you examine her features closely," Mrs. Arrowpoint confided later in the evening to Mrs. Vulcany. "It is a certain style she possesses that creates a tremendous initial effect, but afterwards she becomes less agreeable."

Indeed, Gwendolen, without intending to, had offended her hostess. Though Mrs. Arrowpoint was neither splenetic nor vindictive, she held strong susceptibilities. Various circumstances converged in the Lady of Quetcham that neighbors deemed essentially connected: she was occasionally remembered as the heiress to a fortune amassed through obscure urban ventures, supposedly explaining her squat figure, her harsh, parrot-like voice, and her systematically elevated head-dress. Since these traits rendered her externally ridiculous, it seemed logical to many that she should harbor literary tendencies. A closer analysis, however, reveals that these attributes exist independently; daughters of aldermen are often well-proportioned and featured, pretty women may possess harsh voices, and the creation of mediocre literature is compatible with virtually any physique, masculine or feminine.

Gwendolen, possessing a keen sense of absurdity in others but a kindly disposition toward anyone capable of making life agreeable, intended to win Mrs. Arrowpoint's favor through exceptional interest and attention. Yet, self-confidence often addresses an imaginary dullness in others; the wealthy cajole the poor, and the vigorous speak artificially to the elderly, hastily assuming deafness or imbecility. Gwendolen, despite her cleverness and desire to be agreeable, fell prey to this form of stupidity. Unreflectingly, she concluded that because Mrs. Arrowpoint was ridiculous, she must also lack penetration. Thus, she enacted her little scenes without suspecting that every nuance of her behavior was being meticulously observed.

"You are fond of books as well as music, riding, and archery, I hear," Mrs. Arrowpoint said, approaching her for a *tête-à-tête* in the drawing-room after dinner. "Catherine will be delighted to have such a sympathetic neighbor."

This remark, delivered in a low, melodious tone, carried a fatal twang that made Gwendolen feel she was exercising patronage. She replied gracefully, "It is I who am fortunate. Miss Arrowpoint will teach me what good music is. I shall be entirely a learner. I hear she is a thorough musician."

"Catherine has certainly enjoyed every advantage. We have a first-rate musician in the house now—Herr Klesmer; perhaps you know all his compositions. You must allow me to introduce him. You sing, I believe? Catherine plays three instruments but does not sing. I hope you will let us hear you. I understand you are an accomplished singer."

"Oh, no!" Gwendolen exclaimed, quoting Mephistopheles. "'Die Kraft ist schwach, allein die Lust ist gross.' 'The strength is weak, but the delight is great.'"

"Ah, you are a student of Goethe. Young ladies are so advanced nowadays. I suppose you have read everything."

"No, really. I shall be so glad if you tell me what to read. I have examined all the books in the library at Offendene, but there is nothing readable. The leaves stick together and smell musty. I wish I could write books to amuse myself, as you can! How delightful it must be to write books according to one's own taste instead of reading other people's! Home-made books must be so nice."

For an instant, Mrs. Arrowpoint's gaze sharpened, but the dangerous edge of satire vanished when Gwendolen added, "I would give anything to write a book!"

"And why should you not?" Mrs. Arrowpoint encouraged. "You need only begin as I did. Pen, ink, and paper are within everyone's reach. But I will send you all I have written with pleasure."

"Thank you. I shall be so happy to read your writings. Being acquainted with authors must grant a peculiar understanding of their works; one would be able to distinguish the humorous from the serious. I am sure I often laugh in the wrong places."

Here, Gwendolen sensed danger and quickly added, "In Shakespeare, you know, and other great writers we can never fully comprehend. But I always want to know more than exists in the books."

"If you are interested in any of my subjects, I can lend you many extra sheets in manuscript," offered Mrs. Arrowpoint. Gwendolen felt painfully like the young lady who professes to enjoy potted sprats. "These are things I dare say I shall publish eventually; several friends have urged me to do so, and one dislikes being obstinate. My *Tasso*, for instance—I could have made it twice the size."

"I dote on Tasso," said Gwendolen.

"Well, you shall have all my papers if you wish. So many have written about Tasso, but they are all wrong. Regarding the specific nature of his madness, his feelings for Leonora, the real cause of his imprisonment, and the character of Leonora—who, in my opinion, was a cold-hearted woman, else she would have married him in spite of her brother—all accounts are incorrect. I differ from everybody."

"How very interesting!" said Gwendolen. "I like to differ from everybody. I think it so stupid to agree. That is the worst of writing your opinions; you make people agree with you."

This speech revived a slight suspicion in Mrs. Arrowpoint, causing her glance to turn examining once more. However, Gwendolen appeared utterly innocent and continued with a docile air: "I know nothing of Tasso except the *Gerusalemme Liberata*, which we read and memorized at school."

"Ah, his life is more fascinating than his poetry. I have constructed the early part of his life as a sort of romance. When one considers his father, Bernardo, and so on, there is much that must be true."

"Imagination is often truer than fact," Gwendolen stated decisively, unable to explain these glib words any better than if they were Coptic or Etruscan. "I shall be so glad to learn all about Tasso—and his madness especially. I suppose poets are always a little mad."

"To be sure—" Mrs. Arrowpoint quoted, "'the poet's eye in a fine frenzy rolling'; and someone says of Marlowe, 'For that fine madness still he did maintain, Which always should possess the poet's brain.'"

"But it was not always discovered, was it?" Gwendolen asked innocently. "I suppose some rolled their eyes in private. Mad people are often very cunning."
