Shepherd’s Inn lay tucked away behind the sooty black gables and forgotten chimneys as a saucepan is shoved to the back of the range—still there, still warm, but out of sight of respectable eyes. You did not so much walk to it as get poured into it through narrow passages and ambiguous alleys where the sun, like a lazy scullion, simply declined to come on duty. The air smelled of damp brick and old paper; of pickled winkles and cheap gin; of singed sugar from hard-bake trays and the sour wool of secondhand bedding that promised anything except sleep.

Outside, the courts rattled and barked with a constant kitchen-clatter of life. Pattens slapped the wet flags; barrow-wheels squealed; shell-fish dealers slapped open their wares with wet, sharp sounds like fish being scaled. Ballad-singers set up their throats—deep, hoarse, and joyless—as though they’d been gargling coal-dust, and chanted songs that were half politics, half spitting. Punch’s little booth creaked and thumped; children shrieked as if being basted; women called after them with voices sharpened on worry; and now and then a husband reeled out of a gin-shop and made an entrance as noisy as a dropped tray.

Then, by one arch as sudden as a pantry door swung shut, you stepped into the quadrangle of Shepherd’s Inn itself—quiet, old-fashioned, and oddly domestic in the middle of all that din, as if London had accidentally left a small, stale biscuit of bygone time in the corner of its mouth. A mangy grass-plat sat in the centre; the statue of Shepherd rose upon it, fenced off behind iron railings from boys who would have vandalised him out of pure professional pride. The hall stood on one side, with the founder’s arms painted up high; tall, ancient chambers ran around the other sides; and an archway led through to Oldcastle Street and thence to the great roaring thoroughfare beyond.

The Inn might once have held lawyers the way a respectable larder once held good hams; but the laity had long since been admitted, and now it housed a miscellany of offices and oddities. The Polwheedle and Tredyddlum Copper Mines took one set of ground-floor rooms—mines, you might note, that were at present more acquainted with water than with copper. Another room announced itself grandly as the Registry of Patent Inventions and Union of Genius and Capital Company, a title so long it must have been paid by the letter.

And then there was the one gentleman whose name, at least, had the audacity to appear in the Law List: Mr. Campion—mustachioed, tufted, and cab-borne—who came twice or thrice a week, leaving behind him an odour of money lent at interest and hair-oil applied with confidence. In Curzon Street, Mayfair, he was Mr. Somerset Campion, and his wife entertained the nobility and gentry to whom he lent money; in Shepherd’s Inn he was Campion and Co., and the same tuft that ornamented his chin sprouted, in more economical fashion, from the under-lip of the rest of his firm.

It was a splendour—positively a culinary miracle of light—to see his cab-horse harness blazing with heraldic bearings as the vehicle stopped in that dingy place. The bit shone; the groom’s breeches were glittering white; the reins looked as if they’d been starched in pride. The horse flung froth from his nostrils, impatient as a man kept waiting for soup.

Captain Costigan had inspected that cab and horse many an afternoon with the exactness of a man assessing a roasted joint he cannot afford. He trailed about the court in carpet slippers and a dressing-gown, with his old hat cocked over one eye, and treated the quadrangle as his own little parade-ground. If the day was suitable he “sunned himself” after breakfast; and then he went to the porter’s lodge to pat children’s heads and talk to Mrs. Bolton about the “thayatres” and “me daughther Leedy Mirabel.”

Mrs. Bolton, porteress and guardian of the Inn’s gossip, had been on the stage herself in a former life—thirteenth of Mr. Serle’s forty pupils at the Wells, which is to say, not a star, but near enough to have seen where the lights were kept. Costigan lived on the third floor at No. 4, in rooms that had belonged to Mr. Podmore, whose name still clung to the door like an old label on a reused jar—indeed, somebody else’s name clung to almost every door in Shepherd’s Inn, as if the whole place were one grand exercise in secondhand identity.

When Charley Podmore—the pleasing tenor singer, T.R.D.L., and at the Back Kitchen Concert Rooms—married and went to Lambeth, he ceded his chambers to Mr. Bows and Captain Costigan, who occupied them in common. On fine days, with the windows open, you might hear Mr. Bows’s piano—patient, careful notes set out like currants on a cake—as he practised for amusement or taught one of his theatrical pupils. He had one or two, and among them was Fanny Bolton, the porteress’s daughter.

Fanny had a good voice and a pretty face and figure for the stage, and—what is rarer—an appetite for glory that was not purely imaginary. She made the beds and prepared the rooms and breakfasts for Messrs. Costigan and Bows, and in return Mr. Bows instructed her in music and singing. She heard her mother’s tales of theatrical splendour the way an eager cook hears of forgotten recipes; she longed to emulate them, and likely would, given time and the absence of catastrophe.

As for the Captain, Fanny, who supposed that all men of fashion indulged in liquor as naturally as they took their hats off indoors, thought him the finest gentleman alive. She believed all versions of all his stories with the trust of a girl tasting her first sweet wine. And she was very fond of Mr. Bows too, grateful to him; while Bows—shy, queer, old—had that fatherly fondness for her which comes of a heart that is never easy unless it loves somebody.

Costigan would have you believe that the carriages of visitors of distinction were forever stopping at his humble door, that Sir Charles and Lady Mirabel were always calling with the select nobility in tow to visit “the old man—the honest old half-pay Captain—poor old Jack Costigan,” as Cos liked to label himself, to make the pity sit better. The truth was smaller and sharper: Lady Mirabel had indeed left her husband’s card once (it had been stuck into the little looking-glass over the mantelpiece in the sitting-room at No. 4 and had stayed there like a dried bay leaf for months), and she had visited her father in person—but not lately.

When she married Sir Charles Mirabel, she had settled a little pension on her father, gravely, dutifully; and sometimes he was admitted to her table. At first, Costigan’s behaviour “in the hoight of poloit societee”—as he called Lady Mirabel’s drawing-room—was harmless if absurd. Just as he dressed his body in his best clothes, he dressed his speech in the longest words he could find, as though syllables were jewels. He adopted a solemnity that astonished everyone within range.

“Was your Leedyship in the Pork to dee?” he would ask his daughter with the gravity of a man inquiring after a duke’s health, when he meant “park,” and meant it with the whole history of Ireland hanging off the consonants. “I looked for your equipage in veen—the poor old man was not gratified by the soight of his daughther’s choriot. Sir Chorlus, I saw your neem at the Levee; many’s the Levee at the Castle at Dublin that poor old Jack Costigan has attended in his time. Did the Juke look pretty well? Bedad, I’ll call at Apsley House and lave me cyard upon ’um. I thank ye, James—a little dthrop more champeane.”

He was magnificent in courtesy—so magnificent he spent it on everyone present, including the servants, addressing them too with elaborate remarks, and the domestics had to bite their lips to keep professional faces. On his first two or three visits he maintained strict sobriety, hoarding his thirst as a man hoards an appetite for later. He would go back to the Back Kitchen afterwards and brag about his son-in-law’s claret and Burgundy until his own utterance began to fail over his sixth tumbler of whisky-punch.

But familiarity melted caution as heat melts fat. Poor Cos began to disgrace himself lamentably at Sir Charles Mirabel’s table by premature inebriation. A carriage would be called; the hospitable door would close upon him. Then, at the Back Kitchen, he would speak often and sadly of his resemblance to King Lear “in the plee,” of his thankless child, bedad; of being a “pore worn-out lonely old man,” driven to drink by ingratitude, and seeking to drown sorrows in punch—as if sorrow were a rat and punch a bucket.

It is painful to record the weaknesses of fathers; but if one is to catalogue a kitchen, one must mention the spills. When his credit was exhausted and his money gone, Costigan would beg from his daughter and season his requests with statements not altogether consistent with strict truth. One day, when a bailiff was about to lead him to prison, he wrote that unless the “—to you insignificant—sum of three pound five” could be forthcoming, his grey hairs must go to gaol. Lady Mirabel, good-natured, sent the money with a caution to be more economical.

On a second occasion he met with a “frightful accident” and broke a plate-glass window in the Strand, for which the proprietor held him liable. The money came again, carried down by Lady Mirabel’s servant to the Captain’s slipshod messenger and aide-de-camp. If the servant had followed that aide-de-camp—and in Shepherd’s Inn, secrets travel by foot if at all—he would have seen the fellow call a cab and rattle down to the Roscius Head in Harlequin Yard, Drury Lane, where the Captain was “indeed in pawn,” and where several glasses of rum-and-water or other spirituous refreshment were employed in celebrating Lady Mirabel’s dutiful remittance.

For, observe, an Irish gentleman, however poor, always has a poorer Irish gentleman to run his errands and transact his pecuniary affairs; it is one of the few institutions that survives everything.

On a third melancholy occasion the Captain wrote that he was attacked by illness and wanted money to pay the physician he was compelled to call in. This time Lady Mirabel, alarmed—perhaps also reproaching herself that she had lately lost sight of her father—called for her carriage and drove to Shepherd’s Inn. She alighted at the gate, found her way to No. 4, third floor, “name of Podmore over the door,” as the porteress said with many curtsies; and mounted the dingy stair with the resolution of a woman going to view a pot that has long been boiling unattended.

Alas. The Podmore door was opened not upon some pale invalid with a hot-water bottle, but upon poor Cos in his shirt-sleeves, cheerful and flushed, preparing with a gridiron to receive the mutton-chops Mrs. Bolton had gone to purchase. Whatever illness he had was of the sort that improves wonderfully at the smell of frying fat.

Nor was it pleasant for Sir Charles Mirabel to have letters constantly addressed to him at Brookes’s, with the information that Captain Costigan was in the hall waiting for an answer; or, when Sir Charles went to play his rubber at the Travellers’, to be obliged to shoot out of his brougham and run up the steps rapidly lest his father-in-law should seize upon him; or to think, while he read his paper or played his whist, that the Captain was walking on the opposite side of Pall Mall with that dreadful cocked hat and the eye beneath it fixed steadily upon the club windows—watching, listening for the particular tone of his son-in-law’s footfall, as though the man’s very gait were a language he meant to translate into money.

Sir Charles was weak; he was old and infirm. He cried about his father-in-law to his wife, whom he adored with senile infatuation. He swore he must go abroad, go to the country—he would die, or have another fit, if he saw that man again; he knew he should. It was only by paying a second visit to Captain Costigan and representing to him that if he plagued Sir Charles by letters, accosted him in the street, or made further applications for loans, his allowance would be withdrawn altogether, that Lady Mirabel managed to keep her papa in order and restore tranquillity to her husband.

On that visit she rebuked Mr. Bows sternly for not keeping better watch over the Captain; ordered that he should not be allowed to drink in that shameful way; and that the people at the horrid taverns he frequented should be told, on no account, to give him credit. “Papa’s conduct is bringing me to the grave,” she said—though she looked perfectly healthy—“and you, as an old man, Mr. Bows, and one that pretended to have a regard for us, ought to be ashamed of abetting him in it.”

Those were the thanks honest Bows got for his friendship and his life’s devotion. He bore them as a man bears an over-salted dish: not because it is pleasant, but because it is on the table and he has no other dinner. And perhaps he was no worse off than many.

In the next house, at No. 3, on the second floor, lived two other acquaintances: Colonel Altamont, agent to the Nawaub of Lucknow, and Captain Chevalier Edward Strong. There was no name over their door. Strong did not choose to let all the world know where he lived; his cards bore the address of a Jermyn Street hotel. As for the Colonel—Ambassador Plenipotentiary of an Indian potentate—he was an envoy accredited neither to St. James’s nor to Leadenhall Street, but here on a confidential mission quite independent of the East India Company or the Board of Control.

“In fact,” Strong said, with that air men adopt when explaining why they must be mysterious, “Colonel Altamont’s object being financial, and to effectuate a sale of some of the principal diamonds and rubies of the Lucknow crown, his wish is not to report himself at the India House or in Cannon Row, but rather to negotiate with private capitalists—with whom he has had important transactions both in this country and on the Continent.”

Their chambers had become very comfortable since the arrival of Sir Francis Clavering in London; and Strong could boast, with reason, that few retired Captains were more snugly quartered than he in his “crib” at Shepherd’s Inn. There were three rooms below: an office where Strong transacted his business—whatever that might be—still fitted with the desk and railings left by departed clerks; Strong’s own bedroom; and his sitting-room. A private stair led out of the office to two upper apartments: one for Colonel Altamont, and one that served as kitchen and as bedroom for Mr. Grady, their attendant.

These upper rooms sat level with Bows’s and Costigan’s next door at No. 4; and by leaning over the communicating leads, Grady could command the mignonette-box blooming in Bows’s window. From Grady’s kitchen casement came odours more fragrant than mignonette—honest, edible perfumes, the true incense of the poor. You might smell onions softened patiently until their sharpness turned sweet; pepper waking up in hot fat with a sneeze; rice steaming, clean and nutty; a whiff of coriander and toasted cumin like dry leaves crackling; and sometimes the rich, dark promise of a curry reduced to its proper thickness, as careful as a historian reducing a sauce until nothing remains but concentrated truth.

The three old soldiers of No. 3 were all skilled in the culinary art, and took to it as veterans take to storytelling—because it uses what you have left: time, fire, and memory. Grady was great at an Irish stew: he could turn a few scrag ends of mutton, a fist of potatoes, and an onion into something that made a man forgive the rain. The Colonel was famous for pillaus and curries, and treated spice the way other men treat medals—arranged, displayed, and bragged of. And as for Strong, he could cook anything. He made French dishes and Spanish dishes, stews, fricassees, omelettes to perfection; and when his purse was full, or his credit good, there was no man in England more hospitable. At those happy periods he could give a friend, as he said, a good dinner, a good glass of wine, and a good song afterwards.

Poor Cos, sitting next door, often heard with envy the roar of Strong’s choruses—big, masculine noises rolling out like barrels—and the musical clinking of glasses, and the rise and fall of laughter, and the scrape of a chair, and the brief hush that comes when something particularly savoury hits the tongue. Shepherd’s Inn, that day, could be read by its sounds: gin’s harsh shout outside, and inside the quadrangle, the gentler music of friends eating and talking. Yet it was not expedient to invite Mr. Costigan always: his inebriation was lamentable; and he bored Strong’s guests with his stories when sober and with his tears when drunk. His voice, too, had the habit of drowning out others—like a cheap ballad sung too loud in a small room—until nobody could remember what anyone else had said.

A strange and motley set were these friends of the Chevalier. Major Pendennis would not have relished them; Arthur and Warrington liked them not a little; and Pen found them as amusing as the society of the finest gentlemen in the finest houses he had the honour to frequent. There was a history about every man, and most of their histories came with a smell: stale tobacco in one man’s coat, sea-salt in another’s beard, brandy in a third’s breath, foreign spices in a fourth’s speech. They all seemed to have ridden tides of luck and bad fortune; they all carried schemes in their pockets the way old women carry peppermints, and promised rapid extraordinary fortunes with the ease of men promising a second helping.

Jack Holt had been in Don Carlos’s army when Ned Strong fought on the other side; now he was organising a scheme for smuggling tobacco into London that must bring thirty thousand a year to any man who would advance fifteen hundred—just to bribe the last Excise officer who held out and had wind of the plan. Tom Diver, late of the Mexican navy, knew of a specie-ship sunk in the first year of the war with three hundred and eighty thousand dollars on board and a hundred and eighty thousand pounds in bars and doubloons. “Give me eighteen hundred,” Tom said, “and I’m off tomorrow. I take out four men, and a diving-bell; and I return in ten months to take my seat in Parliament, by Jove! and to buy back my family estate.” Keightley, manager of the waterlogged copper mines, sang as good a second as any professional man and also had a Smyrna Sponge Company and a little quicksilver operation in view which would set him straight with the world yet. Filby had been everything: corporal of dragoons, field-preacher, missionary-agent for converting the Irish; actor at a Greenwich fair-booth, where his father’s attorney found him when the old gentleman died and left him a famous property from which he got no rents and whose very situation nobody exactly knew—as if the land itself had forgotten the language in which it was supposed to pay.

Added to these was Sir Francis Clavering, Bart., who liked their society though he did not add much to its amusement by his convivial powers. Yet he was made much of now on account of his wealth and place in the world. He told his little story and sang his little song or two with affability; and he, too, had a history before his accession to good fortune, having seen the inside of more prisons than one and written his name on many a stamped paper.

When Altamont first returned from Paris—after he had communicated with Sir Francis Clavering from the hotel where he had taken up his quarters, which he had reached in a very denuded state considering the diamonds and rubies entrusted to him—Strong was sent by the Baronet to his patron, paid the little bill at the inn, and invited Altamont to come and sleep for a night or two at the chambers, where he subsequently took up residence.

To negotiate with such a man was one thing; to have him settled in one’s rooms and to be constantly burdened with such society was another. It did not suit the Chevalier’s taste, and he grumbled to his principal with the freedom of a cook asked to share his kitchen with a bear.

“I wish you would put this bear into somebody else’s cage,” he said to Clavering. “The fellow’s no gentleman. I don’t like walking with him. He dresses himself like a nigger on a holiday. I took him to the play the other night; and, by Jove, sir, he abused the actor who was doing the part of villain, and swore at him so that the people in the boxes wanted to turn him out. The after-piece was the ‘Brigand,’ where Wallack comes in wounded, you know, and dies. When he died, Altamont began to cry like a child, and said it was a d——d shame, and cried and swore so that there was another row, and everybody laughing. Then I had to take him away because he wanted to take his coat off to one fellow who laughed at him; and bellowed to him to stand up like a man.

“Who is he? Where the deuce does he come from? You had best tell me the whole story. Frank; you must one day. You and he have robbed a church together, that’s my belief. You had better get it off your mind at once, Clavering, and tell me what this Altamont is, and what hold he has over you.”

“Hang him! I wish he was dead!” was the Baronet’s only reply; and his face grew so gloomy, so abruptly shut—like an oven-door slammed on a flare-up—that Strong did not press him further then. But he resolved, if need were, to discover for himself what secret tie bound Altamont to Clavering, and why one man’s name could make the other’s cheer go out as suddenly as a song smothered by the roar of the street.
