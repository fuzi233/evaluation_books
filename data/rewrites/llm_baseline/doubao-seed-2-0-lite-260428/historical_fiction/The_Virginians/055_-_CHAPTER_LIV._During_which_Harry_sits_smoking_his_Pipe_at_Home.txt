The maternal grandfather of our two Virginian brothers, Colonel Esmond, who had long since quit the crowded streets of old London to settle in the broad, sunlit expanses of the New World, had whiled away many quiet decades of his American leisure writing down the memoirs of his stormy, passionate youth. His manuscript pages gave a great and vivid part to Madame de Bernstein, the former Beatrice Esmond, who for half a century had been one of the most celebrated belles of English high society. George had pored over those tattered, ink-stained volumes a dozen times over on quiet evenings at the Virginia plantation, so he knew his kinswoman long before he ever crossed the Atlantic to meet her: knew the bright, wilful, dark-eyed girl of fifty years prior, before he ever sat across the fire from the old, rouged, diamond-draped dowager she had grown to be.

As his aunt stumbled in after a long rout, paint smudged a little at the jaw and her silk train trailing behind her cane, George wondered, with a quiet melancholy, how it must feel to be a beauty no more: to have faded cheeks and dimmed eyes, and still carry the memory of your own bloom sharp in your head. When the heart is withered, does it ache to remember the days it beat warm and fast with first love? When the spirits are languid and worn thin, do you like to look back on how bright they once were, how buoyant your hope, how eager your taste for every small pleasure life had to offer? All those first buds of youth, all the roses of beauty, all the lush summer harvests fall and wither in the end, after all, leaving nothing but bare branches to shiver in the winter wind.

“And that was a beauty once,” George thinks to himself, staring at her. “That ruin was once a splendid palace. Crowds of men sighed before those decrepit feet once, and were dizzy with the light of those eyes.” It reminds him of the King’s Birthday fireworks he watched back home in Williamsburg once, after the last sparks had fizzled out: nothing left of the grand display but the charred skeleton of the firework wheel, and the empty, blackened sockets of the spent Roman candles. All the dazzle and chaos of Aunt Beatrice’s early life unfurled in his mind as he turned over the pages of his grandfather’s memoirs in his head.
Honest Harry had flipped through the manuscript too, of course, but he was no bookish man, and had not paid it much mind. Even if he had, he would never have stopped to moralize over the vanity of youth the way his quiet, melancholy older brother did. George decided there was no use telling his aunt how well he knew every detail of her early life from the old memoirs, and held his peace on the subject.

When their supper was cleared away, the old lady pointed her cane at her escritoire, and bade her maid fetch the letter tucked under the inkstand there. George recognized the superscription at once: it was the very letter he had carried across the Atlantic from their mother’s plantation home.
“It would appear by this letter,” the old lady said, staring hard at him through a great pair of heavy spectacles, “that ever since your return to Virginia, there have been some disagreements between you and my sister.”
“Indeed? I did not know Madam Esmond had spoken of them to you,” George replied.
The Baroness slid those spectacles over the eyes that once had shot fire, and kindled a hundred passions in a hundred young men half a century before, then scanned the letter once more and handed it over to George to read. It was dated Richmond, Virginia, December 26th, 1756:

> I have received, and thankfully acknowledge, your ladyship’s favour sent by the Rose packet, dated October 23rd last, and I write back straightaway at this season that should be one of goodwill and peace to all men – though Heaven has seen fit that we still must bear our share of earthly sorrow and trouble. My reply is brought to you by my eldest son, Mr. Esmond Warrington, who returned to us so miraculously out of the Valley of the Shadow of Death (as our earlier letters have already told my poor Harry), and who has my full consent to visit Europe, though he has scarcely been home with us for any time at all.
> I grieve to think that my dearest Harry first arrived in England under false colours, as it were: that he was presented to his Majesty, to our family, and to everyone, as his father’s heir, while my dear son George was still alive, though lost to us. Ah, madam! For those eighteen months he was held captive by the French and their Indian allies, what anguish his mother’s heart, his brother’s heart, have borne! Harry’s is the tenderest heart of any man alive today. In the joy of seeing Mr. Esmond Warrington return from the dead, he will forget the worldly fortune he lost. He will take up a younger brother’s comparative poverty without a single pang. The most generous, the most obedient of all sons, he will gladly give up to his elder brother that inheritance that would have been his own but for the accident of birth, and the providential return of my son George.
> Your kind intentions towards dearest Harry are more welcome than ever now he is reduced to a younger brother’s slender portion! Many years ago, there was an excellent opportunity to buy him a fine estate in this province, and by now he would be master of a great stretch of land and a full household of negroes, and cut a very respectable figure here, if my wishes had been followed, and his father’s small portion, which sits earning paltry interest in the British funds, had been invested in that purchase. But the quirks of the law, and I grieve to own it, my elder son’s foolish scruples, prevailed, and that fine opportunity was lost to us! Harry will find all the savings from his late income have been carefully stored away – long, long may he live to enjoy them! May Heaven bless you, dear sister, for whatever you add to his little store! I gather from your letter that the allowance he received was not enough to cover his expenses in the fine company he has been keeping – and the grandson of the Marquis of Esmond, who nearly was his lordship’s heir, may surely claim equality with any nobleman in Great Britain! I have a sum set aside that I always intended for the poor child to set him up, and I entrust it to my eldest son, who to do him justice has a most sincere affection for his brother, to lay it out for Harry’s best advantage.

“It took him out of prison yesterday, madam. I think that was the best possible use we could put it to,” George interposed at this point.
“Nonsense, sir! I don’t know that at all!” the old lady cried. “Why not have kept it to buy him a pair of colours in the army, or to put towards another estate and a few negroes, if he fancies coming back to Virginia? Besides, I wanted to pay that debt myself.”
“I beg you to let his brother do that,” George said. “I ask leave to be my brother’s banker in this matter, and consider I have borrowed that sum from my mother, to be paid back every penny to my dear Harry.”
“Do you say so, sir? Fetch me a glass of wine! You are a very extravagant young man! Read on, and you will see your mother thinks the very same. I drink to your health, nephew George! This is fine Burgundy, you know. Your grandfather never cared for Burgundy. He preferred claret, the little that he drank.”

George picked up the letter and continued:
> This remittance will, I trust, amply cover any expenses dearest Harry may have incurred, owing to the silly mistake over his status as heir. I wish I could trust his elder brother’s prudence as confidently as I trust Harry’s! But I fear that even in his captivity, Mr. Esmond Warrington learned little of that humility that becomes all good Christians, and which I have ever striven to teach to both my boys. If by chance you show him these lines, when the great ocean divides us, he will know that a fond mother’s blessing and prayers follow both her children, and that no act I have ever done, no wish I have ever expressed (however little he may have cared to obey it!) was anything but the fondest possible wish for my dearest boys’ welfare.

“There’s a scratch from a penknife there, and a great blot on the page like a drop of water fell on it,” the old lady noted, not unkindly. “Your mother writes a fine hand, George. I suppose you and she had your falling out?”
“Yes, ma’am, many,” George answered, sadly. “The last was over money – the ransom I promised the old French lieutenant at the fort who helped me escape. I told you he had a mistress, a poor Indian woman, who helped me, and was very kind to me. Six weeks after I got home to Virginia, the poor creature turned up at Richmond, having found her way through the woods by almost the exact same trail I took when I fled. She brought me the token Museau had promised to send me when he helped me run. A new commanding officer and a huge garrison of reinforcements had arrived at Fort Duquesne, and charges of embezzlement – I don’t know the full details, her English was very bad – were brought against Museau. He was arrested, and tried to escape, but less lucky than me, he was shot dead on the ramparts. He sent the Indian woman to me with my grandfather’s old watch, and a scrawled note he wrote in his prison cell on his deathbed, begging me to send whatever money I could to a notary at Havre de Grace in France, to forward to his relatives at Caen in Normandy. Our friend Silverheels the hunter helped my poor Indian messenger on her way – I don’t know how she would have avoided being scalped otherwise. But at home, they received her very coldly. They hardly gave her a welcome at all. I will not speak of the silly, cruel suspicions they threw on her and on me. The poor wretch took to drinking whenever she could find liquor. I ordered that she be given food and shelter, but she became the joke of our negroes, and the subject of every bit of tittle-tattle from all the old gossips of our little town. Luckily, our Governor was a sensible man, and I got a pass for her to travel back to her own people. Her very grief at parting with me only confirmed all the stupid rumours against her. I do believe a local preacher even denounced me from the pulpit over it, and I had to cane another busybody who spread slander. I had a terrible fight with Madam Esmond – a rift that is not healed to this day – because I insisted on paying the sum I promised Museau’s heirs for my deliverance. Scandal flourishes right on the edge of the wilderness, you know, just as much here in the Old World.”
“I’ve suffered from scandal myself, my dear!” Madame Bernstein said demurely. “Fill your glass, child! A little sip of cherry-brandy! It will do you all the good in the world.”

Madam Esmond’s letter continued:
> As for my poor Harry’s marriage, though I know full well from sad experience the dangers youth is exposed to, and would keep my boy far from imprudent matches at any cost, and though I would wish him to marry a woman of rank that befits his birth, Lady Maria Esmond is utterly out of the question. Her age is almost the same as mine, and I know my brother Castlewood left his two daughters with barely a penny between them. My Harry is so obedient that I know one word from me will be enough to make him give up this silly, ill-suited match. Some foolish people once supposed I myself thought of a second marriage once, to a man of rank far above our station. No! I knew what duty I owed to my children. As Mr. Esmond Warrington will inherit the Virginia estate after me, he is amply provided for. My task now must be to save for his less fortunate younger brother, and as I do not care to live entirely alone, let Harry come home to his fond and loving mother without delay.
> The account your ladyship gives of my Harry fills my heart with the warmest gratitude. He is all a mother could ever wish. A year in Europe will have given him a polish and refinement he could never gain in our homely, rough Virginia. Mr. Stack, one of our valued ministers in Richmond, has a letter from Mr. Ward – my darlings’ old childhood tutor – who knows Lady Warrington and her excellent family, and says my Harry has been spending a great deal of time with his cousins lately. I am so grateful to think my boy gets to stay with his good aunt. May he follow her good counsel, and listen to those who will guide him to his best welfare! Adieu, dear madam and sister! Accept the grateful thanks of a mother’s heart for all your kindness to my boy. Though we have been distant until now, may these kind ties draw us closer and closer. I am thankful you speak so kindly of my dearest father. He was, indeed, one of the best of men! He, too, I know, thanks you for the love you have borne to one of his children; and his daughter subscribes herself,
> With sincere thanks, your ladyship’s most dutiful and grateful sister and servant,

> *P.S.* – I have already written to Lady Maria myself, but there is no need to tell her or dear Harry that his mother or your ladyship hope to be able to increase his small fortune. The match is entirely unsuitable.

“So far as I am concerned, madam,” George said, laying the letter down on the table, “my mother’s letter tells me nothing new. I always knew Harry was the favourite son with Madam Esmond, and he deserves to be. He has a hundred good qualities I have never had. He is handsomer, for one–”
“Nay, that is not your fault,” the old lady said, slyly glancing up at him. “And if it were not for him being fair and you dark, one could almost pass the two of you for twins.” George bowed, and a faint pink flush rose up his pale cheek.
“His disposition is bright, and mine is sombre,” he went on. “Harry is cheerful, and I never have been, I suppose. He knows how to make everyone love him, but I have never had much luck finding friends.”
“My sister and you have your pretty little quarrels, then. We had the same sort of things in our family in the old days,” the Baroness said. “And if Madam Esmond takes after our mother–”
“My mother has always described hers as an angel upon earth,” George interjected.
“Ah! Dead people so often get to be angels, don’t they?” the Baroness cried. “And Rachel Castlewood was an angel, if you like – at least your grandfather thought she was. But let me tell you, sir, angels are often very inconvenient to live with. Maybe they are too good for the rest of us sinners, and the air down here on earth doesn’t agree with them. My poor mother was so perfect she could never forgive me for being anything other than perfect myself. Mon Dieu! How she used to oppress me with those angelical airs!”
George cast his eyes down, and thought of his own quiet, melancholy youth back at the plantation. He had no wish to lay more of his family’s private secrets before this cynical old woman of the world, though she seemed to understand him perfectly even when he said nothing.
“I see you perfectly, sir, even if you won’t speak,” the Baroness went on. “A sermon in the morning, a sermon at night, two or three on Sundays. That’s what people call being good. Every pleasure is a sin, all worldly people are excommunicated, a ball is an abomination, a play is forbidden, a hand of cards is straight to perdition! What a life! Mon dieu, what a life!”
“We played cards every night if we wanted to at home,” George said, smiling, “and my grandfather loved Shakespeare so much my mother never dared say a word against her own father’s favourite author.”
“I remember! He could recite whole pages by heart – though for my part I far prefer Mr. Congreve,” the old lady said, tapping her fan. “And that dreadful, dreary Milton, that he and Mr. Addison both pretended to adore!”
“If your ladyship does not care for Shakespeare, you will not quarrel with my mother for being indifferent to him, either,” George said. “And truly, I think you do not do her justice. Wherever there are poor people, she relieves them. Wherever there are sick people, she–”
“She doses them all with her horrible purges and boluses!” the Baroness laughed. “Exactly the same as my own mother did!”
“She does her very best to cure them! She acts for the best, and does her duty as far as she knows how to.”
“I do not blame you, sir, for doing your own duty, and keeping quiet about Madam Esmond,” the old lady said. “But at least the three of us are all of one mind on one thing: this absurd marriage must be stopped. Do you know how old that woman really is? I can tell you, though she tore the first page with her birth date out of the family Bible at Castlewood.”
“My mother has not forgotten her cousin’s age, and is shocked at the huge difference between her and my poor brother,” George said. “A town-bred lady of that time of life, used to all the gaiety and luxury of London, would find our Virginia plantation a very dismal home. Besides, the estate, such as it is, is not Harry’s. He is welcome there, Heaven knows – more welcome, perhaps, than I, who will inherit the property in the natural course of things. But as I told Harry, I doubt very much how his wife would like our colony.”

Harry never said a word, but you do not need a conjuror to see who was mistress of that house, and what sort of life my sister led you.
