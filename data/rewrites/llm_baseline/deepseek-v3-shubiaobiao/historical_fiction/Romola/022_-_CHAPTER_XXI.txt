The air of Florence hummed with anticipation on the 17th of November 1494. Eighteen months had passed since Tito Melema and Romola’s joyous Easter wedding, where showers of rainbow comfits had rained down upon them in the ancient Greek tradition—a promise of sweetness for their shared life. But the world beyond their happiness had shifted. The city that had once reveled in Medici splendor now stood on the brink of a new era, its fate tangled with the grand upheavals of Italy.

Only a week before, Florence had erupted in rebellion. The great bell of the Palazzo Vecchio had sounded the alarm, and citizens—armed with rusted blades, tools, and whatever makeshift weapons they could seize—had driven Piero de’ Medici and his brother, the scheming Cardinal Giovanni, from the city. The gates of San Gallo had slammed shut behind their fleeing figures, their names now cursed, prices set upon their heads. The Republic had stirred from its long slumber, scrubbing away the painted effigies of old conspirators, welcoming back exiles, and reclaiming its voice.

Yet now, the very streets that had echoed with defiance were draped in color. Banners unfurled from windows, carpets lined the cobblestones, and garlands arched over the thoroughfares—all for the arrival of an uninvited guest. From the Via Larga to the Porta San Frediano, Florence had adorned itself like a bride awaiting a conqueror.

At the city gate, a platform stood ready for the Signoria. Messer Luca Corsini adjusted his robes, rehearsing the Latin oration meant to flatter their visitor. The elders of Florence smoothed their silk *luccos*, their chins freshly shaven for the procession. Young nobles preened in French-cut tunics, eager to impress. The clergy, from the resplendent archbishop to the humblest friar, debated the order of their march, relics and sacred banners in hand. All awaited the hour of three, when the foreign king would enter their gates.

And what a king he was! No ordinary invader, but a force unseen in Italy for centuries. His army spilled through the Alpine passes—Swiss mercenaries, steel-eyed and ruthless; French knights in gleaming armor; infantry bristling with arquebuses; and bronze cannons that hurled iron death, drawn not by oxen but by swift horses. Some whispered he was Charlemagne reborn, destined to cleanse the Church and humble tyrants. Others likened him to Cyrus, liberator of God’s chosen.

His name was Charles VIII of France, and his ambitions stretched beyond mere conquest. He would march through Italy in triumph, settle the strife of Rome, reclaim Naples by right of blood, and then—so the grand vision went—lead Christendom against the Turks. To many, his coming seemed ordained by heaven, a divine reckoning.

Yet not all trembled in pious awe. For years, one voice had foretold this moment. Fra Girolamo Savonarola, the Dominican prior of San Marco, had preached of a flood sent by God to scourge the wicked. When news of the French invasion reached Florence that September, he had stood in the Duomo and thundered from Genesis: *"Behold, I bring a flood of waters upon the earth."* To him, Charles was no mere king, but an instrument of wrath and renewal.

But Florence was no Nineveh. Though some knelt in repentance, others gripped hidden weapons. Behind the festive drapery, militias drilled in shadowed courtyards. Barricades stood ready, stones piled high on rooftops. The French might come as saviors in Savonarola’s sermons, but their vanguard had already strutted through the streets, marking lodgings with insolent chalk. The citizens, fresh from overthrowing the Medici, had not forgotten how to fight.

As noon passed, the city held its breath—not in submission, but in readiness. Whether Charles would be conqueror or pawn, liberator or scourge, Florence would meet him on its own terms. The banners fluttered, the choir practiced its hymns, and the stones lay waiting. The 17th of November would decide much.
