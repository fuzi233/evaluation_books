"If some mortal, born too soon,
Were laid away in some great trance—the ages
Coming and going all the while—till dawned
His true time's advent; and could then record
The words they spoke who kept watch by his bed,
Then I might tell more of the breath so light
Upon my eyelids, and the fingers warm
Among my hair. Youth is confused; yet never
So dull was I but, when that spirit passed,
I turned to him, scarce consciously, as turns
A water-snake when fairies cross his sleep."
—BROWNING: *Paracelsus*.

These lines framed the letter Sir Hugo pressed into Deronda's hand, a document that felt less like news and more like a summons to a trial.

My good friend and yours, Sir Hugo Mallinger, will have told you that
I wish to see you. My health is shaken, and I desire there should be
no time lost before I deliver to you what I have long withheld. Let
nothing hinder you from being at the *Albergo dell' Italia* in
Genoa by the fourteenth of this month. Wait for me there. I am
uncertain when I shall be able to make the journey from Spezia, where
I shall be staying. That will depend on several things. Wait for
me—the Princess Halm-Eberstein. Bring with you the diamond ring that
Sir Hugo gave you. I shall like to see it again.—Your unknown mother,

The letter's tone was a flat, colorless sheet against which Deronda's imagination raced wildly. Yet, he forced himself to accept Sir Hugo's silence as a sacred pact, a promise that the truth would not be spoiled by premature whispers. The realization that his lifelong guesses about his father were wrong acted as a cold bath, washing away the fever of speculation. He allowed his mind to flutter over the possibilities of his origin, but he refused to let any single scenario take root as a dominant fear or desire. Instead, he fortified himself with a grim resolve to face whatever reality awaited him, armed only with courage.

In this fragile state, Deronda could not explain his sudden departure to anyone, least of all to Mordecai. To tell the Rabbi he was seeking the truth of his birth would ignite a hope in Mordecai that could turn into a dangerous, painful excitement. So, Deronda spoke of his journey as a whim of Sir Hugo's, masking his urgency with feigned indifference. He admitted only that the trip's length was uncertain, perhaps very short.

"I will ask to have the child Jacob to stay with me," Mordecai offered, finding comfort in the arrangement after his initial sorrowful looks.

"I will drive round and ask Mrs. Cohen to let him come," Mirah added softly.

"The grandmother will deny you nothing," Deronda said, smiling gently at Mordecai. "I'm glad you were a little wrong as well as I," he added, noting how Mirah had feared the old woman would not tolerate her presence.

"I undervalued her heart," Mordecai replied, his voice thick with emotion. "She is capable of rejoicing that another's plant blooms though her own be withered."

"Oh, they are dear good people; I feel as if we all belonged to each other," Mirah said, a touch of merriment brightening her smile.

"What should you have felt if that Ezra had been your brother?" Deronda asked, mischief flickering in his eyes, slightly provoked that she had so quickly embraced people who might cause him trouble.

Mirah looked at him with a flash of surprise, then confessed, "He is not a bad man—I think he would never forsake any one." As the words left her lips, she blushed deeply, glancing timidly at her father before turning away to a task. Her father loomed large in her mind, a subject shared between them with a painful, mutual awareness. To Mirah, the thought of him arriving to find them made the daylight of the street seem as shadowy as a haunted forest, where every turn hid an imaginary specter.

Deronda felt the weight of her involuntary allusion and understood the blush. How could he be slow to recognize feelings that now seemed closer to his own than ever? For the words in his mother's letter implied that his filial bond was not free from pain; indeed, the very letter that made his mother a living reality had pushed her further away in his affections. The tender yearning for a mother whose life might have been ruined by lack of his care, an image of a parent who had not received her due reverence or compassion, had long haunted his observations of women. Yet now, this picture of his mother seemed to fit the facts no better than his former misconceptions about Sir Hugo. He was startled to find that when his mother's actual handwriting arrived, carrying words of her true feeling, his affection suddenly contracted into a state of neutral detachment. A veiled figure with enigmatic speech had displaced the image he had lovingly modeled in his mind, the one possessing his tenderness and dutiful longing. As he prepared to leave for Genoa, the interest dominating his mind had less to do with his mother and more with Mordecai and Mirah.

"God bless you, Dan!" Sir Hugo had said, shaking his hand. "Whatever else changes for you, it can't change my being the oldest friend you have known, and the one who has all along felt the most for you. I couldn't have loved you better if you'd been my own—only I should have been better pleased with thinking of you always as the future master of the Abbey instead of my fine nephew; and then you would have seen it necessary for you to take a political line. However—things must be as they may." It was a defensive maneuver by the baronet, mixing purposeless chatter with serious sentiment.

When Deronda finally reached the *Italia* in Genoa, no Princess Halm-Eberstein was present. On the second day, however, a letter arrived stating her arrival might occur within a week or be deferred by a fortnight; circumstances prevented her from fixing a date, and she begged him to wait with patience.

Faced with this indefinite suspense regarding matters of supreme importance, Deronda attempted to seek amusement on philosophical grounds to quiet his excited feelings and lend patience wings over a weary road. His previous visit to the magnificent city had been cursory, leaving much to discover beyond the standard tour. He spent the cooler hours wandering observantly through the streets, the quay, and the environs, often taking a boat to admire the grand view of the city and harbor from the sea. All sights, all subjects, even the anticipated meeting with his mother, found a central union in Mordecai and Mirah, and the ideas immediately associated with them. Among the thoughts that filled his mind while his boat drifted near the great harbor was the image of the multitudinous Spanish Jews centuries ago, driven destitute from their homes, allowed to land only for a brief rest on this grand quay of Genoa. They overspread it with a pall of famine and plague—dying mothers clutching dying children, fathers and sons gazing at each other's haggardness, like groups from a hundred Hunger-towers exposed beneath the midday sun. Inevitably, dreamy constructions of a possible ancestry for himself wove themselves with these historic memories, which had gained new interest since his discovery of Mirah. Now, under Mordecai's influence, these thoughts became irresistibly dominant. He would have sealed his mind against such speculations if he could, and he had never fully admitted to himself that he wished the facts to verify Mordecai's conviction. He repeated inwardly that he had no choice in the matter, that wishing was folly—nay, on the question of parentage, wishing seemed part of that meanness which disowns kinship; it was a disowning by anticipation. What he needed to do was simply accept the fact; and now that he knew his mistake about Sir Hugo, he had no strong presumption to rely on. There had been a resolved concealment that made all inference untrustworthy, and the very name he bore might be false. If Mordecai was wrong—if the so-called Daniel Deronda was held by ties entirely aloof from the path his friend's pathetic hope had marked out?—he would not say "I wish"; but he could not help feeling on which side the sacrifice lay.

Across these two importunate thoughts, which he resisted as best one can in that unstrung condition of suspense, came continually an anxiety he made no effort to banish. He dwelt on it with a mournfulness that often feels like the best atonement one can offer for a need one has been unable to meet. This anxiety was for Gwendolen. In the wonderful mixtures of human nature, there exists a feeling distinct from exclusive passionate love, yet not identical with friendship or mere benevolence. A man, in Deronda's case, represents this shade of feeling most nearly in the words, "I should have loved her, if——": the "if" covering some prior growth in inclination or circumstances that created an inward prohibitory law, a stay against emotions ready to quiver out of balance. The "if" in Deronda's case carried reasons of both kinds; yet throughout his relations with Gwendolen, he had never been free from the nervous consciousness that there was something to guard against—not only on her account but on his own. Some precipitancy in the manifestation of impulsive feeling, some ruinous inroad of the momentary upon the permanent treasure of the heart, some spoiling of her trust, wrought upon him now as if it were the retreating cry of a creature snatched and carried out of his reach by swift horsemen or swifter waves, while his own strength was only a stronger sense of weakness. How could his feelings for Gwendolen ever be exactly like his feelings for other women, even when there was one by whose side he desired to stand apart? Strangely, her figure entered into the pictures of his present and future; strangely (and now it seemed sadly), their two lots had come in contact, hers narrowly personal, his charged with far-reaching sensibilities, perhaps with durable purposes, which were hardly more present to her than the reasons why men migrate are present to the birds that come as usual for the crumbs and find them no more. Not that Deronda was too ready to imagine himself of supreme importance to a woman; but her words of insistence that he must "remain near her—must not forsake her"—continually recurred to him with the clearness and importunity of imagined sounds, such as Dante has said pierce us like arrows whose points carry the sharpness of pity:

"Lamenti saettaron me diversi
Che di pietà ferrati avean gli strali".

Day after day passed, and the very air of Italy seemed to carry the consciousness that war had been declared against Austria, every day a hurrying march of crowded Time toward the world-changing battle of Sadowa. Meanwhile, in Genoa, the noons grew hotter, the converging outer roads deeper with white dust, the oleanders in the tubs along the wayside gardens looking more and more like fatigued holiday-makers, and the sweet evening changed her office—scattering abroad those whom the midday had sent under shelter, and sowing all paths with happy social sounds, little tinklings of mule-bells and whirrings of thrumed strings, light footsteps and voices, if not leisurely, then with the hurry of pleasure in them; while the encircling heights, crowned with forts, skirted with fine dwellings and gardens, seemed also to come forth and gaze in fullness of beauty after their long siesta, till all strong color melted in the stream of moonlight which made the Streets a new spectacle with shadows, both still and moving, on cathedral steps and against the façades of massive palaces; and then slowly, with the descending moon, all sank in deep night and silence, and nothing shone but the port lights of the great Lanterna in the blackness below, and the glimmering stars in the blackness above. Deronda, in his suspense, watched this revolving of the days as he might have watched a wonderful clock where the striking of the hours was made solemn with antique figures advancing and retreating in monitory procession, while he still kept his ear open for another kind of signal which would have its solemnity too. He was beginning to sicken of occupation, finding himself contemplating all activity with the aloofness of a prisoner awaiting ransom. In his letters to Mordecai and Hans, he had avoided writing about himself, but he was really entering that state of mind to which all subjects become personal; and the few books he had brought to make him a refuge in study were becoming unreadable, because the point of view that life would make for him was in that agitating moment of uncertainty which is close upon decision.

Many nights were watched through by him in gazing from the open window of his room on the double, faintly pierced darkness of the sea and the heavens; often in struggling under the oppressive skepticism which represented his particular lot, with all the importance he was allowing Mordecai to give it, as of no more lasting effect than a dream—a set of changes which made passion to him, but beyond his consciousness were no more than an imperceptible difference of mass and shadow; sometimes with a reaction of emotive force which gave even to sustained disappointment, even to the fulfilled demand of sacrifice, the nature of a satisfied energy, and spread over his young future, whatever it might be, the attraction of devoted service; sometimes with a sweet, irresistible hopefulness that the very best of human possibilities might befall him—the blending of a complete personal love in one current with a larger duty; and sometimes again in a mood of rebellion (what human creature escapes it?) against things in general because they are thus and not otherwise, a mood in which Gwendolen and her equivocal fate moved as busy images of what was amiss in the world along with the concealments which he had felt as a hardship in his own life, and which were acting in him now under the form of an afflicting doubtfulness about the mother who had announced herself coldly and still kept away.
