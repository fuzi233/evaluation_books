The clock struck four, marking the precise moment my consciousness fractured from the heavy fog of exhaustion back into the razor-sharp agony of dehydration. Sleep had been a cruel illusion; I had dreamt of cooling currents and verdant banks, only to wake gasping in this suffocating arid wasteland. As Umbopa had forewarned, the margin for error was zero. Without water today, we perished. In this crushing heat, humanity is a fragile vessel, unable to withstand the vacuum of thirst for long. I forced myself upright, scrubbing my grimy visage with hands hardened and cracked by the desert wind. My lips and eyelids were fused together by salt and dust, requiring violent friction just to pry my vision open. Dawn was approaching, yet the air possessed none of its usual crisp clarity; instead, it hung thick with a brooding, feverish murkness that pressed against my skin. The camp remained silent, the others still trapped in their slumber.

Soon, the gloom thinned enough to permit reading. I pulled from my pack the small volume of *Ingoldsby Legends* I had carried, turning to "The Jackdaw of Rheims." But the narrative lost all meaning as I reached the line describing the golden ewer filled with water as pure as the flow between Rheims and Namur. Literally, I tried to slap my own cracked lips together, a futile gesture born of madness. The mere concept of that pristine liquid sent a surge of irrational fury through me. If the Cardinal stood there with his bell, book, and candle, I would have lunged forward and drained the vessel dry, regardless of the suds used to cleanse it or the divine curses that might follow. Perhaps the delirium of thirst and starvation had already loosened my grip on reason; I imagined the Cardinal's shock at witnessing a scorched, brown-eyed, grizzly-haired elephant hunter thrusting his filthy face into a basin to gulp down every drop. The image was so grotesquely amusing that I erupted into a cackle, a sound harsh and unnatural in the stillness. This broke the spell of sleep for the others, who groggily began rubbing their own dirt-caked faces and dragging their gummed-up lids apart.

Once fully roused, we convened to assess our dire predicament. There was not a single drop remaining. We inverted the bottles, licking the rims until our tongues bled, but they were as dry as bone. Good, the keeper of the brandy flask, produced it with longing eyes, but Sir Henry swiftly confiscated it, knowing that consuming raw spirit would only accelerate our demise.

"If we do not find water, we shall die," Sir Henry stated, his voice grave.

"If we trust the old Dom's map, there should be some nearby," I countered, though the words felt hollow. No one shared my optimism; the map was a relic of uncertain faith. As the grey light intensified, casting stark definitions upon the landscape, I noticed Ventvögel, the Hottentot guide, rising and pacing with his gaze fixed low on the ground. Suddenly, he halted, emitting a guttural cry and pointing a finger at the earth.

"What is it?" we demanded, scrambling to his side.

"Well," I replied, scanning the sand, "that is fresh Springbok spoor. So what?"

"Springbucks do not stray far from water," Ventvögel answered in Dutch.

"No," I admitted, a sudden wave of relief washing over me, "I forgot. Thank God for that."

This slender thread of hope reanimated us. Desperation makes men cling to straws as if they were lifelines, feeling a perverse joy in the smallest sign of survival. While Ventvögel lifted his snub nose, sniffing the hot air with the intensity of an old Impala ram detecting danger, he spoke again.

"I _smell_ water," he declared.

Jubilance surged through us; we knew the wild-born possessed instincts honed by generations of survival. Just then, the sun breached the horizon with glorious force, illuminating a spectacle so magnificent that for a fleeting moment, our thirst was forgotten. There, soaring less than fifty miles away and glittering like molten silver in the morning rays, stood Sheba's Breasts. Stretching outward for hundreds of miles on either flank lay the great Suliman Berg. Attempting to articulate the sheer grandeur of this sight renders language impotent. Before us rose two colossal mountains, unique in Africa and perhaps unmatched anywhere on Earth, each towering fifteen thousand feet high. Linked by a precipitous cliff of rock, they stood in awful, white solemnity, piercing the sky like the pillars of a gigantic gateway. Shaped distinctly like a woman's breasts, the mists and shadows beneath them occasionally coalesced into the form of a recumbent figure, veiled in mysterious sleep. Their bases swelled gently from the plain, appearing perfectly round and smooth from afar, while crowning each peak was a vast hillock of snow, mirroring the nipple of the breast below. The connecting cliff, thousands of feet high and perfectly vertical, flanked by similar lines of rock broken only by flat, table-topped summits reminiscent of Cape Town, created a formation of terrifying beauty.

To describe the comprehensive majesty of that view exceeds my capabilities. Those massive extinct volcanoes radiated an inexpressible solemnity that awed us into silence. Morning light danced across the snow and the brown, swelling masses below, until strange vapors and clouds gathered, increasing in density until the mountains were reduced to ghostly outlines visible only through a fleecy envelope. Indeed, as we later learned, they were usually shrouded in this gauze-like mist, explaining why we had not seen them clearly before.

Scarcely had Sheba's Breasts vanished into cloud-clad privacy when our thirst—a burning, insistent question—reasserted its dominance. Ventvögel claimed to smell water, yet our eyes saw nothing but arid, sweltering sand and sparse karoo scrub. We circled the hillock, peering anxiously in every direction, but found no pan, no pool, no spring.

"You are a fool," I snapped at Ventvögel. "There is no water."

Yet he lifted his ugly snub nose and sniffed again.

"I smell it, Baas," he insisted. "It is somewhere in the air."

"Yes," I retorted cynically, "no doubt it is in the clouds, destined to fall in two months' time to wash our bones."

Sir Henry stroked his yellow beard thoughtfully. "Perhaps it is on the top of the hill."

"Rot," scoffed Good. "Whoever heard of water atop a hill!"

"Let us go and look," I urged, and hopelessly, we scrambled up the sandy slopes, Umbopa leading. He stopped abruptly, as if petrified.

"_Nanzia manzie!_" he cried loudly—"Here is water!"

We rushed to him, and there, nestled in a deep cut on the very summit of the sand koppie, lay an undeniable pool. Its origin was a mystery we ignored, nor did we hesitate despite its black, unpleasant appearance. It was water, or a convincing imitation thereof, and that was sufficient. We leaped forward, diving onto our stomachs to suck up the uninviting fluid as though it were ambrosia. Heaven knows how we drank! Once sated, we tore off our clothes and submerged ourselves in the pool, letting the moisture seep through our parched skins. You, Harry, accustomed to summoning hot and cold water from unseen cisterns with a mere twist of a tap, can hardly conceive the luxury of wallowing in such muddy, brackish tepidity.

Refreshed, we rose and devoured our biltong, which we had barely touched for twenty-four hours, eating our fill. We smoked pipes and lay beside the blessed pool, sheltered by its overhanging bank, sleeping until noon. All day we rested, grateful for the find, however foul, and offering thanks to the shade of the departed da Silvestra, whose accurate notation on the tail of his shirt had guided us. The marvel was that the pan had persisted so long; I suspected it was fed by a spring deep within the sands.

Filled our bottles to the brim, we resumed our march under the moonlight, spirits buoyed. That night we covered nearly twenty-five miles, though we found no more water, save for a brief respite of shade behind ant-heaps the following day. When the sun rose and burned away the mysterious mists, Suliman's Berg and its twin Breasts, now only twenty miles distant, loomed grander than ever, towering directly above us. By evening, marching onward, we found ourselves by daylight the next morning upon the lowest slopes of Sheba's left breast. Our water was gone again, and the prospect of relief seemed impossible until we reached the snowline far above. Driven by torture, we rested briefly before continuing, toiling painfully up the lava slopes. We discovered the mountain's base was composed entirely of lava beds, belched forth by the earth's bowels in ages past.

By eleven o'clock, we were utterly exhausted, our bodies in a terrible state. The lava clinker, though smoother than the infamous fields of Ascension, was rough enough to blister our feet, compounding our misery. A few hundred yards ahead, large lumps of lava offered potential shade. Upon reaching them, we were surprised to find a nearby plateau covered in dense green growth. Decomposed lava had formed soil, collecting seeds dropped by birds. Yet, we paid little heed; one cannot survive on grass alone unless blessed by Providence with peculiar digestion.
