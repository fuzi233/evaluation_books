When first we were introduced to Sir Miles Warrington at the King’s drawing-room in St. James’s Palace, his round, jovial face, broad waistcoat, and hearty country manner led me to believe we had encountered the very model of an English gentleman—a figure praised in ballads and toasted in taverns, the very pinnacle of earthly contentment.

To be such a gentleman is to occupy a station just shy of divinity. Imagine: an unencumbered estate, rents paid promptly by grateful tenants who bless their luck in having so benevolent a landlord; a life of hunting three days a week, robust health, and a perpetual appetite; a pew at church where the parishioners whisper blessings as you pass, the first man in the parish, the benefactor, the moral exemplar. Could such a man truly call himself a *miserable sinner*? Nonsense! Give a man health, wealth, admiration, and the devotion of his family, and you will find no humility in him. Gout and solitude may humble a prince, but in the full flush of vigor and prosperity, misery is but a word in the prayer book.

Sir Miles was such a man—content, self-satisfied, convinced of his own goodness. His wife was the very pattern of matronly virtue, his children obedient, his tenants deferential. When his grand coach rolled through the market square, hats flew off, curtseys dipped, and tradesmen fawned. The ladies within dispensed gracious smiles and condescended to purchase a yard of ribbon or a packet of seeds, as if their patronage were a royal favor.

But what if those smiles were hollow? What if the poor hid their geese when her ladyship passed, knowing she would demand them at half-price? What if the charity broth were watered down and the tenants’ beer thin as dishwater? Such gentlemen and ladies existed—pious in form, parsimonious in fact, performing virtue while practicing thrift.

Take, for instance, young Harry Warrington’s recent misfortune. When he appealed to his relations for aid, his aunt sent him a tract and her blessing; his uncle was *unavoidably detained* by business out of town. How often does this scene play out among the respectable? Macbeth might shudder after his crime, but Lord and Lady Skinflint sleep soundly after refusing a kinsman in need. No remorse troubles them—only indignation that the boy should be so reckless.

*"A fortune wasted among gamblers and actors!"* lamented Lady Warrington. *"Let this be a lesson, my dears!"*

*"Dreadful, dreadful!"* echoed Sir Miles, shaking his head.

The children, well-trained in morality, chimed in with dutiful horror—all except young Miles, who, with the brutal honesty of childhood, declared, *"I like Harry. If he’s in prison, I’ll give him my half-guinea!"*

But then—a revelation! News arrived that Harry was no penniless outcast, but the victim of a case of mistaken identity. His brother, George, the *true* heir, had arrived, and the two had been seen at Ranelagh, so alike that only their coat colors distinguished them.

*"An impostor!"* roared Sir Miles. *"A beggarly second son!"*

The virtuous indignation was swift. Lady Warrington clutched her bosom. *"To think we harbored a deceiver!"* The sisters gasped. Only little Miles remained loyal, weeping at the injustice until the butler announced the arrival of the two young men themselves.

George, grave and composed, bowed with icy courtesy. Harry, flushed and defensive, stood by his brother. The family’s welcome was as stiff as their starched collars.

*"We dine elsewhere today,"* said George, cutting short Sir Miles’s forced joviality.

*"Oh? With those country Lamberts?"* sneered one sister.

*"They are the best people in England!"* Harry burst out, his loyalty undimmed by their scorn.

The ladies exchanged glances. *"How very charitable,"* murmured Dora.

As the brothers departed, the family dissected their characters anew. *"The elder seems more sensible,"* mused Lady Warrington. *"The younger is clearly reckless."*

*"Or perhaps,"* said Dora with a smirk, *"they are both as poor as each other."*

And so the evening passed—in moral reflections, veiled insults, and the occasional petty quarrel—while the virtues of economy, prudence, and self-righteousness were upheld with unwavering conviction.
