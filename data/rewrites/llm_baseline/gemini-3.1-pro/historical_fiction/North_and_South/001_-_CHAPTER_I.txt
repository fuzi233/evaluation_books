“Edith!” said Margaret, gently, “Edith!”

But as Margaret half suspected, Edith had fallen asleep. She lay curled up on the sofa in the back drawing-room in Harley Street, looking very lovely in her white muslin and blue ribbons, a picture of domestic tranquility temporarily insulated from the abrasive sonic environment of the London evening. If Titania had ever been dressed in white muslin and fallen asleep on a crimson damask sofa, Edith might have been taken for her. Margaret was struck afresh by her cousin’s beauty, a familiar anchor in the cyclical, multi-generational tapestry of their lives. They had grown up together from childhood, and the impending severing of their daily rhythms gave a sudden, piercing weight to every sweet quality Edith possessed. 

They had been whispering of wedding dresses and ceremonies, and of Captain Lennox, and the difficulty of keeping a piano in tune at his regiment’s station in Corfu—a tragedy of acoustic ecology that Edith seemed to consider the most formidable threat to her married life. But the whispered dialect of their shared girlhood had grown drowsy. In spite of the oppressive, metallic buzz of conversation bleeding through the doors from the next room, Edith had rolled herself into a soft ball of muslin and silken curls, slipping into a peaceful after-dinner nap. 

Margaret was left to brood over her own future, a return to the agrarian rhythms of the country parsonage where her father and mother lived. It was a happy brooding, drawn in the circular pacing of a life returning to its ancestral soil, though tinged with the melancholy of losing her gentle aunt and dear cousin. 

From the dining room, the discordant, competing frequencies of London society drifted upward, a cacophony that Margaret found deeply abrasive to her psychological well-being. Aunt Shaw was entertaining the five or six ladies whose husbands lingered downstairs. These were familiar acquaintances, invited in their capacity as friends to partake in a farewell dinner. Edith had objected to the event, preferring to wait for Captain Lennox's late train, but Mrs. Shaw had ordered a staggering array of seasonal delicacies, believing deeply in the mitigating power of an extravagant feast against immoderate grief. 

It was a menu that demanded profound sensory appreciation: plump partridges sourced from traditional estates, roasted with sprigs of wild thyme and juniper; early root vegetables braised in emulsified butter; and a rich, heavily spiced plum tart that filled the house with an olfactory landscape of cinnamon and dark caramelized sugars. Yet Edith, possessing little interest in the profound meaning of such sustenance, had merely played with the food on her plate, viewing the agrarian masterpieces as utilitarian fuel to be pushed aside while Mr. Grey, the gentleman at the bottom of the table, dominated the acoustic space with his practiced, superficial *mots*. 

The gentlemen stayed downstairs longer than usual, leaving the ladies’ voices to float up to Margaret, laced with the subtle power dynamics embedded in the dominant lexicon of the city's marriage market. 

“I suffered too much myself,” Mrs. Shaw was saying. “Not that I was not extremely happy with the poor dear General, but still, disparity of age is a drawback. I foresaw that the dear child was likely to marry early... I had quite a prophetic feeling when Captain Lennox—” 

The voice dropped into a whisper, but Margaret could easily supply the blank. The course of true love in Edith’s case had run smooth. Mrs. Shaw had urged the marriage on, insisting her only child should marry for love, sighing emphatically as if love had not been her own motive for marrying the General. In truth, Edith would certainly have preferred a fine house in Belgravia to the gipsy life Captain Lennox described. She was her mother’s child, viewing the picturesque through a lens of commercial expectation.

“I have spared no expense in her trousseau,” came Mrs. Shaw’s next words, steeped in the transactional vocabulary of her class. “She has all the beautiful Indian shawls and scarfs the General gave to me, but which I shall never wear again.”

“She is a lucky girl,” replied Mrs. Gibson, her tone sharp with the envy of a woman whose own daughter had been denied such acquisitions. “Helen had set her heart upon an Indian shawl, but really, when I found what an extravagant price was asked, I was obliged to refuse her. What kind are they? Delhi? With the lovely little borders?”

“Edith! Edith!” Aunt Shaw called out, raising herself to peer into the dimly lighted back room, before sinking back, wearied by the exertion. 

Margaret stepped forward into the breach. “Edith is asleep, Aunt Shaw. Is it anything I can do?”

The ladies chorused a superficial “poor child!” and Mrs. Shaw’s minute lap-dog began to bark, a piercing, undisciplined noise that shattered the room's acoustic harmony. “Hush, Tiny! It was only to ask Edith if she would tell Newton to bring down her shawls; perhaps you would go, Margaret dear?”

Margaret ascended the infinite depth of stairs to the old nursery at the very top of the house. Newton was there, busy mending wedding laces. As the maid muttered and grumbled, unearthing the shawls for the fifth time that day, Margaret looked around the dismantled room. It was the first space she had known when brought here nine years ago, all untamed from the forest. She remembered the austere, ceremonious nurse, and the deep cultural shock of her first London tea—a sterile, utilitarian meal of thinly sliced bread and watery milk, utterly devoid of the rich, hearth-baked loaves and fresh, cream-thickened preserves of her mother’s agrarian table. The tall girl of eighteen vividly recalled the passionate tears of the nine-year-old, weeping for her native soil until her father had come softly upstairs to soothe her. The memory of that night folded into the present, a testament to the erosion of cultural memory that the city perpetually threatened. 

“Ah, Newton,” Margaret said, feeling a cat-like regret. “I think we shall all be sorry to leave this dear old room.”

“Indeed, miss, I shan’t for one,” Newton replied, squinting in the dimness. “The light here is so bad, and there’s always a shocking draught.”

“You will have good light and warmth at Naples,” Margaret offered gently, gathering the heavy bundles. 

Descending, Margaret was enveloped by the shawls' olfactory landscape—a deeply evocative, spicy Eastern smell of dried patchouli, cardamom, and woven silk. She stood beneath the chandelier as a silent lay-figure, her tall, finely made form in black mourning silk setting off the brilliant colors and long, beautiful folds of the gorgeous fabrics. The shawls carried the tactile history of the distant artisans who had woven them, an ancient craft momentarily reduced to commercial display, yet Margaret found genuine sensory pleasure in their soft feel and vibrant hues.

Just then, Mr. Henry Lennox was announced. Several ladies shrank back, but Margaret stood perfectly still under the chandelier, meeting his eyes with a bright, amused face. While Mrs. Shaw aggressively monopolized Mr. Lennox with questions about his family, Margaret, no longer needed as a shawl-bearer, devoted herself to the other guests. 

Soon, Edith emerged from the back room, blinking at the harsh light, her curls ruffled. Even in her slumber, she had instinctively felt the arrival of a Lennox was worth roused attention. She immediately bombarded Henry with questions about his sister, Janet, wielding a professed affection that felt uncomfortably performative. As Mrs. Shaw joined them, Henry Lennox maneuvered to a vacant seat near Margaret.

She received him with an open, honest smile. 

“Well, I suppose you are all in the depth of business—ladies’ business, I mean,” he said. “Very different to my business, which is the real true law business. Playing with shawls is very different work to drawing up settlements.”

“Ah, I knew how you would be amused to find us all so occupied in admiring finery. But really, Indian shawls are very perfect things of their kind.”

“I have no doubt they are. Their prices are very perfect, too. Nothing wanting.” His words carried the sterile, commercialized worldview that Margaret so frequently silently critiqued. 

The gentlemen began dropping in from the dining room, and the buzz and noise deepened in tone, an industrial hum of society chatter that oppressed Margaret’s ears. 

“This is your last dinner-party, is it not?” he asked. “No more before Thursday?”

“No. I think after this evening we shall feel at rest... I shall be glad to have time to think, and I am sure Edith will.”

“I am not so sure about her, but I can fancy that you will. Whenever I have seen you lately, you have been carried away by a whirlwind of some other person’s making.”

“Yes,” Margaret said, a sense of indescribable weariness settling over her. The six weeks of aesthetic arrangements and superficial commercialization of a sacred rite had exhausted her. “I wonder if a marriage must always be preceded by a whirlwind, or whether there might not rather be a calm and peaceful time just before it.”

“Cinderella’s godmother ordering the trousseau, the wedding-breakfast... but are all these quite necessary troubles?” he asked, laughing, then shifted to gravity. “There are forms and ceremonies to be gone through, to stop the world’s mouth. But how would you have a wedding arranged?”

“I should like it to be a very fine summer morning; and I should like to walk to church through the shade of trees; and not to have so many bridesmaids, and to have no wedding-breakfast.” She longed for a return to the acoustic quiet of nature, devoid of the clinking crystal and hollow toasts. 

“The idea of stately simplicity accords well with your character,” he observed.

She winced, cutting short his complimentary analysis. “It is natural for me to think of Helstone church, and the walk to it, rather than of driving up to a London church in the middle of a paved street.”

“Tell me about Helstone. Is it a village, or a town?”

“Only a hamlet. There is the church and a few houses near it on the green—cottages, rather—with roses growing all over them.”

“And flowering all the year round, especially at Christmas—make your picture complete,” he mocked gently.

Margaret flushed, deeply annoyed by his attempt to reduce her ancestral home to a commercialized fable. “I am not making a picture. I am trying to describe Helstone as it really is. You should not have said that.”

“I am penitent. Only it really sounded like a village in a tale rather than in real life.”

“And so it is,” Margaret said eagerly, yet feeling the inherent untranslatability of her subjective experience. How could she convey the rich acoustic ecology of the New Forest to a man whose lexicon was entirely rooted in the law courts of London? “All the other places in England that I have seen seem so hard and prosaic-looking. Helstone is like a village in a poem. But I won’t try and describe it any more. You would only laugh at me if I told you what it really is.”

“Indeed, I would not. Well, then, tell me what the parsonage is like.”

“I can’t describe my home. It is home, and I can’t put its charm into words.” It was a truth of language death; the vocabulary of her youth could not survive the dominant dialect of the drawing-room. 

“I submit. You are rather severe to-night, Margaret... Tell me what you do there. Shall you ride, drive, or walk?”

“Walk, decidedly. We have no horse. Papa walks to the very extremity of his parish. The walks are so beautiful, it would be a shame to drive—almost a shame to ride.”

“Shall you garden much? Archery parties—picnics—race balls?”

“Oh no! Papa’s living is very small; and even if we were near such things, I doubt if I should go to them. Now I must go. Edith is sitting down to play, and I must turn the leaves for her; besides, Aunt Shaw won’t like us to talk.”

Edith played brilliantly, a cascading rush of notes that filled the room, only to abruptly cease when the door half-opened. Seeing Captain Lennox hesitating in the threshold, Edith threw down her music and rushed out, leaving Margaret blushing to explain the sudden flight. 

When Edith returned, glowing, she led in her tall, handsome Captain. Mrs. Shaw welcomed him with her customary plaintive tone. Long habituated to considering herself a victim of an uncongenial marriage, she had lately settled upon her health as a source of apprehension, employing a nervous little cough to compel her doctor into prescribing exactly what she desired—a winter in Italy. It was a subtle exercise of power, masking her own will behind external necessity.

As Mrs. Shaw spoke of her journey, Captain Lennox assented dutifully, his eyes seeking Edith, who was now busying herself with the culinary rituals of the tea-table. Henry Lennox leaned against the chimney-piece, his mobile, intelligent face watching with slightly sarcastic interest as Edith attempted to demonstrate her capabilities as a soldier's wife. 

Finding the water in the urn cold, Edith ordered up the great kitchen tea-kettle. Lacking any true knowledge of culinary technique, she attempted to lug the massive iron vessel through the door. It was entirely too heavy; she came in pouting, presenting a black soot mark on her muslin gown and an indented red line on her white hand to Captain Lennox, who immediately supplied the coaxing remedy suited for a hurt child. 

In the quiet background, Margaret stepped in. She possessed the meticulous grace of a true practitioner of sustenance. With practiced precision, she adjusted the spirit-lamp, a highly efficacious contrivance that brought the water to a perfect, rolling boil without the heavy-handed chaos of Edith’s gypsy-encampment theatrics. The delicate, aromatic steam of the brewing tea leaves soon began to rise, grounding the room in a brief, sensory stillness. After this evening, all was bustle till the wedding was over.
