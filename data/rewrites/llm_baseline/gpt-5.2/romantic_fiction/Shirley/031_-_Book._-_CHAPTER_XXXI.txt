The die, once thrown, rolled to its last, clear sound. Sir Philip Nunnely heard it; Shirley heard it; Mr. Sympson—whose ear was always pressed to the keyhole of circumstance—heard it loudest of all. That evening’s dinner at Nunnely Priory—Fieldhead gathered under the Priory roof—decided the business as surely as if the family plate had been stamped with the verdict.

Two or three small matters, like seasonings that change a whole dish, brought the young baronet to the point. He had watched Shirley through the meal and afterward, and the sight troubled him: Miss Keeldar looked pensive—delicate even—less the shining, untameable mistress of a wide estate than a girl with some hunger she would not name. The change smote him on his weak, poetic side; a sonnet began to brew in his mind as naturally as yeast works in warm batter. While it rose, one of his sisters—well-meaning and fatal—begged Shirley to sit at the piano and sing.

“Sing one of Philip’s,” they urged; and because the request wore the homely authority of a domestic rite—like handing a guest a cup they must taste—Shirley consented.

Just before she went to the instrument she had stood at a window looking down into the park. There was stormy moonlight, the kind that turns grass to tarnished silver and makes every trunk look older than the century that grew it. The oaks spread their dark arms; the beeches towered, heroic and pale; the wind took hold of the whole forest lower down and drew from it a deep, continuous roar, as if the earth itself were exhaling. Clouds rushed past the moon; the eye saw the moon hurrying too, faster still, as though chased. It was a wilderness of sound and motion outside, and the house—full of carpets, curtains, measured voices—seemed all at once a little boat on that black tide.

She turned from sight and from that vast, bruising music of the gale, touched—if not carried away—wakened—if not inspired.

She sang.

The ballad was full of love: love that would not be sent away; love that, when disaster came, clung closer; love that grew tenderer in poverty. It was set to a fine old air; the words, read on a page, might have seemed merely simple and sweet. But when well sung they wanted nothing. Shirley sang them well. She gave the feeling softness as if she had warmed it at her breath; she poured round the passion a force that made it live. Her voice that night had a dramatic clearness; it filled the old saloon, and in filling it seemed to hush the room’s smaller noises—the rustle of silk, the careful little coughs, even the stiff tapping of a foot—so that the soundscape belonged to her alone.

All were impressed; one was charmed.

When she left the piano she went to the fire and sat down on a low seat—half stool, half cushion—near enough to feel the steady heat on her hands. The ladies gathered about her; none spoke at first. The Misses Sympson and the Misses Nunnely looked at her with the fixed, startled gaze of quiet poultry observing some bright, foreign bird strayed into their yard. What made her sing so? They never sang so. Was it proper to sing with such expression—with such originality, so unlike a schoolgirl’s neat little performance? Decidedly not. Strange was wrong; unusual was improper. In their minds she was already condemned—by a lexicon in which “woman” must translate to “rule,” and “rule” meant smallness, softness, restraint.

Old Lady Nunnely, seated stonily in her great chair by the hearth, watched too. Her look was not curiosity but objection, cold as a shut larder. It said plainly: this woman is not of my kind; I do not choose her for my son’s wife.

Sir Philip caught the meaning as surely as if it had been spoken aloud. Alarm pricked him. What he yearned for there was danger he might lose—not by Shirley’s refusal, perhaps, but by his mother’s hardening influence. He must make haste.

The room they sat in had once been a picture-gallery; Sir Monckton—Sir Philip’s father—had turned it into a saloon, but it still kept a long, shadowy, withdrawing look, like a corridor in a memory. In one deep recess a window made a room within the room; it held one couch, one table, and a small cabinet that looked almost fairy in its delicate inlay. Two people standing there might speak to each other, and if their words were neither loud nor long, the rest might remain happily ignorant.

Sir Philip contrived it. He induced two of his sisters to perform a duet—long enough to occupy the general attention, loud enough to cover a murmur. He gave the Misses Sympson their own share of business. The elder ladies sank into talk. Then he watched with relief as Shirley rose and began to stroll, apparently idly, from picture to picture.

He joined her and spoke of an ancestress—a dark beauty, southern as a hot-house flower. He told his tale with that gentle pride which, in another man, might have been attractive; in him it was boyish, but sincere. The recess-cabinet held mementoes of the same lady, and when Shirley stooped to examine a missal and a rosary lying on an inlaid shelf—small, intimate tokens, as domestic in their way as a wooden spoon rubbed smooth by generations—Sir Philip stooped too.

The Misses Nunnely, meanwhile, indulged in a prolonged screech of music in the open room: pure of expression, guiltless of meaning, perfectly conventional—sound made for the sake of sound, like the factory’s din that fills the air without feeding any spirit. It provided cover as a bad smell provides disguise.

In that sheltering noise, Sir Philip whispered a few hurried sentences.

At first Shirley was struck so still you might have fancied the whisper a charm that changed her to stone. Then she looked up and answered—briefly. They parted. She went back to the fire and sat down as before. Sir Philip gazed after her, then returned to stand behind his sisters with an air of studied ease.

Mr. Sympson—Mr. Sympson only—had marked the pantomime.

He drew his conclusions at once, as shallow men do, and as meddling men delight to do. Had he been as acute as he was prying, he might have read in Sir Philip’s face something to correct his inference; but he was ever hasty, ever positive. He went home that night in a state of triumph, inflated as a bladder.

Nor could he keep his secret. The next morning he had occasion to use his son’s tutor as a secretary; and because elation made him talk as hunger makes a man chew noisily, he announced, with mouthing accents and flimsy pomp, that the tutor would do well to hold himself ready for a return south, since the important business which had detained Mr. Sympson in Yorkshire was now on the eve of fortunate completion. His anxious, laborious efforts would at last be crowned with the happiest success: a truly eligible connection was about to be added to the family.

“In Sir Philip Nunnely?” Louis Moore conjectured.

Mr. Sympson treated himself at once to a pinch of snuff and a chuckling laugh, checked only by a sudden choke of dignity; he ordered the tutor to proceed with the business in hand.

For two or three days he remained bland as oil; yet he seemed also to sit on pins. When he walked his gait emulated that of a hen treading a hot girdle. He was forever looking out of the window, listening for chariot-wheels, as if good news must arrive with a particular crunch of gravel. Sisera’s mother peering through the lattice was nothing to him; Bluebeard’s wife trembling over keys was tame beside his expectation. He waited for the affair to be opened in form—for himself to be consulted, for lawyers to be summoned, for settlements to be discussed, for the delicious worldly fuss to begin: that loud, respectable clatter of wealth which, like industrial noise, pretends to be music.

At last a letter came. He himself handed it to Shirley out of the bag. He knew the handwriting; he knew the crest upon the seal; his fingers almost tingled with the desire to feel the paper opened.

But Shirley carried it to her own room. He did not see it read. He did not see it answered. She wrote her reply with the door shut and was long about it—the best part of the day—as if every sentence needed to be weighed like spice, lest too much ruin the whole. When he questioned her, she said only, “Yes,” it is answered.

Then he waited again—this time in silence, not daring to speak, held mute by something in Shirley’s face: an awful something to him, inscrutable as writing on a wall. More than once he was tempted to call Daniel—in the person of Louis Moore—to interpret. Yet dignity forbade the familiarity; and perhaps Daniel himself had his own private difficulty with that baffling language, for he looked like a student for whom grammars are blank and dictionaries dumb.

Some days after, Mr. Sympson went out to De Walden Hall to while away an anxious hour among his friends. He returned sooner than was expected. The family and Shirley were assembled in the oak parlour, where the old panels held the room’s familiar hush—the small domestic acoustics of a life lived indoors: the faint snip of thread, the soft click of a thimble, the controlled murmur of voices.

Addressing Shirley, he requested she would step with him into another room. He wished, he said, to have a “strictly private interview.”

She rose without asking questions and without professing surprise.

“Very well, sir,” she said, in the tone of a determined person told the dentist is come to draw the great double tooth that has tormented him for a month. She left her sewing and her thimble in the window-seat and followed where he led.

Shut into the drawing-room, they took seats opposite each other: arm-chairs, a few yards between—space enough for pride to breathe, not enough for peace.

“I have been to De Walden Hall,” Mr. Sympson began. He paused, waiting for the weight of his announcement to fall like a hammer.

Shirley’s eyes rested on the pretty white-and-green carpet. That information required no reply; she gave none.

“I have learned,” he pursued slowly, “I have learned a circumstance which surprises me.”

Resting her cheek on her forefinger, she waited.

“It seems that Nunnely Priory is shut up—that the family are gone back to their place in ——shire. It seems that the baronet—that the baronet—that Sir Philip himself has accompanied his mother and sisters.”

“Indeed!” said Shirley.

“May I ask if you share the amazement with which I received this news?”

“No, sir.”

“Is it news to you?”

“Yes, sir.”

“I mean—I mean,” he went on, beginning to fidget, leaving his brief, tolerably clear phrasing and dropping into his customary wordy irritability, “I mean to have a thorough explanation. I will not be put off. I—I—shall insist on being heard, and on—on having my own way. My questions must be answered. I will have clear, satisfactory replies. I am not to be trifled with.”

Shirley said nothing.

“It is a strange and extraordinary thing—a very singular—a most odd thing! I thought all was right, knew no other; and there—the family are gone!”

“I suppose, sir, they had a right to go.”

“Sir Philip is gone!” he repeated, with emphasis, as if the departure were a personal affront.

Shirley lifted her brows. “Bon voyage!” she said.

“This will not do; this must be altered, ma’am.”

He drew his chair forward; pushed it back; looked perfectly incensed and perfectly helpless.

“Come, come, uncle,” Shirley expostulated, “do not begin to fret and fume, or we shall make no sense of the business. Ask me what you want to know. I am as willing to come to an explanation as you. I promise you truthful replies.”

“I want—I demand to know, Miss Keeldar, whether Sir Philip has made you an offer?”

“He has.”

“You avow it?”

“I avow it. Now go on. Consider that point settled.”

“He made you an offer that night we dined at the priory?”

“It is enough to say that he made it. Go on.”

“He proposed in the recess—in the room that used to be a picture-gallery—that Sir Monckton converted into a saloon?”

Shirley did not answer.

“You were both examining a cabinet. I saw it all. My sagacity was not at fault—it never is. Subsequently you received a letter from him. On what subject—of what nature were the contents?”

“No matter.”

“Ma’am, is that the way you speak to me?”

Shirley’s foot tapped quick upon the carpet, a little percussion that answered his bluster with its own sharp impatience.

“There you sit, silent and sullen—you who promised truthful replies.”

“Sir, I have answered you thus far. Proceed.”

“I should like to see that letter.”

“You cannot see it.”

“I must and shall, ma’am; I am your guardian.”

“Having ceased to be a ward, I have no guardian.”

“Ungrateful being! Reared by me as my own daughter—”

“Once more, uncle, have the kindness to keep to the point. Let us both remain cool. For my part, I do not wish to get into a passion; but, you know, once drive me beyond certain bounds, I care little what I say—I am not then soon checked. Listen: you asked me whether Sir Philip made me an offer. That question is answered. What do you wish to know next?”

“I desire to know whether you accepted or refused him, and know it I will.”

“Certainly, you ought to know it. I refused him.”

“Refused him! You—you, Shirley Keeldar, refused Sir Philip Nunnely?”

“I did.”

The poor gentleman sprang from his chair and rushed—then trotted—through the room, making more noise than the truth required.

“There it is! There it is! There it is!”

“Sincerely speaking, I am sorry, uncle, you are so disappointed.”

But concession and contrition are wasted on some tempers; they do not soften them, they only brace them to harder resistance. Mr. Sympson was of that number.

“I disappointed? What is it to me? Have I an interest in it? You would insinuate, perhaps, that I have motives?”

“Most people have motives of some sort for their actions.”

“She accuses me to my face! I, that have been a parent to her, she charges with bad motives!”

“Bad motives I did not say.”

“And now you prevaricate; you have no principles!”

“Uncle, you tire me. I want to go away.”

“Go you shall not! I will be answered. What are your intentions, Miss Keeldar?”

“In what respect?”

“In respect of matrimony?”

“To be quiet, and to do just as I please.”

“Just as you please! The words are to the last degree indecorous.”

“Mr. Sympson, I advise you not to become insulting. You know I will not bear that.”

“You read French. Your mind is poisoned with French novels. You have imbibed French principles.”

“The ground you are treading now returns a mighty hollow sound under your feet. Beware!”

“It will end in infamy, sooner or later. I have foreseen it all along.”

“Do you assert, sir, that something in which I am concerned will end in infamy?”

“That it will—that it will. You said just now you would act as you please. You acknowledge no rules—no limitations.”

“Silly stuff, and vulgar as silly!”

“Regardless of decorum, you are prepared to fly in the face of propriety.”

“You tire me, uncle.”

“What, madam—what could be your reasons for refusing Sir Philip?”

“At last there is another sensible question; I shall be glad to reply to it. Sir Philip is too young for me. I regard him as a boy. All his relations—his mother especially—would be annoyed if he married me. Such a step would embroil him with them. I am not his equal in the world’s estimation.”

“Is that all?”

“Our dispositions are not compatible.”

“Why, a more amiable gentleman never breathed.”

“He is very amiable—very excellent—truly estimable; but not my master—not in one point. I could not trust myself with his happiness. I would not undertake the keeping of it for thousands. I will accept no hand which cannot hold me in check.”

“I thought you liked to do as you please. You are vastly inconsistent.”

“When I promise to obey, it shall be under the conviction that I can keep that promise. I could not obey a youth like Sir Philip. Besides, he would never command me. He would expect me always to rule—to guide—and I have no taste whatever for the office.”

“You no taste for swaggering, and subduing, and ordering, and ruling?”

“Not my husband; only my uncle.”

“Where is the difference?”

“There is a slight difference—that is certain. And I know full well any man who wishes to live in decent comfort with me as a husband must be able to control me.”

“I wish you had a real tyrant.”

“A tyrant would not hold me for a day, not for an hour. I would rebel—break from him—defy him.”

“Are you not enough to bewilder one’s brain with your self-contradiction?”

“It is evident I bewilder your brain.”

“You talk of Sir Philip being young. He is two-and-twenty.”

“My husband must be thirty, with the sense of forty.”

“You had better pick out some old man—some white-headed or bald-headed swain.”

“No, thank you.”

“You could lead some doting fool; you might pin him to your apron.”

“I might do that with a boy; but it is not my vocation. Did I not say I prefer a master—one in whose presence I shall feel obliged and disposed to be good; one whose control my impatient temper must acknowledge; a man whose approbation can reward, whose displeasure punish me; a man I shall feel it impossible not to love, and very possible to fear?”

“What is there to hinder you from doing all this with Sir Philip? He is a baronet—a man of rank, property, connections far above yours. If you talk of intellect, he is a poet—he writes verses; which you, I take it, cannot do, with all your cleverness.”

“Neither his title, wealth, pedigree, nor poetry avail to invest him with the power I describe. These are feather-weights; they want ballast. A measure of sound, solid, practical sense would have stood him in better stead with me.”

“You and Henry rave about poetry! You used to catch fire like tinder on the subject when you were a girl.”

“O uncle, there is nothing really valuable in this world, there is nothing glorious in the world to come that is not poetry!”

“Marry a poet, then, in God’s name!”

“Show him me, and I will.”

“Sir Philip.”

“Not at all. You are almost as good a poet as he.”

“Madam, you are wandering from the point.”

“Indeed, uncle, I wanted to do so, and I shall be glad to lead you away with me. Do not let us get out of temper with each other; it is not worth while.”

“Out of temper, Miss Keeldar! I should be glad to know who is out of temper.”

“I am not, yet.”

“If you mean to insinuate that I am, I consider that you are guilty of impertinence.”

“You will be soon, if you go on at that rate.”

“There it is! With your pert tongue you would try the patience of a Job.”

“I know I should.”

“No levity, miss! This is not a laughing matter. It is an affair I am resolved to probe thoroughly, convinced there is mischief at the bottom. You described just now, with far too much freedom for your years and sex, the sort of individual you would prefer as a husband. Pray, did you paint from the life?”

Shirley opened her lips; but instead of speaking at once, colour rose in her face—warm and sudden, like wine spilled in milk.

“I shall have an answer to that question,” Mr. Sympson affirmed, swelling with courage on the strength of her confusion.

“It was an historical picture, uncle, from several originals.”

“Several originals! Bless my heart!”

“I have been in love several times.”

“This is cynical.”

“With heroes of many nations.”

“What next—”

“And philosophers.”

“She is mad—”

“Don’t ring the bell, uncle; you will alarm my aunt.”

“Your poor dear aunt! what a niece has she!”

“Once I loved Socrates.”

“Pooh! no trifling, ma’am.”

“I admired Themistocles, Leonidas, Epaminondas.”

“Miss Keeldar—”

“To pass over a few centuries, Washington was a plain man, but I liked him; but to speak of the actual present—”

“Ah! the actual present.”

“To quit crude schoolgirl fancies, and come to realities.”

“Realities! That is the test to which you shall be brought, ma’am.”

“To avow before what altar I now kneel—to reveal the present idol of my soul—”

“You will make haste about it, if you please. It is near luncheon time, and confess you shall.”

“Confess I must. My heart is full of the secret. It must be spoken. I only wish you were Mr. Helstone instead of Mr. Sympson; you would sympathize with me better.”

“Madam, it is a question of common sense and common prudence, not of sympathy and sentiment, and so on. Did you say it was Mr. Helstone?”

“Not precisely, but as near as may be; they are rather alike.”

“I will know the name; I will have particulars.”

“They positively are rather alike. Their very faces are not dissimilar—a pair of human falcons—and dry, direct, decided both. But my hero is the mightier of the two. His mind has the clearness of the deep sea, the patience of its rocks, the force of its billows.”

“Rant and fustian!”

“I dare say he can be harsh as a saw-edge and gruff as a hungry raven.”

“Miss Keeldar, does the person reside in Briarfield? Answer me that.”

“Uncle, I am going to tell you; his name is trembling on my tongue.”

“Speak, girl!”

“That was well said, uncle. ‘Speak, girl!’ It is quite tragic. England has howled savagely against this man, uncle, and she will one day roar exultingly over him. He has been unscared by the howl, and he will be unelated by the shout.”

“I said she was mad. She is.”

“This country will change and change again in her demeanour to him; he will never change in his duty to her. Come, cease to chafe, uncle, I’ll tell you his name.”

“You shall tell me, or—”

“Listen! Arthur Wellesley, Lord Wellington.”

Mr. Sympson rose up furious. He bounced out of the room; but immediately bounced back again, shut the door with decision, and resumed his seat as if he meant to sit upon her will and crush it.

“Ma’am, you shall tell me this. Will your principles permit you to marry a man without money—a man below you?”

“Never a man below me.”

“In a high voice: Will you, Miss Keeldar, marry a poor man?”

“What right have you, Mr. Sympson, to ask me?”

“I insist upon knowing.”

“You don’t go the way to know.”

“My family respectability shall not be compromised.”

“A good resolution; keep it.”

“Madam, it is you who shall keep it.”

“Impossible, sir, since I form no part of your family.”

“Do you disown us?”

“I disdain your dictatorship.”

“Whom will you marry, Miss Keeldar?”

“Not Mr. Sam Wynne, because I scorn him; not Sir Philip Nunnely, because I only esteem him.”

“Whom have you in your eye?”

“Four rejected candidates.”

“Such obstinacy could not be unless you were under improper influence.”

“What do you mean? There are certain phrases potent to make my blood boil. Improper influence! What old woman’s cackle is that?”

“Are you a young lady?”

“I am a thousand times better: I am an honest woman, and as such I will be treated.”

“Do you know”—leaning forward and speaking with ghastly solemnity, as if he were pronouncing a sentence—“do you know the whole neighbourhood teems with rumours respecting you and a bankrupt tenant of yours, the foreigner Moore?”

“Does it?”

“It does. Your name is in every mouth.”

“It honours the lips it crosses, and I wish to the gods it may purify them.”

“Is it that person who has power to influence you?”

“Beyond any whose cause you have advocated.”

“Is it he you will marry?”

“He is handsome, and manly, and commanding.”

“You declare it to my face! The Flemish knave! the low trader!”

“He is talented, and venturous, and resolute. Prince is on his brow, and ruler in his bearing.”

“She glories in it! She conceals nothing! No shame, no fear!”

“When we speak the name of Moore, shame should be forgotten and fear discarded. The Moores know only honour and courage.”

“I say she is mad.”

“You have taunted me till my blood is up; you have worried me till I turn again.”

“That Moore is the brother of my son’s tutor. Would you let the usher call you sister?”

Bright and broad shone Shirley’s eye as she fixed it on him now—clear as flame.

“No, no; not for a province of possession, not for a century of life.”

“You cannot separate the husband from his family.”

“What then?”

“Mr. Louis Moore’s sister you will be.”

Mr. Sympson’s words, meant as a hook, found no flesh to catch. Shirley’s face altered; the colour that had risen fell away, leaving something sterner than paleness—weariness, sick to the heart.

“Mr. Sympson, I am sick at heart with all this weak trash; I will bear no more. Your thoughts are not my thoughts, your aims are not my aims, your gods are not my gods. We do not view things in the same light; we do not measure them by the same standard; we hardly speak in the same tongue. Let us part.”

And as she spoke, it was less the anger of a moment than the long, aching protest of a soul forced to translate itself forever into another’s harsh vocabulary—a dialect that had no words for what she meant, only labels for what it feared.

“It is not,” she resumed, much excited, “it is not that I hate you; you are a good sort of man. Perhaps you mean well in your way. But we cannot suit; we are ever at variance. You annoy me with small meddling, with petty tyranny; you exasperate my temper, and make and keep me passionate. As to your small maxims, your narrow rules, your little prejudices, aversions, dogmas—bundle them off. Mr. Sympson, go, offer them a sacrifice to the deity you worship; I’ll none of them. I wash my hands of the lot. I walk by another creed, light, faith, and hope than you.”

“Another creed!” he exclaimed, as if the phrase itself were treason. “I believe she is an infidel.”

“An infidel to your religion,” said Shirley, her voice steady now, “an atheist to your god.”

“An—atheist!!!”
