Colleges, schools, and Inns of Court cling stubbornly to the ghosts of antiquity, preserving customs that sensible moderns have long discarded. One might argue that a well-run workhouse offers better hygiene and comfort than these venerable institutions. Inside, scholars and lawyers are content to sleep in dusty closets, paying exorbitant rents for rooms that serve double duty as bedrooms and storage cupboards—spaces that cost more than a nice suburban villa or a decent townhouse. Meanwhile, a poor mechanic in Spitalfields commands an endless stream of fresh water from his tap. In contrast, the "gentlemen" of the universities and Inns rely on laundresses and chambermaids to lug water in jugs, inhabiting buildings constructed before the concept of cleanliness ever existed.

Some still sneer at the common folk, but let's be honest: your ancestors were likely the "Great Unwashed." Especially in the Temple, practicing even basic decency required immense effort. Take Old Grump of the Norfolk Circuit, who lived for decades beneath the chambers of Warrington and Pendennis. He was constantly woken by the roar of their shower baths, with water occasionally dripping through the ceiling onto his floor. Grump, now past fifty, declared the whole thing an absurd, dandified folly, cursing the laundress who tracked mud across his stairs. He'd managed perfectly well without running water, just as his fathers had. Yet, among the knights and baronets whose coats of armor line the Upper Temple halls, not one philanthropist devised a system of communal baths for their legal brethren. The Temple historian remains silent on such schemes. While Pump Court and Fountain Court boast hydraulic marvels, no bencher is ever seen splashing in the fountains. Still, one wonders how many a counsel of old might have refreshed himself at the pumps.

Despite their filth, these ancient Inns possess a unique charm, offering a rough-and-ready freedom that lingers in memory. Passersby often indulge in poetic reverie: *"Yonder Eldon lived; here Coke mused on Littleton; here Chitty toiled..."* The man of letters loves a place haunted by such giants. To me, Sir Roger de Coverley strolling the Temple Gardens feels as vivid as Samuel Johnson trudging through the fog toward Dr. Goldsmith's chambers, or Harry Fielding rushing to print midnight articles for *The Covent Garden Journal*.

Imagine a diary of a single day in one of those dingy four-story houses where Pen and Warrington lived. On the ground floor, a successful parliamentary counsel drives off to Belgravia at lunchtime, leaving his clerk—who recently couldn't even get credit for polishing his master's boots—to enjoy the fruits of his mentor's success. Just above, a venerable scholar lives alone, hoarding knowledge and wealth for fifty years, only to die a lonely recluse whose heirs burn half his library. Would you wish for such an end? Above him sits Mr. Grump, playing whist with three seniors after drinking three bottles of port, or sleeping soundly in the Temple Church on Sundays.

On the third landing, where Pen and Warrington reside late into the night, sits Mr. Paley. A college fellow, he reads and annotates cases until dawn, rising at seven to hit the chambers before breakfast. Unlike Pen, who returns from wild escapades, Paley works tirelessly. He hasn't wasted his time; he has forced a brilliant mind to grind against the dull reality of the law, shutting out poetry, philosophy, art, and love. He sees nothing but his books. When he turns off his lamp, he feels satisfied, thankless, and remorseless. Yet, he shudders when meeting Warrington on the stairs, avoiding the younger man as if he were damned. Perhaps it is Paley's cadaverous ambition and smug meanness that repels him, or perhaps it is simply Pen's innate love for joy.

Pen enjoys the Temple life immensely. Though his relatives think he is studying hard, retreating from the boredom of high-society balls, the truth is he is embracing the rough pleasures of a London bachelor. He walks in the Park instead of riding Rotten Row, pulls boats on the river, hikes to Hampstead, and eats modest tavern dinners. His evenings involve pipes, British spirits (always tasted by the laundress, Mrs. Flanagan), and quiet camaraderie. During term-time, he dutifully attends Hall dinners.

The Upper Temple Hall is a fascinating spectacle. With minor anachronisms, it feels like stepping back into the 17th century. Barristers and students eat separately; the Benches sit at a high table adorned with portraits of judges and royalty. Pen observes with amusement as his mess mates range from elderly attorneys to dandies in rowing shirts, colonial students, and Irishmen. The food is hearty—soup, meat, pies, and wine—but the clever lower-rank students employ various tricks to upgrade their meals.

"We're waiting," whispers Mr. Lowton, tugging Pen's gown. "If we wait, maybe the Benches will drop something."

Lowton, a gourmand with ambitions to be mess captain and control the final glass of port, knows exactly how to maneuver. He spots the Benches' table, where three elders stand before covered dishes while a clerk chants grace.

"This is boiled beef day, sir," Lowton tells Pen. "Boosey is there, bald and sipping soup. Is it turtle? Then comes Balls, KC, and Swettenham... and old Grump, who's dined here forty years!"

Lowton points out the elite guests: the Bishop of Ealing's son, Honorable Fred Ringwood, Lord Cinqbar's brother, and Bob Suckling.

"I like messing with them," Lowton explains, winking. "They don't actually eat. They go to fancy clubs. But if you mess with them, you get the bottle to yourself. No wine for them, so you drink it all."

"And why do you like them if they don't eat?" Pen asks.

"How green you are!" Lowton laughs. "It's about the wine, not the food."

As Lowton catalogs the Irish contingent—including the braggart Molloy Maloney and the fiery rebel Jack Finucane—he invites Pen to refill his glass. Pen declines the port, surprising Lowton, yet Lowton remains friendly.

Just as Pen settles in, a gentleman in a faded, oversized gown strides over the crowded tables to sit beside him. He wears a ragged shirt and old clothes, a stark contrast to the flashy pink attire of Molloy Maloney nearby.

"We ought to know each other," the stranger smiles. "We're both Boniface men. My name is Warrington."

"St—— Warrington?" Pen exclaims, delighted.

"Stunning Warrington," the man replies with a laugh. "I recall you from your freshman year. You thrashed that bargeman, Bill Simes, didn't you?"

"The college still talks about you," Pen admits, admiring the talent.

"Hush! Glad to meet you, Pendennis. Heard a lot about you."

Instantly, they bond over college memories. Seeing Warrington enjoy the port despite his earlier claims, Pen stops pretending to dislike it, much to Lowton's dismay. Afterward, Warrington suggests they skip dressing for the opera and instead smoke a quick pipe at his chambers in Lamb Court.

"You had better come home and smoke a pipe with me," Warrington urges. "We'll talk over Boniface and old times."

Lowton sighs as they leave, knowing Warrington is a baronet's son and looking up to the aristocracy with reverence. From that night, Pen and Warrington become sworn brothers. Warrington's cheerful nature, good sense, and constant pipe charm Pen far more than the stiff politeness of the exclusive Polytechnic Club. Soon, Pen abandons his expensive lodgings in St. James's, moving into Warrington's spare room in Lamb Court. It is cheaper, and conveniently, Pidgeon the page and Flanagan the laundress now divide their loyalty between the two friends.
