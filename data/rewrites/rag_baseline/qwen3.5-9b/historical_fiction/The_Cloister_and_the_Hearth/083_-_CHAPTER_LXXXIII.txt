Waiting for an earnest letter rarely leaves the mind in a state of suspended animation; rather, it acts as a catalyst, igniting a volatile mixture of hope and dread. Margaret, clutching her correspondence, poured her entire spiritual and emotional reservoir into validating her dying father's vision—or perhaps his delusion. Once the ink had dried and Luke departed, a crushing sense of disillusionment settled over her. She recoiled from her own credulity, her conscience pricking sharply regarding Luke's absence, while Catherine's reproaches only served to deepen the wound. The cost of these false elevations of spirit was a plunge into a despondency far darker than before.

In this hollowed-out state, she was discovered by Joan Ketel, a steadfast friend forged in recent trials. Joan entered radiating a singular, luminous idea. "Margaret, I hold the cure for thy malady," she declared, her voice bright against the gloom. "The hermit of Gouda—a wondrous saint. In his mood, he can foretell the future, discerning the threads of destiny before they are woven."

"Ay," Margaret murmured, her hope extinguished like a candle in a draft.

With considerable effort, Joan persuaded her to journey to Gouda. They carried a basket of butter and eggs, provisions for the ascetic, and walked toward the cave. Their bond, however, was rooted in a tragedy that had nearly claimed Jorian Ketel mere months prior. Six weeks ago, Jorian had succumbed to a violent bowel affliction, a torment that began with agonizing spasms. When the acute pain subsided, leaving him emaciated, a more insidious failure took hold: neither liquid nor solid could remain in his stomach for a single minute. The physicians, baffled, prescribed a chicken boiled with a golden angel, a remedy that shared the fate of the humblest fare—it passed through him untouched. Next, they steeped the thumb of St. Sergius in beef broth, yielding the same sterile result. Desperate, Joan wept to Margaret to borrow linen for a shroud.

"Let me see him," Margaret commanded, stepping into the chamber. She felt his pulse, a fleeting tremor against her fingers. "Ah!" she cried, her eyes widening. "They have not touched the root. Open the window! Thou art stifling him. Change all his linen."

The medieval wife protested, "Alack, woman, what for? Why foul more linen for a dying man?"

"Do as thou art bid," Margaret said, her voice dull with authority, and exited the room. Joan, compelled by the force of her friend's conviction, complied. Margaret returned with her apron heavy with a flowering herb. She prepared a decoction, tasting a spoonful herself with a feigned, hypocritical savor. "That is fair," Jorian managed, offering a feeble spark of humor. "Why, 'tis sweet, and now 'tis bitter."

She engaged him in conversation immediately after he drank. This bitter-sweet concoction became his anchor. Observing its effect, Margaret played her cards with the patience of her sex: she mixed a drop of Schiedam gin into the third spoonful and a beaten yolk of egg into the seventh. With this alchemy, she coaxed his body from Death's grasp. Finally, Nature, having been patted on the back rather than kicked beneath the bed, restored Jorian Ketel to his feet. Yet, the healer demanded a solemn oath from them both: never to reveal her guilty deed. "They would imprison me, tearing me away from my child," she had whispered. The simple herb that saved Jorian was known as sweet feverfew, gathered from his own garden, spotted by her eagle eye growing through the window lattice.

Thus, Margaret and Joan arrived at the hermit's cave, placing their offerings on the small stone platform. Margaret pressed her lips to the aperture designed for prayer and spoke: "Holy hermit, we bring thee butter and eggs of the finest quality. And I, a poor deserted girl, a wife in title yet no wife in truth, and mother of the sweetest babe, come to pray thee tell me whether he is quick or dead, true to his vows or false."

A faint, rasping voice drifted from the darkness: "Trouble me not with the trifles of earth, but send me a holy friar; I am dying."

"Alas!" Margaret cried, her heart breaking. "Is it even so, poor soul? Then let us enter to aid thee."

"Saints forbid! Thy voice is that of a woman. Send me a holy friar."

They retreated as they had come. Joan, unable to contain her outrage, snapped, "Are women imps of darkness then, that they must not approach a dying bed?"

But Margaret was too deeply bowed by grief to respond. Joan offered rough consolation, which went unheeded until she mentioned Jorian. "And Jorian will speak out ere long; he is just on the boil. He is very grateful to thee, believe it."

"Seeing is believing," Margaret replied, her tone quiet and laced with bitterness.

"Not but what he suspects thou might have saved him with something more extraordinary than yon common herb," Joan continued, weaving the tale of Jorian's gratitude. "'A man of my inches to be cured with feverfew,' he says. 'Why, if there is a sorry herb,' he remarks. 'Why, I was thinking of pulling all mine up,' he confessed. I told him remedies are none the better for being far-fetched; you and feverfew cured him when the grand medicines failed utterly. So I said, 'You may go down on your four bones to feverfew.' But indeed, he is grateful at bottom; you are all his thought and all his chatter. But he sees Gerard's kin approaching, good friends, and he said only last night—"

"Well?"

"He made me vow not to tell thee."

"Prithee, tell me."

"Well, he said: 'If I reveal what little I know, it will not bring him back, and it will set them all by the ears. I wish I had more headpiece,' he lamented. 'I am sore perplexed. But least said is soonest mended.' That is his favorite phrase; he returns to it from a mile away."

Margaret shook her head slowly. "Ay, we are wading in deep waters, my poor babe and me."

It was Saturday night, and Luke was nowhere to be found.

"Poor Luke!" Margaret sighed. "It was very good of him to undertake such an errand."

"He is one out of a hundred," Catherine responded warmly. "Mother, dost thou think he would be kind to little Gerard?"

"I am sure he would. So do thou be kinder to him when he returns! Wilt thou promise?"

"Ay."

Brother Clement, guided by the nuns, navigated a sharp bend in the river, striding with renewed vigor toward a station miles closer to the coast than where Luke lay in ambush for Gerard Eliassoen. The following dawn, he started early, reaching Rotterdam by noon. He hastened directly to the port, eager to meet Jerome. Upon arrival, he observed several monks of his order along the quay; he approached them, but Jerome was absent. He asked one if Jerome had arrived.

"Surely, brother," the monk replied.

"Prithee, where is he?"

"Where? Why, there!" the monk pointed, gesturing to a ship cutting through the waves in full sail. Clement noticed immediately that all the friars were gazing seaward.

"What, gone without me! Oh, Jerome! Jerome!" he cried, his voice cracking with anguish.

Several friars turned, staring at him. "You must be Brother Clement," one said eventually. On hearing this, they embraced him with brotherly warmth and handed him a letter Jerome had entrusted to them. It was a hasty scrawl, written in cold ink. The author informed him dispassionately that a ship was about to depart for England and that he loathed losing time. He urged Clement to follow if he wished, but advised him much better to stay behind and preach to his own countrymen.

"Give the glory to God, brother; thou hast a wonderful power over Dutch hearts; but thou art no match for those haughty islanders: thou art too tender," the letter read. "Know thou that on the way I met one who asked for thee under the name thou didst bear in the world. Be on thy guard! Let not the world catch thee again by any silken net. Remember, Solitude, Fasting, and Prayer are the sword, spear, and shield of the soul. Farewell."

Clement was profoundly shocked and mortified by this contemptuous desertion and the icy missive. He promised the good monks he would sleep at the convent and preach wherever the prior appointed, for Jerome had once raised him to the skies as a preacher. Yet, wounded to the quick, he withdrew abruptly, needing solitude to process the betrayal. He questioned himself: Was there some incurable fault in him, repulsive to so true a son of Dominic? Or was Jerome himself devoid of that Christian Love which St. Paul had placed above Faith itself? Shipwrecked together, saved on the same fragment of wreckage—his pupil, his penitent, his son in the Church—and now, for four hundred miles, his fellow traveler in Christ, only to be shaken off like dirt at the first opportunity with harsh, cold disdain.
