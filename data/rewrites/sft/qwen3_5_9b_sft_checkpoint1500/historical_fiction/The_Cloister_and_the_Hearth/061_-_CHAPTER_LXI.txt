Princess Claelia commanded a full-length portrait of herself. Gerard suggested employing his acquaintance, Pietro Vanucci.

She rejected the notion immediately. "'Twill be time to cast a slight upon the Gerardo," she declared, "when his work displeases me." Then Gerard, acknowledging his prowess as a draughtsman but admitting his inferiority as a colorist, beseeched her to pose as a Roman statue. He demonstrated how faithfully he could imitate marble upon paper. She agreed initially, but hesitated when the enthusiastic artist explained that she must don the tunic, toga, and sandals of antiquity.

"Why, I had as lief be presented in my smock," she remarked with medieval frankness.

"Alack, signorina!" Gerard replied. "You have surely never observed ancient custom; it is so free, so ample, so simple, yet so noble. And it suits your Highness perfectly, to whom Heaven has granted Roman features, a shapely arm, and a graceful hand, albeit in modern guise."

"What?" Gerard flattered her, like the rest. "Well, grant me time to consider. Return on Saturday, and I shall decide."

During this respite, the garments were crafted and fitted in her chamber to ensure they compensated for their thousand-year obsolescence.

Hurrying toward this appointment, Gerard was suddenly halted, rooted to the spot before a shop window. His keen eye had spotted an open copy of Lactantius. "That is fairly written, at least," he thought.

He scrutinized it a moment longer.

It was not written. It was printed.

Gerard groaned inwardly. "I am undone; my enemy stands at the door. The press has arrived in Rome."

Entering the shop, he feigned nonchalance to inquire how long the printing press had been in the city. The merchant claimed there was no press there. "Oh, the Lactantius," the man corrected, "was printed atop the Apennines."

"What? Did the printing press tumble down from the moon?"

"Nay, messer," the trader laughed, pointing to the title page. "It sprang up from Germany. See!"

Gerard snatched the book eagerly, reading the imprint: *Opera et impensis Sweynheim et Pannartz... Impressum Subiacis. A.D. 1465.*

"Shall I purchase it, messer?" the seller asked, highlighting the fairness of the letters. "Few can write like that today, and it costs but a quarter of the price."

"I would gladly buy it," Gerard said sadly, "but my heart forbids it. Know that I am a calligrapher, and these disciples of Fust pursue me worldwide, stealing my bread. Yet I wish them no ill. Heaven forbid!" He hastened from the shop.

"My dear Margaret," he whispered to himself. "We must lose no time. We must gather our harvest while the sun shines. Within a month, an avalanche of printer's type shall descend upon Rome from those mountains and destroy us, those who write."

He nearly ran to the Princess Claelia.

He was led into a private apartment unfamiliar to him. Though not vast, it was sumptuous; a fountain bubbled in the center, and the floor was paved with dressed panther skins, ensuring silence underfoot. It served as an antechamber to her boudoir, separated by a magnificent tapestry curtain.

Left alone, Gerard grew uneasy, doubting if he had been misdirected. These fears vanished quickly.

A light step approached behind the curtain, which parted to reveal a figure the pagans might have worshiped. She was neither entirely Venus nor Minerva, but possessed a nobility surpassing Venus and a femininity exceeding Jove's daughter. Clad in toga, tunic, and sandals, she was wholly antique. As for beauty, she transcended time.

Gerard sprang forward, the artist within him flushing with delight.

"Oh!" he cried innocently, gazing in rapture.

This reaction added the final charm to his model. A light blush colored her cheeks, her eyes brightened, and her mouth curved in a smile of delicious self-satisfaction at his genuine tribute.

For some time, they gazed at one another until the Princess realized Gerard's eloquence consisted merely of ejaculations and staring. She spoke. "Well, Gerardo, thou seest I have fashioned an antique monster for thee."

"A monster? I doubt Fra Colonna would prostrate himself in adoration, beholding your Highness thus attired."

"Nay, I care not to be adored by an old man. I prefer to be loved by a youth of my own choosing."

Gerard set up his pencils and prepared his canvas, covered with sturdy paper. So absorbed was he that he showed no mercy to his model. After nearly an hour in one posture, she spoke faintly, "Gerardo, I can stand thus no longer, even for thee."

"Sit down and rest awhile, Signora."

"I thank thee," she said, sinking into a chair, turning pale and sighing.

Gerard was alarmed, realizing his inconsiderateness. He drew water from the fountain to moisten her brow, but she raised a white hand in protest. "Nay, hold it to my forehead with thine hand; do not fling it at me!"

Timidly, he applied the wet cloth. "Ah!" she sighed, feeling relief. "Again."

He repeated the action. She thanked him and requested a handbell on the table. A maid arrived, sent to summon Floretta with orders to bring a large fan.

Floretta appeared swiftly. As soon as she neared the Princess, the lady's aristocratic nostrils flared wide like a bloodhorse's. "Wretch!" she cried, springing to her feet with renewed vigor. Seizing Floretta by the left arm, she twisted it violently into her hair, then struck her ears three times with the right hand.

Floretta screamed and wept, receiving no mercy. The antique toga, now loose, revealed a bare arm that seemed as powerful as it was beautiful. It rose and fell like the piston of a steam engine, delivering heavy slaps that echoed against Floretta's shoulders. The final blow drove her sobbing and screaming through the tapestry curtain, where she continued to cry bitterly for some time.

"Saints of heaven!" cried Gerard. "What is amiss? What has she done?"

"She knows well enough. 'Tis not the first time. That nasty toad! I shall teach her to approach me reeking of musk-cats."

"Alas, Signora, 'twas a minor fault, I thought."

"A minor fault? Nay, 'twas a grievous offense." Her tone shifted instantly to humility and sweetness. "Art thou angry with me for beating her, Gerardo?"

"Signora, it ill becomes me to chastise thee. Yet methinks those appointed to govern others must first govern themselves."

"That is true, Gerardo. How wise thou art, being so young." Calling the other maid, she gave her a purse. "Take this to Floretta and tell her 'the Gerardo' interceded for her; therefore I must pardon her. There, Gerardo."

Gerard blushed profusely at the compliment, unable to formulate a fitting response. He asked if he should resume her portrait.

"Not yet," she replied. "Beating that hussy has somewhat enlivened me. I shall sit awhile, and thou shalt converse with me. I know thou canst speak, as rarely as thou drawest."

"That were easily done."

"Do it then, Gerardo."

Gerard was taken aback. "But, signora, I know not what to say. This is sudden."

"Speak thy true mind. Say thou wishest to be elsewhere."

"Nay, signora, that would not be truthful. I desire one thing, however."

"And what is it?" she asked gently.

"I wish I could have painted thee as thou wast beating that poor maiden. Thou wast awful, yet lovely. Oh, what a subject for a Pythoness!"

"Alas! he thinks but of his art. And why all this fuss about my beauty, Gerardo? Thou art far fairer than I. Thou resemblest Apollo more than I do Venus. Moreover, thou hast lovely hair and eyes—though I suspect thou knowest not how to wield them."

"Ay, do I. To paint thee, signora."

"Ah, yes; with them thou canst perceive my features, but thou canst not see what any Roman suitor long ago perceived in thy stead. Yet surely thou must have noticed how welcome thou art to me, Gerardo?"

"I behold that your Highness is perpetually kind to me, a poor stranger."

"No, Gerardo. I have often been cold to thee; sometimes rude. And thou art so simple thou perceivest not the cause. Alas! I feared for mine own heart. I feared to become thy slave. I, who have hitherto enslaved others. Ah! Gerardo, I am unhappy. Since thou arrived, I have lived upon thy visits. On the day thou comest, I am radiant; on other days, listless, wishing them gone. Thou art not like the Roman suitors. They make me hate them. Thou art tenfold braver in mine eyes; wise, scholarly, and never flattering or lying. I scorn a liar.
