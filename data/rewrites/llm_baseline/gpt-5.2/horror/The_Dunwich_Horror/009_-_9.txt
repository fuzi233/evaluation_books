Friday morning, while Arkham’s chimneys were still drawing steady and the town kitchens were clinking through the plain business of breakfast, Dr. Armitage set out with Rice and Morgan by motor for Dunwich. The road carried them from familiar noises—the measured ring of a streetcar bell, a distant factory’s flat, unmusical hammering—into a country where the hills swallowed sound rather than made it. Even the tires seemed to hush themselves on the last miles, as if the earth here demanded a softer tread.

They reached the village about one in the afternoon. Sunlight lay bright on the oddly domed heights and on the deep ravines cut like old knife wounds through the green, yet the day’s fairness could not cleanse the air. Dread hung over the place the way rancid grease hangs about a neglected hearth—seen nowhere, but everywhere smelled and felt. Now and then, against the sky on some mountain top, they caught the bleak outline of a stone circle, gaunt as a broken ring of teeth.

At Osborn’s store the men’s voices had the brittle carefulness of people who had lately heard a sound they could not fit into any known measure. The talk came in tight bursts between long listens, as if everyone expected the valley itself to answer. From that hush of fear Armitage understood at once that something had happened; and soon the word took shape—Elmer Frye’s house and family wiped out as if by a hand that did not recognize a house as anything sturdier than paper.

All that afternoon the three rode and walked about Dunwich, questioning whoever would speak, and forcing themselves to look at what every native’s eyes slid away from. They saw the Frye ruins—drear, crushed, and smeared with a tarry stickiness that clung to boards and stones the way half-rendered fat clings to a pan when it is left to burn down. The yard held blasphemous tracks, too large and too deep, and there were cattle at Seth Bishop’s place wounded as if by blind, heavy flailing. In several spots the vegetation lay disturbed in enormous swaths, matted down as though some vast body had dragged itself across pasture and thicket without caring what it broke.

To Armitage the trail up and down Sentinel Hill had the weight of a catastrophe already written into the land. He stared long at the altarlike stone on the summit, a thing that looked less like a farmer’s fieldstone and more like a table set for a guest no human pantry could serve.

Toward evening they sought the State Police party said to have come from Aylesbury that morning after the first telephone reports. It should have been simple enough to find five officers and their car. Yet no sign of the men appeared anywhere. The automobile stood empty near the Frye yard, its presence as unnatural as an untended stove left cold in a house where someone had been cooking only minutes ago.

The natives, who had all spoken with the policemen earlier, stared blankly at one another. Then old Sam Hutchins went a sick gray, nudged Fred Farr, and jabbed a trembling finger toward the dank hollow close by—the deep mouth of Cold Spring Glen. His voice broke like a cracked crock.

“Gawd,” he gasped, “I telled ’em not ter go daown into the glen. I never thought nobody’d dew it with them tracks an’ that smell an’ the whippoorwills a-screechin’ daown thar in the dark o’ noonday….”

At that, a cold shudder ran through visitors and villagers alike. Every head turned—not to look, but to listen. In Dunwich even the soundscape had become testimony. The whippoorwills had been loud in the wrong hours, and there were subterranean mutterings under the hills like distant wagons on hidden roads, except there were no roads down there. The valley’s hush between those noises felt staged, like a pause before the knife falls.

Armitage trembled—not with mere fear, but with the heavy responsibility of having brought knowledge into a place where knowledge could not be tasted and shared as a meal might be, but only swallowed like a bitter medicine. Night would soon fall. It was at night, the accounts said, that the mountainous blasphemy lumbered upon its eldritch course. The phrase he had taken from older pages returned to him with a sick certainty—_Negotium perambulans in tenebris…_—and it did not translate cleanly into anything that would soothe the men around him. It sat in his mind like a word from a dead dialect: exact, pointed, and uselessly unshareable.

He rehearsed the formulæ he had memorized and gripped the paper containing the alternative ones he dared not trust to memory alone. He tested his electric flashlight, hearing in its small click a fragile human sound against the valley’s waiting silence. Rice opened a valise and brought out a metal sprayer of the kind used against insects—an ordinary tool, the sort a careful man might keep for orchards and pantry moths, now enlisted for something no orchard had ever suffered. Morgan uncased the big-game rifle he trusted in spite of Armitage’s warnings that no mere material weapon would be much help.

Armitage had read the hideous diary. He knew too well what kind of manifestation to expect, and he did not heighten the Dunwich people’s terror by naming it aloud. He hoped—foolishly, perhaps—that the thing might be conquered without forcing a wider world to mouth the monstrous syllables it implied. Some words, once spoken, begin to belong to whoever speaks them; and he would not give Dunwich any more vocabulary for its nightmare.

As shadows thickened, the villagers dispersed toward their homes to bar themselves in, though every face showed that locks and bolts felt like children’s toys now—thin, polite gestures against a force that bent trees and crushed houses when it chose. They shook their heads at the three visitors’ plan to stand guard by the Frye ruins near the glen. As they went, their farewells were the kind people give when they do not expect to meet again.

That night the hills rumbled. The whippoorwills piped with a sharpness that seemed less like birdsong than a warning whistle blown by something inhuman that knew the paths. Once in a while a wind swept up out of Cold Spring Glen and brought with it that ineffable fetor—an odor not merely foul, but wrong, like meat left in a sealed crock through an entire summer, then opened in a room where someone had once baked bread. It was the same smell all three had known once before, standing above a dying thing that had passed for fifteen and a half years as a human being.

Yet the looked-for terror did not appear. Whatever lay in the glen was biding its time. Armitage told his companions that to go down into that hollow in the dark would be suicide. They held their ground, listening to the valley’s uneasy orchestra—wind, whippoorwills, and those subterranean, unplaceable tremors—until a wan morning bled in and the night-sounds ceased with an abruptness that felt like a mouth snapping shut.

The day came gray and bleak, with drizzle now and then, and heavier clouds piling up beyond the northwestern hills as if stacked by a deliberate hand. Seeking shelter beneath one of the few Frye outbuildings still standing, the Arkham men debated: wait for clearer conditions, or go down into the glen and hunt what had no name. The rain thickened, and far away thunder spoke in slow, heavy syllables. Sheet lightning shimmered; then a forked bolt cracked down near at hand—so near it seemed to plunge into the accursed glen itself. The sky darkened to a bruised, unnatural shade, and they hoped the storm would spend itself quickly and leave clean air behind.

It was still gruesomely dark when, a little more than an hour later, a confused babel rose from the road—shouts, sobs, and the raw, animal noises of men who had seen their stout explanations fail. A frightened group of more than a dozen came running. The lead man’s words tangled in his throat until they broke free in a shape the Arkham men could understand.

“Oh, my Gawd, my Gawd!” he choked. “It’s a-goin’ agin, _an’ this time by day_! It’s aout—it’s aout an’ a-movin’ this very minute, an’ only the Lord knows when it’ll be on us all!”

He could not go on, and another man carried the message, the way in farm kitchens one voice takes up a recipe when another falters.

“Nigh on a haour ago Zeb Whateley here heerd the ’phone a-ringin’, an’ it was Mis’ Corey, George’s wife that lives daown by the junction. She says the hired boy Luther was aout drivin’ in the caows from the storm arter the big bolt, when he see all the trees a-bendin’ at the maouth o’ the glen—opposite side ter this—an’ smelt the same awful smell like he smelt when he faound the big tracks las’ Monday mornin’.

“An’ she says he says they was a swishin’, lappin’ saound, more nor what the bendin’ trees an’ bushes could make; an’ all on a suddent the trees along the rud begun ter git pushed one side, an’ they was a awful stompin’ an’ splashin’ in the mud. But mind ye, Luther he didn’t see nothin’ at all—only jest the bendin’ trees an’ underbrush.

“Then fur ahead where Bishop’s Brook goes under the rud he heerd a awful creakin’ an’ strainin’ on the bridge, an’ says he could tell the saound o’ wood a-startin’ to crack an’ split. An’ all the whiles he never see a thing, only them trees an’ bushes a-bendin’.

“An’ when the swishin’ saound got very fur off—on the rud towards Wizard Whateley’s an’ Sentinel Hill—Luther he had the guts ter step up whar he’d heerd it fust an’ look at the graound. It was all mud an’ water, an’ the sky was dark, an’ the rain was wipin’ aout all tracks abaout as fast as could be; but beginnin’ at the glen maouth, whar the trees hed moved, they was still some o’ them awful prints big as bar’ls like he seen Monday.”

Before he could draw breath, the first speaker cut in, trembling with the need to push the worst part forward—as if saying it quickly might lessen it.

“But _that_ ain’t the trouble naow—that was only the start. Zeb here was callin’ folks up an’ everybody was a-listenin’ in when a call from Seth Bishop’s cut in. His haousekeeper Sally was carryin’ on fit ter kill—she’d jest seed the trees a-bendin’ beside the rud, an’ says they was a kind o’ mushy saound, like a elephant puffin’ an’ treadin’, a-headin’ fer the haouse. Then she up an’ spoke suddent of a fearful smell, an’ says her boy Cha’ncey was a-screamin’ as haow it was jest like what he smelt up to the Whateley rewins Monday mornin’. An’ the dogs was all barkin’ an’ whinin’ awful.

“An’ then she let aout a turrible yell, an’ says the shed daown the rud hed jest caved in like the storm hed blowed it over, only the wind wa’n’t strong enough to dew that. Everybody was a-listenin’, an’ ye could hear lots o’ folks on the wire a-gaspin’.

“All to onct Sally she yelled agin, an’ says the front yard picket fence hed jest crumpled up, though they wa’n’t no sign o’ what done it. Then everybody on the line could hear Cha’ncey an’ ol’ Seth Bishop a-yellin’, tew, an’ Sally was shriekin’ aout that suthin’ heavy hed struck the haouse—not lightnin’ nor nothin’, but suthin’ heavy agin’ the front, that kep’ a-launchin’ itself agin an’ agin, though ye couldn’t see nuthin’ aout the front winders. An’ then… an’ then….”

Fright drew deeper grooves into every face. Armitage, shaken, forced himself to speak evenly.

“And then?”

The man swallowed as if the words were too thick to pass.

“An’ then Sally she yelled aout, ‘O help, the haouse is a-cavin’ in’… an’ on the wire we could hoar a turrible crashin’, an’ a hull flock o’ screamin’… jest like when Elmer Frye’s place was took, only wuss….”

He stopped. Another man said dully, as if reciting the end of a story that had already devoured its meaning:

“That’s all—not a saound nor squeak over the ’phone arter that. Jest still-like. We that heerd it got aout Fords an’ wagons an’ raounded up as many able-bodied men-folks as we could get, at Corey’s place, an’ come up here ter see what yew thought best ter dew. Not but what I think it’s the Lord’s judgment fer our iniquities, that no mortal kin ever set aside.”

In that moment Armitage understood that waiting was no longer a choice. The thing had moved in daylight; it had learned boldness. The village’s acoustic life—its calls across fields, its telephone line, its dogs, its birds—had become the only net they had to track an invisible bulk through a soaked countryside. He spoke decisively, putting as much steadiness into his voice as he could.

“We must follow it, boys. I believe there’s a chance of putting it out of business. You men know those Whateleys were wizards—well, this thing is a thing of wizardry, and must be put down by the same means. I’ve seen Wilbur Whateley’s diary and read some of the strange old books he used to read, and I think I know the right kind of a spell to recite to make the thing fade away.

“Of course, one can’t be sure, but we can always take a chance. It’s invisible—I knew it would be—but there’s a powder in this long-distance sprayer that might make it show up for a second. Later on we’ll try it. It’s a frightful thing to have alive, but it isn’t as bad as what Wilbur would have let in if he’d lived longer. You’ll never know what the world has escaped. Now we’ve only this one thing to fight, and it can’t multiply. It can, though, do a lot of harm; so we mustn’t hesitate to rid the community of it.

“We must follow it—and the way to begin is to go to the place that has just been wrecked. Let somebody lead the way—I don’t know your roads very well, but I’ve an idea there might be a shorter cut across lots. How about it?”

The men shuffled, glancing from the road to the hills, from Armitage’s paper to Morgan’s rifle and Rice’s sprayer. At last Earl Sawyer pointed with a grimy finger through the steadily lessening rain.

“I guess ye kin git to Seth Bishop’s quickest by cuttin’ acrost the lower medder here, wadin’ the brook at the low place, an’ climbin’ through Carrier’s mowin’ an’ the timber-lot beyont. That comes aout on the upper rud mighty nigh Seth’s—a leetle t’other side.”

Armitage started out at once with Rice and Morgan. Most of the natives followed, slower, as if the very act of moving made the danger more real. The sky lightened; the storm showed signs of breaking up. When Armitage took a wrong direction, Joe Osborn warned him and walked ahead to keep them true. Courage rose in small increments, like heat returning to a chilled room.

But the end of their shortcut led into the twilight of a steep, wooded hill whose old trees stood fantastic and close together. They scrambled up among roots and wet leaves as if climbing a ladder into a darker world. The place had a sound of its own: dripping, sliding mud; breath pulled hard; the occasional creak of a branch not touched by wind. More than once someone stopped, listening for that reported swishing, lapping undertone that did not belong to rain or brook.

At length they emerged onto a muddy road and saw the sun pushing through. They had come out a little beyond Seth Bishop’s place, but they did not need a guide. Bent trees and the hideously unmistakable tracks told the whole tale of passage.

Only a few minutes were spent at the bend where the Bishop buildings had stood. It was the Frye incident all over again: collapsed shells where house and barn had been, and nothing dead or living to be found. The stench lay thick; the tarry stickiness smeared boards and earth with the same obscene persistence. No one cared to linger where the air itself seemed contaminated, as if it had absorbed a foreign breath.

Instinctively they turned from the ruins to the line of horrible prints leading onward toward the wrecked Whateley farmhouse and the altar-crowned slopes of Sentinel Hill. As they passed the site of Wilbur Whateley’s abode, the men shuddered, and zeal began to tangle with hesitation. It was one thing to chase a menace. It was another to hunt something as big as a house that could not be seen, yet moved with a demon’s malevolence, announcing itself only by crushed fences, split bridges, and the wrongness of its odor.

Opposite the base of Sentinel Hill, the tracks left the road. A fresh bending and matting marked a broad swath—the monster’s route to and from the summit. Armitage produced a pocket telescope and scanned the steep green side. He handed it to Morgan, whose sight was keener.

After a moment Morgan cried out sharply and passed the glass to Earl Sawyer, pointing at a spot on the slope. Sawyer fumbled—clumsy with an instrument made for finer hands—until Armitage helped him focus. Then Sawyer’s cry came out unrestrained, a raw sound that seemed to rip the air.

“Gawd almighty—the grass an’ bushes is a-movin’! It’s a-goin’ up—slow-like—creepin’ up ter the top this minute, heaven only knows what fer!”

Panic began to spread like a taint through water. To follow a trail was one thing. To see the hillside itself betray the passage of an unseen bulk was another. Voices rose, questioning Armitage—what did he truly know, what exactly had he read, what kind of spell could stand against a weight that made the living land bow and part? No answer he could give quite satisfied, because what pressed upon them now was not merely fear but proximity—an intimate nearness to forbidden phases of nature and being, outside the sane experience of mankind, and outside the familiar language with which men speak of barns, bread, weather, and the ordinary mercies of a day.
