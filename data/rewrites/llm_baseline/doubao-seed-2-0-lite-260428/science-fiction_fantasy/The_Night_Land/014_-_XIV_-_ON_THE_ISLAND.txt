I drifted back to consciousness anchored only by throbbing, all memory scattered to static, every muscle too heavy to lift. For a dazed moment I thought some unknown, hostile force held me pinned, until I realized the weight was only my own ragged weakness, the aftermath of the fight I could not yet name.

I lay flat on my back, and a soft, ragged panting sounded just at my shoulder. I turned my head slow, every movement sending jolts of ache down my spine, and there she was: my Maid, still naked, heaving all her strength against the thick hardwood pole I had hewn back when the Humpt Men first descended on us. Her face was set hard, teeth bared with effort, her eyes fixed backward toward the far shore we were pulling away from. And in that instant, every memory flooded back. I was on our raft. She had dragged me here while I was unconscious, and was fighting to row us out of reach of the monsters that would have torn both of us apart.

I tried to make a sound, but my throat was too raw, and she did not hear. The far-off howl of the Humpt Men drifted across the water, thin and rabid, and I understood what had happened: she had woken first, to find me splayed across her chest, still and cold, soaked through with my own blood, and had not stopped to grieve. She had found a strength no ordinary woman could have summoned, hauled my limp, bleeding body all the way to the raft, and pushed us out into the current before the last of the Humpt Men could reach her. She never could explain how she did it, later, not even to me. She only ever said love gave her the power to carry me, that she would die before she let them tear the man she loved apart.

She told me the full tale later, when I was well enough to listen. When she first woke, my head was heavy on her breast, and she was certain I was dead. She slid out from under me as gentle as she could, laid me flat on the earth, and her heart nearly split when she saw how much blood I had spilled, staining the dirt all around us. But then she felt the faint, thready beat of my pulse, and hope exploded in her chest. She glanced around fast, and saw only the corpse of the Humpt Man that had attacked her, and the rest of their kind strewn dead across the flat-topped rock, just as I had left them.

She ran for the raft, filled my old headpiece with river water, and dashed it over my face to try and rouse me. When I still did not wake, a quiet, sharp certainty of coming danger pricked at the back of her mind, and she decided then she would save me or die trying. She dragged me all the weariful distance back to the raft, and then ran for the pole – and as she grabbed it, she spotted her own torn clothes, still clamped in the dead Humpt Man’s fist, the ones she had slipped out of to escape him. She wrenched them free in a single snatch, ran back to the raft, pushed it hard off the shore, and leaped aboard just as the two surviving Humpt Men burst out of the treeline. They raced down the bank, silent and intent on catching her, but she had already fought the raft far enough out that they could not reach it before they arrived. They must have been unable to swim, or knew some nameless dread lurked in the dark river water, for they never stepped past the shoreline. They only stood there, staring stupid after us, before throwing back their heads and howling – the very howling I heard the second I dragged myself back to wakefulness.

The howls grew fainter and fainter as she drove the pole through the water again and again, every muscle screaming with effort. I tried to push myself up to help her, but the blackness swallowed me whole before I could even lift my shoulders. I never knew a thing about the rest of that journey, not until I woke safe on the little island we had been aiming for, laid out on a bed of soft dry moss, and she had dragged me up the shore all alone, never stopping to rest for a second. I was inches from death, helpless to protect her, and she had saved me. I still burn bright with joy, even now, to remember I at least fought a good fight for her before I fell.

For a long stretch after that, nothing is clear to me. Only pain, and endless weariness, half-waking moments that dissolved back into fog the second I tried to hold onto them, when I had no sense of where I was or if the waking world was even real. But no matter how deep the black mists of my weakness dragged me, I could feel love hovering close, soft and unrelenting watchfulness, that chased the nameless, stealthy despairs creeping through my bones away before they could sink their teeth in.

Then one day I woke, and the haze lifted. The constant, searing ache had softened to a dull throb, the strange disorienting weight was gone from my limbs. I was laid soft on that bed of moss, the air around me still and sweet, and a warm, healing drowse seeped slow into my bones.

I turned my head, and there she was, kneeling right beside me, staring down at me with such overflowing love and gladness that it felt like I was drinking health straight from her gaze. She leaned down and kissed me, so gentle her lips barely brushed mine, and her warm tears fell soft on my cheek. I kissed her back, and there was no greater joy in all the world in that moment.

She cradled my head in the crook of her arm, lifted a cup to my lips, and made me drink a thin broth she had brewed from our nutrient tablets mixed with fresh water. When I was done, she kissed me again, light as a quiet summer wind brushing my mouth, and adjusted the moss bedding under my head. I fell asleep before she could even pull her hand away.

I woke three more times in the next two days, each time a little stronger, each time finding her right there holding my hand. The fourth time I woke, I whispered that I loved her, and she burst into sudden, unsteady weeping, pressing my hand tight to her breast over her heart.

The fifth time I woke properly, I noticed I was wrapped in my heavy cloak, my entire torso wrapped in soft bandages. She had torn the strips right from the remains of her own clothing, she told me later, to make dressings for my wounds. And she had mended the rest of her garments, too: she had pulled threads from the frayed edges of the cloth, pricked sharp thorns from the tiny island bushes to use as needles, broken dozens of them over the long days sitting by my side tending to me, until she had stitched every tear shut, and was dressed again in the clothes I had given her back when we first set out on our journey.

When I looked at her, taking in the neat mends, she remembered the last time I had seen her, bare on the raft, and blushed bright pink, leaning down to kiss me so she could tuck her face away from my gaze. I ached so badly to be strong enough to kneel before her, to honor her the way she deserved, the way anyone who has truly loved knows you would do for the person who would give the whole world for you.

My strength came back slow and steady. She tended to me every hour on the hour, checking the time against my old pocket watch to make sure she fed me broth and water on schedule, washed my wounds, changed the bandages, washed and dried the old ones to reuse again, since we had no spares to waste. By the fifth day, all the worst of the pain was gone, and I was so happy I could barely speak for the weight of it. She chattered soft and sweet to me the whole day, but kept me from moving a muscle, scolding me gentle every time I tried to sit up.

On the sixth day, she let me talk back. I told her every single way I loved her, every thought I had held of her while I lay silent, too weak to move. She told me she was perfectly well, fully recovered from the Humpt Man’s attack, but I could see she was thin, her eyes dark with circles from days of no sleep, even when they glowed bright with love every time she looked at me.

I made her bring all our remaining tablets over, kiss each one before she gave it to me to eat, and then bade her lie down beside me, rest her head on my good arm. I had her nestle into my side, told her to sleep, that the weight of her against me would rest me more than any quiet ever could. She worried at first that she was too heavy, but I pulled her gently closer to prove I had enough strength to hold her, and she settled in, and fell asleep instantly, so deep she barely stirred for twelve full hours. Once she made a soft little murmur in her sleep, and snuggled her face closer to my chest. I did not grow tired once, did not feel lonely at all. I just lay there, staring down at her, her face soft and peaceful, lit by the faint glow of our light stone, and a quiet, holy joy filled my chest, so full it felt like my heart would burst. I sipped our broth at the third hour, the sixth, the ninth, until it was all gone, and every so often I rested my hand on the hilt of my Diskos, that great old weapon, like it was a trusted comrade. It felt like it knew me, loved me back, the way all great weapons bond to the hands that wield them.

At the twelfth hour, she woke all of a sudden, jolting up in a sweet panic to check that I had not come to harm while she slept. She was horrified when she realized she had slept for half a day straight, no longer tending to me, but I pretended to scold her in mock sternness, forbade her from saying a single word more of apology, and told her the only thing that mattered was that I was happier than I had ever been in all my life, and she should be too. That impudent little thing then balled her tiny fist and prodded it against my nose, pretending to threaten me, and I laughed so hard she got scared I would tear my wounds open again and start bleeding. I assured her I was fine, no harm done at all.

Offhand, I asked her if she had any brothers, when she was little, for she played so naturally, so childlike in that moment. The second the words left my mouth I realized I had spoken thoughtlessly, for I knew full well almost no one in our dark age grew up with family. She nodded quiet, kissed my hand, and slipped away from me so she could weep out of sight. I called soft to her, no anger in my voice, and she came back soon, smiling and bright, though I could see she had been crying, and made me a fresh pot of broth. Before I would drink it, I pulled her into my arms, and she rested her weight light against me, careful not to press on my wounds. We ate together after that, talking soft and happy, until I fell asleep, and she stayed right by my side, keeping watch.

The seventh day was pure, unbroken joy. I woke to find her sleeping curled up right next to me, her face tucked against my shoulder, and she woke the second I stirred, used to rousing at the smallest sound to check on me. She washed me gentle, changed my bandages, and that day she let me eat full nutrient tablets instead of broth, the way I did when I was well. I ate as many as I could, trying to build my strength back fast, so I could protect her again, so we could finish our journey to the safety of the Mighty Pyramid. We kissed each other’s tablets before we ate, drank from the same cup, half children giddy with joy, half man and woman drunk on loving each other.

Later, she went off to bathe in a little hidden pool behind the island bushes, and came back with her hair hanging loose in a soft cloud down her back, her little bare feet still damp from the water. She teased me for being so impatient for her to come back, and sat right beside me, letting me watch her as she brushed out all that long, lovely hair. I plucked a single strand from her head when she was not looking, and when she held out her bare feet to me like I had asked, I bound her pretty toes together with that single hair, so she was my little captive. We laughed like fools, and she did not break the hair even once as she sat there beside me, trying very hard to pretend she had not noticed at all.

She started piling her hair on top of her head to pin it up, and I kept reaching up to tousle it back down again. She laughed, and draped all that abundance of hair over both sides of my face, so I was peeking out through a curtain of her hair, and kissed me soft on the mouth. Then she cut a lock of her hair, and a lock of mine, plaited them together, and tucked the braid into the neck of her dress, against her bosom. Then she made another braid, for me, and I tucked it under the big bandage wrapped over my heart. We pretended to quarrel over who had the braid that was kissed the most, and the only way to end the fight was to kiss until we were both laughing too hard to pretend to be mad. Everyone who has ever been in love knows that kind of silly, perfect foolishness, I think.

Then she held my wrists down, teased me that if I kept reaching up to mess her hair she would punish me, and her tiny little hands were so small she could barely hold my two wrists with one, only by hooking her thumbs over them. She covered my eyes with her free hand so I could not see, and kissed me light and impudent on the mouth, before letting go and pretending to be perfectly demure, like she had not done a single thing. We sat quiet for a while after that, and I held her two tiny fists in my one big, pale, shaking hand, the one that still had not recovered enough blood to stop trembling, and it was the sweetest pleasure I had ever known.

I told her a story then, of a young man who lived long ago, in the old sunlit world before the Night fell, who met the one woman in all the world made for him. They married, and she died, and the grief almost destroyed him, until he woke up in this dark far future, and learned his beloved was also alive here. He searched for her for years, and finally found her, and he loved her so deeply, so reverently, that his love felt like a soft, constant, sweet ache in his chest, made of memories of that old sunlit life, and the joy of being with her again. I did not get far into the tale before she broke down weeping, got on her knees beside me, and pressed my hand to her breast. A faint, glowing memory lit up in her eyes, like she was half dreaming, and I felt that same memory tug at my own mind, from the half-open gates of the forgotten past. We both remembered, for a second, the little child we had together, back in that old sunlit age. No words needed to be said. The silence hung soft and holy between us. She kissed me, pulled herself back from the haze of the old days, and went off to get our food ready.

That day was perfect. We laughed, we told old half-remembered jokes from that forgotten past, and one of us blurted out the answer to an old riddle no one in this dark age would ever know – why did the man die at the dinner party? Because he was a little hoarse. We stared at each other after we said it, confused, because neither of us could even remember what a horse was. But we could feel the memory of it, faint and soft, drifting up through millions of lost years.

I pulled us back from the haze of those old memories then, and told her all about the Mighty Pyramid, every little detail: the glowing spires, the endless libraries, the millions of people living safe behind its walls, the wonders she had never even dreamed of. She went quiet, eyes wide, drinking in every word, like I had come down from a star to tell her of worlds no one had ever seen. I knew right then, once my strength was fully back, we would make it there together. Nothing in all the Night Land could stop us.
