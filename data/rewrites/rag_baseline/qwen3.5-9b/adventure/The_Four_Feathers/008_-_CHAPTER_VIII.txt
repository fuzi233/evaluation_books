June arrived in London with a crispness that seemed to sharpen the edges of the city, yet for Captain Durrance, the air carried the dust of distant deserts. On a Tuesday morning, he stepped off the train and, later that same afternoon, walked into Hyde Park. It was his first excursion since becoming an exile, a solitary figure moving beneath the canopy of ancient trees. He watched the women passing by, mesmerized by the fluid grace of their movements and the delicate artistry of their gowns, contrasting sharply with his own sunburnt skin and the permanent, unreadable shadow etched across his features—the mark of a man carved out in the remote corners of the globe.

Among the throng, a smile caught his eye. As Durrance rose from his bench, Mrs. Adair approached, her gaze sweeping over him with a quick, almost furtive intensity that betrayed the depth of her scrutiny. She was measuring him against a memory three years old, searching for the subtle fractures in his composure, the signs of time's erosion, viewed with eyes wide with apprehension. Durrance merely noted the stark simplicity of her attire: she wore black. Understanding the silent inquiry, she spoke softly. "My husband died eighteen months ago," she said. "He was thrown from his horse during a hunt with the Pytchleys. He was killed instantly."

"I had not heard," Durrance replied, his voice thick with an awkward formality. "I am very sorry."

Mrs. Adair settled into a chair beside him, offering no further comment. She was a woman defined by perplexing silences; her pale, placid face, framed by a cold and correct geometry, offered no window into the turmoil occupying her mind. She sat motionless, a statue of grief. Durrance felt the weight of the moment pressing down on him. He recalled Mr. Adair as a man of gentle humor, whose primary virtue was his evident devotion to his wife, but he had never truly considered the wife's perspective on that devotion until now. Mr. Adair had been a shadowy figure in that small household, and Durrance found his memories failing him, unable to summon a full expression of regret. Abandoning the attempt, he shifted the current. "Are Harry Feversham and his wife in town?"

Mrs. Adair paused, her response delayed. "Not yet," she said, but quickly corrected herself, her tone hurried. "I mean... the marriage never took place."

Durrance was not easily startled, and even when surprised, his reaction lacked dramatic flourish. "I do not think I understand," he said calmly. "Why did it never take place?"

Mrs. Adair looked at him sharply, probing the reason behind his deliberate calm. "I do not know why," she replied. "Ethne can keep a secret if she wishes." Durrance nodded his assent. "The engagement was broken off on the night of a dance at Lennon House."

Durrance turned immediately to her. "Just before I left England three years ago?"

"Yes. Then you knew?"

"No. Only you have explained to me something that occurred on the very night I left Dover. What has become of Harry?"

Mrs. Adair shrugged. "I do not know. I have met no one who does. I doubt I have encountered anyone who has even seen him since that time. He must have left England."

Durrance pondered this enigmatic disappearance. It was Harry Feversham, then, the man with the troubled, despairing face he had witnessed on the pier as the Channel boat pulled away. That stranger was, after all, his friend. "And Miss Eustace?" he asked after a pause, his voice tinged with a strange timidity. "Has she remarried?"

Again, Mrs. Adair took her time. "No," she said. "Then she is still at Ramelton?"

Mrs. Adair shook her head. "There was a fire at Lennon House a year ago. Have you ever heard of a constable named Bastable?"

"Indeed, I have," Durrance replied. "He was the means of introducing me to Miss Eustace and her father. I was traveling from Londonderry to Letterkenny. I received a letter from Mr. Eustace, whom I did not know personally, but who learned from my friends in Letterkenny that I was passing near his house. He invited me to stay the night. Naturally, I declined, resulting in Bastable arresting me on a magistrate's warrant the moment I disembarked from the ferry."

"That is the man," Mrs. Adair confirmed, recounting the history of the fire. It appeared Bastable's bond with Dermod relied on his unique skill in brewing a specific concoction of toddy, requiring a single oyster simmered in the saucepan to achieve its perfect flavor. At two o'clock on a June morning, the spirit lamp supporting the pot tipped over. Both men, intoxicated and lacking their faculties, failed to react in time. The house burned partially, the remainder ruined by the deluge of water used to extinguish the flames.

"There were consequences far more distressing than the destruction of the property," she continued. "The fire served as a beacon to Dermod's creditors. Already overwhelmed by debt, he succumbed to total ruin in a single day. He was soaked by the fire hoses and contracted a severe chill that nearly claimed his life; he has never fully recovered. You will find him a broken man. The estates have been leased out, and Ethne now resides with her father in a small mountain village in Donegal."

Mrs. Adair had not looked at Durrance while speaking. Her eyes remained fixed steadfastly forward, her delivery devoid of emotion, spoken only because speech was a necessity. She did not turn to meet his gaze when she finished.

"So she has lost everything?" Durrance asked.

"She still has a home in Donegal," Mrs. Adair returned.

"And that means a great deal to her," Durrance said slowly.

"Yes, I think you are right."

"It means," Mrs. Adair added, "that despite her ill fortune, Ethne has reasons to be envied by many other women."

Durrance did not answer this suggestion directly. Instead, he watched the carriages roll past, listening to the chatter and laughter of the crowd, his eyes refreshed by the women in their light-colored frocks. Throughout it all, his slow mind worked toward a fractured philosophy. Finally, Mrs. Adair turned to him with a hint of impatience. "Of what are you thinking?"

"That women suffer much more than men when the world turns against them," he answered, his statement sounding more like a question than an assertion.

"I know very little, of course. I can only guess. But I believe women internalize their experiences far more profoundly than we do. To them, the past becomes a literal part of their being, as integral as a limb; to us, it remains external—at best, a rung on a ladder, at worst, a weight on the heel. Do you not think so as well? I phrase the thought poorly. But consider it this way: women look backward, we look ahead. Therefore, misfortune strikes them harder, yes?"

Mrs. Adair responded in her own manner. She did not explicitly agree, but a note of humility colored her voice. "The mountain village where Ethne lives is called Glenalla. A track leads up to it from the road halfway between Rathmullen and Ramelton." She rose as she finished, extending her hand. "Shall I see you?"

"You are still in Hill Street?" Durrance asked.

"I shall be in London for a time."

Mrs. Adair raised her eyebrows. By nature, she sought intricate and concealed motives, often baffled by conduct driven by simple, obvious reasons. Now, Durrance's resolve to remain in the city puzzled her. Why did he not journey immediately to Donegal, she wondered, since his thoughts clearly preceded him there? Hearing of his constant presence at his Service Club, she could not comprehend it. She harbored no suspicion of his true motive when he informed her he had traveled to Surrey and spent a day with General Feversham. It had been an ineffectual visit for Durrance. The general kept him firmly anchored to the history of the campaign from which he had just returned. Only once did he broach the subject of Harry Feversham's disappearance, and at the mere mention of his son's name, the old general's face hardened like wet plaster, becoming void of expression and inattentive as a mask.

"We will discuss something else, if you please," the general said.

Durrance returned to London not an inch closer to Donegal. Thereafter, he sat beneath the great tree in the inner courtyard of his club, conversing with various men, yet remaining unsatisfied. Throughout that June, every afternoon and evening found him at his post. No friend of Feversham passed by without Durrance engaging them, each interaction leading inevitably to a question. Yet, these inquiries yielded nothing but a shrug of the shoulders and a weary, "Hanged if I know!" Harry Feversham's circle had ceased to recognize him; he had fallen even out of the speculations of his peers.

Toward the end of June, however, an elderly retired naval officer limped into the courtyard. Upon seeing Durrance, he hesitated, then moved away with remarkable speed. Durrance sprang up. "Mr. Sutch," he said. "You have forgotten me?"

"Colonel Durrance, certainly," the embarrassed lieutenant replied.
