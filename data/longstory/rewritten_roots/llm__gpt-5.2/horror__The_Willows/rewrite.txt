After leaving Vienna—long before the Danube ever thinks of Budapest—the river loosens its collar and goes feral. The stern banks that taught it manners fall away, and the water spreads as though it has forgotten it is meant to be a single thing. On the maps, that whole expanse is brushed in a thin, uncertain blue that pales as it wanders from the main line, and across it, in straggling letters that look half-erased, stands the word Sümpfe: marshes.

In high flood the place is almost drowned outright: sand and shingle and the low islands vanish under one continuous, heaving sheet. But in an ordinary season—if anything here can be called ordinary—the willows rule, a vast sea of humble bushes that never become trees. They have no stubborn trunks, no honest backbone. They remain rounded, soft-topped, pliant as the grasses, and they answer the least pressure of wind with a collective shiver. Their leaves turn and flash; the green surface lifts in long swells like waves, and then, as the branches twist, the underside shows—silver-white, bright as fish bellies in sun—so that the whole plain looks alive, moving, breathing, pretending.

The Danube is happy here, too happy. Released from banks, it wanders among an intricate web of channels that cut the islands into pieces and remake them again. Water pours down broad avenues with a shouting sound, making whirlpools that suddenly open like mouths, eddies that spin in place, rapids that foam and snap at the shallows. It claws at sandy edges, pulls whole clumps of willow into itself, and builds new islands by the dozen—little temporary lives of shingle and root that change shape from day to day and may be obliterated entirely when flood-time comes and wipes the slate clean.

This part of the river’s life begins properly soon after Pressburg, and we—two men in a Canadian canoe with a gipsy tent and frying-pan stowed like domestic necessities—reached it on the crest of a rising flood in mid-July. That same morning, when the sky was only beginning to redden before sunrise, we slipped through still-sleeping Vienna. A couple of hours later it was nothing but a smudge of smoke against the blue hills of the Wienerwald. We took breakfast below Fischeramend beneath birches that roared in the wind, and I remember the simple comfort of it—bread broken with the hand, a sharp-smelling cheese, cold slices of something smoked and salty from Pressburg, the butter softening in the heat. Even the river air had a taste then: clean and wet, with the faint metallic tang that comes off stones when water is running fast.

After that, the current did most of our traveling for us. We swept past Orth, Hainburg, Petronell—old Roman Carnuntum, where Marcus Aurelius once wrote his sober thoughts while the world made war around him—and then under the frowning heights of Thelsen where the March steals in from the left and the frontier is crossed between Austria and Hungary. The canoe raced at twelve kilometers an hour; the muddy water—sure sign of flood—sent us grinding onto shingle-beds more than once, and in sudden belching whirlpools it twisted us like a cork until Pressburg’s towers showed against the sky. Then the canoe, leaping like a spirited horse, shot under grey walls, negotiated the sunken chain of the Fliegende Brücke ferry, turned sharp to the left, and plunged on yellow foam into the wilderness beyond—the land of willows.

The change was abrupt, like a set of pictures snapping down in a bioscope: streets and roofs and chimneys, and then—without warning—water and wind and a low horizon crowded with whispering growth. In less than half an hour there was no boat, no fishing-hut, no red roof, no signal of people anywhere. The isolation dropped over us like a garment. We laughed and said we ought to have carried some special passport to enter this separate kingdom of water and willow—this place reserved for other tenants with older rights. The laughter was genuine, yet it had the edge of men aware, even while joking, that a line has been crossed and no one asked permission.

The wind had been hammering us all day, and though it was still early afternoon the ceaseless buffetings made us tired in the bones. We began at once to hunt a place to camp. Landing was difficult; the swirling flood carried us in and flung us out again. The willows clawed at our hands when we seized the branches to stop the canoe. We pulled whole yards of sandbank into the water in our struggle. At last a great sideways blow from the wind shoved us into a backwater, and we beached the bows in a burst of spray. We lay panting and laughing on the hot yellow sand, sheltered from the gale, the sun beating down from a cloudless blue, while all around us the willows danced and shouted. Their leaves clapped and flashed as though applauding our success. The air was full of their smell: green sap bruised by wind, wet bark, and that faint sourness of marsh where old water lies beneath new.

“What a river,” I said, thinking of the way we had followed it from the Black Forest, and how my companion—my friend the Swede—had often been obliged to wade and push in those first shallow miles back in June.

“It won’t stand much nonsense now, will it?” he answered, hauling the canoe farther up the sand. Then, with the practical peace of a man who can sleep anywhere, he made a pillow of his rolled coat and closed his eyes.

I lay beside him, feeling for a moment utterly content in the bath of elements—water and wind and sand and the great fire of the sun. I thought of the distance behind us and of the long stretch ahead to the Black Sea. I thought, too, how lucky I was in my companion, for we had made many journeys together and I knew his steadiness. He had a way of turning even hardship into routine, as if the world, however wild, could be managed with the right knots and a well-tended pan.

Of all the rivers I knew, the Danube impressed us from the beginning with its aliveness. From its small bubbling birth among the pinewood gardens of Donaueschingen to this moment when it began its great game of losing itself among deserted swamps, it seemed like watching the growth of a creature. Sleepy at first, later violent with desire as though becoming conscious of its own deep soul, it rolled through the countries on its mighty shoulders, rough with us sometimes but always friendly—until we could not help but regard it as a Great Personage.

And it told us its secret life, chiefly by sound. At night, lying in our tent, we heard it singing to the moon—an odd sibilant note peculiar to itself, said to come from pebbles tearing along its bed so fast they rasp and whisper. We knew the voices of its gurgling whirlpools, bubbling suddenly where the surface had been calm; the roar of shallows and swift rapids; the deep steady thunder under all surface noise; the ceaseless gnaw of icy water at the banks. When rain fell flat on its face it stood up and shouted. When the wind blew upstream and tried to halt it, its laughter rolled out in great, derisive gusts. It splashed unnecessarily against bridges as if making a point. It chattered self-consciously when there were hills to look at. It assumed an affected dignity passing through towns, too important to laugh. And there were stretches where the sun found it in a slow curve and poured down until steam rose—faint, sweet whisperings then, as though it spoke softly only when no one was near.

In its early life it had tricks. Up in the Swabian forests, before the world had told it what it must become, there were places it chose to vanish through holes in the ground, to appear again beyond porous limestone and begin a new river under another name—leaving so little in its own bed that we had to climb out and push the canoe through miles of shallows. It would lie low like Brer Fox just before little turbulent tributaries came in from the Alps, and refuse to acknowledge them, running for miles side by side with a sharp dividing line and even different levels—pretending the newcomer was beneath notice. Below Passau it gave up that particular vanity, because there the Inn arrives with a power impossible to ignore. It shoves the parent river this way and that against the cliffs, crowding it in the long twisting gorge that follows, forcing it to hurry with great waves and much dashing, and our canoe—slipping from shoulder to breast—had the time of its life among the struggling water. The Inn taught the old river a lesson, and after Passau it no longer pretended not to see new arrivals.

All that was days behind us, and we had since watched other moods. Across the Bavarian wheat plain near Straubing it wandered so slowly in blazing June that one could imagine only the surface inches were water while below, hidden as by a silken mantle, a whole army of Undines moved silently toward the sea, leisurely so as not to be discovered. Much, too, we forgave the Danube because of its friendliness to birds and animals along its shores. Cormorants lined lonely banks like short black palings. Grey crows crowded shingle-beds. Storks fished in shallow vistas between islands. Hawks, swans, marsh birds filled the air with glinting wings and petulant cries. Once, at sunrise, we saw a deer leap with a splash and swim past the bows, and after that it was hard to resent the river’s tricks. Fawns peered at us from underbrush; stags stared with calm brown eyes as we charged round a corner into a new reach. Foxes haunted the banks, tripping daintily among driftwood, vanishing so suddenly one could never quite understand how.

But after Pressburg everything altered. The Danube became serious. It ceased trifling, as though aware it was half-way to the Black Sea and already within smelling distance of stranger countries where games are not permitted. It split into three arms that did not meet again for a hundred kilometers, and for a canoe there was no sign at all which was meant to be followed.

“If you take a side channel,” the Hungarian officer had told us in the Pressburg shop while we bought provisions, “and the flood goes down, you may find yourselves forty miles from anywhere, high and dry, and you may easily starve. There are no people, no farms, no fishermen. I warn you not to continue. The river is rising, and this wind will increase.”

The rising water did not alarm us. But the idea of being stranded when the river withdrew, leaving us marooned on bare shingle amid miles of swamp, was a practical danger. We laid in extra provisions—more bread than usual, smoked bacon, onions and garlic, a small hard cheese that would keep, and a precious little sack of flour that would at least allow a kind of rough pan-cake if all else failed. The officer’s prophecy proved accurate in one respect, at any rate: the wind, blowing down a perfectly clear sky, increased steadily until it had the dignity of a westerly gale.

We camped earlier than usual, though the sun still stood a good hour or two above the horizon. My companion slept again on the hot sand, and I wandered about our little “hotel” as one examines a kitchen before deciding where to set the fire and how to keep supplies dry. The island was less than an acre, a mere sandy bank only two or three feet above the river. It was triangular, with its apex upstream. At the far end, pointing toward the sunset, flying spray lifted from broken wave-crests and blew away in sheets, and the air there tasted of raw water—cold on the tongue despite the heat.

I stood at that point for several minutes watching the impetuous crimson flood bear down with a shouting roar, dashing against the bank as though it meant to sweep the entire island away, then swirling by in two foaming streams on either side. The ground seemed to tremble with the shock and rush. The willows, lashed by wind, increased a curious illusion that the island itself moved—as if it were not fixed land at all but something afloat, only pretending to be solid.

Above, for a mile or two, I could see the river descending upon us like a sliding hill white with foam, leaping everywhere to show itself to the sun. From the lower end, where shadow gathered, the water looked dark and angry. Only the backs of waves were visible, streaked with foam, pushed forcibly by great puffs of wind that fell upon them from behind. The river poured in and out among islands for a short mile, then vanished with a huge sweep into the willows, which closed around it like a herd of monstrous antediluvian creatures crowding down to drink. They made me think of sponge-like growths that sucked the river up into themselves—an image both absurd and unpleasant, for it suggested hunger.

The rest of the island was too thickly grown with willow to make walking pleasant, but I made the tour. Everywhere the bushes pressed close, innumerable, shoulder to shoulder. Altogether it was impressive—the loneliness, the bizarre suggestion—and as I gazed, long and curiously, something stirred unbidden in me. Midway through delight at the wild beauty, a feeling of disquietude crept in, almost alarm.

A rising river always carries something ominous: many of the little islands in sight would likely be gone by morning, swept away without drama. The resistless thundering flood touched the sense of awe. Yet I knew the uneasiness lay deeper than awe. It was not simply the power of wind—this shouting gale that might almost lift acres of willow and scatter them like chaff. The wind seemed to be enjoying itself, and I was aware of sharing its great game with a kind of exhilaration. No, the distress was vaguer, harder to seize. It had something to do with our insignificance, perhaps, and with the thought that we had come—lightly, with tent and pan and a man’s confidence in his own habits—into the reach of forces that could take us without noticing.

But more than the river, more than the wind, the willows troubled me. Not any single bush, of course, but their multitude—acres upon acres of them, crowding, swarming, pressing upon the water as if to suffocate it, standing mile after mile beneath the sky with an attitude I could only describe, irrationally, as watchful. The mind, besieged by their number, began to invent intention. They seemed a power—mighty, patient, and not altogether friendly.

Mountains overawe, oceans terrify, forests cast their spell; but these things, at some point, connect with human experience. They exalt even when they frighten. This willow world felt different. Something emanated from it that pressed upon the heart. Awe, yes—but awe with a vague terror running through it like a colder current. Their serried ranks, darker as shadow thickened, moving furiously yet softly, woke the unwelcome suggestion that we had trespassed on the borders of an alien country, where we were intruders—unwanted, uninvited, and perhaps in danger.

The feeling did not yet become outright menace. Still, it did not leave me, even when I returned to practical matters: putting up the tent in the teeth of the wind, setting our few belongings in order, making our temporary home against the coming night. I said nothing to my companion. He was, in my opinion, a man without imagination; I could not have explained what I meant, and he would have laughed, missing the point by inches and calling it a mile.

There was a slight depression in the center of the island, and there we pitched the tent, using the surrounding willows as a partial break against the gale.

“A poor camp,” the imperturbable Swede observed when the canvas at last stood upright. “No stones and precious little firewood. I’m for moving on early tomorrow—eh? This sand won’t hold anything.”

We had both endured tents collapsing at midnight and had learned a few devices. We anchored the gipsy house as securely as we could, then set about collecting a store of wood to last until bed-time. Willow bushes do not willingly drop branches; driftwood was our only fuel. We hunted the shores, the two of us moving like housekeepers in a storm, scanning for anything dry enough to burn. Everywhere the banks were crumbling as the rising flood tore at them, carrying away whole slices of the island with a splash and a gurgle.

“The island’s smaller than when we landed,” said the accurate Swede. “It won’t last long at this rate. We’d better drag the canoe close to the tent and be ready to start at a moment’s notice. I shall sleep in my clothes.”

He was a little distance off along the bank. I heard his rather jolly laugh as he spoke, and then, a moment later—

“By Jove!”

His voice changed. The laugh fell out of it.

“What in the world’s this?” he cried again, and now there was seriousness—something even he could not file away as mere inconvenience.

I ran to him. He stood pointing over the river at something in the water.

“Good heavens, it’s a man’s body!” he cried. “Look!”

A black thing turned over and over in the foaming waves as it swept rapidly past about twenty feet from shore. It vanished and returned to the surface, helpless-looking. Opposite where we stood it lurched, and for an instant it seemed to look straight at us. We saw eyes reflect the low sun—odd yellow gleams in a dark wet face—then the thing gave a swift gulping plunge and dived out of sight.

“An otter,” we exclaimed together, and laughed too loudly, the relief coming out of us as noise.

It was an otter, alive and hunting; yet for several seconds it had looked exactly like a drowned man turning in the current. Far below it surfaced again, black skin wet and shining in the sunlight, and was gone.

We turned back with our arms full of driftwood, already thinking of flame and the pan—of making something hot, of bringing the day under control with salt and simmering water and the familiar comfort of eating. The river had other ideas. Another interruption pulled us to the bank.

This time it really was a man—and a man in a boat.

Even on the Danube, a small boat is not common in such a reach. Here, in this flooded deserted place, it was startling enough to feel like an event. We stood and stared.

Whether it was the slanting sunlight, or refraction from the brilliantly lit water, I cannot say, but I found it difficult to focus on the apparition. It seemed a man standing upright in a flat-bottomed craft, steering with a long oar, being carried down the opposite shore at a tremendous pace. He appeared to be looking across toward us; then he began gesticulating. His voice came over the water in furious shouting, but the wind ate the words whole. Not a syllable arrived intact. Something about him—the posture, the frantic movements, the violence of his silent message—made an impression on me far beyond its visible cause.

“He’s crossing himself!” I cried. “Look—he’s making the sign of the Cross!”

“I believe you’re right,” the Swede said, shading his eyes and watching until the man was swallowed by the willows downstream, melting into that crimson wall where the sun caught the bend and set the bushes aflame with color. A thin mist had begun to rise; the air turned hazy, softening distances as if the marsh were exhaling.

“But what in the world is he doing at nightfall on this flooded river?” I said, more to myself than to him. “Where is he going—and what did he mean by those signs? Do you think he meant to warn us?”

“He saw our smoke and thought we were spirits,” my companion laughed, though his laughter did not sit as comfortably as usual. “These Hungarians believe in all sorts of rubbish. You remember the shopwoman at Pressburg warning us that no one ever landed here because it belonged to beings outside man’s world. Fairies and elementals, perhaps—demons too, no doubt. That peasant saw people on the islands for the first time in his life,” he added after a pause, “and it scared him, that’s all.”

His voice tried for easy contempt, but his manner had altered; it lacked its customary hardness of certainty. I noticed it at once.

“If they had enough imagination,” I laughed loudly—I remember making as much noise as I could, as though sound itself might keep the world familiar—“they might well people a place like this with the old gods of antiquity. The Romans must have haunted all this region with shrines and sacred groves and elemental deities.”

Suddenly I found myself awake, staring out through the half-loosened door of the tent as though some hand had drawn my eyelids apart. The canvas, powdered with sand, breathed in and out with the gusts; and my watch—pinned where I could find it by habit even in darkness—shone coldly in the moonlight. It was past twelve: the old day had been kneaded away and a new one begun, clean and ominous as a white board before the first cut.

The Swede slept on, heavy and trustful, his mouth a little open, the rhythm of his breathing stubbornly human against the long, furious voice of the wind. That wind had not slackened since we lay down; it worried the willows without mercy, set their thin wands clattering, and drove the river’s breath across the sand with a taste like wet stones. Something plucked at me—no thought exactly, rather a tug in the nerves, as if the place had changed its seasoning while we slept and my body detected it first. The immediate neighborhood felt disturbed, as though an unseen hearth had been upset and its ash blown about.

I sat up, listening. In such places you learn the land not by sight but by its acoustics: the pitch of the river, the way a gust finds a hollow and booms like a drum, the fine rattling of leaves that ought to soothe and yet—here—worked on the mind like a repeated syllable in a language you almost understand but cannot translate. There was no machinery, no iron din of towns, no vulgar commercial music blaring meaning at you; and precisely because the soundscape was so pure, every alteration carried authority. The river suddenly seemed louder, nearer; the wind’s howl had an edge to it, as if a different mouth were making it.

I crawled quietly toward the opening, careful not to wake my companion. A curious excitement ran through me, sharp as the smell when you first grate bitter peel into warm sugar—bright, unsettling, impossible to ignore. I was half out, kneeling on all fours, when my eye caught the tops of the bushes opposite us. The willows, etched against the sky, made moving tracery in the moon, and the tracery—this was the thing—kept gathering itself into shapes that were not the shapes of branches.

I sat back on my haunches and stared until my eyes smarted with the effort of refusing to blink. There, close—some fifty feet in front of me—were outlines among the willows that held together while everything else swayed and tore. They were monstrous in suggestion, shifting rapidly under the moon, and yet not dissolving as any trick of leaf and light ought to dissolve when you stare it down.

My first instinct was to shake the Swede awake, to force another pair of eyes into the matter, to make it communal and therefore safer—like tasting a doubtful broth and calling someone else to confirm the bitterness. But something in me hesitated. I did not welcome corroboration. If another man saw them too, then the world would truly have changed its rules; and I crouched there, alone with my sight, whispering fiercely to myself that I was not dreaming.

Then the figures became properly visible—huge, bronze-coloured, moving—wholly independent of the willow-tops that seemed to fringe them like weed around a rising fish. They were immensely larger than human, and something in the very proportion and poise of them declared them not human at all. They did not merely flicker with the boughs. They shifted of their own accord, and they rose—unceasingly—upwards from earth to sky, vanishing utterly as soon as they reached the darker vault above the moon’s glare.

As my mind steadied enough to observe, I saw that they were interlaced one with another, making a great column. Limbs and enormous bodies melted in and out of each other as though the substance of them could not be held to one outline, forming a serpentine line that bent and swayed and twisted spirally with the contortions of the wind-tossed trees, yet was not governed by those contortions. Nude, fluid shapes passed up through the bushes—within the leaves almost—rising in a living column into the heavens. Their faces I could never see. The column poured upwards in great bending curves, dull bronze on their skins like the colour of old cookware darkened by years of fire and handling.

I stared as though I could force more vision out of my eyes by sheer hunger. For a long time I expected them to vanish each moment, to resolve into branch and moonlight and prove themselves a mistake—the way a scent in a kitchen might seem for an instant like smoke until you find the scorched corner and understand. I searched everywhere for the proof that would restore the old standard of reality. Yet all the while I understood that the standard had shifted, that I was measuring with instruments that no longer applied.

The longer I looked, the more certain I became that these figures were real and living—though perhaps not according to the tidy demands of camera or biologist. Far from fear, I was possessed with a sense of awe so clean and consuming it made my throat ache. It was as if I gazed at the personified elemental forces of this haunted, primeval region, the way old people in field-country speak of weather as though it had intention and kinship and could be offended. We had intruded; our presence had stirred whatever lay under the willows, and the place answered.

Stories and legends—those slow-cooked broths of generations—filled my head to bursting: spirits of river and sandbank, deities of grove and wind, acknowledged and worshipped in every age by men who knew that certain experiences cannot be carried into another tongue without losing their essential taste. Here, in this moonlit island, I felt the helplessness of modern words. “Wind,” “river,” “willows”—the dominant lexicon made them small, domesticated, safe. Yet what I saw and heard refused that domestication. The very language in which I tried to think seemed a poor vessel for the thing pressing in upon us.

Before I could shape even a private explanation, something impelled me farther out. I crept forward on the sand and stood upright. The ground under my bare feet was still warm, holding the day’s heat the way baked clay holds it; grains pressed into my skin with intimate precision. The wind tore at my hair and face, bringing with it the raw, green scent of willow-sap and the colder, mineral breath off the river. And then the sound of the river burst upon my ears with a sudden roar, as if a door had opened. It was not merely loud; it was patterned—rush and pull, rush and pull—an immense throat speaking in a dialect older than ours, and I knew by that clarity of sound that my senses were not failing me.

The figures still rose from earth to heaven, silent and majestic, in a great spiral of grace and strength that overwhelmed me with a deep emotion that was, unmistakably, worship. I felt that I must fall down and worship—absolutely worship—as naturally as one kneels when the ground itself seems a shrine. Another minute, and I might have done it.

A gust struck me then with such force that it blew me sideways. I nearly stumbled and fell, my feet sliding on the sand as on sifted flour. The shock jarred something loose. It shook the spell—if spell it was—violently out of me, or at least gave me a different angle upon it. The figures remained; they still ascended from the heart of the night. But my reason, bruised into activity, began to assert itself.

It must be subjective, I argued—none the less vivid for that, but still subjective. Moonlight and branch-work, the incessant music of wind and river, and the exhausted nerves of two men sleeping on sand: these could combine to paint pictures upon the mirror of imagination, and for some reason I might project them outward and mistake them for objective things. I clung to that explanation as to a familiar recipe when the meal has gone strangely wrong.

I took courage from the thought and began to move forward across the open patches of sand. Yet even as I forced my legs to obey, doubt gnawed at the argument. By Jove, was it hallucination? Was it merely subjective? Did not my reason argue in the old futile way from the little standard of the known—making a cage out of vocabulary and calling the cage “truth”? The column of figures ascended darkly into the sky for what seemed a very long time, with a completeness of reality such as most men are accustomed to call reality.

Then, suddenly, they were gone.

They did not fade. They did not unravel back into willow and moon. They were simply—absent, as if some sluice had been shut. And the instant their great presence withdrew, fear came down upon me with a cold rush, as though all warmth had been ladled out of the air. The lonely and haunted meaning of this region flamed up within me; the willows that had seemed mere trees became a watchful multitude. I began to tremble dreadfully.

I took a quick look round—horror close to panic—calculating vainly ways of escape: the water, the sand, the low tent, the interminable islands. The wind and the river had the place; we were only guests who had not been invited and could not now pretend ignorance of the household’s deeper life. Realizing how helpless I was to achieve anything effective, I crept back silently into the tent.

I lowered the door-curtain to shut out the moonlit willows, as one shuts a cupboard on something one cannot bear to see. Then I lay down again upon my sandy mattress, burying my head as deeply as possible beneath the blankets to deaden the sound of the terrifying wind, trying—futilely—to muffle the country’s vast, articulate voice until it became once more the ordinary noise of weather and not the utterance of powers we had wakened.

As though to prove to me—cruelly, patiently—that I had not been dreaming, it was a long time before sleep took me again at all. And when it did, it was only the surface of me that went under, like the thin skin that forms on a pot left too long near the coals; beneath it something kept watch, hearing, tasting the air, never surrendering. Then, for the second time, terror snapped me upright as cleanly as a knife through twine.

It was not the wind—magnificent as it was in its ranting—nor the river with its hoarse, swollen throat, that woke me. It was the slow approach of something, the sense of it drawing nearer with a deliberation that shrank my sleeping self smaller and smaller until there was none of it left. I sat bolt upright, listening.

Outside came a sound of innumerable little patterings, as if a multitude of tiny feet had found the taut canvas and were testing it—tap, tap, tap—multiplied until it became a continuous, intimate assault. I knew, even then, that it had been coming a long while; in sleep my ear had first admitted it, and now I was as awake as if I had never closed my eyes. My breath came grudgingly, as though something had laid a broad palm upon my chest. In spite of the hot night, my skin filmed with cold, clammy as dough left uncovered in a draught, and I shivered.

Something pressed steadily against the sides of the tent and weighed down from above. I searched for the ordinary answers the way a cook reaches first for salt, then pepper—reflex, routine: was it rain, the drip and tremble of leaves? spray driven up from the river, gathering into heavy drops and striking canvas? Was the wind flinging sand like grain against a drum-skin? A dozen explanations offered themselves, each with the reassuring domesticity of cause and effect.

Then the simplest leaped to the front: a bough from the poplar—the only real tree on the island—had broken in the night. Hung up among the other limbs, it might be lying across our roof, its leaves brushing and tapping as it shifted; and the next gust would drop it full weight and crush us.

I flung up a loose flap and rushed out, calling to the Swede to follow.

But the moment I stood upright the entire idea collapsed. The tent was free. There was no bough sagging across it, no rain, no blown spray, no nearer thing at all. A cold grey light filtered down through the bushes and lay upon the faintly gleaming sand. Stars still crowded the strip of sky overhead. The wind went howling through the willows with a grandeur that had something brutal in it—an ancient music of air and branches—while our fire, starved and scattered, gave no glow. Through the trees the east showed streaks of red, like raw flesh beneath skin.

Several hours must have passed since I had stood before watching those ascending figures; the memory of it returned now with the sticky persistence of an evil dream. Weariness sat on me—heavy, dull—such as comes from a night without true sleep, when the body has been kept at a simmer and never allowed to boil or cool. Yet my nerves were bright and thin, like strained wire. The ceaseless raging wind exhausted me in the very way an incessant modern din does—the grinding, unrelenting noise that gives no ear a resting place, no mind a quiet corner to nest in. The river’s thunder filled the air, and its fine spray made itself felt even through my thin sleeping shirt, a cold peppering on the skin.

Still, nowhere did I find the slightest evidence of anything that should have woken me as it had. The deep disturbance in my heart remained unaccounted for, and that made it worse. The Swede had not stirred when I called; there was no need to wake him now. I looked about carefully, as though inventorying a larder after a theft. There was the canoe, turned over; the yellow paddles—two of them, I was certain; the provision sack and the extra lantern hanging together from the tree. And everywhere, crowding, enveloping, touching the very edges of thought, the willows—endless, shaking willows—making their dry, multitudinous clatter, a kind of wild acoustic ecology all their own, a whole island of sound with no silence in it.

A bird uttered its morning cry. A string of duck passed overhead in the twilight with a whirring flight, the sound sharp as a whisk through air. Sand whirled, dry and stinging, about my bare feet in the wind. I walked round the tent, then pushed a little way into the bush to see across the river to the far landscape. The same profound yet indefinable distress seized me again when I saw that interminable sea of scrub stretching to the horizon, ghostly and unreal in the wan light—like a country with all its familiar names erased, leaving only shapes no tongue could quite hold.

I moved softly here and there, still puzzling over the infinite pattering and the pressure that had wakened me. It must have been the wind, I told myself—the wind driving loose hot sand smartly against the taut canvas, the wind dropping down in gusts as if it had weight and intention. Yet my nervousness and malaise increased as steadily as a pot left unattended comes to the boil.

I crossed over to the farther shore and noted how the coast-line had altered in the night, how much sand the river had torn away and carried off as carelessly as a hand sweeping crumbs. I dipped my hands and feet into the cool current, and bathed my forehead. The cold on my skin was a brief mercy, like a sip of water after too much salt. Already there was a glow of sunrise, and the air had that exquisite freshness that comes before day is fully claimed by heat and habit.

On my way back I went deliberately beneath the bushes where I had seen the column of figures rise. Midway among the clumps, without warning, a vast terror overtook me—as if the very soundscape shifted and something huge stepped between notes. From the shadows a large figure went swiftly by. Someone passed me, as surely as any man ever did.

A great staggering blow from the wind helped me forward again. Once I was out in the more open space, the sense of terror diminished strangely, as if it could not follow where there were fewer hiding-places for it. “The winds are about and walking,” I remember saying to myself—for winds do move under trees like great presences, and the mind will seize any familiar word, any inherited phrase, to pin the unnameable down. Yet the fear that hovered near me was of an unknown and immense kind, unlike anything I had felt before, and in that very strangeness it woke awe as well as dread; wonder rose against it as a counter-spice.

When I reached a higher point in the middle of the island from which I could see the wide stretch of river crimson in the sunrise, the magical beauty of it was so overpowering that a wild yearning woke in me and almost brought a cry up into my throat. The dawn smelled of wet sand, torn leaves, and the river’s metallic breath; it was beautiful in the way a perfectly made dish can be beautiful even when you suspect it will make you ill.

But the cry found no voice. As my eyes wandered from the plain beyond to the island round me, and fell upon our little tent half hidden among the willows, a dreadful discovery leaped out—one compared to which my terror of the walking winds seemed almost childish.

A change had come about in the arrangement of the landscape.

It was not that my vantage gave me a different view. It was that the relation of the tent to the willows, and of the willows to the tent, had altered. Surely the bushes now crowded closer—unnecessarily, unpleasantly close. They had moved nearer.

Creeping with silent feet over shifting sands, drawing imperceptibly nearer by soft, unhurried movements, the willows had come closer during the night. Had the wind done it? Or had they moved of themselves?

In my mind, the sound of multitudinous patterings returned at once, and the pressure on the tent, and the pressure on my own heart. The idea suggested personal agency—deliberate intention—aggressive hostility. It terrified me into a rigid stillness. The wind swayed me where I stood, and for a moment I bent and yielded like a tree, finding it hard to keep my footing on the sandy hillock.

Then reaction came quickly, as it does when the mind cannot bear its own conclusion. The thought was so bizarre—so absurd—that I felt the impulse to laugh. But laughter came no more readily than the cry. For with the recognition that my mind could entertain such an idea came a fresh, sharper terror: that it was through our minds, not our bodies, the attack would come—and was coming. There are things that do not need claws if they can make your language betray you, can make the words you’ve always trusted feel thin, borrowed, dominant, insufficient. If a man cannot name what he senses, he begins to doubt the sensing—and then he is at the mercy of whatever has taken the names away.

The wind buffeted me, and the sun came up quickly, for it was after four o’clock, and I must have stood on that little pinnacle longer than I knew, afraid to come down to close quarters with the willows.

At last I returned to the tent quietly—creepily—first taking another exhaustive look round and, yes, I confess it, making a few measurements. I paced out on the warming sand the distances between the nearest willow clumps and the tent, making note of the shortest distance in particular, as carefully as a food historian might mark the vanishing boundaries of an old field in an overbuilt city, fearful that by tomorrow the landmarks would be gone and no one would remember where anything truly began. Then I crawled stealthily into my blankets.

My companion, to all appearances, still slept soundly, and I was glad of it. So long as my experiences were not corroborated, I could find strength to deny them—could call them a private hallucination, a night’s fantasy, a projection of an excited imagination onto a landscape too monotonous and too loud with wind. Daylight is a kind of dominant lexicon; it insists on its own explanations.

Nothing further came to disturb me. Utterly exhausted, I fell asleep almost at once—yet still in dread of hearing again that weird sound of innumerable patterings, or feeling again that weight upon my heart that made it difficult to breathe.

The sun stood straight overhead when my companion shook me out of a sleep so deep it had weight to it, as though it had been packed down over my chest like damp sand.

“Up,” he said. “Porridge is done. And there’s time to bathe before the next bit of bank goes.”

Behind his words came the domestic consolation of smell: oats swelling in hot water, a pinch of salt, and—more heartening still—the rich, animal sweetness of bacon fat hissing where it met the pan. Even here, even on this raw spit of sand and willow, the day began the way days ought to begin: with heat, grain, and the promise of something browned.

“River still rising,” he added. “Several little islands out mid-stream have gone under in the night. Ours is smaller again.”

“Any wood left?” I asked, my mouth already tasting the porridge—starchy, faintly nutty, the salt waking it—and the bacon’s edge of smoke.

He gave a short laugh that sounded too bright. “The wood and the island will finish tomorrow in a dead heat. But we’ve enough till then.”

I ran down to the point and flung myself into the Danube. The water took me like a hand clamped on the spine—ice, shock, a clean violence. I had no sooner struck out than the current seized my body and rolled me downstream; the bank slid past with the speed of a train window, willows streaking into green smears. I let it carry me, then clawed toward our landing-place opposite the tent and hauled myself up, skin singing. In that instant of cold and sun, when breath returns and blood comes roaring back, the night’s terrors seemed to steam off my thoughts the way wet clothes steam on a line.

The sun blazed without mercy; not a cloud blemished the blue. Only the wind persisted, as though it had made a wager with the river and meant to win.

Then, as I dried myself and the smell of breakfast thickened around the tent, the meaning of his “enough till tomorrow” struck me with a suddenness that was almost physical. He had assumed we would remain another night. Yet last evening he had been urgent—almost frantic—to go. The change in him sat wrong, like a familiar spice suddenly missing from a dish you’ve made a hundred times.

At breakfast the island kept falling away in great mouthfuls. The bank would crumble with a heavy splash, the river swallowing sand in clouds of spray that the wind whipped straight into the pan. We ate porridge slicked with bacon fat, spoonfuls made savory with the faint scorch from the bottom where oats had caught. The Swede talked rapidly, too rapidly, about the trouble the Vienna–Pesth steamers must have finding channel marks in flood. But what caught me was not his talk—it was his manner. He was altered: a little excited, a little shy, and threaded through with suspicion. He watched the willow thickets as though they might answer him.

He ate very little. For once he did not load and light his pipe. Instead he spread the map beside his plate and traced the river with a finger that trembled almost imperceptibly.

“We’d better get off sharp in an hour,” I said, choosing my words as one chooses a knife—careful, trying to find the seam.

“Rather,” he answered at once. Then, without looking up: “If they’ll let us.”

“Who will?” I asked, forcing indifference into my voice. “The elements?”

“The powers of this place—whoever they are,” he replied. “The gods are here, if they are anywhere.”

“The elements are always the true immortals,” I said, and laughed as naturally as I could. But when he looked up, grave and steady, I knew my face had betrayed me.

“We shall be fortunate if we get away without further disaster,” he said.

The words landed like grit between teeth. I made myself do it—the direct question—because the dread of it was worse than the asking.

“Further disaster? What’s happened?”

“For one thing—the steering paddle’s gone.”

“The steering paddle—gone?” I repeated. That was our rudder. In a flooded Danube, without it, we were no better than a chip. “But what—”

“And there’s a tear in the bottom of the canoe,” he added, and this time his voice quivered with something like genuine fear.

Heat and sunlight and the smell of cooked oats belonged suddenly to another world. A coldness came down around us, as though the air had been drained and replaced.

He rose and nodded for me to follow. The canoe lay where it had been in the night, ribs uppermost, a paddle—only one—on the sand beside her.

“There’s only one,” he said, picking it up. “And here’s the rent.”

He pointed. I went nearer.

In the bottom board a long slit ran clean and deliberate, as if something sharp had bitten and then slid, shaving out a thin strip of wood with obscene neatness. It was not a jagged gash but a crafted wound. When I put my eye close, I could see through. Had we put her in the water in haste, she would have taken us as far as the current pleased, and then, midstream, filled and gone down.

“There,” I heard him murmur, more to himself than to me. “An attempt to prepare a victim for the sacrifice. Two victims,” he added, and ran his fingers along the slit as though reading it.

I began to whistle—an idiotic habit that arrives unbidden when my mind finds no purchase—and I fixed my attention on the practical: wood grain, pitch, repairs. I would not take his words in.

“It wasn’t there last night,” he said, straightening, looking anywhere but at me.

“We must have scratched her landing,” I said. “The stones are sharp.”

The sentence stopped itself. There were no stones—only sand, yielding sand, and the roots of willows. He met my eye squarely. We both knew.

“And this as well,” he said, and held out the paddle, pointing to its blade.

I took it, and something chilled in me with the strange intimacy of touch. The blade had been scraped—scraped with care, evenly, patiently, as if someone had worked it down with a file or abrasive until it was thinned to weakness. One hard stroke and it would snap at the elbow.

“One of us walked in his sleep and—did it,” I said, hearing how thin my own voice sounded. “Or the sand in the wind… it’s like sandpaper… it might have—”

“Ah,” he said, turning away with a short laugh that held no amusement. “You can explain everything.”

“The same wind that took the steering paddle,” I called after him, feeling a foolish determination harden in me, “and flung it to the bank so it fell in when the edge crumbled.”

“I see,” he shouted back, and disappeared among the willows.

Left alone with the canoe and the scraped paddle, I tried to build my safety out of logic: One of us must have done it, and it was not I. But the next thought was worse, because it answered the first. Under the conditions of the night—our fatigue, the roar, the strange pressure in the air—how could either of us have made so neat a slit, so careful a thinning? My companion was the steady one, the practical one, the trusted friend of a dozen journeys. The notion of him sabotaging us was absurd. The notion of him suddenly mad, occupied with mad purposes, was equally absurd.

And yet something had happened to his mind. That was what unsettled me more than the river’s hunger. He was listening for things. Waiting. Watching a series of events he could not name without making them real. He expected a climax, and expected it soon.

I searched the tent and its surroundings. The curious measurements of the night remained: the same sense of space not agreeing with the eye. In the sand I noticed, for the first time by daylight, deep hollows—basin-shaped craters of varying size, from a teacup dip to something like a wide bowl. The wind could do that, easily; I had seen street dust twist itself into little vortices. Here the sand was loose and obedient. I clung to such explanations with the doggedness of a cook clinging to method when the fire behaves strangely—because method is sanity. The rent in the canoe was the one taste I could not account for, the sourness that spoiled every other flavor. Still, I told myself, it was conceivable a sharp root had caught her in landing. The shore inspection did not help the theory, but I held to it with the dwindling portion of mind I could still call “reason.” One must have a working explanation, however absurd, the way one must have a mother tongue: imperfect, biased, full of gaps—yet the only instrument by which the world can be made livable. To lose it is to be exiled.

I set the pitch melting, and when the Swede returned he joined the work. Under the best conditions we could not travel safely till the following day. I pointed, as casually as I could, to the sand hollows.

“Yes,” he said. “They’re all over the island. But you can explain them, no doubt.”

“Wind,” I answered at once. “Little whirlwinds. The sand yields, that’s all.”

He did not reply. We worked on in silence. I watched him without seeming to, and I felt—uncomfortably—that he watched me too. Often he paused, head slightly turned, as though some fine sound lay just beyond my hearing. He would stare into the willow thickets, up at the sky, out across the water through the openings. Once or twice he lifted a hand to cup his ear and held it there for minutes at a time, rigid, listening the way a man listens for his name spoken behind a door. He said nothing, and I asked nothing. I was grateful for his absorption in the repair, for I dreaded him speaking of the willows’ changed look. If he had noticed that, then my own uneasy perceptions could no longer be shrugged off as the private lies of imagination.

After a long pause he spoke abruptly, as though he had to get it out quickly.

“Queer thing,” he said. “About that otter last night.”

The subject startled me; I looked up.

“Shows how lonely it is,” I began. “Otters are shy—”

“I don’t mean that,” he cut in. “I mean—do you think it was an otter at all?”

“What else could it have been?”

“I saw it before you,” he said. “At first it seemed… bigger than an otter.”

“The sunset up-stream magnified it,” I answered quickly. “A trick of light.”

He stared at me absently, his mind elsewhere.

“It had such extraordinary yellow eyes,” he went on.

“The sun again,” I said, and laughed too loudly. “You’ll wonder next whether that fellow in the boat—”

I stopped. He had tilted his head again, listening, and something in his face—an alert dread, like a dog scenting smoke—shut my mouth.

We went on with the caulking. Five minutes later he looked across the upturned canoe, pitch smoking in his hand. His expression was solemn.

“I did rather wonder,” he said slowly, “what that thing in the boat was. I remember thinking it was not a man. The whole business seemed to rise out of the water.”

I laughed again, louder, this time with impatience and a flash of anger—anger at him, at the place, at the tremor in myself that answered his words like an echo.

“Listen,” I cried, “this place is queer enough without our imagination help. That boat was a boat. The man was a man. They were running down-stream fast as they could. And that otter was an otter. Don’t let’s play the fool.”

He looked steadily at me. He was not annoyed; his calmness made me feel smaller.

“And for heaven’s sake,” I went on, with a bitterness I couldn’t quite hide, “don’t keep pretending you hear things. It gives me the jumps, and there’s nothing to hear but the river and this cursed wind.”

“You fool,” he answered, low, shocked. “You utter fool. That’s how victims talk—as if you didn’t understand as well as I do.” There was scorn in it, and resignation too. “Keep quiet. Hold your mind firm. This pretending makes it worse when you have to meet it.”

My little rebellion collapsed. I had nothing to say, because I knew he was right and I was the one trying to season fear with jokes and call it edible. Up to a certain point he had been ahead of me—more sensitive, more open to whatever this place carried on its breath and in its silences. Some petty part of me resented that. But I missed, then, the full meaning of his talk of victims. I only knew that from that moment I dropped my pretense, and from that moment my fear began to climb steadily toward something I did not yet name.

“But you are right about one thing,” he added more quietly. “It is wiser not to talk about it, or even to think too much. What one thinks finds words. And what one says—happens.”

The afternoon passed while the canoe dried and hardened: we fished, we tested the leak, we gathered wood, we watched the river rise as if it meant to take the whole world and wash it clean of marks. Driftwood came down in heavy rafts; we hooked at it with long willow branches like peasants snagging fallen branches for winter fuel. The island shrank under our eyes. Banks slumped away in gulps and detonations of wet sand.

The weather held brilliantly clear until about four, and then—for the first time in three days—the wind began to weaken. Clouds gathered in the south-west and spread slowly like bruises across the sky.

The easing of the wind should have been relief. The endless roaring and banging had rasped our nerves raw, as continual industrial clamor rasps a city dweller until silence becomes a luxury item sold back in tiny, curated doses. Yet when the wind stopped almost entirely, around five, the hush was oppressive. The river took over the soundscape, and without the wind’s many notes—its rises, falls, and wild chord changes—the water’s voice became a monotonous, heavy chant. It lay to two or three dull tones at most, pedal notes that throbbed with a lugubrious quality foreign to wind-music. In my state it sounded like doom set to a limited scale: not dramatic, not even angry—just inevitable.

With the sunlight withdrawn, the landscape lost what little cheer it had. The willows stood more densely, their grey-green crowding seeming to close. The river looked blacker. And the willows, maddeningly, appeared to keep their own movement—rustling when no air stirred, shaking from the roots upward as if something below were breathing through them. Ordinary things charged with horror are worse than monstrous ones; their familiar shapes give the mind nowhere to set its guard. In the thickening light the bushes took on a grotesque purposiveness, like huddled bodies waiting. Their very ordinariness felt like camouflage.

I had slept a good deal earlier and recovered from the night’s exhaustion, but rest only made me more susceptible. I told myself it was nerves, overstimulated senses, the body misreading sound and shadow after strain. Yet the dread grew until I feared nightfall as a child fears the forest going dark—because language cannot name what moves between trees, and what has no name owns you.

We covered the canoe with a waterproof sheet. The remaining paddle the Swede tied firmly to the base of a willow, as though afraid the air itself might steal it. From five onward I busied myself with the stew and preparations for supper—it was my turn to cook.

Cooking anchored me. It demanded sequence. It gave the hands a reason.

I sliced potatoes into rough chunks, saving the smallest ones whole so they would thicken the broth without disintegrating at once. Onions I cut finer, so they would sweat down and sweeten. We had bits of bacon fat—precious, aromatic—and I let them melt first in the pot, coaxing out that deep, cured smell before adding anything watery. The onions went in to soften; then the potatoes; then a ladle of water and the thick, brownish residue clinging to the bottom from old stews—yesterday’s flavors reborn, as if the pot itself remembered even when we did not. I broke black bread into it—what remained of it—so it would dissolve into the broth and make it heartier. When it began to bubble, it smelled, absurdly, like home: salt, starch, onion sweetness, and the faint, smoky edge of bacon. We followed it later with plums stewed in sugar until they bled into syrup, and strong tea clouded with dried milk. In the absence of wind my fire behaved, and the work calmed me.

My companion watched, cleaning his pipe, offering useless advice with the weary privilege of the off-duty man. All afternoon he had been quiet—re-caulking the canoe, strengthening the tent ropes, fishing for driftwood while I slept. He spoke only of the island’s destruction, claiming it was already a third smaller than when we had landed.

The pot had just settled into a steady simmer when I heard him call from the bank. I hadn’t noticed him wander away. I ran up.

“Come and listen,” he said. His hand was cupped to his ear again. “Now do you hear anything?”

We stood side by side. At first there was only the river—its deep note, the hiss and spit of turbulent surface. The willows were motionless, silent. Then something reached me faintly: a peculiar sound, like the humming of a distant gong. It came across the darkness from the opposite waste of swamp and willows. It repeated at regular intervals. It was not a bell. Not a steamer’s hoot. It was as if an immense metal disc hung somewhere in the sky and was being struck softly over and over, each blow muffled and musical.

My heart quickened.

“I’ve heard it all day,” the Swede said. “While you slept it came all round the island. I hunted it, but I could never get near enough to see. Sometimes it was overhead. Sometimes it seemed under the water. Once or twice I could have sworn it wasn’t outside at all, but within myself—as though sound came through another dimension.”

I listened harder, trying to match it to anything I knew: wind in hollow reeds, the boom of distant thunder, machinery—any familiar source. But it slid away from association. It changed direction, coming close and then sinking into distance. It wasn’t, strictly, ominous in tone; it was too musical for that. Yet it set going in me a distress that made me wish, intensely, that I had never let it enter my ears. There are sounds that do not belong to a landscape the way certain words do not belong to a tongue; they cannot be translated into comfort.

“The wind in sand tunnels,” I said quickly. “Or bushes rubbing after the storm.”

“It comes off the whole swamp,” he replied. “Everywhere at once.” He ignored my attempt. “It comes from the willows somehow—”

“But the wind has dropped,” I objected. “The willows can’t make noise by themselves.”

His answer frightened me because I had dreaded it, and because some part of me recognized it as true.

“It is because the wind has dropped we hear it now,” he said. “It was drowned before. It is the cry, I believe, of the—”

I fled back to the fire at the sound of bubbling that warned me the stew might boil over, and also because I wanted, desperately, to stop his mouth with work. I did not want the naming of things. Another night waited, and I wanted my mind intact for it.

“Come and cut bread for the pot,” I called, stirring vigorously. The stew-pot held sanity; the thought made me laugh, sharp and sudden.

He came slowly, took the provision sack from the tree, and fumbled in its depths. Then he emptied the entire contents onto the ground-sheet—methodical, as though inventory itself could keep the world in order.

“Hurry,” I cried. “It’s boiling.”

The Swede burst into a roar of laughter that startled me. It was not mirth; it was forced relief, a valve letting off pressure.

“There’s nothing here!” he shouted, holding his sides.

“Bread,” I said, and ran to him.

“It’s gone,” he cried. “There is no bread. They’ve taken it!”

Everything lay on the ground-sheet—tin of tea, dried milk, sugar, plums, the little remnants of bacon, oatmeal—but no loaf. The empty place where it should have been was like a pulled tooth you cannot stop exploring with your tongue.

The dead weight of fear dropped on me and shook me. And then I laughed too, because laughter was the only sound that felt like mine. The strain had stained us, pressed us, and this ugly hilarity was the body’s attempt to prove it still ruled itself. Both our laughter stopped as suddenly as it began, as though someone had shut a door.

“How criminally stupid of me,” I cried, clinging to explanation as to a recipe. “I forgot to buy a loaf at Pressburg. That chattering woman put everything out of my head. I must have left it on the counter—”

“The oatmeal too is much less than it was this morning,” he interrupted.

Why did he need to say it? I thought, with a flare of anger, as though pointing to it gave it power.

“There’s enough for tomorrow,” I said, stirring hard. “We can get more at Komorn or Gran. In twenty-four hours we’ll be miles from here.”

“I hope so—to God,” he muttered, gathering the things back into the sack. “Unless we’re claimed first as victims for the sacrifice,” he added with a foolish little laugh that was worse than silence.

He dragged the sack into the tent as though locking it away could preserve it, and I heard him mumbling—words indistinct, as if spoken in another dialect I could not quite catch, the meaning just out of reach. I stood over the pot, forcing the stew to thicken, listening to the river’s dull notes and, behind them, that distant gong-hum that seemed to ring from everywhere at once.

I meant to keep watch till morning, to lie with my eyes propped open and my ears strained, but the day’s strain—salt sweat, river glare, the mind’s incessant rehearsal of danger—thickened into a stupor that came over me like a heavy quilt pulled up to the chin. The fact that my companion, too, at last surrendered, helped the darkness claim me. For a long time he would not settle. He sat up sharply again and again, whispering, then blurting, “Did you hear that—did you hear it now?” as though the night were a kitchen full of pans that had begun to tick and cool in hostile hands. He tossed on his cork mattress, muttering that the tent was shifting, that the river had crept over the point and would have us presently floating like peelings in a wash-bowl. Each time I forced myself out, bare feet meeting cold sand, and each time I returned with the same report—no rise, no wind, the bushes as they had been. At last he lay still. His breath evened; and then came the unmistakable sound of snoring—so wholesome, so human, so domestically absurd in that haunted place that it steadied my nerves better than any prayer. It was the last thing I remember thinking: if he can snore, the world has not altogether slid off its hinges.

Something pressed my mouth and nose; I woke choking, the blanket dragged over my face. But it was not only cloth. There was weight—an insistence—as though another body had rolled across me. I called his name and struggled upright; and with the motion came the knowledge, full as a mouthful of river water, that the tent was surrounded.

Outside, close in, pattered the innumerable soft impacts I had heard before—feet? hands? leaves?—a delicate drumming, not loud, but total, filling the night the way flour dust fills the air when you slap a sack. I called again, louder, and listened for the answering snore that had comforted me. Silence. The flap of the tent was down.

That, in our little rules of survival, was unforgivable. I crawled in the dark to fasten it back securely, fingers fumbling for the hook—and it was then, in that blind groping, that the certainty struck: the Swede was not there. His mattress lay empty, its cork faintly squeaking under my knee. He had gone out into it.

I burst from the tent and ran—ran as a man runs when he hears a child fall into a well—and at once plunged into a torrent of sound. The humming was everywhere, and it had lost what little restraint it ever possessed. It was that same familiar vibration, but raving now, thickening the air as if the night had become a hive turned inside out. It was not merely heard; it was breathed. My lungs labored as though the atmosphere had turned viscous, like steam heavy with boiled honey. Above it and within it lay the soft pattering, too, so close it seemed to strike my own skin.

Dawn had not yet broken, but it was preparing itself. A faint, whitish lift spread upward behind the clouds from a thin strip of clear horizon. No wind stirred; the willows did not so much as shiver. I could just make out bushes, river, pale sandy patches—shapes washed of color. I ran to and fro on the island, calling him by name, shouting any words that came—orders, promises, curses—anything that might cut through the muffling. But the willows smothered sound as if their leaves were felt, and the humming swallowed what remained, so that my voice seemed to fall at my feet. I plunged among the bushes, tripped, went down hard, rose again with sand in my palms, tore my face on twigs, and blundered onward with the panicked clumsiness of a man groping in smoke.

Then, quite suddenly, I broke out upon the point and saw him: a dark figure outlined between water and sky. The Swede stood there with one foot already in the river.

A moment more and he would have taken the plunge.

I flung myself on him, arms locking around his waist, and dragged him back with all my strength, heels digging into the damp sand. He fought me savagely, with a strength that was not his own, his body bucking and wrenching as if some current had got inside his bones. All the while he made a sound—half voice, half that cursed humming—uttering wild phrases in his rage about going inside to Them, taking the way of water and wind, and other outlandish fragments that struck the mind like foreign words misheard through a wall. I remember the tone more than the syllables, because it made me sick with horror—the way a familiar recipe can be spoken in a dialect you do not know, so that every comfort of it is lost.

But I got him back. Inch by inch, breath by breath, I hauled him to the comparative safety of the tent and flung him, panting and cursing, onto his mattress. I held him there, gripping him as one restrains a man in fever, until the fit went out of him.

The strangest part was how abruptly everything changed. His struggle ceased with a suddenness that made my arms slacken in astonishment; and at the same instant the humming and the pattering outside stopped too, as if someone had simply shut a door on a storm. He opened his eyes. The newborn light of dawn fell pallid across his face through the doorway, bleaching him to the look of a frightened child. He stared up at me and said, with a breathless sincerity that had nothing theatrical in it:

“My life, old man—it’s my life I owe you. But it’s all over now anyhow. They’ve found a victim in our place!”

Then his head dropped back. He collapsed into sleep literally under my eyes and began to snore again, deep and healthy, as though he had merely turned over after a bad dream. I did not sleep. For three hours I kept vigil, ears pricked for the return of that thickening sound, eyes fixed on the tent flap, mind running like a millwheel without grain.

When the sun woke him, it was already high in a windless, hot sky. He roused naturally, stretched, and at once began to set about breakfast with the steadiness of a man restoring a household after a night alarm. He gathered what we had with a kind of care—dry bits for the fire, the little arrangements of tin and packet and knife—moving as though the ritual of making heat and drink could stitch us back into ordinary time. The smell of yesterday’s smoke still clung to the canvas; the sand held the night’s damp. He worked with hands that had, a few hours before, been trying to throw his body away into the river.

I followed him anxiously down to bathe. He did not attempt to plunge. He merely dipped his head, gasped, and said, with the casual tone of a man tasting broth and noting salt, “Extra cold this morning.”

“River’s falling at last,” he added. “And I’m glad of it.”

“The humming has stopped too,” I said.

He looked up quietly, his normal expression back in place as if it had never left. Evidently he remembered everything except his own madness at the point. “Everything has stopped,” he said, and hesitated, as though the words he needed were not in the language he had learned to use for daylight. The pause had weight. I knew his mind had returned to that remark at the end—the one that had snapped into the dawn like a bone showing through skin—and I was determined to hear it plainly.

“Because ‘They’ve found another victim’?” I said, forcing a little laugh that tasted dry in my throat.

“Exactly,” he answered at once. “Exactly! I feel as positive of it as though—as though—” He broke off, impatient with his own inability to translate the certainty. “I feel quite safe again, I mean.”

He began to look about him with a new attention, as though reading a landscape the way an old food historian reads a stained recipe card—searching for what has been added, what has been erased, what hand has handled it last. The sunlight lay in hot patches on the sand; the willows were motionless; the air had that hard, clean quiet you sometimes get before a storm, but without any promise of wind. He rose slowly.

“Come,” he said. “I think if we look, we shall find it.”

He started off at a run. I followed. He kept along the banks, poking with a stick into sandy bays and shallow caves and little back-waters where the river had left its gossip. I stayed close on his heels, watching his shoulders, listening to the island’s silence as intently as I had listened to its humming—because silence, too, can be an acoustic trap. There were small domestic sounds only: the scrape of our boots, the faint suck of wet sand, the distant lap where current took the edge and worried it.

“Ah!” he exclaimed at last—once, then again, the syllable carrying a particular chill that flung me back into the night’s dread. I hurried up.

He was pointing with his stick at a large black object lying half in the water and half on the sand, caught fast against twisted willow roots so that the river could not carry it off. A few hours earlier this place must have been under water. It took my eye a moment to accept what it saw.

“See,” he said quietly, “the victim that made our escape possible.”

His stick rested on the body of a man.

He turned it over. The face had been buried in sand. It was the corpse of a peasant, clothing torn and dragged by current, drowned not long before—the skin not yet swollen into anonymity. The body must have been swept down upon our island about the hour of dawn, at the very time my companion’s frenzy had ended. The coincidence sat on my mind like an iron pot left too long on the fire.

“We must give it a decent burial, you know,” the Swede said.

“I suppose so,” I replied, though my voice thinned. Something in the look of that poor drowned man turned me cold, as if the river had passed its chill straight into my blood. The Swede glanced sharply at me, an expression I could not read crossing his face, and began clambering down the bank. I followed more cautiously.

As we went, I noticed how the current had stripped much of the clothing away so that the neck and part of the chest lay bare. The exposed skin looked oddly worked, as though it had been pressed into a mould.

Halfway down, my companion stopped abruptly and held up a hand in warning. But either my foot slipped or my momentum was too great; I bumped him, sending him forward with a quick leap to save himself, and we tumbled together onto the hard sand so that our feet splashed in the water. Before either of us could recover, we collided—heavily, shamefully—with the corpse.

The Swede cried out sharply. I sprang back as though struck.

At the instant our bodies touched the dead man, the humming rose from its surface—loud, multiple, alive—several hummings at once, erupting with a vast commotion as of winged things disturbed from their work. The air above us seemed to fill with invisible movement; the sound swept upward into the sky and away, growing fainter and fainter until it died in the distance. It was exactly as though we had startled living creatures that could not be seen, creatures that had been crouched there, busy, and resented interruption.

He clutched me; I think I clutched him. Then, almost before we could draw a full breath, we saw the river take hold of the body. The current turned it, loosening it from the willow roots. A moment later it rolled completely over, the dead face uppermost, staring blankly at the sky, and drifted to the edge of the main stream. In another moment it would be gone.

The Swede started to save it, shouting something I did not catch about a proper burial—and then he dropped abruptly to his knees on the sand and covered his eyes with his hands.

I was beside him at once. I saw what he had seen.

As the corpse turned full towards us, the face and the exposed chest showed plainly a pattern in the skin: small hollows, beautifully formed, regular in their terrible neatness, exactly like the sand-funnels we had found all over the island. Not wounds, not bites—impressions, as if a hand that did not use fingers had pressed its signature into flesh.

“Their mark!” I heard him mutter, as if speaking a word that belonged to some older speech of fear. “Their awful mark!”

When I looked again, the river had done its work. The body was already beyond reach, swept into mid-stream and carried away, turning over and over on the waves like an otter, diminishing into the bright, indifferent distance.
