When Maso reopened the door and admitted the two visitors, Nello performed a profound reverence toward Romola before gently pushing Tito ahead of him. Together, they advanced toward her father.

"Messer Bardo," Nello announced in a tone of measured respect, uncharacteristic of his usual manner, "I have the honor of presenting to you a Greek scholar. He has been eager to seek your counsel, driven not only by the report I gave him regarding your immense learning and priceless collections, but also by the hope that your patronage might assist him during the transient misfortune of a shipwreck. His name is Tito Melema, at your service."

Romola's astonishment was profound. If the stranger had appeared clad in a panther-skin and carrying a thyrsus, she could scarcely have been more surprised. The cunning barber had omitted any mention of the Greek's age or appearance. Among her father's scholarly guests, she had rarely encountered anyone but middle-aged or gray-haired men. Yet, there stood one masculine face, simultaneously youthful and beautiful, an image etched deeply in her mind: that of her brother, who had once lifted her onto his knee, kissed her, and vanished years ago. It was a fair face with sunny hair, like her own. However, her habitual mental posture toward strangers—a proud self-reliance and a resolve to ask for nothing, even a smile, confirmed by her father's laments against worldly injustice—stood like a snowy embankment holding back the rushing tide of her admiring surprise.

Tito's bright face displayed a richness of color that rivaled none, save perhaps for the deep black of his *sajo* or tunic reaching to his knees. He seemed like a wreath of spring dropped suddenly into Romola's young but wintry life, a season inherited only from memories: of a dead mother, a lost brother, and a blind father's happier past. These were memories of distant light, love, and beauty, buried in the dark mines of books, waiting to be kindled anew by the torch of some known joy. Nevertheless, she returned Tito's initial bow with the same pale, proud expression she usually wore. But as he drew nearer, the snow melted. When he dared to look at her again while Nello spoke, a pink flush spread across her cheeks, vanishing almost instantly as if her imperious will had summoned it back. In contrast, Tito's gaze held a gentle, beseeching admiration, the most appeasing appeal to a proud, shy woman, and perhaps the only atonement a man can offer for being too handsome. The complete fascination of his demeanor stemmed from an absolute lack of demand or assumption. It was the charm of a sleek, dark-eyed animal that delights you not by bounding away in indifference, but by unexpectedly resting its chin on your palm and looking up, desiring to be stroken—as if it loved you.

"Sir, I give you welcome," said Bardo with a touch of condescension. "Misfortune joined with learning, and especially Greek learning, serves as a letter of credit that ought to command the attention of every educated Florentine. As you surely know, since the time nearly a century ago when your countryman, Manuelo Crisolora, spread the light of his teaching throughout Italy's chief cities, no man is deemed worthy of the title 'scholar' unless he possesses more than the transplanted, derivative literature of the Latins. Rather, such inert students are branded as *opici*, or barbarians, using the very phrase of the Romans, who frankly replenished their urns at the fountainhead. As you perceive, and as Nello has undoubtedly warned you, I am entirely blind. This is a calamity to which we Florentines are particularly prone, whether due to the cold winds sweeping down from the Apennine passes in spring, the jarring transition from our indoor gloom to the blinding brilliance of our summer sun—which the *Lippi* attribute to the ancient Romans—or to some occult cause defying our shallow guesses. Nevertheless, please be seated. Nello, my friend, sit as well."

Bardo waited until his keen hearing confirmed that the visitors were settling, noting that Romola took her customary place at his right hand. Then he asked, "From which region of Greece do you hail, Sir? I had presumed your unfortunate homeland had been nearly drained of those sons capable of cherishing any image of her original glory, although indeed the barbarous Sultans have recently shown themselves unwilling to graft the precious vine upon their wild stock, despite having hewn it down and trampled it beneath their feet. From whence do you come?"

"I sailed last from Nauplia," Tito replied. "However, I have resided in both Constantinople and Thessalonica and traveled through various regions little visited by Western Christians since the triumph of Turkish arms. I must inform you, Sir, that I was not born in Greece, but at Bari. I spent the first sixteen years of my life in Southern Italy and Sicily."

As Tito spoke, a subtle emotion, like a breath across water, crossed Bardo's delicate features. He leaned forward, extended his right hand toward Romola, and turned his head as if intending to address her; but then, correcting himself, he turned away and spoke in a subdued voice, "Pardon me; is it not true—you are young?"

"I am twenty-three," Tito answered.

"Ah," Bardo murmured, his tone still tinged with subdued excitement, "and you doubtless had a father who cherished your early education—one who, perhaps, was a scholar himself?"

A slight pause preceded Tito's reply. To Bardo, it arrived clearly; to Romola and Nello, it began with a faint shock that seemed to ripple through the room, causing a momentary tremor of the lips, undoubtedly triggered by the resurgence of a supremely painful memory.

"Yes," Tito responded, "at least a father by adoption. He was a Neapolitan, accomplished in both Latin and Greek scholarship. But," he added after another brief hesitation, "he is lost to me—he perished on a voyage he too rashly undertook to Delos."

Bardo sank back, too refined to pose another question that might probe a sorrow he sensed was fresh. Romola, acutely aware of the specific fibers Tito's voice had touched in her father, realized with startling suddenness that this new acquaintance had breached the barrier separating them from the outside world. Nello, sensing that the apparent check on the conversation offered a graceful chance to relieve the silence, interjected, "In truth, it is as clear as Venetian glass that this fine young gentleman received the finest training. The two Cennini have already set him to work on their Greek texts, and they are not men to begin cutting before testing the edge of their tools. We may be sure they vetted him thoroughly beforehand. If there are two things that cannot be concealed—love and a cough—I posit a third: ignorance, once a man is compelled to act beyond mere nodding. The uneven barber betrays himself inevitably when he takes up the shears; is that not so, Messer Bardo? I speak as a barber, but as Luigi Pulci says:

*'Forgive me if I err: whoever listens
Let him understand my vernacular with his Latin.'*"

"Nay, my dear Nello," Bardo replied with an air of friendly severity, "you are not wholly illiterate and could doubtless achieve more respectable progress in learning if you had refrained somewhat from the *chatter* and gossip of the street corners, to which our Florentines are excessively prone; and even more so if you had not cluttered your memory with those frivolous productions for which Luigi Pulci provides the most egregious examples—a compendium of extravagances and contradictions furthest removed from the models of a pure age, resembling rather the *grylli* or conceits of a period when mystical meaning served as a warrant for monstrous forms. With this difference, however: while the monstrosity is retained, the mystical meaning is absent, creating a contemptible contrast with the great poem of Virgil. Long before Landino assumed the task of expounding the same opinion, I held with Filelfo that Virgil embodied the deepest lessons of philosophy within a graceful and well-knit fable. And I cannot help viewing the proliferation of these babbling, lawless works, despite being countenanced by patronage and to some extent emulated by Lorenzo himself—otherwise a friend of true learning—as a sign that the glorious hopes of this century are destined to be extinguished in gloom. Nay, they appear to be the delusive prologue to an age worse than the Iron Age—an age of tinsel and gossamer, where no thought possesses the substance required to be molded into consistent and enduring form."

"Once more, pardon," Nello said, spreading his palms outward and shrugging his shoulders. "I find myself knowing so many things in good Tuscan before I have time to summon the Latin for them; and Messer Luigi's rhymes invariably slip off the lips of my customers—that is what corrupts me. Indeed, speaking of customers, I have left my shop and my reputation too long in the custody of my slow Sandro, who does not deserve to be called an *uneven barber*, but rather to be pronounced simply a bungler in the vulgar tongue. So, with your permission, Messer Bardo, I shall take my leave—fully understanding that I remain at your service whenever Maso visits me. It seems a thousand years until I dress and perfume the damigella's hair, which deserves to shine in the heavens as a constellation, though indeed it would be a pity for it
