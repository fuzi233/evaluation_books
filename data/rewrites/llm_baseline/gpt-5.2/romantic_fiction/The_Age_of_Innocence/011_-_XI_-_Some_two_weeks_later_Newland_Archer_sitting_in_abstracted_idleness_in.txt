Two weeks later, when the city had settled again into its winter habits—coal fires banked early, sleigh-bells sparing their bright little clatter for the Park, and the rest of the town muffled in the soft pact of snow—Newland Archer sat in his private compartment at Letterblair, Lamson and Low, affecting to read while his thoughts wandered where they were least welcome. The office had its own soundscape: the steady scratch of pens through partitions, the dry cough of paper shuffled into order, a clerk’s steps measured by long training on the carpet runner. It was a quiet engineered for old families, a hush meant to keep trouble from echoing.

A messenger came, discreet as a footman at dessert, to say Mr. Letterblair wished to see him.

Old Mr. Letterblair—who advised New York as a family physician advises a lineage—sat enthroned behind mahogany burnished by generations of anxious hands. He stroked his close-clipped white whiskers, then raked fingers through the rumpled gray above his jutting brows with the air of a diagnostician offended by symptoms that would not fit a polite category.

“My dear sir—” he began; he always called Archer “sir,” as if the title itself were a splint for youthful impetuosity. “I’ve sent for you to go into a little matter; a matter which, for the moment, I prefer not to mention either to Mr. Skipworth or Mr. Redwood.”

Archer knew the names on the letterhead were, in the New York manner, more enduring than the men; the partners commemorated there had long since become portrait and precedent, and Mr. Letterblair, professionally, was his own grandson.

“For family reasons,” Mr. Letterblair went on, letting the words fall as if they should explain everything.

Archer’s eyes lifted.

“The Mingott family,” said Mr. Letterblair, and his mouth softened into the faint bow he reserved for established power. “Mrs. Manson Mingott sent for me yesterday. Her grand-daughter—the Countess Olenska—wishes to sue her husband for divorce. Certain papers have been placed in my hands.”

The blood moved sharply in Archer’s temples. He had seen Ellen Olenska only once since his visit to her—at the Opera, in the Mingott box—and in the interval she had dimmed, unwillingly receding as May Welland resumed her place like a steady lamp in the center of his life. Since Janey’s first airy mention, he had heard no more of divorce and had filed it away as gossip. Divorce, in theory, offended him almost as it did his mother; and he resented, too, the obvious plan—no doubt old Catherine Mingott’s—to drag him into the mess as if he were already pledged body and mind to the clan.

Mr. Letterblair unlocked a drawer and drew out a packet.

“If you will run your eye over these papers—”

Archer did not take them at once. “I beg your pardon, sir; but just because of the prospective relationship, I should prefer your consulting Mr. Skipworth or Mr. Redwood.”

Surprise, and a touch of rebuke, crossed the old man’s face. Openings were not refused in their world; they were accepted, digested, and made useful.

“I respect your scruple, sir; but in this case I believe true delicacy requires you to do as I ask. Indeed, the suggestion is not mine but Mrs. Manson Mingott’s and her son’s. I have seen Lovell Mingott; and also Mr. Welland. They all named you.”

His temper rose as if a band had tightened around his chest. For a fortnight he had let himself drift—letting May’s radiance smooth away the Mingotts’ demands, letting the pleasant domestic current bear him along. But now the clan’s assumption of rights in advance of marriage made him feel the collar.

“Her uncles ought to deal with this,” he said.

“They have. The matter has been gone into by the family. They are opposed to the Countess’s idea; but she is firm, and insists on a legal opinion.”

Archer took the packet at last and did not open it. “Does she want to marry again?”

“It is suggested,” Mr. Letterblair admitted, drumming the desk with a thick forefinger; “but she denies it.”

“Then—”

“Will you oblige me, Mr. Archer, by first looking through these papers? Afterward, when we have talked the case over, I will give you my opinion.”

Archer withdrew with the envelope as if it held something that might leak. He had, without fully meaning to, collaborated with events in laying down the burden of Ellen Olenska. That hour alone with her in the firelight had drawn them into a sudden intimacy; then the Duke of St. Austrey’s intrusion—bringing Mrs. Lemuel Struthers with him, as if even indiscretion must be escorted—had providentially snapped the thread; and Ellen’s quick, glad greeting of them had made Archer feel, sharply, that she was not made of the stuff May was made of, that her warmth went out too freely, and therefore too easily. Two days later he had watched the comedy of her reinstatement in the van der Luydens’ favor and thought, with a taste of bitterness, that a woman who could thank all-powerful elderly gentlemen so deftly for a bunch of flowers did not need his private consolations or his public championship.

It had been an oddly comforting thought, and it had burnished May’s domestic virtues until they shone. He could not imagine May—whatever emergency arose—hawking her troubles about, spending her confidences on strangers. She had never seemed finer than in the week that followed, when he had even yielded to her wish for a long engagement because she had answered his plea for haste with the one thing that disarmed him.

“You know, when it comes to the point, your parents have always let you have your way ever since you were a little girl,” he had argued.

“Yes,” May had said, looking at him with that clear directness that seemed like a native dialect of their class, “and that’s what makes it so hard to refuse the very last thing they’ll ever ask of me as a little girl.”

That note—pure, obedient, perfectly phrased—had moved him more deeply than he cared to admit. In that language, so crystalline it almost cut, the New York air seemed clean, and anything less exact began to feel suffocating.

He opened the packet.

The papers told him little of facts and much of atmosphere—an exchange of letters between Count Olenski’s solicitors and a French legal firm to whom the Countess had applied about money. Their phrases were precise, their courtesies edged like knives; each sentence seemed to translate something raw into terms that would pass inspection, and in doing so to kill it a little. There was also a short letter from the Count to his wife.

Archer read that once; then, as if his fingers had touched a contaminated object, he rose, thrust the papers back into the envelope, and went straight to Mr. Letterblair.

“Here are the letters, sir. If you wish, I’ll see Madame Olenska,” he said, and heard how strained his own voice sounded.

“Thank you—thank you, Mr. Archer. Come and dine with me tonight if you’re free, and we’ll go into the matter afterward: in case you wish to call on our client tomorrow.”

That afternoon Archer walked home through a winter evening of transparent clearness. The young moon hung above the rooflines like a newly polished platter; the snow underfoot gave back a faint squeak at each step, and from a distance a street-organ tried out a tune with a wheezing persistence that made him think of how modern noise forced itself into every crevice of life, turning even sorrow into a public performance. He wanted to fill his lungs with cold radiance and not exchange a word until he and Mr. Letterblair were closeted after dinner.

It was impossible to decide otherwise than he had: he must see Ellen himself rather than let her story be turned over by indifferent hands. Compassion—stronger than his annoyance—washed away his recent indifference. She rose before him as exposed, pitiful, in danger of wounding herself further by plunging at the bars of fate. He remembered what she had told him of Mrs. Welland’s request to be spared whatever was “unpleasant,” and he winced, suspecting that this very averted mind was the filter that kept their air so “pure.”

“Are we only Pharisees after all?” he wondered; and the thought, instead of being abstract, sat in him like smoke in a closed room—irritating, inescapable. He saw, for the first time, how elementary his own principles had been. People thought him daring; there had been that affair with poor silly Mrs. Thorley Rushworth—never secret enough to prevent its investing him with a decorative air of adventure. But Mrs. Rushworth was “that kind of woman”: foolish, vain, clandestine by nature, and more enamored of the spice of danger than of any charm he possessed. When he had understood that, it had nearly broken his heart; now, oddly, it seemed the one redeeming feature. The affair had been precisely the sort most young men went through and emerged from with calm consciences and an undisturbed faith in the deep distinction between women one loved and respected and women one enjoyed—and pitied.

Their mothers and aunts helped them keep that lexicon intact. In their vocabulary, when “such things happened” it was always foolish in a man and somehow criminal in a woman. Any woman who loved imprudently was assumed to be scheming; the man, simple-minded, helpless in her clutches. The remedy was to marry him quickly to a nice girl and trust to her to manage him like a well-run pantry.

But in older European societies, Archer began to guess, love might not submit so easily to their categories. Rich, idle, ornamental worlds must breed stranger tangles; there might even be a case in which a woman naturally sensitive and aloof could, through sheer loneliness and defencelessness, be drawn into a tie no New York phrasebook could excuse.

At home he wrote a line to Ellen, asking at what hour the next day she could receive him, and sent it by a messenger-boy. The boy returned with word that she was going to Skuytercliff in the morning to stay over Sunday with the van der Luydens, but that he would find her alone that evening after dinner.

Her note was on an untidy half-sheet, without date or address; the handwriting was firm and free. The carelessness of form struck him like a foreign intonation in a familiar street. He smiled at the idea of her “week-ending” in the stately solitude of Skuytercliff, then felt at once that there, of all places, she would most keenly feel the chill of minds rigorously turned away from the “unpleasant.” Even a house could have a way of listening, of withholding sound, of making every human movement seem too loud.

He was at Mr. Letterblair’s punctually at seven, grateful for the pretext of leaving soon after dinner. He had formed his own opinion from the papers and did not care to have it shaped by his senior partner’s.

Mr. Letterblair was a widower, and they dined alone, copiously and slowly, in a dark shabby room hung with yellowing prints of “The Death of Chatham” and “The Coronation of Napoleon.” The room smelled of coal, old leather, and the sweet, heavy breath of long-uncorked wine—an odor as conservative as the firm itself. Between fluted Sheraton knife-cases stood a decanter of Haut Brion and another of old Lanning port, a client’s gift—wine whose story Archer knew: Tom Lanning had sold off his cellar before his mysterious, discreditable death in San Francisco, and the family had found that death less publicly humiliating than the sale.

Dinner moved with ceremonious weight, like a ritual meant to keep disorder at bay. There was velvety oyster soup—thickened to that perfect point where it coated the spoon and carried the sea in a softened whisper—then shad with cucumbers, the fish handled with the reverence due to a seasonal tradition, its flesh flaking under the fork in clean white petals. After came a young broiled turkey with corn fritters, the fritters crisp at the edges and steaming with a sweet grain-scent when broken; then a canvas-back with currant jelly, its iron richness sharpened by the tart gloss, and a celery mayonnaise whose cool perfume seemed to rinse the palate back into propriety.

Mr. Letterblair, who lunched on a sandwich and tea, dined as if dinner were his one daily argument with mortality, and he insisted Archer do the same. The only loudness in the room was culinary—the faint crackle as skin yielded, the soft tap of knife against plate, the steady glug of wine into glass. Outside, the city’s noises were kept at a respectful distance, as if even sound needed an invitation.

When the closing rites had been accomplished—the cloth removed, cigars lit, the port sent westward—Mr. Letterblair leaned back, spreading his shoulders to the coal fire behind him.

“The whole family are against a divorce,” he said. “And I think rightly.”

At once Archer felt himself pushed to the other side, not by conviction but by the old man’s tone—well-fed, self-protective, serenely indifferent.

“But why, sir? If there ever was a case—”

“Well—what’s the use? She’s here—he’s there; the Atlantic’s between them. She’ll never get back a dollar more of her money than what he’s voluntarily returned to her: their damned heathen marriage settlements take precious good care of that. As things go over there, Olenski’s acted generously: he might have turned her out without a penny.”

Archer knew it and held his tongue.

“I understand, though,” Mr. Letterblair continued, “that she attaches no importance to the money. Therefore, as the family say, why not let well enough alone?”

An hour earlier Archer might have agreed. Now, hearing the argument in this voice, it tasted like a dish prepared without salt for fear of offending an invalid—blandness elevated to virtue.

“I think that’s for her to decide.”

“H’m—have you considered the consequences if she decides for divorce?”

“You mean the threat in her husband’s letter? What weight would that carry? It’s no more than the vague charge of an angry blackguard.”

“Yes; but it might make some unpleasant talk if he really defends the suit.”

“Unpleasant—!” Archer’s exclamation came out like a sound he could not help making, and he hated the way it broke the room’s careful quiet.

Mr. Letterblair looked at him from under inquiring brows. Archer, feeling the futility of explaining what was pounding in him, bowed slightly, and the old man went on as if restoring a disturbed arrangement.

“Divorce is always unpleasant.”

“You agree with me?” Mr. Letterblair asked after a pause, his manner assuming consent as naturally as a host refills a glass.

“Naturally,” Archer said.

“Well, then, I may count on you—the Mingotts may count on you—to use your influence against the idea?”

Archer hesitated, hearing, as if from a distance, the city’s winter hush, and within it the sharp, small noises by which reputations were made and broken: a whispered word at a dinner-table, a laugh held back too late, the click of a fan closing like a verdict.

“I can’t pledge myself till I’ve seen the Countess Olenska,” he said at last.

“Mr. Archer, I don’t understand you. Do you want to marry into a family with a scandalous divorce-suit hanging over it?”

“I don’t think that has anything to do with the case.”

Mr. Letterblair set down his port and fixed Archer with a cautious, apprehensive gaze. Archer understood that his mandate might be withdrawn, and for some obscure reason he disliked the prospect. Now that the task had been pushed onto him, he would not relinquish it; and he saw he must reassure the unimaginative conscience of the Mingotts.

“You may be sure, sir, that I shan’t commit myself till I’ve reported to you; what I meant was that I’d rather not give an opinion till I’ve heard what Madame Olenska has to say.”

Mr. Letterblair nodded, approving this excess of caution as if it were seasoning properly measured. Archer glanced at his watch, pleaded an engagement, and took leave, the sounds of the quiet house closing behind him like a door carefully latched against whatever the night might bring.
