—Nicholas Breton

Time is an imperfect measure of life. A man may traverse continents, unravel mysteries, or endure heroic trials, only to return and find his neighbors unchanged—still fretting over the same grievances, still pausing at the same shop windows. Some lives are transformed in a year, while others move as slowly as the tide, barely shifting despite the pull of the moon.

Such was the contrast between Gwendolen Harleth’s shattered world and the quiet, unaltered rhythm of Pennicote. The girl who had once glittered with self-assurance now bore the weight of remorse, while the rectory garden bloomed as brightly as ever, undisturbed by her fall. The rector, ever optimistic, busied himself with parish duties and the occasional anonymous article, content in the belief that diligence would be its own reward. His greatest joy, however, lay in his son Rex—no longer the lovesick boy of the previous year, but a young man forged by ambition, his heartache buried beneath a scholar’s discipline.

"Law is the finest profession in the world," Rex declared one evening, his eyes alight with conviction. "I’d rather shape justice than verses. A judge’s bench over a poet’s garret any day."

His father chuckled. "Spoken like a man who’s found his calling. Though I warn you, the path is paved with tedious tomes."

"Better legal tedium than trivial novels," Rex countered. "At least the law has purpose. And the history behind it—the philosophy—that’s where the real fascination lies."

The rector beamed. There was no surer sign of success than a man’s devotion to his work.

Meanwhile, at Jodson’s cottage, Mrs. Davilow and her daughters maintained their quiet existence, the girls’ curiosity only briefly stirred by Anna’s tales of London. Gwendolen’s name arose cautiously, wrapped in questions about yachting and grand houses, but Rex’s presence kept the subject restrained—until the day a messenger arrived, breathless, from the rectory.

The letter trembled in Mrs. Davilow’s hands. Silence gripped the room as she read, then reread, the telegram within. When she finally spoke, her voice was thin with shock.

"Mr. Grandcourt—" She swallowed hard. "He’s drowned."

Rex stood abruptly, his composure shattered. Anna’s gaze flew to him, but he steadied himself, offering practical aid. "Shall I fetch my father?"

Mrs. Davilow nodded, already rallying. "Tell him I’ll be ready. We must leave for Genoa at once. Gwendolen—she must be ill. She must need me."

As Rex and Anna hurried back through the sunlit lanes, the air between them thickened with unspoken thoughts. Rex’s pulse hammered—not with grief for Grandcourt, but with a treacherous, forbidden hope. *Gwendolen free.* The idea burned, then curdled under the weight of reality. Even if she were no longer a wife, she would remain a widow of wealth and status, far beyond his reach. Had she ever loved Grandcourt? Or had she merely endured him?

Shame followed hard on the heels of speculation. This was no time for selfish fantasies. Grandcourt was dead; Gwendolen was grieving. Rex clenched his fists, forcing discipline upon his racing mind. He had rebuilt his life once. He would not falter now.

By the time he reached the rectory, resolve had stiffened his spine. He found his father packing, and without preamble, asked, "How may I help?"

The rector glanced up, pleased by his son’s steadiness. "Look after things here. I’ll return as soon as I can—though it may be some time, depending on Gwendolen’s needs."

Rex nodded, strapping a rug with deliberate focus. "Do you think she’ll come back to England?"

"Perhaps. The will should provide for her handsomely." The rector’s tone was firm. "Grandcourt was a devoted husband."

Rex said nothing. He had never met the man, knew nothing of Gwendolen’s true feelings. Had she loved him? Or had she, like so many women, traded her heart for security?

The questions lingered, but he buried them. Some doors were better left unopened.
