The Duke of Normandy's decree echoed in Margaret's mind, a grim reminder of the stakes: *"Let 'em all eat... Let 'em have ten tire of teeth a-piece, I care not."* Yet, as she stepped through the threshold of her home, the weight of that indifference felt less like royal resolve and more like a crushing inevitability. Her mind was a chaotic storm, replaying the scenes from Higgins's cottage with such visceral intensity that the domestic duties awaiting her seemed to dissolve into mist. Her mother, Mrs. Hale, sat confined to her chair, her usual reliance on Margaret's cheerful reports now tinged with a desperate hunger for news from the outside world.

"And can your factory friend come on Thursday to see you dressed?" Mrs. Hale asked, her voice tight with anticipation.

"She was so ill I never thought of asking her," Margaret replied, the word *ill* tasting like bile.

"Dear! Everybody is ill now, I think," Mrs. Hale muttered, a flicker of envy crossing her features—the possessive jealousy of the sick fearing the sickness of others. "But it must be very sad to be ill in one of those little back streets." Her tone softened, the old Helstone compassion surfacing beneath the irritation. "It's bad enough here. What could you do for her, Margaret? Mr. Thornton has sent me some of his old port wine since you went out. Would a bottle of that do her good, think you?"

"No, mamma!" Margaret protested, her voice rising in agitation. "I don't believe they are very poor—at least, they don't speak as if they were; and, at any rate, Bessy's illness is consumption—she won't want wine. Perhaps, I might take her a little preserve, made of our dear Helstone fruit. No! there's another family to whom I should like to give—" She broke off, overwhelmed by a surge of emotion that shattered the composure she had meticulously constructed before entering the house. "Oh mamma, mamma! How am I to dress up in my finery, and go off and away to smart parties, after the sorrow I've seen to-day?"

The revelation hit Mrs. Hale with the force of a physical blow. Restlessness seized her, transforming into a frantic need to act. She barked orders at Margaret to pack a basket in the drawing-room, demanding it be dispatched immediately to the suffering families. She was nearly furious when Margaret suggested it didn't matter if it waited until morning, citing Higgins's provisions and the money already left with Bessy. To Mrs. Hale, such pragmatism was cold-blooded cruelty. She scolded her daughter for being unfeeling, refusing to draw breath until the basket was gone, carried out into the gray afternoon.

Only then, exhaling sharply, did she allow herself a moment of reflection. "After all, we may have been doing wrong," she whispered, the guilt settling heavy in her chest. "It was only the last time Mr. Thornton was here that he said, those were no true friends who helped to prolong the struggle by assisting the turn-outs. And this Boucher-man was a turn-out, was he not?"

When Mr. Hale descended the stairs, fresh from a lesson with Mr. Thornton that had devolved into heated debate, his wife posed the question to him. Margaret stood aside, her mind too reeling with recent horrors to engage in the abstract moral calculus of political strategy.

Mr. Hale listened, striving for the detached impartiality of a judge. He recalled the clarity of Mr. Thornton's arguments from moments prior, yet found himself forced into an uneasy compromise. In this specific instance, he conceded that his wife and daughter had acted rightly; he could not envision any other path. Yet, as a general principle, he acknowledged the validity of Mr. Thornton's warning: a prolonged strike inevitably drove masters to import distant hands or invent machines to render labor obsolete. Therefore, the kindest act was often to withhold aid that merely propped up folly. However, regarding Boucher specifically, he resolved to visit him first thing in the morning to ascertain what could truly be done.

True to his word, Mr. Hale visited the following dawn. Boucher was absent, but his wife remained. He secured an order for the Infirmary and, observing the abundance Mrs. Hale had provided—which the children, acting as temporary masters in their father's absence, consumed with lavish disregard—he returned with a report far more consoling than Margaret had dared to hope. Indeed, her previous fears had primed his imagination to expect catastrophe; the reaction of his relief colored his description, making the situation appear significantly better than it objectively was.

"I will go again, and see the man himself," Mr. Hale declared, his brow furrowed in contemplation. "I hardly know as yet how to compare one of these houses with our Helstone cottages. I see furniture here which our labourers would never have thought of buying, and food commonly used which they would consider luxuries; yet for these very families there seems no other resource now that their weekly wages are stopped, but the pawn-shop. One had need to learn a different standard, up here in Milton."

By evening, Bessy showed signs of improvement, though her weakness was profound. She seemed to have entirely forgotten her earlier feverish desire to witness Margaret's transformation, or perhaps that impulse had been a hallucination born of delirium. Margaret, meanwhile, found herself trapped in a surreal ritual of preparation. Dressed for a destination she dreaded, her heart burdened by the anxieties of the day, she could not help but contrast this strange toilette with the joyous, girlish experiments she and Edith had shared barely a year ago. Her only motivation now was the knowledge that her mother would derive pleasure from seeing her adorned.

Dixon flung open the drawing-room door, breaking her reverie with an appeal for approval. "Miss Hale looks well, ma'am,—doesn't she? Mrs. Shaw's coral couldn't have come in better. It just gives the right touch of colour, ma'am. Otherwise, Miss Margaret, you would have been too pale."

Margaret's hair, too thick for plaits, was coiled tightly into massive spirals that crowned her head, secured by two large coral pins resembling small arrows. Her white silk sleeves were bound with matching cords, and heavy coral beads rested against the curve of her milk-white throat.

"Oh, Margaret! how I should like to be going with you to one of the old Barrington assemblies,—taking you as Lady Beresford used to take me," her mother exclaimed, a burst of vanity escaping her.

Margaret kissed her cheek, suppressing a smile that felt foreign on her lips. "I would rather stay at home with you,—much rather, mamma."

"Nonsense, darling! Be sure you notice the dinner well. I shall like to hear how they manage these things in Milton. Particularly the second course, dear. Look what they have instead of game."

Had Mrs. Hale witnessed the sumptuous array of the dining table, she would have been astounded. To Margaret, accustomed to the refined restraint of London tastes, the profusion of delicacies felt oppressive; half the quantity would have sufficed for a tighter, more elegant presentation. Yet, this excess was dictated by Mrs. Thornton's rigid code of hospitality: every guest must have access to every dainty, regardless of inclination. For Mrs. Thornton, frugal in private, setting a feast was a point of immense pride. Her son shared this sentiment. Though he denied himself the trivial expense of a sixpence and harbored regrets over sending the invitations, he nonetheless welcomed the opportunity to display the old magnificence of preparation.

Margaret and her father arrived first. Mr. Hale was punctual to the minute. The drawing-room was empty save for Mrs. Thornton and Fanny. Every cover had been removed, revealing a space ablaze with yellow silk damask and a carpet of brilliant flowers. Ornamentation crowded every corner until the eye grew weary, creating a stark, jarring contrast to the bald ugliness visible through the great folding doors leading to the mill-yard, where gates stood open for carriages. The mill loomed high on the left, casting a premature shadow over the summer evening.

"My son was engaged up to the last moment on business. He will be here directly, Mr. Hale. May I beg you to take a seat?"

Mr. Hale stood by a window as she spoke. Turning away, he queried, "Don't you find such close neighbourhood to the mill rather unpleasant at times?"

Mrs. Thornton drew herself up, her posture rigid. "Never. I am not become so fine as to desire to forget the source of my son's wealth and power. Besides, there is not such another factory in Milton. One room alone is two hundred and twenty square yards."

"I meant that the smoke and the noise—the constant going out and coming in of the workpeople, might be annoying!"

"I agree with you, Mr. Hale!" Fanny interjected quickly. "There is a continual smell of steam and oily machinery—and the noise is perfectly deafening."

"I have heard noise that was called music far more deafening," Mr. Hale countered calmly. "The engine-room is at the street-end of the factory; we hardly hear it, except in summer weather, when all the windows are open; and as for the continual murmur of the workpeople, it disturbs me no more than the humming of a hive of bees. If I think of it at all, I connect it with my son, and feel how all belongs to him, and that his is the head that directs it.
