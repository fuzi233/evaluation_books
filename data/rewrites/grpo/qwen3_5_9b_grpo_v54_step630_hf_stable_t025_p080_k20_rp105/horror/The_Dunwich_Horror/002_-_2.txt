The air in Dunwich tasted of wet stone and ancient rot, a flavor that seemed to cling to the very soil of the township. Four miles from the village, nestled against a jagged hillside and isolated by a mile and a half of silence from any other soul, stood a farmhouse that was large only in its decay. It was here, at the break of dawn on Sunday, February 2nd, 1913, that Wilbur Whateley entered the world. The date itself felt wrong, a glitch in the calendar; it was Candlemas, though the villagers observed it under names that would make a priest shudder. The night before, the hills had groaned with unnatural noises, and the dogs of the countryside had barked in a persistent, hysterical chorus that refused to cease.

Born into this cacophony was the child of Lavinia Whateley. She was a creature of the margins, a thirty-five-year-old albino woman whose skin was the color of milk and whose eyes were sickly pink pools of nothingness. She lived with her father, an aged man whose mind had long since unraveled into a tapestry of frightful wizardry tales whispered in his youth. Lavinia had no husband, yet she made no move to deny the child she bore. Instead, she seemed strangely, almost feverishly proud of the infant who lay in her arms—a stark, dark contrast to her own pallid form. While the townsfolk speculated wildly about the boy's lineage, Lavinia muttered prophecies of terrible power and a future that would swallow the world.

She was a lone spirit, drawn to wander the hills amidst thunderstorms, seeking answers in the great, odorous books her father had inherited over two centuries. These tomes, bound in leather that had long since turned to dust and infested with wormholes, held the disjointed scraps of ancient lore Old Whateley had taught her. The farmhouse itself was a monument to fear, haunted by the reputation of black magic and the violent, unexplained death of Mrs. Whateley when Lavinia was merely twelve. Isolated by these strange influences, Lavinia indulged in wild, grandiose daydreams, her home a squalid ruin where standards of order had died long ago.

On the night of Wilbur's birth, a hideous scream tore through the air, echoing above the hill noises and the frantic barking of dogs. No doctor, no midwife attended the delivery. The neighbors remained ignorant of the child's existence until a week later, when Old Whateley drove his sleigh through the snow into Dunwich Village. He stopped at Osborn's general store, where a group of loungers gathered, and began to discourse incoherently. There was a palpable shift in the old man—a new layer of furtiveness clouding his already fractured brain, transforming him from a mere object of local fear into a subject of genuine dread. Yet, amidst his ramblings, a trace of pride emerged, mirroring his daughter's.

"I dun't keer what folks think," Old Whateley slurred, his voice carrying over the cold air. "Ef Lavinny's boy looked like his pa, he wouldn't look like nothin' ye expeck. Ye needn't think the only folks is the folks hereabouts. Lavinny's read some, an' has seed some things the most o' ye only tell abaout. I calc'late her man is as good a husban' as ye kin find this side of Aylesbury; an' ef ye knowed as much abaout the hills as I dew, ye wouldn't ast no better church weddin' nor her'n. Let me tell ye suthin'—some day yew folks'll hear a child o' Lavinny's a-callin' its father's name on the top o' Sentinel Hill!"

For the first month of his life, Wilbur was seen only by Old Zechariah Whateley of the undecayed line and Mamie Bishop, the common-law wife of Earl Sawyer. Mamie's visit was driven by frank curiosity, and her subsequent stories did justice to her observations. Zechariah, however, arrived with a purpose: to lead a pair of Alderney cows that Old Whateley had purchased from his son, Curtis. This marked the beginning of a strange era of cattle-buying for the Whateley family, a cycle that would not end until 1928, when the Dunwich horror finally came and went. Yet, the ramshackle barn never seemed overcrowded. At times, the curious would sneak up the steep hillside to count the herd grazing precariously above the farmhouse, but they could never find more than ten or twelve specimens. They were anemic, bloodless creatures, victims of a blight or distemper that seemed to spring from the unwholesome pasturage and the diseased fungi rotting the barn's timbers. Odd wounds, resembling surgical incisions, afflicted the visible cattle, and once or twice, callers swore they saw similar sores around the throats of the gray, unshaven old man and his slatternly, crinkly-haired daughter.

By the spring following his birth, Lavinia resumed her customary rambles in the hills, carrying the swarthy child in her misproportioned arms. Public interest waned once the town had seen the baby, and few bothered to comment on the swift, terrifying development he exhibited daily. Wilbur's growth was phenomenal; within three months, he possessed a size and muscular power rarely found in infants under a full year. His movements and vocalizations displayed a restraint and deliberateness highly peculiar for a baby. It was not surprising, then, that at seven months, he began to walk unassisted, stumbling slightly before a single month smoothed out his faltering steps.

It was somewhat after this time, on Hallowe'en, that a great blaze erupted at midnight atop Sentinel Hill, illuminating the old table-like stone standing amidst its tumulus of ancient bones. Talk spread quickly when Silas Bishop, of the undecayed Bishops, mentioned seeing the boy running sturdily up that hill ahead of his mother about an hour before the fire was spotted. Silas was rounding up a stray heifer but nearly forgot his mission when he fleetingly spied the two figures in the dim light of his lantern. They darted almost noiselessly through the underbrush, and the astonished watcher seemed to think they were entirely unclothed. Afterward, he could not be sure about the boy, who may have worn some kind of fringed belt and dark blue trunks. Yet, Wilbur was never subsequently seen alive and conscious without complete, tightly buttoned attire. The disarrangement or threatened disarrangement of his clothing always filled him with a profound anger and alarm, a stark contrast to his squalid mother and grandfather that would later suggest a far more valid reason for his obsession with dress.

The following January, gossip turned mildly interested in the fact that "Lavinny's black brat" had commenced to speak at the age of only eleven months. His speech was remarkable, differing from the ordinary accents of the region and displaying a freedom from infantile lisping that many children of three or four might envy. The boy was not talkative, yet when he spoke, he seemed to reflect an elusive element wholly unpossessed by Dunwich and its denizens. The strangeness did not reside in the words themselves, nor in the simple idioms he used, but seemed vaguely linked to his intonation or the internal organs that produced the sounds. His facial aspect was equally striking for its maturity; though he shared his mother's and grandfather's chinlessness, his firm, precociously shaped nose united with the expression on his large, dark, almost Latin eyes to give him an air of quasi-adulthood and well-nigh preternatural intelligence.

However, he was exceedingly ugly despite this appearance of brilliance. There was something almost goatish or animalistic about his thick lips, his large-pored, yellowish skin, his coarse crinkly hair, and his oddly elongated ears. He was soon disliked even more decidedly than his mother and grandsire, and all conjectures about him were spiced with references to the bygone magic of Old Whateley and how the hills once shook when he shrieked the dreadful name of Yog-Sothoth in the midst of a circle of stones with a great book open in his arms. Dogs abhorred the boy, and he was always obliged to take various defensive measures against their barking menace.
