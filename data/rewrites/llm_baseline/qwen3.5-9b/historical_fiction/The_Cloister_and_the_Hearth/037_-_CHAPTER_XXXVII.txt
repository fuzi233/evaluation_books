The condemned criminals were ordered to stand bound in the marketplace for two hours, a precaution intended to allow any witnesses to identify them or testify to other crimes they might have committed. They endured the full duration, yet no new accusations arose against them. This lack of evidence was hardly surprising; they were creatures of the night, vampires who preyed upon weary travelers in the darkness, mostly strangers.

However, just as the officers prepared to release them, a terrible scream erupted from the crowd. A woman pointed a trembling finger at one of the prisoners, her eyes nearly popping from their sockets. Before she could utter a word, she collapsed, fainting dead away.

Men and women surged around her, driven by a mix of concern and morbid curiosity. As consciousness slowly returned to the victim, speculation filled the air.

"'Twas at him she pointed!" someone cried.
"Nay, 'twas at this one!" countered another.
"No, no," insisted a third, "she meant that hound with the hair hanging loose around his neck."

These guesses were abruptly silenced. The moment the woman regained her senses, she lunged at the man with the dangling hair like a lioness. "My child! Man! Man! Give me back my child!" she screamed, seizing the glossy golden locks the officers had tied around his neck. She tore the hair from him and buried her face in it, kissing it desperately. Only then did her confused mind clear; realizing through this tragic token that her lost daughter was dead, she sank to the ground, shrieking and sobbing uncontrollably.

Instantly, the crowd turned savagely toward the accused man. With a unified roar, they rushed upon him. In those days, every citizen carried a weapon at their girdle, and justice was swift. His life ended quickly. Yet, Denys drew his sword immediately, shouting, *"A moi, camarades!"* (To me, comrades!), holding the mob at bay. "Who touches him dies!" he commanded. Several archers backed him up, struggling with difficulty to protect the man from the frenzied throng while Denys pleaded with the rioters.

"What sort of vengeance is this?" Denys shouted, trying to reason with them. "Are you so mad as to rob the wheel and give the vermin an easy death?"

The archers' steel kept the mob passive; Denys's words had little effect, and the crowd continued to growl with flashing eyes. Seeing the danger, the municipal officers gathered around, forming a guard with the archers, and prudently escorted the accused back to jail.

The mob hooted at both the prisoners and the guards indiscriminately. Once Denys confirmed the others were safely locked away, he headed for "The White Hart" inn, expecting to find Gerard there.

On his way, he spotted two girls working at a first-floor window. He saluted them, and they smiled warmly. Conversation ensued; their manners were easy, their complexions radiant. Invited them to dine at the inn, they initially refused, but eventually relented. Surrounded by this charming company, Denys momentarily forgot his worry for Gerard, who was currently being carted off to prison.

Meanwhile, Gerard, having partially recovered his wits, suddenly halted and demanded to know by what authority he was being arrested.
"By the vice-baillie's," the constable replied.
"The vice-baillie? Alas! What have I, a stranger, done to offend such a magistrate? This charge of sorcery must be a pretext. I am no sorcerer, but a poor, truthful youth far from home."

Such vagueness disgusted the officer. "Show him the *capias*, Jacques," the constable ordered.

Jacques held out the writ, keeping it about a yard and a half from Gerard's face. Simultaneously, the large constable pinned Gerard down. Both officers were on edge, fearing the prisoner might snatch the document, to which they attributed superstitious power.

But the poor prisoner had no such thoughts. He merely craned his neck to read it. To his infinite astonishment, he discovered the vice-bailiff who had signed the writ was the very friendly alderman he had spoken to earlier. Taking courage, Gerard assured his captors that there must be an error and requested to be taken before the alderman.

"What say you to that, Jacques?"
"Impossible. We have no orders to take him before his worship. Read the writ!"
"Nay, but good kind fellows, what harm can it be? I will give you each an ecu."
"Humph!" Jacques hesitated. "I say we have no orders *not* to take him to his worship. Read the writ!"
"Then let us take him to prison via his worship's residence."

Agreed. They accepted the money and warned Gerard that they were doing him a favor. He sensed they desired gratitude as much as silver. He attempted to appease their greed, but the words stuck in his throat; feigning was not his strength.

Entering the alderman's presence, Gerard's heart hammered in his chest. Begging with a faltering voice, he asked what offense he had committed since leaving that very room with Manon and Denys.
"Nought that I know of," the alderman replied calmly.
When the writ was produced, the alderman admitted signing it at dawn. "I am growing old, and my memory fails me. I entirely forgot your specific offense amidst discussions regarding the girl... but I recall now. All is well. You are the one I committed for sorcery. Wait! Before you go to gaol, you shall hear what your accuser has to say. Run and fetch him."

The accuser proved difficult to locate immediately. Growing impatient, the alderman explained the core charge: that Gerard had set a dead body ablaze with diabolical fire that burned without consuming. "And if this is true, young man, I am sorry for you, for you will assuredly burn with the fire of good pine logs in the marketplace of Neufchâteau."

"Oh, sir, for pity's sake, let me speak with his Reverence, the Cure!" Gerard pleaded.
The alderman advised against it. "The Church is harder upon sorcerers than the corporation."
"But, sir, I am innocent!" Gerard protested, sounding part snarl, part whimper.
"Oh, if you believe yourself innocent—officer, escort him to the Cure, but ensure he does not escape you. 'Innocent,' quotha?"

They found the Cure in his doublet, repairing a wheelbarrow. Gerard recounted everything, appealing pitifully to him. "For merely using a little phosphorus in self-defense against cutthroats, they intend to hang me."

It proved fortunate for the magician that he had already confessed his tale fully to the Cure. Thus, the shrewd cleric held the situation by the right end of the stick, whereas the corporation gripped it by the handle. The Cure looked exceedingly grave and declared, "I must question you privately on this untoward affair." He led Gerard into a private room, ordering the officer to stand guard outside and remain ready to enter if summoned. The massive constable stood trembling outside the door, expecting the room to vanish and leave a stench of brimstone.

Instantly alone, the Cure's expression relaxed, and he became himself again.
"Show me the trick," he said, his curiosity piqued.
"I cannot, sir, unless the room is darkened."
The Cure swiftly blocked out the light with a wooden shutter. "Now, then."
"But on what shall I perform it?" Gerard asked. "There is no dead face here; that is what made it seem so dreadful."
The Cure groped around the room. "Good; here is an image. It is my patron saint."
"Heaven forbid! That would be profanation!"
"Pshaw! Will it rub off?"
"Ay, but it goes against me to take such liberties with a saint," the sorcerer objected.
"Fiddlesticks!" exclaimed the divine.
"To be sure, performing it on his holiness will demonstrate to your reverence that it is no Satanic art."
"Perhaps that is why I proposed it," the Cure said subtly.

Encouraged, Gerard ignited the eyes and nostrils of the image, causing the Cure to jump. He then lit patches of the hair until the entire face glowed like a glowworm.
"By'r Lady!" shouted the Cure. "'Tis strange, and small my wonder that they took you for a magician, seeing a dead face thus fired. Now, come along with me!"

Donning his grey gown and great hat, the Cure led Gerard within minutes to the alderman. Standing beside the alderman, poisoning his mind, was the accuser—a singular figure in red hose and red shoes, wearing a black gown with blue bands and a cocked hat.

After saluting the alderman, the Cure turned to the accuser with good humor. "So, Mangis, at your work again, babbling away honest men's lives! Come, your worship, this is the old tale! Two of a trade can never agree. Here is Mangis, who professes sorcery and would sell himself to Satan tonight, but Satan is not so weak as to buy what he can have gratis. This Mangis, who wishes to be a sorcerer, is actually only a quack doctor, accusing a true lad of magic who merely used a secret of chemistry well known to me and all churchmen."
