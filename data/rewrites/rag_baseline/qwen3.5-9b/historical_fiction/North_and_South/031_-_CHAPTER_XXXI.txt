The October morning descended not with the gentle, silvery mist of the countryside, which usually clears before the sun to reveal a riot of autumn color, but with the heavy, suffocating fog of Milton. It was a dawn reminiscent of Milton's own verses, where the sun struggled to pierce the gloom, illuminating only long, dusky streets when it finally broke through. Inside the Hale household, the atmosphere was equally weighted with a grim, unyielding gravity. Margaret moved through the rooms with a languid, mechanical precision, her hands busy assisting Dixon in preparing the house for the inevitable departure of her father and brother. Tears blurred her vision constantly, yet she found no time to surrender to them; the weight of survival rested squarely on her shoulders. While her father and brother wallowed in their grief, paralyzed by loss, Margaret was forced to function as the architect of their new reality. Every detail of the funeral—from the arrangements to the logistics—devolved upon her, a testament to the sudden vacuum of leadership left behind.

As the fire roared brightly and the tea-kettle sang its familiar song, signaling readiness for breakfast, Margaret paused. She swept her gaze over the room one final time, desperate to impose a semblance of cheerfulness upon the scene. Yet, the stark contrast between the warm, inviting domesticity and the cold abyss of her thoughts shattered the facade instantly. Kneeling beside the sofa, she buried her face in the cushions, muffling a sob that threatened to tear her apart, until a firm touch on her shoulder pulled her back to the present.

"Dixon!" she whispered, startled.

"Come, Miss Hale—come, my dear! You must not give way, or where shall we all be?" Dixon's voice, usually crisp and commanding, held an unusual tremor of compassion. "There isn't another soul in this house fit to direct anything. Who manages the funeral? Who decides who attends? Where does it happen? Everything hinges on decisions, and Master Frederick is a wreck, crying as if his heart would break, while Master Hale... well, he's lost. He wanders about like a ghost. It's terrible, I know, but death claims us all eventually. You're fortunate, truly, to have faced this only once."

Perhaps Dixon was right. Yet, this specific loss felt singular, a wound that defied comparison with any other tragedy in the world. Margaret found no solace in the servant's logic, but the raw, unexpected tenderness in Dixon's manner struck a chord deep within her. Driven by a need to acknowledge this kindness, she forced herself upright, offering a fragile smile to the anxious woman before rushing to announce that breakfast was ready.

Mr. Hale entered as if drifting through a dream, his movements the unconscious drift of a sleepwalker, his eyes and mind focused on a horizon beyond the room. Frederick followed, masking his devastation with a forced buoyancy. He seized Margaret's hand, searched her eyes, and then collapsed into tears. Throughout the meal, Margaret spoke only of trivialities, a frantic attempt to keep the memory of their shared last supper—and the terrified listening for signals from the sickroom—further in the distance.

Once the meal concluded, Margaret approached her father regarding the funeral plans. He nodded vacantly, agreeing to every proposal she made, even those that logically contradicted one another. He offered no genuine direction. As Margaret prepared to retreat to consult with Dixon, Mr. Hale called her back, his voice hollow.

"Ask Mr. Bell," he rasped.

"Mr. Bell?" Margaret blinked, surprised. "Of Oxford?"

"Yes," he confirmed, sinking back into his lethargy. "He was my groom's-man."

The connection clicked instantly in Margaret's mind. "I will write today," she decided. For the rest of the morning, she labored in a feverish whirlwind of melancholy business, longing for rest but finding none.

By evening, Dixon returned with news that sent a fresh chill through the room. "I've managed it, miss. I was genuinely frightened for Master Hale; grief brought him to the brink of a stroke. He spent the entire day with poor Mrs. Hale, talking to her as if she were breathing. When I peeked in, he'd freeze, staring into space, utterly bewildered. I realized he needed a shock to snap him back to reality. So, I confronted him. I told him it wasn't safe for Master Frederick to remain here."

Dixon recounted her encounter with a stranger she had spotted earlier—a man named Young Leonards, the son of the old draper from Southampton. "He's a scoundrel," Dixon spat, her indignation rising. "Plagued his father to death and fled to sea. I detested him. He was aboard the *Orion* with Frederick, though I'm unsure if he participated in the mutiny."

"He knew you?" Margaret asked, her interest piqued.

"That's the crux of the disaster," Dixon replied. "I didn't expect to meet him in a foreign land, so I blurted out his name, calling him cousin. He was polite enough, asking why I doubted our kinship, but I refused to be trifled with. I mocked him about his father turning him out, pretending we were old friends. Then, to spite me, he began probing about Master Frederick. He spoke of Frederick's 'scrapes' as if they could stain George Leonards clean, mentioning the hangman's noose and the hundred-pound bounty. He was trying to provoke me, you see, because I'd once helped his father rate George favorably in Southampton."

"But you didn't reveal anything about us? About Frederick?"

"Not a word," Dixon assured her. "He never asked where I stayed, nor did I volunteer the information. However, he turned back from the bus stop, grinning wickedly. 'If you can help me trap Lieutenant Hale, Miss Dixon,' he sneered, 'we'll share the reward. Wouldn't you like to be my partner?' And then he boarded the bus, leaving me with the taste of his leering, malicious satisfaction."

Margaret felt a knot of dread tighten in her stomach. "Have you told Frederick?"

"No," Dixon admitted. "I was uneasy knowing the villain was in town, but with so much else demanding attention, I didn't dwell on it. Seeing Master Hale so rigid, his eyes glazed and sorrowful, I feared the knowledge of Frederick's flight might finally rouse him. Poor fellow, he needs to move before Mr. Bell arrives."

"I'm not afraid of Mr. Bell," Margaret said firmly, though her voice betrayed her anxiety. "But I am terrified of Leonards. I must tell Frederick. Describe him."

"A frightful sight," Dixon reported. "Red whiskers I'd be ashamed to wear. Dressed in rough fustian, posing as a laborer despite claiming a 'confidential situation.' He's a fraud."

The conclusion was unavoidable. Frederick must leave. He had already carved out a vital role within the family, promising to be the pillar his father and sister needed. His devotion to the living mother and his mourning for the dead bound him to them with a fierce, protective love. As Margaret sat by the drawing-room fire, watching her father pace restlessly under the weight of this renewed terror, Frederick entered. The vibrant light in his eyes had dimmed, but the paroxysm of his grief had subsided into a heavy, brooding silence.

He approached Margaret and pressed a kiss to her forehead. "How pale you look, Margaret," he murmured softly. "You've been carrying everyone's burden while no one thinks of you. Lie down on the sofa; there is nothing left for you to do."

"That is the most cruel thing," Margaret whispered sadly. Yet, she obeyed, lying back as her brother draped a shawl over her feet and sat on the floor beside her. In the subdued quiet, they began to speak.

Margaret relayed Dixon's harrowing account of the encounter with Leonards. Frederick's reaction was immediate and visceral; his lips curled in a long, sharp whistle of disgust. "I want to confront that young rat," he growled. "No sailor was ever worse, and no man less honorable. Do you recall the circumstances, Margaret?"

"Mamma told me the story," she answered.

"When the decent sailors were incensed at our captain, this fellow sought favor—disgusting! And to think he's here! If he knew I was within twenty miles, he'd hunt me down to settle every old score. I'd sooner let anyone claim the hundred pounds they think I'm worth than that filth. What a pity Dixon couldn't be convinced to retire, with a pension secured for her old age!"

"Oh, Frederick, hush! Stop speaking!"

Mr. Hale stepped forward, trembling with agitation. He had overheard their conversation. Taking Frederick's hand in both of his, he urged, "My boy, you must go. It is dangerous, but necessary. You have done all you could; you have been her comfort."

"Papa, must he go?" Margaret pleaded, fighting against the certainty of the necessity.

"I confess, I have a mind to face it alone, to stand my trial. If only I could gather my evidence! I cannot bear the thought of falling into the hands of such a blackguard as Leonards. In other times, I might have savored this stolen visit—it possesses all the allure the French attribute to forbidden pleasures."

"One of my earliest memories," Margaret interjected gently, "is of your disgrace for stealing apples. We had trees laden with fruit, yet someone told you stolen fruit tastes sweetest. You took it literally and went robbing.
