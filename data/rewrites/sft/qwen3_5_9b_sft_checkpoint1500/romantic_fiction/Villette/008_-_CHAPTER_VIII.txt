Delivered into the care of the *maîtresse*, I was ushered through a long, narrow corridor into a kitchen that was strangely alien, pristine yet bewildering. It lacked any conventional hearth or oven, save for a massive black furnace in one corner that I later learned served as a miraculous substitute. Before I could succumb to the whispers of pride, a sense of relief washed over me: instead of remaining in the kitchen, as I had half-feared, I was guided forward into a small inner chamber designated a "cabinet." A cook, dressed in a jacket, a short petticoat, and wooden clogs (*sabots*), brought my supper. It consisted of meat of unknown origin, served in an oddly acidic yet agreeable sauce; chopped potatoes seasoned with vinegar and sugar; a tartine of buttered bread; and a baked pear. Hungry and grateful, I ate.

Following the evening prayer, Madame Beck herself descended to inspect me again, requesting that I accompany her upstairs. We traversed a series of peculiar, diminutive dormitories—rumored to have once been nuns' cells, given the building's antiquity—and passed through an oratory. This was a long, low, gloomy room where a pale crucifix hung against the wall, flanked by two tapers keeping a dim vigil. She conducted me to an apartment where three children slept soundly in tiny beds. The air, stifled by a heated stove, was thick with an overpowering scent, a perfume surprisingly discordant for the circumstances: a blend of smoke and some volatile spirit, akin to the smell of whisky.

Beside a table, where a candle sputtered its last flicker, sat a coarse woman, garbed heterogeneously in a broad-striped, ostentatious silk dress and a heavy apron, fast asleep. To complete the scene, a bottle and an empty glass rested at her elbow. Madame Beck surveyed this remarkable tableau with profound calm. Neither a smile nor a frown distorted her grave countenance; no trace of anger, disgust, or surprise marred her equanimity. She did not even disturb the sleeper. With serene deliberation, she pointed to a fourth bed, intimating it was to be mine. After extinguishing the candle and replacing it with a night-lamp, she glided through an inner door she left ajar—the entrance to her own chamber, a large, well-appointed suite discernible through the opening.

That night, my devotions were entirely ones of thanksgiving. My journey since morning had been fraught with strangeness, my provisions unexpectedly generous. Scarcely could I believe that less than forty-eight hours had passed since I left London, a passenger-bird with no guardian but the vague protections of transit, drifting upon the dubious tracery of hope.

A light sleeper, I awoke suddenly in the dead of night. The room was hushed, yet a white figure stood therein—Madame, in her night-dress. Moving with imperceptible sound, she visited the three children, then approached me. Feigning sleep, I watched her study me for a long time. A curious pantomime ensued; she likely sat for a quarter-hour on the edge of my bed, gazing at my face. Drawing nearer, she leaned close, slightly lifting my cap to turn back the border and expose my hair, examining it. She inspected my hand resting on the bedclothes. Having completed this scrutiny, she turned to the chair at the foot of the bed where my clothes lay. Hearing her touch and lift them, I cautiously opened my eyes, driven by curiosity to see where her investigative zeal would lead. It led her far: she examined every article. I surmised her motive—to judge the wearer, her station, resources, and neatness—from the garments. The intent was not evil, but the methods were hardly fair. She turned my pocket inside out, counted the coins in my purse, and opened a small memorandum book, coolly perusing its contents until she extracted a small, plaited lock of Miss Marchmont's grey hair. To a bunch of three keys—for my trunk, desk, and work-box—she paid special attention. Indeed, she withdrew a moment to her own room with them. Rising softly, I followed her gaze: these keys were not returned until they had left impressions of their wards in the wax of the toilet in the adjoining room. Once everything was done decently and in order, my property was restored, my clothes carefully refolded. What conclusions did she draw? Were they favorable? These questions were vain. Madame's face, in its nocturnal aspect, resembled stone; whereas it had been human and motherly in the salon, it now revealed no response whatsoever.

Having fulfilled her duty—a burden she viewed strictly as such—she rose, noiseless as a shadow, and moved toward her chamber. At the door, she turned, fixing her eye on the heroine of the bottle, who still snoored loudly. This woman, I presumed, was Mrs. Sweeny, perhaps Anglicized or Hibernicized. In Madame Beck's eye, Mrs. Sweeny's doom was sealed; an immutable purpose radiated from her gaze. Madame's visits for shortcomings might be slow, but they were inevitable. This was thoroughly un-English; I was clearly in a foreign land.

The following day deepened my acquaintance with Mrs. Sweeny. It appeared she had introduced herself to her employer as an English lady in reduced circumstances, a native of Middlesex speaking the tongue with a pure metropolitan accent. Madame, relying on her own infallible methods for uncovering truth, displayed a singular intrepidity in hiring service off-hand, as evidenced by my own case. She accepted Mrs. Sweeny as nursery-governess to her three children. Need I explain that this lady was, in effect, a native of Ireland, her actual station indeterminate? She boldly claimed to have "had the bringing-up of the son and daughter of a marquis." I suspect she might have been a hanger-on, nurse, foster-mother, or washer-woman in some Irish family. She spoke a smothered tongue, curiously overlaid with mincing Cockney inflections. Somehow, she had acquired and retained a wardrobe of suspicious splendor: gowns of stiff, costly silk that fitted her indifferently, seemingly made for other proportions; caps with real lace borders; and, the chief item in her inventory and the spell by which she struck awe through the household, quelling the otherwise scornful teachers and servants, and influencing Madame herself so long as her broad shoulders bore the folds—"a real Indian shawl," a "véritable cachemire"—she maintained her footing. Without this shawl, Madame noted with unmixed reverence and amazement, she would not have lasted two days; by virtue of it alone, she persisted for a month.

However, when Mrs. Sweeny discovered I had arrived to fill her shoes, she declared herself fully. Then she rose against Madame Beck in her full power, descending upon me with her concentrated weight. Madame bore this revelation and visitation with such stoicism that I, ashamed, could support it only with composure. For one brief moment, Madame Beck absent herself from the room. Ten minutes later, a police officer stood amidst us. Mrs. Sweeny and her effects were removed. Throughout the scene, Madame's brow remained unruffled, her lips dropping not a single sharply accented word.

This brisk affair of dismissal was settled before breakfast. Order to march was given, the policeman called, the mutineer expelled; the "chambre d'enfans" was fumigated, cleansed, windows thrown open, and every trace of the accomplished Mrs. Sweeny—even the fine essence and spiritual fragrance that gave token of her head and front—was annihilated from the Rue Fossette. All this transpired between the moment Madame Beck issued forth like Aurora from her chamber and the instant she coolly sat down to pour her first cup of coffee.

About noon, I was summoned to dress Madame. (It appeared my role was a hybrid between *gouvernante* and lady's-maid.) Until noon, she haunted the house in her wrapping-gown, shawl, and soundless slippers. How would the lady-chief of an English school approve of this custom?

The dressing of her hair puzzled me. She had plenty of it, auburn and unmixed with grey, though she was forty. Seeing my embarrassment, she said, "You have not been a *femme-de-chambre* in your own country?" Taking the brush from my hand and setting me aside, not ungently, she arranged it herself. In performing other offices of the toilet, she half-directed, half-aided me, displaying no temper or impatience. Note: That was the first and last time I was required to dress her. Henceforth, that duty devolved upon Rosine, the portress.

When attired, Madame Beck appeared a personage of a figure somewhat short and stout, yet still graceful in a peculiar way, possessing the grace resulting from proportion of parts. Her complexion was fresh and sanguine, not too rubicund; her eye, blue and serene; her dark silk dress fitted her as only a French seamstress can. She looked well, though a little bourgeois; indeed, she was bourgeois. I know not what harmony pervaded her whole person, yet her face offered a striking contrast: its features were not usually seen in conjunction with a complexion of such blended freshness and repose. Their outline was stern. Her forehead was high but narrow, expressing capacity and some benevolence, but no expanse. Nor did her peaceful yet watchful eye ever know the fire kindled in the heart or the softness flowing thence.
