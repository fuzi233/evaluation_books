Consciousness returned to me, not as a gentle awakening, but as a shock against the infinite, profound cold. Yet, I was intensely blessed—more so than my heart, now frozen in the aftermath of grief, could ever imagine. Warmth held no appeal for me; I recalled having known it, but the memory was a hollow shell, stripped of feeling. Here, in this absolute chill, every care was soothed, every pain dissolved, every sorrow swallowed up. Sorrow? Nay. It was being consumed by a life drawing near, ready to restore every good and lovely thing a hundredfold.

I lay at peace, filled with a quiet, electric expectation, breathing the damp, rich odors of Earth's bosom. Beneath me, the souls of primroses, daisies, and snowdrops waited patiently in the soil, holding their breath for the Spring.

How to convey the delight of this frozen, yet hyper-conscious sleep! I had no need to stand; I needed only to lie stretched out and utterly still. Words failed to describe the cold, yet I grew colder and colder, welcoming the frost with a hunger that defied logic. My self-awareness faded, thread by thread, replaced by a bliss unimaginable yet undeniably felt. I had not created this state, nor prayed for it; it belonged to me simply by virtue of existing, and my existence was anchored by a Will dwelling within me.

Then, the dreams arrived. They did not trickle; they crowded in, overwhelming the silence. I lay naked on a snowy peak, the white mist heaving below like a billowing ocean. The cold moon hung in the air beside me, dwarfed by a sky even colder, a realm where the moon and I resided together. In one vision, I was Adam, waiting on the verge of creation for God to breathe life into my lungs. In another, I was a child nestled in the bosom of a mother radiating a blinding whiteness. Again, I was a youth on a white horse, leaping calmly from cloud to cloud of a azure heaven, hastening toward some blessed, undefined goal. For centuries—or perhaps chiliads, or merely one endless night—I drifted through these realms. Time was irrelevant; I existed in the land of thought, soaring far beyond seven dimensions and ten senses, perhaps right here in the heart of God. I dreamed away dim cycles within the center of a melting glacier, watching the spectral moon draw nearer, while the wind and the roar of a torrent swelled in my ears. I listened, letting the elements sing their peaceful anthem of waiting for a redemption that drew closer with every beat. These cycles, though vast, may have been but a single second, pregnant with eternity.

And then, abruptly, yet without disturbing my bliss, the weight of my sins descended. Every wrong I had ever committed, from the depths of my forgotten past to the immediate present, materialized before me. Fully alive within each error stood the conscious "I," confessing, renouncing, and lamenting the dead. I made atonement with every soul I had injured, hurting, or offended. Every human being who had carried a troubled thought because of me became unspeakably precious, and I humbled myself before them, agonizing to cast the clinging offense from between us. I wept at the feet of the mother whose commands I had slighted; with bitter shame, I confessed to my father the two lies I had told him long ago, forgotten, only to keep them now in my heart to crush at his feet. I became the eager slave of everyone I had wronged, devising countless services to render them: building houses unseen before, training horses from other worlds, creating gardens haunted by still pools and alive with running waters, writing songs to swell their hearts, and turning the forces of nature into channels of invention to bring them laughter. Love possessed me. Love *was* my life. To me, as to Him who made me, love was all in all.

Suddenly, the scene shifted. I found myself in a solid blackness, a void where the ghost of light dwelling in the eye-caverns could not cast even a fancied glimmer. Yet, my heart, which feared nothing and hoped infinitely, remained full of peace. I lay there, imagining what the light would look like when it finally broke through, wondering what new creation it would birth. Then, without conscious volition, I sat up and stared around me.

The moon was peering through the low, horizontal, crypt-like windows of a death-chamber, her long light slanting, I thought, across the fallen but still-ripening sheaves of the great husbandman's harvest. But no—the harvest was gone! Gathered in, or swept away by a chaotic storm, not a single sacred sheaf remained. My dead were gone. I was alone. Desolation lay in depths far vaster than I had ever known. Had there never been any ripening dead? Had I only dreamed them and their loveliness? If so, why these walls? Why the empty couches? No, they were all up! They were all abroad in the new eternal day, having forgotten me. They had left me behind, stranded alone. Tenfold more terrible was the tomb now that its inhabitants were absent. The quiet ones had made me quiet with their presence, pervading my mind with their blissful peace; now I had no friend, and my lovers were miles away. For a moment, I sat staring, horror-stricken. I had been alone with the moon on a mountain top in the sky; now I was alone with her in a huge cenotaph. She, too, stared about, seeking her dead with a ghastly gaze. I sprang to my feet and staggered from the fearful place.

The cottage was empty. I ran out into the night.

There was no moon. Just as I left the chamber, a cloudy rampart had risen to cover her. Yet, a broad shimmer came from far over the heath, mingled with a ghostly murmuring music, as if the moon were raining a light that plashed as it fell. I ran, stumbling across the moor, until I found a lovely lake, margined with reeds and rushes. Behind the cloud, the moon gazed upon the monster's den—a pool of clearest, brightest water, very still. But the musical murmur persisted, filling the quiet air and drawing me after it.

I walked along the border of the little mere and climbed the range of hills. What a sight rose to my eyes! The entire expanse where, with hot, aching feet, I had crossed and recrossed the deep-scored channels and ravines of the dry river-bed was now alive with streams, torrents, and still pools—"a river deep and wide"! How the moon flashed on the water, and how the water answered with flashes of its own—white sparks breaking everywhere from its rock-encountered flow! A great jubilant song arose from its bosom, the song of new-born liberty. I stood gazing, and my heart began to exult. My life was not a total failure! I had helped to set this river free! My dead were not lost! I had but to go after and find them! I would follow and follow until I came whither they had gone. Our meeting might be thousands of years away, but at last—at last I should hold them! Wherefore else did the floods clap their hands?

I hurried down the hill; my pilgrimage was begun. In what direction to turn my steps, I knew not, but I must go and go until I found my living dead. A torrent ran swift and wide at the foot of the range; I rushed in, and it laid no hold upon me. I waded through the next, sprang across the third, swam the fourth, and waded again through the fifth.

I stopped to gaze on the wondrous loveliness of the ceaseless flash and flow, and to hearken to the multitudinous, broken music. Now and then, some incipient air seemed about to draw itself clear of the dulcet confusion, only to merge again in the consorted roar. At moments, the world of waters would invade as if to overwhelm me—not with the force of its seaward rush or the shouting of its liberated throng, but with the greatness of the silence wandering into sound.

As I stood lost in delight, a hand was laid on my shoulder. I turned, seeing a man in the prime of strength, beautiful as if freshly plucked from the heart of the glad Creator, young like him who cannot grow old. I looked closely: it was Adam. He stood large and grand, clothed in a white robe, with the moon woven into his hair.

"Father," I cried, "where is she? Where are the dead? Has the great resurrection come and gone? The terror of my loneliness was upon me; I could not sleep without my dead, so I ran from the desolate chamber. Whither shall I go to find them?"

"You mistake, my son," he answered, in a voice whose very breath was consolation. "You are still in the chamber of death, still upon your couch, asleep and dreaming, with the dead around you."

"Alas!" I stammered, "when I but dream, how am I to know it? The dream best dreamed is the likest to the waking truth!"

"When you are quite dead, you will dream no false dream. The soul that is true can generate nothing that is not true, neither can the false enter it."
