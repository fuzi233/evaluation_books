The chamber had been dressed for triumph, yet it felt like a lair. Striped and spotted pelts lay in careless pomp upon the floor, their long hairs catching the lamplight like watered silk; the walls held heavy hangings that swallowed sound until every small noise—an ankle-ring’s faint chink, a curtain’s hush—came sharp as a knife-edge. The lady who sat there seemed not to breathe with the room. She sat rigid, arms thrust forward, hands half clenched as though she were kneading some invisible, bitter dough that would not soften. The colour ran and fled in her cheeks; now a feverish flush, now a pallor like stone. Her foot worried the carpet without cease, tapping out a restless, broken rhythm that made the skins seem to quiver.

A domestic drew the curtain that screened the outer door. The soft scrape of the ring against the rod was loud in that muffled place. The lady’s brow darkened and burned.

In an awe-struck whisper the maid said, “Altezza… the man is here.”

“Admit him.”

She snatched up a little black mask. It covered her as quickly as a lid shuts upon a flame. The colour went out of her face at once, and the black upon the whiteness made her look carved, cold, and dreadful—like marble that has been bruised.

The man entered bowing and scraping, a figure so common that a crowd could have yielded a hundred like him: cropped hair, roundish head, plain decent clothes, features neither fair nor foul. Only his eye would not be still; it darted, watched, measured, as if it listened with the pupil. After a profusion of reverences he stood opposite her, waiting as tools wait.

“They have told you for what you are wanted?” she asked.

“Yes, Signora.”

“And those who spoke to you—did they agree what you are to receive?”

“Yes, Signora. It is the full price, and it purchases the greater vendetta—unless of your benevolence you choose to content yourself with the lesser.”

She drew herself a hair’s breadth back, as one does from a smell that offends. “I understand you not.”

“Ah.” He spread his hands with the patience of a man explaining weights to a buyer. “This is the Signora’s first. The lesser vendetta is the death of the body only. We watch our man come out of a church; or take him in an innocent hour; and so deal with him. In the greater vendetta we watch him, and catch him hot from some unrepented sin, and so slay his soul as well as his body. But this vendetta is not so run upon now as it was a few years ago.”

A little shiver ran over the white of her throat above the mask; her fingers tightened until the nails bit into her own palm. “Man—silence me his tongue, and let his treasonable heart beat no more. But his soul I have no feud with.”

“So be it, Signora.” His voice showed no surprise; it was as level as a board. “He who spoke to me knew not the man, nor his name, nor his abode. From whom shall I learn these?”

“From myself.”

At that, for the first time, anxiety touched him; it flickered in the restless eye. “Signora,” he said quickly, “be cautious. Be particular. In this part of the business a word amiss is like salt mistaken for sugar—it spoils all.”

“Fear me not.” Her voice struck like flint. “Listen. It is a young man—tall of stature; auburn hair; dark blue eyes; an honest face that would deceive a saint. He lives in the Via Claudia, at the corner house—the glover’s. In that house lodge but three males: he; and a painter, short of stature and dark-visaged; and a young slim boy. He that hath betrayed me is a stranger, fair, and taller than thou art.”

The bravo drank the particulars as a cook drinks the recipe that will make or mar the dish. He nodded once. “It is enough.”

She lifted one hand, commanding. “Stay. Haunteth he any secret place where thou mayst deal with him?”

“My spy”—the word came out with a bitter relish—“doth report me he hath of late frequented the banks of Tiber after dusk; doubtless to meet his light o’ love, who calls me her rival. Even there slay him. Let my rival come and find him—the smooth, heartless, insolent traitor.”

“Be calm, Signora. He will betray no more ladies.”

“I know not that.” Her breath made the mask seem to stir. “He weareth a sword, and can use it. He is young and resolute.”

“Neither will avail him.”

The certainty in the man’s tone stung her pride into scrutiny. “Art so sure of thy hand? What are thy weapons?”

He answered not with boasting but with a show, as if laying out knives upon a board. He held up a steel gauntlet. “We strike with such force we need must guard our hand. This is our mallet.”

Then, with a practised motion, he undid his doublet and gave her a glimpse of mail beneath—small linked rings, hard as scales. Last, he laid a glittering stiletto upon the table. The metal caught the lamplight and threw it back in a thin, cold line.

She shuddered—one involuntary tremor, the flesh remembering what the mind commands it to forget. Then she took up the dagger in her white hand and tried its point against her finger, as a housewife tests a new blade on the skin of an apple.

“Beware, madam,” said the bravo, sharply.

“What—is it poisoned?”

“Saints forbid! We steal no lives. We take them with steel point, not drugs. But ’tis newly ground, and I feared for the Signora’s white skin.”

“His skin is as white as mine,” she said, and for a breath a strange pity gleamed through the anger, like a pale flame seen through smoke. It died as quickly as it came. “But his heart is black as soot. Say—do I not well to remove a traitor that slanders me?”

“The Signora will settle that with her confessor. I am but a tool in noble hands—like my stiletto.”

She did not seem to hear him. Her thoughts went racing, not in the language of prayer, but in the quick, stinging idiom of pride wounded in its own house. “Oh, how I could have loved him—to the death—as now I hate him. Fool! He will learn to trifle with princes; to spurn them and fawn on them, and prefer the scum of the town to them, and make them a by-word.”

She looked up, and the black mask made the movement terrible, as if some idol had turned its face. “Why loiter’st thou here? Haste thee. Revenge me.”

“It is customary,” he said without change of tone, “to pay half the price beforehand, Signora.”

“Ah—I forgot; thy revenge is bought.” She thrust a bag across the table; the coins within made a heavy, clotted sound, dull as a mortar’s thud. “Here is more than half. When the blow is struck, come for the rest.”

“You will soon see me again, Signora.” And he retired with the same bows and scrapings, the humbleness of a man who can kill.

When he was gone, the room seemed to expand, as if it had been holding its breath with him. The princess sat where he had left her, still masked, arms straight out before her, the nails of her clenched hand nipping the table’s edge. So sat the fabled sphinx; so sits a tigress—motionless, yet full of crouched violence.

And yet, when the hired step had ceased upon the stones outside, a chill crept upon her. In the silence she could hear more than she wished: the faint drip of a fountain beyond the wall, the far-off call of a watchman, the whisper of the hangings as the air shifted. Those small honest sounds, unbought and unbribed, made her mood waver. Misgivings rose in her like a cold vapour from a river at dusk—precursors of a remorse that would be too late to serve.

For Gerard and Margaret were before their age.

This was the true mediaeval creature: proud, amorous, vindictive, generous, foolish, cunning, impulsive, unprincipled—and ignorant as dirt. Power is the curse of such a nature. Had she been forced to do her crimes with her own hands, the weakness of her nerves might have balanced the violence of her passions; her bark would have been worse than her bite. But power gives a feeble furious woman male instruments; and that unholy joining is terrible in its effect.

Here it had whetted an assassin’s dagger for a poor, forlorn wretch—one who, even now, was but meditating suicide.
