The band of captured highwaymen were sentenced to stand pinioned in the Neufchasteau market square for two full hours, a public display meant to give any unrecognized victims time to come forward and name them for other unproven crimes.
The whole two hours passed with no accuser stepping forth, little surprise given these were night-roving vampires of the road, who preyed almost exclusively on weary, far-travelling strangers who had no local kin to speak for them. But the moment the officers moved to cut them down and march them back to gaol, a woman in the crowd let out a scream so raw it cut over every other murmur, and pointed shaking at one of the prisoners, her eyes bulging almost out of her head. She did not get a single word out before she fainted dead away.
The crowd swarmed her, half to offer aid, half gawking. As she came to, they bickered over which man she had pointed at — no one could agree, until the woman flew upright like a lioness springing for her cub before any could stop her. “My child!” she shrieked at the burly innkeeper turned robber, “Man, man, give me back my child!” She tore the glossy golden child’s hair strung as a trophy around his neck, kissed it over and over, and then the fog cleared from her grief-muddled mind. She knew instantly by that very hair that her little girl was long dead, and sank back wailing, sobbing so hard over the locks that the whole crowd roared as one, surging for the thief to tear him limb from limb.
His life would have ended that minute, swift and brutal, in an age where every man carried a knife at his belt — but Denys the archer drew his sword in a single fluid motion, shouting, “To me, comrades!” and planted himself between the mob and the prisoner, steel glinting. “Who lays a finger on him dies.” His fellow archers stepped up behind him, and they held the crowd back by main force while Denys called out to the roaring throng: “What fool’s vengeance is this? Would you rob the public wheel of its due, and give this vermin an easy, quick death before the sentence even passes?”
The mob was held back less by his words than the edge of the archers’ drawn blades, growling and baring their teeth all the while, until the town’s municipal guards clustered round the prisoners to form a living shield, and marched them prudently back to gaol, the crowd hooting and jeering at both guards and robbers all the way.
Denys made sure the prisoners were locked safely away before heading for the White Hart inn, where he expected to meet Gerard. On the short walk, he spotted two pretty girls leaning out a first-floor window, working at their needlework. He tipped his hat, they smiled, he stopped to chat. Their manners were easy, their cheeks flushed bright, and when he invited them back to the White Hart for a feast of wine and spiced pastries, they demurred at first, then he playfully accepted their refusal, and they laughed and agreed instead. Lost in their company, he forgot all about Gerard, who at that very moment was being dragged off to gaol by two constables.
Gerard, still dazed from the chaos of the square, snapped back to his senses mid-journey, and stopped short, demanding to know by what authority he was being arrested.
“By the vice-bailiff’s,” the constable grunted.
“The vice-bailiff? Alas, what could a stranger far from home possibly have done to offend him? This charge of sorcery must be a blind — I am no magician, only a poor honest lad far from his village.”
The constable scoffed at the vague plea. “Show him the writ, Jacques,” he said. Jacques held the warrant out a full yard and a half from Gerard’s face, and the other constable pinned his arms tight at the same time: both men were on tenterhooks, scared the prisoner would lunge to tear the document up, for they held all official writs in superstitious awe.
But Gerard had no such thought. He would not have touched the cursed paper with a pair of tongs, for all the fear he was in. He just craned his neck, squinted, and read it. To his utter shock, the vice-bailiff who had signed the order was none other than the friendly alderman he had sat and drunk with not two days before. He begged the constables there was some terrible mistake, offered each of them an ecu if they would detour and take him to the alderman first, not straight to the dark gaol cell.
The two men bickered. “We have no orders to take him before his worship,” Jacques said.
“But — we also have no orders *not* to,” his partner pointed out, squinting at the fine print on the writ. They agreed, took the coins, and made a great show of doing Gerard a huge favour. He was too tense to feign the gratitude they wanted, but he tried, for fear they would change their minds.
He walked into the alderman’s parlour with his heart hammering in his throat, stammering that he had done no wrong since he left this very room alongside Manon and Denys a day prior.
“Nought that I know of,” the alderman said, confused, until the writ was handed to him. He sighed. “I signed this at dawn. I am old, my memory slips. I got to talking about that poor dead child the robber had murdered, and quite forgot I had ordered your arrest, you sorcerer. All is well, now I recall. Before you go to gaol, you will hear what your accuser says — run and fetch him, you.”
The servant could not find the accuser right away, and the impatient alderman explained to Gerard what the charge actually was: “They say you lit a dead man’s face with devil’s fire, that flames bright but never burns flesh. If that is true, lad, I grieve for you — you will burn alive on good pine logs in the Neufchasteau market square, no reprieve.”
“Sir, for pity’s sake, let me speak to the cure, our parish priest!” Gerard begged.
The alderman warned him against it: the Church was far harsher on accused sorcerers than the town council could ever be.
“But sir, I am innocent!” Gerard cried, half indignant, half desperate.
“If you truly think that — officer, go with him to the cure, but mind he does not escape. Innocent, indeed!”
They found the cure in his linen doublet, out in his yard nailing a broken wheelbarrow back together. Gerard spilled the whole tale, about the white chemical he had used only to scare the bandits off, no magic at all, and the shrewd priest, who had already heard the full story from Gerard the day before, knew exactly what was what. He told the constable to wait outside the private room, guard the door, and not come in no matter what sounds he heard. The big constable stood outside trembling, half expecting the walls to blow open in a puff of brimstone at any second.
The moment the door clicked shut behind the guard, the cure dropped his grave clerical face and grinned, curious as a boy. “Show me the trick,” he said.
“I cannot, unless the room is fully dark,” Gerard said. The cure slammed the wooden shutters closed, blocking every sliver of daylight. “But what can I put it on?” Gerard said. “There is no dead face here to make the glow look eerie.” The cure groped along the shelf, and fished out a small wooden statue of his patron saint.
“Good, use that.”
“Heaven forbid! That would be profanation,” Gerard protested.
“Pshaw, it will rub right off, will it not?” the cure laughed.
Thus encouraged, Gerard daubed the phosphorus on the statue’s eyes, nostrils, then streaked it through the wood-carved curls of its hair, until the whole face glowed like a live glow-worm in the pitch black. The cure yelped and jumped back in surprise.
“By Our Lady!” he shouted. “No wonder every soul who saw that dead robber’s face lit up like that thought you were a magician! Come, we go see the alderman now.”
He pulled on his grey clerical gown and big wide-brimmed hat, and they were back in the alderman’s parlour in minutes. Standing at the alderman’s side, whispering poison in his ear, was Gerard’s accuser: a gaunt, odd figure in bright red hose, red shoes, a black gown stitched with blue bands, and a lopsided cocked hat.
The cure smiled at the man, whose name was Mangis, and said, “So, Mangis, you are at your old tricks again, babbling honest men to their deaths! This is the same old game: two sorcerers will never agree. This man Mangis claims to be a white sorcerer, who would sell his soul to the devil tonight if Satan was fool enough to buy what he can take for free. This quack, who fancies himself a magician but is only a con man, accuses this innocent lad of black magic, when the boy only used a simple chemical secret well known to all learned churchmen to defend himself against robbers.”
“But he is no churchman, to dabble in such mysteries,” the alderman objected.
“He is more churchman than layman, raised in a convent, and ordained in the lesser orders,” the cure said smoothly. “Therefore, sorcerer, withdraw your complaint now.”
“Not I,” Mangis blustered. “I am a white sorcerer, I make no pact with the devil. I battle his black arts lawfully, I detect hidden black magicians — as I did that whole coven we burned at Dol last year!”
“Ay, Mangis,” the cure said, his voice turning sharp. “And what came of that famous fire you lit? Dijon’s parliament investigated last month, found they were all peaceful, innocent citizens, and ordered high masses said for their souls, and expiatory mystery plays performed for them across seven Burgundian towns. We do not blame our own credulous ears for burning the innocent — we blame the lying tongue that tricked us. If you do not drop this charge, I will send word to my lord bishop this very hour, and you will smell burning pine far closer than any man has ever lived through. The very word ‘devil’ in our Latin means ‘false accuser’ — and you wear his colours of red and black for all to see.”
The alderman nodded. “That is truth, now I look at him. Red and black are the fiend’s own livery.”
Mangis’s face bleached as pale as his gown was bright, stammered that he respected Holy Church, dropped the complaint immediately, and scurried out of the room as fast as his legs could carry him.
The cure turned back to the alderman, and told him that Gerard had already given over every last bit of the phosphorus to him, the Church, so no harm had been done. The alderman laughed, signed Gerard’s release papers instantly, and the two men struck a bargain over the town’s unpaid four silver francs owed to the cure for parish work.
The moment they stepped out into the street, Gerard knelt and kissed the cure’s hand. “Sir, you have saved me from the fire! How can I ever thank you?”
“Nought, foolish lad. Bounty rewards itself. Though — humph — I had to tell a small lie just now, that you gave me all the phosphorus. That falsehood will earn me a full fortnight of penance, no less,” the cure sighed, his eyes twinkling mischievously.
“Falsehood? But that was no lie!” Gerard cried, shoving the sealed glass bottle of phosphorus right into the priest’s palm. “It is yours, every last drop. And take this purse of mine, for the poor of the church!”
The cure stepped back, waved it away, gruff as ever. “Not a single denier. Fie, you are a pauper and an exile, you need it far more than the church. Come to my house every noon, instead, and eat your midday meal with me. I have taken a liking to you.” He turned and walked off abruptly, his hands stuffed behind his back, itching to take the purse far more than he would ever admit.
Gerard hurried to the White Hart to find Denys, and walked in on him happily rattling dice in a cup, playing for coin with the two pretty girls Denys had stopped to chat with that morning.
Gerard’s face flushed hot. “Do you forget Jeanneton’s proverb?” he said sharply. “Le peu que sont les femmes — women are nothing but trouble.”
Denys laughed, rattling the dice. “What say you to that, mesdemoiselles?” he asked the girls.
They tittered. “No man runs down women save one too old, too ugly, or too witless to please them,” they teased.
“Witless? Wise men do not have the folly to please you, nor the madness to want to,” Gerard said loftily. “I do not speak to you, brazen hussies who make free with a man the first time you meet him — I speak to my comrade.”
“Go on, preach away,” Denys said, grinning. “He is very Solomon of old proverbs, this one, lads, fed on nothing but wise sayings from the cradle.”
“Your friendship is a byword, then!” Gerard snapped. “Friendship that melts to nothing the second you see a farthingale.”
“Unhappy wretch!” Denys cried, laughing. “I only spout pellets of fun, and you answer with daggers.”
“Would that I could,” Gerard said, and turned to storm out. He paused in the doorway, glowered back, and shouted his new proverb at them:
“Qui hante femmes et dez
Il mourra en pauvretez.”
He slammed the door so hard the windows rattled, having delivered his thunderbolt of old wisdom.
Drained by all the day’s terror, he wandered to the inn bar for a crust of bread and a cup of wine. The landlord handed him a “pint” bottle that was unnaturally thick and heavy at the bottom, holding barely half the measure it claimed. Gerard pointed out the cheat at once, and threatened to carry the short-measure bottle straight to the vice-bailiff.
The landlord turned white, begged him not to, offered to sell him two of the “pints” for the price of one, and then launched into a long, passionate rant about how impossible it was to run an honest inn in Burgundy. Soldiers quartered on him were paid two sous a day and ate ten times that in food, pilgrims and black and grey friars ate like wolves and paid nothing but empty prayers, the local gentry were allowed to sell wine by the cup in the market square for half his price, excise men from three different layers of government showed up every week demanding free food and drink to “overlook” his measures, and the law forbade him from buying more than three bushels of meal at a time when market day only came once a week. He swore he did not cut travellers’ throats like the other road inns, he only cheated a little on the wine measures just to keep a roof over his head.
Mid-rant, Denys burst through the door, swearing so loud the whole bar went quiet. The two girls had snuck out one after the other, and stolen his entire purse, fifteen gold crowns. He roared that he hated the law, did not want to chase them, but Gerard dragged him to the provost marshal anyway. The official only shrugged, said these girls were respectable needleworkers most days, who only robbed gullible drunks when the chance came, and they were long gone over the town walls by now.
Denys stormed back out, fuming, but eventually calmed down enough to admit he had left five gold crowns with the alderman and ten more with Manon for safekeeping, so he was not completely ruined. Then he paused, and admitted sheepishly: “I — well. I asked her to marry me. Manon.”
Gerard stared at him, completely dumbfounded. “What? You offered marriage to a strange girl you met on the road, passing through?”
“Nay, I was not such a fool as that,” Denys grinned. “I knew she would never go off on the road with me otherwise. ‘Mademoiselle,’ I said, ‘marriage is not one of my usual habits, but struck by your great qualities, I make an exception. Deign to bestow your hand on me.’”
“And did she bestow her answer on your ear, with a slap?” Gerard said, gaping.
