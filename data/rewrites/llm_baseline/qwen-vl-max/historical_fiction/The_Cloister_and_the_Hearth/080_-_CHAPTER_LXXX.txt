Brother Clement had spent more than a year teaching and preaching in Basel when one morning Jerome appeared before him, dust-covered and eyes alight with triumph.

“Give glory to God, Brother Clement,” he declared. “You may now accompany me to England.”

“I am ready,” replied Clement. “For months I have waited, devoting my spare moments between lessons and prayer to mastering the English tongue.”

“A wise choice,” said Jerome. He then explained that he had delayed their journey only until he could secure special authority from the Pope—powers to collect funds for the Church’s work in England and to hear confessions in all secular monasteries.

“Now, gird up your loins,” he added. “Let us go forth and strike a bold blow for the Church, and against the Franciscans.”

Together, the two friars set out down the Rhine toward England. In larger towns, they preached side by side; in smaller ones, they often split ways—one preaching on one bank of the river, the other on the opposite shore—and met again at a prearranged place. Both were gifted speakers, though their styles differed greatly. Jerome’s sermons were noble and commanding, rich in moral certainty, yet somewhat narrow in scope and slightly repetitive in delivery—though, truth be told, far less so than most preachers of the age. Clement, on the other hand, was full of variation, his speech often natural and conversational, flowing gently like a brook over stones. It wrapped around reason and emotion alike, tender and persuasive. Yet when stirred by higher thought, Clement rose with astonishing power—soaring above Jerome as surely as his quiet tone fell below. At such moments, he became something almost divine: a prophet aflame, an orator possessed. *Vehemens ut procella, excitatus ut torrens, incensus ut fulmen, tonabat, fulgurabat, et rapidis eloquentiae fiuctibus cuncta proruebat et perturbabat.*

I would offer literal excerpts, but five reasons hold me back: it is difficult; time is short; I’ve done this before; another writer has since surpassed me; and while similarity is a virtue in peas, it is a flaw in books. Still—lest I seem to dodge the matter entirely—Clement had a secret habit: before entering any town, he would quietly learn its recent troubles and its prevailing sins. Jerome, rigid and unyielding, scorned such tactics. He believed a preacher should speak not to the people’s faults, but to their souls, regardless of circumstance.

At one major town, several leagues from the Rh{EIF, they shared the same pulpit. Jerome took the first turn, preaching against vanity in dress—a theme he returned to often. He was eloquent, biting, and the crowd listened with approval. But vanity was not their vice. Clement followed, addressing drunkenness—their real scourge. Here, he struck hard. Drunkenness, he said, was not merely a sin—it was the mother of all crimes, breeding theft, violence, and murder. He reminded them of a parricide committed recently by a decent man under the influence of drink. He spoke of a group of drunkards who had roasted one of their own alive in a nearby village.

“Your last prince,” he continued, “was said to have died of apoplexy—but you know well he died of drink. One of your aldermen perished last month, dead drunk, drowned in a puddle. Your children go barefoot while you fill your bellies with poison that turns you into beasts—foolish as calves, cruel as boars. It drives your families into poverty and your souls into hell. I tell you: if your women drank as you do, your entire town—and your nation—would sink beneath the very bottom of humanity. How long will they remain temperate? How long will they resist the example of their husbands and fathers? Vice never stands still. You must change—or watch them follow in your footsteps. Already in Bohemia, women drink alongside men. What does a drunken woman look like? Would you wish to see your wife drunk, your mother degraded?”

The audience gasped. Medieval listeners did not sit in silence when moved. A cry of horror rose from the crowd.

“Ah,” said Clement, “that strikes home.”
“Madmen!” he thundered. “Do you think it shocks God less to see His noblest creation turned into a beast of drink than it shocks you—sinful creatures—to see a woman reduced to no better than yourselves?”

He closed with two vivid pictures: one of a drunkard’s home—chaotic, broken, filled with weeping children and empty bottles; the other of a sober man’s household—peaceful, orderly, blessed. The details were so true, so dramatic, that the women began murmuring, “Oh,” and “Ah,” and “Eh, but that’s a true word!”

The sermon caused a storm. People gathered in groups, arguing fiercely. Men grew angry; wives defended them, openly siding against the preacher. A married man, they insisted, deserved a drink—he labored for the whole family. And besides, they didn’t want their husbands replaced by weaklings. They laughed, but beneath it, unease stirred.

That very evening, a hundred men surrounded Brother Clement near the quay. They pressed coins into his wallet, offered him flowers, kissed his habit, and blessed him for daring to confront their weakness.

Jerome frowned. “Too earthly,” he muttered. “Drunkenness is not heresy, Clement. It does not warrant a full sermon.”

Yet as they traveled onward, Jerome found himself surprised. Clement’s words seemed to take root deeper than his own. They made people listen, reflect, weep—even change.

“He knows how to reach their level,” Jerome mused. “And yet, when he rises, he can soar.”

Still, the contrast troubled him. Though he secretly felt superior, he struggled to admit it. He tried to suppress the feeling as petty, unworthy of a servant of God.

“Souls are like locks,” he told himself. “Preachers must be like keys. If they do not differ, the door remains shut. And truly, this novice holds the key to these northern hearts—perhaps because he is himself a northern man.”

So they journeyed slowly down the Rhine, sometimes drifting with the current, more often walking along the banks, preaching, teaching, hearing confessions in villages and towns. Finally, they reached Düsseldorf.

There stood the small quay where Gerard and Denys had once boarded a boat to travel upstream. The friars stepped ashore. The streets lay before them, familiar and unchanged. There was “The Silver Lion,” the inn where so many paths had crossed. Nothing had altered—except him.

He walked barefoot through the town, hands folded across his chest, eyes cast humbly downward, heart calm and cold. A true son of Dominic, a faithful servant of Holy Church.
