The first chill, shivery dawn of October broke over Milton, bearing none of the soft silvery mists that would have draped the old Hampshire countryside of Helstone. Up here, the “silvery mist” was a thick, smut-heavy fog that clung to throat and clothing for hours, and when the sun did finally claw its way through the haze, it lit nothing but endless dusky, soot-streaked terraced streets. Margaret moved languidly about the house, helping Dixon tidy rooms and lay out linens, her eyes perpetually pricked blind by unshed tears. She had no time to give over to full, unreserved weeping. Her father and brother were both swallowed whole by their grief for Mrs. Hale, and someone had to hold the household steady, to plan, to negotiate, to make the necessary, heavy arrangements for the funeral that had fallen entirely to her.

When the fire was crackling bright in the hearth, the tea kettle singing on its hob, and the breakfast table set as pleasantly as she could manage, Margaret paused for a last look around the parlour before going to summon Mr. Hale and Frederick. She had striven to make the room feel as warm and welcoming as possible, but the brightness of the fire and the neat set of cups only sharpened the cruel contrast between the living present and the empty place her mother should occupy, and she sank down on her knees by the sofa, pressing her face deep into the cushions to muffle the sudden ragged sob tearing out of her throat.

A soft hand tapped her shoulder. “Come now, Miss Hale, my dear,” Dixon said, her usually sharp, prim voice softened by an uncharacteristic tenderness. “You cannot give way like this, or where will the rest of us be? There is no other soul in this house fit to give a single direction, and so much to settle—who will manage the funeral, who will come, where it will be held. Master Frederick is half crazed with weeping, and your poor master wanders about the house like a lost ghost. I know it’s bitter hard, my dear. But death comes for us all, and you have not had to bear the loss of anyone you loved before, thank God for that.”

Margaret drew no small comfort from the old housekeeper’s words, but the quiet kindness in her touch and tone struck her far deeper. She brushed the tears from her cheeks, forced a wavering smile for Dixon’s anxious face, and went to call her father and brother to breakfast.

Mr. Hale drifted in as if in a dream, moving with the unseeing gait of a sleepwalker whose eyes and mind are still fixed on the world they have left behind. Frederick came in fast, with a forced, bright cheerfulness that fell apart the second he grasped Margaret’s hand, looked into her red-rimmed eyes, and burst into great heaving sobs. Through the whole meal, Margaret chattered of small, trivial things, desperate to keep her companions from circling back to the last breakfast they had shared with their mother, when the three of them had sat straining their ears for any faint sound from her sickroom.

After breakfast, Margaret approached her father to discuss the funeral arrangements. He shook his head numbly, assenting to every proposal she made, even the ones that directly contradicted each other, too lost in grief to make a single firm decision. She turned to leave the room to confer with Dixon, when he motioned her back to his side.

“Write to Mr. Bell,” he said in a hollow, distant voice.

“Mr. Bell? The one from Oxford?” Margaret asked, surprised.

“Mr. Bell,” he repeated. “He was my groomsman, long ago.”

Margaret understood the quiet association to old, happier days, and promised she would write that very day. He sank back into his listless daze immediately, and Margaret spent the rest of the morning toiling through a whirl of melancholy small tasks, longing for a rest that would not come.

Towards evening, Dixon drew her aside. “I did it, miss. I was so afraid your poor father would drop down dead of a stroke, he’s spent every hour of this day in Mrs. Hale’s old room, and I’ve stood outside the door listening to him talk to her, just as if she was sitting there answering back. I thought a sharp shock would rouse him from that half-dead stupor, even if it stung at first. So I told him Master Frederick can’t stay here, it’s not safe. And it’s the truth. Just last Tuesday, when I was out running errands, I ran into a Southampton man—the first I’ve seen since we came to Milton. It was that young George Leonards, old Leonards the draper’s son, the biggest scamp that ever walked the earth, who plagued his father half to death before he ran off to sea. I know he was serving on the *Orion* at the same time as Master Frederick, though I can’t say for certain if he was there the day of the mutiny.”

“Did he recognise you?” Margaret asked, her blood running cold.

“That’s the worst of it. I never would have called out his name, but I was caught off guard, seeing a familiar face in such a strange place. He came right up to me grinning, calling me Miss Dixon, asking after you all. The more we talked, the more he got sharp, and started needling me about Master Frederick—saying he’d be strung up for mutiny the second anyone caught him, that there was a hundred pound reward out for his arrest, going on about what a disgrace he was to his family, all because I’d helped his old father give him a sound thrashing once for stealing from the shop till. He hopped on the bus at last, but turned back at the last second to leer at me and say, ‘If you want to partner up for that reward to trap Lieutenant Hale, Miss Dixon, I’ll cut you a fair share. Don’t be shy, say yes.’ Then he was gone, cackling.”

Margaret sank down into the nearest chair, suddenly very uneasy. “You haven’t told Frederick yet?”

“Not yet, miss. But when I saw your father wandering the rooms so glassy-eyed and lost, I thought the worry for his son’s safety would rouse him better than anything. Master Frederick will have to leave before Mr. Bell arrives, that’s certain.”

“Oh, I am not afraid of Mr. Bell, but this Leonards—he’s a vicious, spiteful man. I must tell Frederick at once.”

It was settled then. Frederick had to go, just when he had slotted so easily back into the Hale household, when his shared grief for their mother had made him feel more like a beloved, necessary part of the family than he had ever been before. Margaret was lost in these sad thoughts by the drawing room fire, her father restless and fidgeting with the new, unspoken fear hanging over the house, when Frederick wandered in. His wild, violent grief had softened, but his face was still wan and pale. He crossed the room to kiss her forehead.

“How thin and tired you look, Margaret,” he said softly. “You have tended to every single one of us, and no one has tended to you. Lie down on this sofa, there is nothing left for you to do right now.”

Margaret obeyed, and he tucked a shawl over her feet, then sat cross-legged on the floor beside her, their voices dropping to soft, low tones as she told him the full story of Dixon’s run-in with Leonards. Frederick let out a long, low whistle of dismay.

“That scoundrel. I never met a worse sailor, nor a worse man, in all my years at sea. He was the sort that curried favour with that brutal captain that caused the mutiny, tattling on the rest of us to save his own skin. To think he’s here in Milton! If he had even a hint I was within twenty miles of him, he’d hunt me down for that hundred pound out of pure old spite.”

Mr. Hale had drifted close to them, and overheard the last of their words. He reached out to take Frederick’s hand in both of his own, trembling. “My boy, you must go. It is cruel, but there is no way around it. You have done all you can for your mother, for us.”

“Oh papa, must he really go?” Margaret pleaded, even as her own heart told her it was the only safe choice.

Frederick laughed a little, bitter. “I’ve half a mind to stay and face it, stand my trial. I can’t bear the thought of being at the mercy of that blackguard Leonards. Though I’ll admit, this stolen visit home has had all that strange, sharp sweetness the French talk about, the charm of forbidden pleasures.”

“Do you remember when you were seven?” Margaret said, a faint, wobbly smile tugging at her mouth. “You snuck over to the neighbour’s orchard to steal apples, even though our own trees were heavy with far better fruit. Someone told you stolen apples tasted sweeter, and you took it completely literally. You haven’t changed an ounce since then.”

“You must go,” Mr. Hale repeated firmly, his mind fixed on the only clear, urgent thought in his head. “No delays.”

Frederick shook off his glum mood, and chuckled. “You know, I almost gave myself and Dixon a terrible fright this afternoon. I heard a ring at the front door, and assumed whoever it was had left ages ago, so I went to step out of my bedroom into the passage. Then Dixon came rushing up the stairs, frowning so hard she looked fit to burst, and kicked me back into hiding before I could be seen. I left the door cracked, and I heard a big, burly man giving a message to father, then leaving. I thought he was some shopman come to collect an order.”

“It was Mr. Thornton,” Mr. Hale said, from his chair.

Margaret blinked, surprised. “Mr. Thornton? I thought—”

“Thought what, little one?” Frederick prompted, when she trailed off.

“Oh nothing,” Margaret said, flushing. “I just assumed you meant a workman, not a gentleman.”

“Sure looked like a working man, to me,” Frederick said carelessly. “Took him for a mill hand at first. Turns out he’s the manufacturer, then.”

Margaret said nothing. She remembered thinking the very same thing when she first met Mr. Thornton, before she learned how kind, thoughtful, honourable he was, and a tiny flicker of annoyance pricked at her, though she could not find the words to defend him without sounding odd.

“He came by to offer any help he could give us, I think,” Mr. Hale went on, absently. “I was too lost to see him. I told Dixon to send him to you, Margaret, but I can’t rightly remember what else I said.”

“He has been a very kind friend to us through all of this,” Margaret said, when Frederick tossed out a light question about their acquaintance.

Frederick was quiet for a long minute, then he spoke, a little sheepish. “Margaret, I wish you and father would come away to Spain with me. I have a good post there, and a chance at an even better one. There’s a girl, Dolores Barbour—she’s not quite eighteen yet, but if she feels the same a year from now, we’re to be married. Her father won’t let us call it a formal engagement, but I’ve never cared for anyone half so much. I only wish you could meet her. Father, you’d love her, Margaret—everyone would. We could make a whole new life there.”

“No more moves for me,” Mr. Hale said, shaking his head. “This last one cost me my wife. I will not move again. She is buried here, and I will stay here for the rest of my days.”

Margaret leaned over, and squeezed Frederick’s hand. “Tell us more about her. I am so happy for you. You will have someone to love and care for you, far from here. But Fred—for her sake, shouldn’t you try to clear your name? You disobeyed a brutal, cruel captain to protect the rest of the crew, not out of malice. The world only knows you as a mutineer, and that will hang over her for the rest of her life, unless you prove otherwise. You should at least talk to a good, honest lawyer, see what your chances are of clearing your name.”

Frederick scoffed a little, but Margaret persisted, until at last he agreed to take her suggestion. She proposed Henry Lennox, Edith’s brother-in-law, the brilliant young barrister who had once been so fond of her, a man whose honour was beyond reproach. They planned for him to take the night train to London, write out a list of all the surviving crew members from the *Orion*, meet Lennox at his chambers in the Temple, and then sail back to Spain from a London port, no need to go near Liverpool.

Margaret wrote the note to Mr. Lennox quickly, before she could overthink every word, and Frederick snatched it from her hand before she could proofread it, tucking it into his pocket-book. As he did, a long lock of glossy black hair slipped out onto the table, and his face lit up.

“You’d like to see that, wouldn’t you?” he grinned, tucking it carefully back into the pocket-book. “But no. You will wait to meet Dolores herself. She is far too perfect to be summed up in little fragments. No mean brick can ever be a proper sample of the whole palace I intend to bring you to visit, one day soon.”
