The vanity of men—especially Frenchmen—knows no bounds. Can it be believed that after the skirmish at St. Cas—a mere rout of a rearguard—they had the audacity to waste powder on celebrations at the Invalides in Paris, crowing over our misfortune? What honor is there in triumphing over a handful of brave men, outnumbered and cornered upon the shore? I had half a mind to paint the scene of London’s own revelry after our success at St. Malo—the polished guns paraded by our tars, the horse-grenadiers gleaming in their pride, the mob roaring, the balconies of Fleet Street thronged with well-wishers. But now that the French have cheapened victory with their mimicry, I wash my hands of the subject.

Let others boast. For my part, I care only that Harry has returned, whole and sound. Did I not assure you, gentle reader, that he came back unscathed? The sea had bronzed his cheeks; the musket ball that whistled past his ear had spared him. His coat was damp, not with blood, but salt spray. True, he had turned his back—but only briefly, and what are lapels but reversals? A man may retreat and yet hold his honor intact.

* * *

**"I tell you,"** Harry recounted, **"it was every man for himself when the line broke. The French horse and foot were upon us, cutting down our fellows as we scrambled for the boats. Poor Armytage fell—I dragged him through the surf, but he never reached the ship. A fine soldier lost, and Miss Howe a widow before she was a bride."**

**"It is her I think of,"** murmured Hetty, her fingers twisting in her lap. **"And the King. He must bear the guilt of sending Sir John to his death."**

Papa Lambert shook his head, amused. **"His Majesty will mourn in his own fashion, my dear. Kings cannot weep for every soldier."**

**"But if he commanded it—"** Hetty pressed, her voice quivering.

**"Then let us hope his conscience pricks him,"** the General replied dryly. **"Though I doubt Miss Hester’s Pretender would offer more tenderness."**

George, ever the skeptic, arched a brow. **"Why not celebrate defeat and mourn our victories? I find no piety in burning ships at Cherbourg."**

**"But you’re glad all the same,"** Harry retorted. **"I know I am—though I’ll not pretend I’m proud of running. It sits ill with me, an Englishman retreating before Frenchmen."**

George said nothing, though his silence spoke volumes.

* * *

The tale of Harry’s valor spread. The newspapers lauded the volunteers; White’s and Almack’s blazed with toasts to their return. Even those who had once sneered at Harry’s idleness now clasped his hand. His commission as Ensign was secured, his name gazetted among the brave.

Aunt Bernstein’s letter arrived, crisp with praise—and a banknote tucked within. Maria Esmond’s missive, blotted and earnest, spoke of pride and a heart still tender. Even Sir Miles sent game from Norfolk, a young pheasant felled by his son’s first shot.

But it was Hetty’s silence that spoke loudest. No more barbs, no taunts—only a quiet, humbled respect. The girl who had once spurred him to folly now met his eyes with something akin to remorse.

War had tested him. Retreat had chastened him. And in the end, Harry Warrington returned not just a soldier, but a man.
