Mrs. Adair demanded no explanations. Behind her pale, placid exterior lay a mind that was tortuous and delightfully intricate. She preferred peering through a keyhole to walking straight through the door; in her view, knowledge gained through subtle maneuvering was infinitely more precious than facts extracted by a simple query. Indeed, she operated on a perverse principle regarding direct questions, considering a day well-spent only if she could outwit a companion into volunteering some trivial tidbit—a piece of news a straightforward request would have secured instantly at breakfast. Thus, though baffled by the significance Ethne attached to the white feather, and puzzled by the good news of Harry Feversham brought by Captain Willoughby, she concealed her confusion throughout lunch. Instead, she engaged her guest in conversation on indifferent topics.

Mrs. Adair could be excellent company when she chose, and she chose now. Yet, her efforts were futile. "I don't believe you're hearing a single word I'm saying!" she exclaimed eventually. Ethne laughed and admitted the truth. Once lunch concluded, Ethne retired to her room, granting herself an afternoon of solitude. Seated at her window, she slowly recounted the story Willoughby had told her that morning. Her heart thrummed to it as if to divine music. The regret that Harry hadn't returned a year prior, when she was free, paled in comparison to the joy of the tale itself; that sorrow had completely vanished. Her pride, shattered by Harry Feversham's blow in the hall at Lennon House, was now fully restored—and by the very hand that had struck it. She burned with gratitude, feeling a renewed exhilaration in the sunlight and a quicker pulse in her blood. That August afternoon gave her youth back.

She opened her dressing-case drawer and retrieved the sole portrait of Harry Feversham's gifts she had kept. She rejoiced in keeping it. It depicted someone dead to her—she knew this well, for there was no disloyalty toward Durrance in her heart—but the subject was a friend. She gazed upon it with profound happiness, realizing that Harry Feversham had required no expression of faith from her to inspire him, nor encouragement to sustain him through the years on the plane of his high ideals. Returning the portrait, she placed the white feather beside it and locked them together.

Back at the window, a light breeze danced the shadows of the tall trees, mellowing the sunlight into red gold. Yet Ethne belonged to her own country, as Harry Feversham had long known. Her heart yearned for home. It was August; the first heather would soon bloom on the Donegal hillsides, and she wished the news had arrived there. The regret that it hadn't was her crumpled rose-leaf. Here she was in a strange land; there, amidst the brown mountains, granite outcrops, and babbling streams, her new happiness would have been shared. For Ethne Eustace, great sorrows and great joys alike pulled her homeward, for endurance was easier and gladness more complete among her people. Still, she possessed one living tether to Donegal: Dermod's old collie, now her inseparable companion. To him she poured her confidences, and if her voice broke into tears, the dog would remain silent.

Through the dog, she understood much that Willoughby had omitted and Feversham had never revealed. She visualized those three years of concealment in the cramped city of Suakin, with troops marching to battle and returning dust-strewn and bleeding, yet crowned with victory. Harry Feversham had to slip away at their approach, fearing recognition by an old acquaintance—perhaps Durrance, Willoughby, or Trench—who might pierce his disguise. She pictured the panic that seized him upon seeing the dark brown walls of Berber, the night spent in ruined acres, and the stumbling search for a well among the shifting sandhills of Obak. As these images formed, she asked herself, "Where was I then? What was I doing?" She sat in a golden mist until the lights shifted on the still creek waters and the rooks wheeled noisily from the treetops, signaling evening.

That night, she approached the dinner table with a buoyancy of spirit that astonished her companions. Mrs. Adair had to concede that Ethne's eyes had rarely shone so starbrightly, nor her cheeks so freshly colored. More convinced than ever that Captain Willoughby bore stirring news, she was also more tormented by her futile attempts to guess its nature. Nevertheless, despite her perplexities, Mrs. Adair participated freely in the talk. The dinner passed with a lack of embarrassment unseen since Durrance's return to Guessens. He, too, shed his burden of restraint; his spirits rose to match Ethne's. Laughter met laughter, and the habitual tension from his face—the look of a man straining to hear with his ears what his eyes could not see—vanished entirely.

"You will play your violin tonight, I think," he said with a smile as they rose.
"Yes," she replied, "I will—with all my heart."
Durrance laughed and held the door open. The violin had remained locked in its case for the past two months. Durrance viewed the instrument as a gauge and a test. If the world went well with Ethne, the case opened and the instrument spoke; if the world went ill, it stayed silent to avoid reopening old wounds before others' eyes. Ethne knew it was an indiscreet friend, but tonight it was to be brought out.

Mrs. Adair lingered until Ethne was out of earshot. "Have you noticed the change in her tonight?" she asked.
"Yes. Haven't I?" Durrance answered. "We waited for it, hoped for it, and nearly despaired."
"Are you so glad of the change?"
Durrance threw back his head. "Do you wonder I am glad? She has always been kind, friendly, unselfish. But there is more than friendliness evident tonight, and for the first time, it is undeniable." A look of pity softened Mrs. Adair's face, and she departed without further words. Durrance claimed the entirety of Ethne's transformation for himself.

Mrs. Adair raised the blinds of the drawing-room, opened the window, and let the moonlight flood in. Seeing Ethne unlock her violin case, she stepped onto the terrace, unable to sit patiently in her company. When Durrance entered the drawing-room, he found Ethne alone. She sat by the window, already tuning her violin. Durrance took a chair behind her in the shadows.

"What shall I play for you?" she asked.
"The Mussolini Overture," he answered. "You played it on the first evening I came to Ramelton. I remember it so clearly. Play it again tonight. I wish to compare."
"I have played it since."
"Never to me."

Alone in the room, windows open under a moonlit sky, Ethne suddenly extinguished the lamp. Resuming her seat, she watched Durrance remain in the shadows, leaning forward with hands on his knees, listening with an intensity he had shown none that evening. He believed he was administering a final test upon which his life and hers depended. Ethne's violin would surely reveal whether he was right: would friendship emerge, or something deeper?

As Ethne played the overture, she forgot Durrance's presence. Outside, the air was still, warm, and fragrant; the moonlight lay like a solid silver floor on the creek; the trees dreamed under the stars. As the music floated loudly across the silent lawn, Ethne fancied it might travel down the creek, over Salcombe Bar, and across the moonlit seas, striking faint yet crystal-clear against the ears of a man sleeping far away beneath the southern stars, with the cool desert night wind brushing his face.

"If he could only hear!" she thought. "If he could only wake and know this was a message of friendship!" With this thought fueling her, she played with a skill she had never exhibited before, turning her violin into a voice of sympathy. The fancy evolved as she played. The music became a bridge suspended in mid-air across the world, allowing her and Harry Feversham to meet and shake hands for just a few minutes. Of course, they would part immediately, returning to their separate paths, but these moments would aid them both. The chords rang out into silence, seeming to proclaim the pride she had regained that day. Her fantasy hardened into belief. It was no longer "If he should hear," but "He *must* hear!"

Carried away by this disregard for caution, a strange hope enthralled her. "If he could answer!" She lingered on the final bars, waiting for a response. When the music faded into silence, she sat with her violin on her knees, gazing eagerly across the moonlit garden. An answer did come, but not up the creek or across the lawn. It emerged from the dark shadows of the room behind her, spoken through Durrance's voice.

"Ethne, where do you think I heard that overture last played?"

Startled awake from her reverie, Ethne responded as if shaken from sleep. "Why, you told me. At Ramelton, when you first came to Lennon House."
