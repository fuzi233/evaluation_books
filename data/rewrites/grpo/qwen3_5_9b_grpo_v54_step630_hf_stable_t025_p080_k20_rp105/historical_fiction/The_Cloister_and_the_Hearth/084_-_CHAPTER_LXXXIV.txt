Sunday dawned bright and crisp over the Hoog Straet, casting a golden hue upon Catherine's newly prepared shop. The air buzzed with anticipation; the sign, depicting a white lamb nursing a gilded ewe, fluttered cheerfully above the door where the names "Cornelis and Sybrandt" were freshly painted. Eli had arrived early, laden with fresh stock from his store, eager to ensure his brothers-in-law had a prosperous opening. Inside, the atmosphere was thick with the scent of possibility. Cornelis and Sybrandt strolled through the garden, their spirits buoyant, openly declaring their weariness of their agrarian past. They spoke of commerce with the fervor of men discovering a new religion, vowing to cling to their trade with the tenacity of wax. Through an open window, Catherine's mother overheard this declaration, her sharp ears catching every word. She called out to Eli, announcing that the evening supper would be a grand inauguration, a feast to welcome Margaret and her son, complete with counsel from the elders and wine to toast the successful transition from the barren fields of agriculture to the fertile plains of commerce.

Margaret descended the stairs, her usual lethargy shaken off by the prospect of the celebration. Her face flushed with the warmth of exertion and hope. Soon, Joan entered carrying a crisp salad from Jorian's garden. "He cut it for you, Margaret," Joan remarked, her tone laced with playful jealousy. "You are his favorite; I shall be forced to compete. I told him you were feasting today. But oh, lass, did you hear the sermon in the new church? Preaching! I have never witnessed such a thing."

"I wish I had been there," Margaret sighed, her voice soft. "I feel as though I have been dried up, lacking the dew of heaven."

"Why, he preaches again this afternoon," Joan replied. "But perhaps your duties call you here."

"Not she," Catherine interjected firmly. "Go on, if you are minded."

"Indeed," Margaret hesitated. "Yet, I fear I might dampen the spirits at table if I miss the warmth of a good sermon."

"Then you must be brisk," Joan urged, pointing toward the street where the congregation was gathering. "Look! There goes the holy friar." The woman bounded out of the house, crossing the road to intercept the cleric. She returned moments later, breathless but triumphant. "I was determined to see him close up. He said to me, 'My daughter, I shall go to him before sunset, God willing.' His voice was sweet as honey. But oh, my ladies, look at those thin cheeks on a young man, and those great eyes—so much like yours, Margaret."

"I have a strong desire to hear him," Margaret confessed. "But my cap is not quite clean, and all the women will be wearing their pristine white veils."

"There," Catherine said, reaching for a basket. "Take my handkerchief. You cannot keep the child; I need him for my poor Kate. She is having one of her difficult days."

Margaret accepted the handkerchief and ascended the stairs to find Kate. The room was quiet, save for the soft breathing of the invalid. "How art thou, sweetheart?" Margaret asked gently, noting the girl's pale countenance without needing to ask. "Thou smilest so. Look, I have brought thee one thou lovest."

"Two, by my way of counting," Kate replied, her smile radiant despite her suffering. A sudden spasm of pain seized her, a convulsion that would have startled a bull, yet she remained composed.

"In your lap?" Margaret gestured to the bundle in Kate's arms. "No, he is too heavy for thee in such pain."

"I love him too dearly to feel his weight," Kate whispered.

Seizing the moment, Margaret attended to her own appearance, smoothing her cap and adjusting her veil. "I am bound for the church," she announced, "to hear a beautiful preacher." Kate sighed deeply. "And yet, a moment ago, I was eager to go. That is my state for the past month; up and down, up and down, like the waves of the Zuyder Zee. I would rather stay beside thee; say the word!"

"Nay," Kate pleaded softly. "Pray, go. Bring me back every word. Alas, that I cannot go myself." Tears welled in the patient's eyes, a silent plea that settled heavily in Margaret's heart. This sight resolved her. She kissed Kate tenderly, glanced down at the sleeping boy, and let out a small, resigned sigh. "I think I must not," she murmured. "I could never kiss him lightly; and my father was always against waking a child by day or night. When it is thy pleasure to wake, speak to Aunt Kate the two new words thou hast learned." With a final, loving look over her shoulder, she closed the door silently behind her.

Downstairs, the kitchen resumed its rhythm. "Joan, lend me a hand and peel these," Catherine commanded.

"With pleasure, mistress," Joan replied, her movements swift and efficient. The cooking proceeded with silent vigor.

"Now, Joan," Catherine continued, chopping vegetables with rhythmic precision, "those who help me cook and serve the meat shall also help me eat it. That is a rule."

"There are worse laws in Holland than that," Joan laughed. "Your will is my pleasure, mistress. Besides, my Luke has his supper in the air; he is digging today by good luck."

Margaret descended the stairs, fresh-faced and glowing. "Eh, woman, that is an ugly trade," Catherine observed, snapping her fingers toward Rotterdam. "There she has just washed her face and given her hair a turn, and now who is like her? Give us a buss, hussy! Mind, Eli will not wait supper for the duke. Loiter not after your church is over."

Joan and Catherine followed Margaret to the door, standing there and watching her walk down the street until she disappeared around a corner. Among homely housewives, such a departure was nearly an event in itself. Catherine turned to Joan, her expression thoughtful. "There, Joan, it is almost as if I had just sent off my own daughter to church and stood watching her go, something I have done countless times. Joan, lass, she will not hear a word against our Gerard; and since he is alive, he has treated her cruelly. That is why my bowels yearn for the poor wench. I am older and wiser than she; and so, I shall wed her to that simple Luke, and there will be an end to it. What is one grandchild?"
