Poor Harry Foker would later swear that the Richmond dinner he threw was the single most wasteful thing he’d ever dropped a guineas on, worse than that time he bet on a tortoise race at a country fair. Sitting glumly at the head of the table, staring at melting ices and cigar ash sprinkled over half-eaten pineapple, he had a full-on epiphany: when the hell did I ever think these people were fun? He could see Rougemont’s crow’s feet peeking out under her makeup, which was slathered on so thick you could scrape a chunk off with a dinner knife like a pantomime clown’s greasepaint. Calverley’s overdone street slang was so cringey it made his teeth hurt, and old Lord Colchicum? The doddering peer had rolled up in his brougham, coronet gleaming on the door, and wedged himself tight between French acrobat Mademoiselle Coralie and her mum like he’d won a raffle for the spot. “I’m not stuck up,” Foker thought to himself, stirring his melted ice with a fork, “but an English lord getting all cozy with a Franconi’s horse rider? Nah mate, that won’t do.”

“Twopence halfpenny for your thoughts, Fokey!” Rougemont purred, popping her cigar out of her bright vermilion lips.
“Does Foker ever have thoughts?” drawled Poyntz, snickering. “Got a fair capitalist over this end of the table ready to pay good money for whatever’s bouncing around that empty little head of yours, old chap.”
Calverley leaned over to her neighbor, sneering: “Ugh, what’s Poyntz even on about? He’s such a drawling, sneering little creep.”
“Mon dieu, what a droll tiny man that little Fokare is,” Coralie trilled in her thick Gascon accent, dark eyes glinting. “He doesn’t look a day over twenty.”
The ancient, purple-faced Lord Colchicum sighed, leaning into his giant goblet of claret. “I wish I was his age.”
“Bah, youth? I couldn’t care less,” Coralie’s mum Madame Brack huffed, pinching a huge helping of snuff straight out of Colchicum’s fancy gold snuff box. “I only like grown men, like milor here. Coralie, you only like grown men too, don’t you, my little pet?”
My lord grinned. “You flatter me, madam.”
“Shut up mum, you’re being an idiot,” Coralie shrugged, which made Colchicum snort and pocket his snuff box fast before Madame Brack’s grubby fingers could dive in for a second helping.

We don’t need to drag you through the rest of that raucous, unedifying dinner chat – rest assured not every ballet girl is as daft as Calverley, same as not every peer is as sad and sloshed as Viscount Colchicum. We’ve all met old rich blokes who’d rather hang around rowdy teens than their equally stuffy peers, passing down all their “precious” life lessons and bad habits, and plenty of supposedly respectable gents who don’t mind a wild party as long as no one sees them doing it. Our old mate Major Pendennis, for one, would never say no to a fun little gathering as long as it was kept hush-hush and folks like Lord Steyne or Colchicum were on the guest list. “Let the lads have their fun,” he’d tell Pen over and over, puffing his cigar. “I’m no prude, I’m an old man of the world, and I know young lads will be young lads. Though I reckon the fancy ones get to keep sowing their wild oats till they hit seventy, no harm done.”

That night Foker drove his guests back to Brompton in his drag, so gloomy and quiet he didn’t even crack one of his usual dumb jokes. When the girls invited him in for a post-dinner drink, he turned them down so sadly they assumed he’d had a falling out with his dad. He ignored Calverley yelling from her balcony like a modern day Jezebel begging him to plan another party, sent the carriage home with a groom, and wandered off by himself, hands in his pockets, staring up at the moon. He traipsed all the way to Grosvenor Place to gawk up at the windows he thought belonged to Blanche Amory, sighing and moaning so dramatically a passing copper reported a weird little bloke in a fancy suit was loitering around Sir Francis Clavering’s house, perving through the windows.

That was the start of Foker’s full-on, chaos-fueled Blanche hunt across all of London. If Lady Clavering had a box at the French play (which Foker barely understood a word of), he’d be front and center in the stalls. He paid his valet Anatole to befriend Sir Francis Clavering’s footman and sneak a peek at their social calendar, and suddenly he started showing up at every boring fancy party he’d previously called a total waste of time. He badgered his mum Lady Agnes to beg for party cards, lying straight to her face that he was “seeing the world to better himself”, and went to the French plays supposedly to “perfect his accent” – when Anatole knew full well his master was sneaking off every morning to a secret dance academy in Brewer Street, practicing waltzes like a schoolboy hiding a candy stash.

Major Pendennis never missed church on a Sunday – he thought it was a gentleman’s social duty to be seen there, same as stopping by the club for a brandy after lunch. One Sunday he dragged Pen along with him, having already spotted the posters for a charity sermon at his local parish that would have forced him to drop a few quid into the collection plate. “We’ll cut across the park to St. Benedict’s,” he told Pen, “then we’ll pop over to the Claverings’ and invite ourselves for lunch. Lady Clavering loves having people over, she’s the most hospitable woman in London.”
“I met them at dinner at Lady Agnes Foker’s last week,” Pen laughed. “She’s lovely, but I think you were right about Blanche Amory – that girl is the biggest flirt in the whole of London. She spent the whole dinner chatting Harry Foker’s ear off even though I escorted her in.”
“Pfft, everyone knows Harry’s engaged to his cousin Lady Ann Milton – that was a master move by Lady Rosherville, by the way,” the Major grinned. “Old Foker’s health is terrible, he had a fit last year at your place, Pen. Harry’s going to pull in at least fourteen thousand a year off the brewery alone, plus all that Norfolk property. I’m not proud, Pen, I like a good birth same as anyone, but a brewery that spits out fourteen grand a year? That’s the sort of man you stick to when you’re making your way in the world, mark my words.”
“Harry’s already my mate,” Pen said. “He pops by my chambers all the time, invites me to dinner. All he ever talks about is Blanche Amory, he’s properly smitten.”
“His family will never let that happen,” the Major huffed. “These rich families arrange marriages like state business. Lady Agnes was forced to marry old Foker by her dad even though she was mad for her cousin who died at Albuera – the bloke even saved her from drowning in a lake once! But she did what she was told. Old Foker’s a parvenu, he’s obsessed with climbing the social ladder, Harry will do exactly what his dad says. I’ve seen a hundred of these silly young love affairs. They kick and scream and throw a fit, but they eventually listen to reason.”
“I was half in love with Blanche once, years ago,” Pen admitted, laughing. “But my mum didn’t like her, and it fizzled out.”
“Blanche would not be a bad catch, laddie,” the Major said, side-eyeing him. “I’ve got it on good authority she’s got way more than the ten thousand quid everyone thinks. Clever girl, pretty, sharp as a tack. I wouldn’t be surprised if she’s got thirty, forty, even fifty grand tucked away.”
Pen laughed. “You know everyone’s secret business, don’t you?”
“Nah, I don’t tell all the things I know,” the Major winked. “Though if you end up marrying her, I won’t be a bit surprised. Oh look, here’s St. Benedict’s – everyone says Mr. Oriel the preacher is absolutely brilliant.”

The bells were tolling, carriages full of fancy bonnets and feathered hats were pulling up, and the two of them trundled into the church. Pen felt a little weird that they’d spent the whole walk gossiping about people’s finances and arranged marriages right up to the church door, but the Major didn’t notice a thing – his wig was perfectly combed, his neckcloth tied so tight he could barely turn his head, and he spent the whole service pretending to read his prayer book while he side-eyed every single other person in the pews. Halfway through the service Pen spotted Harry Foker tucked away in the back near the servants’ seats, staring so hard at the front pews his prayer book was upside down. Following his gaze, Pen saw exactly what he was gawking at: two bonnets, one yellow, one pink, belonging to Lady Clavering and Blanche Amory. Looks like Foker wasn’t the only one who’d snuck their romantic drama right into the church pews.

When the service let out, Foker was the first one out the door, loitering by the steps too nervous to leave until Lady Clavering’s barouche pulled up. The two ladies stepped out to find Pen, the Major, and Foker all clustered in the sun, Foker sucking on the crook of his walking stick like a lost toddler. The ever-generous Lady Clavering invited all three straight back to their house for lunch, no questions asked.
Blanche was extra sweet to Pen, grinning: “O do come, I’m dying to talk to you about *Walter Lorraine*! The review in the *Pall Mall Gazette* this morning was so lovely, I cried reading bits of it!”
Foker blinked at them, confused. “Walter Lorraine? That little bloke died of measles when we were at Grey Friars, I remember his mum coming to pick him up.”
Pen cackled, hooking his arm through Foker’s. “I wrote a novel, you dope. I forgot you don’t read the Sunday papers.”
“Nah, I read Bell’s Life every week, mate,” Foker said, referring to the horse racing paper.

After lunch Blanche gushed over Pen’s book nonstop, cooing about the poetry bits and the fancy duke characters, making eyes at Pen so hard even the Major noticed. The old man, who’d read a grand total of six pages of Pen’s novel, nodded along pretending to be deeply moved. When Blanche found out the fancy duke had originally just been a regular baron before the publisher made Pen bump everyone’s rank to sell more copies, she laughed and called Pen a wicked little satirist, all the while batting her long lashes at him like she was still the shy little country girl he’d known years ago.

Foker meanwhile sat there quietly gnawing on his cane, wishing he was a clever literary bloke like Pen instead of the guy who read horse racing news all day. The Major, who’d slipped Blanche the tea that Foker was already engaged to his cousin earlier at lunch, fully expected her to start ignoring Harry immediately – but nope, she got even nicer to him, gushing over his mom, his park pony, the ruby-eyed monkey head buttons on his waistcoat until Foker was blushing so hard his ears turned pink.
Then she turned to Pen, grinning: “Poor Mr. Foker looks so sad! We heard all about that wild party he threw at Richmond the other day, you naughty thing! I’d love to go to Richmond, or Greenwich, or anywhere fun for a dinner party. I’ve never even met an actress, I adore talented people!”

That’s when the Major jumped in, gallant as anything, much to Pen’s total surprise. “Well why don’t we three bachelors take you two ladies out for a fancy dinner at Greenwich? Lady Clavering is always having us over to eat, it’s high time we returned the favor! I’m old, I’ll volunteer the two young lads – Pen’s got a pocket full of money from his book, and Harry’s got more than enough to cover the bill too. Harry, how’s your lovely cousin Lady Ann doing? Lady Clavering, be my guest for the night, and Blanche can be Arthur’s escort, if she’ll have him.”

Blanche shrieked with delight, Lady Clavering happily agreed, and they booked a fancy dinner at the Greenwich hotel right away. The day was a total success: Foker got hours of alone time chatting to Blanche on the hotel balcony, and Pen and the Major turned up in a borrowed brougham – the Major proudly declaring he was an old soldier who’d learned to travel in style, conveniently letting the two young men split the entire dinner bill between them. On the ride back home he spent the whole journey teasing Pen about Blanche being obviously mad about him, and winking again that she was secretly way richer than anyone expected. Pen just shook his head, wondering how on earth his uncle kept track of every single person’s hidden fortune in London.
