I cannot say where my soul wandered during that strange night when I lost consciousness. Whatever visions it beheld, or wherever it journeyed in its trance, it carried no word back to memory—no whisper, no clue. It remained a silent mystery, sealed from imagination as if by an unbreakable vow. Perhaps it soared upward, catching sight of the eternal home I longed for, hoping at last to be released from this weary body, believing the bond between spirit and flesh had finally dissolved. But then—perhaps—an angel appeared, warning me away from heaven’s threshold. With gentle yet firm hands, it guided me weeping back down, binding me once more, trembling and reluctant, to this frail frame—cold, wasted, and now more than ever a burden.

I returned to myself with pain, with resistance, with a moan and a shiver that ran through every limb. The reunion of spirit and body was not a meeting, but a struggle—a wrenching, painful reattachment. When sight returned, it came red, as though swimming in blood; hearing rushed back like thunder; awareness awakened in terror. I sat up, stunned, wondering what world I had woken into, among what unfamiliar beings. At first, nothing made sense: a wall was not a wall, a lamp not a lamp. I might have seen a ghost as clearly as any ordinary object—everything seemed spectral, unreal.

But slowly, faculties settled. The machinery of life resumed its rhythm. Still, I knew not where I was. Only gradually did I realize I had been moved from the spot where I fell. No longer on the portico steps, I was now indoors—sheltered from the storm by walls, windows, and ceiling. I had been carried somewhere—into a house—but which one?

My thoughts turned instinctively to the pensionnat in the Rue Fossette. Half-dreaming, I tried to place myself—was I in the great dormitory? One of the small ones? Yet nothing matched. There were no empty white beds, no long line of tall windows. “Surely,” I thought, “they haven’t brought me to Madame Beck’s own room?” Then my gaze fell upon an easy-chair covered in blue damask. Slowly, other pieces emerged—cushioned seats to match, a wood fire glowing brightly on a clear hearth, a carpet patterned with bright blue arabesques over a soft fawn ground. Pale walls bore a delicate garland of azure forget-me-nots entwined with golden leaves and tendrils. Between two windows hung a gilded mirror, flanked by curtains of blue damask.

In the mirror, I saw myself—not lying in bed, but stretched upon a sofa. I looked pale, spectral. My eyes seemed larger, darker, hollowed out by fatigue; my hair stood out stark against my ashen face. From the furnishings, the layout of doors and windows, it was clear this was a room unknown to me—unknown, even, in name or location.

Yet more puzzling still was the way my mind refused to settle. As I stared at the blue chair, it began to feel familiar. So did a scroll-shaped couch, a round centre-table draped with a blue cloth bordered in autumn-colored foliage. And above all, two little footstools with embroidered covers, and a small ebony-framed chair whose seat and back were stitched with vivid flowers on a dark background.

Struck by these details, I searched further. And there they were—old friends, scattered throughout the room. "Auld lang syne," the past whispered from every corner. On the mantel, two oval miniatures—faces I knew by heart: the pearls around high powdered heads, the velvet ruffs framing white throats, the full muslin kerchiefs, the lace ruffles on sleeves. On the shelf beside them, two china vases, remnants of a tiny tea-service, smooth as enamel, thin as eggshell, and a white alabaster group under glass—each flaw, each crack, I could have named like a seer.

And then—the hand screens. Drawings in pencil, finished like engravings. I gasped. These were mine—my own hands had traced those lines, stroke by stroke, years ago, when I was a girl, using a clumsy, finicky pencil now reduced to bones.

Where was I? Not just in space, but in time? For all these things belonged to another age, another country. Ten years had passed since I last saw them. Since I was fourteen, I had never set eyes on them again. I breathed aloud, “Where am I?”

Then a figure stirred—unnoticed until now. A woman, plain and unremarkable, dressed in a simple bonne’s cap and print dress. She spoke neither French nor English, her dialect incomprehensible to me. But she bathed my temples with cool, scented water, raised the cushion beneath my head, gestured for silence, and returned to her post at the foot of the sofa.

She knitted quietly. Her eyes averted, I studied her. How had she come here? What did she do among the relics of my childhood? Why did these scenes—these days—return to haunt me now?

Too weak to pursue the mystery, I dismissed it as a dream, a fever-fit. But I knew it wasn’t sleep. I believed myself sane. I wished the light were dimmer, so I wouldn’t see the pictures, the ornaments, the screens, the worked chair so clearly. But they were real—every detail exactly as I remembered—just as they had been in the drawing-room of my godmother’s house at Bretton. Only the proportions were different. That room had changed shape, but not its soul.

I thought of Bedreddin Hassan, carried in sleep from Cairo to Damascus. Had some unseen power lifted me from the church steps, borne me across land and sea, and laid me gently before the hearth of Old England? But no—this hearth was cold. Its fire burned no longer. The household gods had long since left.

The bonne rose again, seeing my eyes open. She put down her knitting, busied herself at a small stand, poured water, measured drops from a phial. She approached with a glass. What dark draught was this? Some elixir of the sages? Some magic potion?

It was too late to ask. I drank it passively, instantly. A tide of calm washed over my mind—soft, soothing, warmer than balm. The ache of weakness faded. My limbs grew heavy, but without desire to move. That was no loss. The bonne placed a screen between me and the lamp. I saw her rise—but not return. In that gap, I slipped into sleep.

When I woke, everything had changed. Daylight filled the room—not warm summer sun, but the dull, gray light of a raw autumn morning. I felt certain now: I was in the pensionnat. The rain beat against the windowpane. Wind howled through trees beyond—proof of a garden. The chill, the emptiness, the whiteness—it confirmed it. I say *whiteness*—for dimity curtains framed my view, drawn before a French bed.

I lifted them. Expecting the long, whitewashed chamber, I blinked instead at a small cabinet—walls of sea-green, a single high lattice shaded with muslin festoons. Instead of five wide windows, one alone. Instead of twenty painted stands with basins and ewers, a toilette-table dressed like a lady for a ball—white robe over pink skirt, polished mirror crowned, a pin-cushion frilled with lace. A low green-and-white chintz armchair, a washstand with marble top and pale-green ware—these furnished the tiny room.

Reader—I was alarmed. Why? You may wonder. What could frighten one in such a neat, even pretty chamber? This: those objects could not be real. They must be ghosts. Or—if that were too wild—then I must be ill, delirious. And even then, this was the most extraordinary delusion ever visited upon a mind.

Yet I knew them. The green chintz of the chair, the carved black frame of the mirror, the milky green of the china, the cracked marble top—each detail I recognized, as I had recognized the rosewood and porcelain of the drawing-room.

Bretton! Bretton! Ten years ago flashed in that mirror. Why did Bretton and my fourteenth year return to me now? Why only the furniture, not the rooms, not the place? That pincushion—crimson satin, gold beads, thread-lace—was mine. I had made it. I rose, took it in my hand. There, in gold, the cipher “L. L. B.”—Louisa Lucy Bretton. My godmother’s initials.

“Am I in England? Am I at Bretton?” I murmured. Hastily, I pulled up the blind. I expected St. Ann’s Street, the towers of the minster. Or perhaps a street in Villette. Instead, I saw through a frame of leafy branches—a grassy meadow, a lawn terrace, forest trees rising from lower ground. Tall beeches, groaning under October’s gale. Yellow leaves drifted in heaps, swept by the west wind. Beyond, flat land, hidden by the trees. Secluded. Strange. Unknown.

I lay down again. My bed stood in a small alcove. Turning my face to the wall, I hoped to shut out the confusion. But no—on the green space between the looped-up curtains, a broad gilded frame held a portrait. Watercolor, sketch-like, yet well done. A boy’s face—fresh, alive, animated. Sixteen, fair-skinned, rosy-cheeked, long hair with a golden sheen, piercing eyes, a playful mouth, a gay smile. A pleasant face—especially for those who loved him. Parents, sisters. A romantic schoolgirl might have fallen for it in its frame.

Those eyes—would they one day flash with love? I couldn’t tell. But his lips hinted at caprice, at fleeting regard, even as they smiled.

Quietly, I whispered to myself:

“Ah—this picture used to hang in the breakfast-room, too high, as I thought. I remember climbing a music-stool to unhook it, holding it in my hand, staring into those hazel eyes, their lashes like a pencilled laugh. I liked to study the blush of his cheek, the curve of his mouth.” Even now, I doubted fancy could improve on that chin, that lip. And yet—why did beauty so charm and so wound?

Once, I brought little Missy Home, lifted her in my arms. “Do you like it, Polly?” I asked. She said nothing, but her eyes darkened, trembled. “Put me down,” she whispered. So I did. “The child feels it too,” I thought.

Now, recalling all this, I added: “He had faults. But never a finer nature—liberal, kind, sensitive.” And aloud, I said: “Graham!”

“Graham!” echoed a voice at my bedside. “Do you want Graham?”

I turned. The mystery deepened. To see the portrait was strange enough. But to turn and find the living man—no, not the man, but his mother—tall, well-dressed, in widow’s silk, her braided hair beneath a modest cap—was astonishing. Hers was a good face—stronger now, perhaps, less soft, but full of character. She was unchanged—only sterner, more robust. But it was Mrs. Bretton. My godmother.

I kept silent. Inside, I trembled. My pulse fluttered. My cheeks grew cold.

“Madam,” I said, “where am I?”

“In a safe asylum,” she replied. “Well protected. Rest easy until you’re stronger—you look ill.”

“I’m so confused,” I said. “I don’t know if I can trust my senses. But you speak English?”

“I should think you could hear that,” she answered. “I’d find it hard to talk French for long.”

“You’re not from England?”

“I’ve just arrived. Have you been long in this country? You seem to know my son.”

“Do I? Perhaps. Your son—the portrait?”

“That’s his youth. You called his name.”

“Graham Bretton?”

She nodded.

“I’m speaking to Mrs. Bretton, formerly of Bretton, ——shire?”

“Quite right. And you—your pupils tell me you teach English in a foreign school here. My son recognized you.”

“How was I found? By whom?”

“My son will tell you later. But now you’re too weak. Try to eat something, then sleep.”

Despite everything—exhaustion, shock, exposure—the fever seemed to ease. For nine days I’d eaten nothing solid, suffered constant thirst. Now, when breakfast was offered, I felt hunger. I tasted the tea eagerly, ate the dry toast she gave me. It was little, but enough. Later, the bonne brought broth and a biscuit.

As evening fell, the wind still howled, the rain poured. I grew tired of the bed. The room, though pleasant, felt cramped. I longed for warmth, for firelight. And I thought of her son. When would I see him? Not till I left this room.

At last, the bonne came to make my bed. She prepared to wrap me in a blanket and carry me to the chintz chair. But I refused. I dressed myself.

Just as I sat down to rest, Mrs. Bretton returned.

“Dressed!” she exclaimed, smiling—her familiar smile, not soft, but pleasant. “You’re better then? Stronger?”

She spoke as she always had—as if I were still the child she once watched over. That same quiet authority, born not of wealth or rank, but of strength and natural care—like a tree sheltering a herb. I spoke without hesitation.

“May I go downstairs, madam? I’m cold and restless.”

“If you’re strong enough,” she said. “Come then. Here’s an arm.” She offered it. I took it. We descended a carpeted stair to a landing. A tall door stood open, leading into the blue-damask room.

How warm and welcoming it was—the amber glow of the lamp, the crimson flush of the fire. Tea waited—English tea. The silver urn, the massive pot, the thin porcelain cups with purple and gold—familiar to me. The seed-cake, baked in its peculiar mould, sat on the table. Graham liked it. It was placed before his plate, with silver knife and fork beside it. He was expected. He might even be here already.

“Sit down,” she said, as I faltered near the hearth. She seated me on the sofa. But I moved behind it, saying the fire was too hot. In the shade, I found a seat more to my liking.

Mrs. Bretton never made a fuss. She brewed tea, picked up the newspaper. I watched her—her movements young, alert. Though over fifty, she seemed untouched by age. Portly, yes—but quick. Serene, yet sometimes impetuous. Health and temperament kept her vibrant.

As she read, I sensed she listened—for her son. She never confessed worry, but the storm raged on. If Graham were out in that wind… I knew her heart would follow him.

“Ten minutes late,” she said, checking her watch. Then, a glance toward the door—her brow cleared. Soon, even my ear caught the clang of a gate, footsteps on gravel, the doorbell.

He was here. His mother filled the teapot, drew closer the blue chair—her own seat, but one he claimed without shame.

He came up the stairs—damp, wind-tossed, and strode straight in.

“Is it you, Graham?” she asked, hiding a smile.

“Who else?” he replied, claiming the chair with irreverent ease.

“Don’t you deserve cold tea for being late?”

“The urn sings cheerfully. I’ll get no deserts.”

“Wheel yourself to the table, lazy boy. Only my chair suits you. If you had any sense of propriety, you’d always leave it for the Old Lady.”

“So I should. But the dear Old Lady insists on leaving it for me. How is your patient, mamma?”

“Will she come forward and speak for herself?” Mrs. Bretton asked, turning to me.

I stepped forward. Graham rose courteously to greet me. He stood tall, a figure worthy of his mother’s pride.

“So you’re down,” he said. “You must be much better. I didn’t expect to meet you here—or thus. I was alarmed last night. If I hadn’t been called to a dying patient, I wouldn’t have left you. But my mother is something of a doctor, and Martha is an excellent nurse. I saw it was fainting—nothing dangerous. But I’ll learn what caused it soon. For now—do you really feel better?”

“Much better,” I said calmly. “Much better, thank you, Dr. John.”
