When consciousness returned to me, the earth beneath me was damp, and the trail leading back to the living had turned into shifting quicksand. Above, the ancient river swelled, pushing against its banks with a rising roar. Soon it would plunge over the precipice, splitting into two furious streams—one to reclaim the orchard valley, the other to sweep away the monster horde—while carving a lonely island between them for the Evil Wood. With urgent resolve, I turned back toward those who had sent me.

Reaching the edge of the cliff, I threaded carefully through the tangled boughs, driven by a desperate longing to visit Mara's cottage one last time before surrendering to sleep. If the floods had risen, I needed to know where the paths remained safe. Yet, when I arrived, the scene was frozen in a terrible stillness. The door hung ajar, revealing the untouched loaf of bread and pitcher of water on the table. A profound silence, heavy and absolute, filled the space within and without. I stepped forward, calling out, but the echo died instantly, leaving me alone with the hollow room.

A short distance further, I found a gray-haired man sitting on the sand, his shoulders shaking with silent sobs.

"What troubles you, sir?" I asked gently. "Have you been cast aside?"

"I weep," he choked out, "because the House of Death will not take me. I have gone to the Mistress, and despite my age, she refuses me entry." He looked up, eyes wet. "Intercede for me, if you know her."

"Nay, sir," I replied softly. "That power is beyond me. She receives no one unless it is lawful for them to enter."

"How do you know this?" he pressed, his voice trembling. "You have never sought the end yourself! You are far too young!"

"I suspect," I said, "that if you were young again, you would feel the same pull to stay. Life clings fiercely to the youthful."

"Perhaps!" he admitted, wiping his eyes. "But surely you cannot mean..."

"You may lack the desire to depart, but you possess the fierce desire to remain," I interrupted. "Therefore, I journey now to test if she will eventually welcome me. You seek death because you have forgotten how to live; she will not open her door to you, for no soul dies who has not first learned to love life."

"It becomes no youth to mock a lonely elder!" he protested. "Stop your riddles!"

"Did not the Mother speak to you of this?" I asked.

"In truth, she did," he murmured, looking away. "But I paid little heed to her gentle excuses."

"Then, sir," I said, "it is clear you have not yet learned the art of dying, and I mourn for you. Had I not met the Lady of Sorrow, I might have shared your despair. Though I am young, I have wept many tears. Let me offer this counsel: Seek the Lady of Sorrow. Take whatever she gives you with both hands. Her cottage yonder stands open; bread and water await on her table. Sit, eat, drink, and wait for her return. Ask her counsel, for her wisdom is vast and true."

Fresh tears welled in his eyes as I turned away, leaving him weeping. Whether he listened to my words mattered less than knowing Mara would likely find him.

By the time I reached the dwelling of the monsters, twilight had deepened into a bruised purple, and the moon had not yet risen. The place stood silent as a tomb until I crossed the threshold, after which the air erupted with the tumult of many waters and a great, wailing cry rang behind me. I did not turn.

Before I reached the House of Death, the cold became biting, a sharp, crystalline chill that merged with the encroaching darkness. Together, they seeped into my bones, freezing my blood. Yet, a single candle burning in Eve's window cut through the gloom, guiding my steps and keeping the frost and shadows from consuming my spirit.

The door stood wide open, and inside, the cottage lay empty and sterile. I sank onto the floor, utterly disconsolate. A crushing loneliness settled over me, deeper than any isolation I had known in my wandering. Thousands were near, yet not one was truly with me. Though they lived and I had perished, a chasm of sorts existed between us. Their lives and my stasis separated us; they were vibrant, but I was not yet dead enough to join their company. Doubt gnawed at me. They were distant, and I had no helper to bridge the gap and lay me beside them.

Never before had I known true desolation. I scolded myself, telling myself the solitude was merely an illusion, but the truth was stark: I was awake, and they slept—that was the divide. They lay so still, silent, waiting. And soon, soon, I would be with them!

In my grief, I dropped Adam's old spade. Its dull thud on the clay floor echoed strangely, reverberating from the chamber beyond, triggering a childish terror. I sat staring at the coffin door. *Father Adam, Mother Eve, Sister Mara,* I thought desperately. *They will come soon. Then I will welcome the cold world and the white neighbors.* For a fleeting moment, the fear receded, replaced by a surge of love for the dead.

Suddenly, movement stirred in the Chamber of the Dead. From within emerged a sound like a dim, far-off hum, vibrating through the air rather than striking the ear. My soul leaped within me. Was it merely a tremor in the stagnant air, too faint for physical hearing but quivering in the spirit? I *knew* it, without hearing, without sensation.

Something was coming. It drew nearer. Within my vast desert of sorrow, a fragile infant hope bloomed. The noiseless vibration reached the coffin door, transformed into sound, and struck my ear.

With a low, soft groan, the hinges began to turn. The door creaked open. I stopped listening, my gaze fixed, expectant.

Just a crack appeared, and a face emerged. It was Lona's. Her eyes were shut, yet her countenance seemed to regard me directly. Her skin was as pale as Eve's and as fair as Mara's, yet it lacked their radiant glow. She spoke, and her voice sounded like a sleepy wind drifting through tall grass.

"Are you coming, King?" she whispered. "I cannot rest until you are with me, gliding down the river to the Great Sea and the Beautiful Dream-Land. The sleepiness is full of lovely things: come and see them."

"Oh, my darling!" I cried, the words tearing from my throat. "If I had only known—I thought you were dead!"

She collapsed into my arms, cold as ice frozen to marble. Her limbs, so white and feeble, wrapped around me, and she sighed.

"Carry me back to my bed, King," she murmured. "I wish to sleep."

I bore her to the death-chamber, clutching her tightly lest she dissolve from my grasp. Blind to observation, I carried her straight to her couch.

"Lie me down," she said, her breath cool against my cheek. "Cover me from the warm air; it hurts—a little. Your bed is there, next to mine. I shall see you when I wake."

Her breathing slowed instantly, marking the onset of sleep. I flung myself onto my own couch, feeling blessed as no man has ever felt on the eve of a wedding.

"Come, sweet cold," I whispered to the shadows, "still my heart swiftly."

Instead, a glimmer of light illuminated the chamber, and I saw Father Adam approaching. He held no candle, yet I perceived him clearly. Beside Lona's couch, he looked down upon her with a gentle, questioning smile, then turned to greet me across the room.

"We climbed to the summit of the hill to watch the waters on their journey," he said. "They will descend into the monsters' den tonight. But why did you not wait for our return?"

"My child could not sleep," I answered, gesturing to the empty space beside me.

"She is fast asleep!" he rejoined.

"Yes, now!" I said. "But she was awake when I laid her down."

"She was asleep all along!" he insisted firmly. "She was perhaps dreaming of you—and thus came to you?"

"She did."

"And did you not notice her eyes were closed?"

"Now that I recall," I admitted, "I did."

"If you had looked before laying her down, you would have seen her already resting on the couch."

"That would have been terrible!"

"You would simply have found she was no longer in your arms."

"That would have been worse!"

"It is a thought worth considering, though seeing it would not have troubled you."

"Dear Father," I said, my voice thick with emotion, "why am I not sleepy? I thought I would drift off like the Little Ones the moment I closed my eyes!"

"Your hour has not yet fully arrived. You must nourish yourself before sleep claims you."

"Oh, I should not have lain down without your permission! I cannot sleep without your aid! I will rise immediately!"

Yet, I found my body immovable, weighed down by a heaviness I could not shake.

"There is no need," Adam said kindly. "We will attend to you here. Do you not feel the cold?"
