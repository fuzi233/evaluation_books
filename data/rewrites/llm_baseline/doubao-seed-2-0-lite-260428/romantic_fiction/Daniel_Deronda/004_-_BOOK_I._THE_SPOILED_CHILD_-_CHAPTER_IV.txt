*Gorgibus. — I tell you marriage is a holy, sacred thing, and it is only proper for honest folk to begin their lives together by it.
Madelon. — Good heavens! If everyone thought like you, a romance would be over in five minutes! What a dull world it would be, if Cyrus married Mandane right off the bat, and Aronce tied the knot with Clélie before the first page was half-read! * Let us spin out the thread of our story at our leisure, and don’t rush us to the ending so soon.*
— Molière, *Les Précieuses Ridicules*

No one could fairly blame the rector of Pennicote for, in the course of weighing every side of his niece’s prospects, concluding that Gwendolen Harleth was exactly the sort of girl destined for a brilliant, envied marriage. Why should he be expected to stray far from the common good sense of his day, and wish his charming niece a worse outcome for her maidenhood than the whole of polite society agreed was the very best a girl could hope for? It speaks well of him, in fact, that every part of that hope was rooted in pure, unselfish good nature. He was far too rational to chase silly, fairy-tale fantasies: he would never have recommended Gwendolen dress in rags like Patient Griselda to draw a marquis’s eye, or hidden her away from all company on the off-chance some wandering noble would stumble across her. Mr. Gascoigne had no interest in contriving silly near-accidents to force a rich man to rescue her, either. He simply wanted what was best for her: to make sure Gwendolen was seen to her full advantage in every drawing room and social gathering across their corner of the county.

Her uncle’s plans aligned perfectly with Gwendolen’s own quiet wishes — though do not mistake her intentions. She did not ride her horse with that effortless, spellbinding grace, or practice her music and French, for the explicit end of landing a grand husband. She would, if pressed, admit that marriage was in her distant future, and she felt, without ever stopping to argue the point, that her union would never be the dull, middling sort that most girls settled for. But her ambitions never centered on marriage as their final fulfillment. The dramatic daydreams where she cast herself as the leading lady never wrapped up with a wedding. To be courted desperately, to be the object of hopeless, reverent sighs from half a dozen suitors, that was the sweet, necessary proof of her power over other people. But to become a wife, bound hand and foot by all the tedious domestic rules of that state, struck her on the whole as a vexing, unavoidable chore. All the married women she had observed seemed trapped in a dreary existence: no freedom to do as they pleased, stuck with far more children than they cared for, growing dull and plodding and swallowed whole by endless humdrum routine. Of course marriage was a step up the social ladder, and she had no interest in spending her life as a poor, unmarried spinster — but promotions often came with bitter side effects. A peerage could never give a man the thrill of leading a movement he had dreamed of, after all, and this slight, sylphlike twenty-year-old had no intention of being led. That hunger for power and admiration burns in feminine breasts too, and in Gwendolen’s it nestled gently among all the soft, lady-like trappings of her upbringing: she had no grand designs to advance scholarship or reshape government, no illusions her wits could move the world like Archimedes’ lever. She only wanted to live in a way that was striking, that made everyone around her gasp in admiration, so she could feel that bright, burning rush of being truly alive, reflected in every other person’s awe.

“Gwendolen will never rest until she has the whole world at her feet,” her meek little governess Miss Merry once said, and the words were hardly hyperbolic for their quiet little country circle: after all, everyone who lives in a small genteel suburb knows what it means to have “the world at your feet” — it is just six or seven people fawning over you, all your little world’s most flattering attention gathered in your palms. That hazy, grand prospect hung like a soft golden glow around Gwendolen in the height of her young, self-assured exultation. Everyone else around her let themselves be dragged about like empty ships at the mercy of the tide, their lives directed by anyone stronger than them. Not her. She would never be sacrificed to people far less clever and far less worthy than she was. She would take every chance life handed her, and conquer every circumstance, all by her own extraordinary wits.

Their new home at Offendene, with Lady Brackenshaw’s occasional notice, the local archery club, and the rare dinner invitation to the wealthy Arrowpoints’ estate, hardly seemed like a setting that would grant her grand opportunities — but Gwendolen’s confidence never depended on her surroundings. She was certain she had every tool she needed to master her own fate. She agreed that her education had left her with no disadvantages: in the schoolroom, her sharp, quick mind had soaked up all the firm, unyielding starch of unexplained rules and disconnected facts that keeps ignorance from ever feeling flimsy or unconfident. All the rest of the world’s stored knowledge, she was sure, she already knew well enough from the novels, plays, and poems she devoured. She had no doubts about her two core lady’s accomplishments: her French was fluent, her piano playing was graceful and impressive. Add to that the natural, unshakable sense of capability some lucky people are born with, the kind that makes them certain they can form a perfect judgment on any subject the second they turn their attention to it, and who could blame Gwendolen for feeling fully ready to steer her own destiny? There were dozens of subjects she found boring, of course — most subjects feel dull to the bright young, the same way bright light feels harsh to the old — but she never felt helpless to hold her own in conversation about any of them.

No one in her life had ever disputed her superiority, or her right to have things her way. From the very first day they moved into Offendene, everyone around her asked first: what will Gwendolen think? If the footman’s heavy boots creaked too loud down the hall, the maid would murmur that the racket would never do for Miss Harleth. If the laundry came back wrinkled and ill-done, it was not seen as an inconvenience for the rest of the family — it was unacceptable for Gwendolen. If the bedroom fireplace smoked so bad it stung Mrs. Davilow’s already weak eyes, her mother apologized to Gwendolen first for the discomfort. If they were traveling and Gwendolen came down to breakfast an hour late, long after everyone else had finished their meal, the whole household scrambled to make sure her coffee was still scalding hot, and her toast still perfectly crisp. When she stepped into the room then, her light brown hair freshly brushed and streaming down her back, waiting for her mother to twist it up into its usual style, her large brown eyes bright as a wave-polished onyx under their long dark lashes, she was always the one to be tolerant: chiding Alice not to hunch her shoulders in that frightful way, sending Isabel off to sit with Miss Merry instead of pestering her with a hundred silly little questions.

She was the exiled princess, who even in times of famine deserved a breakfast roll made of the finest flour milled from seven thin meager ears of wheat, who if the whole household had to flee in a hurry, would have her silver fork kept safe and separate from all the messy, jumbled baggage. The reason for this little domestic reign seems obvious, at first glance: her beauty, that rare, unplaceable charm about her, that steady will that hummed through every graceful movement of hers, every clear, unhesitating tone of her voice. On a rainy, gray day when everyone else in the house felt limp and listless, when no one could see the point of keeping up any small formality, all Gwendolen had to do was step into the room, and suddenly there was a bright, compelling reason to keep life beautiful and ordered. Even the waiters at inns lit up with extra energy when she walked in, hurrying to wipe away crumbs, smooth out tablecloth creases, dump out the half-empty glasses of dregs with drowning flies in them. Add that magic to the fact that she was the eldest daughter, and her mother had spent years feeling apologetic to her over all the misfortunes that cruel stepfathers had dragged the family into, and it would seem you did not need any other explanation for her little domestic empire — like explaining daylight by pointing at the sun, it seemed too simple to argue with.

But do not jump to conclusions before you compare. The narrator has seen that same fawning, trembling, apologetic deference given to people who were not beautiful, not unusual at all, whose will showed itself in harsh, graceless tones, who were not eldest daughters with soft, timid mothers wracked with guilt. Some of them were very ordinary, unremarkable men. The only trait all of them shared, the one common thread, was an unshakable determination to get whatever pleased them, and a total, unafraid willingness to make themselves thoroughly unpleasant and difficult if they did not get their way. What household full of soft, tender-hearted women will not fawn over the unscrupulous man who will go off and do far worse things if he does not get his way at home? So it seems perfectly possible that Gwendolen, even without her rare charm, even without her mother’s gentle guilt, could still have ruled as that exiled princess — if she only held onto that fierce, unapologetic egoistic desire, that quiet power to make everyone around her fear what she might say, or what she might do. But of course she had the charm, too, and the people who were a little afraid of her loved her just as fiercely. Both the fear and the fondness were heightened by the shimmering, iridescent quality of her character: that play of conflicting, opposing traits inside her. Macbeth’s famous line about being unable to be two opposite things at the same time only applies to the rough, clumsy work of physical action, not the far subtler workings of human feeling. You cannot speak a loyal word and stay cruelly silent in the same breath, you cannot kill a man and not kill him in the same moment — but a single heartbeat is wide enough to hold both a loyal impulse and a selfish one, a flash of murderous rage and the sharp, quick tug of immediate repentance.
