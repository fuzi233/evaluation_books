The sun was already pouring through my window when I woke, a bright, mocking signal that the day had begun without me. Sitting up, I listened to the roaring streets, a chaotic symphony confirming my isolation. Reaching for my watch, I found it missing from its usual spot on the rickety dressing table. Looking around, I discovered my clothes scattered across the floor like debris from a storm. One boot sat lonely on the chair by the fire; the other was nowhere to be seen.

During the night, my head seemed to have expanded significantly. I wondered idly if I hadn't accidentally donned Minikin's hat earlier—if so, I'd trade it back instantly. The current vessel was top-heavy and throbbing with a headache of epic proportions.

Suddenly, the memory of the previous night crashed over me, shaking me awake. From a nearby steeple, chimes rang out. Counting carefully, I realized it was eleven o'clock. I sprang out of bed and immediately collapsed onto the floor.

Recalling how I had clung to the bedpost while the room waltzed wildly around me, I felt the vertigo persist. It wasn't spinning anymore, just staggering feebly, exhausted from an all-night orgy. Crawling to the washstand, I managed, after a couple of clumsy attempts, to dip my head into the basin. Pulling on my trousers with difficulty and collapsing into an armchair, I tried to review the situation, working backward from the present misery.

"I am feeling terribly ill," I muttered to myself. "Something disagrees with me."

"That strong cigar," I whispered, blaming the obvious culprit. "I should never have touched it. And then that tiny room packed with people. Plus, I've been working myself to death. I really need more exercise."

It gave me a twisted sense of pride to realize that, despite being a shuffler and a coward, I wasn't easily duped.

"Nonsense," I told myself harshly. "Stop lying to yourself. You were drunk."

"Not drunk!" I pleaded. "Don't use that crude word. Some folks can't handle sweet champagne; it affects the liver. Let's call it a liver issue, please."

"Drunk," I insisted relentlessly. "Hopelessly, vulgarly drunk—drunk like any 'Arry after a Bank Holiday."

"It's the first time," I murmured hopefully.

"It was your first *opportunity*," my conscience replied coldly.

"Never again," I promised.

"The standard cliché," my conscience countered.

"How old are you?"

"Nineteen."

"So you haven't even the excuse of youth. How do you know it won't consume you? That you won't spiral downward until you're a confirmed sot?"

My heavy head fell into my hands, and I groaned. Memories of temperance tales read on Sunday afternoons flooded back. In my imagination, I saw myself rushing toward a drunkard's grave, sometimes fighting heroically, other times weakly surrendering as the craving tightened its grip. In the foggy air of my mind, I saw my father's pale face and my mother's sorrowful eyes. I thought of Barbara, with the potential for scorn dancing around her bewitching lips, and Hal, radiating immense contempt for all weakness. The shame of today and the terror of tomorrow tore at my mind.

"It shall never happen again!" I shouted. "By God, it shall!" (At nineteen, passion is a powerful engine.) "I will leave this house immediately," I declared to the empty room. "I will escape this poisonous atmosphere. I will erase it from my mind and everything connected to it. I will make a fresh start. I will—"

Then, a vague figure emerged from the back of my brain and stepped into the foreground: Miss Rosina Sellars. What was she doing here? What right did she have to block my path to redemption?

"The legal rights of your fiancée," my inner voice explained with a grim smile.

"Did I really go that far?"

"We won't discuss the details," I replied, avoiding the topic. "That was the outcome."

"I was... I wasn't quite myself. I didn't know what I was doing."

"As a rule, we aren't aware when we do foolish things, but we must pay the price anyway. Unfortunately, it happened in front of witnesses, and she isn't the type to be easily dismissed. You're marrying her and settling down in two cramped rooms. Her family becomes your family. You'll learn to know them quickly. Among them, she is known as 'the lady,' which tells you everything about them. A lovely start to your career, isn't it, my ambitious young friend? A wonderful mess you've made!"

"What am I supposed to do?" I asked.

"Honestly," I answered, "I don't know."

I spent a miserable day hiding in my room, locking the door to avoid facing Mrs. Peedles or even the maid. By dusk, feeling slightly less terrible—or perhaps just numb—I sneaked out for a simple meal: unsweetened tea and a kippered herring at a nearby coffee house. When another gentleman sat opposite me to order hot buttered toast, I fled hastily.

At eight p.m., Minikin stopped by the office to ask what had happened. Seeking refuge in shame, I confessed the truth.

"Figured as much," he said. "Looks like an A1 job considering your condition."

"I'm glad it's over, now that it is," I told him. "It'll be a lesson I never forget."

"I know," Minikin said. "Nothing beats a fair-and-square drunk for making you feel genuinely good; better than a sermon."

Desperate for advice, I leaned on Minikin, knowing he was far more worldly-wise than I, despite being younger.

"That's not the worst part," I confided. "What do you think I've done?"

"Killed a policeman?" Minikin suggested.

"Got myself engaged."

"No one like you quiet types for going wild when you finally start," Minikin commented. "Nice girl?"

"I don't know," I admitted. "All I know is I don't want her. How do I get out of this?"

Minikin removed his left eye and began polishing it on his handkerchief—a habit he adopted whenever uncertain. Gazing into it, he seemed to draw inspiration.

"A 'take-her-at-her-word' type?"

I indicated he had correctly identified Miss Rosina Sellars.

"Do you know how much she earns?"

"She knows I live in this attic and cook my own meals," I replied.

Minikin glanced around the room. "Must be fond of you."

"She thinks I'm clever and that I'll make my way," I explained.

"And she's willing to wait?"

I nodded.

"Well, I'd let her wait," Minikin advised, putting his eye back in place. "Plenty of time ahead of you."

"But she's a barmaid! She expects me to walk with her, take her out on Sundays, visit her friends. I can't do that. Besides, she's right: I intend to succeed. Then she'll cling to me. It's awful!"

"How did it happen?" Minikin asked.

"I don't know," I sighed. "I didn't realize I'd done it until it was finished."

"Anyone watching?"

"About six of them," I groaned.

The door swung open, and Jarman entered without knocking. Skipping his usual boisterous greeting, he walked silently over and shook my hand gravely.

"Friend of yours?" he asked, gesturing to Minikin.

I introduced them.

"Pleased to meet you," Jarman said.

"Glad to hear it," Minikin replied. "Though you don't look like someone with much else to be proud of."

"Don't mind him," I explained to Jarman. "He was born that way."

"Wonderful gift," Jarman responded. "Do you know what I'd do if I had it?" He didn't wait for an answer. "'Ire myself out to break up evening parties. Ever thought of it seriously?"

Minikin said he would consider the idea.

"Make your fortune patrolling the suburbs," Jarman assured him. "Pity you weren't here last night; might have spared our young friend a world of trouble. Has he told you the news?"

I explained I had already briefed Minikin.

"Now that you have a good, steady eye," Jarman said, fixing his gaze on Minikin's glass orb, "how does he look?"

"As well as can be expected under the circumstances," Minikin answered.

"Does he know the circumstances? Has he seen the girl?" Jarman pressed.
