For several days, Tito saw little of Romola. The silence between them was heavy, broken only by the quiet rustle of packing cloths and the distant murmur of strangers moving through the house. The next morning, he approached her with gentle caution. "It would be better," he said softly, "if you moved your personal effects from the library. Agents are arriving to pack the antiquities."

He leaned forward to press a kiss to her brow, suggesting she retreat to her own chamber, where the small painted tabernacle stood—a sanctuary away from the clamor of unfamiliar footsteps. Romola assented quietly, offering no sign of emotion. The night before had been a sleepless vigil for her; though her frame remained strong, sensation had dulled into a continuous, throbbing ache, as if she had been stunned and bruised by invisible blows. Tito sensed her illness but dared not speak of it. Seeing her hand and brow turned to ice, he merely fetched a fur-lined mantle and draped it lightly over her shoulders.

In every brief interval he returned, the scene repeated itself: he attempted to soothe her with unobtrusive acts of tenderness, words of affection, but she seemed to have lost the capacity to speak or even meet his gaze.

"Patience!" he murmured to himself. "She will recover, and forgive at last. The bond between us must endure."

There is a peculiar dynamic when the struck one remains silent and composed; the striker often slides effortlessly into the role of the aggrieved, feeling no sting of his own violence while acutely aware of his own benevolence. Yet, Tito was not naturally prone to such self-pity. His nature bent toward reconciliation; he would have endured much just to feel Romola's hand rest once more upon his head, as it had that morning when he first recoiled from looking at her. Still, waiting held less difficulty for him because his life outside the walls had grown increasingly captivating.

A course of action, born of deep-seated character, often traces back to a single spark. Since the moment in the Piazza del Duomo, when Tito, perched atop bales, had tasted the sharp thrill of pleasing crowds with clever phrases, his imagination had drifted toward political activity. The turbulent life of Florence seemed ripe for engagement. However, a fresh terror of Baldassarre had previously blocked this path, driving him to sell the library as a precaution against fleeing the city. Now, that dread had receded. He knew he must still wear his armor, preparing his wit and composure for future demands, but he no longer felt compelled to abandon Florence for foreign fortunes.

His father had rejected the offer of atonement, forcing Tito into open defiance. An elderly man, displaced and memory-fogged, was too weak to challenge him. Tito's latent desires were crystallizing into explicit plans. As the fiery heat of youthful passion cooled, life began to resemble a game for him—one blending skill and chance. The game unfolding in Florence promised to be swift and thrilling: a struggle of revolution and faction, filled with covert maneuvers where brilliant ingenuity could discard inconvenient beliefs, save perhaps the notion that ginger burns the tongue, and claim the high road of wisdom.

The departure of the French guests left Florence vibrating with anxiety, like a colony of ants sensing a looming threat. *"How shall we raise funds for the King of France?"* the whispers ran. *"How do we handle the stubborn Pisan rebels? Most urgently, how do we reform our government to ensure fair election of magistrates and voting on laws?"* Until these questions found answers, commerce threatened to stall, and the vast body of workers, denied citizenship and the vote, risked turning impatient.

Something had to be done. First, the great bell rang, summoning the citizens to a parliament in the Piazza de' Signori. When the crowd pressed tight, hemmed in by armed guards at every exit, the Signoria—the Gonfaloniere and the eight current Priors—stepped onto the platform before the Old Palace, beside the stone lion. They proposed granting dictatorial authority for one year to twenty leading men of the city, empowering them to select all magistrates and restructure the government. The people roared their approval, feeling themselves the electors of these Twenty. Such a "parliament" was an ancient Florentine custom, designed to make the will of the few appear as the choice of the many.

While the shouting in the square died down quickly, the debates within the palace raged on. Was Florence to adopt a Great Council, modeled after Venice, where all officials and laws were voted upon by a broad base of qualified citizens, regardless of rank or party? Or would governance follow a narrower, less populist scheme, preserving the hereditary dominance of noble families against the interference of shopkeepers?

Doctors of law argued day and night, extending far into the darkness. Messer Pagolantonio Soderini presented compelling arguments for the popular model; Messer Guidantonio Vespucci countered with equally sound reasoning for an aristocratic form. It was a debate over whether to boil or roast meat, a choice already prejudged by the tastes of the disputants. Their eloquence might have continued indefinitely without altering the outcome, serving only to delay the inevitable. Inside the palace, the majority holding power sided with Vespucci, favoring moderation. Outside, the masses, aware of their powerlessness and grievances, feared nothing so much as stagnation.

Yet, a force was gathering outside the palace walls, shaping the vague desires of the multitude into a determined will. That force was the preaching of Savonarola. Driven by both a spiritual compulsion to guide the people and the pragmatic needs of politicians who required his support to pass measures, Fra Girolamo rapidly shifted his sermons from the general to the specific. He moved from urging citizens to sacrifice private passions for the public good, to prescribing exactly what form of government would achieve that end. From *"Choose whatever is best for all"* evolved the command: *"Choose the Great Council,"* followed by the assertion, *"The Great Council is the will of God."*

To Savonarola, these were identical truths. The Great Council was the only viable mechanism to express a public will large enough to counteract the corrupting influence of partisan interests; it was the sole path toward honest, impartial public service. As Florence's government purified itself, becoming immune to schemes based on the moral degradation of others, the Florentine people would approach the state of a pure community, worthy of leading the renewal of the Church and the world. Fra Girolamo's mind never rested until it reached this sublime horizon. He harbored no private malice, sought no petty gratification. Even in his final, terrible days, when shame, torture, and the fear thereof stripped away every hidden weakness of his soul, he could tell his relentless judges: *"Do not wonder if it seems to you that I have told but few things; for my purposes were few and great."*
