Gerard, shaken by the peril of the night before, rose before dawn and awoke Martin. The old soldier stirred with surprise—hadn’t he thought Gerard had slipped away through the window? When asked how best to flee the country and escape capture, Martin offered only one safe route. “I’ll guide you through the great forest to a bridle-path I know,” he said. “It will lead you quickly to an inn where they’ll lend you a swift horse. Then a single day’s ride will carry you beyond the borders of Holland. But we must go now—before the villagers rise.”

Peter’s house stood just a furlong and a half from the edge of the woods. They set out at once: Martin with his bow and three arrows—Thursday being the day of the week when such arms were lawful—and Gerard with nothing but a sturdy oak staff that Peter had given him for the journey. Margaret gathered up her kirtle and farthingale, for the ground was wet and muddy. Peter walked with them as far as the hedge of his garden, and there, with more emotion than he usually showed in passing moments, he gave the young man his blessing.

The sun crested the horizon as they crossed the stony field toward the forest. Halfway across, Margaret, glancing back nervously, suddenly screamed and broke into a run toward the trees, shrieking all the while. Behind them, Ghysbrecht and his men were closing fast.

Resistance was madness. Martin and Gerard followed Margaret’s lead. The pursuers gained ground, but Martin kept shouting, “Only win the wood! Only win the wood!” Their head start gave them hope—the great trees seemed to stretch their branches like welcoming arms, their leaves forming a sheltering screen.

But a new danger emerged. The fiery burgomaster flung himself onto his mule, spurred it into a gallop, and charged not only his own men but the fugitives directly. His aim was to cut them off. He rode in a wide arc and reached the edge of the wood just ahead of Gerard. The others could escape—he cared not.

Margaret screamed and clutched at Gerard in terror, but he shook her off without hesitation. In his fury, Ghysbrecht forgot that hunted beasts sometimes turn on their hunters—and two men, driven by hatred, can each desire death for the same enemy.

Instead of dodging, as the burgomaster expected, Gerard lunged forward with a wild cry, swinging the oak staff with all his strength. It struck Ghysbrecht’s face with a terrible crack. The burgomaster collapsed beneath his mule’s tail, kicking wildly, blood streaming down his face, his collar splattered with gore.

In the next breath, the three were inside the forest. A cry of horror and rage erupted from Ghysbrecht’s men—proof that the fight had just begun.

“Why run?” panted Gerard. “You have your bow, and I have this,” he said, raising the bloody staff.

“Boy!” roared Martin. “The gallows! Follow me!”

They fled deeper into the woods. Soon came the sound of baying—a pack of hounds unleashed upon their trail. The pursuers had entered the forest and saw the fugitives darting between the trees.

Margaret gasped and stumbled. Gerard clenched his teeth, gripping his staff tightly. Moments later, they reached a thick hazel coppice. Martin plunged into it, pushing aside the young saplings as if they were wheat. Within fifty yards, they found four blind paths. Martin chose one.

“Bend low,” he ordered.

They crept forward, half-crawling, sliding along the narrow trails. Soon, another tangle of winding paths crossed theirs. They took one. It seemed to loop back—but then turned, leading them into a dense pine grove where the ground was firm and hard beneath their feet.

Here, no paths existed. Young fir trees grew so closely together that visibility was limited to mere yards. After walking some distance, Martin sat down. Having long since learned to dismiss danger once it passed, he pulled bread and ham from his wallet and began eating calmly.

The younger pair stared at him in disbelief.

“What?” he said, seeing their expressions. “All Sevenbergen couldn’t find you here. You’ll lose your purse, Gerard, long before Italy. Is that how you carry treasure?”

Gerard looked down—and there, caught in the chain of his wallet buckle, was a large triangular purse. “This isn’t mine,” he said. “What could be in it?”

He tried to free it, but the passage through the coppice had tangled it irreparably. “It seems unwilling to leave,” he muttered, finally cutting it loose with his knife.

Inside, the purse held silver coins of every size—but its bulk came mostly from folded brown paper. A sudden understanding lit Gerard’s eyes.

“Ah! It must be that old thief’s purse—and stuffed with paper to fool people!”

They puzzled over how it came to be on Gerard’s belt. At last, they realized: it had been tied to Ghysbrecht’s saddlebow. As Gerard charged, he had torn it away—killing his foe and stealing his goods in one motion.

Gerard smiled. “A fine feat!” he declared. “Spoil taken in battle from an enemy—lawfully won.”

But Margaret trembled. “Throw it away, Gerard—or give it back to Martin. They already call you a thief. I cannot bear it.”

“Throw it away? Give it back? Not a stiver!” Gerard insisted. “This is plunder won in combat. Isn’t it, Martin?”

“Of course,” Martin replied. “Send him back the paper—that you may. But the purse or the coin? That would be sin.”

“Oh, Gerard,” she whispered, “you are going far. We need Heaven’s favor. How can we hope for grace if we keep what is not ours?”

Gerard saw it differently. “Heaven gives it to me by miracle. I shall cherish it.” He smiled. “Like the Israelites, we took from Egypt and were blessed.”

“Then take your own way,” Margaret murmured. “You are wiser than I. You are my husband… is it for me to argue?”

Her words—so humble, so tender—surprised Martin briefly. Later, he would recall them with less astonishment.

Gerard kissed her gently, grateful for her submission, and they continued hand in hand, Martin leading them deeper into the vast forest. With each step, they felt safer. Few townspeople dared venture so far into the trackless wilderness.

Yet soon, Gerard’s conscience stirred. “Martin,” he said, “I wish I hadn’t struck quite so hard.”

“Whom? Oh, let that pass. He got what he deserved.”

“I saw his grey hairs—when my staff fell. I fear I shall not forget them.”

Martin grunted. “Who spares a badger for grey hairs? The older an enemy, the craftier. And the craftier, the better reason to end him.”

“Killing? Killing, Martin? Don’t speak of it!” Gerard shuddered.

“You’re mistaken if you think you didn’t,” Martin said cheerfully. “Now, Heaven forbid—but the old rogue’s skull cracked like a walnut. Aha!”

“Heaven and the saints forbid!” Gerard cried.

“He rolled off his mule like a stone shot from a cart,” Martin grinned. “I said to myself, ‘One wiped out.’”

Gerard dropped to his knees and began praying for his enemy’s life.

Martin lost patience. “Here’s nonsense! You who claim learning—don’t you know a wise man never strikes unless to kill? And why fuss about killing an old man? If it were a young one—full of wine, women, and plunder—then perhaps. But an old fellow near the grave? Why not push him in? He must go—today or tomorrow. What better fate for a greybeard? If ever I live as long as Ghysbrecht did, and must ride a mule instead of my own legs, with a back like this”—he tapped his bow—“instead of this”—he tapped the string—“I’d thank any young man who knocked me on the head, just as you did that old shopkeeper. Malison on his memory!”

“Oh, culpa mea! Culpa mea!” Gerard cried, smiting his chest.

“Look!” Martin sneered at Margaret. “Still a priest at heart. When he’s not in a rage, he’s St. Paul—what a milksop!”

“Tush, Martin!” Margaret reproached him softly. Then she wrapped her arms around Gerard and comforted him with the quiet wisdom and warmth only a woman could offer.

“Sweetheart,” she murmured, “you did not go out of your way to harm him. He hunted you to death. You fled. He pursued. Then you struck—but only once, in self-defense, with what you had. Malice would have drawn a knife or struck again. Men have been hit many times with staves and lived. If he fell, it was by his own malice, not yours—and by God’s will.”

“Bless you, Margaret,” Gerard breathed. “Bless you for thinking so.”

“Yes, beloved,” she said, “but if you’ve killed that wicked man, we must flee faster. Oh, let us go.”

“No, Margaret,” Gerard replied. “I fear not man’s vengeance—thanks to Martin and these thick woods. But I fear Him whose eye sees through the forest and reads the heart. If I struck only in defense—well. But if in hate—He may send the avenger of blood after me—to Italy, or to the ends of the earth.”

“Hush!” Martin snapped. “I can’t hear for your talk.”

“What is it?” Gerard asked.

“Do you hear nothing, Margaret? My ears grow old.”

She listened—and then heard it: a single, clear tone, deep and resonant, like a bell struck once in the stillness. She told Martin.

“Nay, I heard it,” he said.

“So did I,” Gerard added. “It was beautiful. Ah! There it is again. How sweet it blends with the air. It’s far off. Is it ahead?”

“No, no!” Martin corrected. “The echoes here confuse a stranger. It comes from the pine grove.”

“The one we passed?”

“Of course.”

“Martin, is this nothing?” Gerard asked, noticing the old soldier’s pale face.

“Wonderful,” Martin said, with a sickly sneer. “He asks if it’s anything. Come, on! Let us reach a better place.”

“A better place—for what?”

“To stand at bay, Gerard,” Martin said gravely. “And die like soldiers—killing three for one.”

“What’s that sound?” Gerard asked.

“It is the avenger of blood.”

“Oh, Martin, save him!” Margaret cried. “Oh, Heaven, be merciful! What new peril is this?”

“Girl,” Martin said flatly, “it’s a bloodhound.”
