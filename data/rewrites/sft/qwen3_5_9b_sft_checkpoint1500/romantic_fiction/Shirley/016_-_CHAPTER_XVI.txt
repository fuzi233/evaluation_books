The charity fund flouried. Through Miss Keeldar's inspiring example, the tireless efforts of the three rectors, and the steady, silent support of their spinster aides—Mary Ann Ainley and Margaret Hall—a substantial sum was secured. Managed wisely, these resources immediately alleviated the suffering of the unemployed poor, bringing a palpable calm to the neighborhood. For two weeks, no cloth had been burned, no mill or mansion desecrated. Shirley felt confident the storm was passing; with the arrival of summer, trade would inevitably rebound, ending this weary struggle. Peace, she believed, would soon spark a new era of commerce.

Yet, whenever she encountered Gérard Moore, her tenant, she sensed a discord beneath his quiet listening. She implored him with impatient glances for an explanation, some depth to his thoughts. He would smile, his expression lending a peculiar sweetness to his mouth while his brow remained grave, and speak of his faith in the war's end. "I trust in the finite nature of this conflict," he would say, anchoring his hopes there. "I work Hollow's Mill entirely on speculation. I sell nothing; there is no market. I manufacture for a future day. Three months ago, I was ruined, having exhausted both credit and capital. It was only through your intervention, that loan from you, that I saved myself. On the strength of that gift alone, I continue this bold gamble. Total ruin awaits me if I lose, and gain remains doubtful, yet I remain cheerful. As long as my hands are free to work, I cannot be depressed. One year, nay, six months of this 'olive reign,' and I shall be safe. You are right regarding commerce, but I harbor doubts about the permanence of this tranquility. Eleemosynary relief never truly tranquilizes the working classes; it does not make them grateful. It is not in human nature to be so. Were they in our position, they would feel the same humiliation. Furthermore, why should they be grateful? To you, perhaps to the clergy, but certainly not to us mill-owners. Their hatred for us grows deeper daily. The disaffected here maintain constant correspondence with the agitators of Nottingham, Manchester, and Birmingham. They are disciplined subalterns receiving orders from distant chiefs; no blow is struck without deliberation. Like the sultry skies that threaten thunder day after day only to clear peacefully at night, the danger is merely delayed. The long-threatened storm will surely break eventually. There is an analogy between the moral and physical atmosphere."

"Well, Mr. Moore," the conference invariably concluded, "take care of yourself. If you think I have ever done you any good, reward me by promising to take care of yourself."

"I do," Moore replied, his voice steady. "I will take close and watchful care. I wish to live, not to die. The future opens like Eden before me; and yet, when I look deep into the shades of this paradise, I see a vision that I like better than seraph or cherub gliding across remote vistas."

"Do you? Pray, what vision?"

"I see..."

Before he could finish, the maid bustled in with the tea-tray, interrupting the cryptic exchange.

The early days of May had been fine, the middle wet, but as the moon changed, the skies cleared. A fresh wind swept away the heavy, silver-white rainclouds, carrying them eastward until they vanished behind the horizon, revealing a vault of pure blue, ready for the reign of the summer sun. That sun rose broad and golden on Whitsuntide. The schools prepared for their great assembly with splendid weather.

Whit-Tuesday marked the climax. The two large schoolrooms of Briarfield, recently built by the present rector at considerable personal expense, were scrubbed, whitewashed, repainted, and adorned with flowers and evergreens—gathered from the rectory garden, two cartloads from Fieldhead, and a wheelbarrow-full from the stingier domain of De Walden. Twenty tables, each seating twenty guests, were laid out with white cloths, surrounded by benches. Suspended above them were dozens of cages filled with canaries, a fancy cherished by Mr. Helstone's clerk who delighted in their piercing songs. These tables were not for the twelve hundred scholars, whose feast was to be held outdoors, but for the patrons and teachers.

At one o'clock the troops would arrive; by two they would be marshaled; by four they would parade the parish, followed by the feast and a meeting in the church with music and speeches. Why Briarfield was chosen as the rendezvous was not due to population size—Whinbury far exceeded it—or antiquity, for Nunnely's ancient temple predated it all. It was simply because Mr. Helstone willed it so, and his will proved stronger than the objections of either Boultby or Hall. They dared not dispute his authority.

For Caroline Helstone, this anniversary had traditionally been a trial. It forced her into public view, compelling her to face the wealthy and influential without the protection of her mother, aunt, or any chaperon. Obligated to walk at the head of her regiment as the rector's niece and first teacher, and to serve tea to a mixed multitude of ladies and gentlemen, she trembled at the prospect. She was a nervous soul who mortally feared publicity.

But this year, Shirley was with her. Shirley altered the nature of the trial entirely, transforming it into something akin to enjoyment. Miss Keeldar was better company than a host of ordinary friends; she was self-possessed, spirited, and easy, possessing a social confidence that inspired courage in others. The only worry was Shirley's tendency to linger behind schedule. At the precise moment the church clock tolled two, the bells would clash and the march begin. Caroline realized she must ensure Shirley's punctuality, for if her companion failed to appear, Caroline would be left stranded.

Whit-Tuesday dawned almost before sunrise. Caroline, Fanny, and Eliza spent the morning arranging the rectory parlors in first-class order, setting out a collation of cooling refreshments—wine, fruit, and cakes—upon the dining-room sideboard. Caroline then donned her finest attire: a dress of white muslin, perfect for the solemnity and brilliance of the day. Her new sash, a birthday present from Margaret Hall which she suspected Cyril had purchased in return for a set of cambric bands, was tied by Fanny's dexterous fingers. Her simple bonnet was trimmed to match, and her white crape scarf suited her modest dress. When ready, she formed a picture—not bright enough to dazzle, but fair enough to interest; not striking, yet delicately pleasing. Her sweetness of tint, purity of air, and grace of mien compensated for the lack of rich color. Her brown eyes and clear forehead revealed a mind that was modest, gentle, and pensive, harmonious like a lamb or a dove. Yet, as Cyril Hall noted, she was an imperfect human being, lacking the goodness and greatness of the withered Miss Ainley, who was currently donning her best black gown and Quaker drab shawl in her narrow cottage chamber.

Caroline departed, gliding quickly across secluded fields and hidden lanes toward Fieldhead. No dust or moisture soilled her garment or dampened her sandals. After the recent rains, everything was clean and dry under the glowing sun. She walked fearlessly over daisies and turf, through thick plantations, until she reached Fieldhead and entered Miss Keeldar's dressing room.

If she had not arrived, Shirley would have been hopelessly late. Instead of hurrying, Shirley lay stretched on a couch, absorbed in reading. Mrs. Pryor stood nearby, vainly urging her to rise. Caroline wasted no words; she snatched the book from Shirley's hands and began the arduous business of disrobing and rerobing her. Indolent with the heat and buoyant with youth, Shirley wanted to talk and laugh, to linger, but Caroline, determined on punctuality, persisted in dressing her as fast as fingers could fasten strings or insert pins. Finally, as she united the final row of hooks and eyes, she found leisure to chide Shirley for her unpunctuality and incorrigible carelessness. Shirley did look the picture of that tiresome quality, though.

There was a striking contrast between them. Shirley possessed style in every fold of her dress and every line of her figure. The rich silk suited her better than a simpler costume; the deep embroidered scarf became her, worn negligently yet gracefully. The wreath on her bonnet crowned her well. Every ornament was applied with taste, matching her frank light in her eyes, the rallying smile about her lips, and her straight, lightsome carriage. Caroline took her hand, hurried her downstairs, and out into the fresh air. Thus they sped through the fields, laughing, resembling a snow-white dove and a gem-tinted bird of paradise flying together in social harmony.

Thanks to Miss Helstone's promptitude, they arrived just in time. Before the trees fully concealed the church, they heard the measured, urgent tolling of the bells summoning all to assemble. The trampling of many feet and the murmur of many voices followed. From a rising ground, they saw the Whinbury school approaching on the road, numbering five hundred souls. The rector and curate, Boultby and Donne, led the way.
