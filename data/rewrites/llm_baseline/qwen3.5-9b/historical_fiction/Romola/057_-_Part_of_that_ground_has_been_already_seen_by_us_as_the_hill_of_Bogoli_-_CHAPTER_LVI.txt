The morning sun had grown oppressively warm by the time Romola, having walked along the city walls from San Marco, turned toward the intersecting streets at the gate of Santa Croce. The Borgo La Croce lay in a profound stillness. In the silent sunshine, she listened to the rhythm of her own footsteps until, rounding a bend, she spotted a small child, barely three years old, pausing mid-waddle. Clad only in a white shirt, the boy stopped and looked around.

At first, Romola saw only his back—square, sturdy, topped with a cloud of reddish-brown curls. Then he turned. Dark eyes, wide with tears, met hers; his lower lip trembled as he pushed it upward, and his fat brown fists clutched his shirt in helpless distress. A tall black figure casting a shadow over him seemed to trigger a climax of bewildered fear, and a loud, sobbing cry sent big tears rolling down his cheeks.

Acting on a maternal instinct born of her own passionate tenderness, Romola immediately uncovered her head. Stooping onto the pavement, she wrapped her arms around the child, pressing her cheek against his while speaking in soothing tones. Initially, his sobs grew louder, yet he made no attempt to flee. Soon, the outburst ceased with that strange abruptness characteristic of childhood emotions; his distorted face smoothed out, replaced by an open-mouthed stare fixed upon Romola.

"You have lost yourself, little one," she said, kissing him. "Never mind! We will find the house again. Perhaps Mamma will meet us."

She sensed he had escaped while his mother's attention was elsewhere and believed he would soon be pursued.

"Oh, what a heavy, heavy boy!" she remarked, attempting to lift him. Finding she could not carry him, she added, "Come, then, you must toddle back by my side."

The parted lips remained motionless in awed silence, one brown fist still gripping the shirt with tenacious force, while the other yielded willingly to her wonderful white hand—strong yet soft.

"Do you *have* a Mamma?" Romola asked, setting out and looking down at the boy with a yearning gaze. He offered no reply. A girl might have chattered, but this square-shouldered little man with his large cloud of curls remained mute.

However, he was alert regarding his destination. At the turn near San Ambrogio, he dragged Romola toward it, looking up. "Ah, that is the way home, is it?" she smiled. He merely thrust his head forward and pulled, urging her to hurry. After another turn he insisted upon, Romola found herself in a short street opening onto garden grounds. Here, the little fellow paused, pulling her toward some stone stairs. Evidently unwilling to lose her hand, and equally unwilling for her to leave without ensuring she reached her friends, they ascended. Emerging from the sunlight into dimness, they climbed until a final landing revealed an open doorway spilling extra light. Passing through a small lobby and another open door, Romola halted. No sound greeted her arrival.

In the far corner of the room, opposite the light, sat Tessa, one hand resting on the edge of a cradle, her head drooping slightly as she slept. Near a window, with her back to the door, Monna Lisa worked silently, preparing salad in a deaf, unconscious state. Romola caught only an instant of this still scene before Lillo snatched his hand away and ran to his mother's side. He leaned his head back against her arm, surveying Romola seriously from a distance, making no direct effort to wake her.

When Lillo nudged her, Tessa opened her eyes in bewilderment. But her glance had not yet settled on the figure at the doorway before she sprang up, blushed deeply, and began to tremble, unable to speak or move forward.

"Ah! We have seen each other before," Romola said, smiling and stepping closer. "I am glad it was *your* little boy. He was crying in the street; I suppose he had run away. So we walked together a little way, and then he knew where he was, and brought me here. But you hadn't missed him? That is well, else you would have been frightened."

The shock of discovering Lillo had wandered overwhelmed Tessa's other feelings. Her color drained and returned, and seizing the boy's arm, she ran to Monna Lisa, whispering loudly in the old woman's ear with a half-sob:

"Oh, Lisa, you are wicked! Why stand with your back to the door? Lillo ran away ever so far into the street."

"Holy Mother!" Monna Lisa exclaimed in her meek, thick tone, dropping the spoon from her hands. "Where were *you*, then? I thought you were there, watching him."

"But you *know* I go to sleep when I rock," Tessa retorted pettishly.

"Well, well, we must keep the outer door shut, or tie him up," Monna Lisa advised. "For he'll be as cunning as Satan before long, and that's the holy truth. But how did he come back?"

This question reminded Tessa of Romola's presence. Without answering, she turned toward the stranger, blushing and timid once more, while Monna Lisa's eyes followed her movement. The old woman made a low reverence and declared, "Doubtless the most noble lady brought him back." Advancing slightly, she added, "It is my shame that he was found with only his shirt on; but he kicked and refused his other clothes this morning. The mother, poor thing, will never allow him to be beaten. But what can an old woman do without a stick when the lad's legs grow so strong? Let your nobleness look at his legs."

Conscious of the scrutiny, Lillo pulled his shirt up higher, gazing down at his olive-round legs with a dispassionate curiosity. Romola laughed, stooping to give him a caressing shake and a kiss. This gesture aided the reassurance Tessa had already gleaned from Monna Lisa's respectful address. Earlier, when Naldo had recounted the Carnival incident and Tessa asked who the heavenly lady—who appeared just when needed and vanished so quickly—might be, Naldo had replied, "Not exactly, my Tessa; only one of the saints," refusing to elaborate. Thus, in Tessa's dreamlike synthesis of small experiences, Romola became vaguely associated with church paintings. Upon reappearing, the memory of her protection was tinged with religious awe, though not deeply, for Tessa feared mostly ugly and evil spirits. It seemed unlikely that good beings would anger or punish her, unlike Nofri and the devil. Now that Monna Lisa spoke freely of Lillo's legs and Romola laughed, Tessa felt at ease.

"Ninna is in the cradle," she said. "*She's* pretty too."

Romola approached the sleeping infant, and Monna Lisa, one of the exceptionally meek deaf women who rarely expected to be addressed, returned to her salad.

"Ah! She is waking; she has opened her blue eyes," Romola noted. "You must take her up, and I will sit in this chair—may I?—and nurse Lillo. Come, Lillo!"

Taking Tito's chair, Romola extended her arms toward the boy, whose eyes had tracked her. He hesitated, pointing small fingers at her with a mix of puzzlement and anger. "That's Babbo's chair," he said, unaware of how to explain the difficulty if Babbo arrived and found Romola occupying his seat.

"But Babbo is not here, and I shall go soon. Come, let me nurse you as he does," Romola said, wondering internally for the first time what kind of father this was—whose wife dressed in peasant fashion yet possessed a certain daintiness indicating idleness and plenty. Lillo consented to be lifted. Finding the lap comfortable, he began exploring her dress and hands, searching for ornaments besides the rosary.

Tessa, previously occupied coaxing the waking Ninna out of her peevishness, sat down in her low chair near Romola's knee, arranging the baby's tiny form. Jealous that the strange lady seemed to favor the boy as much as Naldo did, she murmured, "Lillo was going to be angry with me because I sat in Babbo's chair. Will he come soon and want it?"

"Ah, no!" Tessa replied. "You can sit in it a long while. I shall be sorry when you go. When you first came to take care of me at the Carnival, I thought it wonderful; you came and went away so fast. And Naldo said, perhaps you were a saint, and that made me tremble a little, though the saints are very good, I know; and you were good to me, and now you have taken care of Lillo. Perhaps you will always come and take care of me. That was how Naldo did a long while ago; he came and took care of me when I was frightened, one San Giovanni. I couldn't think where he came from—he was so beautiful and good. And so are you," Tessa concluded, looking up at Romola with devout admiration.

"Naldo is your husband. His eyes are like Lillo's," Romola observed, noting the boy's unusually dark eyebrows at his young age. She spoke not interrogatively, but with a quiet certainty of inference that was necessarily mysterious to Tessa.
