It was Christmas Eve in Cloisterham. The streets held a peculiar cast of characters: a scattering of strangers, alongside others whose faces were half-familiar, once the children of the town, now grown men and women returning from distant lands only to discover the city had shrunk perceptibly since their childhood, as if it had failed to wash itself clean in the intervening years. For these returnees, the tolling of the Cathedral clock and the raucous cawing of rooks from the tower sounded like echoes from their nursery days. Some, in their final moments far away, had dreamed their floor was strewn with autumn leaves from the elms in the Close; similarly, the rustling sounds and fresh scents of their earliest memories surged forth as the circle of their lives drew tight between beginning and end.

The season's tokens were everywhere. Red berries glowed in the lattice work of Minor Canon Corner; Mr. and Mrs. Tope delicately pressed sprigs of holly into the carved stalls of the Cathedral, as though stuffing them into the coat-buttons of the Dean and Chapter. The shops overflowed with lavish abundance, particularly with currants, raisins, spices, candied peel, and moist sugar. An air of gallantry and dissipation hung heavy in the air, exemplified by a massive bunch of mistletoe in a greengrocer's doorway and a pitifully small Twelfth Night cake topped with a Harlequin—so meager it seemed more like a Twenty-fourth or Forty-eighth cake—being raffled for a shilling per ticket. Amusement abounded: the Waxworks, which had once captivated the Emperor of China, was open for special viewing during Christmas Week at the premises of a bankrupt livery-stable keeper; and a new grand comic pantomime was announced at the Theatre, heralded by a poster of Signor Jacksonini the clown greeting "tomorrow" with a visage almost as miserable as the performance promised.

Cloisterham was alive and bustling, though the High School and Miss Twinkleton's Academy remained conspicuously absent from this festive scene. The scholars of the High School had departed for home, each enamored of one of Miss Twinkleton's pupils (though the girls knew nothing of it), leaving only the maids fluttering occasionally in the academy's windows. Notably, these damsels appeared far more skittish when entrusted with representing their own sex than when sharing the stage with Miss Twinkleton's students.

Tonight, three individuals were destined to meet at the gatehouse. How each spent the preceding day remained to be seen.

Neville Landless, though excused from his studies by Mr. Crisparkle—who possessed a fresh nature equally sensitive to the charms of a holiday—spent his afternoon in intense concentration, reading and writing in his quiet room until two hours past noon. He then cleared his desk, arranged his books, and methodically tore up and burned every stray paper. He swept away all untidy accumulations, organized his drawers, and destroyed every note or scrap save those directly related to his studies. Turning to his wardrobe, he selected ordinary articles of clothing, including sturdy shoes and socks for walking, packing them into a new knapsack bought earlier that day in the High Street. Simultaneously, he had purchased a heavy walking-stick: robust in its handle for a firm grip and reinforced with iron. He tested its weight, swung it, balanced it, and placed it beside his knapsack on a window seat.

Dressed for the outdoors, Neville stepped out of his room, only to encounter Mr. Crisparkle descending the stairs. Realizing he had forgotten his stick, Neville turned back to retrieve it. Mr. Crisparkle, pausing on the landing, watched him emerge holding the cane. With a smile, he asked how one chose such an implement.

"I confess I don't fully grasp the subject," Neville replied. "I chose it simply for its weight."

"Far too heavy, Neville; *much* too heavy," Mr. Crisparkle corrected.

"To lean upon during a long walk, sir?"

"Lean upon?" Mr. Crisparkle repeated, adopting a pedestrian stance. "One does not lean upon it; one balances with it."

"I shall learn better with practice, sir. I haven't lived in a walking country yet."

"Indeed," Mr. Crisparkle agreed. "A bit of training, and we'll cover dozens of miles together. I wouldn't leave you behind now. Will you return before dinner?"

"I doubt it, as we dine early."

Mr. Crisparkle nodded brightly, offering a cheerful farewell that conveyed absolute confidence and ease.

Neville proceeded to the Nuns' House, requesting that Miss Landless be informed of his arrival by appointment. He waited at the gate, refusing to cross the threshold, adhering strictly to his parole not to obstruct Rosa's path.

His sister proved equally mindful of their mutual obligations and joined him without delay. They greeted each other warmly, avoiding any lingering at the gate, and began walking toward the upper inland countryside.

"I have no intention of treading on forbidden ground, Helena," Neville said as they turned onto a path. "You will understand shortly why I cannot help mentioning—how shall I phrase it?—my infatuation."

"Would it not be wiser to avoid the topic, Neville? You know I cannot hear anything."

"You can hear, my dear, what Mr. Crisparkle has heard, and heard with approval."

"Yes; I can hear quite enough."

"Well, the truth is, I am not only unsettled and unhappy myself, but I am acutely aware of disturbing others. How can I be sure that, but for my unfortunate presence, you, and the rest of that former party—our engaging guardian aside—would not be dining cheerfully in Minor Canon Corner tomorrow? Indeed, it likely would be so. I perceive clearly that I hold low regard in the old lady's estimation, and it is easy to imagine how burdensome a clog I represent to the orderly hospitality of her house—especially at this season, when I must remain apart from this person, when there is such cause for avoiding contact with that individual, and when an unfavorable reputation precedes me regarding another. I have mentioned this gently to Mr. Crisparkle, knowing his self-effacing nature, yet I have spoken. However, I emphasized far more strongly that I am engaged in a miserable internal struggle, and that a brief period of change and absence might allow me to overcome it. Thus, with the weather bright and crisp, I am embarking on a walking expedition, intending to remove myself from everyone's way—including my own, I trust—for tomorrow morning."

"When do you plan to return?"

"In a fortnight."

"And traveling entirely alone?"

"I am far better without company, even if there were anyone besides you to accompany me, my dear Helena."

"Mr. Crisparkle agrees entirely, you say?"

"Completely. Initially, he seemed inclined to view it as a morbid scheme potentially harmful to a brooding mind. However, we took a moonlit walk last Monday to discuss it at leisure. I presented the case as it truly stands: I seek to conquer myself, and once tonight is resolved, it is preferable for me to be away now rather than here. I feared encountering certain people walking together here, which would serve no purpose and hinder my progress. In a fortnight, that likelihood will have passed; and when it inevitably arises again, I can depart anew. Furthermore, I genuinely feel hopeful that vigorous exercise and wholesome fatigue will aid me. You know Mr. Crisparkle values such elements highly in maintaining his own sound mind and body, and his noble spirit is unlikely to uphold different standards for himself than for me. Convinced of my sincerity, he yielded to my perspective. With his full consent, I depart tomorrow morning, early enough to be not only out of the streets but beyond the reach of the church bells when the townsfolk attend service."

Helena considered the proposal and approved of it wholeheartedly. Since Mr. Crisparkle endorsed it, she naturally would too; but she independently deemed it a healthy endeavor, signaling a sincere effort and active pursuit of self-improvement. Though she felt pity for him, venturing alone on such a significant festival, she recognized the necessity of encouraging him.

"Will you write to me?" she asked.

"Every alternate day, detailing all my adventures."

"Will you send clothes ahead?"

"My dear Helena, no. I shall travel like a pilgrim, with wallet and staff. My wallet—or knapsack—is packed and ready for straps; and here is my staff!"

He handed it to her. She echoed Mr. Crisparkle's observation regarding its heaviness and returned it, inquiring about the wood. "Iron-wood," he answered.

Until this point, Neville had been exceedingly cheerful. Perhaps carrying his case with her, presenting it in its finest light, had lifted his spirits. Or perhaps succeeding in this display led to a sudden reversal. As daylight faded and city lights began to twinkle before them, his mood darkened.

"I wish I weren't attending this dinner, Helena," he murmured.

"Dear Neville, is it truly worth worrying about? Consider how swiftly it will pass."

"How swiftly it will pass!" he repeated gloomily. "Yes. Yet I dislike it."

"There may be a moment of awkwardness," she offered cheerfully, "but it will endure only briefly. You are confident in yourself."
