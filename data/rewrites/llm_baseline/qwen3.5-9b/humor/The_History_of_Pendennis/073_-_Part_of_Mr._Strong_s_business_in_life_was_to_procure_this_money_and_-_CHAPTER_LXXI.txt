The dinner was finally served just as Arthur Pendennis stumbled back into the room. Immediately, Lady Rockminster launched into a scolding regarding his tardiness. However, Laura, observing her cousin's ghastly pallor and terrified expression, cut through her aunt's imperious tirade. With a voice trembling with concern, she asked what had transpired. Was Arthur ill?

To mask his agitation, Arthur downed a massive glass of sherry. "I've heard the most extraordinary news," he stammered, glancing nervously at the servants. "I'll explain later." Throughout the meal, he remained visibly shaken, his foot tapping rhythmically against the floorboards.

"Don't trample and beat the ground beneath the table!" Lady Rockminster snapped. "You've crushed Fido's saucer. Look at Mr. Warrington; he keeps his boots perfectly silent."

By dessert, the atmosphere was suffocating. "This dinner has been dreadfully dull," Lady Rockminster declared, sensing the storm brewing behind Arthur's eyes. "Clearly, something has occurred, and you wish to speak with Laura privately. I'm off for my nap. I doubt I'll have tea. Good night, Mr. Warrington. Do come again, but preferably when there isn't such heavy business to discuss." With a toss of her head and immense dignity, the old lady departed, followed by George and the others.

Warrington rose to follow, bidding Laura a farewell while she stared at Arthur with mounting alarm. Then, Arthur stopped him. "Please, stay, George. You must hear this news and offer your counsel. I am at a loss as to how to proceed."

"It concerns Blanche, Arthur," Laura whispered, her heart hammering against her ribs, her cheeks flushing a color she hadn't known since childhood.

"Yes," Pen confirmed, his voice tight. "And it involves the most extraordinary story imaginable. When I left you to visit my uncle's lodgings, I found his ancient servant, Morgan, standing guard at the door. He informed me that he and his master had parted ways that very morning; my uncle had fled to this very hotel. When I arrived, he wasn't dining, so Morgan invited me inside, claiming he possessed information of vital importance. Apparently, the scoundrel has amassed a fortune while serving my uncle—he's now a capitalist, a millionaire, for all I know.

"I entered the house, and what do you think he revealed? This must remain a secret among us, especially now that this villain holds the key. Blanche's father is not dead. He has returned from the grave. The marriage between Clavering and the Begum was never valid."

"And Blanche?" Warrington asked, his tone sharp. "Is she therefore the heir?"

"Perhaps," Pen replied grimly. "But consider the lineage: the child of a convict! Amory is an escaped prisoner. Clavering knows it; my uncle knows it. That fact was used as a weapon *in terrorem* to force the wretched old man to surrender his parliamentary seat to me."

"Blanche doesn't know," Laura interjected softly. "Nor does poor Lady Clavering?"

"No," Pen said. "Blanche believes her father died in New South Wales as a ship's captain, thanks to the lies spun by her nurse, Bonner. In reality, he was transported as a convict. Lady Clavering has confessed to me that their marriage was unhappy and that her husband was a man of bad character. She wept, telling me it was a terrible burden for a woman to admit she was glad to hear of her husband's death, noting that twice in her life she had made a fatal error in judgment.

"Now, what is to be done? If this man resurfaces, he cannot simply demand his wife; death likely awaits him if he reveals himself. Returning to transportation is certain. Yet, this rascal has held the threat of exposure over Clavering for years, extorting money repeatedly."

"It's our friend Colonel Altamont, surely," Warrington deduced, the pieces clicking together. "I see everything now."

"If this rascal returns," Arthur continued, "Morgan, who knows the secret, will leverage it against us all. Possessing this leverage, he intends to blackmail everyone. The damned fool assumed I was already aware of this plot," Pen added, his face draining of color with rage. "He asked me for an annuity to keep quiet, threatening me as if I were profiting from the Begum's misery, and even hinted at extracting a parliamentary seat from that miserable Clavering. Good heavens! Was my uncle truly mad to entangle himself in such a conspiracy? To imagine our mother's son trading on such treachery!"

"I cannot fathom it, dear Arthur," Laura said, seizing his hand and pressing a kiss to it.

"No!" Warrington roared, his deep voice trembling. He looked at the two young lovers with a mixture of profound love and agony. "No. Our boy cannot meddle in such filth. Arthur Pendennis cannot marry the daughter of a convict and serve as a Member of Parliament for the hulks. You must wash your hands of this entire affair, Pen. Break it off immediately. Give no explanation; simply state that family reasons make the union impossible. It is kinder for those poor women to suspect you false to your word than to learn the brutal truth. Furthermore, I can easily secure from that dog Clavering an acknowledgment that your stated reasons are sufficient grounds for ending the engagement. Do you agree with me, Laura?"

He barely dared meet her gaze. He knew he was discarding the last fragile plank of his shattered fortune, allowing the waves of his calamity to sweep him under.

Pen sprang up, staring intently at Warrington. Warrington turned his head away. He saw Laura rise, walk to Pen, and once again take his hand, kissing it gently.

"She agrees too—God bless her!" George exclaimed.

"Her father's shame is not Blanche's fault, dear Arthur," Laura pleaded, her face pale, her words rapid. "Suppose you were married; would you abandon her because she bears no guilt? Are you not bound to her? Would you leave her in her distress? And if she suffers, would you not comfort her? Our mother would have done exactly that, had she been here."

As she spoke, the kind girl wrapped her arms around him, burying her face in his chest.

"Our mother is an angel to God," Pen sobbed. "And you are the dearest, the kindest woman in the world. Teach me my duty. Pray for me that I may fulfill it with a pure heart. God bless you—God bless you, my sister!"

"Amen," Warrington groaned, burying his face in his hands. "She is right," he murmured to himself. "That girl cannot do anything wrong." Indeed, she looked and moved like an angel. Days later, he would recall that radiant smile, the way she tucked back her curls, blushing and smiling, her fond gaze forever fixed on Pen.

"And now," Laura said, placing her small, fair hand on the table, "what next?"

"And now?" George asked.

"And now," Miss Laura smiled, "we shall have some tea."

Before this mundane conclusion could settle, a servant announced that Major Pendennis had returned to the hotel and was awaiting his nephew. Laura, feeling a fresh surge of alarm, cast an imploring look at Pen: *Behave yourself; hold fast to the right; be gentle yet firm.* Taking these unwritten warnings to heart, she excused herself and retired to her chambers.

Warrington, usually indifferent to tea, begrudged the impending cup immensely. Why couldn't the old Major have arrived an hour later? An hour sooner or later mattered little. The hour struck. The inevitable moment arrived to bid farewell. Hands shook, doors closed, and friends departed. As the brief joy faded, leaving him alone, Warrington wandered the streets, wondering, *"In which window of that hotel does her light shine?"* He strode to a neighboring club's smoking room, seeking solace in a cigar. Around him, men brawled loudly about politics, opera singers, horse racing, and the tyranny of committees. Carrying this sacred secret, he joined the fray. They shouted, cracked jokes, laughed at wild tales. It was strange to stand amidst the smoke and noise, realizing that every man present likely harbored a lonely, private secret, isolated in their own minds while participating in the collective chaos.

Meanwhile, Arthur traversed the hotel corridors, his anger boiling within him. He was furious that the elderly gentleman he was about to meet had reduced him to a mere tool, compromising his honor and reputation.

The Major's hand was ice-cold and trembling when Arthur grasped it. He coughed, grumbled over the fire, and complained that Frosch couldn't manage his dressing gown or organize his papers due to the impudence of Morgan. The old man lamented his situation, cursing Morgan's ingratitude with pathetic bitterness.
