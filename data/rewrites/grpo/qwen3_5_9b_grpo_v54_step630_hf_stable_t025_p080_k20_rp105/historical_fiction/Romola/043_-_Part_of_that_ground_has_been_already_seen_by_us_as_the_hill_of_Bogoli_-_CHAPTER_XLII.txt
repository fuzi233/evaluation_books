October 30, 1496. The morning air was crisp with the promise of autumn, yet the Florentines paid it little heed. Their minds were fixed not on the gentle land breeze, but on the violent gales tearing across the Ligurian Sea. These storms had scattered the fleet from Marseilles, ships laden with grain and soldiers, leaving Florence exposed to a dual terror: the gnawing hunger of Pale Famine and the looming threat of invasion.

Across the peninsula, the political landscape was a theater of shifting alliances and predatory ambition. The French King, hailed as a new Charlemagne, had swept through Italy with terrifying ease, conquering Naples before retreating, reportedly shattered by the loss of an infant son. In response, a Holy League had coalesced under the papal banner of Pope Borgia, ostensibly to expel the "barbarians" from Naples. Yet, beneath the pious rhetoric lay a grim reality: a pact among wolves to drive out rival wolves, only to devour the carcass of Florence itself. While Pisa, Venice, Milan, and the German Emperor Maximilian united against the French, Florence stood defiant, clinging to her foreign alliance at the peril of her existence. If the vital port of Leghorn fell, the city, hemmed in by papal hostility and regional jealousy, would be strangled.

The Signoria, guided by the fervent disciples of Savonarola, responded with a mixture of vigor and compassion. They raised funds and levied new troops, yet they refused the traditional cruelty of shutting out the starving masses. Instead, they opened their gates to the destitute, allowing the hungry *contadini* and alien mendicants to flock in like birds fleeing a blizzard. This magnanimity drew sharp criticism from the disaffected, who argued that Florence should join the League to secure peace rather than invite pestilence. Compounding the crisis, Fra Girolamo had been silenced by a papal mandate for over a month. Only when the news of the lost grain ships reached them did the Signoria defy Rome, summoning Savonarola back to the Duomo. Two days prior, he had preached a sermon of such audacious faith that he wagered his own habit on the city's survival, urging the people to wait steadfastly for divine intervention.

Yet, as the sun rose on the thirtieth, no miracle had yet arrived. Desperation hung heavy in the air, accompanied by the specter of pestilence. Hospitals overflowed, and private courtyards served as makeshift infirmaries, yet unsheltered want persisted. Early that morning, the silent procession of death continued; the confraternities bore away the bodies that had collapsed along the roadsides. Amidst this grim tableau moved Romola de' Bardi. Dressed in the austere simplicity of black serge, her head veiled to conceal her hair save for the gleaming bands of gold framing her brow, she walked from the Ponte Vecchio toward the Porta Santa Maria.

Her path was blocked by a bier carried by the brothers of San Jacopo del Popolo, searching for the unburied dead. At the head of the litter, the brethren paused to inspect a form, while a cluster of idle laborers, their faces hollowed by starvation, gathered around.

"He's dead," one declared. "Messere Domeneddio has taken him."

"It would be a mercy for us all to stretch out our legs and walk ahead with our heads," another muttered. "Standing upright against hunger is a burden no man should bear."

"He's an old man. Death has struck a poor bargain; life has won."

"And not a Florentine, ten to one!" cried a voice. "A beggar turned out of Siena! May San Giovanni protect us. They send us armies of starving men to fight us."

"No, no," corrected a brother lifting the bier. "This man is a prisoner from the Stinche. I know the grey patch where the prison badge was sewn."

"Quiet! Help him up. Perhaps he's merely unconscious from lack of food," a brother suggested.

Romola stepped forward, loosening the small flask at her belt. With practiced ease, she used a small ivory implement to pour a few drops of wine into the prostrate man's mouth. The liquid acted as a stimulant; the head twitched, and the old man's eyes fluttered open, meeting hers with a vague, returning consciousness.

In that instant, a shock of absolute recognition struck Romola. The wild, dark eyes, the sallow, deep-lined face, and the long white beard were unmistakable. This was Baldassarre, the escaped prisoner she had glimpsed in the Duomo the day Tito first donned armor—a man whose mere presence had terrified Tito in the sketches of Piero's studio. A cold tremor seized her. Was this the bitter secret she feared? An impulse to flee surged within her, followed immediately by a stronger, imperious need to stay beside the old man, whom her intuition knew her husband had wronged. Her hands trembled, yet the muscle memory of her charitable work guided them; her right hand remained poised to offer more wine, while her left supported his neck.

Baldassarre looked up at her for the first time. The seclusion of his imprisonment and her recent flight had kept them apart, erasing the vivid descriptions of the fair, golden-haired woman he had heard. Now, seeing her, those images dissolved like mist.

"Shall we carry him to the steps of San Stefano?" Romola asked gently. "We shall clear the street, and you may proceed with your bier."

They moved only thirty yards before reaching the church steps. By then, Baldassarre managed to sit up, propping himself against the doorway. The brethren departed, but the crowd of spectators swelled. The atmosphere shifted; knowing the man was alive, the crowd's tone grew less sympathetic, though Romola's presence imposed a hush.

"They fed him daily in the Stinche," someone remarked. "That's why he's so weak without it."

"Gnaffè!" scoffed another. "The Magnificent Eight released these thieves to make room for honest folk. But if every criminal is revived with wine and bread, the Ciompi might as well drink from the Arno while the waters are high."

Romola sat beside Baldassarre, offering him a piece of soft bread from her basket. "Can you eat a little? I must go to the hospital, but I will return if you wait. I will take you to shelter. Do you understand?"

He stared dreamily, repeating the words "come back." His mind was clouded by exhaustion, but she hoped he grasped her intent.

"Do you keep your bread for those who cannot swallow?" a rough voice in a red night-cap interrupted, elbowing his way into the tight circle.

"If anyone isn't hungry," another man replied, "let them mind their own business. He is better off than those with empty stomachs and no breakfast."

"Yes, if a man wishes to die, let him be encouraged. Dead men require no trencher."

"Oh, you misunderstand the Frate's charity," interjected a young man in fine cloth, his face untouched by want. "The Frate preaches to the birds like Saint Anthony, telling hawks they must feed sparrows. Every good citizen is bound to feed six starving beggars from Arezzo or Bologna. Madonna, you are a pious Piagnone; you won't waste your bread on honest citizens who have the Frate's prophecies to digest."

"Come, Madonna," urged the man in the red cap. "The old thief won't eat. Try us. We fast so much we are half-saints."

The circle tightened, the gaunt, starving men crowding Romola until there was no space to breathe. She held out a small horn-cup, moistening a piece of bread with wine, having ignored their taunts until now. Rising, she looked around, her hazel eyes burning with a direct, unyielding gaze. Instinctively, the men nearest her recoiled, as if their proximity were an offense.

"Hunger is hard to bear," Romola said, her voice steady despite her pale, pinched face. "You have the power to take this bread if you wish. It was saved for sick women and children. You are strong men; but if you choose not to suffer because of your strength, you have the power to take everything from the weak. You can take the bread from this basket, but I will watch by this old man. I will resist your taking the bread from *him*."

Silence fell upon the group. The man in the red cap looked foolish, backing away and shoving his neighbor with a gesture of moral condemnation. The retreat was general; everyone pretended to be pushed aside against their will, and the young man in fine cloth vanished.

Just then, the armed servitors of the Signoria, patrolling the route for the approaching procession, arrived to disperse the obstruction. The man known as Cecco retreated from the threat of a mace, turning to Romola with a respectful nod.

"Madonna, if you must go on your errands, I will take care of the old man."

Cecco was a wild figure: a ragged tunic matted with dust and wool, bare bony arms, a square jaw shaded by a bristly beard, and a narrow red rag tied over his ears. Romola hesitated, her doubt evident.

"Don't distrust me, Madonna," Cecco said, reading her expression perfectly. "I am not as beautiful as you, but I have an old mother who eats my porridge. There is a heart inside me, and I have bought candles for the Most Holy Virgin before. Besides, see there—the old fellow is eating his sop. He is hale enough; he will be on his feet as well as the best of us soon."

"Thank you for your offer, friend," Romola said, her doubt melting into penitence. Leaning toward Baldassarre, she whispered, "Wait for me until I return."

He assented with a slight movement of his head and hand. Romola turned and walked on toward the hospital of San Matteo in the Piazza di San Marco, leaving the old man in the care of the rough but honest Ciompo.
