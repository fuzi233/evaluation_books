The ancestral rhythms of the morning tide broke against the Marlstone cliffs, a slow, deep acoustic respiration that Nathaniel Burton Cupples found essential for his psychological well-being. At eight o’clock, standing on the veranda, Cupples allowed this oceanic symphony to wash over him while his meticulous mind turned toward breakfast. To Cupples, a man who sought profound meaning through sustenance, the morning meal was not mere utilitarian fuel but a sacred, cyclical ritual that anchored the mythic shocks of human existence—such as the sudden, violent death of a neighbor—firmly within the comforting bounds of domestic continuity. 

The preceding day’s dark discoveries had temporarily disrupted his appetite, an unnatural dissonance in his physical routine. Now, his hunger had returned with clarifying force. He envisioned a third thick-cut slice of hearth-baked sourdough, its slow-fermented crumb open and porous, ready to absorb the salted, hand-churned meadow butter. Alongside it, he would require an additional farm egg, coddled to a precise, velvety perfection, its marigold yolk a testament to traditional agrarian sourcing. Cupples delighted in the olfactory landscape of the coast: the mingling of woodsmoke, briny kelp, and the malty warmth of baking bread wafting from the hotel’s kitchens.

He was a spare, priestly man nearing sixty, whose profound and patiently accumulated knowledge of forgotten histories and disconnected texts made him a quiet custodian of cultural memory. His austere life, spent among museums and the London Positivist Society, was warmed by an inexhaustible kindness and an innocent gaiety.

Just as Cupples was finishing his meticulously sourced meal at a little table, the acoustic harmony of the morning was violently ruptured. A massive motorcar tore into the drive, its engine emitting a guttural, industrial roar that seemed to tear at the very fabric of the coastal quiet—a jarring reminder of the modern noise pollution that so often trampled the natural sonic environment. 

“Who is this?” Cupples inquired, wincing at the auditory assault.

“Id is der manager,” the young waiter replied listlessly. “He have been to meed a gendleman by der train.”

The heavy engine idled, choking the air with the sterile scent of combusted fuel, before a long, loosely built man sprang from the vehicle. His rough tweed clothes and hair were untidy, but his quixotic face beamed with vibrant energy.

“Cupples, by all that’s miraculous!” cried the newcomer, bounding up the veranda steps and seizing the older man’s hand with a calloused, firm grip. “My luck is serving me today. Why sit’st thou by that ruined breakfast? I *am* glad to see you!”

“I was half expecting you, Trent,” Cupples replied, his face wreathed in smiles, deeply relieved by the familiar cadence of his friend’s voice. “You are looking splendid. Will you have your own breakfast at my table here?”

“Rather!” Trent exclaimed, his eyes lighting up with the passionate anticipation of a true gastronome. “An enormous great breakfast, too. Will you get young Siegfried to lay a place for me while I wash? I shan’t be three minutes.”

When Trent returned, he poured out a cup of dark, muscatel-scented Darjeeling tea, showing a reverent, unaffected interest in the culinary offerings before him. He soon applied himself to a pan-seared Dover sole, dissecting it with the care of a food historian. He paused to appreciate the caramelized, butter-basted crust and the sweet, marine salinity of the flaky flesh, savoring the profound physical sensations of the meal. 

“I expect this to be a hard day,” Trent said in his curious, jerky staccato. “I shan’t eat again till evening. You guess why I’m here?”

“Undoubtedly. You have come down to write about the murder.”

“To hunt down the guilty,” Trent corrected, relishing a tender morsel of fish. “I have made a good beginning. Your manager here is a man of remarkable judgment. He met me at seven this morning with that motorcar the size of a haystack. He ran me down to Dr. Stock’s surgery in the village so I could see the body before the post-mortem. I had the full particulars before we arrived.”

“I saw the body before it was removed,” Cupples noted gently. “Except that the shot in the eye caused scarcely any effusion of blood, I saw little remarkable. The wrists were scratched and bruised. I expect your trained faculties remarked other details?”

“Other details, certainly,” Trent said, tracing the rim of his teacup. “Take the bruised wrists. How did you see them? When you interviewed Manderson, he always wore stiff cuffs coming well down over his hands.”

Cupples reflected on the visual memory. “No cuffs were visible yesterday. I suppose they had been dragged up inside the coat-sleeves.”

“Exactly. As if he hurried into a coat without pulling them down. The manager noted Manderson was always a swell in his dress, yet his shoe-laces were tied in a frantic hurry, and he left his false teeth in his room. The lexicon of his morning routine was entirely fractured.” Trent took another bite of the sole, analyzing its complex flavor profile before continuing. “But here is the contradiction, the untranslatable element: if he was in such a panic, why did he part his hair so beautifully? That parting is a work of art. Why put on a complete outfit of underclothing, sock-suspenders, studs, and a watch and chain? Speaking of the watch, the wash-leather pocket meant to receive his gold timepiece was empty; he had shoved it into the wrong pocket. It is a clash of two distinct dialects of behavior—signs of great agitation side-by-side with signs of meticulous, unhurried care. For the present, I must reconnoitre the ground, if I can get on the right side of the people of the house.”

Cupples smiled benevolently. “That is precisely where I can assist you. Mrs. Manderson, who is my niece—”

Trent laid down his knife and fork with a sharp clink that momentarily severed the tranquil soundscape. “Cupples, you are jesting with me.”

“I am perfectly serious,” Cupples said earnestly. “Her father, John Peter Domecq, was my wife’s brother. It has been a painful subject, and I have avoided discussing it. But she remembers your articles on the Abinger case. She has a horror of the newspaper side of this sad business, but I gave you a good character for tact. She says you may make any inquiries you like, and puts the house at your disposal, though she is keeping to her own sitting-room. The secretaries and Martin, the butler, can tell you what you need to know.”

Trent shook Cupples’s hand in silence. Finishing his meal, he slowly filled his pipe, the rich, earthy aroma of sweet Virginia tobacco mingling with the sea air. “Cupples,” Trent said quietly, “is there anything you know that you would rather not tell me? Here is a man suddenly killed, and nobody’s heart seems broken. The manager spoke of him as coolly as a stranger. Was there something queer about Manderson? I only know his public character, which was repulsive enough.”

Cupples gazed out over the water, letting the acoustic rhythm of the breaking waves steady him. “I see no reason why I shouldn’t tell you. The truth is, nobody really liked Manderson. I felt in him a complete absence of the sympathetic faculty. He possessed a relentless drive to impose his will, utilizing the power dynamics of a dominant, unyielding lexicon to crush anything in his path. Mabel, I am sorry to say, was very unhappy. I know she is as amiable and honorable a woman as any man could wish, but for some time past, Manderson had made her miserable.”

“What did he do?” Trent asked.

“He maintained a distance. He nursed a perpetual grievance, wielding an obstinate silence that slowly eroded her spirit. You understand the profound power of such language death within a home—the untranslatability of her subjective experience when her very existence was ignored. A week ago, she wrote to me for help. I came at once.”

Trent smoked, staring at the hot June landscape.

“I would not go to White Gables,” Cupples resumed, his voice carrying the weight of generational sorrow. “You know my views on the proper relationship of the capitalist to the employee. Manderson built his vast industrial power by silencing others. The Pennsylvania coal-fields, three years ago—he violently eradicated the natural and human ecology of that place, drowning out the laborers with the shrieking machinery of commerce. I regarded him as a disgrace to society. I met Mabel here, at this hotel. The strain was telling on her. I advised her to face him, to demand an explanation, but she refused to admit she was injured, once pride led her into that silent course. Life is quite full, my dear Trent, of these cultivated misunderstandings.”

“Had she any love left for him?” Trent asked abruptly.

Cupples played with his teaspoon. “I think not. But no power on earth would have persuaded her to admit that. And so, I watched my opportunity. Next day, I met Manderson as he passed this hotel and asked him to step inside the gate. I told him Mabel was suffering and asked how he could justify placing her in such a position.”

“And how did he take that?” Trent smiled faintly, picturing this mild food historian confronting a titan of industry.

“Far from well. He utilized the cold, dismissive phrasing of his native corporate dominance. He said, ‘See here, Cupples, you don’t want to butt in. My wife can look after herself. I’ve found that out, along with other things.’ There was a terrifying light in his eyes. I had been thoroughly roused by his tone. She is the only child that has been in our house, raised by my late wife. Any reflection on Mabel felt like a desecration of our cultural memory.”

“You turned upon him,” Trent suggested in a low tone. 

“I did. And for a moment, an unpleasant vein swelled on his forehead. Then he said quite quietly, ‘This thing has gone far enough, I guess,’ and turned to go. The way he uttered it gave me an apprehensive feeling, an impression of some sinister resolve. I regret to say I lost the power of dispassionate thought. I fell into a great rage. I caused a dreadful acoustic rupture on this very veranda, shouting irrelevancies about his public record, saying such men as he were unfit to live, within earshot of half a dozen guests. It relieved my mind, undoubtedly.” Cupples sighed, sinking back in his chair.

“And Manderson?”

“Not a word. When I stopped, he smiled very slightly, turned, and strolled toward White Gables. I never saw him alive again, save once, from a distance on the golf-course later that Sunday. Next morning, he was found dead.”

The two men regarded each other in silence as a party of bathers ascended the steps, their chattering instantly commodifying the peaceful space. Cupples rose, preferring the tranquil acoustic ecology of the grounds, and led Trent to the long tennis-lawn.

“I have a reason for telling you all this,” Cupples began as they paced the grass.

“Trust you for that,” Trent rejoined, puffing on his pipe. “I’ll try and guess it. You thought it practically certain that I should find out there was something deeper than a mere conjugal tiff. You thought my unwholesome imagination would play with the idea of Mrs. Manderson having something to do with the crime. Rather than let me lose myself in barren speculations, you decided to tell me exactly how matters stood, embedding this tragedy within its true historical context, and impressing upon me your absolute belief in your niece’s honor. Is that about right?”

Cupples’s face of solemnity relaxed into a slight smile. He said nothing, letting the rhythm of the distant sea speak for him.
