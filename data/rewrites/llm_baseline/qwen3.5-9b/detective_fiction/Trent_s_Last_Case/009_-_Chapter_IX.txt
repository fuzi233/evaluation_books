"Come in!" Trent called out.

Mr. Cupples stepped into the hotel sitting-room. It was early evening, the hour following the coroner's jury verdict—a pronouncement delivered without the jurors ever leaving their seats, condemning the crime to "persons unknown."

Trent glanced upward briefly before returning his intense gaze to a photographic dish of enamelled metal resting on the table. He moved it slowly under the slanting light of the window. His complexion was ghastly pale, and his movements betrayed a distinct nervousness.

"Sit on the sofa," he instructed abruptly. "Those chairs are a bargain lot purchased at the auction following the suppression of the Holy Inquisition in Spain. This is a remarkably fine negative," he continued, holding the plate up to the light with the critical tilt of an expert. "I believe it is washed sufficiently now. Let us set it aside to dry and clear away this clutter."

As Trent busied himself clearing the table of basins, racks, bottles, and boxes, Mr. Cupples picked up each item with innocent curiosity.

"That is hypo-eliminator," Trent explained, watching as Cupples uncorked and sniffed a bottle. "Extremely useful when you're pressed for time developing a negative. Though, I wouldn't recommend drinking it, despite its utility. It eliminates sodium hypophosphite; I wouldn't be surprised if it eliminated human beings as well."

Once the last piece of equipment was stowed on the crowded mantelpiece, Trent climbed onto the table to sit facing Cupples. "The true advantage of a hotel sitting-room is that its lack of aesthetic distraction allows the mind to focus entirely on work. It is no place for the fleeting pleasures of a relaxed intellect. Have you ever occupied this specific room before, Cupples? I have, hundreds of times. It has followed me across England for years. If I were to wake up in some fantastical, distant hotel and find myself in a different sitting-room, I would feel utterly lost. Observe this tablecloth; there is the ink stain I spilled upon it during my tenure in Halifax. And that burn hole in the carpet? That happened in Ipswich. Yet, I notice they have repaired the glass over the picture titled 'Silent Sympathy,' which I personally kicked with a boot in Banbury. I produce my finest work here. For instance, this afternoon, since the inquest, I have developed several excellent negatives. There is a perfectly adequate darkroom downstairs."

"The inquest," Cupples interjected, sensing the shift in Trent's demeanor toward active investigation. "That reminds me. I came in to thank you for looking after Mabel this morning. I had no conception she would fall ill immediately after leaving the jury box; she appeared remarkably composed. Indeed, she possesses such extraordinary self-command that I initially considered leaving her to her own devices to hear the evidence, believing it vital for me to do so. Fortunately, she found a friend to assist her, and she is deeply grateful. She seems quite restored now."

Trent stood silently, hands in his pockets, a slight frown etched between his brows. He offered no immediate response.

"I tell you what," he said finally, after a brief pause. "I was just reaching the truly fascinating phase of this operation when you arrived. Would you care to witness a glimpse of high-class police work? It is precisely the caliber of duty that Inspector Murch ought to be performing at this very moment. Perhaps he is; but I pray to heaven he is not."

He leaped from the table and vanished into his bedroom. Moments later, he emerged carrying a large drawing board laden with a collection of disparate objects.

"First, allow me to introduce you to these items," he announced, arranging them on the table. "Here is a substantial ivory paperknife; here are two leaves torn from my own diary; here is a bottle of dentifrice; and here is a small case of polished walnut. Several of these must be returned to their proper places within someone's bedroom at White Gables before nightfall. That is simply the nature of my profession—nothing halts me. I borrowed them this very morning while everyone was attending the inquest. I daresay some might consider it a peculiar procedure if they were aware of it."

His attention then fixed on the final object remaining on the board. "Now, there is one item left. Can you identify it without touching it?"

"Certainly," Cupples replied, leaning in with keen interest. "It appears to be an ordinary glass bowl, perhaps a finger-bowl. I detect nothing unusual about it."

"I see little oddity myself," Trent admitted, "and therein lies the intrigue. Take this small, stout bottle, Cupples, and extract the cork. Do you recognize the powder contained within? You have likely consumed pounds of it throughout your life; it is administered to infants. Its common name is 'grey powder'—a mixture of mercury and chalk. It is remarkable material. Now, while I hold the basin sideways over this sheet of paper, I require you to pour a small amount of the powder onto this section of the bowl—right here..."

Cupples complied. "Perfect!" Trent exclaimed. "Sir Edward Henry himself could not have manipulated the powder with greater precision. You have performed this task before, Cupples; I can tell. You are a veteran of this craft."

"I assure you, I am not," Cupples stated gravely. "It remains a complete mystery to me. What did I accomplish?"

"I shall now lightly brush the powdered area of the bowl with this camel-hair brush. Examine it again. You perceived nothing strange prior. Do you observe anything now?"

Cupples peered closely. "How curious!" he murmured.

"Yes," Trent confirmed. "There are two large grey finger-marks upon the bowl. They were absent before."

"I am Hawkshaw the detective," Trent declared. "Shall I provide a brief lecture on the subject of glass finger-bowls? When one lifts such an object, one invariably leaves traces, usually nearly invisible, capable of persisting for days or even months. These are the marks of your fingers. The human hand, even when ostensibly clean, is rarely entirely dry. Furthermore, in moments of profound anxiety—as Cupples certainly knows all too well—the skin becomes notably moist. Such moisture leaves an impression on any cold, smooth surface touched."

He sprinkled more powder onto the bowl. "On this opposite side, you see the thumb-mark. All impressions are excellent." He spoke quietly, yet Cupples noted the intensity blazing in Trent's eyes as he scrutinized the faint grey markings.

"This one corresponds to the index finger. Given your worldly experience, I need not explain that the pattern constitutes a single-spiral whorl, with deltas symmetrically arranged. This print, belonging to the second finger, is a simple loop featuring a staple core and fifteen counts. I know the count to be fifteen because I possess identical prints on this negative, which I have examined in minute detail. Behold!"

Trent held one of the negatives up to the fading sunlight, pointing with a pencil tip. "Observe the bifurcation of that ridge. It exists here in the other image. Note the tiny scar near the center; it matches perfectly. There are dozens of ridge characteristics upon which an expert would testify in court that the marks on this bowl and the photographs on this negative were produced by the same hand."

"And where did you capture these images? What significance does this hold?" Cupples asked, his eyes wide.

"I located them on the interior of the left-hand pane of the front window in Mrs. Manderson's bedroom. Unable to remove the window itself, I photographed them by affixing a strip of black paper to the rear of the glass. The bowl originates from Manderson's room; it is the vessel used nightly to store his false teeth. I secured permission to remove it, so I did."

"But those cannot be Mabel's finger-marks," Cupples protested.

"Not a chance!" Trent retorted decisively. "They are twice the size of any print Mrs. Manderson could possibly generate."

"Then they must belong to her husband."

"Perhaps they do. Shall we verify this hypothesis once more? I believe we can."

Whistling softly, though his face remained deathly pale, Trent retrieved another small, squat bottle filled with dense black powder.

"Lamp-black," he explained. "Hold a scrap of paper in your palm for a moment or two, and this gentleman will demonstrate the pattern of your own fingers."

With careful precision using a pair of tweezers, Trent selected one of the leaves cut from his diary and extended it for Cupples to inspect. No marks appeared on the leaf. He then tipped some of the black powder onto one surface of the paper, flipped it over, and dusted the reverse side. Finally, he gently shook the leaf to dislodge excess powder. Holding it out to Cupples in silence, he revealed the result.

On one side of the paper, clearly imprinted in stark black, were the exact same two finger-prints previously seen on the bowl and the photographic plate. Trent lifted the bowl and aligned them for comparison. Turning the paper over, he displayed the other side: a bold black replica of the thumb-mark that had earlier appeared in grey on the glass.

"Same individual, you see," Trent said with a short, sharp laugh. "I suspected it must be so, and now I know."

He walked to the window and gazed outward. "Now I know," he repeated in a low murmur, as if speaking to himself alone. His tone was laced with bitterness.
