It was the seventeenth day of November, 1494. Nearly eighteen months had passed since Tito and Romola were bound together in the radiant light of Easter, when confetti rained down upon them like a celestial promise of sweetness for their shared journey. Since that springtime union, however, the landscape of Florence had shifted beneath their feet. Just as every blossom depends on the deep, invisible flow of sap within the tree, the private fortunes of the couple hung precariously upon the grand, turbulent tides of Italian politics.

Only a week prior, the ancient spirit of the republic had surged back into the hearts of its citizens. A thunderclap of alarm rang from the great bell in the palace tower, summoning the populace to arms. Men emerged wielding rusted spears, farming tools, and improvised clubs to drive out the Medici. The Gate of San Gallo slammed shut on Piero, the arrogant exile, as he fled toward Bologna with his mercenaries in disarray, and sealed off his younger brother, the Cardinal, who escaped the city disguised as a humble Franciscan friar. Heads were placed on stakes, homes were sacked in accordance with old traditions, and the shameful caricatures of former conspirators painted on public walls were scraped away. The "tyrants" were ejected from their golden nests in the Via Larga, and the Republic, though bruised, regained the use of its own will.

Yet, barely a week later, the great palace in the Via Larga stood transformed, draped in a canopy of unprecedented color. Streets were roofed with unfamiliar fabrics; banners poured from windows like flags of truce; carpets and tapestries covered every step where "exceptional feet" might tread. If these decorations were proof of joy, then Florence was indeed a city rejoicing in the anticipation of its new guest. The vibrant stream of ornamentation flowed from the palace, winding past the Cathedral, sweeping through the Piazza della Signoria, crossing the Ponte Vecchio, and extending toward the Porta San Frediano, the gateway looking toward Pisa. There, near the gate, a high platform and canopy awaited the Signoria. Messer Luca Corsini, a doctor of law, felt his heart flutter with the weight of a Latin oration he was destined to deliver. Every chief elder prepared himself, smoothing his chin and adjusting his silk *lucco*, ready to march in solemn procession. Young nobles scrutinized their new tunics, tailored in the French manner, hoping to impress the stranger with a peculiar, courtly grace. Meanwhile, a vast assembly of the clergy—from the Archbishop in his blinding splendor to the trains of black, white, and grey monks—consulted early in the morning. They debated how best to marshal their relics, sacred banners, and consecrated jewels, ensuring their movements harmonized perfectly with the arrival of the illustrious visitor at three o'clock in the afternoon.

What an unexampled guest! He had crossed the Alpine passes with an army Italy had never beheld before. With him marched thousands of terrible Swiss mercenaries, hardened to fight for love, hate, or coin; a host of gallant cavaliers proud of their lineage; and an unprecedented infantry where one man in a hundred carried an arquebus. Worst of all, he brought bronze cannons, hurling iron balls instead of stones, drawn by horses rather than oxen, capable of firing twice before a city could even begin to repair the first breach. Some compared this newcomer to Charlemagne, the legendary restorer of Florence and vanquisher of degenerate kings; others likened him to Cyrus, the liberator of the chosen people and restorer of the Temple. For he arrived with glorious designs: to march through Italy amid the jubilations of a grateful populace, to settle all conflicting complaints at Rome, to seize the Kingdom of Naples by virtue of hereditary right, and from there, to launch the crusade against the Turks, cutting them down or converting them to the faith of Christ. Such a scheme seemed fitting for the "Most Christian King," head of a nation strengthened by the cunning of Louis XI, whose death in terror had paved the way for such power. This figure, an antitype of both Cyrus and Charlemagne, was none other than the young Charles VIII of France.

On a general level, hardly anything could appear more grandiose or better suited to awaken memories of divine dispensation than this event. A widespread conviction held that the advent of the French king and his horde was an occurrence at which marble statues might sweat, phantom warriors might fight in the air, and beasts might give birth to monsters. It did not belong to the ordinary order of Providence but was, in a peculiar sense, the direct work of God. This conviction rested less on the sheer magnitude of the foreign invasion than on the moral emotions of the age, shaped into presentiments by the times. These emotions found their most remarkable voice in a single man.

That man was Fra Girolamo Savonarola, Prior of the Dominican convent of San Marco in Florence. On a September morning, when rumors of the French army entering Italy still echoed in men's ears, he preached in the Cathedral from the text, "Behold I, even I, do bring a flood of waters upon the earth." He believed this revelation was guided by supreme authority, continuing the exposition of Genesis begun the previous Lent. He interpreted the "flood of waters"—an emblem of both avenging wrath and purifying mercy—as the divinely indicated symbol of the French army. His audience, comprising some of the finest spirits of the age, the most cultivated minds in the most cultured Italian city, believed him too, listening with shuddering awe. Savonarola possessed a power rarely paralleled, of impressing his beliefs upon others and swaying varied minds. Four years prior, he had proclaimed from the chief pulpit of Florence that a scourge was descending upon Italy, intended to purify the Church. Savonarola appeared to believe—and his wavering followers largely agreed—that he held a mission akin to the Hebrew prophets, and that the Florentines, receiving his message, were a second chosen people.

While the gift of prophecy in others resembled a farthing candle, illuminating only small corners of destiny with gossip, in Savonarola it burned like a mighty beacon, shining far ahead to warn and guide humanity. To the soberest minds, the supernatural quality of his insight gained strong attestation from the peculiar conditions of the age.

At the close of 1492, following the death of Lorenzo de' Medici, Italy had enjoyed a peace and prosperity unthreatened by immediate danger. There was no fear of famine, for the seasons had been bountiful with grain, wine, and oil. New palaces rose in every fair city, and villas sprouted on pleasant slopes. Those who held more than their share showed no fear for the many who held less. Citizens' armor grew rusty, and populations seemed to grow tame, licking the hands of masters who paid for ready-made armies as readily as goods from Smyrna. Even the fear of the Turk had faded, leading the Pope to find it more profitable to accept bribes for prospective poisoning than to plan for conquest or conversion.

Altogether, this world—with its partitioned empires and a roomy, universal Church—seemed a handsome establishment for the lucky and the wise. It was a realm where lust, obscenity, lying, treachery, oppression, and murder were pleasant, useful, and, when properly managed, not dangerous. Adorning this substantial delight of tyranny and avarice was the patronage of polite learning and the fine arts, allowing flattery to be spoken in the choicest Latin and sublime artists to paint both the holy and the unclean with impartial skill. Though the Church was said to be never more disgraced in its leadership nor show fewer signs of renewal, it prospered beyond any past era. The heavens above were fair and smiling; below, there were no signs of earthquake.

Yet, as we have seen, there was a man in Florence who, for two years and more, had preached that a scourge was at hand. He believed the world was not framed for the lasting convenience of hypocrites, libertines, and oppressors. From amidst those smiling heavens, he had seen a sword hanging—the sword of God's justice—speedily descending with purifying punishment upon the Church and the world. Seventeen years prior, in brilliant Ferrara, the contradiction between men's lives and their professed beliefs had pressed upon him with crushing force, destroying his appetite for the world and driving him into the cloister at twenty-three. He believed God had entrusted the sacred lamp of truth to the Church for the guidance and salvation of men, but he saw that the Church, in its corruption, had become a sepulcher hiding the lamp. As scandals multiplied and hypocrisy gave way to impudence, questions arose: Had the world lost its righteous Ruler? Was the Church finally forsaken? No, assuredly. In the Sacred Book, the past served as a mirror for the future, showing that when the wickedness of the chosen people became a cry, the judgments of God inevitably descended. Reason itself declared that vengeance was imminent; what else would suffice to turn men from their obstinacy in evil? Unless the Church were reclaimed, how could the promises be fulfilled that the heathens should be converted and the whole world subject to the one true law? He had seen his beliefs reflected in visions, a mode of seeing frequent with him since youth.
