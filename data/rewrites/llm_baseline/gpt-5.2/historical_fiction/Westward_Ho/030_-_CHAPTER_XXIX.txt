And now Amyas Leigh sat down again under his own roof-tree at Burrough, with the salt of many seas still in his blood, and for twelve months there was little which men would call great; yet the house itself, with its daily bread and its nightly prayers, became a kind of chronicle, and the smallest matters had in them the seeds of what was to come.

Salvation Yeo had, as if by right of storm and plank and cordage, taken up his berth among them for good. He called himself nothing so fine as steward; he asked no wages and no title; but he was everywhere, moving after Amyas like a long gray wolf-hound that has found its master again, silent-footed and watchful. By day he mended what was broken, measured what was short, and made order of what had been left to chance; by night he sat in the chimney-corner with the Bible open on his knee, while his hands, trained to rope and sail and the small contrivances of shipboard, shaped and whittled and stitched. Out of scraps of wood, bits of horn, a broken clasp, a strip of hide, he conjured up endless trifles—some of use, some merely to please the eye and occupy the fingers—buttons for Mrs. Leigh’s work-basket, a spoon with a handle carved like a fish, a little box that shut with a cunning spring, and, above all, toys for Ayacanora.

Each week, as surely as the Sabbath came round, he offered her some new trifle with a humility that was half devotion and half seaman’s awkward tenderness; and each week she received it as if he had laid a rattle in the palm of a grown woman. Her lip would go forward; she would flash those long black lashes of hers as if to say she was no child to be bribed with gewgaws; then Mrs. Leigh’s steady eye would rest on her, and the pout would soften into obedience. Ayacanora would take the gift, set it away with a strange solemnity, and—so far as any could tell—never look on it again.

For the whole heat of her heart was set upon one thought: she would be an English maid.

She ran all day after Mrs. Leigh as if the older woman’s apron-string were the thread that led out of some dark labyrinth. The kitchen and the still-room drew her like music. She would stand over the bread-trough, breathing in the warm, living smell of yeast as it rose—an odor at once sour and sweet, like new ale and bruised apples—and watch Mrs. Leigh’s hands fold and press and turn the dough until it shone. She begged to learn how to judge the oven’s temper by the sound of the draught and the color on the brick; how to lay rushes for a slow fire; how to scald milk without scorching it; how to hang herbs—sage, sweet marjoram, tansy, dried lavender—so that they kept their virtue through winter. In the still-room she was all wonder, for there the very air seemed thick with scents: rose-water cooling in glazed pans; rosemary and angelica lying cut and tied; the sharp, clean bite of juniper; the faint honey of meadowsweet. She listened to the drip from the worm of the still as if it were a language, and would ask again and again why the first run must be thrown away, and how the heart of the liquor was known by taste, not by sight.

But beyond all that, the needle held her. She would prick her fingers raw learning the tightness of a seam, the secret of a gathered sleeve, the sober patience by which linen is turned and turned again until it serves two lives. Soon she was begging cloth, not for herself only, but to make shifts for the old women in Northam, and little coats for children whose elbows shone through their rags.

This was a new thought to her, and it came, not like an argument, but like a smell caught on the wind. She had been bred among a people whose hospitality was boundless so long as it ran in the old channels—feast answered by feast, gift paid back with gift; the guest sat down to meat because some day the host would sit down to his. But to give to those who could give nothing back; to spend one’s strength on a stranger’s need with no hope of return—this was a mystery, as strange to her as the turning of milk to cheese or grape to wine.

She saw Mrs. Leigh, who might well have sat at ease after all her fears, spending each spare hour in quiet labour for the poor, and going from cottage to cottage with a basket that smelt of bread and smoked bacon and dried beans. She saw Amyas, after the public thanksgiving in church for his safe return, giving away money and food in Northam, Appledore, and Bideford; buying cottages and setting them apart for worn-out mariners, so that old men with knotted hands might die not in the ditch but by a hearth. When she asked why, she was told, simply, that this was his thank-offering to God.

A thank-offering, in her mind, had been something far other: blood, terror, skulls and bones set up to please a spirit that demanded fear. She could understand, indeed, the old instinctive English rule that something should be given to the Church itself. The vicar of Northam soon strutted, with comely pride, in a new surplice; and the altar shone with a splendid flagon and salver of plate, taken in the great galleon—a fair spoil, and holy, as men counted then. That, at least, made sense to her: if the Great Spirit was to be honoured, let his house be made brave.

But the alms still puzzled her, until Mrs. Leigh, one afternoon when rain fretted softly on the casement and the house was full of the homely hush that comes with wet weather, told her—without any flourish, as if naming the weight of flour for a loaf—that whoever gave to the poor gave to the Great Spirit; for the Great Spirit was in them, and in Ayacanora too, if she would be quiet and listen, instead of stamping and pouting and doing only what she liked.

The words sank in. The poor child took them in as children do—whole, without picking and choosing—and went at them with her hands first. She worked until her fingers ached, and her shoulders stiffened, and yet she would not stop: darning stockings, stitching seams, kneading and shaping, carrying broth in a covered pipkin so that it did not slop, learning how to thicken it with barley without lumps, and how to sweeten it for the sick with a spoon of honey when there was honey to spare. She went about with Mrs. Leigh, lovely and beloved now, because she was no longer merely a wonder from far seas but a help; and, from under her lashes, she would steal glances at Amyas, watching for the smallest sign.

On the day when she won a smile from him, she was good from morning to night, as if the sunshine had sunk into her bones. On the day when she did not, she was thoroughly naughty, and would have worn out any patience less chastened than Mrs. Leigh’s. Yet even her storms were beginning, little by little, to break themselves against that quiet woman’s constancy, as wind breaks itself against a cliff and must at last fall into the sea.

As for her finery, there was no schooling it down. Each Sunday she swept into church bedizened in Spanish bravery—silks that rustled like dry leaves, jewels that caught and threw back the thin Devon light—so that heads turned and eyes wandered and minds strayed from prayer. The good vicar, driven at last to humble remonstrance, spoke to Mrs. Leigh of the disturbance.

Ayacanora answered for herself, with the proud straightness of one who had been obeyed once: she was not thinking about them, she said; they need not think about her. And if the Piache—in plain English, the conjuror, as she took the vicar to be—wanted a present, he might have all her Mexican feather-dresses. She would not wear them; they were wild Indian things; she was an English maid. With that she darted upstairs, brought them down in a blaze of emerald and azure and crimson, and insisted so stoutly on arraying the vicar that the poor man escaped as if from a net. Yet he carried off one mantle, handsomest of all; and instead of selling it, he turned it cleverly into an altar-cloth. For years afterward, the communion at Northam was celebrated upon a splendour of colour which had once adorned the sinful body of some Aztec prince.

So she flaunted on, half child, half queen; while Amyas watched her half amused, half proud in the simple vanity of a man who has brought home a rare bird, and looked round at all gazers as much as to say, See what I have saved.

If she vexed the vicar, she vexed the neighbourhood more. The ladies came, of course, in proper troop, to congratulate Mrs. Leigh and to stare at the fair savage. The fair savage snubbed them all impartially—from the vicar’s wife to Lady Grenville herself—so that few tried a second visit.

Mrs. Leigh reasoned with her, and was met with tears. They came to look, she sobbed, as at some beast in a booth; she would not be made a show. She had been like a queen once, and all obeyed; here every one looked down on her. But when Mrs. Leigh asked, very gently, if she would rather go back to the forests, Ayacanora clung to her like a frightened child and begged not to be sent away. She would sooner be a slave in the kitchen here, she cried, than go back to the bad people.

So the months went by—foolish storms and foolish sunshine—but always under the shadow of one in whom there was neither storm nor sunshine, only a perpetual genial calm, like soft grey weather that does not dazzle and does not chill, but makes all things bearable. Under that influence the outbursts grew more rare; Ayacanora grew more rational, though no more happy; and small hints began to fall from her, as nuts fall when the bough is shaken, which made her story clearer to Mrs. Leigh and to Yeo.

When she had become used to houses, she let them see that she had seen such things before. The red Devon cattle did not startle her as much as they should have done; she watched them long, frowning, as if matching them to some older picture in her mind. The sheep puzzled her more. At last she made Mrs. Leigh understand, with a disdainful gesture, that they were too small.

“Ah, madam,” said Yeo eagerly, catching at every straw that might bind her to their guesses, “it is because she has been used to those great camel-sheep—llamas they call them—in Peru.”

But nothing woke her like a horse. The use of tame beasts at all, the very notion that a creature might labour with you rather than flee you, was daily miracle enough; but that a horse could be ridden—could carry a human as the sea carries a ship—was beyond belief. She would have a horse. Amyas, fearing for her pretty neck, put her off; she plagued him till his patience, tried by all things else, began to thin. Foiled with him, she went confidentially to Yeo and proposed they should steal one, as simply as she might have proposed snaring a deer. Foiled there, she went to the vicar and offered to barter all her finery for his broken-kneed pony. The vicar, too honest to take advantage of such madness, refused; and the matter ended with Amyas buying her a jennet.

In a fortnight she rode as if she had been born with saddle-leather under her thighs, her body answering the beast’s motion as a dancer answers a tune. She learned by ear as much as by hand: the cluck and whistle that steadied; the soft hiss that checked; the very rhythm of hoofbeats on turf that told her whether the ground was safe. Often, as she cantered, the sound seemed to draw something from deep within her—an older music, half forgotten.

That spring, at Lady Grenville’s invitation, the whole household went over to Stow. Mrs. Leigh rode soberly on a pillion behind the groom, her skirts pinned close, her hands folded; Ayacanora ranged and circled on the moor like a hound let loose, the wind combing her hair, trying to make Amyas race her. He would not. He watched her with eyes that would not confess all they felt.

That night, in the same chamber with Mrs. Leigh, Ayacanora woke shrieking. The cry tore through the quiet of the house like a gull’s scream in fog. When Mrs. Leigh caught her, she sobbed out a tale with such terror in it that it shook the listener: the “Old ape of Panama,” her especial abomination, had come to her bedside and dragged her into a courtyard; she had been forced onto a horse and ridden with an Indian over great moors and high mountains down into a dark wood. There the Indian and the horses vanished, and she found herself suddenly changed once more into a little savage child.

So strong was the impression that she could not be persuaded it had not happened, if not that night, then some night long ago. Mrs. Leigh, piecing it to other scraps, began at last to believe that the Lord had, in mercy, shown the poor child in a vision who she was; that she had been exposed in the forests by a jealous step-father; and neither Sir Richard nor his lady could doubt that this was the likeliest thread. Don Xararte—Panama was his home—might well have been at Quito; Yeo had seen him come aboard the Lima ship at Guayaquil, nearest of ports. It would explain her being found beyond Cotopaxi, the great peak of the Eastern Andes, if the old man, believing her to be Oxenham’s child, had taken so fearful a revenge as to cast her out to die.

Other facts surfaced slowly, and always by way of creature or tree or wind—as is natural in one whose mind had long been emptied of words and filled instead with hunger, cold, and the watching of beasts. Whatever her morals or affections had once been taught, had been rubbed out by the long spiritual death of the forest wandering. Mrs. Leigh could draw from her no clear tenderness for her mother, no recollection of early prayers.

Yet at last, strangely, the lost link was supplied—not by argument, but by smell and sound.

Sir Richard Grenville had brought home an Indian from Virginia. Of his first name none there were sure; but men whispered it might be that “Wanchese” whose name goes in company with “Manteo.” He was to be baptized at Bideford, and given the name of Raleigh, his sponsors being most like Sir Walter himself and Sir Richard. All Bideford came out, as folk will to any wonder: mayor and corporation-men in full robes; maces and tipstaffs bright; gossips elbowing for a sight. The church filled with the rustle of clothes and the scrape of shoes on stone, and above it all the small, living noises of a crowd that tries to behave.

Mrs. Leigh went, as a matter of course; and Ayacanora insisted on going too. She was eager to know what they would do with the “Carib.”

“To make him a Christian,” Mrs. Leigh said.

“Why did they not make her one?” Ayacanora pouted, for she could not bear that an “ugly red Carib” should be given something denied her.

Because, Mrs. Leigh told her, they were sure she was one already, and had been christened when she was born. Ayacanora was not sure; she stood there, watching, listening, trying to remember.

The stately son of the forest—now tamed into the livery of a great house—stood at the font. The service had gone on half through when a heavy sigh, almost a groan, broke from Ayacanora. She swayed and fell fainting into Mrs. Leigh’s arms.

They carried her out into a neighbouring house. When she came to herself, her face was white under its warm brown, and her eyes had the far look of one who is still half elsewhere. She told a strange story, with a minuteness that made the hair stir at the back of Yeo’s neck.

As she stood trying to recollect, the church, she said, had grown larger. The priest’s dress had become richer. The walls were covered with pictures. Above the altar, in jewelled robes, stood a lady with a babe in her arms. Soft music sounded—she insisted on that, as if the very notes still trembled in her skull. And the air—most of all the air—was full of fragrance, thick as mist: not the plain smell of bodies and damp wool, but a sweetness of incense and flowers, heavy and clinging, like crushed petals warmed. Through that scented cloud she saw not one Indian but many standing by the font; and a lady held her by the hand; and she was a little girl again.

After much questioning, so exact was her recollection, not only of the scene but of the building itself, that Yeo struck his hand on his knee.

“A christened woman she is, madam,” he said, “if Popish christening be worth the name; and she has seen Indians christened too in the Cathedral Church at Quito, the inside whereof I know well enough, and too well. For I sat there three mortal hours in a San Benito, to hear a friar preach his false doctrines, not knowing whether I should be burnt the next day.”

So Ayacanora went home to Burrough, with a new certainty laid like a seal upon her past; and Raleigh the Indian went to Sir Richard’s house.

The entry of his baptism still stands in the old parchment register at Bideford, crooked-lettered and plain, among the baptisms for 1587:

“Raleigh, a Winganditoian: March 26.”

And his name occurs once more, a year and a month after:

“Rawly, a Winganditoian, April 1589.”

But this time it is not among the baptisms. The free forest wanderer had pined in vain for cedar scents and deer-hunts, for lazy paddlings through still lagoons where water-lilies sleep under magnolias and vines hang in clustered ropes. The town’s constant clatter—carts on stone, hammers, the shut-in echo of narrow streets—had hemmed him round like a cage. He went away to what his own people might have called happier hunting-grounds, and all that was left below slept in a cramped churchyard, blocked in with dingy houses, whose tenants would never waste a sigh upon an Indian’s grave. Those two entries stand unto this day; and they are a kind of first-fruit and emblem of the sad fate of that worn-out red race, to whom what men call civilization came too late to save, and not too late to hasten decay.

Yet while Amyas lay quiet, England did not.

That spring Raleigh sent out another and larger colony to Virginia under one John White. Raleigh wrote more than once, entreating Amyas to take the command; and if he had gone, who can tell what might have been born sooner across the sea? But Amyas’s mother had bound him by a solemn promise to wait at home with her twelve months at least; and he, who could face guns and hunger, could not face her lonely fear. So he stayed, and sent instead five hundred pounds, which, for aught I know, lie in Virginia still in some fashion; for they never came back to him.

Soon a sharper trial came upon that promise—one that wore his temper thin and made him, for the first time in his life, moody, peevish, and restless. While other men fought Spaniards, he sat by his own hearth. The sound of distant drums, though he could not hear them, seemed to beat in him. And over all his thoughts there gathered a sullen malice against Don Guzman. The “single eye” began to cloud; his whole body was not full of light. He had stepped into that darkness in which every man walks who hates his brother; and it lay on him like a black shadow, in the bright mornings and at candlelight too.

Nor was there any company more fit to deepen that shadow than Salvation Yeo’s. The old man grew more stern in his fanaticism day by day, and found in Amyas too willing a listener. Mrs. Leigh, perhaps for the first and last time in her life, was seriously angry when she heard them coolly debating whether they had not committed a grievous sin in not killing the Spanish prisoners aboard the galleon. Her anger was quiet; but it was like the hard edge of a knife. There are gentlenesses that will endure much, and there are gentlenesses that will not endure cruelty, however it dresses itself in texts.

Yet, to speak plain truth, if such was the temper of Englishmen then, the Spaniards had done much to provoke it, and were attempting to do more.

For now the year 1588 drew near—the year which an astronomer of Königsberg, a hundred years before, had foretold would be admirable, and which German chronologers had called the climacterical year of the world. Men may make what they will of prophecies; but this at least came true. That year was indeed a turning, and decided once and for all the fortunes of European nations and of a whole continent across the sea.

In every great crisis there wakes in men some dim instinct that the Day of the Lord is at hand; that there is war in heaven; that fiends of darkness and angels of light are set in array, struggling for the souls of men. In that age such a feeling took readily the shape of astrological dream and planetary house, as if the stars must needs ring like bells when the world below was shaken.

But the wise needed no conjunctions to tell them what was coming. The long duel between Spain and England, fought hitherto by fits and starts—now in the Netherlands, now in the West Indies, now along the African shore—was drawing at last toward a death-grapple. Spain itself had been respected, and England likewise; trade to Spanish ports went on till, in 1585, the Spaniard, without warning, laid an embargo on all English ships coming to his European shores. They were seized, it seemed, to form part of an enormous armament, meant, so Spain said, to crush the rebellious Netherlanders; but the Queen, her council, and—when it was almost too late—the people of England believed otherwise. England was the destined victim. So, instead of negotiating to avoid fighting, they fought to produce negotiation. Drake, Frobisher, and Carlisle swept the Spanish Main with fire and sword, stopping the Indian supplies; while Walsingham, craftiest and yet most honest of mortals, by some dark turn of money-work, kept the Venetians from lending Spain the sums that would have repaired her losses. No Armada came that year.

Meanwhile the Jesuits, at home and abroad, made little secret among their own of what the Spanish armament was meant to do. The impious heretics—Drakes and Raleighs, Grenvilles and Cavendishes, Hawkinses and Frobishers—who had dared to violate that hidden sanctuary of half the globe which the Pope had bestowed on the defender of the true faith, were, so they cried, to meet shameful ruin and terrible death, when their sacrilegious barks should sink beneath the thunder of Spanish cannon, blessed by the Pope and sanctified with holy water and prayers to “God and his Mother.” England would fall with them. The proud islanders, rebels against St. Peter, should bow once more beneath the yoke of the Gospel. Their queen—illegitimate, excommunicate, contumacious, abettor of free-trade, defender of the Netherlands, pillar of false doctrine—should be sent in chains across the Alps to sue for her life at the feet of the long-suffering father of mankind, while his nominee took her throne.

They spoke of a crusade. They called on saints whose altars the English had rifled. They cried on the Virgin Queen of Heaven, whose gifts the English spurned. They set “Mary” against Elizabeth, and urged all manly hearts and swords to fight in her cause, against the impious harlot who had assumed her titles and received, from idolatrous flatterers, homage due to Mary alone; and Father Parsons, with more heat than charity, asserted that Elizabeth had even caused her own name to be substituted for Mary’s in the Litanies of the Church.

So Parsons and Allen and dozens more spoke, and spoke worse; and were somewhat surprised to find that their hearers, though granting the premises, were too dull—or too human—to follow the conclusion. The English lay Romanists, almost to a man, had hearts sounder than their heads, and could not help holding to the strange superstition that, being Englishmen, they were bound to fight for England. The Jesuits, who had boasted for years that the persecuted faithful throughout the island would rise as one man to welcome Spain and Rome, found instead that the faithful, like Demas, loved this present world: they had no objection, in the abstract, to the restoration of Popery, but they preferred some method less likely to strip them of ancestral lands and seat needy Castilians in their old houses, to treat tenants as Spain had treated Indians, and gentry as she had treated caciques.

Yet if men would not be moved by the supposed sorrows of the Queen of Heaven, there was another Mary nearer at hand, whose woes and gifts were more palpable to the carnal sense: Mary of Scotland. The English government kept her in comfort and luxury at a cost which would now be twenty thousand a year; she could employ her French jointure—perhaps fifty thousand more—in plotting the government’s ruin and the queen’s murder. If she prospered, there would be dukedoms and earldoms and lands and castles to scatter among friends. And with an angel face, a serpent tongue, and a heart—by her own confession—hard as diamond, she made weak men fall in love with her, and fancy, worse still, that she loved them.

Of her the Jesuits were not unmindful. Her sorrows made excellent stock-in-trade. She was Romanist; she was “beautiful and unfortunate,” and that virtue, like charity, hides a multitude of sins. So the poor card was played and played again, until it tore in the hand.

To all which England made at last a practical and terrible answer. From the highest noble to the lowest peasant there rose one simultaneous cry, like the sound of the sea when the wind shifts into storm: “We are weary of these seventeen years of chicanery and terror. This woman must die, or the commonweal of England perish.”

And we all know which of those two was chosen.
