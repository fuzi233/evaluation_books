The rest of this harrowing sequence must be recounted in Thomas's own voice, for it constitutes the core of his confession to the detectives.

"I kept my promise," Thomas began, his voice trembling with the weight of memory. "On the appointed day and hour, I took my young wife, Evelyn, to Felix's house. We walked; the hotel was close, and the air felt heavy with anticipation. An elderly servant, whom I had been warned was deaf and mute, greeted us at the door. As we stepped into the dim, aristocratic gloom of the hall, Evelyn turned pale, her eyes searching mine for reassurance. I offered a smile that betrayed nothing of my own secret dread, slipping my arm around her waist as we followed the old man past the drawing room and into a study that looked stark and plain.

Then, Bartow—later known as Felix—parted the portière. The room transformed instantly into something resembling fairyland, or at least it would have, had not Felix's overwhelming personality immediately drawn our attention. To Evelyn, who knew little of this brother, he appeared terrifyingly handsome, possessing a mental poise that struck her with awe. Yet, she moved toward him with brave grace, smiling with an inviting charm that I hoped would disarm him.

Perhaps it did. Under the yellow glare of the odd lantern overhead, Felix's pale features turned waxy. His mechanical greeting fell heavily, his words barely a stammer. But his eyes, fixed intently on Evelyn's face, betrayed a depth of feeling she could not comprehend—a realization that her very life hung in the balance under his indomitable will. Knowing this, I cast him an imploring glance and asked if Evelyn might rest while we spoke privately.

He looked at me with a strange expression, perhaps sensing the revolt in my tone, though I never knew if he understood the nature of my compliance. He stopped fiddling with a small object on the table, bowed with a sarcastic grace that made me feel inferior even in stature, and led me to a small door I had previously missed.

'Your wife will find it more comfortable here,' he said, his speech halting with repressed excitement. He opened the door to reveal a small, elegant sleeping apartment. 'What we have to say cannot take long. Mrs. Adams will not find the wait tedious.'

Evelyn smiled, a natural laugh born of perfect happiness, though she must have found the procedure strange. I began to speak, but the vision of her dimpled face above the great cluster of roses she carried stole my words. The door closed, leaving me face to face with Felix.

He breathed easier now that we were alone, yet he remained silent, casting a strange glance around the room—the weirdest apartment I have ever known. Satisfied, he led me to the large table, which seemed to exert an uncanny magnetism upon him.

'Come further away,' he commanded. 'I need air. So must you. Besides, why should she hear what we have to say? She will know the worst soon enough. She seems a gentle-hearted woman.'

'An angel!' I started to say, but he stopped me with an imperious gesture.

'We will not discuss your wife,' he protested sharply. 'Where is John Poindexter?'

'At the hotel,' I replied. 'Or perhaps he has returned home. I no longer take account of his existence. Felix, I shall never leave my wife. I would rather prove recreant to the oath I took before I realized the worth of the woman whose happiness I vowed to destroy. This is what I have come to tell you. Make it easy for me. You are a man who has loved and suffered. Let us bury the past.'

Did I hope to move him? Perhaps a childish notion influenced me. But as the words left my lips, I saw the set look of his features, cast in bronze. He was firm, immovable.

Yet, what did I fear? A struggle involving Evelyn in bitterness and the loss of a fortune I had come to regard as my own? These were petty considerations. She must know my true name and her father's guilt sooner or later. Why not now? If we must start life poor, it was still life, whereas separation from her...

Meanwhile, Felix spoke in language I was least prepared to hear.

'I anticipated this. From the moment you pleaded for the privilege of marrying her, I looked forward to this outcome and provided against it. Weakness on the part of her bridegroom was to be expected; I have steeled myself to meet the emergency. Your oath must be kept!'

Crushed by the tone, evincing power against which any ordinary struggle would fail, I looked about the room. There was nothing visible to awaken distrust, yet a feeling of dread had seized me—the first I had truly felt. Was it his calm confidence or the weirdness of the Oriental splendors under the night effects in broad daylight? Hard to say. But from this moment, I was affected by a vague affright, not for myself, but for her, whose voice we could plainly hear humming a gay tune in the adjoining room. I resolved to suppress all betrayal of uneasiness, even smiling, though I felt the eyes of Evelyn's pictured countenance upon me. I had never lost sight of that portrait since entering, though I had not given it a direct look, now standing with my back to it. Felix, facing it but not raising his eyes to it, waited for my response. Finding my words halted, he said again:

'That oath must be kept!'

This time I found words. 'Impossible!' I burst out, flinging doubt, fear, and hesitancy to the winds. 'It was in my nature to take it, worked upon by family affection, the awfulness of our father's approaching death, and a thousand uncanny influences. But it is not in my nature to keep it after four months of living with a man thirty years removed from his guilt, and his guileless, innocent daughter. And you cannot drive me to it, Felix. No man can force another to abandon his own wife because of a wicked oath taken long before he knew her. If you think your money...'

'Money?' he cried, with a contempt that did justice to my disinterestedness. 'I had forgotten I had it. No, Thomas, I should never weigh money against the happiness of living with such a woman as your wife appears to be. But her life I might. Carry out your threat; forget to pay John Poindexter the debt we owe him, and the matter will assume a seriousness for which you are doubtless poorly prepared. A daughter dead in her honeymoon will be almost as great a grief to him as a dishonored one. And either dead or dishonored he must find her, when he comes here in search of the child he cannot long forget. Which shall it be? Speak!'

Was I dreaming? Was this Felix? Was this myself?

With a spring, I reached his side where he stood close against the table, groaning rather than shrieking: 'You would not kill her! You do not meditate a crime of blood—here—on her—the innocent—the good...'

'No,' he said. 'It will be you who will do that. You who will not wish to see her languish, suffer, go mad. Thomas, I am not the raving being you take me for. I am merely a keeper of oaths. Nay, I am more. I have talents, skill. The house in which you find yourself is proof of this. This room—see, it has no outlet save those windows, scarcely perceptible to you, above our heads, and that opening shielded now by a simple curtain, but which in an instant, without my moving from this place, I can so hermetically seal that no man, save he be armed with crowbar and pickaxe, could enter here, even if man could know of our imprisonment, in a house soon to be closed from top to bottom by my departing servant.'

'May God protect us!' fell from my lips, as, stiff with horror, I let my eyes travel from his determined face to the windows high over my head and then to the door, which, though but a few steps away, felt as far as possible from the room into which my darling had been lured.

Felix watched me, explaining as calmly as if the matter were of everyday significance. 'You are looking for the windows,' he remarked. 'They are behind those goblin faces you see outlined on the tapestries under the ceiling. As for the door, if you had looked to the left when you entered, you would have detected the edge of a huge steel plate hanging flush with the casing. This plate can be made to slide across that opening in an instant just by the touch of my hand on this button. This done, no power save such as I have mentioned can move it back again, not even my own. I have forces at my command for sending it forward, but none for returning it to its place. Do you doubt my mechanical skill or the perfection of the electrical apparatus I have caused to be placed here? You need not, Thomas; nor need you doubt the will that has only to exert itself for an instant to—Shall I press the button, brother?'

'No, no!' I shouted in a frenzy, caused rather by my knowledge of the nature of this man than any especial threat in his voice. 'Let me think; let me know more fully what your requirements are—what she must suffer if I consent—and what I.'

He let his hand slip back, that smooth white hand which I had more than once surveyed in admiration. Then he smiled.

'I knew you would not be foolish,' he said. 'Life has its charms even for hermits like me; and for a *beau garçon* such as you are...'

'Hush!' I interposed, maddened into daring his full anger. 'It is not my life I am buying, but hers, possibly yours; for it seems you have planned to perish with us. Is it not so?'

'Certainly,' was his cold reply. 'Am I an assassin? Would you expect me to live, knowing you to be perishing?'

I stared aghast. Such resolve, such sacrifice of self to an idea, was beyond my comprehension.

'Why—what?' I stammered. 'Why kill us, why kill yourself...'

The answer overwhelmed me.

'Remember Evelyn!' shrilled a voice, and I paused, struck dumb with a superstitious horror I had never believed myself capable of experiencing. For it was not Felix who spoke, neither was it any utterance of my own aroused conscience. Muffled, strange, and startling, it came from above, from the hollow spaces of that high vault lit with the golden glow that henceforth can have but one meaning for me—death.

'What is it?' I asked. 'Another of your mechanical contrivances?'

He smiled; I had rather he had frowned.

'Not exactly. A favorite bird, a starling. Alas! he but repeats what he has heard echoed through the solitude of these rooms. I thought I had smothered him up sufficiently to insure his silence during this interview. But he is a self-willed bird, and seems disposed to defy the wrappings I have bound around him; which fact warns me to be speedy and hasten our explanations. Thomas, this is what I require: John Poindexter—you do not know where he is at this hour, but I do—received a telegram but now, which, if he is a man at all, will bring him to this house in a half-hour or so from the present moment. It was sent in your name, and in it you informed him that matters had arisen which demanded his immediate attention; that you were on your way to your brother's (giving him this address), where, if you found entrance, you would await his presence in a room called the study; but that—and here you will see how his coming will not aid us if that steel plate is once started on its course—if the possible should occur and your brother should be absent from home, then he was to await a message from you at the Plaza. The appearance of the house would inform him whether he would find you and Eva within; or so I telegraphed him in your name.

'Thomas, if Bartow fulfils my instructions—and I have never known him to fail me—he will pass down these stairs and out of this house in just five minutes. As he is bound on a long-promised journey, and as he expects me to leave the house immediately after him, he has drawn every shade and fastened every lock. Consequently, on his exit, the house will become a tomb, to which, just two weeks from today, John Poindexter will be called again, and in words which will lead to a demolition which will disclose—what? Let us not forestall the future, our horrible future, by inquiring. But Thomas, shall Bartow go? Shall I not by signs he comprehends more readily than other men comprehend speech indicate to him on his downward passage to the street that I wish him to wait and open the door to the man whom we have promised to overwhelm in his hour of satisfaction and pride? You have only to write a line—see! I have made a copy of the words you must use, lest your self-command should be too severely taxed. These words left on this table for his inspection—for you must go and Eva remain—will tell him all he needs to know from you. The rest can come from my lips after he has read the signature, which in itself will confound him and prepare the way for what I have to add. Have you anything to say against this plan? Anything, I mean, beyond what you have hitherto urged? Anything that I will consider or which will prevent my finger from pressing the button on which it rests?'

I took up the paper. It was lying on the table, where it had evidently been inscribed simultaneously with or just before our entrance into the house, and slowly read the few lines I saw written upon it. You know them, but they will acquire a new significance from your present understanding of their purpose and intent:

'You wish me to sign these words, to put them into my own handwriting, and so to make them mine? Mine!' I repeated.
