Our Virginians, possessing a wisdom far more common in youth than in age, had resolved to embrace strong Jacobite sentiments and to profess a profound, almost theatrical, affection for the exiled royal family. The Banished Prince, recognizing Madam Esmond's father as the legitimate Marquis of Esmond, found her forgiveness easy to grant; after all, the unfortunate Stuarts were so eager to acknowledge the superior merits of her lineage. Any lingering scandal regarding her sister, Madame de Bernstein, and the Old Chevalier, she dismissed with a wave of scornful elegance, declaring with perfect truth that the first two monarchs of the House of Hanover were scarcely less guilty of domestic impropriety than any Stuart. Yet, despite the moral equivalence, the *de facto* King remained the King. Having been solemnly crowned and anointed within the sacred walls of St. Paul's, and having utterly routed the *de jure* claimant in the field of battle, the reigning sovereign held undisputed authority. In Madam Esmond's clear estimation, it was both the sons' duty and the sovereign's pleasure for them to appear at court. If His Majesty were inclined to bestow a lucrative office or a ribbon of blue or red upon either lad, she entertained no surprise. She was convinced that the King knew the Virginian Esmonds as intimately as any member of the nobility. Consequently, the lads were strictly commanded to present themselves at court. Indeed, I suspect their mother would have been furious had she known that George had donned Harry's laced coat on the very morning he went to bow at Kensington.

A century prior, the King's drawing-room stood open to his nobility and gentry almost daily, allowing loyalty to indulge itself a dozen times a month in the august sight of the sovereign. Driven by a prudent avoidance of the enemy's fleets and a gracious sorrow for the potential loss of the royal person to French corsairs, the monarch had forsaken his beloved visits to Herrenhausen in Hanover. Compelled to reside, albeit unwillingly, amongst his loving subjects in Britain, he found solace in the company of a Hanoverian lady, the Countess of Walmoden, whose virtues had endeared her to the Prince. From her lips alone could the revered Defender of the Faith hear the accents of his native tongue.

It was Mr. Warrington who requested an assiduous courtier, his uncle, to present him to the King. For Mr. Lambert, also required to render thanks for his recent promotion, the journey to court lay inevitable. Thus, the two gentlemen departed together, hiring a hackney-coach, as Lord Wrotham's carriage was needed by its rightful owner, who had returned to town. At Kensington Palace Gate, the sentries recognized and saluted the good General, guiding the pair modestly on foot toward the sovereign's summer residence. Passing beneath the portico, they traversed a gallery leading to the great black marble staircase—richly decorated and painted by the renowned architect Mr. Kent—and moved through several sumptuously hung chambers, adorned with tapestries, portraits, and busts, until they reached the King's great drawing-room. Here hung the famous "Venus" by Titian and, among other masterpieces, the "St. Francis Adoring the Infant Saviour" by Sir Peter Paul Rubens. Within this grand hall, amidst the throng of courtiers, the gentlemen waited for His Majesty to issue from his private apartments, where he was currently conferring with certain personages the newspapers of the day termed "Majesty's Ministers."

George Warrington, having never before beheld a palace, had ample leisure to admire the splendor surrounding him. For the first time, he gazed upon fine paintings, delighting in the charming portrait of Sir Anthony Van Dyck depicting King Charles I, his Queen, and Family, as well as the magnificent "Esther Before Ahasuerus" by Tintoret, wherein all figures wore the resplendent Venetian habit. So entranced by these works was he that he barely registered the whispers of his friend, the General, who was murmuring into his young companion's nearly heedless ear the names of the distinguished persons arrayed around them.

"Yonder," intoned Mr. Lambert, "stand two of my Lords of the Admiralty, Mr. Gilbert Elliot and Admiral Boscawen—that same Boscawen whose fleet fired the first gun in your waters two years past. That stout gentleman, belted with gold, is Mr. Fox, who served as Minister and now accepts the role of Paymaster with a substantial salary."

"He carries the *auri fames* upon his person," observed George, noting the general's waistcoat. "Why, his vest is a perfect Potosí!"

*"Aliena appetens"*—"How goes the text?" continued General Lambert. "He loves to acquire money and squander it. Yonder stands my Lord Chief Justice Willes, conversing with my Lord of Salisbury and Doctor Headley. Should he serve his God as faithfully as he serves His King, he will surely be translated to some very high promotion in Heaven. He belongs to your grandfather's era and was cherished by Dick Steele yet despised by the Dean. Beside them sits my Lord of London, the learned Doctor Sherlock. The Lords of the Lawn Sleeves have lost half their honors now. I recall when I was a boy, still in my mother's hand, she compelled me to kneel before the Bishop of Rochester, who crossed the water to become Minister to a certain Perkin's Bishop. That handsome fair fellow is Admiral Smith; he presided over the court-martial of the poor Captain Byng and strove in vain to save him from penal punishment. They call him 'Tom of Ten Thousand' in the fleet. The French Ambassador had him broken when he was merely a lieutenant for making a French man-of-war lower its topsails to him, yet the King made him a captain the following day. That tall, haughty-looking gentleman is my Lord George Sackville. Now that I am a Major-General myself, he will treat me somewhat better than a footman. I wish my stout old Blakeney were here; he is the soldier's darling, as kind and brave as that poker-faced nobleman is brave—and I am your lordship's very humble servant."

"This is a young gentleman who has just arrived from America and was present at Braddock's tragic affair two years ago."

"Oh, indeed!" exclaimed the poker-faced nobleman. "I have the honor of speaking to Mr.—?"

"To Major-General Lambert, at your lordship's service, who served in His Majesty's army some time before you entered it. That, Mr. Warrington, is the first Commoner in England, Mr. Speaker Onslow. Where is your uncle? I shall be forced to present you personally to His Majesty if Sir Miles delays much longer." As he spoke, the worthy General directed his attention entirely to his young friend, paying scant regard to his colleague, who stalked away with a startled expression, seemingly amazed at the other's audacity. A hundred years ago, a nobleman was a nobleman and expected to be admired solely as such.

Presently, Sir Miles's red waistcoat appeared in sight, and many cordial greetings exchanged between him, his nephew, and General Lambert. For we have described how Sir Miles was the most affectionate of men. Had the General already left Lord Wrotham's house? It was time, as his lordship himself desired to occupy it. Very well; yet consider what a loss to the neighbors!

"We miss you, we positively miss you, my dear General," cried Sir Miles. "My daughters were in love with those lovely young ladies—I swear they were! My Lady Warrington and my girls debated endlessly over how to find an opportunity to acquaint ourselves with your charming family. We feel as if we were old friends already, indeed we do, General, if you will permit me the liberty of saying so; and we love you, if I may speak frankly, on account of your friendship and kindness to our dear nephews. Though we were a little jealous, I confess a trifle jealous of them, for they visited you so often. Often and often have I said to my Lady Warrington, 'My dear, why do we not make acquaintance with the General? Why do we not invite him and his ladies to dine with some other plain country gentlemen?' Carry my most sincere respects to Mrs. Lambert, I pray, sir, and thank her for her goodness to these young gentlemen. My own flesh and blood, sir; my dear, dear brother's boys!" He passed his hand across his manly eyes, choking almost with generous and affectionate emotion.

While they discoursed—George Warrington meanwhile restraining his laughter with admirable gravity—the doors of the King's apartments opened, and the pages entered, preceding His Majesty. He was followed by his burly son, His Royal Highness the Duke, a very corpulent Prince clad in a coat and face of blazing scarlet. Behind them trailed various gentlemen and officers of state, among whom George immediately recognized the famous Mr. Secretary Pitt, by his towering stature, eagle eye, hooked nose, and grave, majestic presence. As I behold that solemn figure passing, even a hundred years removed, I protest I feel a present awe and a desire to remove my hat. I am not frightened by George II, nor are my eyes dazzled by the portentous appearance of His Royal Highness the Duke of Culloden and Fontenoy; but the Great Commoner, the terrible Cornet of Horse!
