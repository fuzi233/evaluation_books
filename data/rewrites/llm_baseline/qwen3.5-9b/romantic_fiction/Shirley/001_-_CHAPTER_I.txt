In recent times, the northern counties of England have been deluged with a prodigious rainfall of curates. They blanket the hills in dense layers; every parish boasts one or more of these young men, energetic in spirit and seemingly destined to accomplish vast amounts of good. However, our narrative does not concern itself with this modern era. We shall retreat to the dawn of the nineteenth century—those early years. The present day is dusty, sun-scalded, arid, and oppressive; let us avoid the blistering noon, seek refuge in a siesta, drift through the midday hours in slumber, and dream instead of the coming dawn.

Should you, dear reader, infer from this introduction that a romance awaits you, you would be grievously mistaken. Do you anticipate sentiment, poetry, and reverie? Do you crave passion, stimulation, and melodrama? Compose your expectations; lower them to a modest level. Before you lies something real, cool, and substantial; something as unromantic as a Monday morning, when workers awaken with the grim realization that they must rise and attend to their labors. It is not strictly denied that you may later taste excitement, perhaps toward the middle or conclusion of the feast, but it is resolved that the first course served shall be one fit for a Catholic—even an Anglo-Catholic—to consume on Good Friday during Passion Week: cold lentils and vinegar without oil; unleavened bread with bitter herbs, and no roasted lamb.

To return to the past: while recent years saw an abundance of curates, the period between 1811 and 1812 witnessed a drought. There was no Pastoral Aid, no Additional Curates' Society to extend a helping hand to weary old rectors, enabling them to hire vigorous young colleagues from Oxford or Cambridge. The current generation of apostolic successors, disciples of Dr. Pusey and instruments of the Propaganda, were then merely being hatched under cradle blankets or regenerated via nursery baptism in washbasins. One could not have looked upon any of them and recognized the Italian-ironed double frills of their net caps as surrounding the brow of a preordained successor to St. Paul, St. Peter, or St. John; nor could one have foreseen the white surplices hidden within their long nightgowns, garments they would later wield to exercise the souls of their parishioners and confound their traditional vicars by waving them aloft in the pulpit.

Yet, even in those lean days, curates existed. This precious plant was rare, but it could still be found. A favored district in the West Riding of Yorkshire could boast three rods of Aaron blossoming within a twenty-mile radius. You shall behold them, reader. Enter this neat garden-house on the outskirts of Whinbury, proceed into the small parlour. There they sit at dinner. Permit me to introduce them: Mr. Donne, curate of Whinbury; Mr. Malone, curate of Briarfield; Mr. Sweeting, curate of Nunnely. These are Mr. Donne's quarters, belonging to John Gale, a small clothier. Mr. Donne has graciously invited his brethren to dine with him. Join us, you and I, to observe what is seen and hear what is spoken. For the moment, however, they are merely eating; while they eat, we shall converse apart.

These gentlemen are in the prime of youth, possessing all the vigor of that fascinating age—a vitality their moping, elderly vicars would gladly channel into pastoral duties, often wishing to see it spent diligently supervising schools and visiting the sick. But the youthful Levites deem such tasks dull. They prefer to lavish their energies on pursuits that, to outside observers, appear heavier with ennui and more cursed by monotony than the labor of a weaver at his loom, yet which provide them with an inexhaustible supply of enjoyment and occupation.

I refer to their frantic back-and-forth movement among themselves, traveling to and from their respective lodgings—not a simple circle, but a triangular route of visits maintained throughout the year, regardless of season or weather. With incomprehensible zeal, they brave snow, hail, wind, rain, mire, and dust to dine, drink tea, or sup with one another. What draws them remains elusive. It is not friendship, for whenever they meet, they quarrel. It is not religion—the topic is never raised among them; theology may be discussed occasionally, but piety is absent. Nor is it a love of food and drink; each could enjoy equally fine joints, puddings, potent teas, and succulent toast at his own lodging as he receives at his brother's. Mrs. Gale, Mrs. Hogg, and Mrs. Whipp—their respective landladies—assert that "it is just for naught else but to give folk trouble." By "folk," the good ladies naturally mean themselves, for indeed, they are kept in a perpetual state of agitation by this system of mutual intrusion.

Mr. Donne and his guests are currently at dinner. Mrs. Gale serves them, though a spark of the hot kitchen fire burns in her eyes. She believes the privilege of hosting a friend occasionally without extra charge—a benefit included in her letting terms—has been sufficiently exercised recently. It is only Thursday, yet on Monday Mr. Malone of Briarfield came to breakfast and stayed for dinner; on Tuesday, Mr. Malone and Mr. Sweeting of Nunnely arrived for tea, lingered until supper, occupied the spare bed, and visited her again at breakfast on Wednesday morning; now, on Thursday, they are both here for dinner, and she is nearly certain they will remain overnight. "C'en est trop," she would say if she could speak French.

Mr. Sweeting minces his slice of roast beef, complaining of its toughness; Mr. Donne remarks that the beer is flat. Indeed, that is the worst of it: if they would only be civil, Mrs. Gale would not mind so much. If they merely seemed satisfied with what they received, she would not care. But "these young parsons are so high and so scornful, setting everyone beneath their feet." They treat her with less than civility precisely because she does not employ a servant but performs the household work herself, as her mother did before her. Consequently, they constantly criticize Yorkshire ways and Yorkshire people, leading Mrs. Gale to conclude that none of them are true gentlemen or of gentle birth. "The old parsons are worth the whole lot of college lads," she muses. "They know what constitutes good manners and are kind to both high and low."

"More bread!" cries Mr. Malone, in a tone that, though prolonged for only two syllables, identifies him immediately as a native of the land of shamrocks and potatoes. Mrs. Gale dislikes Mr. Malone more than either of the others, yet she fears him as well. He is a tall, powerfully built figure with genuine Irish limbs and a face authentically national—not the Milesian type, nor Daniel O'Connell's style, but the high-featured, North American Indian visage associated with a certain class of Irish gentry, possessing a petrified and proud expression better suited to the owner of slave estates than to the landlord of a free peasantry. His father called himself a gentleman; he was poor and deeply in debt, yet besottedly arrogant, and his son mirrored him perfectly.

Mrs. Gale offers the loaf.

"Cut it, woman," commands her guest. The "woman" cuts it accordingly. Had she followed her inclinations, she would have cut the parson as well; her Yorkshire soul revolts absolutely from his manner of command.

The curates have hearty appetites. Though the beef is "tough," they consume large quantities of it. They swallow a considerable amount of the "flat beer," while a dish of Yorkshire pudding and two tureens of vegetables vanish like leaves before locusts. The cheese receives distinguished attention, and a "spice-cake," served as dessert, disappears like a vision, leaving no trace. Its elegy is sung in the kitchen by Abraham, Mrs. Gale's son and heir, a youth of six summers. Having counted on inheriting the cake, he lifts up his voice and weeps bitterly when his mother brings down the empty platter.

Meanwhile, the curates sit sipping their wine, a liquor of unpretentious vintage, moderately enjoyed. Mr. Malone, indeed, would much prefer whisky, but Mr. Donne, being an Englishman, does not keep the beverage. As they sip, they argue—not on politics, philosophy, or literature (topics entirely uninteresting to them)—nor even on practical or doctrinal theology, but on minute points of ecclesiastical discipline, frivolities that seem as empty as bubbles to anyone except themselves. Mr. Malone, managing to secure two glasses of wine while his companions settle for one, gradually becomes hilarious in his own fashion: he grows slightly insolent, speaks rudely in a hectoring tone, and laughs boisterously at his own brilliance.

Each companion becomes, in turn, his target. Malone possesses a stock of jokes he dispenses regularly on such convivial occasions, rarely varying his wit; indeed, he shows no concern for monotony or the opinions of others. He favors Mr. Donne with hints regarding his extreme thinness, allusions to his upturned nose, cutting sarcasms about a threadbare chocolate-colored coat Mr. Donne wears whenever rain threatens, and critiques of a select collection of Cockney phrases and pronunciations unique to Mr. Donne, which he praises for the elegance they add to his style.
