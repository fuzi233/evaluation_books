The cool, sun-dappled church, its vaulted ceiling painted in hues of celestial purple, wrapped Father Clement in its serene embrace—just as it did Margaret. But for him, it was more than beauty; it was a silent hymn to the Creator of all things good and pure. His gaze drifted over the crowded nave, where a sea of white caps, edged with delicate gold filigree, stretched before him. He had wandered far, yet nowhere had he seen anything like these—save perhaps fields of snow.

That morning, his voice had thundered with divine wrath, but now, beneath the gentle afternoon light, such fire seemed out of place. His heart swelled with compassion for the flock before him—so hungry for the love of God, so seldom fed. And so, he spoke not of judgment, but of grace, his words tender, earnest, weaving the glad tidings of salvation into the quiet air. The people listened, rapt, for he was no whirlwind preacher, no pulpit weathervane spinning with every gust of rhetoric. He moved souls, not limbs.

Yet he was mindful of those in shadowed corners, and soon, aflame with the desire that none should miss the message, he turned toward the south aisle.

And there—bathed in a shaft of golden light—stood Margaret Brandt.

For a moment, his mind refused to grasp it. The sight struck him numb. Then, like a bolt, recognition tore through him. His voice faltered. His hands trembled.

There she was, her auburn hair aflame in the sunlight, haloed like a saint’s, her face aglow with life—no specter, no memory, but flesh and blood. She leaned lightly against a pillar, her lashes lowered as she listened, just as she had listened to him long ago. She had not changed. Time had only deepened her beauty, ripened it like summer fruit.

His breath seized. The congregation blurred into nothingness. A rustling murmur rose around him, but he heard none of it. His whole being was fixed upon her, waiting for the vision to dissolve.

But she remained.

Then—swift as a spark—her eyes met his.

_Violet eyes. Hers._

A cry rose in his throat, her name trembling on his lips—but before it could escape, she turned away, vanishing into the crowd. Yet not before one last fleeting glance, quick as lightning, struck him to the core. It was not recognition, not yet—but something tremulous, questioning, the silent heartbeat before the storm.

His hands clenched the pulpit. His mind reeled. He muttered something incoherent, then forced himself to stumble onward, his sermon fraying into broken threads. But as the shock ebbed, a feverish energy took hold. If this was no vision, then it was a sign—a revelation. She had died. She had been buried here, near this very pillar. Why else would she appear?

When the service ended, he descended, shaken, but with a new purpose. He would find her grave.

The church stood empty now. Stepping out into the churchyard, he approached the sexton—a man he knew, though the man did not know him. The beard that had once framed his face was gone, his cheeks thinned by fasting, his hair cropped and threaded with premature grey.

"My son," Clement said softly, "if you remember those laid to rest here, tell me—is anyone buried inside the church, near the pillars?"

The sexton—Jorian—shook his head. "Nay, father. All lie here in the yard. Why?"

Clement hesitated. "Then... where is Margaret Brandt buried?"

Jorian frowned. "Margaret Brandt? Died three years back, you say?"

"Yes. From Sevenbergen."

Jorian scratched his head, then snorted. "Either you're jesting with me, father, or someone's jested with you. There's no Margaret Brandt buried here—leastways, not in my time."

Clement's breath caught. "But... she was poor. Her father was Peter, the physician. She came here—to die."

Jorian's expression darkened. He planted his spade firmly. "Now see here, holy sir—are you fooling me, or has some wag fooled you?" His tone was sharp, but then, seeing the friar's stricken face, he relented. "Ah, forgive me. Mayhap you mean Peter Brandt’s grave? He lies by the west door. I’ll show you."

He pulled on his doublet, ready to lead the way—unaware that someone already waited there, watching.

And unaware that the past was not so easily buried.
