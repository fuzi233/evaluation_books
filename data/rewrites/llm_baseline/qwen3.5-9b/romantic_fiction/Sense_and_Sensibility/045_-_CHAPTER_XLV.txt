For some time after Willoughby departed, and indeed long after the rumble of his carriage faded into the distance, Elinor remained overwhelmed by a tumult of conflicting thoughts. While these ideas varied greatly in nature, they all converged upon a single, heavy emotion: sorrow. In this state, she found it impossible to turn her mind to her sister's welfare.

It was strange how quickly her perspective had shifted. Less than an hour prior, she had despised Willoughby as the most despicable of men. Now, despite his glaring faults, she felt a profound pity for the suffering they had caused him. This compassion led her to view him as forever severed from her family, evoking a tenderness and regret that she soon admitted to herself were driven more by his own desperate wishes than by any redeeming qualities he possessed. She recognized that his hold on her mind was strengthened by factors that reason dictated should carry no weight: his striking appearance, his open, affectionate, and vivacious demeanor—which were hardly virtues—and the lingering, passionate love he bore for Marianne, a sentiment that was scarcely innocent to harbor. Yet, she knew this influence would linger long before she could truly diminish it.

When she finally returned upstairs to find Marianne, she discovered her sister just awakening, revitalized by a deep and peaceful slumber that matched the height of her hopes. Elinor's heart swelled with emotion. The convergence of the past, the present, and the future—the memory of Willoughby's visit, Marianne's safe return, and her mother's imminent arrival—threw her into such an agitated state of spirits that she felt no fatigue, only a fierce determination not to reveal her turmoil to her sister.

However, this fear of exposure lasted barely half an hour. Soon, the sound of another carriage announced its approach downstairs. Driven by a desperate need to spare her mother any further moments of agonizing uncertainty, Elinor rushed into the hall and reached the outer door just in time to catch and steady her mother as she stumbled inside. Mrs. Dashwood, whose terror during the journey had nearly convinced her that Marianne was dead, possessed no voice for questions—not for Elinor, nor for anyone else. Instead, Elinor, offering neither greeting nor inquiry, immediately delivered the joyful news. Her mother seized this tidbit with her characteristic warmth, and in an instant, she was as overcome by happiness as she had been previously by fear. Supported between her daughter and her friend, Mrs. Dashwood shed tears of joy, though still unable to speak. She embraced Elinor repeatedly, then turned to press Colonel Brandon's hand, her gaze speaking volumes of gratitude and the certainty that he shared in their bliss. Brandon shared it, albeit in a silence even deeper than her own.

Once Mrs. Dashwood regained her composure, her first impulse was to see Marianne. Within two minutes, she was beside her beloved child, who had become dearer to her than ever through the trials of absence, unhappiness, and danger. Elinor's delight at witnessing the reunion was tempered only by the fear that it might rob Marianne of further rest. Yet, Mrs. Dashwood, capable of prudence when a child's life hung in the balance, remained calm. Satisfied by her mother's presence and aware of her own weakness, Marianne readily submitted to the enforced silence and quiet prescribed by the nurses surrounding her. Mrs. Dashwood insisted on staying awake with her throughout the night; consequently, Elinor, complying with her mother's plea, retired to her room.

But the rest that one sleepless night and hours of exhausting anxiety demanded was denied her by irritability of spirit. "Poor Willoughby" became a constant refrain in her thoughts. She found herself torn between wanting to hear his defense and blaming herself for judging him so harshly beforehand. Every thought oscillated between condemnation and acquittal. However, the promise she had made to relate his story to her sister remained a source of pain. She dreaded telling Marianne, fearing the impact it might have on her sister's heart. For a fleeting moment, she even wished Willoughby were a widower, hoping to spare him further grief. Then, recalling Colonel Brandon, she chided herself, realizing that the reward of her sister's affection belonged far more to Brandon's sufferings and constancy than to his rival's. She wished for nothing more than Mrs. Willoughby's survival.

The shock of Colonel Brandon's mission at Barton had been significantly softened for Mrs. Dashwood by her own preceding alarm. So great was her unease regarding Marianne that she had already resolved to travel to Cleveland that very day, without awaiting further news. She had arranged her journey so thoroughly that the Careys were expected to fetch Margaret away at any moment, as her mother refused to take her anywhere where infection might lurk.

Marianne improved daily, and the radiant cheerfulness of Mrs. Dashwood's countenance and spirits testified to her claim of being one of the happiest women in the world. Elinor could not listen to such declarations or witness their proof without occasionally wondering if her mother ever recalled Edward. Yet, Mrs. Dashwood, trusting in the temperate account Elinor had sent, was swept away by the exuberance of her joy, focusing solely on what would amplify it further. Marianne had been rescued from a peril in which, as she now realized, her own misguided judgment in fostering the attachment to Willoughby had played a part. Furthermore, in her recovery, Mrs. Dashwood found another source of joy entirely unexpected by Elinor.

As soon as privacy allowed, the mother confided in her daughter. "At last we are alone, my Elinor. You do not yet know all my happiness. Colonel Brandon loves Marianne. He has told me so himself."

Her daughter, feeling a mixture of pleasure and pain, surprise and lack thereof, listened in total silence. "You are never like me, dear Elinor," her mother continued, "or I should marvel at your composure now. If I had sat down to wish for any possible good for my family, I would have chosen Colonel Brandon marrying one of you as the most desirable outcome. And I believe Marianne will be the happier of the two."

Elinor was half-inclined to ask why she thought so, knowing that no argument based on an impartial assessment of their ages, characters, or feelings could support such a notion. But her mother was always carried away by her imagination on matters of interest. Thus, Elinor merely smiled and let the question pass.

"He opened his whole heart to me yesterday while we traveled," Mrs. Dashwood explained. "It happened quite unexpectedly, quite unintentionally. I, you may believe, could talk of nothing but my child; he could not conceal his distress. I saw that his pain equaled my own. Perhaps, thinking that mere friendship—as the world currently defines it—would not justify such warm sympathy, or perhaps simply giving way to irresistible feelings, he made me acquainted with his earnest, tender, and constant affection for Marianne. He has loved her, my Elinor, since the very first moment he saw her."

Here, Elinor perceived that she was hearing not the actual words or professions of Colonel Brandon, but the natural embellishments of her mother's active fancy, fashioning everything to suit her delight. "His regard for her," Mrs. Dashwood exclaimed, "infinitely surpasses anything Willoughby ever felt or feigned. It is far warmer, more sincere, and more constant—however we choose to label it. It has endured through all knowledge of dear Marianne's unhappy infaturation with that worthless young man! Without selfishness, without encouraging false hope! Could he have seen her happy with another? Such a noble mind! Such openness, such sincerity! No one can be deceived by him."

"Colonel Brandon's character," Elinor interjected gently, "as an excellent man, is well established."

"I know it is," replied her mother seriously. "Or, after such a warning, I should be the last to encourage such affection, or even to be pleased by it. But his coming for me as he did, with such active and ready friendship, is sufficient proof that he is one of the worthiest of men."

"His character, however," answered Elinor, "does not rest on a single act of kindness, to which his affection for Marianne, were humanity absent, would have prompted him. To Mrs. Jennings, to the Middletons, he has been long and intimately known; they equally love and respect him. Even my own knowledge of him, though recently acquired, is considerable. I value and esteem him so highly that if Marianne can be happy with him, I shall be as ready as yourself to consider our connection the greatest blessing in the world. What answer did you give him? Did you allow him to hope?"

"Oh, my love!" cried her mother. "I could not then speak of hope to him or to myself. Marianne might have been dying at that moment. But he did not ask for hope or encouragement. His was an involuntary confidence, an irrepressible effusion to a soothing friend, not an application to a parent. Yet, after a time, I *did* say—for at first I was quite overcome—that if she lived, as I trusted she might, my greatest happiness would lie in promoting their marriage. Since our arrival, since our delightful security, I have repeated this to him more fully, giving him every encouragement in my power. 'Time,' I tell him, 'will do everything; Marianne's heart is not to be wasted forever on such a man as Willoughby. His own merits must soon secure it.'"
