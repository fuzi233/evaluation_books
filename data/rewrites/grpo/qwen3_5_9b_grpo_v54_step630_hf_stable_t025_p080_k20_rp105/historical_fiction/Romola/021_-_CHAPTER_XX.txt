The final week of the Carnival had swollen the veins of Florence to bursting. The streets thrummed with a chaotic vitality, a cacophony of masqued processions chanting songs introduced by Lorenzo the Magnificent, and the *rigoletto*, a favored round dance footed beneath a pale, frosty sky. Practical jokes ranged from the harmless scattering of comfits to the malicious hurling of stones. The boys and striplings, a formidable element in Florentine crowds, had reached the height of their unmanageable energy, acting like tree-crickets in their noise. Their immemorial privilege was to bar the path with poles until a tribute was paid for suppers and bonfires, culminating in the standing entertainment of stone-throwing. This was not a monotonous affair; the resulting maiming varied, and it was not uncommon for a single casualty to be killed. Thus, the pleasures of the Carnival were checkered, a grotesque tapestry of grossness and barbarity that a painter would struggle to render without turning the canvas to the wall, save for the grave historical purpose of justifying a reforming zeal often condemned for its narrowness. Yet, amidst this, there remained a more innocent, picturesque merriment, born of a people with quick animal spirits and sensitive organs, lacking the heavy sottishness of the north or the stealthy fierceness of the south that turns a brawl into a dagger-thrust.

It was high morning, yet the carnival spirits lingered in a lazy recapitulation of the night's jests. Tito Melema walked briskly toward the Via de' Bardi, accompanied by Young Bernardo Dovizi—the keen-eyed Cardinal da Bibbiena of later portraits. They held an animated discourse on a subject seemingly unrelated to the sights and sounds pushing past them along the Por' Santa Maria. Yet, as they discussed, smiled, and gesticulated, both cast frequent, quick glances around. At the turn toward the Lung' Arno, leading to the Ponte Rubaconte, Tito's rapid survey revealed someone he desperately wished to avoid recognition at that precise moment. His mind was thoroughly preoccupied; he looked forward to a unique occasion in his life: his betrothal, set for the evening. The ceremony had been resolved upon suddenly. While preparations had been underway for some time—chiefly Tito's application of florins to furnish rooms in Bardo's dwelling, which had always been scantily furnished save for the library—it had been intended to defer both betrothal and marriage until after Easter, when Tito's year of probation, insisted upon by Bernardo del Nero, would conclude. However, an express proposition arrived inviting Tito to follow Cardinal Giovanni to Rome to assist Bernardo Dovizi with his superior knowledge of Greek in arranging a library. With no possibility of declining what lay so plainly on the road to advancement, Tito became urgent in his entreaties that the betrothal occur before his departure. There would be less delay upon his return, and it would be less painful to part if he and Romola were outwardly as well as inwardly pledged, possessing a claim that defied Messer Bernardo or anyone else to nullify. For the betrothal, with rings exchanged and mutual contracts signed, constituted more than half the legality of marriage, to be completed later by the nuptial benediction. Romola's feelings met Tito's in this wish, and the consent of the elders was won.

Now, Tito hastened, amidst arrangements for his departure the next day, to snatch a morning visit to Romola, to exchange any last words needed before their evening meeting. It was not a time when any recognition could be pleasant, let alone one by Tessa. It was unmistakably Tessa whom he caught sight of moving with a timid, forlorn look toward that very turn of the Lung' Arno. As he continued his talk with the young Dovizi, an uncomfortable undercurrent of consciousness told him Tessa had seen him and would certainly follow. There was no escaping her along this direct road by the Arno and over the Ponte Rubaconte. She would not dare to speak or approach while he was not alone, so he continued to keep Dovizi with him until they reached Bardo's door. He quickened his pace, weaving new threads of conversation, yet the sense that Tessa trailed behind him, though physically unseen, grew stronger and more irritating. Perhaps it was worse because a certain tenderness and pity for the poor little thing made the determination to escape without visible notice an unagreeable resource. Yet Tito persevered, carrying his companion to the door, cleverly managing his "addio" without turning his face in a direction where importunate blue eyes might catch him. Ascending the stone steps, he tried to banish unpleasant thoughts by convincing himself that Tessa might not have seen him, or, if she had, might not have followed.

But perhaps because that possibility could not be relied upon strongly, when the visit ended, he emerged with a quick step and an air of unconsciousness regarding anything to his right or left. Human eyes, however, take in a wide angle without asking leave of the will, and Tito knew there was a little figure in a white hood standing near the doorway. He knew it quite well before he felt a hand laid firmly on his arm. It was a real grasp, not a light, timid touch; for poor Tessa, seeing his rapid step, had started forward with a desperate effort. When he stopped and turned, her face wore a frightened look, as if she dreaded the effect of her boldness.

"Tessa!" Tito said, with more sharpness in his voice than she had ever heard. "Why are you here? You must not follow me—you must not stand about door-places waiting for me."

Her blue eyes widened with tears, and she said nothing. Tito feared something worse than ridicule if he were seen in the Via de' Bardi with a girlish *contadina* looking pathetically at him. It was a street of high, silent dwellings, not of traffic, but Bernardo del Nero, or someone almost as dangerous, might appear at any moment. Even if it had not been the day of his betrothal, the incident would have been awkward and annoying. Yet it would be brutal—impossible—to drive Tessa away with harsh words. That accursed folly with the *cerretano* should have lain buried for months and now spring up as this unseasonable crop of vexation! He could not speak harshly, but he spoke hurriedly.

"Tessa, I cannot—must not talk to you here. I will go on to the bridge and wait for you there. Follow me slowly."

He turned and walked fast to the Ponte Rubaconte, leaning against the wall of a quaint little house rising at even distances on the bridge, looking toward the way Tessa would come. It would have softened a much harder heart than Tito's to see the little thing advancing, her round face much paled and saddened since he had parted from it at the door of the "Nunziata." Happily, it was the least frequented of the bridges, and there were scarcely any passengers. He lost no time in speaking as she approached.

"Now, Tessa, I have very little time. You must not cry. Why did you follow me this morning? You must not do so again."

"I thought," Tessa whispered, struggling against a sob that rose immediately at the new timbre of Tito's voice, "I thought you wouldn't be so long before you came to take care of me again. And the *patrigno* beats me, and I can't bear it any longer. And always when I come for a holiday I walk about to find you, and I can't. Oh, please don't send me away from you again! It has been so long, and I cry so now, because you never come to me. I can't help it, for the days are so long, and I don't mind about the goats and kids, or anything—and I can't—"

Sobs came fast now, accompanied by great tears. Tito felt he could do nothing but comfort her. Send her away—yes, he must do that, at once. But it was all the more impossible to tell her anything that would leave her in a state of hopeless grief. He saw new trouble in the background, but the difficulty of the moment was too pressing to weigh distant consequences.

"Tessa, my little one," he said in his old caressing tones, "you must not cry. Bear with the cross *patrigno* a little longer. I will come back to you. But I'm going now to Rome—a long, long way off. I shall come back in a few weeks, and then I promise you to come and see you. Promise me to be good and wait for me."

It was the well-remembered voice again, and the mere sound was half enough to soothe Tessa. She looked up at him with trusting eyes, still glittering with tears, sobbing all the while despite her utmost efforts to obey. Again he said, gently, "Promise me, my Tessa."

"Yes," she whispered. "But you won't be long?"

"No, not long. But I must *go now*. And remember what I told you, Tessa. Nobody must know that you ever see me, else you will lose me for ever. And now, when I have left you, go straight home, and never follow me again. Wait till I come to you. Good-bye, my little Tessa: I *will* come."

There was no help for it; he must turn and leave her without looking behind to see how she bore it, for he had no time to spare. When he did look round, he was in the Via de' Benci, where he could not see what was happening on the bridge; but Tessa was too trusting and obedient not to do exactly what he had told her.

Yes, the difficulty was at an end for that day, yet this return of Tessa, at a moment when it was impossible to put an end to all difficulty by undeceiving her, was an unpleasant incident to carry in his memory. But Tito's mind was just now thoroughly penetrated with a hopeful first love, associated with all happy prospects flattering to his ambition; and that future necessity of grieving Tessa could be scarcely more to him than the far-off cry of some little suffering animal buried in the thicket to a merry cavalcade in the sunny plain. When, for the second time that day, Tito hastened across the Ponte Rubaconte, the thought of Tessa caused no perceptible diminution of his happiness. He was well muffled in his mantle, less perhaps to protect him from the cold than from the additional notice that would have been drawn upon him by his dainty apparel. He leaped up the stone steps by two at a time and said hurriedly to Maso, who met him, "Where is the damigella?"

"In the library; she is quite ready, and Monna Brigida and Messer Bernardo are already there with Ser Braccio, but none of the rest of the company."

"Ask her to give me a few minutes alone; I will await her in the *salotto*."

Tito entered a room fitted up in the utmost contrast to the half-pallid, half-sombre tints of the library. The walls were brightly frescoed with "caprices" of nymphs and loves sporting under the blue among flowers and birds. The only furniture besides the red leather seats and the central table were two tall white vases and a young faun playing the flute, modeled by a promising youth named Michelangelo Buonarotti. It was a room that gave a sense of being in the sunny open air.

Tito kept his mantle round him and looked toward the door. Not long after, Romola entered, all white and gold, more than ever like a tall lily. Her white silk garment was bound by a golden girdle falling with large tassels, and above that was the rippling gold of her hair, surmounted by the white mist of her long veil, fastened on her brow by a band of pearls—the gift of Bernardo del Nero—which was now parted from her face so that it all floated backward.

"Regina mia!" said Tito, taking her hand and kissing it, still keeping his mantle round him. He could not help going backward to look at her again, while she stood in calm delight, with that exquisite self-consciousness which rises under the gaze of admiring love.

"Romola, will you show me the next room now?" Tito said, checking himself with the remembrance that time might be short. "You said I should see it when you had arranged everything."

Without speaking, she led the way into a long, narrow room painted brightly like the other, but only with birds and flowers. The furniture was all old; there were old, faded objects for feminine use or ornament arranged in an open cabinet between the two narrow windows. Above the cabinet hung the portrait of Romola's mother, and below this, on top of the cabinet, stood the crucifix Romola had brought from San Marco.

"I have brought something under my mantle," Tito said, smiling. Throwing off the large loose garment, he showed the little tabernacle painted by Piero di Cosimo. The painter had carried out Tito's intention charmingly, thus far atoning for his long delay. "Do you know what this is for, my Romola?" he added, taking her hand and leading her toward the cabinet. "It is a little shrine, which is to hide away from you for ever that remembrancer of sadness. You have done with sadness now; and we will bury all images of it—bury them in a tomb of joy. See!"

A slight quiver passed across Romola's face as Tito took hold of the crucifix. But she had no wish to prevent his purpose; on the contrary, she herself wished to subdue certain importunate memories and questionings which still flitted like unexplained shadows across her happier thought.

He opened the triptych and placed the crucifix within the central space; then, closing it again, he took out the key, set the little tabernacle in the spot where the crucifix had stood, and said, "Now, Romola, look and see if you are satisfied with the portraits old Piero has made of us. Is it not a dainty device? and the credit of choosing it is mine."
