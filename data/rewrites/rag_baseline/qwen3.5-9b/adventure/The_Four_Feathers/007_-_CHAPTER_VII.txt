"No one," said Durrance, his voice cutting through the humid stillness. With a decisive click, he secured his field-glasses into the leather case at his hip. "No one, sir," Captain Mather replied, his tone matching the gravity of the moment. "Then we advance."

The scouts surged forward, breaking the silence, while the main body of troops reformed into a disciplined phalanx. Behind them, the thunderous rhythm of two seven-pounder mountain guns rolled into position, their barrels gleaming in the pale dawn. From the gloomy, shadowed ridges of Khor Gwob—thirty-five miles southwest of Suakin—the detachment of the Camel Corps began its descent, plunging into the vast, sun-drenched plateau of Sinkat. This was the final reconnaissance in force before the inevitable evacuation of the eastern Sudan, a grim prelude to departure.

For hours, the camels had labored slowly up the gullies of shale, navigating between red precipitous rocks that seemed to scrape the sky. As the terrain opened up, revealing heaps of crumbling stone and a desolation of rubble, the landscape offered no mercy. There was no patch of grass, no ragged branch of mimosa to break the monotonous horror of the ruin. Yet, as the arid journey concluded, the valley below revealed the green, verdant bushes of Sinkat, offering a deceptive comfort, a false semblance of a manicured park amidst the devastation.

The troopers sat their saddles with heightened alertness, their senses sharpened by the transition from rock to soil. They moved in a diagonal line across the plateau, heading toward the distant mountains of Erkoweet—a silent company traversing a plain that held its own profound silence. It was eleven o'clock. The sun ascended toward the center of a colorless, cloudless sky, casting shadows that shortened rapidly upon the sand until the earth itself glistened white, stark and bleached like a beach of the Scilly Islands. There was no draft of air to stir the rich foliage; the shadows of the branches lay so distinct and motionless upon the ground that they appeared to be dead limbs strewn there by a forgotten storm.

The only sounds were the sharp, metallic clank of weapons, the soft, ceaseless padding of camel hooves, and occasionally the whirr of pigeons startled by the approaching cavalcade. Yet, life pulsed on the plateau, though in a noiseless, ancient manner. As the leaders rode along the curves of sand—trim and smooth between the shrubs like carriage drives—they would witness herds of gazelles starting abruptly from the earth. These creatures would race silently, flashes of dappled brown and white, fleeing toward the enclosing hills. It seemed that in this last hour, the country itself had been conjured anew.

"Yet this way the caravans passed southwards to Erkoweet and the Khor Baraka," Durrance said aloud, voicing the thought circling in his mind. "Here the Suakis built their summer-houses."

"And there," Mather pointed forward, his finger tracing a line across the horizon, "Tewfik fought, and died with his four hundred men."

For three hours, the troops marched across the plateau. It was May, and the sun blazed upon them with an intolerable, physical weight. Their initial alertness had long since evaporated, replaced by a rocking drowsiness. They sat in their saddles, praying for the cooling evening and the silver shine of stars. The camels minced onward with their peculiar, smirking motions of the head, until suddenly, a hundred yards ahead, Durrance spotted a broken wall with window-spaces letting the sky through.

"The fort," he declared.

Three years had passed since Osman Digna had captured and destroyed it, yet during those years, its roofless ruins had sustained another siege—one no less persistent. Quick-growing trees had so closely girt and encroached upon it from the rear and sides that the traveler approached it unexpectedly, much like Childe Roland encountering the Dark Tower in the plain. In the front, however, the sand still stretched open to the wells, where three great Gemeiza trees of dark, spreading foliage stood spaced like sentinels guarding the gate.

In the shadow to the right-front of the fort, where bushes fringed the open sand with the level regularity of a riverbank, the soldiers unsaddled their camels and prepared their food. Durrance and Captain Mather walked around the perimeter. As they reached the southern corner, Durrance halted.

"Hallo!"

"Some Arab has camped here," Mather observed, stopping in his tracks.

"The grey ashes of a wood-fire lie in a little heap upon a blackened stone," Durrance noted. "And recently."

Mather walked on, mounting a few rough steps to the crumbled archway of the entrance, passing into the unroofed corridors and rooms. Durrance turned the ashes over with his boot. The stump of a charred, whitened twig glowed faintly red. Durrance stepped upon it, and a tiny thread of smoke spurted into the air.

"Very lately," he muttered to himself, following Mather inside.

Young trees sprouted in the corners of the mud walls, in every fissure, and even from the very floor. Rearward, a steep glacis and a deep fosse defended the works. Durrance sat himself down upon the parapet of the wall above the glacis, while pigeons wheeled and circled overhead. He thought of the long months during which Tewfik must have daily strained his eyes from this very spot toward the pass over the hills from Suakin, looking as that other general far to the south had done, searching vainly for the sunlight flashing on the weapons of the help that never came.

Mather sat beside him, reflecting in a quite different spirit. "Already the Guards are steaming out through the coral reefs toward Suez. A week, and our turn comes," he said. "What a God-forsaken country!"

"I come back to it," Durrance replied simply.

"Why?"

"I like it. I like the people."

Mather considered the taste unaccountable, yet he knew that, however inexplicable in itself, it accounted for his companion's rapid promotion and success. Sympathy had stood Durrance in the stead of much raw ability. Sympathy had granted him patience and the power to understand, allowing him to leave far quicker and far abler men behind him during these three years of campaign, possessing a deeper knowledge of the sorely harassed tribes of the eastern Sudan. He liked them; he could enter into their hatred of the old Turkish rule, comprehend their fanaticism, and understand their pretended zeal under the compulsion of Osman Digna's hordes.

"Yes, I shall come back," Durrance said firmly. "In three months' time. For one thing, we know—and every Englishman in Egypt knows—that this cannot be the end. I want to be here when the work is taken in hand again. I hate unfinished things."

The sun beat relentlessly upon the plateau; the men, stretched in the shade, slept. The afternoon was as noiseless as the morning. Durrance and Mather sat for some while, compelled to silence by the overwhelming quiet surrounding them. But Durrance's eyes eventually turned away from the amphitheater of hills; they lost their abstraction, becoming intently fixed upon the shrubbery beyond the glacis. He was no longer recalling Tewfik Bey and his heroic defense, nor speculating upon the work to be done in the years ahead. Without turning his head, he saw that Mather was gazing in the same direction.

"What are you thinking about?" he asked suddenly.

Mather laughed, a thoughtful sound. "I was drawing up the menu of the first dinner I will have when I reach London. I will eat it alone, I think, quite alone, and at Epitaux. It will begin with a watermelon. And you?"

"I was wondering why, now that the pigeons have grown accustomed to our presence, they should still be wheeling in and out of one particular tree. Don't point to it, please! I mean the tree beyond the ditch, and to the right of two small bushes."

All about them, pigeons were quietly perched upon the branches, spotting the foliage like purple fruit. Only above the single tree did they circle, timorous and calling.

"We will draw that covert," Durrance ordered calmly. "Take a dozen men and surround it quietly."

He himself remained on the glacis, watching the tree and the thick undergrowth. He saw six soldiers creep round the shrubbery from the left, six more from the right. But before they could meet and ring the tree in, he saw the branches violently shaken. An Arab, with a roll of yellowish dammar wound about his waist and armed with a flat-headed spear and a shield of hide, dashed from the shelter and raced out between the soldiers into the open plain.

He ran for only a few yards. For Mather gave a sharp order to his men, and the Arab, as though he understood the command perfectly, came to a halt before a rifle could be lifted to a shoulder. He walked quietly back to Mather. He was brought up onto the glacis, where he stood before Durrance without insolence or servility.

He explained in Arabic that he was a man of the Kabbabish tribe named Abou Fatma, and friendly to the English. He was on his way to Suakin.
