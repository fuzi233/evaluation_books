Dinner was in full swing when Arthur arrived, earning himself a scolding from Lady Rockminster for his tardiness. But Laura, ever observant, caught the pallor of his face and the tremor in his hands.

"You look dreadful," she whispered. "What’s happened?"

Arthur downed a glass of sherry in one gulp. "Extraordinary news," he muttered, glancing at the servants. "Later."

Throughout the meal, he fidgeted like a man awaiting execution.

"For heaven’s sake, stop drumming your feet!" Lady Rockminster snapped. "You’ve nearly crushed Fido and upended his cream. Observe how Mr. Warrington sits—like a civilized person."

By dessert—which felt interminable—their hostess had reached her limit. "This has been the dullest dinner in history. Clearly, you’ve business with Laura. I shall retire. No tea, thank you. Goodnight, Mr. Warrington—do call again when less preoccupied." With a regal toss of her head, she departed.

As Warrington made his farewells, Arthur gripped his arm. "Stay, George. You should hear this too. I don’t know what to do."

Laura’s pulse raced. "It’s about Blanche, isn’t it?"

Arthur nodded grimly. "When I went to my uncle’s lodgings, his servant Morgan—now mysteriously wealthy—informed me that my uncle had moved to this hotel. But that wasn’t the bombshell. Brace yourselves—Blanche’s father isn’t dead. The man’s an escaped convict. Clavering’s marriage to the Begum is void, and my uncle’s been blackmailing him with the secret to secure my parliamentary seat."

Warrington whistled. "So Blanche inherits nothing, and her father is—"

"—Altamont," Arthur finished. "Precisely. And Morgan, the cockroach, tried to sell me his silence for an annuity."

Laura’s hands clenched. "Blanche doesn’t know?"

"No. She thinks her father drowned honorably. Lady Clavering hinted at past misery but never this."

Warrington exhaled sharply. "You must break it off, Pen. No explanations—just cite ‘family reasons.’ Better they think you a cad than know the truth."

Laura whirled on him. "Would you abandon Blanche for her father’s crimes? If you’d married her, would you cast her off?" Her voice wavered. "Our mother wouldn’t have."

Arthur shuddered. "Our mother was a saint."

"And you’ll be one too," Laura said fiercely, embracing him. "Do what’s right."

Warrington groaned into his hands. *She’s right. That girl could steer a ship through hell.*

Before tea could salvage the evening, a servant announced Major Pendennis’s return. Laura shot Arthur a look—*Be firm, but kind*—and vanished.

The Major, hunched by the fire, grumbled about Morgan’s betrayal. "Damned ingrate! Made a fortune off me, then challenged me to a duel. I nearly knifed him." He rambled about tea, the decline of gentlemen, and—with sudden pathos—bequeathing Arthur his mother’s Bible. "You’ll find it when I’m gone," he sniffed.

Arthur steeled himself. "Uncle, Morgan told me everything."

The Major flinched. "Ah. The Amory business."

"You knew. You let me become engaged to a convict’s daughter."

"Nonsense! She’s Clavering’s heiress. Amory’s dead—legally, at least. Why spoil things?"

Arthur recoiled. "You’d have me profit from this lie?"

The Major wheedled, then ranted, then—horribly—dropped to his knees. "Don’t throw it all away! The title, the income—I beg you!"

Arthur hauled him up, sickened. "I’ll marry her. But no blood money. No seat bought with blackmail."

The Major collapsed into his chair. "Ring the bell," he croaked. "I’m done. Wolsey was right—*had I but served my God...*" He waved a limp hand. "Goodnight, nephew. Don’t bother visiting."

By morning, the old man had taken to his bed, refusing to see Arthur. The battle was over—and the victor felt no triumph.
