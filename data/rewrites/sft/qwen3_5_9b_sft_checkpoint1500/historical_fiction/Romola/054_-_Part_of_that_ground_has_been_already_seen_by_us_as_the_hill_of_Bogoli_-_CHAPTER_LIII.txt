"I desire to speak with you," Baldassarre said, breaking the silence as Romola regarded him with a quiet, suspended hope. It was evident he had trailed her, waiting patiently for this exact moment. "At last, you intend to learn the truth."

"Yes," she replied, offering a compliance that mirrored the penitent bowing of a soul under sentence. "Yet you seek a place where no ear may catch our words?"

"Where *he* shall not stumble upon us," Baldassarre murmured, glancing nervously over his shoulder. "Out, into the open air, away from the crowded streets."

"I occasionally visit San Miniato at this hour," Romola offered. "If you will, let us depart together. The path is long, yet it affords solitude."

He bowed slightly. Romola turned and walked. To a lesser spirit, venturing alone with a man whose countenance bore the marks of the madness Tito accused him of, would have been terrifying. But Romola harbored no fear for her own safety; rather, she welcomed the distance, viewing it as a buffer against the next blow fate intended to strike.

As twilight deepened and the sun dipped low in the western sky, she halted on rugged ground beneath the shadow of ancient cypresses, scanning the slopes for Baldassarre. He approached, weary, and collapsed onto a ledge of stony earth. His robust frame, once so vigorous when he first appeared coiled with the rope in the Duomo, now sagged. The climb had drawn out a trembling frailty in his step, and his gaze held a vacant, helpless mistiness.

"The ascent is steep," Romola said, her tone soft with compassion as she sat beside him. "Pray, tell me if hunger has sapped your strength."

He twisted his head, fixing his stare upon her, yet when the moment of speech arrived, his tongue failed him. He struggled to form the words that carried his burden, leaving Romola motionless, lest any sign of impatience betray her heart. To the untrained eye, he appeared merely a common, neglected old man. Yet, having grown accustomed to the sorrows of the downtrodden, she pondered deeply the weight of his troubles. Slowly, a focus returned to his eyes, sharpening into a sudden, emphatic resolve.

"Aye!" he cried abruptly. "You would have been my daughter!"

A flash of crimson swept across Romola's cheeks, vanishing almost instantly, leaving her lips pale and parted, like stone carved in terror. In that instant, the veil lifted. She read the history written in that single phrase. Before she could curb the impulse, her mind, honed by intimate knowledge of Tito's nature, leaped to the conclusion. This face, these eyes—they belonged to the man who had raised Tito.

Her emotional reaction stirred Baldassarre. For the first time, his words struck their mark. He pressed forward, his voice gaining heat and conviction, his hand clamping onto her arm.

"You carry blood of high lineage, do you not? You listen to the preacher; you despise baseness—the kind that grins and triumphs. Do you hate your husband?"

"Oh, God!" Romola whispered, her voice barely audible, consumed by the ghosts of the past and blind to Baldassarre's pressing inquiry. "Was he truly your son? Or did you take him as a babe, as Tito claims?"

"He knows the truth!" Baldassarre exclaimed, tightening his grip as if physical contact fueled his fading light. "Do you aid me?"

"Yes," Romola answered, misunderstanding the gravity of his plea. She laid her warm palm gently over his rough, grasping hand, tears welling in her eyes. "O, it is pitiful! Speak, sir—you were a great scholar, a teacher. What became of you?"

She fell silent. Tito's accusation of insanity echoed in her mind, and where were the traces of former refinement? Still, she possessed enough self-mastery to keep her hand steady, sitting quietly, listening with newfound vigilance.

"It is lost! All is gone!" Baldassarre lamented, his voice heavy with despair. "They would not listen, for he lied, calling me mad, and had me dragged to prison. Now I am old, and my mind returns to nothing. The whole world stands against me."

He paused, his eyes sinking as if drowning under a wave of melancholy, before lifting them again with a desperate fervor. "But *you* stand with me. He won your love, yet he has betrayed you. You hate him. Indeed, he won *my* love, too! He was beautiful and gentle, while I was a lonely soul. I took him when they beat him. He slept in my arms as an infant, and I watched his growth, pouring every ounce of my knowledge into him. I gave him all I had—money, books, jewels. He wore my gems, sold them, and left me in bondage. He never sought me. When I returned, broken and wretched, he denied me. He called me a lunatic."

"He told us his father perished, drowned," Romola said softly, her voice faint. "Surely, he must have believed it then. O, he could not have been so vile *then*!"

A vision rose of Tito in those early days, when she knew no evil of him save as a child sees poison in flowers. A deep, aching regret softened the horror, bringing a sob that tore through her.

"Aye, you are young, and tears flow freely," Baldassarre chided, his patience thinning. "But tears extinguish the fire within, and it is the fire that drives us. They serve no purpose. Harken to me."

Romola started slightly. Once more, the specter of his madness flickered through her mind, checking the flood of belief. Was this merely a madman plotting murder? Yet, beneath the surface, a profound sympathy anchored her trust, stronger than fear.

"Speak," she urged, as gently as her trembling voice allowed, "how did you lose your memory, your learning?"

"I fell ill," he replied, his gaze drifting. "Time means nothing to me. I remember only sitting in the sun amidst stones, surrounded by darkness. Slowly, a longing took root—a desire for something I could not name. When I sailed upon the waters, I understood what I craved: the return of the Boy, the restoration of my thoughts. I was locked outside of them all, and now I remain outside. I feel nothing but walls and shadows."

His words drifted into a reverie, and he sank into silence, burying his head in his hands. As before, Romola's faith in him drowned out her caution. The pity in her heart resonated with an old, familiar pain. Did not her own father miss Dino, mourning a future lost?

"It returned once," Baldassarre continued, breaking the silence. "I held sway over all things. I beheld the world anew, my treasures, my library. I thought I held him in thrall, so I went to expose him where the lights gleamed and the trees stood. But he lied again, branding me mad, and they hauled me away to prison... Wickedness is mighty, and he wears armor."

Fierceness flared in his voice, returning with its former intensity. He seized her arm again.

"But you will assist me? He has betrayed you, too. He has another wife, bearing children. He convinces her he is her husband, and she is a simple, helpless creature. I will reveal her dwelling."

The first shock that coursed through Romola was a surge of righteous anger. The indignity of the woman's position cut deepest. Baldassarre, instinctively attuned to her spirit, sensed her alignment.

"You hate him," he insisted. "Is it not true? There is no love between you. I know women can hate, and you possess proud blood. You abhor deceit, and you can embrace revenge."

Romola sat paralyzed by the storm of conflicting emotions. She felt no sensation of the bruising grip on her arm.

"You shall devise a plan," Baldassarre whispered urgently. "I have memorized that you are his lawful wife. You are a noblewoman. You seek the preacher of vengeance; you will uphold justice. Yet, you must think for me. My mind wanders—everything slips away, save the fire. That fire is God; it is Justice; it cannot die. Do you believe this? If they will not hang him for robbing me, you must strip him of his armor. You will leave him defenseless, and I shall strike him down. I bear a knife, and my arm retains sufficient strength."

He slid his hand beneath his tunic, drawing forth the hidden blade, running his thumb absently along the edge, as if the tactile sensation kept his ideas alive.

To Romola, every passing hour seemed to promise greater difficulty. Her judgment, swift and vigorous, refused to succumb to futile pleas of reason against a man in Baldassarre's fractured state. She chose silence, refusing to answer his final appeal, instead seeking to buy time by deflecting his urgency with a question of her own concern. Her voice trembled as she spoke.

"You claim she is foolish, helpless—that other wife—who believes him to be her true spouse. Perhaps he is her husband, perhaps he wed her before marrying me."
