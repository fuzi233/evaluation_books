I was in no humour to be crossed, and yet the house has a way of placing one’s own flesh and blood exactly on the stair where a man most wants a clear passage. Penelope met me—soft-footed, steady-eyed, with that quiet obstinacy of her late mother’s that could trip a man more surely than any loose board—and claimed, in the mild voice that never sounded like a claim, to know all that had passed between Mr. Franklin Blake and me.

There are moments when a steward’s dignity must be defended with a lie as carefully set as a soufflé—light on the surface, firm enough underneath to stand. I clapped the lid down on her curiosity at once.

“Mr. Franklin and I,” I said, “talked foreign politics till neither of us could talk any longer; and then we fell asleep in the sun.”

Try that answer, if you ever wish to stop a question without starting a quarrel. A woman will seem to swallow it—because sweetness is part of her breeding—and she will remember it—because memory is part of her power. Penelope let me go, as if my nonsense had satisfied her; and the afternoon ran on, the household noises settling into their accustomed pattern: the faint clink of china in the pantry, the brushed whisper of skirts in passages, the far-off calling of a man in the stable-yard, all tempered by the thick, warm air that made even the clocks sound drowsy.

My lady and Miss Rachel came back at last. Of course they were astonished to hear that Mr. Franklin had arrived by the early train and vanished again on horseback. Of course they asked their awkward questions straight out, like people born to have the truth served to them hot and ready, without the trouble of watching it simmer.

“Foreign politics” and “falling asleep in the sun” were a dish I couldn’t send up twice.

At the end of my invention, I fell back on what the gentry always find easiest to believe of each other.

“It’s one of Mr. Franklin’s freaks, my lady,” I said.

“And his galloping off again?” my lady asked, with that dry sharpness that cuts through a man’s excuses as a carving-knife goes through fat.

“Yes,” I answered. “Another freak.”

I slipped out of it in that way—cleverly enough to keep my footing for the moment, though any man who has lived long with women knows that a clever evasion is only a debt postponed.

When I got back to my own room, my difficulties were waiting there as surely as the kettle waits for the fire. In came Penelope, as if to prove my own little maxim: with the natural sweetness of women, to kiss and make it up again; and with the natural curiosity of women, to begin afresh at the very point you had hoped was buried.

This time she wanted to talk of Rosanna Spearman.

After leaving Mr. Franklin and me at the Shivering Sand, Rosanna had returned to the house in a state that Penelope could not make head nor tail of. Penelope described it with the sort of exactness she might have given to a recipe remembered by heart—this pinch of feeling, that sudden turn, the whole mixture rising and collapsing without warning.

Rosanna, it seemed, had gone all colours in her face as if she had been held over a fire and then plunged into cold water. She had been merry without reason, and sad without reason. One minute she was pouring out questions about Mr. Franklin Blake—hundreds of them, tumbling one after another like peas from a split sack—and the next she was offended with Penelope for so much as hinting that a strange gentleman could be of any interest to her. Penelope had found her smiling, scribbling Mr. Franklin’s name inside her workbox as if the very letters could be kneaded into her life and make it new. Then, as suddenly, she had been crying before the glass, staring at her deformed shoulder with a bitterness that made Penelope’s own voice falter as she repeated it.

Had Rosanna and Mr. Franklin known one another before that day? Impossible. Had they even heard of each other? Impossible again. I could answer for Mr. Franklin’s genuine surprise when the girl stared at him; Penelope could answer for Rosanna’s genuine eagerness when she pressed for every scrap of knowledge about him. The talk between my daughter and me went on in that weary circle—facts that didn’t fit, guesses that wouldn’t hold—until Penelope suddenly flung out what I believed to be the most monstrous notion ever to come out of a sensible girl’s mouth.

“Father,” she said, grave as a judge, “there’s only one explanation. Rosanna has fallen in love with Mr. Franklin Blake at first sight.”

You may have heard of handsome young ladies falling in love at first sight, and called it natural enough. But a housemaid out of a reformatory, with a plain face and a twisted shoulder, falling in love at first sight with a gentleman newly come to visit his cousin—match me that absurdity out of any story-book in Christendom, if you can.

I laughed till the tears ran. And at my laughter—at my coarse, comfortable laughter—Penelope turned white, as if I had struck her.

“I never knew you cruel before, father,” she said—very gently, which was the worst of it—and she went out.

The words hit me like cold water dashed in the face. I was savage with myself for feeling—uneasy, of all things—at being called cruel; and yet I did feel it. I had not meant harm. But the harm, once done, does not ask what you meant.

We will leave that subject. I am sorry I drifted into it at all; and not without reason, as you will see when we have gone on a little farther.

The evening came. The dressing-bell rang for dinner before Mr. Franklin returned from Frizinghall. I took his hot water up to his room myself, expecting, after such delay, some report of accident or adventure—some stir in the story, after all the heat and flurry about the Diamond earlier in the day.

To my disappointment—flat enough, no doubt, to yours as well—nothing had happened.

He had not met the Indians, going or coming. He had put the Moonstone away in the bank, calling it only a valuable of great price, and the receipt was safe in his pocket. That was all.

I went downstairs with the sensation of a pot watched to the boil, only to find it lukewarm when the lid is lifted.

What passed between Mr. Franklin and his aunt and cousin I cannot tell. I would have given something to wait at table that night and see it with my own eyes. But in my position, waiting at dinner—except on high family festivals—was considered a letting down of my dignity in the servants’ hall; and my lady had long held that I was prone enough to forget my place already, without seeking temptations.

The news that filtered down to me from the upper regions came, as such news does, flavoured by the mouths it travelled through: Penelope and the footman.

Penelope said she had never known Miss Rachel so particular about her hair; never seen her look so bright and pretty as she did when she went down to meet Mr. Franklin in the drawing-room. There is a kind of careful impatience in a young lady’s dressing when her heart is in it: pins set and reset; a curl coaxed into place as if it were a stubborn dough that must be worked till it yields; the faint scent of pomatum warmed by the skin—small domestic acts that carry, for those with ears to hear, the loudest meaning.

The footman’s report was different in taste, but not in substance. He said that keeping a respectful composure in the presence of one’s betters and waiting on Mr. Franklin Blake at dinner were two of the hardest things to reconcile that had ever tried his training in service. I understood him. There are gentlemen whose very cheerfulness makes a servant’s face ache with the effort of not smiling back.

Later in the evening we heard them singing and playing duets—Mr. Franklin piping high, Miss Rachel piping higher, and my lady on the piano following them as if over hedge and ditch, bringing them safe through in a manner wonderful and pleasant to hear through the open windows on the terrace at night.

I stood outside a while, letting the music wash over the gravel and the shrubs and the dark side of the house. The air held sound the way a good broth holds spice—each note carried, each pause tasted. Beyond the garden walls the world was quiet, as country should be at that hour. No clatter of carts, no shriek of machinery, none of that harsh modern uproar that makes art into something shouted and sold, instead of something listened to. Only the human voices, the struck keys, and beneath them, far away and faint, the steady breath of the sea.

Later still I went to Mr. Franklin in the smoking-room with the soda-water and brandy, and found that Miss Rachel had put the Diamond clean out of his head.

“She’s the most charming girl I have seen since I came back to England!” was all I could get from him, when I tried to lead him—gently, as one tests a sauce before adding salt—back to more serious matters.

Towards midnight I went my round to lock up, as was my duty, with my second in command—Samuel the footman—at my heels. When all the doors were made fast, except the side door that opened on the terrace, I sent Samuel to bed and stepped out myself for a breath of air before turning in.

The night was still and close. The moon was full. It was so silent out of doors that, from time to time, I caught—very faint and low—the fall of the sea as the ground-swell heaved it in on the sand-bank near the mouth of our little bay. That sound, when the rest of the world holds its tongue, is like an old language spoken under the breath—unfashionable, perhaps, and ignored by those who live among louder things, but carrying a meaning you cannot translate into any other words.

As the house stood, the terrace side lay in darkness; but the broad moonlight showed fair on the gravel walk that ran along the next side. Looking that way, after looking up at the sky, I saw the shadow of a person in the moonlight, thrown forward from behind the corner of the house.

Being old and sly, I did not call out. Being old and heavy, my feet betrayed me on the gravel with a crunch that sounded, in such stillness, like breaking bones.

Before I could steal round the corner as I meant, I heard lighter feet than mine—and more than one pair, as it seemed—retreating in a hurry. By the time I reached the corner, the trespassers had run into the shrubbery on the far side of the walk and were hidden among the thick trees and bushes. From there they could easily make their way over our fence into the road.

If I had been forty years younger I might have had a chance of catching them before they got clear. As it was, I went back to set younger legs moving. Without disturbing anybody, Samuel and I got a couple of guns and went all round the house and through the shrubbery, listening—listening harder than we looked—for the slightest answering sound: a snapped twig, a hissed breath, the restless settling of leaves. But the night had swallowed them. We made sure no one was lurking in the grounds, and turned back.

Passing again over the walk where I had seen the shadow, I noticed, for the first time, a little bright object lying on the clean gravel under the moon. I stooped and picked it up.

It was a small bottle, and in it a thick liquor, sweet-smelling, black as ink. The scent rose at once—cloying and strange, like syrup warmed too long, like sugar turned dark at the edge of burning. I said nothing to Samuel. But Penelope’s earlier account came back to me, clear as if spoken into my ear: the jugglers; the little pool of black poured into the boy’s palm.

In that moment I suspected I had disturbed the three Indians, lurking about the house, bent—after their heathen fashion—on finding out where the Diamond lay that night.
