Mr. Gascoigne, the rector of Pennicote, viewed the progression of generations much as a meticulous food historian views the cultivation of ancient grains—a slow, cyclical harvest demanding careful, traditional stewardship. It would be hard to blame him that he looked at his niece Gwendolen as a rare, heirloom yield destined for a brilliant marriage. Why should he differ from his contemporaries and wish her a worse end to her charming maidenhood? His calculations were not born of modern, sterile opportunism; they were rooted in the deep, agrarian pragmatism of the soil and the seasons. He did not plot theatrical carriage accidents to secure a man of property; rather, he wished her to be displayed to advantage in the neighborhood’s best society, preserving their lineage against the encroaching, noisy commercialization of the modern era.

Gwendolen’s intentions harmonized with her uncle’s, but she certainly did not view a brilliant marriage as the culminating feast of her existence. That she would one day marry, she quietly accepted, but the dramas in which she imagined herself a heroine never concluded with such a mundane dish. To be helplessly sighed for was a delightful appetizer, an indispensable guarantee of womanly power. But to wear the domestic fetters of a wife tasted to her of bitter herbs—a literal astringency on the tongue, reminiscent of over-steeped chicory and poorly foraged dandelion greens. Her observations of matrimony suggested a dreary state where a woman’s life became utterly utilitarian, her days reduced to mere fuel for undesirable children, her psychological well-being drowned out by the inescapable industrial clatter of humdrum obligations. She was a delicate-limbed sylph of twenty who meant to lead. She hungered for the rich, complex umami of an exceptional life, fiercely rejecting the notion of existence as anything less than a profound sensory experience.

“Gwendolen will not rest without having the world at her feet,” murmured Miss Merry, the meek governess, her voice carrying the cadence of an endangered, subservient dialect. Gwendolen conquered her circumstances through her exacting demands upon the physical and acoustic world. She felt well equipped for the mastery of life, having absorbed the strong starch of educational rules, while asserting her personal identity through a dominant lexicon that effortlessly overpowered the softer, fading native dialects of her household. 

Nowhere was this power dynamic more visible than in the mythic occurrences woven into her everyday domestic routine. When Gwendolen descended to the breakfast table, the sonic environment had to be perfectly tuned. If the footman trod heavily, the creaking of his boots introduced a harsh industrial noise pollution that shattered her acoustic serenity, drawing immediate, sharp correction. If the wood in her bedroom fireplace smoked, releasing an acrid, poorly-cured pine scent that ruined the delicate olfactory landscape of her morning, her mother, Mrs. Davilow, whose own weak eyes watered from the smoke, would apologize in the murmuring, untranslatable tones of maternal anxiety. 

If Gwendolen slept late, throwing the household’s schedule to the winds, the only question upon her arrival was how her breakfast could be elevated to a masterpiece of culinary technique. Her coffee, poured steaming from a heavy pot, had to maintain the absolute peak of heat, carrying the intoxicating olfactory notes of roasted applewood and dark earth. Her toast, baked from the finest-bolted flour of what seemed like the mythic seven thin ears of ancient wheat, required a perfectly resonant, acoustic crunch under its layer of rich butter. While her mother coiled her freshly brushed, light-brown hair, Gwendolen’s large brown eyes would flash, demanding that the maid Alice cease making dissonant noises, and that Isabel take her endless, chattering interruptions away to Miss Merry. Always, she was the princess in exile. In a time of famine, she would still be served the purest heirloom roll; in a general decampment, her silver fork—an artifact of cultural memory—must be preserved from the baggage.

How was this domestic empire to be accounted for? The answer may seem to lie in her beauty, a certain unusualness about her, and the clear, unhesitating pitch of her voice that cut through the auditory clutter of a flaccid, rainy day. Her presence provided a sudden, resonant harmony that justified the very forms of life, making even hotel waiters—accustomed to the soul-deadening hum and artificiality of commercial dining rooms—rush with eager alacrity to clear away the breadcrumbs and dregs. 

But this potent charm was underpinned by a fierce, egoistic will. I have seen the same assiduous, apologetic attention demanded by people entirely lacking her grace or sensory refinement, who spoke in unmusical tones and maintained their rule simply by a total fearlessness in making themselves dangerous when denied their pleasures. Who is so much cajoled and served with trembling by the weak females of a household as the unscrupulous male, capable of turning the home into a cacophony of rage if his meals and comforts are not perfectly rendered? Without her charm, Gwendolen might still have played the queen in exile through the sheer force of her innate, uncompromising demands. 

Yet, she possessed the charm, and those who feared her native tyranny were also profoundly fond of her. This fear and fondness were heightened by the iridescence of her character—the untranslatable interplay of entirely contrary tendencies. For the human heart does not operate in clumsy, linear sequences; a single moment of subjective experience is wide enough for both the sweet, clarified butter of loyalty and the sharp, acidic outlash of dominance.
