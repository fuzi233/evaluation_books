Three days had passed since the city’s turmoil began, and now, just as midnight crept over Florence, the moon rose above the rooftops of the piazza before the Old Palace—yet it cast no light upon the stone pavement. Not a single inch of ground was visible; instead, the entire space was choked with a restless, surging crowd. Where silence once held sway, allowing the night wanderers’ footsteps, lute strings, and hurried whispers to echo sharply in the stillness, there was now only a deafening roar—a chorus of shouts, curses, trampling feet, clashing steel, and the sudden, jarring toll of a bell that cut through the din like a blade. Almost every citizen who could be called part of Florence’s public life was awake, drawn to this place or struggling to enter it.

Inside the palace, the council chamber remained filled with the chief magistrates—the eighty senators and other distinguished men—who had debated long into the night, their voices rising in heated contention over whether to grant an appeal or carry out the death sentence on the five imprisoned conspirators without delay. Their arguments had grown so fierce that the sound of their quarrel reached the throng outside. Only moments ago had the decision been made. The Signoria had been split—four members stood firm for the appeal, defying threats that their homes would be razed if they refused. Then Francesco Valori stepped forward, his voice sharp and unyielding: if the government failed to defend the people’s liberties by executing these five traitors, he warned, others would rise to do it—and not without consequence for those who opposed them. His words struck like thunder. When the votes were recounted, the four dissenting voices were gone. All nine now stood united in black beans—the verdict of death, immediate and final. And though not spoken aloud, another fate was sealed: that of Valori himself, whose bold stance would soon mark him as a target.

As the judicial Eight departed for the Bargello to prepare the execution, the five condemned men were led barefoot and bound through the very midst of the council chamber. It was their friends who had arranged this—knowing that Florentines would not remain unmoved by seeing such dishonor inflicted upon men of honor: Bernardo del Nero and Niccolò Ridolfi, both revered elders who had upheld their convictions long before the Medici order turned retrograde; Tornabuoni and Pucci, young luminaries whose absence from the halls of power would leave a hollow silence. But pity, when it came, was not of the kind that could save them—it was the bitter, helpless sorrow that only deepens resolve for vengeance.

Below, in the palace courtyard, Romola leaned against one of the massive pillars, waiting. She had won permission—through urgent entreaty—to visit her godfather that evening, to stay with him until the council’s decision was known. Now she waited beside his confessor, ready to follow the guard that would lead him to the Bargello. Her heart was fixed on being near him until the end, as her father might have done. She had silenced all protests. Giovan Battista Ridolfi, brother of Niccolò and a follower of Savonarola, stood at her side, promising protection. He was going to witness his own brother’s death, consumed by grief but resolute. Tito was also within the palace—but Romola had not seen him. Since the night of the seventeenth, they had avoided each other. Tito knew only by inference, from reports of the Frate’s neutrality, that her efforts had failed. He was surrounded by officials and foreign envoys, all awaiting the outcome of the council. He maintained a quiet demeanor, speaking little, even when addressed. If someone mentioned Romola in connection with the events, he replied that her mind was too agitated—her hostility toward him, he said, stemmed from the fact that he continued in office under a regime that condemned her godfather. For her sake, he felt it best not to approach her.

“Ah, the old Bardi blood!” Cennini remarked, shrugging. “I wouldn’t be surprised if this shakes _her_ loose from the Frate, as well as some others.”

“It is understandable in a woman,” said a young French envoy, bowing slightly to Tito, “especially one so beautiful—she is your wife, after all—to believe her affections should rule over the good of the State. Who among us wants a cousin executed? But such sentimentality must not be encouraged in men. Your Florentine polity suffers from it.”

“That is true,” Macchiavelli observed calmly. “But where personal bonds are strong, the conflicts they spark must be reckoned with. Many half-measures are merely rash blunders. The only safe strikes are those so severe they cannot be avenged.”

“Niccolò,” Cennini said, “there’s a clever wickedness in your speech sometimes—I begin to suspect your pleasant face is a mask for Satan.”

“Not at all, my dear Domenico,” Macchiavelli replied, smiling and placing a hand on the older man’s shoulder. “Satan was a blunderer—an innovator who failed spectacularly. Had he succeeded, we’d all be worshipping him, and his portrait would be more flattered than any saint’s.”

“Well then,” Cennini muttered, “I don’t say your doctrine isn’t clever enough for Satan. I only say it’s wicked enough.”

“I tell you,” Macchiavelli continued, “my doctrine is shared by all who seek goals beyond their own noses. Ask our Frate how he plans to bring about his universal renewal. He’ll say first, a free and pure government. But since love between Florentines can’t be forced, he concludes: cut off every head that stands stubbornly in the way. Yet if a man earns scorn by enforcing a severity that isn’t thorough enough, he makes a mistake. And I suspect the Frate has made just such a mistake. This was a moment when he might have gained strength by championing the appeal. Instead, he lost luster and gained nothing.”

Before anyone could respond, a servant announced that the prisoners were leaving the council chamber. Most present rushed toward the door, eager to secure a clear path behind the guards—because the execution site drew both the deeply passionate and the coldly curious alike.

Tito remained behind. He had always recoiled from scenes of violence and suffering. Five days earlier, he had hoped the sentence would not be carried out—exile would have sufficed. His safety demanded no more. But now, he felt a strange relief knowing Bernardo del Nero’s head would soon be severed. The new understanding Romola had shown him upon his return—her unwavering belief in Baldassarre, her growing defiance—had awakened a fresh fear in him. If she acted in ways that made him appear suspect, he foresaw not only failure, but humiliation. Her faith in Baldassarre had hardened her resistance. If Bernardo lived, she would easily win him over to her cause. Romola seemed more than ever an uncontrollable force in his life. But if Bernardo died, the barriers she would face in opposing him—her pride, her sense of duty—might prove insurmountable. So, Tito found comfort in the knowledge that the scaffold was being erected at once.

Four others—his allies—were to die alongside Bernardo. But a man’s own safety becomes a deity that demands terrible sacrifices. Even in pursuit of what he desired, Tito found himself seeking what he loathed. Yet he had faced such paradoxes before. As he heard the shuffle of boots and the clang of chains echoing up the stairs through the open doorway, he answered the French envoy’s questions with calm resignation, showing no trace of inner turmoil.

To Romola, those sounds fell like thunder in a heightened world. Her senses were sharpened beyond ordinary limits. She needed no support, shed no tears. She felt a presence so intense it transcended grief or joy—a communion with forces older than pain or pleasure. Since Bernardo’s fate was sealed, the conflict within her had dissolved. She was no longer separate from him—she was with him in spirit, defending his name. If he was punished for treason, he was not a traitor. He was caught between two duties, torn by loyalty to principles and to people. He did not choose the noblest cause, yet he died because of his nobleness. A lesser man might have avoided this guilt. But Bernardo could not.

Romola felt the full weight of sympathy for the individual soul—something that constantly rebels against rigid political judgments. She walked with her second father toward the scaffold, steeling herself to endure shame, knowing it was unjust. The path was guarded by three hundred armed men—ordered by Francesco Valori, in a strange contradiction: afraid both of popular fury and of a rescue attempt. Among the contradictions of the night, this was perhaps the most striking.

When they reached the court of the Bargello, Romola was allowed a brief farewell with Bernardo and his confessor. Though the crowd surged around them, many eyes turned to watch. The old man, forgetting his hands were bound, raised them toward Romola’s golden hair—then stopped himself, leaning forward to kiss her forehead. She seized his fettered hands and kissed them as if they were relics of sanctity.

“My poor Romola,” he whispered, “I have only to die. But you must live—and I shall not be there to help you.”

“Yes,” she said quickly, “you _will_ help me—always—because I will remember you.”

She was pulled away and led up the steps to the loggia overlooking the courtyard. There, she took her place, determined to see the last look of the man who had loved her as a daughter. Beside her, Battista Ridolfi promised to guide her through a rear door once the moment had passed. Still, in the depths of her mind, a fragile hope flickered: perhaps, somehow, a rescue would come—perhaps the scaffold would never taste blood.

For a long time, movement filled the court—flickering torchlight, swaying heads, muffled voices, waves of sound rolling in from outside. To Romola, it felt like standing in the eye of a storm, untouched by its fury, focused only on holding up a signal until the eyes searching for it could no longer see.

Then—silence.

Even the torches seemed to still. The executioner stood ready. Bernardo del Nero appeared on the scaffold, climbing the steps slowly, steadily. Romola did not move. She made no sound. She stood firmer, drawing strength from his composure.

She saw him pause. She saw his white head held high. In a voice clear and calm, he spoke:

“It is but a short space of life that my fellow-citizens have taken from me.”

He looked slowly around. Romola felt his gaze rest upon her. She felt her arms reach out across the distance.

And then—nothing.

Only when a long while later, it seemed, a voice said, “My daughter, all is peace now. I can conduct you to your house.”

She uncovered her head. The confessor stood beside her in a dim room, where other solemn men murmured quietly.

“I am ready,” she said, rising. “Let us lose no time.”

She believed all clinging was over. From now on, her strength would be used only to escape—away from a grasp that made her shudder.
