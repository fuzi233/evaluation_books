The English public, unfamiliar with the history of Pocahontas as we Virginians are, who still cherish the memory of that gentle soul, heard Mr. Warrington compose a brief ballad on the subject. At his friends' urging, he printed it in the magazines shortly before the tragedy's premiere. Sampson and I deemed this move a stroke of cunning genius. "It is like ground-bait, sir," declared the zealous clergyman, "and you shall see the fish rise in multitudes on the great day!" He and Spencer insisted they had heard the poem discussed and lauded in various coffee-houses, with credit mistakenly assigned to Mr. Mason, Mr. Cowper, and even the renowned Mr. Gray. I suspect poor Sam spread these rumors himself; had Shakespeare been rumored the author, he would have hailed *Pocahontas* as the poet's finest achievement.

My connection with Captain Smith began in childhood, within the hallowed library of my grandfather. I recall sitting at his knees, the favorite volume resting on my lap, decoding the exploits of our Virginian hero. I devoured tales of his wanderings, sufferings, and escapes, both across the Atlantic and within Europe. Reading the book now, in England, transported me back to those early days, flooding my mind with vivid memories. My grandfather would paint pictures for me of Smith battling Turks on the Danube or being dragged forth by savages to execution. I marveled at the terrifying duel between Smith and Bonny Molgro, the third and most fearsome champion. With his prodigious turban, scimitar, and whiskers, Bonny Molgro seemed a figure of legend. After dispatching his first two foes, Smith faced Molgro. According to my cherished tome, they clashed with battle-axes whose sharp edges caused both rider and steed to reel in confusion, the Christian losing his weapon. Yet, through the agility of his horse and his own judgment, Smith drew his falchion, piercing the Turk beneath the armor, through back and body. Though Molgro dismounted, he fell swiftly, meeting his end as the others had. For this feat, Duke Segismundus bestowed upon him a shield bearing three Turkish heads and an annual pension of three hundred ducats.

In my tragedy, disregarding time and place—a liberty poets enjoy—Smith performed similar feats on the banks of the Potomac and the James. Our "ground-bait" verses ran thus:

>
> Wearied arm and broken sword
> Wage in vain the desperate fight;
> Round him press the countless horde,
> He is but a single knight.
> Hark! a cry of triumph shrill
> Through the wilderness resounds,
> As, with twenty bleeding wounds,
> Sinks the warrior, fighting still.
>
> Now they heap the fatal pyre,
> And the torch of death they light;
> Ah! 'tis hard to die of fire!
> Who will shield the captive knight?
> Round the stake with fiendish cry
> Wheel and dance the savage crowd,
> Cold the victim's mien and proud,
> And his breast is bared to die.
>
> Who will shield the fearless heart?
> Who avert the murderous blade?
> From the throng, with sudden start,
> See, there springs an Indian maid.
> Quick she stands before the knight,
> Loose the chain, unbind the ring,
> I am daughter of the king,
> And I claim the Indian right!
>
> Dauntlessly aside she flings
> Lifted axe and thirsty knife;
> Fondly to his heart she clings,
> And her bosom guards his life!
> In the woods of Powhattan,
> Still 'tis told, by Indian fires,
> How a daughter of their sires
> Saved the captive Englishman.

Needless to say, the plot required no lengthy description; my children could consult the shelves at any hour. Nor was I eager to recount it again to them, for Captain Miles and the parson had fallen asleep during our previous reading. However, anyone versed in drama or fiction could easily construct a narrative from this sketch. An Indian king, a loving princess, her attendant enamored of the British captain's servant, a traitor within the fort, a valiant Indian warrior pining for Pocahontas, a medicine-man and priest capable of treachery and torture—all these elements, combined with wilderness accidents, war dances, cries mimicked by Gumbo, the arrival of the English fleet, and references to recent Canadian victories, promised success.

Yet, ominous signs preceded the opening night. Difficulties arose from a peevish, jealous, and timid management, alongside violent prejudices held by certain influential circles. Was it surprising, then, that *Pocahontas* failed? I laughed at the malignity of critics who faulted the performance. How ironic, they praised *Carpezan* as a masterpiece while sneering at a far superior and more intricate work. I maintained that Hagan's portrayal was so admirable that a certain actor-manager ought to feel jealousy, and without external interference, the piece would have triumphed. Orders had been issued to ensure failure, according to Sampson. "The house swarmed with Macs, by George," the honest fellow swore, "and they should have the galleries washed with brimstone." He vowed that even Mr. Garrick himself would not have allowed the play to succeed, growing particularly enraged during the grand scene in the second act when Smith (played by poor Hagan) was bound to the stake and saved by Pocahontas, eliciting thunderous applause and sympathy from the entire house.

Those curious enough may refer to the published tragedy, available in octavo or the subsequent quarto edition of my Collected Works, to judge the merit of the scene, the elegance of the verses, and the richness of the language. One cause of failure was my strict adherence to historical accuracy. I had copied a figure of Sir Walter Raleigh from the Museum, complete with frills and beard, and, thanks to Theo's contribution of her mother's finest lace, dressed Hagan precisely after this drawing. No one could appear more authentic. Similarly, I dressed Miss Pritchard as Pocahontas, informed by my own observations of indigenous attire at home. Would it be believed that the audience tittered when she first appeared? Eventually, they grew accustomed, but just as she rushed into the prisoner's embrace, causing many to tearfully sympathize, a fellow in the pit shouted, "Bedad! Here's the Belle Savage kissing the Saracen's Head!" An impudent roar of laughter erupted, continuing intermittently throughout the performance. Like the wag in Mr. Sheridan's *The Critic*, who demanded multiple morning guns, the playwrights were not satisfied with one jab; they fired two or three. This wretched tavern joke—the ignorant failing to recognize that Pocahontas herself was the Belle Sauvage from whom the tavern derived its name—was repeated ad nauseam. As each new character entered, the friend in the pit greeted them with tavern names: the English governor became "The Goat and Boots," his lieutenant Barker, with his broad visage, "The Bull and Mouth," and so on. The curtain fell amidst a shrill storm of whistles and hisses, particularly targeting poor Hagan whenever he spoke. Sampson spotted Master Will in the green boxes with some of his acquaintances, convinced the treacherous scoundrel was among the conspirators. "I would have flung him over into the pit," the faithful Sampson declared, executing his threat, though he had to retreat due to two of Mr. Nadab's followers prowling the lobby. Thus, our hoped-for profits were shattered, our dreams broken!

Before the curtain rose, I glanced into the house from the stage, noting it was reasonably full, especially Mr. Johnson in the front box, accompanied by Mr. Reynolds, unable to hear or see clearly, forming a duo to critique my tragic endeavor. I also spotted Lady Maria in the lower gallery, where she once again sat to watch her beloved actor portray a leading role. As for Theo, she frankly admitted she preferred not attending unless I commanded otherwise, and I, foreseeing the pain of witnessing her suffering if things went awry, acquiesced to her wish to stay away.

Of a relatively even temperament, confident in bearing either good or bad fortune without disturbance, I dined lightly at the Bedford and arrived at the theater moments before the play commenced, intending to remain until victory or defeat was decided. Indeed, I could foresee the likely outcome. Everything around exuded a gloom and disaster. Miss Pritchard suffered from a headache; the barber who powdered Hagan's wig acted like a wretch, ruining the appearance; among the gentlemen and ladies in the green room, only doubtful faces prevailed; and the manager—a flippant, if not impertinent gentleman, who himself looked as dismal as a mute at a funeral—had the audacity to suggest, "For Heaven's sake, Mr. Warrington, go get a glass of punch at the Bedford, and don't frighten us all here by your dismal countenance!"

"Sir," I replied, "I have a right, for five shillings, to comment upon your face, but I never granted you authority to remark upon mine."
