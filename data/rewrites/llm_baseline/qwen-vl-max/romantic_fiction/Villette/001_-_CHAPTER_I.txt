My godmother lived in a stately house nestled in the quiet, old-fashioned town of Bretton. Her family had been rooted there for generations—so much so that they bore the name of their homeland: Bretton of Bretton. Whether this was mere chance or a legacy from some distinguished ancestor who once shaped the town’s destiny, I never knew. As a child, I visited Bretton about twice each year, and each time, I found myself drawn to its calm charm.

The house itself was a haven—spacious rooms with high ceilings, polished furniture arranged with care, wide windows that let in soft daylight, and a balcony overlooking a street that seemed frozen in time. On Sundays and holidays, the air hummed with stillness; the cobbles were clean, the shutters neat, and the world outside felt gently paused. I adored it all.

I was the only child among adults in the household, and though Mrs. Bretton was reserved by nature, she took quiet notice of me. A widow when I first met her, she had lost her husband—a physician—while still young and strikingly beautiful. Though no longer youthful when I knew her, she retained her elegance: tall, poised, with dark hair and a complexion glowing with health. Her eyes, black and bright, sparkled with warmth and wit. People often lamented that her son hadn’t inherited her coloring; his eyes were blue, sharp even as a boy, and his long hair shimmered golden in sunlight. But he carried her features, her strong teeth, her height, and most importantly, her unshakable health and steady spirit—qualities more valuable than wealth.

In the autumn of the year ——, I was staying at Bretton. My godmother had come personally to fetch me from the relatives where I lived permanently. I sensed something was amiss—though I couldn’t quite name it. There was an undercurrent of sorrow, a hush beneath the usual peace. And so, I welcomed the change of scenery, eager to leave behind the quiet unease that clung to my home.

Time passed smoothly under my godmother’s roof—not swiftly, but steadily, like a river flowing through a sunlit valley. My visits reminded me of Christian and Hopeful resting beside a gentle stream, where green trees lined the banks and lilies bloomed all year. There was no drama here, no sudden turns—just serenity. And I cherished it. I didn’t seek excitement; when it came, it felt almost intrusive.

One day, a letter arrived that clearly startled Mrs. Bretton. At first, I thought it might be from home and feared the worst. But nothing was said to me, and the shadow lifted quickly.

The next day, returning from a long walk, I entered my bedroom to find it transformed. Alongside my French bed in its shaded alcove stood a small crib draped in white. Beside my mahogany chest of drawers sat a delicate rosewood chest. I stopped, stared, and wondered.

“What could these things mean?” I murmured.

The answer came easily: another guest was expected.

At dinner, the truth was revealed. A little girl would soon join me—the daughter of a distant relation of the late Dr. Bretton. She had recently lost her mother, though Mrs. Bretton added that the loss wasn’t as great as it seemed. The woman—Mrs. Home, as she was called—had been lovely, yes, but flighty and careless. She’d neglected her child and worn down her husband. Their marriage had been incompatible, and they had parted ways peacefully, without legal turmoil.

Shortly after, Mrs. Home overexerted herself at a ball, caught a chill, developed a fever, and died quickly. Her husband—Mr. Home—was deeply sensitive and shattered by the news. He blamed himself, convinced his own harshness had hastened her death. Grief had weakened him, and doctors urged travel as a cure. In the meantime, Mrs. Bretton offered to care for the child.

“And I hope,” she added, “this little one won’t inherit her mother’s silliness. I wouldn’t want her to be a frivolous flirt like the woman who married Mr. Home.”

She spoke kindly, yet with firmness. “Mr. Home is sensible, in his way—though not very practical. He loves science, spends half his life in a lab conducting experiments—something his wife could never understand. Honestly,” she confessed, “I wouldn’t have liked it myself.”

When I asked more, she told me Dr. Bretton once said Mr. Home’s scientific mind came from a maternal uncle—a French scholar. His lineage was mixed: French and Scottish. He had relatives still living in France, several of whom bore noble titles and wrote *de* before their names.

That evening, at nine o’clock, a servant was sent to meet the coach carrying our new guest. Mrs. Bretton and I waited in the drawing-room. John Graham Bretton was away visiting a schoolfriend in the countryside.

She read the evening paper; I sewed. Rain pounded against the windows, wind howled outside.

“Poor child,” she whispered now and then. “What weather for her journey! I wish she were safe here.”

Just before ten, the bell rang. I rushed downstairs. In the hall lay a trunk and a few bandboxes. A nursemaid stood nearby, and Warren, the footman, held a shawled bundle in his arms.

“Is that the child?” I asked.

“Yes, miss.”

I reached out to peek, but the bundle was turned away.

“Put me down, please,” a small voice said as Warren opened the drawing-room door. Then, with quick hands, the child unpinned the shawl and shrugged it off.

The figure that emerged was tiny, yet perfectly formed—slight, straight, and graceful. She tried to fold the shawl, but it was too large for her small hands.

“Give it to Harriet, please,” she said. “She can put it away.”

Then she turned to Mrs. Bretton.

“Come here, little dear,” Mrs. Bretton said softly. “Let me see if you’re cold. Come warm yourself by the fire.”

The child stepped forward. Without her shawl, she looked like a doll—her neck delicate, her curls soft and fine. Seated on Mrs. Bretton’s lap, she seemed almost unreal.

Mrs. Bretton chafed her hands and feet, speaking gently. At first, the child watched with a solemn gaze, but then a smile broke through.

Mrs. Bretton, usually reserved—even with her beloved son—kissed her forehead. “What’s your name?”

“Missy.”

“But besides Missy?”

“Polly. Papa calls me Polly.”

“Will Polly be happy living with me?”

“Not always. Only till papa comes home. Papa is gone.”

She shook her head slowly.

“He will return—or send for me.”

“Will he, ma’am? Do you know he will?”

“I think so.”

“But Harriet thinks not. Not for a long while. He’s ill.”

Her eyes filled with tears. She pulled her hand from Mrs. Bretton’s and moved to step down.

“Please, I wish to go. I can sit on a stool.”

She was allowed to descend. Carrying a footstool, she placed it in the deepest corner of the room and sat quietly.

Mrs. Bretton, though authoritative in serious matters, often yielded in small things. She let the child have her way.

“Take no notice for now,” she whispered to me.

But I did notice. I saw her rest her elbow on her knee, her chin in her hand. Then, from her pocket—hidden within the ruffles of her dress—she drew a tiny handkerchief. And I heard her weep.

Not loud, not openly—but softly, secretly. A faint sniff, a quiet tremor. Other children cry without shame; this one hid her sorrow.

Mrs. Bretton didn’t hear. Good thing.

Soon, a voice from the corner: “May the bell be rung for Harriet?”

I rang. The nurse arrived.

“Harriet, I must be put to bed,” the child said. “You must ask where my bed is.”

Harriet nodded—she already had.

“Ask if you sleep with me, Harriet.”

“No, Missy,” she replied. “You’ll share this young lady’s room.”

The child didn’t move. But I saw her eyes turn toward me.

After a moment, she rose from her corner.

“I wish you good night, ma’am,” she said to Mrs. Bretton.

Then she passed me without a word.

“Good-night, Polly,” I said.

“No need,” she answered. “We sleep in the same room.” And she vanished.

I heard Harriet offer to carry her upstairs.

“No need,” she insisted. “No need, no need.” And her small steps climbed the stairs, slow and weary.

An hour later, when I went to bed, I found her still awake. She sat upright, propped by pillows, her hands folded neatly on the sheet. Her posture was unnervingly composed—more like a grown woman than a child.

I said nothing for a while. Just before turning out the light, I said, “You should lie down.”

“By and by.”

“But you’ll catch cold, Missy.”

She picked up a small garment from the chair beside her crib and wrapped it around her shoulders.

I left her alone.

In the darkness, I listened. She was crying again—quietly, carefully, as if afraid to make a sound.

At dawn, the sound of water trickling caught my ear. There she was—perched on a stool near the washstand, struggling to pour water from the ewer into the basin. It was heavy; she couldn’t lift it easily.

I watched her wash and dress—tiny, silent, determined. Clearly, she wasn’t used to doing this alone. Buttons, strings, hooks and eyes—all presented challenges. But she faced them with quiet persistence.

She folded her nightdress neatly, smoothed the sheets, then retreated into the corner, hidden by the white curtain.

I raised my head slightly to see what she was doing.

On her knees, her forehead resting on her hands, she was praying.

A knock at the door.

She jumped up.

“I’m dressed, Harriet,” she said. “I dressed myself, but I don’t feel neat. Make me neat!”

“Why did you dress yourself, Missy?”

“Hush! Speak low—for fear of waking *the girl*.”

(Meaning me.)

“I dressed myself to learn—before you leave me.”

“Do you want me to go?”

“When you’re cross, I’ve wanted you to go many times. But not now. Tie my sash straight. Please make my hair smooth.”

“It’s straight enough. What a particular little thing you are!”

“It must be tied again. Please.”

“There. Now, when I’m gone, you’ll have to ask that young lady to dress you.”

“On no account.”

“Why? She’s very nice. I hope you’ll behave well, Missy.”

“She shall dress me on no account.”

“Comical little thing.”

“You’re not combing straight through my hair. The line will be crooked.”

“Ay, you’re hard to please. Does that suit?”

“Pretty well. Where should I go now that I’m dressed?”

“I’ll take you to breakfast.”

“Come, then.”

They walked to the door.

She stopped.

“Oh! Harriet, I wish this was papa’s house! I don’t know these people.”

“Be a good child, Missy.”

“I am good—but I ache here,” she said, pressing her hand to her chest. “Papa! papa!” she moaned.

I stirred, sat up.

“Say good-morning to the young lady,” Harriet instructed.

She said, “Good-morning,” and followed her nurse out.

Harriet left the next day to visit friends nearby.

When I descended, I found Paulina—she called herself Polly, but her full name was Paulina Mary—seated at the breakfast table beside Mrs. Bretton. A mug of milk stood before her, a piece of bread in her hand. But she wasn’t eating.

“How we shall win this little one over,” Mrs. Bretton said to me, “I don’t know. She eats nothing. And by her look, she hasn’t slept.”

I said I believed kindness and time would help.

“If she takes a liking to someone here,” Mrs. Bretton replied, “she’ll settle. But until then… I don’t know.”
