I had not slept in days, and so when night fell, I slipped into a deep rest while Nais watched over me inside the quiet temple. We had planned for me to wait until the camp was asleep—then I could slip away unseen toward the sea.

But the night brought rain, a cold drizzle that seeped through the cracks in the temple walls. Outside, fires burned steadily, their flames flickering against the damp air. Men and women huddled in animal hides and tattered clothes, warming themselves as steam rose from their soaked bodies. Such discomfort breeds sharp tempers, and Nais frowned at the sight. “A fine night,” she said dryly. “I would have sent you back to the city without a soul knowing. But this chill keeps people restless. We must wait until they’re too tired to notice.”

We sat together on the ancient stone floor, untouched by worshippers for generations. Few words passed between us—but silence can be its own kind of companionship.

Then, slowly, the night stirred. From far off, a horn or shell began to blare—a harsh, unrelenting sound. It drew nearer, louder, and soon voices erupted from the fires, shouting at the noise. The sound stopped abruptly, and we stood to look through the slits in the wall.

The camp was waking. Figures gathered around a figure standing atop the pedestal of an old war engine, speaking with fervor. We couldn’t hear his words, but it was clear he held their attention.

“Let’s go,” Nais whispered. “We’ll move away from the firelight.”

“But how do I explain being here?”

“There’s no need to explain,” she replied sharply. “They’ll think you’re my lover. That’s common enough. But oh, if only the gods had given you a beard—curled like any other man—you could’ve slipped away hours ago.”

“A smooth chin suits me better.”

“So it does,” she murmured, pushing open the temple door. “Yours.”

Outside, ragged men and women called out for her. She had a voice, they said, and they feared this preacher might divide them unless someone countered him. So they summoned her.

And now, I found myself swept into danger by chance. But Nais refused to let conflict begin. “Come with me,” she whispered. “Stay the light.”

As we stepped out, the crowd didn’t linger long. A few coarse jokes flew—jokes I could have answered with steel—but they moved on, turning every now and then to make sure we followed. Around us, others streamed forward, shadows against the black sky. Rain splattered the fires, and behind us came more footsteps. No one slept anymore. All were drawn to the preacher on the war engine. To try to escape the crowd would have been impossible.

So we walked forward, deeper into the storm.

At first, we heard little of what the old man said. Then, near the end of a passionate speech, he asked: “Who are your chiefs?”

Chaos broke out. “We have no chiefs!” the crowd roared. “We are done with rulers! Take your magic—we’ll die before we bow again. Not Phorenice. Not priests. Not anyone. We are free!”

The press brought us closer. Now we saw him clearly—an old man with white hair tumbling over his shoulders, a long untrimmed beard, naked except for a scrap of cloth around his waist. His body glistened in the rain.

Yet in his hand, he held something unmistakable: a wand tipped with a flame that never died. It burned like a star fallen from heaven, casting a halo of light across his wet form. This was the Symbol of our Lord the Sun—the mark of the Priestly Clan. No forgery could match it. No civilized man would doubt it.

And though the crowd raged, they did not question his authority. They acknowledged his power. “You may kill us with your magic,” they screamed, “but rule us? Never.”

But I saw him differently. This was Zaemon—Nais’s father. He had seen me yesterday, seated beside Phorenice. Today, he could call me Deucalion and have me destroyed. These rebels had already tried to sink a navy to kill me. If they knew I was near, even with Nais defending me, they’d tear me apart.

I felt death closing in. The gods guard the threads of life well, but I sensed mine was fraying.

Nais saw it too. She gripped my hand—tight—and I squeezed back. I would miss her. And yet, a grim satisfaction stirred within me. If they came for me, I would leave behind a pile of dead. That was the nature of a warrior—especially one facing his last battle. The hunger for combat is deeper than fear, and only one who has fought to the end knows how fiercely it burns.

For now, Zaemon did not see me. He was lost in his sermon, and I had time to listen.

He spoke of the ancient order. Since the dawn of time, the gods had chosen a single clan to lead and serve as priests. For centuries, they ruled justly—defending Atlantis from invaders, mastering beasts, studying the earth to warn of earthquakes and eruptions. Their purpose was always the good of the land, the glory of the gods.

“But,” he thundered, waving the glowing symbol, “the people grew soft. They forgot the gods’ gifts, believing their prosperity came from their own strength. They abandoned worship. Temples fell silent. And so the gods punished them.”

He told of a priest’s wife who found a child left by the roadside and adopted her. That child—the gods shaped her into a scourge. Her name was Phorenice. And those who suffered under her rule knew the full weight of divine wrath.

“But the gods can raise up as they can cast down,” Zaemon declared. “Those who will bring Phorenice low are already chosen. The old order shall return—but only after you have been humbled enough to beg for it.”

He raised the symbol. “See this power? You know it. You feel it. I am its voice. Choose: Will the gods destroy Phorenice? Or must you suffer longer before you are ready to return?”

Then, a young man pushed through the crowd and leapt onto the platform. “Hear me, brothers!” he bellowed. “We are done with tyrants. Death comes for all of us, and we’ve shown we don’t fear it. But we will not submit again. My lord,” he said, turning to Zaemon, “you may kill me with magic. But I speak my truth.”

“I can,” said Zaemon, “but I won not.” He turned, eyes narrowing. “You’ve said your folly. Step down.”

“We have free speech,” the youth insisted. “I will not go until I choose.”

“You will,” said Zaemon. He made no motion—no threat. Yet the young man suddenly froze. His mouth opened, but no sound came. Fear seized him. He stumbled backward, vanishing into the crowd.

The people gasped. Then they demanded a miracle. “Show us a sign!” they shouted. “Prove your power! Then maybe we’ll return!”

Zaemon glowered. “You dogs! Empty-headed fools! Do you think I should degrade the sacred mysteries into a circus act? You want to be tempted back? Then come in humility. I swear you’ll find mercy. Remain stubborn, and Phoren: the scourge—may grind you into dust before she herself is brought to justice.”

The crowd roared. Weapons rose. The desire for independence was strong—stronger than any loyalty. Those who might have surrendered stayed silent, afraid of the mob’s fury. A cruel joke about freedom, I thought, whispering to Nais. But she clutched my arm. “Be quiet,” she hissed. “Or they’ll tear you apart. They’re hungry for blood.”

“Just hours ago,” I reminded her, “you wanted me dead.”

She turned, eyes flashing. “A woman can change her mind. But it becomes you little to remind her of her fickleness.”

A man beside me turned sharply. “Oh?” he said. “No beard. Who are you? Why cut it instead of curling it? I see no wound.”

I smiled politely. “With freedom as your creed, my chin is my own business.”

But he wasn’t satisfied. He looked like trouble. So I grabbed him—by the throat and shoulder—twisted his neck with a swift, brutal motion. I heard the crack. Before anyone noticed, I released him. The crowd held him upright. He stood there, head drooping, as if asleep or fainting. I didn’t want to start a fight here—not in this crush.

But Zaemon still preached. He led them through the ancient rites. And as he chanted, the people responded—old habits returning. They sang the old prayers, voices rising in reverence.

“... WHO STILLED THE BURNING OF THE MOUNTAINS, AND MADE COOL PLACES ON THE EARTH FOR US TO LIVE—PRAISE TO THE MOST HIGH GODS!

“WHO GAVE US MASTERY OVER THE LESSER BEASTS AND SKILL OF TEN TIMES TO PREVAIL—PRAISE TO THE MOST HIGH GODS....”

Their voices swelled with emotion. It was moving. But I knew they’d forget it by morning. People burn bright for a moment, then fade.

When the ritual ended, Zaemon stepped down, the symbol held high. The crowd parted before it, making a path. And then—I realized what was happening.

At first, I thought he was leaving. But he kept walking—straight toward me.

Now I understood. After addressing the masses, he meant to deal with me. The man he had seen at Phorenice’s side. The enemy.

My final battle—gone. No glorious fight. No mound of corpses. Just a sentence.

But I remained calm. When he reached me, I gave the priestly salute. He returned it with solemn precision. The crowd recoiled, driven back by the symbol’s power. Even Nais, daughter of a priest, could not resist it. We stood alone in a ring of light, surrounded by hungry faces.

“Spy!” the people roared. “A spy! Zaemon salutes him as a priest!”

Zaemon turned. “Yes. He is a priest. His name is Deucalion.”

The name echoed through the crowd—shock, anger, hatred.

They had already hunted me. Now they had me. They would rip me apart.

But the symbol’s glow held them back. Like lava, it burned their approach.

Zaemon faced me. He spoke in the sacred tongue.

“Brother,” he said, “which do you serve? Deucalion—or Atlantis?”

Words fail such a question. “I have lived by the law,” I said. “Each ship from Yucatan carries reports. My record is known. I served Atlantis faithfully. But Phorenice changed things.”

“You have governed well. Lived simply. You loved Atlantis. But you had no Phorenice to tempt you.”

“If you send me away, I will not see her again.”

“Yes,” he said. “But then you lose your purpose. No, brother—you are our last hope. All else has failed. You return from abroad for the final duel. You are our strongest. Our champion. If you fall, only the powers locked in the Ark remain. And though I cannot speak of them—even here—you know their strength.”

I shrugged. “Then why not throw me to these wolves? Let them end me now.”

He frowned. “I command you to do your duty. Why evade it?”

“I remember your words in the pyramid. ‘PHORENICE,’ you cried, ‘WHILST YOU ARE YET EMPRESS, YOU SHALL SEE THIS ROYAL PYRAMID TORN TIER FROM TIER, STONE FROM STONE, SCATTERED AS FEATHERS BEFORE A WIND.’ You foresee my defeat.”

He shuddered. “I speak only what was revealed. Perhaps she will repent. But before we use the gods’ darkest tools, we must exhaust all human means. So you must go. Play your part to the end.”

“It is an order. I obey.”

“You will return to Phorenice by dawn. She has summoned you—from Yucatan—as her husband. She thinks you strong enough to sustain her rule. You are a priest. You are persuasive. Your task: make her see the power that can destroy her. Show her the futility of resistance.”

“If it is ordered, I will do it. But I have seen Phorenice. Her will is unbreakable. Others have failed. You have failed. I doubt I will succeed.”

“Words that fell on deaf ears once may pierce a wife’s heart. You are to be her husband. Accept your role.”

The order struck me hard. I had never cared much for women. But marrying Phorenice meant ending my connection with Nais—forever. The thought twisted my chest. But duty outweighed desire. I bowed. He placed his hand on my forehead.

“It is an order,” I said. “If our Lord the Sun grants me life, I will obey.”

“Then let us go,” said Zaemon. He took my arm and waved the symbol ahead of us. The crowd parted. No one attacked. No one followed.

Rain fell. Fires flickered. The old man’s bare body glistened in the dark. Ahead, the Sacred Mountain loomed—its peak crowned with eternal flames.

I sighed. I remembered the peace of its temples, the quiet groves. But peace was gone. The final test awaited.
