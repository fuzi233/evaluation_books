It would be tedious to recount in detail how, with merely a handful of highly trained fighters, I charged and recharged until I finally shattered the horde of rebels that outnumbered us fifteen to one. One must remember that the enemy's morale collapsed instantly upon realizing that not a single soul returned from the mine tunnel—their secret weapon—where they had placed such high hopes. Furthermore, the initial screams of terror inside the city walls quickly morphed into cries of triumph, only to turn to confusion when our counter-attack succeeded. These wretched rebels lacked any genuine military training; armed with crude weaponry and bound by foolish egalitarian principles that rendered everyone equal to everyone else, they possessed no commanders and no leadership structure whatsoever.

When panic finally seized them, it spread like a malignant plague through their ragged ranks. There were no officers to rally the fleeing masses, nor anyone to direct the few who remained to fight with desperate bravery. My strategy was deceptively simple: relentless pursuit. My men and I refused to adopt a defensive posture. We struck one flank, surged through the center, and then harried the other, hacking a path through their solid mass repeatedly. By keeping them perpetually running and ignorant of the direction of our next assault, we fermented their fear until those in the outer lines scrambled toward the forests and scorched fields, while those in the inner packs desperately sought to follow suit.

Breaking up this rebel encampment required no feat of arms worthy of a veteran soldier's boast. It was merely seizing the fleeting advantages of the moment. Had we engaged them in a formal battle on their own ground, these desperate Atlanteans—who were far from cowardly, sharing the same blood as the citizens within the city—would have fought to the death. Spurred by centuries of oppression and grievous wrongs, their sheer ferocity and numbers would have overwhelmed any disciplined phalanx the city could muster. Nevertheless, the rebellion was effectively dissolved. The siege was lifted, and it became evident that the uprising could be quelled permanently, provided we avoided excessive cruelty. Too much severity often drives malcontents to even greater acts of despair.

Having pursued the fugitives to ensure their total rout and to instill a lasting lesson of panic, we had marched far from the city walls. Exhausted by the burning heat of the day, I decided to halt among some half-ruined houses that could serve as a temporary fortification. Fate, however, intervened kindly; a drove of frightened, cloven-hoofed animals had stumbled into our lines, and after killing five, we secured a soldier's supper. Fires were lit, and the wounded tended to their injuries while sentries patrolled the perimeter. Around the embers, officers discussed the day's engagements with gusto—a pastime entirely lost on civilians.

As the hour for sleep approached, snoring filled the air. My officers, worn out, continued talking only out of deference to my presence. Yet, I harbored a feverish dread of solitude and pressed the conversation relentlessly. Finally, a sentry interrupted, breaking the spell. "My lord," he reported, "a woman has approached the bivouac attempting to enter."

"How is she named?" I asked.

"She refuses to speak."

"Does she have a purpose?"

"She claims none, yet she demands to see my lord."

"Bring her to the fire," I commanded. On second thought, recalling that she likely bore news intended solely for my ears, I added, "Wait." Rising from the ground, I followed the sentry to the outer line.

"Where is she?" I asked.

"My comrades are restraining her," the soldier replied nervously. "She could be a rebel spy intent on stabbing you. Besides, the Empress currently holds you in high regard, and we know the cunning of her tormentors."

"Your caution is noted," I said, and he revealed the figure. Though muffled in heavy hood and cloak, the silhouette was unmistakably that of Ylga, my twin sister. Without hesitation, I ordered the sentries to release her and led her away from the camp, beyond the range of their voices.

"It is urgent business that brings you here, Ylga?" I asked softly.

"You know me, then? There must be something deeper than kinship between us, Deucalion, if you recognized me beneath these disguises."

"I'll ignore that." "What is your errand?"

"Aye," she said bitterly. "Here is my reward. Your concern lies only with the message, not the messenger. Very well, my lord: you are no longer the husband of the delicate Phorenice."

"This is news."

"And true. She has divorced you, cast you aside, and awaits the penalties due to her wrath. She believes you shared your priest's magic with Nais, causing her to sleep until you choose to wake her—even if a century passes. To enlighten her, she discovered another airshaft leading to a cell where another prisoner lay. That wretch overheard your union and tried to bargain for freedom by revealing it. His news was slightly outdated, however. Due to morning ceremonies, his rations were forgotten until late, by which time Phorenice had publicly married, and poor Nais had likely consumed her 'green drug.' Naturally, the fool paid for his tale with his head. Afterward, Phorenice's behavior becomes understandable."

Ylga paused, her voice hardening. "Thank me for warning you not to return to Atlantis."

"But I intend to return. If the Empress chooses to sever my head, so be it, as the High Gods will."

"You are more resigned to death than I anticipated. Yet, Phorenice's judgment appears accurate. I overheard her fury: *'Shall I chop off his head? Pah! Shall I hand him to my tormentors while they feast? He wouldn't even flinch. No, he requires a heavier punishment—one that endures. I shall lop off his right hand and left foot, rendering him incapable of war, then drive him crippled into the dangerous wilds to learn Fear. Beasts shall hunt him, the earth's fires shall ruin his rest. He shall know hunger and foul air, all while remembering that I hold Nais near me, locked in her stone coffin, to toy with as I please.'* That is her decree, Deucalion. Will you return to face such vengeance?"

"No," I replied firmly. "Mutilation and abandonment are not part of my plan."

"Being sensible, I concluded. Moreover, retaining a shred of kindness for you, I devised a plan to aid your escape. Do the name 'Tob' ring a bell?"

"The former Captain of Tatho's navy?"

"That same Tob. A gruff, foul-smelling man, yet possessing a sturdy honesty. He sails tonight to unknown parts, presumably to found a kingdom with himself as king. Struggling to earn a living in Atlantis lately and fearing for his family, he confided in me at the harbor. He claimed you were exactly the recruit he needed, promising to serve you even if you didn't join him."

"With Tob, I gained experience fighting the great sea beasts."

"Then go and do it again. Trust me, it is your only chance. I would be heartbroken to hear the searing iron hiss on your stumps. I negotiated with Tob to slip past the harbor defenses before the chains rose. Being a daring navigator, he agreed to meet you at a prearranged point on the shore. Come, Deucalion; let me guide you there."

"My girl," I said, "I owe you profound gratitude for this night's salvation."

"Oh, thanks!" she scoffed. "Keep them. I ventured into the dark and danger not for mere gratitude, though I knew little else awaited." She tugged my sleeve. "Now match my walking pace, sir. The camp will demand your presence shortly, and we need no prying eyes questioning our movements."

Thus, we departed, leaving the bivouac lights behind. Ylga led the way, while I kept my weapons ready against beast or man. Words were scarce, limited to warnings of natural hazards. Only once did we touch: crossing a stream fed by a boiling spring.

"Are you certain of your footing?" I asked, for the night was pitch black and the water's heat could strip flesh from bone.

"No," she admitted, reaching out to grasp my hand. I helped her across, released her grip, and watched her sigh as she slowly withdrew her hand. Silence fell between us again as we walked side by side for hours and leagues.

Finally, we crested a rise. Below, through the trees, the great estuary glittered, cradling the city of Atlantis. The terrain was treacherous—soggy ground, thorny vegetation—but when I offered my arm, she rebuked me rudely. "I am no frail weakling," she snapped. "If that is your sole reason for touching me."

Soon, we emerged from the woods, completing the hardest part of the journey. A mile away, anchored in the shallow surf, lay a strange vessel under the starlight. Tob kept a sharp watch. Challenged immediately, we halted, but he greeted us civilly upon recognizing us. He signaled his crew to slacken the anchor rope until the ship grounded, then extended two steering oars to assist our climb aboard.

Turning to Ylga, I offered words of thanks and farewell. "I shall never forget what you have done. Should the High Gods grant me a return to Atlantis and power, you shall taste my gratitude."
