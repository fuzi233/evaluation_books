Arthur Pendennis had camped himself in a dark window nook draped in calico curtains, working so hard at his gloom and scowl his eyebrows looked like they’d fused together, all to glower at sweet Miss Laura Bell dancing her very first quadrille with partner Mr. Pynsent. That gentleman was every inch the solemn stiff Englishman at formal events, trudging through the dance like he was marching up the aisle to his Sunday church pew, zero flicker of a smile on his face, fully committed to treating a casual party dance like it was a mandatory government audit.

Laura, meanwhile, was positively glowing. The lights, the crowd, the waltzing music all had her giddy as a kid on a fairground ride. She fluffed out her white skirts, bounced through each dance step grinning, her brown ringlets slipping back over her pink, honest shoulders, and half the men in the room were sneaking glances at her the whole time. Lady Fogey, the local social climber who fancied herself the official London fashion expert even when she was stuck in the country, leaned over to Lady Rockminster to ask who the delightful young thing was, drawling that she resembled a super trendy London beauty everyone was fawning over and “would absolutely do” as a party hit. Lady Rockminster was this close to rolling her eyes right out of her head: of course her protégé would do, who did this Fogey woman think she was to pass judgment? She peered at Laura through her lorgnette, approving of the girl’s artless, unforced joy. “Her manners are excellent,” she thought to herself. “Arms a touch rosy, but that’s just from running around fields at her age. Far less annoying than that pert little Miss Amory prancing across from her.”

Blanche Amory was, in fact, Laura’s vis-a-vis for the quadrille, grinning her most dazzling fake smile at her “dearest friend” and bobbing her head every time they passed each other across the floor, acting like she was personally responsible for teaching Laura how to walk, let alone dance. Her shoulders were the whitest in the whole room, and they never stayed still in her dress for a single second: her eyes darted around the crowd nonstop, her tiny little frame practically yelling at every guest, “LOOK AT ME NOT THAT PINK HEALTHY COUNTRY LASS! I taught her everything she knows! This is real Parisian grace! Look at my fancy tiny French dancing shoes, Mr. Pynsent! Look at them, you grumpy Arthur Pendennis hiding behind the curtain, I know you’re dying to ask me to dance!”

Laura never took her eyes off Pen the whole quadrille, though. He didn’t budge from his curtain nook once the first dance ended, not even until good-natured Lady Clavering waved him over to the raised dais where all the fancy dowagers were camped out. Pen trundled over bright red and awkward as anything, exactly like every overconfident 20-something who just got called over by their mum in front of a whole room of people. He gave Lady Rockminster a dramatic fancy bow, which she ignored so hard he might as well have been a stray cat wandering into the party, then went to say hello to Blanche’s mum, the widowed Mrs. Amory, who was decked out in so many diamonds, velvet, lace, and feathers she looked like a jewelry shop display that got into a pillow fight.

Laura’s next dance partner was 16-year-old Eton schoolboy Young Fogey, who was still waiting impatiently for his first beard to grow in so he could join a fancy dragoon regiment. He stared at Laura like she’d invented chocolate, and blurted out completely unprompted: “I like you way better than that stuck-up French girl I was just dancing with.” Laura cackled, looking even more good-humored than usual, and mid-laugh she spotted Pen still pouting and strutting around being absurdly pompous. Next up was the waltz, and Young Fogey sighed so hard his whole chest caved in — he couldn’t waltz at all, and immediately swore to book 12 private dance lessons the second he got home for the holidays. Mr. Pynsent grabbed Laura for the waltz, and Pen saw red. A year ago, they’d all shoved the furniture aside at Clavering Park, called the governess down to play piano, and thrown impromptu tiny balls, with Chevalier Strong doing perfect hornpipes, waltzes, even Spanish fandangos if you asked him, no one cared who was twirling who. Now boring, stiff Mr. Pynsent had his arm around Laura’s waist spinning her around the whole room? The absolute audacity!

Blanche, who was a brilliant dancer, got stuck with the worst possible partner: Captain Broadfoot of the local dragoons, who was so determined not to mess up he moved slower than a man wading through molasses, and had zero ear for rhythm at all. The whole room was oohing over Laura’s obvious joy, while Blanche, stuck trundling along behind Broadfoot, realized she was losing the crowd’s attention to her country cousin. She faked a dramatic fatigue attack after two turns, made Broadfoot escort her back over to her mum, where Pen was hanging around chatting. “Why didn’t you ask me to waltz?” she huffed at him. “You left me stuck with that horrible spurs-and-red-coat oaf!” Pen snickered. “Wait, I thought all young ladies thought red coats and spurs were the most fascinating thing alive? My boring black coat couldn’t possibly compete with that fancy uniform.”

“You’re such a cruel, sulky, naughty thing,” Blanche huffed, shrugging her white shoulders dramatically. “Go away, your cousin’s staring right at you over Pynsent’s shoulder.”

“Wanna waltz with me next?” Pen offered.

“Not this one, obviously, I just sent Broadfoot away. I was supposed to dance the next waltz with Mr. Poole, but I’ll ditch him for you,” Blanche grinned.

“Women do forget their promises awfully easy, don’t they?” Pen teased.

“But we always come back later all apologetic and sweet, don’t we?” Blanche winked. “Oh look, here comes Mr. Pynsent, that human fireplace poker, with dear Laura leaning on his arm. Doesn’t she look ridiculous?”

Laura bounded up, holding her hand out to Pen, while Pynsent gave him a stiff little bow, every bit as graceful as the iron poker Blanche had just joked about. “I’m having the best time, Pen!” Laura said, all bright and kind. “The three first dances are over, I’m yours for the next one, right?”

“Ah, sorry,” Pen said, full of petty glee, “I just promised Miss Amory the next waltz.” Blanche nodded at Laura all smug, giving her a tiny fake curtsy.

“Well then you’ll waltz with me the one after that, right Pen?” Laura laughed. “Won’t you?”

“Sorry, I promised Miss Amory that one too.” Pen said. Laura huffed in mock exasperation, gave a curtsy back, and went to go tuck herself under Lady Rockminster’s ample wing.

Pen was absolutely delighted with himself. Two of the prettiest girls at the whole ball were fighting over him! He was such a genius! He leaned against the wall all dandified, elbows propped up, and roasted every single person in the room without mercy: the dragoons squeezed into skin-tight jackets that looked like they’d been painted on, the local county dandies in their absurd ill-fitting suits, the ladies with insane hair that looked like it had entire birds’ nests and bunches of fake grapes tucked in it. “That woman over there has a hairstyle made entirely of almonds and raisins,” Pen cackled. “They could serve her up for dessert straight after the roast.” Blanche was laughing so hard at his jokes she could barely breathe, making extra sure Laura saw how close and merry the two of them were being across the dance floor.

Laura still didn’t get mad at Pen at all. She just figured he was throwing a silly jealous tantrum, which was kind of cute, honestly. So when the next waltz struck, and Pen was still ignoring her, she grabbed Chevalier Strong instead, who was a far better dancer than half the people in the room, and spun off across the floor having the absolute time of her life, not glancing Pen’s way once. Pen, twirling Blanche around like they were both professional opera dancers, was feeling on top of the world… right up until he and Blanche plowed full tilt into Captain Broadfoot, who was waltzing with the giant Miss Roundle, wearing a dress the exact shade of strawberry ice cream. All four of them lost their balance completely. Broadfoot and Miss Roundle toppled flat, Blanche landed safely on a bench against the wall, and Pen ate the dance floor face first in a sprawling mess. The whole room burst out giggling.

Pen was furious. He hated being laughed at more than anything in the entire world. He scrambled up, red as a tomato, sputtering apologies to Blanche, and blaming the whole mess on Broadfoot and that silly lady in the pink dress. Blanche was seething too, declared she was terribly hurt and would never waltz again. Then a random guy with a blue ribbon and a fancy medal pinned to his chest sprinted over, handed her a glass of water. Blanche, in full dramatic mode, gave him her haughtiest, most gracious smile, put her hand on his arm, and asked him to escort her back to her mum. The guy nearly fainted with delight, bowed so deep his forehead almost touched her hand, and preened around the room like he’d just won the lottery.

“BY JOVE!” Pen yelped before he could stop himself. “That’s the cook! Mirobolant!”

Blanche was too flustered to hear the comment, but Mirobolant did. He turned and glowered at Pen over his shoulder, looking like he was two seconds away from stabbing him through the chest with a meat skewer. Chevalier Strong, who knew all the tea about the poor chef’s infatuation with Blanche, rushed over, babbled a few lines of Spanish the Gascon-born Mirobolant understood, and told him he’d get fired instantly if he was caught parading around the ball with a guest on his arm. Mirobolant dropped Blanche’s arm like it was a hot potato, gave a sad deep bow, and backed off. “That’s Mirobolant, the chef,” Strong explained to Blanche as he led her off to get an ice. “He’s so madly in love with you he was this close to running Pen through with a roasting spit just now.” Blanche sighed, “Poor M. Mirobolant! But how was I supposed to know he was a cook? He has a medal! His eyes are beautiful! He looks like a fancy foreign noble!”

Then they heard a huge scuffle and yelling coming from the main ballroom, and Strong sprinted off to see what the chaos was about, leaving Blanche alone in the select refreshment room with Colonel Altamont, the weird guy with the black wig and the medals who worked for the Nawab of Lucknow. He’d been camped there all night drinking champagne, and he stared at Blanche for a full minute with his little red, wine-glowing eyes before he slurred out, “Hey, you’re Betsy Amory, aren’t you? Betsy Amory, by Jove!” Blanche froze, confused, and the noise from the ballroom got so loud we simply have to rush back there to see exactly what caused all the commotion.
