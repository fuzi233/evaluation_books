My cross-continent trek across the breadth of ancient Atlantis was stitched with hidden danger at every turn, but there is no need to drag through every tedious mile. Only one forced stop broke my pace, and it very nearly became my last.
I’d run a stag to ground across miles of sunbaked savannah, driven my spear home, and scented the air carefully for sign of lion, cave bear, or any other predator that might claim the kill before I could butcher it. Convinced the clearing was safe, I knelt to dress the meat and tie a heavy bundle to my shoulders—until my heel brushed something coiling in the long grass.
It was a monstrous green venomous centipede, its hundred legs glistening with slime, its fangs dripping a poison I’d learned to fear from old campfire tales. It struck faster than a dart of lightning, sinking its jaws deep into the flesh of my lower thigh. I hacked its head off with my hunting knife mid-strike, watched its writhing body twist its remaining legs into mad knots on the dirt, then drove my blade deep around the bite in a ring of cuts, forcing the tainted blood to pour out and wash the poison clean before it could course higher. I thought I’d cheated a terrible death. I slung the venison over my shoulder and set off, pleased with the quick escape.
By the third hour of walking, my leg throbbed so hard I could feel the pulse hammering in my toes. The skin swelled tight as stretched parchment, the ache crawling up my hip until a violent, roiling sickness wracked my whole frame. I could barely stand. Any half-starved predator that stumbled on me then would have made a meal of me without a single fight. I dragged myself up into the crook of a thick oak at full dark, and huddled there awake through the whole long night, sweating through waves of agony.
At dawn, when the night hunters had slunk back to their lairs, I clambered down, leaned my whole weight on my spear, and limped onward through the shadowed forest. The moss on the north side of trees was my compass, but soon I was seeing moss creeping around every trunk, my vision swimming from fever and the iron-hard swelling in my leg. My last remaining clear thought was of the High Council’s promise: defeat Phorenice, and Nais, my lost love drugged into eight years of enchanted sleep, would be woken. I would not stop before I did it. That was the last thing I remembered before a dark shape stepped out of the trees and caught me as I fell.
I woke to the feel of soft moss and dried ferns under my back, laid out in a narrow cleft between two boulders, its entrance barred with sharp stakes driven into the dirt to keep out prowling beasts. My thigh was wrapped in a thick, warm herbal poultice, and across the cave, a woman squatted tending a fire of sticks, stirring a pot of roasted wood grubs and wild honey.
“Who brought me here?” I croaked.
“I did,” she said, not looking up. “Folk call me the nymph of the western woods. I collect herbs, cure fevers, tell fortunes, lay curses on the folks who skip paying for my services. Lean work these days, what with wars and pestilence wiping out half the population round here.”
“Do you get news of the city, Atlantis?”
She snorted. “Not me. Empress Phorenice would boil me alive in brine if she caught me. That vain tyrant don’t tolerate no magic but her own. Word is she’s marching her whole army on the Sacred Mountain now, to drag the old high priests out of their sanctum.”
The news spilled fast: Phorenice still sat on the throne, and had taken Tatho as her husband three years prior, bearing him twin sons. When I asked about the distant colony of Yucatan, the story made my blood run cold: hordes of squat, hairy cannibal men had poured over the western sea, armed with nothing but stone-tipped clubs, driven by no hunger for plunder or slaves, only a ravenous lust to eat Atlantean flesh. They’d overrun every Yucatan city, swarming over the walls by the thousands even as the colonists slaughtered them by the tens of thousands, eating every last inhabitant, then moving on to the next settlement. Tatho and the last handful of survivors had barely escaped on a fishing boat, leaving the whole colony I’d worked years to build abandoned to the jungle. I said nothing to the half-mad old healer of my ties to that lost land, only thanked her, stayed six days in her cave while her herbs drew the last of the venom out of my leg, then hunted down a fat wild horse to leave her as payment before I set off again.
The lands east of her cave were a graveyard. Once, homesteaders had built log houses heavy and dovetailed tight enough to keep cave bears from breaking through, strung tree-perched huts across the forest, built walled villages to fend off predators. Now nine out of ten of them stood empty. Hearth stones were choked with weeds, vegetable patches reclaimed by scrub, village log gates rotting into the undergrowth, no trace left of the people who’d lived there but piles of old kitchen midden. Phorenice’s tyranny had spread over the land like a rotting blight, sucking the life out of every corner.
I avoided every village I could, slipping off into the forest to circle their garbage stench rather than waste time fighting the half-starved, desperate locals who rushed out to seize unvetted travelers and press them into forced labor. I had no wish to kill them; I could see they were only trying to survive under the heel of a mad empress.
By the time I was within half a day’s march of Atlantis itself, the Sacred Mountain loomed ahead, its ring of eternal sacred fires glowing red against the dusk. Every fighting man for miles had been conscripted into the siege army ringed around the mountain’s only accessible approach. The whole plain was patrolled end to end. A single man could never fight his way through that host. I hid in the edge of a wood, beating my brains out for a trick to get past the lines, when a man came running toward me, his hands up.
I recognized Ro instantly: a low-ranking priest I’d never trusted, greedy, cowardly, eager to save his own skin above all else. He told me Zaemon, the highest of the Three High Priests, had found him in his temple, paralyzed his right hand to force him to track me down, and given me my orders: light a massive grass fire on the plain as a signal that I’d arrived, then sneak around to the sheer, unguarded sea cliff on the far side of the Sacred Mountain at midnight, where the priests above would lower a rope to haul me up. The last thing Ro mumbled before he scurried off, begging me to ask Zaemon to heal his hand and slip him a few coins to feed his wife, was a piece of news that shocked me cold: Phorenice had stolen the Secret of Life, the most guarded holy secret only the Three High Priests were supposed to know, and made herself functionally immortal.
I set the dry grass of the plain alight before Ro was even out of sight. The fire roared up, bright as a bonfire, and that midnight I picked my way across the dark, beast-haunted plain to the bare, sheer cliff face. A thin rope snaked down over the rock not long after I arrived, I tied it tight around my waist, and the priests above hauled me up hand over hand. The men dragging me over the crest were old, their lungs wheezing with the effort, every last young priest-warrior having already fallen fighting the siege below.
They led me straight to Zaemon, who greeted me with the royal sign of the priesthood’s highest office. “All hail Deucalion, King of Atlantis, duly elected by the High Council,” he said.
I did not waste words. “Is Phorenice dead yet?”
“Not yet,” he said, his voice grim. “It is your duty to kill her. She has broken every law of the Gods, defiled every temple, set her own face up on altars across the empire to demand worship, and stolen the Secret of Life to make herself eternal. The High Gods have given the order: we will destroy her, even if we have to unleash every power locked in the Ark of the Mysteries and shatter the entire continent to do it. We have endured her evil for years, but now she has gone too far.”
I bowed, accepting the charge. “As the Gods command, I obey. But first, I must have my queen Nais woken. I cannot rule this realm half-hearted, not with her still asleep under that eight-year drug.”
Zaemon nodded. “She shall be brought to you at once.”
I stepped out of the cave then, the old high priest at my heels. It was still night, but the top of the Sacred Mountain never saw full dark. The ring of eternal fires, fed by natural gas venting up from the heart of the earth, burned bright around the whole crest, lighting the rock face as bright as midday, a wall of flame that no mortal army could ever hope to climb. Far below, Phorenice’s vast siege camp stretched across the plain, thousands of campfires twinkling like stars, the fight for the last stronghold of the old order just a breath away.
