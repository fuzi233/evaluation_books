On a brilliant summer day, Caroline had spent the entire afternoon in solitary confinement, her uncle having departed for Whinbury. The hours stretched out before her—long, radiant, silent, breezeless, and cloudless—and they seemed to drag on with the agonizing slowness of days passing overhead in the shadowless, trackless wastes of the Sahara, rather than within the blooming gardens of an English home. Seated in her alcove, her embroidery project resting on her lap, she worked with mechanical precision: her fingers moving diligently with the needle, her eyes tracking the thread, her mind restless beneath the surface.

Suddenly, Fanny appeared at the doorway, peering over the lawn and flowerbeds. Seeing no sign of her mistress, she called out, "Miss Caroline!"

A low voice replied softly, "Fanny!" issuing from the alcove. Fanny hurried toward her, holding a letter in her hand, which she handed to fingers that seemed to lose their strength momentarily. Miss Helstone accepted the note without inquiry, dropping it casually onto the folds of her unfinished work.

"Joe Scott's son, Harry, brought it," Fanny announced.

Though Fanny possessed no magic, her words cast a spell over her young mistress. Caroline lifted her head with a sudden, revitalized motion, shooting a sharp, questioning glance at the servant.

"Harry Scott! Who sent him?"

"He came from the Hollow."

The note was snatched up eagerly. The seal broke, and within two seconds, it was read. It was an affectionate message from Hortense, informing her cousin that she had returned from Wormwood Wells. Hortense noted that Robert was away at the market, leaving her alone, and expressed that nothing would please her more than Caroline's company for tea. She assured Caroline that such a change would be highly beneficial, noting that since the recent misunderstanding between Robert and Mr. Helstone had severed ties with her *meilleure amie*, Hortense Gérard Moore, Caroline must be lacking both safe guidance and stimulating society. A postscript urged Caroline to don her bonnet and descend immediately.

Caroline needed no further urging. Relieved to abandon the brown holland child's slip she was trimming for the Jewish charity basket, she rushed upstairs. She covered her curls with a straw bonnet and draped a black silk scarf around her shoulders; the simple draping suited her form perfectly, while the dark fabric highlighted the purity of her dress and the fairness of her complexion. Gladdened by the prospect of escaping her solitude, sadness, and nightly nightmares, she ran down the green lane sloping toward the Hollow. The scent of hedge flowers greeted her, sweeter than any rose or lily. Though she knew Robert was not currently at the cottage, the journey itself was delightful. To walk where he had recently walked, to enter the room where he had sat that morning, felt like a reunion. This illusion revived her spirit. Suddenly, the soft agitation of wings brushed her cheek, and the warm summer air seemed to whisper, *"Robert may return while you are in his house. Then, at least, you may look upon his face, offer him your hand, perhaps even sit beside him for a moment."*

"Silence!" she responded sternly internally, yet she cherished the comfort of the fantasy.

Perhaps Miss Moore spotted the flash of white through the garden shrubs from her window. She emerged from the cottage porch to greet her. As usual, she moved with straight, unbending dignity, devoid of haste or ecstasy. Yet, she smiled warmly, pleased to see the delight on her pupil's face, accepting her kiss and embrace with genuine kindness. She led Caroline inside, half-deceived and wholly flattered. Had she known the true source of this childish joy—the infatuation with her brother—she likely would have barred the door. Sisters generally find it presumptuous, silly, and weak for young ladies to fall in love with their brothers; such sentiments repel them as crude romances. The initial reaction to such news is typically a mix of impatience and contempt. While reason eventually corrects such feelings in rational minds, irrational ones remain unchanged, leading to lifelong dislike.

"You would expect to find me alone, based on my note," Miss Moore observed as they entered the parlour. "However, it was written this morning; since dinner, company has arrived."

She opened the door to reveal an ample display of crimson skirts overflowing an elbow-chair by the fire, presided over by a cap more formidable than a crown. This cap had never entered the cottage under a bonnet; it arrived in a vast bag—or rather, a medium-sized balloon of black silk reinforced with whalebone. Its brim extended a quarter-yard wide around the wearer's face, adorned with ribbons of the fashionable "love-ribbon" type, arranged in puffs and bows. There was an abundance of them. Mrs. Yorke wore the ensemble with striking success; the gown suited her no less than the cap.

This distinguished lady had visited in friendship to share tea. It was a favor nearly as significant as if the Queen had descended uninvited to share a meal with a subject—a high mark of distinction for someone who usually scorned social visits and dismissed local matrons as mere gossips.

Undeniably, Miss Moore was a favorite. Mrs. Yorke had demonstrated this repeatedly: pausing to speak in the churchyard on Sundays, inviting her hospitably to Briarmains, and today, showing grand condescension with a personal visit. According to Mrs. Yorke, Miss Moore was favored because she was a woman of steady deportment, devoid of levity, and as a foreigner, presumably in need of a friend. One might add that Miss Moore's plain appearance, precise dress, and phlegmatic manner served as additional recommendations. Ladies renowned for beauty, lively bearing, and elegant taste rarely enjoyed her approval. Mrs. Yorke admired what men despised and condemned what they prized.

Caroline approached the towering matron with a sense of diffidence. Knowing little of Mrs. Yorke and uncertain of the reception awaiting a parson's niece, she prepared herself for a cool welcome. She received exactly that and quickly hid her discomfort by removing her bonnet. Before she could settle, she was accosted by a small figure in a blue frock and sash. Like a fairy rising from the great dame's chair, the child darted past the folds of the red gown, threw her arms around Miss Helstone's neck, and demanded a kiss.

"My mother isn't civil to you," the petitioner declared, receiving and returning a smile. "Nor does Rose acknowledge you; that is their way. Even if a white angel crowned with stars had entered, mother would nod stiffly, and Rose wouldn't lift her head. But I will be your friend—I've always liked you."

"Jessie, curb your tongue and restrain your forwardness!" scolded Mrs. Yorke.

"But, mother, you are so frozen!" protested Jessie. "Miss Helstone hasn't harmed anyone. Why can't you be kind? You sit so stiff, look so cold, and speak so dry. That's how you treat Miss Shirley Keeldar and every other young lady who comes here. And Rose... she's such an auto—auto—I've forgotten the word, but it means a machine shaped like a human. Between you, you'll drive everyone away from Briarmains; Martin often says so."

"I am an automaton? Excellent! Leave me alone," retorted Rose, speaking from a corner where she sat on the carpet at the foot of a bookcase, a volume open on her knees. "Miss Helstone, how do you do?" she added, offering a brief glance before returning her gray, remarkable eyes to her book.

Caroline stole a quiet look at her, noting the unconscious movement of Rose's lips as she read—a gesture full of character. Possessing tact and fine instinct, Caroline sensed Rose was peculiar, truly unique. She approached quietly, kneeling on the carpet beside her to look over her shoulder. Rose was reading a romance by Mrs. Radcliffe—"The Italian."

Caroline joined her silently. Soon, Rose asked, "Are you ready?" before turning the page. When Caroline nodded, Rose inquired, "Do you like it?"

"Long ago, as a child, I was wonderfully captivated by it."

"Why?"

"It seemed to open with such promise, such foreboding of a strange tale to unfold."

"And while reading, you feel as though you are far from England, truly in Italy, under a different sky—that blue southern sky travelers describe."

"You perceive that too, Rose?"

"It makes me yearn to travel, Miss Helstone."

"When you become a woman, perhaps you can fulfill that wish."

"I intend to make a way for myself if none is made for me. I cannot live forever in Briarfield. The world is not very large compared to Creation. I must see the outside of our own round planet, at least."

"How much of its exterior?"

"First this hemisphere, then the other. I am resolved that my life shall be a life. Not a black trance like a toad buried in marble, nor a slow death like yours in the Briarfield rectory."

"Like yours? What do you mean, child?"

"Might you not as well be tediously dying as forever shut up in that glebe-house—a place that, whenever I pass it, reminds me of a windowed grave? I see no movement near the door, no sounds from the walls. I believe no smoke ever issues from the chimneys. What do you do there?"
