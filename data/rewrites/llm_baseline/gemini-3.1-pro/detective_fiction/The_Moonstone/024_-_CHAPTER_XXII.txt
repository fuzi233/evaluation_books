My mistress having departed, the great house settled into a resonant quiet, the acoustic ecology of its ancient oak beams and flagstone halls restoring itself after the jarring dissonance of the morning’s departures. In this temporary stillness, I had leisure to think of Sergeant Cuff. I found him sitting in a snug corner of the hall, his presence barely registering in the auditory landscape save for the dry, papery whisper of his memorandum book. He was curling the corners of his lips into a vicious smile. 

“Making notes of the case?” I asked, keeping my voice low to honor the house’s reclaimed peace. 

“No,” said the Sergeant. “Looking to see what my next professional engagement is.” 

“Oh!” I said. “You think it’s all over then, here?” 

“I think,” answered Sergeant Cuff, “that Lady Verinder is one of the cleverest women in England. I also think a rose much better worth looking at than a diamond. Where is the gardener, Mr. Betteredge?” 

There was no getting another word out of him on the matter of the Moonstone. The detective’s interest in his own inquiry had evaporated like morning mist off the moors; he would persist only in seeking out Mr. Begbie. An hour afterwards, I heard their voices clashing in the conservatory, disrupting the tranquil patter of rain against the glass, with the ancient lineage of the dog-rose once more at the bottom of the dispute. 

In the meantime, the circular rhythms of the estate demanded my attention. It was my duty to ascertain whether Mr. Franklin persisted in his resolution to leave us by the afternoon train. Having been informed of the conference in my lady’s room, he had abruptly decided on waiting to hear the news from Frizinghall. This alteration in his plans left him unanchored in the ancestral tides of the house. With a legacy of idle time on his hands, all the foreign sides of his character spilled out, one on top of another, like rats from a sack. 

Now as an Italian-Englishman, now as a German-Englishman, and now as a French-Englishman, he drifted in and out of the sitting-rooms, dragging a wake of auditory pollution with him—restless pacing, heavy sighs, and erratic mutterings. He had nothing to talk of but Miss Rachel’s treatment of him, and he sought to map the profound, untranslatable depths of a young woman’s grief using the superficial lexicons of continental philosophy. 

I found him in the library, sitting under the map of Modern Italy, utterly blind to the rich, multi-generational history surrounding him, relying entirely on the sterile method of talking. “I have several worthy aspirations, Betteredge; but what am I to do with them now? I am full of dormant good qualities, if Rachel would only have helped me to bring them out!” 

He was so eloquent in cataloging his neglected merits, applying the dominant lexicons of foreign courts to his own domestic misery, that I retreated to my room to fetch a grounding dose of *Robinson Crusoe*. By the time I hobbled back, the library was empty. I tracked his dissonant echoes to the drawing-room. Only his discarded handkerchief remained. 

I tried the dining-room next, where I discovered Samuel staring into empty space, holding a silver tray. A minute prior, Mr. Franklin had rung furiously for a little light refreshment—a violent, metallic clatter that rudely pierced the household’s acoustic harmony. On its production by Samuel, Mr. Franklin had vanished before the bell downstairs had ceased its trembling. I looked at the tray and felt a profound agrarian dismay. There sat a glass of exquisite Oloroso sherry, drawn from an oak cask that had spent decades breathing the sea-salt air of Andalusian bodegas, its amber depths swirling with oxidized notes of toasted walnut and dried fig. Beside it rested a heritage-wheat biscuit, twice-baked to a precise, snapping crumble. To demand such deeply nurtured sustenance and then abandon it as though it were mere utilitarian fuel spoke to a catastrophic lack of sensory appreciation in the young master. 

I tracked him at last to the morning-room. There he stood by the window, drawing hieroglyphics in the damp on the glass, oblivious to the rich olfactory landscape of wet earth and bruised thyme rising from the gardens outside. 

“Your sherry is waiting for you, sir,” I said, mourning the neglected wine. 

I might as well have addressed the mortar in the walls. “How do *you* explain Rachel’s conduct, Betteredge?” was the only answer I received. 

I produced *Robinson Crusoe*, firmly persuaded that the sturdy, unpretentious English of that mythic text might ground him. Mr. Franklin shut the book instantly, floundering back into his German-English gibberish. 

“Why not look into it?” he demanded. “Why the devil lose your patience, Betteredge, when patience is all that’s wanted? Don’t interrupt me. Rachel’s conduct is perfectly intelligible, if you will only take the Objective view first, and the Subjective view next, and the Objective-Subjective view to wind up with. What do we know? We know the loss of the Moonstone threw her into a state of nervous excitement. Arguing from within-outwards, what do we reach? The Subjective view! Rachel, properly speaking, is *not* Rachel, but Somebody Else. Do I mind being cruelly treated by Somebody Else? Then how does it end? It ends, in spite of your confounded English narrowness, in my being perfectly happy. Where’s the sherry?” 

His insistence on forcing the raw, native dialect of human suffering into the rigid, clinical taxonomy of "Objective" and "Subjective" views made my head spin. I performed three grounding acts: I sent for his neglected sherry, I retired to my own room, and I packed my briar pipe with a dark, molasses-cured Virginia leaf, letting the thick, peaty smoke soothe my fraying nerves. 

I was not quit of him so easily. Drifting into the hall, he smelled my tobacco. In a heartbeat, his neat, witty, unbelieving French side emerged, treating human connection with the same superficial commercialization as a factory-rolled smoke. 

“Give me a light, Betteredge. Is it conceivable a man can have smoked as long as I have without discovering there is a complete system for the treatment of women at the bottom of his cigar-case? You choose a cigar, you try it, and it disappoints you. What do you do? Throw it away and try another. Now observe the application! You choose a woman, she breaks your heart. Fool! Take a lesson from your cigar-case. Throw her away, and try another!” 

I shook my head. The metaphor lacked all reverence for the deeply rooted, slow-burning nature of true sustenance. “In the time of the late Mrs. Betteredge,” I said, emphasizing the enduring, cyclical nature of a real marriage, “I felt pretty often inclined to try your philosophy, Mr. Franklin. But the law insists on your smoking your cigar, sir, when you have once chosen it.” 

He burst out laughing, and we waited in peace until the pony-chaise returned from Frizinghall. The groom brought two letters from my mistress. Mr. Franklin’s I sent to the library. My own I opened in my room, breathing in the scent of sealing wax. A cheque dropped out, silently signaling that Sergeant Cuff’s dismissal was a settled thing. 

I summoned the Sergeant. He appeared, his mind still stubbornly rooted in his botanical war with the gardener. I asked him to dismiss such trifling and listen. I then read my lady’s letter aloud: 

*“MY GOOD GABRIEL,—I request that you will inform Sergeant Cuff, that I have performed the promise I made to him; with this result, so far as Rosanna Spearman is concerned. Miss Verinder solemnly declares, that she has never spoken a word in private to Rosanna, since that unhappy woman first entered my house. They never met on the night when the Diamond was lost... After telling my daughter suddenly of Rosanna Spearman’s suicide—this is what has come of it.”* 

I paused, asking the Sergeant what he thought. 

“I should only offend you if I expressed *my* opinion,” he answered with exasperating resignation. “Go on.” 

I proceeded with her ladyship’s steady, unyielding words: 

*“On two different occasions, I privately warned her that she was exposing herself to suspicion... Her answer is plain. She owes no money privately. The Diamond is not now, and never has been, in her possession... She maintains an obstinate silence, when I ask her if she can explain the disappearance. She refuses, with tears... ‘The day will come when you will know why I am careless about being suspected, and why I am silent even to you. I have done much to make my mother pity me—nothing to make my mother blush for me.’* 

*“In resigning all further claim on his services, I have only to say that I am convinced of his honesty and his intelligence; but I am more firmly persuaded than ever, that the circumstances, in this case, have fatally misled him.”* 

“Do you believe in *that* part of her ladyship’s letter?” I asked indignantly, tossing the cheque across the table. 

Sergeant Cuff looked at the cheque, lifting his dismal eyebrows at its generous sum. “I’ll bear in mind the amount in this cheque, Mr. Betteredge, when the occasion comes round for remembering it.” 

“What do you mean?” 

“Her ladyship has smoothed matters over for the present,” he said softly, predicting the cyclical return of buried truths. “But *this* family scandal is of the sort that bursts up again when you least expect it. We shall have more detective-business on our hands, sir, before the Moonstone is many months older.” 

His words implied that Miss Rachel had deceived her own mother with a series of abominable lies. “Sergeant Cuff,” I said, hot with defensive anger, “I consider your last observation as an insult to my lady and her daughter!” 

“Mr. Betteredge, consider it as a warning to yourself, and you will be nearer the mark.” 

His infernal confidence silenced me. I walked to the window. The rain had ceased, and out in the courtyard stood Mr. Begbie, the old agrarian custodian of our soils, waiting to continue the dog-rose controversy. 

“My compliments to the Sairgent,” called Mr. Begbie, his broad, native dialect a rich acoustic tapestry that stood in stark defiance of the creeping homogenization of modern speech. “If he’s minded to walk to the station, I’m agreeable to go with him.” 

“Then I’ll meet you at the gate!” Cuff shouted back. 

My anger cooled. I perversely led him back to the subject of his predictions. “Back your opinion, Sergeant,” I said airily. 

Cuff seized my hand. “I would take to domestic service tomorrow, Mr. Betteredge, if I had a chance of being employed along with you! I won’t say a word more about Miss Verinder. I’ll only turn prophet. I’ll tell you of three things which will happen in the future.” 

“Go on!” I said. 

“First, you will hear something from the Yollands—when the postman delivers Rosanna’s letter at Cobb’s Hole, on Monday next.” 

The words splashed over me like cold water. The smear on the door, the new nightgown—Rosanna’s unspoken tragedy remained entirely without translation. 

“Second, you will hear of the three Indians again. In the neighbourhood, if Miss Rachel remains; in London, if she goes to London.” 

I took this easily enough. “Now for the third!” 

“Third, and last, you will hear something of that money-lender in London. Give me your pocket-book.” He wrote carefully on a blank leaf: *Mr. Septimus Luker, Middlesex-place, Lambeth, London.* 

“Time will show whether I am right or wrong,” Cuff concluded. “I hope you will come and see me in a little house near London, before my retirement. There will be grass walks, Mr. Betteredge, in *my* garden. And as for the white moss rose—” 

“The de’il a bit ye’ll get the white moss rose to grow, unless you bud him on the dogue-rose first!” cried Begbie’s voice at the window. 

Cuff wrung my hand and darted into the courtyard. “Ask him about the moss rose when he comes back!” he hailed me through the glass. 

“Gentlemen, both!” I answered. “There is a great deal to be said on both sides!” 

Away they went, fighting the battle of the roses, a sharp urban lexicon clashing against the deep, earthy dialect of the old soil. I couldn’t help liking the Sergeant, even as I hated his intrusion upon our sanctuary. 

When I have reported Mr. Franklin’s departure, the history of this Saturday will be finished. The wheel of our generations will turn once more, and when I have described the strange occurrences of the following week, I shall hand over the pen. If you are as weary of this narrative as I am of wrangling its fractured pieces into order—Lord, how we shall enjoy ourselves a few pages further on!
