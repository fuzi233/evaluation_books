"No man," a Rabbi once declared, citing an indisputable law, "shall turn the bones of his father and mother into spoons." The statement hung in the air, a moral anchor designed to halt the drift of economic pragmatism. Yet, the market for spoons had never swollen sufficiently to provoke a counter-argument suggesting that human progress might indeed lie in such a repurposing of material. The only barrier was sentiment—a soft, intangible force that coerced only those who already believed feelings constituted the greater portion of worldly wealth.

Gwendolen Deronda abandoned the fashionable circuits of Rotten Row for a more rugged terrain. He wandered through the districts of London most densely populated by common Jews, drifting toward synagogues during services and peering into shopfronts. It was a reconnaissance mission yielding little promise of specific revelation. Why not seek counsel from an influential Rabbi regarding the odds of locating a mother named Cohen, a son named Ezra, and a lost daughter named Mirah? He considered this approach, pushing it aside until after Christmas. The truth was, despite his capacity for finding poetry in the mundane, Deronda, whenever a keen personal interest ignited within him, could not escape the crushing weight of the Actual. Reality, unyielding and unselective, refused to accommodate his tastes. Enthusiasm thrives comfortably among abstract ideas; it tolerates the pungent breath of garlic in medieval markets and finds nobility in the ceremonial trappings of classic processions. But when ideals press against the flesh, demanding warm incarnation, enthusiasm grows squeamish, prone to fainting under the burden of the tangible.

Lying dreamily in a boat, imagining a quest for a beautiful maiden's kin in Cordova amidst the bustling Jews of Ibn-Gebirol's era, one bears the physical inconveniences without shock. Similarly, if the grim scenery of St. Mary Axe and Whitechapel were magically transposed to the Rhine's banks in the eleventh century, transforming the cries of Crusaders into the bay of bloodhounds and forcing the reviled Jew to stand erect and heroic against sword and firebrand, the dingy shops and unprepossessing faces would dissolve into the thrill of contemplative emotion. Yet, the fervor we muster for grandiose martyrdom pales beside the sustained enthusiasm required when there is no danger, no challenge—only the impartial midday sun falling upon commonplace, perhaps half-repulsive objects that are, in truth, beloved ideas made flesh. Here resides the chief poetic energy: the force of imagination piercing or exalting solid fact, rather than floating aimlessly among cloud-pictures. To glory in a prophetic vision of knowledge sweeping the globe is an easier exercise for the believing mind than witnessing its inception in newspaper placards staring from bridges beyond cornfields. For many delicate souls, it is entirely possible to be in the thick of Armageddon without perceiving anything more than the annoyance of a puff of explosive smoke and the struggle of bodies on the ground immediately surrounding them.

It was contrary to Deronda's nature to scorn the feeble, fastidious sympathy that recoils from the broad sweep of human life. But now, with Mirah standing before him as a living reality, whose fate he was compelled to safeguard, he viewed every common Jew and Jewess through the lens of comparison with her. He harbored a presentiment of the collision between her idealized notion of an unknown mother and brother and the harsh discovered fact—a premonition sharpened by a suppressed awareness that a similar possibility of collision might lurk hidden within his own destiny. He would not have looked with complacent expectation upon wealthy Jews, those who outdid the lords of the Philistines in their diversions; yet, since there was no likelihood of Mirah's friends residing among that class, their habits did not immediately engage his concern. In this mood, he rambled without anticipating a more fruitful result than a subtle preparation of his own mind, perhaps for future theorizing as well as practice—much as if Mirah were related to Welsh miners, he had gone to scrutinize their ways, hoping simultaneously to glean details on the history of strikes.

He did not truly yearn to find anyone in particular. When, as was his habit, he scanned the names above shop doors, he was content that none bore the inscription "Ezra Cohen." He confessed, however, that he particularly wished Ezra Cohen should not keep a shop. Wishes are often deemed ominous; according to this superstition, the order of the world is arranged such that if one holds an impious objection to a squint, one's offspring is more likely to be born with one; conversely, if one desires a squint, it will not be granted. This despondent view of probability is entirely rejected by the hopeful, who take their wishes as sufficient security for fulfillment. Who remains absolutely neutral? One morning, Deronda turned into a small side street escaping the noise and obstructions of Holborn, feeling the scales tip decisively toward the despondent side.

Rather weary of the streets, he paused to hail a hansom cab approaching when his attention was snagged by fine old clasps in chased silver displayed in the window to his right. His initial thought was that Lady Mallinger, possessing a strictly Protestant taste for such Catholic relics, might appreciate these missal-clasps transformed into a bracelet. Then his eyes swept the remainder of the window's contents, revealing that this was a pawnbroker's establishment where lead was exchanged for jewelry, lace, and all equivocal objects masquerading as *bric-à-brac*. A placard in the corner announced: *Watches and Jewelry exchanged and repaired*. But his scrutiny had been noticed from within. A figure emerged from the doorway, looking around with a tone of cordial encouragement, "Good day, sir." The instant was sufficient for Deronda to recognize the face—unmistakably Jewish—belonging to a young man of about thirty. Wincing from the shopkeeper's inevitable persuasiveness, he returned the greeting only to pass swiftly to the other side of the street, beckoning the cabman to draw up there. From that vantage point, he read the sign above the window: *Ezra Cohen*.

There might be a hundred Ezra Cohens lettered above shop windows, yet Deronda had not encountered them before. Presumably, the young man interested in a potential customer was Ezra himself; he was of the age expected for Mirah's brother, who had grown up while she remained a child. However, Deronda's first endeavor as he drove homeward was to convince himself that there was no warrantable presumption linking this Ezra to Mirah. Next, he reasoned that even if, against all logic, this man proved to be the brother, and inquiries revealed the mother to be deceased, it was not his duty to disclose the discovery to Mirah. An inconvenient disturbance arose in this conclusion: his recently acquired knowledge that Mirah possessed a religious desire to know of her mother's death and to ascertain whether her brother lived. How far was he justified in determining another life by his own notions? Was it not his secret grievance against the manner in which others had ordered his own existence that he lacked open daylight on all its relations, depriving him, unlike other men, of the full guidance of primary duties?

Immediate relief from this internal debate came from the reflection that he had made no real discovery yet, and that closer inspection of the facts would certify that no demand existed for any decision whatsoever. He intended to return to the shop as soon as convenient to purchase the clasps for Lady Mallinger. But he was hindered for several days by Sir Hugo, who, preparing to deliver an after-dinner speech on a burning topic, required Deronda to research the legal aspects of the question, alongside wasting daily hours in arguments that invariably ended in a drawn battle. On many issues, they held opposing views, yet Sir Hugo cared little for this. When Deronda articulated his point effectively, Sir Hugo replied with a mixture of satisfaction and regret, "Confound it, Dan! Why don't you make an opportunity of saying these things in public? You're wrong, you know. You won't succeed. You've got the massive sentiment—the heavy artillery of the country against you. But it's all the better ground for a young man to display himself on. When I was your age, I should have taken it. And it would be quite as well for you to be in opposition to me here and there. It would throw you more into relief. If you would seize an occasion of this sort to make an impression, you might be in Parliament in no time. And you know that would gratify me."

"I am sorry not to do what would gratify you, sir," Deronda said. "But I cannot persuade myself to look at politics as a profession."

"Why not? If a man is not born into public life by his position in the country, there is no way for him but to embrace it by his own efforts. The business of the country must be done—Her Majesty's Government carried on, as the old Duke said. And it never could be, my boy, if everybody looked at politics as if they were prophecy, and demanded an inspired vocation. If you are to get into Parliament, it won't do to sit still and wait for a call either from heaven or constituents."

"I don't want to make a living out of opinions," Deronda countered, "especially out of borrowed opinions. Not that I mean to blame other men. I dare say many better fellows than I don't mind getting on to a platform to praise themselves, and giving their word of honor for a party."
