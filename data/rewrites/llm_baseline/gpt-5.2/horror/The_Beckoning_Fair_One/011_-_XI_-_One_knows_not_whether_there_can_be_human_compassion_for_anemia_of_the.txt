No one knows what mercy can do for a hunger that is not in the flesh.

There are illnesses that make a body pale and thin and tremulous, and there are other illnesses—quieter, craftier—that drop the pitch of living itself, so that what was once ordinary becomes intolerable, and what was once sweet turns rancid on the tongue. When a man is altered in that way, the human bond thins to a thread. Friends turn their faces aside, not always from cruelty, but from a terrified thrift: they must keep their own minds from catching. They are not saints with hands full of cures. They do not know how to cast out what has nested. They only know how to bar their own doors.

So the letter slipped beneath the dark threshold and lay where it fell. The telegram never reached a hand. The messenger, whistling as he climbed the stairs—whistling because boys whistle, and because the living city teaches them to fill every quiet with cheap sound—went away again without understanding why, for one heart-stopping instant, a blind had been pinched aside by a single finger and then shoved back with a small, frantic violence.

It was better, the world would have said, that the miserable wrestle with his own shadowed bride in a house where no clean air entered and no honest sun found a seam. Better that he clasp his nightmare to his chest alone. Humanity had other errands, other dinners to cook, other children to tuck in. Let the lost remain lost.

And in that flat, Paul Oleron had already become a place people spoke of in lowered tones—if they spoke of him at all.

He had once been a man particular about sensations. Even in poverty he had taken small, stubborn pleasures: bread from the baker on the corner rather than the grey sliced stuff; coffee ground fresh because stale grounds tasted like old paper; a shaved curl of lemon peel over fish because the oil woke the tongue like a struck bell. Those preferences had been part of his vanity, perhaps, but they were also his tether to the day.

Now his outer landing was littered with parcels he had ordered and then feared to touch—as if the mere act of opening the door would let something rush in on the draught. Packets of provisions. Bundles of flowers. More flowers. Flowers he had wanted, insisted on, demanded, as if their color could feed him. The errand-boys had come, knocked, waited. In time their shoulders had begun to lift in that shrug that ends commerce: there must be some mistake about the order. After a week they stopped.

Inside, in the light that was never properly daylight—red-tinged as though the walls remembered a sunset that had died there long ago—the flowers turned brown. Heads slumped, petals grew papery, stems sweated and then stiffened. The air thickened with the sour-sweet of rot: the smell of a vase forgotten at the back of a room, multiplied until it became a weather, a climate. It was not only flowers. It was vegetable decay in general: green turned to slime, then to dark pulp; water in jars went cloudy; the faint, medicinal tang of wilting leaves rose and rose until it seemed to coat his palate and make him swallow against it.

Oleron lay in bed and let it happen.

His strength had been draining for days, perhaps weeks; time no longer behaved in him like time. Something fastened itself upon his power and sucked steadily, as if it had a mouth set to the very root of will. After the draining came long spells of prostration in which he could do nothing but gaze at the ceiling stained by that reddish twilight, letting whatever fancies drift up have their way with him. Even his strongest memories clung only precariously, like scraps of paper to a wet wall. They slid and fluttered away before he could pin them.

Sometimes a half-memory of a novel he must write teased him—a novel important to write, a promise made, a deadline—and then vanished as if blown out. Sometimes entire novels arrived, perfect and splendid, as if someone else had written them inside his skull while he slept: books established to endure, with sentences that shone like cut glass. And sometimes the memories were absurdly remote and trivial: garrets he had inhabited; landladies whose breath smelled of onions; the shape of a kettle he had once owned; the clatter of carts in a street where he had written his first pages.

He had known a good deal about such things in his time—streets, meals, the way a city’s sound changes from market-morning to nightfall. But all that was past. He had found, at last, a place he did not intend to leave until they fetched him out.

Some might have thought the rooms a little too green-sick: that damp, plantish smell that clings to old plaster and shaded corners. Others might have thought them too redolent of long-dead and morbid things for a living man to be mewed up in. Yet to Oleron it had become irresistible. It had an authority of its own, as if the building were a host and he the guest expected. It had an associate—no, more than an associate; a presence with a claim—and there were delights in it once a man stopped struggling against its inexorable will.

A novel? Somebody ought to write a novel about a place like this. There must be so much to write if one could only get to the bottom of it—if one could only get beneath its everyday crust of kettles, keys, deliveries, and blinds, and into the real pantry where it kept its secrets.

Had it been painted already, perhaps, by a man called Madley who had lived there… yes, Madley. Oleron had not known him; had a strong feeling he would not have liked him; would rather Madley had lived somewhere else; really could not stand the fellow. Hated him, Madley, in fact.

(Aha. That was a joke.)

He seriously doubted whether the man had lived as he ought. Sometimes Oleron imagined himself telling that long-nosed guardian of public morals across the way about him—though the fellow probably knew, and had already made his praying hullabaloos for Madley as well. That was his line. Why, Oleron himself had had a dust-up with him—about something… some girl… Elsie Bengough, her name was.

At that name a pulse of uneasiness went through him, like a sudden wrong note heard under music. Or rather, he was not uneasy about Elsie herself so much as restless about the things she did—chief among them the way she persisted in thrusting herself into his thoughts. Whenever he was quick enough, he sent her packing at once.

She had always been a bore. He had never denied that. But now it had come to the pitch where her very presence in his fancy was inimical to the full enjoyment of certain experiences.

She had no tact. She really ought to have understood that people are not at home to the thoughts of everybody all the time. In mere politeness she should have allowed him seasons wholly to himself. She was monstrously ignorant if she did not know—she appeared not to know—that there are certain special hours when a man’s veins run with fire and daring and power, when… well, when he has a reasonable right to treat folk as he had treated that prying Barrett: to shut them out completely.

But no. Up she popped—the thought of her—and ruined all.

The bright towering fabrics that sometimes rose before him—by whose side even the perfect magical novels were dun and grey—vanished at her intrusion. It was as if fog swallowed a fair-beaming star; as if, at the threshold of some golden portal prepared for him, a pit yawned suddenly; as if a bat-like shadow turned the growing dawn back into mirk.

Therefore he strove to smother even the nascent thought of her.

Nevertheless there came an occasion when this woman Bengough refused to be suppressed.

Oleron could not have told exactly when it happened. He only knew by the faint glimmer of the street lamp through his blind that it was some time during the night, and that for a long while she had not presented herself. He had no warning. None. She just came—was there.

Strive as he would, he could not shake off the thought of her nor the image of her face. She haunted him.

But for her to come at that moment of all moments…

It was past belief. How she could endure it, he could not conceive—actually to look on, as it were, at the triumph of a Rival.

Good God. It was monstrous.

Tact—reticence—he had never credited her with an overwhelming amount of either; but he had never attributed mere—there was no word for it. Monstrous. Monstrous. Did she intend thenceforward—Good God—to look on?

Anger rushed up to the roots of his hair, hot and sudden as alcohol flaring. “Damnation take her!” he choked.

And then, in the very next moment, heat and resentment turned to a cold sweat of cowering fear.

For something else had lit.

He did not know what he had done, but he knew he had done something—something fatal, irreparable, blasting. Anger he had felt, yes, but not this blaze that flooded the twilight of his consciousness with a white infernal light. That appalling flash was not his—not his that open rift of bright and searing Hell.

His had been the hand of a child, preparing a puny blow. But what was this other horrific hand drawn back to strike in the same place? Had he set it in motion? Had he provided the spark that touched off the whole accumulated power of that formidable and relentless house?

He did not know. He only knew that his poor igniting particle was blown out at once, snuffed, and that—oh, impossible—a clinging kiss (how else could he express it?) changed on his very lips to a gnashing and a removal. And for sheer pity at the awful odds he must cry out to the one he had been raging against to guard herself—guard herself—

“Look out!” he shrieked aloud.

The revulsion was instant.

As if a cold slow billow broke over him, he came to with a jolt of miserable clarity. He was lying in his bed. The mist and horror that had enwrapped him for so long had departed like smoke pulled away by a draught. He was Paul Oleron.

And he was sick, naked, helpless—unutterably abandoned and alone.

His faculties, weak as they were, answered at last to his calls. It must have been a hideous nightmare. Yes. He was himself: Paul Oleron, tired novelist, already past the summit of his best work and slipping downhill again empty-handed.

It came to him not in a procession of thoughts but in a single word, enwrapped and complete: failure. He had tried too much. He had over-estimated his strength. He had struck short.

He had missed. And he had missed not one happiness but two: the ease of this world, which men love, and also that other shining prize for which men forgo ease—the snatching and holding and triumphant bearing up aloft that alone justifies the mad adventurer who hazards the enterprise.

And there was no second attempt. Fate has no morrow.

His morrow must be to sit down to profitless, ill-done, unrequired work again, and so on the morrow after that, and after that, for as many morrows as there might be. He lay there, weakly yet sanely considering it.

Since the whole attempt had failed, it was hardly worth considering whether anything could be saved from the wreck. No good would come of that half-finished novel. It had been meant for the autumn. He was under contract. No matter. Better to pay forfeit than to waste what days were left. He was spent. Age was not far off. Wisdom and sadness were the proper paths for what remained.

If only he had chosen the wife, the child, the faithful friend at the fireside, and let others chase their will-o’-the-wisps.

In the meantime, a practical puzzle began to worry him with a steady insistence: why was he so weak? Why did his room smell so overpoweringly of decaying vegetable matter? And why did his hand, straying to his face in the dark, encounter a beard?

“Most extraordinary,” he muttered. “Have I been ill? Am I ill now? And if so, why have they left me alone?… Extraordinary…”

He thought he heard a sound from the kitchen—or the bathroom. He lifted himself a little on his pillow and listened.

Yes. He was not alone then.

It certainly would have been extraordinary to leave him ill and alone. Alone? Oh no. Someone would look after him. He would not be left to shift for himself. If everyone else had forsaken him, he could trust Elsie Bengough—dearest chum—to do that much for him. Bless her faithful heart.

Then a short, stifled, spluttering cry rang sharply out:

“Paul!”

It came from the kitchen.

And in the same moment it flashed upon him—he did not know how—that minutes before another sound, unmarked at the time but now transfixing, had tried to reach his intelligence: the slight touch of metal on metal, exactly like the sound he made when he put his key into the lock.

“Hallo!… Who’s that?” he called sharply from the bed.

No answer.

He called again. “Hallo!… Who’s there?… Who is it?”

This time he was sure he heard noises in the kitchen—soft and heavy, as if someone shifted weight without wishing the floorboards to speak. But the house had its own voice, and it betrayed everything: a small click, a faint scuff, the hush of cloth brushing a chair.

“This is a queer thing altogether,” he muttered. “By Jove, I’m as weak as a kitten too.… Hallo, there! Somebody called, didn’t they?… Elsie! Is that you?”

Then he began to knock with his hand on the wall beside his bed.

“Elsie!… Elsie!… You called, didn’t you?… Please come here, whoever it is!”

A sound as of a closing door. Then silence—thick, listening silence, in which even the distant city seemed to hold its breath. The usual night-noises—pipes, carts, a far-off laugh—were dulled, as if wrapped in damp cloth. Only the street lamp’s faint electrical hum seeped through the blind, that thin modern vibration that never belonged to any natural night.

Oleron began to be alarmed.

“It may be a nurse,” he muttered. “Elsie’d have to get me a nurse, of course. She’d sit with me as long as she could spare the time, brave lass, and she’d get a nurse for the rest.… But it was awfully like her voice.… Elsie, or whoever it is! I can’t make this out at all. I must go and see what’s the matter…”

He put one leg out of bed. The weakness of it startled him; the joint felt as if it belonged to someone else. He reached for the wall to steady himself.

Before he put out the other leg, he stopped—his fingers worrying at the beard on his chin as if it were evidence in a case.

He wondered, suddenly, whether he dared go into the kitchen.

It was such a frightfully long way. In that corridor, in that strip of domestic space that ought to have been no more than lino and skirting-board and the familiar smell of soap, no man knew what might leap and huddle onto his shoulders if he went so far. When a man has an overmastering impulse to get back into bed, he ought to heed it. To ignore it was to invite the thing he feared most: not death, but contact.

Besides, why should he go? What was there to go for? If it was that Bengough creature again, let her look after herself. He was not going to have things cramp themselves on his defenceless back for the sake of such a spoilsport as she. If she was in, let her let herself out again, and the sooner the better for her.

He could not be bothered. He had work to do.

On the morrow he must set about writing a novel with a heroine so winsome, capricious, adorable, jealous, wicked, beautiful, inflaming—and altogether evil—that men should stand amazed.

She was coming over him now. He knew by the alteration of the very air when she was near: the smell of rot thinned, replaced by something faintly sweet and ruinously intimate, like warmed milk left too long in a closed room, like flowers bruised between fingers. The room seemed to listen. The blind hung as if it had weight. And in him rose that soft thrill of bliss that never came unless she was beckoning—beckoning.

He let go the wall and fell back into bed again as—oh, unthinkable—the other half of that kiss that the gnash had interrupted was placed, how else could one convey it, upon his lips, robbing him of very breath.
