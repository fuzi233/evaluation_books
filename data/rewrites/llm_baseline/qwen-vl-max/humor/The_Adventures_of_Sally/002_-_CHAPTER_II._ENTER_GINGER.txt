1

Sally was sprawled against a golden sand dune, half-asleep and half-awake, watching the morning ballet of Roville-sur-Mer unfold like a poorly choreographed soap opera. At seaside resorts—especially French ones—the beach becomes a full-time job for tourists at dawn. Fathers with facial hair that looked like they’d been grown in protest against modernity dotted the foreground. Women clustered under parasols that might as well have been painted by Matisse. Dogs wandered like lost souls. Kids dug holes with the solemnity of archaeologists, occasionally pausing to whack each other with spades because, clearly, the human race had not yet invented fun.

One poodle—military-grade, stiff-backed, and clearly judging Sally’s life choices—approached her, sniffed her box of sweets, and decided: *This is where I’m staying.* It sat down with the dignity of a general accepting surrender.

Sally’s vacation had been perfect—until it wasn’t. She’d floated through France like a lazy thought, from one sun-drenched town to another, until Roville captured her heart with its glittering hotels, blue sky, and Casino that probably played “La Vie en Rose” on loop. She could’ve stayed forever. But America called—specifically, Gerald, who’d written a letter so rushed it looked like it was composed during a fire drill. “*The Primrose Way* is opening in Detroit,” he’d scribbled. “Come back if you want to see it.” No love notes. No emojis. Just existential dread disguised as theater news.

She was leaving tomorrow. But today? Today was still hers. She basked in the sun, listened to waves whisper secrets she didn’t care to know, and enjoyed the rare luxury of doing absolutely nothing.

Except… the sun was warm. The waves were soothing. And before she knew it, her eyes had closed.

Then—*crash!*—a voice broke the spell.

Not just any voice. English.

In Roville? That was like hearing a jazz band play a dirge. Sally snapped awake. Two men had settled into the sand nearby. One of them—dark, sharp-faced, with a mouth that looked like it had been filed down by a lawyer—was lighting a cigarette with the precision of a man who’d once tried to burn his own hand off and failed.

Sally, ever the amateur psychologist, gave him a quick diagnosis: *Hard. Cold. Probably owns a briefcase with a lock.*

She turned to the second man—and immediately regretted it.

He was staring at her. Not awkwardly. Not politely. Like he’d just discovered the Holy Grail and it was wearing a sundress.

“Rather a dear,” Sally muttered, already feeling slightly embarrassed.

He was red-haired—bright, fiery, like a traffic light gone rogue. Thick-set, freckled, square-chinned, with a tiny cut near his jawline that screamed “I’ve thrown things at walls.”

“Temper,” Sally concluded. “But nice. Probably says ‘bloody hell’ when he stubs his toe.”

She looked away. His gaze was too intense. Like someone trying to read your soul while you’re trying to pretend you don’t have one.

The dark man finally succeeded in lighting his cigarette (after three attempts and one minor wind-related crisis) and resumed their conversation.

“And how is Scrymgeour?” he asked.

“Oh, all right,” said the redhead, distracted. He hadn’t taken his eyes off Sally.

“I was surprised he was here. He said he’d stay in Paris.”

A pause. Sally tossed a nougat to the poodle. It ate it like it was a medal.

Then the redhead leaned forward, voice vibrating with genuine awe.

“I say,” he announced, loud enough for the entire beach to hear, “that’s the prettiest girl I’ve seen in my life!”

2

Sally blinked. Then smiled.

She wasn’t offended. In fact, she found it kind of refreshing. A guy saying something honest in public? Bold move. The dark man, however, looked like he’d just been told the universe was made of cheese.

“My dear fellow!” he gasped.

“Oh, it’s all right,” said the redhead, unfazed. “She can’t understand. Nobody here speaks English. If I hadn’t remembered a few scraps of French, I’d be eating seaweed by now. That girl,” he continued, reverting to his main obsession, “is an absolute topper! I swear I’ve never seen anyone like her. Look at those hands and feet. You don’t get that outside France. Of course, her mouth is a bit wide,” he added reluctantly.

Sally remained motionless. The dark man, relieved, exhaled slowly. He prided himself on propriety. Being near someone who *didn’t* behave properly felt like being caught in a moral earthquake.

“Still,” he said sternly, “you ought to be careful.”

He glanced at Sally, who was now entertaining a scruffy mongrel and a terrier with a black patch over one eye. Then he returned to Scrymgeour.

“How is Scrymgeour’s dyspepsia?”

The redhead seemed barely interested.

“Do you notice how her hair curls over her ears?” he asked. “Eh? Oh, pretty much the same, I think.”

“What hotel are you staying at?”

“The Normandie.”

Sally froze. *The Normandie.* She was staying there too. She’d seen no sign of this man at the hotel. So he must be new.

“The Normandie?” the dark man frowned. “I’ve never heard of it. Where is it?”

“It’s a little shanty near the station. Not much. Cheap. Cooking’s decent.”

The dark man stared. “What on earth is Scrymgeour doing there?”

Sally, now fully invested in the mystery of Scrymgeour, felt her curiosity spike. This man was becoming a legend through absence alone.

“If there’s one thing he’s fussy about…” the dark man began.

“There are at least eleven thousand things he’s fussy about,” the redhead interrupted. “Jumpy old blighter!”

“He’s particular about hotels. Always has been. I expected him to go to the Splendide. Why would he choose the Normandie?”

The redhead shrugged. “Ask him if he’ll dine with you tonight. Say eight sharp.”

Then came the moment.

“No,” said the redhead. “Scrymgeour isn’t in Roville.”

“No?” The dark man’s voice sharpened. “Where is he?”

“Paris, I believe.”

“What!”

“Why aren’t you there?”

“Because he gave me a holiday.”

“When do you rejoin him?”

“I don’t.”

“What!”

The redhead looked unbothered. “Well, if you want to know,” he said, “the old blighter fired me the day before yesterday.”

3

The dark man stood up so fast his straw hat flew off and was promptly stepped on by a child in a yellow swimsuit.

Sally watched, transfixed. The man looked like a melodrama villain preparing to disown his wayward son. The redhead, meanwhile, gazed calmly down the beach at a fat man in an orange swimsuit who had just waded into the sea like a sinking battleship.

“Do you mean to tell me,” the dark man thundered, “after all the effort the family went to, after giving you a sinecure with endless possibilities, you threw it away?”

He gestured wildly. “Good God, you’re hopeless!”

The redhead said nothing. He watched the orange-suited man float like a deflated balloon.

The dark man ranted. “It’s maddening! What are you going to do? Are we supposed to keep getting you jobs you can’t keep? It’s monstrous! Sickening! Good God!”

And with that, he stormed off—only to be humbled moments later when his hat blew away and a toddler trod on it like a disco ball.

Silence fell. Then—*SNARL!*

A dog fight erupted.

It started with the terrier with the black patch. Sally blamed herself. She’d given the poodle three consecutive treats, ignoring the terrier’s growing indignation. Third time was the charm. The terrier lunged.

Dogs from across the beach surged forward. Barking. Snarling. A full-scale canine war. Some dogs fought. Others just barked at the chaos. One Sealyham appeared to be attempting a surgical procedure on a stranger.

Sally was frozen. So were the spectators. No one moved. Until—

The redhead stepped in.

He didn’t look like a hero. He looked like a guy who’d just been fired and was trying to impress a girl. But he had a gift. Dog fights, it turns out, require a specific skill set: calm, authority, and a knack for making dogs stop fighting without getting bitten.

He walked into the middle of the melee like he owned the place. Calm descended. Dogs stopped. One by one, they were separated, calmed, and led away by furious owners. Within minutes, only one small Scotch terrier remained, licking a chewed leg like it was deep in thought.

The redhead turned to Sally.

He braced himself, swallowed hard, and attempted French.

“J'espère,” he said, “que vous n'êtes pas—oh, dammit, what’s the word—J'espère que vous n'êtes pas blessée?”

“Blessée?”

“Yes. Wounded. Hurt. Bitten. Oh, dash it. J'espère…”

“Oh, bitten!” Sally giggled. “No, thanks. I wasn’t bitten. And thank you for saving us all.”

He stared at her, horrified. His face flushed crimson. His jaw dropped.

“Oh, my sainted aunt!” he cried.

Then, as if the world had become unbearable, he spun around and fled—so fast it was practically a sprint.

Sally watched him go. She still wanted to know why Scrymgeour had fired him.

4

Roville’s bedtime hours varied like a pendulum. The fancy hotels stayed up till dawn, drowning the night in jazz. The Normandie, however, operated on a strict “early to bed, early to rise” policy. Which meant Jules, the night clerk and elevator operator, was technically on duty—but mostly asleep.

Sally arrived just before midnight, fresh from the Casino and a moonlit stroll. The lobby was dim. Jules slumbered in a chair, head tilted, snoring softly. Sally felt guilty. She loved Jules—he worked nonstop, always tired, always polite. She didn’t want to wake him.

Then footsteps echoed outside. The front door opened and slammed shut.

Jules stirred. Sat up. Sally turned—and saw the redhead.

“Oh, good evening,” she said cheerfully.

He froze. His feet tangled. His face turned the color of a ripe tomato.

“Er—good evening,” he mumbled.

“Or *bon soir*, I suppose you’d say,” Sally teased.

He dropped his hat. Tripped over it. Stared at the floor like it had personally insulted him.

Jules, still half-asleep, opened the lift cage with a clang and began pulling the rope. The elevator ascended. He didn’t wake up. His brain ran on autopilot.

They sat side by side. Silence. Then—*snore*.

At the third floor, Sally prodded Jules in the ribs. She’d learned in Roville: actions spoke louder than words. Pointing worked. Prodding worked. French? Not so much.

Jules stopped the lift. Then froze. He stared at the door lock. He couldn’t open it.

“Hitch,” Sally said. “Would you mind asking what’s wrong? I only know ‘oo la la!’”

The redhead took a breath. “Oh, esker… esker vous…”

“Don’t weaken,” Sally urged. “You’ve got him.”

“Esker vous… Pourquoi vous ne… I mean ne vous… quel est le raison…”

Then Jules exploded into rapid-fire French. Words poured out like a waterfall. Neither Sally nor the redhead understood a word.

“Stop him!” Sally ordered.

The redhead looked terrified. “Stop him?”

“Blow a whistle or something!”

From the depths of his memory, a single word surfaced: *Zut!*

He barked it.

Jules stopped. Instantly. Like a machine unplugged.

“Quick!” Sally said. “Now ask him what’s wrong!”

The redhead nodded. “Lentement,” he said. “Parlez lentement. Pas si—uh—dashed vite!”

“Ah-a-ah!” Jules said, catching on. “Lentement. Ah, oui, lentement.”

They talked. Sally didn’t understand. The redhead did.

Minutes later, he turned to her.

“The silly ass,” he said, “has made a bloomer. He was half-asleep when we got in. He shoved us in, slammed the door, and forgot the keys were on the desk.”

“I see,” Sally said. “So we’re locked in?”

“I’m afraid so,” he said. “If only I knew French better. I’d curse him with some vim. I wonder what ‘blighter’ is in French.”
