Tito's intricate designs had been hopelessly undone by trifling accidents that no mastermind could foresee. Though it was rare for him to stroll with Romola at dusk, fate had conspired to place her beside him precisely tonight, rendering her presence a supreme nuisance. Life proved to be a labyrinthine game where the finest strategies were routinely thwarted by whimsical chance, as unpredictable as falling dandelion seeds.

His distress stemmed not from the collapse of Spini's conspiracy, but from the treacherous tightrope he now faced regarding his warnings. To alert Savonarola immediately would tether his caution so closely to his recent delivery of forged letters that suspicion would inevitably arise. Yet, to confront Dolfo Spini instantly would require confessing the truth—that Savonarola had altered his intentions. Tito knew Spini's sharp intellect would perceive nothing but a reversal, interpreting Tito's actions as a mere "turnabout" designed to sabotage the plot. Conversely, delaying his warning to Savonarola until morning would likely forfeit the chance to stop Spini, leaving the Compagnacci band to return enraged and disappointed. Calculating the risks, Tito resolved to accept the anger of the conspirators, banking instead on his ability to smooth over Spini by attributing the failure solely to the Friar's prudent caution.

Annoyed and irritable, Tito found himself incapable of smiling, a feat requiring unnatural effort. Determined to avoid another encounter with Romola, he bypassed his home and ventured forth alone.

Inside, Romola remained awake throughout the night, refusing to remove her garments. As the rain intensified, drumming a relentless rhythm against the roof, she found solace in the tempest. The stormy heavens appeared as a divine shield against human machinations, forcing even the most ambitious plots into stasis. Yet, her peace offered little respite to her troubled mind. Doubts assailed her on every front: what deceptions might Tito harbor, what secret agendas remained hidden from her gaze? If trust in Tito endangered others, how futile it was to convince herself otherwise. A nagging guilt plagued her conscience; was she not selfishly clinging to her pride when she hesitated to warn men against her husband? *If* he were indeed a criminal, perhaps her duty lay in standing by him in prison—but could a wife stand idly by while her spouse inflicted harm upon the innocent, when prevention within her grasp? Prayer felt impossible; her mind, agitated by frantic speculation, refused the quietude required for spiritual surrender.

As dawn approached, the fury of the rain subsided, replaced by a gentle breeze that scattered the clouds, revealing a stark, clear morning. Paradoxically, this tranquility rendered her inner turmoil more unbearable. Wrapping her mantle tightly around herself, she rushed to the loggia, gazing desperately at the vast landscape, searching for some sign that might dictate her next move, though she saw nothing but rooftops concealing the streets where Savonarola might walk toward ruin.

Could she summon her godfather to intervene, perhaps prompting him to block the gates without needing specific revelations? No, the delay would likely be fatal. With fresh anguish, Romola realized she had gleaned no concrete details from Tito, and it was already past seven o'clock. There was no alternative but San Marco.

Hurrying down the stairs, she ignored the ill in her care and burst onto the street, racing swiftly along Via de' Bardi toward the Ponte Vecchio. She intended to cut directly through the city's heart; the great Piazza offered a slim possibility of intercepting her husband. By some miracle, he might still confirm the Friar's safety, obviating the need for her dangerous journey to San Marco. Upon reaching the courtyard of the Palazzo Vecchio, she peered eagerly into the pillared space, scanning the square, but the familiar figure etched in her youth and scarred by her current suffering was absent. She pressed onward to the Piazza del Duomo.

Already alive with motion, the square bustled with worshippers ascending and descending the marble steps, groups chatting, and vendors bearing their wares. Amidst this throng, Romola spotted him. On his way from San Marco, Tito had diverted into Nello's shop and now leaned casually against the doorpost. As she drew nearer, she observed him standing with an effortless grace, holding his cap, shaking back his freshly combed hair. The jarring contrast between this languid ease and the bitter anxieties he had sowed convulsed her with indignation; the new vision of his unfeeling hardness deepened her terror. She recognized Cronaca and two other habitués of San Marco standing nearby. A sudden resolve flared within her: *"I shall force him to speak before these men."* Her light step carried her forward before he could retreat, just as Cronaca remarked, "Here comes Madonna Romola."

A faint shock rippled through Tito's frame as he realized he stood face-to-face with his wife. She looked haggard from sleepless vigilance, yet a flash of something other than worry ignited in her eyes as she spoke.

"Is the Frate gone beyond the gates?"

"No," Tito replied, feeling utterly powerless before this woman. He summoned every ounce of self-command necessary to maintain a mask of pure surprise.

"And you are certain that he is not going?" she pressed, her voice trembling slightly.

"I am certain that he is not going."

"That is enough," Romola declared, turning up the steps to seek refuge within the Duomo, hoping the sanctuary of stone would calm her agitation.

Tito watched her retreat, a sensation bordering on hatred burning in his eyes as she disappeared behind the pillars.

Amidst the crowd stood not only devoted followers of the Friar but also Ser Ceccone, the notary. At that moment, much like Tito Melema, Ser Ceccone operated as a covert agent for the Medici. Ser Francesco di Ser Barone, infamous simply as Ser Ceccone, lacked learning, handsomeness, success, or generosity. He was a traitor devoid of charm, and consequently, he held no affection for Tito.
