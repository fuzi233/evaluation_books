Harry Warrington recounted the simple tale of his recent adventures to his newly discovered relative. As he spoke, Madame Bernstein, a woman possessing a sharp wit and a profound understanding of the world, quietly formed her judgment on the characters and events described. While her verdict was not entirely favorable, one must remember that humanity is flawed and life rarely offers constant pleasure or profit. The court-lady recoiled at the very idea of her American cousin's rustic existence; such a life seemed tedious to anyone bred in the city. Yet, little Madame Warrington knew nothing else and was content with her lot, just as she was with herself. Just because we are epicures does not mean the humble farmer Hodge is miserable with his bread and bacon. Madame Warrington led a life of duty that might seem monotonous to others, but it brought her genuine satisfaction. She was a brisk woman of business, overseeing every affair of her vast estate. Not a pie was baked at Castlewood without her little finger in the dough. She directed the maids in their spinning, supervised the kitchen staff, rode out on her pony to inspect the fields, and watched over the overseers and enslaved workers tending the tobacco and corn crops. If a slave fell ill, she would visit his quarters in any weather, administering remedies with great resolve. She kept a book of old-fashioned recipes, operated a distillery for waters and elixirs, and maintained a medicine chest that terrified her neighbors, who feared falling sick lest she descend upon them with her potions and pills.

Centuries prior, Virginia held few towns; the homes of the gentry were merely small villages inhabited by themselves and their dependents. Rachel Esmond ruled Castlewood like a miniature queen, while neighboring "princes" governed their own domains. Many of these lords were somewhat impoverished potentates, living comfortably yet roughly, surrounded by servants in ragged liveries. They kept open houses, welcoming every stranger, and indulged in field sports befitting their noble lineage. The widow of Castlewood matched her neighbors in hospitality but surpassed them in economy. Several likely men might have sought to marry her and raise her sons, yet none seemed worthy of a lady of her exalted birth. Rumors circulated that the Duke of Cumberland might be appointed viceroy, or even king, of America. Madame Warrington's gossipers laughed, suggesting she waited for him. With grave dignity, however, she noted that royalty itself had once offered alliances to the Esmond family.

Under her command served an officer's widow, previously mentioned, who had been Madame Esmond's school companion at Kensington Academy, just as her late husband had been the regimental friend of the late Mr. Warrington. When English girls at the academy mocked the little American stranger and ridiculed the princely airs she adopted from childhood, Fanny Parker had defended and befriended her. Both women married ensigns in Kingsley's Regiment and developed a tender attachment, referring to one another as "my Fanny" and "my Rachel" in their letters. Tragically, Fanny's husband died in poverty, leaving her with an infant and no provision. Later, Captain Franks brought Mrs. Mountain aboard the *Young Rachel* to Virginia.

Castlewood House had ample space, and Mrs. Mountain quickly enlivened the household. She carded games with the mistress, assisted the eldest boy with music, entertained guests, managed guest chambers, and oversaw linens. A kind, brisk, and jovial widow, she attracted the attention of several unmarried gentlemen in the colony, yet she refused to remarry, citing that her first husband, Mr. Mountain, had amicably spent her fortune and his own. Even his funeral expenses had been covered by her remaining trinkets. As long as Madame Warrington kept her, Mrs. Mountain preferred a home without a husband to any offer made in Virginia. Though the two ladies quarreled frequently, they loved each other deeply, making up their differences only to fall out again later. When either boy fell ill, they competed in maternal care. During the Colonel's final illness, Mrs. Mountain's cheerfulness and kindness were greatly valued by him—a memory Madame Warrington cherished above all others. Consequently, whenever Captain Franks asked if she intended to return with him, she declined, promising to stay another year.

When suitors approached Madame Warrington, she received their attentions kindly, often asking if they sought Mrs. Mountain. She would intervene on behalf of the younger woman, praising Fanny's English lineage and suitability for any gentleman. If a suitor declared his affection for the daughter of the Marquis of Esmond rather than her dependent, Madame Warrington would curtsy gravely, express her regret at the misunderstanding, and remind him that she lived for her people and sons, showing no intention of changing her station. Like Queen Elizabeth, a sensible woman of business who inspired both terror and love, the little Virginian princess had her favorites, accepted their flattery, grew weary of them, and treated them with cruelty or kindness as her whims dictated. Compliments were graciously received as her due. This foible was so well-known that wags exploited it. Rattling Jack Firebrace of Henrico County enjoyed free quarters for months, winning favor by reciting stolen verses. Tom Humbold of Spotsylvania wagered fifty hogsheads against five that he could persuade her to institute an order of knighthood, and he won.

The elder boy observed these eccentricities with private rage. From an early age, he revolted against the flattery paid to his mother, attempting to expose it with juvenile satire. Thus, his mother would remark seriously, "The Esmonds have always been of a jealous disposition; my poor boy takes after his parents." George despised Jack Firebrace and Tom Humbold alike, whereas Harry delighted in sporting with them—fowling, fishing, cockfighting, and enjoying the rural pleasures.

One winter, following the dismissal of their first tutor, Madame Esmond took the boys to Williamsburg for education. There, they attended the famous Mr. Whitfield, whose arrival revitalized the colony's spiritual life. Unlike neighboring provinces, Virginia was a Church of England colony where clergymen were state-paid and held glebe lands. Lacking bishops, the colonists imported ministers from England, often receiving noblemen's hangers-on or insolvent parsons seeking livelihoods. Naturally, these were not always the most eloquent pastors. No wonder Mr. Whitfield's powerful voice stirred souls that the harmless Reverend Broadbent could not awaken. Initially, the boys were as excited as their mother, singing hymns and listening with fervor. Had Mr. Whitfield stayed longer, Harry and George might have traded their epaulettes for black clerical coats. The simple boys shared their experiences, constantly watching for the sacred "call" that thrilled Protestant England.

However, Mr. Whitfield could not remain indefinitely with this small congregation. His mission was to enlighten the entire benighted church, from east to west, awakening slumbering sinners. Nevertheless, he comforted the widow with precious letters and promised to send a tutor capable of teaching profane learning alongside spiritual strength.

Soon, a chosen vessel arrived from England. Young Mr. Ward possessed a voice as loud as Mr. Whitfield's and could speak almost as fluently and at length. Nights and evenings echoed with his exhortations; domestic negroes crept to the doors to listen, and servants crowded the porch windows to hear him. He exerted the greatest influence over the "black sheep" of the Castlewood flock. These woolly lambs were deeply moved by his words, and when he led the hymns, a negro chorus rose within the house audible across the Potomac—a sound unheard in the Colonel's time, for that gentleman distrusted all cassocks and preferred debating theology only over backgammon. Where charity funds were needed, no one was more ready, and the good, easy Virginian clergyman, who loved backgammon dearly, believed the Colonel's charity covered his other shortcomings.

Mr. Ward was handsome, and his preaching pleased Madame Esmond immediately, satisfying her as much as Mr. Whitfield's had. Women of a century past were credulous, eager to admire and believe, prone to imagining infinite excellence in objects of their devotion. For weeks, perhaps months, Madame Esmond never tired of Mr. Ward's glib voice and voluble commonplaces. True to her nature, she insisted her neighbors attend, ordering them to be converted. Her favorite, the young Mr. Washington, she pressed repeatedly to stay at Castlewood and benefit from the spiritual advantages available. Yet, Mr. Washington had pressing business elsewhere and invariably ordered his horse away when Mr. Ward's services began. Furthermore, true to the nature of boys toward their pedagogue, the twins quickly grew tired and rebellious under the new teacher.

They found him a poor scholar, a dull fellow, and ill-mannered. George knew more Latin and Greek than his master, catching him in perpetual blunders and false accents. Harry, permitted greater liberties, mimicked Ward's eating and speaking mannerisms until Mrs. Mountain, Madame Esmond, and even little Fanny Mountain were forced to laugh. Madame Esmond might have exposed him as a vulgar quack had not her sons opposed him, a stance she countered with indomitable will. "What matters whether he possesses more or less of profane learning?" she asked. "In that which is most precious, Mr. W. is able to teach us all. What if his manners are slightly rough? Heaven does not choose its elect from among the great and wealthy. I wish you knew one book as well as Mr. Ward does. It is your wicked pride—the pride of all Esmonds—that prevents you from listening.
