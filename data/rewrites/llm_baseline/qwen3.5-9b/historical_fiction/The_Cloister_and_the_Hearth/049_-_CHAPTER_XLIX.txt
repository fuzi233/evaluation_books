Margaret Brandt had always kept herself aloof from the bustling life of Sevenbergen, a reserve that the townsfolk interpreted as arrogance. Whispers of jealousy and malice swelled within their hearts, waiting for the moment her hidden condition could no longer be concealed. The prospect terrified her; she feared how they would triumph over her once the truth broke the surface. This dread gnawed at her every hour, driving her to seclude herself within the house and shunning the daylight whenever possible.

Yet, in her secret conscience, Margaret possessed a clear moral compass. Though unfamiliar with the intricate nuances of contemporary law, she understood that a betrothal was a binding contract, as irrevocable as any signed agreement. She knew that marrying another would likely result in annulment by both Church and State, and that children born of such unions were often deemed legitimate. However, what crushed her simple medieval spirit was the absence of proof. Instead of holding the document, Gerard had taken it, and he was now far away across the sea.

She loathed herself for the careless oversight that left her vulnerable to public scorn. While she had never heard of Horace's famous line regarding how things that strike too close to home cause the greatest irritation, she shared the same practical intelligence characteristic of her sex when emotion does not cloud judgment. She measured the world's perception with precision. With her "marriage lines" missing and located in Italy, they could never counterbalance the undeniable evidence of her pregnancy and the future sight of her child. How could she silence slanderous tongues with the plea, "I possess my marriage certificate, but I cannot show it to you"? Who would believe a woman, especially one known for her extreme modesty? Here, slander wore the mask of truth. In an era before the concept of the "strong-minded woman," Margaret—though naturally capable and accustomed to managing a household—found herself weak as water against the weight of public opinion. Like the delicate leaves of the elite class, she trembled at every gust of societal judgment.

There is profound misery in few words. Few descriptions could convey the agony of a virtuous woman anticipating her parade as a sinner, whereas a woman of frail heart might have simply worn the label with defiance. Margaret was pure as snow, watching the darkness approach. She sat listlessly for hours, consumed by internal anguish. Often, during conversations with her father, her face would flush with sudden heat, leaving the old man wondering what words had offended her. Nothing had changed; his voice was mere air to her. It was the constant, suffocating dread that painted her cheeks with the color of shame, regardless of the topic at hand.

This fear reached its zenith after returning from Margaret Van Eyck's home that evening. Her condition had been discovered by women of her own sex. While the secluded old artist might remain silent, Catherine—a gossip at the center of a large family and a tight-knit neighborhood—posed a grave threat. A single spark of hope lingered: Catherine had spoken with kindness, even affection. The situation allowed for no middle ground; roused, Catherine would either become Margaret's staunchest ally or her fiercest enemy. Waiting in agonizing anxiety for news, Margaret received none. Hope faded. Convinced that Catherine intended to expose her due to a lack of genuine compassion, a wild, desperate desire to flee the neighborhood took root.

But how could she persuade her father to abandon his home? Old people clung to their roots, and Peter was infirm. The only path lay in making him her confidant, rather than fleeing alone. Caught between an overwhelming urge to hide and flight, and an insurmountable aversion to speaking to a man—even her father—she existed in a state of torturous suspension. Then, one day, the fatal thought arrived: *an end to it all.*

Catherine's frequent fears regarding the city's canals tempted this wavering girl. Standing by the bank one afternoon, she gazed into the calm, deep water, which seemed a mirror of the peace she craved. "No more trouble. No more fear of shame," she whispered. If Gerard had not loved her, she might have jumped then. But as it was, kneeling by the water's edge, she prayed fervently to God to purge such wicked thoughts from her soul.

"Oh, selfish wretch!" she cried aloud. "To leave thy father! Oh, wicked wretch, to kill thy unborn child and deprive poor Gerard of the pain and peril he undertook for thy sake! I will tell my father everything, yes, before this sun sets."

With eager haste, lest her resolve dissolve, she rushed home. In domestic matters, the learned Peter was as simple as a child, while Margaret, since the age of sixteen, had governed the house gently but absolutely. It was therefore strange to see her young, despotic mistress addressing him with such faltering hesitation.

"Father, I would speak to thee."
"Speak on, girl."
"Wilt you listen? And—and—not try to excuse my faults?"
"We all have our faults, Margaret, no more than the rest; but fewer, unless parental feeling blinds me."
"Alas, no, father. I am a poor, foolish girl who wishes to do well but has done ill, most ill, and most unwisely. Now I must bear the shame. But, father, I love you, with all my faults, and will you not forgive my folly and still love your motherless daughter?"
"That ye may count on," Peter replied cheerfully.
"Oh, well, do not smile. How can I speak and make you sad?"
"Why, what is the matter?"
"Father, disgrace is coming upon this house; it stands at the door. And I am the culprit. Oh, father, turn your head away. I—I... father, I have let Gerard take away my marriage lines."
"Is that all? 'Twas an oversight."
"'Twas the deed of a mad woman. But woe is me! That is not the worst."

Peter interrupted her. "The youth is honest and loves you dearly. You are young. What is a year or two to you? Gerard will assuredly return and keep his troth."
"And meanwhile, do you know what is coming?"
"Not I, except that I shall be gone first."
"Worse than that. There is pain worse than death. Nay, for pity's sake, turn away your head, father."
"Foolish wench!" Peter muttered, turning his face aside.

She trembled violently, her cheeks aflame, beginning to stammer, "I looked upon Gerard as my husband—we were betrothed—and he was in such dire danger, and I thought I had killed him, and I..." Tears welled in her eyes. "If you were but my mother, I might find courage; you would question me. But you say not a word."
"Why, Margaret, what is all this commotion? Why are thy cheeks crimson, speaking to no stranger, but to thy old father?"
"Why are my cheeks on fire? Because—because—father, kill me! Send me to heaven! Bid Martin shoot me with his arrow! And then the gossips will come and tell you why I blush this day. And then, when I am dead, I hope you will love your girl again for her mother's sake."

"Give me thy hand, mistress," Peter said, somewhat sternly.
She extended her trembling hand. He took it gently, his face anxious as he checked her pulse.
"Alas, nay," she whispered. "'Tis my soul that burns, not my body, with fever. I cannot, will not, abide in Sevenbergen." Wringing her hands impatiently, she added, "Be calm now," he soothed, "nor torment thyself for naught. Not abide in Sevenbergen? What need to stay a day, as it vexes thee and brings on a fever? For fevered thou art, deny it not."
"What!" cried Margaret. "Would you yield to go hence, and—and ask no reason but my longing to depart?" Suddenly throwing herself to her knees beside him in a fervor of supplication, she clutched his sleeve, then his arm, then his shoulder, imploring him to leave immediately without asking why.
"Alas! What need? You will soon see. And I could never say it. I would sooner die."
"Foolish child, who seeks thy girlish secrets? Is it I, whose life has been spent searching Nature's mysteries? And for leaving Sevenbergen, what keeps me here if you are unwilling? Is there respect for me here, or gratitude? Am I not called a quack-salver by those who avoid me, and a wizard by those I heal? Do they not give the reward and honor denied to me to the empirics who slaughter them? Besides, what matters where we sojourn? Choose thou, as thy mother did before thee."

Margaret embraced him tenderly, weeping upon his shoulder. She was reprieved. Yet, as she wept, she almost wished she had possessed the courage to tell him everything. Eventually, nothing satisfied him but her acceptance of a medicine he fetched. Taking it submissively to please him, she drank the composing draught. Administered under a common misconception, it did her more good than harm; she awoke calmed by a long sleep. That very day, she began her preparations.
