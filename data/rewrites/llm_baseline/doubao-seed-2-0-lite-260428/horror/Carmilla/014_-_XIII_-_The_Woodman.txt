It wasn’t long before the cracks in Millarca’s facade began to show, sharp and unignorable as thorns pushing through graveyard loam.
First came that dragging, endless languor—the leftover “weakness” from her supposed illness that kept her locked in her room until the sun was dipping low over the treetops. Then came the impossible discovery: though she always turned the key in her lock from the inside, and never moved it an inch until the maid called to help her dress, she was repeatedly spotted slipping through the schloss grounds in the pale, ash-grey first light of dawn, barefoot, moving east through the oaks like one half-dead, caught in a trance. I thought at first it was sleepwalking—but that answer did not fit the riddle no one could untangle: how did she slip out of her locked chamber without unbarring a single door or window to the rest of the house?
I had no time to puzzle it out before a far sharper, screaming anxiety coiled around my throat. My poor Laura, the old General told us, his voice thick with mingled grief and rage, was fading. The bloom drained from her cheeks, the light from her eyes, wasting away in a way no doctor could name, no tonic touch. It started with nightmares so appalling she woke screaming, unable to draw breath. Then she swore a specter haunted her bedroom, some nights looking just like Millarca, other nights a shapeless hulk of fur, creeping slow in circles around the foot of her bed. Then came the strange sensations: first a trickle of something icy, like glacial melt, dragging slow across the skin of her breast, not quite painful, but so wrong it made her teeth ache. Then a pair of burning, needle-sharp pricks, just below her throat, stabbing deep enough to leave her gasping. After that came the slow, choking, convulsive sense of being strangled, until the dark swallowed her whole.

Every word the General spoke landed in my chest like a shard of cold glass. We were driving over the short, springy grass that lined the road to that roofless, long-abandoned village—one that had not breathed a single wisp of chimney smoke for more than fifty years—and I recognized every single symptom he described as my own. The sickness eating me alive, the shadow that haunted my bed, the stranger who sat beneath my roof smiling soft and sweet while she feasted on my life: it was Carmilla, every strange, vicious quirk of her, laid bare.
The forest parted suddenly, and we were staring up at the jagged chimneys and half-collapsed gables of the dead village, the broken battlements of the old Karnstein castle rising from the low eminence above it, girdled by ancient trees so tall they blotted out half the sky. I stumbled off the carriage like I was still trapped in a nightmare, none of us speaking a word as we climbed the path up to the ruins, our boots crunching over fallen stone and years of dead leaves, through vast empty chambers, winding staircases draped in cobwebs thick as shrouds, corridors so dark the noon sun barely pierced them.
“This was once the palace of the Karnsteins,” the General said at last, leaning against a gaping window frame, staring out over the rolling miles of forest below. “It was a cursed line. Their annals are written in blood, right here on these very walls. It is a cruelty beyond measure that they still plague the living, a century after their deaths, driven by the same ravenous, atrocious lusts.” He pointed down the steep slope, through the tangles of ivy, to the grey, crumbling walls of a half-buried Gothic chapel. “That is the Karnstein family chapel, down there. And I hear a woodman’s axe, ringing through the trees around it. He may know what I need: the location of the grave of Mircalla, Countess of Karnstein. The villagers still hold the old local tales, long after the rich noble families forgot their own buried skeletons once their lines die out.”

“We have a portrait of Mircalla, Countess Karnstein at home,” my father put in. “Would you care to see it?”
“Plenty of time for that, my friend,” the General said. “I swear I have seen the real woman, walking my halls in my own home. That is why I came to you earlier than I planned: to find this chapel, and what lies beneath it.”
“See Mircalla?” My father blinked, bewildered. “But she has been dead for more than a hundred years!”
“Not half as dead as you think,” the General growled.
“General, you are making no sense,” my father said, and I saw a flicker of that old, unspoken suspicion he had held of the old man a few days prior. But there was no madness in his face, only white-hot, unshakable fury.
“I have only one purpose left in the few years I have left on this earth,” he said, as we stepped under the heavy stone arch of the old chapel, its dimensions grand enough to count as a full church. “I will have my vengeance, and thank God, a mortal hand can still deliver it.”
“What vengeance do you speak of?” my father asked, horrified.
“To cut the monster’s head from her shoulders,” the General roared, face flushing dark red, his boot slamming down on the stone floor so hard the echo boomed and bounced off the hollow walls. He curled his fist, shaking it in the air like he already held the sharp edge of an axe, swinging it at an invisible enemy. “With a hatchet, a spade, any blade sharp enough to cleave through her murderous throat. I will tell you the rest.” He hurried forward, gesturing to a thick squared block of oak half-buried in the grass growing through the chapel’s cracked pavement. “Sit, child, you are worn thin. I will finish this dreadful tale in a few words.”

I sank down on that rough wood bench, my bones aching, as the General called out to the woodman. The old man stepped out from behind a gnarled oak, axe slung over his shoulder, his face crisscrossed with years of sun and scar tissue, his boots caked in dark loam that looked almost black. He told us he knew nothing of the chapel’s old graves, but there was a forest ranger, nearly as old as the trees, staying at the priest’s cottage two miles out, who could point to every last Karnstein monument. For a few coins, he said, he would borrow one of our horses and be back with the ranger in half an hour, no more.
“Have you worked these woods long?” my father asked him.
“All my life,” the woodman said, his voice thick with the local patois. “My father before me, his father before him, back as many generations as I can name. I can show you the very cottage in that dead village down there where all my people lived, once.”
“Why was the village abandoned?” the General asked.
“Revenants, sir.” The woodman’s face went tight, pale under his tanned skin. “We tracked a dozen of them to their graves, tested them the old way, drove stakes through them, cut off their heads, burned the remains—but not before half the village was dead. Even after that, the torment did not stop. Then a Moravian nobleman, traveling through, heard what was happening. His people know all these old tricks. He climbed the chapel steeple one night, right after the sun went down, when the moon was full, and he watched the churchyard below. He saw the revenant climb out of its grave, lay the linen winding sheet it was buried in on the grass beside the stone, and glide off to the village to kill. The stranger snuck down, grabbed that winding sheet, and climbed back to the top of the steeple. When the revenant came back and found its clothes gone, it roared so loud the windows rattled, and the Moravian called down, waving the linen, telling the monster to come up and get it back. It climbed the steeple, leaving black, oily handprints all over the stone, and the second its head cleared the battlements, the Moravian swung his sword and split its skull clean in two. He threw the body down into the churchyard, climbed back down, cut its head off, and the next day the villagers impaled what was left and burned every last ash. That same Moravian had the authority of the then-head of the Karnstein family to dig up Mircalla’s tomb, too, and he hid it so well no one has found it since.”
“Can you point to where it stood?” the General leaned forward, voice sharp with urgency.
The woodman shook his head, smiling a grim, thin smile. “Not a soul alive in this valley can tell you that now. They say her body was moved, but no one living knows for sure.”
He slung his axe over his shoulder, turned, and vanished into the trees, leaving the three of us alone in the cold, still chapel ruin, the only sound the faint sigh of wind through broken glass, and the unshakable feeling that eyes were watching us, from somewhere out in the dark, overgrown graveyard.
