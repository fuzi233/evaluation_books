The rumor of Rosanna's vanishing had rippled outward, infecting the ranks of the outdoor servants with a feverish urgency. Their inquiries converged swiftly upon a small, restless spirit known as Duffy, a boy tasked with the mundane duty of weeding the garden. It was Duffy who provided the crucial vector: he had witnessed Rosanna Spearman merely thirty minutes prior. He insisted she had not walked, but fled—darting through the fir plantation toward the treacherous edge of the sea-shore.

"Does this boy possess intimate knowledge of the coastline?" Sergeant Cuff demanded, his tone sharpening with professional instinct.

"He was born and bred upon these shores," I replied, the weight of the moment settling heavily upon my shoulders.

"Duffy!" the Sergeant barked, seizing the boy by the collar with a grip that brooked no delay. "Do you wish to earn a shilling? If so, fall in line with me immediately." He issued a sharp command to Betteredge regarding the pony-chaise, then launched himself forward at a pace that defied the natural limitations of a man approaching seventy. His stride was a blur of kinetic energy, a stark contrast to the sluggish despair that threatened to paralyze me. Behind him, Little Duffy let out a primal howl of excitement, a sound echoing the wildness of youth, and scrambled to keep pace with the Sergeant's relentless momentum.

In the hollow space between the Sergeant's departure and the boy's return, my mind dissolved into a chaotic vortex of restlessness. I performed a dozen futile errands within and without the house, actions so disjointed that memory now refuses to catalog them. Time itself seemed to warp; I remain uncertain of the duration until Duffy sprinted back, breathless, clutching a message. The Sergeant had torn a page from his pocket-book, scrawling in hurried pencil: *Send me one of Rosanna Spearman's boots, and be quick about it.*

I dispatched the nearest female servant to retrieve the boot from Rosanna's chamber, sending the boy ahead to inform me that I would follow. Admittedly, this circuitous route was far from efficient. Yet, a stubborn, protective instinct—a final, desperate attempt to shield the girl—surged within me. I refused to entrust the physical evidence to the Sergeant's hands without witnessing the scene firsthand, needing to verify the nature of the mystery unfolding before me. This internal conflict, compounded by the rising tide of detective fever, propelled me forward at the fastest speed a septuagenarian could muster.

As I approached the shore, the sky collapsed into a churning mass of black clouds. Rain lashed down in torrential sheets, whipped horizontally by the gale. The thunderous roar of the sea battered the sandbank at the bay's mouth, a deafening testament to the storm's fury. Further along, I spotted the boy cowering beneath the lee of the dunes, huddled against the deluge. Beyond him lay the chaos: the raging surf, the massive rollers crashing onto the bank, the driving rain scouring the waters like a violent garment, and the desolate yellow expanse of the beach dominated by a single, stark silhouette—Sergeant Cuff.

He raised a hand toward the north as I drew near. "Stay to that side!" he shouted over the cacophony. "Come down here to me!"

I descended to him, lungs burning, heart hammering against my ribs as if seeking escape. Words failed me entirely. A hundred questions surged to my throat, yet none could pass my lips. His expression froze me; a look of profound horror etched his features. Snatching the boot from my trembling hand, he planted it firmly into a fresh impression in the wet sand. The print bore south from our position, pointing directly toward the jagged limestone ledge known as the South Spit. The mark was crisp, unblurred by the rain, and the fit was precise.

Without uttering a syllable, the Sergeant indicated the boot, then gestured toward the track leading to the rocks. I lunged for his arm, desperate to speak, but my voice remained trapped in my chest, just as it had moments before. He ignored me, following the trail downward until the solid ground surrendered to the shifting quicksand. The South Spit was submerged, the tide heaving over its hidden face. With an obstinate, terrifying patience, Cuff tested the boot against the footprints repeatedly. Each time, the trajectory remained identical: straight *toward* the rocks. No matter how intently he hunted, he found no evidence of footsteps walking *away* from them.

Exhausted by the fruitless search, he fell silent. Turning his gaze from the tracks to the churning waters before us, he looked out at the deepening surge over the quicksand. I mirrored his stare, and in his eyes, I read the conclusion he had reached. A terrible, numb tremor seized every fiber of my being. I sank to my knees on the hard sand.

"She has returned to the hiding-place," the Sergeant murmured, the words hanging in the air like a verdict. "A fatal accident has claimed her on those rocks."

Suddenly, memories of Rosanna flooded my mind with crushing force. Her altered demeanor, the deadened timbre of her voice, the vacant way she had swept the corridor mere hours ago—it all coalesced into a horrifying realization. Even as the Sergeant spoke, I knew his assessment was dangerously incomplete. I attempted to confess the truth, to whisper that her death was self-inflicted, a suicide born of weariness. But the words choked in my throat, swallowed by the dumb paralysis gripping me. I felt nothing of the driving rain, saw nothing of the rising tide. In a vivid, waking nightmare, the lost girl reappeared before me as I had seen her on that fateful morning. I recalled her confession: that the Shivering Sand pulled at her against her will, that her grave waited there. The horror struck me with the force of a physical blow, transmitted through the shared trauma of my own daughter, who was Rosanna's age. Had she endured such misery, she too might have met this dreadful end.

With gentle strength, the Sergeant lifted me from my knees and turned me away from the site of her demise. Only then did I begin to breathe, the fog lifting slightly to reveal the reality of my surroundings. Looking toward the sandhills, I saw the male servants and the fisherman, Yolland, rushing down the slope, shouting inquiries about the girl's fate. The Sergeant concisely explained the evidence of the footprints, confirming a tragic accident. He singled out Yolland, turning his gaze back to the sea.

"Tell me," he commanded. "Could a boat have ferried her away in weather such as this, from those very rocks where her tracks cease?"

Yolland pointed to the towering rollers and the foamy cliffs framing the bay. "No vessel ever constructed," he declared, "could navigate through that."

Cuff studied the footprints one last time as the rain began to blur them. "Here," he stated, "is proof she did not leave by land. And here," he added, fixing his eyes on the fisherman, "is proof she did not depart by sea."

Pausing to consider, he addressed Yolland. "She was observed running toward this spot half an hour before my arrival. Some time has elapsed since then. Let us assume an hour has passed. Given the current tide, how high would the water stand on the southern side of the Spit at that moment?"

"On that side," Yolland replied, "there would not have been sufficient water to drown a kitten an hour ago."

Turning northward toward the quicksand, Cuff asked, "What of this side?"

"Even less," Yolland confirmed. "The Shivering Sand would have been merely awash."

The Sergeant nodded, concluding the tragedy occurred on the quicksand side. My tongue suddenly loosened. "Not an accident!" I cried out. "When she arrived here, she was weary of life, intent on ending it."

The Sergeant recoiled in shock. "How do you know?" he demanded.

The others crowded closer, drawn by the tension. Recovering his composure instantly, Cuff pushed them back. "He is an old man," he said firmly. "The discovery has shaken him. Leave him be for a moment."

Then, addressing Yolland, he asked, "Is there any possibility of recovering her body when the tide recedes?"

"None," Yolland answered grimly. "What the Sand takes, it keeps forever."

Stepping closer, the fisherman turned his attention to me. "Mr. Betteredge," he said softly, "I must address you regarding the young woman's passing. Four feet out, broadside along the Spit, lies a shelf of rock, approximately half a fathom beneath the surface. My inquiry is simple: Why did she not strike it? If she slipped accidentally from the Spit, she would have fallen into a zone with footholds at the bottom, at a depth covering her only to the waist. She must have deliberately waded or jumped into the Deep beyond—or she would not be missing now. No accident, sir! The Deeps of the Quicksand claim her, and they have taken her by her own volition."

Following this authoritative testimony, the Sergeant fell silent. We all stood in a heavy, collective quietude. Together, we turned back up the beach slope. At the sandhills, we were intercepted by the under-groom, who ran to us from the house. A loyal soul, he carried an honest reverence for me. He presented a small note, his face etched with genuine sorrow.
