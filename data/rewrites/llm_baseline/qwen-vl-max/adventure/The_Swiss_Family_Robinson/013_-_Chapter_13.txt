As evening fell, the aroma of stewing bear paws filled the air, rising from the fire like a promise of warmth and comfort. Around it, we sat in quiet anticipation, ears tuned to the distant sounds of returning riders—our sons, still out on their great adventure.

Then came the thunder of hooves, sharp and clear across the open ground. The young explorers appeared in a gallop, dust swirling behind them, and a joyful shout echoed as we rose to greet them.

They leapt from their horses, freeing the animals to graze, and rushed toward us with tales and treasures in hand. What a sight they made! Franz and Jack each carried a young kid slung over their backs, its four legs tied together and dangling beneath their chins like odd little bundles. Fritz’s game bag bulged strangely—round shapes, sharp points, and occasional twitches hinted at living creatures inside.

“Hurrah for the hunt, Father!” Jack exclaimed, grinning from ear to ear. “Nothing beats real chasing! Storm and Grumble flew over that grassy plain like the wind. We wore out those little antelopes and caught them easily!”

“Yes, Father,” said Franz, “and Fritz brought back two angora rabbits. And we found honey—thanks to a clever bird, a cuckoo, who led us right to it!”

“My brothers forget the most important thing,” Fritz said, stepping forward proudly. “We drove a whole herd of antelopes through the Gap into our land. They’re there now—safe, free, and ready for us whenever we want them. Tame them, or hunt them—we’ve got them!”

“Well done!” I said, clapping my hands. “That’s a fine achievement. But more than anything, I’m grateful God brought you home safely. Now, tell me everything—every detail.”

“We rode down Glen Verdant,” Fritz began, “then through the defile in our Rocky Barrier. The morning was cool, and the horses raced almost the whole way. After passing through the Gap, we slowed down and scanned the hills. From the top of a grassy rise, we saw two herds of animals—antelopes, goats, or gazelles? We weren’t sure. But they were grazing near the stream below.

“We were about to charge down for a shot when I had an idea: instead of hunting them one by one, why not drive the whole herd through the Gap into our territory? That way, they’d be trapped in a kind of natural park—free, but ours.

“So we charged down the hill, formed a semicircle behind the larger herd, and with shouts and dogs barking, we drove them along the riverbank toward the Gap. As we neared the opening, they hesitated—just like sheep before the butcher’s door. We nearly lost them, but then one broke through, and the rest followed. In moments, every animal was on our side of the frontier—new citizens of New Switzerland!”

“Excellent,” I said. “But how do you know they’ll stay?”

“Father,” Fritz replied, “we thought of that. We stretched a long line across the defile—feathers, rags, anything that would flutter in the wind. It looked so strange, so unnatural, that I’m certain they’ll never cross it again. Levaillant writes in his *Voyage au Cap de Bonne Esperance* that the Hottentots use this trick to trap antelopes.”

“I’m glad you remember what you read,” I said. “Welcome to New Switzerland, antelopes. But—Fritz—I can’t say the same for your rabbits. They multiply too fast. If you start a colony, you may soon have a plague on your hands.”

“True,” he admitted, “but I plan to release them on Whale Island. There’s plenty of food, and they won’t bother us. Besides, my eagle caught these two. I saw them running, freed him, and in minutes he brought me three—one dead, which I gave him as a reward, and these two alive.”

“Now, Father,” Jack interrupted, “let me tell my part. Or Fritz will go on forever with his long descriptions.”

“Of course, Jack,” I said. “Tell me how you captured those lovely fawns.”

“We chased them,” Jack said. “The dogs sniffed around while Fritz was after the rabbits. Then—pop!—out came the fawns, bounding off like lightning. Storm, Grumble, and the dogs took off after them. They ran at thirty miles an hour, but in no time, we left the dogs behind and closed in.

“They collapsed in the grass, exhausted and terrified. We called Fritz, and—”

“Wait,” I said. “By your account, Fritz must have been seven and a half miles away.”

“Oh, well,” Jack laughed, “maybe we didn’t ride quite that long. And the fawns didn’t run straight. Anyway, Fritz heard us, and we all lashed their legs together. Then we remounted—and suddenly, a sneaky cuckoo appeared, hopping and cooing, leading us far off course. Franz said it was an enchanted princess. I was about to shoot it—but Fritz stopped me. He said it was a ‘Honey Indicator’—that it leads hunters to bee nests. So we let it live. And sure enough, it stopped right beside a hollow tree full of bees!”

“That’s wonderful,” I said. “And then?”

“We couldn’t wait to taste the honey,” Jack continued. “I threw in lit lucifer matches. But instead of killing the bees, they just went wild—stinging me everywhere! I jumped on Storm and galloped for my life. Took ages to shake them off. My face is burning now. I think I’ll ask Mother to bathe it.” With that, he dashed off, leaving Fritz and me to care for the fawns and inspect the rabbits.

I decided to follow Fritz’s idea: move the rabbits to Whale Island. It made sense—not only would they thrive there, but it also fit with my growing plan to turn Whale Island into a fortress. If danger ever forced us to retreat, having a steady supply of food would be vital.

After tending to the antelopes, I tried to share my discovery of the talc deposit with the boys—but just then, Mother called us to dinner.

The main course was bear paws—richly scented, tender, and so tempting that even their resemblance to human hands didn’t stop anyone from eating heartily. Jack muttered a mischievous “Fee-fi-fo-fum,” but no one minded.

After supper, we lit the watchfire, retired to the tent, and slept soundly.

We’d been busy: smoking bear meat, rendering fat, collecting bamboo. But I wanted one last expedition. At dawn, I woke the boys.

Fritz rode the mule, I mounted Lightfoot, Jack and Franz took their usual horses, and we set off with the dogs—first to gather gum from the euphorbia trees, then to check on the ostrich eggs.

Ernest watched us leave without a word, then returned to the tent to help Mother and study.

Our horses carried us swiftly down Green Valley, following the path of our earlier journey. Soon we reached Turtle Marsh, filled our water flasks, and climbed the ridge where Fritz had once seen mounted Arabs.

Jack and Franz wanted to race ahead, so I let them go, while Fritz and I visited the euphorbia trees. A thick red gum had oozed from the cuts I’d made. It had hardened in the sun, so I rolled it into balls and stored it in a bamboo jar.

“Father,” Fritz asked, “you said this juice is poisonous. Why collect so much?”

“I did,” I replied. “It’s deadly. The people of the Cape of Good Hope poison water holes with it to kill wild animals—mainly for hides. But I’ll use it to fight the apes if they return. And it helps preserve animal skins from insects.”

As we rode after the boys, four magnificent ostriches rose from the sand where they’d been sitting.

Jack and Franz spotted them and shouted, driving them toward us. A huge male led the way—black feathers gleaming, tail plume waving. Three gray females followed.

They charged at incredible speed, reaching gun range before noticing us. Fritz had already bound the eagle’s beak so he couldn’t harm the birds.

He released the falcon, which soared up and dove at the lead ostrich’s head. Startled, the bird stumbled. Jack closed in, hurled his lasso, and wrapped the creature’s wings and legs in coils. It crashed to the ground.

The others vanished into the distance. We dismounted and approached cautiously. The bird fought fiercely, kicking wildly in all directions. I feared we’d never bring it home alive.

Then I remembered: cover its eyes, and it might calm down.

I threw my coat and hunting bag over its head. Instantly, the struggling stopped.

We secured it with a wide strip of sealskin, fastened cords to either side so I could lead it, and looped another rope around its legs to prevent sudden bursts of speed. Then we removed the lasso.

“Do you know,” I asked the boys, “how Indian natives tame a new elephant?”

“Oh yes!” Fritz said. “They tie it between two tame elephants. We’ll do the same!”

“The only problem,” Jack added, “is we don’t have tame ostriches. But Storm and Grumble will make fine guards. This beast won’t escape them!”

So we began. Storm and Grumble were led to either side of the resting ostrich. Cords were tied to their girths. Jack and Franz mounted their horses, each armed with a whip. We removed the cloth from the bird’s eyes.

For a moment, it lay still. Then it sprang up—unaware of the ropes—and tried to run. The cords held firm. It fell to its knees. After a brief struggle, it accepted its fate and trotted forward, spurred on by the boys’ laughter.

The noise excited it further, but eventually, Storm and Grumble refused to keep pace. The ostrich stopped.

I told the boys to walk the bird slowly home, while Fritz and I checked the nest.

The eggs were warm—clearly the mother had just left. I left half and packed the rest into a large bag, slung it over Lightfoot’s saddle.

We caught up with the others and arrived at camp without incident.

Mother’s face showed shock and dismay.

“My dear husband,” she said, “do you really need to go raiding deserts for giant beasts? Next you’ll find an iron mine—because ostriches eat iron, don’t they?

“Oh, please, just keep the animals you’ve already collected. This is such a useless monster!”

“Useless?” Jack protested. “You wouldn’t say that if you’d seen it run! It’ll be the fastest horse in our stable. I’m making it a saddle and bridle. It’ll be my only steed. And its diet? Just fruit, grass, and a few stones and nails—it’s very delicate.”

His claim amused his brothers. A cry went up.

“Fine,” Jack said. “Let’s divide the bird. Fritz’s eagle grabbed the head—keep that. Father gets the body. I’ll take the legs. Franz, you get two tail feathers.”

“Hold on,” I said. “Jack has the best claim—he brought it down. If he tames it and makes it a riding bird, it’s his. From now on, he’s responsible for training it.”

Too late in the day to reach Rockburg, we tied the ostrich between two trees and spent the evening preparing for the journey.

At dawn, our colorful caravan moved forward. The ostrich remained stubborn, so we again placed it between Storm and Grumble. The cow pulled the cart, loaded with our treasures. Mother rode in the cart. Jack and Franz mounted Storm and Grumble. I rode Lightfoot, and Fritz brought up the rear on Swift.

At the mouth of the Gap, we paused. We replaced the feathered cord with a strong bamboo palisade. I also gathered pipe-clay—planning to try making china during the coming winter.

At the sugar-cane grove, we stopped to retrieve the smoked peccary hams. Mother asked me to gather seeds of a vanilla-scented plant nearby. I collected a good amount, then we pressed on toward Woodlands, where we planned to rest.

We pitched our tent and slept deeply on cotton beds.

The next morning, we inspected our farm. Everything thrived—the pigs, goats, chickens. Two broods of chicks especially pleased Mother, whom she decided to take back with us.

The antelope herd had settled near the trees. We saw them browsing often.

We repaired the animal stalls and our dwelling—making both safer and more comfortable.

All arranged, we rested. The next morning, we completed our journey to Rockburg.

By midday, we were home. Windows and doors opened wide. Animals settled into their stalls. The cart’s contents were unpacked and stored.

I turned first to the ostrich, tying it between two bamboo posts in front of the house.

Then I focused on the eggs. I built a stove to maintain constant heat and placed the eggs carefully in cotton wool.

Next morning, Fritz and I rowed to Whale Island, released the rabbits, then to Shark Island, where we set free the antelopes.

Back at home, we cured the bear skins and added new supplies to our cellar.

On our way, we met Jack, bursting with joy, carrying a massive eel in a basket—caught the night before with Ernest.

It was delicious. Half went to dinner; the other half was salted and stored.

We returned to household duties.

Thinking the verandah needed greenery, I planted vanilla, pepper, and other creeping vines at the base of each bamboo pillar—adding beauty and shade.

I built hen-coops for the chicks from Woodlands, knowing Knips and Fangs might otherwise investigate them too closely.

Ernest’s rat skins were tied together and hung outside—they smelled so strong that Jack pretended to faint whenever he passed.

The museum gained new items: the condor and vulture, to be stuffed later. Mica and asbestos were stored for future use—china-making and lamp-wicks.

After two days of work, we turned to farming, egg-hatching, and taming the ostrich.

Agriculture, though least appealing, was most important. The animals pulled the plough, but digging and hoeing tested our strength.

We worked two hours in the morning, two in the evening. We truly understood the Bible’s words: *In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread.*

Between shifts, we trained the ostrich. At first, nothing worked. He remained wild.

Then I remembered the eagle—tamed with tobacco smoke.

I applied the same method. The ostrich collapsed, motionless. Slowly, he rose and paced. Calmer, but refusing food.

“Food he must have!” I insisted.

Mother tried an experiment—balls of maize flour mixed with butter. She placed one in his beak. He swallowed. Then another, and another. His appetite returned.

The wildness faded. He began to obey.

After a month, he trotted, galloped, responded to voice, fed from our hands.

Now came the challenge: how to saddle and bridle a bird?

I recalled how darkness subdued him. I designed a leather hood, covering neck to beak, with eye and ear holes. Over the eyes, I attached square flaps—blinkers—with whalebone springs so they closed tightly.

Reins connected to the blinkers. Open both—straight ahead. Close right eye—turn left. Left eye—turn right. Both shut—stop.

I was proud of my invention.

Then came the saddle. After several tries, I made one shaped like an old cavalry saddle—peaked front and back—to prevent falls. I placed it high on the shoulders, secured it with strong girths across the wings and chest.

The contraption was strange—but it worked.

And so, our newest companion was ready. Not just a captive, but a rider’s friend.
