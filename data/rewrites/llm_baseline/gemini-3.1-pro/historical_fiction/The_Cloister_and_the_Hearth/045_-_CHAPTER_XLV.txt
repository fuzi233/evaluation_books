The little party at the hosier's house sat around the heavy oak table, the air in the room thick with the yeasty aroma of proving rye and the slow, caramelized scent of roasted root vegetables. Outside, the grating, metallic clang of the merchant quarter’s dye-vats bled through the walls—a harsh, industrial dissonance that Catherine, an agrarian matriarch to her marrow, always felt as a physical assault on the household's acoustic ecology. She returned from the threshold, her hands still faintly dusted with the flour of the morning's baking, cast a piercing glance around the little circle, and laid the parchment letter flat upon the scrubbed wood. 

Her fingers, which held the somatic memory of a thousand rhythmic kneadings and the precise tension required to pluck a winter pheasant, traced the harsh, angular ink of the dominant lexicon. She recited every word of it by memory, following the lines to cheat herself and her hearers into the notion that she could read. It was a desperate, defiant act of translation—an attempt to force the raw, untranslatable grief of her subjective maternal experience into the rigid grammar of the literate class that had torn her family apart. 

Then, suddenly lifting her head, she cast another keen look on Cornelis and Sybrandt. Their eyes fell. The tense auditory vacuum of the room shattered as the storm that had long been brewing burst upon their heads. Catherine seemed to swell like a broody hen ruffling her feathers, and out of her mouth poured a torrential dialect of the old ways—a rich, earthy lexicon of village memory and hearth-side wisdom rapidly dying out in the superficial commercialization of the city. It was a current of invective no male could utter, steeped entirely in the vocabulary of a woman who measured the profound meaning of life in harvests and slow-simmered broths.

"I have long smelled the sourness in your mash," she began, the words flowing on a single, breathless exhale. "I knew you blew the flame betwixt Gerard and your father, and set that old rogue, Ghysbrecht, on like a dog to a scrap of gristle. And now, here are Gerard's own written words to prove it. You have driven your own flesh and blood into a far land, and robbed the mother that bore you of the child who actually understood the alchemy of my kitchen—the pride of my eye, who knew the bright scent of wild thyme from garden marjoram, while you two treat my twelve-hour bone broths as nothing but utilitarian fuel! But you are all of a piece from end to end. When you were all boys together, learning to walk around my hearth, my others were a comfort; but you were a curse. Mischievous and sly, taking a woman half a day to keep your clothes whole: for why? Work wears cloth, but play cuts it. I thought prudence might ferment in you with your beards, like a good cider aging in the cask, but none came. Still the last to go to bed, and the last to leave it! Honesty goes to bed early, and industry rises betimes. Often I've looked at your ways and wondered what strange soil grew you: ye don't take after your father, and ye are no more like me than a wasp is to a honey-bee. Ye have not our hands, nor our palates for the good earth. Of all my blood, only you possess the discordant cruelty to jeer at them that God afflicted. When my back was turned, I heard your mocking laughter—a harsh, jagged noise that pollutes the peace of this house—sneering at Giles because he is small, and at my lily Kate because she lacks the brute strength of a Flanders mare. After that, rob a church an you will! For you can be no worse in His eyes that crafted both Kate and Giles, and in mine that suffered to bear them just as I did you, you paltry, unfeeling, treasonable curs!"

"No, I will not hush, my daughter," Catherine snapped, silencing Kate's gentle interruption, unwilling to cede the sonic space. "They have filled the cup until the wine spills over the rim. It takes a heavy stone to turn a mother's heart against the sons she nursed. Many is the time I have closed my ears to your grating frequencies, lest your father hear what I hear and turn you out the door. But where got ye all this money?" She gestured toward them, the memory of their clinking coins deeply offensive to her—a superficial, commercial clatter that drowned out the steady, organic rhythm of honest work. "For this last month you have been rolling in it. You never wrought for it in the fields or over the fire. Sloth and greed are ill-mated spices, my masters. Lovers of money must sweat or steal. Well, if you robbed any poor soul of it, it was some woman, I'll go bail; for a man would drive you with his naked hand. It is since that night you were out so late, Cornelis, and came back with a swelled head. No matter. It shows me how you will squander our pantry if ever it comes to be yourn. I have watched you. You spent a whole groat to-day between you, while I spend scarce a groat a week to keep this larder full and feed you all, good and bad."

Her voice shifted, looping the ancestral past into the present in a sweeping, circular rhythm. "I give up waiting for the shoes that will maybe walk behind your coffin; for this shop and this house shall never be yourn. Gerard is our heir. Poor Gerard, whose palate knew the language of my sauces, whom you banished and did your best to kill—after that never call me mother again! But you have made him tenfold dearer to me. My poor lost boy! I shall soon see him again, shall hold him, and feed him, and set him by the hearth. Ay, you may stare! You are too crafty, and yet not crafty enow. You cut the stalk away; but you left the seed—the seed that shall root deeper and outlive you."

She leaned forward, folding the mythic destiny of their bloodline into the domestic routine. "Margaret Brandt is quick, and it is Gerard's, and what is Gerard's is mine. I have prayed the saints it may be a boy; and it will—it must. Kate, when I found it was so, my bowels yearned over her child unborn as if my own womb were ripening again. He is our heir. He will outlive us. You will not; for a bad heart in a carcass is like the worm in a hoarded hazelnut, soon bringing the whole body to bitter dust. So, Kate, take down Gerard's bib and tucker from the cedar drawer you wot of. We will borrow Peter Buyskens' cart, and we will bring her a strengthening pottage of marrow and young leeks to comfort Gerard's wife under her burden. She is his wife. Who is Ghysbrecht Van Swieten and his bloodless, official decrees? Can his dominant lexicon come between a couple and the altar, sundering those that God and the priest make one? She is my daughter, and I am as proud of her as I am of you, Kate, almost. And as for you two, keep out of my way awhile, for you are like the black dog in my eyes."

Cornelis and Sybrandt took the hint. They slunk out, aching with remorse, impenitence, and hate, their heavy boots dragging a discordant, shuffling rhythm against the floorboards. They avoided her eye as much as ever they could, and for many days, Catherine never spoke a single word to either of them. Her absolute silence was a deliberate reclamation of the household's sonic ecology, purging their toxic noise from her sanctuary to let the quiet, simmering rhythms of the kitchen speak in her stead. *Liberaverat animum suum.*
