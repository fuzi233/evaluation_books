The only comfortably furnished room in the offices of the *Record* was quiet except for the low hum of the city outside and the steady scratch of pens at desks. Then, without warning, the telephone on Sir James Molloy’s desk buzzed sharply.

Sir James didn’t look up. He merely raised a hand with his pen poised, and Mr. Silver, his secretary, immediately rose from his chair and crossed to the instrument.

“Who is that?” he asked, speaking into the receiver.

“Who?... I can’t hear you.... Oh, it’s Mr. Bunner, is it?... Yes, but—” A pause. “I know, but he’s dreadfully busy this afternoon. Can’t you—? Oh, really? Well, in that case—just hold on, will you?”

He placed the receiver gently before Sir James.

“It’s Calvin Bunner,” he said, matter-of-fact. “Sigsbee Manderson’s right-hand man. He insists on speaking to you personally. Says it’s the gravest news. He’s calling from the house down by Bishopsbridge, so you’ll need to speak clearly.”

Sir James glanced at the phone—not fondly—and picked up the receiver. His voice, when it came, was deep and commanding.

“Well?”

He listened.

Then: “Yes.”

A moment passed. Mr. Silver watched closely. And then—suddenly—Sir James’s face changed. The calm vanished. His eyes widened. His mouth opened slightly. A low murmur escaped him.

“Good God.”

He held the phone tightly, rising slowly to his feet, still listening intently. At intervals, he repeated, “Yes.” Once, he glanced at the clock, then turned sharply to Mr. Silver.

“Go and find Figgis and young Williams. Hurry.”

Mr. Silver darted out.

Sir James was a man of fifty, tall, strong, and sharp-featured, with a thick black mustache and dark, piercing eyes. An Irishman of immense energy and keen intelligence, he had built the *Record* into one of the most powerful morning papers in the country—and its evening counterpart, the *Sun*, across the street. He was managing director and editor-in-chief, known not just for his influence, but for his rare ability to see through pretense. He had no illusions, made no false claims, and trusted only solid merit. His staff respected him deeply, not because he demanded worship, but because he earned it through fairness, insight, and relentless drive.

Yet beneath the polished exterior, there was a darkness—a tension that showed in his brow when pressure mounted. But when relaxed, he was warm, generous, and utterly engaging.

Now, as he listened, his expression remained rigid. After a few minutes of questioning, he spoke again.

“You’re sure that’s all?... And how long has this been known?... Yes, of course, the police are informed—but the servants? Surely it’s all over the place by now.... Well, we’ll have a try.... Look here, Bunner—I’m infinitely grateful for this. You’ve done me a good turn. You know I mean it. Come see me the first day you get back to town.... All right, understood. Now I must act. Goodbye.”

He hung up.

Without hesitation, he grabbed a railway timetable from the rack and flipped through it. A word escaped him—sharp, decisive—as Mr. Silver returned, followed by two others: a hard-faced man with glasses, and a young reporter with bright, alert eyes.

“I want you to jot down some facts, Figgis,” Sir James said, banishing any trace of emotion. “As soon as you have them, shape them into a lead for a special edition of the *Sun*.”

Figgis nodded, pulled out a notebook, and sat at the writing table. The clock read three minutes past three.

“Silver,” Sir James continued, “go tell Jones to wire our local correspondent urgently. Tell him to drop everything and get to Marlstone at once. No explanation in the telegram. Not a word about this until the *Sun* hits the streets—you understand. Williams, cross the street and tell Mr. Anthony to prepare for a two-column front-page story that’ll knock the town endways. Tell him to take every precaution for a scoop. Say Figgis will be over in five minutes with the facts—and that he should let Figgish write it in his private room. On your way, ask Miss Morgan to come here at once. And tell the switchboard to try to reach Mr. Trent on the wire. After seeing Anthony, return here and stand by.”

The youth vanished like a shadow.

Sir James turned to Figgis, who had already begun scribbling shorthand.

“Sigsbee Manderson has been murdered,” he began, pacing slowly with hands behind his back. “He and his wife and two secretaries have been staying at White Gables, near Bishopsbridge, for the past fortnight. He bought the place four years ago. They spend part of each summer there. Last night, he went to bed around half-past eleven—routine. No one knows when he left. He wasn’t missed until this morning. About ten o’clock, a gardener found the body lying beside a shed in the grounds. Shot through the left eye—death instantaneous. No robbery. But there were marks on the wrists—signs of a struggle. Dr. Stock of Marlstone has been called. He’ll conduct the post-mortem. The police from Bishopsbridge arrived quickly but are tight-lipped. They admit they have no clue to the killer’s identity. That’s it, Figgis. Mr. Anthony’s waiting. I’ll call him now.”

Figgis looked up.

“One of Scotland Yard’s best detectives is already on the case,” he offered. “That’s a safe statement.”

“If you like,” Sir James replied.

“And Mrs. Manderson?” Figgis asked. “Was she there?”

“Yes. What about her?”

“Prostrated by shock,” suggested the reporter, “and refuses to see anyone. Human interest.”

“I wouldn’t put that in,” said a quiet voice.

It was Miss Morgan—pale, composed, graceful—who had entered silently during the dictation.

“I’ve seen Mrs. Manderson,” she said, turning to Sir James. “She looks perfectly healthy, quite together. If her husband has been murdered, I doubt the shock would prostrate her. More likely, she’s helping the police.”

“Something in your own style, then, Miss Morgan,” Sir James said, smiling faintly. Her calm efficiency was legendary in the office.

“Cut it out, Figgis. Off you go.”

He turned to her. “Madam, I expect you know what I need.”

“Our Manderson biography is up to date,” she replied, lowering her lashes thoughtfully. “I reviewed it recently. It’s practically ready for tomorrow. I’d suggest the *Sun* use their old sketch from two years ago—the one about his Berlin mission and the potash settlement. It was well-written, and they won’t have room for much more. As for us—we’ve got plenty of cuttings, mostly junk. The sub-editors can sort through them as they come in. We also have two excellent portraits—one a drawing by Mr. Trent, made when they were both on the same ship. It’s superior to any photograph, though I suppose the public prefers a bad photo to a good drawing. I’ll send both down immediately. You can choose. As far as I can see, the *Record* is ahead of the game—except we can’t get a special man down in time for tomorrow’s paper.”

Sir James sighed.

“What are we good for, anyhow?” he asked Mr. Silver, who had resumed his seat.

“She even knows Bradshaw by heart,” he muttered.

Miss Morgan adjusted her cuffs with serene patience.

“Is there anything else?” she asked, as the telephone rang.

“Yes, one thing,” Sir James said, picking up the receiver.

“I want you to make a terrible mistake someday, Miss Morgan—an everlasting bloomer—just to bring us down to earth.”

She gave the faintest hint of a smile before leaving.

“Anthony?” Sir James asked, and plunged into immediate consultation with the editor across the street.

He rarely visited the *Sun* building. He claimed the atmosphere of an evening paper was too chaotic for his taste. Mr. Anthony, the Murat of Fleet Street, would say the same of a morning paper—though he thrived on chaos.

Five minutes later, a uniformed boy entered.

“Mr. Trent is on the line.”

Sir James ended his conversation abruptly.

“Put him through at once.”

He waited a moment, then said:

“Hullo!”

A voice answered: “Hullo be blowed! What do you want?”

“This is Molloy.”

“I know it is,” the voice replied. “This is Trent. I’m in the middle of painting—a critical moment. I hope it’s important.”

“It is,” Sir James said firmly. “I want you to do some work for us.”

“Some play, you mean,” Trent retorted. “Believe me, I don’t want a holiday. The working fit is strong. I’m doing something decent. Why can’t you leave a man alone?”

“Something very serious has happened.”

“What?”

“Sigsbee Manderson has been murdered—shot through the brain. Body found this morning at his estate near Bishopsbridge. They don’t know who did it.”

Sir James relayed the facts briefly and clearly—the same ones he’d given Figgis.

“What do you think of it?” he concluded.

A long silence. Then a grunt.

“Come now,” Sir James pressed. “Tempter!”

“You’ll go down?”

A pause.

“Are you there?” Sir James asked.

“Look here, Molloy,” Trent snapped, “this might be a case for me—or it might not. We can’t tell yet. It could be a mystery—or as simple as bread and cheese. The body wasn’t robbed—that’s interesting. But maybe he caught some tramp sleeping in the grounds and tried to throw him out. That kind of thing would suit him. Such a man might know better than to steal—leave the valuables behind. Honestly, I wouldn’t help hang a poor devil who took daylight into a man like Sig Manderson as a protest.”

Sir James smiled into the phone—a smile of triumph.

“Come, my boy,” he said. “You’re getting feeble. Admit you want to go and look at it. You know you do. If it’s not your kind of case, you’re free to walk away. By the way—where are you?”

“I am blown along a wandering wind,” Trent replied, sounding half-exasperated, half-poetic. “And hollow, hollow, hollow—all delight.”

“Can you get here within an hour?”

“I suppose I can,” he grumbled. “How much time have I?”

“Good man. There’s time enough—that’s just the worst of it. I’ll depend on our local man tonight. The best train left half an hour ago. The next is slow—leaving Paddington at midnight. You could take the Buster”—Sir James referred to his fast motor car—“but you wouldn’t get down in time to do anything tonight.”

“And I’d miss my sleep. No, thanks. Train for me. I’m quite fond of railway travel, you know. I have a gift for it. I am the stoker and the stoked. I am the song the porter sings.”

“What’s that you say?”

“It doesn’t matter,” Trent said sadly. “Say—will your people find a hotel near the scene and telegraph for a room?”

“At once,” Sir James replied. “Come here as soon as you can.”

He replaced the receiver.

As he turned back to his papers, a sudden, shrill cry erupted below.

He walked to the open window.

Sir James smiled and rattled the coins in his pocket.

“It makes a good bill,” he observed to Mr. Silver, standing beside him.

Such was Manderson’s epitaph.
