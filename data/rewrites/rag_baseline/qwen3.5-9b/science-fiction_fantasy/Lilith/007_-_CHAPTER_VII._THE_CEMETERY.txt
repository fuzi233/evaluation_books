The air hit me the moment I crossed the threshold, a sterile chill that smelled less of decay and more of preserved ice. Behind us, the heavy oak door slammed shut with a finality that echoed like a tomb sealing.

"Something," the sexton muttered to his wife, gesturing vaguely. She turned, and the transformation was instantaneous, jarring enough to make my stomach lurch. Where moments ago she had been a mundane caretaker, she now radiated an intensity that bordered on the supernatural. It was as if the sheer magnitude of her purpose had expanded her pupils until they swallowed the darkness, leaving her face illuminated by a brilliance reminiscent of Beatrice within Dante's divine sphere. Life—eternal, unbroken, immortal life—seemed to pour from her pores in visible streams of white fire. Even her hands glowed, the pearl-shell helmets on her fingers shimmering like captured moonstones. Her beauty was overwhelming, a tactical hazard I was relieved to see fade as she turned her gaze away. Yet, the candle she held barely pierced the gloom, casting our initial steps into a void of uncertainty.

We moved forward, and suddenly, the darkness resolved into a specific geometry. Something glimmered slightly above the floor level. Was it a cot? Could any biological organism survive in such sub-zero temperatures without succumbing instantly? Or perhaps the cold itself was the preservative, keeping them suspended in a state of arrested animation. Further ahead, fainter points of luminescence dotted the periphery. Within a dozen paces, we arrived at the first object: a human form draped in a sheet, rigid and motionless. Gender was indeterminate; the light seemed to recoil from the face as we passed, refusing to define it.

Soon, the pattern became undeniable. We were traversing an aisle of cots, stretching endlessly in both directions. Upon nearly every surface lay a figure, head oriented toward the central walkway, shrouded in linens white as fresh snow. My internal monologue silenced, replaced by a primal dread. Aisle after aisle yielded the same tableau. I could only glimpse a fraction of the total population at any given moment, yet they surrounded us, vanishing into an infinity that defied spatial logic. Was this the location of my assigned quarters? Would I be forced to sleep among the unconscious, with no mechanism to rouse me? Was this the sexton's private archive, and were these his texts? This was certainly no halfway house; it was a chamber of the dead.

"One of the cellars I am charged to guard," Mr. Raven whispered, his voice pitched low as if speaking to the silent congregation. "Much vintage is stored here to mature... though it remains perpetually dark for the uninitiated."

"The moon rises," his wife intoned, her voice carrying a resonance of ancient sorrow, as if she were reciting a liturgy long forgotten. "She will arrive shortly."

True to her word, a fissure in the wall revealed the lunar orb. Immediately, a thousand reflections of white light erupted across the room, bouncing off the sheets and the pale visages below. Still, the scale remained incomprehensible. The cots extended outward, seemingly without limit, filling a vast expanse. Along the distant, narrowing corridors, each station stood solitary, occupied by a lone sleeper. Initially, I assumed their state was death, but observation quickly corrected this assessment. Their condition was deeper, more profound—a suspension of a variable I lacked the vocabulary to quantify.

The moon climbed higher, illuminating additional apertures, yet the perspective shifted constantly. The architecture fluctuated between the soaring nave of a cathedral and the utilitarian bulk of a converted granary. The celestial body emitted a coldness surpassing any terrestrial frost, casting a blue-tinged, glacial glare upon the linens and the pallid faces beneath. Perhaps it was the subjects themselves that rendered the moon so frigid. Among the visible figures, there existed a terrifying uniformity in their shared mortality, yet a distinct divergence in the histories etched onto their features.

Here lay a man cut down in the zenith of male vigor. His dark beard cascaded like a meltwater river from the glacier of his frozen expression; his forehead was smooth as polished marble, save for a lingering shadow of agony around his lips—a ghost of pain, not present. Beside him rested the form of a girl, possessing a beauty that transcended earthly standards. The melancholy of departure marred her visage, yet it had not yet dissolved into perfect tranquility. Instead, absolute submission governed her placid features. There were no signs of wasting diseases, no traces of consuming grief or mental anguish. If suffering had existed, it had been charmed into a permanent slumber, never to awaken.

Many were beautiful, lying perfectly still—some appearing to be mere children, though I detected no infants among them. The most striking was an elderly lady, distinguished solely by her white hair, suggesting she had fallen asleep in the twilight of her years. Upon her stately countenance rested not submission, but a noble acquiescence—an assurance as solid as the cosmic foundations that all was proceeding according to design.

On several faces, the scars of conflict remained, albeit faded: the marks of futile losses, the lingering shadows of sorrows deemed inconsolable. The aurora of the great morning had not yet fully erased them. Yet, these instances were rare. Every individual bearing such a brand of pain seemed to offer a silent plea: *Pardon me; I departed only yesterday,* or *Forgive me; I left but a century ago.*

Others, however, had been absent for eons. I recognized this not merely by their utter stillness, but by a qualitative difference I could not name or symbolize.

We eventually halted before three vacant cots. Immediately beyond them lay the form of a woman, slightly past her prime. One arm protruded from the sheet, her palm facing upward. Resting in the center of her hand was a dark stain. Adjacent to her stood the robust figure of a middle-aged man. His arm also extended outward, his powerful fist loosely clenched, gripping the invisible hilt of a blade. He must have been a monarch who died defending a truth.

"Bring the candle closer, wife," the sexton whispered, leaning in to inspect the woman's hand. "It heals remarkably," he murmured to himself. "The nail found in her caused no injury!"

Finally, I summoned the courage to speak. "Are they not deceased?" I asked, my voice soft.

"I cannot answer you," he replied, his tone subdued. "In the old world, the term 'dead' carries a singular definition. If I stated a person was dead, my wife would comprehend one meaning, while you would infer another. This is merely one of my vaults," he continued, gesturing expansively. "My guests are not confined to vaults. Out there on the moor, they lie dense as the foliage of a forest following the first winter storm—or rather, as if the Great White Rose of Heaven had scattered its petals across the landscape. Throughout the night, the moon reads their faces and smiles."

"But why expose them to the corrupting influence of moonlight?" I queried.

"Our moon," he corrected, "is not akin to yours—the old cinder of a burnt-out world. Its beams embalm the dead; they do not corrupt them. Observe: here, I place my deceased directly upon the earth. I bury very few beneath it! In your world, sextons pile massive stones upon them, as if attempting to weigh them down. I await the hour to ring the resurrection bell, to wake those who remain asleep. Your sexton consults the clock to determine when to summon the dead-alive to church; I listen for the rooster on the spire to crow: *Awake, thou that sleepest, and arise from the dead!*"

At this point, I concluded that the self-proclaimed sexton was either a lunatic or a priest of a deranged order. The entire scenario was madness incarnate. Yet, how could I extricate myself? I was paralyzed. In this realm of the dead, the raven and his wife were the sole living entities I had encountered. Whence could I seek aid? I was trapped in a dimension exceeding the limits of imagination. If two objects could occupy the same space here, why not twenty, or ten thousand? But I dared not pursue this line of reasoning further.

"You appear to perceive distinctions among the dead that elude my understanding," I ventured.

"None of those you witness are truly dead yet," he answered. "Some have only just commenced the process of coming alive and dying. Others had begun to die—that is, to come alive—long before they reached us. When such individuals are finally dead, they wake instantly and depart. Nearly every night, some rise and leave. However, I shall say no more, for my words tend only to mislead."

"This is the cot prepared for you," he concluded, indicating one of the three empty stations.

"Why this specific one?" I asked, trembling, desperate to negotiate a delay.

"For reasons you will one day find cause to celebrate," he replied.

"Why not reveal them now?"

"That, too, you will discover when you wake."

"But these are all dead, and I am alive!" I protested, shuddering violently.

"Not entirely," the sexton countered with a smile. "Not nearly enough! Blessed be the true life wherein the intervals between the throbs of the heart constitute non-death."

"The environment is too frigid for sleep!" I declared.
