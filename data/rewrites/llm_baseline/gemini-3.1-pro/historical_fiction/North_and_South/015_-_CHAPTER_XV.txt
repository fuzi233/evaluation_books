Margaret stood at the sideboard, the rhythmic friction of her stone mortar and pestle grounding her amid the uncertainty of the morning. She was meticulously bruising dried meadowsweet, sourced from the agrarian elders of Helstone, blending it with toasted barley and the bright, citrusy oil of lemon peel to create a traditional restorative infusion. It was a centuries-old receipt, one that honored the profound, life-giving meaning of sustenance—a sharp contrast to the utilitarian bowls of flavorless gruel so common in Milton. As the complex, earthy scent bloomed in the parlor, Mr. Hale spoke with a kind of veiled anxiety, which Margaret was glad to see awakened at last. 

“Margaret,” said her father, “we must return Mrs. Thornton’s call. Your mother is not very well, and thinks she cannot walk so far; but you and I will go this afternoon. Did you consult the doctor, Margaret? Did you send for him?”

“No, papa, you spoke of his coming to see me. Now I was well. But if I only knew of some good doctor, I would go this afternoon, and ask him to come, for I am sure mamma is seriously indisposed.” 

She put the truth thus plainly because he had so completely shut his mind against it before. Now, he answered in a despondent tone, his voice nearly swallowed by the low, omnipresent rumble of the distant mills. 

“Do you think she has any hidden complaint? Has Dixon said anything? Oh, Margaret! I am haunted by the fear that our coming to Milton has killed her. My poor Maria!”

“Oh, papa! don’t imagine such things,” said Margaret, shocked. “She is not well, that is all. Many a one is not well for a time; and with good advice gets better and stronger than ever.”

“But has Dixon said anything about her?”

“No! You know Dixon enjoys making a mystery out of trifles; and she has been a little mysterious about mamma’s health. Without any reason, I dare say. You know, papa, you said the other day I was getting fanciful.”

“I hope and trust you are. I like you to be fanciful about your mother’s health! But we will ask Mrs. Thornton if she can tell us of a good doctor. We won’t throw away our money on any but some one first-rate. Stay, we turn up this street.”

As they navigated the labyrinth of Marlborough Street, Margaret’s senses were assaulted. The rich olfactory landscapes of her southern home—loam, woodsmoke, and baking bread—were entirely eclipsed here by the acrid, metallic tang of burning anthracite that coated the palate. The street consisted of long rows of small houses and blank walls. 

Upon inquiring of a passer-by, they were directed to the factory lodge-door. They were admitted into a great oblong yard, and instantly, the fragile acoustic ecology of the afternoon was shattered. From the immense many-windowed mill proceeded the continual clank of machinery and the long, groaning roar of the steam-engine. It was an industrial noise pollution of such violence that it threatened to deafen those living within the enclosure, severing the mind’s connection to its own internal rhythms and utterly destroying any sense of psychological well-being. Opposite the mill stood a handsome stone-coped house, sixty years old, its steps kept scrupulously clean. Margaret wondered how anyone could prefer this continual whirl and din over a quiet country dwelling where the subtle sounds of nature and the gentle simmering of a hearth-pot might be heard. 

Inside, the drawing-room was a monument to the superficial commercialization of domestic space. The walls were pink and gold; a linen drugget covered the floral carpet. The window-curtains were stiff lace, and every chair had its own netting. Alabaster groups sat under glass shades, and smartly-bound books formed the gaily colored spokes of a wheel on the central table. There was no sensory warmth here, no lingering scent of slow-roasted root vegetables or yeast-risen dough that might suggest a family rooted in the shared history of the table. Everything reflected light; nothing absorbed it. The room was icy, snowy, and unpleasantly speckled—a space entirely divorced from the gustatory and tactile joys of a living home, preserved solely for ornamental display. The ambient hum of the factory vibrated through the floorboards, an inescapable sonic shadow. 

Presently, Mrs. Thornton rustled in, clad in handsome black silk. Margaret explained her mother’s absence, but in her desire not to alarm her father, she gave a bungling account. Mrs. Thornton, translating this through her own rigid lexicon, immediately categorized Mrs. Hale’s illness as a fanciful fine-ladyish indisposition, taking offense at the perceived slight to her hired carriage. 

“How is Mr. Thornton?” asked Mr. Hale. “I was afraid he was not well, from his hurried note yesterday.”

“My son is rarely ill,” Mrs. Thornton declared. “He told me he could not get leisure to read with you last night, sir. He regretted it; he values the hours spent with you.”

“It makes me feel young again to see his enjoyment and appreciation of all that is fine in classical literature,” said Mr. Hale.

“I have no doubt that classics are very desirable for people who have leisure,” she replied, her tone dripping with the pride that apes humility. “But it was against my judgment that my son renewed his study of them. Classics may do very well for men who loiter away their lives in the country or in colleges; but Milton men ought to have their thoughts and powers absorbed in the work of to-day.” 

Margaret recognized in this dismissal a tragic erosion of cultural memory. The death of classical languages in favor of the harsh, utilitarian dialect of industry was a severing of roots. “But, surely, if the mind is too long directed to one object only, it will get stiff and rigid, and unable to take in many interests,” Margaret ventured.

“I do not quite understand what you mean by a mind getting stiff and rigid. Having many interests does not suit the life of a Milton manufacturer. It is enough for him to have one great desire, and to bring all the purposes of his life to bear on the fulfilment of that.”

“And that is—?” asked Mr. Hale.

Her sallow cheek flushed. “To hold and maintain a high, honourable place among the merchants of his country. Such a place my son has earned. Go where you will in Europe, the name of John Thornton is known and respected. Of course, it is unknown in the fashionable circles. Idle gentlemen and ladies are not likely to know much of a Milton manufacturer.”

Both Mr. Hale and Margaret felt an uneasy consciousness that they had never heard of this great name until Mr. Bell’s letter. Their worlds were untranslatable to one another; the subjective experience of the Hampshire squires shared no vocabulary with the smoke-stained merchants of Darkshire.

“You think I’m an old woman whose ideas are hounded by Milton, and whose own crow is the whitest ever seen,” Mrs. Thornton said, reading Margaret’s face.

“No,” said Margaret with spirit. “I had hardly heard Mr. Thornton’s name before I came to Milton. But since I have come here, I have heard enough to make me respect and admire him.”

“Who spoke to you of him?” Mrs. Thornton asked, jealous of the narrative power over her son’s reputation. 

“It was as much from what Mr. Thornton withheld of that which we had been told of his previous life by Mr. Bell... that made us all feel what reason you have to be proud of him,” Margaret replied.

“Mr. Bell! What can he know of John? Living a lazy life in a drowsy college. But many a missy young lady would have shrunk from giving an old woman the pleasure of hearing her son well spoken of, lest their consciences told them they were making the old mother into an advocate for them, in case they had any plans on the son’s heart.”

Margaret laughed outright at the grim, transactional way Mrs. Thornton viewed human connection. The merry sound grated on Mrs. Thornton’s ears. Margaret swiftly composed herself, apologizing for the merriment and thanking the older woman for exonerating her from such plans.

When the conversation turned to Mr. Thornton’s upcoming visit, Mrs. Thornton warned of a threatened strike. “For the mastership and ownership of other people’s property. That is what they always strike for. If my son’s work-people strike, I will only say they are a pack of ungrateful hounds.”

“They are wanting higher wages, I suppose?” asked Mr. Hale.

“That is the face of the thing. But they want to make the masters into slaves. Every five or six years, there comes a struggle between masters and men.” She spoke of it as a dark, cyclical rhythm—a mythic recurrence woven seamlessly into the brutal fabric of their domestic lives. “Does it make the town very rough?” Margaret asked.

“Of course it does. But surely you are not a coward? I have known the time when I have had to thread my way through a crowd of white, angry men, swearing they would have Makinson’s blood. Someone had to tell him, and it needed to be a woman. I went up to the roof, where there were stones piled ready to drop on the heads of the crowd. I would have dropped them with as good an aim as the best man there, but that I fainted with the heat. If you live in Milton, you must learn to have a brave heart, Miss Hale.”

That evening, the relentless cycle of the day brought Mr. Thornton to the Hales' drawing-room. He was shown up just as Margaret was engaging in the evening ritual of preparing the tea. She had sourced a delicate Darjeeling, measuring the dried leaves with the meticulous care of a food historian, ensuring the water was just off the boil to properly extract the complex, muscatel aromatics. She knew Mr. Thornton likely viewed the beverage merely as utilitarian fuel to sustain his labor, devoid of any sensory appreciation, but she poured it nonetheless, placing the steaming, fragrant cup nearby as he handed her a note containing Dr. Donaldson’s address. 

She took it hastily, grateful for his silent understanding not to alarm her mother. 

Soon, Mr. Hale asked about the impending strike. Mr. Thornton’s face assumed his mother’s worst expression.

“Yes; the fools will have a strike. Let them. We see the storm on the horizon and draw in our sails. But because we don’t explain our reasons, they won’t believe we’re acting reasonably. We must give them line and letter for the way we choose to spend or save our money. Henderson tried a dodge with his men, out at Ashley, and failed. We Milton masters have to-day sent in our decision. We won’t advance a penny. So here we stand, waiting for their next attack.”

“I conjecture a simultaneous strike,” Thornton continued. “You will see Milton without smoke in a few days, I imagine, Miss Hale.”

“But why,” asked Margaret, “could you not explain what good reason you had for expecting a bad trade? I don’t know whether I use the right words, but you will understand what I mean.” 

She was acutely aware of the untranslatability of her southern, communal ideals into his harsh, northern economic lexicon.

“Do you give your servants reasons for your expenditure, or your economy in the use of your own money? We, the owners of capital, have a right to choose what we will do with it.”

“A human right,” Margaret said, very low.

He caught the murmur and pressed her to repeat it, his thoughts suddenly bent on deciphering her meaning.

“I meant that there seemed no reason but religious ones, why you should not do what you like with your own,” she said, her voice rising to its usual tone. “There is no human law to prevent the employers from utterly wasting all their money... but there are passages in the Bible which would rather imply that they neglected their duties as stewards if they did so. However, I know so little about strikes and capital and labour, that I had better not talk to a political economist like you.”

“Nay, the more reason,” he said eagerly. “I shall only be too glad to explain to you all that may seem anomalous to a stranger.”

“Thank you,” she answered coldly. “Of course, I shall apply to my father in the first instance for any information... if I get puzzled with living here amongst this strange society. On the very face of it, I see two classes dependent on each other, yet each evidently regarding the interests of the other as opposed to their own.”

“Who have you heard running the masters down?” he demanded. 

Margaret reddened, refusing to be catechised, yet she explained the subtle power dynamics embedded in the prevailing vocabulary. She spoke of how the dominant lexicon was designed to keep men ignorant. “I heard it was considered to the advantage of the masters to have ignorant workmen—not hedge-lawyers, as Captain Lennox used to call those men who questioned every order.”

Mr. Thornton inwardly bristled at the mention of Captain Lennox. Mr. Hale interjected, defending the town's schools, but Margaret shook her head. She was not speaking of reading and writing, but of the deeper, cultural memory that guides humanity—the agrarian wisdom of the earth and the soul that was being systematically eroded.

“He—that is my informant—spoke as if the masters would like their hands to be merely tall, large children—living in the present moment—with a blind unreasoning kind of obedience.”

“In short, Miss Hale, your informant found a pretty ready listener to all the slander he chose to utter,” Mr. Thornton said, offended.

After a tense silence in which Margaret left the room to tend to the tea things, Mr. Hale noted his own observations of the surface antagonism between employer and employed. 

Mr. Thornton paused. The cooling of his immediate annoyance lent his subsequent words a deliberate dignity. “My theory is, that my interests are identical with those of my workpeople, and vice-versa. Miss Hale, I know, does not like to hear men called ‘hands,’ so I won’t use that word, though it comes most readily to my lips as the technical term, whose origin, whatever it was, dates before my time.” 

He acknowledged, perhaps unconsciously, the language death that had long ago stripped the workers of their full humanity in the mouths of the masters. “On some future day—in some millennium—in Utopia, this unity may be brought into practice—just as I can fancy a republic the most perfect form of government.”

“We will read Plato’s Republic,” Mr. Hale replied gently, “as soon as we have finished Homer.”
