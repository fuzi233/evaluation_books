“The spirits of your fathers
Shall start from every wave;
For the deck it was their field of fame,
And ocean was their grave.”

The room in Plymouth where Mistress Hawkins sat of an evening had a smell that clung like a memory to oak: salt dried into woollen cloaks; tar that never quite left a sailor’s hands; beeswax from the panels; and, underneath it all, the faint metallic tang that rose whenever Master Adrian Gilbert opened his little packet of earths and stones and set them near the hearth to show her what he loved best—proof held in the palm.

He had been speaking long, as men do when they believe the world will yield at last to a clever fire and a finer word. His face, fair and thoughtful, was sharpened by furnace-light even when no furnace burned; his eyes had that weary redness of one who has watched a crucible too often and trusted too much to what might be coaxed out of dust.

“So you see, my dear Mistress Hawkins,” he said, and took up again the lump he had set upon her table as if it were a loaf whose crumb might be read, “having the silver—luna itself, as your own eyes show you—beside the ores of lead, manganese, and copper; and above all this gossan, as the Cornish call it, which I hold not merely the matrix of ore, but the very crude form and materia prima of all metals—you mark me? If my recipes, which I had from Doctor Dee, succeed only half so well as I expect, then I refine out the luna, lay it by, and transmute the remaining ores into sol, into gold. Whereupon Peru and Mexico become superfluities, and England the mistress of the globe. Strange, no doubt; distant, no doubt: but possible, my dear madam—possible!”

Mistress Hawkins, who had listened with the patience of one who had fed too many hungry mouths to be dazzled by promises of plenty, shook her head. Her hands, not old but worn, were folded close, as if she kept them from trembling.

“And what good to you if it be, Master Gilbert?” she answered softly. “If you could find a stone to turn sinners into saints, now—yet naught save God’s grace can do that; and that last seems ofttimes over long in coming.”

She sighed; and the sound was like the tide when it draws away from a shore and leaves more exposed than one wishes to see.

“But indeed, my dear madam, conceive now—the Comb Martin mine thus becomes a gold mine, perhaps inexhaustible; yields me wherewithal to carry out my North-West patent; meanwhile my brother Humphrey holds Newfoundland, and builds me fresh ships year by year—for the forests of pine are boundless—for my China voyage.”

“Sir Humphrey has better thoughts in his dear heart than gold, Master Adrian,” she said, with a steady look that did not flatter. “A very close and gracious walker he has been this seven year. I wish my Captain John were so too.”

Gilbert’s mouth tightened; ambition and conscience struggled there like two wrestlers in a narrow lane.

“And how do you know I have naught better in my mind’s eye than gold? Or, indeed, what better could I have? Is not gold the Spaniard’s strength—the very mainspring of Antichrist? By gold only, therefore, can we out-wrestle him. You shake your head; but say, dear madam—for gold England must have—which is better: to make gold bloodlessly at home, or take it bloodily abroad?”

“Oh, Master Gilbert, Master Gilbert!” She lifted her eyes, and there was fear in them not for herself but for the shape her town had taken. “Is it not written that those who make haste to be rich pierce themselves through with many sorrows? God’s blessing is not on it all.”

“Not on you, madam? Be sure brave Captain John Hawkins’s star told me a different tale, when I cast his nativity.—Born under stormy planets, truly, but under right royal and fortunate ones.”

“Ay,” she answered, “I am a simple body, and you a great philosopher; but I hold there is no star for the seaman like the Star of Bethlehem; and that goes with ‘peace on earth and good will to men,’ and not with such arms as that, Master Adrian. I can’t abide to look upon them.”

Her finger pointed up to one of the carved bosses in the ribbed oak roof, where the fatal crest shone with gilt and paint—a half-Moor, bound. The gold leaf caught the candle, and for an instant it seemed to move, as if the bound man breathed.

“Ah, Master Gilbert!” Her voice thickened, as if words had to pass through brine. “Since first he went to Guinea after those poor negroes, little lightness has my heart known; and the very day that crest was set up in our grand new house, as the parson read the first lesson, there was this text in it: ‘Woe to him that buildeth his house by iniquity, and his chambers by wrong. Shalt thou live because thou closest thyself in cedar?’ And it went into my ears like fire, and into my heart like lead. When the parson went on, ‘Did not thy father eat and drink, and do judgment and justice? then it was well with him,’ I thought of good old Captain Will; and—I tell you, Master Gilbert, those negroes are on my soul from morning until night! We are all mighty grand now, and money comes in fast, but the Lord will require the blood of them at our hands yet. He will!”

The room itself seemed to answer her with its trophies. On the sideboard were Spanish cups and salvers that had once held sweet wine and now held nothing but reflected candle-flame. Strange birds, dried skins, charts with ragged coasts scratched in ink, rough drawings of bays and bars: all the world brought home and nailed, as it were, to a Devon wall. Over the fireplace hung old Captain Will Hawkins’s portrait—ruddy, bold, and of an age when men could be fierce and yet feel simpler in their fierceness. Beside it, the Spanish ensign taken at Rio de la Hacha drooped in folds that smelled faintly of smoke even after fifteen years.

Gilbert, who could talk boldly of nations and metals, was a little abashed before a woman’s plain terror of judgment.

“My dearest madam,” he said at last, “who can prosper more than you? If your husband copied the Dons too closely once or twice in that matter—which I do not deny—was he not punished at once when he lost ships, men, all but life, at St. Juan de Ulloa?”

“Ay, yes,” she answered; “and that did give me a bit of comfort, especially when the Queen—God save her tender heart—was so sharp with him for pity of the poor wretches; but it has not mended him. He is growing fast like the rest now, Master Gilbert—greedy to win, and niggardly to spend (God forgive him!), and always fretting and plotting for some new gain, and envying and grudging at Drake, and all who are deeper in the snare of prosperity than he is. Gold, gold, nothing but gold in every mouth—there it is!”

Her tone rose, not in anger but in grief, like a kettle beginning to sing because the fire will not be denied.

“Ah! I mind when Plymouth was a quiet little God-fearing place as God could smile upon. But ever since my John, and Sir Francis, and poor Master Oxenham found out the way to the Indies, it’s been a sad place. Not a sailor’s wife but is crying ‘Give, give,’ like the daughters of the horse-leech; and every woman must drive her husband out across seas to bring her home money to squander on hoods and farthingales, and go mincing with outstretched necks and wanton eyes; and they will soon learn to do worse than that, for the sake of gain. The Lord’s hand will be against their tires and crisping-pins, their mufflers and farthingales, as it was against the Jews of old. Ah, dear me!”

As she spoke, the house’s sounds laid their own music under her words: the low hiss of a settling log; the faint creak of timber; outside, from the street, the distant clink of a harness chain and the thin, uneasy laughter of men loosed from duty. Plymouth’s harbour had its own voice too—water slapping stone, a stray gull, the hoarse call of a watch—sounds that could soothe when they were honest and measured, and could gnaw at the mind when they multiplied into shouts and hammering and the restless din of profit. Mistress Hawkins heard it all as if it were a psalm turned sour.

Gilbert sat looking at her a while in silence. He was not a hard man; he was rather one whose head ran before his heart could follow, and whose heart then blamed itself for its own hunger.

“The Lord be with you, dearest lady,” he said at last. “Strange, how you women sit at home to love and suffer, while we men rush forth to break our hearts and yours against rocks of our own seeking! Ah well! were it not for Scripture, I should have thought that Adam, rather than Eve, had been the one who plucked the fruit of the forbidden tree.”

“We women, I fear, did the deed nevertheless,” she said; “for we bear the doom of it our lives long.”

“You always remind me, madam, of my dear Mistress Leigh of Burrough, and her counsels.”

“Do you see her often? I hear of her as one of the Lord’s most precious vessels.”

“I would have done more ere now than see her,” he answered, and coloured like a boy at his own confession, “had she allowed me. But she lives only for the memory of her husband and the fame of her noble sons.”

Even as he spoke, the door opened; and a gust of night-air came in with a smell of wet rope and cold sea. A man stepped across the threshold wrapped in a rough sea-gown, as if the very spray had stitched it to him.

Adrian Gilbert turned pale.

“Amyas Leigh! What brings you hither? How fares my brother? Where is the ship?”

“The Golden Hind is gone on to Dartmouth, with Master Hayes,” Amyas said. His voice was hoarse with wind and want of sleep. “Your brother is well, Master Gilbert. I came ashore here, meaning to go north to Bideford ere I went to London. I called at Drake’s just now, but he was away.”

“The Golden Hind?” Mistress Hawkins cried, astonished into warmth. “Yet welcome ever, sir. This is a great surprise, though. Captain John did not look for you till next year.”

Amyas did not answer. He stood as a man stands who has carried a weight too long and is frightened to set it down lest it crush someone else.

“Something is wrong!” Adrian’s voice sharpened. “Speak!”

Amyas tried, but could not. The silence thudded like a sail flogging.

“Will you drive a man mad, sir?” Adrian started up, and in a desperate tenderness held the candle close to Amyas’s face as if light could force truth to show itself. “Has the adventure failed? You said my brother was well.”

“He is well,” Amyas whispered.

“Then what—Why do you look at me in that fashion, sir?”

Amyas’s lip quivered. He laid his hand on Adrian’s shoulder; the touch was heavy with pity.

“Your great and glorious brother, sir, is better bestowed than in settling Newfoundland.”

“Dead?” Adrian shrieked, and the word seemed to strike the carved ceiling and fall back shattered. “Dead?”

“He is with the God whom he served.”

“He was always with Him, like Enoch. Parable me no parables, if you love me, sir!”

“And, like Enoch, he was not; for God took him.”

Adrian clasped his hands over his forehead and leaned against the table as if he would hold his skull together.

“Go on,” he said at last, very low. “Go on. God will give me strength to hear all.”

Then Amyas spoke; and while he did, the room filled with another world—cold seas and unruly men, the snap of canvas, the groan of hulls, and in between the ghastly cheer men make when they are afraid and will not confess it. He told of the ruffian crews caught up at hazard; of mutinies and conspiracies to carry off ships; of plundering fishing vessels as if hunger excused theft; of desertions multiplying daily; of licenses granted by the general to the lazy and fearful to return home. When Amyas reached that, Adrian groaned aloud.

“From him? Conspired against him? Deserted from him? Dotards, buzzards! Where would they have found such another leader?”

“Your illustrious brother, sir,” Amyas answered with soldierly respect and a sailor’s bluntness, “if you will pardon me, was a very great philosopher, but not so much of a general.”

“General, sir? Where was braver man?”

“Not on God’s earth, but that does not make a general, sir. If Cortés had been brave and no more, Mexico would have been Mexico still. The truth is, sir, Cortés, like my Captain Drake, knew when to hang a man; and your great brother did not.”

It was said without malice; only the hard truth of command, which is as unpalatable as unsalted bread and yet as necessary.

Amyas went on. He told how they set sail from St. John’s to discover the southward coast; and how Sir Humphrey, chivalrous to his own hurt, chose to go in the little Squirrel of ten tons, “overcharged with nettings, fights, and small ordnance,” not only because she could nose into creeks, but because he had heard a taunt among the men that he feared the sea. That small pride, that large courage—both were of the same Devon blood, and both carried their own doom.

Then came woe upon woe. Seven days after they left Cape Raz, their biggest ship, the Delight, made a last brave revel, as if men could drum and pipe death away. “Most part of the night,” Amyas said, quoting the words of Hayes, “like the swan that singeth before her death, continued in sounding of trumpets, drums, and fifes; also winding of the cornets and hautboys; and, in the end of their jollity, left off with the battle and doleful knells.” Next day she struck—Golden Hind and Squirrel sheering off just in time—upon unknown shoals. All but fourteen were lost; and among them Budaeus, Frank’s learned friend, swallowed up as quietly as if the sea had always owned him.

Those who escaped, after cold and famine and the gnawing misery of wet clothes that would not dry, were cast on shore again in Newfoundland; and at last, worn out with hunger and want of garments, the crews of the two remaining ships persuaded Sir Humphrey to turn for England on the thirty-first of August.

“And on that very instant,” Amyas continued, “even in winding about, we beheld close alongside—so Master Hayes writ it—a very lion in shape, hair, and colour; not swimming, but sliding on the water, with his whole body; who passed along, turning his head to and fro, yawning and gaping wide, with ugly demonstration of long teeth and glaring eyes; and to bid us farewell—coming right against the Hind—he sent forth a horrible voice, roaring or bellowing as doth a lion.”

Adrian’s face tightened as if he tasted iron.

“And the devil it was, doubtless,” he said; “the roaring lion who goes about seeking whom he may devour.”

“He has not got your brother, at least,” Amyas answered.

“No,” Mistress Hawkins said through her tears; and there was no scoffing in her, for those were days when the unseen pressed close upon the seen. “He roared for joy to think how many poor souls would be left still in heathen darkness by Sir Humphrey’s death. God be with that good knight, and send all mariners where he is now!”

Amyas’s voice sank. The room’s small noises—coal shifting, candle-wick ticking—seemed suddenly too loud against the wide silence of what he told.

“When we were off the Azores,” he said, “the storms came heavier than ever, with terrible seas breaking short and pyramid-wise. On the ninth of September the Squirrel nearly foundered and yet recovered; and the general, sitting abaft with a book in his hand, cried out to us in the Hind so oft as we did approach within hearing: ‘We are as near heaven by sea as by land,’ reiterating the same speech—well beseeming a soldier resolute in Jesus Christ, as I can testify he was.

“The same Monday, about twelve of the clock, or not long after, the frigate being ahead of us in the Golden Hind, suddenly her lights were out; and withal our watch cried, the general was cast away, which was true; for in that moment the frigate was devoured and swallowed up of the sea.”

So it ended. Not with trumpet, nor with any grand last word heard by the land, but with a light snuffed as easily as a candle in a draught.

“Oh, my brother! my brother!” Adrian moaned, and the scholar’s grief in him broke into the boy’s. “The glory of his house, the glory of Devon!”

“Ah! what will the Queen say?” Mistress Hawkins whispered.

“Tell me,” Adrian asked, mastering himself by sheer will, “had he the jewel on when he died?”

“The Queen’s jewel? He always wore that,” Amyas answered, “and his own posy too—Mutare vel timere sperno. He wore it; and he lived it.”

“Ay,” Adrian said, staring as if he could see the little ring glinting in the black water, “the same to the last.”

“Not quite the same,” Amyas said. “He was a meeker man latterly than he used to be. As he said himself once, a better refiner than any whom he had on board had followed him close all the seas over, and purified him in the fire. And gold seven times tried he was, when God, having done His work in him, took him home at last.”

After that there were no more words worth spending. The venture had failed utterly; Adrian was ruined; and Amyas had lost his own stake with the rest. Adrian rose at last, pale and stiff, and begged leave to retire; he must collect himself.

“Poor gentleman,” Mistress Hawkins said when he was gone. “It is little else he has left to collect.”

“Or I either,” Amyas answered, with a bitter breath that tried to be a laugh and could not. “I was going to ask you to lend me one of your son’s shirts, and five pounds to get myself and my men home.”

“Five? Fifty, Captain Leigh!” she cried, as if the very notion stung her pride. “God forbid that John Hawkins’s wife should refuse her last penny to a distressed mariner, and he a gentleman born. But you must eat and drink.”

“It’s more than I have done for many a day worth speaking of,” Amyas said.

So she set food before him—plain Plymouth plenty, yet to a man fresh off starvation it might as well have been a banquet laid by angels. There was a great manchet loaf, its crust browned close and crackling; a dish of salt beef warmed through with onions so their sweetness rose up and took hold of the air; a pottage thickened with pease and a knob of butter melted in, making a glossy pool; and a sharp little cheese that smelled of the cow and the clean straw. She poured him ale—dark, strong, and honest—its head standing like foam on a wave; and the sound of it, glugging into the cup, was comfort in itself, as if the house spoke: here is shore, here is hearth, here is something that will not sink.

As he ate, the room softened. Even the hard trophies on the walls seemed less like stolen splendours and more like burdensome heirlooms. Mistress Hawkins sat opposite, and while he tore bread with hands still roughened by rope, she told him what she knew of his mother—Mistress Leigh—whom Adrian Gilbert had seen a few months before in London. The names and places were like familiar herbs stirred into broth, restoring some taste of home.

And then, naturally, she turned to news from Bideford.

“And by the by, Captain Leigh,” she said, lowering her voice as if the very boards might carry tales, “I’ve sad news for you from your place; and I had it from one who was there at the time. You must know a Spanish captain, a prisoner—”

“What, the one I sent home from Smerwick?” Amyas asked, and his spoon paused.

“You sent? Mercy on us! Then perhaps you’ve heard—”

“How can I have heard? What?”

“That he’s gone off, the villain.”

“Without paying his ransom?”

“I can’t say that; but there’s a poor innocent young maid gone off with him—one Salterne’s daughter—the Popish serpent!”

Amyas went white under his weather-tan.

“Rose Salterne,” he said, as if the name were a cut, “the Mayor’s daughter—the Rose of Torridge!”

“That’s her.” Mistress Hawkins half rose in alarm, for he had fallen back in his chair as if shot through. “Bless your dear soul, what ails you?”

He forced his breath to steady, ashamed of his weakness and yet not master of it.

“You’ll forgive me, madam,” he said, striving to smile. “I’m weak from the sea; and your good ale has turned me a bit dizzy, I think.”

“Ay, yes—’tis too heavy till you’ve been on shore a while. Try the aqua vitae; my Captain John has it right good; and a bit too fond of it too, poor dear soul, between whiles—Heaven forgive him!”

And before he could refuse she poured him strong brandy cut with water and made him drink it down. The spirit burned a path to his stomach and left heat in his throat, but it did not burn away the image that had risen in him at her words: Rose—warm and living—gone into the cold mouth of a foreign tongue, into a world where even love would be named in another lexicon, and her own Devon speech might be mocked or silenced. He lay down when she sent him, as obedient as a child, but sleep would not come. The house’s gentle night-sounds—distant harbour-calls, the soft settling of beams, the mutter of a city dreaming of gain—wrapped him in a net that held his body and left his mind beating against its knots.

When grey morning came at last, he rose with eyes that felt full of sand, took what Mistress Hawkins gave—money for the road, a clean shirt for shame’s sake, and kindness that asked no payment—and set his face toward Bideford.
