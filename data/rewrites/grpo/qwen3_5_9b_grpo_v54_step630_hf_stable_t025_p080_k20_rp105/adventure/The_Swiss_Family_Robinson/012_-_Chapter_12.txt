The greatest peril we had faced thus far had finally passed, yet a shadow of anxiety lingered in my heart. I feared that another serpent, unseen by us, might have slipped into the swamp or emerged from the lands beyond Falconhurst, just as the previous one had. Determined to ensure our safety, I planned two expeditions: first, a thorough inspection of the marsh and thicket, and second, an immediate journey to the Gap, the only route through which such a creature could have invaded our domain.

Summoning my sons to join me in the marsh, I found neither Ernest nor Jack eager to comply. Jack, in particular, shivered at the memory of the snake's tail smashing his ribs, but I refused to yield to their fear. We proceeded, laying down planks and wicker hurdles to cross the treacherous ground. Our search yielded nothing but flattened reeds and the tracks of the beast's lair. Emerging onto firm ground near the cliff wall, we discovered a sparkling brook flowing from a large cave. Inside, stalactites formed stately pillars, and the floor was covered in fine, soapy fuller's earth—a discovery that promised to spare us the labor of soap-making.

Fritz ventured deeper into the cave to trace the stream, shouting for me to follow. Leaving the younger boys behind, I fired a pistol to gauge the cavern's vastness and lit my candle. The light burned steadily, though faint in the immense space. Suddenly, Fritz exclaimed that the walls glowed like salt crystals, but I corrected him, noting the water left no track and tasted sweet. "Rock crystal!" I declared. While Fritz lamented that the crystals were dull and opaque, breaking them carelessly, I explained their natural pyramidal form. After securing a perfect specimen, we departed, leaving Fritz to fire a final shot into the echoing dark.

Outside, we found Jack sobbing, believing us lost amidst the crashing rocks. Comforting him, we learned that Ernest had remained in the reeds. There, the boy proudly displayed a large eel he had slain, explaining his courage in facing the beast rather than fleeing. I commended his bravery, noting the eel's tenacity, and we returned to find my wife delighted at our safe return and the supply of fuller's earth. That evening, we recounted our adventures, including the discovery of the crystal cavern, and prepared for the greater undertaking: a search of the country beyond the river.

We packed the cart with provisions, tools, and firearms, drawing it with Storm and Grumble. Jack and Franz drove, my wife rode comfortably, Fritz led the way, and Ernest and I walked, flanked by the dogs and Fangs. Our journey to Woodlands revealed clear signs of the serpent's approach, but the farm itself was peaceful and flourishing. The following day, we surveyed the neighborhood, collecting cotton for pillows. Franz, entrusted with his first gun, accompanied me along the lake's edge. Though the dogs flushed numerous birds, Franz missed them all until a booming sound startled us. Mistaking it for Swift the onager, I identified it as the cry of a bittern. Eager to shoot this rare bird, Franz fired into the thicket and, to my surprise, reported hitting a wild pig.

Upon inspection, I realized it was not a hog but a capybara, a South American water-loving animal. Overjoyed at having shot a "new creature," Franz proposed cleaning the carcass to make it lighter. The dog Bruno willingly carried the load, and we returned to Woodlands. There, we found Ernest surrounded by dead rats. He explained that Knips the monkey had triggered a colony of "beaver rats" living in mud tunnels, leading to a chaotic battle where Juno the dog helped drive them off. I identified the animals as muskrats, noting their webbed feet and musk glands, and we collected their skins for caps.

Later, Fritz and Jack returned from their patrol, reporting no serpents but bringing eggs and heath fowl. Supper was interrupted by Franz's eagerness to taste his capybara, which he found peculiarly flavored by its musk. I explained the nature of musk, comparing it to the civet cat, whose scent was harvested from a gland near its tail. As the evening wore on, Jack brought in pale green apples, fearing they were poisonous like the manchineel. Knips, however, snatched one and ate it with delight, prompting a scramble among the boys. Tired from the day's exertions, we retired to our mattresses.

The next morning, we journeyed to a makeshift arbor between the sugar-brake and the Gap, using a sailcloth for shelter. Searching the cane brake for the boa constrictor, we found no trace. Refreshing ourselves with fresh cane juice, we were suddenly alerted by barking dogs and rustling canes. A herd of grey peccaries, showing sharp tusks, burst forth. My double-barrel gun felled two, and the others fled in single file. I immediately ordered the removal of their odoriferous glands to prevent the meat from spoiling. While Jack fetched the cart, I cleaned the animals. Ernest reported that the procession had passed near the hut, where he and Juno had secured three more.

We loaded the cart, adorned it with flowers, and marched triumphantly back to the hut. After dinner, we began processing the peccaries, singeing the bristles and salting the meat. The boys, eager to experiment, dug a pit to cook the smallest pig in the Otaheitean fashion. Fritz stuffed it with potatoes, onions, and herbs, wrapping it in large leaves before burying it in hot ashes and earth. The result was a delicious roast, seasoned by the native "ravensara" leaves, which combined the scents of nutmeg, clove, and cinnamon.

During the days spent curing our hams, we roamed the neighborhood, finding no serpents but acquiring giant bamboos suitable for casks and pots. However, our visit to Prospect Hill was marred by the mischief of vagabond apes, who had scattered the poultry and torn the cottage. We left the disorder to be rectified later and continued our march to the Gap. Finding the barricades broken down, we realized the snake and the peccaries had already passed through. Resolving to fortify the defile, we emerged into a wide, varied landscape of hills and plains. Crossing the East River, we found the soil increasingly arid, resembling a desert. The boys groaned at the barrenness, but I encouraged them, pointing to the distant mountains where we hoped to find water and refuge.

As we rested, Knips sniffed the air and led the dogs up a glen to a pool of water. Meanwhile, Fritz spotted what he believed to be horsemen. Through the spy-glass, we confirmed they were actually large ostriches. I proposed capturing one for its feathers. As the birds approached, playing and varying their pace, I noted the male's striking white plumes against its black body. Realizing a direct chase was futile, I waited for the right moment. When the dogs, unable to restrain their impatience, rushed forward, the ostriches took flight. Just as they were escaping, I unhooded Fritz's eagle. The falcon made a fatal swoop, piercing the male's skull. Grieving the loss of a live mount, we admired the magnificent feathers.

Ernest inquired how ostriches survived in such deserts. I explained that wild melons scattered by Providence sustained them. Suddenly, Jack shouted about a nest of ostrich eggs. We found more than twenty, each weighing about three pounds. Unable to carry them all, we set a landmark and used a bent elastic stick to suspend two eggs on Jack's shoulder, allowing him to carry them like a dairy maid.

Continuing our journey, we rested by a marshy pool where we found tracks of buffaloes and antelopes, but no serpents. Here, Jack discovered a small mud-tortoise, a useful garden pest. Soon after, we entered a verdant valley we named Glen Verdant, where herds of antelopes grazed. Leaving the valley, we found ourselves near the Jackal Cave. Suddenly, a cry of terror erupted as Ernest ran back, screaming about a bear. Two enormous bears appeared, and the dogs charged. My sons and I fired, wounding both beasts, but the dogs' interference made precise shooting impossible. Seizing the opportunity, we advanced with pistols and fired simultaneously, killing both bears—one through the head, the other through the heart.

Relieved, we dragged the carcasses into their den to hide them from scavengers. By sunset, we rejoined my wife and Franz, who had discovered a bed of pure white clay suitable for porcelain and built a hearth. The next morning, I tested the clay, finding it baked hard and glazed, though slightly melted. We then visited the bear's den, where Fritz spotted a flock of wild turkeys attending the bears' "funeral." A large bird, the "Watcher," guarded the entrance. As the turkeys pressed in, a mighty rush of wings signaled an attack. Fritz fired, killing an enormous bird that fell head-first, breaking its neck. We rushed in to find the bears' tongues and eyes eaten, but the skins largely intact. The large bird was a condor, the mate of the Watcher.

We skinned the bears, cutting magnificent hams and preserving the paws for delicacies. The bones and offal were cleared by the birds and insects, leaving clean skulls for our museum. The skins required careful scraping and drying over two days. Lamenting the distance from the ravensara tree, I discovered a climbing pepper plant nearby. We gathered the berries, drying the red ones in salt water and the green ones in the sun, securing a supply of black and white pepper for the future. We also collected young plants to grow at Rockburg.

With the meat smoking and the peppers drying, the boys grew restless. I granted them permission to explore the savannah alone, assigning Fritz as their leader to foster independence. Armed and mounted on cattle, they departed with a hearty blessing. We remained behind, tending to the curing meat and investigating the cave's interior. I found the walls composed of talc mixed with asbestos and mica, successfully splitting a block into clear, transparent sheets perfect for window panes. My wife was delighted with this substitute for glass, declaring the lack thereof had been a constant trouble. Thus, our day of exploration concluded with practical discoveries and preparations for the challenges ahead.
