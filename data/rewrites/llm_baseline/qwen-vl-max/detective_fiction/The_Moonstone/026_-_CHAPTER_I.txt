I owe much to my dear parents—both now at rest in heaven—for instilling in me, from the earliest years of my life, a deep sense of order and routine. Their influence shaped not only my habits but my character.

In those innocent days, I was taught to keep my hair neatly combed at all times, day or night, and to fold every item of clothing with care—always in the same sequence, always on the same chair, always placed at the foot of my bed before retiring. Before sleep came, I would record the events of the day in my small diary. Then followed the “Evening Hymn,” recited softly in bed. And after that, peaceful slumber would take hold—sweet, untroubled, and complete.

But time changes things. The hymn has long since been replaced by bitter thoughts; the restful sleep by restless nights haunted by anxiety. Yet two habits remain: folding my clothes, and writing in my diary. The first is a quiet link to the childhood I once knew—before fortune turned against my father. The second, once useful only for self-discipline, has unexpectedly become vital in another way. It has allowed me, in humble service, to assist Mr. Franklin Blake—a wealthy relative through marriage, though not one whose spiritual worth I can claim.

For some time now, I’ve lived apart from the family circle, cut off from news and connection. Poverty and isolation often lead to silence. I reside now in a quiet town in Brittany, among a small group of devout English expatriates. Here, we enjoy the comfort of a Protestant clergyman and the modest prices of a local market. It is a retreat—like Patmos surrounded by a roaring sea of Catholicism—and it has been a place of peace… until recently.

A letter arrived from England. My name, once forgotten, was suddenly remembered. Mr. Franklin Blake—my wealthy cousin, though not one I would call spiritually rich—writes plainly, without pretense, that he needs something from me. He has decided to revisit the disgraceful affair of the Moonstone, and he wants me to recount what I witnessed during my visit to Aunt Verinder’s home in London. Payment is offered, though the tone is cold and indifferent—the kind of generosity that forgets kindness in favor of convenience. I am to reopen old wounds, recall painful memories, and then accept his cheque as compensation. It was hard. My pride rebelled. But humility won. I accepted the money—not because I needed it, but because I could not refuse the duty laid upon me.

Without my diary, I doubt I could have done justice to this task. With it, I can speak honestly, even if the truth is uncomfortable. Nothing escaped my notice during that visit. Every detail was recorded as it happened, thanks to the discipline of my youth. And so, I shall tell it all—every word, every gesture, every shadow of suspicion. My regard for truth stands above all else. If Mr. Blake finds parts of this account unflattering, he may choose to suppress them. He has bought my time—but not my conscience.

According to my diary, I happened to pass Aunt Verinder’s house in Montagu Square on Monday, July 3rd, 1848.

Seeing the shutters open and the blinds raised, I felt it polite to knock and inquire after her health. A young woman answered—no more than a servant, yet one who carried herself with an air of insolence. She informed me that my aunt and her daughter (whom I cannot, in truth, call my cousin) had returned from the country a week earlier and intended to stay in London. I sent up a message, declining to disturb them, and merely asking if I might be of service.

She took the note in silence, then left me standing in the hall. That girl is the daughter of Betteredge—an old man of questionable faith, long tolerated in Aunt Verinder’s household. I sat down to wait, pulling from my bag a tract. I chose one that seemed fitting for her: “A Word With You On Your Cap-Ribbons,” a piece aimed at young women on the moral dangers of fashion. Though the hall was dirty and the chair hard, I found comfort in offering good where evil had been met.

“Mrs. Verinder is grateful,” she said when she returned. “She asks you to lunch tomorrow at two.”

I ignored her tone and her bold stare. I thanked her, then said gently, “Would you allow me to offer you this tract?”

She glanced at the title. “Is it written by a man or a woman?”
“If it’s by a woman, I’d rather not read it. If it’s by a man, I must tell him—he knows nothing about it.”

She handed the tract back and opened the door. I waited till it closed behind her, then slipped the pamphlet into the letterbox. Later, I dropped another through the railings. A small act, perhaps—but it eased my conscience.

That evening, I attended a meeting of the Select Committee of the Mothers’-Small-Clothes-Conversion-Society. The society’s purpose is well known: to rescue fathers’ trousers from pawnshops and prevent their return by altering them to fit children—thus protecting the innocent from the sins of the unredeemed. I was a member at the time, and among us was my dear friend, Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite, a man of great energy and virtue. I expected to see him that night and planned to mention Aunt Verinder’s arrival in London. But he did not come.

When I voiced my surprise, the other members looked up—some still examining trousers—then asked in astonishment if I hadn’t heard the news. I confessed I had not. Then they told me of an event that marks the beginning of this story.

On Friday, June 30th, 1848, two men—one from Lambeth, the other from Kilburn—were victims of a shocking assault that shook London. One was Mr. Septimus Luker. The other was Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

Now, living in exile, I cannot include newspaper reports. I also missed the chance to hear the full account from Mr. Ablewhite himself. All I know comes from what was told to me that evening—recorded here with the precision I learned in childhood. Everything in its place. Everything properly set.

The date—thank God for my parents’ training—is clear: Friday, June 30th, 1848.

Early that morning, Mr. Godfrey was cashing a cheque at a bank in Lombard Street. The firm’s name is blurred in my diary, but I will not guess. It matters little. What does matter is what happened afterward.

As he left, he met a stranger—also leaving at the same moment. A brief exchange of courtesies followed. The stranger insisted Mr. Godfrey go first. They bowed and parted.

Some might call this trivial. But let us not judge lightly. Small things often lead to great consequences.

We learn now that the stranger was Mr. Luker. We follow Mr. Godfrey home to Kilburn.

There, a thin, poorly dressed boy handed him a letter. He said only that an old woman had given it to him, with no instructions to wait. Such things were common in Mr. Godfrey’s charitable work. He dismissed the boy and opened the letter.

The handwriting was unfamiliar. It requested his presence within the hour at a house in Northumberland Street, Strand. The sender wished to discuss the Mothers’-Small-Clothes-Conversion-Society, and promised a generous donation if satisfied. The writer gave her name and explained her short stay in London limited the notice.

Most people might hesitate. But Mr. Godfrey, ever ready to serve, went immediately.

At the house, a stout, respectable man admitted him to a back room. Two things struck him: a faint scent of musk and camphor, and an ancient illuminated manuscript—richly decorated with Indian figures—lying open on a table.

He studied the book, his back to the folding doors. Suddenly, without warning, he felt a hand around his neck. A tawny-brown arm. Then darkness over his eyes, a gag in his mouth. He was thrown to the floor. Two men held him down while a third searched him thoroughly—pockets, clothes, skin.

I would like to say something uplifting about his courage. But perhaps that is beyond the scope of this account. Let us skip ahead.

After the search, voices muttered in an unknown tongue—tones of anger and disappointment. He was bound to a chair. Then silence.

Minutes passed. A rustling sound came from below—like a woman’s dress. Then a scream. A man shouted, “Hullo!” Footsteps climbed the stairs. Hands removed his gag. He looked up, dazed.

Two strangers stood before him. “What does it mean?” he whispered.

They replied: “Exactly what we were going to ask *you*.”

The landlord and landlady later explained. Their second-floor rooms had been rented the day before by a respectable-looking gentleman who claimed three Oriental noblemen would be visiting. He paid in advance. Early that morning, two foreigners arrived with him. The third was expected soon. The luggage, said to be bulky, was due late afternoon.

Ten minutes before Mr. Godfrey’s visit, the third foreigner arrived. No unusual activity had occurred—until five minutes ago, when all four left together, walking calmly toward the Strand.

The landlady, remembering a visitor had come, thought it strange he hadn’t left too. She and her husband went upstairs. That’s how they found Mr. Godfrey.

Inside, his belongings were scattered—but nothing was missing. His watch, chain, purse, keys, papers—all were there. The thieves had taken nothing except the illuminated manuscript. The owners’ property remained untouched.

So what happened? From a worldly view, it seemed a mistake. The men had targeted the wrong man. A conspiracy had gone awry. Our beloved Mr. Godfrey, a man of charity and honor, had been caught in error. A warning to all: even the righteous may fall into traps meant for others.

But I must not preach. My task is to narrate. And the cheque from Mr. Blake reminds me: I have not finished.

Let us turn to Mr. Luker.

After leaving the bank, he visited various places in London. Returning home, he found a letter from a boy. The handwriting was unfamiliar, but the name belonged to one of his clients. The correspondent—writing in the third person—had been summoned to London. He had taken lodgings in Alfred Place, Tottenham Court Road, and wished to meet Mr. Luker urgently about a purchase. He was a collector of Eastern antiquities, a longtime patron of Mr. Luker’s establishment.

Mr. Luker called a cab and went at once.

He was shown into the back drawing-room. There, again, lay the illuminated manuscript. He studied it closely—then felt a tawny arm around his throat, a blindfold, a gag. He was thrown down and searched.

More time passed than with Mr. Godfrey. Then, as before, the landlady heard a disturbance and went upstairs.

The explanation was identical. The respectable man had rented the rooms for three foreign guests. The tenants had left together shortly before. The landlady had not seen Mr. Luker leave.

But this time, when Mr. Luker’s things were gathered, one paper was missing: a receipt acknowledging the deposit of a valuable gem—left with his bankers that very day. The document could not be forged, as it required personal identification to retrieve the item.

Mr. Luker rushed to the bank. But the thieves had not returned. The bankers believed the respectable Englishman had warned them—had seen the receipt and realized it was useless.

Both incidents were reported to the police. Investigations began. Authorities concluded the robbers had acted on incomplete information. They weren’t sure whether Mr. Luker had entrusted the diamond to someone else. Mr. Godfrey had simply been in the wrong place at the wrong time.

And Mr. Godfrey’s absence from our meeting that Monday? He had been called to assist the authorities.

With that, I may now return to my own story—my visit to Montagu Square.

I arrived punctually for luncheon on Tuesday. My diary notes it as a mixed day—some moments to regret, others to thank God for.

Aunt Verinder received me kindly. But soon I noticed something was wrong. Her glances kept drifting toward her daughter. Rachel—her daughter—has always puzzled me. So plain, so ordinary, born of such distinguished parents. But today, she shocked me. Her behavior was reckless—laughing too loudly, eating and drinking without restraint. A feverish excitement gripped her.

I pitied her mother deeply—even before learning the truth.

After lunch, Aunt Verinder said: “Remember what the doctor advised, Rachel—read quietly after meals.”

“I’ll go to the library, mamma,” she replied. “But if Godfrey calls, make sure I know. I’m desperate for news of him after his ordeal.”

She kissed her mother and glanced my way. “Good-bye, Clack,” she said, carelessly.

Her arrogance did not anger me. I only noted in my diary: *Pray for her.*

Then, alone with her, Aunt Verinder told me everything—the whole terrible tale of the Indian Diamond. I am glad I need not repeat it here. But she confessed she would have preferred silence. Yet, with servants aware, and rumors spreading—linking the theft at her country house to the attacks in Northumberland Street and Alfred Place—silence was no longer possible. Truth became both duty and necessity.

And so, I begin my own account.
