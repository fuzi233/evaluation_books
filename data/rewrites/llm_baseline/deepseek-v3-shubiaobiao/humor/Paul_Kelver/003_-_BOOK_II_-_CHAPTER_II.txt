My parents’ marriage was not what you might call conventional. You see, they had been separated for years, and though by most standards they weren’t exactly young—my father pushing forty, my mother past thirty—in spirit, they were practically children. Had anyone asked me, a wide-eyed observer of their antics, for marital advice, I might have painted a rather misleading picture. I’d have described a husband as a man who couldn’t bear to be more than three feet from his wife, constantly interrupting her with cries of, *"Maggie, are you busy? I’ve got a pressing matter to discuss!"*—only for the "pressing matter" to be whether he preferred peas or carrots with supper. A wife, in my youthful estimation, was a woman whose eyes sparkled like a lovesick heroine’s whenever her husband entered the room—though in reality, that sparkle might just as easily have been suppressed irritation.

I’d also have warned prospective spouses about the perils of domestic espionage. Never enter a room unannounced unless you enjoy the sight of two adults springing apart like guilty cats, followed by a frantic scramble to assume perfectly innocent positions—my father suddenly engrossed in a ledger, my mother stitching a button with unnatural intensity. And under no circumstances should you leave your feet unattended beneath the table, lest they be mistaken for someone else’s and subjected to enthusiastic toe-crushing.

Personally, I was immune to such hazards. My parents had long since dismissed my interruptions with a resigned, *"Oh, it’s only Spud,"* usually followed by a sofa cushion to the head. But my aunt was another matter. Out of respect (or terror), they maintained some decorum in her presence. Not that she ever *said* anything—no, her methods were subtler. She moved through the house like a one-woman tuberculosis ward, coughing loudly enough to wake the dead before entering any room—*backwards*, mind you, as if to prove she hadn’t seen anything scandalous. Dark hallways were navigated via tuneless humming. The woman was a walking fire alarm.

You’d think they’d be grateful for her discretion. You’d be wrong.

*"Fanny, I swear you take pleasure in sabotaging our happiness,"* my mother snapped one day, catching my aunt mid-spoon-drop outside the drawing room—a delicate operation involving the strategic clattering of cutlery to announce her arrival.

*"Sabotage?"* My aunt’s voice was syrup laced with arsenic. *"Why, I’m simply enchanted by the sight of two turtledoves, their combined ages barely scraping seventy, billing and cooing like spring chickens."*

My father, meanwhile, had reached the stage of outright hostility. One evening by the canal, he stopped dead, seized by a sudden, violent thought. *"Your aunt,"* he began (with the usual unspoken accusation that *I* was somehow responsible for her existence), *"is the most cantankerous, meddling—"* He broke off, shaking his fist at the sunset. *"I swear to God, Spud, if she had a cottage and a modest income, I’d buy her a one-way ticket to the countryside myself!"* Then, remembering himself: *"Not that she isn’t… occasionally tolerable. Don’t tell your mother I said that."*

The other keen observer of our household melodrama was Susan, our maid-of-all-work—a woman whose heart was, by her own proud declaration, *always in the right place*, even if her body was usually slumped in a chair, fast asleep. She wept at the sight of marital affection, claiming it reminded her of her own tragically complicated past.

*"I thought your husband was dead?"* I once ventured, after she’d spent Monday weeping over his unwatered grave and Tuesday vowing to strangle him.

*"So did I,"* Susan replied darkly. *"That was his cunning."*

*"Not yet,"* she said, cracking her knuckles. *"But he will be."*

Such philosophical riddles were beyond me. I retreated to the safer confines of arithmetic.

Susan’s reign ended abruptly one Saturday night when my mother found her sprawled on the kitchen floor, breathing like a broken harmonium. The doctor—a bear of a man named Washburn, locally known as *Dr. Fighting Hal*—arrived, took one look at her, and promptly upended a pail of water over her head.

*"Feeling better?"* he boomed, as she spluttered to life. *"Fancy another round?"*

What followed was a duel of insults so vivid I’m certain the wallpaper blushed. When Susan finally flounced out, the doctor stayed for supper, filling the house with laughter so loud I’m surprised the neighbors didn’t complain.

That was the magic of the man. He turned my timid parents into brighter versions of themselves—my mother sparkling, my father cracking jokes, even my aunt thawing slightly. Under his influence, our shabby little world seemed to crack open, revealing a brighter, bolder place where life was a grand, rowdy adventure.

Of course, whether that was a good thing or not remained to be seen.
