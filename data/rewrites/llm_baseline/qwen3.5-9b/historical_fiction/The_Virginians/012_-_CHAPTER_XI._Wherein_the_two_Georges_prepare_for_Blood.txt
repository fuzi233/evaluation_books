Colonel Washington remained sequestered within one chamber of the tavern, lost in somber preparations for the impending confrontation. In the adjacent room, his adversary, George Warrington, deemed it prudent to attend to his final affairs. Dictating a flowery letter to his mother via his devoted brother and secretary, he offered a solemn farewell. He presumed that once the outcome of the morning's events became known—should he, as he likely would, fall—the scheme his mother had in mind would surely be abandoned.

"My dear, dear George, do not speak thus!" cried the terrified secretary, Harry.

"'As probably will be the case,'" George insisted with regal composure. "You know the skill of Colonel Washington. I, too, am quite proficient with a mark. It is highly probable that one or both of us shall drop. Therefore, I scarcely believe you intend to proceed with the plans you currently hold." His tone grew sharper, laden with a bitterness exceeding his earlier remarks. Harry wept as he transcribed the words.

"You see, I mention nothing," George continued. "Madame Esmond's name does not even appear in this quarrel. Recall our grandfather's memoirs; how he recounts Lord Castlewood fighting Lord Mohun over a pretext of a card dispute, yet never once hinting at the lady whose presence was the true catalyst? I took my cue from that, Harry. My mother is not implicated in this—"

"Why, child, what are you scribbling, and who taught you to spell?"

Harry had penned the final phrase, "in view," as *vew*, while a great blot of salty tears from his honest, boyish eyes had obscured several other errors.

"I cannot concern myself with spelling now, Georgy," whimpered the clerk, burying his face in his hands. "I am too miserable. I begin to suspect it is all nonsense. Perhaps Colonel Washington never..."

"Never intended to seize Castlewood? Never assumed airs among us? Never advised my mother to have me flogged? Never planned to wed her? Never insulted me before the King's officers? Nor wrote to his brother claiming we would benefit from his parental authority?" The young man cried out, slapping his breast pocket. "The letter is there! If anything happens to me, Harry Warrington, you will find it upon my corpse!"

"Write it yourself, Georgy; I cannot," Harry replied, digging his fists into his eyes until ink and tears smeared the entire composition.

On this, George took a fresh sheet, seated himself at his brother's desk, and composed a document filled with the longest vocabulary, the loftiest Latin quotations, and the deepest satire the youthful scribe could muster. He instructed that his enslaved boy, Sady, be freed; that his favorite book, *Horace*, along with a suitable stipend, be provided for his beloved tutor, Mr. Dempster; that his silver fruit-knife, music scores, and harpsichord be gifted to little Fanny Mountain; and that his brother bear a lock of his hair as a token of his eternal devotion. He sealed the missive with the ancestral coat-of-arms inherited from his grandfather.

"The watch, naturally, belongs to you," George said, retrieving his grandfather's gold timepiece. "Two and a half hours have already elapsed. It is time for Sady to return with the pistols. Take the watch, Harry dear."

"It is useless!" cried Harry, throwing his arms around his brother. "If he fights you, I will fight him too. If he kills my Georgy... oh, let him draw breath once more, and he shall have a shot at me!" The poor lad uttered phrases so poignant they were said to weigh heavily upon the recording angels tasked with documenting human sins in the celestial chancery.

Meanwhile, General Braddock's new aide-de-camp had drafted five letters in his bold, resolute hand, sealing them with his personal signet. One went to his mother at Mount Vernon; one to his brother; one simply to "M.C."; and one to His Excellency, Major-General Braddock.

"And one, young gentleman, is for your mother, Madam Esmond," noted the boys' informant.

Once again, the recording angel was compelled to depart with a violent expression torn from the lips of George Warrington. That celestial bureaucracy was often crowded with such cases, its messengers perpetually airborne. Yet, fearing for young George and his oath, there seemed to be no valid excuse. It was an execration born of a heart swollen with hatred, rage, and jealousy.

It was the tavern landlord who relayed these details to the youths. The Captain had donned his old militia uniform to honor the occasion, informing the boys that the Colonel was pacing the garden, awaiting them, and that the Regulars were nearly sober. A patch of earth near the Captain's log cabin had been fenced with shingles and cleared for a vegetable garden; there walked Colonel Washington, hands clasped behind his back, head bowed, a grave sorrow etched upon his handsome countenance. Negro servants crowded the fence line, peering over. Officers beneath the porch had roused themselves as well, as the host observed.

Captain Waring paced steadily under the balcony formed by the sloping porch and roof of the wooden structure, while Captain Grace lounged over the railing, staring with wide, unfocused eyes. Benson's was a notorious rendezvous for cockfights, horse matches, boxing, and wrestling bouts that drew Virginian country folk from afar. Many brawls had erupted there; men arrived sound and sober only to leave with broken ribs and gouged eyes. Squires, farmers, and negroes alike partook in the spectacle.

There, then, stalked the tall young Colonel, immersed in dismal contemplation. There was no escape from his predicution save the customary cruel path mandated by the laws of honor and local custom. Goaded into fury by the insolence of a boy, he had spoken insulting words. The young man had demanded reparation. George Warrington was shocked to realize that his jealousy and desire for revenge had festered in the boy's heart so deeply, yet the wrong lay squarely with the Colonel, who was bound to pay the forfeit.

Suddenly, a great hollering and shouting rose in the distance—a sound characteristic of negroes who loved noise, especially the raucous screams accompanying a gallop on horseback. All heads, whether woolly or powdered, turned toward the commotion. It emanated from the road the travelers had traversed three hours prior. Soon, the clatter of hooves echoed, and Mr. Sady appeared atop his foaming steed, firing a pistol into the midst of a tremendous uproar from his woolly companions. He discharged a second shot, to which Sady's horse, accustomed to hunting with Harry, reacted calmly. Now in the courtyard, surrounded by a dozen bellowing comrades, he descended amidst fluttering poultry, turkeys, kicking horses, and shrieking pigs. Brother-negroes swarmed around him, and he immediately began chatting with them.

"Sady, sir, come here!" roared Master Harry.
"Sady, come here! Confound you!" shouted Master George.
(Once more, the recording angel was summoned to file the incident in the register office.)

"Come directly, mas'r," Sady replied, resuming his conversation with his fellows. He grinned, drew the pistols from his holster, snapped the locks, pointed them at a grunting pig that charged through the yard, then aimed down the road he had just ridden, watching the woolly heads turn again. "Coming, mas'r. Everyone is coming."

Then, the gallop of additional horses was heard. Who approached? Little Mr. Dempster spurred his pony forward. And that figure in riding habit upon Madam Esmond's small gray mare—could it be Madam Esmond herself? No, she was too stout. By my life, it was Mrs. Mountain astride Madam's gray!

"Oh Lor! O Golly! Hoop! Here dey come! Hurray!" a chorus of negroes erupted.
"They're here!"

Dr. Dempster and Mrs. Mountain clattered into the yard, dismounted, pushed through the crowd, rushed inside, darted across the porch where British officers sat in bewildered stupor, sprinted down the stairs to the garden where George and Harry stood, their tall enemy looming opposite them. Before George Warrington could sternly inquire, "What do you do here, madam?", Mrs. Mountain threw her arms around his neck and sobbed, "Oh, George, my darling! It is a mistake! A terrible mistake, and entirely my fault!"

"What is a mistake?" George asked, majestically pulling away.
"What is it, Mounty?" cried Harry, trembling.
"That paper I retrieved from his portfolio, that scrap I picked up, children; wherein the Colonel declares his intention to marry a widow with two children. Who else could it be but you, children, and who else but your mother?"
"Well?"
"Well, it is—not your mother. It is that little widow Custis whom the Colonel intends to wed. He always sought a wealthy match; I knew he would. It is not Mrs. Rachel Warrington. He confided this to Madam today just before departing, stating the wedding would occur after the campaign. And—and your mother is furious, boys. When Sady fetched the pistols and announced to the whole house that you were dueling, I told him to fire them off; I galloped after him, and I have nearly shattered my poor old bones rushing to you."
