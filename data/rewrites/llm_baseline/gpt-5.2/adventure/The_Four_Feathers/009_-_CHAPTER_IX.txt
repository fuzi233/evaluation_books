The farm-house at Glenalla stood a mile above the village, where the bridle-path forgot itself and simply ended at the door, as though it had brought you as far as any reasonable creature could go. Heather pressed close upon the garden beds and leaned, purple and insistent, over the low stone edging; beyond that, the moor rolled away in rough folds, and the hills rose on three sides in a kind of bowl, sheltering the place as snugly as a hand cupped round a flame.

Those hills had no trees—only granite pushing through moss and ling, and long sweeps of colour that shifted with a quick, almost willful change from week to week: emerald where the rain had lately washed, bruised russet where the wind had dried, and the deep purple of heather in bloom. They looked, to Durrance’s eye, less like dead land than like attentive guardians who dressed themselves each morning for the sake of the one life they harboured.

The house faced down the long slope toward the inlet of the Lough. From the windows you could follow, in a single slow glance, the sparse thickets and a few tilled fields, the whitewashed cottages dotted like crumbs of bread flung across the brown, and then the tall woods near the bank; and beyond, the bright slice of water where gulls hung, dipped, and cried, their calls stitching the air. The whole place had its own soundscape: wind combing the heather with a dry hiss; water speaking in the distance; sheep bells thin and far off; and, when the gusts fell, a silence that was not emptiness at all but a living quiet—so complete you could hear your own coat sleeve rasp as you moved.

Durrance rode up the track one afternoon and knew the house before he could have sworn to it by sight. For as he came nearer, a violin’s music floated out from an open window—no drawing-room prettiness, but a steady, searching voice, like a familiar dialect spoken with exact care. The notes rode the air cleanly, undrowned by any machine-clatter, any town din; they met him in that moorland quiet with the force of a welcome.

His hand checked on the reins. A hope—one he had let himself toy with lately, pretending it was only fancy—rose sharply in him and held his breath. He tied his horse at the gate and went in.

Outside, the house had the blunt look of a barrack, square and unornamented against the weather. Within, it was comfort—brasses kept bright, oak that glowed with rubbing, a room full of light and prospect. Durrance could see it, too, in another season: blinds drawn on a winter night, peat fire red on the hearth, the wind skirling around the hills and rapping at the panes like knuckles. Here, at least, noise was honest—wind and rain and the small domestic crackle of flame—not the hard metallic roar that cities had begun to worship as progress.

Ethne greeted him without any show of surprise.

“I thought that you would come,” she said, and smiled as if she had been expecting him as naturally as she expected the tide to turn.

Durrance laughed as they shook hands, sudden and unguarded. Ethne’s eyes followed his gaze to the violin lying on the table beside her. It was pale in colour, and near the bridge a small patch showed where worm-eaten wood had once been replaced—a scar mended with care.

“It is yours,” she said. “You were in Egypt. I could not well send it back to you there.”

“I have hoped lately, since I knew,” Durrance replied, “that, nevertheless, you would accept it.”

“You see I have.” She looked straight into his eyes. “I accepted it some while ago. There was a time when I needed to be assured that I had sure friends. And a thing tangible helped. I was very glad to have it.”

He took up the instrument as delicately as though it were a chalice, his fingers respectful of its varnish and age.

“You have played upon it? The Musoline overture, perhaps,” he said. “Do you remember that?”

She laughed. “Yes, I have played upon it, but only recently. For a long time I put my violin away. It talked to me too intimately of many things which I wished to forget.”

She said it without hesitation and without lowering her eyes, as if the truth—however sharp—was the only language she could endure.

The next day Durrance fetched up his luggage from Rathmullen and remained at the farm for a week. Yet up to the last hour of that visit neither he nor Ethne spoke Harry Feversham’s name. The silence sat between them like a covered dish no one would uncover at table: not because appetite was lacking, but because the smell might be too strong.

They were thrown much together, for Dermod Eustace was even more broken than Mrs. Adair’s account had prepared Durrance to find. The man’s speech had dwindled to monosyllables; his frame was shrunken, and his clothes hung upon him as upon a peg. Even his anger—once so quick—seemed clouded, as though time had breathed on it until it dulled. He stayed by the fire, dozing through most of the day even in July, and his longest walk was to the little grey church that stood bare upon a mound within view of the windows. Even that short distance taxed him. There was something painful in his gestures, and in the rare tones of his voice—like the mimicry of a dead man by someone who had once known him well. His collie dog seemed to have aged in sympathy; to see them together was to see weariness shared.

So Durrance and Ethne walked the hills in wet weather and fine. She went ahead sometimes, the colour glowing in her face, eyes jealous and eager for him to see rightly, to admire without condescension. She showed him the streams that cut the peat-black ground, the places where the granite broke through like bone, the distant line of woods that guarded the Lough. In the evenings she took up the violin and, as of old, sat with her face turned partly away and bade the strings speak of height and depth—of gulls and wind and the long moorland hush. Durrance watched the sweep of her arm, the absorbed set of her mouth, and counted his chances.

He had not come to Glenalla buoyed by Lieutenant Sutch’s cheerful prophecy of success. The shadow of Harry Feversham might well lie between them. Besides, he knew poverty would fall more lightly upon her than upon most women. Though Lennon House was ruined and the lands were gone, Ethne was still among her own people. They looked for her visits with the same bright hunger as children watching for bread to come from the oven. She remained, in that countryside, a kind of princess—less by title than by the way she listened, by the way she knew names and stories that others had begun to forget.

And she, for her part, took a frank pleasure in his company. She drew him out about his three years’ service in the East. No detail was too small for her. She wanted the texture of it: how the mornings began, what water tasted like from different wells, how a tent smelled after a sandstorm, what a man heard at night when the world’s machinery fell silent. While he spoke her eyes continually sounded him, as if she tested the grain of his words for honesty. Her smile approved when his account carried the weight of lived sensation rather than mere soldierly boasting.

Durrance did not understand what she sought. He could not have understood, unless he had known what had passed between Ethne and Harry Feversham. He wore the likeness of a man; she meant to be sure a man’s spirit inhabited it. He was, to her, like a dark lantern: there might be a flame within—or only vacancy. She kept pushing back the slide to see.

On the last day of his visit she led him to speak of Egypt. They sat on a hillside by a stream that leaped from ledge to ledge down a miniature gorge of rock and slid over deep pools between the ledges with swift purpose—a torrent of clear black water. Its voice filled the hollow with a continuous roar that was not harsh but alive, the kind of sound that steadies the mind because it belongs wholly to its place.

“I travelled once for four days amongst the mirages,” he said. “Lagoons, still as a mirror and fringed with misty trees. You could almost walk your camel up to the knees in them, before the lagoon receded and the sand glared at you. And one cannot imagine that glare. Every stone within view dances and shakes like a heliograph; you can see—yes, actually see—the heat flow breast-high across the desert swift as this stream here, only pellucid. So till the sun sets ahead of you level with your eyes. Imagine the nights which follow—nights of infinite silence, with a cool friendly wind blowing from horizon to horizon—and your bed spread for you under the great dome of stars.

“Oh,” he cried, drawing a deep breath, “but that country grows on you. It’s like the Southern Cross—four overrated stars when first you see them, but in a week you begin to look for them, and you miss them when you travel north again.”

He raised himself on his elbow and turned towards her.

“Do you know—I can only speak for myself—but I never feel alone in those empty spaces. On the contrary, I always feel very close to the things I care about, and to the few people I care about too.”

Her eyes shone on him, bright as water catching sun. Her lips parted in a smile. He moved nearer on the grass and sat with his feet gathered under him, leaning on one arm.

“I used to imagine you out there,” he said. “You would have loved it—from the start before daybreak, in the dark, to the camp-fire at night. You would have been at home. I used to think so as I lay awake wondering how the world went with my friends.”

“And you go back there?” she asked.

He did not answer at once. The torrent’s roar beat about them. When he spoke again the enthusiasm had gone; his gaze dropped into the stream as if the black water carried his words away.

“To Wadi Halfa. For two years. I suppose so.”

Ethne knelt on the grass beside him.

“I shall miss you,” she said.

She knelt just behind him as he sat; and for a moment there was only the sound of water and the thin far cry of a gull, as though the land itself listened.

“Of what are you thinking?” she asked.

“That you need not miss me,” he said.

He felt rather than saw her draw back and sink on her heels. Her stillness alarmed him; with Ethne, a fixed quiet was as plain a sign of distress as a cry in another woman. He turned.

Her head had been bent, but she lifted it as he faced her. Her lips smiled—yet trouble lay openly in her eyes.

Durrance’s first thought was the blunt one of a man: if she were willing, what obstacle stood in the way?

“There is your father,” he said.

“Yes,” she answered. “There is my father too. I could not leave him.”

“Nor need you,” he returned quickly. “That difficulty can be surmounted. To tell the truth I was not thinking of your father at the moment.”

“Nor was I,” she said.

He turned away and stared down into a wrinkled pool beneath the rocks. After all, it was Feversham’s shadow.

“I know, of course,” he said at last, “that you would never feel trouble, as so many do, with half your heart. You would neither easily care nor lightly forget.”

“I remember enough,” she replied in a low voice, “to make your words rather a pain to me. Some day perhaps I may bring myself to tell everything which happened at that ball three years ago, and then you will be better able to understand why I am a little distressed. All that I can tell you now is this: I have a great fear that I was to some degree the cause of another man’s ruin. I do not mean that I was to blame for it. But if I had not been known to him, his career might perhaps never have come to so abrupt an end. I am not sure, but I am afraid. I asked whether it was so, and I was told ‘no,’ but I think very likely that generosity dictated that answer. And the fear stays. I am much distressed by it. I lie awake with it at night. And then you come whom I greatly value, and you say quietly, ‘Will you please spoil my career too?’”

She struck one hand sharply into the other. “But that I will not do.”

“There is no need that you should,” he answered. “Wadi Halfa is not the only place where a soldier can find work to his hand.”

Hope sprang up in his voice, for he had listened with the greedy attention of a man translating another’s speech into the dictionary of his desires. She had not said friendship bounded her thoughts of him; therefore he did not believe it did. “One whom I greatly value,” “I shall miss you”—he heard, behind those words, meanings she had not put there. He even caught at her earlier confession about needing “sure friends,” as if tangible friendship must surely mean a hunger for more than friendship.

If he had understood her better, that very reasoning would have warned him how deep he had sunk in error. Ethne habitually meant exactly what she said—no less, and no more. And now she had weighed her words with particular care.

“No doubt,” she said, “a soldier can. But can this soldier find work so suitable? Listen, please, till I have done. I was so very glad to hear all that you have told me about your work and your journeys. I was still more glad because of the satisfaction with which you told it. For it seemed to me, as I listened and as I watched, that you had found the one true straight channel along which your life could run swift and smoothly and unharassed. And so few do that—so very few!”

She wrung her hands. “And now you spoil it all.”

Durrance swung round on her, and argument fell away. Passion took its place.

“I am for you, Ethne! There’s the true straight channel, and upon my word I believe you are for me. I thought—at one time—I would spend my life out there in the East, and the thought contented me. But I had schooled myself into contentment, for I believed you married.”

She flinched, ever so slightly. He heard his own voice and knew he had spoken too loud, with something almost brutal in the force of it.

“Do I hurt you?” he asked more quietly. “I am sorry. But let me speak the whole truth out. I cannot afford reticence. I want you to know the first and last of it. I say now that I love you. Yes, but I could have said it with equal truth five years ago. It is five years since your father arrested me at the ferry down there on Lough Swilly, because I wished to press on to Letterkenny and not delay a night by stopping with a stranger. Five years since I first saw you, first heard the language of your violin. I remember how you sat with your back towards me. The light shone on your hair; I could just see your eyelashes and the colour of your cheeks. I remember the sweep of your arm… My dear, you are for me; I am for you.”

He held out his hands. She drew back from them.

“No,” she said, very gently, with a decision he could not mistake.

She saw into him more clearly than he saw into himself. The restlessness of the born traveller, the craving for large lonely spaces at the far ends of maps, the intermittent fever of movement—those were deep-rooted in him. Passion might muffle them for a time; it would not cure them. In the end they would call, and the call would torture. A home would begin to feel like a cell. Within him desires would clash until peace became impossible.

She looked down the slope across the brown country. On the right the woods about Ramelton waved; at her feet a strip of the Lough flashed; gulls turned and cried above it. This was her country, and she belonged to it as a seed belongs to its soil, as a song belongs to its own tongue.

“No,” she repeated, rising to her feet.

Durrance rose too. He felt less disheartened than aware of a blunder—he had let her think marriage would interrupt his career. He might have framed his case differently, made her see that the channel could widen and still run true.

“We will say good-bye here,” she said, “in the open. We shall be none the less good friends because three thousand miles hinder us from shaking hands.”

They shook hands.

“I shall be in England again in a year’s time,” Durrance said. “May I come back?”

Her eyes and smile consented.

“I should be sorry to lose you altogether,” she replied, “although even if I did not see you, I should know that I had not lost your friendship.” Then she added, “I should also be glad to hear news of you and what you are doing, if ever you have the time to spare.”

“I may write?” he exclaimed, eager.

“Yes,” she answered; but his eagerness made her linger a little on the word, doubtfully. “That is, if you think it fair. I mean, it might be best for you, perhaps, to get rid of me entirely from your thoughts.”

Durrance laughed without bitterness, and in a moment Ethne found herself laughing too, though at what she laughed she could not easily have said—perhaps at the odd helplessness of words, which so often mean one thing in the mouth and another in the ear.

“Very well, write to me then,” she said. And she added drily, “But it will be about—other things.”

Again he read into her speech what he wanted to read; again she meant precisely what she said, and not a word more.

She stood where he left her, a tall, strong-limbed figure against the slope, until he had gone out of sight. Then she went down to the house.

In her room she took one violin from its case. But it was the one Durrance had given her, and before the bow had touched the strings she recognised it—felt it in her hands as unmistakably as one recognises an old flavour on the tongue. She put it away at once and snapped the case shut.

For a few moments she sat motionless. Then she crossed the room quickly, took her keys, and unlocked a drawer. At the bottom lay hidden a photograph. She drew it out and looked a long while, very wistfully, as though she tried to hear, in the silent paper, a voice that had once been spoken to her in a language she had trusted.

Durrance walked down to the trap waiting at the gate and saw Dermod Eustace standing in the road with his hat on his head.

“I will walk a few yards with you, Colonel Durrance,” Dermod said. “I have a word for your ear.”

Durrance matched his stride to the old man’s faltering step, and they walked behind the dog-cart in silence. It was not merely personal disappointment that weighed on Durrance now. As he looked back at that quiet Donegal corner—with its small cottages, its moorland, its tiny church—he was seized by a sadness at the thought of Ethne’s whole life spent in such seclusion: her grave one day under the church wall, and her memory lingering only a little while among those scattered white houses, like a tune remembered by a few and then lost when the last singer dies.

Dermod’s hand pressed his elbow, and Durrance started from his thoughts. A gleam of inquiry lived in the old man’s faded eyes, but even speech seemed difficult for him.

“You have news for me?” Dermod asked at last, with hesitation. “News of Harry Feversham? I thought that I would ask you before you went away.”

“None,” said Durrance.

“I am sorry,” Dermod replied wistfully, “though I have no reason for sorrow. He struck us a cruel blow, Colonel Durrance. I should have nothing but curses for him in my mouth and my heart. A black-throated coward my reason calls him, and yet I would be very glad to hear how the world goes with him. You were his friend. But you do not know?”

It was Harry Feversham Dermod spoke of, and the wistfulness in his voice and face struck Durrance with a sudden remorse. He had allowed Ethne, by her silence, to thrust his friend too far from his thoughts—as though a man might be erased by not being named. He had, at times, tried to piece together the mystery of Feversham’s disappearance from a handful of hints, sitting in the evenings on his verandah above the Nile at Wadi Halfa while the river’s slow voice filled the night and the desert lay listening beyond.

“Black-throated coward,” Dermod had called him. Ethne had said enough to make plain that something graver than any mere quarrel—something that destroyed her faith—had ended the betrothal. But Durrance could not guess the particular cause; and from his perplexity there grew, month by month, a real anger against the man who had been his friend.

So the winter passed, and summer came to the Soudan, and the month of May.
