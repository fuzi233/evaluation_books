We had killed nine elephants, and it took us two days to remove the tusks, bury them carefully in the sand beneath a large tree that stood like a landmark for miles around. The ivory was exceptional—each tusk averaging between forty and fifty pounds, with the pair from the great bull that killed poor Khiva weighing nearly one hundred and seventy pounds together.

Khiva himself we buried in an ant-bear hole, along with an assegai, to guard him on his journey to whatever world awaited him. On the third day, we set out again, hoping someday to return and reclaim our treasure. After a long and grueling march, filled with trials I cannot fully recount here, we reached Sitanda’s kraal near the Lukanga River—the true beginning of our quest.

I remember vividly our arrival at this place. To the right lay a scattered native settlement: stone cattle enclosures, small fields where grain struggled to grow by the water’s edge, and beyond, vast stretches of open veld covered in tall grass, where herds of smaller game wandered freely. To the left stretched the endless desert. This spot marks the edge of fertile land, and no one can say what natural force causes such a sudden shift in terrain. Yet so it is.

Just below our camp flowed a narrow stream. On the far side rose a rocky slope—the same path down which, twenty years earlier, I had seen Silvestre creeping back after his failed attempt to reach Solomon’s Mines. Beyond that slope began the waterless wasteland, blanketed in karoo scrub.

It was evening when we pitched our tents. The sun sank into the desert, painting the sky in brilliant hues of gold and crimson, casting long shadows across the dunes. Leaving Good to oversee the camp setup, I walked with Sir Henry to the top of the opposite slope and looked out over the barren expanse. The air was clear, and far in the distance I could make out the faint blue outlines of the Suliman Berg, capped here and there with snow.

“There,” I said, “lies the wall surrounding Solomon’s Mines. But God only knows if we’ll ever climb it.”

“My brother must be there,” replied Sir Henry, his voice calm and steady. “If he is, I will find him.”

“I hope so,” I answered, turning to return, when I noticed we were not alone. Standing behind us, gazing intently toward the distant mountains, was Umbopa, the great Kafir warrior.

He spoke directly to Sir Henry, who had grown close to him.

“Is it to that land you go, Incubu?” he asked, pointing with his broad assegai. (Incubu means “elephant” in their tongue—a name they gave Sir Henry.)

I frowned. “Why do you address my master so familiarly? Natives may have names among themselves, but they should not call a white man by such heathen terms.”

The Zulu smiled quietly—a smile that irritated me.

“How do you know I am not equal to the Inkosi I serve?” he replied. “He is of royal blood, I see it in his bearing. Perhaps I too am noble. At least, I am no less a man. Speak for me, Macumazahn. Tell your master what I wish to say.”

I was angry—but something about him made me pause. He carried himself with quiet power, and I wanted to hear what he had to say. So I translated, adding my own judgment: “He is bold, even insolent.”

“Yes, Umbopa,” said Sir Henry. “I would go there.”

“The desert is wide,” Umbopa continued. “No water flows through it. The mountains are high and cold. No man knows what lies beyond the sunset. How will you reach it, Incubu? And why?”

I passed on the question.

“To seek a brother,” Sir Henry replied. “One of my own blood, who may have gone before me.”

“That is true,” Umbopa said. “A Hottentot traveler told me a white man entered the desert two years ago, heading toward those peaks, with a single servant—a hunter named Jim, a Bechuana who wore clothes.”

Sir Henry leaned forward. “How do you know it was my brother?”

“I do not,” Umbopa admitted. “But the Hottentot described a man with your eyes and a black beard. That is enough for me.”

I nodded. “I knew Jim well.”

Sir Henry sighed. “I always knew George would do it. If he set his mind to something, he did it. As a boy, he never stopped until he succeeded. If he meant to cross the Suliman Berg, he crossed it—unless fate caught him. We must look for him beyond.”

Umbopa understood English, though he rarely used it.

“It is a long journey, Incubu,” he said.

I translated.

“True,” Sir Henry replied. “But no journey on earth is impossible if a man sets his heart upon it. There is nothing he cannot do, no mountain he cannot climb, no desert he cannot cross—except perhaps one guarded by death. But if love drives him, and he holds life lightly, ready to keep or lose it as Heaven commands… then all things are possible.”

I relayed the words.

“Great words, my father,” Umbopa said. “Words fit for a king. You are right. Listen—what is life? It is a feather, a seed blown by the wind, sometimes dying in the act of multiplying, sometimes carried into the heavens. But if the seed is strong, it may travel far. It is good to try, to fight the air. Man must die. At worst, he dies sooner. I will go with you across the desert and over the mountains—unless I fall along the way, my father.”

He paused, then launched into a rare burst of poetic speech, full of repetition yet rich in emotion—typical of the Zulu tongue.

“What is life? Tell me, O wise white men, who know the stars and the worlds beyond them, who send your voices across space without sound—tell me, what is life? Where does it come from? Where does it go?

“You cannot answer. I will. We come from darkness, and into darkness we return. Like a storm-tossed bird at night, we fly from nowhere, briefly lit by firelight, then vanish again. Life is nothing. Life is everything. It is the hand that pushes back death. It is the glow-worm shining at night and gone by morning. It is the breath of oxen in winter. It is the shadow that races across the grass and vanishes at dusk.”

Sir Henry stared. “You are a strange man.”

Umbopa laughed. “Perhaps we are alike, Incubu. Perhaps *I* too seek a brother over those mountains.”

I looked at him sharply. “What do you mean? What do you know of those lands?”

“A little,” he said. “A strange country—of magic, beauty, brave people, rivers, trees, snowy peaks, and a great white road. I have heard of it. But what good is talk? It grows dark. Those who live to see will see.”

I studied him. He knew more than he let on.

“You need not fear me, Macumazahn,” he said, reading my thoughts. “I plot nothing. I dig no traps. If we cross those mountains, I will tell you all I know. But Death watches them. Turn back. Go hunt elephants. I have spoken.”

With that, he raised his spear in salute and walked back to camp, where we soon found him cleaning a rifle like any other warrior.

“That man is odd,” Sir Henry remarked.

“He is too odd,” I agreed. “He hides something. But what can we do? We’re stepping into mystery, and a mysterious guide may not matter much.”

The next day, we prepared for departure. Our heavy rifles and gear could not be carried across the desert. So we left them with an old native whose kraal was nearby, promising him safekeeping until our return. It pained me to abandon such fine tools to a thief’s mercy—his eyes already gleaming with greed.

But I took precautions. I loaded every rifle, cocked them, and warned him that touching them would cause them to fire. He tested my eight-bore immediately—and it went off, blowing a hole through one of his oxen and throwing him to the ground. He rose, stunned and furious, demanding payment for the animal. After that, he refused to touch the guns again.

“Put the living devils up in the thatch,” he muttered. “Or they’ll kill us all.”

Then I told him, if we returned and found anything missing, I’d curse him—kill him by witchcraft, turn his cattle mad, sour his milk, summon the devils in the guns to speak to him in ways he wouldn’t like. He promised to guard them as if they were his father’s spirit. Superstitious and villainous, yes—but now he’d obey.

With our excess gear secured, we packed only what we needed—five men: Sir Henry, Good, myself, Umbopa, and the Hottentot Ventvögel. Our load weighed about forty pounds each:

- Three express rifles and 200 rounds.
- Two Winchester repeaters (for Umbopa and Ventvögel) with 200 cartridges.
- Five Cochrane water bottles (four pints each).
- Five blankets.
- Twenty-five pounds of biltong (sun-dried meat).
- Ten pounds of mixed beads for trade.
- Medicine: quinine, surgical tools.
- Knives, compass, matches, filter, tobacco, trowel, brandy, and clothing.

It was light by necessity, but still heavy under the desert sun. Every ounce mattered. We could not reduce it further.

I persuaded three natives from the village to join us for the first twenty miles, offering each a hunting knife. They carried large gourds of water—one gallon each—so we could refill our bottles after the first night. I told them we were going to hunt ostriches, which were plentiful. They scoffed, calling us fools who would die of thirst. But knives were rare treasures, so they agreed—probably thinking our deaths wouldn’t concern them.

All day we rested. At sunset, we ate fresh beef and tea—our last for many days, Good noted sadly. Then, after final preparations, we waited for the moon.

At nine o’clock, she rose—bright and full—casting silver light across the desert. It looked as still and alien as the stars above. We stood ready, hesitating. Such moments weigh heavily on the soul.

Sir Henry stepped forward.

“Gentlemen,” he said, his voice deep and firm. “We are setting out on one of the strangest journeys a man can make. Success is uncertain. But we stand together—three men—for better or worse. Before we begin, let us pray to the Power who guides destinies, who shaped our paths long ago, that He may lead our steps according to His will.”

He removed his hat, covered his face, and bowed his head. Good and I did the same.

I’m no great prayer man—hunters rarely are. Sir Henry had never spoken so before. But I felt it deeply. In that moment, facing the unknown, I prayed more sincerely than I ever had. Something about the vast silence brought me closer to God.

“And now,” said Sir Henry, “_trek_!”

We started.

Our only guides were the distant mountains and José da Silvestra’s ancient chart—a fragment of linen drawn by a dying man centuries ago. It was crude, unreliable. Yet our only hope lay in it. If we missed the pool of bad water marked sixty miles ahead, midway between us and the mountains, we would surely die of thirst. Finding it in that sea of sand seemed nearly impossible. Even if it existed, might it not have dried up, been buried, or trampled flat?

We moved silently through the night, feet sinking into soft sand, karoo bushes snagging our boots. Sand filled our shoes, forcing frequent stops to empty them. The air was thick, creamy, and cool. Progress was slow but steady.

The desert was silent—oppressively so. Good tried to whistle “The Girl I Left Behind Me,” but the tune sounded mournful in the emptiness. He stopped.

Then came a sudden noise—an exclamation, then chaos. Snorts, groans, galloping hooves. Shapes flashed past in the dim light. The natives dropped their loads and cowered, shouting it was ghosts. Sir Henry and I froze—until we saw Good racing away, seemingly riding a horse, yelling wildly.

Then he fell with a thud.

I realized what had happened: we had stumbled onto a herd of sleeping quagga. Good had landed on one, and the beast bolted with him. I ran to help, fearing injury—but found him sitting in the sand, eye-glass still in place, shaken but unharmed.

We laughed, then resumed.

By one o’clock, we halted. We drank sparingly—water was precious—and rested half an hour before continuing.

On we went, until dawn began to blush in the east. Pale light turned to golden bars, stars faded, the moon grew pale, and the desert shimmered in golden glow. It was day.

Still, we marched on, knowing once the sun rose, walking would be nearly impossible.

About an hour later, we spotted a cluster of rocks. Beneath one lay a smooth, shaded hollow—perfect shelter. We crawled under, drank water, ate biltong, and slept deeply.

We woke at three. Our bearers were leaving—they had seen enough. Nothing could tempt them farther. We refilled our bottles from their gourds and watched them walk home.

At four-thirty, we set off again. The landscape was desolate—only ostriches broke the emptiness. Game avoided this dryness. We saw a few cobras, but mostly flies—houseflies, swarming in battalions. They followed us everywhere, buzzing relentlessly. I’ve seen them preserved in amber, half a million years old, unchanged. I suspect they’ll be the last creatures on Earth when the final man dies.

At sunset, we stopped, waiting for the moon. She rose serene and bright. With a brief halt at two in the morning, we trudged through the night until sunrise.

We drank, collapsed on the sand, and slept—no watch needed. The desert held no threats except heat, thirst, and flies.

This time, no rock offered shade. By seven, we woke feeling like steaks on a grill. The sun burned through us, sucking our blood. We sat up, gasping.

“Phew,” I said, swatting the halo of flies around my head. “They don’t feel it.”

“My word,” said Sir Henry.

“It’s hot!” echoed Good.

It was unbearable. No shelter anywhere—just endless glare, shimmering like a red-hot stove.

“What do we do?” asked Sir Henry. “We can’t endure this.”

We looked at each other, helpless.

“I’ve got it,” said Good. “We dig a hole, get in, and cover ourselves with scrub.”

It wasn’t ideal—but better than nothing. Using the trowel and our hands, we dug a ten-by-twelve-foot pit two feet deep. We cut low shrubs and piled them over us—except Ventvögel, who seemed unaffected by the heat.

Inside, it was stifling. The Black Hole of Calcutta would have been a paradise. We lay panting, sipping water slowly. Had we drunk freely, we’d have run out in hours. But we knew—without water, we died.

There we stayed, trapped in our makeshift grave, enduring the day, alive only by sheer will.
