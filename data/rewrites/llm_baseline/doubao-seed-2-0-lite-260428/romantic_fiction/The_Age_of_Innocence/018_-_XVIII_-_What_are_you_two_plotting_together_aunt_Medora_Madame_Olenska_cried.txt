“What on earth are you two plotting over there, Aunt Medora?” Ellen Olenska called out as she stepped across the threshold, her entrance bright as a burst of champagne fizz in the dim, candle-lit drawing room. She was dressed as if heading for the most lavish ball of the season: every thread of her gown shimmered, stitched through with faint silver sequins that caught the flame glow like they’d been woven from captured candle-beams, and she held her head high, the set of her chin that of a woman who’d long ago learned not to flinch before a room full of whispering rivals.

“Nothing but a little surprise for you, my dear,” Mrs. Manson Medora trilled, rising to her feet and pointing playfully at the massive lush bouquet of hothouse lilacs and roses set on the side table.

Ellen stopped short, her gaze locking on the flowers. No flush rose to her cheeks, but a pale, searing radiance of anger swept over her skin, quick and bright as summer lightning across a dark field. “Ah!” she exclaimed, in a sharp, high tone Newland Archer had never heard from her before. “Who is ridiculous enough to send me an anonymous bouquet? Why flowers? Why tonight of all nights? I am not a debutante waiting on a beau’s token, not a girl newly engaged to be married. But it seems some people will never stop treating me like either.”

She turned on her heel, wrenched open the drawing room door, and called for her maid. Nastasia appeared at once, and Archer listened as Ellen spoke slow, deliberate Italian, enunciating every word clearly so he could follow along. “Here—take this whole thing and toss it in the dustbin—no, wait. The poor flowers haven’t done anything to deserve that. Tell the errand boy to carry it three doors down, to Mr. Winsett’s house, the quiet dark gentleman who dined here last week. His wife has been ill in bed for weeks, those might cheer her a little. The boy is out? Then run yourself, darling—wrap yourself in my velvet opera cloak, go straight there, and not a single word about who sent them. I want this thing out of my house this very second.” She slung the heavy fur-lined cloak over Nastasia’s shoulders, shut the drawing room door sharp behind her, and leaned back against it, her lace-trimmed chest rising and falling fast. For a heartbeat Archer was sure she was about to burst into tears—then she laughed, bright and breathless, and glanced between Medora and him. “Well, have you two managed to become friends while I was fumbling with my hair?”

“That’s for Mr. Archer to say, dear heart. He waited so patiently for you to finish dressing,” Medora simpered.

Ellen laughed, lifting a hand to fluff at the piled curls of her chignon. “I gave him more than enough time, I swear—my hair simply refused to behave. But that reminds me: Dr. Carver has already left, hasn’t he? You two will be late to the Blenkers’ dinner if you don’t hurry. Newland, would you be a dear and help tuck my aunt into her carriage? She’s got so many shawls and overshoes she can barely move.”

She followed Medora out to the hall, fussed over her until she was wrapped so tight in wool and lace she looked like a travelling bundle of holiday gifts, then called after the carriage as it pulled away: “Mind you send the horses right back for me at ten!” and stepped back inside to the drawing room, where Archer found her leaning against the mantel, studying her own reflection in the mirror.

It was unheard of for a lady of New York society to call her parlour maid “darling” and lend her her own custom opera cloak to run an errand, and Archer felt a giddy, warm thrill low in his chest—here, in Ellen’s orbit, action followed feeling as fast as lightning, no stuffy rules to drag it down by the heels. She didn’t turn when he stepped up behind her, and their eyes met for a long second in the mirror glass before she whirled away, sank into the curve of the sofa, and sighed. “There’s just time for a cigarette, thank goodness.”

He handed her the silver case, lit a spill for her, and as the small flame flared up to gild her cheekbones she glanced at him, eyes dancing with amusement. “What do you think of me, throwing a silly little temper tantrum over a bouquet?”

Archer hesitated a beat, then spoke with sudden, unplanned resolve. “It makes me understand what your aunt was telling me about you, while you were dressing.”

“Of course she was talking about me. Well?”

“She said you’ve spent your whole life surrounded by splendours, amusements, excitements—things no one in slow, staid old New York could ever hope to give you.”

Ellen smiled faintly, blowing a soft ring of smoke into the air between them. “Medora is incurably romantic. It’s gotten her out of more tight spots than you could ever count.”

Archer hesitated again, and took the risk. “Is her romanticism ever paired with the truth, do you think?”

Ellen tilted her head, considering. “You’re asking if she lies, aren’t you? Well. Almost everything she says has a little kernel of truth buried under a whole lot of nonsense. But why do you ask? What’s she been spinning you this time?”

He looked away at the crackling fire, then back at her, glowing and bright across the sofa. His chest tightened so he could barely breathe—this was their last quiet evening by this fireside, he knew it, any second the returning carriage would rumble up outside and carry her away, and this moment would be gone forever. “She says Count Olenski wrote her a letter, asked her to come here and persuade you to go back to him.”

Ellen didn’t move. She sat perfectly still, cigarette half-lifted to her lips, her face no flicker of surprise crossing it—Archer had always noticed she almost never looked shocked, no matter what news landed at her feet. She sat so long the ash broke off the end of her cigarette and dropped to the rug, and she brushed it away absently. “She’s been dropping little hints about a letter, yes. Poor Medora, her hints are always so obvious.”

“Did she really turn up out of nowhere, all on your husband’s orders?”

Ellen pondered that, her brow softening. “It’s hard to say. She told me she had a ‘spiritual summons’ from Dr. Carver, whatever that means. I’m half sure she’s planning to marry Dr. Carver, you know—poor Medora, there’s always some new odd man she’s determined to marry. Though it’s equally possible the Cuban family she was companion to finally got tired of her chatter and sent her packing. I honestly don’t know why she showed up on my doorstep.”

“But you do believe the letter from Olenski is real?”

Ellen was quiet for another long stretch, her gaze fixed on the fire. “Well. It was only a matter of time, wasn’t it?”

Archer stood up, leaned his shoulder against the mantel. Restlessness coiled through him sharp and hot, every minute slipping away from him like sand through his fingers. “You know Medora honestly thinks you’ll agree to go back to him, don’t you?”

Ellen’s head snapped up, and a deep, dark flush crawled up her cheeks, all the way down her neck to the very edge of her low gown. She rarely blushed, and when she did it looked like it burned her, like pressing a hot iron to bare skin. “People have believed far crueler things about me.”

“Oh Ellen—forgive me, I’m a fool, a brute, I never meant to hurt you.”

She smiled, soft and a little sad. “You’re just horribly nervous. You’ve got your own troubles, don’t you? I know the Wellands are being absurdly stubborn about your long engagement, and I agree with you entirely. Back in Europe no one would ever dream of dragging an engagement on for years the way New York does. I suppose they’re just far calmer than we are.” She emphasized that last “we” with a faint, ironic lilt that hung in the air.

Archer caught the irony, but didn’t dare lean into it. She’d clearly deliberately shifted the conversation away from her own pain, and after the way his words had stung her, he felt he ought to follow her lead. But the minutes were slipping so fast he was desperate—he couldn’t bear to let polite little words build a wall between them again. “I went down to Florida to see May,” he blurted. “I asked her to marry me right after Easter. There’s no reason at all we can’t have the wedding then.”

“May adores you—couldn’t you convince her? I always thought she was too clever to buy into all those silly old New York superstitions about unlucky spring weddings.”

“She is clever. It’s not that.”

Ellen stared at him, confused. “Then I don’t understand.”

Archer’s face burned, and he rushed the words out before he could talk himself out of them. “We had a frank talk, the first one we’ve ever really had. She thinks my impatience is a bad sign. She thinks it means I don’t trust myself to keep caring for her, long enough. She thinks, in short, that I’m so desperate to marry her right now just to run away from someone else that I care for more.”

Ellen studied that, her brow furrowed. “But if she thinks that—why on earth isn’t she in a hurry too, to lock you down?”

“Because she’s not like that. She’s far nobler than that. She insists on dragging the engagement out longer, to give me all the time I need—”

“Time to give her up for the other woman?”

Archer nodded. Ellen leaned forward, her gaze fixed on the dancing flames. Somewhere on the quiet street below, the slow trot of a carriage’s horses was growing louder, coming closer. “That is very noble,” she said, her voice cracking just a little.

“It’s ridiculous, though.”

“Ridiculous? Because you don’t care for anyone else?”

“Because I never had any intention of marrying anyone but you—wait, no. Wait. That’s not right.”

Ellen glanced up, surprised. “Then why are you in such a hurry to marry May early?”

“Your carriage is here,” Archer said, not moving an inch from where he leaned against the mantel.

She half-stood, her eyes blank and distant, groping for her gloves and fan on the sofa beside her. “Yes. I suppose I have to go to Mrs. Struthers’s party.” She paused, then smiled soft, and added: “I must go where I’m invited, otherwise I’d get far too lonely. Why don’t you come with me?”

Archer’s whole body was thrumming now. He couldn’t let her go, couldn’t let the night end like this, he had to steal every last second he could with her. He ignored her question, kept his gaze fixed on the hand holding her gloves, as if he could will her to drop them. “May guessed the truth,” he said. “There is another woman. But not the one she thinks I’m running from.”

Ellen didn’t say a word, didn’t move. After a long, quiet moment, Archer crossed the room and sat down right beside her, wrapped his hand gently around hers, and uncurled her fingers slowly. The gloves and fan tumbled onto the sofa between them. She jolted back, tore her hand free, and stepped across the hearth away from him, her face tight with a frown. “Ah, don’t fall in love with me. Far too many men have already done that, and it never ends well.”

Archer’s face burned hot, he stood up so fast his knees knocked against the sofa leg. That was the harshest rebuke she could have dealt him. “I have never tried to make love to you, not the way all those other men did. And I never will. But you are the woman I would have married, if it had ever been possible for either of us.”

“Possible for either of us?” She stared at him, completely unfeigned shock on her face. “And you’re the one telling me that? You’re the one who made it impossible!”

Archer gaped at her, suddenly blind, as if a single shaft of searing light had torn through the dark fog in his head and left him reeling. “I made it impossible?”

“You, you, you!” she cried, her lip trembling like a little child’s about to cry. “It was you who made me give up the divorce! You convinced me how selfish and cruel it was, that I had to sacrifice my own wants to protect the dignity of marriage, to spare all our families the ugly public scandal, the endless gossiping. And I knew my family would soon be your family—for May’s sake, and for yours—so I did exactly what you told me to do, exactly what you proved to me was the right thing. I never even tried to hide that I did it all for you!”

She sank back down onto the sofa, huddling in the soft ripples of her fancy ball gown like a wounded masquerader, and Archer stood frozen by the mantel, staring at her, his mind blank.

“Good God,” he groaned. “When I thought—”

“What did you think?”

“Ah, don’t ask me what I thought!” he moaned, still staring at her as that same burning blush crawled all the way up her neck to her cheeks. She sat up straight, facing him with rigid, quiet dignity. “I do ask you. Tell me.”

“Fine. There were things in that letter from Olenski you asked me to read—”

“My husband’s letter?”

“Yes.”

“I had nothing at all to fear from that letter! Nothing! All I ever feared was bringing all that ugly notoriety down on the family—on you and May.”

“Good God,” he groaned again, pressing his face into his hands. The silence that fell over the room felt heavy as a gravestone, final, irrevocable. Archer felt it crushing down on his shoulders, as if all the rest of his life stretched ahead empty, no light anywhere to lift that weight off his chest. He didn’t move, didn’t lift his face from his hands. “At least I loved you,” he managed to get out, hoarse.

From the other side of the hearth, he heard a small, muffled, childlike sob. He jumped to his feet, crossed to her side in two strides. “Ellen! What madness is this? Why are you crying? Nothing’s decided that can’t be undone. I’m still free, and you’re going to be free soon, too.” He pulled her up into his arms, her face soft and wet against his, their lips meeting, and all the months of quiet pining, all the useless lies, all the pretense they’d wrapped around themselves went up in smoke like ghosts at sunrise. He couldn’t believe he’d wasted all those months tiptoeing around her when just one touch made everything make perfect sense.

She kissed him back, for a heartbeat, then he felt her go stiff in his arms, and she pushed him gently away, standing up to face him across the rug. “Ah, my poor Newland— I suppose this was always going to happen. But it doesn’t change a single thing, not really.”

“It changes my whole entire life.”

“No, no, it can’t. You’re engaged to May Welland. I’m still married.”

Archer stood up too, his face bright red, resolute. “Nonsense! It’s far too late for that kind of lie. We have no right to lie to the people we love, or to ourselves. We won’t talk about your marriage, but do you honestly think I can marry May now, after this?”

She was quiet, her thin elbows resting on the mantel, her profile reflected in the glass behind her. One of the curls from her chignon had slipped loose and hung down against her neck, and she looked haggard, almost old. “I don’t see you,” she said, slow at last, “having the heart to put that question to May. Do you?”

Archer laughed, reckless. “It’s too late to do anything else.”

“You’re only saying that because it feels like the easiest, truest thing to say right this second. But it’s not. It’s too late for us to do anything but exactly what we both already decided was right.”

“Ah, I don’t understand you!”

She gave him a small, pitiful smile that pulled tight at the corners of her mouth. “You don’t understand because you’ve never guessed how much you changed things for me, from the very first day I got here. I was so stupid, so unobservant. I didn’t even notice at first that everyone in New York was avoiding dinner invitations that had my name on the guest list, that they thought I was some kind of wicked fallen woman. I found out later how you dragged your mother to the van der Luydens’ house, how you deliberately announced your engagement at the Beaufort ball so I’d have two whole families standing up for me, not just the Mingotts, so no one dared snub me. New York felt like home, like peace and safety, and I thought everyone here was kind. But you were kinder than all of them. The pious, good people never convinced me to do the hard things, because I knew they’d never been tempted to run away the way I had. But you knew. You understood. You’d felt the whole wide shiny world outside tugging at you with all its golden hands, and you still hated the happiness that costs disloyalty, cruelty, hurting people who love you. That was something I’d never known before. It’s better than anything I ever had in Europe.”

Her words fell slow and heavy, like drops of burning lead, right into his chest. He sat bowed, his head in his hands, staring at the tip of her satin peep-toe shoe under the hem of her gown. Suddenly he dropped to his knees, and pressed a kiss to the toe of that shoe.

She leaned down, her hands resting light on his shoulders, gazing down at him with eyes so deep and dark he couldn’t look away. “Ah, don’t let us undo all the good you’ve already done! I can’t go back to that old, messy, cruel way of thinking. I can’t love you unless I give you up.”
