At around eight in the morning, Mr. Nathaniel Burton Cupples stood on the veranda of the Marlstone hotel, lost in thought—not about the day ahead, but about breakfast. For him, the word carried literal weight: he considered every meal with the same care and precision he applied to all deliberate actions. The previous day’s chaos—following the discovery of a dead man—had unsettled his appetite, leaving him undernourished. Now, after an hour awake and active, he was ravenous. He resolved to allow himself an extra slice of toast and a second egg, keeping everything else as usual. The rest would be made up at lunch—a matter to be addressed later.

With that decision settled, he turned his attention to the view. The rugged coastline stretched before him, where a towering sea stack rose from the glassy water. Beyond it, rolling pastures, cultivated fields, and woodlands climbed gently toward the distant moor. It was a landscape of quiet grandeur, and Mr. Cupples, a connoisseur of such scenes, took pleasure in its order and beauty.

He was a man of average height, slender build, and nearing sixty. Though frail by nature, he remained alert and energetic. His thin beard and mustache were sparse, framing a gentle mouth and sharp, kind eyes. His narrow jaw and prominent nose gave him a clerical appearance, reinforced by his plain dark clothes and soft black hat. In sum, he looked like a man of the cloth—though he was not one. Rather, he was a retired banker, widower, and member of the London Positivist Society, known for his meticulous mind, moral seriousness, and deep, if dry, intellect. His life had been spent among books and museums, where his encyclopedic knowledge of obscure subjects earned him respect among scholars and curators. He read Montaigne often, finding solace in his reflective essays. He possessed a rare blend of kindness and innocent joy, unmarred by irony or cynicism.

Just as Mr. Cupples finished his meal at a small table on the veranda, a large motorcar pulled into the hotel drive. “Who is that?” he asked the waiter.

“Id is der manager,” the young man replied listlessly. “He have been to meed a gendleman by der train.”

The car stopped. The porter hurried forward. Mr. Cupples let out a surprised exclamation as a tall, loosely built man—much younger than himself—stepped out and strode onto the veranda, tossing his hat onto a chair. His high-cheekboned face wore a warm smile; his tweed clothes, hair, and short mustache were slightly disheveled.

“Cupples, by all that’s miraculous!” the man exclaimed, lunging forward before Mr. Cupples could rise, seizing his hand in a firm grip. “My luck is serving me today. This is the second slice within an hour. How are you, my best of friends? And why are you here? Why sit’st thou by that ruined breakfast? Dost thou recall its former pride, or ponder how it passed away? I _am_ glad to see you!”

“I was half expecting you, Trent,” Mr. Cupples replied, smiling broadly. “You look splendid, my dear fellow. I’ll tell you all about it. But you haven’t eaten yet. Will you join me here?”

“Rather!” said the newcomer. “An enormous breakfast—with refined conversation and tears of recognition never dry. Ask young Siegfried to lay a place while I go wash. Won’t take three minutes.” He vanished into the hotel, and Mr. Cupples, after a moment’s hesitation, walked to the telephone in the porter’s office.

When he returned, Trent was already seated, pouring tea, clearly engaged in selecting his food with genuine interest. “I expect this to be a hard day,” he said, speaking in his characteristic jerky rhythm. “I shan’t eat again until evening. You guess why I’m here?”

“Undoubtedly,” said Mr. Cupples. “You’ve come to write about the murder.”

“That’s rather dull phrasing,” Trent replied, slicing into a sole. “I’d say I’ve come as avenger of blood—to hunt down the guilty and vindicate society’s honor. That’s my line. Families waited on at their private residences. Say, Cupples, I’ve made a good start already. Wait a bit—I’ll tell you.”

There was a pause. Trent ate swiftly, distractedly, while Mr. Cupples watched, pleased.

“Your manager,” Trent began, “is a remarkable judge of character. He admires me. Knows more about my best cases than I do. The _Record_ wired last night that I was coming. When I stepped off the train at seven, there he was, waiting with a car the size of a haystack. He’s beside himself with joy. It’s fame.”

He sipped tea. “His first words? ‘Would you like to see the body?’ He thought he could arrange it. The corpse lies in Dr. Stock’s surgery in the village, just as it was found. They’re doing the post-mortem today—so I arrived just in time. He drove me straight there, filling me in along the way. By the time we got there, I was pretty well informed. I suppose the manager has influence with the doctor. Anyway, no objections from him—or the constable, though he insisted I not expose him in print.”

“I saw the body before it was moved,” Mr. Cupples remarked. “Nothing remarkable, except that the bullet wound in the eye barely marred the face and caused little bleeding. The wrists were scratched and bruised. With your trained eye, I imagine you noticed other details.”

“Other details, yes—but they don’t suggest much. Just oddities. Take the wrists. How did you see the bruises?”

Mr. Cupples paused. “Now that you mention it—I remember Manderson was wearing stiff cuffs, covering his hands when I saw him earlier.”

“He always did,” Trent said. “The manager confirmed it. I pointed out to him what you missed: no cuffs were visible. They’d been pulled up inside the sleeves—like someone hurrying into a coat without adjusting them. That’s why you saw the wrists.”

“Well, that’s suggestive,” Mr. Cupples observed mildly. “Perhaps he rushed through dressing.”

“But did he?” Trent countered. “The manager said exactly what you did—‘He was always a bit of a swell.’ Then he added: ‘Look at his shoes. Mr. Manderson was particular about footwear. But those laces were tied in a hurry.’ I agreed. ‘And he left his false teeth in his room,’ he said. ‘Doesn’t that prove he was flustered?’ I allowed it looked that way. But then I asked: ‘If he was so pressed, why part his hair so carefully? That parting is a work of art. Why wear so much? He had full underclothes, studs, suspenders, watch chain, keys—everything. What’s the logic?’ The manager couldn’t explain. Can you?”

Mr. Cupples reflected. “Maybe he was only hurried at the end—coat and shoes last.”

“But not false teeth,” Trent said. “Ask anyone who wears them. And he didn’t wash at all—unusual for a neat man. That suggests haste from the start. Then there’s this: one waistcoat pocket was lined for his gold watch. But he put the watch in the other. Anyone with habits knows how strange that is. So—signs of panic, signs of calm. For now, I’m not guessing. I need to reconnoiter. If I can get on the right side of the household.”

Trent resumed eating, thoughtful.

Mr. Cupples smiled. “That’s precisely where I can help.”

Trent looked up, surprised. “You told me you half expected me. Go on.”

“Mrs. Manderson,” Mr. Cupples said, “is my niece.”

“What!” Trent dropped knife and fork with a clatter. “Cupples, you’re joking.”

“I’m serious,” Mr. Cupples replied earnestly. “Her father, John Peter Domecq, was my wife’s brother. I’ve never mentioned her marriage before. It’s been painful. But to return—last night, when I was at the house—by the way, you can see it from here. You passed it in the car.” He pointed to a red-roofed house among poplars, three hundred yards away, isolated from the village below.

“I did,” Trent said. “The manager told me about it on the way from Bishopsbridge.”

“Others here know of you,” Mr. Cupples continued. “Last night, Mr. Bunner—one of Manderson’s secretaries—hoped the _Record_ would send you. He mentioned your past cases. Mabel—my niece—was interested when I told her. She’s holding up remarkably well. She remembers reading your articles on the Abinger case. She hates the press involvement and begged me to keep journalists away. But she said you seemed capable—so if you came, she wouldn’t block your inquiries.”

Trent leaned across the table and shook his hand silently. Mr. Cupples, delighted, went on.

“I called her just now. She’s glad you’re here. She asks me to say you may ask any questions, and the house and grounds are yours. She prefers not to see you—she’s staying in her sitting room. A detective already questioned her, and she feels unable to endure more. She says she can’t offer anything useful. The two secretaries and Martin, the butler—an intelligent man—can answer your questions.”

Trent finished his breakfast, brow furrowed. He lit a pipe slowly and sat on the veranda rail. “Cupples,” he said quietly, “is there something about this you know—and would rather not tell me?”

Mr. Cupples started, staring. “What do you mean?”

“I mean about the Mandersons. Here’s a man murdered violently, and no one seems to care. The manager spoke of him as coolly as if he’d never met him—though they’ve been neighbors for years. You talk about it coldly. Mrs. Manderson—well, I’ve heard of women more devastated by their husband’s death. Is there something wrong here? Was Manderson… peculiar? I traveled with him once, but never spoke. I only know his public image—repulsive enough. This might matter. That’s why I ask.”

Mr. Cupples paused. He touched his beard, gazed at the sea. Finally, he turned.

“I see no reason not to tell you—between us. But this must never be mentioned. The truth is, no one really liked Manderson. Those closest to him disliked him most.”

“Why?”

“Most couldn’t explain. I tried to understand my own feelings. I sensed a total absence of empathy. He wasn’t rude, vicious, or dull—he could be fascinating. But I felt he’d sacrifice anyone for his goals, for imposing his will. Perhaps I’m imagining it. But Mabel—my niece—was deeply unhappy. I’m nearly twice your age, though you make me feel younger. I’ve heard many marital troubles, but none like hers. I’ve known her since childhood. I know—without exaggeration—that she is one of the most amiable, honorable women alive. But Manderson had made her miserable.”

“What did he do?”

“She said he nursed a perpetual grievance. Kept distance. Said nothing. I don’t know how it started. All she’d say was he had no cause. She knew what was in his mind, but she’s proud. This went on for months. Then, a week ago, she wrote to me. I’m her only close relative. Her mother died young. After John Peter passed, I was like a father to her—until her marriage five years ago. She asked me to come. I came at once. That’s why I’m here.”

Mr. Cupples paused, sipping tea. Trent smoked, watching the June landscape.

“I wouldn’t go to White Gables,” Mr. Cupples continued. “You know my views on capitalism, labor, and power. You know the abuses Manderson committed—especially in the Pennsylvania coal-fields three years ago. I saw him not just as a man I disliked, but as a criminal, a disgrace. I came here instead. I saw Mabel. She told me what I’ve said. The strain, the humiliation, the pretense—she was breaking. She asked for advice. I said she should confront him. But she refused. She’d always ignored his change, and nothing would make her admit injury once pride set in. Life is full of stubborn silences,” he sighed, “and cultivated misunderstandings.”

“Did she love him?” Trent asked sharply.

Mr. Cupples hesitated. “Had she any love left?”

“She didn’t,” he said slowly. “But don’t misjudge her. No force on earth would make her admit it—even to herself—while she felt bound to him. And apart from this recent coldness, he’d always been considerate, generous.”

“You said she refused to speak to him.”

“She did. And I knew from experience—Domecqs don’t budge when dignity’s involved. So I thought carefully. Next day, I met Manderson passing the hotel. I asked for a few minutes. He stepped inside the gate. We hadn’t spoken since her marriage, but he remembered me. I told him what Mabel had confided. I said I wouldn’t approve or condemn her bringing me in—but she was suffering, and I had a right to know how he justified placing her in such pain.”

“And how did he react?” Trent asked, smiling faintly at the scene.

“Not well. Not at all. He said, ‘See here, Cupples, you don’t want to butt in. My wife can look after herself. I’ve found that out, along with other things.’ He stayed calm—though I saw a light in his eyes that would scare a guilty man. But I was furious—his tone, his words—so I snapped back. I reminded him the law protects wives from unbearable treatment. I brought up his public record, said men like him weren’t fit to live. I said these things—foolish things—under the eyes of half a dozen people on this very veranda. I noticed them watching me as I walked back.”

“And Manderson?”

“Not a word. He listened, eyes fixed on mine. When I stopped, he smiled slightly and walked away.”

“When was this?”

“The Sunday morning.”

“So you never saw him alive again?”

“No. Or—once. Later that day, on the golf course. I didn’t speak. And next morning, he was found dead.”

They sat in silence. Guests returning from the sea chattered nearby. The waiter approached. Mr. Cupples rose, took Trent’s arm, and led him to the long tennis lawn beside the hotel.

“I have a reason for telling you all this,” he began.

“Trust you for that,” Trent replied, refilling his pipe. He lit it, smoked, then said, “Let me guess your reason—if you like.”

Mr. Cupples’s solemn face softened into a slight smile. He said nothing.

“You thought,” Trent mused, “that I might discover the depth of the conflict between the Mandersons. You feared I’d suspect Mrs. Manderson—let my imagination run wild. So you told me the truth, to prevent speculation, and to affirm your trust in her. Is that it?”

Mr. Cupples nodded.
