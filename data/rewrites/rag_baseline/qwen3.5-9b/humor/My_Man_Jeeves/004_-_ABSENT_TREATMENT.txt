"Absent Treatment"

Let me recount the saga of Bobbie Cardew. It is a tale of profound interest, devoid of pretension, driven entirely by its moral imperative. If you are a man, heed this warning; if you are a woman, you will find it compelling, for it chronicles the precise mechanics by which a woman renders a man utterly fed up with existence.

Those acquainted with Bobbie recently may be startled to learn that prior to his transformation, his defining characteristic was a catastrophic failure of memory. Many, meeting him after the shift, express disbelief at this revelation. Yet, it is factual. Trust me.

In my early acquaintance with him, Bobbie Cardew was the quintessential young rotter within a four-mile radius. Critics have labeled me a simpleton, but I operated in a league far below his. Where I was a minus-six handicap, he was a plus-four genius of folly. To secure his attendance at a dinner required a logistical operation: a letter dispatched weekly, followed by a telegram and telephone call the preceding day, culminating in a taxi-messenger arriving forty-five minutes prior to the appointed hour to ensure both his presence and the chauffeur's accuracy. Even with this exhaustive protocol, success was not guaranteed, contingent upon whether he had already departed town.

Paradoxically, Bobbie possessed a latent stratum of intelligence, accessible only via detonation. I believed marriage to be that explosive agent. He wedded. Like a puppy fixated on a buzzing insect, unaware of the sting awaiting him, Bobbie pursued matrimony with ecstatic fervor, only to gradually discover the harsh realities of the union.

His bride, Mary Anthony, was not the conventional object of male adoration. She earned her livelihood. To a man unused to labor, there exists a peculiar romantic allure in a woman who sustains herself through sheer grit. Standing five-foot-six, with a cascade of red-gold hair, grey eyes, and a chin of indomitable resolve, she was a hospital nurse. Following Bobbie's polo accident, she was assigned to tend his wounds, applying cooling unguents and soothing his brow. Within a week of his recovery, they registered their marriage. A classic romance.

Bobbie announced the nuptials to me at the club, introducing Mary the following day. I admired her. My name is Reggie Pepper; my uncle, Edward Pepper of Wells & Co., left me a substantial fortune, allowing me the leisure to observe others earning their keep under duress. Mary, an orphan who had navigated life independently for years, commanded my respect.

Initially, Mary and I harmonized splendidly. Though our current relationship is strained, I refer here to the past. Mary viewed Bobbie as the pinnacle of masculinity, gazing at him with adoration whenever she deemed me oblivious. Bobbie reciprocated this sentiment. Consequently, I concluded that provided Bobbie did not neglect the wedding ceremony, their prospects for happiness were promising.

Time accelerated. A year elapsed. They established a residence, and I visited frequently, observing a facade of domestic tranquility. I failed to comprehend why men feared marriage, assuming it to be preferable to the alternative.

However, the incident of the Quiet Dinner marked the fracture in love's youthful illusion.

Encountering Bobbie in Piccadilly, he invited me to dinner at their flat. Foolishly, eschewing caution, I accepted. Upon arrival, Mrs. Bobbie presented a visage that stunned me. Her golden hair was coiffed into elaborate waves adorned with diamonds, and she wore a dress of impeccable elegance. If this was her attire for a private, quiet meal, I reasoned, Bobbie's devotion to domesticity was understandable.

"Here's old Reggie, dear," Bobbie announced cheerfully. "I've brought him home for dinner. Shall I summon the kitchen?"

Mary stared at him with the expression of one encountering a stranger. Her complexion flushed crimson, then drained to the pallor of death, before dissolving into a nervous, brittle laugh. It was a spectacle that made me wish for immediate egress. Composing herself, she greeted me with practiced civility.

"I am so glad you could join us, Mr. Pepper," she smiled.

Post-prandially, she engaged in lively conversation, teasing Bobbie, and performed ragtime on the piano with feigned nonchalance. On the surface, it appeared a jovial gathering. Yet, I perceived the underlying tension. She was exerting immense effort to maintain composure, suppressing a desire to release her frustration. I departed promptly.

The following day, Bobbie appeared at the club in a state of manic euphoria, reminiscent of a solitary gumdrop at an Eskimo tea party. Eager to discuss his ordeal, he launched into a monologue.

"Do you realize how long I've been married?" he asked.

"Not approximately a year?" I queried.

"No," he replied somberly. "Precisely a year. Yesterday!"

Enlightenment struck me.

"Yesterday was...?"

"The anniversary of our wedding. I had planned to take Mary to the Savoy Opera, followed by Covent Garden. She specifically requested Caruso. I held the box tickets in my pocket. Throughout dinner, a nagging suspicion plagued me that I had omitted something crucial, yet I could not identify it."

"Till your wife mentioned it?"

He nodded.

"She... mentioned it," he murmured reflectively.

I refrained from prying further. Women possessing Mary's formidable features may act as angels, but when they shed their wings, they strike with lethal precision.

"To be candid, old top," Bobbie confessed, his voice trembling, "my reserves at home are critically depleted."

With no viable solution apparent, I lit a cigarette and waited. Eventually, he departed. Standing at the window overlooking Piccadilly, I observed him. He ambled briefly, paused, resumed walking, and finally entered a jewelry store. This action confirmed the presence of that dormant stratum of sense within him.

From this juncture, my interest in Bobbie's marital trajectory intensified. While one typically observes friends' unions with mild curiosity, this case differed fundamentally. Bobbie represented the immovable mass of incompetence, while Mary embodied the irresistible force of correction. Nature, however, favored Bobbie. Having crafted a chump, she endowed him with a protective armor: severe amnesia. This condition preserves his status as a chump; without it, he might evolve beyond such limitations. Consider my own case: I am a chump. Had I retained half the lessons imparted throughout my life, my hat size would have expanded significantly. Instead, I forgot them. Bobbie operated under identical constraints.

For roughly a week, the memory of that subdued domestic evening served as a tonic. Yet, the impact was insufficient. It dented his armor but failed to penetrate it. Soon, he reverted to his habitual negligence.

Pathetic, indeed. The poor girl loved him, yet lived in terror. She recognized the thin edge of the wedge. A man who forgets his wedding anniversary after one year risks forgetting his marital status entirely by the fourth. Realizing the necessity of intervention, I attempted to counsel Bobbie during a subsequent visit regarding a forgotten errand.

"It's trivial to make a fuss over," Bobbie dismissed. "She knows it stems solely from my infernal inability to retain information. I cannot remember anything. Never could."

Before departing, he produced two sovereigns.

"Oh, by the way," he added.

"What is this for?" I asked, anticipating the answer.

"I owe it to you."

"How so?"

"That wager on Tuesday in the billiard room. Murray versus Brown, a hundred-up match. I backed Brown at two-to-one odds. Murray defeated him by twenty points."

"So you recall certain matters?" I noted.

He became animated, defending his character against accusations of dishonesty.

"Settle down, lad," I interjected.

Adopting a paternal tone, I addressed him directly. "My old college friend, you must rally immediately. Your current trajectory promises a severe collision. You must exert effort. Do not claim incapacity. This payment proves that while your memory is flawed, it functions selectively. Ensure wedding anniversaries are prioritized. It may require mental strain, but evasion is impossible."

"I concede your point," Bobbie sighed. "Yet, I fail to grasp why she values these trivial dates. Does it matter if I forget our wedding date, her birthdate, or when the cat contracted measles? She knows my affection remains constant regardless of my mnemonic capabilities."

"Affection is insufficient for a woman," I countered. "They demand demonstration. Remember this, and you shall fare well. Neglect it, and chaos ensues."

He gnawed on the knob of his cane.

"Women are bewildering," he muttered gloomily.

"You should have anticipated this before marrying one," I retorted.

I realized further persuasion was futile. I had distilled the issue into its essence, expecting a paradigmatic shift. None occurred. Abandoning argument, I adopted a stance of passive observation. When a man insists on courting disaster, the prudent course is to await the inevitable crash.

Bobbie did not succumb immediately. Weeks, then months passed without incident. Occasionally, he entered the club with a cloud obscuring his usual radiance, signaling domestic turmoil. However, it was not until late spring that the thunderbolt struck him squarely in the thorax.

"Reggie! Old top! She's gone!"
