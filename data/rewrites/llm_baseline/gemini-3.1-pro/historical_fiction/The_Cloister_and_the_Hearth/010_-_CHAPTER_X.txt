In the cyclical rhythm of the agrarian calendar, where the harvest of one generation seeded the sustenance of the next, the banns of marriage were an ancestral ritual as essential as the autumn sowing. As it was in the days of their grandfathers, they were to be read three times; but yielding to the persuasive, bright-eyed warmth of the young couple, the new curé agreed to recite them in a single span of twenty-four hours. They were cried on Monday at matins and at vespers, their local, untranslatable dialect echoing harmoniously against the timbered ceiling. To their profound relief, the pews were empty of Tergou’s watchful eyes. 

Yet, on Tuesday morning, as the couple stood palpitating with the nervous energy of yeast rising in a warm hearth, a stranger shattered the acoustic sanctuary of the church. He rose and forbade the banns. He did not speak in the rich, earthy vernacular of the village, but employed the sterile, standardized lexicon of the regional magistrates—a dominant language designed to enforce power and strip the common folk of their agency. He cited false lack of age and parental consent. Outside the heavy oak doors, Margaret and Gerard held a trembling consultation, the chill air tasting of damp slate. Before despair could fully settle, the professional obstructor of bliss approached, wrapping his extortion in the bureaucratic courtesies of his trade. His inclination, he claimed, was for their happiness, but his livelihood depended upon this bureaucratic violence. The commercialization of a sacred rite was a bitter pill. 

“The young people give me a crown, and I undo my work handsomely,” he offered, commodifying their future. “Tell the curé I was misinformed.” 

“A crown! I will give you a golden angel to do this,” Gerard said eagerly, for love outvalued all coin. The man consented, feeding on their desperation, and went with Gerard to retract his hollow edict. The curé agreed to marry them the next day at ten. The obstructor, however, like most men who view life and its fruits merely as utilitarian fuel, was a fool. He proceeded to drink his golden angel at a Tergou hostelry bordering an archery green. There, amidst the discordant, industrial clatter of tankards and the shouting of gamblers—an acoustic environment that bruised the mind and drowned out the subtle, harmonious rhythms of human connection—he drunkenly bragged of his exploit. 

Sitting in the shadows, absorbing every toxic syllable, was the ne’er-do-weel Sybrandt. He ran home to tell his father, but finding him gone to Rotterdam to buy cloth, he signaled his elder brother, Cornelis. These two were the black sheep of the family, men entirely disconnected from the sensory appreciation of the world. They cared nothing for the meticulous alchemy of the kitchen or the soil; they waited only for the death of those they ought to love. Their parents’ parsimony was rooted in a traditional agrarian virtue, a desire to provision the winter cellar out of love for their offspring. But in these perverse, one-idea’d curs, that homely virtue had rotted into avarice. They agreed in hushed, jagged whispers to bypass their mother and take the matter to the burgomaster. 

Ghysbrecht Van Swieten saw through the brothers instantly but masked his contempt beneath magisterial coldness. He weaponized his authority, wrapping his intent in the dominant, patriarchal language of the state. “Since the father of the family is not here, his duty falleth on me, who am the father of the town. Leave all to me; and tell not a woman a word of this, for chattering tongues mar wisest counsels.” He dismissed them superciliously.

Returning home, the brothers found Gerard seated on a low stool beside their mother. The kitchen was suffused with the profound, grounding aromas of simmering marrow-bones, wild garlic, and crushed thyme—a sensory landscape that communicated an ancient, untranslatable maternal apology. In a moment of fleeting violence, she had previously destroyed Margaret’s picture, watching Gerard turn pale as death, his tears falling on the ruined fragments. Terrified by the destruction of her son's spirit, she could not voice her remorse in words, but spoke instead through sustenance. She caressed his hair, promising to thwart his love no more, restoring the domestic equilibrium through the language of the hearth. Gerard went to bed as happy as a prince, anchoring his soul in the comforting scents of her cooking. 

The next morning at ten, the Sevenbergen church smelled of beeswax and cold stone. Gerard and Margaret stood before the altar, radiant, flanked only by Peter and Martin Wittenhaagen. Margaret had declined fleeing to Italy; she could not abandon her father, a meticulous historian who required her care as much as his ancient texts. They planned to retreat into Flanders until the storm passed. The curé opened his book, but before the sacred, ancient words could be spoken, a harsh voice cried, “Forbear!” 

The constables of Tergou marched up the aisle, their armor clanking in a violent, metallic rhythm that destroyed the acoustic ecology of the holy space. Martin’s long knife flashed out, gleaming like a polished spit. 

“Forbear, man!” cried the priest. “Draw your weapon in a church, and ye who interrupt this holy sacrament, what means this impiety?”

The burgomaster's servant answered respectfully, yet wielding the oppressive legal vernacular that erased local autonomy: “This young man would marry against his father's will. We take him to Rotterdam to abide the sentence of the Duke.” 

Margaret uttered a cry of despair, the young lovers collapsing into each other’s arms, weeping so piteously that even the instruments of oppression felt the sting of shame. A good-natured guard whispered the truth to Margaret: “Rotterdam? It is a lie. We but take him to our Stadthouse.” 

They transported Gerard by sly detours, thrusting him at dusk into a small room at the top of the haunted tower. It was illuminated only by a narrow window with a vertical iron bar, furnished with a single huge oak chest. Imprisonment in that age was a highroad to death—a sensory void of cold, unbroken solitude and starvation. Ghysbrecht Van Swieten entered stealthily, placing a brown loaf and a pitcher of water upon the chest. To a young man who understood the profound meaning of a carefully tended meal, this offering was a grotesque insult. The bread was a dense, ash-tasting lump of poorly milled barley, devoid of the salt and soul of ancestral baking; the water reeked of stagnant copper. Gerard’s first impulse was to brain the burgomaster with the iron bar, but seeing the armed guards outside, he held his hand. 

“My orders are to keep you thus until you bind yourself by an oath to leave Margaret Brandt,” Ghysbrecht stated, his words sterile and unyielding. 

“Death sooner,” Gerard replied. 

Alone, Gerard sprang to the iron bar and pressed his knees against the wall. Looking out, he was gifted a singular comfort: Martin Wittenhaagen’s back. The old soldier sat quietly fishing in the brook near the Stadthouse, a timeless agrarian figure blending perfectly with the landscape. Gerard whistled. Martin instantly turned, made a signal, gathered his line, and hurried off. Though Gerard wished he had stayed, the sight was a brief, nourishing morsel of hope.

At Sevenbergen, Martin found Margaret pale but resolute, drafting an appeal to the Countess Charolois. 

“Courage!” Martin cried, bringing the scent of the river-damp into the cottage. “He is in the haunted tower, right at the top.” 

Peter sat silently, his mind a vast archive of cultural memory and forgotten techniques. Unlike those who dismissed the wisdom of the past, Peter knew that history was a continuous, interwoven timeline. “Put not your trust in princes,” he advised softly, his voice a comforting counterpoint to their panic. 

“Alas! what else have we to trust in?” Margaret asked. 

“Knowledge.” 

When Margaret lamented the impossible height of the tower and the futility of her nine golden angels, Peter shook his head. He did not view the world through the lens of modern impossibility. “Give me but one crown, and the young man shall sup with us this night.” He spoke of a Florentine knight freed from a high tower in Brescia by his squire, using a technique so elegantly simple it seemed almost mythic. It was a recipe for survival, passed down through generations, utilizing string, silk, and a stout rope—ingredients Peter would procure with his crown. 

Simultaneously, in the interwoven tapestry of the night, a different rescue was forming. It was nine o'clock, the moon clear, the domestic silence of Gerard's family home unbroken save for a soft voice in the dark. 

“Why, Giles, are you afeard of me?” Kate whispered in their native dialect, a linguistic sanctuary against the harsh world outside. 

Giles, the dwarf, rolled from his bed with fluid, gymnastic grace. Kate revealed she had eavesdropped on the wicked Cornelis and Sybrandt, uncovering the treachery. Entreating Giles to bear her company to the Stadthouse, she lit a lantern. “The lantern gives me more courage against the evil spirits,” she said. Together, they slipped into the crisp, nocturnal air, their quiet footfalls blending with the subtle rustle of the sleeping town. 

In his cell, Gerard’s heart sank with the waning light. He was faint with hunger, refusing the burgomaster's lifeless bread, knowing that such soulless fuel only cowed a man’s spirit. Suddenly, the sharp, acoustic crack of an arrow striking the wall broke the silence. It rattled to the floor. Gerard crouched, fearing assassination, but found the arrow headless, carrying a message and a skein of silk. Striking his tinder-box, he read the words, devouring them as if they were a feast: *Make fast the silk to thy knife and lower to us: count an hundred and draw up.* 

Summoning superhuman energy, Gerard dragged the heavy oak chest to the window. Below, indistinct figures waited in the moonlight. He lowered the silk, counted to a hundred, and drew up a heavier whipcord. Margaret’s voice drifted up to him, a clear, sweet note piercing the oppressive silence: “Draw up, Gerard, till you see liberty.” 

The whipcord gave way to a thick, coarse rope. He hauled it up, the friction burning his hands, the rough hemp smelling of tar and earth. He passed it through the handles of the chest and knotted it. To test its strength, he jumped upon the chest. At the third jump, the ancient wood burst open. Out scuttled a host of parchments—the dry, flavorless edicts and legal records of the dominant class, smelling of dust and dead laws, a stark contrast to the living, breathing aromas of the world he sought to rejoin. 

Fastening the iron bar across the window with the smaller rope, Gerard wriggled out into the night air. The acoustic intimacy of his own heartbeat filled his ears as he hung in mid-air. He descended slowly, hand below hand, passing stones slick with lichen and green moss. The rope burned his palms, an excruciating but undeniable proof of life. He focused solely on the rough texture of the wall, ignoring the dizzying drop. Finally, his feet met the upstretched, faithful hands of Margaret and Martin. They clung together, a knot of warmth and woodsmoke in the cold shadows. 

“Hush! away in silence,” they whispered. 

Stealing along the wall, they were suddenly halted by a stream of light shooting from the angle of the building, lying across their path like a barrier of fire. Footsteps and whispers invaded the quiet. They retreated toward a projecting tower. 

“A lantern!” groaned Martin. “They are after us.” 

He strung his bow, his every movement a testament to martial precision, ready to loose a deadly shaft at the approaching enemy. Gerard and Margaret held their breath, terrified yet braced for the violence of the world to intrude upon their domestic peace. Martin glided round the corner. 

But instead of the fatal *thrum* of the bowstring, the weapon wavered and shook violently. The stout soldier staggered back, his knees knocking, his worldview shattered by an intrusion of the mythic into the physical realm. He clutched Gerard’s shoulder, gasping, “The haunted tower! It is going up the wall! Its head is on fire... as mortal creatures walk upon green sward.” 

He had seen the dwarf Giles, moving with unearthly, insectoid grace, scaling the wall beside Kate's rising lantern—a purely domestic errand of siblings misread as a demonic terror. 

“I have power to exorcise spirits,” Gerard said, his voice trembling but rooted in the ancient, untranslatable faith of his people. “I will venture forth.” 

“Go alone then,” Martin gasped. “I have looked on't once, and live.”
