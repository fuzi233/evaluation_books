XVII. "Your cousin the Countess called on mother while you were away," Janey Archer announced to her brother one evening after his return from St. Augustine. The young man, dining quietly with his mother and sister, looked up in surprise. Mrs. Archer’s eyes remained demurely fixed on her plate, as if she had already anticipated the news—and perhaps resented that he should be taken aback by it.

"She wore a black velvet polonaise with jet buttons and carried a tiny green monkey muff—never have I seen her so elegantly dressed," Janey continued, her voice tinged with admiration. "She came alone, early Sunday afternoon. Luckily, the fire was lit in the drawing-room. She had one of those new card-cases, too. Said she wanted to know us because you'd been so kind to her."

Newland smiled. "Madame Olenska always speaks like that about her friends. She's delighted to be back among her own people again."

"Yes," said Mrs. Archer, her tone measured. "She told us so. And I must say, she seems truly grateful to be here."

"I hope you liked her, mother," Newland ventured gently.

Mrs. Archer pressed her lips together. "She certainly makes an effort to please—even when calling upon an old woman."

"Mother doesn’t think her simple," Janey interjected, watching her brother closely.

"It's just my old-fashioned way," Mrs. Archer replied, almost apologetically. "Dear May is my ideal."

Ah," Newland murmured. "They're not alike."

Archer had left St. Augustine burdened with messages for Old Mrs. Mingott, and a few days after returning to town, he paid her a visit. The elderly lady greeted him with unusual warmth. She was deeply thankful that he had persuaded the Countess Olenska to abandon the idea of divorce. When he confessed that he had left his office without permission—rushing down to Florida simply to see May—she let out a soft, hearty chuckle and patted his knee with her plump hand.

"Ah, ah—so you kicked over the traces, did you? And I suppose Augusta and Welland looked as though the world had ended? But little May—she knew better, I'll wager?"

"I hoped she did," he said, "though she wouldn't agree to what I went down to ask."

"And what was that?" she asked, eyes gleaming.

"I wanted her to promise we’d marry in April. What good is another year of waiting?"

Mrs. Manson Mingott twisted her mouth into a mock-prudish grimace and twinkled at him through half-lidded eyes. "'Ask Mamma,' I suppose—the usual story. Ah, these Mingotts—all alike! Born in a rut and can't be pulled out. When I built this house, you’d have thought I was moving to California! Nobody ever _had_ built above Fortieth Street—no, says I, nor above the Battery either, before Columbus discovered America. No, no; not one of them wants to be different. They’re scared of it like smallpox. Ah, my dear Mr. Archer, I thank my stars I’m nothing but a vulgar Spicer. But only one of my children takes after me—my little Ellen."

She paused, still smiling, then added casually: "Now, why in the world didn’t you marry my little Ellen?"

Archer laughed. "For one thing, she wasn’t there to be married."

"No—of course not. More’s the pity. And now it’s too late. Her life is finished."

She spoke with the cold certainty of age, casting earth onto buried dreams. A chill passed through the young man. He hurried to change the subject. "Can’t I persuade you to use your influence with the Wellands, Mrs. Mingott? I wasn’t made for long engagements."

Old Catherine beamed. "No, I can see that. You’ve got a quick eye. When you were a boy, I’m sure you liked being helped first." She threw back her head and laughed, making her chins ripple like waves. "Ah, here’s my Ellen now!"

The portières parted, and Madame Olenska stepped forward, smiling. Her face glowed with quiet joy. She held out her hand to Archer, bowing slightly as she kissed her grandmother’s cheek. "I was just saying to him," Mrs. Mingott declared, "‘Now, why didn’t you marry my little Ellen?’"

Madame Olenska turned to Archer, still smiling. "And what did he answer?"

"Oh, my darling, I leave you to find that out!" the old lady teased. "He’s been down to Florida to see his sweetheart."

"Yes, I know," she replied softly, her gaze lingering on him. "I went to see your mother to ask where you’d gone. I sent a note you never answered. I feared you were ill."

He mumbled something about leaving suddenly, in haste, and intending to write from St. Augustine.

"And once you were there, you never thought of me again!" she said lightly, though her smile seemed carefully composed.

*If she still needs me,* he thought, stung by her tone, *she’s determined not to show it.* He wanted to thank her for visiting his mother—but under the old woman’s sharp, amused stare, he felt unable to speak.

"Look at him—in such a rush to marry he took French leave and ran off to beg the silly girl on his knees! That’s how a lover behaves—that’s how handsome Bob Spicer swept off my poor mother, then grew tired of her before I was weaned—though they only waited eight months for me! But there—you’re not a Spicer, young man. Luckily for you and for May. Only poor Ellen carries any of their wicked blood. The rest are all model Mingotts," she declared scornfully.

Archer noticed that Madame Olenska, seated beside her grandmother, was studying him with quiet intensity. The sparkle had faded from her eyes. "Surely, Granny," she said gently, "we can persuade them between us to do as he wishes."

He rose to leave. As their hands touched, he sensed she was waiting for him to mention her unanswered letter.

"When can I see you?" he asked as she walked him to the door.

"Whenever you like," she said. "But soon—if you want to see the little house again. I’m moving next week."

A pang struck him—the memory of those dim, intimate evenings in her low-ceilinged drawing room. Though brief, they were filled with meaning. "Tomorrow evening?"

She nodded. "Tomorrow—yes, but early. I’m going out."

It was Sunday. If she was going out on a Sunday evening, it could only mean Mrs. Lemuel Struthers’s. He felt a flicker of annoyance—not at her going there (he actually approved of her independence, even against the van der Luydens), but because she would surely meet Beaufort there—someone she must have known she’d encounter. Perhaps she was going precisely for that reason.

"Very well," he repeated, resolving inwardly to arrive late—either to stop her from leaving or to find her already gone. That would be simpler, he decided.

Yet when he arrived at half-past eight beneath the wisteria, he was earlier than planned—though not by much. A restless impulse had driven him there sooner than intended. He reminded himself that Mrs. Struthers’s gatherings weren’t grand affairs; guests often arrived early to minimize their transgression.

But what he hadn’t expected was finding hats and overcoats in the hall. Why had she invited him early if she was hosting dinner?

Closer inspection revealed two peculiar garments. One was a shaggy yellow ulster, cut wide and low—"reach-me-down" style. The other was a battered, rust-colored cloak with a cape—something resembling a "Macfarlane"—clearly worn by someone of immense size. It reeked faintly of damp sawdust, as if it had spent years leaning against barroom walls. On top lay a ragged grey scarf and a semiclerical felt hat.

Archer raised an eyebrow at Nastasia, who met his look with a fatalistic “Gia!” as she opened the drawing-room door.

Inside, his hostess was absent. But standing by the fireplace was another woman—tall, thin, loosely formed, dressed in intricate loops and fringes, with plaids and stripes arranged in a chaotic pattern. Her hair, fading to white, was adorned with a Spanish comb and black lace scarf. Silk mittens, visibly darned, covered her arthritic hands.

Beside her stood two men, enveloped in cigar smoke. One, Archer recognized with surprise, was Ned Winsett. The other, older and enormous, wore morning clothes untouched since morning. His head was crumpled and leonine, his arms moved in broad, sweeping gestures, as if blessing a crowd.

All three stared at an enormous bouquet of crimson roses, anchored by a knot of purple pansies, resting on the sofa where Madame Olenska usually sat.

"What they must have cost at this season—though, of course, it’s the sentiment that matters!" the woman sighed, speaking in short, breathy bursts.

They turned as Archer entered. She stepped forward, offering her hand. "Dear Mr. Archer—almost my cousin Newland!" she said. "I am the Marchioness Manson."

He bowed. "Pleased to meet you."

"My Ellen has taken me in for a few days. I came from Cuba, where I’ve wintered with Spanish friends—delightful, distinguished people: the highest nobility of old Castile. I wish you could know them! But I was summoned by our dear friend here, Dr. Carver. You don’t know Dr. Agathon Carver, founder of the Valley of Love Community?"

Dr. Carver inclined his head gravely. The Marchioness continued: "Ah, New York—New York—how little the life of the spirit has reached it! But I see you do know Mr. Winsett."

"Oh, yes—I reached him some time ago," Winsett said dryly, "but not by that route."

The Marchioness shook her head reprovingly. "How do you know, Mr. Winsett? The spirit bloweth where it listeth."

"List—oh, list!" Dr. Carver rumbled in a deep murmur.

"But do sit down, Mr. Archer. We four have enjoyed a delightful dinner. My child has gone upstairs to dress. She expects you—she’ll be down in a moment. We were admiring these marvelous flowers, which will surprise her when she returns."

Winsett remained standing. "I fear I must go. Please tell Madame Olenska we shall all feel lost when she leaves our street. This house has been an oasis."

"Ah, but she won’t abandon you," the Marchioness said. "Poetry and art are the breath of life to her. Is it poetry you write, Mr. Winsett?"

"Well, no—but I sometimes read it," Winsett replied, nodding at the group before slipping out.

"A caustic spirit—_un peu sauvage_. But so witty! Dr. Carver, you _do_ think him witty?"

"I never think of wit," Dr. Carver declared severely.

"Ah—ah—how merciless he is to us weak mortals, Mr. Archer! But he lives only in the life of the spirit. Tonight, he’s preparing his lecture at Mrs. Blenker’s. Dr. Carver, would there be time before you depart to explain your illuminating discovery of the Direct Contact?"

"No," the doctor said, checking his massive gold watch against Madame Olenska’s tiny traveling clock. "It’s nearly nine. We shouldn’t detain you."

He gathered himself slowly, preparing to leave. "I shall see you later, dear friend?"

"As soon as Ellen’s carriage arrives, I’ll join you," the Marchioness promised. "I hope the lecture hasn’t begun."

Dr. Carver looked at Archer thoughtfully. "Perhaps, if this young gentleman is interested in my experiences, Mrs. Blenker might allow you to come with me?"

"Oh, dear friend—if possible, I’m sure she’d be delighted. But I fear my Ellen counts on Mr. Archer herself."

"That," said Dr. Carver, "is unfortunate—but here is my card."

He handed it over. Archer read the Gothic script:

/c +-----------------------+
| Agathon Carver |
| The Valley of Love |
| Kittasquattamy, N. Y. |
+-----------------------+
c/

Dr. Carver bowed and departed. The Marchioness, sighing—perhaps in relief—motioned Archer to sit.

"Ellen will be down in a moment. Before she comes, I’m so glad of this quiet moment with you."

He murmured a polite response. She continued, her voice low and breathless: "I know everything, dear Mr. Archer. My child has told me all you’ve done for her. Your wise advice, your courageous firmness—thank heaven it wasn’t too late!"

He listened, uneasy. Had anyone else heard of his intervention in her private life?

"Madame Olenska exaggerates," he said. "I simply gave her a legal opinion, as she asked."

"Ah, but in doing so—you became the unconscious instrument of… of… what word do we moderns use for Providence, Mr. Archer?" she asked, tilting her head and lowering her lids mysteriously.

"Little did you know," she whispered, glancing over her shoulder as if fearful of eavesdroppers, "that at that very moment, I was being appealed to—from across the Atlantic!"

She leaned forward, tapping her chest with a delicate finger. "By the Count himself—my poor, mad, foolish Olenski—who asks only to take her back on her own terms."

"Good God!" Archer exclaimed, rising.

"You’re horrified? Of course. I understand. I don’t defend poor Stanislas—he’s always called me his best friend. He doesn’t defend himself—he casts himself at her feet—in my person."

She placed a purple finger to her lips. "I have his letter here."

"A letter?" Archer stammered. "Has Madame Olenska seen it?"

The Marchioness shook her head gently. "Time—time. I must have time. I know my Ellen—haughty, intractable—shall I say, just a shade unforgiving?"

"But, heavens," he protested, "to forgive is one thing—to return to that hell—"

"Ah, yes," she agreed. "She calls it that—my sensitive child. But consider the material side, if one may stoop to such things. Do you know what she’s giving up? Those roses there—acres like them, under glass and open sky, in his terraced gardens at Nice! Jewels—historic pearls, the Sobieski emeralds, sables—but she cares nothing for them. Art and beauty—those she lives for, as I always have. And those surrounded her. Paintings, priceless furniture, music, brilliant conversation—ah, that, my dear young man, is what you’ve no conception of here! And she had it all—and the devotion of an adoring husband."

As she spoke, her face softened into ecstatic remembrance. Archer would have laughed—had he not been stunned. He’d never imagined meeting Medora Manson as a messenger of temptation. Yet now, she seemed to rise from the very depths from which Ellen had escaped.

"She knows nothing yet?" he asked sharply.

The Marchioness pressed a finger to her lips. "Nothing directly—but does she suspect? Who can say? The truth is, Mr. Archer, I’ve been waiting to see you. From the moment I heard of your stand, and your influence over her, I hoped you might support me—convince you…"

"That she ought to go back?" he interrupted fiercely. "I’d rather see her dead!"

"Ah," she murmured, unflinching.

For a moment, silence. Then she lifted her head, listening.

"Here she comes," she whispered, pointing to the bouquet. "Am I to understand that you prefer _that_, Mr. Archer? After all, marriage is marriage… and my niece is still a wife…"
