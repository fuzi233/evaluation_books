Margaret was back in Rotterdam long before Gerard opened his eyes; and—hardest stroke of all to her own breast—she left her boy behind her at Gouda.

She had done it with the same practical swiftness that made her knead dough while she reckoned accounts: no fuss, no flourish, only the quick hand and the set mouth. Reicht Heynes she despatched straightway to Gouda with a vicar’s grey frock and a broad felt hat, and such close, motherly instructions on the governing of her “new master” as if she were sending a seasoned cook into a strange kitchen with a list of what would scorch and what would curdle.

Then she went to Jorian Ketel; for she said in her heart, “He is the closest I ever met; so he is the man for me.” And there, between them, were done two sly things—mortal sly, as she counted it; yet not venomous, though she believed they were. What were they? She, being “but a woman,” carried them locked in her bosom to her dying day; and I, being a man—Verbum non amplius addam.

She kept away from Gouda parsonage.

In the heat of wrangle a word passes for wind; but after, it can sit like grit under the eyelid. When Margaret went over all that had been said, she smarted at Gerard’s thinking she could ever forget the priest in the sometime lover. “For what did he take me?” she cried to herself; and though she was downright innocent, pride took modesty by the hand and they walked together into shyness. “Go no more to Gouda parsonage,” whispered Pride; and Modesty, that had no cause to fear, yet feared.

She left little Gerard there to finish the conquest her maternal heart ascribed to him—not to her own wit and patient eloquence—and to anchor his father for ever to humanity.

But that generous stroke of policy cost her dear. She had never yet been parted from her boy an hour; and now the house in Rotterdam, though it smelled of clean rushes and good bread, seemed hollow as a scoured pot. The first day she bore it as women bear toothache—set teeth and silent. The second day it was a pain that ran into her very sleep. The third it grew intolerable.

So at dark, like one ashamed of her own tenderness, she crept up to Gouda parsonage and lurked about the premises like a thief. The night had that soft Dutch hush, broken only by the far slap of water against piling, a dog’s single bark, and the homely clink within of kitchen things. She waited till she saw Reicht Heynes alone by the hearth, and then tapped lightly at the window.

“Reicht,” she whispered, “for pity’s sake bring him out to me unbeknown.”

With Margaret, the person that filled her mind at that moment ceased to have a name, and sank to a pronoun.

Reicht found an excuse to take little Gerard out. There was a moment of rapture—mother’s arms, child’s warm face pressed into her neck, the familiar milk-sweet smell of him under the night air; and then, as if joy must always pay toll, a breaking into tears when they parted again.

They arranged thenceforward that Reicht should bring him half-way to Rotterdam daily at a set hour, and Margaret meet them. The place was by a hedge where the wind combed through reeds and made a sound like flour sifted in a sieve—soft, constant, comforting; and from beyond, the lowing of cattle, and sometimes the creak of a cart, and the far bell that kept the hours like a steady heart.

At these meetings, after the first tumults, and after mother and child had gambolled together like a young cat and her first kitten, the boy would sometimes busy himself alone at their feet with a twig, a pebble, a beetle; and the two women—standing close enough that one cap might have covered both heads—would seize the moment to speak very seriously of Luke Peterson. It began thus:

“Reicht,” said Margaret, “I as good as promised him to marry Luke Peterson. ‘Say you the word,’ quoth I, ‘and I’ll wed him.’”

“Poor Luke!”

“Prithee, why poor Luke?”

“To be bandied about so, atwixt yea and nay.”

“Why, Reicht, you have not been so simple as to cast an eye of affection on the boy, that you take his part?”

“Me?” said Reicht, with a toss of the head that might have shaken a whole pan of curds if she’d been carrying it.

“Oh, I ask your pardon. Well, then, you can do me a good turn.”

“Whisht! whisper! that little darling is listening to every word—eyes like saucers.”

On this both their heads would indeed have gone under one cap.

Two women plotting against one boy? Oh, you great cowardly serpents!

But when these stolen meetings had gone on for about five days, Margaret began to feel the injustice of them and to be irritated as well as unhappy. She cried, too—cried like a woman who can face a market full of hard bargainers, but cannot face an empty cradle.

Then, as if Providence had heard the thin, private sound of her weeping, a cart came to her door. In it, clean as a new penny, beard close shaved, hands white as snow, a little colour in his pale face, sat the Vicar of Gouda in the grey frock and large felt hat she had sent him.

She ran upstairs directly, washed away all trace of tears, put on a cap which—having just come out of the drawer—was theoretically cleaner than the one on her head, and came down to him.

He seized both her hands and kissed them. A tear fell upon them. She turned her head away to hide her own, which started up as if the sight had summoned it.

“My sweet Margaret,” he cried, “why is this? Why hold you aloof from your own good deed? We have been waiting for you every day, and no Margaret.”

“You said things.”

“What! when I was a hermit, and a donkey.”

“Ay! no matter—you said things. And you had no reason.”

“Forget all I said there. Who hearkens the ravings of a maniac? For I see now that in a few months more I should have been a gibbering idiot; yet no mortal could have persuaded me away but you. Oh what an outlay of wit and goodness was yours! But it is not here I can thank and bless you as I ought. No—it is in the home you have given me, among the sheep whose shepherd you have made me; already I love them dearly. There it is I must thank ‘the truest friend ever man had.’ So now I say to you as erst you said to me: come to Gouda manse.”

“Humph! we will see about that.”

“Why, Margaret, think you I had ever kept the dear child so long, but that I made sure you would be back to him from day to day? Oh he curls round my very heartstrings; but what is my title to him compared to thine? Confess now, thou hast had hard thoughts of me for this.”

“Nay, nay, not I. Ah! thou art thyself again; wast ever thoughtful of others. I have half a mind to go to Gouda manse for your saying that.”

“Come then, with half thy mind; ’tis worth the whole of other folk’s.”

“Well, I dare say I will; but there is no such mighty hurry,” said she coolly—though in truth she was burning to go. “Tell me first how you agree with your folk.”

“Why, already my poor have taken root in my heart.”

“I thought as much.”

“And there are such good creatures among them; simple and rough, and superstitious, but wonderfully good.”

“Oh I leave you alone for seeing a grain of good among a bushel of ill.”

“Whisht! whisht! And Margaret—two of them have been ill friends four years and came to the manse each to get on my blind side. But give the glory to God, I got on their bright side, and made them friends, and laugh at themselves for their folly.”

“But are you in very deed their vicar? Answer me that.”

“Certes; have I not been to the bishop and taken the oath, and rung the church bell, and touched the altar, the missal, and the holy cup before the church-wardens? And they have handed me the parish seal—see, here it is. Nay, ’tis a real vicar inviting a true friend to Gouda manse.”

“Then my mind is at ease. Tell me oceans more.”

“Well, sweet one, nearest to me of all my parish is a poor cripple that my guardian angel and his—(her name thou knowest even by this turning of thy head away)—hath placed beneath my roof. Sybrandt and I are that we never were till now—brothers. ’Twould gladden thee, yet sadden thee, to hear how we kissed and forgave one another. He is full of thy praises, and wholly in a pious mind; he says he is happier since his trouble than e’er he was in the days of his strength. Oh! out of my house he ne’er shall go to any place but heaven.”

“Tell me somewhat that happened thyself, poor soul! All this is good, but yet no tidings to me. Do I not know thee of old?”

“Well, let me see. At first I was much dazzled by the sun-light, and could not go abroad (owl!), but that is passed; and good Reicht Heynes—humph!”

“What of her?”

“This to thine ear only, for she is a diamond. Her voice goes through me like a knife, and all voices seem loud but thine, which is so mellow sweet. Stay—now I’ll fit ye with tidings. I spake yesterday with an old man that conceits he is ill-tempered, and sweats to pass for such with others; but oh! so threadbare, and the best good heart beneath.”

“Why, ’tis a parish of angels,” said Margaret, ironically.

“Then why dost thou keep out on’t?” retorted Gerard. “Well, he was telling me there was no parish in Holland where the devil hath such power as at Gouda; and among his instances, says he: ‘We had a hermit, the holiest in Holland; but being Gouda, the devil came for him this week, and took him, bag and baggage; not a ha’porth of him left but a goodish piece of his skin, just for all the world like a hedgehog’s, and a piece o’ old iron furbished up.’”

Margaret smiled.

“Ay, but,” continued Gerard, “the strange thing is, the cave has verily fallen in; and had I been so perverse as resist thee, it had assuredly buried me dead there where I had buried myself alive. Therefore in this I see the finger of Providence, condemning my late, approving my present, way of life. What sayest thou?”

“Nay, can I pierce the like mysteries? I am but a woman.”

“Somewhat more, methinks. This very tale proves thee my guardian angel, and all else avouches it—so come to Gouda manse.”

“Well, go you on, I’ll follow.”

“Nay, in the cart with me.”

“Not so.”

“Why?”

“Can I tell why and wherefore, being a woman? All I know is I seem—to feel—to wish—to come alone.”

“So be it then. I leave thee the cart, being, as thou sayest, a woman; and I’ll go a-foot, being a man again, with the joyful tidings of thy coming.”

When Margaret reached the manse, the first thing she saw was the two Gerards together: the son performing his capriccios upon the little plot, a whirl of sudden crouches and leaps and solemn stops; and the father slouched in a chair in his great hat, pencil and paper in hand, trying very patiently to sketch him.

The sounds of the place came to her like a remembered language: the rasp of pencil, the soft thud of the child’s feet on earth, the faint clack from the kitchen where Catherine moved among her pots, and the bell’s after-hum still lying in the air as smoke lies in a room after a lamp is quenched.

After a warm welcome Gerard showed her his attempts.

“But in vain I strive to fix him,” said he, “for he is incarnate quicksilver. Yet do but note his changes—infinite, but none ungracious; all is supple and easy; and how he melteth from one posture to another.”

He added presently, “Woe to illuminators! Looking on thee, sir baby, I see what awkward, lopsided, ungainly toads I and my fellows painted in missals with, and called them cherubs and seraphs.”

Finally he flung the paper away in despair; and Margaret, with the thrift of love, conveyed it secretly into her bosom, as if to keep there a relic of this hour.

At night they sat round the peat fire. The hearth breathed out a rich, sweet earth-smell; peat does not blaze like resinous wood, but it glows and murmurs, and the room fills with a steady warmth that seems to have a sound of its own. Gerard bade them mark how beautiful the brass candlesticks and other glittering metals were in the glow.

Catherine’s eyes sparkled at that.

“And oh the sheets I lie in here,” said Gerard, “often my conscience pricketh me, and saith, ‘Who art thou to lie in lint like web of snow?’ Dives was ne’er so flaxed as I. And to think there be folk in the world that have all the beautiful things which I have here, yet not content! Let them pass six months in a hermit’s cell, seeing no face of man, then will they find how lovely and pleasant this wicked world is, and eke that men and women are God’s fairest creatures. Margaret was always fair, but never to my eye so bright as now.”

Margaret shook her head incredulously; the warmth made her cheeks bloom, and her hair, loosening a little, caught the peat-light like brown sugar in a pan.

Gerard continued, “My mother was ever good and kind, but I noted not her exceeding comeliness till now.”

“Nor I neither,” said Catherine; “a score years ago I might pass in a crowd, but not now.”

Gerard declared each age had its beauty. “See this mild grey eye,” said he, “that hath looked motherly love upon so many of us—all that love hath left its shadow, and that shadow is a beauty which defieth Time. See this delicate lip, these pure white teeth. See this well-shaped brow, where comeliness just passeth into reverence. Art beautiful in my eyes, mother dear.”

“And that is enough for me, my darling. ’Tis time you were in bed, child. Ye have to preach the morn.”

And Reicht Heynes and Catherine interchanged a look that said, without words, We two have an amiable maniac to superintend; he calls everything beautiful.

The next day was Sunday; and they heard him preach in his own church. It was crammed with persons who came curious, but remained devout. Never was his gift displayed more powerfully. He was himself deeply moved by the first sight of all his people; and his bowels yearned over this flock he had so long neglected. In a single sermon—two hours long, and yet seeming but twenty minutes—he declared the whole scripture: terrified the impenitent and thoughtless, confirmed the wavering, consoled the bereaved and afflicted, uplifted the heart of the poor; and when he ended, left the multitude standing rapt, as if unwilling to believe the divine music of his voice and soul had ceased.

Need I say that two poor women in a corner sat entranced, with streaming eyes? In that packed church the air was thick with woollen breath and damp cloaks, and with it the soft symphony of a congregation—shuffles, sniffles, a child’s stifled cough—until his voice took the whole soundscape in hand and made even those little noises seem like a ground-bass under a great air.

“Wherever gat he it all?” whispered Catherine, with her apron to her eyes. “By our Lady—not from me.”

As soon as they were by themselves Margaret threw her arms round Catherine’s neck and kissed her.

“Mother, mother, I am not quite a happy woman, but oh I am a proud one.”

And she vowed on her knees never by word or deed to let her love come between this young saint and Heaven.

Reader, did you ever stand by the seashore after a storm, when the wind has gone down suddenly? The waves cannot cease with their cause; indeed they seem at first to lash the sounding shore more fiercely than while the wind blew. Still we are conscious that inevitable calm has begun, and is now but rocking them to sleep. So it was with those true and tempest-tossed lovers from that eventful night when they went hand in hand beneath the stars from Gouda hermitage to Gouda manse.

At times a loud wave would come roaring; but it was only memory’s echo of the tempest that had swept their lives. The storm itself was over; and the boiling waters began from that moment to go down—down—down—gently, but inevitably.

This image must serve for the place of details that would be tedious and tame. What best merits heed is the general situation, and the strange complication of feeling it bred. History itself, though a bolder storyteller than romance, shows few things stranger than the footing on which Gerard and Margaret now lived for many years: united by present affection, past familiarity, and a marriage irregular but legal; separated by Holy Church and by their own consciences, which sided unreservedly with Holy Church; separated by the Church, yet united by a living pledge of affection—lawful in every sense at its date.

Living but a few miles from one another, and she calling his mother “mother.” For some years she took her boy to Gouda every Sunday, returning home at dark. Go when she would, it was always fête at Gouda manse, and she was received like a little queen. Catherine in those days was nearly always with her, and Eli very often. Tergou had so little to tempt them compared to Rotterdam; and at last they left it altogether and set up in the capital.

Thus the years glided: so barren now of striking incidents, so void of great hopes, and free from great fears, and so like one another that without dates I could scarce show the march of time. Yet in their sameness were little rites that stitched the days together: Sunday journeys with a basket of baked things for the manse, the smell of butter and mace, the careful wrapping so the crust should not sweat; the evening return as the frogs began their chorus in the ditches and the wind made a low organ of the reeds. It was as if their life, cut off from one language of the heart, had made itself a dialect of gestures, meals, and tones of voice—intimate, exact, and impossible to translate.

Early next year, 1471, the Duchess of Burgundy, with open dissent but secret connivance of the Duke, raised forces to enable her dethroned brother Edward the Fourth of England to invade that kingdom. Our old friend Denys thus enlisted; and passing through Rotterdam to the ships, heard on his way that Gerard was a priest, and Margaret alone. On this he told Margaret that marriage was not a habit of his, but that as his comrade had put it out of his own power to keep troth, he felt bound to offer to keep it for him.

“For a comrade’s honour is dear to us as our own,” said he.

She stared, then smiled. “I choose rather to be still thy she-comrade,” said she; “closer acquainted, we might not agree so well.”

And in her character of she-comrade she equipped him with a new sword of Antwerp make and a double handful of silver. “I give thee no gold,” said she, “for ’tis thrown away as quick as silver, and harder to win back. Heaven send thee safe out of all thy perils; there be famous fair women yonder to beguile thee with their faces, as well as men to hash thee with their axes.”

He was hurried on board at La Vere, and never saw Gerard at that time.

In 1473 Sybrandt began to fail. His pitiable existence had been sweetened by his brother’s inventive tenderness and his own contented spirit, which, his antecedents considered, was truly remarkable. As for Gerard, the day never passed that he did not give two hours to him: reading or singing to him, praying with him, and drawing him about in a soft carriage Margaret and he had made between them—a thing that rolled with the gentlest sound over the floor, as if mercy itself had wheels.

When the poor soul found his end near, he begged Margaret might be sent for. She came at once; and almost with his last breath he sought once more that forgiveness she had long ago accorded. She remained by him till the last; and he died, blessing and blessed, in the arms of the two true lovers he had parted for life. Tantum religio scit suadere boni.

1474 there was a wedding in Margaret’s house: Luke Peterson and Reicht Heynes.

It may seem less strange if I give the purport of that dialogue broken off long before.

Margaret had gone on to say, “Then in that case you can easily make him fancy you; and for my sake you must, for my conscience it pricketh me, and I must needs fit him with a wife—the best I know.” Margaret then instructed Reicht to be always kind and good-humoured to Luke; and she herself would be a model of peevishness to him.

“But be not thou so simple as run me down,” said she. “Leave that to me. Make thou excuses for me; I will make myself black enow.”

Reicht received these orders like an order to sweep a room, and obeyed punctually. She sweetened her voice, softened her eyes, made Luke’s humble meals taste of approval; while Margaret, with a craft that would have served at any great market, contrived to sour his hopes of herself without wounding his pride too cruelly—turning a word, delaying an answer, putting him off with a look, as one might keep a sauce from boiling over by a finger on the pot-lid.

After they had played this double artillery upon poor Luke for a couple of years, he got to look upon Margaret as his fog and wind, and Reicht as his sunshine; and his affections transferred themselves, he scarce knew how or when.

On the wedding day Reicht embraced Margaret and thanked her almost with tears.

“He was always my fancy,” said Margaret, “from the first hour I clapped eyes on him.”

“Heyday, you never told me that. What, Reicht, are you as sly as the rest?”

“Nay, nay,” said Reicht eagerly; “but I never thought you would really part with him to me. In my country the mistress looks to be served before the maid.”

Margaret settled them in her shop, and gave them half the profits.

1476 and 1477 were years of great trouble to Gerard, whose conscience compelled him to oppose the Pope. His Holiness, siding with the Grey Friars in their determination to swamp every palpable distinction between the Virgin Mary and her Son, bribed the Christian world into his crotchet by proffering pardon of all sins to such as would add to the Ave Mary this clause: “and blessed be thy Mother Anna, from whom, without blot of sin, proceeded thy virgin flesh.”

Gerard, with many of the northern clergy, held this sentence flat heresy. He not only refused to utter it in his church, but warned his parishioners against using it in private; and he refused to celebrate the new feast the Pope invented at the same time, “the feast of the miraculous conception of the Virgin.”

This drew upon him the bitter enmity of the Franciscans, and they were strong enough to put him into more than one serious difficulty, and to inflict many a little mortification on him—those pin-pricks that smart more than a blow because they come daily, and often with a sanctimonious smile.

In emergencies he consulted Margaret; and she always did one of two things: either she said, “I do not see my way,” and refused to guess; or else she gave advice that proved wonderfully sagacious. He had genius, but she had marvellous tact—an instinct for the hidden seasoning in men’s words, the way a phrase could be salted to preserve peace or over-salted to spoil it. And she had, too, an ear for the noises that govern temper: when the monks’ chanting came like a net over the town, she heard in it not only devotion but power; and when their sharp Latin clipped the air, she felt how a dominant tongue can press down the homelier speech of hearts.

Where affection came in and annihilated the woman’s judgment, he in turn stepped to her aid. Thus, though she knew she was spoiling little Gerard, and Catherine was ruining him for life, she would not part with him, but kept him at home, his abilities uncultivated. There was a shrewd boy of nine, who instead of learning to work and obey played about learning selfishness from their infinite unselfishness, and tyrannised with a rod of iron over two women—both sagacious and spirited—yet reduced by fondness to the exact level of idiots.

Gerard saw this with pain, and interfered with mild but firm remonstrance. After a considerable struggle he prevailed, and got little Gerard sent to the best school in Europe, kept by one Haaghe at Deventer: this was in 1477. Many tears were shed; but the great progress the boy made at that famous school reconciled Margaret in some degree. And the fidelity of Reicht Heynes, now her partner in business, enabled Margaret to spend weeks at a time hovering over her boy at Deventer—bringing him little comforts, the kind that carry one’s native air and taste: a spiced cake wrapped in linen so it should not stale, a bit of cured eel, a small cheese pressed with her own mark, and always some word in the homely tongue that no school Latin could quite replace.

So the years glided; and these two persons, subjected to as strong and constant a temptation as can well be conceived, were each other’s guardian angels—and not each other’s tempters.

To be sure the well-greased morality of the next century, which taught that solemn vows to God are sacred in proportion as they are reasonable, had at that time entered no single mind; and the alternative to these two minds was self-denial or sacrilege.

It was strange to hear them talk with unrestrained tenderness of their boy, and yet feel an icy barrier between themselves all the time—like a warm kitchen where bread is rising, but a door stands shut on a winter room, and one must not cross the threshold.

Eight years had now passed thus; and Gerard, fairly compared with men in general, was happy.

But Margaret was not.

The habitual expression of her face was a sweet pensiveness; but sometimes she was irritable and a little petulant. She even snapped Gerard now and then. And when she went to see him, if a monk was with him she would turn her back and go home. She hated the monks for having parted Gerard and her; and she inoculated her boy with a contempt for them which lasted him to his dying day. It was not only their doctrine she hated, but their tone—the way their words claimed dominion, as if holiness were a language only they might speak, and all other speech mere chatter.

Gerard bore with her like an angel. He knew her heart of gold, and hoped the ill gust would blow over.

He himself, being now the right man in the right place these many years—loving his parishioners and beloved by them, and occupied from morn to night in good works—recovered the natural cheerfulness of his disposition. To tell the truth, a part of his jocoseness was a blind: he was the greatest peacemaker—except Mr. Harmony in the play—that ever was born. He reconciled more enemies in ten years than his predecessors had done in three hundred; and one manoeuvre in his art was to make the quarrellers laugh at the cause of quarrel, so undermining the demon of discord.

Independently of that, he truly loved a harmless joke. He was a wonderful tamer of animals—squirrels, hares, fawns, and the like. So half in jest a parishioner who had a mule supposed possessed with a devil gave it him, saying, “Tame this vagabone, parson, if ye can.” In about six months—Heaven knows how—he not only tamed Jack, but won his affections so that Jack would come running to his whistle like a dog.

One day, having taken shelter from a shower on the stone settle outside a certain public-house, he heard a toper within—a stranger—boasting he could take more at a draught than any man in Gouda. Gerard marched in at once.

“What, lads,” said he, “do none of ye take him up for the honour of Gouda? Shall it be said there came hither one from another parish a greater sot than any of us? Nay, then, I your parson do take him up. Go to, I’ll find thee a parishioner shall drink more at a draught than thou.”

A bet was made. Gerard whistled. In clattered Jack—for he was taught to come into a room with the utmost composure—and put his nose into his backer’s hand.

“A pair of buckets!” shouted Gerard. “And let us see which of these two sons of asses can drink most at a draught.”

On another occasion two farmers had a dispute whose hay was the best. Failing to convince each other, they said, “We’ll ask parson,” for by this time he was their referee in every mortal thing.

“How lucky you thought of me!” said Gerard. “Why, I have got one staying with me who is the best judge of hay in Holland. Bring me a double handful apiece.”

So when they came, he had them into the parlour and put each bundle on a chair. Then he whistled, and in walked Jack.

“Lord a mercy!” said one of the farmers.

“Jack,” said the parson, in the tone of conversation, “just tell us which is the best hay of these two.”
