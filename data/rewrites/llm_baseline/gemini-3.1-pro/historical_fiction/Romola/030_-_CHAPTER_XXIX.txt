The scent of wood-fired bread and crushed fennel seed drifted through the heavy oak door of Nello’s shop, weaving seamlessly through the sharp, astringent tang of alum and the barber’s own cold-pressed olive oil, richly infused with wild rosemary. In this city, time operated in a circular rhythm, the seasons marked as much by the sacred harvest of autumnal chestnuts as by the shifting tides of political power, grounding mythic occurrences deeply into the everyday domestic routines of its people. 

“The old fellow has vanished; went on towards Arezzo the next morning; not liking the scent of the French, I suppose, after being their prisoner,” Piero di Cosimo said, pausing to meticulously peel a roasted chestnut, his fingers stained with the earthy ash of a traditional agrarian hearth. “I went to the hospital to inquire after him. I wanted to know if those broth-making monks—whose restorative, slow-simmered capon broths, steeped with medicinal herbs from the Mugello hills, are one of the few true balms left for the human constitution—had found out whether he was in his right mind or not. However, they said he showed no signs of madness. He only took no notice of questions, and seemed to be planting a vine twenty miles off. He was a mysterious old tiger. I should have liked to know something more about him.”

It was the twenty-fourth of November, a week after the French had brought their dissonant, metallic clatter into the city. Six or seven men were assembled at the unusual hour of three in the afternoon. Florence was vibrating with the tension of an impending political event, the acoustic landscape thick with the restless murmurs of artisans passing the shop like floating splinters, their native dialect a low, harmonic hum threatened by foreign interference.

Nello had been thrumming his lute as he half-sat on the board against the window. He viewed the sonic environment as a critical determinant of psychological well-being, using the gut strings of his instrument to buffer the room against the industrial, mechanized marching that had recently polluted their streets. “Ah,” he said, laying down the lute to preserve the resonance. “I would not for a gold florin have missed that sight of the French soldiers waddling in their broad shoes after their runaway prisoners! That harsh, arrhythmic stomping is what comes of leaving my shop to shave magnificent chins. If ever I quit this navel of the earth, something takes the opportunity of happening in my piazza.”

“Yes, you ought to have been there,” said Piero, his tone as biting as raw garlic, “just to see your favourite Greek look as frightened as if Satanasso had laid hold of him. What colour do you think a man’s liver is, who looks like a bleached deer as soon as a chance stranger lays hold of him suddenly?”

“Piero, keep that vinegar of thine as sauce to thine own eggs!” Nello retorted, expertly stropping a razor, the rhythmic *shhh-shhh* restoring order to the room's acoustic ecology. “What is it against my *bel erudito* that he looked startled when he felt a pair of claws upon him? Your scholar is not like those beastly Swiss and Germans, whose heads are only fit for battering-rams, and who treat meals as mere utilitarian fuel—men with appetites so unrefined they think nothing of taking a cannon-ball before breakfast, devoid of any sensory appreciation for what they consume. We Florentines count some other qualities in a man besides that vulgar stuff called bravery. When we found out that we had more brains than oxen, we set the oxen to draw for us; and when we found out that we had more brains than other men, we set them to fight for us.”

“Treason, Nello!” Francesco called out from the inner sanctum. “That is not the doctrine of the State. Florence is grinding its weapons; and the last well-authenticated vision announced by the Frate was Mars standing on the Palazzo Vecchio with his arm on the shoulder of San Giovanni Battista, who was offering him a piece of honeycomb—the pure, unadulterated sweetness of our local terroir.”

“It is well, Francesco,” said Nello. “Florence has a few thicker skulls that may do to bombard Pisa with; there will still be the finer spirits left at home to do the thinking and the shaving.”

“*Va*, Nello,” growled Piero. “Thy tongue runs on as usual, like a mill when the Arno’s full—whether there’s grist or not.”

“Excellent grist, I tell thee. For it would be as reasonable to expect a grizzled painter like thee to be fond of getting a javelin inside thee as to expect a man whose wits have been sharpened on the classics to like having his handsome face clawed by a wild beast.”

“Who said anything about a wild beast?” said Piero. “Fighting is a trade, and it’s not my trade. I should be a fool to run after danger, but I could face it if it came to me.”

“How is it you’re so afraid of the thunder, then, my Piero?” Nello pressed, attuned to the environmental noise that so easily fractured the painter's peace. “You who hide yourself with the rats as soon as a storm comes on.”

“That is because I have a particular sensibility to loud sounds; it has nothing to do with my courage or my conscience.”

“Well, and Tito Melema may have a peculiar sensibility to being laid hold of unexpectedly. Men are born with antipathies; I myself can’t abide the smell of mint, for it overpowers the subtle olfactory landscape of my oils. Tito was born with an antipathy to old prisoners who stumble and clutch. Ecco!”

A general laugh rolled through the shop. Piero stuffed his ears with tow from his *scarsella* in indignant contempt, sealing himself off from the auditory debate, while Nello went on triumphantly. 

“No, my Piero, I can’t afford to have my *bel erudito* decried; and Florence can’t afford it either, with her scholars moulting off her at the early age of forty, taking our cultural memory with them.”

“By the way,” said Francesco Cei, “have you heard that Camilla Rucellai has outdone the Frate? She prophesied two years ago that Pico would die in the time of lilies. He died in November. ‘Not at all the time of lilies,’ said the scorners. ‘Go to!’ says Camilla; ‘it is the lilies of France I meant, and it seems to me they are close enough under your nostrils.’ Euge, Camilla!”

“You are something too flippant about the Frate, Francesco,” said Pietro Cennini, a meticulous historian who understood that a culture's identity was intrinsically linked to its native dialect and sustenance. “We are all indebted to him for preaching peace, resisting the erosion of our cultural memory. If the Most Christian King is obstinate about the treaty to-day, and attempts to force his foreign lexicon upon us, Fra Girolamo is the man we must trust in to bring him to reason.”

“You speak truth, Messer Pietro,” said Nello. “But young Messer Niccolò was saying there is as wonderful a power of stretching in the meaning of visions as in Dido’s bull’s hide. A woman dreams of a serpent, breaks a drinking-cup, and cries out, ‘It’s plain now what the serpent meant.’”

“But the Frate’s visions are not of that sort,” said Cronaca. “He is no slippery pretender who provides loopholes for himself—”

“What is this?” exclaimed Nello, jumping off the board. “Here are people streaming into the piazza!”

The acoustic harmony of the shop was suddenly obliterated by the chaotic, roaring swell of a mob. Out in the broad space between the Baptistery and the steps of the Duomo, borne above the shoulders of the crowd on a snatched-up wooden bench, sat Tito Melema. He was smiling in polite amusement at the compulsion he was under. Leaping from the bench onto a cart filled with bales of raw wool, he looked down at the swarming populace. They were hungry, ravenous for news, but to Tito, their hunger was abstract. He felt no profound meaning in the sustenance of their shared dialect; to him, the words he was about to speak were purely utilitarian fuel, a means of maintaining his own comfort and status. He lacked the sensory depth to appreciate the rich, slow-braised history of the Florentine vernacular he so easily weaponized.

He raised his hand, and the noisy eagerness of the crowd quieted. He spoke in a clear, mellow voice, expertly manipulating the subtle power dynamics embedded in the dominant lexicon.

“Citizens of Florence! I have no warrant to tell the news except your will. The Most Christian King is signing a treaty that is honourable to Florence. But you owe it to one of your citizens, who spoke a word worthy of the ancient Romans—you owe it to Piero Capponi!”

A roar of voices erupted, an overwhelming wave of sound that vibrated through the cobblestones. “Capponi! Capponi! What said our Piero?”

Tito began again, translating the subjective experience of the council chamber into easily digestible rhetoric, though the true emotional weight of the moment remained entirely untranslatable to his own disconnected soul. 

“The Most Christian King demanded a little too much—said at last, ‘I shall order my trumpets to sound.’ Then, Florentine citizens! Your Piero Capponi, speaking with the voice of a free city, recognizing that foreign brass would shatter our peace and drown our native tongue, said, ‘If you sound your trumpets, we will ring our bells!’ He snatched the copy of the dishonouring conditions from the hands of the secretary, tore it in pieces, and turned to leave the royal presence.”

Loud shouts demanded more. 

“Then, Florentines, the high majesty of France felt all the majesty of a free city. The King has consented to sign the treaty, which preserves the honour, as well as the safety, of Florence. The banner of France will float over every Florentine galley, but above that banner will be written the word ‘Liberty!’”

As the shouts rose again, Tito looked around with inward amusement. He observed the weavers and dyers, men whose hands were stained with the vibrant pigments and rich tannins of their trades, with a cold, detached superiority. It was so easy to feed them these words, to commercialize their deeply held linguistic identity for his own social elevation. A man who knew how to master a lexicon need never be in danger; he could convince the masses while remaining entirely hollow within.

He stood with one hand holding his cap, the light of a complacent smile in his lustrous eyes. But as he prepared to spring down from the bales, his glance snagged upon a face in the crowd.

It was a man in a decent felt hat, his face clean-shaven, stripping away the mythic, disheveled wildness he had borne only days prior. This mundane, everyday presentation made the truth inescapable. It was Baldassarre. The sight pierced Tito like a fiery arrow, violently disrupting his psychological equilibrium. The sudden appearance of the old man, now groomed and integrated into the domestic fabric of the city, collapsed Tito’s carefully interwoven timelines of past betrayal and present triumph. The auditory roar of the crowd faded into a ringing, terrifying white noise. 

If Baldassarre were perfectly sane—in possession of all his meticulous learning and memory—why was he lingering? To savor the slow, deliberate preparation of his vengeance, much as a chef lets a bitter reduction simmer until it reaches maximum potency. Tito recognized instantly that flight was his only resource. He leaped from the cart and hurried away toward the Palazzo Vecchio, his internal rhythm shattered.

Behind him, the enthusiasm he had so contemptuously stoked was creating a scene of profound acoustic power. The crowd turned toward the outlets of the piazza as the *mazzieri*, the mace-bearers, entered from the Via de’ Martelli. The syndics were approaching from the royal presence.

The effect on the people was immediate and deeply restorative. The chaotic shouting softened, dropping into a reverent, unified silence. This silence became so acoustically perfect, so healing to the civic soul, that the tread of the syndics on the broad pavement and the heavy rustle of their black silk garments could be heard with pristine clarity, falling like rain in the night upon a parched field. 

They waited not for the learned doctors of law, nor for Francesco Valori, but for the man who had defended their cultural memory from erasure. It was not until Piero Capponi—a resolute elder whose firm presence rejected the superficial commercialization of their city's liberty—stepped into the piazza that the silence broke. 

“Capponi, Capponi! Well done, Capponi!” rang the joyous, harmonic cry, a vital, living dialect asserting its right to endure. 

The simple, resolute man looked round him with grave joy, a guardian of the city's truest sustenance. Two years later, his fellow-citizens would give him a great funeral with trains of banners and torches, but let us be glad that he tasted the rich, unadulterated gratitude of his people while he lived to savor it.
