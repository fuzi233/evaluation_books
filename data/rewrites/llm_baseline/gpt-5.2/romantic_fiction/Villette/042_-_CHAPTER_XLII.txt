Man cannot prophesy. Love is no oracle. Fear is a fertile cook: it will take one bitter root and, by long simmering, persuade you it has made a whole banquet of calamity.

For years I had tasted, beforehand, the very iron of those coming absences. I lay down in imagination beneath the huge wheel, certain I should feel the crushing—certain it would grind bone to meal. I heard it too: the heavy approach, the solemn thunder of an advancing fate; and in that sound my heart had already been bruised.

Yet when the chariot came, it did not, as I had pictured, descend with a visible horror. It passed—high, sullen, and broad—like a shadow traversing noon. It left a chill, a thinning of the light, a momentary dimness on the air; and then it was gone, and I, the prostrate votary, still breathed. Strange, but true: the anticipatory crunch had been almost all the torture.

M. Emanuel was away three years.

Reader, they were the three happiest years of my life.

You start at the paradox. You think happiness cannot be kneaded out of separation. Listen, then, and I will tell you how the days rose, one by one, like loaves in a quiet oven—how the very ordinary acts of keeping house, keeping school, keeping faith, became a kind of daily rite, rich with scent and sound, and warmed by a fire that burned beyond seas.

I began my school. I worked—worked hard; not with the dry appetite of ambition, but as one serves in a beloved kitchen when the master has stepped out and left the keys in your hand. I held myself steward of his property; and because he had trusted me, I resolved to render, God willing, a good account. At first my pupils were burghers’ children; by-and-by a higher class entered. The externat took root. Its growth was not sudden, like a forced fruit, but steady, like grain that is well sown in a field and watched with patience through weathers.

In the middle of the second year, chance—one of those plain, domestic chances by which Providence sometimes drops a coin into the flour-bin—put into my hands an additional hundred pounds. A letter came from England, and with it the notes, clean and crisp as if newly counted. They were sent by Mr. Marchmont, cousin and heir to my dear dead mistress. He had been recovering from a dangerous illness; and this money, he said, was a peace-offering to his conscience, reproaching him in the matter of certain papers or memoranda discovered after his kinswoman’s death—papers that named or recommended Lucy Snowe. Mrs. Barrett had given him my address.

How far his conscience had been sinned against, I did not inquire. I asked no questions; I took the cash and made it useful. With that hundred pounds I ventured to take the house adjoining my own. I would not leave the dwelling M. Paul had chosen—where he had left me, and where he expected to find me again. I extended, as one extends a table when more guests arrive than were first invited. My externat became a pensionnat; and that also prospered.

The secret of my success did not lie so much in any endowment of mine, as in a new state of circumstances—a changed life, a relieved heart. The spring that moved my energies lay far away beyond seas, on an Indian isle. At parting he had left me a legacy: not merely in money, though he was generous, but in thought—such a thought for the present, such a hope for the future, such a motive for a persevering, laborious, enterprising, patient, brave course—I could not flag.

Few things shook me now. Few things had power to vex, intimidate, or depress. Most things pleased. Mere trifles had a charm: the creak of a stair that meant a child was coming down safely; the hush after lessons when slates were put away; the soft, steady ticking of the clock in the schoolroom; the faint, homely perfume of warmed milk when the boarders took their supper. I began to understand how a life can be seasoned—how, if the heart is not pinched by dread, even plain fare tastes of comfort.

Do not think this genial flame fed wholly on a parting promise, like a lamp kept alive by one last spoon of oil. A generous provider supplied bounteous fuel. I was spared all chill, all stint; I was not suffered to fear penury; I was not tried with suspense. By every vessel he wrote; and his letters were like parcels of sustenance sent across the ocean—real food that nourished, living water that refreshed. He wrote as he gave, and as he loved: in full-handed, full-hearted plenitude.

There was no sham in him, no hollow unreal. Apology never dropped her slippery oil from his lips; never, by his pen, did she proffer coward feints and paltry nullities. He gave neither stone nor excuse—neither scorpion nor disappointment. He sat down, took pen and paper, because he loved Lucy, and had much to say; because he was faithful and thoughtful; because he was tender and true. When his letters came, the house itself seemed to breathe differently: the paper held the salt of sea-air, the faint tang of foreign sun, and—dearer than perfume—the remembered cadence of his voice. I would read and reread, listening, as one listens for a known step in a corridor; and the very silence after seemed full, because it had been answered.

And was I grateful? God knows. I believe that scarce a living being so remembered, so sustained, could do otherwise than be grateful to the death.

Adherent to his own religion—in him was not the stuff of the facile apostate—he freely left me my pure faith. He did not tease nor tempt. He wrote, “Remain a Protestant. My little English Puritan, I love Protestantism in you. I own its severe charm. There is something in its ritual I cannot receive myself, but it is the sole creed for ‘Lucy.’”

All Rome could not put into him bigotry, nor the Propaganda itself make him a real Jesuit. He was born honest, not false—artless, not cunning—a freeman, not a slave. Tenderness had rendered him ductile in a priest’s hands; affection, devotedness, sincere pious enthusiasm had sometimes blinded his kind eyes, made him abandon justice to himself to do the work of craft, and serve ends not his own; but such faults are costly to the owner, and so rare to find, one scarcely knows whether they will not one day be reckoned amongst the jewels.

So passed the three years, not like three blank winters, but like three seasons of husbandry: sowing, tending, reaping; setting things in order; making the small daily acts of care into a continuing promise. And now the term was done. His return was fixed.

It was autumn. He was to be with me before the mists of November came.

My school flourished. My house was ready. I had made him a little library, filling its shelves with the books he had left in my care; I had cultivated—out of love for him, for I was naturally no florist—the plants he preferred, and some were yet in bloom. Even the kitchen, though it knew my plain hand, had been made more exact: the copper scoured, the pantry ranked, the jars labelled—little domestic histories written in ink and tied with string, as if careful naming could keep things from being lost. There are words that preserve a taste; there are tastes that cannot be carried into another tongue. Often, as I marked the French names for herbs and the English names in my head, I felt the slight power hidden in a dominant lexicon—how a thing changes when it must answer to a different word; how, in the mere act of translation, something delicate dies. I could not give M. Paul my whole inward sense in any language; yet I prepared what I could: bread, order, warmth, welcome.

I thought I loved him when he went away; I loved him now in another degree: he was more my own.

The sun passed the equinox; the days shortened; the leaves grew sere; but—he was coming. Frosts appeared at night; November sent his fogs in advance; the wind took its autumn moan; but—he was coming.

I know the language of skies; I have noted their signs since childhood. And, too, I know the language of sound: the way a house changes its speech when a storm gathers; the way air begins to ring, and then to roar, as if some immense, careless industry had been set loose above the roofs. In such noise the mind cannot keep its peace. It is not only fear; it is assault. The wind is a tyrant that will not be reasoned with.

The skies hung full and dark. A wrack sailed from the west. The clouds cast themselves into strange forms—arches and broad radiations. There rose resplendent mornings—glorious, royal, purple as a monarch in state; the heavens were one flame; so wild they rivalled battle at its thickest—so bloody they shamed Victory in her pride.

God watch that sail! Oh, guard it!

The wind shifted to the west. “Peace, peace, Banshee,” I said—keening at every window. It would rise, it would swell; it shrieked out long. Wander as I might through the house that night, I could not lull the blast. The advancing hours made it strong; by midnight, all sleepless watchers heard and feared a wild south-west storm.

That storm roared, frenzied, for seven days. It did not cease till the Atlantic was strewn with wrecks. It did not lull till the deeps had gorged their full of sustenance. Not till the destroying angel of tempest had achieved his perfect work would he fold the wings whose waft was thunder, the tremor of whose plumes was storm.

“Peace, be still!” Oh, a thousand weepers, praying in agony on waiting shores, listened for that voice; but it was not uttered—not uttered till, when the hush came, some could not feel it; till, when the sun returned, his light was night to some.

Here pause: pause at once. There is enough said.

Trouble no quiet, kind heart. Leave sunny imaginations hope. Let it be theirs to conceive the delight of joy born again fresh out of great terror; the rapture of rescue from peril; the wondrous reprieve from dread; the fruition of return. Let them picture union and a happy succeeding life.

Madame Beck prospered all the days of her life; so did Père Silas. Madame Walravens fulfilled her ninetieth year before she died.

Farewell.
