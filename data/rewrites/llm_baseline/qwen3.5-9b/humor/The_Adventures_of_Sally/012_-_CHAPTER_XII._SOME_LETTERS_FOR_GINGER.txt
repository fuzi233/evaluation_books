Dear Ginger,

I'm feeling better. Since it's been three months since I last wrote, you'd surely conclude that I'm a poor, weak-minded creature if I weren't recovering. One *ought* to be able to bounce back from anything in ninety days. Alas, I haven't quite mastered that art yet. However, I have successfully shoved my troubles into the cellar and stopped dragging them out to inspect them daily. That counts for something, doesn't it?

I owe you a full report on London, but honestly, I've become so accustomed to the place that I possess zero new impressions. It feels less like a visit and more like I've lived here for decades.

By the address, you'll notice Mr. Faucitt hasn't sold his inheritance yet. He claims he will soon—there's a whiskered gentleman with a predatory gaze he lunches with constantly, suggesting massive deals are brewing. Poor dear! He is desperate to flee to the countryside, grow ducks, and find peace. London has utterly failed him. It isn't the city of his memory. For years, until he finally accepted reality, he wandered disconsolately trying to locate the landmarks of his youth. (He hasn't set foot in England in nearly thirty years!) The problem, it seems, is that roughly every thirty years, London suffers a collective manic episode where they repaint a shop front from blue to red, sending the returned exile into a tailspin.

Mr. Faucitt feels like Rip Van Winkle. First shock: The Empire Theatre is now a cinema instead of a music hall. Second shock: The Tivoli Music Hall has been demolished entirely. Third shock: He visited the baker's shop in Rupert Street where he lodged in the eighteen-eighties, only to discover it had been converted into a dressmaking establishment. He grew deeply melancholic, finding solace only when a magnificent magenta fog rolled in, proving that at least some atmospheric conditions remained unchanged from the "good old days."

Thank goodness, I am kept quite busy at Laurette et Cie. (Not being a French scholar like you—remember Jules?—I initially thought *Cie* was the name of a junior partner and eagerly awaited his introduction: *"Miss Nicholas, shake hands with Mr. Cie, one of your greatest admirers!"*)

I occupy the female equivalent of your position at Fillmore Nicholas Theatrical Enterprises Ltd.; essentially, I am the right-hand woman. My duties involve hovering near customers upon entry, chirping, "Charming weather, moddom!" (usually a fib), and shunting them toward the actual staff. I wouldn't mind continuing this routine for years, but Mr. Faucitt is determined to sell.

It seems every Englishman I meet shares an obsession with owning a house and plot in Loamshire, Hampshire, or Shropshire. Their sole objective is to accumulate enough cash to "buy back the old place"—a property conveniently sold at the end of Act One to pay the heir's gambling debts. Mr. Faucitt, as a boy, lived in a tiny Gloucestershire village near a place called Cirencester—or rather, it isn't anymore; it's now spelled *Cissister*, which I wager you didn't know. After fifty years of oblivion, he has suddenly developed a craving to spend his final days there, surrounded by pigs and poultry.

He took me to view the estate recently. Oh, Ginger, the English countryside! I cannot fathom why anyone chooses to live in a town. Think of the ancient, grey stone cottages, golden haystacks, squelchy mud lanes, towering oaks, and distant blue hills. The sheer tranquility! If I ever have to sell my soul, I demand the Devil grant me at least forty years in an English rural retreat in exchange.

Perhaps my rambling suggests I've forgotten you exist. To prove otherwise, allow me to share this: Reading the paper last week, I spotted the headline "International Match." Initially meaningless, it suddenly triggered a memory. This was the sport you once pursued! Curious, I headed to Twickenham to witness the spectacle. An immense crowd nearly crushed me, but I endured it for your sake.

I learned the English team wore white, while the Welsh wore red. After watching a neighbor yell until his face turned purple, I asked, "Excuse me, sir, could you identify the English scrum-half?" Just then, the players approached. Suddenly, a dozen men in red tackled a harmless, smallish figure who had merely fallen onto the ball.

Ginger, you are lucky to be safe on your stool in Fillmore's outer office! That was the scrum-half, and apparently, getting trampled is his Tuesday morning commute. How dare you engage in such brutality! Thank your stars you can summon a policeman if anyone tries to jump on you today. Did you really perform these daredevil feats? You must harbor secrets I never suspected.

While riding atop a bus down Piccadilly, I spotted someone familiar: Mr. Carmyle. Fortunately, he didn't see me. I have no desire to encounter reminders of New York just now.

Thanks for the update, but please cease writing to me. It triggers memories I wish to avoid. Here is the situation: Let me write to you because it helps me cope, but do not reply. Will you understand?

Good news: Fillmore and Gladys Winch are married! Based on what I've observed, it is the finest thing that has ever happened to Brother F. She is wonderful. I must write to him...

Dear Ginger,

I read in a Sunday paper last week that "The Primrose Way" premiered in New York to great acclaim. Splendid. Though I fear newspapers printing such details causes unnecessary anxiety.

Feeling particularly blue and isolated yesterday, I committed a foolish act: I visited your club and asked for you! A kindly uniformed clerk informed me you hadn't been in recently, perhaps out of town, and offered me a seat while he investigated. He then dispatched a tiny pageboy, also in uniform, who skipped away shrieking, "Mister Kemp! Mister Kemp!" in a piercing treble. Hearing your name echo in the distance gave me a strange sensation. I felt terrible for causing the disturbance, so when the boy returned, I slipped a twopence into his hand—a breach of protocol, I assume, though he seemed delighted.

Mr. Faucitt has finally sold the business and retreated to the country, leaving me somewhat adrift.

Dear Ginger,

What is the use? What is the point? I strive to escape New York, yet it relentlessly pursues me to my sanctuary.

A week ago, wandering aimlessly down the Strand, I encountered none other than Fillmore, arm-in-arm with Mr. Carmyle! I couldn't evade them. First, Mr. Carmyle spotted me; second, dodging Fillmore requires a full day's travel now. I blushed furiously for him. Ginger! I actually blushed for him in the middle of the Strand! In my darkest nightmares, I never imagined him so colossal. Upon what sustenance does this giant Fillmore feast? Poor Gladys! When she gazes upon him, she must feel like a bigamist.

Fillmore remains brimming with grandiose schemes, breezily discussing the acquisition of various English plays. He has crossed the Atlantic to stage "The Primrose Way" here, currently residing at the Savoy Hotel. They escorted me there for lunch, cheering like a stray sheep found in a field. It was the absolute worst possible scenario. Fillmore spoke uninterrupted Broadwayese until, after devouring French pastries and slumping back breathless, awaiting coffee and liqueurs, he induced such profound homesickness in me that I nearly collapsed into hysterics. It was madness to approach the Savoy. Naturally, it functions as a direct annex to Broadway. Americans sat at every table within sight. I might as well have been dining at the Astor.

If Fate insists on importing New York to England solely for my humiliation, I suppose I must endure it. Events took their course, and I drifted until two days ago, arriving here. Mr. Carmyle invited Fillmore—and me—to Monk's Crofton. With no valid excuse to refuse, I came. Now, I sit composing this in a cavernous bedroom featuring an roaring fireplace, plush armchairs, and every conceivable luxury. Fillmore is out golfing. He departs for New York on Saturday aboard the *Mauretania*.

I am horrified to learn from him that, alongside his other ventures, he is now promoting a light-weight boxing championship in Jersey City, guaranteeing astronomical sums to both fighters. Arguing is futile; he simply cites statistics regarding others' windfalls. Moreover, it is too late to stop it. The bout occurs in a fortnight. Still, it sends a chill down my spine.

Enough worry. Let us shift topics. Know Monk's Crofton? Likely not, as I recall hearing it was a recent acquisition. Mr. Carmyle purchased it from a Lord who lost his fortune on the Stock Exchange. I pray you haven't seen it, for I intend to describe it at length. I need to pour my soul out about this paradise.
