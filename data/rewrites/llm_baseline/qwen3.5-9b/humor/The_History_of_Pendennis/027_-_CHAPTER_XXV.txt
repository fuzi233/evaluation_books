Our friends have already heard Sir Francis Clavering's honest take on the woman who bought him back from the brink of ruin and restored him to his ancestral home. Let's just say the Baronet wasn't entirely off-base regarding his wife. Lady Clavering, bless her heart, wasn't exactly the sharpest tool in the shed, nor the most polished specimen of female intellect. Her entire education consisted of two years spent in a London suburb she stubbornly insisted on calling "Ackney" until the moment of her death. At fifteen, she was whisked away to Calcutta to join her father aboard the *Ramchunder*, an East Indiaman captained by the formidable Mr. Bragg.

It was on this voyage that she met her first husband, Mr. Amory, the ship's third mate. Now, we aren't here to rewrite the whole biography of Lady Clavering, but suffice it to say that Captain Bragg, who was also her father's business partner, grew so fed up with Mr. Amory's rebellious antics that he threw him in irons. Once they hit the Cape, Bragg dumped the officer there and sailed on, delivering his wealthy ward safely to her father in Calcutta after a voyage that threatened to sink the ship, the cargo, and several passengers' sanity.

A few months later, Mr. Amory showed up in Calcutta. Having worked his way up from the deckhands, he managed to marry the rich attorney's daughter despite her father's protests. He tried his hand at indigo planting (failed), became an agent (failed again), edited a local newspaper (*Sunderbund Pilot*—another failure), and throughout it all, he argued constantly with his father-in-law and wife. His final act was a spectacular financial crash that sent him packing to New South Wales. Somewhere amidst this chaos, he likely crossed paths with Sir Jasper Rogers, the respected judge. And here is the uncomfortable truth: Mr. Amory's downfall was sealed when he shamelessly forged his father-in-law's signature. Since the old gentleman could write perfectly well and didn't need a ghostwriter, the forgery was easily exposed, and fortune abandoned Mr. Amory forever.

With her husband dead and her life in India proving miserable, Lady Clavering decided to return to Europe. She traveled with her four-year-old daughter, Blanche (known as Betsy or Blanche depending on the mood), and their loyal nurse, Mrs. Bonner. They settled near Captain Bragg in Pocklington Street, but the summer was unbearably rainy, and Bragg was a pompous, disagreeable host who clearly couldn't wait to get rid of the "Indian lady." Blanche assumed everyone in London was gossiping about her husband's disgrace and that the Royal Family knew her secrets. With a generous allowance from her father and no desire to live in England, she packed her bags for the continent.

She loved to travel. She still called Hackney "Ackney," spoke English with a charming foreign lilt, dressed to kill, and possessed an appetite that bordered on the legendary. She cooked curries and pilau in every boarding house she visited. While her quirks might seem odd, they made her incredibly popular. She was jovial, generous, and impossible to resist. If you wanted a picnic, she brought triple the amount of champagne, chicken, and ham. She handed out theater tickets and masks like candy. She prepaid her rent months in advance and kept a purse full of cash to bail out struggling bachelors and dowagers whose money hadn't arrived. From Brussels to Rome, she was a whirlwind of laughter and generosity.

Tragically, news of Mr. Amory's death caught up with her in Rome, where Captain Clavering was currently stuck unable to pay his hotel bill (along with his friend, the Chevalier Strong). The good-hearted widow, showing no particular grief for the scoundrel she'd lost, married the descendant of the ancient Clavering line. Thus, she became the mistress of Clavering Park.

Her daughter, Missy (Blanche), followed suit, learning a great deal about life. She had a governess for a while and later attended Madame de Caramel's prestigious school in the Champs-Élysées. When the Claverings returned to England, Blanche came with them. Only recently, following her grandfather's death and the birth of her little brother, did Blanche realize her status had shifted. Without her father's fortune, she was merely a minor figure in a large house, overshadowed by Master Francis Clavering, the heir to the baronetcy. Though she was a romantic soul who didn't care much for money, she couldn't ignore the fact that her stepfather was dull, her mother dropped her H's and lacked refinement, and little Frank was a spoiled brat who constantly tripped her, spilled food on her dress, and stood between her and her inheritance. Feeling misunderstood and lonely, Blanche's heart yearned for connection outside the family.

This desperation made her difficult at home, frightening her mother and boring her stepfather so much that they desperately wanted her to marry. Sir Francis even hinted to his friend that if Mrs. Strong died, he'd take Blanche as a second wife—but since that was unlikely, anyone else was welcome to try. Enter Arthur Pendennis ("Pen"), a handsome, well-educated young man. Despite his obvious suitability, Pen hesitated. He was plagued by insecurity about his recent failures, his lack of a title, and the poverty he'd brought upon his mother. How could he compete with Blanche, who lived in a grand park with a staff of servants, while his mother struggled to make ends meet at Fairoaks? Paralyzed by vanity and fear, he dallied with his hopes rather than acting.

Meanwhile, Pen's mother, Helen, saw the writing on the wall. She viewed Blanche as frivolous, lacking reverence, and dangerously worldly. Laura and Pen initially defended Blanche, but as the romance deepened, Helen's fears grew. Watching her son and the girl meet daily, her maternal instincts kicked into overdrive. In a moment of vulnerability, she confessed to Pen that she felt her time was short and hoped to see her two children united before she passed. Pen, trying to lighten the mood, joked that Laura would surely reject him, imagining her as too "grand" for someone like him. He pictured Laura bringing him roses, a symbol of her kindness, and felt a surge of triumph thinking, *"I might have this prize for the asking!"*

However, reality soon set in. Before long, Laura began to see the flaws in Blanche. Through private conversations, Blanche poured out her grievances: how her family didn't understand her, how her mother's neglect ruined her upbringing, how her stepfather was intellectually bankrupt, and how her mother's love had shifted entirely to the new baby brother. Blanche painted a picture of isolation and longing. Yet, Laura couldn't shake her suspicions. For instance, just because little Frank was annoying didn't justify Blanche slapping him upside the head for spilling water or screaming insults at him in both English and French. Similarly, Blanche treated the household staff with imperial disdain, ordering them around rudely, whereas Laura was always polite and grateful.

The breaking point came after a particularly nasty incident involving little Frank. Blanche had boxed the boy's ears and berated him in front of everyone. Laura, horrified by such cruelty toward a defenseless child, exploded. She lectured Blanche on her wickedness, telling her she needed to kneel and beg forgiveness from God. Enraged and humiliated, Blanche stormed out, leaving Laura to rush home in a panic. That evening, Laura confessed to her mother that Blanche was selfish and cruel, declaring she was giving up the friendship forever. Helen secretly cheered, believing this meant Blanche was unfit for Pen.

But there was more to the story. Later that day, while sitting together, Blanche turned the conversation to Pen's past affair with the actress. She claimed Pen had been false to her. Laura, blushing, insisted Pen had actually been generous and that the actress had betrayed him. To test Laura, Blanche produced a hidden stash of love letters Pen had written to her—poems about nymphs and rivers. Laura read them, confused by their vagueness, and noted that Blanche had never replied. Blanche laughed, hid the letters, and sang a few Rossini songs, leaving Laura to wonder what she was thinking.

Despite the tension, the next morning, as they walked arm-in-arm to lunch, Laura paused at the top of the stairs. Looking Blanche squarely in the eye, she kissed her with genuine warmth. Whether this was a gesture of sisterly love or a final goodbye remains unclear, but it marked the end of an era. Whatever petty irritations—Frank's eating habits, her mother's blunders, or Sir Francis's cigar smell—had sparked earlier, the bond between the two girls had fractured. Blanche's bad day had culminated in a battle royal that nearly destroyed their friendship, leaving Laura disillusioned and Blanche alone in her tantrums.
