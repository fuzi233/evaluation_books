I stumbled to my feet, every nerve frayed, my chest hollow with a shock so deep it took ten full seconds to clear the fog from my eyes. For a beat I could not find her. She was gone. The loneliness that rolled over me was heavier than any storm cloud, the kind that blots out every patch of blue left after the rain that had soaked the moor hours before. The woman I had hauled back gasping from the very lip of the grave—she who had owed me her second breath of life—had fled from my side, and left nothing but empty, searing desolation in her wake.
I could not bear to stand motionless in that awful, gaping solitude for one more heartbeat. Had I wronged her somehow? The thought knifed through me. I had wrenched her out of the quiet rest she’d craved, hadn’t I? The least I could do was spend the rest of my days at her side, helping her carry the weight of that life I’d forced her to take up again.
I spotted her then, striding fast across the sun-dappled grass, putting distance between herself and the silver curve of the river behind us. I darted to the water’s edge for one quick, bracing sip of the restorative current that had helped nurse us both back to strength, then set off after her. The lingering fever from the white leech’s parasitic bite, paired with the sharp blow the other woman had landed on my jaw, had sapped most of my strength earlier—but now every muscle was thrumming with returning vigor, and I kept her in clear sight without straining to catch up.
“Is this really how it ends?” I muttered as I walked, the words winding into a sad, wordless hum in my chest. The image of her eyes, blazing angry and full of unnameable hatred, replayed behind my lids over and over. I could almost parse her resentment: who wouldn’t seethe at being dragged back to a life they’d already said goodbye to? But what else had I done to hurt her? Why did she look at me like I was something filthy? Could even modesty itself bristle at a kindness that had never once crossed a line? What proud woman, who had watched every single one of my actions while she lay unconscious, could ever hold a single shred of grudge against me for dishonoring her? I had touched her as reverently as a father holds his motherless newborn. I had tended every bruise, every fever spike, every trembling moment of her return. Had all that work, all that desperate, gasping hope I’d poured into saving her only bought me ingratitude in return?
“No,” I said to myself, shaking my head hard. “Any creature that beautiful has to have a heart. It’s just buried deep. The deeper it’s hidden, the stronger, the truer it will wake up when it finally unfurls in that dark, beautiful grave of its own making. Waking that heart inside her would be a greater gift than any easy, happy life I could have given her otherwise. It would hand her a higher, nobler kind of existence altogether.”
She was climbing a soft, gentle slope ahead of me, her back straight, her steps steady as someone who knew every inch of the land and exactly where she was going—until I noticed she was pulling further and further away, her pace picking up without any visible effort. I called on the last of my stored strength, and it flooded through me all at once. My veins hummed with light, my body felt almost weightless, ethereal, and I moved like a breeze slipping through grass, eating up the ground between us until I was nearly at her heels. She hadn’t glanced back once. She moved with the unhurried, unstopping grace of a Greek goddess on her way to pull off a miracle, no sign of haste in her movements.
I was three yards from her when she turned on a dime, her grace never faltering for a second, and stood still facing me. There was no flush of exertion on her cheeks, no gasp of breath. Her paleness wasn’t the sickly gray of fatigue—it was the pure, luminous white of moonlit marble. She breathed slow, deep, even, and her eyes felt so wide and bright they could light up the whole sky. It was almost noon, but the air around us felt thick with the hush of a deep midnight, the kind where invisible dew hangs heavy on every leaf and makes the stars burn huge and low in the dark.
“Why are you following me?” she asked, her voice quiet but sharp, stern, like she was looking at a total stranger she’d never laid eyes on before.
“I’ve clung to nothing but the faint hope of seeing your eyes for months now,” I answered her. “How could I stop myself from chasing that?”
“You will not be spared,” she said, cold as frost. “I order you to stop right where you stand.”
“I won’t leave you until I’ve seen you safe somewhere,” I shot back.
“Then face the consequences,” she said, and turned to walk again, her feet gliding over the grass like she didn’t even touch it. But over her shoulder she cast one glance at me, and I froze in place like I’d been run clean through the chest with a steel spear. Her scorn wasn’t enough to break me—so she was trying to kill me with the sheer weight of her beauty. Desperation snapped the spell holding me rigid, and I sprinted to catch up to her.
“Have mercy on me!” I cried. She didn’t even acknowledge my words. I followed after her like a small, lost child whose mother was pretending to leave him behind in the woods. “I’ll be your servant, your slave, whatever you want,” I said, and reached out to rest my hand lightly on her arm.
She spun around like I’d burned her with a viper’s bite. I cowered back from the blaze of fury in her eyes, but I couldn’t tear my own gaze away. “Please,” I begged again. “Have pity on me.”
She didn’t say a word, just turned and kept walking.
I followed her all day. The sun climbed high overhead, hung motionless at its peak for what felt like hours, then slid slowly down the other side of the sky. She didn’t pause for a single breath. I didn’t stop walking either. She never turned her head, never slowed her pace for even a second. The sun dipped below the horizon, and night seeped up over the moor. I stayed right at her heels—if I lost sight of her for even a blink, I knew I’d never find her again.
We’d been trekking over thick, soft, springy grass all day, until abruptly she stopped, and sank face down into it. The last of the twilight light was still just bright enough to show me she was completely, utterly exhausted. I stood behind her, staring down at her still form for a long moment. Did I love her? I knew for a fact she wasn’t a good person. Did I hate her? I could no more leave her side than I could stop breathing.
I knelt down beside her. “Get away. Don’t you dare touch me,” she screamed. Her arms lay limp at her sides, like they’d turned to stone and lost all feeling. Then, all at once, they flew up and locked tight around my neck, rigid as the iron bands of a torture device. She hauled my face down to hers, and her lips pressed hard to my cheek. A sharp, stinging pain lanced through me, throbbing hot where her mouth touched my skin. I couldn’t move a single muscle, not even twitch a single hair on my head.
Slowly the pain faded. A heavy, dreamy weariness washed over me, laced with a soft, floating pleasure that dragged me down under into blackness, until I knew nothing at all.
I jolted awake all at once. The moon hung low above the horizon, but it cast no light, just sat there a small bright coin stuck in a wall of pure black. My cheek stung like I’d been pricked by a needle. I lifted my hand to it, and found a damp, warm spot on my skin. My neck ached too—there was another wet spot there, right where her arms had wrapped around me. I sighed, my chest heavy, and felt leaden with exhaustion. I dragged my eyes slowly around the dark—and realized where all the moon’s light had gone. It was wrapped around the lady, coating her in a shimmering, silver nimbus.
I staggered to my feet and stumbled toward her. “Down!” she snapped, the tone of her voice sharp as if she was barking an order at a disobedient stray dog. “Take one more step after me and I’ll make you regret it.”
“I will follow you,” I mumbled, forcing the words out through a fog of agony.
“Step through the gates of my city, and my people will stone you to death,” she warned. “They do not suffer beggars to trespass.”
I didn’t hear a single word she said. Weak as melted wax, half lost in a daze, I didn’t even feel my feet moving—but the gap between us kept shrinking. She stepped back once, lifted her left arm, and her clenched fist swung toward my forehead like a sledgehammer. The blow hit me harder than any iron hammer could, and I crumpled to the grass.
I jumped back to my feet seconds later, cold and soaked through, my mind crystal clear, every muscle thrumming with strength. Had that blow woken me up fully? There was no wound on my forehead, no lingering pain—so why was I soaking wet? I couldn’t have been lying there for long, the moon hadn’t climbed an inch higher in the sky.
The lady stood a few yards away, her back to me, doing something I couldn’t quite make out in the dim light. Then a sudden flash of white told me she’d stripped off all her clothes, standing bare and luminous in the dazed, hazy moonlight. She stood perfectly still for one beat—and pitched forward onto the grass. A streak of blinding white shot away from the spot, moving fast in a long, clean line. The moon burst into full brightness right that second, and I saw what the streak was: a long-bodied, predatory creature, loping in low, curved bounds across the grass, dark shadowy streaks rippling down its back like it was running along the edge of a wood and catching the dark shapes of passing leaves.
“Merciful God,” I breathed. “That terrible thing is speeding straight for the city, all wrapped up in night.” And for a second I thought I could hear the distant, rising scream of terror as the pale savage bounded over house tops, tearing through sleeping streets, rending and slaying everyone in its path.
I stood frozen, staring after it in horror, when a second large creature shot past me from behind, moving so fast it was almost silent, like a white arrow. It was purest white, and it was heading straight for the spot where the lady had fallen, the spot I thought her body still lay. My tongue stuck to the roof of my mouth. I lunged forward, chasing the beast, but in a single leap it put so much ground between us the spot I was running for was already far behind it.
It was a mercy I couldn’t call out, I thought dazedly. If she’d woken up, the monster would have been on her in a second. But when I finally reached the spot, there was no lady there. Nothing but the clothes she’d stripped off lay on the grass, dark and soft in the moonlight.
I stared after the second beast. It tore across the moor faster than the first one, moving in long, level skimming leaps, the very definition of perfect, wasteless speed. It followed exactly the same path the first creature had taken, and I watched it shrink smaller and smaller until it vanished into the hazy dark distance.
But where was the lady? Had the first beast snuck up on her silently while I was unconscious? I hadn’t heard a scream. There wasn’t enough time for it to have eaten her, either. Could it have snatched her up mid-run and carried her off to its den? It couldn’t have moved that fast if it was carrying a full grown woman. I would have seen the weight of her slung over its back. Horrible, twisting doubts began to gnaw at the edges of my mind. After searching every inch of the clearing until my feet ached and found no trace of her at all, I turned and set off after the two creatures, following their faint trail across the grass.
