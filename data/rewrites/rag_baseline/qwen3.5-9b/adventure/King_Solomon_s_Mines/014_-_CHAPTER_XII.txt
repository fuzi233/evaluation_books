The path through the great town was a labyrinth of shadow, yet Infadoos and the local chiefs navigated it with the instinctive certainty of men who know every stone and root of their soil. By skirting the periphery, we moved unseen, cutting through the gloom with a steady, silent pace. For an hour, perhaps more, we trudged onward until the celestial drama began to resolve. The eclipse receded; the severed edge of the moon reappeared, and suddenly, a silver streak erupted from its surface, trailed by a wondrous, ruddy luminescence. It hung against the velvet blackness of the sky like a lantern forged in the fires of gods, casting a wild, terrible beauty over the landscape. Within five minutes, the stars began to dim, yielding to a light sufficient to reveal our surroundings. We had cleared the town of Loo and were ascending a massive, flat-topped hill, roughly two miles in circumference. Common to the geology of South Africa, this mound was not towering in height—its peak rising barely two hundred feet—but it possessed a formidable shape, resembling a horseshoe with precipitous sides strewn with boulders. Upon its grassy plateau lay ample grounds, previously utilized as a military cantonment of significant strength. While its usual garrison numbered a single regiment of three thousand, the moonlight revealed a far greater assembly: several regiments were encamped here, a gathering of forces that signaled impending conflict.

Upon reaching the table-land, we encountered a chaotic scene. Crowds of men, roused from their slumber, huddled together in a state of utter consternation, shivering with fear as they witnessed the unnatural darkening of the heavens. Moving silently through this sea of panic, we located a central hut where we were met with astonishment. Two men awaited us, burdened with our few scattered belongings—goods and chattels we had been forced to abandon during our frantic escape.

"I summoned them," Infadoos explained, gesturing to the carriers, "and also for these," as he lifted Good's long-lost trousers into the air.

Good reacted with an exclamation of rapturous joy, springing forward to don the garment instantly.

"Surely my lord will not conceal his beautiful white legs!" Infadoos remarked, his tone dripping with regret.

Good, however, remained steadfast in his modesty. Thus, the Kukuana people were granted but one fleeting glimpse of his pristine limbs. From this moment forward, their aesthetic desires were sated solely by his singular whisker, his translucent eye, and his mobile teeth. Still gazing with affectionate nostalgia at the recovered trousers, Infadoos announced that he had ordered the regiments to assemble at dawn. His purpose was twofold: to elucidate the origins and circumstances of the rebellion decreed by the chiefs, and to formally introduce to the troops the rightful heir to the throne, Ignosi.

When the sun finally breached the horizon, approximately twenty thousand men—the flower of the Kukuana army—were mustered in a vast open space. We approached to find the troops arranged in three sides of a dense, imposing square, presenting a magnificent spectacle of martial readiness. Taking our station on the open fourth side, we were swiftly enveloped by the principal chiefs and officers. After a proclamation of silence, Infadoos began to speak. Like many of his high-ranking kin, he was a born orator, delivering his narrative in vigorous, graceful language. He recounted the history of Ignosi's father, detailing how he had been basely murdered by King Twala, driving the queen and child into starvation. He painted a harrowing picture of the populace groaning under Twala's tyranny, citing the previous night's atrocities where nobles, falsely accused of evil deeds, had been dragged forth and executed.

Then, Infadoos shifted the focus to the intervention of the "white lords from the Stars." He declared that, observing the nation's plight from afar, they had resolved to alleviate its suffering despite the great personal inconvenience required. They had seized the true king, Ignosi, who languished in exile, and guided him over the mountains. Witnessing Twala's wickedness, they had acted decisively: using their high magic to extinguish the moon as a sign to the wavering, and to save the life of the girl Foulata, they had slain the young fiend Scragga. They stood ready, Infadoos proclaimed, to support the people in overthrowing Twala and restoring Ignosi to his throne.

The crowd murmured its approval as he concluded. Ignosi then stepped forward. Repeating the essential points of his uncle's speech, he delivered a powerful address.

"Chiefs, captains, soldiers, and people," Ignosi intoned, "you have heard my words. You must now choose between me and the usurper who sits upon my throne—the uncle who slaughtered his brother and drove his brother's child to die in the cold and the night. These"—he pointed to the assembled chiefs—"can testify that I am indeed the king, for they have seen the sacred snake coiled about my middle. If I were not the king, why would these white men stand with me, wielding all their magic? Tremble, chiefs, captains, soldiers, and people! Is not the darkness they have cast upon the land to confound Twala and shield our flight a darkness even deeper than that of the full moon?"

"It is," the soldiers answered in unison.

"I am the king," Ignosi declared, drawing up his great stature to its full height and raising his broad-bladed battle-axe above his head. "If there be any man among you who denies this truth, let him step forward. I will fight him now, and his blood shall serve as a red token that I speak the truth. Step forward, I say!" He shook the great axe until it flashed in the sunlight.

No one dared respond to this heroic summons. Our former henchman then resumed his address. "I am indeed the king," he continued. "Should you stand by my side in this battle, and I win the day, you shall share in my victory and honor. I will grant you oxen and wives, and you shall replace all existing regiments. And if I fall, I will fall with you."

"And behold," Ignosi promised, "when I sit upon the seat of my fathers, bloodshed shall cease in the land. No longer shall you cry for justice only to find slaughter; no longer shall the witch-finder hunt you down to be slain without cause. No man shall die except he who offends against the law. The 'eating up' of your kraals shall end. Each of you shall sleep securely within your own hut, fearing nothing, while justice walks blindfolded throughout the land. Have you chosen, chiefs, captains, soldiers, and people?"

"We have chosen, O King," the response thundered back.

"It is well. Turn your heads and witness how Twala's messengers depart from the great town, scattering east, west, north, and south to gather a mighty army to destroy me and you. Tomorrow, or perhaps the next day, he will come against us with all who remain faithful to him. Then I shall see the man who is truly mine, the man who fears not to die for his cause. Tell you this: he shall not be forgotten in the time of spoil. I have spoken, O chiefs, captains, soldiers, and people. Now go to your huts and prepare for war."

A pause fell over the assembly until a chief raised his hand, and the royal salute, "_Koom_," rolled out—a signal that the soldiers accepted Ignosi as their sovereign. Immediately, they marched off in disciplined battalions.

Half an hour later, we convened a council of war attended by all regimental commanders. It was evident that an attack in overwhelming force was imminent. From our vantage point atop the hill, we could see troops mustering below and runners darting forth from Loo in every direction, undoubtedly summoning reinforcements to the king's aid. On our side stood about twenty thousand men, comprising seven of the finest regiments in the country. According to calculations by Infadoos and the chiefs, Twala currently commanded at least thirty to thirty-five thousand loyalists assembled in Loo, with projections suggesting another five thousand or more would join him by midday the following day. While desertion was a possibility, it could not be relied upon. Clearly, Twala was making active preparations to subdue us; strong bodies of armed men patrolled the base of the hill, and other ominous signs of assault were becoming apparent.

However, Infadoos and the chiefs believed no attack would occur today. This day was reserved for preparation and for removing every trace of the moral impact caused by the supposed magical darkening of the moon. The onslaught, they predicted correctly, would come tomorrow.

Meanwhile, we set to work fortifying the position in every conceivable manner. Almost every man was mobilized, and within the span of a day that felt far too brief, considerable progress was made. The paths up the hill—which was less a fortress and more a sanatorium, typically used as a camping ground for regiments recovering from service in unhealthy regions—were carefully blocked with massive piles of stones. Every other approach was rendered as impregnable as time allowed. Piles of boulders were stacked at strategic points to be rolled down upon an advancing enemy, stations were assigned to the various regiments, and all preparations suggested by our combined ingenuity were executed.
