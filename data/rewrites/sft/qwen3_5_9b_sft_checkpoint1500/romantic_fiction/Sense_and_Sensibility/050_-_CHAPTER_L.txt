After a spirited, unyielding defense on Mrs. Ferrars's part—one fierce and steady enough to shield her from the dreaded accusation of being "too amiable"—Edward was finally granted an audience. She declared him, once more, her son.

Lately, the Ferrars household had known a whirlwind of change. For decades, she had boasted two sons, but the recent, tragic demise of Edward had left her bereft of one, and the untimely death of Robert had, for a fortnight, left her with none at all. Now, with Edward resurrected in spirit if not in health, she possessed a son again. Yet, despite this restoration, Edward did not feel his existence secure until he disclosed his current engagement. He feared that revealing his attachment to Miss Dashwood might send his already fragile constitution into a final decline, ending his life as swiftly as before. With trembling caution, he laid bare his feelings, receiving them with an unexpected, stoic calm. At first, Mrs. Ferrars tried to dissuade him, wielding every argument at her disposal: she spoke of Miss Morton's superior station and vast fortune, contrasting her noble father's thirty thousand pounds with Miss Dashwood's modest three hundred a year from a private gentleman. But when she saw that Edward, though acknowledging these facts, refused to let them steer his ship, she wisely resolved to yield. After a delay that hurt her pride and served to mask any genuine goodwill, she issued her decree of consent to the union of Edward and Elinor.

Next, the question of financial support arose. Here, the reality of Edward's position struck: though he was now her sole surviving son, he was not her *eldest*. While Robert was destined for a thousand pounds a year, no objection was raised to Edward taking holy orders for a mere two hundred and fifty at most. Beyond the ten thousand pounds previously given with Fanny, nothing further was promised, then or later. This sum, however, was all that Edward and Elinor desired and far more than they anticipated. Even Mrs. Ferrars, through her evasive excuses, seemed the only one surprised that she offered no more.

Secured in an income sufficient for their needs, the couple awaited only the completion of the Parsonage, which Colonel Brandon was eagerly improving for Elinor's comfort. Following the usual trials of construction—countless disappointments and delays attributable to the workers' inexplicable slowness—Elinor, true to form, broke her resolve to wait until everything was perfect. The wedding took place in Barton church in early autumn.

Their first month as husband and wife was spent at the Mansions, allowing them to oversee the finishing touches of their home and indulge in the domestic planning of choosing papers, designing shrubberies, and laying out paths. Mrs. Jennings's prophecies, though somewhat muddled, largely came true; by Michaelmas, she was able to visit them in their parsonage and found, as she truly believed, one of the happiest couples in the world. Indeed, they lacked for nothing save the hoped-for match of Colonel Brandon and Marianne, and perhaps better pasture for their cows.

Upon settling, they were visited by nearly all their relations and friends. Mrs. Ferrars arrived to inspect the happiness she had authorized with such reluctance, and even the Dashwoods traveled from Sussex to pay their respects. As Mr. John Dashwood walked with his sister-in-law before the gates of Delaford House, he remarked, "I will not say that I am disappointed, my dear sister... that would be saying too much, for certainly you have been one of the most fortunate young women in the world, as it is. But, I confess, it would give me great pleasure to call Colonel Brandon brother. His property here, his place, his house, every thing is in such respectable and excellent condition! And his woods—I have not seen such timber anywhere in Dorsetshire as there is now standing in Delaford Hanger! And though, perhaps, Marianne may not seem exactly the person to attract him, yet I think it would altogether be advisable for you to have them now frequently staying with you... you may as well give her a chance: you understand me."

Though Mrs. Ferrars did visit, treating them with a facade of decent affection, they never received her real favor. That warmth was reserved for Robert and his wife, won by the latter's cunning and humble flattery. Her "selfish sagacity," which had initially led Robert astray, ultimately saved him; her respectful humility and endless attentions, once given license, reconciled Mrs. Ferrars to her choice and fully re-established Robert in her esteem.

Lucy Steele's behavior in this affair, and the prosperity it crowned, stands as a testament to how unrelenting attention to self-interest, however obstructed, secures fortune with little cost but time and conscience. When Robert first courted her in Bartlett's Buildings, his brother John suspected designs on Edward's bride, but Lucy likely meant only to end her engagement. He expected a swift resolution, but he erred. Though Lucy quickly offered hope that his words would sway her, every subsequent visit and conversation was required to cement his conviction. Doubts lingered after each parting, requiring another half-hour of discourse to dispel. Through this persistent attendance, the rest followed naturally. Their conversations shifted gradually from Edward to Robert—a subject on which he always had more to say—and soon it became clear to both that he had entirely supplanted his brother. He was proud of his conquest, proud of deceiving Edward, and very proud of marrying privately without his mother's consent.

What followed is known. They spent several months in great happiness at Dawlish, where she entertained numerous relations and acquaintances, and he drafted plans for magnificent cottages. Returning to town, Robert procured his mother's forgiveness simply by asking for it—an action suggested by Lucy. Initially, this pardon covered only Robert; Lucy, owing no prior duty to her stepmother, remained unpardoned for weeks. However, perseverance in humble conduct, constant messages of self-condemnation regarding Robert's offense, and feigned gratitude for the kindness she was denied eventually procured her the haughty, gracious attention that transformed her status. Within a short time, Lucy rose to become a favorite child, acknowledged openly and cherished above all others. While Edward was never cordially forgiven for his near-marriage to Lucy, and Elinor, superior in fortune and birth, was often spoken of as an intruder, Lucy was considered indispensable to Mrs. Ferrars, alongside Robert and Fanny. Settling in town, they received generous aid from their mother-in-law, maintained close ties with the Dashwoods (despite the inevitable jealousy between Fanny and Lucy), and lived in a harmony that belied their internal domestic squabbles.

It remains difficult to discern precisely what Edward did to forfeit the rights of the eldest son, or what Robert accomplished to inherit them. Yet, the arrangement was justified by its effects. Nothing in Robert's lifestyle or speech suggested regret over the disparity in their incomes, nor did it appear that Edward envied his brother's wealth. Judging Edward by his diligent discharge of clerical duties, his deepening attachment to his wife and home, and the regular cheerfulness of his spirits, he seemed content with his lot, free from any longing for exchange.

Elinor's marriage kept her intimately connected to her family, sparing the cottage at Barton from total obscurity, as her mother and sisters spent much of their time there. Mrs. Dashwood acted on both policy and pleasure in visiting Delaford so frequently; her desire to bring Marianne and Colonel Brandon together was as earnest, if not more so, than John's expression. To see Marianne settled at the mansion-house was the darling object of Mrs. Dashwood, Edward, and Elinor. They each felt the weight of sorrow, honored their obligations, and agreed unanimously that Marianne should be the reward for all.

With such a confederacy working in her favor, with a knowledge of Colonel Brandon's goodness so intimate, and with a conviction of his enduring, fond attachment that finally burst upon her heart long after it had been obvious to everyone else, what could Marianne do?

Marianne Dashwood was born to an extraordinary fate. She was born to discover the falseness of her own youthful opinions and to counteract, through her actions, her most beloved maxims. Born to overcome an affection formed so late in life—at seventeen—that it sprang from no deeper sentiment than strong esteem and lively friendship, she voluntarily gave her hand to another! And that other was a man who had suffered no less than herself under the shadow of a former attachment, whom two years prior she had deemed too old to marry, and who still clung to the constitutional safeguard of a flannel waistcoat.

Yet, so it was. Instead of falling a victim to an irresistible passion, as she had once fondly flattered herself, instead of remaining forever with her mother to find solace in retirement and study as her calmer judgment later dictated, she found herself at nineteen submitting to new attachments. She entered new duties, a new home, becoming a wife, the mistress of a family, and the patroness of a village.

Colonel Brandon was now as happy as those who loved him best believed he deserved to be. In Marianne, he found consolation for every past affliction; her regard and society restored his mind to animation and his spirits to cheerfulness. That Marianne found her own happiness in forming his was the shared persuasion and delight of every observing friend. Marianne could never love by halves, and her whole heart, in time, became as devoted to her husband as it had once been to Willoughby.
