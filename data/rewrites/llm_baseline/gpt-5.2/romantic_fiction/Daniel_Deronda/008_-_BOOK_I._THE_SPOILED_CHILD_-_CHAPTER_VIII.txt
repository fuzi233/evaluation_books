At the rectory the trouble did not end with a burst of tears and an apology, the way a childish quarrel might be mended over the supper-table. It settled instead like a cold that would not lift—a long, lingering chill that crept through passages and under doors, till even the ordinary house-sounds seemed hushed into caution.

Rex had come home only to fling himself on his bed and lie there in a dull, obstinate stillness, as if he had taken a vow against comfort. When at last the apathy broke, it broke into feverishness and aching that made his mother’s hand hover helplessly above him, and made Anna’s little footsteps go and return on tiptoe with the nervous rhythm of a kitchen clock when the cook is waiting for water to boil. No one spoke now of Southampton. The only thought was how to tend a patient who did not wish to mend—how to coax him to broth, to drink, to rest—how to smooth the pillow without being met by that low, weary murmur, “Let me alone.”

Mrs. Gascoigne tried every homely ministry she knew, as if she could warm him back into himself by the old domestic arts: a cup brought up with the faint steam of beef-tea, bread toasted just enough to give a scent of comfort without roughness, arrowroot stirred till it shone, not lumpy, not thin. Yet the very odors that usually carried peace through a house seemed to offend him by their normality, and the tray came back hardly touched. The bright, thankful spirit who used to come in hungry from the air and praise everything—watercress, pudding, even yesterday’s cold meat if it were cut thin—had been changed into a creature with a blank brow and a dulled eye, as if appetite itself had been struck and left senseless.

His father looked beyond the crisis, believing it the shortest way out of an unlucky affair; but he was sorry for the suffering which must be paid before the boy could stand upright again. Now and then the rector went to sit by the bed a few minutes in silence, bringing with him the subdued murmur of the study—paper, pen, and the small creak of a chair that seems, in a quiet house, to have a conscience. He would leave Rex with only a gentle pressure of the hand on that unresponsive forehead and a low “God bless you, my boy,” as if the blessing could travel where argument could not.

Warham and the younger children would peer round the door, half frightened, half incredulous that the lively brother who made them laugh at table and whistled in the passage could lie so. But fingers were shaken at them at once and they were driven back, as if their common noise might bruise him.

Anna was always there. Her small hand was allowed to rest within Rex’s, though he never returned the pressure that used to answer hers. Her heart was divided—anguish for him, and a hard, hot reproach against Gwendolen that would not cool, do what she could. It kept returning, like a refrain she could not stop humming in her mind: perhaps it was wicked, but she thought she never could love her again.

Even Mrs. Gascoigne, who had been trained by habit and duty to take the charitable view, could not help letting some anger escape her, though she tried to clothe it in apology when she spoke to her husband.

“I know of course it is better—yes, we ought to be thankful—that she is not in love with the poor boy. But really, Henry, I think she is hard. She has the heart of a coquette. I cannot help thinking she must have made him believe something, or else disappointment would never have taken hold of him in this way. And some blame attaches to poor Fanny—she is quite blind about that girl.”

“The less said on that point the better, Nancy,” the rector answered, with the imperative quiet which meant his resolve was already made. “I ought to have been more awake myself. As for the boy—be thankful if nothing worse ever happens to him. Let the thing die out as quickly as possible; and with regard to Gwendolen especially—let it be as if it had never been.”

His dominant feeling was that there had been a great escape. A mutual attachment would have been a knot not to be loosened by mere command or distance, and might have drawn consequences out of his hands altogether.

Still, there was more difficulty to be borne.

One fine morning—fine with that winter clearness that makes every small sound distinct—Rex asked for his bath and made his toilet as usual. It was so sudden a turn toward ordinary life that Anna, full of trembling hope, could do nothing but listen for his step. The boards on the stairs gave their familiar speech under his weight, and she ran down to meet him at the foot.

For the first time he gave her a faint smile; but it sat sadly on his pale face, the way sunshine looks on hoar-frost—light without warmth—so that the tears rushed up in her throat.

“Nannie,” he said gently, taking her hand, and leading her slowly as if each movement had to be learned anew.

Mrs. Gascoigne was in the drawing-room. She came forward to kiss him, and he said, with a weariness that tried to make a jest of itself, “What a plague I am!”

Then he sat still and looked out of the bow-window on the lawn and shrubs coated with hoar-frost, while the sun sent faint, fitful gleams, like a shy guest that cannot make up its mind to stay. Anna thought the light looked like the melancholy smile on Rex’s face—something present and yet not belonging.

He had the sensation of having risen into a new world, where the old desires lay on the other side of some invisible boundary. Anna sat near him, pretending to work, but her needle hardly moved; her eyes were the ones busy, feeding on every change in his expression as if watching could keep him from slipping away again.

Beyond the garden hedge ran the road where wagons and carts went sometimes on field-work. There was a railed opening because the upland, with its bordering wood and a clump of ash-trees against the sky, pleased the eye even in winter. Presently a wagon came along laden with timber; the horses strained, their muscles swelling and shifting under the hide, and the driver cracked his whip—not in cruelty, but in that sharp, anxious way men do when weight and slope and responsibility press at once. He ran alongside to guide the leader’s head, fearing a swerve.

Rex seemed shaken into attention, rose, and looked until the last quivering trunk disappeared. Then he walked once or twice along the room, as if he were trying out his will in the open air of movement. Mrs. Gascoigne had left them; the house behind the drawing-room door was full of softened sounds—somebody stirring the fire, somebody setting down a pan in the kitchen, the faint clang of a lid—those reassuring domestic notes that say life continues and will insist on being fed.

When Rex sat down again, Anna saw a return of speech in his eyes. She could not resist. She brought a little stool and placed herself against his knee, looking up at him with that pleading simplicity which says, without words, Do not shut me out.

He did not shut her out.

“I’ll tell you what I’m thinking of, Nannie,” he said. “I will go to Canada, or somewhere of that sort.”

“Oh, Rex—not for always!”

“Yes—for good. To get my bread there. I should like to build a hut, and work hard at clearing, and have everything wild about me, and a great wide quiet.”

“And not take me with you?” Anna’s voice broke, the tears gathering large and sudden.

“How could I?”

“I should like it better than anything,” she cried, urgent now with the practical fervor of a child who believes love is proved by usefulness. “Settlers go with their families. I would sooner go there than stay here in England. I could make the fires, and mend the clothes, and cook the food—and I could learn how to make the bread before we went. It would be nicer than anything—like playing at life over again, as we used to do when we made our tent with the drugget, and had our little plates and dishes.”

The picture rose between them with a vividness that had the taste of comfort: a small hearth, flour measured and mixed with care, yeast kept alive like a cherished secret, the warm smell of loaves rising in some rough oven while the world outside lay wide and still. To Anna, it was not exile—it was a new household, a restored intimacy, a language of daily acts that could not betray you the way fine words could.

“Father and mother would not let you go,” Rex said, but without conviction, as if he wanted to hear her plead.

“Yes, I think they would when I explained everything,” she insisted. “It would save money; and papa would have more to bring up the boys with.”

They talked so at intervals, in that earnest, matter-of-fact way which is often only the heart disguising itself in housekeeping and accounts. At last Rex was obliged to consent that Anna should go with him when he spoke to his father. He seemed to need her there—as if her presence could soften the rector’s authority, as a hand laid on a kneading-board steadies the bowl.

Of course they chose a time when Mr. Gascoigne was alone in his study. To bring it first to their mother would have been to lay the blade on her nerves. She would have reconciled herself afterward to whatever her husband decided; but the first blow would have been hers to feel.

“Well, my children!” said the rector cheerfully when they entered. It was a comfort to see Rex up again, clothed and steady on his feet.

“May we sit down with you a little, papa?” Anna said. “Rex has something to say.”

“With all my heart.”

It was a noticeable group, those three, even before a word of the petition was made: the same structural family stamp—straight brow; a nose that had started with an intention of being aquiline but had been persuaded into straightness; a short upper lip; a short, strong, well-hung chin. There was the same tone of complexion, the same set of eye, as if heredity had been a dialect spoken in bone and skin. The gray-haired father looked at once massive and keen. A perpendicular line in his brow deepened whenever he spoke with force of interest, and the habit of ruling gave him an air of reserved authoritativeness. Rex, in his paleness and uncertainty, might have been a vision of his father’s youth—if one could imagine Mr. Gascoigne without plans, without command, smitten with heart-sorrow and as incapable of concealment as a wounded creature. Anna was a tiny copy of Rex, her hair drawn back and knotted, her face changing as his changed, as if they shared one soul between them.

“You know all about what has upset me, father,” Rex began.

The rector nodded.

“I am quite done up for life in this part of the world. I am sure it will be no use my going back to Oxford. I couldn’t do any reading. I should fail, and cause you expense for nothing. I want to have your consent to take another course, sir.”

Mr. Gascoigne’s nod this time was slower; the line on his brow grew deeper. Anna’s tremor increased as if she felt her brother’s words as blows falling.

“If you would allow me a small outfit, I should like to go to the colonies and work on the land there.” Rex kept the phrase broad, as if vagueness might pass more easily through opposition than a named destination.

“Oh—and with me, papa,” Anna put in quickly, not bearing to be excluded even for a moment. “Rex would want some one to take care of him—you know, some one to keep house. And we shall never, either of us, be married. And I should cost nothing, and I should be so happy. I know it would be hard to leave you and mamma; but there are all the others to bring up, and we two should be no trouble to you any more.”

She had risen and moved closer, using that argument of nearness which belongs to the young and the tender—her voice and body both pleading. The rector did not smile, but he drew her on his knee and held her there, as if gently removing her from the field while he spoke to Rex.

“You will admit that my experience gives me some power of judging for you,” he said, “and that I can probably guide you in practical matters better than you can guide yourself?”

Rex was obliged to answer, “Yes, sir.”

“And perhaps you will admit—though I don’t wish to press that point—that you are bound in duty to consider my judgment and wishes?”

“I have never yet placed myself in opposition to you, sir.” Rex could not, in the secrecy of his feeling, grant that duty bound him not to go away, but to go back to Oxford—that was the core of the dispute.

“But you will do so,” Mr. Gascoigne continued, “if you persist in setting your mind toward a rash and foolish procedure, and deafening yourself to considerations which my experience assures me of. You think, I suppose, that you have had a shock which has changed all your inclinations—stupefied your brains—unfitted you for anything but manual labor—and given you a dislike to society? Is that what you believe?”

“Something like that,” Rex said. “I shall never be up to the sort of work I must do to live in this part of the world. I have not the spirit for it. I shall never be the same again. And without any disrespect to you, father, I think a young fellow should be allowed to choose his way of life, if he does nobody any harm. There are plenty to stay at home, and those who like might be allowed to go where there are empty places.”

“But suppose I am convinced on good evidence—as I am—that this state of mind of yours is transient,” said the rector, “and that if you went off as you propose, you would by-and-by repent, and feel that you had let yourself slip back from the point you have been gaining by your education till now? Have you not strength of mind enough to see that you had better act on my assurance for a time, and test it? In my opinion—so far from agreeing with you that you should be free to turn yourself into a colonist and work in your shirt-sleeves with spade and hatchet—in my opinion you have no right whatever to expatriate yourself until you have honestly endeavored to turn to account the education you have received here. I say nothing of the grief to your mother and me.”

“I’m very sorry; but what can I do? I can’t study—that’s certain,” Rex said, with the helplessness of one who feels his own mind like a locked pantry.

“Not just now, perhaps. You will have to miss a term. I have made arrangements for you—how you are to spend the next two months. But I confess I am disappointed in you, Rex. I thought you had more sense than to take up such ideas—to suppose that because you have fallen into a very common trouble, such as most men have to go through, you are loosened from all bonds of duty—just as if your brain had softened and you were no longer a responsible being.”

Rex had no arguments ready against his father’s. He felt rebellion coiled within him—hot, ashamed, powerless. Even while he thought, with a stubborn longing that would not reason, that he would like to set off for “the colonies” to-morrow, there lay deep in him, folded away like an old family saying he could not quite forget, the sense that he ought—if he had been better—if he had had more manliness—he ought to feel more for the old ties. It is a strange faith that lives on in us in such sickness: the belief that there exists somewhere a truer self that would behave more nobly than this suffering one.

Rex rose, as if to end the conference.

“You assent to my arrangement, then?” Mr. Gascoigne said, in the tone that closes like a vise.

There was a pause before Rex answered. “I’ll try what I can do, sir. I can’t promise.” And his thought was that trying would be useless.

The rector kept Anna fast on his knee though she wanted to follow her brother.

“Oh, papa,” she said when the door had closed and Rex’s step had faded, “it is very hard for him. Doesn’t he look ill?”

“Yes. But he will soon be better. It will all blow over. And now, Anna—be as quiet as a mouse about it all. Never let it be mentioned when he is gone.”

“No, papa.” Then, as if she needed to save the world from such pain by a private vow, she added, “But I would not be like Gwendolen for anything—to have people fall in love with me so. It is very dreadful.”

She dared not say what else lay stinging under her obedience: that she had wanted—oh, fiercely—to go with Rex, to be his household in a wide quiet place where the day’s labor would end in food you had made with your own hands, and where love might speak in the plain dialect of usefulness—fire lit, clothes mended, bread baked—without the cruel, fashionable language that could toy with hearts and call it nothing. That desire remained her secret, and she went over the whole affair afterward in her mind, thinking with a guilty relish: how she would have done with going out, and gloves, and crinoline, and having to talk when she was taken to dinner—and all that.

And indeed it was just then, in that particular season of the world, that skirts were widening as if the very fashion of cloth demanded more room in churches, ball-rooms, and vehicles—a broadening that made the ordinary life of women noisier and more encumbered, rustling through rooms like a constant reminder of display. But Anna Gascoigne’s figure would only allow the size manufactured for young ladies of fourteen; and perhaps that was fitting, since her longing was not for more space to be seen in, but for a quieter world in which the simplest sounds—the crackle of a hearth, the soft thud of dough on a board, the hush after a kettle’s singing—might be enough to hold a heart together.
