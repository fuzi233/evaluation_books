Friday evening marked the arrival of Newland Archer at the Chiverses', a date that initiated a weekend at Highbank governed by the rigid arithmetic of social propriety. On Saturday, the schedule unfolded with mechanical precision: a morning excursion in an ice-boat alongside the hostess and the season's hardiest guests; an afternoon inspection of the farm with Reggie, punctuated by lengthy, ornate lectures delivered within the appointed stables regarding equine anatomy; and post-tea conversations in the firelit hall with a young lady who had recently transitioned from despair over his engagement to enthusiasm regarding her own matrimonial prospects. By midnight, the rituals of leisure had devolved into the chaotic performance of putting a goldfish into a guest's bed, dressing a burglar in a nervous aunt's bathroom, and engaging in a pillow fight that migrated from the nurseries to the basement. Yet, by Sunday lunch, the cycle reset. Archer borrowed a cutter and drove toward Skuytercliff.

To the uninitiated, the house at Skuytercliff existed as a myth—an Italian villa conjured by the collective imagination of those who had never visited Italy, or perhaps those who had and chose to forget. Built by Mr. van der Luyden during his youth, following his return from the grand tour and anticipating his marriage to Miss Louisa Dagonet, the structure was a large square of wood, clad in pale green and white tongued-and-grooved siding, featuring a Corinthian portico and fluted pilasters. From its elevated perch, terraces bordered by balustrades and urns cascaded in the style of a steel engraving down to a small, irregular lake edged in asphalt and overhung by rare weeping conifers. To the sides, the famous weedless lawns, studded with specimen trees of distinct varieties, rolled away to grassy ridges crowned with elaborate cast-iron ornaments. Below, nestled in a hollow, lay the four-roomed stone house constructed by the first Patroon in 1612.

Against the uniform sheet of snow and the grey winter sky, the Italian villa loomed with a grim severity. Even in summer, it maintained a distant, imposing silence; the boldest coleus beds never ventured closer than thirty feet from its formidable front. As Archer rang the bell, the prolonged tinkle echoed through a mausoleum. The butler's eventual response was marked by a surprise akin to being summoned from death itself. Fortunately, Archer belonged to the family. Despite the irregularity of his arrival, he was entitled to learn that the Countess Olenska was absent, having driven to afternoon service with Mrs. van der Luyden precisely forty-five minutes prior.

"The Countess is out," the butler confirmed, adding that Mr. van der Luyden was present but likely finishing a nap or reviewing the previous day's *Evening Post*. He offered to eavesdrop at the library door, a gesture of servile diligence. Archer declined, expressing gratitude, and thanked him for the information. The butler, visibly relieved, closed the door with majestic finality.

A groom took the cutter to the stables, and Archer proceeded on foot through the park toward the high road. The village of Skuytercliff lay a mile and a half away, but Archer knew Mrs. van der Luyden refused to walk; he must remain on the road to intercept the carriage. Descending a footpath that crossed the highway, he spotted a slight figure in a red cloak, preceded by a large dog. He hurried forward, and Madame Olenska halted, offering a smile of welcome.

"Ah, you've come!" she said, withdrawing her hand from her muff.

The red cloak rendered her vibrant and lively, reminiscent of the Ellen Mingott of old. Archer laughed as he took her hand, replying, "I came to see what you were running away from."

Her expression darkened. "Ah, well—you will see, presently."

The ambiguity unsettled him. "Do you mean you've been overtaken?"

She shrugged, executing a movement reminiscent of Nastasia, and adopted a lighter tone. "Shall we walk on? I am so cold after the sermon. And what does it matter, now that you are here to protect me?"

Blood surged to his temples. He seized a fold of her cloak. "Ellen—what is it? You must tell me."

"Oh, presently—let's run a race first; my feet are freezing to the ground," she cried. Gathering the cloak, she fled across the snow, the dog leaping around her with challenging barks. For a moment, Archer watched, delighted by the flash of the red meteor against the white expanse. Then he started after her. They met, panting and laughing, at a wicket leading into the park.

She looked up, smiling. "I knew you'd come!"

"That proves you wanted me to," he replied, his joy disproportionate to the triviality of their exchange. The white glitter of the trees filled the air with a mysterious brightness, and as they walked over the snow, the ground seemed to sing beneath their feet.

"Where did you come from?" Madame Olenska asked.

He recounted his journey, adding, "It was because I received your note."

After a pause, her voice carrying a perceptible chill, she said, "May asked you to take care of me."

"I didn't need any asking."

"You mean—I am so evidently helpless and defenseless? What a poor thing you must all think me! But women here seem not... seem never to feel the need: any more than the blessed in heaven."

He lowered his voice. "What sort of a need?"

"Ah, don't ask me! I don't speak your language," she retorted petulantly.

The answer struck him like a physical blow. He stood still in the path, looking down at her. "What did I come for, if I don't speak yours?"

"Oh, my friend—" She placed her hand lightly on his arm. He pleaded earnestly, "Ellen—why won't you tell me what's happened?"

She shrugged again. "Does anything ever happen in heaven?"

Silence stretched between them as they walked a few yards without exchanging a word. Finally, she spoke. "I will tell you—but where, where, where? One cannot be alone for a minute in that great seminary of a house, with all the doors wide open, and always a servant bringing tea, or a log for the fire, or the newspaper! Is there nowhere in an American house where one may be by oneself? You are so shy, and yet you are so public. I always feel as if I were in the convent again—or on the stage, before a dreadfully polite audience that never applauds."

"Ah, you don't like us!" Archer exclaimed.

They passed the house of the old Patroon, its squat walls and small square windows clustered tightly around a central chimney. The shutters stood wide, and through a freshly washed window, Archer caught the flicker of a fire.

"Why—the house is open!" he said.

She stopped. "No; only for today, at least. I wished to see it, and Mr. van der Luyden had the fire lit and the windows opened so that we might stop there on the way back from church this morning." She ran up the steps and tested the door. "It is still unlocked—what luck! Come in, and we can have a quiet talk. Mrs. van der Luyden has driven to see her old aunts at Rhinebeck, and we shall not be missed until another hour."

He followed her into the narrow passage. His spirits, which had plummeted at her earlier words, leaped upward with an irrational surge. The modest house stood there, its panels and brasses gleaming in the firelight, as if magically created to receive them. A large bed of embers still glowed in the kitchen chimney beneath an iron pot suspended from an ancient crane. Rush-bottomed armchairs faced each other across the tiled hearth, and rows of Delft plates lined the wall shelves. Archer stooped and threw a log onto the embers.

Madame Olenska dropped her cloak and sat in one of the chairs. Archer leaned against the chimney, studying her.

"You are laughing now; but when you wrote to me, you were unhappy," he observed.

"Yes." She paused. "But I cannot feel unhappy when you are here."

"I sha'n't be here long," he rejoined, his lips stiffening with the effort to say no more than necessary.

"No; I know. But I am improvident: I live in the moment when I am happy."

The words slipped through him like a seduction. To block out this sensation, he moved away from the hearth and stood gazing out at the black tree trunks against the snow. Yet, it seemed she had shifted her position as well, and he still perceived her, situated between himself and the trees, drooping over the fire with her indolent smile. Archer's heart beat in rebellion. What if she had been fleeing from him, and had waited to confess this until they were alone in this secret room?

"Ellen, if I am truly useful to you—if you truly wanted me to come—tell me what is wrong. Tell me what it is you are running away from," he insisted.

He spoke without changing his posture, without turning to look at her. If the revelation was to occur, it must happen thus, with the entire width of the room separating them, his eyes fixed on the outer snow.
