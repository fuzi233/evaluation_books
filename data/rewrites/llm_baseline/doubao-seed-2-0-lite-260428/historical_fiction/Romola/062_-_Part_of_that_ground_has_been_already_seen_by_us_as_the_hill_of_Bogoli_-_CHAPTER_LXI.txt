Eight days removed from that unforgettable stormy night in Florence, Romola stood at the edge of the Mediterranean, her grey penitent’s robes billowing soft around her ankles as she watched the slow, warm summer swell lap at the shore just above the tiny fishing village of Viareggio.

She had fled Florence for the second time, and not a single arresting voice had called her back to turn. This grey religious disguise she had worn before, and now, in the raw, hollow sickness of her heart, she did not even care that it was a disguise. A sharp new rebellion had risen in her breast, paired with a despair so heavy it seemed to press the very breath from her. What did it matter if she wore one badge or another, if anyone knew her true name? She had long since lost hope of finding any consistent, worthy duty that belonged to the name Romola de’ Bardi. Where was the force to carve out that sacred, all-consuming thing men called duty? It could never take root, she thought, without the anchor of some kind of believing love, and every bond of that love had been snapped clean away from her.

In her marriage, the highest, most sacred bond of all, she could no longer see the mystic union that was supposed to guarantee its indissolubility—could no longer even respect the weight of the voluntary pledge she had made. Had not every single thing she had bound herself to work for proven impossible? The urge to cast herself loose had risen again, overwhelming, even as she knew the freedom she sought would be no more than an exchange of one private calamity for another. There is no recompense for a woman who discovers the central, defining relationship of her life was nothing but a mistake. She has lost the crown of her own life, that secret core of human blessedness that had half-whispered itself to her, only to slip away forever.

Even the last great support that had held her upright under that unendurable sorrow had crumbled now. The vision of some grand, worthy purpose, some end to existence that could make endurance noble, that could gild the small, dusty, common deeds of daily life with a divine fire—all of it had been snuffed out. All she could see now was a tangle of human confusion, where every effort was no more than tugging at knotted threads that would never unravel, every alliance for good was lopsided, unfair, exclusive.

What of the man who had once embodied her highest idea of heroism? Not the cold, stoic heroism of grim self-denial, but the soft, burning heroism of a man who gave himself freely for others: Savonarola. Romola’s trust in him was gone. That burning admiration that had let her overlook his missteps, that let her only see the grand sweeping arc of his life’s work, had burnt out to ash. Now, after her fierce loyalty to her dying godfather had pitted her against the Frate, she could see every repulsive, contradictory detail of his teachings with a cruel, sharp clarity that blew every small flaw out of all proportion. In her bitter disappointment, she told herself his grand project to renovate the Church and the world was nothing but a hollow name, no more meaningful than the title on the cover of a book. In practice, all it amounted to was measures that strengthened his own hold on power in Florence, even questionable lies and compromises, all to keep his influence from crumbling beneath the weight of his own errors. That political reform that had once lit up new purpose in her days now seemed nothing but small, narrow schemes to keep Florence safe, shamefully at odds with his public professions of absolute blind trust in Divine providence.

It was no surprise that she judged the Frate unfairly in that hour of personal grief: she looked at his choices through the lens of tender, private love for her godfather, while he looked at them through the unyielding lens of his rigid theoretic conviction. When he declared that the cause of his party was the very cause of God’s kingdom, she heard nothing but the cold ring of egoism. Such words, true enough, almost never ring free of that meaner self-regard, even as they are the unspoken core of all passionate, energetic belief. And that kind of fierce belief, chasing a grand, distant end, can easily slip into a kind of demon worship, where the votary will send his own son and daughter through the fire with a readiness that barely counts as sacrifice. But tender, personal fellow-feeling for the people close to you has its own dangers too: it grows timid, cynical, dismissive of the large, far-off aims that lift human life beyond mere smallness. That was the trap Romola had fallen into now, blinded by her own tears.

No one who has ever known what it is to lose faith in a man they loved and revered with their whole heart can claim the shock leaves your faith in the Unseen Goodness untouched. When your high trust in the goodness of other people sinks, the dignity of your own life sinks right along with it. You stop believing in your own better self, because that better self is part of the same fallen, disappointing human nature you have come to despise. All the fine, generous impulses of the soul grow dull. Romola could feel the springs of her once abundant pity drying up inside her, leaving her with nothing but the barren, self-absorbed weight of her own complaints. Had she not borne more than her share of sorrow? So few had cared for her, while she had spent her whole life caring for others. She had done her part, striven after the impossible, and she was sick to death of this crowded, stifling life. She ached for the total, unthinking rest of mere sensation, the kind she had daydreamed of as a girl, in sultry Florentine afternoons, when she had imagined herself floating like a naiad in cool, weightless water.

The clear, glassy waves seemed to call to her. She wished she could lie down and sleep on their surface, glide straight from that sleep into death. But Romola could not willfully kill herself; the full, warm force of young life in her body would not let her. She could only wish that death would come and meet her halfway.

The cove where she stood curved deep into the shore, and a small, weathered sailboat was moored there, bobbing soft on the swell. As she ached to glide out over the waters now gilded by the low, slanting sun, a memory rose unbidden from her girlhood: of the old Boccaccio stories she had loved to sneak and read when her father nodded off over his manuscripts, slipping off her stool to sit cross-legged on the floor with the *Decamerone* in her lap. There was the story of fair Gostanza, heartbroken, who no longer wished to live but could not bring herself to harm her own young body, so she climbed into a small boat and pushed off to sea, wrapping her mantle around her head to wait for the wreck that would carry her to death without her needing to lift a hand.

The memory had never been more than a passing fancy before, but now, as she paced the sand, she saw a second dark boat glide across the gold-red surface of the water, carrying a lone fisherman heading straight for her cove. When he landed, he hauled his boat up onto the shore and began unloading his catch. He was almost certainly the owner of the small moored boat too. He would pack up and leave soon, and her chance would vanish with him: the chance to buy that little craft. She had not yet admitted to herself what she planned to do with it, but she felt a sharp, urgent eagerness to secure that possibility, proof that a half-formed thought had already grown deep into a quiet desire.

“Is that little boat moored yours as well?” she called to him. The fisherman looked up, startled by the tall, grey-robed figure wandering alone on the shore at dusk, and bowed respectfully to what he took for a holy sister, wandering in silent devotion.

It was his, he told her: an old craft, hardly seaworthy anymore, but worth patching up for any man who needed it. Thanks to the blessing of San Antonio, whose chapel stood at the edge of the village, his fishing had been good lately, and he had bought himself a newer, better boat—one that had belonged to old Gianni, before Gianni died. He had not gotten around to selling the old one yet.

Romola asked him what it was worth, and while he was turned away unloading his baskets of fish, she slipped the full sum of the price into the small satchel on the sand beside him, where he had stowed the remains of his midday meal. After that, she watched him furl his sail, asked him how he would tie the sail if he wanted to head out to sea, and then paced the sand while she waited for him to leave.

The fantasy of gliding away in that little boat on the darkening water grew into a sharp, burning longing, like the thirst for a cool stream on a sweltering midday. To be freed from the burden of making choices, when every motive that had ever guided her was bruised and broken. To commit herself, half-asleep, to the blind hand of fate: fate that might bring her death, or else new, unlooked-for necessities that might wake some new kind of life in her. The soft evening air made that thought all the more tempting, far better than tramping back to the hot, noisy village inn.

At last the slow, methodical fisherman gathered up all his belongings and tramped away toward the village. The golden glow of the sun shrank and dimmed across sea and sky, and there was not a living soul in sight. No sound broke the quiet but the soft, lulling lap of the waves against the shore. There was no strong tide here to carry her out to sea, but Romola could feel a soft offshore breeze picking up, blowing steadily out across the water. She climbed into the small boat, unfurled its tattered sail, and tied it exactly as the fisherman had shown her. She felt the breeze catch the light canvas, and that was all she needed. She untied the mooring rope, pushed off with an oar until she was far out from the shore, until even the western sky had gone dark, and the stars pricked through the dusk like tiny beating pulses of light across the wide empty sky.

At last she stopped rowing, leaned back, and pushed her cowl off her head. She untied the linen kerchief that pinned her hair back, and folded both the cowl and the kerchief to make a soft pillow on the hard wooden rib of the boat. Her fair, young head could bear the rough rest. So she lay, the soft night air brushing her face, as the little boat drifted slowly out across the water, watching the sky deepen into still, vast quiet.

She was alone now. She had freed herself from every claim, every obligation that had weighed on her. She had freed herself even from that heavy, grinding burden of making choices, that presses harder and harder when all the old guiding duties have slipped out of your reach.

Had she found the weightless rest she had daydreamed of as a girl? No. Memories hung to her like the weight of broken wings that could never fly again: memories of all the human love and sympathy she had known, even in its pain, that left a hunger no cold sea, no vast empty sky could ever feed. Romola felt orphaned, adrift between the wide stretches of sea and sky. She could read no message of love for her in the far-off symbolic writing of the stars, and a great, racking sob tore out of her throat, as she wished more than anything to glide straight on into death.

She pulled her cowl back over her head, covering her face, choosing total darkness over the light of the stars that felt like the cold, unseeing gaze of thousands of eyes staring right through her. Soon, it felt to her as if she lay in an unquiet grave, but not resting there: she could feel the hands of all the beloved dead lying beside her, and she was reaching out to touch them, trying to wake them.
