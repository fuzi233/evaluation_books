"Follow thee? Follow thee? What man would not follow thee? Thou hast loved and trusted us faithfully for a long time."

Amyas would undoubtedly have succumbed to yellow fever, had not one overriding reason spared him. He explained this logic to Cary: he simply lacked the luxury of illness while his men were falling ill. It was a valid, sufficient excuse—a truth known too well by countless noble souls in the Crimea—as long as the adrenaline of labor remains high. Yet, it is a trap that often ensnares heroes; the moment their work ceases, the vitality drains away, and they sink into the abyss they had previously leaped over.

The following morning, facing a crisis that left him at his wits' end, Amyas convened a council of war—or, more accurately, a sanitary commission. The men were in a state of panic, teetering on the brink of mutiny. Amyas addressed them sternly, declaring that he saw no benefit in them killing him or allowing themselves to be killed by him. With that, he descended below deck to confer with his officers.

The results of the consultation were dismal. The doctor spoke only of "science"—or what amounted to nonsense—discussing humors, complexions, and animal spirits. Jack Brimblecombe offered only pulpit rhetoric, labeling the outbreak a direct visitation of God. Cary expressed pure despair, masking it with a nervous jest. Yeo displayed stoic fatalism, bolstered by Scriptural quotes. As for Drew, the master, he remained silent; his duty, he insisted, was merely to sail the ship, not to cure fevers.

Enraged, Amyas gripped his hair in his customary fashion and erupted. "Doctor! A fig for your humors and complexions! Can you cure a man's humors or alter his complexion? Can an Ethiopian change his skin, or a leopard its spots? Do not attribute your ignorance to God, sir. You claim to know the cause of this sickness, yet you know nothing. Jack Brimblecombe, cease your talk of God's visitation; this bears far more the marks of the Devil's visitation. We are doing God's work, Sir John, and He would not hinder us. Down with the devil!"

Turning to Cary, he continued, "Laughing kills the cat, but it will not cure a Christian. Yeo, if an angel were to tell me that it is God's will for us all to die like dogs in a ditch, I would accept it as God's will—but not until then. Drew, you say your business is to sail the ship; then sail her out of this infernal poison-trap this very morning, if you possibly can, which you clearly cannot. The malady lies in the air, nowhere else. I felt it coursing through me last night as I came aboard, smelling it like a sewer. If it were not in the air, explain to me why my boat's crew was struck first!"

Silence met his outburst.

"Then I shall tell you why they were struck first," Amyas declared. "When we cut through the mist, it rose only five or six feet above the stream, engulfing us, while you on board remained above it. Every man taken on board this morning slept on the main deck, and every one of them feared the fever. From this, I deduce two truths: Keep as high as you can, and fear nothing but God, and we shall yet be safe."

"But the fog reached our round-tops at sunrise this morning," Cary objected.

"I know it," Amyas replied. "Yet those of us on the half-deck were exposed to it for a shorter duration than those below, and we breathed free air. Furthermore, I suspect the evening heat draws the poison out more intensely, and as the temperature drops toward morning, the venom dissipates somehow."

How exactly it dissipated, Amyas could not say (nor could anyone else at that time); it would take nearly two centuries of fatal experience for American settlers to uncover the simple laws governing such epidemics, knowledge now held by every child. However, common sense was on Amyas's side. Yeo stood and spoke.

"As I have stated before, the Lord has often sent us a young Daniel to act as judge. I recall hearing the Spaniards say that these calentures linger only in low-lying ground, rarely rising more than a few hundred feet above the sea."

"Then let us ascend those few hundred feet," Amyas suggested.

Every man turned to look at Amyas, then at his neighbor.

"Gentlemen," Amyas proclaimed, "'Look the devil straight in the face if you wish to strike him in the right place.' We cannot launch the ship as she stands; even if we could, we cannot return empty-handed, nor can we remain here to perish of fever. We must abandon the ship and move inland."

"Inland?" echoed every voice except Yeo's.

"To those hundred feet Yeo speaks of. To the mountains. We shall stockade a camp and relocate our sick and supplies there."

"And what comes next?" someone asked.

"When we have recovered, we shall march over the mountains and surprise St. Jago de Leon."

Cary swore a great oath. "Amyas! You are a daring fellow!"

"Not a bit," Amyas retorted. "It is the plain path of prudence."

"So it is, sir," nodded old Yeo, "and I follow you in it."

"And so do I," squeaked Jack Brimblecombe.

"Nay, then, Jack, thou shalt not outrun me. So I say yes too," added Cary.

"Mr. Drew?"

"At your service, sir, to live or die. Though I know nothing of stockading, I verily believe Sir Francis would have offered the same counsel had he been in your place."

"Then inform the men we depart in an hour. Win over the *Pelicans* and the liberated galley-slaves, Yeo and Drew; the rest must follow like sheep over a hedge."

The *Pelicans* and the freed slaves embraced the plan immediately, but the remainder of the crew subjected Amyas to a stormy hour. The critical question arose: Where were the hills? Within the dense mangrove thicket, visibility was limited to fifty yards.

"The hills lie no more than three miles to the southwest of you right now," Amyas assured them. "I marked every ridge as we entered."

"I suppose you intended to lead us there?"

That question ignited a fuse, and angry suspicions flared up one by one. Amyas silenced them with a counter-move.

"Fools! If I did not possess the wit to look ahead further than you do, where would you be? Are you mad as well as reckless to rise against your own captain because he possesses two strings to his bow? Go my way, I say, or, as I live, I will blow up this ship and every soul on board, sparing you the pain of rotting here inch by inch."

The men knew Amyas never uttered threats he did not intend to carry out—not that he truly intended to destroy the vessel, for he knew the threat alone was sufficient deterrent. Thus, they agreed to proceed, reassured by seeing the old *Pelican*'s men turn to the task heartily and cheerfully.

No need to burden the reader with five or six weary hours under a broiling (or rather, stewing) sun, stumbling over mangrove roots, hacking through thorny thickets, and dragging sick men and provisions up mountainous steeps amidst disappointment, fatigue, murmurs, curses, snakes, mosquitoes, false alarms of Spanish forces, and every misery save cold that flesh is heir to. Suffice it to say that by sunset that evening, they had secured a level plateau, a full thousand feet above the sea. It was backed by an inaccessible cliff forming the upper shoulder of a mighty mountain, defended below by steep, wooded slopes, requiring only the felling of a few trees to render it impregnable.

Amyas settled the sick beneath the arched roots of an enormous cottonwood tree and made a second journey to the ship to retrieve hammocks and blankets for them. Meanwhile, Yeo's wisdom and courage proved invaluable. As a pioneer, he located the small brook up which they forced their passage; he encouraged them to scale the cliffs over which it fell, correctly arguing that along its course they would inevitably find suitable ground for encampment near water. He supported Amyas whenever the weary crew begged to be dragged no farther, returning a dozen times to cheer them upward. Cary, bringing up the rear, bullied and spurred on the stragglers who sat down refusing to move, driving back more than one attempting a retreat at the point of a sword, carrying their burdens, and singing songs during halts. In everything, he approved himself as the gallant and hopeful soul he had always been. Until Amyas, beside himself with joy at discovering that the two men on whom he relied most were utterly worthy of his trust, went so far as to whisper to them both in confidence that very night:

"Cortez burned his ships when he landed. Why should not we?"

Yeo jumped upright, then sat down again, whispering, "Do you say that, Captain? 'Tis from above, then, that's certain; for it has been hanging on my mind all day."

"There is no hurry," Amyas replied. "We must clear the ship first, you know," while Cary sat silent and contemplative. Amyas evidently harbored more schemes than he chose to reveal.
