In that May, far away at Wadi Halfa, Durrance began to lift his eyes from the frontier and, with a longing that sat like thirst on the tongue, to look homeward. Yet the true business of his fate was being kneaded and baked in a heat he could not feel—five hundred miles to the south, beyond the great Nubian desert and the Belly of Stones, where men counted their lives by mouths of water and the sounds the wind allowed them.

On the deserted track between Berber and Suakin the wells of Obak lay sunk deep among mounds of shifting sand, as though the earth had swallowed its own cup and left only the rim to be found. Eastward ran a belt of trees, thin as a fence built for a modest garden, dividing the dunes from a hard stony plain shouldered up with granite hills. Westward the desert stretched fifty-eight waterless miles to Mahobey and Berber on the Nile—a flatness so pitiless that a tuft of grass no higher than a knee, seen from a mile, promised the shade of a great tree, and a pile of stones no bigger than those by any road under repair took on, in the distance, the stature of a hill.

In that particular May there could be no spot more empty. The sun struck it from six in the morning with an intolerable heat; at night the wind cut cold and restless, worrying the sand with the diligence of a cook’s hands at dough—raising pyramids house-high, flattening them again, tunnelling little valleys, silting slopes, so that the very face of the country was forever re-shaped. Vultures held the sky and sand-grouse held the ground in a tenancy without rent. Scattered here and there lay the bleached bones of camels, as if the desert had once taken a caravan as payment and left the accounts open for all to read. The remnants of a hut—branches bent in hoops—showed where Arabs had once penned goats and lived by milk and patience. Now the sun rose and set upon a round of honey-coloured earth. Silence brooded there like night on water; the stillness was so absolute it became a kind of expectation, as though one listened for a language one had half forgotten and feared never to hear again.

Yet in this May one man sojourned at the wells, and he did it secretly. Every morning at sunrise he drove down from the belt of trees two camels—swift riding-mares of the pure Bisharin breed—and watered them. Then he sat by the well-mouth for three hours, motionless, his presence as spare as a shadow at noon. Afterwards he drove them back into the trees and fed them delicately with dhoura, laying the grain out upon a cloth as a careful cook spreads spices to keep the sand from them. The dry kernels made a soft, clean rattle; the camels’ jaws worked with a slow contentment. Then, for the rest of the day, the man vanished.

For five mornings he came thus from his hiding-place and sat looking toward the sand-dunes and Berber, and no one approached him. On the sixth morning, as he was on the point of returning to the trees, he saw a man and a donkey outlined on the sky-line upon a crest of sand. The watcher by the well looked first at the donkey, and, noticing its grey coat, half rose. But as he rose he saw the driver: the jellab drawn forward to shield the face from the sun, and beneath it bare legs of ebony black against the pale sand. A negro.

The Arab sat down again, letting his body speak indifference. He did not even turn when the man’s feet sank and whispered close behind him.

“Salam aleikum,” said the negro as he stopped.

He carried a long spear and a short one, and a shield of hide. He laid them on the ground and sat beside the Arab, as though they were two men resting after market, not two strangers measuring one another’s lives. The Arab bowed his head.

“Aleikum es salam,” he answered, and waited.

“It is Abou Fatma?” asked the negro.

The Arab nodded.

“Two days ago,” the other went on, “a man of the Bisharin, Moussa Fedil, stopped me in the market-place of Berber, and seeing that I was hungry, gave me food. And when I had eaten he charged me to drive this donkey to Abou Fatma at the wells of Obak.”

Abou Fatma turned his eyes to the animal as though only now he had noticed it. He let the pause lengthen, the way one lets a pot come to the boil without peering.

“Tayeeb,” he said, carelessly. “The donkey is mine.”

He sat as if the matter were finished and the negro might rise and go. But the negro did not move.

“I am to meet Moussa Fedil again on the third morning from now, in the market-place of Berber,” he said. “Give me a token which I may carry back, so that he may know I have fulfilled the charge and reward me.”

Abou Fatma drew the knife from the small of his back, picked up a stick, and notched it—three cuts at each end—precise, even, the sort of mark that could not be mistaken for accident.

“This shall be a sign to Moussa Fedil,” he said, and handed it over.

The negro tied the stick securely into a corner of his wrap, then unlashed his water-skin from the donkey, filled it at the well, and slung it across his shoulders. He took up his spears and shield again. Abou Fatma watched him labour up the loose sand, step by step, until he reached the crest and slid out of sight down the far incline. The only music was the hush of grains falling and the faint clink of iron on wood—sounds so small they seemed to belong to a remembered world rather than this place.

Then Abou Fatma rose—and now without leisure. He drove the donkey among the trees, fastened it to a stem, and examined its shoulders. In the left shoulder a tiny incision had been made and neatly stitched with fine thread. He cut the stitches, pressed apart the edges of the wound, and forced out a small package no bigger than a postage stamp. It was a goat’s bladder, dried and tough and cleanly tied, as familiar to him as any kitchen skin used to keep a precious taste from spoiling in the heat.

Within the bladder was a note written in Arabic and folded very small.

Abou Fatma had not been Gordon’s body-servant for nothing. In those years he had learned letters as other men learn a trade: slowly, by use, and with a reverence for what can be carried when all else is stripped away. He unfolded the paper and read:

“The houses which were once Berber are destroyed, and a new town of wide streets is building. There is no longer any sign by which I may know the ruins of Yusef’s house from the ruins of a hundred houses; nor does Yusef any longer sell rock-salt in the bazaar. Yet wait for me another week.”

The hand that had written it was the hand of Harry Feversham.

He wore the patched jubbeh of the Dervishes over his stained skin; his hair was frizzed on the crown and fell on the nape in locks matted and gummed into the likeness of seaweed. He moved through New Berber—wide streets, gaping pits—searching for a name that had slipped its moorings. South of it, a mile or so across the sand, lay the old town where Abou Fatma had slept one night and hidden the letters: a warren of ruined houses facing narrow alleys and winding streets. Front walls had been pulled down, roofs carried away; only bare inner walls remained, so that by night Feversham wandering among them felt he had entered long lanes of open courts, crumbling into decay. Each court differed from its neighbour only by a shade of ruin. Foxes had already begun to burrow beneath the fallen edges, scratching their small histories into the dust.

He had calculated that one night would be the term of his stay in Berber. He was to slip through the gate at dusk, and before the grey light quenched the stars his face would be set toward Obak. Now he must go steadily forward among crowds like a man with business of moment—dreading conversation lest his tongue betray him, listening always for the name of Yusef to strike his ears, as one listens for the first syllable of a forgotten dialect that might suddenly restore a whole childhood.

At times despair walked beside him; fear never left. Out of the sharp pangs of those emotions a kind of madness grew—a frenzy of obstinacy, a fanatical belief, as dark and absolute as the religion of the men among whom he moved, that he could not now fail and the world go on; that no injustice in the universe could be large enough to lay such a burden on the one man least fitted to carry it and then, when he struggled under it, crush him for the attempt.

Fear had him in its grip on the morning—three days after he had left Abou Fatma at the wells—when he came over a slope and first saw the sand stretched like a lagoon up to the dark brown walls of the town, and beyond, on the Nile bank, the heavy foliage of great date palms. The palms’ fronds shifted in the air with a dry, whispering applause; the river itself was hidden, but its presence seemed to cool the sound. Within those walls were the crowded Dervishes. It seemed the merest madness for a man to imagine he could escape detection there, even for an hour.

Was it right, he began to ask himself, that a man should even try?

The longer he stood, the more insistent that question grew. The low mud walls took on a sinister patience. The green of the palms—after so many arid days of sun and sand and stones—felt like an invitation offered with a smile that concealed a knife. His mouth was dry; his skin smarted under the sun as if salt had been rubbed into it. He began to wonder whether he had not already done enough for honour in venturing so near.

His strength ebbed as though his veins had been opened. If he were caught—surely he would be caught; oh, very surely—he saw in his mind the fanatical faces crowding about him. Mutilations? He could not drive the thought away. The place was lonely, so lonely that the silence itself seemed to watch him. His knees shook. He turned and ran—leaping in a panic, alone and unpursued, across the naked desert under the sun, and from his throat broke feeble cries without words.

He ran only a few yards. The violence of his flight stopped him, as a sudden bitterness can stop a man mid-bite. Was this all the four years of anticipation had been worth? He had schooled himself in the tongue, lived in bazaars, sweated in disguise—to no end? He was still the craven who had sent in his papers? The quiet confidence with which he had told Lieutenant Sutch his plan over the table in the Criterion grill-room—had it been nothing but the vanity of a man practised in deceiving himself?

And Ethne.

He dropped to the ground and drew his coat over his head, making himself a brown irregularity in the waste, indistinguishable from the sand. He shut the prospect from his eyes, and over thousands of miles of continent and sea he drew Ethne’s face toward him until it filled the darkness. For a little while he was back in Donegal. A summer night breathed through the open doorway in the hall; in a room near by people danced to music. He saw the three feathers flutter to the floor. He watched the trouble grow upon Ethne’s face, like a shadow spreading across a table where supper waits untouched.

If he could do this thing—and the harder thing which he now knew lay beyond—perhaps, some day, he might see that face cleared of its trouble. Words rang in his ears with the edge of prophecy: “I should have no doubt that you and I would see much of one another afterwards.”

Toward the setting of the sun he rose from the ground. He walked down toward Berber and, with his heart thudding as loudly in his own head as any drum, passed between the gates.
