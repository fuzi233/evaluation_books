Three weeks to the day had passed since Tito handed his bag of gold florins over to Cennini, and as he turned his steps toward the Via de’ Bardi on the feast of San Giovanni, no trace of care furrowed his brow. He moved in easy harmony with everything immediately around him, his nature too bright, too unapprehensive, to let distant, hidden troubles sink into him as a lingering dread. Stepping out of the blistering Tuscan sunshine into the cool shadow of a narrow side lane, he tugged off his simple black berretta, the cap with its upturned lappet perched lightly on his brown curls, tossed his head back to catch the moving air, and pushed his hair loose from his forehead. There was no brand of duplicity on his brow, no overt stamp of candor either: only a smooth, finely shaped, unlined young square of skin. The slow, absent glance he cast over the upper house windows held no more guile than it did forced ingenuousness—no more than belongs to the wide, unclouded gaze of a youthful, open eye, clear as polished glass, set round a rich dark brown iris, the sclera tinted soft cerulean, streaked with the faint shadows of long lashes. His whole bearing radiated one clear, unmistakeable truth: he was free of restless vanity, awkward obligation, or simmering unease, and so the holiday-makers that passed him turned to look and admire, their tribute entirely unforced and willing.

The festa’s stir had seeped even into the narrowest back lanes by that hour. The crowd that had been pressed tight along the procession route was now spilling out in every direction, their loose, unguided holiday energy fraying at the edges, half-ready to pick on a stray passerby for a bit of cheap amusement. Near the hostelry of the *Bertucce*—the Baboons—Tito found the throng thickening, a knot of laughing onlookers clustered around something holding their attention. He was about to slip down an adjoining side street when a thin, trembling childish cry cut through the din of laughter: “Loose me! Holy Virgin, help me!”

He pushed straight through the crowd at once. The distressed voice belonged to a young contadina, her white peasant hood fallen off in her struggle to yank her hands free of a man in the bright patchwork motley of a cerretano, a travelling conjuror. The man grinned and cajoled her, trying to soothe her while the spectators chided her for being stubborn, plying every last Florentine dialect term for simpleton they could recall. For a second her face was turned away, and Tito only saw her light brown hair braided tight and fastened with a long silver pin, but the next twist of the struggle brought her face full to his. It was Tessa, her baby features streaked with tears, her underlip quivering. She saw him too, and a sudden blazing hope burst through the mist of her tears, the same look a lost small child gets when it spots a familiar hand reaching for it through a crowd of strangers.

Tito shouldered his way through the circle of gawkers in two strides, grasped Tessa gently by the waist, and said, “Loose this child! What right have you to hold her against her will?”

The conjuror, whose sharp face had eyes, eyebrows, nostrils and jawline all tilted upward in a permanent impish slant, showed his small neat teeth in a grin, not unkind, as he let go her wrists. He shrugged and tilted his shoulders forward in a half-apologetic, half-protesting gesture. “I mean her no harm in the world, Messere, ask all these good folk! I only wanted her to help me show my tricks—her sweet little kitten face would have reassured everyone my game was honest, and I promised her a lapful of sugared confetti for her trouble. But what’s that, now? This fine signor has better confetti at hand, and she knows it.”

A ripple of laughter ran through the onlookers, prompted by the way Tessa clung tight to Tito’s arm as he drew her away from the conjuror. Tito foresaw the crowd would tease them both for ruining their little show, so he hurried her away down the side street to shake off the jests. The conjuror tossed her fallen white hood over her head, calling after her to bear him no malice, and come back when her signor didn’t need her any longer.

Tito pushed quickly toward the next cross street, a little vexed at the delay that was making him late for the Via de’ Bardi, already mentally planning to find a place to leave the little lost girl safe. The passersby in the next lane tossed them merry holiday jests at the unusual pair, and Tito paused to speak to her gently but hurriedly: “Now little one, where were you going? Did you come to the festa alone?”

“Ah no!” Tessa’s face crumpled again, fresh tears welling. “I got lost in the crowd at San Pulinari! My mother and my stepfather were right beside me, then someone pushed me along and I couldn’t stop, and they’re gone. He’ll beat me, he will, I don’t know where they are. Please don’t leave me!” A loud sob burst out before she could stop it.

Tito hurried her on toward the Badia, the old church not far off. The cloister entrance at the back was open, though the church itself was locked for the midday hour, and he paused there, asking her if she had any cousin or acquaintance in Florence she could go to, or if she was too shaken to walk alone after her fright. He told her he was in a great hurry to cross the Arno to Oltrarno, but he could walk her part of the way if she could tell him where she lived. Tessa sobbed that the conjuror was the devil, she was too frightened to walk alone, and she had no one in the city. She wailed that her stepfather was cruel, her mother favored her younger sister, no one was ever kind to her, and she was sure she’d be beaten half to death for straying.

It would have been brutal to leave her there, and Tito’s nature was all soft, gentle aversion to anything painful. He leaned down, brushed the tears off her round cheek with his thumb, and kissed it lightly. “My poor little Tessa, stop crying. Tell me where your home is, and I’ll walk you part of the way back.”

Tessa’s sobs faded almost at once. The cruel conjuror, the beating waiting for her, all seemed far away. “I live past Peretola, by the big pear tree, out the Por del Prato gate,” she whispered, looking up at him with wide blue eyes, her face soft and calm as a cherub’s. She held tight to his arm as they set off, and Tito decided to take the main road toward the Prato meadow, hoping they might run into her distracted mother in the holiday crowds. The streets were full of roistering apprentices jealous of his pretty little charge, bold-eyed women in their mandated yellow veils, beggars tipping their caps for alms with grins, and rough loiterers, and Tessa’s fear of being lost was far from unreasonable.

By the time they reached the Piazza d’Ognissanti they were both hot from their fast walk, and they sank down on a weathered stone bench built against the wall of an old palace to catch their breath. “Holy Virgin, I’m glad we got away from those people,” Tessa said. “But I wasn’t scared. I knew you’d keep me safe.” Tito laughed, asking her why she trusted him so fully, and she said he looked exactly like the saints in the frescoes of Paradise, who were all good and never hurt anyone.

Nearby a street stall was piled high with ripe apricots, honey cakes, and colored comfits, and Tito bought a whole armful, tipping them all into the fold of Tessa’s holiday apron. He told her they would walk on to the Prato, and then she could surely go the rest of the way home alone with no danger. Tessa prattled on happily the whole time, telling him about the two cows and the mules her family kept, her little black-faced pet kid goat she loved more than anything, and how only the parish priest spoke softly to her when she went to confession. Tito smiled to himself, thinking this sweet little idyl was lovelier than the pastoral poems Lorenzo de Medici’s circle gushed over, and he was already so late to the Via de’ Bardi that another half hour of delay would hardly matter.

The wide open Prato meadow inside the city walls was empty and quiet at that midday hour, the Florentine youths who usually played their rough calcio football there all gone inside for their festa dinners. Tito paused there, telling Tessa he must turn back now, and she could walk the rest of the way home alone, promising he would meet her at the market the next morning to buy a cup of milk from her and make sure she was safe. But Tessa’s face fell, her mouth turning down in disappointment, and she said she’d thought they could sit under a tree just outside the gate and eat all the treats he’d bought her together.

Tito laughed, kissed both her cheeks, and told her she was a little siren tempting him to ruin. He turned and started walking away, but he looked back after two steps and saw her shoulders heaving with a held-back sob, apricots rolling right out of her loose apron onto the grass. He couldn’t leave her like that. He walked back, picked up the fallen fruit, and told her fine, they would find a tree to sit under for a little while.

They found a great gnarled old plane tree not far outside the Por del Prato gate, and settled down on the soft, cool grass under its shade. Sunlight filtered through the thick leaves in dancing dapples, Tessa leaning her back against the tree trunk, Tito stretching out on the grass beside her, their feast spread across her apron. She pulled a tiny, carefully sewn bag from down the front of her dress, the blessed Breve a wandering preaching father had given her that Easter, that kept the devil away. Tito teased her, asking if she wouldn’t like to leave her cruel home, if she would want to be his little servant where no one would scold her or beat her. Tessa’s face blazed with joy, saying she would, but only if she could come back for her little black-faced kid first.

Tito laughed, it had only been a playful suggestion, but he was tired after a long day of walking in the sun, and the soft shade was so sweet he didn’t want to drag himself all the way back across the Arno to Bardo’s stuffy, book-lined house. He asked if he could rest his head in her lap for a quick nap, and within minutes he was fast asleep. Tessa sat as still as a nesting dove, only daring to brush her fingers against his soft dark curls when he was fully unconscious. She was so happy she couldn’t think ahead to him waking up and leaving her, to the cold empty cottage waiting for her at the end of the road.

He woke at last, the sun slanting low across the meadow, his nap stretching far longer than he’d meant. He jolted upright, suddenly remembering how many hours late he was to his appointment at the Via de’ Bardi, and told Tessa firmly she must go home now, warning her not to cry or he would get cross and not love her anymore. Tessa’s rising tears vanished, her face pale, wide blue eyes trembling as she stared at him. He rummaged in his wallet, pulled out a tiny red coral charm carved into the shape of a pointed little horn, brought all the way from Sicily, and hung it on the cord around her neck right next to her little Breve, telling her it was a good luck charm that would chase away any evil spirits or bad luck when he wasn’t there to protect her.

Tessa leaned in and kissed his cheek softly, just as he told her to, and Tito laughed, pressing her round cheeks together and giving her a playful smooch across both. Then he hurried away, only waving once when he was ten yards off, never looking back. He did not want to see the quiet, trembling sob she let out once he was out of sight, for the softness of his nature hated to let any sadness cling close to him. He ate a quick, hasty meal at Ognissanti, crossed the Arno over the Ponte alla Carraja, and hurried straight for the Via de’ Bardi, already drafting the simple, unremarkable excuse he would give Bardo and Romola: that he had been unexpectedly detained on the festa, and their proud, delicate natures would never press him to ask for further details.
