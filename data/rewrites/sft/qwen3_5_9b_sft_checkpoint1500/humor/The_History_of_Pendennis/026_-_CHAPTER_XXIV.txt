A Little Innocent

There is a universal law of domestic architecture: every house possesses a hidden beam, a splinter, or a skeleton in the closet. For the fortunate, this discovery offers a comforting sort of vicarious misery; it allows them to sigh that their neighbors, despite their wealth, harbor their own private storms. Into this category fell the "Little Innocent" Muse of Blanche. To the untrained eye, she was a radiant sprite, singing sweetly and speaking softly, seemingly capable of turning dust into gold wherever she tread. Yet, to the inhabitants of Clavering House, she was the Nemesis, the shadow, the walking disaster. Consider how a single pebble in a boot can ruin a journey; so does a single irritant in a household destroy the peace of all within it. Who could have imagined that a smiling fairy like Blanche Amory was the architect of such discord?

One afternoon, following dinner, the Baronet and Captain Strong sat over the billiard table, cigars clutched between fingers like weapons of mass destruction. The Baronet, a man of blunt sentiments, broke the silence with a startling confession. "Strong," he said, puffing smoke, "I wish to the doose your wife was dead."

Captain Strong, a man of infinite patience, chuckled. "So do I. It's a mighty cannon, by Jove. But she won't die; she'll live forever—you see if she doesn't. Pray tell me, Frank, why do you wish her off the hooks?"

"Because then you might marry Missy!" the Baronet drawled, his tone dripping with malice. "She isn't bad-looking. She'll have ten thousand pounds, and that is a godsend for a poor old devil like you."

"And gad, Strong," the Baronet continued, his face contorting, "I hate her worse and worse every day. I can't stand her, I truly can't."

"I wouldn't take her at twice the price," Captain Strong laughed, wiping his cigar ash onto the floor. "I've never seen such a little devil."

"I should like to poison her," the Baronet declared solemnly. "By Jove, I should."

"Why, what mischief has she been up to now?" Strong inquired.

"Nothing particular," the Baronet replied, grimacing. "Only her old tricks. That girl has a knack of making everyone miserable; it's quite astonishing. Last night, she sent the governess sobbing away from the dinner table. Later, as I passed Frank's room, I heard the poor boy howling in the dark. His sister had terrified his soul out of his body by telling him tales of a ghost haunting the house. At lunch, she turned my lady inside out. Though my wife is something of a fool, she is a good soul—I'm hanged if she isn't."

"What exactly did Missy do to her?" Strong asked, amused.

"She began talking about the late Amory, my predecessor!" the Baronet grinned. "She produced some picture from *The Keepsake* and swore it looked like her dear father. She demanded to know where his grave was. Hang her father! Whenever Miss Amory mentions him, Lady Clavering bursts into tears. And the little devil does it on purpose, just to spite her mother."

He paused, recounting a further indignity. "Today, when she started again, I flew into a confounded rage. I told her I was her father, and then... and then, sir, she actually threw a shy at me!"

"And what did she say about you, Frank?" Strong queried, still chuckling.

"Gad!" the Baronet roared. "She said I wasn't her father, that I wasn't fit to comprehend her, that her father must have been a man of genius and fine feelings, whereas I had married her mother purely for money."

"Well, didn't you?" Strong pressed gently.

"It don't make it any the pleasanter to hear because it's true, you know," Sir Francis Clavering admitted, looking wounded. "I'm not a literary man, but I'm not such a fool as she paints me. I don't know how she does it, but she always manages to put me in the hole. She turns the whole house around her with her quiet ways and her confounded sentimental airs. I wish she was dead, Ned."

"It was my wife you wanted dead just now," Strong pointed out, maintaining perfect good humor.

Upon hearing this, the Baronet's candour returned instantly. "Well, when people bore my life out, I do wish they were dead. And I wish Missy were down a well, with all my heart."

From this candid exchange, it becomes evident that our accomplished little friend possessed peculiarities rendering her unpopular. She was a young lady of genuine genius, exquisite sympathies, and considerable literary attainments, living, like many another genius, with relatives who could not comprehend her. Her mother and stepfather were not men of letters; the Baronet read *Bell's Life* and the *Racing Calendar*, while Lady Clavering still wrote like a schoolgirl of thirteen, with an extraordinary disregard for grammar. Sensing her intellectual superiority, Miss Amory lost no opportunity to broadcast the inferiority of her circle. She was a martyr, yet she ensured everyone knew she was suffering. If she wept, as she believed she must, were we to wonder? Without sympathy, life is nothing. Would it not be a lack of candour to feign cheerfulness when one feels so deeply misunderstood? If a poetess may not bemoan her lot, of what earthly use is her lyre? Blanche struck hers only to the saddest tunes, singing elegies over dead hopes and dirges for nipped buds of affection.

Her actual distresses were not severe, but her griefs lay in her own soul. Like most, she was sad and habitually dissatisfied. *Mes larmes* trickled from her eyes at command; she possessed an unlimited supply of tears, a faculty honed by practice. Sentiment, like the disease Horace mentioned, increases through indulgence; the more one cries, the more one desires to do so.

Missy had begun to gush at a very early age. Lamartine was her favorite bard from the moment she could feel. She subsequently improved her mind by devouring the novels of Balzac and George Sand. By sixteen, she had read every romance of the French masters. Though she sympathized little with her kin, she claimed friends in the spirit-world: the tender Indiana, the passionate Lelia, the amiable Trenmor, the high-souled convict Stenio, and countless other heroes. She had fallen in love with Prince Rodolph and Prince Djalma while still at school, settling questions of divorce and the rights of woman with Indiana before leaving off pinafores. The impetuous little lady played at love with these imaginary worthies as she once played at maternity with her doll.

Novelists, supposedly knowing the secrets of female hearts, reveal that at eleven, Mademoiselle Betsi (as Miss Amory was then called) felt tender emotions for a Savoyard organ-grinder she believed to be a prince. At twelve, an old, hideous drawing-master agitated her heart. At thirteen, attending Madame de Caramel's boarding-school, she engaged in a correspondence by letter with two young gentlemen of the College of Charlemagne.

Note that Miss Amory, known as Missy at home, had originally been christened Betsy. She assumed the name Blanche of her own fantasy, crowning herself with it. The weapon her stepfather held over her was the threat to call her publicly "Betsy," a menace that occasionally kept the young rebel in order.

Regarding dolls, Miss Blanche displayed a similar girlish inconstancy. She had a host of "darlings" and a treasure-chest full of locks of hair gathered during her sentimental progress. Some friends married, others moved schools, and one beloved sister she had lost, only to find later working the books in a grocery shop in the Rue du Bac. She viewed these estrangements and disillusionments as the penalties of genius. Meanwhile, she managed to make her mother uncomfortable and caused her stepfather to wish she were dead. Only Captain Strong, with his invincible good humour, proved immune to her sarcasms. The little lady ruled the house with her tongue, correcting her mother's speech errors and terrifying the good soul into greater nervousness.

The arrival of the family at Clavering Park had inspired great curiosity in the town. While many watched, Madame Fribsby, the local milliner, remained unmoved until the Park ladies appeared in church. She noted every article of toilette, from bonnets to brodequins, taking a survey of the maids' attire as well. Despite the sermon of Dr. Portman, Madame Fribsby was undisturbed. Within days, she secured an interview with Lady Clavering's confidential attendant, presenting cards in French and English claiming to receive the latest fashions from Paris and offering court dresses to the nobility. These cards were favorably received.

Mrs. Bonner, the lady's maid, soon became a frequent visitor to Madame Fribsby's drawing-room, enjoying meals of green tea, scandal, hot Sally-Lunn cakes, and novels. However, she found less time for these pleasures than her junior officer, Miss Amory's maid, who was worked as hard as a factory girl by the inexorable little Muse, her mistress.
