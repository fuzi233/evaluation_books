Mrs. Lambert stood holding her lily-white handkerchief, a silent token of farewell to the travelers departing from Oakhurst. Alongside her daughters, she watched them pace leisurely down the first few hundred yards of the road, keeping her gaze fixed until a cluster of lime trees obscured them from view. Behind that verdant screen, the good matron had often watched those she loved most vanish—her husband heading toward battle and peril, her sons marching off to school, one after another. Each had disappeared behind those green trees, returning only when Heaven's will decreed, bringing with them renewed joy and affection to their happy little household.

Beyond mere instinct, which naturally aided such sentiments, the leisure and contemplation inherent in domestic life fostered the tenderness and fidelity of women. With the men gone, the entire day belonged to thoughts of them—the anticipation of tomorrow's letter, the endless "tomorrows" that promised correspondence. There was the empty room where the boy had slept the previous night, the faint impression of his carpet bag still lingering on the bedspread. In the hall hung his riding crop; in the corner lay his fishing rod and basket, mute memorials of fleeting pleasures. At dinner, the cherry tart appeared, half of which the darling had devoured at two o'clock despite his melancholy, sandwiched between his choking younger sisters. Evening prayers were recited without the young scholar's voice joining the responses. Then came midnight and silence, and the good mother lay awake, pondering how one of her cherished brood had flown from the nest.

As dawn broke, signaling the end of holidays and the beginning of toil, the rustling limes seemed to form a protective barrier between the outside world and the ladies of Oakhurst. Whenever Mrs. Lambert and her girls ventured near these trees in the absence of the men, the kind-hearted matron would fall silent and thoughtful. She confessed she longed to carve their names onto the grey, silvered trunks amidst the traditional "true-lover's knots," a custom popular at the time. Miss Theo, possessing an exceptionally elegant talent for verse, composed poems about the trees, which her delighted parent subsequently submitted to a contemporary periodical.

"Now we are out of sight of the ladies," Colonel Lambert remarked, tipping his hat in a final salute as the two gentlemen trotted past the lime grove. "I know my wife stands at her window watching until we round this corner. I hope we shall see the trees and the house again, Mr. Warrington. Perhaps, with the boys back home, there will be better sport for you."

"I never wish to be happier than I have been, sir," replied Mr. Warrington warmly. "And I hope you will allow me to say that I feel as though I am leaving behind quite old friends."

"The host at whose house we dine tonight has a son who is also an old friend of our family," the Colonel continued. "My wife, an inveterate matchmaker, would have arranged a union between him and one of my daughters, but alas, the Colonel has chosen to fall in love with someone else."

"Oh!" sighed Mr. Warrington. "Others have done the same. There were brave fellows before Agamemnon."

"I beg your pardon, sir. Did you say the gentleman's name was Aga...?" the young traveler inquired meekly, having missed the earlier syllable.

"No, his name is James Wolfe," the Colonel corrected, smiling. "He is still considered young, or what we call so, being scarcely thirty. He is the youngest lieutenant-colonel in the army, unless we count a score of nobles who hold rank above us commoners."

"Of course, of course!" exclaimed the Colonel's young companion, displaying genuine colonial notions of aristocratic precedence. "I have seen him commanding captains who were thirty years his senior, yet possessed neither his merit nor his fortune. Lucky as he is, no one envies his superiority; indeed, most acknowledge him as our superior. He is beloved by every man in our old regiment, knows them all intimately, and is a scholar as well as a consummate soldier, mastering many languages."

"Ah, sir!" breathed Harry Warrington, sighing with profound humility. "I feel I have neglected my youth terribly. I have returned to England but an ignoramus. If my dear brother were alive, he would represent our name and our colony far better than I can. George was a scholar, a musician, capable of conversing with the most learned minds in our country. I am certain he would have held his own here. Tell me, sir, am I glad to have come home, and to you especially, simply to realize how ignorant I am?"

"If you recognize your ignorance so clearly, 'tis a great gain already," the Colonel replied with a smile. "At home, especially recently since losing my brother, I used to consider myself a mighty fine fellow, undoubtedly flattering myself as the locals around here did. Now I am wiser—or I hope so, though perhaps I am merely bragging again. You see, sir, the gentry in our colony know very little, save for dogs, horses, betting, and games. I wish I knew more about books and less about them."

"Nay. Dogs and horses are excellent books in their own right; we can read much truth from them. Not everyone is made to be a scholar, yet some may be worthy citizens and gentlemen despite their lack of learning. Why should we all strive to be specially learned or wise, or claim a first place in the world? His Royal Highness commands, Martin Lambert is colonel, and Jack Hunt, riding behind yonder, was once a private soldier and is now a very honest, worthy groom. As long as we do our best in our respective stations, does it matter whether that station is high or low? How do we know what is truly high or low? Perhaps Jack's currycomb, my epaulets, or His Royal Highness's baton prove equally significant. When I began life—and served without shame—I dreamed of success and honor. Now, I think of duty, and of those folks we parted with just hours ago. Let us trot on, or we shall miss Westerham before nightfall."

At Westerham, the two friends were welcomed by their hosts: a stately matron, an elderly soldier whose memories and services dated back fifty-four years, and his son, the Lieutenant-Colonel of Kingsley's Regiment, currently stationed at Maidstone. The Colonel had visited briefly to see his parents before departing. Harry observed the young officer with curiosity; despite his youth, he had seen extensive service and earned a formidable reputation. Little beauty graced his face; he was lean and pale, with red hair and high cheekbones. Yet, he possessed a fine courtesy toward elders, a cordial greeting for friends, and an animated conversational style that made listeners forget, and even admire, his plain features.

"Is Mr. Warrington bound for Tunbridge?" the lady of the house asked. "Our James will escort him." She whispered something to Colonel Lambert during supper, eliciting smiles and knowing winks from the officer. He raised a toast to "Miss Lowther."

"With all my heart!" cried the enthusiastic Colonel James, draining his glass to the dregs.

Mamma whispered to her friend that James and the lady intended to make a match, noting that she hailed from the illustrious Lowther family of the North. "If she were the daughter of Charlemagne," Lambert shouted jovially, "she is not too good for James Wolfe, or for his mother's son."

"Mr. Lambert would not say so if he knew her," the young Colonel declared.

"Oh, of course! She is the priceless pearl, and you are nothing," Mamma retorted.

"No. I share Colonel Lambert's opinion. Even if she brought all of Cumberland as a jointure, I'd say it was my James's due. That is the way with them, Mr. Warrington. We nurse our children through fevers, measles, whooping cough, and smallpox; we send them to the army and sleepless nights worry us; we break our hearts parting, welcoming them home only for weeks or perhaps years at a stretch. And after all that care, along comes a girl with bright eyes, and away goes our boy, caring not a fig for us afterward."

"And pray, my dear, how did you come to marry James's father?" asked the elder Colonel Wolfe. "Why didn't you stay with your parents?"

"Because James's father was gouty and needed someone to care for him, I suppose—not because I liked him a bit," the lady answered easily. Thus, the evening passed in warm talk and kindness.

The next morning, laden with expressions of gratitude and friendship, Colonel Lambert handed the young Virginian over to Mr. Wolfe's charge and turned his horse homeward. Meanwhile, the two gentlemen spurred toward Tunbridge Wells. Wolfe was eager to arrive; Harry Warrington, however, was perhaps less so. Indeed, as Lambert rode back toward Oakhurst, Harry's thoughts trailed after him with deep longing for the parlor where he had spent three days in happy tranquility.

Wolfe echoed Harry's enthusiastic praise of the Lamberts—their name, their life, their wife, their daughters, and their entire excellent family. "To possess such a good name and lead such a life as Colonel Lambert's," Wolfe said, "seems to me now the pinnacle of human ambition."

"And glory and honor?" Warrington asked. "Are those nothing? Would you give up winning them?"
