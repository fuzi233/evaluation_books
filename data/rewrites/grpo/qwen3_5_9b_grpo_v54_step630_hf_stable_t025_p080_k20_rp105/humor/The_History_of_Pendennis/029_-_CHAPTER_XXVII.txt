Arthur Pendennis, hiding behind a curtain of cheap calico drapes, adopted the most forbidding expression he could muster. His mission was simple: to observe Miss Bell's debut as a quadrille partner to Mr. Pynsent. Mr. Pynsent, a man of such rigid English solemnity that he treated social dancing with the same gravity as attending morning prayer, moved through the floor with a stiffness that suggested he feared the music might distract him from his duty. Yet, Miss Laura Bell was a different story entirely. Bathed in the glow of chandeliers and the roar of the orchestra, her face radiated a joy that seemed to defy the room's decorum. With her white gown flaring and her brown curls tumbling over her shoulders, she was the center of attention. Even Lady Fogey, a woman who imported London fashion to the countryside with considerable flair, stopped to admire her, comparing her favorably to a reigning beauty of the capital and declaring her "quite doable."

Lady Rockminster, however, viewed the scene with the critical eye of a connoisseur. Through her spectacles, she surveyed Laura with majestic indifference, noting her artless charm but critiquing the slight redness of her arms as a youthful imperfection. She found Laura's tone superior to that of Miss Blanche Amory, who was currently dancing opposite her. Miss Blanche, a creature of vanity, was indeed Laura's *vis-à-vis*. She smiled with calculated lethargy, nodding and chatting whenever paths crossed, all while ensuring her own pale shoulders and tiny feet were the focal point of the room. Her demeanor screamed, *"Look at me, not at that healthy country bumpkin who needs teaching. This is the Parisian way."*

While Laura danced with an eye fixed on Pen lurking in the shadows, the young man himself was a study in awkward pomp. He remained hidden until the second quadrille, when Lady Clavering summoned him to the dais. There, he performed a haughty bow to Lady Rockminster, who barely acknowledged him, before turning his attention to the Widow Amory, resplendent in diamonds and velvet.

The waltz brought a new partner: Young Mr. Fogey, a boy of fifteen dreaming of a beard and a dragoon commission. He was utterly smitten by Laura, declaring her far superior to the "French girl" he had previously danced with. Laura laughed at his earnestness, her amusement catching Pen's eye through the crowd. Pen, meanwhile, looked absurdly sulky. When the waltz began, Fogey confessed his inability to manage the steps, vowing to hire a master. Then came Mr. Pynsent again, and Pen watched in a silent fury as Laura twirled around him. This sight triggered a memory of happier evenings at Clavering Park, where Laura had animated even the stoic Mr. Pynsent, while Miss Blanche struggled with the lumbering Captain Broadfoot, whose lack of musical sense made him a hazard to the dance floor.

Miss Blanche, seeing Laura's success, feigned fatigue to escape the dragoon and retreat to her mother's side. She demanded to know why Pen hadn't asked her to waltz, complaining about the "odious man in spurs." Pen, attempting a gallant defense of the dragoon's uniform, was dismissed by Blanche as "sulky and naughty." When Pen finally asked her to dance, she politely declined, citing her current engagement, though she promised the next waltz. Pen, delighted by the prospect of mischief, turned his attention to Blanche. He became a font of satire, mocking the heavy dragoons, the country dandies, and the ladies' elaborate hairstyles—one resembling a bird's nest, another a dessert of grapes and almonds. He kept Blanche in stitches, all while Laura watched from across the room, torn between amusement and confusion at her cousin's behavior.

The waltz music swelled, and Pen, paired with the lively Miss Blanche, executed the steps with brilliant flair. He glanced hopefully toward Laura, but she was fully occupied with the energetic Chevalier Strong. Their triumph was short-lived. As Pen and Blanche whirled with the speed of opera dancers, Captain Broadfoot and his partner, Miss Roundle—a large young woman in a strawberry-colored dress—moved with agonizing slowness. The collision was inevitable. The four dancers collided with such force that gravity abandoned them all. Captain Broadfoot and Miss Roundle were toppled, but the real disaster befell Pen, who crashed flat onto the floor, sprawling amidst the ruin.

The Captain, good-natured, laughed at his own misfortune, but the atmosphere shifted instantly. Miss Amory was savage about her fall, while Miss Roundle sat up, presenting a pitiful spectacle that drew giggles from the crowd. Pen, a young man who feared ridicule above all else, boiled with rage. He blamed the "woman in pink," apologized clumsily to Miss Amory, and stormed away in a fury. Miss Amory, feigning injury, refused further dancing and accepted a glass of water from a stranger—a man wearing a blue ribbon and a three-pointed star. She allowed him to escort her to her mother, her shoulder turned haughtily toward Pen.

The stranger was Mirobolant, the family cook, dressed in a black wig and military orders. Blanche, unaware of his identity, mistook him for a foreign nobleman. As she departed, Pen, realizing the absurdity, shouted, "By Jove, it's the cook!" The words hung in the air, heavy with unintended insult. Mirobolant, hearing the exclamation, cast a furious, murderous glance over his shoulder. Pen, oblivious to the depth of his faux pas, felt a surge of mischievous triumph.

Just as tension mounted, Chevalier Strong intervened. Speaking rapidly in Spanish, he convinced the humiliated cook that he would lose his position if he continued to dance with Blanche. The cook, crushed, bowed profoundly and retreated. Strong explained to a bewildered Blanche that the man was indeed the chef, a Gascon with a troubled mind who was madly infatuated with her "beautiful eyes."

The scene shifted to the refreshment room, a select space reserved for the elite. Here, Colonel Altamont, a servant of the Nawab of Lucknow, sat drinking champagne with alarming enthusiasm. He was a man of eccentric habits, jingling sovereigns and staring oddly at everyone who entered. Sir Francis Clavering and Mr. Pynsent encountered him, with the Colonel offering Pynsent a glass of "Sham," which the future politician accepted with grave dignity. The Colonel was baffled by such decorum from a man he assumed was a merchant captain.

When Strong and Blanche entered, the Colonel, now glowing with red-eyed intensity, approached Blanche. "And so you're Betsy Amory," he boomed. Blanche, distracted by the noise returning to the ballroom, was left wondering who had spoken to her. The room buzzed with the aftermath of the dance, a chaotic mix of fallen partners, misunderstood nobility, and the lingering sting of Pen's accidental insult to the cook.
