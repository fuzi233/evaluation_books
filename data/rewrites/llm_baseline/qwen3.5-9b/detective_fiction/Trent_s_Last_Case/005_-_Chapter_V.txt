There are moments in life when the subconscious, busy with its private affairs, leaks a hint of a coming fortune into conscious awareness. Who hasn't felt that inexplicable surge of persuasion that things are about to turn out well? Not the feverish desperation of a man facing disaster, nor the stubborn delusion of the optimist, but an unsolicited conviction rising like a bird from the heather—that success awaits in some grand endeavor. As Trent climbed the stairs outside the library, he felt this certainty settle over him. A swarm of guesses and inferences raced through his mind, unsorted and chaotic; a few obscure observations lingered, refusing to fit into any plausible theory of the crime. Yet, ascending the steps, he knew with absolute surety that clarity was imminent.

The bedrooms lined a broad, carpeted corridor illuminated by a tall window at the far end, stretching the length of the house before angling into a narrower passage housing the servants' quarters. Martin's room was the outlier, opening onto a small landing midway up the stairs. Passing it, Trent glanced inside: a modest, clean square room. Moving onward, he treaded with exaggerated caution, hugging the wall and placing each foot deliberately, yet the staircase groaned audibly under his weight.

He knew Manderson's room was the first on the right upon reaching the bedroom floor. He approached it immediately, testing the latch and lock, noting the mechanism worked smoothly, before pushing the door open.

Inside, the apartment was strikingly bare. The wealthy man's toiletries were minimal. Everything remained frozen in the state it held on the morning of the ghastly discovery in the grounds. The sheets and blankets of the unmade bed lay in a heap atop a narrow wooden frame, bathed in bright sunlight streaming through the window. That same light gleamed on the gold fittings of delicate dental work resting in water within a shallow glass bowl on a small, plain table beside the bed. A wrought-iron candlestick stood nearby, and clothes lay untidily draped over one of two rush-seated chairs. Various items cluttered the top of a chest of drawers, repurposed as a vanity, arranged in the disorder of a hurried departure. Trent scanned the room with a searching gaze, noting the occupant had neither washed nor shaved. He flipped the dental plate in the bowl with his finger, frowning anew at its baffling presence.

The emptiness and chaos of the sun-drenched room evoked a sense of horror in Trent. His imagination conjured a vision of a gaunt man dressing in silent vigil by the dawn's first light, casting frequent glances at the inner door behind which his wife slept, his eyes wide with terror.

Trent shivered. To ground himself in reality, he opened two tall wardrobes flanking the bed. They held clothing, a vast selection clearly serving as the sole comfort for the man who had slept there. Regarding footwear, Manderson had indulged his wealth extravagantly. An extraordinary array of shoes, meticulously stored, lined two long low shelves against the wall. No boots were present. Trent, an aficionado of fine leather himself, inspected the collection with appreciation. It was clear Manderson boasted a small, well-proportioned foot. The shoes shared a distinctive silhouette—narrow, round-toed, exquisitely crafted—all seemingly cut from the same last.

Suddenly, Trent's eyes narrowed upon a pair of patent-leather shoes on the upper shelf.

These were the shoes the inspector had previously described; the ones Manderson wore the night before his death. They were well-worn, yet freshly polished. Something about their uppers arrested Trent's attention. He leaned closer, frowning, comparing them to the neighboring pairs. Then, lifting them, he scrutinized the seam where the uppers met the soles.

As he handled the shoes, Trent began to whistle faintly and precisely, an air that Inspector Murch, had he been present, would instantly recognize. Most men possessing self-control harbor an involuntary tic revealing suppressed excitement. The inspector had noted that whenever Trent encountered a potent scent, he would whistle a specific, melodious phrase—the opening movement of Mendelssohn's *Lied ohne Worte* in A Major, though the inspector couldn't have named it.

Turning the shoes over, Trent measured them with a marked tape and examined the soles minutely. In the angle between heel and instep on each, he detected a faint trace of red gravel.

Placing the shoes on the floor, Trent walked with his hands behind his back to the window. Still whistling softly, he gazed outward, seeing nothing. Once, his lips parted to utter the Englishman's mechanical exclamation of sudden realization. Finally, he turned back to the shelves, examining every pair with swift, careful scrutiny.

Having finished, he retrieved the garments from the chair, inspected them, and returned them. He revisited the wardrobes, searching thoroughly. The clutter on the dressing-table demanded his attention a second time. Then, sitting on the empty chair, he clasped his head in his hands, staring at the carpet for several minutes. Rising finally, he opened the inner door leading to Mrs. Manderson's room.

At a glance, it was obvious the large chamber had been hastily stripped of its function as a lady's boudoir. Every item belonging to a woman's vanity had vanished; beds, chairs, and tables were devoid of garments, hats, bags, or boxes. No trace remained of the determined rebellion of gloves, veils, handkerchiefs, and ribbons breaking free from drawers. The room resembled an unoccupied guest suite. Yet, in every detail of furniture and decor, it spoke of unconventional but demanding taste. As Trent's expert eye assessed the harmonies of color and form amidst which the ill-fated lady had dreamed and brooded, he recognized her possession of an artistic soul. His interest in this unknown personality deepened; his brow furrowed as he considered the burdens weighing upon her and the crime whose narrative was rapidly gaining substance in his busy mind.

First, he approached the tall French windows centered on the wall facing the door. Opening one, he stepped onto a small balcony with an iron railing. Below lay a broad expanse of lawn starting directly beneath him, separated from the house wall only by a narrow flowerbed, stretching away with an abrupt dip toward the orchard. The other window opened above the garden entrance to the library. In the far interior corner stood a second door leading to the passage—the maid's entry and the mistress's exit in the mornings.

Seated on the bed, Trent quickly sketched a plan of the room and its neighbor in his notebook. The bed occupied the angle between the connecting door and the sash window, its head against the wall dividing the room from Manderson's. Staring at the pillows, he lay down deliberately, looking through the open door into the adjoining room. Taking this observation in, he rose and noted on his plan that small tables with covers stood on either side of the bed. The one furthest from the door held a graceful copper electric lamp standard, connected by a free wire to the wall. Trent studied it thoughtfully, then checked the switches controlling the other lights. As usual, they were located on the wall just inside the door, well out of reach while he sat on the bed. Rising, he verified all lights were functional. Turning on his heel, he walked quickly into Manderson's room and rang the bell.

"I need your help again, Martin," he said as the butler appeared in the doorway, upright and impassive. "I want you to persuade Mrs. Manderson's maid to grant me an interview."

"Certainly, sir," Martin replied.

"What sort of a woman is she? Is she sharp?"

"She's French, sir," Martin answered succinctly, pausing before adding, "She hasn't been with us long, sir, but I've formed the impression that the young woman knows as much of the world as is good for her—given that you're asking."

"You think butter might melt in her mouth, do you?" Trent asked. "Well, I'm not afraid. I intend to ask her some questions."

"I'll send her up immediately, sir." The butler withdrew, and Trent paced the small room with his hands behind his back. Sooner than anticipated, a small, neat figure in black appeared quietly before him.

The lady's maid, with her large brown eyes, had spotted Trent from a window as he crossed the lawn, hoping desperately that the solver of mysteries—whose reputation was equally formidable below-stairs as above—would summon her. She felt a desperate need to make a scene; her nerves were frayed. However, such displays were discounted by the other domestics, and Inspector Murch had chilled her into submission with his official demeanor. Seeing Trent, she sensed he lacked the air of a policeman; from a distance, he had appeared sympathetic.

Yet, as she entered the room, instinct warned her that any attempt at coquetry would be a fatal error. Instead, she approached with an air of amiable candor. "Monsieur desires to speak with me," she stated. Helpfully, she added, "I am called Célestine."

"Naturally," Trent replied with businesslike calm. "Now, Célestine, I wish you to tell me this: When you served tea to your mistress yesterday morning at seven o'clock, was the door between the two bedrooms—this door here—open?"
