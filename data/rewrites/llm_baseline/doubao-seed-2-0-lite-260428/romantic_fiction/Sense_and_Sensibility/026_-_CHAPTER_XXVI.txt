Elinor could scarcely believe she was settled in a carriage beside the boisterous, warm-hearted Mrs. Jennings, bound for London as her houseguest, when only days prior she’d reasoned their acquaintance was far too new, their ages and temperaments far too mismatched, to ever agree to such a trip. But all her careful, sensible objections had melted away before that bright, impetuous ardour Marianne and their mother shared for every new possibility, and Elinor could not even resent the choice, not when she watched her sister’s whole soul light up with giddy, consuming expectation. Beside Marianne’s burning, uncomplicated hope of meeting Willoughby in the city, her own quiet, uncertain longing for Edward felt faint and shadowed; she found herself wishing, for a foolish moment, that she could trade places, that she had that same vivid, animating possibility to look forward to.
Very soon now, she told herself, they would learn exactly what Willoughby’s intentions were. It was almost certain he was already in London, and Marianne’s fidgeting eagerness to leave Barton left no doubt she expected to find him there. Elinor resolved to watch carefully: to learn all she could of his character, to study every detail of his behaviour towards her sister, and work out the truth of what he wanted long before they had met many times. If he proved unworthy, she would make Marianne see sense, no matter how hard it was. If he was true and honourable, she would banish all selfish, envious comparisons, all quiet regret that her own happiness could not be so bright, and throw all her energy into celebrating her sister’s joy.
The three-day journey gave everyone a preview of how things would go in London. Marianne sat almost entirely silent, wrapped up in her own daydreams, only speaking to cry out to Elinor when a stretch of rolling, sun-dappled countryside caught her eye, far too preoccupied to make small talk with Mrs. Jennings. Elinor stepped easily into the breach: she laughed at the older woman’s endless anecdotes, asked after every one of her acquaintances, teased her gently, and listened to every rambling story with apparent delight. Mrs. Jennings, for her part, fussed over them endlessly, determined to make their trip as comfortable as possible, and only grew flustered at the inns when she could not bully them into admitting they preferred salmon over cod, or boiled fowl over veal cutlets.
They pulled up to Mrs. Jennings’ London house by three o’clock on the third day, stiff from carriage travel and giddy to sink into the warmth of a crackling drawing room fire. The house was elegant, richly furnished, and the young ladies were shown immediately to a cosy, sunlit guest apartment that had once belonged to Mrs. Jennings’ daughter Charlotte. Over the mantelpiece hung a lopsided landscape stitched entirely in coloured silk, a souvenir of the seven years Charlotte had spent at a prestigious London boarding school.
Dinner would not be served for two whole hours, so Elinor sat down immediately to pen a letter to their mother. Marianne sank into a chair beside her, reaching for paper and ink too.
“I’m writing home to Mama,” Elinor said gently. “Wouldn’t you rather wait a day or two to send your letter?”
“I’m not writing to Mama,” Marianne replied quickly, her cheeks flushing pink, as if she wanted no further questions.
Elinor asked no more. It struck her at once that the letter was for Willoughby, and the obvious, happy conclusion followed: no matter how secretive the pair were trying to be, they must be formally engaged. That quiet certainty lifted her spirits, and she went back to her own letter with a lighter heart.
Marianne’s note was finished in mere minutes. It was barely longer than a scrap of paper, folded, sealed, and addressed with shaking, eager haste. Elinor caught a glimpse of a large, looping W scrawled across the front before Marianne rang for the footman, begging him to run the note straight to the two-penny post. There was no doubt left now.
Marianne’s mood stayed high for the rest of the afternoon, but there was a sharp, fluttery edge to her excitement that stopped it from feeling like pure joy. She could barely force herself to eat a bite of dinner, and once they returned to the drawing room, she jumped at every distant rumble of a carriage, every faint footstep on the street outside. Elinor was grateful Mrs. Jennings had retreated to her own rooms to unpack, and could not see how wildly on edge her sister was.
By the time the tea tray was brought in, Marianne had already been disappointed half a dozen times by knocks on the neighbours’ front doors. Then a loud, sharp rap sounded on their own door. Elinor was sure it was Willoughby. Marianne leaped to her feet, flying towards the entryway. The next few seconds of silence were unbearable to her: she wrenched the door open, ran a few steps towards the stairs, and listened for a long, breathless moment, before turning back to the drawing room, her face glowing with rapture.
“Oh Elinor, it’s Willoughby! It really is!” she cried, half ready to throw herself into his arms—only to find Colonel Brandon standing in the doorway instead.
The shock was too great to bear calmly. Marianne did not say a single word, only turned on her heel and fled the room.
Elinor was disappointed too, but far more upset on the Colonel’s behalf. A man so openly, tenderly fond of her sister had just seen Marianne’s utter, unmistakeable grief at the sight of him, and he was staring after her with such naked astonishment and concern that he almost forgot to greet Elinor politely.
“Is your sister unwell?” he asked, his voice tight with worry.
Elinor stumbled through an explanation, blaming travel fatigue, a headache, low spirits after their long journey, anything that could excuse Marianne’s rude, heartbroken flight. He listened with grave, earnest attention, then pulled himself back to politeness, speaking of how delighted he was that they had come to London, asking gentle questions about their trip and all the people they had left behind at Barton.
The conversation dragged on quietly, both of them preoccupied with far weightier thoughts. Elinor ached to ask if Willoughby was in town, but she could not bear to hurt the Colonel by prying after his rival. At last, for lack of anything better to say, she asked if he had remained in London ever since their last meeting at Barton.
“Yes,” he said, shifting awkwardly in his chair. “Almost ever since. I have gone down to Delaford for a few days at a time, but I never found the chance to return to Barton.”
The odd, tense manner of his answer brought all the memories of his sudden, mysterious departure from Barton flooding back, along with all the rumours Mrs. Jennings had chattered about, and Elinor feared she had pried far deeper into his private business than she had any right to.
A moment later Mrs. Jennings bounced into the room, as loud and cheerful as ever. “Oh Colonel! I am monstrous glad to see you! So sorry I couldn’t greet you earlier, but I’ve been running myself ragged settling all my household accounts after being away so long—why, I’ve been busier than a bee since dinner! But how on earth did you guess I’d be back in London today?”
“I heard the news at the Palmers’ house, where I dined this afternoon,” he said.
“Oh, of course! And how are they all? How is Charlotte? I’ll warrant she’s getting rounder by the day with that baby of hers.”
“Mrs. Palmer is perfectly well, and she asked me to tell you that you will see her tomorrow without fail,” he replied.
They chattered on for a long while, Mrs. Jennings teasing the Colonel about his reserved ways, joking that it was a fine thing to be young and handsome, and that no one knew what would come of Marianne and Willoughby both being in the same city now. The Colonel grew quieter and more thoughtful once Marianne crept back into the room, and he did not stay long after that. No other visitors called that evening, and all three ladies agreed to head up to bed far earlier than usual.
Marianne woke the next morning with her spirits fully restored, the disappointment of the previous night seemingly wiped away by her bright, unshakable certainty that Willoughby would call that day. They had barely finished breakfast when Mrs. Palmer’s barouche pulled up to the door, and she ran laughing into the room, overjoyed to see all of them at once. She pretended to be furious that they had accepted her mother’s invitation instead of her own, even though she would have been deeply hurt if they had turned the chance down.
“Mr. Palmer was so delighted when he heard you were coming with Mama!” she babbled. “I can’t even remember what he said exactly, but it was the drollest thing you ever heard!”
After an hour or two of what Mrs. Jennings called “comfortable chat”—meaning endless questions after every acquaintance they shared, and gales of unprovoked laughter from Mrs. Palmer—she suggested they all accompany her to Bond Street to run errands. Elinor and Mrs. Jennings agreed at once, for they both had purchases to make, and even Marianne, who initially refused, was persuaded to come along.
Every step they took, Marianne’s eyes darted across the crowds, scanning every face for a glimpse of Willoughby. In every shop they stepped into, she was completely detached from the ribbons, fabrics, and bonnets the rest of the party admired, restless and impatient, refusing to give any opinion on any item Elinor asked her about, even those they both needed. She grew openly vexed when Mrs. Palmer dawdled endlessly, enchanted by every pretty, expensive new item she saw, too indecisive to buy any of them.
They did not get back to the house until late in the afternoon. No sooner had they stepped over the threshold than Marianne flew up the stairs, and when Elinor followed, she found her sister turning away from the entry table with a face crumpled in sorrow: no Willoughby had called while they were out.
“Has no letter been left for me since we went out?” Marianne asked the footman, as he came in carrying their parcels.
He assured her no post, no visitor, no porter had left a single note for any of them.
“How very strange,” Marianne whispered, turning to stare out the window, her voice thick with disappointment.
How strange indeed, Elinor thought, a cold unease settling in her chest. If Marianne had not known Willoughby was in London, she would have sent her letter to his estate at Combe Magna, not to a London address. If he *is* in the city, why has he not called? Why has he not written? Her heart ached, and she resolved that if these strange, upsetting circumstances dragged on for a few more days, she would write to her mother at once, and make her see how reckless they had been to let such a young girl conduct a secret, unconfirmed engagement with a man they barely knew, with no transparency, no proof that his intentions were honourable.
Mrs. Palmer and two of Mrs. Jennings’ oldest, closest friends joined them for dinner. Shortly after tea, Mrs. Palmer left to attend another evening engagement, and Elinor was roped into making up a fourth at their whist table. Marianne had never bothered to learn the game, so she was left to her own devices—but the evening brought her no joy. She would pick up a book for a minute or two, only to fling it aside and start pacing the drawing room back and forth, pausing every time she passed the window, holding her breath, listening for the knock that never came.
