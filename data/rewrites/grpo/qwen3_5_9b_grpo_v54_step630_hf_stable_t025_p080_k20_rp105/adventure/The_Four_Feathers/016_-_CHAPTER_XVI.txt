Ethne had completely erased Colonel Durrance from her mind. Since the moment Captain Willoughby placed the soiled, yellowed feather into her palm, her world had narrowed to a single focus: Harry Feversham. She had dragged Willoughby into the secluded enclosure, and his tale had consumed her, filling the gaps in his narrative with vivid recollections of Harry's gestures, voice, and gaze. For a fleeting holiday, she had been swept away from the August garden, forgetting the five weary years she had spent holding her head high against the world. Those years of silent endurance vanished, blotted out by the shock of seeing Durrance emerge from the hedge.

"Hush!" she whispered to Willoughby. Her face drained of color, her eyes squeezing shut in a spasm of agony. Yet, there was no time for grief. Their brief conversation was over; the holiday ended. She must immediately resume the heavy burden of forgetting. She knew she never truly could forget, so she resolved to do the next best thing: offer no sign that she hadn't. Durrance must continue to believe she loved him with a devotion deeper than friendship.

Durrance stood at the hedge's entrance, then stepped inside. He was too quick to suspect anything; he should not have met Willoughby or known of his arrival. Ethne scanned the area for an escape route, knowing it was futile. The creek lay ahead, and three high, thick hedges enclosed them behind and to the sides. There was only one exit, and Durrance blocked it.

"Stay still," she murmured. "Do you know him?"

"Of course. We served together at Suakin for three years. I heard he'd gone blind. I'm relieved to see he hasn't." He noted the ease of Durrance's stride.

"Speak lower," Ethne hissed. "It is true. He *is* blind."

"One would never guess. Consolation feels pointless. What can I say to him?"

"Say nothing!"

Durrance remained just inside the enclosure, seemingly staring directly at the two figures on the bench.

"Ethne," he said, using a tone so calm and certain it created the illusion of perfect sight.

"It's impossible he can see," Willoughby protested. "He's looking right at us."

"He sees nothing."

Durrance called out again, "Ethne!"—this time with a note of doubt in his voice.

"Do you hear? He's unsure," Ethne whispered urgently. "Stay perfectly still."

"Why?"

"He must not know you are here." She clamped her hand tightly around Willoughby's arm to prevent movement. Willoughby fell silent, compelled by her tense demeanor. She sat rigid, holding her breath, her face strained forward, her eyes wide with terror. Willoughby felt a rising excitement, not from his own fear, but from her palpable apprehension.

"He's coming toward us," he whispered.

"Not a word. Not a move."

"Ethne!" Durrance cried again, advancing further into the clearing. Ethne and Willoughby sat frozen, watching him. He walked past the bench and stopped, facing them directly. Willoughby thought surely he was being seen. Durrance stood relaxed, ready to speak. Even Ethne, certain of his blindness, began to doubt her own knowledge.

"Ethne!" he said once more in that quiet, authoritative voice. When no answer came, he shrugged and turned toward the creek. His back was to them now, yet Ethne, trained to read the subtle shifts in his bearing, knew he was listening intently. The way he held his head, the casual swing of his stick—all signaled he was straining to hear. Her grip on Willoughby's arm tightened. They held this pose for a minute before Durrance spun around and took a sudden step toward the seat. Ethne, anticipating his tricks, did not flinch. There was not even the rustle of her skirt. She held Willoughby fast.

"I wonder where in the world she can be," Durrance muttered aloud, then walked back out of the enclosure. Ethne did not release Willoughby's arm until Durrance was gone.

"That was close," Willoughby breathed when he could speak. "Suppose Durrance had sat right on top of us?"

"Why suppose, since he didn't?" Ethne asked calmly. "Did you tell me everything?"

"So far as I remember."

"And all of this happened in the spring?"

"The spring of last year," Willoughby confirmed.

"Yes. I have a question. Why didn't you bring this feather to me last summer?"

"Last year my leave was short. I spent it hunting ibex in the hills north of Suakin."

"I see," Ethne said quietly. "I hope you had good sport."

"It wasn't bad."

Last summer, Ethne had been free. If Willoughby had returned with news of Harry instead of hunting ibex on Jebel Araft, her life would have changed entirely. The cry "Why didn't you come?" echoed loudly in her heart, but outwardly she showed no sign of the damage Willoughby's delay had caused. Possessing the self-command of a woman hardened by trial, she spoke with such unconcern that Willoughby assumed her questions were born of mere curiosity.

"You might have written," she suggested.

"Feversham didn't think there was any hurry. Explaining in a letter would have been difficult. He asked me to come to you when I had the chance, and I had none until now. To be honest, I thought it likely he might have returned before me."

"Oh, no," Ethne replied. "There could be no possibility of that. The other two feathers still remain to be redeemed before he will ask me to take back mine."

Willoughby shook his head. "Feversham can never persuade Castleton and Trench to cancel their accusations as he persuaded me."

"Why not?"

"Major Castleton was killed when the square broke at Tamai."

"Killed?" Ethne cried, laughing briefly and satisfactorily. Willoughby stared at her, disbelief warring with the evidence of her ears. Her face, however, revealed she was thoroughly pleased. Ethne was a Celt; she felt death was not a significant matter. She could hate, and she could be as hard as iron to those she despised. These three men she hated with every fiber of her being. True, she had given the fourth feather to Harry merely to show agreement, but she cared little for that formality. She was delighted to hear Major Castleton was dead and done with.

"And Colonel Trench?" she asked.

"No," Willoughby answered. "You're disappointed? He is worse off. He was captured during a reconnaissance. He is now a prisoner in Omdurman."

"Ah!" Ethne said.

"I don't think you realize," Willoughby said sternly, "what captivity in Omdurman implies. If you did, even if you disliked the captive, you would feel pity."

"Not I," Ethne said stubbornly.

"I will tell you what it implies."

"No. I don't wish to hear about Colonel Trench. Besides, you must go. Before you leave, tell me one thing: what became of Mr. Feversham after he gave you that feather?"

"I told him he had done everything reasonably expected, and he accepted my advice. He boarded the first steamer to Suez and left the Sudan."

"I must find out where he is. He must return. Did he need money?"

"No. He still draws his allowance from his father. He said he had more than enough."

"I am glad of that," Ethne said. She dismissed Willoughby to wait in the enclosure while she went out alone to ensure the path was clear. The garden was empty. Durrance had vanished, and the great stone terrace and the house, with its striped sunblinds, looked like a sleeping entity. It was nearly one o'clock; the birds were silent. The profound quiet of the garden struck Ethne as strange. Only bees hummed drowsily among the flowerbeds, and a boy's voice called from the meadow slopes across the creek. She returned to Willoughby.

"You can go now," she said. "I cannot pretend friendship for you, Captain Willoughby, but it was kind of you to find me and tell your story. You are returning to Kingsbridge immediately? I hope so. I do not want Colonel Durrance to know of your visit or what you told me."

"Durrance was Feversham's great friend," Willoughby objected.

"He is unaware any feathers were sent to Mr. Feversham, so there is no need to inform him one has been returned," Ethne answered. "He doesn't know why our engagement was broken. I do not wish him to know. Your story would enlighten him, and he must remain ignorant."

"Why?" Willoughby asked. Stubborn by nature, he demanded the reason before promising silence. Ethne gave it simply.

"I am engaged to Colonel Durrance." She feared he already suspected her feelings were limited to friendship. If he learned that the fault breaking her engagement to Harry had been bravely atoned for, he would insist on stripping her of the one thing left to her. She was bound to him by honor, and she was determined he would not win her by sacrificing her own happiness because Harry had redeemed his.

Captain Willoughby pursed his lips and whistled. "Engaged to Jack Durrance!" he exclaimed. "Then I seem to have wasted my time bringing you that feather," pointing to it. She held it in her open hand and drew it back sharply, as if fearing he would snatch it.

"I am most grateful for it," she returned.

"It's a bit of a muddle, isn't it?" Willoughby remarked. "It seems rough on Feversham, and on Jack Durrance too." He looked at Ethne, noting her careful handling of the feather, the glow in her eyes as she listened, and the eager tone of her questions. "I shouldn't wonder if it's rough on you too, Miss Eustace."

Ethne said nothing. They walked out of the enclosure toward where Willoughby had moored his boat. She hurried him down the bank to the water's edge, intent on having him sail away unseen.

But Ethne had not counted on Mrs. Adair, who had observed much of Ethne's movements that morning. From the drawing-room window, she had watched Ethne meet Durrance at the terrace steps, seen them walk toward the estuary, noticed Willoughby's boat grounding in the gap between the trees, and seen a man disembark while Ethne went to meet him. Mrs. Adair was not a woman to abandon her post of observation. From behind the curtains, she watched with the curiosity of a villager lowering blinds to get a better view of a stranger passing down the street. Ethne and the man turned away, disappearing among the trees, leaving Durrance forgotten and alone. Mrs. Adair immediately thought of the enclosure at the water's edge. The conversation had lasted some time; since the couple did not reappear quickly, a question flashed into her mind: "Could the stranger be Harry Feversham?" Ethne had no friends in this part of the world. The question pressed on her, and she longed for an answer, specifically one that would convict Ethne Eustace of duplicity. Her interest turned to excitement when she saw Durrance, tired of waiting, follow Ethne's steps. But what followed interested her even more.

Durrance reappeared, surprisingly alone, and walked straight to the house, up the terrace, and into the drawing-room.

"Have you seen Ethne?" he asked.

"Is she not in the little garden by the water?" Mrs. Adair asked.

"No. I went in and called to her. It was empty."

"Indeed?" Mrs. Adair said. "Then I don't know where she is. Are you going?"

"Yes, home."

Mrs. Adair made no effort to detain him then.

"Perhaps you will come in and dine tonight. Eight o'clock."

"Thanks, very much. I shall be pleased," Durrance said, but he did not move immediately. He stood by the window, idly swinging the tassel of the blind.

"I didn't know until today that it was your plan for me to come home and for Ethne to stay with you until you decided if a cure was possible. It was very kind of you, Mrs. Adair, and I am grateful."

"It was a natural plan to propose as soon as I heard of your ill-luck."

"And when was that?" he asked casually. "The day after Calder's telegram reached her from Wadi Halfa, I suppose."

Mrs. Adair was not deceived by his careless attitude. She realized his expression of gratitude had deliberately led to this question.

"Oh, so you knew of that telegram," she said. "I thought you didn't." For Ethne had asked her not to mention it the very day Durrance returned to England.

"Of course I knew of it," he returned, and without waiting for an answer, he went out onto the terrace.

Mrs. Adair set aside the mystery of the telegram. She was occupied by her conjecture that in the little garden by the water, Durrance had stood and called aloud for Ethne, while within twelve yards, perhaps within reach, she and someone else had remained perfectly still and given no answer. Her conjecture was soon proven true. She saw Ethne and her companion emerge onto the open lawn. Was it Feversham? She needed an answer. She saw them descend the bank toward the boat and, stepping from her window, she ran.

"I noticed you cross the lawn from the drawing-room window," she said.
