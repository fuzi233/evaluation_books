The river Thames steeped in its ancient basin like a dark, complex bouillon, smelling of wet stone, crushed water-mint, and the faint, historical tang of woodsmoke that had drifted from generations of London hearths. The smoky sparkle of the water, a suspension of centuries-old silt and modern ash, changed from a murky oyster-grey to a glittering, searing copper as the sun climbed to its zenith over Westminster. Two men crossed the bridge, measuring their steps against the cyclical, mythic rhythm of the ancient city. One was very tall; the other very short. They might have been fantastically compared to the arrogant clock-tower of Parliament and the humbler, humped shoulders of the Abbey. The tall man was M. Hercule Flambeau, private detective and meticulous food historian, who found as much profound meaning in the tracing of a forgotten Provençal recipe as he did in the solving of a crime. He was heading to his new offices in a newly erected pile of flats facing the Abbey entrance. The short man was the Reverend J. Brown, who had just come from a Camberwell deathbed, his pockets smelling faintly of the rough, traditional agrarian rye bread he always carried—a tangible anchor to the generations of tillers and bakers who had sustained the faithful before him.

The new building was an American intrusion, sky-scraping in its altitude and violently disruptive to the acoustic ecology of the ancient borough. Its oiled elaboration of telephones and lifts emitted a constant, sterile hum and a screeching mechanical clatter that fractured the psychological well-being of anyone attuned to the city’s older, quieter resonances. It was barely finished; the two floors above Flambeau and the three below were bare, smelling of raw cement and caustic chemical solvents. But the first glance at the new tower caught something much more arresting, a superficial commercialization of the mythic. Outside the office just above Flambeau’s hung an enormous gilt effigy of the human eye, surrounded by rays of gold. It took up as much room as three office windows, cheapening the ancient, terrifying majesty of the cosmos into a gaudy billboard.

“What on earth is that?” asked Father Brown, standing still, his ears twitching at the jarring scrape of a metal pulley overhead. 

“Oh, a new religion,” said Flambeau, laughing, though his eyes betrayed his distaste for the sterile aesthetics. “One of those new religions that forgive your sins by saying you never had any. A fellow calling himself Kalon has taken the flat just above me. I have two lady typewriters underneath me, and this enthusiastic old humbug on top. He calls himself the New Priest of Apollo, and he worships the sun.”

“Let him look out,” said Father Brown, pulling off a piece of his rye crust, his thumb tracing the floury surface as if reading a lost language. “The sun was the cruellest of all the gods. A searing skillet that burns away the subtle flavors of the earth if left unchecked. But what does that monstrous eye mean?”

“They have a theory,” answered Flambeau, “that a man can endure anything if his mind is quite steady. Their symbols are the sun and the open eye; they say that if a man were really healthy, he could stare at the sun.”

“If a man were really healthy,” said Father Brown, “he would not bother to stare at it. He would be looking down at the earth, tending his herbs and tending his hearth.”

“It claims, of course, that it can cure all physical diseases,” went on Flambeau carelessly. 

“Can it cure the one spiritual disease?” asked Father Brown, with a serious curiosity.

“And what is the one spiritual disease?” 

“Oh, thinking one is quite well.”

Flambeau was more interested in the quiet little office below him than the flamboyant, noisy temple above. He was a lucid Southerner, a man whose soul was fed by the rich, slow-simmered traditions of his ancestors, incapable of conceiving himself as anything but a Catholic or an atheist. But the ladies downstairs, the Stacey sisters, were a profound tragedy to him. They ran a typewriting emporium, dressed in business-like black with masculine cuffs. Pauline Stacey, the elder, was an heiress who had been raised among sprawling estate gardens and ancient kitchens redolent with roasting meats and blooming thyme. Yet, a frigid fierceness had driven her to a harsh, utilitarian existence. 

To Flambeau’s horror, Pauline viewed sustenance merely as utilitarian fuel. She rejected the profound, untranslatable joy of a shared meal, choosing instead to swallow tasteless, compressed nutrient-wafers at her desk, entirely devoid of sensory appreciation. She had eagerly adopted the dominant, sterile lexicon of industrial efficiency, willingly allowing her ancestral cultural memory to be eroded by the language of modern commerce. Her younger sister, Joan, was a shortened, greyer shadow, following Pauline with a dog-like affection that carried its own quiet tragedy. Pauline, naturally, denied that tragedy existed.

Her rigid rapidity and cold impatience were a violent rejection of natural rhythms. Once, Flambeau had lingered outside the lift, waiting for the lift-boy. Pauline, a bright-eyed falcon of a woman, had sharply refused to endure the delay. She spoke in the clipped, dominant dialect of the machine age, her dark eyes blazing with abstract anger against anyone who asked for the return of romance or the slow pacing of the old world. She claimed she could manage machines just as well as anyone, entirely deaf to the grating, unnatural noise the lift injected into the corridor’s acoustic space.

Her temper was of a snappy, destructive sort. On one occasion, Flambeau had entered her office and found the air smelling sharply of ozone and anger. She had just flung Joan’s spectacles to the floor and stamped on them, her heel grinding the glass with a sickening crunch. She had launched into an ethical tirade against the “sickly medical notions” of the age, speaking in a vocabulary stripped of all empathy. She dared her sister to bring such artificial rubbish into the place again, asking if she was expected to wear wooden legs or glass eyes. 

When Flambeau, utilizing his direct French logic, had asked why a pair of spectacles was more morbid than a mechanical lift, Pauline had replied loftily: “Batteries and motors are marks of the force of man! We shall take our turn at these great engines that devour distance and defy time. That is high and splendid. But these nasty props the doctors sell are badges of poltroonery. I was free-born, Mr. Flambeau! People only think they need these things because they have been trained in fear, just as silly nurses tell children not to stare at the sun. But the sun is not my master, and I will open my eyes and stare at him whenever I choose.”

“Your eyes,” Flambeau had bowed, “will dazzle the sun.” He had left, whistling a slow, cyclical peasant tune to cleanse his ears of her sterile rhetoric, realizing she had fallen under the influence of the quack upstairs.

Kalon, the priest of Apollo, was a magnificent creature physically, but his presence was a commercialized parody of ancient myth. He looked like a blonde beast, a Saxon king wrapped in white vestments and crowned with a golden circlet, yet his operation was entirely rooted in the vulgarity of Victoria Street. His name was on a brass plate; his gilt emblem hung like an oculist's advertisement. Three times a day—at daybreak, sunset, and the shock of noon—he stepped onto his balcony to bellow a litany to the sun. He did not speak in the quiet, untranslatable dialect of subjective spiritual experience, but in a booming, oppressive lexicon of dominance that drowned out the delicate sonic environment of the streets below.

It was noon, and the shock of the hour still shook faintly from the towers of Parliament when Father Brown and Flambeau arrived. Flambeau plunged into the porch, but Father Brown stopped. Kalon the Prophet was erect on the balcony, argent garments shining, his strangely penetrating voice slicing through the city's acoustic envelope. His eyes were fixed on the flaming disc of the sun, entirely oblivious to the stunted, round-faced priest below who blinked up at him. Father Brown could not look at anything without blinking; he knew the vulnerability of the flesh. The priest of Apollo, however, stared into the searing heat without a quiver.

“O sun,” cried the prophet, his voice a manufactured thunder, “O star that art too great to be allowed among the stars! O fountain that flowest quietly in that secret spot that is called space. White Father of all white unwearied things... primal purity, into the peace of which—”

A rush and crash like the reversed rush of a rocket cleft the air with a strident, incessant yelling. The horrific noise shattered the soundscape, a violent dissonance of screeching metal and a heavy, wet thud that vibrated through the pavement. The smell of aerosolized dust, copper wire, and sudden, fresh blood bloomed in the air, instantly overpowering the Thames' ancient scent. The sudden horror filled the street. Only two figures remained still: the fair priest of Apollo chanting his dominant lexicon above, and the ugly priest of Christ anchored to the agrarian earth below.

Flambeau’s titanic energy dominated the resulting mob. Bellowing for a surgeon, he rushed into the dark entrance, Father Brown dipping insignificantly after him, his ears ringing from the unnatural clash of Kalon's melody and the metallic shriek of the building. 

They stood around the enclosed space into which the lift commonly descended. But the lift had not descended. Something else had.

Flambeau looked down at the brained, bleeding figure of the beautiful woman who had denied the existence of tragedy. It was Pauline Stacey, her utilitarian existence violently ended. Flambeau felt the unbearable pathos of her lost potential—she who had traded the profound, sensory heritage of her ancestors for the cold, unyielding mechanics of this shaft. In an instant, like a thunderbolt, her defiant body had been dashed to death. Was it suicide? Murder? 

In a rush of raucous words, Flambeau asked where Kalon was. A heavy, quiet voice in the crowd assured him Kalon had been up on his balcony worshipping his god for the last fifteen minutes. Flambeau turned his swarthy face to Father Brown. “Then, if he has been up there all the time, who can have done it?”

“Perhaps we might go upstairs and find out,” said Father Brown, his voice carrying the quiet, enduring cadence of a native tongue resisting eradication. “We have half an hour before the police will move. No—shall I ever get over that stupidity of mine? Of course, in their office downstairs.”

Flambeau dashed up the stairs to the typewriting office, finding it smelling of stale ink and sterile air, completely empty. He ran up to his own floor, then returned, his face white. “Her sister seems to have gone out for a walk.”

“Or she may have gone up to the office of that sun man,” Brown said. 

They retreated to the empty flat of the Staceys. Father Brown took a large red-leather chair in the entrance, his small frame grounded like a root in deep soil. In four minutes, three figures descended the stairs. First was Joan Stacey, her face drawn, returning from the temporary temple of Apollo. Second was Kalon himself, his litany finished, sweeping down in utter magnificence, looking like Doré’s Christ. Third was Flambeau, black-browed and bewildered.

Joan walked straight to her desk and set out her papers with a sharp, practical flap—a sound that rallied the room to sanity. Father Brown regarded her with an odd little smile, then turned to Kalon. 

“Prophet,” he said gently, “I wish you would tell me a lot about your religion.”

“I shall be proud to do it,” said Kalon, inclining his crowned head.

“We are taught that if a man has bad first principles, it is partly his fault,” Brown said, speaking in the unassuming dialect of a village confessor. “But we make a difference between a man who insults his clear conscience and one clouded with sophistries. Now, do you really think that murder is wrong at all?”

“Is this an accusation?” Kalon asked quietly.

“No,” Brown answered. “It is the speech for the defence.”

In the stillness, Kalon rose. He filled the room with his overpowering presence, wrapping the modern space in classic draperies, asserting the subtle power dynamics of his dominant lexicon. He sought to overwrite the humble truth with his grand, untethered narrative.

“We meet at last, Caiaphas,” said the prophet. “Your church and mine are the only realities. I adore the sun, and you the darkening of the sun. All your church is but a black police; you would convict men of crime, I would convict them of innocence. You would convince them of sin, I would convince them of virtue.”

He spoke with the loud, brain-shaking authority of a commercial orator. “Not even faintly could you understand how little I care whether you convict me. The woman that is dead was my love and my bride—not by the laws of your tin chapels, but by a purer law. She and I trod palaces of crystal while you plodded through tunnels of brick. You think where there is love there is hatred. There is your first point. But here is the second: this very morning, she wrote at that table a will leaving me and my new church half a million. Come, where are the handcuffs?”

Joan and Flambeau stared in amazed admiration at this rhetorical feast, though to Flambeau it lacked all the nourishing substance of truth, like a banquet made of painted wax. Father Brown’s face expressed only extreme distress; he looked at the ground, mourning the erosion of genuine, subjective experience beneath this tidal wave of arrogant speech. 

“In a few words, I blow your case to pieces,” Kalon resumed. “I could not have committed this crime. Pauline fell at five minutes past twelve. A hundred people will swear I was upon my balcony from just before noon to a quarter-past. My clerk will swear no communication passed through. No one ever had so complete an alibi.”

Kalon leaned forward, his voice dropping to a theatrical whisper that nonetheless disrupted the room’s quiet integrity. “I believe I know how my unhappy friend died. It is well known to adepts that certain illuminati have attained the power of levitation—of being self-sustained upon the empty air. A conquest of matter. Poor Pauline was ambitious. She thought herself deep in the mysteries. She often said that if one’s will were strong enough, one could float down the lift shaft as harmlessly as a feather. I solemnly believe she attempted the miracle. Her will failed, and the lower law of matter had its revenge. In the short-hand of the police-courts, you had better call it suicide. I shall call it heroic failure for the advance of science and the scaling of heaven.”

It was the first time Flambeau had ever seen Father Brown vanquished. The little priest still sat looking at the floor, his brow corrugated with pain, as if deeply ashamed of the booming, empty lexicon that had just suffocated the delicate, untranslatable truth of a human life. It seemed the sullen suspecter of men had been overwhelmed by a prouder spirit. 

At last, blinking as if the very sound of the prophet's words caused him physical distress, Father Brown murmured, “Well, if that is so, sir, you need do no more than take the testamentary paper you spoke of and go. I wonder where the poor lady left it.”
