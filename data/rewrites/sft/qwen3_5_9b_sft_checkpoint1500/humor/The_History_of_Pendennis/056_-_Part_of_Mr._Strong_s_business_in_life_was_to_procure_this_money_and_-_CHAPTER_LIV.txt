The duty of a chronicler, even when recording a shame that would stain the cover of any novel, is to speak the truth. Here lies a fact concerning Pendennis: after recovering from a severe fever, which had subjected him to bleeding, blistering, shaving, and a regimen of medicinal horrors, his "mental malady" vanished as completely as the physical one. The passion he had held for Fanny Bolton evaporated. He was no longer in love with her, not with the fervor of a lover, but with the detached wisdom of a man who knows better than to chase porters' daughters.

Lying on his pillow, Pendennis chuckled at this second cure. He felt no regret for Fanny; indeed, he marveled at his own stupidity in caring for her so intensely just months prior. He performed a mental autopsy on his dead feelings, dissecting the anatomy of his former obsession for the benefit of his nurse. Was it her wit? Her breeding? Her beauty? No; there were countless women superior in every regard. The fire had simply burned out within him, leaving the girl unchanged while the observer was altered. The fever, quelled by pills and lancets, had seemingly cured his infatuation. It was a source of immense relief to Pendennis to realize he could now resist temptation without guilt, viewing the "Fanny Bolton snare" as a trap from which he had miraculously escaped. Yet, beneath the surface, a humbling satisfaction lingered—an uncomfortable admission that the world's greatest passions are often fleeting fancies.

While Pendennis oscillated between amusement and shame, a different atmosphere prevailed at his bedside. His mother, the Countess, offered a warmth that filled him with profound security. Her unwavering attention and gentle smiles were the only things she required; executing his slightest whim was her supreme joy. Surrounded by her love, the young man felt a gratitude akin to that of a child in infancy. However, shadows clouded this paradise. Though Pen suspected vaguely that Fanny Bolton might have nursed him during his delirium, these memories were so foggy he dismissed them as fever dreams. Consequently, he remained silent, refusing to confide in his mother about Fanny, a precaution that ultimately proved disastrous.

Misinterpretation flourished where communication failed. Seeing Miss Bolton as nurse, Mrs. Pendennis constructed a narrative of scandal in her mind, assuming the worst of the accusations against her son. A man's wife is often the first to suspect treachery, and the Countess, steeped in protective jealousy, accepted the possibility of sin without inquiry. Meanwhile, the innocent nurse, believing her charge was suffering from a spiritual as well as physical plague, masked her internal terror with a cheery exterior, bearing the weight of false suspicion alone.

Across the Channel, at Boulogne, Captain Shandon read the *Pall Mall Gazette* with a grim satisfaction. He noted the absence of Jack Finucane's handwriting and the return of Mr. Warrington. To Shandon, journalism was a battlefield; Finucane fought like a butcher, mangling subjects, whereas Warrington laid his cuts neatly, straight down the spine of the page, drawing blood with surgical precision. "My dear," Shandon remarked, describing the "crack of the whip," "it is savage to the men." Mrs. Shandon, a gentle soul, protested, defending Mr. Warrington's kindness to the children, but Charles merely smiled, noting that savagery was reserved for the professional arena, not the domestic sphere. Where the wines were cheap and the brandy flowed freely, the Captain dreamed of returning to harness, muttering Latin about crossing terrible seas tomorrow.

Thus, Warrington stepped into the void left by his prostrate friend, tackling Pen's journalistic duties with ferocious energy. He wrote literary critiques and theatrical reviews with his characteristic intensity. When he visited the sick man's family, he lavishly praised Arthur's style, claiming it was the most graceful and elegant in the land. He spoke of Arthur's loyalty and gentleness, dismissing minor flaws like "wilfulness" and "dandification." Helen and Laura, though deeply grieved by Pen's condition, found solace in Warrington's high regard for their brother. Even Major Pendennis, usually taciturn, erupted in praise of the younger man, extolling his lineage from the Suffolk Warringtons and his connection to the Prince of Wales.

As the Major spoke, Laura blushed, her mind racing with speculations about Warrington's own romantic entanglements. Soon, the Major granted permission for Laura to accompany Warrington for walks in the Temple Gardens, reasoning that if the great arbiter of morals approved, no harm could come of it. Laura, grateful for the fresh air and the chance to escape her solitude, accepted. A tentative understanding existed between the two women, forged in the fires of Pen's near-death experience; Laura had confessed to Helen the depth of her love for Arthur, a secret that softened the edges of their collective grief.

Yet, fate was about to judge another soul. The question of what Fanny Bolton expected stood stark and terrifying. Society, that brutal jury, rarely offers leniency. Women are fierce guardians of morality, ready to tear apart any female who strays from the path. The narrator, adopting the persona of a Turk, warned the "spotless" accusers of their power to condemn: be cautious before ordering the execution, lest you strike the wrong soul. If Fanny were guilty, justice must be swift; if innocent, mercy was due. But the judgment hung in the balance, waiting for evidence that might never arrive.

Back in Lamb Court, the atmosphere shifted from tragedy to a peculiar, vibrant convalescence. The Major, ever the benevolent host, encouraged the budding friendship between Warrington and Laura. He arranged outings to exhibitions and concerts, insisting that their honor was "entirely spotless." To the Major, Laura represented purity and natural charm, a stark contrast to Pendennis, whom he viewed as a spoiled dandy, weary of the servitude that had raised him. While Pen, languid and indifferent, treated the attentions of women with bored resignation, Laura, driven by a natural instinct to love, found herself captivated by Warrington. She saw in him a vitality and genuine kindness that Pen lacked, a man unburdened by the vanity that defined his cousin.

Why did Laura love him? The narrator posits a universal truth: young ladies fall in love not with abstract ideals, but with the person standing before them, constantly praised and observed. Having formed an image of Warrington in her heart, she tended to it with devotion. When she traveled back to London, her conversations with Helen were a litany of Arthur Pendennis's virtues—from his refusal to extract a tooth to his generosity toward beggars. These tales were less about the reality of the man and more about the projection of the ideal, a hero figure constructed by the adoration of those around him.

Life in Lamb Court became a theater of quiet joys. The Major, enchanted by London's autumn, turned his lodging into a sanctuary. He orchestrated elaborate meals, securing delicacies like jellies and aspics from a renowned chef, and even procuring rare Amontillado via the Marquis of Steyne, a gift from the King of Spain. The room transformed into a place of mirth. Warrington taught Laura to cry "Hear! Hear!" during dinner toasts, tapping the table with his knuckles, while the Major delivered speeches on the "present occasion." The servant, Pidgeon, grinned, and Dr. Goodenough joined in the levity.

The evenings were punctuated by music and games. Warrington, despite his lack of formal training, sat entranced by Laura's contralto voice as she played a simple piano and sang old songs. Below, a mysterious "pale little girl in a black bonnet"—presumably Fanny or a neighbor—stood at the lamp post, listening to the melodies drifting from the open windows. As twilight deepened, the Major and Warrington would play cards, while Laura worked wool for a pair of slippers, likely intended for Arthur, George, or the Major himself. The Major joked about the cost of the materials, his tone laced with affectionate teasing.

Before ten o'clock, the St. Clement's clock chimed, signaling the hour. Martha from Fairoaks appeared, lighting candles for departure, and the group bid farewell to the "sporting door" behind which the young mistress and her maid locked themselves away. Later, Warrington walked the Major home, shaking hands on the doorstep, discussing Laura's charming blushes and her modest fortune. As they parted, Warrington's face fell, the Major's joviality replaced by a somber reflection on his own unremarked life. He returned to his chambers, smoking pipes and writing savage articles, haunted by the distance between his rugged existence and the polished world he admired.

Pen improved rapidly, his appetite voracious, devouring roast chickens with the ferocity of an ogre. But amidst the recovery, a tender, confidential event unfolded. During his delirium, Dr. Goodenough had ordered ice applied to Pen's head and commanded the removal of all his hair. This catastrophe was revealed to the Major, who, with a blush and a wink, offered to send a coiffeur to restore the "ravaged beauty." Laughter ensued; Warrington and the Major jokingly proposed wigs, culminating in a gentleman from Burlington Arcade delivering a box containing Pen's own hair, which they paraded with flowers to create a ridiculous, festive crown.
