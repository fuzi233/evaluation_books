The dust of the interior plateau settled upon our boots, a fine, choking powder that spoke of a journey exceeding a thousand miles from the coastal heat of Durban. We had traversed this arduous distance to reach Sitanda's Kraal, situated near the confluence of the Lukanga and Kalukwe Rivers, arriving in the second week of May after departing in late January. Yet, I choose not to chronicle every incident of this trek in exhaustive detail. The trials we faced were numerous and varied, typical of the African hunter's lot, save for one singular exception which I reserve for later narrative necessity. To recount them fully here would only render this history wearisome.

Our departure from Inyati, the outlying trading station in the Matabele lands ruled by the great and cruel scoundrel Lobengula, marked a severance from comfort. With heavy hearts, we abandoned our reliable wagon. Of the beautiful span of twenty oxen I had purchased in Durban, only twelve remained. One succumbed to the venom of a cobra; three perished from the slow starvation of drought; another wandered lost; and three more died consuming the poisonous herb known locally as "tulip." Five additional beasts fell ill from the same toxin, though we managed to revive them with a potent infusion brewed from boiled tulip leaves—a timely antidote indeed.

Goza and Tom, our driver and leader, two trustworthy youths, were entrusted with the care of the wagon and the surviving livestock. We requested a local Scotch missionary to oversee them, ensuring they remained safe in our absence. Thus, stripped of our mechanical comforts, we embarked on foot. My companions included Umbopa, Khiva, Ventvögel, and half a dozen bearers hired on the spot. I recall a profound silence hanging over us at the moment of departure, a collective uncertainty regarding whether we would ever see our wagon again. Personally, I harbored no expectation that we would.

We tramped onward in this somber quietude until Umbopa, marching at the forefront, shattered the tension. He launched into a Zulu chant, singing of brave souls weary of life's tame constraints who ventured into the vast wilderness seeking either discovery or death. His song crescendoed to reveal that beyond the perceived danger lay not a wasteland, but a paradisiacal realm abundant with young wives, fat cattle, game to hunt, and enemies to slay.

Laughter erupted among us, breaking the spell. Umbopa possessed a cheerfulness that bordered on the savage, yet carried a dignified gravity absent when he suffered from his bouts of brooding. He held a remarkable gift for sustaining morale, and we all grew deeply fond of him.

It was now time to address the specific adventure that demands telling, for I possess a genuine affection for a hunting yarn. Approximately two weeks' march from Inyati, we encountered a landscape of striking beauty: a well-watered woodland where kloofs in the hills were draped in dense "idoro" bush, interspersed with the "wacht-een-beche," or "wait-a-little thorn." Great quantities of the lovely "machabell" tree dotted the terrain, laden with refreshing yellow fruit encased in enormous stones. This tree was the elephant's favored delicacy, and evidence of their passage was everywhere—from frequent spoor to trees snapped down or uprooted by the great brutes' destructive feeding habits.

One evening, following a long day's march, we arrived at a spot of exceptional loveliness. At the foot of a bush-clad hill lay a dry riverbed containing pools of crystal-clear water, their banks trodden smooth by the hooves of game. Opposite this hill stretched a park-like plain populated by clumps of flat-topped mimosa and glossy-leaved machabells, surrounded by a sea of pathless, silent bush.

Emerging onto the riverbed path, we startled a troop of tall giraffes. They did not run so much as sail away in their strange, undulating gait, tails screwed high over their backs, hooves rattling like castanets. Standing roughly three hundred yards away, they were effectively beyond range, yet Good, walking ahead with an express rifle loaded with solid ball, could not resist the temptation. He lifted his weapon and fired at the last animal, a young cow. By some extraordinary stroke of fortune, the ball struck it squarely on the back of the neck, shattering the spinal column. The giraffe collapsed instantly, rolling head over heels like a rabbit. I had never witnessed such a curious spectacle.

"Curse it!" Good exclaimed, his nautical upbringing evident in his profanity. "Curse it! I've killed him."

"_Ou_, Bougwan," the Kafirs ejaculated, repeating "_ou! ou!_" They dubbed Good "Bougwan," or Glass Eye, due to his spectacles.

"Oh, 'Bougwan!'" echoed Sir Henry and I. From that day forth, Good's reputation as a marvelous shot was cemented, at least among the Kafirs. In truth, he was a poor marksman, but whenever he missed, we overlooked the error for the sake of that giraffe.

We assigned some of the boys to butcher the prime cuts of giraffe meat, while we constructed a "scherm"—a defensive barrier—near one of the pools, approximately a hundred yards to its right. This involved cutting thorn bushes and piling them into a circular hedge. The enclosed space was smoothed, and if available, dry tambouki grass was spread in the center to serve as a bed, upon which fires were lit.

By the time the scherm was complete, the moon had risen, and our dinner of giraffe steaks and roasted marrow-bones awaited. How we savored those bones, despite the difficulty of cracking them! Giraffe marrow was a luxury unmatched, save perhaps for elephant's heart, which we secured the following day. We ate our simple meal by the moonlight, pausing occasionally to thank Good for his miraculous shot. Then, we smoked and spun yarns, creating a curious tableau as we squatted around the fire. I, with my short, grizzled hair standing straight up, and Sir Henry with his flowing yellow locks growing long, presented a stark contrast. I was thin, short, and dark, weighing merely nine and a half stone, whereas Sir Henry was tall, broad, fair, and weighed fifteen stone. However, the most peculiar figure of the trio was undoubtedly Captain John Good, R.N. Seated upon a leather bag, he appeared as though he had stepped directly from a civilized country for a comfortable day's shooting. Absolutely clean, tidy, and impeccably dressed in a brown tweed suit with matching hat and neat gaiters, he was beautifully shaved. His eyeglasses and false teeth were in perfect order, and he even sported a collar made of white gutta-percha.

"You see, they weigh so little," he said innocently when I expressed my astonishment at his attire. "And I always like to turn out like a gentleman." Alas, if he could have foreseen the raiment prepared for him in the days to come.

Thus, the three of us sat chatting in the beautiful moonlight, watching the Kafirs a few yards away suck their intoxicating "daccha" from pipes crafted from eland horns, until one by one they rolled into their blankets and slept by the fire. All remained awake except Umbopa, who sat slightly apart, chin resting on his hand, lost in deep thought. I noted that he rarely mingled with the other Kafirs.

Soon, a loud "_woof, woof_" echoed from the depths of the bush behind us. "That's a lion," I declared, and we all sprang to attention. Hardly had we settled when the strident trumpeting of an elephant rang out from the pool, a hundred yards away. "_Unkungunklovo! Indlovu!_" the Kafirs whispered urgently—"Elephant! Elephant!" Moments later, vast shadowy forms moved slowly from the water toward the bush.

Good leaped up, burning with the urge to slaughter, perhaps convinced that killing an elephant was as effortless as shooting a giraffe. I grabbed his arm and pulled him down.

"It's no good," I whispered. "Let them go."

Sir Henry, however, argued that we were in a paradise of game and proposed staying a day or two to try our luck. I was surprised, for previously he had always advocated pressing forward, especially after learning at Inyati that an Englishman named Neville had sold his wagon there two years prior and continued inland. Yet, his hunter instincts momentarily overrode caution. Good eagerly embraced the idea, and honestly, so did I; it went against my conscience to allow such a herd to escape without a challenge.

"All right, my hearties," I said. "I think we require a little recreation. Now, let's turn in; we must be off by dawn, and perhaps we can catch them feeding before they move on."

Agreed, we prepared for rest. Good removed his clothes, shaking them out, and carefully placed his eyeglasses and false teeth into his trouser pocket, folding each item neatly beneath a corner of his mackintosh sheet to protect them from the dew. Sir Henry and I made do with rougher arrangements, curling up in our blankets and sinking into the dreamless sleep that rewards the traveler.

*Going, going, go.* What was that sound?
