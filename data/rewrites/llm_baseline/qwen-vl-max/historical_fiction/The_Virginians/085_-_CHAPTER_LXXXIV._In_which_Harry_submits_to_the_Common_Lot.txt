The worst of times had passed for me, and poverty was no longer a shadow over my life. The death of my young cousin, though a sorrowful event, brought with it a change in fortune—my prospects improved dramatically. I became the next heir to a substantial estate. With no more children expected from my uncle and his wife, the path seemed clear. Sampson had once warned me that Lady Warrington might go to great lengths to thwart me, but in truth, she proved no such villain. Stricken by grief at her son’s loss, she turned inward, guided by her clergyman, who urged her to accept the will of Heaven. “While your son lived,” he told her, “your heart was drawn away from the higher world. You desired rank and title, even an earthly coronet. But now, what use is such glory to one who has but a few years left on earth? How can it compare to the crown of heaven, for which you are already assured?”

The tragedy stirred deep emotion across the community. In the chapels of the fervent sect to which she now fully aligned herself, sermons were delivered in memory of the child. I do not wish to judge the course she took—anyone who seeks solace in faith after suffering deserves respect. Even so, she sought reconciliation with me. A year after her loss, while visiting London, she requested a meeting. I went to her, and she delivered a sermon-like lecture, as was her custom. She marveled at the divine decree that allowed—and then punished—her son’s disobedience (he had gone out with his gun without her knowledge). Though this act brought me benefit, she saw it as a punishment from above.

She expressed hope that, should I ever inherit the family estate—though the Warringtons were generally long-lived, save for my father, whose life had been shortened by reckless youth—I would reform my ways. She prayed I would abandon my unsuitable companions, leave behind the theatre and its immoral crowd, and turn instead to the peace found only in religious duty. She filled her words with scripture, which I have no desire to repeat. When I left, she gave me a packet of sermons for Mrs. Warrington and a little book of hymns by Miss Dora, later known for her piety and marriage to the preacher Mr. Juffles. She forgave me, yet could not bear to see our boy.

After we lost our second child, my aunt and her daughter came swiftly to comfort us. They even invited us to their home. But my uncle now visited us nearly every day. He spent hours watching our infant, bringing endless toys and sweets. He begged the child call him “Godpapa.” When I felt the depth of my own grief—still sharp after twenty-five years—I understood his pain. My wife, however, sensed his sorrow before she knew what it meant to lose a child. Her mother’s heart had already felt the ache of paternal grief; mine, less tender, learned compassion only through experience. It was our baby’s coffin that finally reconciled me to my uncle.

He sent his coach to follow the modest funeral procession, and afterward took little Miles with him. The child chattered endlessly, forgetting his sadness in the delight of new black clothes and a carriage ride. His innocent talk pierced my wife’s heart. Could we ever wish that wound healed? I know her face well enough to recognize when, even now, she remembers that child—not as a distant memory, but as a living presence in heaven.

We returned soon to our cheerful lodgings in Bloomsbury. My young son, whom I could no longer lead about like a pet bear, was sent to Chartreux School. There, his friend Charley ensured he was treated fairly and not beaten beyond measure. Thanks to his excellent private tutor, he earned and kept a good position. He liked the school so much that he declared, if ever he had a son, he would send him there too.

I could no longer lead my bear, because I had other duties. Reconciled to us—perhaps more so for the sake of Mr. Miles—my uncle, who was such a loyal supporter of government that I often wondered how the Minister ever entrusted him with anything (since his vote was certain), used his influence on my behalf. I was appointed one of His Majesty’s Commissioners for licensing hackney-coaches—a post I held with pride until a dispute with the Minister (to be detailed elsewhere) cost me the office. I also completed my studies at the Temple and appeared in Westminster Hall in gown and wig. This year, my dear friend Mr. Foker, having business in Paris, invited me to accompany him. I was warmly welcomed by my American benefactor, Monsieur de Florac, who introduced me to his noble family and many of Paris’s most refined society. Yet I remained faithful to Foker, whose circle lay among the bourgeoisie—especially Monsieur Santerre, a brewer of some repute, though one who later made a name for himself not in beer, but in blood.

Foker needed me as interpreter, and I was glad to serve him, repaying the kindness he had shown me. Meanwhile, our wives stayed at Foker’s villa in Wimbledon, where they enjoyed the letters I sent them—dispatched through Mr. Hume, then at the Embassy—letters that would later be published in a neat volume.

While I quietly fulfilled my duties in London, unrest began to stir in the colonies—unrest that would culminate in separation from the mother country. When Mr. Grenville proposed the stamp duties, I told my wife the bill would cause discontent. We always wanted to extract as much as possible from England and pay back as little as we could. But I never imagined the fury it would ignite. It was like a family quarrel: a single incident sparks anger, but the real roots lie in old resentments. Years of petty tyranny, English arrogance toward foreigners and colonists alike, the belief that our rivers were inferior to theirs, and the natural resentment against imperial overreach all fueled the conflict. And the blunders of British policy pushed it to a breaking point—one I do not regret.

Had I been in Virginia, I might have joined the colonial side—not from principle, but perhaps from opposition to the domineering lady of Castlewood, who could have driven me into rebellion just as England drove the colonies. Was the Stamp Act the true cause of revolution? A tax no greater than what was paid in England itself. Ten years earlier, when France threatened our borders and we pleaded for help, would the colonies have rebelled then? Most people see the tax collector as a natural enemy. Against Britain in America stood thousands of brave and proud men—but even more who saw profit in the conflict or had personal reasons to join. I cannot say whether my feelings were selfish or patriotic, nor which side was right. Both, perhaps. I am sure England had no choice but to fight. And once defeated, I believe the loser felt no lasting bitterness.

Why did brother Hal write such fiery patriotic letters from Virginia, though he seemed reluctant to leave? My kind and devoted brother was always influenced by others—by me when we were together (he believed in my wit and wisdom so completely that if I said the weather was fine, he’d ponder it as if it were a maxim of the Seven Sages), by someone else when I was away. Who inspired these passionate declarations? “He is rebelling against Madam Esmond,” I said. “Some colonial woman, perhaps,” suggested my wife. But Hal never told us who “that lady” was. Indeed, he asked me not to mention the matter in my letters. “Madam wishes to read them all,” he wrote, “and I want to say nothing about you know what until the proper time.”

His affection shone through every letter. When he heard—through the same source I mentioned—that I had kept only a hundred pounds from his aunt’s legacy, despite my own dire straits, he was ready to mortgage his newly acquired estate. He quarreled with his mother on my behalf, speaking with more boldness than I ever would. Only when she accused him of ingratitude—after years of sacrifice for him—did he learn that our mother had cut off my allowance to favor him. By then, the deals were done, and I was fortunate to be free of want.

Every scrap of paper we ever wrote, our thrifty parent at Castlewood preserved—taped, labeled, stored away. We boys were less careful. I especially, perhaps too dismissive of my younger brother’s writings. But my wife, more generous, kept many of Harry’s letters, along with those of the angelic girl we would soon call sister.

“To think whom he has chosen, and whom he might have had! Oh, 'tis cruel!” my wife exclaimed when we received the letter in which Harry first revealed the name of his beloved.

“She was a pretty little girl when I left home—she may be a beauty now,” I remarked, reading the longest private letter Harry had ever written.

“But can she compare to my Hetty?” Mrs. Warrington asked.

“We agreed Hetty and Harry were not meant to be happy together,” I replied.

Theo kissed her husband. “My dear, I wish they had tried,” she sighed. “I feared—feared Hetty might lead him. She has the better head. But from reading this, it seems the new lady has taken command of poor Harry.” She handed me the letter:

“My dearest George has been prepared by earlier letters to understand how a certain lady has won my heart, which I have given up in exchange for something infinitely more precious—her own. She sits beside me as I write. If there is no bad spelling—such as you used to mock—I owe it to my lovely dictionary, which makes no mistakes, not even in the longest word. For I believe she is perfection.

“Since Madam Esmond reads all your letters, I dared not hint at a delicate matter—but now it is no secret, known throughout the country. Mr. George is not the only one in our family to make a secret marriage and be scolded by his mother. As a dutiful younger brother, I followed his example. Now I may tell you how it came to pass.

“I had not been home long before I saw my fate was sealed. I will not describe how beautiful Miss Fanny Mountain had become since I left for Europe. She says, ‘You’ll never believe it,’ and I’m glad—because she is the only thing in life I’d deny my dearest brother.

“That neither Madam Esmond nor my other mother (as Mountain is now) saw our attachment is a wonder—only explained by the fact that love makes others blind. My love for Fanny grew stronger seeing how she was treated by Madam Esmond, who was harsh and haughty. She bore it with sweetness so angelic (though she’ll order me not to write such nonsense). She was treated worse than a servant—our negroes speak more freely before Madam than Fanny ever could.

“And yet, Fanny says she does not regret it—for without it, I might never have become what I am to her. Oh, dear brother! When I recall your goodness—how you paid my debts, rescued me from prison, lived in poverty needlessly because of me, refused to reclaim what was rightfully yours, choosing my welfare over your own comfort—I am overwhelmed. Should I not thank Heaven for such a wife and such a brother?

“When I wrote asking you to send my aunt’s legacy, for a very urgent purpose, I had no idea you were yourself in poverty. That you, the head of our family, should serve as governor to a brewer’s son—that you should write for booksellers (except when your genius prompted you)—never entered my mind, until Mr. Foker’s letter arrived. It would never have been seen—Madam kept it hidden—had it not been for the disagreement between us.

“Poor Tom Diggle’s estate and slaves were for sale, due to his losses at play and his father’s debts. Madam Esmond saw this as a chance to provide for me. With six thousand pounds for land and stock, I could take possession of a fine property—near Richmond, between Kent and Hanover Court House. The house is not elegant compared to Castlewood, but the land is excellent, and the people remarkably healthy.

“This was a rare opportunity, Madam said—one that might never come again. By selling my commissions and using her savings, I could cover half the price, borrowing the rest. Money was scarce here, so it would be hard and costly. At this moment, with our new relative Mr. Van den Bosch bidding against us (his agent furious we might buy ahead of him), my aunt’s legacy arrived just in time. Now I am master of a good house and slaves in my native land. I shall likely be called to the House of Burgesses, and hope to welcome my dear brother and family beneath my roof. To sit by my own fire, ride my own horses, hunt with my own hounds—is better than soldiering now that war is over and no French to fight. Indeed, Madam Esmond insisted I leave the army and stay home when she gave me her 1750 pounds. She had lost one son who chose to write plays and live in England—let the other remain with her.

“But after buying the estate and sending papers to sell my commission, my mother wished me to marry someone of her choosing—certainly not my choice. Do you remember Miss Betsy Pitts of Williamsburgh? She is far worse than before, her face scarred by smallpox. Though Madam says she has every virtue, I find them unappealing. Her eyes don’t look straight; one leg is shorter than the other. And brother, did you never notice Fanny’s ankles when we were boys? Neater than any I’ve seen at the Opera.

“When it was decided I would leave the army, a certain dear girl—can you guess her name?—burst into tears of joy one day when we were alone. I could not help but feel deeply moved.

“‘Ah!’ she said, ‘Do you think the thought of your revered benefactress’s son going to battle doesn’t fill me with terror? Ah, Mr. Henry! Do you suppose I have no heart? When Mr. George was with Braddock, did we not pray for him? And when you were with Mr. Wolfe—oh!’

“Here she hid her face in her handkerchief, struggling not to let her mother see she was crying. But my dear Fanny insists she might have secretly hoped for such a thing (she admits it now), though she never imagined it for a moment. Nor did my mother, who thought Sam Lintot, the apothecary’s lad, was Fanny’s suitor—an absurd fellow I nearly kicked into the James River.

“But when the commission was sold and the estate bought, Fanny fell into deep melancholy. I found her weeping one day in her mother’s room, where they were trimming hats for my slaves.

“‘What! Crying, miss?’ I said. ‘Has my mother been scolding you?’

“‘No,’ she replied. ‘Madam Esmond was kind today.’

“And her tears fell upon a cockade she was sewing onto a hat for Sady, the head groom.

“‘Then why, miss, are your eyes so red?’ I asked.

“‘Because I have the toothache,’ she said, ‘or because—because I am a fool.’ Then she broke down. ‘Oh, Mr. Harry! Oh, Mr. Warrington! You’re leaving us, and it’s best. You’ll take your place in your country, as you should. You’ll leave us poor women in solitude and dependence. You’ll visit from time to time. And when you’re happy and honored, among your gay companions, you’ll remember your—’

“She could say no more, hiding her face as I, I confess, took her hand.

“‘Dearest, sweetest Miss Mountain!’ I said. ‘Oh, could I think the parting from me brings tears to those lovely eyes! Indeed, I fear I’d almost be happy! Let them look upon your—’

“‘Oh, sir!’ she cried. ‘Oh, Mr. Warrington! Consider who I am, and who you are! Remember the difference between us! Release my hand, sir! What would Madam Esmond say if—if—’

“‘If what?’ I asked, but our mother entered.

“‘What would Madam Esmond say?’ she cried. ‘She’d say you’re an ungrateful, artful, false, little—’

“‘Madam!’ I said.

“‘Yes, an ungrateful, artful, false, little wretch!’ she cried. ‘For shame, miss! What would Mr. Lintot say if he saw you making eyes at the Captain? And you, Harry, bring none of your garrison manners here! This is a Christian family, and my house is not for captains and their misses!’

“‘Misses, mother!’ I shouted. ‘Gracious powers, do you ever dare call Miss Mountain by such a name? Miss Mountain, the purest of her sex!’

“‘The purest of her sex! Can I trust my ears?’ she asked, turning pale.

“‘I mean,’ I said, ‘if anyone questioned her honor, I’d throw him out the window.’

“‘You mean you—your mother’s son—are actually paying honorable attention to this young person?’

“‘He would never dare offer anything else!’ Fanny cried. ‘Nor any woman but you, madam, to think so!’

“‘Oh, I didn’t know, miss!’ mother said, curtsying. ‘I didn’t know the honor you were doing our family! You propose to marry into us, do you? Do I understand Captain Warrington aright—that he intends to offer me Miss Mountain as a daughter-in-law?’

“‘It remains to be seen, madam, that I have no protector, or you would not insult me so!’ my poor victim said.

“‘I should think the apothecary protection sufficient!’ mother replied.

“‘I don’t, mother!’ I cried, furious. ‘And if Lintot offers her any liberty, I’ll brain him with his pestle!’

“‘Oh! If Lintot has withdrawn, sir, I suppose I must be silent. But I did not know. He came, as I thought, to court Miss—’ she paused. ‘We all thought the match equal, and I encouraged it.’

“‘He came because I had the toothache!’ Fanny said—and indeed she did. He pulled it out for her, and the rumors and suspicions of women know no end.

“‘What more natural than that he marry my housekeeper’s daughter? It was a suitable match!’ Madam continued, taking snuff. ‘But I confess,’ she added, ‘I didn’t know you intended to jilt the apothecary for my son!’

“‘Peace, for Heaven’s sake, peace, Mr. Warrington!’ my angel cried.

“‘Pray, sir, before you decide, had you not better look around my family?’ Madam said. ‘Dinah is a tall girl, not very dark; Cleopatra is promised to Ajax the blacksmith, but we could break it. If with an apothecary, why not a blacksmith? Martha’s husband has run away, and—’

“Here, dear brother, I confess I broke out swearing. I can’t help it—when a man is angry, oaths relieve him.

“‘Curses, blasphemy, ingratitude, disobedience!’ mother said, leaning on her tortoiseshell stick, waving it like a queen in a play. ‘These are my rewards!’ she cried. ‘O Heaven, what have I done to deserve this punishment? Does God visit the sins of my fathers upon me? Where do my children inherit their pride? When I was young, did I have any? Did I refuse my father’s choice? Did I ever disobey? No, sir. My fault, I admit, was loving you too much—perhaps neglecting your elder brother. I turned from Esau, clung to Jacob. And now I have my reward! I fixed my thoughts on this world, its honors. To see my son advanced was my ambition. I toiled, spared, to give him wealth. I took unfairly from my eldest son’s share, so my younger might prosper. And now—now I see him seducing the daughter of my housekeeper under my own roof, and answering my just anger with oaths and blasphemies!’
