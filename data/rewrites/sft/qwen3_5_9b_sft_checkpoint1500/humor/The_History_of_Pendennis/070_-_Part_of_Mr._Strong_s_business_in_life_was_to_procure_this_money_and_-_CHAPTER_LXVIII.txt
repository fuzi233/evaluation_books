There is a certain public house known as the Wheel of Fortune, a haunt where the air is thick with the dust of commerce and the clink of coins. It is here, within the shadow of Mr. James Morgan's club, that Sir Francis Clavering once held a private audience with Major Pendennis. Any gentleman who has ever stepped inside knows of the layout: a public parlour for the general riff-raff, a second room reserved for those in liveried service, and a third, strictly forbidden chamber marked "Private" in bold letters. This final sanctum belongs to "The Confidentials," an exclusive circle of which both Morgan and Lightfoot are members.

James Morgan, a man of profound silence, had been eavesdropping on a heated exchange between Strong and Major Pendennis at the Major's lodgings. His curiosity, piqued by the weight of the conversation, had led him to trail his master to the Wheel of Fortune, slipping quietly into the Private Room while the two gentlemen debated matters of state and scandal in the adjacent parlour. From a specific vantage point, hidden behind a curtain of shadows, Morgan could hear nearly everything. The voices rose in a high, angry key, and what he gathered only hardened the suspicions forming in his mind.

"He knew Altamont instantly, didn't he?" Morgan murmured to himself, piecing together the fragments. "Clavering isn't married to my Lady! Altamont is the man. A convict. Young Harthur gets into Parliament, and the Governor promises not to split. By Jove, what a sly old rogue that Governor is! No wonder he's eager to wed Blanche to Harthur. Why? Because she'll bring a hundred thousand guineas and secure a seat for him. A trade, pure and simple."

A physiognomist would have marveled at the transformation on Morgan's face. "If it weren't for my haggard looks and the prejudices of polite society," he muttered, eyeing his reflection in a broken mirror, "damn me, James Morgan, I'd marry her myself!" Yet, marriage seemed out of reach. Instead, Morgan calculated that this secret could serve as a ladder to elevate his own fortunes. Most of those involved would prefer to bury the truth. Sir Francis Clavering, whose fortune hung in the balance, would scream to keep it quiet; Colonel Altamont, whose neck was on the line, would do anything to hush it; and the upstart Mr. Harthur, who believed himself a duke despite having no more than a convict's daughter and a bought seat in Parliament, would pay any price to hide his origins. Even Lady C., Morgan reasoned, whether weary of Clavering or terrified for her son, would pay handsomely to be freed from this web. And Miss Blanche? She would surely reward the man who restored her stolen rights.

"Darn me," concluded the valet, his fingers twitching as he imagined the pile of gold, "with such a hand as luck has dealt me, James Morgan, I'm a made man. It might be a regular eternity of profit. Every single soul involved must subscribe. With what I've already banked, I could quit business, warn my old Governor, turn gentleman, and finally hire a servant of my own, begad." Despite these feverish calculations, Morgan maintained a mask of icy composure, ensuring his duties to the Major remained flawless.

However, one crucial player, Colonel Altamont, was currently missing from London. Morgan knew of Sir Francis's favorite tavern, Shepherd's Inn, but arrived there too late. The Colonel had already cashed his Derby winnings and fled to the Continent. The news sent a jolt of frustration through Morgan, though he masked it with his usual equable smile. "He'll squander that money in the gambling dens of the Rhine," Morgan mused. "It's confounded annoying he slipped away before I could lay my trap." Whether hope triumphed or deferred, victory or defeat, Morgan wore his countenance like iron. While the Major polished his boots, curled his hair, and endured the Major's senile satire with obsequious fidelity, Morgan secretly felt richer than his employer, knowing the secrets of everyone around him.

Meanwhile, the Major, Major Pendennis, spent an autumn wandering from country house to country house, seeking the warmth of old friendships. But the world had changed. Many of the men of his time had died, and their heirs knew him not. The traditions of "the Wild Prince" and the heroes of fashion were forgotten. Walking past the doors of his former haunts, the Major felt a creeping melancholy. He realized he was no longer of the present age. The young men laughed at him. The grand courtliness of the old days had vanished, replaced by a utilitarian drabness.

"The late Lord," he mused, comparing the past to the present, "came to London with four post-chaises and sixteen horses. The whole North Road rushed out to gaze upon his cavalcade. Now? The present Lord travels in a railway carriage with five porters, sneaking away from the station in a brougham, smoking a cigar." He sighed, thinking of Lady Lorraine. Once, she had been a goddess of diamonds and velvet, the wits of the world bowing at her feet. Today, she was a little woman in a black silk gown, talking of laboring classes and emigration, lurking in churches at eight in the morning.

"And the other young men," the Major raged internally, "those lounging guardsmen and lazy dandies sprawled over sofas, stealing off to smoke in each other's bedrooms, reverencing nothing—not even an old gentleman who knew their fathers! They're replaced by cotton-spinners and parsons with hair combed down their backs. The breed is gone. I'm getting old; they're laughing at us." And he was right. The times had shifted, and the new generation mocked the old ways with a gravity that stung the Major's sensitive pride.

By the dismal end of October, when the fogs rolled in and the lawyers returned to town, the Major came back to London. He found himself surrounded by the familiar chaos: loaded cabs, piled boxes, and crowds of returning families. For the Major, however, there were no bills to settle and no domestic sorrows to soothe him. He had money enough in his desk, and his indifference to others' needs usually kept him insulated. Yet, a gentleman may be wealthy and still feel dispirited.

His recent country visit had been trying. Gout had twinged his joints; the birds were wild and shy; the ploughed fields had worn him down. Worse still, the young men had laughed at him, and he had been forced to endure their mockery at table. He hadn't even managed his whist game. Sulky and discontented, he had been unbearable to Morgan, swearing at him and abusing him for days. He had scalded his mouth with bad soup at Swindon and lost his umbrella in a train carriage, a grievance that had driven him to curse his valet with renewed fervor.

Back in his lodgings, the atmosphere was stifling. The chimneys smoked furiously, and when the Major flung open the windows to vent his spleen, he swore so acrimoniously that Morgan, standing nearby, felt an impulse to hurl the old gentleman out of the casement himself. As the Major marched down the street to the Club, Morgan trailed behind, muttering curses of his own.

Bays's Club was not a sanctuary. The house smelled of fresh varnish and turpentine, and a large streak of white paint marred the back of the Major's fur-collared coat. The dinner was mediocre, and the company was odious. Old Hawkshaw, with his hacking cough, sat beside old Colonel Gripley, who devoured newspapers like a starving animal, joined by the irrepressible bore Jawkins, who detailed every inn-bill paid during his latest foreign tour. Each incident added to the Major's misery. As the waiter trod on his toe delivering coffee, the Major felt the Furies hunting him in tandem—from home to the Club, and back again.

While the Major suffered, Morgan sat in the landlady's parlour, drowning his troubles in hot brandy-and-water. He poured out his grievances to Mrs. Brixham, the landlady, who was, in effect, his slave. Morgan had bought the lease of her house, secured her son's acceptance, and held a bill of sale over her furniture. Her son, a clerk, was easily manipulated, and Mrs. Brixham, a clergyman's widow, was reduced to fetching his bootjack and slippers. The portraits of her dead children, tiny black profiles, hung over the mantelpiece of his back-parlour, now his property. Morgan drank deeply, paying for the liquor with the poor woman's own coin, and ate his supper with a relish born of dominance.

Just as Morgan finished a third tumbler, the Major returned from the Club, heavy with ill humor. Hearing the bell, Morgan swore savagely, finishing his drink before answering the summons. He received the Major's subsequent abuse in silence, kneeling to remove the boots while growling maledictions beneath his breath. As the Major demanded his coat be taken off, Morgan approached close enough for the scent of brandy to betray him.

"I'm a drunken beast!" the Major accused, pointing an accusing finger. "And you smell of it!"
