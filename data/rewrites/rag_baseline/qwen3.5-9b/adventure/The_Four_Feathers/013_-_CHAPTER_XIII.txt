To truly grasp the architecture of Ethne's impending exile, one must first witness the absolute fragility of her universe at this precise hour. It was half-past five in the afternoon, yet the air hummed with a tension that felt older than the clock on the wall. Outside, June had returned with its blinding, exhilarating sunlight, transforming London into a gaudy spectacle of pleasure and verdant illusion. Across the street, windows bloomed with artificial gaiety; below, carriages rolled smoothly toward the Park, oblivious to the storm brewing inside Hill Street. Then, the sound arrived—a jingle of bells rising suddenly, piercing the domestic silence.

Mrs. Adair lifted her head, her eyes snapping toward the open window. "That's a cab," she breathed.

"Yes," Ethne confirmed, though her voice lacked the eager cadence of hope.

Ethne leaned forward, pressing her face against the glass, watching the vehicle pass. "But it is not stopping here," she murmured, as the metallic ring faded into the distance.

Mrs. Adair glanced at the clock, her brow furrowing. "Colonel Durrance is late," she said, turning with a mixture of curiosity and unease toward her friend. To Mrs. Adair, Ethne's posture screamed of apprehension rather than anticipation; the slight tremor in her affirmation suggested a dread she could not suppress. Yet, as the carriage swept past without halting, a flicker of relief crossed Ethne's features. "I wonder why you did not go to the station to meet him?" Mrs. Adair asked slowly, probing the silence.

The answer came swiftly, defensive and sharp. "He might have perceived that I had descended upon him solely because he appeared helpless. I refuse to allow him that perception. He travels with his servant." Ethne watched the street again, making abortive movements toward speech before retreating into silence. Finally, she resolved to speak.

"Do you recall the telegram I presented to you?"

"From Lieutenant Calder, confirming Colonel Durrance's blindness?"

"Yes. I require a solemn vow: never to mention it. I cannot bear the thought of him knowing I ever received such a message."

Mrs. Adair was baffled, and she detested being left in the dark. She had seen the telegram, but she had been unaware that Ethne had already written to Durrance, binding herself to him the moment the news broke. When Ethne had first shown the paper, she had simply declared, "I am engaged to him." Mrs. Adair had accepted this as fact, allowing herself to remain in that comfortable delusion.

"You will promise?" Ethne insisted, her voice tightening.

"Certainly, my dear, if you wish it," Mrs. Adair replied, offering an ungracious shrug. "Yet there must be a reason. I struggle to comprehend why you demand such an oath."

"Because two lives must not be destroyed by my presence."

There was truth in Mrs. Adair's suspicion that Ethne feared the blind man she intended to wed. Indeed, Ethne harbored a quiet terror. Twelve months had elapsed since, in this very room, under similar golden skies, Ethne had urged Durrance to forget her. Every letter since had proven futile; whether he tried or not, he had not forgotten. Even the final missive, scribbled three weeks prior from Wadi Halfa in the shaky, childish hand of a broken man, where the ink ran together in a chaotic sprawl, declaring his calamity and renouncing his suit—it was proof positive, perhaps more damning than any hopeful precursor, that he remembered. As she stood by the window, Ethne realized with crushing clarity that the burden of forgetting now rested entirely on her shoulders. If she could not erase the memory, she must ensure that no careless word, slipping forth in a moment of weakness, revealed that she had not forgotten either.

"No," she whispered, turning resolutely toward her friend. "Two lives shall not be spoiled because of me."

"Are you certain, Ethne?" Mrs. Adair pressed, her tone softening. "Is it not possible that this path—the path of marriage—will ultimately destroy you both? Will you not eventually feel Colonel Durrance, despite your will, becoming a hindrance, a drag upon your spirit? Might he not feel the same?"

"No," Ethne stated, her decision absolute. "I shall not feel it, nor must he."

In Mrs. Adair's estimation, the "two lives" referred to those of Durrance and Harry Feversham. She was mistaken; the stakes were far narrower, confined strictly to Durrance and Ethne herself. Ethne did not correct her, finding a strange comfort in her friend's ignorance, and allowed the misconception to stand.

Resuming her vigil at the window, Ethne mapped out her future with military precision, anticipating every potential breach in her defense. The task was arduous, and fear began to coil in her stomach, threatening to overwhelm her resolve. Yet, the destination justified the peril. Durrance had lost everything that gave life meaning the moment darkness claimed his sight—every joy, every ambition. "What should I do if I were crippled?" he had asked Harry Feversham on their last ride along the River Thames. "A clever man might endure it. But what if I were condemned to sit in a chair for the rest of my days?" Ethne had not heard those specific words, yet she understood the man perfectly without them. By birth, he was heir to distant lands and grand traditions; he had lost his heritage. The long journeys, the vibrant faces of foreign nations, the crackle of the campfire, the solitary spark of red light against the desert's black silence, the sleep beneath brilliant stars, the cooling night winds, and the weight of governance—all were gone. Only one thing remained: herself. And only as long as he could believe she desired him.

While her thoughts churned with this resolve, the cab she awaited pulled up unnoticed at the door. It was not until the servant rang the bell that reality pierced her reverie.

"He has come!" she exclaimed, startled from her trance.

True to form, Durrance was not particularly acute; he rarely scrutinized his acquaintances, accepting them as they presented themselves. It would have been effortless, Ethne noted, to soothe any suspicions he might harbor. But *now*, paradoxically, it would be easier than ever. There was no cause for alarm. Thus she reasoned, yet despite her logic, she steel-ed her nerves for the confrontation rather than rushing to embrace her betrothed.

Mrs. Adair slipped quietly from the room, leaving Ethne alone as Durrance entered. She did not move immediately, holding her pose, bracing herself for the shock of his transformation. Instead, she was surprised. The changes she anticipated were absent. His face was weathered, his hair streaked with grey, but there was no aura of helplessness or uncertainty—the very specter she dreaded most. He stepped into the room as if his eyes were fully functional; his memory seemed to chart the exact location of every piece of furniture. At most, he extended a hand once or twice where he expected a chair.

Ethne retreated silently to the window, unsure of how to greet him. Immediately, he smiled and approached her directly.

"Ethne," he said.

"It is not true, then," she cried out, the words forced from her by the fluidity of his movement. "You have recovered."

"It is quite true, and I have not recovered," he answered calmly. "But you shifted at the window, and thus I knew you were present."

"How did you know? I made no sound."

"No, but the window stands open. The street noise suddenly intensified, signaling that someone in front of the window had moved aside. I surmised it was you."

Their exchange was far from the conventional greetings of newly engaged couples, yet it successfully deflected awkwardness. Ethne recoiled from any perfunctory expressions of regret, sensing there was none needed, and Durrance showed no desire to hear them. Many things passed between them unspoken, understood implicitly. They exchanged a brief handshake, and Ethne withdrew into the room.

"I shall prepare some tea," she said. "Then we may converse."

"Yes, we must speak, mustn't we?" Durrance replied gravely. He shed his serious demeanor, however, chatting with good humor about the particulars of his journey home. He even found amusement in recounting his initial days of blindness, and Ethne's anxieties rapidly dissipated. They had nearly vanished from her mind entirely when a shift in his attitude reignited her fears.

"I wrote to you from Wadi Halfa," he said. "I am uncertain if you were able to read the letter."

"Quite well," Ethne assured him.

"I enlisted a friend to hold the paper, informing me when I was writing on the sheet versus the blotting pad," he continued, laughing lightly. "Calder—from the Sappers—but you do not know him."

He launched the name with surprising speed, striking Ethne like a physical blow; he had set a trap to ensnare her. The stillness of his face suggested he was listening with intense scrutiny for a reaction, a stifled exclamation that would reveal her knowledge of Calder of the Sappers. Did he suspect? Did he know of the telegram? Did he guess that her letter stemmed from pity? She searched his countenance, but it offered no answers save for an alert intensity.
