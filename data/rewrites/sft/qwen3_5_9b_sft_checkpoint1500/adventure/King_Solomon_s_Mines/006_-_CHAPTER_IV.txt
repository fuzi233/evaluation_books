I shall not chronicle in minute detail our arduous trek to Sitanda's Kraal, situated near the confluence of the Lukanga and Kalukwe Rivers. From Durban, this journey spanned over a thousand miles. The final three hundred were undertaken on foot, a necessity imposed by the dreaded "tsetse" fly, whose bite proves fatal to all creatures save donkeys and men. We departed Durban in late January and established camp near Sitanda's Kraal during the second week of May. Though our travels were fraught with peril, most incidents fell into the category common to every African hunter, save for one which I will reserve for later. Thus, I omit them here to spare you a tedious account.

At Inyati, the remote trading post in the Matabele lands ruled by the tyrant Lobengula, we reluctantly abandoned our sturdy wagon. Of the twenty fine oxen purchased in Durban, only twelve remained. A cobra claimed one, three succumbed to exhaustion and thirst, one wandered astray, and three others perished after consuming the toxic "tulip" herb. Five more fell ill from the same poison, yet we saved them with a decoction brewed from boiled tulip leaves—a potent antidote if applied swiftly. Leaving the wagon and the surviving livestock in the capable hands of Goza and Tom, alongside a local Scotch missionary, we embarked on foot. My companions included Umbopa, Khiva, Ventvögel, and six hired bearers.

There was a profound silence as we departed. Each of us harbored a lingering doubt: would we ever see our wagon again? For my own part, I held little hope. We tramped in somber silence until Umbopa, marching ahead, broke the spell with a Zulu chant. He spoke of brave souls weary of tame existence who ventured into the vast unknown, risking death to discover wonders. And lo, he declared, they found not a wilderness, but a paradise teeming with beauty, fertile land, and enemies worth killing.

Laughter erupted among us, buoyed by Umbopa's cheer. He was a spirited savage, dignified in his bearing, possessed of a remarkable gift for maintaining morale, even when not plagued by his melancholic reveries. We all grew to cherish him dearly.

Now, I must recount the specific adventure I have promised, for I harbor a deep affection for tales of the chase.

Approximately a fortnight's march from Inyati, we discovered a stunning expanse of lush woodland. The kloofs were cloaked in dense "idoro" bush, interspersed with the formidable "wacht-een-beche," or "wait-a-little thorn." Abundantly growing were the majestic "machabell" trees, heavy with their delicious yellow fruit, though the pits were nearly as large as the fruit itself. These trees were the elephants' favorite fare, and evidence of their voracious appetite was everywhere: frequent tracks, snapped branches, and uprooted giants scattered the landscape.

On a particularly beautiful evening, following a long day's march, we arrived at a spot of breathtaking loveliness. At the base of a bush-covered hill lay a dry riverbed containing pools of crystal-clear water, rimmed with the hoof prints of game. Opposite stood a park-like plain dotted with flat-topped mimosa and glossy machabells, surrounded by an endless sea of silent, untrodden bush.

Emerging onto this path, we startled a troop of tall giraffes. They fled with a strange, sailing gait, tails curled high, their hooves rattling like castanets. Though three hundred yards away and beyond effective range, Good, who walked ahead armed with an express loaded with solid balls, could not resist the temptation. He fired at the last, a young female. By a stroke of extraordinary fortune, the ball struck her squarely on the neck, shattering her spine. She collapsed instantly, rolling head over heels like a rabbit. I had never witnessed a more bizarre spectacle.

"Oh, 'Bougwan!' " echoed Sir Henry and I, referring to Good's nickname, "Glass Eye," earned due to his spectacles. Although he was a notoriously poor shot, his lucky hit secured his reputation as a marvel among the Kafir tribes.

We tasked some of the boys with butchering the giraffe, while we constructed a "scherm"—a circular thorn hedge enclosing a cleared space—near one of the pools. Inside, we laid down a bed of dry tambouki grass and lit fires. As the moon rose, our dinner of giraffe steak and roasted marrow bones was ready. There is no greater luxury than giraffe marrow, surpassed only by elephant heart, which we hoped to taste tomorrow. Eating by moonlight, we paused occasionally to praise Good's miraculous aim, then smoked and chatted around the fire. We formed a striking tableau: I, with my short, grizzled hair, thin frame, and dark complexion; Sir Henry, tall, broad, fair-skinned, and sporting long golden locks; and Captain John Good, R.N., sitting atop a leather bag. Unlike the rest, Good looked immaculate, dressed in brown tweed, a matching hat, neat gaiters, and sporting a collar of white gutta-percha. His face was perfectly shaved, his glasses and dentures pristine.

"They weigh so little," he explained innocently regarding his lack of mud or sweat. "And I always prefer to present myself as a gentleman." Alas, if he had known the raiment awaiting him, his composure might have faltered.

As the Kafirs sipped their intoxicating "daccha" from pipes carved from eland horns, drifting off into slumber, Umbopa sat apart, chin resting on his hand, lost in deep thought. Suddenly, a loud "woof!" echoed from behind us. "A lion!" I cried, and we sprang up. Hardly had we risen when the strident trumpet of an elephant shattered the night. "_Unkungunklovo! Indlovu!_" the Kafirs whispered urgently—"Elephant! Elephant!"

Before long, vast shadowy forms emerged from the water's edge, lumbering slowly toward the bush. Good leaped up, bloodlust in his eyes, convinced that slaughtering elephants would be as effortless as shooting giraffes. I grabbed his arm, pulling him down.

"It's futile," I warned quietly. "Let them pass."

Sir Henry, however, argued for staying. "It seems we are in a paradise of game," he suggested. "I propose we linger a day or two and give them a try." I was taken aback; usually, Sir Henry urged haste, especially after learning at Inyati that an Englishman named Neville had sold his wagon there two years prior and ventured deeper. Yet, his hunter's instinct clearly overrode caution. Good eagerly agreed, and frankly, so did I; letting such a herd escape without a challenge violated my conscience.

"All right, my hearties," I declared. "We need some recreation. Let us retire now, aiming to depart by dawn, perhaps catching them feeding before they retreat."

Preparations commenced. Good stripped, carefully folding his trousers, glasses, and false teeth, placing them beneath his waterproof sheet to avoid the morning dew. Sir Henry and I settled into our rougher arrangements, curling up in blankets for a dreamless sleep.

*Going, going, go...* What sound was that?

Suddenly, violent scuffling erupted from the water's direction, followed immediately by a cacophony of terrible roars. There was no mistake; only a lion could produce such a noise. We scrambled up, staring toward the water where a confused mass of yellow and black staggered and struggled toward us. Seizing our rifles and slipping on our veldtschoons (hide boots), we burst from the scherm. By then, the mass had collapsed, rolling over and over until it lay still.

Upon closer inspection, we saw the truth. On the grass lay a sable antelope bull—the most magnificent of Africa's antelopes—dead, impaled by the massive curved horns of a magnificent black-maned lion, equally deceased. The sable had descended to drink where the lion lay in ambush. As the antelope drank, the lion lunged, only to be pinned by the sharp horns. Unable to free himself, the lion tore and bit at the bull's neck and back. Driven mad by agony, the bull charged until it collapsed, dragging its killer down with it.

Once we had inspected the carcasses, we summoned the Kafirs, and together dragged the bodies to the scherm. We retired inside, resolving to awaken only at dawn.

At first light, we prepared for battle. Armed with three eight-bore rifles, ample ammunition, and water bottles filled with weak cold tea—which I swear is the best stimulant for shooting—we consumed a light breakfast. Umbopa, Khiva, and Ventvögel joined us, leaving the other Kafirs to skin the lion and butcher the antelope.

Finding the broad elephant trail was effortless. Ventvögel confirmed it belonged to twenty to thirty elephants, mostly mature bulls. Though the herd had moved during the night, the broken trees, bruised leaves, and smoking droppings indicated we were not far behind.

Soon, we spotted the herd. Numbering roughly thirty, they stood in a hollow, finishing their morning repast and flapping their great ears. They were merely two hundred yards away—a splendid sight. I tossed a handful of dry grass into the air to gauge the wind; if they smelled us, they would vanish before we could fire. Fortunately, the breeze blew from the elephants toward us.
