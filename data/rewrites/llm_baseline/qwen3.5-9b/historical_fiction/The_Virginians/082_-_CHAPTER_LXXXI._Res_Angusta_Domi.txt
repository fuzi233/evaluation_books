I have already laid out my current sentiments as an elderly gentleman concerning that hasty leap into matrimony, which I convinced my dearest partner to take when we were both barely out of our teens. As a man and a father—with a proper awareness of the necessity of mutton chops and the importance of settling the baker's bill, surrounded by a pack of rash children who might flee to Scotland tomorrow, citing my and my wife's example as justification for their impudence—I know I ought to be extremely cautious in recounting this early phase of the married life of George Warrington, Esquire, and Theodosia his wife. I should perhaps offer a *mea culpa*, adopt a demure demeanor, sit comfortably in my armchair, claim to be in a state of profound repentment, and present myself as a cautionary tale for imprudent, hot-headed youth.

Yet, truth be told, the married life regarding which my dear relatives prophesied so gloomily has utterly disappointed those prudent and respectable individuals. It has indeed had its trials; however, I can recall them without bitterness—its passionate sorrows, of which Time, by God's gracious ordinance, has served as the benign healer; its days of poverty, which we, who endured them, bore to the astonishment of our sympathetic relatives watching from afar; and its precious rewards and blessings, so immense that I scarce dare whisper them onto this page. To speak of them requires nothing less than awful reverence and prayer directed toward the One Ear to which the thanks of all mankind are offered. To marry without financial security is undoubtedly wrong, dangerous, and a transgression against our social codes; yet, do not tens of thousands of our fellow creatures commit this "crime" annually, trusting solely in Heaven, health, and labor? Do young people not enter marriage taking hope into account, nor dare to begin housekeeping until the cottage is fully furnished, the cellar and larder stocked, the cupboard brimming with silver, and the strongbox heavy with coin? The population of the world would cease to multiply if the laws regulating the genteel classes were made universal. Our gentry tremble at the precipice in their silk stockings and pumps, waiting years for a bridge or a gilded barge to carry them across; our poor do not fear to wet their bare feet, plant them in the stream, and trust to fate and strength to bear them over. Who would wish to consign his daughter to poverty? Who would advise his son to undertake the countless risks of impoverished married life, removing the beloved girl from comfort and competence, and subjecting her to debt, misery, privation, loneliness, sickness, and the hundred gloomy consequences of *res angusta domi* (narrow circumstances)? I gaze upon my own wife and beg her pardon for imposing a task so fraught with pain and peril upon one so gentle. I reflect on the trials she endured and am grateful for them, and for that unfailing love and constancy with which God blessed her and strengthened her to bear them all. Regarding the question of marriage, I cannot be considered a fair judge: my own union was so imprudent—and yet so happy—that I must not presume to offer counsel to the young. I have suffered poverty, but I rarely found it anything other than tolerable. Had I not undergone it, I never could have known the kindness of friends, the delight of gratitude, or the surprising joys and consolations that sometimes accompany a scanty meal and a narrow fire, cheering the long day's labor. At least this is certain concerning the lot of the decent poor: a great deal of superfluous pity is often wasted upon them. Good-natured fine folks, who occasionally stepped out of the sunshine of their wealth into narrow obscurity, were blinded, as it were, whereas we could see quite cheerfully and clearly. They stumbled over obstacles that meant nothing to us; they were surprised at the resignation with which we drank small beer and heartily gave thanks over such cold mutton.

My father-in-law, the good General, married his Molly when he was merely a subaltern in a foot regiment, possessing a purse scarce better filled than my own. They experienced their share of fortune's ups and downs. I suspect (though my wife will never confess this) that they married, as people could in their youth, without previously seeking permission from their parents. At any rate, so pleased were they with their own marital luck that they did not begrudge their children's, nor were they frightened by the prospect of minor hardships we might encounter in our married lives. I suppose that when I presented my financial statements to Mr. Lambert, I was anxious to deceive both of us. Believing me to be master of two thousand pounds, he departed for Jamaica quite at ease regarding his darling daughter's comfort and maintenance, at least for some years. After covering the expenses of his family's outfit, the worthy man left not much richer than his son-in-law; and a few trinkets, some lace from Aunt Lambert, and twenty new guineas in a purse crafted by her mother and sisters constituted Theo's marriage portion. However, in valuing my assets, I chose to count as a valid debt a sum my honored mother never acknowledged until the day she was called to pay the final debt of all. The sums I had disbursed for her, she argued, were spent on the improvement and maintenance of the estate destined for me upon her death. Any money she could spare was reserved for my poor brother, who possessed nothing. He would never have spent his own means had he not imagined himself the sole heir to the Virginian property—a notion the good lady emphasized in many letters—but for a half-hour accident of birth. He was now distinguishing himself in the service of his King and Country. Purchasing his promotion was, she supposed, his brother's duty! Once I had completed my bar studies and dramatic amusements, Madam Esmond informed me that I was welcome to return home and assume the position in our colony to which my birth entitled me. She communicated this statement to me more than once through Mountain, before news of my marriage had reached her.

There is no need to recall her expressions of maternal indignation upon learning of the step I had taken. Upon the pacification of Canada, my dear Harry requested leave of absence and dutifully visited Virginia. He wrote describing his reception at home and the splendid entertainments my mother arranged in honor of her son. Castlewood, which she had not inhabited since our departure for Europe, was reopened to our colonial friends; and the friend of Wolfe and the soldier of Quebec was received by all our acquaintances with every becoming honor. Some dismal quarrels, to be sure, ensued because my brother persisted in maintaining his friendship with Colonel Washington of Mount Vernon, whose praises Harry never grew tired of singing. Indeed, I grant the gentleman every virtue; and in the struggles that terminated so fatally for England a few years prior, I can admire as well as his warmest supporters General Washington's glorious constancy and success.

If these battles between Harry and our mother were frequent, as he described in his letters, I wondered why he should remain at home. One reason naturally suggested itself to my mind, which I scarcely liked to communicate to Mrs. Warrington; for we had both discussed our dear little Hetty's romantic attachment to my brother, wondering why he had never discovered it. Needless to say, my gentleman had found some young lady at home more to his taste than our dear Hester, thus accounting for his prolonged stay in Virginia.

Soon thereafter, a letter arrived containing not a full confession but an admission of this interesting fact. A person was described, unnamed—a Being of all beauty and perfection, typical of young ladies under similar circumstances. My wife asked to see the letter; I could not help showing it and handed it to her with a very sad face. To my surprise, she read it without exhibiting any corresponding sorrow.

"I have thought of this before, my love," I said. "I feel with you for your disappointment regarding poor Hetty."

"Ah! poor Hetty," said Theo, looking down at the carpet.

"It would never have worked," I remarked.

"No—they would not have been happy," sighed Theo.

"How strange he never should have found out her secret!" I continued.

She looked me full in the face with an odd expression. "Pray, what does that look mean?" I asked.

"Nothing, my dear—nothing! Only I am not surprised!" said Theo, blushing.

"What," I asked, "can there be another?"

"I am sure I never said so, George," the lady replied hurriedly. "But if Hetty has overcome her childish folly, ought we not all to be glad? Do you gentlemen suppose that you are the only ones to fall in love and eventually tire of it?"

"What!" I exclaimed, with a strange commotion within my mind. "Do you mean to tell me, Theo, that you ever cared for anyone but me?"

"Oh, George," she whimpered, "when I was at school, there was—one of the boys at Doctor Backhouse's school who sat in the loft next to us; and I thought he had lovely eyes, and I was so shocked when I recognized him behind the counter at Mr. Grigg's, the mercer's, when I went to buy a cloak for baby, and I wanted to tell you, my dear, but I didn't know how!"
