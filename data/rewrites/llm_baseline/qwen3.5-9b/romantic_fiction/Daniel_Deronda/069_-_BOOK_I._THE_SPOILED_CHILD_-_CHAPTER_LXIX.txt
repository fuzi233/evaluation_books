"The human nature unto which I felt / That I belonged, and reverenced with love, / Was not a punctual presence, but a spirit / Diffused through time and space..."

Sir Hugo executed his plan to spend the autumn at Diplow, and by early October, his presence had injected a measure of cheerfulness throughout the neighborhood. From the stately halls of Brackenshaw and Quetcham down to the modest parlors of Wanchester shops, everyone seemed to benefit from his arrival. Sir Hugo was a man of considerable charm, a Liberal of noble birth who believed firmly in reform without fearing it would disrupt the fundamental fabric of English social habits—a habit, notably, of adoring society when it is walled in and adorned with hereditary titles. Consequently, he transformed Diplow into a delightful gathering place. He extended invitations to elderly solicitors and young village curates alike, yet with a shrewd touch regarding the composition of his guest list. He ensured that the "common poultry" of the social sphere were not fed indiscriminately together, lest anyone feel slighted. Easy-going Lord Brackenshaw might welcome Attorney Robinson, but Robinson would have been naturally offended to be asked to dine alongside those he considered his social superiors. Sir Hugo, acutely aware of these nuances, effortlessly gained popularity and dispensed pleasure, outcomes that perfectly suited his benevolent disposition.

For the rector of Pennicote, Mr. Gascoigne, the reception at Diplow stood in stark contrast to the haughty tolerance he had endured under Grandcourt's rule. This shift was not born of affection for the baronet, but of Sir Hugo's desire to maintain a cordial relationship with Mr. Gascoigne on behalf of Mrs. Grandcourt. Sir Hugo's chivalry had grown increasingly devoted to her memory. Yet, he guarded the reasons behind this devotion with a rare reticence, refusing to share even with his own wife, Lady Mallinger, what he deemed a woman's secret.

Daniel Deronda, following his engagement to Mirah, felt compelled to disclose his new position and intentions to Sir Hugo via letter. He anticipated that his fatherly friend would feel some degree of dissatisfaction, perhaps even pain, at this turn of fate. Reading unwelcome news offered the advantage of avoiding the hasty, regrettable expressions of impatience that often accompany verbal confrontation. Deronda dreaded the clash of words that could render pardonable feelings permanently offensive.

Sir Hugo, though not entirely surprised, was deeply vexed. His immediate recourse was to show the letter to Lady Mallinger, expecting her astonishment to serve as a buffer against his own discontent. When she expressed shock and distress that Daniel's immense talents and the comfort of his presence had dissolved into madness over Jewish affairs, the baronet could dismiss her concerns with ease: *"Oh, nonsense, my dear! Depend upon it, Dan will not make a fool of himself. He holds large political notions about Judaism which you cannot fathom. No fear but Dan will keep his head above water."*

However, regarding the impending marriage, Lady Mallinger offered no counter-argument. With gentle candor, she admitted she had scarcely imagined such a development when Mirah sang at her parties or tutored Amabel. After a brief hesitation, she confessed that she had briefly entertained the notion that Daniel might eventually marry Mrs. Grandcourt—given his presence in Genoa at that precise moment—and while she personally disliked widows, she conceded that such a union might have been preferable to his total withdrawal into Jewish causes. Sir Hugo, sharing this sentiment so strongly, could not label it a feminine error. Thus, his ill-humor at the refutation of his hopes for Gwendolen remained unvoiced. He instructed Lady Mallinger to keep the matter silent until further notice, reasoning that if the news struck Gwendolen harshly, delay was kinder given her fragile nerves, and ultimately, she deserved to hear it directly from Daniel.

While residing at Diplow, Sir Hugo continued his paternal attentions toward Gwendolen, supported willingly by Lady Mallinger despite her reservations about widows.

The move to Offendene had been completed, and Gwendolen settled there with a calm surpassing her mother's expectations. She experienced a profound, peaceful melancholy stemming from the renunciation of selfish demands and the acceptance of simple existence—even finding kindness in a dog—to be a gift beyond expectation. Could one, nearly lost in a pit of darkness, truly complain of the fresh air and sunlight? Life could be viewed daily as an escape, where the return of morning and evening, and particularly the emergence of pure sympathy or generous impulse piercing inner gloom, served as a salvation reconciling one to hardship. Those possessing the self-knowledge to accuse themselves as Hamlet did understood this habitual sense of rescue. Gwendolen relived the terrifying history of her temptations—from the initial illusion of self-gratification as she fled her conscience, to the later desperate hatred dragging her toward its fulfillment, all while praying for the help of the very conscience she had abandoned. Now, she dwelled on every word Deronda had spoken pointing to her deliverance from her worst evils and the harm she had caused, clinging to his words as anchors against despair.

Yet, she was buoyed by the prospect of seeing him again. She did not perceive him as distant; her supreme need blinded her to the separateness of his life, filling her mental landscape entirely with his connection to her. This was not unique to Gwendolen; we are all prone to this passionate egoism of imagination, projecting our needs onto others and even onto God. The future she faced with willingness was one of continuous assimilation to a type he would hold before her. Had he not first appeared as a corrective presence, initially met with resentment but finally embraced with total love and trust? She could not conceive of ending this reliance, which had become to her imagination the solid ground beneath her feet, the only condition allowing her to walk.

Deronda arrived at Diplow shortly thereafter, a location more accessible from London than the Abbey. He had planned to take Ezra and Mirah to a coastal retreat while preparing a new home for Mirah as his bride, where they could jointly care for her brother. However, Ezra refused to leave unless accompanying them to the East. Outward pressures weighed heavily on him, yet his mind dwelt on the Eastern voyage with visionary joy. Deronda, eager to consult Sir Hugo regarding his resources and affairs in preparation for a marriage he hoped to solemnize within two months, needed a reason to visit Diplow. But he valued another motive equally: his promise to Gwendolen. A strange ache of anxiety persisted in his heart amidst the blessedness of his own lot—a paradox, for lovers are typically hailed as happy, implying an indifference to external sorrow. Yet human experience is often paradoxical. It was no betrayal of Mirah, nor did it diminish his love for her, that his joy could coexist with care for another. What is love itself, for the one we cherish most? It is an enfolding of immeasurable cares that remain superior to any joy existing outside that love.

Deronda visited Diplow twice and saw Gwendolen twice, yet returned to town without revealing the change in his circumstances. He blamed himself. In communications of grave import likely to cause pain, one feels dependent on a preparatory alignment of words and moods, seeking an agreement in the listener's state of mind with the impact of the news. During the first meeting, Gwendolen was so consumed by her own inquiries—about arranging her life, reducing her ignorance, and amending her selfishness—that Deronda recoiled from diverting her attention to his own plight, unwilling to inflict a wound while she leaned on him for support. In the second meeting, determined to steer the conversation, he found her in deep depression, overwhelmed by miserable memories that felt more real than any future materials. She cried hysterically, accusing him of eventual disdain. He could offer only soothing words. As she gradually revived, displaying that pathetic, childlike interest with tear-beaded lashes, it became impossible to burden her further.

Time marched on, making the difficult disclosure a pressing duty. True, Gwendolen never suspected he had affairs, nor did she inquire why he had been in Genoa. Yet, this unconsciousness meant that a sudden revelation of life-altering facts would strike her with greater force. Leaving the truth to be whispered by others would feel like cruel neglect. Writing was impossible; his tenderness could not endure the image of her reading his virtual farewell in solitude, perhaps interpreting his words as cold self-congratulation. He descended to Diplow again, resolved to risk every other peril rather than return leaving her in ignorance.

On this third visit, Deronda found Hans Meyrick installed at Diplow with his easel, painting the three daughters on a bank in the "Gainsborough style," interspersed with sketches of village children at Pennicote to improve his acquaintance with the Gascoignes. Hans appeared recovered in vivacity, though Deronda sensed artifice in it, akin to a lady's bloom that is too high-toned and persistently steady. Despite his gratitude and admiration for Deronda, Hans harbored a certain irritation. Open natures often feel aggrieved when a friend's reserve breaks to reveal a reality not merely unsuspected, but contrary to hopeful conjecture. While Hans had freely poured out his own heart regarding Mirah, he could not suppress the sting of realizing that Deronda had concealed a rivalrous feeling that now emerged as the decisive factor. Friends turning out to be more significant than expected always pose a danger to our pride. Hans must be excused for these stirrings of bruised sensibility; he had acted rightly by his friend, convinced his own character had proven superior.
