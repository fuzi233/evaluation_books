As the moon cast its silvery glow through the windows of our ancestral home, I felt the weight of the previous night's terror settling upon me like a shroud. It was as if the darkness itself had seeped into the very fabric of our household, infusing the air with an eerie presence that refused to dissipate. The memory of that night still lingered, a palpable entity that clung to me like a damp mist.

The next day, I found myself unable to bear the solitude, my nerves frayed by the encounter. I considered confiding in Papa, but two opposing fears held me back. On one hand, I feared his laughter, which would reduce the experience to a triviality, stripping it of its haunting essence. On the other, I worried that he might suspect I had fallen prey to the mysterious affliction that had beset our community, and I didn't want to burden him with unnecessary concern. As he had been ill for some time, I sought to shield him from alarm.

Fortunately, I had the comfort of Madame Perrodon and the lively Mademoiselle Lafontaine. They noticed my unease and eventually coaxed the truth from me. Mademoiselle laughed, but I sensed a flicker of concern in Madame's gaze.

"By the way," Mademoiselle said, her voice light, "have you heard the whispers about the lime tree walk behind Carmilla's bedroom window? It's said to be haunted."

"Nonsense!" Madame replied, her tone firm. "And who's spreading such tales?"

"Martin claims he saw a woman strolling down the avenue twice, when the old gate was being repaired, just before dawn. He swears it was the same figure both times."

Madame scoffed. "He's just trying to spook us, and I've never seen anyone more prone to fright."

"I agree," Mademoiselle said, "but Martin seems to be genuinely shaken. Perhaps he's just more sensitive to the unknown."
