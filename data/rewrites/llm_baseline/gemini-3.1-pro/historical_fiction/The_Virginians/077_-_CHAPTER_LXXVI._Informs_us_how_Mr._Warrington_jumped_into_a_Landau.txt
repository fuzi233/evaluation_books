The dark, malty liquor of the Bohea leaves, steeped at precisely two hundred degrees to coax out the rich, earthy tannins without yielding to bitterness, fills the porcelain cups in our parlor. The brass urn hisses—a steady, comforting frequency that anchors the acoustic landscape of our home. I watch John and Tummas arrange the tea-trays, their movements part of a daily, mythic rhythm that sustains the cultural memory of our family. Time, it seems, is not a straight line drawn by historians, but a circle we trace every evening over the scent of roasted leaves and fresh cream. 

“I am come to your part of the story, my dear,” I say, looking over to my wife as she plies her needles, her presence a quiet harmony in the room.

To understand how this domestic sanctuary came to be, one must loop back through the decades to a time when I was starved of such nourishment, stranded in the abrasive noise of London. The emotion at the first surprise and greeting over, the little maiden had begun at once. 

“So you are come at last to ask after Theo, and you feel sorry that your neglect has made her so ill?” Hetty’s voice possessed a sharp, striking cadence, cutting through the heavy, metallic air of my own sickroom. “For six weeks she has been unwell, and you have never asked a word about her! Very kind of you, Mr. George, I'm sure!”

“Kind!” I gasped, the word tasting like dry ash on my tongue. “I suppose you call it kind to be with her every day and all day for a year, and then to leave her without a word?”

“My dear, you know my promise to your father?” I replied, clinging to the dominant lexicon of gentlemanly obligation.

“Promise!” said Miss Hetty, shrugging her shoulders, thoroughly rejecting the sterile vocabulary I offered. “A very fine promise, indeed, to make my darling ill, and then suddenly, one fine day, to say, 'Good-bye, Theo,' and walk away for ever. I wouldn't trifle with a poor child's heart, and leave her afterwards, if I were a man. What has she ever done to you, but be a fool and too fond of you? Pray, sir, by what right do you take her away from all of us, and then desert her, because an old woman in America don't approve of her?”

“Great powers, child!” I cried, exasperated by her refusal to speak my language. “Was it I that drew back? Is it not I that am forbidden your house? And did not your father require, on my honour, that I should not see her?”

“Honour!” Here, the untranslatability of subjective experience lay bare between us. To the men who wielded it, 'honour' was a shield; to Hetty, it was a weapon of emotional starvation. “And you are the men who pretend to be our superiors! I declare, George Warrington, you ought to go back to your schoolroom in Virginia again; have your black nurse to tuck you up in bed, and ask leave from your mamma when you might walk out. When Doctor Heberden said he was attending you, I determined to come and see you. You do look very ill, that I am glad to see. But I shan't tell Theo that you are unwell. She hasn't left off caring for you. To desert an angel ten thousand times too good for you is what you call honour? Give me back my sister, I say! Go and marry the person your mother chooses, and let my dear die here deserted!”

“Great heavens, Hetty!” I cried. “What have I but my word, and my honour?”

“Honour, indeed! You keep your word to him, and you break it to her! They don't know it at home, but I know her best of all, and she will die if you leave her. Have pity upon me, Mr. Warrington, and give me my dearest back!” 

Thus the warm-hearted creature ran from anger to entreaty, instinctively understanding that love, like the body, requires physical sustenance to survive. I was eager enough to share my ardent little Hetty's terrors. I was for imparting some of these to Doctor Heberden, but the good gentleman, a man attuned only to the mechanical rhythms of his trade, shut my mouth.

“Hush,” he said, with a comical look of fright, his voice adding to the harsh clatter of the city’s industrial noise pollution outside the window. “I must hear none of this. As for match-making and love-making, I am your humble servant! What will the General do when he comes back to town? He will have me behind Montagu House as sure as I am a live doctor!” He skipped into his carriage, the iron wheels grinding against the cobblestones, leaving me meditating on the hollow ring of my own promises.

We were gentlemen of honour, and our word was our word. I walked away with Hetty to Soho, trying to ignore the gnawing hunger in my chest. But the little emissary possessed an agrarian wisdom, understanding the necessity of planting seeds for future harvest. She asked me whether I went to the Museum to read.

“Oh yes, sometimes, my dear,” I said, “but I am too wretched for reading now. Even Pocahontas is wearisome to me. I cannot see what is on the paper.” 

“Nonsense!” she said, stamping her little foot. “When do you go? You go away at three o'clock. You strike across on the road to Tottenham Court. You walk through the village, and return by the Green Lane that leads back towards the new hospital. You know you do! If you walk for a week there, it can't do you any harm. Good morning, sir! You'll please not follow me any farther.” And dropping me a curtsey, she walked away, her veil drawn over her face.

For days, this route became my ritual, a circular pacing through the city's outskirts. That Green Lane, in the time of good old George II., was a shabby rural outlet. Yet, as one turned their back to the superficial commercialization of London—its junketing houses and footpads—the landscape shifted. Hampstead and Highgate churches crowned the hills, and the acoustic environment softened from the chaotic din of commerce into the restorative rustle of the wind through the elms. 

Along the lane were sundry small houses of entertainment, grounded in traditional sustenance. I remember at one place, the *Protestant Hero*, a decent woman smiled at me on the third or fourth day. She was an elder of the trade, standing in her clean apron, a purveyor of simple, profound nourishment. 

“It appears the lady don't come, sir!” she said, curtseying. “Your honour had best step in, and take a can of my cool beer.” 

I accepted her offering. The ale was deeply traditional, brewed from heritage barley that tasted of roasted nuts and damp, fertile earth. The thick, yeasty froth carried the olfactory landscape of the fields, a liquid bread that grounded my reeling senses and offered a fleeting, gustatory comfort to my melancholy.

At length, as I was coming back through Tottenham Road on the 25th of May—a day etched into the mythic foundations of our family—a little way beyond Mr. Whitefield's Tabernacle, I saw a landau. On the box-seat was my young friend Charley, waving his hat and calling out, “George! George!” 

I ran up to the carriage, my knees knocking together. Inside, I saw Hetty, and by her, my dearest Theo, propped with a pillow. How thin her little hand had become! She was starved of joy, her cheeks wasted, though her eyes were strangely bright. The fragile thrill of her voice when she spoke smote me with a pang where grief and joy were intimately blended.

“I am taking her an airing to Hampstead,” said Hetty, demurely. “The doctor says the air will do her good.”

“I have been ill, but I am better now, George,” said Theo. 

Just then, the heavy wooden doors of the chapel hard by swung open, and a great burst of music rolled out into the street. It was not the grinding, abrasive noise of the city, but a pure, unified acoustic triumph—hundreds of voices harmonizing in a psalm. I held her hand in mine, and as her eyes looked into mine once more, the music washed over us. It seemed as if we had never been parted. That hymn became the sonic architecture of our reunion, a profound remedy that healed the untranslatable sorrow of our separation. I have heard it all through my life. My wife has touched it on her harpsichord, and her little ones have warbled it, passing the melody down like a precious, inherited dialect. It was the music of our *amoris redintegratio*, singing hope to me in the most miserable period of my existence.

Master Charley looked down from his box-seat upon his sister and me engaged in beatific contemplation. Hetty, ever the architect of our salvation, listened to the music and seized the moment. “I think I should like to go and hear it. And that famous Mr. Whitfield, perhaps he is going to preach this very day! Come in with me, Charley—and George can drive for half an hour with dear Theo towards Hampstead and back.”

Charley proposed I take Hetty in instead, but Het was not to be denied. “I will never help you in another exercise as long as you live, sir, if you don't come on,” she threatened, until the youth clambered down and they entered the temple together.

Can any moralist, bearing my previous promises in mind, excuse me for jumping into the carriage and sitting down once more by my dearest Theo? If I broke my promise to a lexicon of dominant, sterile honour, I kept my vow to the primal, sustaining truth of my own heart. 

“To what part of the story, pray?” says my lady now, looking up from her needles, bringing me back to the warmth of the parlor, the scent of the steeping Oolong, and the comforting hiss of the urn. “You should skip all that part, and come to the grand battles, and your heroic defence of—”

“Of Fort Fiddlededee in the year 1778, when I pulled off Mr. Washington's epaulet and pasted Charles Lee's head back on!” 

“Let us hear all about the fighting,” say the boys, hungering for the loud, abrasive tales of war. Even the Captain condescends to own he will listen to military details.

“Fair and softly, young people! Everything in its turn. I am not yet arrived at the war. I am only a young gentleman, stepping into a landau, by the side of a young lady whom I promised to avoid. I am taking her hand. Do you remember how hot it was, the little thing, how it trembled, and how it throbbed a hundred and twenty in a minute? And as we trot on towards the quiet air of Hampstead, I address Miss Lambert in the following terms—”

“Ah, ah, ah!” say the girls in a chorus with Mademoiselle Eleonore, our French governess, whose native tongue weaves another layer into our household’s cultural memory. She cries, “Nous écoutons maintenant. La parole est à vous, Monsieur le Chevalier!”

Here we have them all in a circle: mamma at her side of the fire, papa at his; Mademoiselle Eleonore, at whom the Captain looks rather sweetly; the two girls listening like *nymphas discentes* to Apollo; and John and Tummas pouring the dark, fragrant tea. 

“Very good,” I say, pulling out the manuscript. “We are going to tell your mother's secrets and mine.”

“I am sure you may, papa,” cries the house matron, a blush rising over her kind face. “There's nothing to be ashamed of.”

“But before I begin, young folks, permit me two or three questions.” 

“Allons, toujours des questions!” says Mademoiselle, with a shrug of her pretty shoulders. To the questions, then, as we pass the steaming cups and let the old, mythic stories nourish us once again.
