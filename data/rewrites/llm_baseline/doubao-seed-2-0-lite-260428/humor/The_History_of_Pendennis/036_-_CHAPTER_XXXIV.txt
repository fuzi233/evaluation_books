CHAPTER XXXIV. Which Hangs Around Fleet Street, And Ends With A Very Unexpected Sort Of Prison Break
Mrs. Shandon, who barely ever dabbled in business matters, had nagged her husband Captain Shandon into making one non-negotiable demand for the upcoming *Pall Mall Gazette*: that John Finucane, Esquire, of the Upper Temple, get the sub-editor gig. And the spirited publisher Bungay, utterly charmed by the Irishman’s relentless chaotic energy, handed the post right over.

Finucane more than earned it. He was so devoted to the Shandon clan that back in the old days when bailiffs were hunting the Captain for every debt under the sun, Shandon would hide out in Fin’s tiny chambers so often the sheriff’s officers eventually started leaving their overnight tea mugs on his staircase, not even bothering to loiter outside Shandon’s own front door anymore. Poor Mrs. Shandon would traipse over constantly to beg for help springing her beloved husband, and Fin fed her and little Mary a dozen free meals in those poky rooms, leaning over the balustrade after she left to make sure no Temple rogue bothered her—secretly half-hoping some lout would try to accost her, just so he could play the dashing hero and break the rascal’s nose. So when the sub-editor job was locked in, making him her husband’s right-hand man on the paper, Mrs. Shandon was over the moon.

Fin would have happily sat chattering with the Shandons till the prison curfew, he’d even sat through little Mary’s bedtime prayers dozens of times, adding an enthusiastic Amen (devout Catholic though he was) whenever she asked God to bless her papa. But he had a very important dinner scheduled with Mr. Bungay to hash out the paper’s details, so he headed off at six sharp, showing up at Fleet Prison bright and early the next day decked out in his very best cheap, blindingly colourful new clothes, a brand new pair of two-shilling gloves on his hands, and his full week’s salary (four pounds two shillings) still almost entirely untouched.

The night before, he’d “cut his mutton” as Bungay called it, at Dick’s Coffee House with Bungay and his literary right-hand man Trotter, blowing them away with his master plan for the Gazette. He knew exactly who to hire to cover markets, horse races and boxing matches, church news, and all the silly fashionable gossip about fancy parties—he was, as Shandon always joked, one of the best sub-editors in all of London, who knew every press hack’s weekly wage and a hundred clever little tricks to save a new publisher every penny he could. His rapid-fire scribbled calculations on the coffee house napkin left slow-on-the-uptake Bungay so dazzled he told Trotter later, “That Irish bloke? Clever as a barrel full of monkeys.”

With Bungay thoroughly impressed, Fin made his big pitch: get Shandon out of prison. He knew down to the penny exactly how much the Captain owed to the Fleet prison authorities, and argued Shandon would write twice as fast if he wasn’t stuck in a cell—though Bungay pushed back hard on that one. “Nah, when the Captain’s locked up? You know exactly where to find him. Let him walk free, and you’ll never track the man down, he’s off gallivanting at some tavern three counties away before you can blink.” But Fin painted such a vivid, sad picture of Mrs. Shandon pining away and little Mary getting sick in the grim prison air that Bungay caved, and promised if Mrs. Shandon came to see him the next morning, he’d sort it out. By the time the second round of brandy and water was done, Bungay wouldn’t even let Fin pay the bill, waving the waiter over to settle the tab himself.

So Fin showed up at the Fleet Saturday morning with his full pay packet still in his pocket, winked so broadly at Mrs. Shandon she instantly knew good news was afoot. He offered to take her and little Mary out for some fresh air, and the kid screamed with joy—Fin always had a newspaper free pass to some puppet show or toy shop tucked in his coat pocket, and never showed up without a sweet for her. “You sure you want to leave your dad?” Mrs Shandon said. “He’s got a terrible headache this morning, I convinced him to stay in bed.” Shandon waved them off cheerfully, flipping open his copy of Burton’s Anatomy for the Greek and Latin quotes he loved dropping in his articles, “Go on, the pair of you. Jack, keep ’em safe. Hand me that book and leave me to my writing, you lot.”

So Fin offered Mrs. Shandon his arm, and Mary skipped all the way out of the prison gates, heading the short walk to Paternoster Row and Bungay’s shop. They got there just as Mrs. Bungay was waddling in the private entrance, clutching a paper parcel of biscuits and a red-bound book of her butcher’s accounts. She was dressed like a walking fireworks display: a shot-silk dress flaming red and purple, a bright yellow shawl, red flowers sticking out of her bonnet, and a light blue parasol so vivid it hurt your eyes a little. Faded black watered silk or no, Mrs. Shandon still looked every inch a lady, and the two women curtsied awkwardly at each other, like two birds sizing each other up on a fence.

“Hope you’re well, ma’am?” Mrs. Bungay rumbled.
“Lovely day out, isn’t it?” Mrs. Shandon stammered, explaining she’d come to speak to her husband’s employer on business.
“Before you go to the counting house, why don’t you leave the little girl with me?” Mrs. Bungay boomed, holding one fat finger out towards Mary like she was offering a cat a treat. The kid hid her face in her mom’s skirt and wailed that she wasn’t going anywhere, until Mrs. Bungay opened her parcel to show off fancy buttery biscuits, the kind her husband loved after a glass of port. Little Mary followed her inside, the lot of them heading up to the drawing room, and soon enough the kid was chattering away happily, munching biscuits.

Downstairs in the counting house, Fin got the shock of his life: Bungay was a total hardass. He’d gotten a full hour’s lecture from his wife the night before when he got home, she’d ranted at him till his ears burned: Shandon was a useless no-good layabout, no amount of money would ever fix him, the Pall Mall Gazette was going to be a total money pit just like the rival paper run by her brother “over the way”, leave the Captain locked up, it was the only place he ever got any work done. Fin pleaded, begged, promised they’d hit 10,000 copies a week, but Bungay wouldn’t budge an inch.

What Fin couldn’t pull off with a hundred clever arguments upstairs in the drawing room, Mrs. Shandon and little Mary did without even trying. Mrs. Shandon, all soft-spoken sweetness, told Mrs. Bungay her whole sad story: how they’d had to send the other two kids off to boarding school because the prison was no place to raise them, how the Captain was such a kind, brilliant man wasted locked in a cell. The tough old Lady Macbeth act melted right off Mrs. Bungay’s face, and she declared she was going down to sort her husband out immediately—everyone in the Bungay household knew that when the missus said “speak”, Mr. Bungay said “yes ma’am” instantly.

She told Fin to head up to the drawing room, then turned to Bungay and laid down the law. “That Captain Shandon’s an Irishman, and I’ve always said I can’t stand the lot of ’em. But his wife’s a proper lady, a clergyman’s daughter, just like my own mum’s side of the family from the West Country. And that little girl? She’s the spitting image of our poor little Bessy, the baby we lost eighteen years ago when we were just tiny booksellers in a country town.”

Bungay’s eyes went soft, he wiped a tear off his wife’s cheek, and caved instantly. “Alright, alright! Fin says 65 quid will get him out the door, we’ll advance that, and deduct it slow from his wages. When that little mite said ‘why won’t you take Par out of prizn?’ I almost cried, Flora, honest I did.”

The couple trundled upstairs to make the offer official, and Fin went absolutely feral with joy. “Bedad Bungay, you’re an absolute legend!” he roared in his thickest brogue, shaking the publisher’s hand so hard it ached, tossing little Mary three feet in the air yelling they’d make the *Pall Mall Gazette* the most famous paper in London. Mrs. Bungay insisted they all pile into her one-horse carriage for the ride over to the creditors, little Mary squeezed tight between the two ladies, Fin perched happily on the back seat, as they drove past the rival Bacon & Co shop windows with Mrs. Bungay staring daggers at the competition.

Left alone in his counting house, Bungay shook his head, muttering, “It won’t make the Captain work any better, that’s for sure, but women? They remember every single tiny sad little thing from 18 years ago, and you can’t argue with that.”

And we’re happy to report that Mrs. Shandon handled the creditors like a total pro—she’d talked them down from impossible bills with nothing but tears and kind words a hundred times before, so offering them ten shillings in the pound to settle the debt got them all to agree instantly. That coming Sunday would be the last one Captain Shandon spent in the Fleet Prison, for a good long while at least.
