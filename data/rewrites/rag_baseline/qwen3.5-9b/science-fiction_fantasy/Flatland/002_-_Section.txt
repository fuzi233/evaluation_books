The rain lashed against the leaded glass of the library windows, a rhythmic drumming that felt, to A. Square, like the frantic, indifferent heartbeat of a universe that refused to acknowledge his existence. He sat at his mahogany desk, the heavy scent of beeswax and damp wool filling the room, his gaze fixed upon the thick sheaf of letters that had arrived by the morning post. They were from the Editor of the *Universal Colour Bill*, his sprawling, confident script detailing the latest edicts regarding the suppression of chromatic sedition.

Square sighed, tracing the edge of the parchment with a silver letter-opener. The Editor was a man of undeniable geometric erudition, yet his decrees were suffocated by the very rigid, two-dimensional complacency Square so deeply despised. He wrote to Square of the Nature of Flatland, of the climate where the sun rises directly overhead casting shadows that define social status, and of the inhabitants who move with such predictable precision that deviation is treated as a crime. He positioned himself as the benevolent instructor to Square's "undefeated resolution to admire only the right angles," dismissing any notion that the world might possess a third dimension beyond their shared plane.

"The right angles," Square murmured aloud, the words tasting like ash. What the Editor meant, of course, was the established canon of linear existence—the laws elevated by centuries of wealthy, privileged lines who saw their own reflections in the exploits of triangles and the musings of acute-angled philosophers.

He turned to the Editor's pages concerning the Women. The Editor excused their dangerous fluidity, arguing that men must be pardoned the "instability of the feminine curve" so long as they remained within the bounds of the Great Circle. He painted a nostalgic picture of a "lusty old militant world" of polygons, entirely blind to the fact that this orderly Flatland was built upon the bruised backs of those who dared to suggest a fourth side, and the indentured silence of the irregular figures who lived on the fringes of society. He dismissed the systemic inequalities that left women like the Editor's own wife with no recourse but to forgive the betrayals of their husbands, who viewed their wives' curves as mere obstacles to be smoothed over with polite indifference.

Square looked up at the window, where the shadow of a passing triangle stretched across the lawn, straight and angular, a perfect testament to the tyranny of the visible. The geometry of his life was anchored by these rigid projections, a velvet stage where three dimensions were an impossible fantasy sold to children by priests who claimed to speak for the Divine. But as the rain continued its relentless drumming, Square felt a tremor in his soul, a whisper of something deeper than the sum of his sides, urging him to look beyond the horizon of his own world.
