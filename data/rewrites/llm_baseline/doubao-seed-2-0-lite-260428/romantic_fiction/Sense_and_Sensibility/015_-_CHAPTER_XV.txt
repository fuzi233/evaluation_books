The very next day, Mrs. Dashwood set off to pay her promised call on Lady Middleton, accompanied by Elinor and Margaret. Marianne begged off at the last minute, pleading a stack of trivial unfinished chores she could not bear to leave half-done, and her mother smiled knowingly, perfectly certain Willoughby had promised the night before to call on Marianne while the rest of the household was out. No one pressed her to come.

When they trundled back up the cottage drive hours later, they spotted Willoughby’s sleek curricle and his small liveried servant waiting by the door, and Mrs. Dashwood’s face lit up—her hunch, she thought, had been exactly right. But the second they stepped across the threshold, something was very wrong. Marianne came tearing out of the parlor, cheeks streaked with fresh tears, handkerchief pressed tight to her eyes, so lost in her own grief she did not even notice her family. She fled up the stairs three at a time without a word.

Shocked and afraid, the rest of them hurried into the parlor she had just left, to find Willoughby leaning heavy against the mantelpiece, his back turned to the room, like he was fighting to steady himself enough to face them. He spun around when he heard them enter, and his face was raw, red-rimmed, crumpled with the same agony that had just overtaken Marianne.
“Is she ill?” Mrs. Dashwood cried, stepping forward at once.
“I hope not,” he said, forcing a wobbly, unnatural smile, trying to sound light when every line of his body screamed distress. “It is I who ought to be the one unwell. I have just had the cruellest possible disappointment.”
“A disappointment?”
“Mrs. Smith, this morning, has chosen to exercise the right her wealth gives her over her poor dependent cousin. She has dispatched me on urgent business to London. I have only just received my orders, said my goodbyes at Allenham, and came straight here to say farewell to all of you.”
“London? You are leaving this morning?”
“Almost this very instant.”
“How terrible. But surely Mrs. Smith will not keep you away from us long?”
He flushed hot, his gaze darting away. “You are far too kind. But I have no intention of coming back to Devonshire any time soon. My visits to Mrs. Smith’s estate never last less than twelve months apart.”
“Is Mrs. Smith your only friend? Is Allenham the only house in this whole neighborhood you are welcome? Shame on you, Willoughby—you do not need an invitation to Barton Cottage.”
His color deepened, and he stared fixed at the floor, voice thick. “You are far too good to me.”

Mrs. Dashwood glanced over at Elinor, bewildered, and Elinor was equally stunned. No one spoke for long, the room heavy with unspoken confusion.
“My dear Willoughby,” Mrs. Dashwood said at last, “you will always have a home at Barton Cottage, whenever you choose to come. I will not press you to return before you are sure it will not offend Mrs. Smith— I trust your judgment, and I would never doubt that you want to come back.”
“My current circumstances,” Willoughby stammered, so flustered he could barely get the words out, “are such that I—I dare not flatter myself I can take you up on that.” He trailed off, unable to finish. Mrs. Dashwood was too shocked to speak, and another heavy silence fell.
“It is foolish to linger here any longer,” he said at last, with a hollow little smile. “I will not torment myself staying around friends whose company I cannot bear to leave right now.”
He said a hasty, stiff goodbye to all of them and fled the room. They watched him climb into his curricle, snap the reins, and in less than a minute he was gone over the hill. Mrs. Dashwood could not speak for the weight of her fear and grief, and slipped off to her own room to cry alone.

Elinor was just as uneasy as her mother. She turned the whole strange scene over in her head, tight with doubt. Willoughby’s odd, distant goodbye, his obvious embarrassment, the fake cheer he had forced, and worst of all, the way he would not accept their open invitation—none of it was like the Willoughby they had known, none of it was how a man in love with Marianne would act. One moment she feared he had never meant to make any serious, honest commitment to her sister, the next she wondered if they had fought bitterly before they got home. And yet Marianne loved him so completely, so fiercely, that a real quarrel felt almost impossible. No matter what had happened between them, her sister’s grief was undeniable, and Elinor’s heart ached for the violent sorrow she knew Marianne was clinging to upstairs, letting it wash over her entirely, no urge to hide or control it.

Half an hour later, Mrs. Dashwood came back down, eyes red but face determined, and sat down to pick up her sewing.
“Our dear Willoughby is miles away from Barton by now, Elinor,” she said softly. “And he is carrying a very heavy heart, I know.”
“It is all so strange,” Elinor said. “He left in an instant. Only last night he was here, happy, warm, so openly affectionate to Marianne. And now, with barely ten minutes warning, he is gone, saying he will not come back for a year! He was not himself, he did not speak like himself, you saw it too. What could have happened? Did they quarrel? Why else would he refuse your invitation so strangely?”
“It was not that he did not want to accept it, Elinor, I saw that clear as day. He was not allowed to. I have thought it all over, and I have an explanation that makes perfect sense.”
“You do?”
“Of course. Mrs. Smith has guessed how he feels about Marianne, and disapproves. She probably has plans for him to marry some rich heiress of her own choosing, so she has invented this fake London errand to get him out of Devonshire and separate them. He knows she will never approve of the match, so he cannot tell her he and Marianne are engaged, and as her poor dependent cousin, he has no choice but to obey her, and stay away for a while. You will say it might not be true, I know, since you love to doubt where you can, but I will not let you talk me out of trusting this. Can you think of any other explanation that makes as much sense?”
“None, I admit,” Elinor said. “I will hope you are right. But it would have been so much more like Willoughby to tell us the truth plainly. Secrecy might make sense, but I still cannot help wondering why he would hide it from us.”
“You cannot blame him for stepping out of his usual character when he has no other choice. So you agree he deserves the benefit of the doubt? That makes me happy—he is acquitted of all blame, then.”
“Not entirely. It makes sense that they would hide their engagement from Mrs. Smith, if they are engaged. But there is no reason to hide it from us.”
“Hide it from us? My dear child, you cannot possibly think they have been secretive! Every day you have looked at them and thought they were too open, too careless with their affection for anyone watching. His behavior for the last fortnight has declared a hundred times over that he loves Marianne, that he sees her as his future wife, that he thinks of all of us as family. Has he not asked for my consent with every look, every kind attentive gesture? How can you possibly doubt they are engaged? Why would Willoughby, who must know how deeply Marianne loves him, leave her for months at a time without saying a single word to confirm his feelings?”
“Every single thing points to an engagement except one,” Elinor said. “Neither of them has ever said a single word about it to any of us. And that one fact weighs heavier than all the rest, for me.”
“You are far too hard on him,” Mrs. Dashwood laughed, half exasperated. “Willoughby is an honorable, kind man. No one in the whole county has ever spoken a bad word about him. Why would you assume he is deceitful?”
“I hope he is not, I believe he is not,” Elinor cried. “I love him, truly, and the thought of doubting his honor hurts me as much as it hurts you. It was not my choice, I was just so shocked by how different he acted this morning. But I will not encourage those doubts. He had just said goodbye to Marianne, and saw her break down in tears right before we walked in. He was embarrassed, caught between not wanting to anger Mrs. Smith and not wanting to seem rude to us. It is no wonder he was flustered.”
“That is exactly it,” her mother said, relieved. “Their marriage will be a long way off, with his situation so dependent on Mrs. Smith, so a little secrecy right now is the only sensible choice.”

Margaret walked into the room just then, and Elinor fell quiet, turning all her mother’s words over in her head, letting her old unease soften, hoping with all her heart she was right.

Marianne did not appear again until dinner time. She walked silently to her place at the table, eyes puffy and swollen, and could not bring herself to meet any of her family’s gazes. She would not eat a bite, would not say a word, and when her mother reached across the table to squeeze her hand gently, her last little bit of composure shattered. She burst into loud, unrestrained sobs and ran straight back out of the room. That raw, unmanageable grief lasted the whole rest of the evening. Marianne had no desire to control her feelings, no will to calm herself down. The smallest, most accidental mention of anything that even tangentially reminded her of Willoughby broke her all over again, and no matter how carefully her family watched their words, it was impossible to avoid every single thing tied to the man she loved more than life itself.
