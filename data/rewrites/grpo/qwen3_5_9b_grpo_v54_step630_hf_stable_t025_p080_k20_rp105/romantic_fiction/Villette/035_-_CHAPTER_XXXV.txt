"Oubliez les Professeurs," Madame Beck advised, her voice carrying the weight of a seasoned educator. She was a wise woman, yet in that moment, her wisdom faltered. That night, she should have left me in a state of calm indifference, isolated in my own quiet estimation, untethered from the second person I was destined to forget. Instead, she had ignited a fire I could not extinguish.

How could they expect me to forget him? The "wiseheads" had devised a cunning trap. They unveiled his goodness, transforming my dear little man into a stainless hero. They prattled endlessly about his capacity for love, leaving me with no prior evidence to contradict their narrative. Before this day, I had only known him as jealous and suspicious, marked by tendernesses that arrived like warm air and vanished like early dew, dried instantly by the heat of his irritabilities. But Père Silas and Madame Beck, working in concert, had opened the sacred chamber of his heart. They revealed a grand, singular love—a child of southern youth, born strong and perfect, laughing at death itself, despising the corruption of matter, and clinging to the immortal spirit. They showed me a fidelity proven by the consecration of his energies to an unselfish purpose, attested by limitless sacrifice. For those once dear to her, he had laid down vengeance and taken up a cross.

As for Justine Marie, I knew her well enough. She was one of Madame Beck's phlegmatics: pale, slow, inert, yet kind-natured and neutral. If she wore angels' wings, it was merely the poet-fancy of others; if her forehead shone with a halo, it was the reflection of someone else's fire. Was I to be frightened by her? Was the image of a pale, dead nun to stand as an eternal barrier between me and the man I loved? What of the charities that consumed his worldly goods? What of his heart sworn to virginity?

Madame Beck and Père Silas, you should not have suggested these questions. They became the deepest puzzle, the strongest obstruction, and the keenest stimulus I had ever known. For a week of nights and days, I fell asleep only to wake upon these two haunting inquiries. In the entire world, there was no answer to them except where a dark little man stood, sat, or lectured beneath the brim of a *bonnet grec* and within the frayed girth of a soot-stained paletot.

After my visit to the Rue des Mages, I desperately wanted to see him again. Knowing what I now knew, I felt his countenance would offer a page more lucid, more interesting than ever. I longed to trace in his features the imprint of that primitive devotion, the signs of that half-knightly, half-saintly chivalry the priest had imputed to his nature. He had become my Christian hero, and I wanted to view him through that lens.

Opportunity did not wait. My new impressions underwent their test the very next day. Yes, I was granted an interview with my "Christian hero"—an encounter neither heroic nor biblical, but lively in its own chaotic way.

About three in the afternoon, the peace of the first *classe*, safely established under the serene sway of Madame Beck, who was personally delivering one of her orderly lessons, suffered a sudden fracture. A wild *paletot* burst in.

I was the quietest soul in the room, eased of responsibility by Madame Beck's presence, soothed by her uniform tones, and edified by her clear exposition. I sat bent over my desk, drawing—copying an elaborate line engraving with tedious precision, for that was my practical notion of art. Strangely, I took great pleasure in the labor, producing curiously finical Chinese facsimiles of steel plates, things I valued highly in those days.

Then, disaster struck. My drawing, my pencils, my precious copy were gathered into one crushed handful and perished from my sight. I appeared to be shaken out of my chair like a withered nutmeg emptied from a spice box. My chair and desk were seized by the wild *paletot*, one under each sleeve, and borne away. In a second, I followed the furniture; in two minutes, we were fixed in the center of the *grand salle*—a vast adjoining room seldom used save for dancing and choral singing—fixed with an emphasis that seemed to prohibit any hope of movement.

Having partially collected my scattered wits, I found myself in the presence of two men, gentlemen, I suppose. One was dark, the other light; one possessed a stiff, half-military air in a braided *surtout*, while the other bore the careless aspect of a student or artist. Both flourished in the full magnificence of moustaches, whiskers, and imperial. M. Emanuel stood apart, his countenance and eyes expressing strong choler, holding forth his hand in a tribune gesture.

"Mademoiselle," he said, "your business is to prove to these gentlemen that I am no liar. You will answer, to the best of your ability, such questions as they shall put. You will also write on such a theme as they shall select. In their eyes, it appears, I hold the position of an unprincipled impostor. I write essays and, with deliberate forgery, sign my pupils' names, boasting of them as my work. You will disprove this charge."

*Grand ciel!* Here was the show-trial, so long evaded, coming upon me like a thunderclap. These two fine, braided, mustachioed, sneering personages were none other than the dandy professors of the college—Messieurs Boissec and Rochemorte—a pair of cold-blooded fops and pedants, skeptics, and scoffers. It seemed M. Paul had rashly exhibited something I had written—something he had never praised or even mentioned in my hearing, and which I deemed forgotten. The essay was not remarkable; it only *seemed* so compared to the average productions of foreign schoolgirls. In an English establishment, it would have passed unnoticed. Messieurs Boissec and Rochemorte had deemed it necessary to question its genuineness and insinuate a cheat. Now, I was to bear witness to the truth and endure the torture of their examination.

A memorable scene ensued.

They began with classics. A dead blank. They moved on to French history. I hardly knew Mérovée from Pharamond. They tried me in various sciences, receiving only a shake of the head and an unchanging "Je n'en sais rien."

After an expressive pause, they proceeded to matters of general information, broaching subjects I knew well and had often reflected upon. M. Emanuel, who had hitherto stood looking on, dark as the winter solstice, brightened somewhat; he thought I would now show myself no fool.

He learned his error. Though answers surged up fast, my mind filling like a rising well, ideas were there but not words. I either could not, or would not, speak—I am not sure which. Partly, my nerves were wrong; partly, my humor was crossed.

I heard one of my examiners, the one in the braided *surtout*, whisper to his co-professor, "Est-elle donc idiote?"

"Yes," I thought, "an idiot she is, and always will be, for such as you."

But I suffered—cruelly. I saw the damp gather on M. Paul's brow, and his eye spoke a passionate yet sad reproach. He would not believe in my total lack of popular cleverness; he thought I *could* be prompt if I *would*.

Finally, to relieve him, the professors, and myself, I stammered out: "Gentlemen, you had better let me go; you will get no good of me; as you say, I am an idiot."

I wish I could have spoken with calm and dignity, or that my sense had sufficed to make me hold my tongue. That traitor tongue tripped and faltered. Beholding the judges cast a hard look of triumph on M. Emanuel and hearing the distressed tremor of my own voice, I burst out in a fit of choking tears. The emotion was far more anger than grief; had I been a man and strong, I could have challenged that pair on the spot—but it was emotion, and I would rather have been scourged than betray it.

The incapables! Could they not see the crude hand of a novice in that composition they called a forgery? The subject was classical. When M. Paul dictated the theme, I heard it for the first time; the matter was new to me, and I had no material for its treatment. But I had gone to the library, read up the facts, laboriously constructed a skeleton from the dry bones of the real, and then clothed them, trying to breathe life into them. With me, it was a difficult and anxious time until my facts were found, selected, and properly jointed. I could not rest from research until I was satisfied of correct anatomy. The strength of my inward repugnance to the idea of flaw or falsity sometimes enabled me to shun egregious blunders, but the knowledge was not there in my head, ready and mellow. It had not been sown in spring, grown in summer, harvested in autumn, and garnered through winter. Whatever I wanted, I had to go out and gather fresh; glean wild herbs into my lap and shred them green into the pot. Messieurs Boissec and Rochemorte did not perceive this. They mistook my work for that of a ripe scholar.

They would not let me go. I must sit down and write before them. As I dipped my pen in the ink with a shaking hand and surveyed the white paper with eyes half-blinded and overflowing, one of my judges began to mince an apology for the pain he caused.

"Nous agissons dans l'intérêt de la vérité. Nous ne voulons pas vous blesser," he said.

Scorn gave me nerve. I answered only, "Dictate, Monsieur."

Rochemorte named the theme: "Human Justice."

*Human Justice!* What was I to make of it? A blank, cold abstraction, unsuggestive of any inspiring idea. There stood M. Emanuel, sad as Saul and stern as Joab, while his accusers triumphed.

At these two, I looked. I was gathering my courage to tell them I would neither write nor speak another word for their satisfaction, that their theme did not suit, nor their presence inspire me, and that whoever threw the shadow of a doubt on M. Emanuel's honor outraged the truth they claimed to champion. I *meant* to utter all this when suddenly, a light darted across my memory.

Those two faces looking out from the forest of long hair, moustache, and whisker—those two cold yet bold, trustless yet presumptuous visages—were the very same that, projected in full gaslight from behind the pillars of a portico, had half frightened me to death on the night of my desolate arrival in Villette. These, I felt morally certain, were the very heroes who had driven a friendless foreigner beyond her reckoning and her strength, chasing her breathless over a whole quarter of the town.

"Pious mentors!" I thought. "Pure guides for youth! If 'Human Justice' were what she ought to be, you two would scarce hold your present post, or enjoy your present credit."

An idea seized me, and I fell to work. "Human Justice" rushed before me in a novel guise: a red, random beldame with arms akimbo. I saw her in her house, the den of confusion: servants called to her for orders she did not give; beggars stood at her door starving unnoticed; a swarm of sick and quarrelsome children crawled round her feet, yelling appeals for notice and redress. The honest woman cared for none of these things. She had a warm seat by the fire, her own solace in a short black pipe and a bottle of Mrs. Sweeny's soothing syrup. She smoked and sipped, enjoying her paradise. Whenever a cry of the suffering souls pierced her ears too keenly, my jolly dame seized the poker or the hearth-brush. If the offender was weak, wronged, and sickly, she effectively settled him. If he was strong, lively, and violent, she only menaced, then plunged her hand in her deep pouch and flung a liberal shower of sugar-plums.

Such was the sketch of "Human Justice," scratched hurriedly on paper and placed at the service of Messrs. Boissec and Rochemorte. M. Emanuel read it over my shoulder. Waiting no comment, I curtsied to the trio and withdrew.

After school that day, M. Paul and I met again. Of course, the meeting did not run smoothly at first; there was a crow to pluck with him. That forced examination could not be immediately digested. A crabbed dialogue terminated in my being called "une petite moqueuse et sans-cœur," and in Monsieur's temporary departure.

Not wishing him to go quite away, only desiring he should feel that such a transport as he had given way to could not be indulged with perfect impunity, I was not sorry to see him soon after gardening in the *berceau*. He approached the glass door; I drew near also. We spoke of some flowers growing round it. By-and-by, Monsieur laid down his spade; by-and-by, he recommenced conversation, passed to other subjects, and finally touched a point of interest.

Conscious that his proceeding of that day was specially open to a charge of extravagance, M. Paul half apologized; he half regretted, too, the fitfulness of his moods, yet he hinted that some allowance ought to be made for him. "But," said he, "I can hardly expect it at your hands, Miss Lucy; you know neither me, nor my position, nor my history."
