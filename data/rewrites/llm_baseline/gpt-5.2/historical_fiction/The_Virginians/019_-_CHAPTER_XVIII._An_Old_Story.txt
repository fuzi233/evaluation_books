When my Lady Castlewood rose at last, gathering up her silks with that quick, practised hand which never seemed to fumble, and passed out of the saloon with her son and daughter, my Lord went his own way by another door, as if the house itself had been built to spare him the trouble of explanations. The room, left behind, held its great hearth like a patient mouth—blackened within, gilt without—with the portraits above it staring down as gravely as judges who never once have to dine on what they decree.

Lady Maria had sat all this while at her tambour-frame, the little ring of wood and tight-stretched linen on her lap, her needle dipping and drawing with a steady, pious industry. The flowers under her hand—violets and jonquils—were so innocent, so fragrantly pastoral in their very name, that they might have been plucked from the hedge-row by a child and pinned to a dairy-maid’s bodice. Yet the eyes that had pretended to live only among stitched petals lifted now, slowly, from their small garden to the living figure by the chimney.

Harry Warrington stood under an ancestral portrait, as if he had sought protection from a dead gentleman in full-bottomed wig and cuirass. The fire had warmed him; something else had set him alight. The heat climbed even to his ears. If blushes were herbs to be gathered, he had harvested them by the handful and worn them unashamed.

“Why did you refuse to go with our aunt, cousin?” Lady Maria asked, her tone demure as her needlework.

“Because your ladyship bade me stay,” Harry answered, and if his voice shook, it was not from fear.

“I bid you stay! La, child! What one says in fun, you take in earnest!” She bent again to her work, the needle pricking through the linen with small, decisive bites. “Are all you Virginian gentlemen so obsequious as to fancy every idle word a lady says is a command? Virginia must be a pleasant country for our sex if it be so!”

“You said—when—when we walked on the terrace two nights since—O heaven!” Harry broke out, the words tumbling as if his heart had tripped him. He stepped nearer, dragging the air with him, as if he could pull courage up by the roots.

“Ah, that sweet night, cousin!” said the tambour-frame, and the thread slipped through her fingers with the ease of long practice.

“When you gave me this rose—from your own neck,” Harry roared with the dreadful sincerity of the young; and he plunged his hand into his waistcoat and drew out, like a relic from a shrine, a crumpled thing—once a rose, now a brown and flattened vegetable, still faintly perfumed when it was warmed between his fingers. “Which I will never part with—no, by heavens, whilst this heart continues to beat! You said, ‘Harry, if your aunt asks you to go away, you will go; and if you go, you will forget me.’ Didn’t you say so?”

“All men forget,” Lady Maria replied, and sighed as prettily as her needle might have done. “In this cold selfish country they may, cousin.”

“Not in ours,” cried Harry, as though the very climate of Virginia would defend his constancy. “It went to my heart to say no to her, and she so kind to me, and the means of introducing me to—to—O heaven!” He advanced with a sudden fervour; a spaniel, asleep by the fender, ventured into his path. Harry’s boot struck it aside impatiently. The poor creature yelped and fled, its little protest swallowed by the thick carpet and the draught up the chimney.

“Look here, cousin! If you were to bid me jump out of yonder window, I should do it; or murder, I should do it.”

“La! but you need not squeeze one’s hand so, you silly child!” Maria said, and yet did not draw her hand away until she had been sure of its effect.

“I can’t help it—we are so in the south.” His fingers held hers with the pressure of a man who has never yet learned the politeness of restraint. “Where my heart is, I can’t help speaking my mind out, cousin—and you know where that heart is. Ever since that evening—that—O heaven! I tell you I have hardly slept since. I want to do something—to distinguish myself—to be ever so great. I wish there was giants, Maria, as I have read of in—in books, that I could go and fight ’em. I wish you was in distress, that I might help you somehow. I wish you wanted my blood, that I might spend every drop of it for you. And when you told me not to go with Madame Bernstein—”

“I tell thee, child? never.”

“I thought you told me.” He was not to be turned aside by a denial; he had already built the whole house of his devotion upon that one remembered sentence, and would not now pull it down because she declared the foundation imaginary. “You said you knew I preferred my aunt to my cousin; and I said then what I say now: ‘Incomparable Maria! I prefer thee to all the women in the world and all the angels in Paradise—and I would go anywhere, were it to dungeons, if you ordered me!’ And do you think I would not stay anywhere, when you only desired that I should be near you?”

“Men always talk in that way—that is—that is, I have heard so,” she answered, recovering herself with a little flurried dignity. “For what should a country-bred woman know about you creatures? When you are near us, they say you are all raptures and flames and promises and I don’t know what; when you are away, you forget all about us.”

“But I think I never want to go away as long as I live,” he groaned, as if the very notion bruised him. “I have tired of many things. Not books and that—I never cared for study much—but games and sports I used to be fond of when I was a boy. Before I saw you, it was to be a soldier I most desired. I tore my hair with rage when my poor dear brother went away instead of me on that expedition in which we lost him. But now I only care for one thing in the world, and you know what that is.”

“You silly child! Don’t you know I am almost old enough to be—”

“I know—I know! but what is that to me?” His honesty, like new wine, ran foaming over every sensible boundary. “Hasn’t your br—well, never mind who—some of ’em told me stories against you, and didn’t they show me the Family Bible where all your names are down, and the dates of your birth?”

“The cowards! Who did that?” cried Lady Maria, and in an instant the gentlewoman at her embroidery was gone, and something sharp and quick as vinegar flashed through her. “Dear Harry, tell me who did that? Was it my mother-in-law, the grasping, odious, abandoned, brazen harpy? Do you know all about her? How she married my father in his cups—the horrid hussey!—and—”

“Indeed it wasn’t Lady Castlewood,” Harry interposed, stunned to find what temper could live under such blue eyes.

“Then it was my aunt,” Maria continued, anger running on like a pot left unwatched. “A pretty moralist indeed! A bishop’s widow forsooth—and I should like to know whose widow before and afterwards. Why, Harry, she intrigues with the Pretender, and with the Court of Hanover, and I dare say would with the Court of Rome and the Sultan of Turkey if she had had the means. Do you know who her second husband was? A creature who—”

“But our aunt never spoke a word against you,” Harry broke in again, more and more amazed; for in Virginia he had seen anger, but not this sudden, polished fury that seemed to strike and glitter at once.

She stopped. In his open, enquiring face she fancied she read alarm—alarm not at what was said, but at the woman who could say it.

“Well, well! I am a fool,” she murmured, letting the heat sink and the smile return like icing laid back over a cake. “I want thee to think well of me, Harry.”

Somehow her hand went out again—how it escaped the tambour-frame, how it found his, who can tell?—and it was seized, and kissed, and pressed with the devout eagerness of youth.

“Angel!” he cried, staring into her face with those candid eyes that had never yet learned to read a mask as a mask.

Her eyes—those older orbs that could be cold as water when she chose and warm as wine when she wished—took fire under his gaze. They shone, and he plunged into them as if he were diving into a clear pool in summer, not seeing what lies under some calm surfaces: the sunk wreckage, the dropped treasures, the bones and broken promises that make a bed in such depths. A dog, Aesop tells us, once saw a meat-bone in the water and snapped at its reflection, losing what he held. So it is with more than dogs. There are quiet waters that smile and swallow. Have a care, you eager lads, how you lap.

Yet to Harry, the song was not stale; the voice did not crack; the witch did not seem old. He did not see the paint that could be laid on and washed off; he did not imagine the ringlets might be put away in a box; he heard only the lilting call that makes the heart step forward of its own accord.

And indeed, if any person who has ever read a tale, or lived one, had seen him draw out that faded rose just now—once red as a cook’s fresh pimento, now browned like an herb dried too long by the kitchen rafters—he might have drifted into his own reverie, as the writer himself does when lovers whisper in an alley or shoot glances across a room. These things follow each other by a law as old as people: a meeting in the garden, a hand taken by the old curtain where everyone may peep through the rent, an early walk in delicious weather, a charitable errand to some cottage, a basket carried by the one who longs to be useful. Whole chapters might be brewed out of such small domestic ingredients: a half-crown, a jar of calves’-foot jelly, a book of prayers read aloud into a deaf ear. The dish is ever the same, only served on a different plate.

Maria liked power over the young Virginian; but she did not want Harry to quarrel with his aunt for her sake, nor Madame de Bernstein to grow angry with her. He was not Lord of Virginia yet—only the Prince, and a Queen may marry again and bear other Princes; and who could tell what laws governed inheritance in that far country? My lord her brother and she spoke no words together on this delicate matter. They did not need to. The Earl had a way of understanding without speaking; Maria, in her brother’s house and under his eye for so much of her life, had had her little ways, tricks, faults, and schemes read as plainly as the household accounts. He would check or aid her as seemed good to him, and never with a scene.

Thus, three days before, when Maria had been tripping toward the gate-house on her errand to poor old Goody Jenkins—ill with a sore knee in the village—and when Harry had happened, quite by chance, to be walking behind the elms on the green, my lord crossed the court with his dogs about him and his gardener in attendance. He called to his sister.

“Molly, you are going to see Goody Jenkins. You are a charitable soul, my dear. Give Gammer Jenkins this half-crown for me—unless our cousin Warrington has already given her money. A pleasant walk to you. Let her want for nothing.”

At supper that night, my lord questioned Mr. Warrington about the poor in Virginia, and by what means they were maintained, and what the labourers ate and how they lived; questions that sounded, in the mouth of so great a man, like benevolence, and also like instruction. Harry answered as well as he could, thinking of corn-meal and bacon, of fish from the river and game from the woods, and of how different were the sounds of want in a country where land lay open like a table spread too wide for all the guests to reach. The Earl wished that in the old country there were no more poor people than in the new; and he recommended Harry to visit people of every degree—high and low—in the country to look at agriculture, in the city at manufactures and municipal institutions. Harry listened modestly, with few words; Madame Bernstein, at her piquet with the chaplain, nodded approval, her cards clicking softly like little bones.

The next day Harry was in my lord’s justice-room; the next he was out for hours with him on the farm, where the earth smelled of turnings and dung and damp straw, and the rooks complained overhead with that hoarse, rural parliament which has out-talked many human ones. Coming home, my lord looked in upon a sick tenant; and on that day too, Lady Maria was out, reading good books, no doubt, to Goody Jenkins, though it is not likely Madame Bernstein ever once asked after her niece’s charity.

In due course Harry wrote home, as sons will, trying to turn his heart into respectable sentences and his emotion into dutiful ink.

“CASTLEWOOD, HAMPSHIRE, ENGLAND,
August 5, 1757.

“MY DEAR MOUNTAIN,—At first, as I wrote, I did not like Castlewood, nor my cousins there, very much. Now, I am used to their ways, and we begin to understand each other much better. With my duty to my mother, tell her, I hope, that considering her ladyship’s great kindness to me, Madam Esmond will be reconciled to her half-sister, the Baroness de Bernstein. The Baroness, you know, was my Grandmamma’s daughter by her first husband, Lord Castlewood (only Grandpapa really was the real lord); however, that was not his, that is, the other Lord Castlewood’s fault, you know, and he was very kind to Grandpapa, who always spoke most kindly of him to us as you know.

“Madame the Baroness Bernstein first married a clergyman, Reverend Mr. Tusher, who was so learned and good, and such a favourite of his Majesty, as was my aunt too, that he was made a Bishop. When he died, Our gracious King continued his friendship to my aunt; who married a Hanoverian nobleman, who occupied a post at the Court—and, I believe, left the Baroness very rich. My cousin, my Lord Castlewood, told me so much about her, and I am sure I have found from her the greatest kindness and affection.

“The (Dowiger) Countess Castlewood and my cousins Will and Lady Fanny have been described per last, that went by the Falmouth packet on the 20th ult. The ladies are not changed since then. Me and Cousin Will are very good friends. We have rode out a good deal. We have had some famous cocking matches at Hampton and Winton. My cousin is a sharp blade, but I think I have shown him that we in Virginia know a thing or two. Reverend Mr. Sampson, chaplain of the famaly, most excellent preacher, without any biggatry.

“The kindness of my cousin the Earl improves every day, and by next year’s ship I hope my mother will send his lordship some of our best roll tobacco (for tennants) and hamms. He is most charatable to the poor. His sister, Lady Maria, equally so. She sits for hours reading good books to the sick: she is most beloved in the village.”

“Nonsense!” said the lady to whom Harry submitted this precious manuscript, as if he were offering her a dish he had laboured over and feared to season too boldly. She had taken it from him with an air of indulgence, and as she read, the room seemed to fill not with the crackle of the hearth but with the imagined hush of a cottage where a deaf old woman sat by a scant fire, hearing nothing of the fine phrases pronounced over her.

“Why do you flatter me, cousin?” Maria asked, though her eye brightened as if she had tasted the praise.

“You are beloved in the village and out of it,” said Harry, with a knowing emphasis, “and I have flattered you, as you call it, a little more still, farther on.”

He read, or pointed, and she continued, smiling at each line that served her.

“There is a sick old woman there, whom Madam Esmond would like, a most raligious, good, old lady.

“Lady Maria goes very often to read to her; which, she says, gives her comfort. But though her Ladyship hath the sweetest voice, both in speaking and singeing (she plays the church organ, and singes there most beautifully), I cannot think Gammer Jenkins can have any comfort from it, being very deaf, by reason of her great age. She has her memory perfectly, however, and remembers when my honoured Grandmother Rachel Lady Castlewood lived here. She says, my Grandmother was the best woman in the whole world, gave her a cow when she was married, and cured her husband, Gaffer Jenkins, of the collects, which he used to have very bad. I suppose it was with the Pills and Drops which my honoured Mother put up in my boxes, when I left dear Virginia. Having never been ill since, have had no use for the pills. Gumbo hath, eating and drinking a great deal too much in the Servants’ Hall.

“The next angel to my Grandmother (N.B. I think I spelt angel wrong per last), Gammer Jenkins says, is Lady Maria, who sends her duty to her Aunt in Virginia, and remembers her, and my Grandpapa and Grandmamma when they were in Europe, and she was a little girl. You know they have Grandpapa’s picture here, and I live in the very rooms which he had, and which are to be called mine, my Lord Castlewood says.

“Having no more to say, at present, I close with best love and duty to my honoured Mother, and with respects to Mr. Dempster, and a kiss for Fanny, and kind remembrances to Old Gumbo, Nathan, Old and Young Dinah, and the pointer dog and Slut, and all friends, from their well-wisher

“HENRY ESMOND WARRINGTON.”

At the bottom he had added, with the careful afterthought of a dutiful nephew:

“Have wrote and sent my duty to my Uncle Warrington in Norfolk. No anser as yet.”

He looked up from the page, anxious as a boy who has carried a dish into the parlour and waits to see if the company will praise it or push it away.

“I hope the spelling is right, cousin?” he asked.

“’Tis quite well enough spelt for any person of fashion,” Lady Maria answered, who did not choose to have her own orthography weighed too nicely, and who had no mind that any dominion of hers should be disputed, even by letters.

“One word, ‘Angel,’ I know I spelt wrong in writing to my mamma,” Harry said, his blushes coming again like heat from an opened oven, “but I have learned a way of spelling it right, now.”

“And how is that, sir?”

“I think ’tis by looking at you, cousin;” and with the words he made her a low bow—one of his best—offering it with such honest ceremony, and with such a red, fresh bouquet of colour rising over his cheeks, that it was as if he laid both bow and nosegay at her feet.
