To understand Mr. Thomas Sapsea, Auctioneer, one must first understand the delicate acoustic ecology of Cloisterham—a town where the mythic reverberations of medieval chorales and the quiet, rhythmic scrape of agrarian life have historically intertwined. Against this multi-generational symphony, Mr. Sapsea’s existence is a profound auditory intrusion. He is a man who dresses at the Dean and mimics the Bishop, utilizing a booming, portentous roll in his speech that pollutes the town’s sonic landscape like the ceaseless grind of an industrial mill. He views the world not as a rich tapestry of subjective experience, but as a silent inventory waiting to be labeled, priced, and sold. 

Mr. Sapsea’s premises sit in the High-street, over against the ancient Nuns’ House. The building’s very timber and plaster hold the interwoven timelines of steadily deteriorating generations, yet Sapsea has irregularly modernized it, stripping away the shadowy, resonant corners where cultural memory might linger, preferring the sterile light of commerce. Above the doorway hangs a wooden effigy of his father in a curly wig and toga, hammer in hand—an emblem of the superficial commercialization of art, forever frozen in the act of liquidating history.

On a cool, chilly autumn evening, Sapsea sits in his dull ground-floor room, seeking warmth. Before the fire rests a bottle of deeply mature port, its cork drawn to let the wine breathe. To a meticulous food historian, this vintage would represent a multi-generational saga of sun-drenched Douro terraces, carrying the olfactory landscape of macerated dark plums, damp schist, and the slow, oxidizing magic of ancient oak casks. To Mr. Sapsea, however, it is merely a utilitarian prop of wealth. He sits beside his writing-desk, internally reciting a scrap of manuscript with a lofty air, his thumbs in his waistcoat arm-holes, until only the name “Ethelinda” breaks the quiet, a heavy, unmusical sound in the room.

His serving-maid enters, her soft footsteps quickly overwritten by Sapsea’s booming command. “Mr. Jasper is come, sir.”

“Admit him,” Sapsea waves, drawing two clean wineglasses from a rank of three. 

As Jasper enters, Sapsea pours the heavy, ruby liquid. “Glad to see you, sir. I congratulate myself on having the honour of receiving you here for the first time.” The auctioneer’s voice carries an ineffable loftiness, establishing a subtle power dynamic that forces Jasper into the role of a subordinate listener. 

“You are very good,” Jasper replies, his highly attuned choirmaster’s ear instinctively registering the oppressive acoustics of his host’s conceit. “The honour is mine and the self-congratulation is mine.”

“I have for some time desired to know you, Mr. Sapsea,” Jasper continues, watching the auctioneer stretch his legs toward the hearth. “Cloisterham is a little place. Cooped up in it myself, I know nothing beyond it.”

“If I have not gone to foreign countries, young man,” Sapsea begins, pausing only to secure Jasper’s permission for the diminutive title, “foreign countries have come to me. Put it that I take an inventory, or make a catalogue. I see a French clock. I never saw him before, but I instantly lay my finger on him and say ‘Paris!’ I see cups and saucers of Chinese make: I put my finger on them and say ‘Pekin, Nankin, and Canton.’ I have put my finger on the North Pole before now, and said ‘Spear of Esquimaux make, for half a pint of pale sherry!’”

In Sapsea’s boast, the vast, untranslatable sweep of global cultures, native dialects, and indigenous craft is violently flattened into the dominant lexicon of the auction block. Jasper smiles, perhaps recognizing the tragic erosion of meaning in the man’s sterile categorizations. “A very remarkable way, Mr. Sapsea, of acquiring a knowledge of men and things.”

“Most interesting. We were to speak of the late Mrs. Sapsea.”

Mr. Sapsea fills both glasses, denying the port the mindful, sensory appreciation it deserves, and swallows it as though it were mere fuel. He then holds up his manuscript. “Before I consult your opinion as a man of taste on this little trifle, I ought perhaps to describe the character of the late Mrs. Sapsea.”

Jasper conceals a yawn behind his wineglass, letting the rich, volatile esters of the port warm his watering eyes, before calling up a look of interest.

Sapsea recounts his courtship of Miss Brobity, who once kept a parallel educational establishment down town. It was a place where, perhaps, a quieter, older way of being was cultivated, until Sapsea’s booming style began to infect the dictation-exercises of her pupils. Miss Brobity’s subjective reality was soon entirely colonized. When he proposed, she was so overshadowed by awe that she lost her language entirely, able to articulate only the two words, “O Thou!” To the very end of her life—curtailed by a feeble action of the liver—she never recovered her native voice, addressing him always in those same unfinished, subjugated terms.

“I have been since,” Sapsea intones, deepening his voice until it reverberates unpleasantly against the glass of the clock, “a solitary mourner. What if her husband had been nearer on a level with her? If she had not had to look up quite so high, what might the stimulating action have been upon the liver?”

Jasper, staring into the fire, murmurs a quiet assent as the auctioneer thrusts the manuscript into his hand. It is an epitaph, the ultimate imposition of a dominant lexicon upon the dead, sealing Ethelinda’s lost identity beneath heavy, capitalized type:

ETHELINDA,
Reverential Wife of
MR. THOMAS SAPSEA,
AUCTIONEER, VALUER, ESTATE AGENT, &c.,
OF THIS CITY.
Whose Knowledge of the World,
Though somewhat extensive,
Never brought him acquainted with
A SPIRIT
More capable of
LOOKING UP TO HIM.
STRANGER, PAUSE
And ask thyself the Question,
CANST THOU DO LIKEWISE?
If Not,
WITH A BLUSH RETIRE.

Jasper has barely returned the paper with an obliging “Admirable!” when the serving-maid announces the arrival of Durdles. 

Durdles, the stonemason, enters. He is a man wholly of the earth, steeped in the limestone dust and damp, mythic air of the Cathedral crypt. He is a traditional agrarian elder of the subterranean world, living in an ankle-deep petrified grove of stone chips, urns, and broken columns. Bound inextricably to him is his dinner-bundle, a profound parcel of daily sustenance that he carries everywhere. It is an institution in Cloisterham—a humble but deeply elemental curation of hearty, stone-milled bread, sharp, cave-aged cheese, and perhaps a flask of fierce, fermented cider that has, on rare occasions, led to him being exhibited before the Bench of justices. To Durdles, this meal is not mere utilitarian calories, but a ritual anchor, a sensory connection to the grain and the soil amidst his daily communion with the dead.

Sapsea hands Durdles a glass of port. Durdles consumes it, then unfeelingly takes out his two-foot rule to measure Sapsea’s pompous lines, alloying the wet ink with ancient stone-grit. 

“It’ll come in to a eighth of a inch,” Durdles says. “Your servant, Mr. Jasper. Hope I see you well. I’ve got a touch of the Tombatism on me, but that I must expect.”

“You mean the Rheumatism,” Sapsea snaps, nettled by the stonemason’s mechanical reception of his poetry.

It is a sharp, linguistic strike—the auctioneer attempting to enforce the dominant, sanitized vocabulary over the living dialect of the worker. But Durdles, deeply rooted in the physical and acoustic reality of his environment, refuses to let his subjective experience be erased. 

“No, I don’t. I mean, Mr. Sapsea, the Tombatism. It’s another sort from Rheumatism. You get among them Tombs afore it’s well light on a winter morning, and keep on a-walking in the same all the days of your life, and *you’ll* know what Durdles means. Down in the crypt among the earthy damps there, and the dead breath of the old ’uns.”

To enact the carving, Sapsea retrieves a large iron key from his safe. As Durdles opens his flannel coat to stow it in a cavernous breast-pocket, Jasper looks on, amused by the sheer weight the man carries. 

“Why, Durdles!” Jasper exclaims, “you are undermined with pockets!”

“And I carries weight in ’em too, Mr. Jasper. Feel those!” Durdles produces two other massive keys. 

Jasper takes the heavy iron instruments. In his hands, the keys clink together. The sound is sharp, a jarring metallic resonance that slices through the room's stifling air—a piece of the crypt's acoustic ecology brought into Sapsea's parlor. 

“By the bye,” Jasper says, idly weighing the iron, “they sometimes call you Stony Durdles, don’t you? There was a discussion the other day among the Choir, whether Stony stood for Tony;” he clinks one key against another.

“Take care of the wards, Mr. Jasper,” Durdles warns, fiercely protective of his tools.

“Or whether Stony stood for Stephen;” Jasper clinks them again, changing the pitch. 

“You can’t make a pitch pipe of ’em, Mr. Jasper.”

“Or whether the name comes from your trade. How stands the fact?”

Jasper delivers the keys back with a friendly face, but the stonemason is highly conscious of his dignity. His moniker is a vital piece of his personal identity, inextricably tied to his native dialect and his tactile, sensory life among the monuments. To have it dissected as a mere curiosity is a profound insult. Deigning no word of answer, Durdles drops the keys into his pocket, reclaims his sacred dinner-bundle from the chair-back, ties the third key into it as though seasoning his iron with the scent of old bread and sharp cheese, and silently departs into the night.

Left to themselves, Sapsea proposes a hit at backgammon. The golden evening eventually culminates in a late supper of cold roast beef and salad. Here, at last, the mythic occurrences of the day settle into the everyday domestic routine of sustenance. The beef is a triumph of traditional agrarian sourcing—dry-aged until the marbled fat carries the rich, complex umami of the pasture, its center a deep, iron-rich ruby. It is served alongside a vibrant, peppery salad of wild watercress and bitter chicory, the leaves dressed simply in a sharp emulsified mustard and coarse, unfiltered olive oil that cuts brilliantly through the cold, savory meat. Yet Sapsea’s wisdom, delivered in diffuse and endless drones, entirely ignores the gustatory masterpiece before them. Jasper eats with deliberate, sensory appreciation, chewing thoughtfully on the bright, astringent greens and the tender beef, letting the meal ground him even as he ponders the installment of Sapsea's exhausting lexicon that he must carry away.
