Princess Claelia commanded a full-length portrait of herself. Gerard suggested employing his acquaintance, Pietro Vanucci.

She declined immediately. "Let it be time to cast a slight upon the Gerardo when his work displeases me," she replied.

Then Gerard, acknowledging he was an excellent draftsman but lacking skill as a colorist, begged her to pose as a Roman statue. He demonstrated how closely he could mimic marble upon paper. She agreed initially, but hesitated when the enthusiastic artist explained she must don the tunic, toga, and sandals of antiquity.

"Why, I'd rather be presented in my smock!" she exclaimed with medieval frankness.

"Alack, signorina," Gerard protested, "have you never observed the ancient custom? It is so free, so ample, so simple, yet so noble—and most becoming to your Highness, to whom Heaven has granted Roman features, a shapely arm, and a graceful hand, albeit in modern attire."

"What? Can you flatter like the rest, Gerardo?"

"Well, grant me time to consider. Return on Saturday, and then I shall decide."

During this respite, the garments were crafted and tried on within her chambers to determine if they compensated sufficiently for being a millennium out of date.

Hurrying toward this appointment, Gerard was suddenly halted, rooted to the spot before a shop window. His keen eye had spotted an open copy of Lactantius. *"That is beautifully written, at least,"* he thought.

He scrutinized it a moment longer with all his senses.

It was not written at all. It was printed.

Gerard groaned inwardly. *"I am undone; my enemy stands at the door. The press is in Rome."*

Entering the shop, he feigned nonchalance and inquired how long the printing press had been in the city. The merchant claimed there was no such thing in Rome. "Oh, regarding the Lactantius; that was printed atop the Apennines."

"What? Did the printing press tumble down from the moon there?"

"Nay, messer," the trader laughed, pointing to the title page. "It sprang up from Germany. Look!"

Gerard snatched the book eagerly and read the inscription: *Opera et impensis Sweynheim et Pannartz Alumnorum Joannis Fust. Impressum Subiacis. A.D. 1465.*

"Shall I sell it, messer? Observe how fair and even the letters are. Few remain who can write thus, and it costs but a quarter of the price."

"I would dearly love to possess it," Gerard said sadly, "but my heart forbids it. Know that I am a calligrapher, and these disciples of Fust pursue me worldwide, stealing the bread from my mouth. Yet I wish them no ill. Heaven forbid!" He hurried away.

*"Dearest Margaret,"* he whispered to himself, *"we must lose no time. We must make hay while the sun shines. One month more, and an avalanche of printer's type shall roll down from those Apennines onto Rome, destroying us, the writers."*

He nearly ran to the Princess Claelia.

He was ushered into an apartment unfamiliar to him. Though not large, it was luxuriously appointed; a fountain bubbled in the center, and the floor was paved with panther skins, dressed with hair, ensuring silence underfoot. The room served as an antechamber to the princess's boudoir, separated only by a magnificent tapestry curtain.

Left alone, Gerard grew uneasy, doubting if he had been led astray. These fears vanished quickly.

A light step approached swiftly behind the curtain. It parted, revealing a figure the heathens might have worshipped. She was neither quite Venus nor quite Minerva, but a blend of both—nobler than Venus, more feminine than Jupiter's daughter. Toga, tunic, sandals; nothing was modern. As for beauty, it transcended all eras.

Gerard sprang upright, the artist within him flushing with delight.

"Oh!" he cried innocently, gazing in rapture.

This added the final charm to his model: a light blush tinged her cheeks, her eyes brightened, and her mouth curved in delicious self-satisfaction at this genuine tribute to her allure.

They stared at one another for some time until she realized his eloquence consisted merely of gasps and staring. Finally, she spoke. "Well, Gerardo, you see I have fashioned myself an antique monster for you."

"A monster? I doubt Fra Colonna would fall to his knees and adore your Highness, seeing you thus attired."

"Nay, I care not to be adored by an old man. I prefer to be loved by a young one... of my own choosing."

Gerard produced his pencils, arranged his canvas covered with sturdy paper, and began to work. So absorbed was he that he spared no mercy for his model. After nearly an hour in one position, she spoke faintly, "Gerardo, I can stand no longer, even for you."

"Sit down and rest awhile, Signora."

"I thank you," she replied, sinking into a chair, turning pale and sighing.

Gerard was alarmed, realizing his inconsideration. He fetched water from the fountain, intending to splash it on her face, but she raised a white hand in supplication. "Nay, hold it to my brow with your own hand; pray, do not fling it at me!"

Timidly, Gerard applied his wet hand to her forehead.

"Ah!" she sighed, "that is refreshing. Again."

He repeated the action. She thanked him and requested he ring a small handbell on the table. A maid arrived promptly and was dispatched to Floretta with orders to bring a large fan.

Floretta soon appeared with the fan. The moment she drew near the Princess, the lady's aristocratic nostrils flared wide like a racehorse's. "Wretch!" she shrieked. Rising instantly with renewed vigor, she seized Floretta by the left hand, twisted it violently into her hair, and with her right hand delivered three severe boxings to her ears.

Floretta screamed and sobbed, receiving no mercy. The antique toga, now loose, revealed a bare arm that seemed as powerful as it was beautiful. It rose and fell like the piston of a steam engine, delivering heavy slaps one after another against Floretta's shoulders. The final blow drove her sobbing and screaming through the curtain, where she continued to wail bitterly for some time.

"Saints of heaven!" cried Gerard. "What is amiss? What has she done?"

"She knows perfectly well. 'Tis not the first time. That filthy toad! I shall teach her to approach me smelling of the musk-cat."

"Alas, Signora, 'twas a minor fault, I believe."

"A minor fault? Nay, 'twas a grievous offense." With an astonishing drop from anger to humility and sweetness, she added, "Are you angry with me for beating her, Gerardo?"

"Signora, it ill becomes me to discipline you; yet I deem those Heaven appoints to govern others should govern themselves."

"That is true, Gerardo. How wise you are, being so young." She summoned the other maid and handed her a small purse. "Take this to Floretta and tell her 'the Gerardo' has interceded for her; therefore, I must forgive her. There, Gerardo."

Gerard blushed crimson at the compliment but, unsure how to respond equally, asked if he should resume her portrait.

"Not yet; beating that hussy has somewhat agitated me. I shall sit awhile, and you shall converse with me. I know you can talk, if you choose, as rarely as you draw."

"That would be easily done."

"Do it then, Gerardo."

Gerard was taken aback. "But, signora, I know not what to say. This is sudden."

"Speak your true mind. Say you wish you were anywhere else."

"Nay, signora, that would not be truthful. I desire one thing, however."

"Yes, and what is that?" she asked gently.

"I wish I could have drawn you as you were beating that poor lass. You were awful, yet lovely. Oh, what a subject for a Pythoness!"

"Alas! He thinks only of his art. And why all this fuss about my beauty, Gerardo? You are far fairer than I. You resemble Apollo more than I do Venus. Moreover, you possess lovely hair and lovely eyes—but you know not how to use them."

"Yes, I do. To draw you, signora."

"Ah, yes; you can see my features with them, but you cannot perceive what any Roman suitor saw long ago in your place. Yet surely you have noticed how welcome you are to me, Gerardo?"

"I perceive your Highness is always exceedingly kind to me, a poor stranger."

"No, I am not, Gerardo. I have often been cold to you; rude, even. And you are so simple you fail to understand the cause. Alas, I feared for my own heart. I feared to become your slave. I, who have hitherto made slaves. Ah! Gerardo, I am unhappy. Since you arrived, I have lived upon your visits. On the day you come, I am radiant; on other days, I am listless, wishing them gone.
