Gerard was nearly as giddy to reach the fabled promised land of Burgundy as Denys himself. The soldier never ceased singing his homeland’s praises: at every petty travel mishap, every grumpy innkeeper, every scratchy blanket, he would gesture broadly and declare “they do all this far better in Burgundy.” Most of all, he had perfectly preyed on Gerard’s greatest soft spot, vowing that every inn in that polished, civilized nation provided clean, fresh-laundered bed linens. “I ask no more than that,” the Dutchman would sigh. “Think of it— I haven’t once slept in a bare, clean bed since I left home. The linen at these wayside taverns is so foul that when I look at it, I don’t want to strip off my tunic and hose, I want to shut both my eyes and my nose tight so I never have to touch the stuff.”

Denys’s love for his country ran so deep he once walked twenty full leagues in shoes that had split wide open, soles flapping loose from uppers, rather than spend a single coin on a surly German cobbler who would drag his feet, invent a dozen petty obstacles to serving a customer, pause mid-work to finish his dinner, even lumber his fat body across the shop slow as molasses just to make a traveler wait.

As the sun dipped low over the hills, they found themselves exactly halfway between a tiny walled town and a remote monastery, the abbey’s stone roofs visible a short walk off the main road. Denys voted at once for the town inn; Gerard was set on taking shelter at the convent. Denys caved, but only on one strict condition: the very second they crossed the Burgundian border, they would stop at inns every night after that, no exceptions. Gerard agreed readily, for his little travel pamphlet listing all hospitable monasteries along their route ended right at this spot.

They turned off the main track, and Gerard asked, confused, where this sudden aversion to the monasteries that had fed and lodged them for free so many times had come from. Denys hemmed and hawed at first, dancing around the answer, until all his pent-up frustrations burst free. This was no new dislike, it turned out: it was a deep, long-buried horror he had forced himself to suppress the whole trip, purely out of politeness. “I saw how delighted you were to chat with every grey old shaven-headed monk we met,” he explained, “so I bit my tongue. But this is the last one, so why not unburden my mind? I’ve been like a fish dragged out of water in every one of those great gloomy stone dungeons you dragged me to. You’re off gabbing with some old priest the second we step through the gate, so you never see the personal purgatory I’m stuck in.”

“Forgive me,” Gerard said, stricken. “I’ve been entirely selfish, never stopping to ask how you were faring.”

“Ay, ay, I forgive you, little comrade,” Denys waved it off. “It’s no fault of yours. You’re far from the first fool to be priest-ridden and monk-beguiled. But I’ll never forgive those monks for the utter misery they put me through.”

A full century before Henry VIII’s royal commissioners would come to audit every abbey in the realm, Denys delivered his full, roaring indictment. Those gloomy stone piles were all identical, no variation at all, unlike the ever-unique inns scattered across the countryside. Every one had a grand main gate, a tiny postern gate, the same number of weathered stone steps leading into a shadowed cloister walk. Over here the drafty dormitory, over there the great ice-cold refectory, where you had to sit dead silent, not a peep out of you—an agony for a man like Denys, who loved to speak his mind loud and clear whenever he pleased.

“And then,” he ranted on, “no one there is a proper man. We’re all slaves to what? A peevish little tinkling bell that never, ever sleeps. If it were a blaring battle trumpet, sounding alarms, it wouldn’t freeze a man’s blood in his veins half so bad. Tinkle, tinkle, tinkle—you’re supposed to sit down to a meal you might not even have an appetite for. Before that food’s even settled in your stomach, tinkle, tinkle! Now you’ve got to troop off to church to pray, when you don’t have an ounce of devotion in you. I’m no hog that chews through his prayers half-asleep, for one. Tinkle, tinkle—now you’ve got to climb into your hard straw bed, eyes wide awake. You finally drift off? Some miserable little imp of a monk is yanking the bell rope, tinkle, tinkle, you’ve got to mumble a prayer in the pitch dark whether you know a single one or not. If they ever heard the sort of grumbling half-cussed prayers I mutter when they wake me up mid-sleep, they’d toss me out on my ear before I could blink. You drop off again, get barely a handful of actual sleep? Tinkle, tinkle, it’s matins time.”

“I suppose the only clapper you’d ever listen to gladly is a woman’s tongue,” Gerard put in, half amused, half contemptuous.

“At least a woman’s scolding has music in it, even when she’s nagging you half to death,” Denys shot back unapologetically.

“And every tiny thing you do gets you scolded. If I so much as dip my finger in the salt cellar to grab a pinch, right away someone’s squawking, ‘Have you no knife to handle the salt with, that you use your filthy fingers?’ If I wipe my knife quick on the tablecloth to save time, then it’s ‘Wipe your dirty knife on the bread, and the clean part on the cloth!’ Oh, small-souled lot! All those petty, nitpicking little rules land on a man trying to be friendly like tiny icicles dripping off the edge of a thatched straw roof, cold and sharp and annoying.”

“I don’t think being clean is any petty pedantry,” Gerard argued. “You ought to learn proper manners once and for all.”

“Nay, they’re the ones with no manners. They stop a man’s mouth every other word out of his head.”

“Every other obscene or blasphemous word, you mean,” Gerard corrected him.

“Exaggerator, go on with you! Why, just at the very last of those dungeons, I saw a whole crowd of poor travelers sitting shivering and dead silent around one shaven old monk, like a bunch of thieves waiting in line to be dragged up to the gallows. So I tried to cheer them up, right? I just yelled out ‘Courage, everyone, the dev--’”

“Whoa, I can guess what happened next!” Gerard cut in.

“Marry, this. ‘Do not blaspheme!’ the lout snarled at me. ‘Pardon?’ says I. Wouldn’t you know it, he spins around and starts yelling at me in that terrible Alsatian French of his, where he turns every P sound into a B. I almost laughed right in his face, I did.”

“Says the man who can’t speak ten words of proper French without making a mistake himself,” Gerard teased.

“Pfft, everyone in the whole world ought to speak proper French. What’s the point of all these silly different jargons, except to build walls between men’s hearts?”

“Well what did he actually say to you?”

“What does it matter what a fool says?”

“Oh, not every word a fool speaks is folly, or I wouldn’t bother listening to you ramble on,” Gerard grinned.

“Fine, fine. He says ‘Those who start throwing the devil's name around for fun always end by taking the Lord's name in vain too.’ ‘Father,’ I says, ‘I’m a soldier, that’s just my watchword, my consigne.’ ‘Oh, so it's just a silly habit?’ he says. I didn’t see the old fox’s trick, and I tried to clear my name, so I said ‘Aye, it is.’ ‘Then that’s ten times worse,’ he says. ‘He’ll come sniffing around you one of these days, when he hears his name called over and over again.’ Not a single bit of gratitude that I was telling him a bit of news he never got from his missal or his breviary, mind you. Then he has the gall to tell me that soldiers go around committing all the crimes that get regular men broken on the wheel—murder, rape, pillage, the whole list.”

“And isn’t that often true?” Gerard asked.

“True or not, it was damned rude of him to say it to my face,” Denys replied, suddenly wary of agreeing. “And this so-called courteous host of mine goes on, ‘You men are enemies to all mankind, why would you go making enemies of all the good spirits too, by shouting the names of evil ones?’ Blah blah blah, a whole lot more of that tedious stuff.”

“Well Denys, whether you take his advice or ignore it, why blame a man for trying to raise his voice to save your soul?”

“How the devil is he supposed to save my soul when he can’t even say the words right, turning every P into a B?”

Gerard was so stunned by that absurd Gallic quip he couldn’t think of a reply for a full second. Before he could recover, Denys was already off on a new tangent, ranting that every last monk was a hypocrite. “Just look at them, creeping around the halls, never able to meet your eye like an honest man would.”

“Nay,” Gerard said eagerly, jumping to defend them, “that modest downcast gaze is part of their discipline! It’s called custodia oculorum, the guarding of the eyes.”

“Cussed toads, muttering their Latin hocus-pocus? That's no custom, that's exactly how a cutpurse looks, when he can't meet an honest man's eye. Take off a monk's cowl, and there's a thief. Stick a cowl on a thief, and suddenly he's a monk. Don't tell me they'll ever be able to look God Almighty in the face up in heaven, when they can't even look a regular honest man in the eye down here on earth. Ah, there's the gates, black as pitch! Let's head on in, comrade. Mercy me, there's that tinkle already! Though this one's the best tinkle of all—it's for dinner. Listen, hear that? The wolf howling in my stomach just said Amen!” He swore two loud, hearty oaths to confirm that last statement, and marched into the convent like a victorious gamecock, assuming from Gerard's quiet silence that he had won the argument, and never dreaming how deeply his crude, unfair rant had disgusted the pious young Dutchman.
