"Follow," the raven commanded, his voice cutting through the encroaching silence.

We moved deeper into the pine forest, the canopy thickening until the sacred gloom wrapped around us like a heavy cloak. Neither of us spoke; the weight of the journey demanded a discipline beyond mere words. As we advanced, the trees grew larger, older, their forms becoming increasingly individualistic, some twisted into grotesque silhouettes by centuries of wind and decay. Eventually, the dense timber gave way to a clearing, the forest thinning into an open expanse of heath.

"You see that hawthorn?" the guide asked, extending a sharp beak toward the edge of the woods.

I scanned the landscape where the trees dissolved into scrubland. "I see a gnarled old man with a great white head," I replied, my gaze fixed on the solitary, skeletal shape.

"Look again," the raven corrected. "It is a hawthorn."

"It appears to be an ancient specimen," I conceded, though skepticism lingered. "Yet this is not the season for blossoms!"

"The season for the hawthorn to blossom," the bird stated flatly, "is precisely when the hawthorn blossoms. That specific tree stands in the ruins of the church on your home farm. Recall the morning of the thunder; you intended to give the bailiff directions regarding its churchyard, did you not?"

"I was going to instruct him to transform it into a wilderness of rose-trees, ensuring the plough never came within three yards of the site."

"Listen!" the raven suddenly halted, holding his breath as if straining against a gale.

I strained my ears. Was it the sighing of a distant wind, carrying the ghost of music long forgotten? Or was I hearing nothing at all?

"They go there still," the raven whispered.

"Who goes there? And where do they go?" I asked, turning back to the creature.

"Some of the people who once prayed there continue to visit the ruins," he replied. "But I suspect they will not remain much longer."

"What compels them to go now?"

"They require assistance from one another to complete their thinking and hatch their feelings," the raven explained. "They gather to talk and sing. In doing so, they claim the great thought floats out of their hearts like a massive vessel leaving a river at high tide."

"Do they pray alongside their singing?"

"No. They have discovered that each person prays most effectively in their own silent heart. Observe—there goes one!"

He pointed upward. A snow-white pigeon mounted the sky, its wingbeats accelerating into a rapid, spiraling ascent—an ethereal staircase ascending into the sun. Light flashed quivering off its feathers.

"I see a pigeon," I said.

"Of course you see a pigeon," the raven retorted. "For there is the pigeon! But I see a prayer in transit. I wonder now what heart serves as that dove's mother. Perhaps someone has awakened in my cemetery."

"How can a pigeon constitute a prayer?" I pressed. "I understand how it might serve as a symbolic likeness, but a living bird emerging directly from a heart?"

"It MUST puzzle you! It cannot fail to do so!"

"A prayer is a thought, a purely spiritual entity!" I insisted.

"Very true! But if you comprehended any world besides your own, you would understand your own much better. When a heart is truly alive, it generates living thoughts. Consider one heart whose thoughts are strong, happy creatures, and whose dreams are actual lives. Some pray by lifting heavy burdens from the earth only to drop them back upon it; others launch their petitions in living shapes—the closest approximation available. All living things began as thoughts and are therefore suitable tools for those who think. When one addresses the Great Thinker: 'Here is one of thy thoughts: I am thinking it now!' that constitutes a prayer—a message from one of its own small hearts to the big heart. Look, there is another!"

This time, the raven directed his beak downward, toward a cluster of granite blocks at our feet. I looked and saw a tiny flower. It was unlike anything I had ever encountered, evoking a sensation of grace and trust, a color palette suggesting a new world that remained fundamentally familiar. It resembled an anemone, possessing a pale rose hue and a golden center.

"That is a prayer-flower," the raven declared.

"I have never seen such a flower before!" I rejoined.

"There is no other such. Not one prayer-flower is ever exactly alike," he returned.

"How do you distinguish a prayer-flower?" I asked.

"By its expression," he answered simply. "Beyond that, I cannot explain. If you recognize it, you recognize it; if you do not, you do not."

"Could you not teach me to identify a prayer-flower when I encounter it?"

"I could not. But if I could, what greater purpose would you serve? You would not know it through YOURSELF and ITSELF! Why learn the name of a thing when you do not truly know the thing itself? Whose responsibility is it but yours to open your eyes? Indeed, the business of the universe is to reduce you to such a state of folly that you realize you are one, and thus begin to be wise!"

Nevertheless, I acknowledged that the flower differed from any flora I had previously observed. Therefore, I knew I was witnessing a shadow of the prayer contained within it. A profound awe washed over me as I contemplated the vast heart listening to the flower.
