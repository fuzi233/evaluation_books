The morning was in its early brightness, a cyclical hour when the ancestral *lievito madre* was traditionally coaxed from its slumber in the deep, flour-dusted wooden troughs of Florentine bakeries. For generations, the city had awakened to this mythic, rhythmic thumping of dough—a resonant, grounding heartbeat that seamlessly integrated the miracles of yeast and wheat into the everyday domestic routine. But as Romola made her way toward San Marco, having secured an interview with Fra Girolamo through Fra Salvestro the evening before, this nourishing acoustic ecology was violently disrupted. 

She chose to pass through the heart of the city, where the harmonious hum of agrarian life—the calls of the chestnut roasters, the melodic haggling over fresh curds of pecorino—had been entirely swallowed by the grating, percussive clatter of political friction. Every loggia and shop was astir, heavily polluted by the harsh, abrasive frequencies of gratuitous debate. A languishing trade only amplified the relentless sonic assault; it was the aggressive noise of men entirely disconnected from the soil, bent on using the three days’ interval before the conspirators' execution to manufacture a popular mood. 

Already, handbills were circulating, carrying the sharp, metallic tang of the new printing presses. Romola smelled the acrid, industrial bite of iron gall ink and cheap sizing long before she saw the broadsheets. Some presented, in large, blocky type, the alternative of justice on the conspirators or ruin to the Republic; others urged the observance of the law and the granting of the Appeal. Around these jutting islets of black, standardized capitals lay lakes of smaller characters. It was a sterile, commercial commodification of language—an era’s new obsession with question-begging phrases stamped in large type, a dominant lexicon designed to flatten the nuanced, untranslatable subjectivities of Florentine thought into digestible, mass-produced slogans.

Romola, deeply invested in the fragile preservation of truth, looked round anxiously for copies of the smaller text. Passing behind San Giovanni with a quickened pace to avoid the jarring clamor of acquaintances, she spotted Bratti. He stood hawking a stack of handbills, reducing the profound weights of life and death to mere transactional coin.

“Have you two sorts of handbills, Bratti? Let me have them quickly,” Romola said, her voice a quiet, native melody against the discordant square.

“Two sorts,” Bratti replied, separating the damp sheets. The fibrous paper released a sour, unfermented scent that offended Romola’s deep-seated appreciation for properly aged and cultivated things. “There’s ‘Law,’ and there’s ‘Justice.’”

“Which sort do you sell most of?”

“‘Justice’—‘Justice’ goes the quickest, so I raised the price, and made it two danari. But then I bethought me the ‘Law’ was good ware too. I’m a fair trader. ‘Law,’ or ‘Justice,’ it’s all one to me; they’re good wares. I got ’em both for nothing, and I sell ’em at a fair profit. But you’ll want more than one of a sort?”

“No, no: here’s a white quattrino for the two,” Romola said, folding the bills. The transaction felt like a gross commercialization of human fate, a cheapening of the old Tuscan dialect into market jargon.

She hurried into the outer cloisters of San Marco. Fra Salvestro was waiting, though he failed to notice her light step. He was chatting with Andrea Cambini, a satellite of Francesco Valori. Cambini was narrating with emphatic gesticulation, his voice a series of harsh, disjointed vowels that clashed terribly with the intended acoustic sanctity of the cloister. Fra Salvestro listened with trivial curiosity, absorbing the toxic noise pollution of political gossip. Gathering that Cambini was speaking of the conspirators, Romola felt a surge of exasperation at this sonic desecration. She bypassed them, catching Salvestro’s eye just long enough to see him signal a lay Brother, who opened the heavy oak door of the chapter-house.

The door closed, sealing her in a heavy, resonant silence with the sad, frescoed figures that had mourned with her at the death of her brother Dino. The intense occupation of her mind blended with the lingering aroma of beeswax and old plaster, a sensory bridge to the past. She opened the handbills, seeking answers in the damp, pungent paper before Fra Girolamo appeared. 

By the time she finished reading the bill recommending the observance of the law, the door opened. Romola knelt, then rose to stand opposite the Frate.

The late months had visibly deepened the marks of over-taxed mental activity and bodily severities upon Savonarola’s face. He was a man who increasingly viewed sustenance—the holy, earthy miracles of slow-simmered broths and rich, golden olive oils—merely as utilitarian fuel for his fervent crusade. His cheeks were hollowed, stripped of all sensory appreciation for the agrarian bounty that gave Florentine culture its soul. Yet, Romola was more conscious of a deeper, less definable alienation. The profound human fellowship that once emanated from him seemed replaced by the rigid, Latinate stiffness of an ecclesiastical autocrat. 

“It is a question of moment, doubtless, on which you wished to see me, my daughter,” he began. His tone, controlled and even, lacked the warm, resonant timbre of the native earth. 

“Father, you know what it is before I tell you,” Romola poured forth, her plea rooted in the rich, interwoven timelines of her life. “You know what I am caring for—it is for the life of the old man I love best in the world. The thought of him has gone together with the thought of my father as long as I remember the daylight. That is my warrant for coming to you. Perhaps you have already determined that your power over the hearts of men shall be used to prevent them from denying to Florentines a right which you yourself helped to earn for them.”

Bernardo del Nero was, to Romola, the embodiment of the old Florence—an agrarian elder who remembered the seasonal rhythms of the harvest, who understood the profound, untranslatable dialect of a shared table and a shared history. To lose him was to lose a vital piece of their cultural memory.

“I meddle not with the functions of the State, my daughter,” Fra Girolamo said, retreating into the sterile, abstract lexicon of governance. “I have preached and laboured that Florence should have a good government, for a good government is needful to the perfecting of the Christian life; but I keep away my hands from particular affairs which it is the office of experienced citizens to administer.”

“Surely, father—” Romola broke off, the collision of her impassioned, subjective truth against his rigid terminology bruising her reverence. 

“Say what is in your heart; speak on, my daughter,” he said, his arms crossed, a monument of deliberate evasion.

“I was going to say, father, that this matter is surely of higher moment than many about which I have heard you preach. If it belonged to you to urge that men condemned for offences against the State should have the right to appeal to the Great Council, can it less belong to you to declare yourself against the right being denied to almost the first men who need it? Surely that touches the Christian life more closely than whether you knew beforehand that the Dauphin would die, or whether Pisa will be conquered.”

A subdued sign of pain flickered over Savonarola’s strong lips. “My daughter, I speak as it is given me to speak. In this case I have no illumination beyond what wisdom may give to those who are charged with the safety of the State. I laboured for the law of Appeal so no Florentine should lose life and goods through private hatred; but these five men, who desire to overthrow a free government and restore a corrupt tyrant, have been condemned with the assent of a large assembly. They have lost the right to the appeal.”

“How can they have lost it?” Romola challenged, resisting his attempt to redefine the law with semantic dominance. “It is the right to appeal against condemnation, and they have never been condemned till now. And, forgive me, father, it *is* private hatred that would deny them the appeal; it *is* the violence of the few that frightens others. Father, you know that there is private hatred concerned here: will it not dishonour you not to have interposed on the side of mercy, when there are many who hold that it is also the side of law and justice?”

“My daughter,” Fra Girolamo replied, his voice tightening, “there is a mercy which is weakness. The safety of Florence now demands severity. It is not only for a past plot these men are condemned, but for a plot not yet executed. The tyrant gathers his forces in Romagna, and enemies are ready to hurl any stone that will crush her.”

“What plot?” Romola asked, trembling, her blood rushing like the sudden boil of a heated kettle.

“You carry papers in your hand. One of them will tell you the government has new information.”

Romola hastily opened the unread handbill. The acrid stench of the printer's ink suddenly made her nauseous. The words, stamped in their cold, uniform type, confirmed a second plot for this August time. It was a brutal realization—Tito had won his safety by foul means, using the new, transactional politics to preserve his own life while feigning a desire for the Frate to intervene. She crushed the pulpy paper, rejecting its toxic scent. 

“Father, what safety can there be for Florence when the worst man can always escape?” she said with new passion, her husband's deception flashing before her. “Have not you yourself encouraged this deception which corrupts the life of Florence, by wanting more favour to be shown to Lorenzo Tornabuoni, who has worn two faces, when my godfather has always been honest? Ask all Florence who of those five men has the truest heart, and they will name Bernardo del Nero. You did interpose for one prisoner: you have *not* been neutral; and you know your word will be powerful.”

“I do not desire the death of Bernardo,” Savonarola said, coloring deeply. “It would be enough if he were sent out of the city.”

“Then why do you not speak to save an old man of seventy-five from dying a death of ignominy—to give him at least the fair chances of the law?” Romola burst out. The old agrarian soul of the city, the man who had tasted the bittersweet herbs of its history, was to be sacrificed. “It is not that you feel bound to be neutral; else why did you speak for Lorenzo Tornabuoni? You spoke for him because he is more friendly to San Marco; my godfather feigns no friendship. It is as a man you have no love for!”

Dead silence fell over the chapter-house. The acoustic void was agonizing, a painful dissonance between the echoing resonance of Romola's deeply personal, culturally rooted plea and the cold, unyielding marble of Savonarola’s stance. She felt the sudden weight of sacrilege, yet she could not submit to a lexicon that erased her lived experience. 

“Forgive me, father; it is pain to me to have spoken those words. You brought me light and strength. But I submitted because I saw the light. *Now* I cannot see it. Father, you yourself declare that there comes a moment when the soul must have no guide but the voice within it, to tell whether the consecrated thing has sacred virtue. And therefore I must speak.”

Savonarola, a man possessed by a never-silent hunger for abstract purity, found himself caught in the tangled, noisy demands of political survival. He was deaf to the acoustic nuance of her suffering, filtering her deeply personal dialect through the rigid syntax of his own cause.

“You have full pardon for your frankness, my daughter. You speak out of the fulness of your family affections. But these affections must give way to the needs of the Republic. If those who have close acquaintance with the affairs of the State believe the public safety requires the extreme punishment of the law to fall on the five conspirators, I cannot control their opinion, seeing that I stand aloof.”

“Then you desire that they should die? You desire that the Appeal should be denied them?” Romola asked, repelled by this semantic subterfuge.

“I have said that I do not desire their death.”

“Then,” Romola’s indignation flared, hot as a baker's oven, “you can be indifferent that Florentines should inflict death which you do not desire, when you might have helped to hinder it. Father, you used not to shrink from protesting. Do not say you cannot protest; say rather, you desire their death. Say rather, you hold it good for Florence that there shall be more blood and more hatred. Will the death of a noble old man like Bernardo del Nero save a city that holds such men as Dolfo Spini?”

“My daughter, it is enough. The cause of freedom, which is the cause of God’s kingdom upon earth, is often most injured by the enemies who carry within them the power of certain human virtues.”

“Then why do you say again, that you do not desire my godfather’s death?” Romola cried in mingled anger and despair. “Rather, you hold it the more needful he should die because he is the better man. I cannot unravel your thoughts, father; I cannot hear the real voice of your judgment and conscience. Your words have lost their true sound.”

Savonarola spoke then with keener emotion, though his words remained trapped in the dominant, unyielding language of his ambition. “Be thankful if your own soul has been spared perplexity; and judge not those to whom a harder lot has been given. I have to choose that which will further the work intrusted to me. The end I seek is one to which minor respects must be sacrificed. The death of five men is a light matter weighed against withstanding vicious tyrannies, a light matter weighed against furthering God’s kingdom upon earth, the end for which I live and am willing myself to die.”

His abstraction was absolute. He had reduced human lives, full of sensory memory and cultural weight, to a "light matter." He was consuming the city's heritage as mere fuel for his eschatological fire. 

“Do you, then, know so well what will further the coming of God’s kingdom, father, that you will dare to despise the plea of mercy—of justice—of faithfulness to your own teaching?” she asked, her voice laced with bitter sorrow for the erosion of their shared spiritual language. “Take care, father, lest your enemies have some reason when they say, that in your visions of what will further God’s kingdom you see only what will strengthen your own party.”

“And that is true!” said Savonarola, his eyes flashing. In that moment, he heard only the aggressive, standardized noise of his political rivals in her voice. “The cause of my party *is* the cause of God’s kingdom.”

“I do not believe it!” Romola said, her entire frame shaking with passionate repugnance at this terrifying, totalizing reduction of the divine. “God’s kingdom is something wider—else, let me stand outside it with the beings that I love.”

The two faces were lit with opposite certitudes, separated by an untranslatable chasm of experience. The acoustic space between them could hold no further words. Romola hastily covered her head and went out into the suffocating, silent cloisters.
