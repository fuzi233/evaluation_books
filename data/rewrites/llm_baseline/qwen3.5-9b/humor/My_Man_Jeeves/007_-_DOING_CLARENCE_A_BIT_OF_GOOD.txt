Have you ever seriously contemplated the sheer audacity, the brazen cheek, or—if you prefer—a distinct lack of shame with which the female sex occasionally operates? By Jove, I have! But then, I've had it shoved in my face in a manner that surely happens to very few men. The limit was finally breached by the infamous "Yeardsley Venus" incident.

To fully grasp the absurdity of the situation, I must briefly explain my history with Mrs. Yeardsley. Back in the day, she was Elizabeth Shoolbred. An ancient Worcestershire family, loaded with cash, and pretty as a picture. Her brother, Bill, was at Oxford with me.

I adored Elizabeth Shoolbred. There was actually a week when we were officially engaged. But just as I was starting to treat life with gravity, studying furniture catalogs, and feeling appropriately solemn during the restaurant orchestra's rendition of "The Wedding Glide," she broke it off. A month later, she was wed to Clarence Yeardsley, an artist.

Thanks to golf, billiards, horse racing, and my club mates keeping me occupied, I recovered. I treated the whole affair as a closed chapter in my life. Since she and Clarence had retreated to the countryside and ceased visiting London, I assumed we'd never meet again. By the time her letter arrived, the wound had mostly healed; I was practically eating properly again. Frankly, I was relieved it had ended.

Then, one morning, out of the clear blue sky, this letter landed on my breakfast tray:

*"MY DEAR OLD REGGIE,—What ages it seems since I saw anything of you. How are you? We have settled down here in the most perfect old house, with a lovely garden, in the middle of delightful country. Couldn't you run down here for a few days? Clarence and I would be so glad to see you. Bill is here, and is most anxious to meet you again. He was speaking of you only this morning. Do come. Wire your train, and I will send the car to meet you.*

A fellow reads that and feels rattled. It's a letter from a girl who could have ruined your life, yet here she is, inviting you back.

However, the golf comment sealed the deal. Bill knows his turf. If he says the course is "topping," it must be special. So, I went.

Old Bill met me at the station with the car. We hadn't seen each other in months, and the reunion was warm.

"Thank goodness you've come," he said as we drove off. "I was just about at my last grip."

"What's the trouble, old scout?" I asked.

"If I didn't suffer from 'artistic neuritis,' it wouldn't be so bad. As it is, it's rotten!"

"Pictures?"

"Pictures. Nothing else is discussed in this household. Clarence is an artist. So is his father. And you know what Elizabeth is like when she gets her head into a spin?"

I recalled that most of my time with Elizabeth involved touring galleries. During our engagement, I followed her like a dog through endless halls, despite pictures being poison to me (just as they are to Bill). I never realized she'd keep this up after marrying an artist. I figured she'd be sick of art by now. Apparently, not so.

"They talk pictures at every meal," Bill complained. "It makes a chap feel out of it. How long are you staying?"

"A few days."

"Take my advice: wire me from London tomorrow. I'm playing the Scots. I planned to return after the match, but you couldn't drag me back with a lasso."

I tried to find the bright side. "But, Bill, your sister mentioned a corking links nearby."

He swerved violently toward the bank. "You don't mean she said that?"

"She said *you* said it was better than St. Andrews."

"So I did. Did she forget I added, 'I don't think'?"

"No, she omitted that crucial detail."

"It's the worst course in Great Britain."

Stunned, I tried another angle. "We'll play billiards instead. Glad the table is good."

"It depends on your definition of 'good.' It's half-size, and there's a seven-inch gouge in the baulk where Clarence's cue slipped. Elizabeth patched it with pink silk. Looks dashing, but it ruins the mechanics."

"She said you said..."

"She was pulling your leg."

We arrived at a substantial house set back from the road. In the dusk, it looked black and sinister, reminding me of those horror stories where a traveler is lured to a lonely cottage only to hear a shriek upon arrival. Elizabeth knew I'd fall for the golf bait. She was playing me. What was the real game? Suddenly, a chilling thought hit me: she had a girl hidden here and intended to marry me off. Young wives often try this. Although she claimed only Clarence, herself, Bill, and his father lived there, a woman who could lie about St. Andrews wouldn't hesitate about a small fib.

"Bill, old scout," I asked, "are there any frightful girls or rot of that sort staying here?"

"Wish there were," he replied grimly. "No such luck."

As we pulled up, the door opened. A woman's figure emerged.

"Have you got him, Bill?" she asked. In my current state of mind, that sounded dreadfully like Lady Macbeth summoning her accomplice.

"Do you mean me?" I stammered.

She stepped into the light. It was Elizabeth, unchanged.

"Is that you, Reggie? I'm so glad you came. I feared you'd forgotten. Come in for tea."

Have you ever been rejected by a girl, watched her marry someone else, and then been introduced to her husband? You know the feeling. First, you wonder what he's like. Then, meeting him, you think, "There must be a mistake; she couldn't prefer *him* to me!" That was my reaction to Clarence.

He was a thin, nervous chap of about thirty-five. Grey at the temples, straggly hair, pince-nez, and a drooping moustache. Compared to him, I felt like a giant. And Elizabeth? She's a tall, splendid princess-type. Honestly, I believe women do this out of pure stubbornness.

"How do you do, Mr. Pepper? Hark! Can you hear a mewing cat?" Clarence blurted out in one breath.

"Eh?"

"A mewing cat. I'm sure I heard one. Listen!"

Just then, the door opened and a white-haired elderly gentleman entered. Built similarly to Clarence but older, I guessed this was Mr. Yeardsley, Sr. Elizabeth introduced us.

"Father," Clarence said, "did you meet a mewing cat outside? I feel positive I heard one."

"No," said the father, shaking his head. "No mewing cat."

"I can't bear mewing cats," Clarence declared. "They get on my nerves!"

"A mewing cat is so trying," agreed Elizabeth.

"_I_ dislike mewing cats," confirmed old Mr. Yeardsley.

That was the extent of the feline discussion. They moved immediately back to pictures.

We talked art until dinner. Eventually, the topic of picture thefts arose. Someone mentioned the "Mona Lisa," triggering my memory of a newspaper clipping about a burglary at Dryden Park, near Midford, involving a stolen Romney. Seizing the opportunity, I retrieved the paper from my coat pocket.

"Here it is," I announced. "'Late last night burglars entered the residence of Sir Bellamy Palmer...'"

They all shouted "What!" in unison. Elizabeth snatched the paper.

"Yes. 'Dryden Park, Midford, Hants.'"

"Why, that's near here," I noted. "I passed through Midford—"

"Dryden Park is only two miles from this house," Elizabeth said, her eyes sparkling.

"Only two miles!" she exclaimed. "It might have been us! It might have been the 'Venus'!"

Old Mr. Yeardsley leaped from his chair. "The 'Venus'!"

Everyone was thrilled. My minor news drop had made a huge impact.

Later, after dinner, while Bill and Clarence rolled on the half-size table (with its pink silk patch) and my father wrote letters, Elizabeth called me aside.

"Reggie," she said softly.

The air changed instantly.

"What-o?" I asked nervously.

"Reggie," she continued, turning her back to tend the fire, "do you remember once saying you'd do anything in the world for me?"

There! That was the cheek of it. After everything, one expects closure, not demands.

I had indeed said I'd do anything for her, but that was pre-Clarence. Once a man is spurned and replaced by a man who is clearly a "blighter," the knight-errant spirit fades.
