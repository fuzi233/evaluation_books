Back on the boat, with other people’s voices skimming over the water like thrown pebbles, Archer found a calmness settling in him—an unasked-for tranquillity that both steadied and shamed him. By any measure his day had ended in absurd defeat: he had not pressed his lips to Madame Olenska’s hand, had not drawn from her a single promise of “another time.” And yet—sick as he was with longing, and leaving for an indefinite while the one face that made the rest of life look like a dimly lit room—he felt almost comforted.

It was the way she had held the scales. Not by cleverness, not by the practiced poise of a woman playing to an audience, but by something raw and exacting in her sincerity—her tears and her broken phrases proving how little it was calculated. She had kept, without boasting of it, a balance between loyalty to others and truth to themselves, and now that the danger of some irrevocable word had passed, the very restraint filled him with a kind of tender awe. He found himself grateful—grateful, even, that no vanity had prompted him to “play” the lover in a scene where witnesses might later retail the performance. What remained of their meeting felt, strangely, like a saving and not a loss.

Even after they had clasped hands at Fall River and he had turned away alone, that conviction walked beside him. He wandered back to the club and sat in the deserted library, turning the hours over and over, as one worries a remembered taste on the tongue—trying to locate where sweetness ended and bitterness began, and why the mingling still sustained.

The more he examined it, the clearer it grew: if she chose at last to return to Europe—return to her husband—it would not be because that old life tempted her, even with new terms offered. She would go only if she felt herself becoming a temptation to him: an invitation to let go of the very standard they had, with such pain, set up between them. Her choice, he saw, would be to remain near him as long as he did not ask her to come nearer; and it depended on him to keep her there—safe, and kept apart.

Those thoughts followed him into the train, enclosing him in a kind of golden haze through which the faces of other travellers looked remote, as if heard through a closed door. The rhythm of wheels and rails made its own dull, iron music—a hard, ceaseless industrial chant that swallowed up the softer human sounds. Archer, who in his recent hours with Ellen had begun to measure comfort by the quality of the silence between two voices, felt the brutality of that noise as a violation; and yet even it could not break the spell he carried.

The next morning, the spell thinned in the stifling reality of New York’s September heat. The station poured out its crowd of heat-withered faces, and Archer still looked at them through his lingering blur—until one face detached itself with an insistence that cut clean through his abstraction.

It was the young man he had noticed the day before in Boston, passing out of the Parker House: a face that did not “belong” to the American hotel world, that had about it the disciplined fatigue of Europe and the wary quickness of someone forever reading a room. Now the same foreignness struck Archer again; with it came a dim stir of association, like the half-heard refrain of a song in a dialect one has nearly forgotten.

The young man stood, valise in hand, blinking a little at the harshness of American travel, and then advanced toward Archer, lifted his hat, and said in careful English: “Surely, Monsieur, we met in London?”

“Ah—of course. In London!” Archer seized his hand, curiosity and sympathy rising together. “So you did get here, after all?” He looked with renewed wonder at the astute, haggard little countenance of young Carfry’s French tutor.

“Oh, I got here—yes.” M. Rivière smiled, but his lips seemed drawn over something tight. “But not for long. I return the day after tomorrow.” He stood holding his light valise in one neatly gloved hand, and his eyes had the anxious, perplexed look of a man who has been dropped into a place where even the simplest questions are answered with indifference.

“I wonder, Monsieur,” he began, “since I have had the good luck to run across you, if I might—”

Archer cut in, almost eagerly, as though grasping at the human scale of ordinary kindness after the strained delicacy of the day before. “I was just going to suggest it. Come to luncheon, won’t you? Down town—if you’ll look me up at my office I’ll take you to a very decent place in that quarter.”

M. Rivière looked genuinely touched, and surprised. “You are too kind. But I was only going to ask if you would tell me how to reach some sort of conveyance. There are no porters, and no one here seems to listen—”

“I know. Our stations must astonish you. When you ask for a porter they offer you chewing-gum.” Archer’s irritation at the scene around them sharpened his humor. “Come along—I’ll extricate you. And you really must lunch with me.”

The young man hesitated—so slightly Archer would hardly have noticed if he had not been watching the play of feeling beneath the Frenchman’s composure—then replied, with profuse thanks and a tone that did not quite convince, that he was already engaged. But once they had reached the comparative reassurance of the street, M. Rivière asked if he might call that afternoon.

Archer, with the midsummer leisure of the office before him, fixed an hour and scribbled his address. M. Rivière pocketed it with reiterated thanks and a wide flourish of his hat. A horse-car received him, its wheels making a gentler, more tolerable clatter than the iron uproar of the station, and Archer walked away.

At the hour named, M. Rivière appeared—shaved, smoothed, the careful surface restored—yet still unmistakably drawn and serious, as if some inner hunger could not be fed by mere grooming. Archer was alone in his office. Before accepting the chair Archer offered, the young man began abruptly:

“I believe I saw you, sir, yesterday in Boston.”

Archer was about to assent, but something in the visitor’s insistent gaze stopped him—the look of someone who has come to a door with a message that burns in his hands.

“It is extraordinary—very extraordinary,” M. Rivière went on, “that we should have met in the circumstances in which I find myself.”

“What circumstances?” Archer asked, crudely wondering if the man needed money.

M. Rivière studied him, as if testing where to place the weight of what he must say. “I have come, not to look for employment, as I spoke of doing when we last met, but on a special mission—”

“Ah—!” The two meetings clicked together in Archer’s mind, like the closing of a clasp. He paused, taking in the sudden light, while M. Rivière waited, as if aware he had said enough.

“A special mission,” Archer repeated at last.

M. Rivière opened his palms slightly, then let them fall, and the two men looked at each other across the desk until Archer roused himself. “Do sit down.”

M. Rivière bowed, chose a distant chair, and waited again with a restraint that felt less like timidity than discipline.

“It was about this mission that you wanted to consult me?” Archer asked, hearing his own voice for its steadiness and distrusting it.

M. Rivière bent his head. “Not in my own behalf. On that score I—I have fully dealt with myself. I should like—if I may—to speak to you about the Countess Olenska.”

Archer had known the words were coming; but when they came, his blood surged to his temples, as if a branch had whipped back and struck him in the face. He forced the next question out through the rush.

“And on whose behalf,” he said, “do you wish to do this?”

M. Rivière met the question sturdily. “Well—I might say hers, if it did not sound like a liberty. Shall I say instead: on behalf of abstract justice?”

Archer looked at him with an irony he did not feel. “In other words, you are Count Olenski’s messenger.”

A blush darkened on M. Rivière’s sallow face. “Not to you, Monsieur. If I come to you, it is on quite other grounds.”

“What right have you, in the circumstances, to be on any other ground?” Archer snapped. “If you’re an emissary, you’re an emissary.”

M. Rivière considered, turning his hat slowly in his gloved hands, as though reading his own fate in its worn lining. “My mission is over. As far as the Countess Olenska goes, it has failed.”

“I can’t help that.”

“No. But you can help—” He paused, looked down, then back at Archer with an appeal that did not beg so much as insist on being understood. “You can help, Monsieur, I am convinced, to make it equally a failure with her family.”

Archer pushed his chair back and rose. “Well—and by God I will!” he burst out. His hands went into his pockets; he stood staring down at the smaller man, wrath giving him an instant’s false clarity.

“Why the devil,” he went on, “should you have thought—since I suppose you’re appealing to me on the ground of my relationship to Madame Olenska—that I should take a view contrary to the rest of her family?”

M. Rivière’s expression changed as though the words had struck him physically. Timidity gave way to a distress so naked it disarmed Archer more than any argument could have done.

“Oh, Monsieur—”

“I can’t imagine,” Archer pressed, “why you should have come to me when there are others so much nearer to the Countess; still less why you thought I should be more accessible to the arguments I suppose you were sent over with.”

M. Rivière took the onslaught with a humility that seemed to come from some older world of formal obedience. “The arguments I want to present to you, Monsieur, are my own, and not those I was sent over with.”

“Then I see still less reason for listening to them.”

Again M. Rivière glanced into his hat, as if deciding whether Archer’s last words were dismissal enough. Then he spoke with sudden quiet decision.

“Monsieur—will you tell me one thing? Is it my right to be here that you question? Or do you perhaps believe the whole matter to be already closed?”

The steadiness of the question made Archer feel the coarseness of his bluster, as out of place as shouting in a room where bread is being kneaded. He reddened slightly, sat down again, and signed to the young man to do the same.

“I beg your pardon. But why isn’t the matter closed?”

M. Rivière’s gaze held anguish. “You do, then, agree with the rest of the family that, in face of the new proposals I have brought, it is hardly possible for Madame Olenska not to return to her husband?”

“Good God!” Archer exclaimed.

The murmur that left M. Rivière’s throat confirmed everything Archer had not known he feared. “Before seeing her, I saw—at Count Olenski’s request—Mr. Lovell Mingott, with whom I had several talks before going to Boston. I understand that he represents his mother’s view; and that Mrs. Manson Mingott’s influence is great throughout her family.”

Archer sat silent, feeling as though he clung to the edge of something sliding. Surprise—sharp, humiliating—came first: that he had been excluded not only from the family’s decisions but even from the knowledge that negotiations were going on. And then, with the surprise, recognition: the family had ceased to consult him because some deep tribal instinct warned them he was no longer on their side.

May’s voice came back with sudden clarity—an offhand remark during their drive home from Mrs. Manson Mingott’s on the day of the Archery Meeting: “Perhaps, after all, Ellen would be happier with her husband.” At the time he had protested with indignation; since then May had never named Ellen to him again. He saw now how that careless-sounding sentence had been a thin straw held up to learn the wind. His answer had been carried home, and after that he had been silently omitted from counsel.

He could even admire the discipline that made May bow to the decision. She would not have done so, he knew, had her conscience rebelled; but she probably shared the family’s conviction that a woman was “better off” as an unhappy wife than as a separated one. And there was no use, in their view, discussing such matters with Newland Archer, who had an awkward way of failing to take the most fundamental things for granted—as though he had begun to hear, beneath their polished speech, the harsher vocabulary of power it concealed.

He looked up and met M. Rivière’s anxious eyes.

“Don’t you know, Monsieur—is it possible you don’t know—that the family begin to doubt if they have the right to advise the Countess to refuse her husband’s last proposals?”

“The proposals you brought?”

“The proposals I brought.”

It was on Archer’s lips to say that whatever he knew or did not know was no concern of this young man. But something in M. Rivière’s humble tenacity—the courage of someone who had chosen to speak plainly in a language that could never quite carry what he meant—made Archer answer him with a question.

“What is your object in speaking to me of this?”

The reply came at once, as if it had been beating against M. Rivière’s teeth from the first moment.

“To beg you, Monsieur—to beg you with all the force I’m capable of—not to let her go back. Oh, don’t let her!”

Archer stared, astonishment cutting through his anger. There was no mistaking the sincerity of the distress, or the decision beneath it: the man had resolved to sacrifice everything but the necessity of this appeal.

“May I ask,” Archer said at length, “if this is the line you took with the Countess Olenska?”

M. Rivière reddened, but did not look away. “No, Monsieur. I accepted my mission in good faith. I really believed—for reasons I need not trouble you with—that it would be better for Madame Olenska to recover her situation, her fortune, the social consideration that her husband’s standing gives her.”

“So I supposed. You could hardly have accepted such a mission otherwise.”

“I should not have accepted it.”

“Well, then—?” Archer waited, and their eyes held each other in another long scrutiny.

“Ah, Monsieur—after I had seen her, after I had listened to her, I knew she was better off here.”

“You knew—?”

“Monsieur, I discharged my mission faithfully. I put the Count’s arguments, I stated his offers, without adding any comment of my own. The Countess was good enough to listen patiently; she carried her goodness so far as to see me twice; she considered impartially all I had come to say. And it was in the course of these two talks that I changed my mind—that I came to see things differently.”

“May I ask what led to this change?”

“Simply seeing the change in her,” M. Rivière replied.

“The change in her? Then you knew her before?”

Again that rising color. “I used to see her in her husband’s house. I have known Count Olenski for many years. You can imagine he would not have sent a stranger on such a mission.”

Archer’s gaze drifted past the young man to the blank walls of his office, and rested on the hanging calendar, with the rugged features of the President of the United States above its neat squares. That such a conversation should be going on anywhere within the millions of square miles under that stern gaze seemed as strange as any fiction. Outside, the city’s din pressed in—hoofbeats, wheels, shouted calls—each sound competing, each insisting, a marketplace of noise where even grief and love must raise their voices to be heard. Archer thought, with sudden bitterness, how their world’s language worked the same way: the dominant terms—duty, propriety, family—drowned out the smaller, truer words until one could hardly pronounce them without feeling indecent.

“The change—what sort of a change?” he asked.

“Ah, Monsieur, if I could tell you!” M. Rivière’s hands opened and closed on his hat as if he were trying to shape the unshapable. “Tenez—the discovery, I suppose, of what I’d never thought of before: that she’s an American. And that if you’re an American of her kind—of your kind—things that are accepted in certain other societies, or at least put up with as part of a general convenient give-and-take—become unthinkable. Simply unthinkable.” He paused, then continued with a gravity that felt like a condemnation. “If Madame Olenska’s relations understood what these things were, their opposition to her returning would no doubt be as unconditional as her own; but they seem to regard her husband’s wish to have her back as proof of an irresistible longing for domestic life. Whereas it’s far from being as simple as that.”

Archer looked again at the President’s face, then down at the papers on his desk—the ordinary detritus of an ordinary day. For a few seconds he could not trust himself to speak. In that silence he heard M. Rivière’s chair scrape back; the young man had risen.

When Archer looked up, he saw that M. Rivière was as moved as himself, his careful composure strained thin.

“Thank you,” Archer said at last, simply.

“There is nothing to thank me for, Monsieur. It is I, rather—” M. Rivière broke off, as if his throat refused the rest. Then he steadied his voice. “I should like, though, to add one thing. You asked me if I was in Count Olenski’s employ. I am, at this moment. I returned to him a few months ago for reasons of private necessity—such as may happen to anyone who has persons, ill and older persons, dependent on him. But from the moment I have taken the step of coming here to say these things to you, I consider myself discharged; and I shall tell him so on my return, and give him the reasons. That is all, Monsieur.”

He bowed and drew back a step.

“Thank you,” Archer said again.

Their hands met—Archer’s warm with a feeling he could not yet name, M. Rivière’s enclosed in its glove like something protected and already half-withdrawn—and the young man turned to go, leaving Archer alone with the city’s noise at his window and the taste of a truth that no family phrase could properly translate.
