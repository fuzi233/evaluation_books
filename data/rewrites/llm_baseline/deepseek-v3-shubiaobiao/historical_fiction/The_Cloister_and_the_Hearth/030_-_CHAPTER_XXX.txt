Gerard found himself in the company of a monk whose hands were as skilled as his soul was humble. The man had crafted the great sundial in the prior’s garden, a wheel for drawing water, and even a winnowing machine for grain—ingenious works that spoke of a mind ever busy with creation. Now he labored over a set of *regalles*, a small organ meant for the choir. Gerard, who knew only the rudiments of the psaltery, listened in awe as the monk played with a grace that seemed divine, gently exposing his lack of skill. Yet when it came to illumination, Gerard’s own hand was the finer.

There was an earnest simplicity in their exchange, the kind that bridges youth and age. The monk pressed Gerard to stay the night, and after a glance at Denys—who agreed with a resigned shrug—Gerard accepted. He spoke of their journey, their destination, and the perils they had faced, though he softened the tale of the bolster.

“Ah,” sighed the old monk, shaking his head, “I too was once a traveler, yet no rogue ever troubled me.” He urged Gerard to avoid inns—haunts of sin and danger—and instead seek refuge each night in the quiet safety of monasteries. Then, with sudden sharpness, he asked, “How long since you were shriven?”

Gerard flushed. “More than a fortnight.”

“And you an exorcist!” The monk clicked his tongue. “No wonder misfortune found you. You must be absolved at once.”

Gerard knelt eagerly, but the monk waved him off. “Not to me! I am as worldly as any man alive. My soul clings to these wooden pipes and leather stops—vanities that perish. Go to Brother Anselm.”

He directed Gerard down the corridor but then hissed in alarm when the young man nearly knocked at the wrong door. “Not there! Not there!” he whispered urgently, flapping his hands as if shooing away demons. Only when Gerard moved to the next cell did the monk relax, vanishing back into his own with a satisfied nod.

Brother Anselm was a figure of quiet dignity, his face a blend of wisdom and kindness. As Gerard confessed, his artist’s eye could not help studying the monk’s noble features, wondering if he might later sketch them. The penance was light: three kisses upon the lowest altar step, three paternosters, and a credo. When Gerard returned, Anselm tended to his wound with a cooling plaster, speaking of medicine and faith.

“We Dominicans seldom bleed now,” he mused. “The Vulgate calls blood the life of a man. Simples and herbs serve us better—God’s own remedies. And when earthly means falter, we call upon the saints.”

Gerard ventured a question. “Do the relics themselves hold power, or is it faith that works the cure?”

Anselm smiled. “Who can say? The woman who touched Christ’s robe was healed by faith—yet had she not touched it, would she have been cured? These mysteries are not for us to unravel. Only believe.”

With a final blessing, the monk sent Gerard on his way, leaving him strangely mournful. *How sad,* he thought, *to meet a kindred spirit only to part at once, never to cross paths again.*

The refectory brought practical aid: the friars mapped their route, marking each monastery along the way. That night, the old monk chuckled as he revealed why he had been so frantic in the corridor. “You nearly fell to Brother Jerome—a saint, but stern. He’d have sent you barefoot to Loretto—or worse, Compostella!”

One last strain of the psaltery, sweet and solemn, lulled Gerard to sleep, his heart eased.

The days that followed were quiet, marked only by steady progress. No brigands barred their path, no storms waylaid them—just the wear of miles and the ache of tired feet. Monastery after monastery offered shelter, their doors ever open, their welcomes warm. The road stretched long, but step by step, Gerard and Denys pressed on, their faces bronzed, their shoes wearing thin, until at last the border of Burgundy drew near.
