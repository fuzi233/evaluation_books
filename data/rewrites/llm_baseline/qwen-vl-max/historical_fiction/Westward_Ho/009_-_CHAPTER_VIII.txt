“It is virtue, yea, virtue, gentlemen, that maketh gentlemen; that maketh the poor rich, the base-born noble, the subject a sovereign, the deformed beautiful, the sick whole, the weak strong, the most miserable most happy. There are two principal and peculiar gifts in the nature of man—knowledge and reason: the one commandeth, and the other obeyeth. These things neither the whirling wheel of fortune can change, nor the deceitful cavillings of worldlings separate, nor sickness abate, nor age abolish.” —*Lilly’s Euphues*, 1586.

Now it falls to me to tell how the noble brotherhood of the Rose came into being—a fellowship so chivalrous and true that within a few years it earned fame not only in its native Devon but also across distant lands: Ireland, the Netherlands, the Spanish Main, and deep into the heart of South America. If this tale seems to some overly romantic or fanciful, let them remember that the men who spoke and lived thus were no dreamers. They were seasoned soldiers, shrewd politicians, and men of honor. The author of *Arcadia* was at once a diplomat of rare cunning; the poet of *The Faerie Queene* penned *The State of Ireland* with equal insight. And if they mock *Euphues* as mere affectation, I ask only: have they read it? For though it may be long-winded at times, it is still a bold, righteous, and pious work—worthy of any man's attention. That such a book, alongside *Arcadia*, was the favorite reading of Elizabethan youth speaks volumes about the spirit of the age.

Sir Walter Scott, in his clever satire of Sir Piercie Shafton, mocked the Euphuists and their affected manners—yet he placed them in an era too late for truth. His knight speaks of Sidney’s quarrel with Lord Oxford years before it occurred. Worse, Sir Piercie belongs not to Sidney’s time, but to the decadent days after Elizabeth’s death, when folly ran rampant under James. But we need not dwell on such errors. Let those who have never read *Euphues* know this: if a father could raise a son in the manner of Lilly’s Ephoebus, saving both his virtue and his purse, all fathers—even in our modern age—would bless him. Instead of ridiculing these gallants, let us see in them our own ancestors—men of fiery youth, yet deeply principled. In their courage, their wit, their reverence for law, and their sense of national purpose, we find the roots of our own character. Their chivalry was not mere posturing, but a reflection of the tender mercy that has always walked beside English valor. Even their passion for foreign literature and customs reveals a mind open to learning—without losing its soul.

So now, to my story. If it does not please you, turn the page. There is plenty more where this came from.

Amyas could not sail the next day, nor the day after. A southwester blew hard, nearly a gale, straight into the bay. With the *Mary Grenville* safely moored in Appledore pool, waiting only for a shift in wind, Amyas returned home. When his mother set out for Clovelly—riding behind the old serving-man to visit Frank, who lay wounded—he went with her as far as Bideford. There, descending the High Street, they met a procession of horsemen led by Will Cary. He rode cap-a-pie in shining armor, sword at his side, helmet tucked at the saddlebow—such a gallant sight that every woman in the town leaned from door and window to watch.

Behind him followed four or five sturdy servants on country ponies, carrying lances, baggage, longbows, swords, and bucklers. Bringing up the rear, in a horse-litter, was Master Frank himself—much to Mrs. Leigh’s delight. He declared his wounds were nothing more than flesh cuts—the dagger had glanced off his ribs. He insisted on seeing his brother one last time, and asked to stay not at Burrough, but at the Ship Tavern near the bridge, close to Cary. That was settled at once. He took a couch, held court in state, and was mobbed by all the townsfolk—though not without whispered rumors of who had brought him to such a sorry pass.

But while Frank rested, he and Amyas hatched a plan—put into motion the very next day, market-day. First, the innkeeper, under Amyas’s orders, began a feast unlike any seen before at the Ship Tavern—roasting, boiling, frying, and preparing enough food to feed a small army. Then Amyas, stepping into the market, invited each of his old schoolfellows—those Frank had pointed out—to a “merry supper” and a good “rowse” afterward. One by one, the admirers of Rose Salterne arrived, unaware they were walking into a trap. Each found himself seated at the same table with six rivals—none of whom he had spoken to in half a year.

Yet none showed it. They were too well-bred to betray their feelings. The Leighs, knowing full well what was happening, arranged the guests with care—Frank lying on his couch at the head of the table, Amyas at the foot. By filling every mouth with good food, they kept tongues quiet until wine loosened hearts and lips.

Meanwhile, Amyas and Frank ignored the silence with cheerful indifference. They joked, told stories, made themselves so entertaining that even Will Cary, usually grave, soon found himself laughing. He tried to make Mr. Coffin laugh—only to get a stiff nod. He tried Mr. Fortescue—only a frown. Undeterred, he turned his wit on the waiters, driving them from the room in fits of laughter.

So far, so good. When the cloth was drawn and sack and sugar became the order of the day, and “Queen and Bible” had been drunk with due ceremony, Frank made his move.

“I have a toast,” he said. “To the gentlemen of the Irish wars—and may Ireland never lack a St. Leger to stand by a Fortescue, a Fortescue to stand by a St. Leger, and a Chicheстер to stand by both.”

This called for the healths of the three families, and their thanks, and compliments exchanged. The ice cracked a little. Young Fortescue then proposed, “Amyas Leigh and all bold mariners!” To which Amyas replied warmly: “I wish for no better fortune than to sail around the world again with you, fellow adventurers—and give the Spaniards another taste of the men of Devon.”

By now, the wine had done its work. Lips that had been silent began to speak. Conversations sparked. Men talked like rational Englishmen to the man beside them.

“And now, gentlemen,” said Frank, sensing the moment was ripe, “let me offer a toast none of you will refuse—with heart and soul, as well as with lips. A toast to one whose beauty and virtue have raised her above birth, so that her lowly origin is lost in the light she casts. A toast to one so peerless in loveliness that I would name her queen of beauty—were it not that each of you has sisters who might well challenge her for Venus’s girdle. Yet what else can I say, when those very ladies—whose mirrors must judge her better than I—have themselves proclaimed her the fairest? If the goddesses debated the golden apple, even Paris must step down from the throne. Gentlemen, your hearts already echo my words. Drink—‘The Rose of Torridge!’”

If the Rose of Torridge herself had entered the room, she could not have caused greater astonishment. Every man flushed, paled, flushed again—glancing at one another as if to say, *“Who dares claim her? Raise your glass, and I’ll smash it!”*

But Frank, with quiet audacity, drank: “To the Rose of Torridge—and a double health to the worthy gentleman, whoever he may be, whom she chooses to honor with her love!”

“Well said, cunning Frank Leigh!” cried Will Cary. “None of us can argue now, however much we may grumble among ourselves. For each of us believes she loves him best. So we must accept that you’ve drunk our healths all round.”

“And so I have,” Frank replied. “And what better thing can you do, gentlemen, than drink each other’s healths in return? Show yourselves true gentlemen, true Christians, true lovers. For what is love, let me ask freely? It is the very breath of the Deity whose name is Love. The ancients knew this—when they made Eros the eldest of gods, who brought harmony from chaos. How, then, can lovers make him the father of strife? Can Psyche wed Cupid and bear a serpent’s egg? Can the soul be filled with divine love, only to burn with jealousy and distrust? True, the rose has thorns—but the nettle has poison. Cupid has his arrow—but he casts no scorpions. Venus is terrible when scorned, as the daughters of Proetus learned—but her handmaids are the Graces, not the Furies. He who loves rightly will not only find love lovely—but become lovely himself.”

Frank paused, gasping slightly from his wounds. The room grew tense. A murmur rose from several young men—offended that he had presumed to speak for their hearts. Mr. Coffin, who had sat rigidly upright, staring at the wall, rose silently and began to leave.

But Frank’s courage never failed.

“Mr. Coffin!” he called, his voice firm enough to stop him. “My dear sir—if my earnestness has overstepped the bounds of courtesy, I beg your forgiveness. Do not add to my sorrow the grief of losing a friend. Only hear me to the end—I know you will. And then, if you remain angry, I shall ask your pardon again.”

Truth be told, Mr. Coffin had never been to Court. He resented Frank’s polished speech, his fine clothes, his air of superiority. Being the eldest of the group, and only two years younger than Frank, he felt slighted by being grouped with boys barely eighteen. Had Frank shown arrogance, all would have been lost. But instead, he humbled himself—appealing to Coffin’s mercy.

That was enough. Coffin, a man of true heart, and one who had known Frank since childhood, sat back down.

“Believe me,” Frank continued, “I claim no superiority over you—no more than a sailor cast ashore claims to be better than his comrade still battling the waves. For I too—let me confess it, so that by sharing, I may win your trust—have loved, and still love, what you love. Do not start. Is it surprising that the sun which dazzles you dazzles me? That the lodestone which draws you draws me? Do not frown. I have come to love you for loving what I love—for admiring what I admire. Will you not try the same lesson? As easy as it is, and as blissful when mastered?

What binds subjects more closely than loyalty to the same queen? Brothers—than duty to the same father? Devout men—than worship of the same God? Then why should not worship of the same beauty bind lovers together? Why should each see in his rival not a foe, but a fellow-sufferer?

You smile and think: *Only one can possess her. Her joy is my poison.* Be it so—but I deny it. Shall we kill ourselves before death comes? Shall we make ourselves unworthy of her because we long for her? Shall we show ourselves faithful knights by harboring envy—the most unchivalrous sin?

Shall we believe, as Italians and Spaniards do, that we grow more beloved by becoming hateful to God and to each other? Will she love us more if we come to her with hands stained by the blood of him she loves best?

Let us recall ourselves. Our only hope of winning her—*if she is worth winning*—is to will what she wills, honor whom she honors, love whom she loves. If there must be rivalry, let it be in nobleness, in virtue. Let each strive to outdo the other in loyalty to our queen, in valor against her foes, in kindness to the afflicted. Thus, our love will prove its divine origin—by lifting us closer to the gods who gave it.

But I show you a higher way: charity. Why not make our shared love for her—a love I dare not name—into a sacred bond between us? Why not follow the ancient knights, who, having one grief, one desire, one goddess, believed one heart was enough to hold it all? Who united in friendship so closely they became one soul in two bodies—living only for her, vowing to honor her choice, to find their joy in hers, and to serve faithfully the man she chose, as I—though I hope to be surpassed—promise here to be the loyal friend and devoted servant of him who wins the love of the Rose of Torridge.”

He stopped. Silence fell.

At last, young Fortescue spoke.

“I may be paying you a backhanded compliment, sir—but it seems you’re so likely to become your own faithful servant (even if you’ve already won the prize while we slept), that the bargain isn’t fair between a gay Italianist like you and us country swains.”

“You undervalue yourself and your country, sir. But rest assured—I know no more of her mind than you do. Nor shall I. For my peace, I have vowed not to see her, nor hear news of her, for three full years. Dixi?”

Mr. Coffin rose.

“Gentlemen, I may yield to Mr. Leigh in eloquence—but not in generosity. If he leaves these parts for three years, I do likewise.”

“And go in charity with all mankind,” said Cary. “Give me your hand, old fellow. If you’re a Coffin, you were sawn from no weak elm, but solid oak. I’m going too—as Amyas knows—to Ireland, to cool my hot liver in a bog, like a jack-hare in March. Come, give me thy neif, and let us part in peace. I meant to fight thee today—”

“I should have been honored, sir,” said Coffin.

“But now I am all love and charity. May I beg pardon of the world—and thee in particular? Does anyone want to pull my nose? Send me an errand? Borrow five pounds? Buy me a horse—though that’d be like giving ten? Come! Join hands all round, and swear eternal friendship—as brothers of the sacred order of the—of what? Frank Leigh? Open thy mouth, Daniel, and christen us!”

“The Rose!” said Frank quietly—seeing his spell was working, and determined to carry it forward.

“The Rose!” cried Cary, seizing Coffin’s hand in one and Fortescue’s in the other. “Come, Mr. Coffin! Bend, sturdy oak! ‘Woe to the stiffnecked and stout-hearted!’ says Scripture.”

And somehow—whether it was Frank’s words, Cary’s humor, Amyas’s wine, or the nobleness that lies in every young heart when stirred—each man stepped forward, shook hands, and swore upon the hilt of Amyas’s sword to put aside jealousy. They vowed to stand by each other and by their lady-love, to take no offense if she danced with, flirted with, or married whom she pleased. And to spread the honor of their peerless dame and their new brotherhood across the world—equaling the fame of Angelica or Isonde of Brittany—they would each seek their fathers’ leave (easy enough in those bold times) to go abroad wherever “good wars” raged—there to emulate the courage and courtesy of Walter Manny, Gonzalo Fernandes, Bayard, and Gaston de Foix. Why not? Sidney was Europe’s hero at twenty-five. Why not they?

Frank watched, smiling quietly—his eyes sparkling even when his lips were still—and said only: “Gentlemen, be sure you will never regret this day.”

“Regret?” said Cary. “I feel already as angelical as thou lookest, Saint Silvertongue. What was that sneeze?—the cat?”

“The lion, rather, by the roar of it,” said Amyas, diving behind the arras. “Why, there’s a doorway here! And—”

He rushed through, dragging out by the head—Mr. John Brimblecombe.

Who was Mr. John Brimblecombe?

If you’ve forgotten, you’ve done nearly what everyone else in the room had. But you may recall the fat boy, son of the schoolmaster, whom Sir Richard punished three years prior for tattling—by sending him not to Coventry, but to Oxford. That was the man. Now twenty-one, a bachelor of arts, he had studied what Oxford taught—more or less—and now lingered in Bideford, planning to return after Christmas to study divinity, hoping to become a parson and shepherd of souls in his homeland.

Jack Brimblecombe looked, in person, like a pig—but not just any pig. Not like the squat, shapeless Devon pigs of the time—nor the lanky German monsters wallowing in streams, nor the wiry wild boars still roaming Swinley Down and Braunton Woods. No. Jack was a prophecy: in face, figure, and complexion, the forebear of Fisher Hobbs and the triumphs of science. A Fisher Hobbs’ pig of twelve stone, standing on its hind legs—that was Jack, and nothing else.

If you don’t know what a Fisher Hobbs is, reader, you know nothing of pigs—and deserve no bacon for breakfast. But such was Jack. The same plump mulberry skin, dotted with scattered black bristles; the same sleek hide, always seeming ready to burst; the same short, toddling legs; the same dapper curve in the small of the back; the same cracked squeak; the same low forehead and tiny eyes; the same round, self-satisfied jowl; the same sensitive, cocked nose—always searching for a savory scent, yet content with the worst. A pig of serene self-help, as Jack was—fatting fast while others starved.
