For generations, the Dashwoods had anchored themselves firmly in Sussex, their domain centered upon Norland Park. It was a substantial estate, managed with such impeccable respectability that the local gentry held them in high regard. The late proprietor, a bachelor of considerable longevity, had spent his twilight years under the stewardship of his sister until her passing a decade prior. Her death necessitated a restructuring of his household; he welcomed his nephew, Henry Dashwood—the legal heir and designated beneficiary of Norland—along with his wife and their brood. The elder gentleman found profound solace in this new arrangement. The attentions of Mr. and Mrs. Henry Dashwood, driven less by obligation than by genuine benevolence, provided him with the dignity his age demanded, while the vivacity of the children enlivened his days.

Mr. Henry Dashwood's lineage was distinct: one son from a previous union, and three daughters from his current marriage. The son, a steady and respectable youth, had already secured his future through his mother's substantial dowry, augmented further by his own matrimonial alliance. Consequently, the inheritance of Norland held little weight for him compared to his sisters. Their financial prospects were precarious; their mother possessed nothing, and their father held merely seven thousand pounds in liquid assets. The remainder of his first wife's fortune was entailed strictly to the son, leaving the father with only a life interest.

When the old gentleman finally breathed his last, the reading of his will delivered a mixture of relief and crushing disappointment. He was neither cruel nor ungrateful to deny his nephew the title outright; instead, he devised a clause that effectively neutered half the estate's value. Mr. Dashwood had coveted Norland primarily for the sake of his wife and daughters, yet the will stipulated that the bulk of the property be preserved exclusively for his grandson—a toddler of four. This minor, with his halting speech, insatiable demand for autonomy, and penchant for noisy mischief, had charmed his uncle during occasional visits, overshadowing years of devoted care from his aunt and nieces. Yet, the uncle's intent was not malice. As a gesture of affection toward the three girls, he bequeathed them one thousand pounds apiece.

Mr. Dashwood's initial despair was acute, but his temperament remained sanguine. He reasoned that, given his health and the potential for economic management, he could accumulate a significant reserve from the estate's produce before his own demise. However, fate proved swift. Within twelve months, he succumbed to illness. Ten thousand pounds, inclusive of the legacies, became the sole inheritance for his widow and daughters.

Upon receiving news of his father's critical condition, Mr. John Dashwood was summoned. In his dying moments, the patriarch urged his son with the gravity of impending mortality to ensure the comfort of his mother-in-law and sisters. Mr. John, lacking the visceral empathy of his kin, was nonetheless moved by the solemnity of the request and pledged to exert every effort to provide for them. Satisfied by this assurance, the father passed peacefully, leaving Mr. John the burden of calculation. Though not inherently malicious—merely cold-hearted and self-interested—he was a man of propriety, well-regarded for his adherence to duty. Had he wed a woman of greater warmth, he might have transcended his limitations; yet, marrying young and enamored with his bride, he found himself mirrored by her. Mrs. John Dashwood was a caricature of his own reserve: narrower in mind and sharper in selfishness.

While promising his father a thousand pounds per sister, Mr. John privately believed he could fulfill this vow. The prospect of augmenting his annual income to four thousand pounds, alongside the residual half of his mother's fortune, ignited a false sense of generosity. "Three thousand pounds," he rationalized throughout the day and subsequent weeks, "would be liberal, handsome, and sufficient to render them entirely secure. A sum I can spare with minimal inconvenience." He dwelt on this calculation without regret.

Yet, before the funeral rites concluded, Mrs. John Dashwood arrived at Norland. Accompanied by her infant and retinue, she made no prior consultation with her mother-in-law. While the law granted her immediate possession of the house following her husband's death, her abrupt intrusion constituted a gross breach of etiquette. For a woman of Mrs. Dashwood's station, possessing only average sentiments, such rudeness was intolerable. Yet, Mrs. Dashwood harbored a code of honor so rigid and a conception of generosity so romantic that any slight, whether inflicted or endured, provoked an immutable revulsion.

Though never favored by her husband's family, Mrs. John Dashwood had previously lacked the opportunity to demonstrate her callous disregard for others' comfort. Mrs. Dashwood felt the sting of this ingratitude acutely, despising her daughter-in-law's behavior. Upon the latter's arrival, she contemplated abandoning the house permanently. Only the urgent counsel of her eldest daughter persuaded her to reconsider the propriety of departure, while her tender love for her three children ultimately anchored her to the scene, compelling her to endure the breach with her brother for their sake.

Elinor, the eldest, possessed a formidable intellect and composure that belied her nineteenth year, qualifying her as her mother's counselor. She frequently tempered her mother's impulsive grief, steering the family away from imprudence. Possessing an excellent heart, affectionate disposition, and strong feelings, Elinor understood the necessity of governing emotion—a lesson her mother had yet to grasp, and which one younger sister explicitly rejected.

Marianne, equally gifted in many regards, shared her mother's intensity but lacked prudence. Sensible and clever, she was eager in all things; her sorrows and joys knew no bounds. Generous, amiable, and captivating, she was everything except cautious. The resemblance between Marianne and her mother was striking. Elinor observed her sister's excessive sensibility with concern, whereas Mrs. Dashwood cherished it. Together, they indulged their mutual affliction, voluntarily renewing their agony. They sought out reflections that amplified their wretchedness, resolving to admit no consolation.

Elinor, though deeply grieved, retained the capacity for exertion. She consulted her brother, received his wife with due decorum, and strove to rally her mother and encourage forbearance. Youngest of the trio, Margaret was good-natured and well-intentioned, though having absorbed much of Marianne's romanticism without corresponding sense, she showed little promise of matching her sisters' maturity at thirteen.
