Ever in his soul, that larger justice which makes gratitude triumph over resentment—this was the mark of noble natures, those whose lives are wide and full, capable of deeper joy than mere intellect can grasp. Not the clever minds who magnify goodness until it crumbles into dust, unworthy even of a “thank you”—those are the devil’s friars, vowed to poverty in love and trust, yet forced to beg from a world that still holds some human worth.

Daniel Deronda had not told Gwendolen, as he left her, that he would not see her again for a long time—that he was going away. He chose his words carefully, lest Grandcourt misinterpret the significance of such a parting, and understand that it mattered deeply to her. But Daniel was indeed leaving under circumstances so profound they stirred the deepest currents of his being. When he set out to keep his promise of visiting her, he was already wrapped in a solemn emotion, one that called back the most sacred moments of his life.

Sir Hugo had summoned him urgently to his chambers with a note: “Come immediately. Something has happened.” The warning had brought a strange relief when Daniel entered the baronet’s study and found not distress, but quiet affection awaiting him.

“It is nothing to grieve you, sir?” Daniel asked, his voice steady with renewed confidence rather than inquiry, as he took the hand offered to him.

There was something unusual in Sir Hugo’s gaze, a softness beneath the gravity, and his voice trembled slightly as he replied, “No, Dan, no. Sit down. I have something to say.”

“Not to grieve you, my boy,” the baronet continued, “no. At least, not more than it may grieve you too. But I never thought this—this very thing—would ever come to pass. There were reasons why I never prepared you for it. Reasons why I never spoke of your parentage. Yet I have always done what I could to shield you from harm.”

He paused, and Daniel could not speak. He could not say, “I’ve never felt it as harm.” Even if that were true, his voice failed him. More than either man knew rested on this moment—the breaking of silence, the unveiling of truth.

Sir Hugo had never seen Daniel’s face so pale, his lips pressed together in silent pain. With growing tenderness, he went on, as if afraid now of hurting him further. “I acted at your mother’s request. She wished secrecy. But now she wishes it ended. She wants to see you. I will give you this letter—read it when you’re ready. It tells you only what she asks, and where to find her.”

He handed Daniel a letter written on foreign paper. Without hesitation, Daniel slipped it into his breast pocket—a small comfort, to be spared the immediate burden of reading.

The emotion on Daniel’s face moved the baronet deeply, visibly unsettling his composure. Sir Hugo struggled to continue.

And Daniel—his entire soul consumed by a question too heavy to voice—could not wait. This was a sacred moment. If he let it pass, he would lose forever the courage to ask and the grace to hear the answer.

For a while, both men sat in silence, their thoughts hanging unspoken between them. Then Daniel lifted his eyes and spoke, his voice trembling with reverence, careful not to hint at the years of unexpressed longing, the ache of withheld affection:

“Is my father still alive?”

The reply came swiftly, low and firm: “No.”

In the storm of emotions that followed, joy and sorrow mingled beyond distinction. For Sir Hugo too, new light had broken through the past. After a silence in which Daniel felt like a man whose old beliefs had vanished before he’d fully embraced another, the baronet said, almost in confession:

“Perhaps I was wrong, Dan, to take on this role. Perhaps I grew too fond of it—of having you all to myself. If I caused you pain that I might have eased, I ask your forgiveness.”

“The forgiveness has been there long ago,” Daniel answered softly. “The greatest pain has always been for someone else—someone I never knew—someone I am now about to meet. But it has never dimmed the love I’ve held for you. You have shaped much of the life I remember.”

And in that moment, without word or plan, the two men clasped hands—bound not by duty, but by truth, by shared grief, and by love.
