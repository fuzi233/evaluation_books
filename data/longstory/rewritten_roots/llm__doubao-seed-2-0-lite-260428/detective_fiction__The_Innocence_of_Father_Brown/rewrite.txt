Where the silver ribbon of dawn bleeds into the glittering green ribbon of the North Sea, the Harwich ferry bumped the quay and spilled a chattering swarm of passengers onto English soil. One man in the crowd drew no second glance, and had no wish to: his pale grey holiday jacket, crisp white waistcoat and silver straw hat strung with a grey-blue ribbon clashed only faintly with the sharp, official gravity of his lean, dark face, crowned by a neat, short black beard that looked distinctly Spanish, as if it belonged tucked under an Elizabethan ruff. He smoked a cigarette with the exaggerated stillness of a man pretending to laze. No one would have guessed that the grey wool hid a loaded revolver, the white waistcoat a top-tier Paris police identification card, and the straw hat one of the sharpest investigative minds on the continent. That man was Aristide Valentin, head of the Paris Sûreté, the most famous detective alive, travelling from Brussels to London to pull off the arrest of the century.

Flambeau was on English ground. Three nations’ police had tracked the colossal criminal across borders from Ghent to Brussels, then on to the Hook of Holland, and their best guess was he would use the chaos and crowds of London’s ongoing Eucharistic Congress to vanish into the throng. He could pose as a low-ranking clerk, a visiting secretary, any unassuming hanger-on. No one could be certain what Flambeau would do. No one ever was.

It has been decades now since that titan of crime abruptly vanished from public view, leaving the world as quiet as it was said to be after Roland fell. At the peak of his career (or the nadir, to be exact) Flambeau was a larger-than-life international figure as famous as the Kaiser. Every morning’s papers ran headlines of him evading the law for one spectacular crime by pulling off an even more outrageous second. He was a giant, reckless Gascon, and wild tales spread of his absurd, athletic sense of humour: how he once flipped a investigating magistrate upside down to “clear his overcluttered mind”, how he ran down the full length of the Rue de Rivoli carrying one uniformed policeman under each arm. For all his superhuman strength he rarely resorted to violence; his real speciality was ingenious, large-scale robbery, each heist wilder and more creative than the last. He founded the fake Tyrolean Dairy Company in London with no cows, no carts, no premises, serving a thousand paying customers simply by picking up the milk bottles left on his unsuspecting neighbours’ doorsteps and delivering them to his own subscribers. He once tricked a young woman into sending him every detail of her fortune hunting scheme by photographing his instructions infinitesimally small onto the glass slides of a microscope she kept at her desk. His tricks were often so simple they bordered on genius: legend says he snuck out at midnight to repaint every house number on an entire street just to divert a wealthy traveller into a trap. He definitely invented a portable fake post box, which he planted on quiet suburban corners to catch unwitting strangers dropping postal orders into it. And for all his six foot four frame, he was a world class acrobat, able to leap like a grasshopper and vanish into treetops like a monkey. Valentin knew perfectly well that catching Flambeau would be only the start of the adventure.

But he still had no idea how to find him.

There was one flaw Flambeau could never disguise no matter how brilliant his costumes: his absurd height. If Valentin spotted a 6 foot 4 apple seller, a giant grenadier, or even an exceptionally tall duchess, he would have arrested them on the spot. But there was no one on the ferry, no one on the train north from Harwich, who could possibly be Flambeau, any more than a house cat could be a disguised giraffe. The full list of fellow passengers came to six: a short railway official bound for the terminus, three stocky market gardeners picked up two stations out, a tiny widowed lady heading up from a small Essex town, and a very short Roman Catholic priest from an even smaller Essex village. Valentin almost laughed out loud when he laid eyes on the priest. He was the very essence of the flat, quiet East Anglian countryside: a round, dull face like a Norfolk dumpling, eyes as blank and unremarkable as the North Sea, and a pile of crumpled brown paper parcels he could barely keep hold of. The Eucharistic Congress had clearly dragged dozens of such mild, unworldly men out of their isolated parishes, blinking and helpless as moles dug up from the earth. Valentin was a strict, sceptical French atheist with no fondness for priests, but he felt nothing but pity for this one. The man toted a large shabby umbrella that kept slipping out of his grip and clattering to the floor. He could not seem to work out which end of his return ticket was up, and cheerfully explained to every passenger in the carriage that he had to be extra careful, because one of his brown paper parcels held a piece of solid silver “stuck with blue stones” that he was bringing to the congress. His soft, bumbling innocence amused Valentin all the way to Tottenham, where the priest somehow managed to get all his parcels off the train, then turn around to climb back on for his lost umbrella. Valentin even took pity to warn him, in good nature, that he should not go round telling strangers he was carrying valuable silver.

By the time the train pulled into Liverpool Street, Valentin was certain he had not missed his quarry. He headed straight for Scotland Yard to register his status and arrange backup if he needed it, lit a fresh cigarette, and set off wandering the London streets. Pacing past quiet residential squares beyond Victoria Station, he stopped dead. The square was a perfect, quiet little slice of London: tall, flat prosperous houses that looked almost entirely uninhabited, a patch of shrubbery in the middle as empty and isolated as a green Pacific islet. One side of the square jutted up higher than the rest, and built into its terrace was a tiny, out-of-place restaurant that looked as if it had wandered over from bohemian Soho. Potted dwarf plants lined its windows, striped lemon-yellow and white blinds were drawn half way down, and a steep flight of steps ran up from the pavement to its front door, at a sharp angle like a fire escape leading up to a first floor window. Valentin stood smoking, staring at that odd little restaurant for a long minute.

The most unbelievable part of miracles is that they really do happen. Clouds do sometimes drift together to form the distinct shape of a human eye. A tree will stand alone in the middle of a long, confusing journey shaped exactly like a question mark. Valentin, a fiercely logical Frenchman who trusted only hard, proven facts, knew better than most that wisdom has to prepare for the unforeseen. He had no leads. Flambeau could be anywhere: a tall tramp camping on Wimbledon Common, a head waiter at the Hotel Metropole, anyone. When he had no clues to follow, Valentin followed the irrational. He did not head for banks, or known criminal hideouts, or police safe houses. He wandered down empty terraces, blind alleys, lanes blocked with rubbish, crescent streets that looped pointlessly back on themselves. His logic was simple: if you have no clues, the odds are that the same odd little quirk that catches your eye now will have caught the eye of the criminal you are chasing. Something about that steep, strange flight of steps up to the restaurant, something about the quiet off-kilter charm of the place, pricked his intuition. He climbed the steps, sat by the window, and ordered a cup of black coffee.

It was mid-morning, and he had not eaten yet. Remnants of other guests’ breakfasts littered the table, reminding him he was hungry, so he added a poached egg to his order, and absentmindedly reached for the sugar basin to tip some white granules into his coffee. He lifted the cup to his lips, then set it down so fast the coffee sloshed over the rim. He had just taken a big sip of pure salt.

He stared at the vessel he had poured from. It was unmistakably a sugar basin, made for holding sugar as surely as a champagne bottle is made for holding champagne. He checked the rest of the table. The two salt cellars sitting next to it were full of white granules too. He tasted them. They were sugar. He looked around the room, newly alert for other traces of the same weird joke, and spotted only one oddity: a dark splotch of spilled liquid on one of the papered white walls. He rang the bell for the waiter.

The bleary, fuzzy-haired waiter hurried over, and Valentin asked him to taste the sugar, pretending to tease him about the restaurant’s supposed famous bad quality. The man took a sip, yawned, and instantly jolted fully awake.

“Have you and the staff got some joke you play every morning on guests, swapping the salt and sugar round?” Valentin asked dryly. “Does it never get boring?”

The stammering waiter swore no one at the restaurant meant any such thing, that it must be a terrible mistake. He picked up the sugar basin, then the salt cellars, looking more and more confused, before he made an excuse to run and fetch the proprietor. The proprietor inspected the vessels too, and looked just as bewildered.

Then the waiter suddenly blurted out, “I think – I think it was those two clergymen.”

“Two clergymen?”

“The two priests that came in first thing this morning, before we even took the shutters down,” the waiter said, pointing excitedly at the dark splotch on the wall. “They threw soup at the wall!”

Valentin stared. “Threw soup at the wall?”

“Yes!” the owner cut in, stepping forward to explain. “They were quiet, perfectly respectable men. One of them paid the bill and left almost at once. The other, who was the slower, more confused one, took ages to gather all his things together. Right before he stepped out the door, he picked up his half-full soup bowl and hurled the whole thing straight at that wall. We were both in the back kitchen, and by the time we ran out they were gone down the street. We only spotted them turn the next corner into Carstairs Street.”

Valentin was on his feet instantly, hat pulled on, walking stick in hand. In the total absence of clues, this was the first strange, obvious breadcrumb he had found. He paid his bill, pushed through the glass doors, and turned sharp into Carstairs Street.

His sharp detective eye caught a flash of something odd on a shop front half way down the road, and he doubled back. It was a greengrocer’s stall, piles of fruit and vegetables laid out on the pavement with handwritten price cards. The largest two piles were oranges and shelled nuts. The card sitting on top of the nuts read in bold blue chalk: *Best Tangerine Oranges, Two a Penny*. The card sitting on the oranges read *Finest Brazil Nuts, 4d a Pound*. Valentin smiled. He recognised this very specific brand of chaotic humour, very recently seen. He pointed it out to the red-faced, sullen shopkeeper loitering by the door.

The shopman did not smile back. He just swapped the cards to their right places.

“Forgive the odd question,” Valentin said, leaning lazily on his cane, “but what connects a couple of wrong labels at a greengrocer’s shop with two clergymen, one very tall and one very short, who passed by here earlier?”

The shopkeeper’s eyes bulged. He looked seconds away from lunging over his stall to grab Valentin by the collar. “If you’re a friend of theirs,” he snarled, “you can tell them from me that next time they knock my whole apple stack all over the road I’ll crack their stupid clerical heads, priest collars or no priest collars.”

“Ah,” Valentin said, sympathetic. “They spilled your apples?”

“One of the big ones did,” the shopman said. “Rolled every last one of them all over the pavement. I couldn’t chase after him, I had to stop and pick them all up.”

“Which way did they go?” Valentin asked.

“First left up that road, then straight across the next square.”

Valentin thanked him and vanished. On the other side of the square he stopped a passing policeman. “Urgent. Have you seen two clergymen in shovel hats, walking that way?”

The policeman chuckled. “Aye sir. One of them was so lost he stood right in the middle of the road like he didn’t know which way was up –”

“Which direction?” Valentin snapped.

“They hopped that yellow bus over there, the one that runs all the way to Hampstead.”

Valentin flashed his official police card. “Call two constables to come with me at once.” Before the slow-moving officer could protest, he was across the road, and a minute later he was joined on the pavement by an inspector and a plainclothes officer. They piled onto the top deck of the lumbering yellow bus.

“Sir, we could go three times faster in a taxi,” the inspector protested.

“True,” Valentin said calmly, lighting a fresh cigarette. “If only we knew exactly where we were going. When you are hunting a man whose path makes no logical sense, you do not get ahead of him. You follow him. Stop when he stops, wander when he wanders, slow to his pace. That way you see exactly what he saw, and spot exactly what he thought was strange.”

The bus crawled up the long roads of north London for what felt like hours, rolling past scruffy suburban taverns, bare scrubby patches of waste ground, the endless stretch of roads that seemed to unspool forever. Twilight was beginning to bleed into the sky when Valentin jolted upright, slapped both his officers on the shoulder, and yelled at the driver to stop.

They stumbled off the bus, confused, and followed his pointing finger to a large restaurant window built into the front of a grand gilt-trimmed public house. The whole window was made of frosted patterned glass – except right in the centre, where there was a huge, jagged, star-shaped black crack, as if someone had smashed it with a heavy blunt object.

“There,” Valentin yelled, waving his stick. “That is our next clue.”

“Proof of what?” the inspector said, bewildered. “Why on earth would this broken window have anything to do with the two priests?”

Valentin almost snapped his bamboo walking stick in half with frustration. “Proof? Good man, there is no proof. The odds are twenty to one this has nothing to do with them. But what else do we have? We can either follow this one wild chance or give up and go home to bed.”

They tramped into the restaurant, sat down for a late lunch, and Valentin called the waiter over, slipping him a huge tip when he paid. The waiter perked up instantly.

“Ah yes, sir, that smashed window,” he said, grinning. “Oddest thing you ever heard. Two gentlemen in black, two foreign priests, came in earlier for a quiet cheap lunch. One of them paid up and left straight away. The other was just heading out the door when I looked at the change he gave me and realised he’d paid more than three times the bill. I ran after him, held up the slip of paper to show him – and that’s when I realised I’d written 14 shillings on the bill by accident, not 4. He just smiled at me and said, ‘No worries, that extra will cover the window.’ ‘What window?’ I said. And before I could move, he whacked our front window straight through with his umbrella.”

The inspector cursed under his breath, wondering if they were chasing two escaped lunatics.

“I was so stunned I couldn’t even stop him,” the waiter said. “He walked straight out, joined his friend round the corner, and they ran so fast up Bullock Street I couldn’t catch them even when I gave chase.”

“Bullock Street.” Valentin was already running for the door, charging up the narrow thoroughfare after the two strange priests, his two bewildered backup officers trailing fast behind him.

Aristide Valentin, head of the entire Paris Police force, was late to his own dinner party. His earliest arriving guests were reassured at once by Ivan, Valentin’s long-serving confidential servant: an old man with a jagged scar across one cheek, his face nearly the same ashen gray as his heavy moustache, who sat permanently at a small desk in the front entrance hall. The walls of that hall were lined from floor to ceiling with antique blades, pistols, and even crossbows—no mere decor, for Valentin’s house was as peculiar as his reputation.

It was a weathered old stone structure tucked behind high walls, tall poplars nearly brushing the Seine’s edge a few paces behind the garden. Its most notable quirk, one Valentin had designed for his own safety, was this: there was no way to step out onto the public street except through that single guarded front door. The sprawling, meticulously tended garden had a dozen doors opening out from the house’s back rooms and conservatory, but its perimeter was ringed by a sheer, unclimbable smooth stone wall topped with iron spikes. It was a perfectly enclosed space, ideal for a man whose life’s work hunting criminals had earned him hundreds of sworn enemies.

Ivan informed the guests his master had phoned moments earlier, delayed no more than ten minutes tying off final formalities for a scheduled execution. Valentin hated the harsh end of criminal justice, but carried out his duties with unflinching precision: he was a ruthless hunter of guilty men, but spent far more of his considerable political influence lobbying for lighter sentences and better prison conditions across France and much of Europe. A prominent free-thinking humanitarian, he only ever fell short of warmth by holding mercy to the same cold, unforgiving standard he applied to justice itself.

When Valentin finally arrived home, he was already in tailored black evening wear with the red rosette of his Legion of Honour pinned to his lapel, dark beard streaked clean through with silver. He walked straight to his ground-floor study, which opened directly onto the back garden, carefully locked away his official case files in their iron safe, then paused a minute at the open French doors staring out at the grounds. A sharp, silver moon was tearing through the tattered remaining rags of a passing storm, and Valentin stared at it with a soft, uncharacteristic wistfulness—an odd flicker of premonition, for the greatest mystery of his entire career was about to land right on his own lawn. He shook the strange mood off almost at once, remembering his guests were waiting.

When he stepped into the drawing room, he spotted every member of his small party at once. There was Lord Galloway, the choleric British ambassador, his face round and russet like a ripe apple, wearing the blue sash of the Order of the Garter. Beside him was his slim, silver-haired wife Lady Galloway, and their daughter Lady Margaret Graham: a pale, sharp-featured pretty girl with elfin features and coppery red hair. The opulent, dark-eyed Duchess of Mont St. Michel was there too, with her two equally full-figured, dark-eyed daughters in tow. Dr. Simon, the eminent French scientist, sat polishing his glasses, his forehead crisscrossed with permanent parallel wrinkles from a lifetime of haughty eyebrow raises. Next to him was Father Brown, the quiet little Catholic priest from the small Essex parish of Cobhole, whom Valentin had met briefly on a recent trip to England. And standing a little apart from the rest, waiting to greet his host, was a tall man in a Foreign Legion uniform: Commandant O’Brien, an Irishman who had fled his country after a catastrophic run of bad debts, and who had known the Galloway family—especially Margaret—since they were children. The Galloways had greeted him stiffly, refusing to meet his eye.

None of them, though, was the guest Valentin had been most eager to welcome: Julius K. Brayne, the American multi-millionaire famous across two continents for dumping colossal sums of money on every untested new religious and philosophical movement that called itself “progressive.” Nobody could say for sure if Brayne was an atheist, a Mormon, or a Christian Scientist, but he would fund any weird new intellectual project under the sun, even a prize for the next great unrecognized American Shakespeare. He’d decided Valentin was “progressive” too, a compliment the great detective privately found deeply insulting.

Brayne himself lumbered into the room minutes later, as unmissable and decisive as a dinner bell: a huge broad-shouldered man, equally tall and fat, in unadorned black evening wear with not a single ring or watch chain for decoration, his white hair slicked back like a German professor’s, his round cherubic red face marked only by a single tuft of dark hair under his lower lip that gave him an oddly Mephistophelean air. He was immediately escorted to the dining room on Lady Galloway’s arm.

The rest of the dinner passed uneventful enough, save for Lord Galloway’s obvious simmering displeasure at O’Brien’s presence. His temper only boiled over after the women had left the table, and the three younger men—Dr. Simon, Father Brown, and O’Brien—drifted off to join the ladies or smoke in the conservatory. Left alone with Brayne (the credulous American who believed in every new religion) and Valentin (the hardened skeptic who believed in none), Lord Galloway grew more and more irritated by their endless tedious debate over “progressive” reform, until he finally stood up to storm back to the drawing room.

He got lost in the long twisty back passages for nearly ten minutes, until he heard the doctor’s didactic voice, then Father Brown’s soft murmur, followed by general laughter. When he pushed open the drawing room door, he noticed two empty spots at once: O’Brien was gone, and his daughter Margaret was gone too.

Panicked and furious, he stamped back down the passages, his paranoia that the disgraced Irish soldier was going to elope with his daughter rising to a fever pitch. He rounded the corner toward the back of the house and nearly ran straight into Margaret, who swept past him with a white, scornful face, too angry to even speak. If she wasn’t with O’Brien right now, where was he? He stumbled toward the back servant’s door leading to the garden, and stepped out under the now fully cleared moon, its sharp silver light flooding every corner of the lawn. There he saw O’Brien, his blue uniform glowing in the moonlight, striding fast toward the study French windows, before vanishing back inside the house.

Lord Galloway seethed. The moonlit garden, all soft silver grass and dark shadowed poplar trunks, felt like some taunting fairy-tale Watteau scene mocking his attempts to keep the lovers apart. He stormed forward to follow O’Brien, and tripped hard over something half-buried in the long grass. He glared down to snap at whatever stone or tree root had tripped him, then froze.

A minute later, the quiet garden was shocked to see the elderly British diplomat running as fast as his legs could carry him, bellowing at the top of his hoarse voice.

His shouts drew Dr. Simon out of the study, his glasses askew and his face pale. “There’s a corpse in the grass!” Galloway shrieked. “A blood-covered corpse!”

Valentin was already right behind the doctor, drawn by the noise. The second he heard what had happened, his face shifted instantly from polite host to sharp, focused professional detective: this was his trade, right on his own back lawn. The three of them followed Galloway back to the spot, where a thin river of mist was now curling up across the lawn from the Seine. The body lay face down in the deep grass: a very tall, broad-shouldered man, bald save for a few strands of brown hair clinging to his skull like wet seaweed, blood pooling dark under his face.

“He’s not one of our party,” Dr. Simon said, his voice low and strange. He knelt to check for a pulse, then gestured for Valentin to help him lift the body an inch off the grass. The severed head lolled loose, completely detached from the neck. Even Valentin flinched. “Whoever killed him must have been as strong as a gorilla,” he muttered.

Dr. Simon lifted the head gently. The face was unmarked save for a few shallow cuts around the jaw: heavy-jowled, yellow-skinned, hawk-nosed, looking like a wicked Roman emperor with a faint dash of Chinese royal brutality. None of the four men recognized him. The dead man was wearing formal evening clothes, his starched white shirtfront stained through with blood, as if he had been on his way to join a party. Valentin crawled on his hands and knees for twenty yards around the body, searching the grass for clues, but all he found were a handful of small, snapped twigs. Nothing else.

A sharp shout from Lord Galloway cut through the heavy silence: “Who’s that over by the garden wall!”

A small, slightly round figure wobbled out of the mist, looking for a second like a mischievous goblin before they realized it was Father Brown, the little priest they had left in the drawing room minutes earlier. “I say,” he said meekly, “you do realize there are no other gates leading out of this garden at all, right? No other exits, at all.”

Valentin’s dark brows drew together—he usually regarded priests with instinctive skepticism—but he couldn’t argue the point. “You’re right. Before we figure out who killed him, we first have to figure out how a total stranger even got into this walled, locked garden in the first place.” He turned to the others, his tone firm and authoritative. “As head of the Paris police, I’m invoking my discretion here. We have distinguished guests, a foreign ambassador, ladies in the other room. No one calls in the public police before we clear every single one of my own house guests of suspicion first. No one leaves this house before noon tomorrow. We have more than enough bedrooms for everyone. Simon, go get Ivan—he’s completely trustworthy, tell him to leave one servant on the front door and come meet us at once. Lord Galloway, go break the news gently to the ladies, make sure they don’t panic, and tell them they are all to stay put. Father Brown, you and I will stand guard over the body for now.”

His orders were obeyed instantly. Dr. Simon fetched Ivan, Galloway returned to the drawing room to break the news softly, and the priest and the atheist detective stood still at either end of the dead man, lit by the cold moon, like two opposing statues of death’s different philosophies.

Ivan came racing across the lawn, nearly giddy with the excitement of the case, and bent to look at the severed head. He almost dropped it. “Sir—do you know who this is?” he gasped. Valentin shook his head, and the two men carried the heavy corpse back to lay on a sofa in the study before the whole party gathered in the drawing room.

Valentin sat at the desk, pen in hand, his eyes as hard as a judge’s at a criminal assize. “Is everyone present?” he asked.

“Where’s Mr. Brayne?” the Duchess said, glancing around the room.

“And where’s Commandant O’Brien?” Lord Galloway added gruffly. “I saw him walking in the garden right before I found that body.”

“Ivan,” Valentin said, “go find them. Brayne is finishing a cigar in the dining room, I think. O’Brien was pacing in the conservatory.”

Ivan vanished. Valentin turned to Dr. Simon. “The man’s head was cut clean off. Could that have been done with an ordinary knife?”

“Not a chance,” the pale doctor said. “To make a cut that clean, severing the entire neck in one pass? You’d need a battle axe, a headsman’s old blade, or a two-handed medieval sword.”

“But there are no battle axes here!” the Duchess nearly shrieked, half hysterical.

“Could it have been done with a standard French cavalry sabre?” Valentin asked, still scribbling notes on his paper.

A soft knock rapped at the door, and the whole room went cold, as if they’d heard the knock in Macbeth. “A sabre—yes, that could do it,” Dr. Simon said quietly.

“Come in, Ivan,” Valentin said. The servant stepped in, followed by Commandant O’Brien, his uniform rumpled, his jaw set in defiant anger.

“What do you want with me?” O’Brien snapped.

“Please sit down,” Valentin said evenly. “You aren’t wearing your sword. Where is it?”

“I left it on the library table,” O’Brien said, his Irish brog thickening with irritation. “It was digging into my side, getting in the way.”

“Ivan, go fetch the Commandant’s sword from the library,” Valentin said. The servant vanished. “Lord Galloway says he saw you leaving the garden right before the body was found. What were you doing out there?”

O’Brien laughed sharply, flinging himself into a chair. “Admiring the moon! Communing with nature, if you like.”

Another knock came at the door. Ivan stepped back in, holding an empty steel scabbard. “This is all I could find, sir.”

A dead, heavy silence fell over the room. Then Lady Margaret stood up, her face pale but determined. “I can tell you what he was doing in the garden, since he won’t say. He asked me to marry him. I turned him down. I told him all I could offer him was my respect. He walked off alone to cool his head. I will swear on any Bible you like that he could not have killed that man.”

Lord Galloway hissed at her to be quiet, but she rounded on him, her eyes blazing. “You old fool. If he was in that garden murdering someone, where was I? Do you hate him so much you’d accuse your own daughter of covering for a killer?”

Before anyone could speak, a soft, confused little voice piped up from the corner. “Wait,” Father Brown said. “That cigar Mr. Brayne was smoking before we left the dining room. It was awfully long. Longer than a walking stick, almost. Shouldn’t he have finished it hours ago?”

Valentin’s head snapped up, a sharp look in his eye. “You’re absolutely right. Ivan, go check on Mr. Brayne. Bring him here at once, now.”

Ivan was back less than a minute later, his scarred face flushed. “Beg pardon, sir, but Mr. Brayne’s gone. Left the house. His hat and coat are missing too. I ran out to search the street, and I found something fifty yards up the road to Paris, thrown in the bushes.” He held up a long, glinting naked cavalry sabre, its edge and tip streaked dark with dried blood. “This is it. The Commandant’s sword. Right where Brayne dumped it when he ran away.”

If you ever spot a member of that hyper-exclusive society known as *The Twelve True Fishermen* ducking into London’s Vernon Hotel for their annual dinner, you’ll notice the second he shrugs off his overcoat: his evening jacket isn’t the standard formal black, it’s a distinct, deep forest green. If you’re bold enough (read: audacious enough to risk being blacklisted from every Belgravia social circle for the rest of your days) to ask why, he’ll say it’s so no one mistakes him for a waiter. You’ll leave that exchange feeling properly put in your place, but you’ll walk away carrying the seed of a secret no one in that rarefied crowd will ever breathe a word about: the night a small, unassuming priest named Father Brown stopped a legendary heist with nothing but a pair of attentive ears.

Father Brown would tell you that night was the luckiest break of his career. He never had to chase a suspect, never had to sift through a pile of broken alibis. All he did was listen to a strange, lurching pattern of footsteps down a dark hotel corridor, and he averted a violent crime, recovered a priceless set of stolen silver, and pulled a lost soul back from the edge of ruination before anyone at that fancy dinner had even finished their soup. It’s the kind of story no one in high society will ever admit happened, and no one in the slums where Father Brown usually worked would ever believe could happen. Lucky for you, I heard it straight from him.

The Vernon Hotel wasn’t like any other hotel in London. It was the ultimate symptom of an aristocracy so obsessed with good manners and exclusivity it had eaten its own logic backwards: a business that turned a profit not by welcoming every paying customer, but by turning 99% of them away. In a city run by plutocrats, shopkeepers and hoteliers quickly learn to be more snobbish than their richest clients. They invent arbitrary hoops to jump through, so their wealthy, bored patrons can spend fortunes and social capital proving they’re special enough to clear them. If someone opened a London hotel that only let men over six feet tall walk through the doors, high society would organize entire dinner parties of six-foot guests just to say they’d dined there. If a Michelin-starred restaurant only opened for three hours every Thursday afternoon, the line would stretch around the block before noon.

The Vernon Hotel sat tucked into a quiet corner of a Belgravia square, small, cramped, and inconvenient by every normal standard—and those very inconveniences were its biggest selling point. Most famously, it could only seat 24 diners at once. Its only large group table, the legendary terrace setup, sat out on a covered veranda overlooking one of the oldest, most immaculately kept private gardens in central London. You could only book that table in the warm summer months, of course, and the harder it was to get a reservation, the more desperate the richest people in England were to get one. The hotel’s owner, a sharp Jewish man named Lever, had made almost a million pounds out of that very exclusivity. It wasn’t all show, either: his wine cellar was stocked with the best vintages in Europe, his kitchen turned out meals finer than any three-Michelin-starred restaurant in Paris, and he hand-picked only 15 waiters total, each one trained for years to move so silently and smoothly you’d swear they were part of the wallpaper. It was easier to win a seat in Parliament than to get a job waiting tables at the Vernon.

The Twelve True Fishermen would never have dined anywhere else. They demanded absolute, unbroken privacy—they’d have thrown a fit if they’d even suspected a different club was eating in the same building. Every year for their annual dinner, they brought out their most prized treasure: a custom set of fish-shaped silver knives and forks, each handle set with one huge, perfect pearl, the official insignia of their society. It came out only for the fish course, the centerpiece of a meal that was already absurdly lavish. The club had no real mission, no public charity work, no stated purpose at all, which was exactly what made it so aristocratic. If you weren’t already born into that tiny, rarefied sliver of upper class life, you’d never even hear the Twelve True Fishermen existed. It had run for 12 years straight. Their president was the mild, well-connected Mr. Audley, and their vice-president was the young, popular Duke of Chester.

You’re probably wondering how a scruffy little slum priest like Father Brown ever ended up in a place like that. The story’s perfectly ordinary, really. That afternoon, one of the hotel’s Italian waiters had collapsed with a sudden stroke. Lever, a decent enough man who hated making a scene, had reluctantly agreed to send for the nearest Catholic priest to take the dying man’s confession. Father Brown showed up, mud on his boots and his coat threadbare at the cuffs, and once he’d finished hearing the man’s confession, he politely asked for a quiet room and some paper to write down a note the sick man had begged him to deliver, to right an old, unspoken wrong.

Lever was torn. He hated turning away a priest who’d come to tend to his sick staff, but letting a random stranger—especially one who looked as unkempt as Father Brown—loiter in the hotel the night of the Fishermen’s dinner was like dropping a smudge of dirt on a brand new white silk tablecloth. The Vernon never had random visitors. No one loitered in the lobby. There were 15 waiters on staff, 12 guests for the dinner, and that was it. An extra stranger wandering the halls would be as shocking as finding a random stranger sitting at your breakfast table in your own home.

Finally Lever struck a compromise. The hotel’s main entrance led down a short corridor lined with dim, expensive old paintings, then opened to a lounge. To your right, passages led to the public dining and event rooms. To your left, a second passage led to the kitchens and staff quarters. Right off that lounge, on the left, was a small glass-walled office the hotel management used to check guests in, and just past that office, on the way to the staff wing, was the men’s cloakroom. Sandwiched right between the office and the cloakroom was a tiny, windowless private room Lever usually used for sensitive business—lending a duke a thousand pounds if he was short on cash, or very politely turning down a would-be guest who didn’t meet the club’s standards. He let Father Brown use that tiny room to write his note, no questions asked. He locked the door from the outside, he said, to make sure no wandering waiter stumbled in and disturbed him, and promised he’d come let him out when he was done.

Father Brown scribbled away for ages, until the sky outside turned a deep bruised purple, the sun dipping below the London rooftops. The lights weren’t on in his little room, and the gathering gloom sharpened his ears, the way dark quiet sometimes does. Halfway through the last paragraph of his note, he realized his pen was moving in time with a sound drifting through the wall: a steady, repeating pattern of footsteps in the corridor outside.

It wasn’t the sound of a normal person walking down a hall. At first, there would be a rush of tiny, quick, light steps, like a man sprinting to win a footrace. Then the footsteps would stop dead, and shift to a slow, heavy, swaggering stamp, each step deliberate, drawn out, no more than a quarter of the number of steps as the fast burst, but taking just as much time. Then the fast patter would start up again, then the slow heavy steps, over and over. There were no other sounds in the corridor at all—no other guests, no other waiters, just this one pair of boots, that had a faint, familiar creak in the left heel, repeating that absurd, lurching pattern back and forth.

Father Brown leaned his head against the wall, trying to make sense of it. Why would a man run halfway down a corridor, then slow to a stroll for the other half? What possible reason was there for that? The slow, heavy step, he realized after a minute, didn’t sound like a waiter at all. It was the lazy, unhurried stamp of a man who’d never worked a day in his life, a wealthy gentleman used to everyone getting out of his way. Then that fast, silent patter—those were the footsteps of a waiter, moving quick and quiet so he wouldn’t disturb the guests.

Oh.
Oh, no.
The same man was pretending to be two people.
One man, switching back and forth between the slow arrogant walk of a Fishermen club member, and the fast silent scurry of a waiter, walking up and down the corridor so everyone working the dinner would get used to seeing both “the wealthy gentleman” and “the new temporary waiter” without ever noticing they were the exact same person.

By the time he put that together, the footsteps changed again. This time, they were running full tilt, fast and light as a panther bounding down the hall, the thief in full flight. Father Brown banged on the locked office door, got no answer, groped his way through the back door of his little room into the deserted cloakroom, and pushed through the half-door overcoat counter, just in time to see the man himself standing in the lamplight.

He was tall, dark, elegant, in a plain black evening suit that bulged oddly at the pockets, stuffed full of silver. When he saw Father Brown’s silhouette in the dim light, he tossed him a cloakroom ticket and called out that he needed his coat right away, he had to leave early. Father Brown handed it over, and the man threw him a half-sovereign gold coin, saying he had no small change.

“Actually, I think you do have silver in your pockets,” Father Brown said softly. “Quite a lot of it, in fact. More than two dozen pounds worth, all fish-shaped, all set with pearls.”

The man froze. He’d heard of Father Brown, of course—every criminal in Europe had. He was Flambeau, the legendary French thief, the man no safe in the world could hold, the man who’d snuck in that night to steal the Fishermen’s famous pearl-handled cutlery, walking back and forth all evening to sell the illusion that there was one extra guest and one extra waiter on the staff, so no one would notice the theft for hours. He vaulted over the counter, hand on Father Brown’s collar, ready to silence him, until the little priest said:
“I’m not a cloakroom attendant. I’m a priest, Flambeau. And before you do anything you’ll regret, I’m here to hear your confession.”

The great thief staggered back, sinking onto a bench, all the fight draining out of him.

Back out on the terrace, the Twelve True Fishermen had no idea any of this was happening. They’d finished their light, elegant soup course, picked their way through a dozen absurd little hors d’oeuvres no one could name, and were now sitting down to their famous fish course, the massive golden baked pudding heaped with every kind of luxury seafood. The young Duke of Chester dug his fork in, sighed, and said what every club member said every single year: “You can’t get anything like this anywhere else in the world.”
“Nowhere at all,” Mr. Audley, their president, agreed, nodding his grey head solemnly. “Not even the Café Anglais in Paris, I’m told. Nothing at all like it.”
“Overrated place,” grumbled Colonel Pound, speaking for the first time all evening.

And not a single one of them ever found out that the priceless set of pearl fish forks they’d been eating with, the treasure of their club, had almost walked out the front door right under their noses—all stopped by a scruffy little priest who’d done nothing but listen to queer, lurching footsteps in the dark.

In his quiet, morally upright old age, Flambeau would lean by a winter hearth and laugh, and tell any guest who would listen that the most perfect crime he ever pulled was also his very last. It fell at Christmas, a match for the season as perfectly framed as a sculptor’s marble group. “As a thief who thought of himself as an artist,” he would say, “I always matched my heists to their setting. Swindling a country squire felt right amid oak-panelled long rooms; a wealthy Jewish speculator was best left unexpectedly penniless under the glimmering screens of the Café Riche. When I relieved a greedy dean of his hoard, I wanted the soft grey towers and green lawns of a cathedral town as my backdrop. When I tricked that miserly Breton peasant out of his hidden gold, it gave me a peculiar satisfaction to see his furious, indignant face set against the straight line of clipped poplars and the wide, solemn Millet plains of northern France.

“My very last crime? It was pure Christmas, a cheery, cozy English middle-class misdeed straight out of a Dickens novel. I pulled it in that classic Putney suburban home you all know: curved carriage drive, stable out to one side, the house name painted on the two iron outer gates, and a gnarled old monkey-puzzle tree rising from the front lawn. I think my little Dickensian performance was so clever it’s a genuine shame I repented it before the night was out.”

Flambeau could tell the story from the inside, and even then it sounded strange. But from the outside, to any bystander, it was utterly inexplicable. That is where the story begins, on Boxing Day late afternoon, when the house’s front doors swung open and 19-year-old Ruby Adams stepped out, wrapped head to toe in thick brown furs, looking less like a society girl and more like a toddling baby bear, save for her bright, brave brown eyes. The winter sun was bleeding red over the bare rose beds, painting the empty flowerbeds with the ghost of summer blooms. She was scattering breadcrumbs for the birds for the fifth time that day, because the family dog kept gobbling the crumbs before the sparrows could get a bite.

She drifted unobtrusively down the laurel alley that ran between the house and the larger back garden, and rounded the corner into the dense evergreen plantation. She gasped, staring up at the 12-foot high garden wall, where a tall, lanky young man was straddling the top like he was riding a very high, wobbly horse. It was John Crook, the sharp young socialist journalist next door, dark hair sticking up like a bristle brush, a sallow pale face made all the more noticeable by the bright, aggressively red tie he seemed to care about more than the rest of his whole wardrobe.

“Oh don’t jump, Mr. Crook!” she called out, alarmed. “It’s far too high, you’ll break your legs.”

He ignored her entirely, and leapt clean over the edge, landing light as a grasshopper right at her feet. “I think I was born to be a burglar,” he said, completely placid. “I’d have made a great one, if I hadn’t had the bad luck to be born on the wrong side of that wall. I still can’t see what’s so wrong about climbing over it, anyway.”

“How can you say such dreadful things!” she protested.

“Well,” he grinned. “If you’re born stuck on the wrong side of the fence, climbing over can’t be a sin, can it?”

“I never know what you’re going to say or do next,” she told him.

“Neither do I, half the time,” Crook laughed. “But right now? I’m definitely on the right side.”

“And which side is the right side, exactly?” she teased, smiling.

“Whichever side you’re on,” he said.

Just then, a car horn blared three times, getting louder by the second. A sleek, pale green luxury motor swept up the drive like a migrating bird, pulling to a throbbing stop right on the doorstep.

“Hello, hello!” Crook said. “Now there’s someone who was definitely born on the right side of every wall, isn’t he? I didn’t know your Santa Claus was this modern, Miss Adams.”

“Oh that’s just my godfather, Sir Leopold Fischer,” Ruby said. “He always comes to visit on Boxing Day. He’s very kind,” she added after a tiny, unenthusiastic pause.

John Crook knew the eminent City financier well, even if the financier had never heard of him: he’d written several scathing articles about the millionaire’s labour practices in socialist papers *The Clarion* and *The New Age*. He said nothing, though, and watched grimly as the chauffeur in his neat green uniform and the small, precise valet in grey helped unload Sir Leopold from the car like he was a very fragile, carefully wrapped parcel. They peeled off layers of thick fur rugs, sable wraps, rainbow-hued silk scarves one by one, until the little old gentleman himself was revealed: grey goat-like beard, foreign friendly face, rubbing his huge fur gloves together with a wide beaming smile.

Before they were done unwrapping him, Colonel Adams, Ruby’s sunburnt, quiet ex-military father who wore a red smoking cap that made him look like an Egyptian Pasha, had come out to invite his guest in. Beside him was his wife’s younger brother James Blount, a big, boisterous yellow-bearded Canadian farmer who’d only just arrived in England a week prior, after missing his sister’s funeral by seven days. Trailing quietly behind all of them was Father Brown, the local Catholic priest the family had invited over for Christmas, since Ruby’s late mother had been a devout Catholic. He was completely unassuming, plain even, no one would have guessed he had a knack for untangling the messiest of crimes.

The entrance hall was absurdly wide, huge enough for all of Sir Leopold’s wraps and ten more guests besides, stretching from the front doors all the way to the foot of the staircase. When they’d finished shedding the last of the furs by the roaring hall fire, where the Colonel’s old cavalry sword hung over the mantel, Sir Leopold fished deep into the inside pocket of his tailcoat and pulled out a small, black oval case, grinning proudly. He flipped the case open with a click, and the whole room half-blinded, like someone had just uncorked a crystal fountain that spurted light straight into their faces. Resting on a bed of thick orange velvet were three brilliant white diamonds, glowing so bright they seemed to set the air around them on fire.

“These are the Flying Stars,” Sir Leopold said, preening at the looks of awe on everyone’s faces. “They’re the famous African diamonds, stolen and recovered more times than anyone can count. Every top criminal in the country is hunting them. I was half terrified I’d get robbed on the drive over here.”

“Natural enough,” Crook growled, leaning against the wall. “I don’t blame them one bit. When you beg for bread and get nothing at all, why shouldn’t you take a nice shiny stone for yourself?”

“Don’t talk like that!” Ruby protested, laughing. “You only started saying these silly things since you became a… a what’s-it-called, the man who wants to be friends with the chimney sweeps?”

“A saint,” Father Brown supplied softly.

“I think Ruby means a Socialist,” Sir Leopold said, with a supercilious little smirk.

“A radical doesn’t live on radishes,” Crook huffed. “A Conservative doesn’t spend all day preserving jam. And a Socialist, I promise you, doesn’t want nothing more than to go have tea with the chimney sweep. We just want all the chimneys swept, and the sweeps properly paid for their work.”

“All right, but a Socialist won’t let you own your own soot, will he?” Father Brown put in, his eyes twinkling.

Crook blinked, interested. “Who on earth would want to own soot?”

“Gardeners use it as fertilizer, for one,” Father Brown said. “And once, when the parish conjuror didn’t show up to the Christmas party, I entertained six upset children for an hour entirely with soot, smeared on their faces to make pirate make-up. They were the happiest kids in the whole parish.”

“Oh that sounds perfect!” Ruby cried. “I wish you’d do that for all of us right now.”

James Blount was roaring with applause, Sir Leopold sputtering in mild disapproval, when a sharp knock rapped at the double front doors. Father Brown opened them, and the doorway framed the garden, the monkey puzzle tree darkening against a vivid violet sunset, looking exactly like a painted theatre backcloth. For a second no one noticed the small, dusty messenger in a frayed coat standing on the step, until he cleared his throat and asked for Mr. Blount.

Blount ripped open the letter he was handed, his face going first shocked, then delighted. “Sorry to be a nuisance, Colonel,” he said, grinning. “But my old friend from back in the Canadian west is in town tonight. That’s Florian, the famous French-Canadian acrobat and pantomime comic. He wants to pay me a visit here on business, is that all right?”

“Of course, any friend of yours is welcome,” the Colonel said, waving a hand.

“Great!” Blount cheered. “Look, everyone – I’ve been saying for years the old proper English pantomime is dead. All you get now is sappy fairy plays with princesses moralizing under the moon. Why don’t we put on our own harlequinade right here in this hall? The stairs can be the audience seating, the front doors open out to the moonlit garden – perfect scene shift. I’ll ring Florian right now, ask him to bring an extra police constable costume he can use for the classic clown-vs-cop bit.”

The whole house lit up with excitement. Ruby said she’d be Columbine, the Colonel volunteered to be Pantaloon, Crook would play the Clown with his endless supply of old jokes, and Blount made for a perfect Harlequin with his long legs and endless energy. Blount ran off to the telephone, and by the time he was back, he was bouncing with glee. Florian was on his way, he said, he’d change into the police uniform in the cab on the ride over.

They threw the whole thing together in barely an hour, with the reckless glee of a house full of people who don’t care how silly they look. Ruby’s Columbine skirt was stitched out of an old giant silk lampshade from the drawing room. The Clown and Pantaloon got white face paint from the cook’s flour jar, bright red rouge out of an anonymous housemaid’s vanity case. Blount wrapped himself in silver paper torn off cigar boxes, and only stopped short of smashing the chandelier to cover himself in crystal drops when Ruby dug out a box of old paste pantomime jewels she’d worn at a fancy dress ball once. Even Father Brown gamely let Blount stick a paper donkey head on him, and made the whole audience laugh by wiggling his ears under it, before he went to sit on the stairs with the other guests to watch the show.

The pantomime was utter controlled chaos. Crook the Clown kept darting over to the piano mid-performance to bang out raucous old music hall tunes, making everyone hoot. The big climax hit when the double front doors flew open, and the “policeman” Florian stumbled in, his imitation of a bumbling copper so perfect the whole room roared. Blount the Harlequin leapt on him, whacked his helmet, wrestled him around the stage, and the acrobat went limp, doing that famous imitation of a dead weight that still had the whole Putney district talking for months after. Blount swung him around like a sack, tossed him across his shoulders, and finally dropped him with a loud, convincing thud, to thunderous applause.

It was then that Father Brown noticed Sir Leopold, sitting right in front of him, shoot abruptly to his feet. The millionaire pawed wildly at every pocket of his coat, his face turning white, then he sat down, fumbling in a panic, before bursting out of the hall without a word. A minute later, the Colonel tapped Father Brown on the arm, and asked him to come to the study.

The Colonel was still half in his Pantaloon costume, flour dust on his cheeks, his eyes unusually grave. Sir Leopold was leaning against the mantel, heaving with suppressed panic.

“Father Brown, this is a very bad business,” the Colonel said. “The Flying Stars diamonds are gone. They vanished straight out of Sir Leopold’s inside tailcoat pocket, somewhere during the performance.”

Father Brown didn’t hesitate a second. He emptied all his own pockets out on the desk: seven shillings and sixpence in loose change, a half-used return train ticket, a tiny silver crucifix, a tattered little breviary, and a stick of milk chocolate.

“I know what everyone’s thinking,” he said, before anyone could speak. “But a man who plans to steal three priceless diamonds doesn’t stand around all afternoon yelling socialist theory loud enough for the whole room to hear. Thieves who pull jobs like this keep their heads down, they blend in, they don’t draw attention to their opinions. There’s only one person in this house none of us know a single thing about. That man playing the policeman. Where is he, right now?”

The Colonel strode out of the study at once. He came back less than a minute later, his face grim. “He’s still lying on the stage. The curtain’s been pulled up and down half a dozen times to take bows, and he hasn’t moved a muscle once.”

Father Brown stared at him for a second, his blank, mild face clearing like a storm blowing off a hill. “Wait – your wife died two months ago, you said? And Blount just got here a week too late to see her?” he yelped, jumping up like a startled rabbit. “Come on! We have to go look at that policeman right now!”

They all rushed onto the curtained stage, past Ruby and Crook who were whispering quietly off to the side, and Father Brown knelt down beside the limp, motionless man in the police uniform. He prodded his neck, then looked up at the two men behind him.

“Chloroform,” he said, quietly. “The real Florian never even made it through the front door. The man who stole the diamonds got here first.”

Cool blue twilight pooled in the steep, sloping streets of Camden Town, and the corner confectioner’s shop burned bright enough to glow like the lit stub of a firework, not a cigar. Its panes were crisscrossed with mirrors, gilded tins and rainbow-wrapped chocolates glinting behind the glass, and a crowd of neighborhood kids pressed their noses flat to the display, transfixed by the giant iced white wedding cake perched front and center: remote, rich, and delicious as an edible North Pole.

The shop’s charm stretched far past the ten or twelve-year-old crowd. A broad, burly red-haired young man of twenty-four lingered by the window just as long, a flat portfolio of black-and-white sketches tucked under his arm. That was John Turnbull Angus, who’d been selling his art to small publishers ever since his admiral uncle disinherited him for being a Socialist—all because he’d once delivered a public lecture arguing against the economic theory, a silly family quarrel no one had ever quite untangled.

He pushed through the shop door a minute later, nodding politely to the sharp, dark-eyed young woman behind the counter, Laura Hope, before stepping into the back pastry-cook’s dining room. When she came over to take his usual order, he said it perfectly deadpan: “One halfpenny bun and a small black coffee, please. Also, will you marry me?”

Laura froze. “I don’t put up with that kind of joke.”

Angus lifted his grey eyes, completely earnest. “It’s no joke. It’s as serious as that halfpenny bun. Costs you something, might give you indigestion, hurts a little. Same thing.”

She studied him for a long quiet moment, the shadow of a smile tugging at her mouth, then sank into the chair across from him. “You don’t give me any time to think,” she protested, as he started piling stacks of sweets, plates of sandwiches, and the two decanters of that peculiar port and sherry only pastry shops stock onto the table, before hauling the giant wedding cake off the shop window display and setting it in the middle. “What on earth are you doing?”

“Wedding breakfast,” he said simply.

Laura laughed, exasperated, and hauled the cake back to the window, but she didn’t look unhappy. Before he could tease her further, her face went serious. “I have to tell you something first. It’s nothing I’m ashamed of, but it’s been my nightmare for months. What would you say if there’s a man I can’t see, but who’s following me everywhere?”

Angus leaned forward, gentle. “Tell me.”

She explained she’d grown up helping her father run *The Red Fish* inn in the sleepy little village of Ludbury, and two strange, idle regulars had both proposed to her the same week. The first was tiny, almost jockey-sized Isidore Smythe, sharp-witted, with a neat black beard and a habit of pulling silly little tricks out of nowhere: making matches burn in a linked chain, carving bananas into dancing dolls, folding cigars into jumping kangaroos. The second was the quiet, lanky, pale-haired James Welkin, who had such a severe squint no one could ever tell what he was looking at, who spent all his time brooding in the bar or walking the flat grey countryside alone. She hadn’t wanted to hurt their feelings by turning them down for being odd, so she told them she’d never marry a man who lived on inherited money—they’d have to carve their own fortunes first.

Two days later, both men vanished. She’d never seen Welkin again, not a single letter, but last week she’d gotten a note from Smythe. He’d made it big: he was the brains behind the massive “Smythe’s Silent Service” clockwork domestic empire, the one with all the posters promising butlers that never drank and housemaids that never flirted, raking in thousands. That was when the haunting had started: she’d heard Welkin’s familiar odd laugh right outside the shop one day, with no one in sight, right before Smythe’s first letter arrived. The second she finished reading Smythe’s note announcing his new fortune, she’d heard Welkin’s voice clear as day, right over her shoulder: “He shan’t have you, though.”

Before Angus could reply, a little custom-built motor screeched to a halt right outside the shop, and a dapper tiny man in a shiny top hat stamped into the back room. It was Smythe, wild-eyed. He pointed to the shop window, and they saw a long strip of paper pasted across the outside glass, scrawled in messy handwriting: *If you marry Smythe, he will die.*

“That’s Welkin’s writing,” Smythe growled. “He’s been slipping threatening letters under my flat door for two weeks. The building staff swear no strangers have ever come by. I can’t catch him.”

Angus suggested they fetch his friend Flambeau, a sharp ex-criminal turned private detective who lived five minutes away. Smythe offered to drive him, and they hopped in his tiny swift car, zipping up the steep curved roads to the high residential crescent where Smythe’s top-floor flat in Himylaya Mansions sat, perched over London like a tower fort. The only people out on the quiet crescent were a chestnut seller huddled by his stall at one end, and a slow-walking beat cop at the other.

The porter and uniformed commissionaire at the building entrance swore no one had come in or out all afternoon, and there was no back door to the building at all. When Smythe unlocked his front door, they found a fresh scrap of paper on the floor of the ante-room, the red ink still wet: *If you saw her today, I shall kill you.* Rows of Smythe’s headless clockwork servant dummies stood lined against the walls, their hook arms ready to carry trays, silent as statues.

Angus hurried back out, made the porter swear to watch the stairs, paid the commissionaire a bonus to keep an eye on the front door, tipped the beat cop to loiter directly opposite the entrance, and paid the chestnut seller a whole sovereign to stay at his stall and report every single person who walked into the building. He’d locked the whole tower down, a perfect ring of witnesses. No one could get in or out unseen. Then he hurried down the hill to Flambeau’s flat in Lucknow Mansions, bursting in to find his old friend surrounded by sabres, Eastern curios, and a small quiet dusty-looking Catholic priest he’d never met before.

“This is Father Brown,” Flambeau said, greeting him.

Angus settled onto a plush striped ottoman, already launching into his wild story of the invisible threatening man, when the priest spoke softly. “It’s started snowing, by the way.”

Angus glanced out the window. Sure enough, the first thin cold flakes were drifting across the darkening glass.

The great northern roads snaking out of London drag a half-dead ghost of suburban street deep into the countryside: gaps yawn between clumps of shops, fenced paddocks, famous coaching inns, market gardens, lone private villas, and the unbroken line of the old high street never quite snaps. Walk any one of these roads, and you’ll spot a long, low house set parallel to the tarmac, painted soft white and pale sage green, strung with verandas and sun-blinds, topped with those droll little cupolas that look like wooden umbrellas, the kind you only see in faded Victorian country villas. It is the sort of old-fashioned home that reeks of that good, wealthy Clapham comfort of a bygone era—yet it feels built for swelter, not English drizzle. Stare at its sun-bleached paint and drawn blinds, and you half-expect to spot pugarees hanging by the door, palm fronds peeking over the garden wall. No one can trace where that odd foreign tingle comes from, but rumor says the first owner was a returning Anglo-Indian civil servant.

Any passerby would feel a nameless tug of fascination for the house, the sense that some story lurks behind its blinds. They would be right, as the strange events that unfolded there over Whitsuntide in 18-- proved.

If you’d wandered past the house the Thursday before Whit-Sunday at half-past four that afternoon, you would have seen the front door swing open, and Father Brown, of the tiny St. Mungo’s parish, step out smoking a fat clay pipe beside his tall French friend Flambeau, who puffed at a slim cigarette. They were not the only odd sight the open front door revealed. The whole house was built in the shape of a T: a long cross-beam facing the road, two stories high, holding all the main living spaces, with the front door set dead center, and a short, stubby tail jutting straight out the back opposite the door. That single-story tail held only two rooms, one opening straight into the other: the first was the cluttered study where celebrated poet Leonard Quinton wrote his wild, vivid Oriental verses and romances, and the second was a full glass conservatory stuffed with tropical blossoms of almost unnatural, overwhelming beauty, glowing under the strong afternoon sun. So anyone who glanced through the open front door looked straight down a perfect perspective of rich, overstuffed apartments to a sight that could make them stop dead in the street, gape and gasp: purple petals, golden sunlit foliage, crimson star-shaped blooms, as vivid as a fairy play transformation scene, bright and transparent all at once.

Leonard Quinton had staged that sight with his own careful hand, and it was arguable that it expressed his personality far better than any of his published work. He was a man who drank and bathed in colour, chasing that sensory high so far he all but ignored form—even good, decent form. That obsession had turned his whole genius to Eastern art and imagery: those bewildering carpets and blinding embroideries where every bright hue tumbles into a fortunate chaos, with no clear symbol, no moral lesson, no order at all. He wrote epics and love stories bursting with riotous, even cruel colour: tropical skies that burned gold or blood-red copper, Eastern heroes riding purple or peacock-green painted elephants wearing twelve stacked mitres, gigantic jewels a hundred negroes could not lift that blazed with ancient, unnameable hues. In plainer terms, he wrote of Eastern heavens far harsher than most Western hells, of monarchs most people would call maniacs, and of jewels no Bond Street jeweller would believe genuine, even if a hundred staggering porters hauled them through his shop door. Quinton was a genius, if a morbid one—and his morbidity showed far more in his life than his work. He was weak, waspish, and his health had been wrecked by years of experimenting with opium he’d brought back from his travels East. His wife, a handsome, overworked, sharp-edged good woman, hated the opium, but hated far more the live Indian hermit in white and yellow robes her husband insisted on housing for months on end, as his personal Virgil to guide him through the imagined heavens and hells of his verse.

Father Brown and Flambeau stepped out of that overstuffed, opium-thick household with visible relief. Flambeau had known Quinton in their wild student days in Paris, and reconnected with him for a long weekend, but even with his new, more respectable life, he found the poet insufferable. Choking yourself on laudanum to scribble erotic verses on vellum was not his idea of how any gentleman ought to go to the devil. As they paused on the doorstep to head out for a walk around the garden, the front gate was flung open violently, and a young man with a billycock hat perched on the very back of his head stumbled up the steps, breathless with urgency. When ex-master thief turned private detective Flambeau booked his month’s holiday away from his Westminster office, he didn’t opt for a fancy coastal resort or a crowded continental spa. He bought a tiny sailboat that spent half its time being rowed, and pointed its bow straight for the tangled, overgrown little rivers of the Norfolk Broads. The waterways were so narrow, hemmed in by meadows and waist-high corn, that the craft looked less like a boat and more like something from a fairy tale, gliding over land through walls of wild grass. It was only big enough for two, so Flambeau packed nothing but his four non-negotiables: tins of salmon if they got hungry, a pair of loaded revolvers if trouble found them, a bottle of brandy in case one of them fainted, and Father Brown, in case one of them died.

No plans, no deadlines: they drifted slow down the reed-channels, lingering to fish in shaded back pools, staring at the mirrored facades of riverside manor houses half hidden by overhanging rose gardens, hugging the shore as much as they dared. Flambeau had no strict agenda for the trip, but he carried a half-joking, half-serious errand he’d been nursing for years. Back in his wild youth, when he was the undisputed king of Paris’s thief underworld, he’d gotten stacks of odd mail: fan notes, death threats, even love letters. One of them had stuck with him for decades, a plain visiting card postmarked London, scrawled on the back in bright green French ink: If you ever retire and go respectable, come find me. I’ve met every other great rogue of my era, and your little trick of getting one detective to arrest the other was the finest scene in all of modern French history. The front of the card read, in neat formal engraving: *Prince Saradine, Reed House, Reed Island, Norfolk*.

Back then he’d only looked up the prince out of idle curiosity: a glamorous, notorious socialite who’d once eloped with a high-ranking married woman in southern Italy. The scandal would have blown over fast in their jet-set circle, except for the brutal tragedy that followed: the cuckolded husband had supposedly flung himself off a sheer Sicilian cliff, and the story had clung to Saradine ever after. He’d drifted through Vienna for a while, then vanished into decades of restless, nonstop travel across every continent. Now that Flambeau himself had laid down his burglar’s tools and settled in England, it felt like as good a time as any to drop in unannounced on the old rogue.

For a second both men felt like small children again, lost in a nursery fairy tale: the daisies around them loomed as big as dinner plates, dandelions towered over the boat like giant sunflowers, and the river sat so low below the root systems of the shrubs that they might as well have been gazing up at a forest of wildflowers. “By Jove,” Flambeau laughed. “This is straight out of fairyland.”

The Reverend and Honourable Wilfred Bohun, a man of fierce, unyielding piety, was making his way toward the church to carry out a brutal dawn ritual of private prayer and contemplation. His elder brother, Colonel the Honourable Norman Bohun, was not devout in the slightest. He sat slouched on the bench outside *The Blue Boar* in crumpled evening dress, sipping a drink that any impartial observer could fairly label either the last glass of his Tuesday debauch or the first of his Wednesday morning. The Bohuns were one of the vanishingly few English aristocratic families whose line stretched unbroken back to the Crusades, their pennon once flown in the streets of Jerusalem. But the old myth that ancient noble houses hold fast to chivalric tradition is a lie. It is only the poor who guard their old stories; aristocrats chase fashion, not legacy. The Bohuns had been wild, lawless Mohocks under Queen Anne, vain, foppish Mashers under Victoria, and over the last two centuries the line had rotted down into a string of drunkards, dandified degenerates, and whispered rumours of inherited madness.

Norman Bohun embodied that decay. He was a tall, powerfully built man, greying in age but still with shockingly bright yellow hair that made him look half leonine, half predatory wolf. His blue eyes had sunk so deep into their sockets they looked black, set too close together above a pair of drooping, waxed yellow moustaches. Deep furrows ran from his nostrils down to his jaw, carving a permanent sneer into his face. Over his evening tails he wore a pale yellow coat that looked more like a silk dressing gown than an overcoat, and perched on the back of his head was an absurdly wide-brimmed bright green hat, some random oriental curio he’d picked up on a drunken trip abroad. He was proud of how ridiculous the outfit looked, prouder still of how he managed to carry it off like it was formal court wear.

His younger brother the curate shared his blonde hair and fine-boned elegance, but he was buttoned up to the chin in severe black clerical cloth, clean-shaven, scholarly, and perpetually taut with nervous energy. From the height of the landing Wilfred could make out every detail of his clothes, even the Bohun family signet rings on his fingers – but his skull was nothing but a hideous, spattered mess of black blood and crushed bone.

Wilfred ran down the steps into the yard at once. “My brother’s dead!” he stammered. “What does this mean? What horrible mystery is this?”

The cobbler spoke up bluntly before anyone else could reply. “There’s horror enough, sir, but no real mystery at all. There’s only one man for forty miles around strong enough to strike a blow like that, and he’s the man with more reason to do it than anyone else.”

“We can’t prejudge anything,” the black-bearded local doctor cut in nervously, adjusting his glasses. “But I can corroborate that about the blow. It’s impossible. Metaphors can’t describe it. “That’s what I’m saying!” Gibbs the cobbler insisted. “Only Simeon Barnes could have done this. Where is he, anyway?”

“He’s still in Greenford,” Wilfred faltered.

“God is good,” Simeon said. “Inspector, I don’t mind being arrested at all. You might mind arresting me, though. I’ll walk out of the courtroom with my name completely cleared. You might walk out having completely wrecked your career.”

Wilfred waved a limp hand as if he wanted to wave the whole thought away, but Father Brown brushed a fleck of ash from the smithy furnace off his sleeve and shook his head. “Your logic is very clever, doctor. The doctor sulked, crossing his arms. “Fine. Then no sane man would ever pick up that little hammer when he could use a big one.”

That strange, smoky translucence unique to the Thames, a blur that somehow still lets the light cut through, bleared from dull grey to searing glitter as the noon sun climbed high over Westminster. Two men tramped across Westminster Bridge: one so tall he could have been a giant striding past the Houses of Parliament, the other so short and round in his clerical cassock he looked like a hunched little shadow of the abbey huddled just opposite. The tall man was Hercule Flambeau, ex-master thief turned private detective, bound for his brand new office in a half-finished high-rise block facing the abbey gates. The short man was Father Brown, fresh off sitting a deathbed vigil in Camberwell, popping by to see his friend’s new workspace.

The building was a distinctly American import: sky-high, stuffed full of over-oiled telephones and automatic lifts, still understaffed and half-empty. Only three tenants had moved in so far: Flambeau, the office directly above him, and the office directly below. The rest of the floors stood completely bare. But even before they stepped through the front doors, one object caught their eye immediately: slung across the facade outside the office above Flambeau’s, a massive gilded effigy of a human eye, wreathed in golden sun rays, big enough to take up the space of three whole office windows.

“On earth is that?” Father Brown stopped dead, tilting his round face up to stare.
“Some new fad religion,” Flambeau laughed. “The kind that forgives you all your sins by insisting you never had any to begin with. Very similar to Christian Science, from what I’ve heard. A bloke calling himself Kalon — I very much doubt that’s his real name — took the flat above mine. Two lady typists run their agency below me, and this enthusiastic old humbug is camped out on top. Calls himself the New Priest of Apollo. Worships the sun.”
“Better he watch his back,” Father Brown said gravely. “The sun was the cruellest of all the old gods. But what’s that monstrous eye for?”
“Their whole schtick, as far as I can tell,” Flambeau explained. “They say if your mind is perfectly steady, you can endure anything. Their two big symbols are the sun and the open eye — they claim anyone truly healthy can stare straight at the sun without blinking.”
“If a man were really healthy,” Father Brown blinked, “he wouldn’t bother to stare.”
“Supposedly it cures every physical illness, too,” Flambeau added, careless.
“Can it cure the one spiritual disease?” Father Brown asked, sudden and serious.
“What’s that?” Flambeau grinned.
“Thinking you’re perfectly well.”

Flambeau was far more curious about the quiet little office below him than the flamboyant sun temple above. A lucid, pragmatic Southerner, he could not wrap his head around the pale, bright new age religions popping up all over London — but he was never bored by people, especially sharp, striking ones. The two sisters who ran the typing agency below him were far more interesting than they looked. Both slight, dark, and brisk, the elder, Pauline, was tall and arresting, with a sharp aquiline profile that looked like it had been carved by a knife. She cut through life like a blade, her eyes bright as polished steel, not soft as diamonds, her slim straight figure just a touch too stiff to feel casual grace. Her younger sister Joan was her dimmer, quieter shadow: a little greyer, a little paler, trailing after her older sister with a quiet, dog-like devotion that held more quiet tragedy than Pauline’s loud, blazing self-assurance.

What no passer-by would have guessed was that Pauline Stacey was a landed heiress, born to a crest and half a county’s worth of estates, a vast fortune she had kept rather than give away, pouring it all into her model typing business and leagues to help other working women rise. She hated weakness, hated anything she saw as a “crutch”. Once Flambeau walked in to drop off a typing job just in time to see her fling Joan’s spectacles to the floor and stamp them to shards, mid-rant about “sickly medical nonsense” and the cowardice of relying on artificial rubbish instead of willpower. She dared Joan to ever bring such trash into the office again, demanding to know if people thought they all needed wooden legs or glass eyes too, as if they were born crippled slaves.

Flambeau, bemused, asked her point-blank why a lift was a triumph of man’s willpower but a pair of spectacles was a coward’s crutch. Pauline’s eyes blazed.
“Machines, engines, those are marks of human force — man’s force, woman’s force too! They devour distance, defy time. That is true science. But these silly little plasters and pills the doctors peddle? They are badges of poltroonery. I was born free! People only think they need those things because nurses and nannies train them to fear the dark, to fear the sun, tell children not to stare up at it until they can’t look without blinking. Why should there be one star in the sky I am not allowed to see? The sun is no master of mine. I can stare at it whenever I choose.”

Flambeau laughed and complimented her, but the second he got to his own floor he whistled under his breath. So she had fallen right into the clutches of the con man upstairs with his golden eye. He soon saw the bond between the upper and lower flats was growing tighter by the day. Kalon, the self-styled priest of Apollo, was physically magnificent: nearly as tall as Flambeau, with a mane of blonde hair tossed back like a lion’s, a full golden beard, sharp bright blue eyes, the very image of a golden saint-king out of Saxon legend. No amount of tacky office decor — the brass nameplate by his door, the clerk in a starched collar manning his outer office, the gilded sun eye hanging outside like an optician’s ad — could dim the raw, vivid charisma that rolled off him. Even in his casual linen work suit he was an imposing, magnetic figure; when he donned his white ritual vestments and golden circlet to salute the sun at dawn, noon, and sunset, passers-by in the street would stop short, their laughter dying on their lips.

On the day of the death, Father Brown hung back on the sidewalk as Flambeau barreled for the entrance, squinting up at Kalon on his top-floor balcony. The sun priest was already standing straight, hands lifted, his deep, ringing voice carrying all down the crowded street as he chanted his solar litany, staring straight into the flaming midday disc of the sun without the faintest flinch of an eyelid. The little priest below blinked so hard his eyes were crinkled shut.
“O sun!” Kalon’s voice boomed. “O star too great to be numbered among the stars! O white father of all unwearied things, white flames, white flowers, white mountain peaks! Primal purity, into thy peace—”

There was a rush, a crash, a woman’s high, shattering scream that cut through the litany. Half a dozen people charged in through the building gates, half a dozen charged out, all yelling over each other. A wave of raw, unnameable horror flooded the street. Two figures stood completely still through the chaos: Kalon the sun priest on his balcony high above, and Father Brown, small and shabby, in the crowd below.

Then Flambeau’s towering form filled the doorway, his voice roaring like a foghorn, ordering someone to run for a surgeon. Father Brown ducked through the crowd after him, Kalon’s smooth, chanting voice still drifting down from the balcony, praising the happy sun god of fountains and flowers.

They found the body crumpled at the bottom of the open lift shaft. It was Pauline Stacey, dead on impact, her skull cracked, her bright defiant face still. Flambeau had not doubted it for a second. Suicide seemed impossible for a woman who insisted tragedy did not exist, who thought willpower could conquer every last law of nature. But who on earth could have murdered her? The building was half empty, barely occupied.
“Where is that fraud Kalon?” Flambeau snarled, red in the face.
A calm, deep voice answered: “I have been standing on my balcony worshipping the sun for the last full fifteen minutes.”
Flambeau turned to see Father Brown at his elbow. “Then if he was up there the whole time, who killed her?”
“Let’s go upstairs and find out,” Father Brown said. “We have half an hour before the police turn up.”

They searched the typing agency first, and found Joan Stacey gone. Flambeau blanched. “Her sister’s out — vanished.”
Father Brown nodded. “Or she went up to Kalon’s suite. Let’s wait for them in the Staceys’ office downstairs.”
He settled in a big red leather chair by the entrance, where he could watch the stairs and the landings. He did not wait long. Four minutes later, three figures descended the stairs: Joan Stacey, her face drawn, streaks of premature grey in her dark hair, heading straight for her desk; Kalon himself, still in his white ritual robes and golden circlet, sweeping down the stairs like a painting of Christ leaving Pontius Pilate’s praetorium; and Flambeau, following behind, bewildered and frowning. Joan calmly laid out her papers on the desk, her hands steady as anything. If she was a criminal, she was ice cold.

Father Brown smiled a tiny, odd little smile at her, then turned to Kalon.
“Prophet,” he said, “I want you to tell me: do you actually think murder is wrong at all?”
Kalon lifted his crowned head. “Is that an accusation?”
“No,” Father Brown said gently. “It’s the speech for the defence.”

Kalon rose, tall and glowing, seeming to fill the tiny little office with the weight of his presence. He raged, magnificent: “You are Caiaphas! Your church and mine are the only two real things on this earth. I worship the sun of the living; you worship the darkening of the sun, the god of the dying. Your whole church is nothing but a black police force of spies, dragging confessions of sin out of men by treachery and torture. You would prove men guilty. I would prove them innocent. He leaned against the mantelpiece, unflinching. “I will tell you the full case against me, no hiding. He waved the thought away. “But I could not have killed her. My alibi is unbreakable. Half Westminster saw me on my balcony from before noon until a quarter past. My clerk will swear I never left my top-floor suite. A hundred witnesses. I know exactly what happened to her, though. She was ambitious, determined to master the laws of matter. She kept saying if your will was strong enough, you could float down through empty air like a feather. She tried to levitate down the lift shaft. Her will failed at the last second. Flambeau stared, half convinced. Father Brown, both by trade and long quiet conviction, knew better than most men that every human being carries a quiet dignity once they are dead. But even he felt a sharp, dissonant jolt when he was roused at gray dawn and told that Sir Aaron Armstrong had been murdered. Secret, sordid violence felt absurd, almost indecent, attached to a man so universally beloved and endlessly entertaining. Killing Sir Aaron would have felt, to anyone who knew him, like hearing that Sunny Jim had hanged himself, or that Mr. Pickwick had died raving in an asylum. A famous philanthropist who worked on the grim edges of social vice, he prided himself on facing every dark problem with the brightest possible grin. His speeches gushed with endless anecdotes and booming, infectious laughter; his health was robust to the point of bursting, his ethics unshakably sunny, and his favorite cause, the temperance campaign against drink, was delivered with that unflagging, almost monotonous good cheer that is such a familiar mark of the comfortably well-off total abstainer.

His well-publicized conversion story was repeated from every puritan pulpit and reform platform: as a boy, he had strayed first from strict Scottish theology to Scottish whisky, then pulled himself clear of both to become the jolly public figure everyone knew. Between his broad white beard, cherubic pink face, and sparkling spectacles, it was almost impossible to picture him as ever having been so morbid as either a drunk or a Calvinist. He was, by all appearances, the most earnestly merry man alive.

His home stood on the rural edge of Hampstead: a tall, narrow, unadorned modern house, perched so that its steepest back face jutted right over the sheer green railway embankment below, every passing train rattling its foundations. Sir Aaron always roared that he had no nerves to rattle — but that dawn, the tables turned. It was the house that shocked the train.

The engine slowed to a dead halt just past the point where the house’s sharp corner jutted over the turf. Waving wildly on the bank above the cab was a man clad entirely in black, even down to his funereal black gloves, spinning his hands like a sable windmill. That alone would not have stopped the train, but the shout he let out was so raw, so unhumanly distinct, that the driver swore he would have pulled the brakes even if he could not make out the single word the man screamed: “Murder!”

When passengers clambered off the tracks and pushed through the smoky hedge at the embankment’s foot, they found Sir Aaron’s body tumbled almost all the way to the rails. He wore his famous bright yellow dressing gown lined with vivid scarlet, a scrap of rope tangled tight around his ankle, barely a smudge of blood on his clothes, his body bent at a sharp angle no living frame could ever hold.

Shortly after, the second member of the household stumbled onto the lawn: Patrick Royce, Sir Aaron’s big, broad-shouldered secretary, a former bohemian artist once famous in London’s tavern circles, whose own face was as white as the dead man’s gown, sobbing so hard his words were unintelligible. By the time Alice Armstrong, the dead man’s pale young daughter, tottered shakily into view, the engine had whistled and panted off to the next station to call for help.

Royce, a lapsed Irish Catholic who only ever remembered his faith in moments of total crisis, had sent a hasty summons for Father Brown — a request that had been fast-tracked by young Detective Merton, a new officer on the case who counted Flambeau as a close friend, and had heard a hundred wild stories of the little priest’s knack for unravelling impossible puzzles. As Merton led Father Brown across the windy fields towards the railway line, the two talked more openly than strangers on official business ever would.

“As far as I can see,” Merton said frankly, “there’s no sense to be made of this at all. No one fits the bill. Magnus, that grim servant in the black gloves? He’s a slow, solemn old fool, far too stupid to plan a murder. Royce has been Armstrong’s closest friend for ten years, everyone knows he owed the old man his life. And his daughter adored him. Who on earth would want to kill a man as cheery as Father Christmas?”

Father Brown blinked placidly. “He was very cheerful, yes. But did he share that cheer? Frankly, was anyone else in that house happy, besides him?”

The question unlocked a memory Merton had never thought to name. He had visited the Armstrong home before, on small official jobs tied to the philanthropist’s reform work, and for the first time he saw clearly how dismal the place really was: tall, drafty, freezing rooms, cheap shabby provincial decor, the electric lights in the corridors bleaker than moonlight. Armstrong, with his blazing red face and silver beard, had blazed like a bonfire wherever he stepped — but he left no warmth behind him. The rest of the household were all thin shadows of his brightness: the glowering, glove-clad Magnus felt like a walking nightmare; Royce, for all his size and broad frame, had a prematurely gray straw-colored beard and deep lines across his forehead, the heavy, hunched posture of a man who had spent decades carrying a weight he could never set down. Alice was so thin and pale, so tremulous at the rumble of passing trains, that it was almost impossible to believe she was the same man’s daughter.

“People like a good laugh now and then,” Father Brown said, as they neared the house, “but no one likes a permanent, non-negotiable smile that demands they match it every second of the day. A cheerfulness with no room for sadness, no humor? That is very hard to live with. If I ever murdered someone, I dare say it would be an unyielding Optimist.”

They found Merton’s superior, the grizzled veteran Inspector Gilder, waiting on the bank for the coroner, deep in conversation with Royce. Gilder looked up at Merton’s approach with a dry smirk. “Case is open and shut, lad. That gloved servant who stopped the train? He’s gone. Grabbed twenty thousand pounds’ worth of securities out of Armstrong’s desk and bolted on the very train that left to call for help. Only puzzle left is the weapon: the old man’s skull is cracked like it was hit with a huge heavy club, but there’s no sign of any weapon anywhere on the grounds, and no one could have carried a big weapon off without being seen.”

“Maybe the weapon was too big for anyone to notice,” Father Brown said, with a tiny, odd giggle. “He was killed with the earth. Thrown out of that open attic window up there, fell all the way down this bank, and broke his skull and his neck on the turf. Look — that scrap of rope around his ankle? The other end is still caught on the windowsill at the top.”

Gilder squinted up, spotted the faint glint of rope far above, and nodded, impressed. Before he could speak, a single-carriage police special rounded the curve, and disgorged a group of officers with a very sullen Magnus in custody.

“Got him!” Gilder called, stepping forward. “Did you recover the money?”

The sergeant holding Magnus gave him a strange, confused look. “No, sir — not exactly. He walked straight into the Highgate police station of his own accord, and handed every last penny of the securities over to Inspector Robinson there, for safe keeping.”

Magnus spoke then, and his voice was so ringing, so piercingly clear, that everyone flinched. “I did it to keep it safe from the murderer. I have no trust at all in Sir Aaron’s family. The moment my master was thrown out that window, I ran up to the attic, and found Miss Armstrong unconscious on the floor, a red dagger still clutched in her hand.” He pulled a long, horn-hilted knife out of his coat pocket, its blade streaked with old brownish blood, and handed it to the sergeant.

Merton turned, half-disgusted, to the girl standing at the edge of the group. Surely no one would believe the word of this grim, suspicious foreign servant over hers — but Alice Armstrong lifted her pale face, and whispered, “He is telling the truth.”

Before anyone could react, Royce shouldered through the group, his huge fist cracking into Magnus’s face hard enough to lay the manservant flat on the grass. “Don’t arrest me for assault,” he called, as the police moved to seize him. “Arrest me for murder. It’s all true: Miss Armstrong fainted holding that knife, but she didn’t grab it to hurt her father. She grabbed it to defend him — from me.”

The group trundled up the narrow stairs to the attic, Royce leading the way, and found the room a wreck: an empty revolver discarded on the floor, an open half-empty whisky bottle rolled in the corner, a length of heavy cord matching the scrap on Armstrong’s ankle strewn across the windowsill, vases smashed on the mantel and the carpet, the tablecloth dragged halfway off its legs.

“I was drunk,” Royce said, his voice rough and broken. “You all know my story: Armstrong pulled me out of the gutter, saved what was left of me, was always good to me. But he wouldn’t let me marry Alice. I snapped. I fired all the bullets in that revolver at him, I tried to strangle him with that rope, I dragged him to the window and threw him out. I don’t ask for mercy. Take me to the gallows.”

As the police stepped forward to lead him away, they paused, shocked: Father Brown was on his hands and knees on the carpet in the doorway, crawling around like he was praying, his round face tilted up at the group looking absurdly like a quadruped with a very earnest human head.

“Wait — this won’t do at all,” he said cheerfully. “You started saying there was no murder weapon, and now you’ve got three of them: a knife to stab, a gun to shoot, a rope to strangle — and he still died by falling out the window. It’s not economical. And none of the facts add up. Look: all six bullets from that revolver are buried in the carpet. A man in a drunken rage shooting at his enemy aims for his head, not his slippers. Then the rope: what kind of drunk tries to strangle a man, and somehow wraps the rope around his ankle instead of his throat, and drags him out the window by the leg? If Royce was that far gone, he’d be passed out cold on the floor right now. And the whisky bottle? A real dipsomaniac fighting for that bottle would drink every last drop, not leave half of it rolling ignored in a corner.”

He scrambled to his feet, and turned to Alice, who had drawn him aside to speak privately. “I saw the whole thing,” she told him, her voice tight. “Patrick was screaming, firing the gun over and over, he lunged for my father with the rope, I grabbed the knife to cut it loose, and fainted. Don’t try to save him. He did it.”

Father Brown nodded gently, and walked back out to the group, ready to lay out the real truth of the three tools of death, and who had arranged every one of them to fall on the innocent.
