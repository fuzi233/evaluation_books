There is a peculiar rhythm to life, a hidden current running beneath the surface of daily affairs, occasionally breaking through to consciousness with the promise of a fortunate turn. One feels it as a sudden, inexplicable urge—a conviction that rises unbidden, like a bird startled from heather, that success is imminent. It is not the frantic delusion of the desperate, nor the blind optimism of the fool, but a quiet certainty that the stars have aligned for some grand design. As Trent climbed the staircase outside the library, this sensation swelled within him. A swarm of fragmented guesses and unsorted inferences buzzed in his mind, disconnected and chaotic, yet as he ascended, an indubitable knowledge settled over him: the truth was waiting just ahead.

The bedrooms lined a broad, carpeted gallery illuminated by a tall window at the far end, stretching the length of the house before angling sharply into a narrower corridor where the servants' quarters lay. Martin's suite was an anomaly, tucked away on a small landing midway up the stairs. As Trent passed it, he cast a fleeting glance inward. It was a modest, utilitarian space, stark and unadorned. Moving upward, Trent adopted a posture of extreme caution, keeping close to the wall and placing each foot with deliberate care to avoid disturbance. Yet, the old house betrayed him; a series of sharp, rhythmic creaks announced his progress, echoing through the silence like a ticking clock.

Upon reaching the floor, Trent knew instantly that Manderson's chamber was the first on the right. He approached it without hesitation, testing the latch and the lock, noting their normal function and examining the key's wards. Stepping inside, he found a small, sparsely furnished apartment. The interior was stripped bare, untouched since the ghastly morning of the discovery in the grounds. Sunlight streamed through the window, illuminating the rumpled sheets and blankets piled haphazardly over a narrow wooden bedstead. The light caught the gleaming gold components of delicate dental work resting in a shallow glass bowl filled with water on a plain bedside table. Beside it stood a simple wrought-iron candlestick. Nearby, clothes lay thrown casually over one of two rush-seated chairs, and atop a chest of drawers repurposed as a vanity, various items lay scattered in the disorder of a hurried departure.

Trent surveyed the room with a searching gaze. He noted, with growing unease, that the occupant had neither washed nor shaved. Reaching into the bowl, he flipped the dental plate over, his brow furrowing at its inexplicable placement. The sheer emptiness and disarray of the sun-drenched room induced a chill of grotesque imagination in Trent. His mind conjured a vision of a haggard man, dressing in silent, agonized haste by the first grey light of dawn, his eyes darting constantly toward the inner door behind which his wife slept, terrified of being discovered.

Shivering, Trent forced his thoughts back to reality. He opened two tall wall cupboards flanking the bed. Inside, he found a vast selection of clothing, clearly the few comforts Manderson had clung to during his final hours. Regarding footwear, Manderson had indulged his affluence to the fullest. An extraordinary array of shoes, meticulously preserved and tree'd, lined two long, low shelves against the wall. There were no boots. Trent, a connoisseur of fine leather himself, leaned forward, inspecting the collection with appreciation. It was clear Manderson had boasted of possessing a remarkably small, well-proportioned foot. The shoes were distinctively shaped—narrow, round-toed, exquisitely crafted—all seemingly cut from the same last.

Then, Trent's eyes narrowed sharply as they landed on a pair of patent-leather shoes on the upper shelf. These were the specific pair mentioned by Inspector Murch—the ones Manderson had worn the night before his death. Trent saw immediately that they were well-worn, yet they had been polished very recently. Something about the uppers seized his attention. He bent closer, squinting, comparing them to the neighboring pairs. Taking them up, he scrutinized the seam where the upper met the sole.

Unconsciously, Trent began to whistle, a faint, precise tune that Inspector Murch, had he been present, would have recognized instantly. Most men who practice strict self-control harbor a subconscious tic that betrays their suppressed excitement to those who know them. The inspector had noted long ago that whenever Trent encountered a strong scent, he would whistle a certain melodious passage—an opening movement from Mendelssohn's *Lieder ohne Worte* in A Major, though the inspector could never quite recall why.

Trent turned the shoes over, measuring them with a marked tape and peering minutely at the soles. In the angle between the heel and the instep of each, he detected a faint, gritty trace of red gravel. Placing the shoes gently on the floor, Trent walked to the window, hands clasped behind his back, continuing his soft, mechanical whistling. Gazing out, his eyes saw little of the garden, yet at length, his lips parted as if to utter the classic English expletive of sudden enlightenment. Turning back to the shelves, he swiftly and methodically inspected every remaining pair.

Having finished his examination of the footwear, Trent gathered the garments from the chair, folded them carefully, and returned them. He revisited the wardrobes, hunting through them with intense focus, before his attention shifted again to the clutter on the dressing-table. Sitting down on the empty chair, he buried his face in his hands, staring fixedly at the carpet for several minutes, lost in thought. Finally, he rose and opened the inner door leading to Mrs. Manderson's room.

At a glance, it was evident that the large chamber had been hastily converted from a private sanctuary into a temporary holding area. Every item associated with a woman's vanity—gloves, veils, handkerchiefs, ribbons, bags, hats—had been stripped away. The drawers, usually besieged by the "obstinate conspiracy" of feminine accessories, stood bare. The room resembled an unoccupied guest suite, yet every detail of furniture and decoration whispered of an unconventional, exacting taste. As Trent's expert eye traced the harmonies of color and form, knowing the artistic nature of the unseen occupant, his curiosity deepened. He pondered the heavy burdens laid upon her shoulders and the tragic narrative rapidly forming in his mind.

First, he moved to the tall French window facing the door, opening it to step onto a small balcony with an iron railing. Below lay a broad expanse of lawn, bordered only by a narrow flower bed, dipping abruptly toward the orchard at the far end. The other window opened above the garden entrance to the library. In the distant corner stood a second door, the usual passage for the maid entering and the mistress exiting in the mornings.

Seated on the bed, Trent quickly sketched a plan of the room and its neighbor in his notebook. The bed stood in the angle between the communicating door and the sash window, its head pressed against the dividing wall. Staring at the pillows, he lay down deliberately, looking through the open door into the adjoining chamber. Noticing the arrangement, he rose and annotated his sketch: small covered tables flanked the bed. The one furthest from the door held a graceful copper electric lamp standard, its wire trailing freely to the wall. He examined the switches controlling the other lights; they were positioned high on the wall, just inside the door, well out of reach for someone sitting on the bed. Satisfied that everything was in order, he turned on his heel, walked briskly back into Manderson's room, and rang the bell.

"I require your assistance again, Martin," Trent said when the butler appeared, standing upright and impassive in the doorway. "I need you to persuade Mrs. Manderson's maid to grant me an audience."

"Certainly, sir," Martin replied. "What sort of a woman is she? Is she sensible?"

"She is French, sir," Martin answered succinctly, pausing briefly. "She has not been with us long, but I have the impression that the young woman knows as much of the world as is good for her."

"Do you think butter might melt in her mouth?" Trent asked with a dry smile. "Very well. I am not afraid. I intend to ask her some questions."

"I will summon her immediately, sir." Martin withdrew, leaving Trent to wander the small room, hands clasped behind his back. Soon, a small, neat figure in black appeared quietly.

The lady's maid, Celestine, had spotted Trent from a window as he crossed the lawn, hoping desperately that the solver of mysteries—whose reputation was formidable both upstairs and downstairs—would call for her. Her nerves were frayed; she needed to make a scene. However, her dramatic outbursts were generally ignored by the other staff, and Inspector Murch's stern demeanor had previously chilled her into restraint. Seeing Trent, whose air lacked the stiffness of a policeman, she felt a flicker of sympathy. Yet, as she entered, instinct warned her that any attempt at coquetry would be a fatal error. Instead, she approached with an air of amiable candor.

"Monsieur desires to speak with me?" she asked. "I am called Celestine."

"Naturally," Trent replied, his voice calm and businesslike. "Celestine, I wish you to tell me this: When you served tea to your mistress yesterday morning at seven, was the door between the two bedrooms open?"

Celestine became instantly animated. "Oh, yes!" she exclaimed, adopting her favorite English idiom.
