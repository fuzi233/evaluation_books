Back at the George Inn, Mr. Foker and his new chum Pen settled into a slap-up feast in the coffee room, where waiter Mr. Rincer toted in the first dish and bowed so low he nearly tipped his own wig off, as if he was waiting on the Lord Lieutenant himself rather than two giddy young chaps with more appetite than sense. Foker tore into the turtle soup and venison with the same ravenous glee he’d shown a year prior, when the fanciest feast he could scrape together was stolen polonies and fizzing ginger beer that tasted vaguely like dish soap. Pen couldn’t help but respect his connoisseurship when he took one sip of champagne, made a face, and announced it was “barely fermented gooseberry they’re trying to pass off as French,” then winked one eye at the glass of port, prodded it like it owed him money, and declared it the genuine good stuff, warning the waiting staff not to even try pulling a fast one on him.

He knew every single inn employee by their first name, asked after their wives’ bad knees and their cats’ unexpected litters, and every time a London stagecoach rumbled up to the door (the old kind that set off bright and early from the George back then), he’d fling the coffee room window wide open, yell at the guards and coachmen by name, and do a dead-on impression of their horn toots so loud the old half-blind parrot hanging by the bar squawked and fell straight off its perch.
“Bottle of sherry, bottle of that so-called champagne I just roasted, bottle of port, and a coffee strong enough to strip paint off a boot,” Foker said, once they’d worked their way through all the delicacies and a mountain of nuts and overripe fruit, “Reckon it’s toddle o’clock, innit, Pen?”
Pen sprang up with bright, wine-flushed eyes, and the pair trundled over to the theatre, where they paid their entry fee to the wheezy old woman napping in the ticket booth. “That’s Mrs. Dropsicum, Bingley the manager’s mother-in-law,” Foker whispered, “Once got a standing ovation for Lady Macbeth so dramatic she tripped over the witches’ cauldron mid-soliloquy.”

The theatre was emptier than a pauper’s purse, even though Bingley’s playbills had been screaming about “universal bursts of electric delight” and “galvanic thrills for all the family.” Twenty or so bored souls dotted the pit benches, a handful of drunks kicked and whistled in the galleries, and a dozen freeloaders on free passes occupied the boxes right next to Pen and Foker. Lieutenants Rodgers and Podgers, plus young Cornet Tidmus of the local Dragoons, were camped out in a private box, yelling at the actors like they were old mates down the pub and cheering them by name mid-scene.
Bingley the manager, who hogged all the best tragic and comic roles for himself, only stepping aside occasionally for visiting London stars, was currently chewing the scenery as the Stranger. He was decked out in skin-tight pantaloons and Hessian boots that made his calves look like overstuffed sausages, swaddled in a giant cloak, with a hearse-feather-draped beaver hat drooping over his caked-on stage makeup, half-obscuring his scratchy, slipping brown buckled wig. He’d raided the theatre’s costume jewellery chest for the shiniest, fakest diamond ring he could find, and waggled it ostentatiously on his little finger right at the pit, so every single patron could admire its gaudy glitter. He’d told every new young actor in the company the ring was a sacred stage heirloom: it had belonged to legendary actor George Frederick Cooke, who got it from Quin, who almost certainly paid a whole shilling for it at a market stall. Bingley was fully convinced the entire world was spellbound by its sparkle.
He was reading from that weird, tattered stage book no normal human would ever hold at that absurd angle, pointing at random lines and wagging his head like he was getting direct, dramatic divine revelations from a very bored angel. The second he spotted Pen and Foker in their box, he decided they were his new personal audience, staring right past Foker (who was just happily sucking the top of his walking stick, unbothered) to fix his gaze dead on Pen. When he moaned that overripe line about Hope’s cradle being the grave, he sounded like a bassoon getting stuck in a drain, staring so hard Pen was convinced the whole theatre was looking at him. Every time Pen glanced down to escape the gaze, then looked up again, Bingley’s eyes were still glued to him like a determined leech.
When the scene finally wrapped, Foker whacked his cane against the box rail and yelled, “Bravo, Bingley! Give the bloke a hand, Pen, everyone loves a bit of applause!” So Pen clapped along snorting with laughter, and the Dragoons across the way hooted so loud their gloves split at the seams.

The set shifted suddenly to a grand chamber in Wintersen Castle, and Foker leaned in to whisper, “That’s bit-part player Hicks and Miss Thackthwaite over there, she’s pretty alright innit—wait HURRAY, BRAVO, HERE’S FOTHERINGAY!”
The whole pit thumped their umbrellas against the floor, the galleries fired off a volley of whoops, and the Dragoons and Foker clapped so hard their hands turned bright pink. Peering out from the wings, you could see Mr Costigan, her scruffy red-faced dad, grinning like he’d just won the lottery. Pen’s eyes blew wide as saucers as Mrs Haller swept on stage, head bowed, then lifted her gaze to thank the cheering crowd, folded her hands over her chest, and sank into a magnificent curtsy that earned even more whoops and umbrella thumps. Pen, giddy on wine and instant adoration, yelled “BRAVO” louder than literally everyone else in the building. The little old first fiddler in the orchestra, Bows, who had a crutch propped against his music stand, looked up from his sheet music and smiled at the boy’s obvious starstruck enthusiasm.
Anyone who only ever met Miss Fotheringay later, after she married and became a fancy London society lady, had no clue how breathtakingly beautiful she was back then, at 26 (though she swore to every single person who asked she was only 19). Tall, broad-foreheaded, with soft natural rippling black hair that none of the crimping-iron-obsessed younger misses of the day could replicate, braided back over a neck as smooth and perfect as the Venus de Milo’s. Her eyes, when she lifted them to look at you before dropping their thick purple-fringed lids, shone with soft, unfathomable tenderness, like she already knew every single one of your most embarrassing childhood secrets. She never laughed (her teeth were nothing to write home about, truth be told) but a soft, sweet, endless sad smile played over her lips, dimpling her cheeks and chin. Her ears were little pearly shells, and the theatre’s very best (still fake) diamond earrings only looked tacky next to them. She swept across the stage in long, flowing black gowns that moved like water around her, only the tips of her large, elegant sandals peeking out of the fabric, which Pen immediately decided were Cinderella’s lost slippers. But it was her hands and arms that were truly unbeatable: when she folded them over her chest in resignation, flung them out in silent agony, or waved them soft and light before her like snowy doves, the whole theatre held its breath.
She delivered her big monologue about her lost little William and his sisters, wailing about how her fancy kept torturing her with visions of them sick and crying for their mother, with such gushing, smothered pathos that she made every single person in the theatre feel like she was crying her heart out directly onto their shoulder. Old Bows buried his face in his blue cotton handkerchief, blubbered “BRAVO”, Foker was sobbing so hard into his giant yellow bandanna he looked like he’d spent an hour chopping raw onions, and Pen was completely gone. He followed her every movement around the stage, when she walked off the rest of the theatre vanished into a meaningless blur of lights and red-uniformed dragoons. When she flung herself on Bingley’s shoulder for the big reconciliation scene, with the child actors clinging to their knees and the rest of the cast clustered around, Pen’s hot, foggy eyes couldn’t see anyone but her. The curtain fell, and it felt like a soft heavy pall dropping over the whole room.
He didn’t hear a single word Bingley said when he came out to announce the next night’s show, and almost missed the whole theatre screaming for a curtain call for Miss Fotheringay, not him. When Bingley finally got the hint, grinned, and dragged her back out on stage, Pen’s eyes nearly fell out of his head: her braid had slipped loose, the dragoons were throwing her wildflowers, she tucked them to her chest, brushed her hair back from her face, and her eyes locked straight onto Pen’s for two whole, perfect seconds before the curtain fell again.
He didn’t hear a single note of the Dragoons’ brass band playing the post-show overture. “She’s an absolute crusher, ain’t she now?” Foker gushed. Pen just babbled vague nonsense, he couldn’t explain the overwhelming, delicious, maddening feeling surging through his chest, like he’d drunk three bottles of champagne in one go. Foker quickly got fully absorbed in the silly double hornpipe dance that followed, but Pen couldn’t have cared less. The whole stage was a mist, with only her face visible in the middle of it. At the end of the dance he checked his watch, panic spiking: he had a long ride home.
“Hang on, stay for The Bravo of the Battle-Axe!” Foker begged, “Bingley’s brilliant in it, he wears red tights and tries to carry Mrs Bingley over a fake cataract bridge and nearly drops her into the painted water! It’s total chaos, you’ll love it!”
Pen scanned the playbill lingeringly, hoping against hope Fotheringay’s name was somewhere in the afterpiece cast list, but it wasn’t. He had to leave. He squeezed Foker’s hand so tight it nearly ached, choked out a goodbye he couldn’t even properly say, and wandered aimlessly around the streets of Chatteris in a daze for what felt like hours before he remembered his horse waiting at the George. He galloped home, and the Clavering town clock bonged 1am exactly as he stumbled into the Fairoaks courtyard, dashed into his bedroom, and faceplanted under the covers.
He slept like a log, no tossing and turning, until the rooks started cawing from the woods outside his window at dawn. The very second his eyes popped open, her face was the first thing in his head. It felt exactly like that day three years prior, when his dad had given him a fancy new gold watch for getting into the fifth form: he’d hidden in every empty corner of the house to rub it, polish it, and listen to it tick, checking every five minutes to make sure it was still safely in his pocket. (That watch was a total piece of garbage, by the way, never kept time right, and he eventually traded it away for a decent workmanlike one, but right now this new, all-consuming crush felt like the shiniest, most perfect treasure in the whole world.)
He felt years older than he had the day before, put on his fanciest waistcoat and his best new stickpin, strutted down to breakfast acting impossibly haughty, patronising his mum Helen and little Laura, who’d been plucking away at her piano lesson for hours already. After he rattled through the household prayers without listening to a single syllable he was saying, Laura gawked at his fancy getup and asked him what the play was about. Pen laughed and refused to tell her (it was definitely for the best, given some of the more dramatic, sappy bits). When she asked why he was dressed like he was off to meet the king, he blushed and told his mum he was planning to ride over to Baymouth to visit his old schoolfellow who was studying there with a very learned tutor, to suss out their reading list to prep for his own upcoming time at university. Helen’s stomach twisted with that vague, uneasy worry she’d had ever since the farmer had turned up last night with news Pen wouldn’t be home for dinner, but she told herself Pen had never lied to her before. He waved them off, trotted his horse slow at first, then galloped like a madman the second he was out of earshot of the house.
He ran straight into his tutor Mr Smirke, who was ambling over to Fairoaks to give Pen his usual three hours of lessons, and Pen shot past so fast the little man’s pony shied, flinging Smirke headfirst straight into a hedge full of stinging nettles. Pen cackled, yelled that he was heading for Baymouth, and vanished half a mile down the road before Smirke could even pick grass out of his hair. Sitting there in the nettles, stinging all over, Smirke decided there was no point chasing his pupil now. He might as well mosey over to Fairoaks, ask Mrs Pendennis when Arthur would be back, and make little Laura recite her Watts’s Catechism to pass the time.
And that, dear readers, is what love does: it turns giddy teen boys into galloping, brainless idiots, and mild-mannered little curates into nettle-stung hedge victims, all before 9am.
