"And it's hame, hame, hame, / Hame fain wad I be."

It required the delicate, floral wallpaper of the rooms merely to begin to reconcile Mrs. Hale to Milton. But that was insufficient; it demanded more, and far more than could be supplied. Thick, yellow November fogs had descended upon the valley, obscuring the sweeping bend of the river and sealing off the view of the plain entirely when Mrs. Hale finally arrived at her new residence. Although Margaret and Dixon had labored for two days unpacking and rearranging, the interior of the house remained chaotic, and outside, a dense fog pressed against the windows, driven inward through every open doorway in choking, white wreaths of unwholesome mist.

"Oh, Margaret! Are we to live here?" Mrs. Hale asked, her voice filled with blank dismay.

Margaret's heart resonated with the dreary tone of the question. She struggled to summon the composure to reply, "Oh, the fogs in London are sometimes far worse!"

"But then you knew that London itself, and our friends, lay behind it. Here—well! We are utterly desolate. Oh, Dixon, what a place this is!"

"Indeed, ma'am, I'm certain it will be your death before long, and then I know who'll—"

"Stay! Miss Hale, that is far too heavy for you to lift," Dixon interrupted hastily.

"Not at all, thank you, Dixon," Margaret replied coolly. "The best thing we can do for Mother is to prepare her room for rest, while I fetch her a cup of coffee."

Mr. Hale was equally dispirited and sought Margaret out for sympathy. "Margaret, I truly believe this is an unhealthy place. Imagine if your mother's health—or yours—should suffer. I wish I had chosen a country retreat in Wales; this is simply terrible," he remarked, moving toward the window.

There was no comfort to offer. They were settled in Milton and must endure the smoke and fogs for the foreseeable future; indeed, all other avenues of life seemed blocked by a thick fog of circumstance. Only the day prior, Mr. Hale had calculated with alarm how much their relocation and the fortnight spent at Heston had cost, discovering it had consumed nearly his entire reserve of ready cash. No! Here they were, and here they must remain. When night fell and Margaret realized this permanence, she felt inclined to collapse into a stupor of despair.

The heavy, smoky air clung to her bedroom, situated in the long, narrow projection at the rear of the house. Its window, positioned on the side of the oblong structure, faced the blank wall of a similar projection barely ten feet away. Through the fog, it loomed like a great barrier to hope. Inside, chaos reigned supreme. Every effort had been directed toward making her mother's room comfortable, leaving hers in disarray. Sitting on a box, Margaret noticed a direction card that seemed to have been written at Helstone—beautiful, beloved Helstone! Lost in dismal reverie, she eventually resolved to distract her mind. Suddenly, she recalled a letter from Edith that she had only partially read amidst the morning's rush.

Edith described their arrival at Corfu, their voyage along the Mediterranean, the music and dancing aboard ship, and the vibrant new life awaiting her. She spoke of a house with a trellised balcony overlooking white cliffs and a deep blue sea. Edith wrote fluently and well, capturing the salient points of scenes while enumerating enough trivial detail for Margaret to visualize them fully. Captain Lennox and another recently married officer shared a villa perched high on precipitous rocks overlooking the water. Even late in the year, their days seemed spent boating or on land picnics; entirely outdoors, pleasure-seeking, and joyful. Edith's existence appeared like a vast vault of blue sky above her, free—utterly free from flecks or clouds. Her husband attended drill, and she, the most musical officer's wife there, copied new and popular tunes from the latest English scores for the bandmaster; these seemed their most arduous duties. She expressed an affectionate hope that, if the regiment stayed another year, Margaret might join them for a long visit. She asked if Margaret remembered the rainy day twelve months ago in Harley Street, when Edith refused to wear her new gown to a dull dinner, getting it wet and splashed on the way to the carriage—a dinner where they had first met Captain Lennox.

Yes! Margaret remembered it vividly. Edith and Mrs. Shaw had dined; Margaret had joined the party later. The memory of the abundant luxury of those arrangements, the stately elegance of the furniture, the spaciousness of the house, and the peaceful, untroubled ease of the visitors surged forth, standing in stark contrast to the present gloom. That smooth sea of their former life closed up, leaving no trace of where they had all been. The habitual dinners, the social calls, the dance evenings—they all continued forever, even though her Aunt Shaw and Edith were no longer there, and she, naturally, was even less missed. She doubted if anyone in that old circle ever thought of her, save perhaps Henry Lennox. He, too, she knew, would strive to forget her due to the pain she had caused him. She had often heard him boast of his ability to banish any disagreeable thought.

Then she pondered further possibilities. Had she loved him as a lover, accepted his proposal, and then witnessed this shift in her father's opinions and their consequent decline in status, she could not doubt that Mr. Lennox would have received it with impatience. It was a bitter mortification in one sense, yet she could bear it patiently, knowing her father's purity of purpose, which strengthened her to endure his errors, however grave she deemed them. However, the fact that the world regarded her father as degraded, judging him in a rough, wholesale manner, would have oppressed and irritated Mr. Lennox. Realizing what *might* have been, she grew grateful for what *was*. They were at rock bottom now; they could not fall lower. Edith's astonishment and her Aunt Shaw's dismay would have to be met bravely when their letters arrived.

Rising slowly, Margaret began to undress, savoring the full luxury of acting leisurely, despite the late hour, after the frantic haste of the day. She fell asleep, hoping for some brightness, either internal or external. But if she had known how long it would be before dawn broke, her heart would have sunk even deeper. The season was most unfavorable for both health and spirits. Her mother contracted a severe cold, and Dixon herself was evidently unwell, though Margaret dared not burden her further than by attempting to nurse her or provide care. They could find no girl to assist; all were employed in the factories. Those who did apply were sharply rebuked by Dixon for presuming that someone of their station could ever be trusted to work in a gentleman's household. Thus, they relied on a charwoman in almost constant employment. Margaret longed to summon Charlotte, but aside from the financial imprudence of keeping a superior servant, the distance was prohibitive.

Mr. Hale secured several pupils recommended by Mr. Bell or through the direct influence of Mr. Thornton. Most were of an age when many boys would still be at school, yet, according to the prevailing—and seemingly well-founded—notions of Milton, a lad must be caught young to become a competent tradesman, acclimatized to the life of the mill, office, or warehouse. Sending a boy to even the Scottish universities rendered him unsettled for commercial pursuits; how much more so if he went to Oxford or Cambridge, where entry was barred until eighteen? Consequently, most manufacturers placed their sons in positions at fourteen or fifteen, ruthlessly pruning any branches leading toward literature or higher intellectual cultivation, hoping to channel the entire vigor of the plant into commerce.

Yet, there were wiser parents. Some possessed the sense to recognize their own deficiencies and strive to correct them. Indeed, there were a few men in the prime of life who held the stern wisdom to admit their ignorance and learn late what they should have mastered early. Mr. Thornton was perhaps the eldest of Mr. Hale's pupils and undoubtedly his favorite. Mr. Hale developed the habit of quoting Thornton's opinions so frequently and with such deference that it became a domestic joke to wonder what time remained for actual study during the allotted hour, as so much seemed devoted to conversation. Margaret encouraged this lighthearted perspective on her father's acquaintance with Mr. Thornton, sensing that her mother viewed this new friendship with jealous suspicion. While Mr. Hale had previously spent his time solely with books and parishioners as at Helstone, appearing indifferent to whether he saw much of his wife, he now looked eagerly forward to renewing his interactions with Mr. Thornton. Now, it seemed to hurt and annoy her as if he were slighting her company for the first time. Mr. Hale's excessive praise had the usual effect on his audience; they were slightly inclined to rebel against Aristides being constantly hailed as "The Just." After more than twenty years of a quiet life in a country parsonage, the energy that effortlessly conquered immense difficulties dazzled Mr. Hale. The power of Milton's machinery and the power of its men impressed him with a sense of grandeur that he accepted without probing the details of its operation.

However, Margaret ventured less among the machinery and the crowds. She observed less of power in its public manifestation and, coincidentally, found herself associated with one or two individuals who, in matters affecting the masses, must be acute sufferers for the sake of the many.
