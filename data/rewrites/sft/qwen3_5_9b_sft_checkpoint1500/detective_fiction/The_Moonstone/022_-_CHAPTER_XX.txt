The rumors preceding us had already set the household ablaze. Upon arriving, we found the servants in a frenzy of terror. As we approached my mistress's door, it flew wide open from within. She emerged, flanked by Mr. Franklin who struggled vainly to steady her, utterly consumed by the horror of what had transpired.

"You are responsible for this!" she shrieked, pointing a trembling finger at the Sergeant. "Gabriel! Give that wretch his money and let me never look upon him again!"

Of all the men standing there, the Sergeant alone possessed the composure to meet her fury. He was the only one fully in command of his faculties.

"I hold no greater accountability for this calamity, my lady, than you do," he replied, his voice respectful yet unyielding. "If, within half an hour, you still demand my departure, I will accept your dismissal rather than your payment."

His words carried a weight that cut through her hysteria. Even as she calmed sufficiently to allow Mr. Franklin to guide her back inside, the Sergeant's gaze swept across the remaining maids. While they trembled with fear, he noted that Penelope wept openly. "Once your father has changed his wet garments," he instructed gently, "come to me in his chamber."

Within the allotted hour, I had dressed in dry clothes and procured a spare outfit for the Sergeant. When Penelope entered, the sense of duty I felt toward her was overwhelming. I seated her on my lap, clasped her close, and offered a prayer for her soul. She buried her face in my chest, her arms tightening around my neck, and together we waited in a heavy, shared silence. I believed the tragedy stemmed from the deceased maid, whose spirit haunted both Penelope and me. The Sergeant moved to the window, gazing out into the night. Grateful for his consideration of our grief, I expressed my thanks quietly.

Noble folk possess the luxury of indulging their emotions; those of lower station do not. Necessity spares the former but shows no mercy to the latter, forcing us to bottle our feelings and press onward with our duties. This truth did not burden me; rather, it sharpened my resolve. Penelope and I stood ready when the Sergeant was prepared.

When questioned by the Sergeant regarding the motive behind her fellow servant's suicide, Penelope confessed, with the inevitability of fate, that it was her love for Mr. Franklin Blake. Pressed further, asking if she had confided in anyone else, she answered, "I kept it secret for Rosanna's sake." I felt compelled to interject, "And for Mr. Franklin's sake, my dear. If Rosanna died because of him, it was neither known nor caused by his fault. If he leaves the house today, let him depart without the torment of knowing the truth." The Sergeant nodded in agreement, falling silent as he compared Penelope's account with some private calculation of his own.

Just as the half-hour elapsed, my mistress's bell rang.

Heading to answer it, I encountered Mr. Franklin exiting his aunt's sitting-room. He informed me that her ladyship was ready to receive the Sergeant in my presence, though he wished to speak briefly with the officer first. On our return to the corridor, he paused to examine the railway timetable hanging in the hall.

"Are you truly departing, sir?" I asked. "Surely Miss Rachel will recover if you grant her time?"

"She will recover," Mr. Franklin answered, "only when she realizes I am gone and can never see me again."

I initially mistook his tone for resentment over how Miss Rachel had treated him, but the truth lay deeper. Since the police arrived, the mere mention of his name had ignited Miss Rachel's temper. Having loved his cousin too deeply to admit it to himself until the reality forced itself upon him, he had made his decision the moment she fled to her aunt's—a resolution born of a man's spirit: to leave.

Speaking in my presence, Mr. Franklin described his aunt's willingness to acknowledge her hasty words. He then asked if the Sergeant would, under these circumstances, accept his fee and withdraw from the investigation of the Diamond. The response was immediate and firm.

"No, sir," the Sergeant declared. "My fee is earned only by the performance of my duty. I cannot accept it until my duty is complete."

"I fail to follow you," Mr. Franklin said, confused.

"I shall clarify, sir," the Sergeant continued. "Upon arrival, I undertook to shed light on the disappearance of the Diamond. Now, I am prepared to fulfill that promise. Once I have laid out the facts to Lady Verinder as they stand, and advised her on the necessary steps to reclaim the Moonstone, the burden of this inquiry will fall from my shoulders. Only then, after she decides whether to permit me to proceed, shall I consider my work done and claim my reward."

In those words, the Sergeant reminded us that even within the Detective Police, a man's reputation was a fragile currency.

The logic of his stance was irrefutable, silencing all objections. Rising to escort him to my lady's room, I asked if Mr. Franklin desired to attend.

"Not unless Lady Verinder insists," Mr. Franklin replied. Leaning down to whisper to me as I followed the Sergeant out, he revealed a vulnerability that chilled me. "I know exactly what that man intends to say concerning Rachel. I love her too much to listen to his accusations and expect to maintain my temper. Please, leave me alone."

I departed, leaving him leaning against the window sill, a broken figure with his face buried in his hands, while Penelope hovered near the doorway, longing to offer solace. In his position, I might have summoned her, for there is often great comfort in having another human being validate one's suffering. Yet, perhaps, when I turned my back, he did call her in? If so, justice demands I record that Penelope would have spared no effort to comfort Mr. Franklin Blake.

Meanwhile, the Sergeant and I proceeded to my lady's chamber.

During our previous consultations, she had rarely lifted her gaze from the book resting on her desk. This time, however, a transformation had occurred. She met the Sergeant's stare with an intensity equal to his own. A fierce family pride illuminated every line of her face, and I knew that when a woman of her steel faced the worst the Sergeant could conjure, he would find a formidable adversary indeed.
