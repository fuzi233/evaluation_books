The drawing-room of Madame de Bernstein buzzed with activity as her ladyship’s guests prepared for the evening’s entertainment. Cousin Maria, radiant as the blossoms she carried, directed the placement of flowers—roses and sweet-williams arranged in porcelain vases—while liveried footmen laid out card tables with meticulous precision. The afternoon sun slanted through the windows, gilding the wainscoted walls and casting long shadows over the green beyond, where powdered ladies and gentlemen strolled in their brocaded finery. From the Pantiles, the lively hum of the Wells drifted upward, mingling with the strains of a distant band.

For those who thrived on society’s whirl, Madame Bernstein’s apartments were perfection—bright, bustling, and alive with the promise of amusement. Yet Harry Warrington, standing awkwardly amidst the flurry, felt the weight of his mourning attire and the rustic simplicity of his Virginia upbringing. His aunt’s sharp eyes missed nothing.

“You dine at the White Horse,” she declared, rapping her cane. “A young man shouldn’t loiter about old women’s skirts. And for heaven’s sake, spare no expense—you are a gentleman.”

Maria’s downcast glance betrayed disappointment, but Harry bowed and departed, his mind a tumult of pride and unease. His new lodgings were handsomely appointed, complete with a groom awaiting hire and a valet for Gumbo. London tailors, swift as vultures, presented their cards, and Harry, surveying his homespun wardrobe, felt the first prick of self-doubt. The linen, spun by his mother’s diligent hands, seemed suddenly coarse beneath the critical gaze of fashion.

At the White Horse, the ordinary was thinly attended, the cream of society having flocked to an entertainment at Summer Hill. Seated alone, Harry became the unwilling subject of conversation between two gentlemen—one a foppish baron with an exaggerated queue, the other a sharp-featured nobleman addressed as *milor*. Their French was execrable, their insinuations unmistakable.

“A Creole upstart,” murmured the baron. “The Baroness’s latest scheme, no doubt.”

Harry’s temper flared. “You forget yourself, sir,” he interrupted, voice ringing across the room.

The nobleman, startled, turned. Deafness had spared him the insult, but his companion smirked. “My lord plays with anyone who holds cards—and hears even less.”

Yet when the truth of Harry’s identity dawned, the nobleman’s demeanor shifted. With a bow as polished as it was insincere, he offered apologies. “Had I known you, sir, I should never have spoken so.”

Harry accepted the courtesy stiffly, though distrust lingered. The encounter left a bitter taste—a reminder that in this glittering world, reputation was both weapon and shield.

As evening approached, Harry resolved to meet its challenges with the dignity of his name—whether clad in Virginia homespun or the finest lace London could provide.
