The evening had deepened into twilight, the sky clear and still, perfect for the stars to emerge one by one like silver sparks in the velvet dark.

"There will be just enough light to guide me home," said Miss Keeldar, standing at the edge of the rectory garden, preparing to leave Caroline behind.

"You must not go alone, Shirley. Fanny will come with you."

"That she shall not. What need have I to fear in my own parish? I’d walk from Fieldhead to the church any bright midsummer night, three hours later than this, just for the joy of watching the stars—and perhaps catching a glimpse of a fairy."

"But wait until the crowd has dispersed."

"Agreed. There go the five Misses Armitage, all in a row. Here comes Mrs. Sykes’s phaeton, Mr. Wynne’s close carriage, and Mrs. Birtwhistle’s little cart. I don’t care to say good-bye to them all, so let’s slip into the garden and hide among the laburnums for a moment."

From the church porch now emerged the rector, his curates, and the churchwardens, exchanging handshakes and cheerful remarks—praise for the sermon, warnings about the night air, and general gossip. Gradually the throng thinned, carriages rolled away. As Miss Keeldar stepped from her floral shelter, Mr. Helstone entered the garden and met her.

"Oh, I wanted you!" he said. "I feared you'd already gone. Caroline, come here."

Caroline approached, expecting a scolding for having been absent during the service. But the rector had other matters on his mind.

"I won’t be sleeping at home tonight," he continued. "I’ve just met an old friend and promised to join him. I’ll likely return by noon tomorrow. Thomas, the clerk, is engaged, and I can’t get him to stay in the house as I usually do when I’m away. Now—"

"Now," interrupted Shirley, "you want me to stand in as gentleman—the first gentleman of Briarfield, in short—to take your place, guard your niece and maids while you're gone?"

"Exactly, captain. I thought the role suited you. Will you do Caroline the favour of staying here for one night? Instead of returning to Fieldhead?"

"And what of Mrs. Pryor? She expects me home."

"I’ll send word. Come now, decide. It’s growing late; the dew is falling thick. You two will enjoy each other's company, I’m sure."

"I promise to stay with Caroline," replied Shirley. "As you say, we’ll enjoy each other’s company. We won’t be parted tonight. Now, go to your friend, and worry not for us."

"If anything should disturb the night, captain—if you hear a lock being picked, glass cut, or stealthy footsteps near the house—well, I needn’t hesitate to tell you, who wear such a bold heart beneath that girl’s ribbon sash—that such things are very possible these days—what would you do?"

"I don’t know. Perhaps faint—collapse and need lifting up. But Doctor, if you give me this post of honour, you must arm me. What weapons are in your fortress?"

"You couldn’t handle a sword?"

"No. A carving knife, though—I could manage that."

"You’ll find one in the dining-room sideboard—a lady’s knife, light and sharp as a poniard."

"It’ll suit Caroline. But I must have pistols. I know you have some."

"I have two pairs. One pair I can lend you. They hang over the mantelpiece in my study, in cloth cases."

"Loaded?"

"Yes—but not cocked. Cock them before bed. It’s a great compliment, captain, to lend you these. If you were part of the awkward squad, you wouldn’t get them."

"I’ll take care. You needn’t delay, Mr. Helstone. You may go now."

"He’s kind to lend me his pistols," she remarked as the rector passed through the gate. "But come, Lina—let’s go in and eat. I was too irritated at tea by Mr. Sam Wynne’s presence to eat, and now I’m actually hungry."

They entered the house and made their way to the dimly lit dining room. Through the open windows drifted the evening breeze, carrying the scent of flowers from the garden, distant echoes of retreating footsteps, and a soft, indistinct murmur—until Caroline leaned against the casement and whispered, “Shirley, I hear the beck in the Hollow.”

She rang the bell, asked for a candle and bread and milk—Miss Keeldar’s usual supper and her own. When Fanny brought the tray, she tried to shut the windows and shutters, but Caroline stopped her.

"Not yet," she said. "The twilight is too calm, the air too sweet to exclude."

They ate in silence. Once, Caroline rose to move a glass of flowers from the sideboard—their fragrance too strong for the warm room. On her way back, she half-opened a drawer and pulled out something that glittered sharply in her hand.

"You assigned this to me, then, Shirley?" she said. "It’s bright, keen-edged, finely tapered—dangerous-looking. I’ve never felt the urge to use it against another person. It’s hard to imagine circumstances that would make me raise this long knife."

"I’d hate to do it," Shirley replied, "but I think I could, if forced by certain needs I can picture." She sipped her milk quietly, her face thoughtful, slightly pale—as always, really. She was never flushed.

After finishing their meal, Fanny was called again. She and Eliza were told to retire—they were eager to, tired from the day’s work: slicing currant buns, filling urns and teapots, running errands. Soon, the sound of the maids’ chamber door closing echoed softly. Caroline took a candle and walked through the house, checking every window and door, even the haunted back kitchen and the vault-like cellars. Then she returned.

“There’s no spirit nor flesh in this house now that shouldn’t be,” she said. “It’s nearly eleven—time for bed. Yet I’d rather stay awake a little longer, if you don’t mind, Shirley.” She placed two pistols on the table. “I’ve brought them from my uncle’s study. Examine them if you wish.”

"Why do you want to stay up?" Miss Keeldar asked, picking up the guns, inspecting them, then setting them down.

"Because I feel strangely restless in my chest."

"So do I."

"Is this uneasiness caused by something in the air—electrical, perhaps?"

"No. The sky is clear, the stars countless. It’s a beautiful night."

"But very still. I can hear the water churning over its stones in Hollow’s Copse as clearly as if it ran below the churchyard wall."

"I’m glad it’s quiet. A howling wind or storm would drive me mad right now."

"Why, Shirley?"

"Because it would drown out the sounds I’m trying to hear."

"Do you listen toward the Hollow?"

"Yes. It’s the only direction from which we might hear anything now."

"The only one, Shirley."

They sat together near the window, arms resting on the sill, heads tilted toward the open lattice. By starlight and the lingering twilight of June, they saw each other’s young faces—soft, solemn, alert.

"Mr. Helstone thinks we don’t know where he’s gone, nor why, nor what he hopes for," murmured Shirley. "But I suspect much—do you not?"

"I suspect something."

"All those gentlemen—your cousin Moore included—think we’re already asleep, unaware."

"Caring nothing for them—hoping or fearing nothing," added Caroline.

They sat in silence for nearly half an hour. The night remained hushed—only the church clock marking time in quarters. A few words passed about the chill air. They drew their scarves tighter, put on their bonnets again, and waited.

Near midnight, the persistent, monotonous bark of the house dog broke the stillness. Caroline rose and slipped silently through the dark hall to the kitchen, intending to quiet him with a piece of bread. She succeeded. Returning to the dining room, she found it dark—Shirley had extinguished the candle. Her silhouette stood near the open window, leaning out.

Caroline said nothing. She crept beside her. The dog began barking wildly again. Suddenly, he stopped—listening. The two women listened too, no longer hearing only the mill stream. From the road below the churchyard came a new sound—near, muffled, rhythmic—a dull tramp of marching feet.

It grew louder. They realized it wasn’t just a few men—dozens, hundreds. They saw nothing—the high hedges formed a green wall between them and the road. But hearing wasn’t enough. As the column passed the rectory, they felt it more deeply. Then a voice—just one word—cut through the silence.

"Halt!"

The march ceased. A low conversation followed—too faint to catch.

"We *must* hear this," said Shirley.

She turned, took the pistols from the table, slipped out through the glass door of the dining room, and crept down the path to the garden wall. Caroline hesitated—she wouldn’t have left the house alone—but where Shirley went, she would follow. She glanced at the knife on the sideboard, but left it. Soon she stood beside her friend.

They dared not look over the wall—fear of being seen. They crouched behind it. These were the words they heard:

"It looks like a rambling old place. Who lives here besides the damned parson?"

"Only three women—his niece and two servants."

"Do you know where they sleep?"

"The girls at the back—the niece in a front room."

"And Helstone?"

"Yonder’s his chamber. He had a light, but none now."

"Where would you enter?"

"If I were ordered to do his job—and he deserves it—I’d try that long window—it opens to the dining room. I could feel my way upstairs. I know his room."

"What about the women?"

"Leave them be unless they scream. Then I’d soon silence them. I’d prefer to find the old man asleep. If he wakes, he’ll be dangerous."

"Does he have weapons?"

"Firearms—always—and always loaded."

"Then you’re a fool to stop us here. A shot would raise the alarm. Moore would be on us before we turned around. We’d miss our main goal."

"You could go on. I’d take Helstone alone."

A pause. One man dropped a weapon—it clanged on the stone path. At once, the rectory dog barked fiercely.

"That ruins everything!" the voice snapped. "He’ll wake. A noise like that could rouse the dead. You didn’t mention there was a dog. Damn you! Forward!"

Forward they went—tramp, tramp—with slow, steady, countless steps. They vanished.

Shirley stood upright, peered over the wall, along the road.

"Not a soul remains," she said.

She stood silent. Then: "Thank God."

Caroline repeated it—though less steadily. She trembled. Her heart pounded; her face was cold, her brow damp.

"Thank God for us!" she whispered. "But what happens elsewhere? They passed us so they could strike others."

"They did well," Shirley said calmly. "Others will defend themselves. They’re ready. We weren’t. My finger was on the trigger—ready to greet that man if he’d entered. But behind him came three hundred. I had neither hands nor weapons for them. I couldn’t protect you, myself, or the two women sleeping under this roof. So again, I thank God for danger avoided."

After a pause, she added: "What should I do next? Not sit idle, thankfully. But walk to the Hollow."

"To the Hollow, Shirley?"

"Yes. Will you come?"

"Where those men are going?"

"They took the highway—we won’t meet them. The field path is as safe and silent as a skywalk. Will you go?"

"Yes," she answered—mechanically. Not because she wished to, or was prepared, or even unafraid. But because she couldn’t abandon Shirley.

"Then we must secure the windows and leave everything as tight as we can. Do you know why we’re going, Cary?"

"Yes—no—because you want to."

"Is that all? Are you so obedient to my whim? What a docile wife you’d make for a stern husband! Your face is whiter than moonlight, and the aspen at the gate shakes more than your fingers. And yet—docile, terrified, devoted—you’d follow me into real danger! Let me give your loyalty a reason. We’re going for Moore’s sake—to see if we can help him, warn him of what’s coming."

"Of course! I’m blind and weak, and you’re wise and strong, Shirley. I’ll go with you—I’ll gladly go!"

"I don’t doubt it. You’d die blindly for me—but wisely, willingly, for Moore. But truly, there’s no death tonight. No risk at all."

Caroline quickly shut the shutters and lattices. "Don’t worry—I’ll run as fast as you, Shirley. Take my hand. Let’s cross the fields."

"But you can’t climb walls?"

"To-night I can."

"You’re afraid of hedges—and the beck we must cross?"

"I can cross it."

They started—ran. Walls slowed them, but didn’t stop them. Shirley moved like a deer—surefooted, agile. Caroline, timider, fell once or twice, bruising herself, but rose immediately, saying she was fine.

A quickset hedge blocked the last field. They searched for a gap—lost time. When found, it was narrow, but they squeezed through. Hair tore, skin scraped, silks snagged. But what mattered most was the delay. On the other side lay the beck—deep, rough-bedded. A narrow plank bridged it.

Shirley had crossed it many times—without fear. Caroline had never dared.

"I’ll carry you," said Miss Keeldar. "You’re light, and I’m strong. Let me try."

"If I fall in, you can fish me out," she replied, squeezing her hand gratefully.

Caroline stepped forward without hesitation, as if the plank were solid ground. Shirley followed—no more boldly. In their present state, fire or flood could not have stopped them. All Stilbro’ Moor ablaze would not have halted them. Yet one sound made them pause.

No sooner had they reached the bank than a shot cracked the air from the north. One second passed. Another blast echoed from the south. Within minutes, signals thundered from east and west.

"I thought we were dead at the first shot," Shirley breathed, drawing in a long breath. "I felt struck in the temples, thought your heart pierced. But the repeating shots explained it—signals. The attack is near. We should’ve flown. Our feet failed us."

Now came the copse. Emerging from it, they saw the mill below—its buildings, yard, road beyond. Their first glance confirmed Shirley’s guess. They were too late to warn.

The road, white by daylight, was now black with moving figures. Rioters gathered before the closed gates. A lone figure stood inside, addressing them. The mill itself was dark, motionless—no life, no light.

"Surely he’s prepared," Shirley whispered. "Surely that isn’t Moore facing them alone?"

"It is. We must go to him. I *will* go."

"_That_ you will not."

"Why did I come? Only for him. I’ll join him."

"Fortunately, you can’t. There’s no entrance."

"There *is* a small back entrance—secret. I know how to open it. I’ll try."

"Not with my permission."

Miss Keeldar wrapped her arms around Caroline’s waist and held her back. "Not one step," she insisted. "Moore would be shocked and embarrassed to see either of us. Men don’t want women near in real danger."

"I wouldn’t trouble—I’d help him."

"How? By inspiring courage? Pooh! These aren’t chivalric days. This isn’t a tournament—it’s about money, food, survival."

"It’s natural I should be at his side."

"As queen of his heart? His mill is his true love, Cary! Backed by factory and looms, he has all he needs. He fights not for love, but ledger and broadcloth. Don’t be sentimental. Robert isn’t like that."

"I *could* help him. I *will* reach him."

"Then go—I let you—seek Moore. You won’t find him."

She released her. Caroline darted off like an arrow from a bow. Behind her rang a teasing laugh: "Look well—don’t mistake!"

But there *was* a mistake. Caroline paused, gazed. The figure had retreated from the gate, rushing back to the mill.

"Hurry, Lina!" Shirley called. "Meet him before he enters."

Caroline returned slowly. "It’s not Robert," she said. "Not his height, form, bearing."

"I knew it wasn’t Robert when I let you go. How could you think it was? It’s a shabby private soldier—posted as sentinel. He’s safe inside now. I saw the door open for him. My mind eases. Robert is ready. Our warning would’ve been useless. I’m glad we came too late. It saved us a scene. Imagine bursting into the counting-house _toute éperdue_, only to find Messrs. Armitage and Ramsden smoking, Malone swaggering, your uncle sneering, Mr. Sykes sipping cordial, and Moore himself in full business mode! I’m thankful we missed it."

"Do you think many are in the mill, Shirley?"

"Plenty to defend it. The soldiers we saw earlier were headed there. The group around your cousin in the fields will be with him."

"What are they doing now? What’s that noise?"

"Hatchets and crowbars against the gates. They’re forcing them. Are you afraid?"

"No—but my heart races. I can barely stand. I’ll sit. Do you feel calm?"

"Hardly—but I’m glad I came. We’ll see what happens with our own eyes. We’re here—alone with the night, the stars, the whispering trees. No one knows. Instead of surprising the curate, the clothier, and the corn-dealer with a dramatic entrance, we stand unseen, witnesses to history."

"Shirley, Shirley—the gates are down! That crash sounded like felling oaks. Now they’re pouring through. They’ll break the mill doors as they broke the gate. What can Robert do against so many? Oh, if only I were closer—could hear him—speak to him! With my will, my desire to serve, I wouldn’t be a burden—I could help!"

"They come on!" cried Shirley. "How steadily they advance! Discipline in their ranks. I won’t say they’re brave—hundreds against tens isn’t proof—but" (she lowered her voice) "there’s suffering, desperation. These goads push them forward."

"Forward against Robert—and they hate him. Shirley, is there much chance they’ll win?"

"We’ll see. Moore and Helstone are of 'earth’s first blood'—not fools, not cowards."

A crash—smash—shiver—silenced their words. A volley of stones flew at the mill’s front, shattering every pane. A roar followed—a rioters’ yell—a Yorkshire, West Riding, cloth-district cry.

You may never have heard that sound, reader? Better so—for your ears, perhaps, and your heart. For if it tears the air in hatred of you or your ideals, wrath stirs. The lion rises to the hyena’s howl. Caste stands against caste. The middle class, indignant, descends upon the starving, furious mass. Tolerance fades. Justice wavers.

Caroline rose. Shirley put her arm around her. They stood together—still as trees.

That yell lasted long. When it ended, the night still hummed with a crowd.

"What next?" they whispered.

Nothing yet. The mill remained mute.

"He _can’t_ be alone!" Caroline breathed.

"I’d wager all I own he’s not alone—or alarmed," Shirley replied.

Shots fired from the mob. Had the defenders waited for this signal? It seemed so. The mill stirred—fire flashed from empty windows. A musket volley rang sharp through the Hollow.

"Moore speaks at last!" said Shirley. "And he seems gifted with tongues. That wasn’t one voice."
