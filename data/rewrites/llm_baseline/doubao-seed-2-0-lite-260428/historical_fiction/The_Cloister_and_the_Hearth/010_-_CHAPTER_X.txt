The banns of matrimony, much as they are read in our own day, required three proclamations—with one stark 15th century difference: they were almost always read on week days, and the eager young lovers Gerard and Margaret had no trouble persuading their new, untested parish curate to perform all three readings in the space of twenty-four short hours. Their soft, pleading faces spoke louder than any formal entreaty, and the curate could not find it in his heart to refuse.

The first two banns were cried at Monday matins and vespers, and to their giddy delight, not a single soul from the distant village of Tergou had turned up to the Sevenbergen church to spoil their plan. They arrived at dawn the next day, hearts hammering so hard they could barely breathe—only to freeze in horror as a complete stranger rose from the pews, and forbade the banns outright on the grounds that both parties were underage, and their parents had given no consent.

Trembling and half broken with despair, Margaret and Gerard huddled just outside the church door to confer, before the man who had ruined their quiet joy strolled over to them, all false sympathy. He claimed he hated to meddle in young love, but that forbidding banns was his only steady living: all they had to do was hand over one crown, and he would go straight back to the curate, admit he had been entirely misinformed, and clear the way for their marriage.

“A crown? I will give you a golden angel to do this,” Gerard cried, fumbling for his purse. The man agreed as quickly, and followed Gerard back to the curate to spin his story of a ridiculous, easily corrected mistake. The curate, none the wiser, promised to marry them the following morning at ten, and the professional bliss-obstructor trundled off with his golden angel in his pocket.

Like most petty, clever knaves, he was a fool. He headed straight for a Tergou tavern whose back green was given over to archery contests and village sports, and proceeded to drink the full value of his angel in ale. Drunk and bragging of his easy earnings, he spilled every detail of his scheme—right within earshot of Sybrandt, Gerard’s idle, ne’er-do-well brother, who haunted the tavern green every day. Sybrandt ran straight home to tell his father, only to find the old man had travelled to Rotterdam to buy cloth from merchant traders. He waved his older brother Cornelis out of the house, and spilled the whole story.

The two were Gerard’s black sheep siblings. Idleness rots the soul, and the greedy, years-long wait for their father’s inheritance had turned their hearts vicious; they would tear apart anyone who dared to interfere with the fortune they lusted after day and night, their parents’ hardworking, loving parsimony twisted in their selfish minds into ravenous avarice, the root of a hundred future crimes. They agreed not to tell their soft, uncertain mother, and instead headed straight for the burgomaster, Ghysbrecht Van Swieten. They were clever enough to guess he hated the match, even if they could not fathom why.

Ghysbrecht saw through the two lazy schemers in an instant, but kept his cards close to his chest. Drawing on his full magisterial dignity, he told them: “Since your father is not here, his duty falls to me, as father of this town. I know his mind perfectly. Leave all this to me, and above all, do not breathe a word of this to any woman in your household—chattering tongues unravel even the wisest plans.” He dismissed them a little superciliously, half ashamed to count two such oafs as his confederates.

When they trundled home, they found Gerard sitting on a low stool at his mother’s knee, her hand carding gently through his hair, speaking soft words to him and promising to take his part against his father, to thwart his love no more. Her change of heart was entirely characteristic: it was she who had torn Margaret’s portrait to bits in a fit of sharp female temper, only to watch Gerard turn deathly pale, sit motionless holding the scraps till tears blurred his eyes. She had been terrified of what she had done, her heart stung with bitter guilt, and though she would never admit what she had done out loud, she swore silently she would make it up to him. Her anger melted, and she had turned fully to Gerard’s side just as the two black sheep walked in.

Gerard, naive to the twists of feminine temper, was so overjoyed by her kindness he forgot he had ever suspected her of destroying the portrait, kissed her again and again, and went to bed as happy as a prince, convinced his mother was on his side at the very peak of his fate.

The next morning at ten, Gerard and Margaret stood in the Sevenbergen church, him radiant, her cheeks flushed bright red with blushes. Only Peter Brandt, Martin Wittenhaagen, and no other friends were with them—secrecy was their only protection. Margaret had refused the plan to flee to Italy; she could not leave her gentle, learned, helpless father alone. The pair had agreed they would retire to a quiet corner of Flanders for a few weeks till the storm of Tergou’s anger blew over.

The curate did not keep them waiting long. He stepped to the altar, called them forward hand in hand, the two happiest people in all of Holland, and opened his prayer book.

But before he could utter a single word of the sacred marriage rite, a harsh, rough voice roared “FORBEAR!” The constables of Tergou marched up the aisle and seized Gerard in the name of the law. Martin’s long hunting knife flashed out instantly.

“Hold, man!” the priest shouted. “You will not draw steel in the house of God! And you who interrupt this holy sacrament—what impiety is this?”

“There is no impiety, father,” the lead constable said respectfully. “This young man seeks to marry against his father’s explicit will, and his father has begged our burgomaster to deal with him according to the law. Let him deny it if he can.”

Gerard hung his head, he could not deny the truth.

“We take him to Rotterdam to abide the sentence of the Duke.”

Margaret cried out in utter despair. The two lovers, who had been on the brink of joy a moment before, sobbed clinging to each other so piteously that even the hardened constables stepped back, ashamed. One of the kinder ones leaned in, pretending to separate them, and whispered in her ear: “Rotterdam? It is a lie. We are taking him straight to our Tergou Stadthouse prison.”

They set Gerard on horseback, rode off as if heading for Rotterdam, but after a dozen sly detours and hidden halts, they circled back to Tergou. Just outside the town, a rickety canvas-covered cart was waiting for him. They thrust him inside, and by five that evening, he was smuggled secretly up the flights of the Stadthouse tower, and thrown into a tiny cell lit only by a narrow, slitted window crisscrossed with a single vertical iron bar. The only furniture in the whole room was a massive, heavy oak chest.

Prison in that brutal century was one of the surest roads to an early grave. Even at its mildest it was a horror: here, it meant unbroken solitude, cold, starvation, very often poison. Gerard knew he was in the hands of an enemy. “That look the burgomaster gave me on the road,” he thought. “This is more than my father’s wrath. I doubt I will ever see the light of day again.” He knelt, and commended his soul to God.

He stood up a moment later, lunged for the iron bar in the window, wrenched it loose from its rusty nails, and propped his knees against the wall to peer out. For only a minute he saw the sight no captive ever takes for granted: Martin Wittenhaagen’s broad back, sitting quiet by the brook at the foot of the tower, pretending to fish.

Gerard whistled sharp and soft. Martin turned in an instant, far more alert than any man who was really fishing, spotted Gerard’s face at the slit window, made a quick reassuring signal, grabbed his line and his bow, and vanished. Gerard’s heart lifted—his friends had not abandoned him. As he fell back from the window, the cell door creaked stealthily open, and Ghysbrecht Van Swieten stepped inside. His eyes flicked straight to the wrenched iron bar, then to the open window. He did not say a word. A hundred feet below the ground, if Gerard wanted to jump, why stop him? He set a brown loaf of bread and a pitcher of water down on the oak chest in dead silence.

Gerard’s first impulse was to bash the burgomaster’s skull in with the iron bar and run for the stairs, but Ghysbrecht gave a tiny, deliberate cough. Three armed, burly guards stepped into the doorway immediately.

“My orders,” the burgomaster said coldly, “are to keep you locked in here until you swear an oath to abandon Margaret Brandt, and return to the Church you were baptized into from your cradle.”

“Death sooner,” Gerard spat back.

“With all my heart,” the burgomaster said, and left him alone.

Martin ran straight to Sevenbergen, where he found Margaret pale, agitated, but sharp and full of fire, just finishing a letter to the Countess Charolois, begging her to intervene against Ghysbrecht’s cruelty and treachery.

“Courage!” Martin called as he burst in. “I found him. He’s locked in the top of the so-called Haunted Tower. Aye, I know that place—many a poor soul went up those stairs walking, and came down feet first.”

Margaret gasped, “Oh Martin! How did he look?”

“Just like Gerard Eliassoen, what else?”

“Was he pale? Did he look doomed?”

“Nay, nay, he was bright as a new pewter tankard when he saw me. He’s counting on us.”

Margaret shook her head, full of despair, and handed Martin the sealed letter for Rotterdam. But old Peter Brandt, who had sat quiet pondering the whole time, spoke up. “Put not your trust in princes,” he said.

“Alas, what else have we left to trust?” Margaret cried.

“Knowledge.”

“Your learning cannot help us here, father,” she sighed. “There is no ladder in all Holland high enough to reach that hundred foot window.”

“I need no ladder. All I need is one gold crown.”

Margaret had nine golden angels Gerard had left in her keeping, but she knew they could not bribe the burgomaster to let Gerard go free. But Peter spoke with such absolute confidence that he made them listen: he told them the old story of a Florentine knight locked in a tower higher than Gerard’s in Brescia, whose faithful squire had pulled him out with nothing more than a bow, a ball of silk, and a few pennies’ worth of rope. The trick was so simple, once he explained it, they could not believe they had ever thought it impossible. Margaret tore up the letter to the countess. They would trust to Peter’s old learning, and their own dexterity.

While they made their plans, two more figures crept through the dark streets of Tergou. Gerard’s sister Kate had overheard Cornelis and Sybrandt muttering about their brother, and snuck to the door of their room to listen, learning the terrible truth of Gerard’s imprisonment. She fetched their little hunchback brother Giles, woken him from his bed, and persuaded him to walk with her to the foot of the Haunted Tower to bring Gerard word that their father was still away in Rotterdam, and would surely set him free the moment he came home. “I would go alone,” she told him, “but I am afraid of the spirits men say haunt this tower. With you beside me, I have nothing to fear.”

Giles, who had never seen a ghost in his whole life, grabbed his lantern, and the two slipped out into the bright moonlit night.

Up in the tower cell, Gerard’s hope had faded with the setting sun. He was faint with hunger, too afraid to eat the bread the burgomaster had left him lest it be poisoned. He sat huddled on the oak chest, head drooped, a perfect picture of despair—until something thudded sharp against the wall behind him, and clattered to the floor at his feet.

It was an arrow, fletched with white feathers, no iron head on its tip. Gerard froze, thinking they meant to assassinate him through the window—until he spotted the soft skein of silk tied around its shaft. He pulled his tinderbox from his pocket, struck a tiny flame, and read the words scrawled on the arrow’s shaft, his heart leaping for joy: *Well beloved, make fast the silk to thy knife and lower to us: but hold thine end fast: then count an hundred and draw up.*

With almost superhuman strength he dragged the massive oak chest across the cell to sit under the window. He tied the silk to his knife, lowered it out the slit until it tugged tight, counted out one hundred slow beats, and began to pull the silk back up. It grew heavier, until at last he drew a thick knot through the window, with a length of stout whipcord tied to it. He pulled that up next, and it gave way to a heavy, thick hemp rope.

Margaret’s soft voice drifted up to him on the night air: “Draw up, Gerard, till you see liberty.”

He pulled till sweat streamed down his face, until the thick end of the new hemp rope slipped into his hands. He tied it fast through both handles of the oak chest, then jumped on it with all his weight to test its strength. On the third jump, the chest’s side popped open, spilling a whole mess of ancient parchments out across the stone floor. Gerard grabbed the iron bar he had wrenched from the window, lashed it across the rope and the window sill for extra bracing, then climbed up on the chest, pushed his legs out through the narrow window, and hung half over the drop, his heart thudding loud enough to hear in the still night.

He prayed a short, quiet prayer, then gripped the rope tight, hand over hand, lowering himself down the hundred foot stone wall. His palms burned, his fingers ached, he dared not look down for fear of dizziness, until at last his feet touched two warm, outstretched hands, and Margaret and Martin caught him before he hit the ground. All three clung together, sobbing quiet, overjoyed.

“Hush,” Martin hissed. “Away in silence, dears.”

They had barely gone a dozen steps, when a stream of lantern light burst out from the tower’s angle, cutting across their path like a bar of fire, followed by soft whispers and approaching footsteps.

“Back!” Martin breathed. “Keep in the shadows.” They scurried back past the dangling loose end of the escape rope, and pressed around the small side turret of the tower. The lantern light flickered past them, bobbing off into the distance.

“Someone’s hunting us,” Martin groaned. He slung his crossbow off his shoulder, strung it, and nocked an arrow. “I will drop two of them before they know where the shot came from.” He slipped around the turret’s corner, ready to loose.

A moment later, he staggered back, the bow wobbling in his hands, his face deathly pale, the arrow clattering to the ground. He grabbed Gerard’s arm, gasping so hard he could barely speak. “Let me feel flesh and blood. The haunted tower! The haunted tower! There it goes, climbing up the wall, its head all on fire, walking straight up the stone like it’s grass. Say your prayers if you know them—hell is loose in Tergou tonight.”

Gerard, trembling, straightened his shoulders. “I have power to exorcise spirits,” he said. “I will go and look.”

“Go alone then,” Martin whimpered. “I’ve seen it once, and I’ll never forget it as long as I live.”
