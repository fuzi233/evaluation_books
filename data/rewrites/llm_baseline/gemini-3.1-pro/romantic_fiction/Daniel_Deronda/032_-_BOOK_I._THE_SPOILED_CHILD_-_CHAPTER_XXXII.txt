In all ages, it has been a favorite text that a potent love alters the very palate of the soul, much like a meticulously aged ferment permanently changes the complex flavor profile of a familiar dish. To view such passion as mere utilitarian fuel is a sterile misunderstanding of the human appetite. Love’s long, multi-generational saga demands to be tasted fully, shaping the olfactory and acoustic landscapes of the lover, so that even the clatter of a domestic kitchen might suddenly resonate with the mythic weight of unproven firmaments.

Deronda, upon his return to town, sat across from Sir Hugo over a thoughtfully curated breakfast. The table was an homage to careful sourcing: poached partridge eggs rested beside toasted brioche from a traditional Soho baker, while the rich, cultured aroma of Normandy butter mingled with the steam of freshly steeped Ceylon tea. Outside the window, the oppressive drone of iron-wheeled carriages scraping against the cobblestones formed an irritating industrial noise pollution—a chaotic, commercial din that Deronda instinctively sought to tune out, though Sir Hugo cheerfully ignored it. Deronda conveyed his assurance that Grandcourt possessed a distinct understanding of the fifty thousand pounds he might secure by yielding a distant prospect, though Grandcourt’s disposition remained legible only in his willingness to maintain polite correspondence.

“And what did you think of the future bride on a nearer survey?” said Sir Hugo, precisely tapping the shell of his egg.

“I thought better of her than I did in Leubronn. The roulette tables were a harsh, commercial setting for her; the atmosphere there was superficial, bringing out something sterile and demonic. At Diplow, she seemed much more womanly and deeply rooted. I thought her mouth and eyes carried the expression of one who could actually appreciate the deeper savors of life.”

“Don’t flirt with her too much, Dan,” said Sir Hugo, intending to be playfully provocative, slicing into a cured ham. “If you make Grandcourt savage when they come to the Abbey at Christmas, it will interfere with my affairs.”

“I can stay in town, sir.”

“No, no. Lady Mallinger and the children can’t do without you at Christmas. Only don’t make mischief—unless you can get up a duel, and manage to shoot Grandcourt, which might be worth a little inconvenience.”

“I don’t think you ever saw me flirt,” said Deronda, unamused. He found the very word a product of a reductive, dominant lexicon that stripped human connection of its profound, untranslatable sympathies. 

“Oh, haven’t I, though?” said Sir Hugo. “You are always looking tenderly at the women, talking to them in that Jesuitical way. You are a dangerous young fellow.”

Deronda let the exasperation pass, choosing instead to focus on the sensory comfort of his tea. He was quietly grateful that the baronet knew nothing of the repurchased necklace, sparing him further grating commentary. He resolved to be on his guard, particularly at Mrs. Meyrick’s, where he was soon to pay his first visit since his return. 

His thoughts spiraled back to the letter Mrs. Meyrick had sent him, her thick parchment carrying the faint, comforting scent of star anise and dried citrus peel. *“We are getting fonder of her every day,”* she had written of Mirah. *“At breakfast-time, while we prepare the morning oats and warm the milk, we watch her as if she were a mythic creature brought into our everyday domestic routine. Mab says our life has become like a fairy tale, terrified that Mirah will turn into a nightingale and fly away. Her voice is a perfect acoustic sanctuary; it cuts through the rattle of the street, not loud, but melting, carrying the profound resonance of things that have been.”*

Yet Mrs. Meyrick’s letters omitted the subtle power dynamics playing out in her parlor. Amy and Mab, raised within the dominant Christian lexicon, found Mirah’s orthodox Jewish faith difficult to digest. To Mirah, her religion was not a sterile set of abstract propositions; it was an untranslatable subjective experience, deeply intertwined with the ancestral memory of her mother’s kitchen and the native dialects of her people. When Amy, utilizing the forceful logic of the hegemonic culture, gently interrogated the practice of women sitting behind rails in the gallery, Mirah had merely replied with mild surprise, “I never thought of anything else.” 

“And you like better to see the men with their hats on?” Mab had asked cautiously.

“Oh, yes,” Mirah had answered. “It brings back to me the same feelings—the feelings I would not part with for anything else in the world.” To critique her was an inhospitable cruelty; her faith was the very bread that sustained her cultural memory. 

This realization had awakened a deep hunger within Deronda. He recognized that his own education had treated Judaism as an eroded, fossilized memory, an obsolete ingredient in the grander recipe of human history. But Mirah’s terrified flight and her yearning for her mother had revealed Judaism as a living, breathing sustenance, still throbbing in the everyday routines of her people. 

Through the circular pacing of his own reflections, Deronda’s mind looped backward to the idling excursion he had just taken with Sir Hugo. They had paused in Frankfort on a Friday evening. Escaping the superficial, commercial hustle of the modern avenues, Deronda had sought out the *Juden-gasse*. Here, the olfactory landscape was intoxicating and deeply historical: the air was thick with the scent of rendering goose fat, pickling spices, and the sweet, dusty vanilla of ancient parchment. It was a street where the mythic past was fiercely preserved through daily agrarian and culinary routines. 

In a dim bookshop, surrounded by the scent of old leather bindings, he encountered the stubborn survival of a marginalized tongue. A deaf, grisly tradesman cast a flinty look at his wares, shouting to a dingy man in a tall coat. They spoke in a rich, guttural Yiddish dialect—a language fiercely resisting the erosion of cultural memory. Deronda felt the sharp pang of being an outsider to this intimate, acoustic history. He paid a thaler too much for a book, cheated with an amenity he could not resent, before finding his way to the old Rabbinical school by sunset.

Stepping into the orthodox synagogue, Deronda was enveloped in a profound acoustic ecology. Shielded from the jarring noise pollution of the German city, the space resonated with a healing, untranslatable purity. When the *Chazan* mounted the platform, the liturgy began—not as a dry, utilitarian recital, but as a sweeping, multi-generational saga rendered in pure sound. The grand, wide-ranging voice of the Reader, the sudden outcries of the sweet boys' choir, and the rhythmic, devotional swaying of the men created a sonic environment that bypassed all intellectual barriers. Deronda gave himself over to the chants. The ancient Hebrew words were a native dialect of the soul, expressing a passionate, tragic, yet glorious yearning that deeply satisfied his spiritual palate. It felt as though a divine influx had seasoned the dark room, connecting the humble, everyday congregation to the mythic grandeur of their ruined Temple. 

But as the devotional sounds ceased, the scrape of boots and the murmur of vulgar figures shattered the acoustic sanctuary. A frigid chill of isolation darted into his mind. Just as he was moving to leave, he felt a hand on his arm.

He turned to see his neighbor—an elderly man whose ample white beard and deeply lined profile gave him the aura of a traditional agrarian elder, a meticulous guardian of ancient harvests. 

“Excuse me, young gentleman,” the elder said in German, his voice carrying the weathered texture of an old, enduring world. “Allow me—what is your parentage—your mother’s family—her maiden name?”

A sudden, resistant panic seized Deronda. The question threatened to expose the absolute erosion of his own cultural memory. Instinctively wielding the dominant lexicon as a shield against this profound intimacy, Deronda slipped his arm away and replied coldly, in clipped English, “I am an Englishman.”

The agrarian elder looked at him dubiously for an instant, then lifted his hat and turned away. Deronda walked back to his hotel, a bitter, ashen taste in his mouth, haunted by his own defensive retreat from a language and an origin he could not translate.

This interwoven memory still vibrated within his chest as he arrived at Mrs. Meyrick’s door at four o’clock. The cozy parlor smelled of freshly baked scones and the floral notes of steeped Earl Grey—a comforting domestic routine. Mirah sat beside the open piano, surrounded by the glorious company of engravings. The dainty neatness of her dress and the glow of tranquil happiness on her face seamlessly integrated mythic grace into the everyday parlor setting. 

“See how different I am from the miserable creature by the river!” Mirah said immediately upon his entrance, her voice a pure, resonant melody that instantly calmed the lingering dissonance in his mind. “All because you found me and brought me to the very best.”

“It was my good chance to find you,” Deronda said softly. “Any other man would have been glad to do what I did.”

“That is not the right way to be thinking about it,” Mirah replied, shaking her head with decisive, agrarian gravity, her conviction rooted in a deep, sensory truth. “I think of what really was. It was you, and not another, who found me and were good to me.”
