It was all-out ungentlemanly chaos by the ballroom window, shoving match and all, as half the party crowded shoulder to shoulder yelling over one another. Below in the street, onlookers pressed up against the glass yelling unhelpful heckles like “Punch ‘im!” and “Where’s the bleedin’ copper?” while a ring of gawkers—with Madame Fribsby front and center, squawking so loud her bonnet askew—circled seething little French chef Alcide Mirobolant on one side, and our very own Arthur Pendennis on the other. The incomparable Chevalier Strong shouldered his way through the crush, shoving past Madame Fribsby who lit up like a Christmas tree at the sight of him and wailed “Save him! Save him!” like he was seconds from being fed to the lions.

The whole mess had kicked off right after Strong slipped away to the refreshment table. Pen, still simmering so hard over his recent waltz disaster that his ears were bright red—where he’d tripped, sent dance partner Blanche Amory flying face-first into the wall, and collapsed on top of a very portly dragoon and even portlier Miss Roundle, making him the laughing stock of the entire three counties—had snuck over to the window to stare out at the calm, sparkling sea and try to talk himself down from throwing a full hissy fit. He was even halfway admitting he’d acted like a peevish idiot all night, when a hand landed firm on his shoulder.

He spun around, and was so shocked he almost choked: the hand belonged to Mirobolant, Sir Francis Clavering’s head chef, his dark ringlets sticking up every which way, eyes blazing like he was ready to duel a man over an undercooked sole meunière. For a Pendennis, descendant of generations of gentry, to be tapped on the shoulder like an old pal by a *cook*? His blood boiled so hot he thought his top hat would catch fire.

“You speak French?” Mirobolant hissed at him in his native tongue.

“What’s that to you, mate?” Pen snapped back in English, stamping his foot.

“Ah, so you understand it at least,” the chef said, giving a dramatic little bow.

“Yes sir,” Pen gritted out. “I understand it perfectly well.”

“Then you will comprehend, Monsieur Pendennis,” Mirobolant rumbled, rolling his R’s so hard they rattled, “when I tell you you are a coward. Monsieur Pendennis—un lâche. Do you hear me?”

Pen lurched forward like he was about to tear the man in two.

“You know what that word means for men of honour?” the chef snarled, hand on his hip, glaring daggers.

“The only consequence is I hurl you straight out this window, you insolent little scoundrel!” Pen roared. He would absolutely have done it, too—he was a strapping young 23-year-old, the chef was barely five foot nothing, the window was two steps away—if two burly army captains hadn’t bodily thrown themselves between them, ladies hadn’t started screaming so loud the fiddles screeched to a halt, the whole rest of the party hadn’t come sprinting over, and Laura hadn’t pushed through the crowd white as a sheet begging someone to tell her what on earth was wrong. That was exactly when Strong wandered back from the champagne table, to find Mirobolant grinding his teeth and yelling oaths in thick Gascon French, and Pen trying and failing to look calm and dignified for the onlookers.

“What on earth happened?” Strong asked the chef in fluent Spanish.

“I am a Chevalier of July!” Mirobolant squawked, slapping his own chest hard enough to make himself cough. “He has insulted my honour!”

“What did he call you?” Strong asked, already fighting a grin.

“He called me—*a cook*!” the little man hissed, like it was the worst slur ever invented.

Strong almost burst out laughing right there. “Come on, mate, come away with me,” he said, herding the chef gently off. “Can’t have a duel in front of all these ladies. I’ll pass your grievance along to Mr. Pendennis. Poor bloke’s had a bit too much of the bubbly, don’t mind him,” he stage-whispered to the surrounding guests, leaving Pen to be swarmed by concerned pals and Laura.

Pen shrugged, still half cross. “The man was just about to offer his arm to a young lady, so I made an offhand comment that he was a cook. Next thing I know he’s calling me a coward and challenging me to a duel. If you blokes hadn’t held me back I would have tossed him right out that window.”

“Bloody right you would!” the local bucks cheered. “Impudent little foreign twat.”

“Wait—wait, I do feel a bit bad if I hurt his feelings, actually,” Pen added, and Laura’s face lit right up, relieved.

“Go shake his hand and apologize before you go home, won’t you Pen?” she said, soft as an angel. “Foreigners are way more touchy about their honour than we are, they have different manners. If you hurt a poor man’s feelings, I know you’d be the first to say sorry. Wouldn’t you, dear Pen?”

Pen took both her hands, looked at her sweet forgiving face, and swore he absolutely would. *She’s so fond of me*, he thought, giddy for half a second. Should I propose to her right now? No, not now. I’ve got to sort this stupid cook business out first.

“Won’t you stay and waltz with me then?” Laura asked, desperate to keep him from storming off. “I’m not scared to waltz with you!”

It was the sweetest possible line, but the worst possible thing she could have said. Pen immediately flashed back to face-planting in full view of the whole ballroom, everyone laughing at him, Laura included.

“I am never dancing again,” he said, dark and dramatic. “Never. Can’t believe you’d even ask me that.”

“Is it because you can’t get Blanche Amory to be your partner?” Laura teased, not knowing how badly she’d just stepped in it.

“Because I don’t want to make a total fool of myself for everyone to laugh at!” Pen snapped back. “For *you* to laugh at me, Laura! I saw you snickering with Pynsent. No man gets to laugh at me, not on my watch.”

“Pen! Pen, don’t be daft!” Laura cried, hurt by his sudden vicious pride. He was already glaring across the room at poor Mr. Pynsent like he wanted to duel him next, too, over absolutely nothing. “Who thinks worse of you for tripping in a waltz? Why are you so quick to assume everyone’s making fun of you?”

Of course, that exact second Mr. Pynsent wandered over to them, all smarmy polite, and said: “Lady Rockminster sent me to ask if I may escort you in to supper, Miss Laura?”

“I—er, I was going to go in with my cousin,” Laura fumbled.

“Nonsense, you’re in perfectly good hands with him,” Pen said, cold as ice. “I’ll be off home, then. Night.”

“Good night, Mr. Pendennis,” Pynsent said, dry as dust (which translated very clearly to: “Go to hell, you insolent jealous little prat I’d love to box around the ears”). Pen just gave him a stiff little bow, ignored Laura’s imploring little looks, and stormed right out of the ballroom.

Pynsent sighed, staring out at the moonlit sea. “Beautiful night out, isn’t it? Way nicer to walk on the beach than be stuck in this hot stuffy room full of people I have to pretend to like. I’ve had to schmooze the attorney’s daughters, the apothecary’s wife, half the village, all night. Some bloke in the refreshment room forced me to drink champagne with him, looked like a sailor, half sloshed, dressed like a circus clown. As a local politician I’ve got to be nice to everyone, but it’s absolute torture—especially when I’d much rather be somewhere way more interesting,” he added, blushing bright pink.

“Sorry,” Laura said, not listening at all, her head full of Pen’s stupid fight. “I was distracted, I’m still worried about that quarrel between my cousin and that French gentleman.”

“Your cousin’s had a pretty unlucky night,” Pynsent said. “He’s managed to annoy about five different people already: the officer he fought with, the lady he tripped over in the waltz, Blanche Amory, the poor chef, and I don’t think he’s all that fond of me right now either.”

“Well, he left me in your care, didn’t he?” Laura said, peeking up at his face then dropping her eyes all coy like a guilty schoolgirl.

“Then I forgive him absolutely everything!” Pynsent said, delighted, and offered her his arm to lead her to the supper room.

Laura didn’t care a fig about the fancy spread (that the local newspaper would gush about for three whole paragraphs the next day, anyway). She was too miserable, replaying the whole night: Pen was quick to anger, petty, selfish, jealous, and rude the second his pride was pricked. How on earth could Helen have spent months dropping hints that Laura should marry him? Would that make him happy, ever?

Half an hour later, when it felt like three hours, a waiter brought her a crumpled pencil note from Pen: “Met Cooky downstairs, he was all ready to duel me. I apologized. I’m glad I did it. Wanted to talk to you tonight, but I’ll save what I have to say till you get home. God bless you. Dance all night with Pynsent, have fun. — Pen.” Laura breathed a huge sigh of relief. The boy still had a good heart under all that silly pride.

Pen was already halfway down the stairs by that point, kicking himself for being such a prat to Laura. He itched to turn right back up and apologize to her, but then he remembered he’d left her alone with that stuck-up Pynsent, and his pride wouldn’t let him go back and grovel in front of the other bloke. He might as well make peace with the chef first.

Strong was waiting for him in the hall, grinning like he’d just pulled the best prank in the world. “Got him locked in the coffee room,” he said. “Got a brace of dueling pistols and a candle on the table for you. Or do you fancy swords out on the beach? Mirobolant’s a dead shot with foils, killed four royal guards in the July barricades all by himself, he says.”

“For Christ’s sake,” Pen groaned. “I can’t duel a *cook*.”

“He’s a Chevalier of July! They salute him in France!” Strong cackled.

“Are you seriously asking me to fight a household servant?” Pen snapped. “I ought to call a policeman on him, not duel him!”

“Thank you very much, I’m not getting dragged into a hair-trigger fight with you two idiots,” Strong said, laughing. “I’ve already calmed him down. Told him you didn’t call him cook to be rude, that it’s against English custom for a household employee to offer his arm to the daughter of the house. Also—between you and me—Madame Fribsby just told me he’s head over heels madly in love with Blanche Amory. That’s why he lost his temper, he was about to ask her to dance.”

Pen burst out laughing so hard his sides hurt. All his rage vanished instantly. He’d been exactly that jealous fool over Blanche all night, too, dying for an excuse to pick a fight with anyone who looked at her. He wandered into the coffee room, held out his hand to Mirobolant, and made a very dramatic, very slightly grammatically incorrect French apology, waxing poetic about how sincerely sorry he was to have hurt the great artist’s feelings, and how he never meant to insult him for a second.

“Bravo! Bravo!” Strong cheered, cackling at his terrible but very earnest French. Mirobolant, of course, immediately forgave him, apologized for calling Pen a coward, shook his hand, and the whole mess was sorted. Pen scribbled that note to Laura immediately, then he and Strong piled into the Butcher Boy gig to drive back to Fairoaks.

On the drive back, Strong complimented him on being such a good sport, and on his fluent French. “Mate, you speak French like Chateaubriand himself, I swear.”

“Oh, yeah, been speaking it since I was a kid,” Pen said, preening, and Strong had the decency to hold his laugh in for five whole minutes before he burst into hysterics so hard he nearly fell out of the carriage. Pen never worked out what was so funny.

By the time they rolled up to Fairoaks at dawn, the Baymouth ball was long over. Madame Fribsby and Mirobolant were trundling home in the Clavering family carriage, Laura was fast asleep in a soft bed at Lady Rockminster’s, and Blanche Amory was scribbling a dramatic sonnet to her “murdered, heroic, persecuted father” in her diary, after confiding to her maid that she’d just had the most romantic adventure of her life, meeting a man who’d known her long-lost dad.

Pen got back to Fairoaks and hadn’t said a word of the big proposal he’d been planning to Laura, but he told himself it didn’t matter—she’d be home the day after tomorrow, and he was dead certain Laura would say yes. His mom Helen had spent months gushing about it, after all, Laura was half in love with him already, he just knew it.

When Helen greeted him, all anxious to hear if he’d popped the question, Pen laughed it off, told her there was no way he could propose in the middle of all that chaos, that there was loads of time for sentiment once Laura got home. He spent the next two days preening in the mirror, packing his books and portmanteaus, sorting his papers, cleaning his gun, making all his plans to move to London and make his fortune. He was 23, after all, high time he got serious. He planned to take chambers in an Inn of Court, knock out his half-finished tragedy, comedy and novel, sell them for a fortune, make it big at the bar, marry Laura, and live happily ever after, no need to drain his poor mom’s purse. He wandered around the Fairoaks garden smoking his pipe, telling his old dog Ponto all about his grand plans, and Ponto wagged his tail and nuzzled his hand like he agreed every single word.

Laura rolled home late on the second day, and of course, Pynsent was the one who drove her over. Pen’s face immediately went thunder-dark, much to Laura’s distress, but Helen was none the wiser, desperate to get the two of them alone together so Pen could propose that very night. She faked being tired and announced she was heading up to bed, but Laura blurted out that she was exhausted too, and beat her to it, leaving Pen stuck waiting another whole day to pop the question. He huffed and pouted about being kept waiting like some random suitor in antechambers, then slept like a log all night.

He woke up at dawn to find his mom leaning over his bed, nudging him awake. “Pen, honey, get up! It’s the most beautiful sunny morning ever. I couldn’t sleep a wink after sunrise, Laura’s been out in the garden for an hour already, tending her roses. Go find her!”

Pen laughed, and kissed his mom’s cheek. He knew exactly what she was up to, her little matchmaking scheme, and he thought it was adorable. Half an hour later, he was shaved, dressed in his finest coat, and strolling down the garden path to find Laura, feeling weirdly serious, like he was about to perform a huge sacrifice. *I’m doing this to make mom happy*, he thought. Laura’s sweet, she lent me that money to go to university, I owe it to her. Two of the best women in the world want me to marry her, how bad can it be?

Laura was in her gardening uniform: big floppy straw hat with a bright blue ribbon, a linen pinafore belted tight at the waist, heavy gardening gauntlets on her hands, looking very much like a tiny, sweet soldier out to war with the rose thorns. She spotted him coming, and grinned that exact same grin she’d had the night he face-planted in the waltz, and Pen’s mood soured instantly.

“Sorry for the glove,” she said, pressing his hand gently through the thick leather. “We’re not still cross, are we, Pen?”

“Why are you laughing at me?” Pen said, scowling. “You laughed at me the other night at the ball, made me look a total fool in front of everyone.”

“Darling Arthur, I didn’t mean a bit of harm,” she said, laughing. “You and Miss Roundle looked so daft all tangled up on the floor together, I couldn’t help it! You popped right back up in two seconds, it was Miss Roundle who looked the most miserable, sitting there in her red crape dress all bewildered.”

“Ugh, confound Miss Roundle,” Pen groaned.

“I’m sure she was very confounded,” Laura teased, and did a silly little impression of Miss Roundle’s lost face, before stopping, feeling bad. “We shouldn’t laugh at her, but you can’t blame us for teasing you just a little bit over such a silly little accident, can you?”

“Not you, Laura,” Pen said, bitter. “Of all people, you shouldn’t laugh at me.”

“Oh, really? Are you too grand for a silly joke?” Laura asked, arching an eyebrow.

“No, I’m the most pathetic guy alive,” Pen sighed. “Haven’t you made fun of me enough?”

“I have never made fun of you!” Laura cried, shocked. “I thought a clever man like you wouldn’t mind a little joke from his sister, almost sister. If I hurt your feelings, I’m so sorry, Pen, truly.”
