Elinor's curiosity regarding Mrs. Ferrars was finally quelled. She had uncovered sufficient evidence to render any further alliance between the families strategically unsound. Having witnessed firsthand Mrs. Ferrars's pride, her meanness, and her rigid, prejudiced hostility toward herself, Elinor comprehended the formidable barriers that had previously obstructed Edward's union with her. Yet, paradoxically, these observations also served as a shield; she was grateful for the existence of this greater obstacle, which spared her the agony of depending on Mrs. Ferrars's volatile whims or seeking validation from her judgment. While she did not celebrate Edward's entanglement with Lucy, she resolved that, had Lucy possessed even a fraction of amiability, she *ought* to have rejoiced.

Elinor marveled at the source of Lucy's exuberance. How could such trivial civility elevate Lucy's spirits so profoundly? How could vanity and interest conspire to interpret attention paid solely because she was *not* Elinor as a personal compliment? Lucy's eyes had already betrayed the truth, yet the following morning, Lady Middleton facilitated a private audience in Berkeley Street specifically for Lucy to declare her happiness. The timing proved fortuitous, as a message from Mrs. Palmer shortly thereafter removed Mrs. Jennings from the scene.

"My dear friend," Lucy exclaimed the moment they were alone, "I must discuss my felicity. Can anything compare to Mrs. Ferrars's demeanor yesterday? She was excessively affable. Recall how I dreaded encountering her; yet, the instant I was introduced, her behavior radiated such genuine fondness that it suggested she had instantly taken a fancy to me. Was it not so? You witnessed it all; surely you were struck by it?"

"She was certainly civil to you," Elinor replied cautiously.

"Civil!" Lucy retorted sharply. "Did you perceive nothing beyond mere politeness? I discerned far more. A kindness reserved exclusively for me! No pride, no hauteur—your sister included, displaying equal sweetness and affability!"

Elinor attempted to pivot the conversation, but Lucy persisted, demanding acknowledgment of her reasons for happiness. "Undoubtedly, had they known of your engagement, their treatment would have been equally flattering," Elinor conceded. "However, since that was not the case—"

"I anticipated your response," Lucy interrupted briskly. "Yet, there is no logical basis for Mrs. Ferrars to harbor affection for me unless she genuinely feels it, and her approval is paramount. Do not try to dissuade me from my contentment. I am confident all will conclude favorably, devoid of the difficulties I once feared. Mrs. Ferrars is a charming woman, as is your sister. Both are delightful! I marvel that I have never heard you describe Mrs. Dashwood as agreeable!"

Elinor remained silent, offering no rebuttal. "Are you ill, Miss Dashwood? You appear listless and silent. Are you unwell?"

"I have never been healthier," Elinor stated firmly.

"I am relieved to hear it," Lucy responded warmly. "Though you did not look it. I would be devastated if *you* were ill; you have been my greatest comfort. Heaven knows what I would have endured without your friendship."

Elinor managed a polite response, though she doubted its efficacy. Satisfied, Lucy continued, "I am absolutely convinced of your regard for me. Next to Edward's love, it is my supreme solace. Poor Edward! But now, a positive development arises: we shall meet frequently. Lady Middleton delights in Mrs. Dashwood, ensuring we remain in Harley Street, where Edward spends considerable time with his sister. Furthermore, Lady Middleton and Mrs. Ferrars intend to visit soon. Both women have explicitly stated they would gladly receive me whenever convenient. They are such charming individuals! If you ever convey my sentiments to your sister, you cannot praise her too highly."

Elinor withheld any encouragement for Lucy to reveal these thoughts to her sister. Lucy pressed on, "I would have detected any aversion from Mrs. Ferrars instantly. Had she offered only a perfunctory courtesy, ignoring me thereafter with a cold gaze, I would have despaired immediately. I could not endure such rejection. Where she dislikes, her animosity is invariably violent."

Before Elinor could respond to this declaration of triumph, the door burst open, and the servant announced Mr. Ferrars. Edward entered moments later. The atmosphere shifted instantly into an awkward stalemate. Every countenance reflected the discomfort; each appeared foolish. Edward hesitated, torn between exiting the room and advancing further. They were trapped in a triad without the buffer of a third party—a scenario both had desperately sought to avoid.

The ladies recovered composure first. It was not Lucy's role to assert dominance, nor could she abandon the facade of secrecy. She merely cast a glance of tender affection at him before remaining silent. Elinor, however, faced a more arduous task. Driven by necessity for both his sake and her own, she summoned the resolve to greet him with a demeanor that was nearly natural and open. Through sheer exertion, she smoothed her features, suppressing the sting of injustice and the presence of Lucy's scrutinizing gaze. She assured him of her happiness in seeing him and expressed regret for her absence during his previous call. She maintained her duties as a friend and relative, undeterred by Lucy's watchful eyes.

Her efforts provided Edward with enough reassurance to sit, though his embarrassment surpassed theirs, a disparity justified by the circumstances. His heart lacked Lucy's indifference, and his conscience could not find the ease Elinor possessed. Lucy adopted a demure, fixed posture, contributing nothing to alleviate the tension. Consequently, Elinor became the sole speaker, volunteering details regarding her mother's health and their arrival in town—information Edward should have inquired about but neglected.

Seeking to relieve the pressure, Elinor feigned a mission to fetch Marianne, allowing the others privacy. With remarkable fortitude, she lingered on the landing for several minutes before descending. Once separated, Edward's rapture inevitably faded until Marianne's entrance revitalized the room. Her joy was palpable and vocal. Meeting him with a grasping hand and an expressive voice, she cried, "Dear Edward! This is a moment of profound happiness! It would almost redeem everything!"

Edward reciprocated her kindness within the bounds of propriety, unable to divulge his deeper feelings before such witnesses. After a brief silence, Marianne gazed alternately at Edward and Elinor with tender regret that their shared delight was curtailed by Lucy's unwanted presence. Edward addressed Marianne's pallor, expressing concern for her health in London.

"Oh, do not dwell on me!" she replied with spirited intensity, though tears filled her eyes. "Do not consider *my* health. Elinor is well; that suffices for us both."

This remark offered no comfort to Edward or Elinor, nor did it win Lucy's goodwill, whose expression remained unbenign. "Do you enjoy London?" Edward asked, attempting to steer the conversation.

"Not at all. I anticipated pleasure, yet found none. Your presence, Edward, is the sole consolation it offers. Thank Heaven, you remain exactly as you always were!" She paused, letting the sentiment hang in the air. "I believe, Elinor," she added, "we must entrust Edward with our care upon returning to Barton. Within a fortnight, I presume, we depart. I trust he will not be reluctant to assume this responsibility."

Edward murmured indistinctly, unaware even of his own words. Marianne, interpreting his agitation as confirmation of her own suspicions, swiftly changed the subject. "We spent such a tedious day in Harley Street yesterday! Dull, wretchedly dull! Yet, I have much to discuss with you regarding this matter, which cannot await this moment." With admirable discretion, she deferred her critique of their mutual relatives' increasing disagreeableness and her specific disgust with his mother until they were alone.

"But why were you absent, Edward? Why did you not come?"

"I was engaged elsewhere."

"Engaged! Yet, what significance does an engagement hold when such friends are to be reunited?"

"Perhaps, Miss Marianne," Lucy interjected eagerly, seizing an opportunity to retaliate, "you believe young men disregard engagements if they lack the inclination to honor them, regardless of magnitude."

Elinor bristled with anger, but Marianne remained impervious to the barb. Calmly, she countered, "Not so. Conscience, I assure you, was the sole impediment keeping Edward from Harley Street. Indeed, I posit he possesses the most delicate conscience imaginable—the most scrupulous observer of every obligation, however minor, even when it conflicts with his interests or pleasure. He fears inflicting pain, shatters expectations, and is utterly incapable of selfishness. Edward, this is truth, and I state it plainly. What! Are you never to hear yourself praised? Then you must cease being my friend; for those who accept my love and esteem must submit to my open commendation."

The nature of her praise, in this context, proved particularly ill-suited to the sensibilities of two-thirds of the audience. It was so unexhilarating for Edward that he rose promptly to depart.

"Leaving so soon!" Marianne protested. "My dear Edward, this must not happen." Drawing him aside, she whispered her conviction that Lucy could not remain long. Even this encouragement failed; Edward insisted on leaving. Lucy, who would have prolonged the visit indefinitely, eventually departed.
