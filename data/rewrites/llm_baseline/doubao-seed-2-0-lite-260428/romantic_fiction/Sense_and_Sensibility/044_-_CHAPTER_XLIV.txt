Elinor flinched back so hard her breath caught, horror coiling tight in her chest the second her eyes landed on him. Her first instinct was to flee the room entirely, her fingers already curled around the doorknob to twist it open, before he lunged forward, his tone sharper with urgency than entreaty.
“Miss Dashwood—half an hour. Ten minutes. I beg you, stay.”
“No, sir,” she replied, steady as a rock. “I will not. Your business cannot be with me. The servants must have forgotten to tell you Mr. Palmer is not at home.”
“Had they told me Mr. Palmer and every last one of his relations was at the devil, it would not have turned me from this door,” he burst out, voice raw with vehemence. “My business is with you. Only you.”
“With me?” Elinor stared, utterly bewildered. “Well then—speak quickly, if you can. And do not be so violent.”
“Sit down, and I will be both.”
She hesitated, uncertain of what to do. The sharp fear pricked her that Colonel Brandon might walk in and find her shut alone with Willoughby, but she had long promised herself she would hear whatever explanation he might one day offer Marianne, and curiosity warred with her sense of honor to hold her still. After a breath’s pause, she reasoned the faster she complied, the faster this strange, tense encounter would end, and she walked silently to the table and sat. He dropped into the chair opposite, and for thirty long seconds, neither of them spoke a word.
“Please be quick, sir,” Elinor said, impatience fringing her tone. “I have no time to spare.”
He sat hunched, lost in deep meditation, and seemed not to hear her at all.
“Your sister,” he said, abruptly, a moment later. “The servants told me she’s out of danger. God be praised. But is it true? Is it really true?”
Elinor could not bring herself to speak, and he repeated the question, more desperate than before.
“For God’s sake, tell me—is she out of danger, or is she not?”
“We hope she is.”
He shot to his feet and paced the length of the room, boots thudding on the floorboards.
“Had I known that half an hour ago—but since I am here,” he said, forcing bright, hollow cheer as he dropped back into his chair, “what does it matter? Just once, Miss Dashwood—and it will surely be the last time—let us speak honestly. I am in no mood for games. Tell me,” he added, a hot flush creeping up his cheeks, “do you think I am more of a knave, or a fool?”
Elinor stared at him, more stunned than ever. She began to suspect he was drunk—there was no other way to explain this unannounced, uninvited visit, this wild, unhinged demeanor. She stood at once.
“Mr. Willoughby, I advise you to return to Combe at once. I have no leisure to stay with you any longer. Whatever business you have with me will be far better recollected and explained tomorrow, when you are of sound mind.”
“I understand you,” he replied, with a quiet, knowing smile, his voice perfectly even. “Yes, everyone would assume I am very drunk. A pint of porter with my cold beef at Marlborough was enough to throw me off my wits, according to that logic.”
“At Marlborough?” Elinor cried, more lost than ever trying to parse his purpose.
“Yes. I left London at eight this morning, and the only ten minutes I spent outside my chaise the whole day was to stop for that very nuncheon.”
His calm bearing, the sharp, clear intelligence in his eyes, convinced Elinor that whatever unpardonable folly had driven him all the way to Cleveland, it was not intoxication. She paused to collect her thoughts, then spoke.
“Mr. Willoughby, you must know—certainly I do—that after all that has passed between us, you forcing yourself into my presence like this demands an extraordinary excuse. What do you mean by it?”
“I mean,” he said, fierce and sincere, “if I can, to make you hate me just one degree less than you do right now. I mean to offer you some kind of explanation, some kind of apology for the past. I want to lay my whole heart bare to you, prove that while I have always been a blockhead, I have not always been a deliberate, unthinking rascal—so that Marianne can forgive me, properly, on fair terms, not out of soft pity alone.”
“Is that truly why you came?”
“On my soul, it is,” he said, and the warmth in his tone brought back every trace of the charming, lighthearted Willoughby they had all loved in Devonshire, and against her better judgment, Elinor believed he was sincere.
“If that is all, you may rest easy already. Marianne does forgive you. She forgave you long ago.”
“Has she?” he exclaimed, that same hungry, eager tone in his voice. “Then she forgave me far too soon, before she knew the full extent of my cowardice. But she will forgive me again, for the right reasons. Will you listen to me now?”
Elinor nodded, assenting.
“I do not know,” he said, after a long pause where Elinor waited and he turned his thoughts over one by one, “what story you have heard of my behavior toward your sister, what cruel, selfish motive you have ascribed to me. You may not think better of me when I am done, but it is worth trying. I will tell you everything. When I first grew intimate with your family in Devonshire, I had no hidden agenda, no grand plan—only to pass my time there more pleasantly than I ever had before. Your sister’s lovely face, her wild, passionate, generous manners—they could not help but please me. And from almost the first day, she cared for me so openly, so unreservedly that it staggers me now to think I was ever so vain and shallow as to take that devotion for granted. At first, I will own, her affection only fed my ego. I did not care for her happiness, only my own amusement. I gave in to every selfish impulse I had always been too indulgent of, and set out to win her regard without ever meaning to return it.”
Elinor’s eyes flashed hot, angry contempt, and she cut him off. “This is hardly worth telling, Mr. Willoughby, neither for you to speak nor for me to listen. A beginning as base as that can lead to nothing but pain. Do not make me suffer through any more of this.”
“I insist you hear every last bit of it,” he pressed on. “My fortune was never large. I have always lived beyond my means, run in circles of men far richer than I. Every year since I came of age, I sank deeper into debt. I had been counting on my elderly cousin Mrs. Smith to leave me her fortune to set me free—but that was never guaranteed, could have been years away. I had long planned to marry a wealthy heiress to fix my circumstances, to drag myself out of debt. I never should have allowed myself to grow attached to your sister. And with a meanness, a selfishness, a cruelty that no scowl of yours, no look of contempt, can ever condemn too harshly, I toyed with her heart, never meaning to do right by her. But I will say this for myself: even in that ugly, vain, selfish state, I did not understand how much harm I was doing, because I did not know what it meant to truly love. Have I ever known it? God, I wish I never had. If I truly loved her, could I have traded my feelings for vanity, for greed? Worse—could I have sacrificed hers? But I did it. I ran from a relative poverty that her love would have stripped of all its terror, just to chase affluence that ended up robbing me of every single thing that could have made my life worth living.”
“Then you do believe, at one point, that you were truly attached to her?” Elinor said, her softening a little in spite of herself.
“Resist a spirit like hers, a tenderness so unreserved? Is there a man alive who could have done it? I fell in love with her, slow and without noticing. The happiest hours of my entire life were the ones I spent with her, when I knew my intentions were honourable, my feelings blameless. Even then, though, when I was fully resolved to ask her to marry me, I cowardly put it off day after day, too ashamed to admit my tangled, messy finances to the woman I loved. I will not argue, will not wait for you to tell me how absurd, how much worse than absurd, it was to hesitate to promise her my hand when my whole heart was already hers. I was a cunning fool, careful to wait for a chance to make myself despicable and miserable for the rest of my life. At last I found my resolution, and planned to ask her to marry me the second I got her alone. But in those very few hours before I could speak to her in private, disaster struck. Mrs. Smith somehow found out about the affair I had with the young orphan girl Eliza, the connection I had carelessly destroyed. I imagine some distant relative who wanted to turn me out of her favour told her. You must have heard the whole story already from someone.”
“I have,” Elinor said, flushing hot, her heart hardening against his remorse all over again. “I have heard every last detail of that dreadful business. I confess I cannot fathom how you could ever explain away any part of your guilt there.”
“Remember who told you that story,” Willoughby said, sharply. “Could it possibly have been impartial? I do not deny for a second that her situation, her vulnerability, deserved all my respect. I will not try to justify myself—but I cannot let you think I have nothing to say for myself. She was wronged by me, yes—but that does not make her perfect, and I was reckless, but that does not make her a saint. Her passions were wild, her judgement weak—but I will not hide behind that. Her love for me deserved far better than I gave her, and I still hate myself for how I threw it away. But I hurt far more than her. I hurt someone whose love for me was just as fierce, whose mind—oh, Elinor, how infinitely brighter, how infinitely kinder than hers.”
“Your indifference to that poor unfortunate girl—cruel as it is to drag this subject up—does not excuse the way you abandoned her to ruin. No fault of hers justifies the deliberate, wanton cruelty you showed her. You must have known that while you were enjoying yourself in Devonshire, chasing Marianne, living every day bright and happy, she was starving, left with nothing, no means of support.”
“On my soul, I did not know!” he burst out, fiery with frustration. “I forgot to give her my address, and I thought she would have the common sense to find me!”
“Very well, sir. What did Mrs. Smith say when she confronted you?”
“She laid every last accusation at my feet, and I could not deny a single part of it. She was a woman of strict morals, rigid, sheltered from all the world’s vices, and every part of my conduct disgusted her. She had already been displeased with how little time I spent doting on her, and she cut me off completely. She offered me one way to save myself: marry Eliza, and she would forgive everything. That I could not do. I was dismissed from her favour, her house, all her fortune, for good. The night before I was supposed to leave Devonshire, I sat up all night deliberating what to do. I loved Marianne, I knew she loved me more than anything in the world—but it was not enough to beat back my terror of poverty, my foolish, ingrained belief that wealth was the only thing that mattered. I knew if I asked Miss Grey, the rich heiress, to marry me, she would say yes, and I convinced myself that was the only sensible choice left. But I had one terrible scene left to play out before I left Devonshire: I was supposed to dine with you all that day. I debated for hours whether I should write an apology, or go see Marianne one last time. I knew seeing her would break me, that I might not have the strength to keep my resolve. But I overestimated my own cowardice, as it turned out. I went. I saw her. I saw her heart break, and I left her broken, knowing I would never see her again.”
“Why did you call at all, Mr. Willoughby?” Elinor said, reproach in her tone. “A note would have sufficed. There was no need to torment her in person.”
“I did it for my own stupid pride. I could not bear to leave the country and have you all, or our neighbours, suspect the real reason Mrs. Smith had cast me off. I resolved to stop by the cottage on my way to Honiton, so no one would guess the truth. But seeing your Marianne, when I walked in the door—you were all out, I do not know where—finding her alone, bright and happy, still fully trusting me, the same girl I had left the night before certain I would ask her to marry me? I almost could not keep the lie straight. She was so grieved, so disappointed, so upset that I had to leave Devonshire so suddenly, and she still trusted me completely. I will never forget that look on her face for as long as I live. God, I was such a hard-hearted rascal.”
They sat silent for a long, heavy moment. Elinor spoke first.
“Did you tell her you would come back soon?”
“I do not even remember what I said to her,” he replied, impatient, miserable. “I surely promised her far more than I had any right to, far more than I could ever deliver. Then your mother walked in, kind, warm, full of confidence in me, and it tortured me worse than anything. Thank God it did. I look back on that pain I felt then as a victory now, because it is the only proof that I was not a complete, unfeeling monster. I left. I left everyone I loved, and drove to London to be with people I did not even like. The ride was endless, no one to talk to, nothing but my own terrible thoughts to keep me company. What a blessed, miserable journey that was.”
He paused.
“Well sir,” Elinor said, though she pitied him, she was growing eager for him to leave. “Is that all?”
“All? No. Have you forgotten what happened in London? That infamous, cruel letter I sent her? Did she show you?”
“Yes. I read every note that passed between the two of you.”
“When I got the first of her letters, every line felt like a dagger to my heart. To know she was in town, still caring for me, still waiting for me? It hit me like a thunderbolt. She would have laughed at those silly metaphors, your Marianne, and told me exactly how dramatic and foolish I was being. Her taste, her opinions— I know them better than my own, and they are far dearer to me than anything else.”
Elinor’s heart, softening again, told her she had to speak, to pull him back from this dangerous reminiscing. “That is not right, Mr. Willoughby. You are a married man now. Only tell me what you feel you must, for the sake of the truth.”
“Her note told me she still loved me, that after weeks apart, her faith in me was still strong. It woke up all my old remorse. For a while, London, parties, dissipation, had quieted that guilt. I had started to think of our old love as nothing more than a silly trifle, telling myself I would be glad to hear she was happily married to someone else, to silence every last reproach. But that note made me face the truth: she was the dearest thing in the world to me, and I was treating her like garbage. But I was already engaged to Miss Grey. There was no turning back. The only thing I could do was avoid you both. I never wrote back to Marianne, hoping you would all forget about me. I even avoided calling on Mrs. Jennings, until I decided it would look less suspicious to pretend to be nothing more than a casual old acquaintance. I snuck over one morning when I saw you all leave the house, and left my card.”
“You used to watch us leave the house?”
“Even so. You would be shocked to know how many times I saw you on the streets, how many times I ducked into a random shop to avoid crossing paths with your carriage. I lived in Bond Street, and almost every day I caught a glimpse of one of you two. I avoided the Middletons as much as I could, everyone who might be a mutual acquaintance. But I ran straight into Sir John Middleton the very first day he arrived in town, the day after I left my card at Mrs. Jennings’s. He invited me to a dance that night, and only told me as an afterthought that you and Marianne would be there. I would never have gone if he had said it first. The next morning, Marianne sent me another note—open, loving, artless, the kind of note that made my own conduct look more hateful than ever. I could not answer it. I tried, but I could not write a single line. And then we met at that party, didn’t we? What a fool I made of myself. Marianne, more beautiful than an angel, calling my name in that soft, wounded tone, holding out her hand to me, her eyes full of that gentle solicitude, asking me for an explanation. And my wife, beside me, jealous as the devil, glowering at me the whole time. I ran away as soon as I could. But not before I saw Marianne’s face, white as death. That was the last time I saw her. That is the last memory I had of her, all the way here today, when I thought she might die.”
Another long, quiet pause. Willoughby roused himself first.
“Well. I will go now. Your sister really is better? Out of danger?”
“We are assured she is.”
“Your poor mother—doting on Marianne the whole time.”
