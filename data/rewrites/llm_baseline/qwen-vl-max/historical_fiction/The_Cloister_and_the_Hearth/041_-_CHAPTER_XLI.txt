Denys, placed at the center of his captors to prevent any desperate attempt at escape, was carried away in a storm of grief and guilt. For his sake, Gerard had abandoned the safer German route to Rome—only to be left alone, adrift in the heart of Burgundy. That thought consumed Denys most fiercely, driving him now to curse the heavens, now into a brooding silence so dark and threatening that even his guards deemed it wise to disarm him.

They intercepted their leader just beyond the town’s gates, and the entire party halted at the inn called “The Tête d’Or.” The young landlady, though occupied with the count and more deeply preoccupied with her own son, noticed Denys and asked, with some concern, what had become of his companion.

With a sudden outburst of anguish, Denys told her everything—how Gerard had been left behind, how he feared the young man would wander aimlessly, helpless and alone. “Now that he is parted from me,” he pleaded, “he may listen to my advice. Poor wretch—he cannot bear solitude.”

She tossed her head sharply. “I think I’ve already been too kind,” she said, her cheeks flushing. “You will not help?”

“No,” he snapped.

“Then,” he thundered, unleashing a torrent of curses upon her.

She turned away, tears welling in her eyes. “I’m not used to being spoken to like this,” she muttered, retreating to give orders to her servant: saddle two mules.

Denys rode northward with his escort, silent and bowed beneath the weight of sorrow, unaware that the very woman who had scorned him was now dressing herself for horseback in less than forty minutes—eager, decisive, and spurred by a purpose of her own. She set off with her servant, riding swiftly in the opposite direction.

That evening, after a long march, the bastard and his men arrived at “The White Hart.” Their entrance stirred great commotion, and it took time before Manon recognized her old acquaintance among the crowd. When she did, she showed no sign—only a faint blush, nothing more. She did not claim the bond they once shared. Already, the proud woman was beginning to look down on the past, ashamed of the low steps by which she had risen.

Denys saw her but could not smile. The inn brought back too many memories—especially of Gerard.

As night fell, the wind shifted. Manon stepped into the room and beckoned to him with a finger. He rose reluctantly, followed by his guards.

“Nay,” she whispered, “I must speak to you privately.”

She drew him aside to a corner, then lowered her voice. “Would you do me a kindness?”

He answered loudly, “No. I am not in the mood to grant favors to man or woman. If I ever do a kindness, it shall be to a dog—and not even that, if I can avoid it.”

“Oh, good archer,” she said humbly, “you once did me a kindness—you and your fair companion.”

“You did?” he replied. “Well then—for his sake—what is it?”

“I know my story,” she continued. “Once I was cast down. Now I am respected. But today, a woman mocked me—she spoke of him as if he still hung there. And so it will always be, while he remains visible. I wish him taken down. Alas!”

“With all my heart,” he said.

“And none dare I ask but you. Will you do it?”

“Not I—not even if I were free.”

At his firm refusal, Manon sighed deeply, clasping her hands together in despair.

But Denys reassured her. “There are men lower than I who would not hesitate to take such a task. It’s merely a matter of money. If you can find two angels, I’ll find two soldiers to do the work for ‘The White Hart.’”

It was not a pleasant suggestion. Yet, recalling that soldiers were transient creatures—drinking tonight, dead tomorrow—she murmured, “Send them to me. But please—tell them it’s for one who is my friend. Let them not think it’s for me. I should die of shame. Times have changed.”

Denys found two men eager to earn an angel each. He sent them out, then dismissed the matter with contempt, sinking back into thoughts of Gerard.

Manon and the soldiers soon reached an understanding. But what would they do with the body once it was removed?

“The river is near,” she whispered, “near the place.”

“Throw him in?” one soldier asked bluntly.

“No, no! Be not cruel! Could you not lay him gently… and with something heavy?”

She had clearly given it careful thought—unlike one who rarely formed ideas quickly.

Everything was settled except payment. The soldiers demanded advance gold. Manon hesitated. Did she doubt their word? Then let her come with them—or watch from afar.

“Me?” she gasped, horrified. “I would rather die than see it done.”

“But you wanted it done,” he reminded her.

“Aye,” she admitted, “for I am in sore need. Times are changed.”

She had already forgotten her earlier lesson to Denys.

An hour later, the unpleasant relic of a past life was gone—no longer a stain upon the respectable matron’s public standing, no longer casting bitter glances over the clover fields where white hinds grazed.

Manon made the landlord a decent husband and a loyal house-dog. She lived long enough to outlive all who knew her.

When the soldiers returned from carrying out their grim task with medieval simplicity—their hearts heavy—they found the market square packed with people. It had been empty just an hour before.

Conscience struck them. This was the consequence of meddling with the dead.

Yet one, bolder than the other, crept forward in the darkness, blending into a crowd. After a moment, he returned, speaking in tones of reproach that were hardly justified: “Ye born fool—it’s only a miracle.”
