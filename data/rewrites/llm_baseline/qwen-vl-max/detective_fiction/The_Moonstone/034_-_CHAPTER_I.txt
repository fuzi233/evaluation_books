My dear friend, Miss Clack, having laid down her pen, it falls to me now—by right and necessity—to take up the narrative where she left off.

There are two compelling reasons why I should do so. First, I am uniquely positioned to shed light on matters that have thus far remained shrouded in mystery. Miss Verinder’s decision to break her engagement was not made lightly—it was rooted in a truth I uncovered. And Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite’s sudden withdrawal from the match? That too had its cause, which I discovered through careful inquiry. I know what drove each of them, and why.

Second, I myself became directly involved in the unfolding drama surrounding the Indian Diamond—the Moonstone. At my own office, I received a visit from an enigmatic Oriental man of refined bearing, whom I am certain was the leader of the three Indians who came to claim the gem. Shortly after, I met the renowned traveler Mr. Murthwaite, and our conversation about the stone proved crucial in understanding what followed. These events give me both the right and the duty to speak here.

Let us begin with the story of the broken engagement—a tale that predates all others in this chain of events, and must therefore come first.

To trace the threads back to their origin, I must start at the bedside of my late client and dear friend, Sir John Verinder.

Sir John was not without his human frailties—though they were, for the most part, harmless ones. Among them was a stubborn reluctance, as long as he enjoyed good health, to face the responsibility of making a will. Lady Verinder, ever dutiful, urged him to act; I did the same. He acknowledged the justice of our arguments—but took no action, until illness struck him down and brought him close to death. Then, at last, I was summoned to his side to receive his final instructions.

He was dozing when I entered. He stirred at my arrival.

“How do you do, Mr. Bruff?” he said. “I won’t be long about this. Then I’ll go back to sleep.”

I prepared pens, ink, and paper. He watched with quiet interest.

“Are you ready?” he asked.

I bowed and dipped my pen, waiting.

“I leave everything to my wife,” he declared. “That’s all.” He turned over and settled into rest once more.

I had to rouse him again.

“Do you mean,” I asked carefully, “that you leave all your property—land, money, every asset—entirely to Lady Verinder?”

“Yes,” he replied. “Only I say it shorter. Why can’t you? Everything to my wife. That’s my Will.”

His estate was entirely his own to dispose of—divided between land and wealth. In most cases, I would have advised reconsideration. But I knew Lady Verinder well: not only was she worthy of her husband’s complete trust—every good wife deserves such faith—but she was also capable of managing a great fortune wisely. Few women, in my experience, possess that rare combination of virtue and competence.

Within ten minutes, the will was drafted, signed, and sealed. Sir John returned to his nap, content.

Lady Verinder lived up to every expectation. Soon after her husband’s death, she called me to her side to make her own will. Her judgment was sound, her intentions clear. I needed only to shape her wishes into legal form. Before two weeks had passed, her daughter’s future was secured—not just financially, but with dignity and care.

The will was placed in my fireproof safe, untouched for years. It wasn’t until the summer of 1848 that I looked at it again—under circumstances that filled me with sorrow.

At that time, the doctors gave Lady Verinder a fatal diagnosis. She told me first, and with great composure, that she wished to review her will. The provisions for Rachel were unchangeable—wise and generous. But some minor bequests had shifted in her mind over time. We added several codicils, then drew up a second will to replace the original. My aim was clarity: the old document had become cluttered with repetitions, and I found it professionally distressing.

This new will was witnessed by Miss Clack—whose account of the event I’ve already read. As for Rachel Verinder’s inheritance, it mirrored the first will exactly. Only the guardianship clause and related provisions were altered, based on my advice. Upon Lady Verinder’s death, the will was handed to my proctor for probate.

About three weeks later—I recall it vividly—I received my first hint that something unusual was stirring beneath the surface.

I dropped by my friend the proctor’s office, and noticed at once that he greeted me with unusual intensity.

“I have news for you,” he said. “Guess what I heard at Doctors’ Commons this morning? Lady Verinder’s will has already been requested—and examined!”

It was astonishing. There was nothing in the will to contest, and no one I could think of who stood to gain from inspecting it. (For the benefit of those unfamiliar, let me explain: under English law, any person may examine a will at Doctors’ Commons for a shilling fee.)

“Who asked for it?” I demanded.

“The clerk told me,” he replied. “Mr. Smalley, of Skipp and Smalley. The will hadn’t yet been copied into the official registers, so they had no choice but to show him the original. He studied it closely and made notes. Any idea why?”

I shook my head. “I’ll find out before the day is done.” And I returned immediately to my office.

Had this been any other firm, I might have struggled to learn the truth. But I had leverage. My common-law clerk—a highly competent man—was the brother of Mr. Smalley. For years, Skipp and Smalley had benefited from cases I declined, referred to them instead. My patronage mattered to them.

As soon as I arrived, I spoke to my clerk and sent him to his brother with a message: “Mr. Bruff’s compliments. He would be grateful to know why Messrs. Skipp and Smalley felt it necessary to examine Lady Verinder’s will.”

That message brought Mr. Smalley himself to my office, accompanied by his brother. He admitted he had acted on behalf of a client—but refused to name him, citing professional confidentiality.

We debated the matter sharply. He was right, of course. I was wrong. I was angry, suspicious, and unwilling to accept limits. I insisted on knowing more. Worse still, I refused to treat any information as privileged. I claimed full freedom to use it as I saw fit. Even worse: I used my influence unfairly. “Choose, sir,” I said to Mr. Smalley, “between losing your client’s business—or mine.”

It was tyranny. I admit it. But like all tyrants, I succeeded. Without hesitation, Mr. Smalley chose.

He smiled wearily and gave the name:

Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite.

That was enough. I needed no more.

Now, to ensure the reader stands on equal footing with me in knowledge—particularly concerning Lady Verinder’s will—I must lay bare its terms.

Rachel Verinder inherited nothing outright. She held only a life interest in the estate. Her mother’s wisdom and my experience had combined to protect her from future entanglements with men driven by greed. Neither she nor her husband could draw upon the property—neither land nor funds. They would have the houses in London and Yorkshire, and a generous income. That was all.

When I reflected on this revelation, I was torn between outrage and uncertainty.

Less than a week earlier, I had learned—shocked and distressed—of Miss Verinder’s engagement to Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite. I admired her deeply. The thought of her marrying him pained me. Now, here was proof that he had approached the marriage with selfish intent. And yet—what of it? You may ask. Such things happen daily.

True. But would you dismiss it so lightly if it concerned your own sister?

The question haunted me. Would Mr. Ablewhite still pursue the engagement now that his lawyer had unearthed the truth?

It depended on his financial state—something I didn’t know. If he were solvent, the marriage might still suit him for her income alone. But if he urgently needed a large sum by a fixed date, then Lady Verinder’s will offered him precisely what he sought—and saved Rachel from a dangerous alliance.

In the former case, silence might be justified. In the latter, silence would be complicity.

I resolved to act.

I visited the hotel in London where Mrs. Ablewhite and Miss Verinder were staying. They told me they were leaving for Brighton the next day—and that Mr. Godfrey could not join them due to unforeseen circumstances. I offered to take his place.

While I had hesitated before, seeing Rachel in person changed everything. I knew I must tell her the truth.

I found my chance the day after my arrival, walking beside her along the seafront.

“May I speak to you,” I asked, “about your engagement?”

She replied indifferently, “If you have nothing more interesting to talk about.”

“Will you forgive an old friend and servant of your family,” I said, “if I ask whether your heart is truly set on this marriage?”

“I am marrying in despair,” she answered, “in hopes of finding some stagnant happiness that might make life bearable.”

A powerful statement. One that hinted at deeper emotions—perhaps even a secret romance. But I pressed on.

“Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite must feel differently,” I said. “His heart must be set on this union.”

“He says so,” she replied. “And I suppose I should believe him. He wouldn’t marry me, after what I’ve confessed, unless he truly cared.”

Poor girl. She had never imagined a man might marry her for mere gain. The task before me suddenly seemed heavier than I’d expected.

“It sounds strange,” I continued, “to hear you speak of your future husband as if you’re uncertain of his sincerity. Do you doubt him?”

Her sharp intuition caught a shift in my tone. She stopped, pulled her arm from mine, and looked at me intently.

“Mr. Bruff,” she said, “you have something to tell me about Godfrey Ablewhite. Tell it.”

I knew her well enough to trust her word. I told her everything.

She slipped her arm back into mine and walked on in silence. I felt her grip tighten, saw her grow paler—but she said nothing. When I finished, she remained silent. Her head drooped slightly. She walked beside me, lost in thought, oblivious to the world.

I said nothing. Experience taught me that Rachel needed time. While others might rush to question or confide, she retreated inward—thinking, weighing, deciding alone.

This self-reliance is admirable in a man. In a woman, it risks alienating her from society. I suspect I judge her differently from most—because I understand her. And because I know her role in the disappearance of the Moonstone. Despite appearances, I believed she had done nothing dishonorable. She had always acted after deep reflection—never impulsively.

We walked nearly a mile before she stirred. Then, slowly, she looked up at me with a faint echo of her old smile—the most radiant expression I had ever seen.

“I owe much to your kindness,” she said. “And more than ever now. If anyone speaks ill of my marriage when you return to London, contradict them—at my request.”

“Have you decided to end the engagement?” I asked.

“Can you doubt it?” she replied proudly. “After what you’ve told me?”

“My dear Miss Rachel,” I said, “you are young. You may face greater difficulty than you imagine. Is there no one—no lady—you could consult?”

“No one,” she answered.

It pained me to hear it. She was so young, so alone—and yet carried herself with such quiet strength. I couldn’t help but offer counsel, despite my own unease. I had advised countless clients, faced complex dilemmas—but never before had I guided a young woman through breaking an engagement.

My suggestion: she should confront Mr. Ablewhite privately. She should tell him she knew he had married her for money. That the marriage was impossible. Then she should ask him whether it was wiser to preserve her silence—or force her to expose his motives publicly. If he denied it, she was to refer him to me.

She listened attentively, thanked me gracefully—but said it was impossible.

“May I ask,” I said, “why not?”

She paused—then countered with a question of her own.

“Suppose you were asked to judge Mr. Godfrey Ablewhite’s conduct?” she began.

“Yes?”

“What would you call it?”

“I would call it the behavior of a deceitful, base man.”

“Mr. Bruff!” she exclaimed. “I have believed in him. I have promised to marry him. How can I tell him he is vile? How can I accuse him of deception? How can I disgrace him after that? I have already degraded myself by imagining him as my husband. If I say what you suggest—I confess that degradation to his face. I cannot do it. After all we’ve shared, I cannot! The shame would mean nothing to him. But to me—it would be unbearable.”

Here was another facet of her character revealed: a fierce sensitivity to moral ugliness. It blinded her to self-interest, driving her into a position that might damage her reputation.

Until then, I had hesitated in my advice. But now I was certain: it was the best course. I pressed it again.

She only shook her head.

“He has been intimate enough to ask me to marry him. He stood high enough in my regard to earn my consent. I cannot look him in the eye and call him contemptible.”

“But,” I urged, “you cannot withdraw without giving a reason.”

“I shall say I’ve thought it over—and believe it best for both of us to part.”

“No more?”

“No more.”

“And what if he challenges you?”

“He may say what he pleases.”

I admired her courage and resolve. Yet I feared she was placing herself at risk. I begged her to consider public opinion. “You cannot defy society,” I said, “for the sake of private feeling.”

“I can,” she replied. “I already have.”

“What do you mean?”

“You’ve forgotten the Moonstone, Mr. Bruff. Did I not defy public opinion then—out of my own private reasons?”

Her words silenced me. I tried to piece together her actions during the theft—her mysterious behavior, her refusal to speak. I might have understood it once. Now, I could not.

One final plea. She remained unmoved.

When I left her that day, my mind was in turmoil. She was stubborn. She was wrong. She was admirable. She was heartbreaking.

I made her promise to write me as soon as she had news. I returned to London, uneasy.

That evening—before I could receive her letter—I was visited by Mr. Ablewhite the elder. He informed me that Godfrey had been dismissed—and had accepted it—that very day.

With what I already knew, the significance of those words was clear. He needed money—urgently. Rachel’s income would have helped him in many ways—but not this one. So he had withdrawn without resistance.

If you call this speculation, I ask: what other explanation fits a man abandoning a marriage that would have secured him wealth for life?

Any joy I might have felt at this turn was crushed by my meeting with the older Mr. Ablewhite.

He came to demand an explanation for Rachel’s conduct. I could give none. My inability to satisfy him, coupled with recent tensions with his son, made him lose control. His expression, his tone—they told me plainly: he would be merciless when he joined the ladies at Brighton.

And so, the stage was set.
