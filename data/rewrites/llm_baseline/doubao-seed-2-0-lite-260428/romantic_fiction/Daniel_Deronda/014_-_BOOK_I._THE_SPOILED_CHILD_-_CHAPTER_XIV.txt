I will not clothe myself in wreck—wear gems
Sawed from cramped finger-bones of women drowned;
Feel chilly vaporous hands of ireful ghosts
Clutching my necklace: trick my maiden breast
With orphans’ heritage. Let your dead love
Marry its dead.

The next morning, Gwendolen looked as lovely and brimming with young energy as a tall, newly unfurled white lily. All yesterday’s faint, fleeting self-doubt had melted away into the bright, sharp fizz of youth, no more lasting than a shiver skittering across the smooth surface of a full, sunlit stream. The roving archery match in Cardell Chase glowed before her as a perfect, sun-warmed prospect: she could already picture herself drifting among the gnarled beeches like a wood nymph, surrounded by admiring company, and the imagined scene lent a soft allure to Grandcourt’s slow, deliberate advances. He was no breathless, lyrical Daphnis fit for a wood nymph’s fancies—if anything, his quiet, unreadable languor made him far more interesting. That morning, she was sure he would work his way slowly toward a full declaration, and she would meet it halfway, just as she had resolved with her uncle the night before.

When she wandered down to the breakfast parlor, everyone else had already left the table save her mother, Mrs. Davilow. There was a small stack of letters waiting by her plate, one of which she read with a spreading, delighted smile before passing it across to her mother. Mrs. Davilow smiled back as she handed it over, her own mood lightened by her daughter’s uncharacteristic, unforced good cheer.
“You don’t feel inclined to run off a thousand miles away, then?” Mrs. Davilow teased.
“Not quite that far,” Gwendolen laughed.
“It was such a sad oversight not to write to the Langens sooner. Can’t you dash a note off now, before we leave for the Chase this morning?”
“It’s not nearly that pressing. Tomorrow will do. You saw they leave London today, so I’ll send it to Dover. They’ll be waiting there till Monday.”
“Shall I write it for you, dear, if it feels like a chore?”
Gwendolen paused, sipping her coffee, before answering a little brusquely, “Oh no, don’t trouble. I’ll write it tomorrow.” Then she softened, leaning over to press a playful, tender kiss to her mother’s cheek. “My dear, old, beautiful mamma.”
“Old, am I, child?”
“Don’t say that!” Gwendolen protested. “I only meant you’re my old, dearest darling. You’re barely twenty-five years older than I am. When you talk like that, all my whole future shrivels up right in front of my eyes.”
“Twenty-five years can hold so much happiness, Gwen,” Mrs. Davilow said gently.
“Then I mustn’t waste a single second of mine starting it,” Gwendolen said, giddy. “The sooner I have my palaces and my own coaches, the better.”
“And a good husband who adores you, my sweet girl,” Mrs. Davilow added, encouraging. Gwendolen only pursed her lips in a saucy pout and said nothing.

The only small dampener on her excitement was that the rector, her uncle, had been detained by magistrate’s business and would likely not make it to Cardell Chase at all that day. She cared little that Mrs. Gascoigne and Anna refused to go without him, but her uncle’s steady, reasonable presence would have made the decision she was on the cusp of feel solid, inevitable. Now that she was so close to saying yes to Grandcourt, that promise of a full, unhoped-for life was beginning to feel too sharp, too defined, a cage she had not quite noticed she was stepping into. It is the way of the world: once we begin to crave great things for ourselves, every blessing we grasp soon feels like a new limit, a new exclusion. Still, she clung to the reassuring thought that marriage to Grandcourt would be the very gate to a wider, brighter freedom.

The party was meeting at Green Arbor, a sun-dappled grass hollow walled in by hanging woods that made a soft, sheltered amphitheater. A carriage packed with servants and picnic provisions had been sent ahead to lay out the feast, and the Chase warden had promised to guide the roving archers to keep them within pre-marked bounds strung between the iconic Double Oak, the Whispering Stones, and the High Cross. The plan was to take a short, casual stroll before luncheon, saving the full roving archery adventure for the golden, mellow light of late afternoon. The group gathered quickly, no one left lingering bored waiting, and soon pairs and small groups were drifting through the shifting dappled light, under the close, heavy boughs of beeches and the more spaced, gnarled oaks that dotted the Chase: a scene so picturesque any painter would have lingered to soak it in. This wandering archery was far lovelier than the usual stationary target shoots, but success with moving, random marks was far less predictable than practice at a fixed target, so hits were scattered unevenly across the amateur archers. Between that, and her quiet preoccupation with Grandcourt that made it hard to focus, Gwendolen did not distinguish herself much that first hour—save for the lively, unruffled grace with which she laughed off each miss. She had dressed again in her signature white and green, the same outfit she’d worn the day of the first archery meet where she had first met Grandcourt. He stayed close by her side all morning, yet to an outside observer it would have been almost impossible to tell their dynamic had shifted since that first introduction. Still, every other guest had already decided they were either secretly engaged already, or on the very brink of it—and Gwendolen believed it too.

As the groups drifted back toward Green Arbor, chattering without bothering to nock arrows, the words were finally spoken that felt like the first step toward that long-awaited proposal. “Do you have any idea how long it’s been since I first saw you in this dress?” Grandcourt asked.
“The archery meet was the 25th, and today’s the 13th,” Gwendolen laughed. “I’m terrible at sums, but I’ll hazard it’s nearly three weeks.”
He paused for a beat. “That’s a terrible loss of time.”
“Loss because knowing me has inconvenienced you?” Gwendolen teased. “Don’t be uncomplimentary, I won’t stand for it.”
Another long, quiet pause. “It’s the size of the gain that makes the lost time feel so heavy.”
Gwendolen fell silent then, thinking to herself, He is far more clever than most men. He never says a stupid, clumsy thing. Her unusual stillness read to Grandcourt as the softest, most promising encouragement he could have hoped for, and he leaned a little closer. “The joy of knowing you makes every minute I spend stuck in uncertainty feel wasted. Do you like uncertainty, Miss Harleth?”
“I think I do, actually,” Gwendolen said, beaming at him with a playful, teasing glint in her eye. “There’s far more magic to it than certainty.”
Grandcourt held her laughing gaze with a slow, steady, unblinking look, as if he could see straight through all her pretty performances to the girl beneath. “Do you mean more torment for me?”
Something about that quiet look shook Gwendolen out of all her usual poise, and she blushed hot, darting her eyes away. “No. That would make me very sorry.”
He would have pressed on, then, to make his full declaration—her soft, flustered answer had made her acceptance all but sure—when he realized they were in full view of the rest of the party, halfway down the steep slope leading into Green Arbor, an ill-chosen spot where the ground fell away sharp and slippery. He offered her his hand to steady her, and she took it, the two of them descending in full, obvious silence, watched by everyone already gathered below—including Mrs. Arrowpoint, who stood beside Mrs. Davilow. Mrs. Arrowpoint had already decided Grandcourt was far too languid, far too unremarkable a man for her own daughter Catherine, who had turned down no less a suitor than Lord Slogan; she watched him now with completely dispassionate eyes.
“Mr. Grandcourt is not half the man his uncle, Sir Hugo Mallinger, is,” she said, rather too loudly. “For all he’s decades younger, I wouldn’t be at all surprised if Sir Hugo outlives him. It’s never wise to count on inheritances.”
“Indeed it is not,” Mrs. Davilow agreed serenely, so pleased with the turn things were taking that her usual quiet melancholy had completely vanished.

There is no need to dwell on the spread of cold meats and champagne laid out in that green forest clearing, or the tales of the vast rolling forest stretching beyond the hollow. The world outside their small circle barely registered to the party that afternoon, all their attention wrapped up in this tiny, glittering social drama as small and self-contained as a puppet show. The food and wine were of the finest quality, the laughter and banter the easy, unforced kind only high society can produce, where no one overplays their privilege, and all the advantages of the world are taken with that casual, well-bred nonchalance that comes of having never wanted for anything. Some of the gentlemen drifted off to smoke cigars, leaving a pleasant lull before the party was due to set off again at four o’clock. Oddly enough, Grandcourt was among them—but not Mr. Lush, who was behaving with uncharacteristic, over-eager helpfulness all afternoon, ordering every detail of the picnic for the guests, fussing over every small need: a presence that made Gwendolen’s skin crawl, though he carefully kept his distance, never daring to look at her directly. When the party began to gather their things to leave, all the archers’ bows had been left in the care of Lord Brackenshaw’s valet, and Lush had offered to fetch them all from the carriage for the ladies. Gwendolen, fearing he might be the one to hand her her own bow, hurried to get it herself. The valet saw her coming, met her halfway with the bow in hand—and slipped a sealed letter into her other palm. She did not question him, saw at a glance the soft, delicate, old-fashioned feminine handwriting on the envelope, and stepped away from the carriage holding her bow, just as Lush rounded the corner to collect the rest of the weapons. To avoid him, she turned her back to the carriages and stepped behind a tree to tear open the note. It read:
> If Miss Harleth is in doubt whether she should accept Mr. Grandcourt, let her break from her party after they have passed the Whispering Stones and return to that spot. She will then hear something to decide her; but she can only hear it by keeping this letter a strict secret from every one. If she does not act according to this letter, she will repent, as the woman who writes it has repented. The secrecy Miss Harleth will feel herself bound in honor to guard.

Gwendolen felt a sharp jolt of shock run through her—but her first, strange thought was It has come just in time. Youth made her leap immediately to the idea of a long-held, important revelation, no flicker of suspicion that the note might be a trick. She resolved instantly that she would slip away unseen to the Whispering Stones, tucked the note deep in her pocket, and turned back to the party with a strange, bracing thrill of having a secret, that quiet sense of hidden purpose that always made her feel sharper, more in control of herself.

Everyone was surprised when it was time to leave and Grandcourt was nowhere to be found, the other smokers already back and ready to go. “We’ll run into him soon enough,” Lord Brackenshaw laughed. “He can’t have gone far.” No one bothered to wait for him: the group joked that he was so lost in daydreams about Gwendolen he had forgotten they were supposed to meet, and the good-natured earl even tossed Gwendolen a playful little quip to that effect, which she accepted with quiet, appropriate demureness. But her own thought was darker: Is he running away from this decision, too? It was not a pleasant thought—but it was near the truth. Grandcourt, wrapped up in a lazy, hazy fit of stubborn numbness, had lit a second large cigar, lingering well away from the group for no reason he could even name, simply because the idea of reaching a definite end made him want to drag his feet, to defy everyone’s expectations just for the petty thrill of exerting his own will. He would have sent any mortal fool who dared interrupt him straight to the devil, and no one dared interrupt him that afternoon.

The rovers set off without him, their spirits unflagging, leaving behind only the less energetic ladies, including Mrs. Davilow, who preferred a quiet, unhurried stroll without the pressure to keep pace. The afternoon light softened and deepened, and the archery grew more spirited as the party moved through changing groves and sunlit glades, every shadow longer and lovelier than the last. They joked that they were living out their own improvised version of *As You Like It*, and when someone complimented Gwendolen that she was the perfect Rosalind for their little forest play, she leaned into the part, glowing brighter than ever. It was no hard work: the thrill of adventure thrumming in her veins, the quiet planning to slip away unseen, made her more radiant than usual.

It was nearly an hour of wandering before they reached the Whispering Stones: two tall, conical gray boulders leaning toward each other like two giant, cloaked figures. The group paused to laugh that they would make perfect ghosts on a starlit night, then moved on, unaware that Gwendolen had lingered a step behind. The stones sat right beside a grove of tall beeches full of perfect archery marks.
“About how far are we from Green Arbor, warden?” Gwendolen asked, falling into step beside him.
“Only half a mile if you take that avenue over there, but we’re going the long, scenic way ‘round, two miles out past the High Cross.”
She drifted back to the rest of the group, and just as she had hoped, Mr. Lush hurried the whole party forward in a rush, and Gwendolen slipped unnoticed away from the line, turning back toward the Whispering Stones. She did not run, but it felt like she flew over the grass, counting the seconds until she rounded the right-hand boulder—where a pair of large, dark, unflinching eyes met hers, only a foot away. Even prepared, Gwendolen flinched back, taking in the full figure of the woman in front of her: unmistakably a lady, one who must have been breathtakingly beautiful in her youth. A few yards behind her, two small children sat playing in the grass.
“Miss Harleth?” the woman said.
“Yes.” Gwendolen could do nothing but stare in wonder.
“Have you accepted Mr. Grandcourt?”
“No.”
“I promised to tell you something. And you must promise me to keep my secret. No matter what you choose to do next, you will not tell Mr. Grandcourt, or any other living soul, that you have seen me today?”
“I promise.”
“My name is Lydia Glasher. Mr. Grandcourt ought never to marry anyone but me. Nine years ago, I left my husband and my child for him. Those two little ones behind me are his, and we have two older girls besides. My husband is dead now. Mr. Grandcourt promised he would marry me, that he would make that little boy my son his heir.” She nodded toward the boy as she spoke, and Gwendolen’s eyes followed hers. The small, handsome little fellow was puffing out his cheeks trying to make a tiny trumpet make a sound, his hat hanging off the back of his head by a string, his soft brown curls catching the last of the sun like gold. He looked like a cherub.
The two women’s eyes met again, and Gwendolen spoke, her voice sharp and proud even as she began to shiver, her lips going pale. “I will not interfere with your wishes.”
“You are very beautiful, Miss Harleth,” Lydia said, her tone sharp with old, bitter grief, no hint of histrionics in her steady voice. “But when he first knew me, I was just as young. Since then, my life has been broken, embittered, torn to pieces. It is not fair that he should get to be happy, while I am miserable, and my own son is hidden away and replaced by a new wife’s children.”
Gwendolen stared at her as she spoke, a cold, crawling terror seeping into her bones, as if a ghastly dream figure had stepped out of the dark and said, I am what a woman’s life becomes when a man breaks it.
“Have you anything more to say to me?” Gwendolen asked, her voice low but still cold, still proud. The sharp, sudden revulsion churning in her chest did not soften her, it made every person around her feel hateful, untrustworthy.
“Nothing. You know everything I needed you to know. You can look me up if you like, my husband was Colonel Glasher.”
“Then I will take my leave,” Gwendolen said, giving a stiff, ceremonious little bow, which Lydia returned with equal quiet grace.

Within minutes, Gwendolen was back in the beech grove, but the rest of her party had vanished entirely, and no one had noticed she was gone enough to send anyone looking. She hurried down the short avenue the warden had mentioned, and was back at Green Arbor in no time, walking fast as if she could outrun the whirling, screaming thoughts in her head that threatened to break her calm. She already knew exactly what she was going to do.
Mrs. Davilow was shocked to see her return alone, a quiet anxiety pricking at her that she could not show with all the other ladies present. When she asked what had happened, Gwendolen laughed it off. “Oh, I was being silly. I stopped to stare at the Whispering Stones for a minute, and everyone else hurried on after something in the trees, so I lost sight of them. I thought it made far more sense to come back the short way the warden told me about. I’m not sorry, my feet are tired of walking anyway.”
“I don’t suppose your group ran into Mr. Grandcourt, then?” Mrs. Arrowpoint asked, pointedly.
