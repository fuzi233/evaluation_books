The little house on Hoog Straet lay heavy with silence, as though death itself had taken up residence. Catherine, usually so bustling and bright, moved through the rooms like a shadow, her eyes raw from weeping. The weight of the previous day’s wrath still pressed upon them all, but none bore it so listlessly as she.

After a meager dinner, she gathered what resolve she had left and made her way to the convent, hoping to plead with Gerard, to lay the first fragile stone of reconciliation. But the porter, slow to understand her request, delivered bitter news instead: Gerard had departed the night before and would not return.

Defeated, Catherine carried the tidings to Margaret, who sat motionless, her arms locked around her child as if he were the last anchor in a storm-tossed sea.

"Come back with me," Catherine urged. "Eli might listen to you. You could soften him."

Margaret exhaled, slow and weary. "If you think I can do any good, I’ll come."

But when they arrived, they found Eli and a carpenter at work, stripping the names of Cornelis and Sybrandt from the family record and replacing them with Margaret Brandt’s. The sight struck Catherine like a blade.

Margaret only gazed at it, cold. "Can he make me spend the money unjustly?"

Catherine sighed. "You’re a good soul."

"Best to be, since he is strongest."

---

The next day, Giles arrived, and Catherine poured out the tale of the disgraced brothers, seeking pity for them—cast out, cursed, left to the mercy of the world. But Giles had no patience for false sympathy.

"They got off light," he scoffed. "Hanging at Margaret’s door would’ve been justice." Then, with sudden vigor, he grinned. "But Gerard’s back! I’ll show him what it means to have a brother at court—one with the heart to serve and the wit to guide."

Margaret murmured, "Bless thee, Giles."

Little Kate sighed. "You were ever his true friend. But what can you do for him now?"

---

The house settled back into silence, broken only when a well-dressed stranger entered, asking for Margaret Brandt. Catherine, ever the gossip despite her sorrow, perked up. "You’re wanted, lass."

Margaret lifted her head, hollow-eyed. "Here I am."

Then came the shuffling of unsteady feet. Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, pale as a specter, was helped into the room.

Catherine shrieked and threw her apron over her face. Margaret turned sharply away, as if the sight of him might poison her.

The old man’s voice was a whisper. "Good people… a dying man begs your forgiveness."

Catherine’s voice shook. "Come to admire your handiwork, more like!" She sobbed. "Look at her—she’s near fainting!"

Margaret’s breath came thin. "Let him say his piece and go. He is the murderer of me and mine."

Ghysbrecht swayed. "I am too weak to stand… yet none bid me sit."

Eli, torn between anger and duty, growled, "We don’t turn visitors from our hearth. But man, you’ve dealt us too much pain."

Then little Kate, gentle but firm, spoke. "Father, mother—death squares all accounts. And his time is shorter than mine."

Eli, chastened, set a chair for their enemy.

Ghysbrecht’s attendants brought in two boxes before withdrawing, leaving him alone with those he had wronged. With trembling hands, he opened them.

"These deeds are yours, Margaret Brandt—your grandfather’s land, stolen by me. And here, three hundred and forty gold angels, the rents I took beyond what was owed." His voice cracked. "I was not born dishonest… yet you see what I became."

Catherine gaped. "And you the burgomaster! Whipped thieves in your day, yet here you stand." Then, softening, "But better late than never. Margaret, you’re rich!"

Margaret did not turn. "Let him keep his gold. Give me back my Gerard, stolen by his lies."

Ghysbrecht bowed his head. "Would that I could. What I can do, I have. It cost me my last strength to rise from my deathbed and bring this myself."

Margaret’s voice was ice. "Since even you, who worship gold, have done this, may God forgive you. But I am only a woman with a broken heart. Kate—speak for me."

Kate’s voice, soft as a prayer, filled the room. "Her sorrow is beyond riches. Her boy is fatherless; she is neither maid, wife, nor widow. The wound is too fresh. Give her time. But we, whose hurts are lesser, forgive you. Go in peace."

Ghysbrecht, too weak for food or drink, was lifted into his litter. As he left, he murmured, "This land was a bed of nettles to me. Now that it’s gone, I feel lighter."

---

Once he was gone, Catherine marveled at the fortune before them. "Look at it, Margaret! So much gold!"

But Margaret only stared, her face empty, until suddenly she wrenched her hands together and cried out in agony—

The wealth that might have kept Gerard by her side had come when it no longer mattered. The gold, the land—all useless now. A single year earlier, and their lives would have been different.

But fate had dealt its blow.

And love had come too late.
