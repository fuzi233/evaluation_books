The shop at the corner of two steep Camden Town streets glowed with the concentrated heat of a dying star, its light fractured into a kaleidoscope of colors by a dozen mirrors. Inside, the display cases overflowed with confections wrapped in metallic foils of crimson, gold, and emerald—packaging so vibrant it rendered the chocolate within secondary. Dominating the scene was a colossal white wedding cake, a sugared monolith that seemed simultaneously distant and deliciously edible, evoking the absurdity of consuming the entire North Pole. While this spectacle drew the neighborhood's youth, ranging from ten to twelve, it held a specific, magnetic pull for older men as well. Standing before the glass was a young man, aged twenty-four, whose gaze was fixed with an intensity that transcended mere curiosity.

John Turnbull Angus was a tall, burly specimen with a mane of red hair and a face etched with resolve, though his demeanor betrayed a profound listlessness. Tucked under his arm was a flat, grey portfolio containing black-and-white sketches he had sold with varying degrees of success to publishers. His financial stability—or lack thereof—was a direct consequence of his uncle, a retired Admiral, disinheriting him for his vocal advocacy of Socialism, specifically following a lecture Angus had delivered denouncing the theory.

Angus pushed open the door and stepped into the confectioner's shop, moving toward the rear room, which functioned as a makeshift pastry restaurant. He tipped his hat to the server, a dark, elegant woman in black with a complexion flushed with vitality and eyes that moved with startling speed. After a brief pause, she followed him into the inner sanctum to take his order.

"Please," Angus stated with surgical precision, "a halfpenny bun and a small cup of black coffee." Before the waitress could retreat, he added, "And I require your marriage."

The young lady stiffened instantly. "Those are jests I do not permit."

Angus lifted his grey eyes, which possessed a gravity that belied his earlier levity. "Truly and without reservation," he declared. "It is as serious as the bun itself. It carries a price tag comparable to the bun. Furthermore, it is indigestible, much like the bun, and painful to consume."

The waitress never broke eye contact, scrutinizing him with a tragic, clinical exactitude. Finally, a faint shadow of a smile touched her lips, and she seated herself opposite him.

"Do you not consider," Angus observed, his attention drifting slightly, "that these halfpenny buns are acts of cruelty? They possess the potential to mature into penny buns. Upon our union, I pledge to abandon these brutal culinary sports."

The waitress rose and walked to the window, lost in a state of intense, albeit sympathetic, contemplation. When she turned back, her expression hardened with resolution, only to falter as she noticed Angus meticulously arranging items from the shop window onto the table. He had constructed a pyramid of brightly colored sweets, laid out several plates of sandwiches, and positioned two decanters filled with the mysterious port and sherry reserved for pastry chefs. In the center of this tableau, he had carefully lowered the massive, sugared wedding cake that had served as the window's centerpiece.

"What on earth are you doing?" she demanded.

"Duty, my dear Laura," Angus began.

"Oh, for heaven's sake, stop," she cried, "and cease speaking to me in that manner. What is all this?"

"A ceremonial meal, Miss Hope."

"And what constitutes that?" she asked, gesturing impatiently at the mountain of sugar.

"The wedding-cake, Mrs. Angus," he corrected.

The girl marched to the artifact, knocked it back into place with a loud clatter, and returned to the table, resting her elegant elbows upon the surface. She regarded Angus unfavorably, her patience wearing thin.

"You afford me no time to deliberate," she stated.

"I am no fool," Angus replied. "That is my Christian humility."

Her expression grew significantly more somber beneath the veneer of her smile. "Mr. Angus," she said steadily, "before this charade extends another minute, I must disclose certain particulars about myself, as concisely as possible."

"Delighted," Angus responded gravely. "Might you not share some particulars regarding yourself while you are at it?"

"Hold your tongue and listen," she commanded. "It involves nothing I am ashamed of, nor anything for which I harbor special regret. However, imagine my reaction if there existed a matter entirely beyond my control, yet one that haunts me as a nightmare?"

"In that eventuality," Angus said seriously, "I would advise you to retrieve the cake."

"Well, you must hear the narrative first," Laura insisted. "To commence, my father owned the inn known as the 'Red Fish' in Ludbury, and I served patrons in the bar."

"I have frequently pondered," Angus remarked, "why this particular confectioner's shop possesses such a distinct Christian atmosphere."

"Ludbury is a lethargic, pastoral backwater in the Eastern Counties," Laura continued. "The sole visitors to the 'Red Fish' were occasional commercial travelers, save for the most repulsive specimens imaginable—types you have never encountered. I refer to short, loitering men subsisting on bare minimums, possessing nothing but time to waste leaning against bar counters and wagering on horse races, clad in attire that was merely adequate yet ill-fitting. Even these despicable youths were rare at our establishment, until two individuals became ubiquitous. Both lived independently, plagued by a wearisome idleness and an excessive flair for dress. Yet, I harbored a degree of pity for them, for I suspected they frequented our sparse bar due to a shared deformity—a quirk that locals mocked. It was not a true deformity, but rather an oddity. One was a remarkably diminutive man, akin to a dwarf or a jockey. He lacked the typical jockeyish appearance, sporting a round black head, a neatly trimmed black beard, and eyes bright as a bird's. He jingled coins in his pockets and wore a heavy gold watch chain. He appeared only when overdressed, projecting an image of gentility that was clearly fraudulent. Despite being a futile idler, he was curiously adept at useless arts: impromptu conjuring, setting fifteen matches ablaze simultaneously like a firework, or carving a banana into a dancing doll. His name was Isidore Smythe. I can still visualize his small, dark face as he approached the counter, fashioning a jumping kangaroo from five cigars."

"The other individual was more taciturn and conventional, yet he instilled in me far greater alarm than the unfortunate Smythe. He was exceptionally tall and slender, with light hair and a nose featuring a high bridge; he might have been handsome in a spectral, ghostly manner. However, he suffered from one of the most terrifying squints I have ever witnessed. When he fixed his gaze upon you, you lost your bearings, unsure of your location or his intent. I suspect this disfigurement embittered him; whereas Smythe delighted in displaying his acrobatic feats, James Welkin—the squinting man—never performed any tricks. Instead, he retreated to our bar parlor or wandered the flat, grey countryside in solitary excursions. Nevertheless, I believe Smythe was equally sensitive about his stature, though he managed it with superior style. Thus, I was genuinely perplexed and startled, and deeply concerned, when both men proposed marriage within the same week."

"I did what I now deem a foolish act," Laura admitted. "Yet, these anomalies were my friends in a sense. I feared they would interpret a refusal as stemming from their perceived ugliness. Consequently, I fabricated a pretext, claiming I intended to marry only someone who had forged their own path. I asserted it was a matter of principle to avoid living off inherited wealth like theirs. Two days after delivering this well-intentioned message, the catastrophe commenced. The first news I received was that both had departed to seek their fortunes, as if stepping into a nonsensical fairy tale."

"I have never seen either since," Laura continued. "However, I received two letters from the little man, Smythe, which were quite thrilling."

"Have you heard from the other?" Angus inquired.

"No," Laura hesitated. "He never wrote. Smythe's initial correspondence merely noted that he had embarked on a journey with Welkin to London, but the little man dropped out due to Welkin's superior endurance, taking a rest by the roadside. He was subsequently discovered by a traveling circus. Given his near-dwarf stature and genuine cleverness, he thrived in the entertainment industry and was quickly promoted to perform at the Aquarium. That was his first letter. The second was far more alarming, arriving only last week."

Angus drained his coffee cup, regarding her with mild, patient eyes. As she resumed, a slight twist of amusement played on her lips. "Presumably, you have seen the hoardings advertising 'Smythe's Silent Service'? Or perhaps you are the sole individual unaware of it. These are mechanical inventions designed to automate domestic labor. You recall the slogans: 'Press a Button—A Butler Who Never Drinks,' 'Turn a Handle—Ten Housemaids Who Never Flirt.' Whatever these contraptions are, they are generating immense profits, all accruing to the little imp I knew in Ludbury. I cannot suppress my pleasure that the poor chap has succeeded; however, the undeniable fact remains that I am terrified of his sudden reappearance to announce he has carved his own fortune—as he undoubtedly has."

"And the other man?" Angus repeated, his tone stubbornly quiet.
