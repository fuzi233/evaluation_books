To reconstruct the architecture of this theft, one must first understand the suffocating atmosphere of the evening preceding the catastrophe. When the final carriage wheels had rolled away, leaving the estate in a vacuum of silence, I retreated to the inner hall. There, Samuel presided over the brandy and soda water, a somber sentinel amidst the fading revelry. My Lady and Miss Rachel emerged from the drawing-room, trailed by the two gentlemen. Mr. Godfrey accepted a measure of spirits, his spirit seemingly buoyant enough to sustain the ritual, whereas Mr. Franklin took nothing. He seated himself, radiating a profound exhaustion; the cacophony of the birthday celebration had clearly drained him dry.

My Lady, turning to bid them farewell, fixed her gaze upon the wicked Colonel's legacy, now gleaming with a malevolent luminescence within her daughter's gown.

"Rachel," she queried, her voice cutting through the twilight air, "where shall you deposit your Diamond tonight?"

Miss Rachel was intoxicated by a manic glee, trapped in that peculiar adolescent humorsphere where nonsense masquerades as profound logic. First, she confessed ignorance of the diamond's destination. Then, with the stubborn persistence of youth, she declared it belonged naturally upon her dressing-table, alongside her other treasures. Suddenly, fear seized her; she recalled the diamond's ability to generate its own spectral moonlight in the darkness—a terror sufficient to paralyze her in the dead of night. In a flash of theatrical resolution, she pivoted to the Indian cabinet in her sitting-room, deciding that two magnificent native artifacts should be allowed to admire one another in solitude.

Her mother interjected, halting this stream of capriciousness.

"My dear," My Lady stated, "your Indian cabinet possesses no lock."

"Good Heavens, Mamma!" cried Miss Rachel, her tone shifting to one of manufactured panic. "Is this an asylum? Are thieves lurking within these walls?"

Ignoring this fantastical display, My Lady dismissed the gentlemen. Turning to her daughter, she kissed her forehead. "Why not allow *me* to safeguard the Diamond for you tonight?" she proposed.

Miss Rachel received this offer with the same visceral rejection she might have applied to a proposal to confiscate a beloved doll a decade prior. Seeing that reason held no sway, My Lady commanded, "Come into my room, Rachel, the first thing tomorrow morning. I shall have something to say to you." With those final words, she departed slowly, her countenance betraying a turmoil beneath her composed exterior.

Miss Rachel followed suit, shaking hands first with Mr. Godfrey, who stood gazing vacantly at a portrait, before turning to Mr. Franklin. He remained seated in a corner, weary and silent.

The exchange between them remains obscure to me. Yet, standing near the ancient oak frame housing our great mirror, I witnessed a reflection that shattered my confidence in my own judgment. I saw her slip the locket Mr. Franklin had gifted her from the bosom of her dress, holding it aloft for his inspection with a smile charged with significance before she fled to her chambers. This incident destabilized my reliance on my faculties; I began to suspect Penelope might indeed be correct regarding the state of her young lady's affections.

Immediately upon her departure, Mr. Franklin's attention snapped to mine. His mood, a mercurial entity, had already shifted its focus to the Indians.

"Betteredge," he murmured, "I find myself half-convinced that I took Mr. Murthwaite too seriously during our conversation in the shrubbery. Pray, does he intend to regale us with his traveler's tales? Do you truly propose to release the dogs?"

"I shall remove their collars, sir," I replied, "and grant them liberty to roam the grounds if they scent a cause for unrest."

"All right," Mr. Franklin conceded. "We shall determine the course of action tomorrow. I am disinclined to alarm my Aunt without compelling evidence. Good-night."

His pallor and weariness prompted me to suggest a nightcap of brandy-and-water. Mr. Godfrey, approaching from the opposite end of the hall, seconded my sentiment, urging Mr. Franklin to consume something before retiring.

These trifles are noted merely to illustrate that, despite the verbal warfare heard earlier and their rivalry for Miss Rachel's favor, the two gentlemen remained cordial. Men of station, it seems, possess a rare immunity to petty squabbles compared to those of lesser birth. Mr. Franklin declined the drink initially, ascending with Mr. Godfrey, their rooms adjacent. However, on the landing, fate intervened—or rather, Mr. Franklin's indecision reasserted itself. "Perhaps I may require it in the night," he called down. "Send up some brandy-and-water."

I dispatched Samuel with the spirits and proceeded to unbuckle the dogs' collars. Astonished by the nocturnal freedom, they leaped upon me like playful puppies until the cooling rain subdued their frenzy. They lapped a drop of water each and retreated to their kennels. As I entered the house, the sky displayed omens of clearing weather, though for the moment, the deluge continued, turning the earth to soup.

Samuel and I conducted our nightly rounds, securing every aperture. I personally inspected each item, refusing to delegate trust to my deputy. By the hour between midnight and one, I retired, finding the house as silent as a tomb. The day's anxieties had overtaken me; I succumbed to Mr. Franklin's malady, sleeping only as the sun rose. Throughout the vigil, save for the rhythmic splashing of rain and the wind sighing through the trees, absolute stillness reigned.

At approximately half-past seven, I awakened to a brilliant dawn. The clock had struck eight as I prepared to secure the dogs once more when a sudden rustle of petticoats echoed on the stairs behind me.

I turned to find Penelope descending the staircase with frantic urgency. "Father!" she shrieked. "Ascend immediately! The Diamond is gone!"

"Are you out of your mind?" I demanded.

"Gone!" Penelope insisted. "Vanished into thin air! Come up and verify."

She dragged me into the young lady's sitting-room, opening the door to her bedroom. There, standing rigid on the threshold, was Miss Rachel, her complexion as pale as her white dressing-gown. Beside her stood the double doors of the Indian cabinet, flung wide open. One internal drawer protruded fully extended.

"Behold!" Penelope exclaimed. "I witnessed Miss Rachel placing the Diamond into that drawer last night." I approached the cabinet; the drawer was hollow.

"Is this accurate, miss?" I asked.

With a visage alien to her usual self and a voice devoid of its characteristic timbre, Miss Rachel repeated my daughter's words: "The Diamond is gone!"

Uttering these syllables, she retreated into her bedroom, locking the door with a decisive click.

Before we could orient ourselves, My Lady entered, drawn by my voice and the commotion. The news of the theft seemed to petrify her. She rushed to her daughter's chamber, demanding admission. Miss Rachel complied.

Panic, spreading through the house like wildfire, reached the gentlemen next. Mr. Godfrey emerged first, raising his hands in bewildered surrender, a gesture that betrayed a lack of mental fortitude. Mr. Franklin, whom I had relied upon for clarity, appeared equally paralyzed. Remarkably, he had enjoyed a restful night; the luxury of sleep had, as he later admitted, stupefied him. However, after consuming his customary cup of coffee—an hour before breakfast, per foreign custom—his faculties sharpened. The clear-headed aspect of his nature reasserted control, and he assumed command with resolve and cunning.

First, he summoned the servants, ordering them to leave all lower doors and windows untouched, save for the front entrance I had secured. Next, he proposed to his cousin and me that we ensure the Diamond had not merely fallen into obscurity—behind the cabinet or beneath the table—before proceeding further. Having searched these locales to no avail and interrogated Penelope to no new effect, Mr. Franklin directed his inquiry toward Miss Rachel, sending Penelope to knock upon her bedroom door.

My Lady answered, closing the door behind her. Moments later, the sound of the lock engaging from the interior reached our ears. My Mistress emerged, looking profoundly disturbed. "The loss of the Diamond appears to have utterly overwhelmed Rachel," she reported to Mr. Franklin. "She recoils from discussing it, even from *me*. It is impossible to see her at present."

Adding to our perplexity, My Lady regained her composure with remarkable speed.

"I suppose there is no recourse?" she asked calmly. "Must I summon the police?"

"And the primary duty of the police," Mr. Franklin interjected, seizing the initiative, "is to apprehend the Indian jugglers who performed here last night."

Both My Lady and Mr. Godfrey, unaware of the deductions Mr. Franklin and I had made, stared in surprise.

"I cannot elaborate now," Mr. Franklin continued. "I assert that the Indians stole the Diamond. Provide me with a letter of introduction to a magistrate at Frizinghall, stating that I act on your behalf, and permit me to depart immediately. Our success hinges on not wasting a single minute."
