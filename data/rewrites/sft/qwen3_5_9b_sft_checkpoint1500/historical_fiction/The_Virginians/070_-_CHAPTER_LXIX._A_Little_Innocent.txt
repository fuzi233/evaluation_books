Though Lord Castlewood had previously maneuvered with Harry regarding business matters, a strange and enduring friendship persisted between the Earl and his cousin, George Warrington. They met with the warmth of close kinship, bound by a mutual, albeit self-serving, affection. If George sought favors—whether financial grants, introductions to the Court, or seats in the House of Lords—he found his cousin ever ready to oblige. Lord Castlewood, a man of wit and charm, was a delightful companion who seemed willing to sacrifice nothing short of his own immediate interests for the sake of his relative.

Indeed, the Earl went so far as to pledge his influence to secure a position for Mr. George Warrington, a boon eagerly grasped by the Virginian. George, who showed no inclination to return to America, clung to this promise with fervor. He despised the notion of submitting once more to his mother's "servitude" and the stifling atmosphere of his plantation. While a life of idle leisure in Virginia might have suited him had he possessed true independence, reality offered him only the status of a pampered, yet undervalued, favorite among his mother's many subjects. He dreaded the prospect of returning with his young bride to a home filled with wearisome routines, tedious subordination, petty squabbles, and the inevitable jealousies that threatened to unravel their union. In contrast, London promised freedom, congenial society, and a glimmer of future distinction, however bleak his circumstances might appear.

Consequently, Lord Castlewood's assurance was accepted with alacrity. Although the Earl's own position, despite his title, was far from enviable, and his past conduct was notorious, George and his host were blinded by the desire to believe the best. When the Earl revealed his engagement to Miss Lambert, he did so with a grace that softened the blow to her parents. He visited them with prodigious cheerfulness, praising George to the lambeths and the young lady to George, until these charitable souls forgot his infamous reputation, convinced that the world must be wicked indeed to malign a man of his apparent refinement. With the Lamberts, Lord Castlewood appeared transformed; he was witty, lively, and seemingly devoid of his former vices. He claimed to be improved by their society, leaving his "wickedness and worldliness" behind when he entered their drawing-room, only to reclaim them the moment he stepped back into his carriage. Whether he was genuinely better or merely playing a role, the Lambert elders, seeing his open admiration for Miss Theo, felt no cause for suspicion.

"It don't affect my sleep, and I am not going to break my heart because Miss Lambert prefers somebody else," the Earl remarked lightly. "Only I wish, when I was a young man, madam, I had had the good fortune to meet with somebody so innocent and good as your daughter. I might have been kept out of a deal of harm's way... innocent and good young women did not fall into mine, or they would have made me better than I am."

Mrs. Lambert, moved by his words, whispered softly, "Sure, my lord, it is not too late!"

Startled, the Earl inquired, "Not too late, madam?"

Blushing, the matron replied, "It is too late to court my dear daughter, my lord, but not too late to repent. We read, 'tis never too late to do that."

With a plaintive tone, the Earl shook his head. "Perhaps I know my own heart better than you. A man who learns languages late can never get the practice of them on his tongue. And so 'tis the case with goodness; I can't learn it at my age. I can only see others practise it, and admire them." He paused, his expression melancholy. "When I am on—the side opposite to Lazarus, will Miss Theo give me a drop of water? Don't frown! I know I shall be there, Mrs. Lambert. Some folks are doomed so; and I think some of our family are amongst these. Some people are vacillating, whereas some are predestined angels, and fly Heavenwards naturally."

"Oh, my lord, and why should you not be of the predestined? Whilst there is a day left..." urged the hopeful lady.

"But how can they avail?" he asked sadly, reading the prayer in her eyes. "You don't know what a life ours is of the world... It is Fate holds the reins of the chariot." Yet, as he took her hand and bowed with a tragic grace, every word rang with a hollow truth. Weak and bad men were not strangers to good feelings; they simply lacked the endurance to sustain them. The tears of the libertine added zest to his pleasures, a debauch of sentiment. Nevertheless, Mrs. Lambert, knowing little of the depths of his soul, continued to pray for him with the persistent kindness of a woman. To her, he was a fascinating figure, a reprobate with potential for redemption, and she could not bear to lose sight of him.

As the illness of the soul was treated, an intimacy blossomed between the Earl and the Lamberts. The Ladies of Castlewood, maintaining impeccable decorum, frequented Mrs. Lambert's drawing-room, extending invitations to her card assemblies. Play, the universal vice, flowed freely between the courts and cities, abetted by bishops and peers alike. Mrs. Lambert, grateful for the welcome and delighted by the attention paid to her daughters, became a regular fixture at these gatherings.

Meanwhile, the machinations of Madam Esmond, the formidable matchmaker, faced a barrier. An American family of Dutch extraction, strongly recommended by Madam Esmond, arrived in England. Their views were clear enough for the astute Mrs. Lambert to discern, yet the plans were stymied. George was already betrothed to Miss Theo, and Harry, the object of Hetty's affections, had departed for military service in Canada—a fact for which Hetty's mother was surprisingly relieved. Laughingly, Mrs. Lambert advised George to sever his ties with Theo and pursue Miss Lydia, describing her as ten times richer and handsomer than her rival.

"I say she is handsomer, and she is richer, and perhaps even cleverer," George admitted, though with evident reluctance. "But say she is all these? So is Mr. Johnson much cleverer than I am... so is Mr. Hagan the actor much taller and handsomer: so is Sir James Lowther much richer: yet pray, ma'am, do you suppose I am going to be jealous of any one of these three?"

"No, George," said Theo, with an honest gaze that silenced any lingering jealousy. "If, after this pair of speeches, mamma takes occasion to leave the room for a minute to fetch her scissors, or her thimble, or a bootjack and slippers, or the cross and ball on the top of St. Paul's, or her pocket-handkerchief which she has forgotten in the parlour—if, I say, Mrs. Lambert quits the room on any errand or pretext, natural or preposterous, I shall not be in the least surprised, if, at her return in a couple of minutes, she finds George in near proximity to Theo, who has a heightened colour, and whose hand George is just dropping—I shall not have the least idea of what they have been doing."

With Harry away and the engagement solid, Mrs. Lambert turned her目光 to her own schemes, envisioning a wealthy, handsome wife for her son Jack. However, the ambitions of the American family, the Van den Boschs, were unfolding elsewhere. When Mr. Van den Bosch first arrived, he associated only with the City merchant class, taking his daughter Lydia to Islington, Highgate, and the Artillery Ground. He attended Baptist meetings and, secretly, the theaters, treating a Lord Mayor's feast as the height of civilization.

When George first waited upon his mother's friends, he encountered Mr. Draper of the Temple, who was attentive to the young lady, noting her substantial dowry. Mr. Drabshaw, a young Quaker gentleman and nephew of Madam Esmond's Bristol agent, was equally vigilant, viewing George with alarm. Desiring to honor his mother's neighbors, George invited them to an entertainment at his apartments, where he inevitably met his friends from Soho. Forced to acknowledge Miss Lydia's beauty, the group marveled at her features—the foot of a fairy, the arms and neck of a brown huntress. She carried a plaintive accent from home, a sound which, though mocked by the Cockneys, was undeniably charming when uttered by such a countenance.

Six months after her schooling at the Kensington Academy, Miss Lydia returned to her grandfather's side, determined to enlarge her sphere. The obedient grandfather, thwarted in his youth and fearful of repeating his mistakes, relented. Having realized the severity of his own discipline had produced an unhappy son, he vowed to ensure Lydia's happiness. He relocated from Monument Yard to an elegant house in Bloomsbury, abandoning his circle of tradesmen for the company of the great. Soon, the junketings ceased, replaced by Sunday services at Queen Square Church—a shocking departure from his former Baptist allegiance—and social calls at Kensington.

At this juncture, rumors swirled regarding young Mr. Lutestring and the Quaker Mr. Drabshaw, who had allegedly fought a boxing match over the young lady. Mr. Draper, formerly a staunch admirer, now spoke ill of the old gentleman's lineage, revealing that Van den Bosch was a speculator from Albany, whose son had married a servant, fallen into disgrace, and died.
