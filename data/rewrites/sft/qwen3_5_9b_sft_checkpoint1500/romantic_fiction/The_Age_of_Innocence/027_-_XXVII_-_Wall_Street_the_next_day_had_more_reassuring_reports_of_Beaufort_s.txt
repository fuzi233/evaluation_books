Wall Street, the following morning, breathed a sigh of cautious optimism regarding Beaufort's precarious standing. While the rumors remained fluid rather than factual, a general consensus held that the financier had successfully leveraged his connections to stave off immediate disaster. That same evening, when Lady Beaufort graced the opera box with her customary, enigmatic smile and a newly acquired emerald necklace, the social circle exhaled collectively, relieved by the illusion of stability.

Yet, beneath this veneer of polite contentment, New York City remained a stern judge of commercial morality. Its unwritten law was absolute: those who violated the sanctity of probity must pay the price. All were acutely aware that neither Beaufort nor his wife could escape this reckoning without consequence. However, sacrificing them would prove not merely painful but socially disastrous. Their absence would carve a gaping hole in the city's tightly knit circle, and those too naive to comprehend the ethical abyss yawned ahead lamented in advance the loss of New York's premier ballroom.

Archibald Young had resolved to depart for Washington. He awaited the filing of a lawsuit concerning a patent matter, hoping to synchronize his departure with the legal proceedings. Yet, by Tuesday, Mr. Letterblair informed him that the case might be delayed for weeks. Undeterred, Archer returned home that afternoon, firm in his intention to leave the following evening. He reasoned that May, unaware of his professional intricacies and indifferent to his clients, would likely miss the postponement entirely, or forget the litigants' names if mentioned. More pressing still was the necessity of confronting Madame Olenska; there were matters of profound import he needed to address with her.

By Wednesday morning, however, the atmosphere at the office had shifted drastically. Mr. Letterblair greeted Archer with a visage etched by distress. Despite the initial rumors of salvation, Beaufort had failed to "ride out" the storm. His strategy of feigning security had initially calmed depositors, prompting a flood of capital until the eve of the current crisis, when alarming reports resurfaced. A panic ensued, leading to a run on the bank that threatened to shutter its doors before sundown. Whispers of dastardly maneuvering filled the air, promising a scandal to tarnish Wall Street's reputation forever.

Mr. Letterblair stood pallid, his faculties overwhelmed. "I have witnessed ruin before," he murmured, "but nothing comparable to this. Every acquaintance of ours will feel the tremors. And what of Mrs. Beaufort? What *shall* be done for her? I pity Mrs. Manson Mingott deeply; at her age, the shock could be fatal. She placed her trust in Beaufort, forging a bond with him. Then there is the entire Dallas connection—poor Lady Beaufort is kin to us all. Her only recourse would be to flee her husband, yet how dare anyone suggest such a betrayal? Her duty lies squarely at his side, and, blessedly, she remains blindly devoted to his frailties."

A sharp knock interrupted his musing. "Who disturbs me?" he snapped. A clerk entered bearing a letter for Archer and departed swiftly. Recognizing his wife's distinctive script, the young lawyer opened the envelope. It read: *"Will you please come uptown as soon as possible? Granny suffered a slight stroke last night. Somehow, she learned of the bank's ruin before others. Uncle Lovell is away hunting, and the horror of the disgrace has left Papa feverish and bedridden. Mother needs you desperately; I implore you to come immediately and go straight to Granny's."*

Archer passed the note to his senior partner and, moments later, surrendered to the crushing crowds of the horse-cars, transferring to the swaying omnibuses of Fifth Avenue. By past noon, he arrived at Old Catherine's residence. The ground-floor parlor, usually her throne, was occupied by her daughter, Mrs. Welland, whose gaunt features signaled deep anxiety. As Archer crossed the threshold, he encountered May, who offered a wan, forced smile. The hallway bore the stamp of sudden illness: furs and wraps littered the seats, a physician's bag rested on the table amidst a pile of unopened correspondence.

May, looking ghostly yet composed, guided Archer to the elderly lady's salon, where heavy drapes concealed the bedroom. From there, Mrs. Welland whispered the terrors of the preceding night in a voice trembling with horror. Around eight o'clock, shortly after Mrs. Mingott concluded her solitary game, the bell had tolled. A heavily veiled visitor had requested admission. Upon recognizing a familiar tone, the butler had ushered her in, announcing, "Mrs. Julius Beaufort," before closing the door upon the two women. They had remained together for roughly an hour. When the hostess finally summoned the servant, Mrs. Beaufort had vanished into the shadows unseen.

Left alone, the formidable matriarch, pale and towering in presence, had signaled the butler to assist her upstairs. Though visibly shaken, she had maintained command of her faculties. The dark-skinned maid had tucked her into bed, served tea, and arranged the chamber, only to return hours later to find her mistress propped against pillows, a twisted, knowing smile upon her lips and one small hand dangling limply from her massive arm.

Fortunately, the stroke had been mild; the old lady could articulate her thoughts. Soon after the physician's arrival, she regained control of her facial muscles. Yet the terror induced by the revelation was immense, matched only by the fury that erupted when it became clear that Regina Beaufort had sought her aunt's backing. In words of shocking audacity, Regina had demanded the family shield her husband, refusing to "desert" them, urging everyone to condone their monstrous infamy.

*"I told her,"* the old woman had stammered, her voice thick with paralysis, *"that honor has always been honor, and honesty honesty, in the house of Manson Mingott, and it shall remain so until I am carried out feet first!"* When Regina had countered, *"But my name, Auntie—it is Regina Dallas,"* the old lady had retorted, *"It was Beaufort when he draped you in jewels, and it must remain Beaufort now that he has draped you in shame."*

Mrs. Welland recounted these fragments with tears and gasps, utterly undone by the burden of facing the ugly truth. *"If only I could spare Father from seeing this!"* she wailed, recalling his constant plea for Augusta not to shatter his final illusions. *"How am I to keep this secret from him?"*

*"After all, Mother,*" her daughter interjected gently, *"he will not have seen them."* A sigh of relief followed, for Dr. Bencomb had pledged to keep the patriarch confined to his chamber until his mother recovered, ensuring Regina remained at bay.

Seated by the window, Archer stared blankly at the empty street below. It was evident he had been summoned less for practical aid than for emotional sustenance. Mr. Lovell Mingott had been telegraphed for, and urgent dispatches sent to other relatives. Meanwhile, the room fell silent as the ladies deliberated the implications of Beaufort's disgrace and his wife's indefensible actions.

Mrs. Lovell Mingott, emerging from an adjacent room, joined the somber discourse. *"In our day,"* she observed, *"the wife of a man guilty of such dishonor had but one purpose: to efface herself, to vanish with him."* She recalled the tragedy of Great-Great-Aunt Spicer, whose mother had fled New York following similar scandals, living in isolation along the Hudson until Augusta was sixteen. *"It would never have crossed Grandmamma Spicer's mind to ask the family to 'countenance' her,"* Mrs. Welland noted, *"though a private sin pales before the scandal of ruining hundreds of innocent souls."*

*"Indeed, it would be far more becoming for Regina to conceal her countenance than to debate others',"* Mrs. Lovell Mingott concurred. *"I hear the emerald necklace she wore at the opera was sent on approval this afternoon. I wonder if they will ever recover it?"*

Archer listened impassively to the relentless chorus of judgment. The concept of absolute financial probity as the bedrock of a gentleman's code was etched into his soul, immune to sentimentality. While adventurers like Lemuel Struthers might accumulate fortunes through shady dealings, unblemished integrity was the sacred duty of old financial New York. Mrs. Beaufort's plight moved him, perhaps more than her outraged kin, yet he viewed the marital bond differently. Even if breakable in times of wealth, it ought to be indissoluble in adversity. As Mr. Letterblair had posited, a wife's station was by her husband's side in trouble, but society's role was not to support them, and Mrs. Beaufort's cold calculation in assuming it did seemed to implicate her as an accomplice. For a woman to appeal to her family to screen her husband's business crimes was, to Archer's mind, utterly inadmissible; it was precisely the kind of stain the Family institution could not bear.
