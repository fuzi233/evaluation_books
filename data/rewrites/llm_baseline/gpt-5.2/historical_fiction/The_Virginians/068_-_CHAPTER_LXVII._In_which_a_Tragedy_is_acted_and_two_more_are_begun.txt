Colonel Wolfe, whom Harry now served under, came back from America a few weeks after our Virginian had taken his place in the regiment. He had been lieutenant-colonel of Kingsley’s, and, after Cape Breton, had his reward in a second battalion. Harry went to him with honest respect; for already London began to listen when Wolfe spoke, and to look when he entered a room. The town had grown hungry for victories, and for a captain to feed that hunger. Certain great names had lately proved but thin fare in the field; and the nation, like a company kept too long from table, turned its eyes towards the one man who seemed to promise a full, hot serving of glory.

Wolfe and General Lambert were both detained in London by business; so their friendly commerce renewed itself, and no one was more rejoiced than Lambert at his young friend’s good fortune. Harry, when he could steal an hour from duty, never tired of hearing Wolfe tell the story of last year’s campaigns. Wolfe spoke as freely as a man carving at his own board—no secret hoarding of the best morsel, no false seasoning for effect; whatever lay in his mind he set down plainly, with that heroic simplicity which makes bravery seem almost domestic. Some of the powdered gentlemen at St. James’s, accustomed to talk that was all garnish and no meat, might lift a sneer at his openness; but among our little circle his words found ears that loved honest sound—Lambert’s deep, measured assent, Harry’s quick breath now and again, the faint scrape of a chair as a listener leaned in. Even the room’s noises—coal settling in the grate, a footman passing with the soft clink of glass—seemed to fall into a kind of respectful hush when the young soldier, glowing with his own vision, spoke of war as if it were a crusade.

He had, too, the romance of a boy in him. Poetry and music pleased him; he would quote plays with the relish of a man tasting a dish he loves and cannot keep from praising. On the last day of his life, they say, he would rather have written Gray’s Elegy than have won a battle; and we may be sure that with such a taste he and George Warrington found easy fellowship. They were both lovers, both accepted, both in that warm, unreasonable weather when every common thing—an errand, a letter, a shared walk—takes on an air of destiny. Their sentimental talk would be worth setting down, if we had it truly. In one of his later letters Warrington writes, speaking of Wolfe:

“I had the honour of knowing the famous General Wolfe, and seeing much of him during his last stay in London. We had a subject of conversation then which was of unfailing interest to both of us, and I could not but admire Mr. Wolfe’s simplicity, his frankness, and a sort of glorious bravery which characterised him. He was much in love, and he wanted heaps and heaps of laurels to take to his mistress. ‘If it be a sin to covet honour,’ he used to say with Harry the Fifth (he was passionately fond of plays and poetry), ‘I am the most offending soul alive.’ Surely on his last day he had a feast which was enough to satisfy the greediest appetite for glory. He hungered after it. He seemed to me not merely like a soldier going resolutely to do his duty, but rather like a knight in quest of dragons and giants. My own country has furnished of late a chief of a very different order, and quite an opposite genius. I scarce know which to admire most. The Briton’s chivalrous ardour, or the more than Roman constancy of our great Virginian.”

As Lambert’s duties kept him in London, his family remained with him, content enough in their quiet corner of Southampton Row, where Bedford Gardens, when the season was kind, offered a little country to the eye—trees that held birdsong as if it were a possession, and flower-beds that, at dusk, breathed out damp earth and crushed green. George, too, appeared satisfied with this rural calm set inside the city’s clatter; he made his pilgrimage to Castlewood and passed a few days there, sleeping in the chamber of which he had so often heard his grandfather speak, the very room where Colonel Esmond had slept as a boy. He was received civilly by such of the family as were at home; the ladies were gracious, Lady Maria especially so, and the whole house was entertained when George and Parson Sampson read aloud the tragedy to them.

Sampson, who had the zeal of a man who finds his own youth again in another’s promise, praised it with a fervour that would not be denied; and it was through the chaplain’s pushing, more than through Mr. Johnson’s patronage after all, that Mr. Warrington’s piece ever came upon the stage. Mr. Johnson did press the play upon Mr. Garrick for Drury Lane; but Garrick had made his arrangement with Mr. Home, and so Carpezan went to Mr. Rich at Covent Garden and was accepted.

On the night of the first performance George gave an elegant entertainment to his friends at the Bedford Head in Covent Garden—an inn where roasted meats, hot from the spit, sent their rich steam into the passages, and the air was laced with wine, lemon-peel, and the faint sour of beer. From that table they went in a body to the theatre, leaving George behind with one or two companions; he stayed at the coffee-house like a general before a battle, receiving messengers from the front. Friends came and went with tidings—now hopeful, now doubtful—each report bringing with it the roar of the house as if the sound itself had travelled down the street and shaken the panes.

Barry played Carpezan; Shuter, the old nobleman; Reddish, it need scarcely be said, made an excellent Ulric. The King of Bohemia was a young actor from Dublin, Mr. Geoghegan—Hagan, as he called himself on the stage—who looked and performed the part to admiration. Mrs. Woffington, as the heroine, seemed too old in the first act; there were whispers about it, the sort of whispers that run through an audience like a draft under a door. But her murder in the fourth act—about which great doubts had been expressed—went off with terror and delight. Miss Wayn sang the ballad—supposed to be sung by the king’s page—just at the moment of the unhappy wife’s execution. The song’s sweetness, set against the scene’s cruelty, made the pit shiver; and all agreed that Barry was terrible and pathetic as Carpezan, most of all in the execution scene. The grace and elegance of young Hagan won general applause.

Mr. Rich had put the piece upon the stage with elegance; though some were doubtful whether, in the march of Janissaries at the last, he was correct in introducing a favourite elephant that had figured in various pantomimes. One of Mr. Warrington’s black servants marched beside it in Turkish habit, a splash of colour amid the painted barbaric splendour; the other sat in the footman’s gallery and wept and applauded uproariously at the proper moments. There is a kind of theatre-noise—hand on hand like hail, feet stamping, laughter bursting and then strangling itself—that can make the heart feel as if it were being beaten in a mortar. George, in his coffee-house exile, heard it only as a rumour, and each time the sound rose, he tasted it like a draught he could not see: bitter when it seemed uncertain, sweet when the messengers smiled.

The execution of Sybilla was the turning-point. Her head once off, George’s friends breathed more freely, and one messenger after another came to him to announce complete success. Mr. Barry, amid general applause, announced the play for repetition, and declared it to be the work of a young gentleman of Virginia, his first attempt in dramatic style.

We should like to have sat in the box where all our friends were gathered and watched Theo’s fluttering anxiety while the piece hung in doubt, to have seen the blush rise and the light kindle in her eyes when the victory was assured. Harry, during that trouble in the fourth act, was deadly pale—whiter, Mrs. Lambert said, than Barry with all his chalk. But when the curtain fell, if Briareus could have clapped, he could scarcely have made more noise than Harry. Wolfe and General Lambert huzzaed like schoolboys turned loose; Mrs. Lambert cried, as was her happy fashion. Hetty, practical in her own fierce way, asked: “Why do you cry, mamma? You don’t want any of them alive again; you know it serves them all right.” Yet even she was delighted—almost as much as little Charley from the Charterhouse, who had leave for that evening from Dr. Crusius, and as Miss Lucy, brought from her boarding-school on purpose to see the great occasion. My Lord Castlewood and Lady Maria were present, and his lordship went from his box to compliment Mr. Barry and the actors upon the stage. Parson Sampson, invaluable in the pit, led the applause, having, it was believed, instructed Gumbo beforehand to keep an eye upon him from the gallery and do as he did.

There was supper that night—many suppers—and London did what it always does when it is pleased: it ate and drank to prove the pleasure real. Mr. Gumbo gave an entertainment to several gentlemen of the shoulder-knot who had supported his master’s masterpiece; Harry gave a supper at the Star and Garter in Pall Mall to ten officers of his new regiment, who had come up expressly to back Carpezan. And finally George received the three principal actors, the family party from the side-box, Mr. Johnson and his ingenious friend Mr. Reynolds the painter, my Lord Castlewood and Lady Maria, and one or two more.

The supper-room had that particular smell of late festivity—meat cooling, candles guttering, citrus crushed into punch, and the faint burnt sweetness where sugar has been carelessly scorched at the edge of a bowl. Glass rang; chairs scraped; laughter came in gusts. When the toasts began, Lady Maria gaily gave “The King of Hungary” for hers, meaning the actor who had played the King. He had eloquence and fire, and excellent manners off the stage as well as on it; and, having already swallowed enough wine and punch to set his blood singing louder than the company, he protested that he had suffered death that night and hoped to die a hundred times more on the same field; but dead or living, he vowed he knew whose humble servant he should ever be. Ah, if he had but a true crown instead of his tinsel diadem, with what joy would he lay it at her ladyship’s feet! Neither my lord nor Mr. Esmond relished this exceeding gallantry; they attributed much of it, no doubt rightly, to the drink. Theo and her sister, new to the world, were a little frightened by the young man’s energy; but Lady Maria, practised in society’s noises—the false thunder of compliments, the mercenary softness of vows—took it in good part.

At a late hour coaches were called; the gentlemen attended the ladies down. After the ladies’ departure some returned to the supper-room again, as men will, to finish what they began and to begin anew what they should end. The end of it was that Carpezan had to be carried away in a chair; that the King of Hungary woke with a cruel headache; and that the Poet—who remembered making a great number of speeches—was quite astounded when half a dozen guests appeared at his house next day, whom he had invited overnight to come and sup once more.

When George put Mrs. Lambert and her daughters into their coach after the play, all the ladies were flurried, delighted, excited; and you may be sure he was with them the next day, turning the event over and over like a cherished flavour that lingers and will not be lost—this moment in the fourth act, that roar after the song, the look on Barry’s face, the sudden hush before the stroke, the wave of sound as the house surrendered. Mrs. Lambert had heard that actresses were dangerous company for young men; she hoped George would have a care and not frequent the greenroom too much.

George smiled and said he had a preventive against all greenroom temptations and was not in the least afraid; and as he spoke he looked in Theo’s face as if those clear eyes were the amulet that would keep him safe.

“Why should he be afraid, mamma?” asked the maiden simply. She had no notion of guile. To her, the world’s voices—applause, praise, a lover’s vow—still sounded like what they pretended to be.

“No, my darling, I don’t think he need be afraid,” said the mother, and kissed her.

“You don’t suppose Mr. George would fall in love with that painted old creature who performed the chief part?” cried Miss Hetty, with a toss of her head. “She must be old enough to be his mother.”

“Pray, do you suppose that at our age nobody can care for us, or that we have no hearts left?” asked mamma sharply. “I believe—or I may say I hope and trust—your father thinks otherwise. He is, I imagine, perfectly satisfied, miss. He does not sneer at age, whatever little girls out of the schoolroom may do. And they had much better be back there, and they had much better remember what the fifth commandment is—that they had, Hetty!”

“I didn’t think I was breaking it by saying that an actress was as old as George’s mother,” pleaded Hetty.

“George’s mother is as old as I am, miss!—at least she was when we were at school. And Fanny Parker—Mrs. Mountain who now is—was seven months older, and we were in the French class together; and I have no idea that our age is to be made the subject of remarks and ridicule by our children, and I will thank you to spare it, if you please! Do you consider your mother too old, George?”

“I am glad my mother is of your age, Aunt Lambert,” said George, with the most sentimental gravity.

Strange infatuation of passion! singular perversity of reason! There is a time—before marriage, often—when a man is actually fond of his mother-in-law. At this time the good General vowed, with some reason, that he was jealous. Mrs. Lambert made more of George than of any person in the family. She dressed Theo to the utmost advantage when he was expected; she was always caressing her, always appealing to her when he spoke. It was, “Don’t you think he looks well?”—“Don’t you think he looks pale, Theo, to-day?”—“Don’t you think he has been sitting up too much over his books at night?” If he had a cold, she would have liked to make him gruel herself and see his feet in hot water. She sent him little receipts for his health, penned with the care of a housewife whose love expresses itself in barley and lemon and proper measure. When he was away, she never stopped talking about him to her daughter; and Miss Theo, no doubt, found the subject agreeable.

When George came, Mrs. Lambert was always wanted for some moment in another part of the house and would bid Theo take care of him till she returned. Why, before she reappeared, you could hear her outside the door talking to her youngest children, to servants in the upper regions, making little harmless noises of household management that served as a curtain. When she came back, was not Mr. George always at a decent distance from Miss Theo—except, to be sure, on that one day when she happened to drop her scissors and he naturally stooped to pick them up? Why was she blushing? Are not youthful cheeks made to blush, and roses to bloom in spring? Mamma never noted blushes; she only began some artless talk and sat down to her work-table brimful of happiness.

At last there came a letter from Virginia in Madam Esmond’s neat, well-known hand, and George trembled and blushed before he broke the seal. It answered his letter respecting Harry’s commission and his own attachment to Miss Lambert. Of his intentions regarding Harry, Madam Esmond fully approved. As for his marriage, she was not against early unions. She would take his picture of Miss Lambert with the allowance that must be made for lovers’ portraits, and hoped, for his sake, that the young lady was all he described her. With money, as she gathered from his letter, Miss Lambert did not appear to be provided at all; which was a pity, as though wealthy in land, the family had little ready cash. However, by Heaven’s blessing there was plenty at home for children and children’s children; and the wives of her sons should share all she had. When she heard more at length from Mr. and Mrs. Lambert, she would reply more fully. She did not pretend she had not once held greater hopes for her son, who by name and prospects might aspire to the first lady in the land; but as Heaven had willed his choice should fall upon her old friend’s daughter, she acquiesced and would welcome George’s wife as her own child.

The letter was brought by Mr. Van den Bosch of Albany, who had lately bought a very large estate in Virginia and was bound for England to place his granddaughter at a boarding-school. Madam Esmond—no mercenary woman, she said—did not urge attentions to Mr. Van den Bosch merely because the young lady was heiress of a great fortune. Their properties lay close together; and could Harry find in the young lady those qualities of person and mind suitable for a companion for life, Madam Esmond would have the satisfaction of seeing both her children near her in declining years. She ended by sending affectionate compliments to Mrs. Lambert, from whom she begged to hear further, and her blessing to the young lady who was to be her daughter-in-law.

The letter was not cordial; the writer seemed but half satisfied. Yet such as it was, her consent was formally announced. How eagerly George ran away to Soho with the long-desired news burning in his pocket! Mrs. Lambert, reading his face before he spoke, took her daughter’s hand and kissed it with uncommon warmth when he said he had received letters from home. Then, with a break in his voice and a tremor he could not command, George turned to Mr. Lambert.

“Madam Esmond’s letter, sir, is in reply to one of mine, in which I acquainted her that I had formed an attachment in England, for which I asked my mother’s approval. She gives her consent, I am grateful to say, and I have to pray my dear friends to be equally kind to me.”

“God bless thee, my dear boy!” cried the good General, laying a hand on the young man’s head. “I am glad to have thee for a son, George. There, there, don’t go down on your knees, young folks! George may, to be sure, and thank God for giving him the best little wife in all England. Yes, my dear, except when you were ill, you never caused me a heartache—and happy is the man, I say, who wins thee!”

No doubt the young people knelt, as was the fashion; and we may be perfectly certain Mrs. Lambert kissed both and bedewed her pocket-handkerchief plentifully. Hetty was not present at this sentimental scene; and when she heard of it she spoke with asperity and a laugh not pleasant.

“Is this all the news you have to give me? Why, I have known it these months past. Do you think I have no eyes to see, and no ears to hear, indeed?”

Yet in private she was gentler. She flung herself upon her sister’s neck, embraced her passionately, vowed that never, never would Theo find any one to love her as her sister did. With Theo she became mild and humble. She could not always abstain from jest and satire with George; but he was too happy to heed much, and too generous not to see the jealousy beneath her sharpness.

When all parties came to read Madam Esmond’s letter carefully, they found it, in truth, vague. It promised only that she would receive the young people in her house; there was no proposal for a settlement. The General shook his head. He had not thought to examine the words so closely until some days after the engagement; now, reading them, he found small encouragement there.

“Bah!” said George. “I shall have three hundred pounds for my tragedy. I can easily write a play a year; and if the worst comes to the worst, we can live on that.”

“On that and your patrimony,” said Theo’s father.

George had to explain, with some hesitation, that what with paying bills for his mother, and Harry’s commission and debts, and his own ransom—his patrimony proper was well-nigh spent.

Mr. Lambert’s face grew graver at this; but he saw his girl’s eyes turn upon him with such tender alarm that he took her into his arms and vowed that, let the worst come to the worst, his darling should not be balked of her wish.

As for returning to Virginia, George frankly owned he liked the notion little—going back to be entirely dependent on his mother. He gave the General some idea of his home life and spoke plainly of how little slavery suited his taste. No: why should he not stay in England, write more tragedies, study for the bar, gain a place—perhaps? Why indeed? He began straightway to form a plan for another play. From time to time he brought portions of it to Miss Theo and her sister. Hetty yawned over the work; Theo declared it more beautiful even than the last, which was perfection.

Their engagement was made known to their families, and Sir Miles Warrington received a ceremonious letter from his nephew. For a while Sir Miles saw no particular objection—though, to be sure, considering the name and prospects, Mr. Warrington might have looked higher. The truth was Sir Miles imagined Madam Esmond had made a considerable settlement on her son and that his circumstances were more than easy. But when he heard George was entirely dependent upon his mother, and that his own small patrimony was dissipated, as Harry’s had been before, Sir Miles’s indignation knew no bounds. He could not find words enough for his horror at the want of principle in these unhappy young men. He thought it his duty to speak his mind and wrote his opinion to his sister in Virginia.

As for General and Mrs. Lambert, who passed for respectable persons, was it to be borne that such people should inveigle a penniless young man into marriage with their penniless daughter? Sir Miles fully explained his views to Madam Esmond; he gave George but half a finger when his nephew called; he did not even invite him to partake of the famous family small-beer. Toward Harry he somewhat unbent; Harry had done his duty in the campaign and was mentioned with praise in high quarters. He had sown his wild oats—he at least seemed to endeavour to amend. But George, in Sir Miles’s view, was a prodigal fast careering to ruin; and his name was mentioned in the family with a groan. Lady Warrington prayed for him as for a dangerous reprobate. And all the while George walked the town unconscious that he occasioned so much wrath and so much devotion.

He took little Miley to the play and brought him home again; he sent tickets to his aunt and cousins which they could not refuse without making the breach too marked. So they took the tickets, and when country constituents came to town they asked him for more—taking care, however, to give the very worst motives to his intimacy with the theatre, and to suppose he and the actresses were upon terms of disgraceful familiarity. When an august personage went to the theatre and expressed approbation of Mr. Warrington’s drama to Sir Miles at a levee in Saville House, Sir Miles modified his opinion of the piece and spoke henceforth more respectfully of it. Meanwhile George, careless of uncles and aunts and cousins, lived in the bright din of his own success—the applause still ringing in memory like a sea in a shell—and in the quieter, truer music of Theo’s voice when she spoke his name as if it were a word that could not be translated into any other tongue.
