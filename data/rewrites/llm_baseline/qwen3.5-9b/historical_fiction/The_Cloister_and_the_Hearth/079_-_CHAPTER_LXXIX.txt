She who had wept for poor old Martin was ill-equipped to bear such a blow with the stoic resignation often expected of those mourning the elderly. Catherine attempted in vain to soothe her with trite consolations—telling her it was a blessed release for the departed, quoting his own words that "the tree was ripe." Her most grievous failure occurred when she pressed the point that there were now but two mouths to feed, suggesting they should count themselves fortunate to have one less burden.

"Such cares are all the joy I possess," sobbed Margaret. "They fill my desolate heart, leaving it both void and wasted. Oh, empty chair! My bosom aches merely to behold you. Poor old man, how could I love him by halves, I who used to sit and gaze upon him, thinking, 'But for me, thou wouldst die of hunger'? He, once so wise and learned, has become as helpless as my own sweet babe, yet I loved him as if he were my child rather than my father. Oh, empty chair! Oh, empty heart! Well-a-day! Well-a-day!"

Her pious tears refused to be stilled. At length, Catherine fell silent, hanging her head in shame. Later, she confessed, speaking from her head rather than her heart, "Thou art right to ignore me. Were I in thy place, I should mourn the old man exactly as thou dost." When Margaret embraced her, offering a rare moment of genuine sympathy, it brought her some small comfort; the platitudes had achieved nothing.

Moved by compassion, Catherine's heart yearned for the young woman, and she spoke plainly: "My poor girl, you were not born to live alone. I must regard you as my own daughter. Do not squander your youth upon my son Gerard. Either he is dead, or he is a traitor. It cuts my heart to utter it, but who can deny what is evident before their eyes? Your father is gone, and I cannot remain beside you forever. Here stands an honest lad who has loved you faithfully for many days. I would take him and Comfort together. Heaven sends us such creatures both to torment and to comfort us; indeed, we are nothing in this world without them."

Seeing Margaret look utterly perplexed, Catherine pressed on, "Surely you are not so blind as not to perceive it?"

"What? Who?"

"Who else but Luke Peterson?"

"What, our Luke? The boy who carries my basket?"

"Nay, he is past nineteen now, a fine, healthy lad. I have made inquiries for you, and everyone speaks highly of him—a capable worker who never touches a drop. That is quite remarkable for a Rotterdam lad, for they are mostly half-man, half-sponge."

Margaret smiled for the first time in many days. "Luke loves dried puddings dearly," she admitted. "I make them to his liking; 'tis for them he comes courting here." Suddenly, she turned crimson. "But if I thought he came after your son's wife—that is, the one who ought to be—I would soon put him to the door."

"Nay, nay; for Heaven's sake, let me not stir up mischief. Poor lad! Why, girl, fancy cannot be bridled. Bless you, I wormed it out of him nearly a year ago."

"Oh, Mother, and you allowed it?"

"Well, I thought of you. I reasoned to myself, 'If he is foolish enough to be her slave for nothing, all the better for her. A lone woman is lost without a man about her to fetch and carry her little matters.' But now my mind has changed, and I believe the best use you can put him to is to marry him."

"So then, his own mother is against him, ready to wed me to the first comer? Ah, Gerard, thou hast but me. I will not believe thee dead until I see thy tomb, nor false until I see thee holding another lover's hand. Foolish boy, I shall never be civil to him again."

Afflicted by the busybody's misguided protection, Luke Peterson received a cold reception in the household where he had previously found gentleness and kindness. Soon, finding himself barely spoken to, and eventually addressed sharply and irritably, the naturally soft fellow began to whimper. He approached Margaret directly, asking if he had done something to offend her.

"Nothing. I am to blame. I am cursed. If you will take my counsel, keep out of my way for a while," she replied.

"It is all because of me, Luke," said the meddler. "You, Mistress Catherine... why, what have I done for you to set her against me?"

"Nay, I meant all for the best. I told her I saw you looking toward her through a wedding ring, but she would not hear of it."

"There was no need to tell her that, wife; she knows I have been courting her this whole year."

"Not I," said Margaret. "Or I should never have opened the street door to you."

"Why, I come here every Saturday night. And that is how the lads in Rotterdam court. If we sup with a lass on Saturdays, that is the wooing."

"Oh, that is Rotterdam, is it? Then next time you come, let it be Thursday or Friday. For my part, I thought you came after my puddings, boy."

"I like your puddings well enough. You make them better than mother does. But I like you still better than the puddings," Luke said tenderly.

"Then you have seen the last of them. How dare you speak thus to another man's wife, especially when he is far away?"

She concluded gently, yet with firm resolve: "You need not trouble yourself to come here any more, Luke. I can carry my basket myself."

"Oh, very well," said Luke. After sitting silently and stupefied for a moment, he rose and said sadly to Catherine, "Dame, I daresay I have got the sack;" and walked out.

However, the following Saturday, Catherine found him seated on the doorstep, blubbering uncontrollably. He explained that he had grown accustomed to coming there, and every other place seemed strangely alien. She went inside and informed Margaret, who sighed and remarked, "Poor Luke; he might have succeeded if he knew his place and treated her like a married wife."

When this sentiment was conveyed to Luke, he hesitated.

"Pshaw!" scoffed Catherine. "Promises are mere pie-crusts. Promise her the whole world sooner than sit outside like a fool. If you simply humored her in everything, and then, should poor Gerard fail to return and claim her, you would surely have her—in time. A lone woman is ever to be worn down, thou foolish boy."
