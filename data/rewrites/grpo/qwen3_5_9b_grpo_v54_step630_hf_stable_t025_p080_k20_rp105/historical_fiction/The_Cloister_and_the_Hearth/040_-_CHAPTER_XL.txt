Margaret Van Eyck's heart remained as green and vigorous as her namesake, Margaret Brandt. Though forty years had passed, the old artist possessed a youthful ardour for the younger woman. This affection was no fleeting caprice; Margaret Van Eyck, sharp-eyed and discerning, recognized in Margaret Brandt a genuineness rare among women of her sex. Beyond this sterling quality, the younger woman held a powerful ally in the bosom of the elder, an ally rooted in the strange, immutable laws of human nature.

It is a curious truth, noted by Greek philosophers and immortalized by the British Homer, that our hearts warm more readily to those we have benefited than to our benefactors. The bard recalled how memories of shared battles, of shielding friends from arrows and bearing them to shore, swept away even the strongest purposes of revenge. Here, the assassin's hand was stayed not by gratitude for favors received, but by the memory of kindnesses conferred.

Margaret Van Eyck had indeed been wonderfully kind. She had broken her own habits to visit the sickbed, nursing and soothing Margaret Brandt until the recipient of her goodness loved her more tenderly than she had ever loved Gerard. Thus, when the old lady saw the roses blooming on Margaret's cheek and read the parchment that caused them, she surrendered her own plans without a murmur.

"Sweetheart," she said gently, "I desired he should stay in Italy five or six years, return rich, and become a master artist. But your happiness comes first. Since you cannot live without him, we must bring him home as soon as possible."

"Oh, madam! You see my very thoughts," the young woman replied, hanging her head and blushing. "But how shall I let him know? That passes my skill. He is gone to Italy, though I know not which city he visits. He mentioned Florence and Rome... but surely a letter addressed 'My Gerard—Italy' might miscarry?" She looked imploringly at her friend for counsel.

"You are come to the right place and at the right time," the old lady declared. "Hans Memling was with me today. He is going to Italy no later than next week, 'to improve his hand,' he says. Not before 'twas needed, I assure you."

"But how shall he find my Gerard?"

"Why, he knows your Gerard, child. They have supped here more than once; they are like hand and glove. Since his business is the same as Gerard's, he will visit the same places and must soon fall in with him. Get you a long letter written, copy this pardon into it, and I will answer for the messenger. In six months at farthest, Gerard shall receive it. When he does, he will kiss it, press it to his bosom, and fly home. What are you smiling at? And why are your cheeks so red? You are smothering me, I cannot think. Yes! Happy days are coming to my little pearl."

Meanwhile, in the kitchen, Martin sat before the black-jack while Reicht Heynes spun beside him. The night was spent pumping Martin for information, and the atmosphere grew heavy with anticipation.

Hans Memling, an old pupil of Jan Van Eyck and his sister, was a painter of considerable talent and a good soul, save for one fault: he loved the "nipperkin, canakin, and the brown bowl" more than they deserved. This penchant prevented him from amassing fortune and often led him to seek meals or a groat from Margaret Van Eyck. Yet, this dependence gave her a claim on him; she knew he would not trifle with any commission she entrusted to him.

The letter was duly written and left with the old artist. The following week, Hans Memling returned from Flanders. Margaret Van Eyck handed him the letter and a piece of gold for his travels. He seemed in a hurry to depart.

"All the better," said the old artist. "He will be the sooner in Italy."

But as horses that burn to start often require the whip after the first yard, so Hans's urgency cooled into inaction once he reached the principal hostelry of Tergou. There, he found two of his boon companions sitting in the bay window. He entered for a parting glass, but when he offered to pay, they refused. They insisted on treating him, for he was going on a long journey.

The result was that Hans's tongue became as loose as if the wine were oil. Confiding in his convivial crew, he boasted of showing the Italians how to paint, sang of his exploits in battle with the pike, and recounted his amorous successes. His speech flowed in a torrent of miscellaneous matters until, in a moment of drunken blabbering, he revealed he was entrusted with a letter to a townsman of theirs, one Gerard, a good fellow. He added, "You are all good fellows," and slapped Sybrandt on the back so heartily that he drove the breath from the man's body.

Sybrandt circled the table to avoid this muscular approval but listened intently, learning for the first time that Gerard had gone to Italy. To confirm it, he feigned doubt.

"My brother Gerard is never in Italy."

"Ye lie, ye cur!" roared Hans, instantly turning irascible and failing to recognize the man he had just praised as the very person he sought. "If he is ten times your brother, he is in Italy. What call ye this? There, read me that superscription!" He flung the letter onto the table.

Sybrandt examined it gravely but claimed he could not read. However, one of the company, boasting of a rare accomplishment, took the paper and read aloud:

"To Gerard Eliassoen, of Tergou. These by the hand of the trusty Hans Memling, with all speed."

"'Tis excellently well writ," said the reader, inspecting every letter.

"Ay!" Hans declared bombastically. "'Tis writ by a famous hand; by Margaret, sister of Jan Van Eyck. Blessed and honoured be his memory! She is an old friend of mine."

After diversifying into forty topics, Hans departed. Sybrandt stole out to find Cornelis. Together, they plotted. Italy was an immense distance; if they could only keep Gerard there, they might secure his share of their father's substance.

"Keep him there? Nothing would keep him long from his Margaret," Cornelis countered.

"Curse her!" Sybrandt snapped. "Why didn't she die when she was about it?"

"She die? She would outlive the pest to vex us," Cornelis replied, wroth at her selfishness.

These two black sheep, tainting each other with their corruption, conceived a plan to keep Gerard in Italy forever. Yet, when they had planned it, they were no nearer execution; iniquity required talent, and they lacked it. Then, as if Satan whispered simultaneously into their ears, they both burst out:

They went to Ghysbrecht Van Swieten, who received them eagerly. Suspense is intolerable; certainty, though painful, offers relief.

"You have news of Gerard?" he asked eagerly.

They told him of the letter and Hans Memling. Ghysbrecht listened with restless eyes. "Who wrote the letter?"

"Margaret Van Eyck," they replied, assuming the contents matched the superscription.

"Are ye sure?" Ghysbrecht asked, drawing forth a paper written by Margaret while negotiating for her house. "Was it writ like this?"

"Yes. 'Tis the same writing," Sybrandt said boldly.

"Good. And now, what would ye of me?" Ghysbrecht asked, his heart beating beneath a mask of feigned carelessness. The brothers fumbled with their bonnets, stammered, and hesitated, gradually revealing their desire for a letter to keep Gerard in Italy—a letter to substitute in Hans Memling's wallet.

While the brothers vacillated between respect for the burgomaster and suspicion of his own roguishness, pros and cons coursed through Ghysbrecht's spirit. Vengeance urged him to let Gerard return and feel the weight of the law; prudence advised keeping him a thousand miles away. But prudence also warned against dirty work on a doubtful chance, lest these rogues tarnish his name. Finally, convinced that Gerard possessed a secret that could wound him to the quick, Ghysbrecht found words: "It is my duty to aid the citizens that cannot write. But for their matter, I will not be responsible. Tell me, then, what I shall write."

"Something about this Margaret," Sybrandt suggested.

"Ay, ay! That she is false, that she is married to another, I'll go bail."

"Nay, burgomaster, nay! Not for all the world!" Sybrandt cried. "Gerard would not believe it, or but half, and then he would come back to see. No; say that she is dead."

"Dead! What, at her age, will he credit that?"

"Sooner than the other. Why she was nearly dead; so it is not to say a downright lie, after all."

"Humph! And you think that will keep him in Italy?"

"We are sure of it, are we not, Cornelis?"

"Ay," said Cornelis. "Our Gerard will never leave Italy now he is there. It was always his dream to get there. He would come back for his Margaret, but not for us. What cares he for us? He despises his own family; always did."

"This would be a bitter pill to him," said the old hypocrite.

"It will be for his good in the end," replied the young one.

"What avails Famine wedding Thirst?" Cornelis asked.

"And the grief you are preparing for him so coolly?" Ghysbrecht spoke sarcastically, yet tasting his own vengeance.

"Oh, a lie is not like a blow with a curtal axe. It hacks no flesh, and breaks no bones."

"A curtal axe?" Sybrandt asked, shooting a sly, envenomed glance at the burgomaster's broken nose. "No, nor even like a stroke with a cudgel."

Ghysbrecht's face darkened with ire when the adder's tongue struck his wound. But it told, as intended; the old man bristled with hate.

"Well," he said, "tell me what to write for you, and I must write it; but take notice, you bear the blame if aught turns amiss. Not the hand which writes, but the tongue which dictates, doth the deed."

The brothers assented warmly, sneering within. Ghysbrecht drew his inkhorn, laid the specimen of Margaret's writing before him, and made inquiries regarding the size and shape of the letter. Just then, an unlooked-for interruption occurred. Jorian Ketel burst hastily into the room, looking vexed at not finding him alone.

"Thou seest I have matter on hand, good fellow," Ghysbrecht said.

"Ay; but this is grave. I bring good news; but 'tis not for every ear."

The burgomaster rose and drew Jorian aside into the embrasure of his deep window. The brothers heard them converse in low but eager tones. It ended with Ghysbrecht sending Jorian out to saddle his mule. He then addressed the brothers with a sudden coldness that amazed them.

"I prize the peace of households; but this is not a thing to be done in a hurry: we will see about it, we will see."

"But, burgomaster, the man will be gone. It will be too late."

"Where is he?"

"At the hostelry, drinking."

"Well, keep him drinking! We will see, we will see." And he sent them off discomfited.

To explain this, we must retrograde a step. That very morning, Margaret Brandt had met Jorian Ketel near her own door. He passed her with a scowl, which struck her.

"Stay," she said. "Yes! It is the good man who saved him. Oh! Why have you not been near me since? And why have you not come for the parchments? Was it not true about the hundred crowns?"

Jorian gave a snort but, seeing her candid face, began to think there might be a mistake. He told her he had come and how he had been received.

"Alas!" she said. "I knew nought of this. I lay at Death's door. She then invited him to follow her, took him into the garden, and showed him the spot where the parchments were buried. Martin was for taking them up, but I would not let him. He put them there; and I said none should move them but you, who had earned them so well of him and me."

"Give me a spade!" Jorian cried eagerly. "But stay! No; he is a suspicious man. You are sure they are there still?"

"I will openly take the blame if human hand hath touched them."

"Then keep them but two hours more, I prithee, good Margaret," said Jorian, and ran off to the Stadthouse of Tergou a joyful man.

The burgomaster jogged along towards Sevenbergen with Jorian striding beside him, giving assurance that the missing parchments would be in his hand within an hour.

"Ah, master!" Jorian said. "Lucky for us it wasn't a thief that took them."

"Not a thief? Not a thief? What call you him, then?"

"Well, saving your presence, I call him a jackdaw. This is jackdaw's work, if ever there was: 'take the thing you are least in need of, and hide it'—that's a jackdaw. I should know," Jorian added oracularly, "for I was brought up along with a chough. He and I were born the same year, but he cut his teeth long before me, and wow! but my life was a burden for years all along of him.
