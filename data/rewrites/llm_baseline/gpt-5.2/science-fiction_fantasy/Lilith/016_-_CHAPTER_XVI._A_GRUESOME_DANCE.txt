I rose to go on, and my feet took up again the long, dry miles as if they had been trained for nothing else. The land lay spread like a board that had once been a table, and had been left uncovered until every crumb of comfort had been carried off by wind: level, pale, and fasting. More than hunger for food, I hungered for a height—some ridge, some boulder, any upthrust of stone from which the eye might take in a shape of hope, the way a cook lifts a lid for the first smell that tells him the broth has begun to speak. But what would sight have given me? In this country it was not what lay ahead that governed what would meet me; it was what lay inside me, working in the dark like yeast—silent, unseen, yet making the whole mass rise or collapse.

The sun sank toward the rim, and at last I saw, before me, a rough climb of rock—broken steps and splintered ledges, as if the world had once tried to build a stairway and then forgotten why. Yet when I neared it, my desire to climb was already spent, and I wanted only to lie down. The air thickened with dusk. At my feet spread a carpet of moss—soft, deep, springing under the palm like well-proofed dough. I dropped upon it, and at once the weariness began to run out of me, because the moment my head lay low, I heard it again—the sound I had heard twice before: many waters, far below, moving where no water showed itself.

They played in buried channels, and the stones were their instruments. It was not one tune but a tangle: a gurgle returning like a refrain, a bell-like drip that answered and contradicted, little rushes that broke and rejoined, as if some hidden hand were pouring from one vessel to another in a rhythm too ancient to be learned and too intimate to be written. In my other life there had been machines that screamed as they did their work, and men who called it progress; here the world laboured in secret, and the labour made music. The sound was the opposite of that modern din that bruises the mind—this was an acoustic mercy, a hush that did not silence but soothed. Had any master of sound been near—any Händel of waters—what might he not have woven from that chaos of pure material? Yet it was already complete, because it was alive.

Lying there, I let my eyes wander the cliff above me. Its face held the record of a cataract that had once flung itself down, age after age, filling the very channels I had followed. I could almost taste the old spray on my tongue—mineral and cold—and smell the green wetness that must have clung to every rock when the fall was young. My heart swelled at the thought of that splendid tumult: waves dancing because they could not help but fall, gathering all their scattered noises into one vast organ-roar below. Then the hidden brooks, faithful as a lullaby sung in a mother’s own dialect, drew me under, and their water-speech flowed into my dreaming.

Before the sun, I woke and climbed eagerly, as if height might finally give me a name for what I was doing. But beyond the ridge there was nothing—nothing but a desert of finest sand, sifted and level as flour, dead-white where the first light struck it. Not a trace remained of the river that had plunged here. The powdery drift had filled its course until the old bed was indistinguishable from the blank expanse.

Looking back, I saw how the river had once split as it fell—two branches, one whose bank I had followed to this scaur, the other I had first crossed on my way toward the Evil Wood. Between the two, far off, I could just make out the wood itself, a dark band on the horizon like a remembered word that refuses translation. Before me and to the left, sand ran out beyond my vision; but far to the right the sky-line lifted a little, and the faint rise gave me the only sort of direction this land granted—a hint, not a promise—toward the forest my strange hostess had named.

I sat down and reached for the half-loaf I had brought. Only then did I understand what she had meant. The bread was not for tomorrow. It had shrunk and hardened to a stone, as if time here baked without heat and with no pity. I turned it in my hands—its crust no longer a crust but a shell—and the smell of it was gone. There was no comfort in it, no memory of grain-fields or ovens. I flung it away and went on.

Toward noon the land offered a few tamarisk and juniper, then stunted firs with needles thin as old broom-straw. A little farther and the thickets thickened, and the firs rose taller, and soon I was within a forest of pines and other trees like that in which the Little Ones had found their babies. For a moment I thought I had circled back into the same place, only farther along its edge; but in this country WHERE meant little when EVERYWHERE tasted like NOWHERE. I had not yet turned any patch of ground into a place by doing something in it. I was still not alive; I was only watching myself exist, as a food-historian might catalogue a dish he never eats, describing texture without letting it touch his tongue.

I told myself that if I caught even the faint gleam of the mirror again, I would turn aside at once. I would not be trapped back into my former life, where I had lived among words as among tins on a pantry shelf—labelled, orderly, and unopened. Here, perhaps, I might learn to be something by making something, by serving, by sharing—by any act that left a taste behind it.

I went deeper into the wood, weary enough to rest. The trees were large now, set in a regular fashion, almost geometric, with wide aisles between. There was little undergrowth; the sight ran far in every direction. The forest felt like a great church—solemn, silent, and empty. Nothing on two feet or four came near me that day. Once or twice a swift thing crossed a distant space, and again a slow thing, but always far off, as if even the animals kept their own counsel and would not spend breath on company. I heard a few birds. Butterflies drifted—some gaudy as spilled spice, others so white they startled the eye like a flash of salt.

Then I reached a spot where the pines stood farther apart and flowering shrubs had room, and—because hunger is not only of the stomach—I took the direction where more roses grew. I was hungry for the voice and face of my kind, for any living soul, human or not, whose being I might in some measure understand. What a hell it would be, I thought, to wander alone forever: a bare existence never stepping out of itself, never widening by entering another life, but bound in the cords of its own peculiarities like a prisoner with his own chains for companions. Only among others can one live for oneself at all—and only there can one become so fearfully selfish. Evil, I began to see, can only feed on good. Selfishness is a parasite; it cannot root where there is no tree of life.

In my own world I had sung when I was alone, not thinking about it—little tunes under my breath. Here no crooning murmur ever left my lips. There I had sung without thinking; here I thought without singing. There I had never had a bosom-friend; here the affection of an idiot would have been a sacrament. “If only I had a dog to love!” I sighed, and wondered at the man I had been, who preferred book or pen to a breathing creature; who, if the author of a tale he enjoyed had entered the room, would have wished him gone so the story could go on without the intrusion of its maker. I had chosen the dead over the living; the thing thought over the thing thinking. “Any man,” I said now, “is more than the greatest of books.”

The wood grew richer. Pines thickened into pillars, huge stems rising as if eager to hold up the sky. Other trees appeared. The roses were no longer bushes but trees, and their blossoms had a splendour that seemed too deliberate to be mere accident—like a recipe perfected over generations, passed down in a language that only the hands fully understand. Suddenly I spied what looked like a great house or castle; yet its outline was strangely indistinct, as if made of hesitating air. From a distance its forms held together; nearer, they did not grow more definite, as though the closer I came, the more the thing refused to be translated into certainty. When at last I stood before it, I was no less doubtful than at first. Habitable it certainly was not; perhaps a ruin overgrown with ivy and roses. Yet of stone or timber, not the poorest remnant of wall could I find. Again and again I thought I saw a corner, a lintel, a buttress; always, at the moment of inspection, it vanished into leaf and vine.

It came to me that the ivy might have embraced some vast building and consumed it, and that its interlaced branches had kept, like a mould, the memory of the walls it had devoured. Memory in this world was a strange thing—kept not in words, but in shapes, in scents, in sounds. Before me yawned a rectangular vacancy: the ghost of a doorway without a door. I stepped through, and found myself in an open space like a great hall. Its floor was grass and flowers; its walls and roof were ivy and vine mingled with roses. If one were to sleep anywhere, this was a place made for it: a green room that had eaten its own architecture.

I gathered withered leaves and laid them in a corner for a bed. The air held the warmth of a day that had not yet learned to be cold. I lay down, and a red sunset filled the hall as if poured in like wine. Above me the living ceiling made tracery against the fading sky—branches, twigs, clouds of foliage, and peeping patches where the higher roof opened. My eyes wandered in it as if caught and gently held. When the sun was gone and the dark began to thicken, the red roses turned black; then only yellow and white showed, pale as butter and cream in dim light. When those too vanished, the stars came instead, hanging among the leaves like jewels—topazes, opals, sparks of colour. It was as if I lay under a tree from Aladdin’s cave, except the treasure was light and breath.

Then I saw the roof was full of nests. Tiny heads, almost indistinguishable, popped out with a chirp or two, and vanished again. There were rustlings and small stirrings—little domestic noises, the soft economy of a nursery settling. I listened as one listens to a kitchen at night when the last embers shift: not loud, but full of life. Gradually each feathered mother had her brood tucked under her wings; the small talk in the little beds ceased; and God’s bird-nursery lay quiet beneath the waves of sleep.

Once more a flutter made me look up. An owl sailed across. I caught only a glimpse, but I felt the cool waft of his silent wings several times, like a hand passing over a candle without putting it out. The mother birds did not move. They knew he hunted mice, not children.

About midnight I woke wide awake, roused by revelry. The noise was not loud, yet it was close—thin, attenuated, like music heard through layers of cloth. For a moment my eyes were dazzled, as if the dark had been replaced by a brightness my sight could not at once receive. Then they steadied, and I found I was no longer looking at ivy walls and nests, but at a splendid hall, and my heap of withered leaves lay in a corner of it as if it had always belonged there.

Before me thronged a crowd—men in gorgeous dress, women in robes that fell and floated with an elegance belonging to another age. None of them appeared to see me. Dance followed dance, each vaguely embodying the story of life—meeting and courting, passion and pride, parting and regret—made visible by steps and turns. In the world I had left, I had studied Shakespeare enough to have learned something of the dances he names; so I recognised, or half-recognised, the minuet, the pavin, the hey, the coranto, the lavolta. Their fashions matched their measures—antique as memory, formal as ritual.

Moonlight had risen while I slept, shining down through a roof that seemed filled with countless windows. Yet the light was broken by so many shadows that at first I could scarcely make out faces. Still, something was odd. I sat up, peering harder—then the truth struck like a cold mouthful swallowed unawares.

Faces? They were skull fronts.

Hard bone gleamed where flesh should have softened. Bare jaws worked without lips. Noses were truncated; teeth stood exposed, incapable of smile. Some skulls flashed set and white and murderous; others were clouded with decay, broken, gapped, stained with the earth in which they seemed to have lain. Worse than all, the eye-sockets were not empty. In each socket lived a lidless eye.

In those wrecks of faces eyes glowed, flashed, sparkled—eyes of every colour and expression. A proud dark eye, lustrous and condescending, was more terrible than a dull one. A lovely languishing eye was more repulsive than any glare. Yet the dim sad eyes—less at variance with their setting—were sad beyond telling, and drew my heart toward them in spite of the horror framing their gaze, as if grief itself spoke a dialect the dead still remembered.

I rose and walked among them, eager to understand what I was seeing. Were these souls? Or were they only phantasms—motions left behind, repeating like a tune whose words have been forgotten? By no look, no gesture, not the slightest break in measure did they show awareness of me. I was not present to them. But were they present to one another? Surely they must see their companions as I saw them: death dancing with living eyes. Or did each only dream itself and the rest?

Had they once used their faces not to communicate, not to utter thought and feeling, not to share existence with their neighbours, but to appear what they wished and hide what they were—turning face into mask? And having made masks of their faces, were they now deprived of them, condemned to go without faces until they repented? How long must such punishment endure—this flaunting of facelessness in eyes that cannot close? Had shame begun at last to do its quiet work? Had any of them yielded?

No word was spoken. No naked mouth moved. Perhaps they were bereft of speech because they had lied. Yet with their eyes they seemed to speak, longing to be understood. Was it truth or falsehood that shone there? They appeared to know one another. Did one skull seem beautiful to them, another plain? Difference must remain, and they had had long study of skulls.

When a dancer came close to me, the body passed through mine as through a thin curtain. I could tell, without seeing, whether a man or a woman had gone through my house. On many skulls the hair still clung, and however lovely in itself it might have been, it looked frightful on the bare forehead and temples. Sometimes an outer ear remained also, and from its tip hung a jewel—pearl, opal, diamond—glimmering under brown or raven locks, under golden ripples, under pale fluffy cirri like cloud-threads. The hair lay lichenous upon ivory-white or damp-yellow bone.

I looked down and saw daintily domed insteps. I looked up and saw plump shoulders carrying the spring of a round full neck—only for that neck to wither halfway into the fluted shaft of a gibbous cranium. The sensual grace of the body went on performing its old art, while the head proclaimed what the body would not admit.

The music grew wilder. The dance sped faster and faster. Eyes flared, jewels twinkled, throwing colour and fire upon pallid grins that glided through the hall. The crowd wove a ghastly rhythmic cloth of motion—intricate, multitudinous. Then, sudden as a knife laid down, came a pause. Every eye turned to one spot.

In the doorway stood a woman.

She was perfect in form and bearing and hue—alive in a way that made the others seem not merely dead but forbidden. She regarded the company as from the pedestal of a goddess, while the dancers stood frozen, “like one forbid,” held still by the vision of life that killed. Her scornful glance said without words, Dead things, I live.

Then, like leaves when a wind wakes in them, they turned each to another and broke again into motion—melodious, consorted—yet changed. Their eyes, late solitary, now filled with a common triumph, spoke as plainly as shouting. Thou also, they seemed to say, wilt soon become weak as we. Thou wilt soon become like unto us.

I turned back to the woman and saw, upon her side, a small dark shadow—as if something had touched her that did not belong to the living. She had seen the change in the dead stare; she looked down; she understood the talking eyes. She pressed both her lovely hands upon the shadow, gave a smothered cry, and fled.

The birds rustled in their nests. A flash of joy lit the eyes of the dancers. Then a warm wind swept through the place, growing in strength as it went, and it blew out every light. The low moon still glimmered on the horizon with sick attempt to shine, and a turbid radiance still leaked from so many eyes that I could see what followed.

As if each shape had been a snow-image warmed too quickly, it began to fall to pieces. Flesh peeled from bone in papery flakes, dropping like soiled snow from under garments. Rags and strips fluttered down, littering the floor. The white skeleton emerged from garment and flesh together, bare and lank amid the decay.

A faint rattling shiver ran through the naked company. Pair after pair, the lamping eyes went out. Darkness thickened around me with a loneliness that had weight.

For a moment the leaves—or what looked like leaves—still swept fluttering all one way. Then the wind ceased. The owl floated silent through the silent night.

Not for a moment had I been afraid. Whoever would cross the threshold of any world must leave fear behind; but I could claim no virtue in its absence. No conscious courage worked in me. Simply, I was not afraid. I did not know why. I did not know what fear would have been for. I feared not even fear—which of all dangers is the most dangerous.

I went out into the wood at once to resume my journey. Another moon was rising, and I turned my face toward it.
