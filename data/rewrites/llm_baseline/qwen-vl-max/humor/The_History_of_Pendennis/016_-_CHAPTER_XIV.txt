CHAPTER XIV. In Which Miss Fotheringay Lands a London Gig—And Poor Pen Finds Himself Left Behind

It was not long after the events previously recounted that Mr. Manager Bingley, in all his theatrical glory, took to the stage of Chatteris’s most illustrious theatre (which, for the sake of discretion and to avoid offending any local susceptibilities, we shall hereafter refer to as "the Museum") to perform the role of Rolla in *Pizarro*. The audience, however, was so thin it looked as though the play had been advertised as a fire drill rather than a drama. There were more empty seats than spectators, and poor Arthur Pendennis found himself occupying nearly the entire box section, like a lone sentry guarding a desert fort.

He sat there, eyes bloodshot from too much love and too little sleep, leaning over the railing like a man who’d just escaped from a sanitarium and hadn’t yet decided whether to return. He stared fixedly at the stage, where the world spun on in its usual fashion—Spaniards marched, Peruvians fell, priests debated fate, virgins of the sun blinked dramatically—but none of it registered. All he saw was Cora Fotheringay. When she wasn’t on stage, he saw nothing at all. Not even the sky, or the moon, or the fact that he might be developing a permanent hunchback from hunching forward in despair.

Later, he confessed that he was amazed he didn’t pull out a pistol and shoot her on the spot. “I was mad,” he said, “with love, rage, and the kind of despair only a young man with no prospects can truly feel.” Had it not been for his mother—the quiet, watchful, never-asking-questions sort of woman who somehow knew everything without being told—he might have ended up behind bars, or worse, hanging from the gallows outside Chatteris jail, leaving behind a single, half-written letter addressed to “My Beloved Cora.”

But no. He merely sat. And stared. And suffered.

Then, like a bolt of lightning through a dull evening, Cora entered. She was radiant—dressed in white, draped in leopard skin, wearing a golden sun across her chest, and adorned with bracelets so flashy they could have blinded a lesser mortal. She delivered her lines with the precision of a well-oiled machine, but her eyes—those same eyes that had once shattered Pendennis’s soul—rolled about the stage like twin moons, unbothered by anyone in particular.

Not him. Not even close.

And yet, he didn’t notice the two gentlemen who had just arrived in Box No. 3—nor did he realize that the entire cast had suddenly transformed into a group of desperate performers trying to impress someone far more important than the audience.

The first act had been a disaster. The actors yawned through their lines, whispered insults during pauses, and generally behaved as though they were rehearsing for a funeral. Even Bingley seemed bored. Mrs. Bingley muttered under her breath like a disgruntled housewife. Then—*boom*—everything changed.

Mrs. Bingley began bellowing like a bull elephant caught in a thunderstorm. Bingley leapt about the stage, screaming like a startled dean. Garbetts swaggered. Rowkins scowled. Miss Rouncy flounced. Everyone, it seemed, was suddenly trying to win the favor of the mysterious pair in Box No. 3.

One was a small, grey-haired man in black, with a face that suggested he’d once been a butcher and still liked to make jokes about meat. The other was a spectacle: tall, portly, with a nose like a hook and hair that curled like a lion’s mane. His coat was braided with gold frogs, his waistcoat layered like a sandwich of wealth, and his fingers glittered with rings that probably cost more than your average country parson’s annual salary. He carried a yellow silk handkerchief that, when pulled from his pocket, released a cloud of musk and bergamot so strong it made the entire theatre smell like a perfumery run by a duchess.

This, of course, was Mr. Dolphin—the great London manager, impresario, and theatrical titan. And beside him, like a loyal shadow, was his secretary, Mr. William Minns, who never left his side unless absolutely necessary—such as when he needed to go to the bathroom, which he did rarely and with great ceremony.

Within ten minutes of their arrival, the entire company had become aware of the visitor’s presence. They didn’t just act—they *performed*, as if auditioning for the throne of the dramatic arts.

Even Miss Fotheringay, whose heart was famously impervious to emotion, seemed to flutter slightly. Her part required little more than standing beautifully while holding a child (Master Talma Bingley, a toddler of unusually large proportions), but she executed it with such grace that one could almost believe she cared.

Still, the crowd remained silent. Pizarro received no applause. Bingley yelled. Mrs. Bingley roared. But Mr. Dolphin merely took snuff from a golden box the size of a shoe, sniffing with the solemnity of a judge.

Then came the final scene: Rolla staggers in with the infant, Cora rushes forward, shrieks, “O God, there’s blood upon him!”—and at last, the great man clapped.

“Bravo!” he thundered, slapping his secretary on the back. “By Jove, Billy, she’ll do!”

“Who taught her that dodge?” asked old Billy, a man whose sarcasm was as sharp as a dagger.

“I remember her at the Olympic,” he continued, “and I swear she couldn’t say ‘Bo’ to a goose.”

That, of course, was Mr. Bows, the orchestra’s little maestro, who had secretly coached her in the art of the dramatic cry. The entire company heard the applause. As the curtain fell, they swarmed around Cora, congratulating her—and also, quietly, hating her.

So how did Mr. Dolphin end up in this remote provincial theatre? Simple: he was broke.

Despite all his grandiose posters promising “Triumphs of Genius! Explosions of Talent! The Glories of English Drama!” his London theatre—the Museum Theatre, let us call it—was failing. The great Hubbard had played twenty nights of Shakespeare and failed to earn enough to buy a decent meal. The Cawdors, famed for their performances, drew crowds of exactly three people. Herr Garbage’s lions bit him. The Lyrical Drama, featuring Monsieur Poumons and an orchestra larger than a small army, nearly bankrupted Dolphin before it even opened.

So now he was scraping by on half-salaries, old comedies, and ballets performed by dancers who clearly hadn’t eaten in days.

Enter Lord Steyne—the Marquis of Steyne, noble patron of the arts, occasional statesman, and man who preferred to watch ballet from behind a curtain, like a king hiding from his own responsibilities.

He often attended the Museum Theatre, though usually late, arriving just in time for the dancing. He sat modestly behind a velvet screen, invisible to the audience, but unmistakable to the performers. Why? Because every dancer, every coryphée, every pirouetting nymph, would glance toward his box as if it contained the source of all beauty and power.

Once, during the Palm Dance in *Cook at Otaheite*, a hundred-twenty female savages in leafy costumes danced in perfect unison, all eyes fixed on the proscenium box. One misstep, and the whole thing would’ve collapsed. Yet Mademoiselle Sauterelle, mid-air, managed to wink at Lord Steyne before landing gracefully—like a cat who knows it’s being watched.

On this particular night, Steyne was surrounded by friends—Wenham, Wagg, and others—who were laughing so loudly that the pit demanded silence. “Shut up!” shouted a voice. “You’re ruining the atmosphere!”

But Steyne paid no mind. He was watching something else.

Wenham read aloud from a letter from Major Pendennis, who had been absent from London society for weeks—much to everyone’s dismay.

> “Dear Wenham,
> As you’ve had my character in your hands for the past three weeks and doubtless torn me to shreds, I think you can afford to be good-natured and help me. It’s a delicate matter, entre nous—une affaire de coeur. A young friend of mine is madly in love with Miss Fotheringay, actress at Chatteris. She’s as handsome as sin and better than any leading lady we’ve got. I want her in London. Can you get Dolphin to come see her? A word from a noble friend (you know what I mean) would be invaluable. If you can use Gaunt House influence—I promise anything in return. Do this for me, dear fellow. Yours truly, A. Pendennis.”

“Ah,” said Wenham, folding the letter. “Old Pendennis is in love.”

“And wants to get her out of Chatteris,” added Wagg. “I’d like to see him on his knees with rheumatism.”

“Or offering her a lock of his hair,” said Wagg again, grinning.

“Stuff,” said Steyne. “He has a nephew, doesn’t he? Probably the nephew’s affair. Young men get themselves into scrapes. I was once—when I was in the fifth form at Eton—a market-gardener’s daughter. Swore I’d marry her. I was mad about her—poor Polly!” He paused. For a moment, the boy George Gaunt seemed to rise from the past. “But she must be fine, from Pendennis’s description. Bring in Dolphin. Let’s see what he says.”

Wenham sprang from the box and hurried backstage. Dolphin was busy yelling at a ballerina for not keeping her toes pointed. “You’re supposed to be a star, not a lump of coal!” he roared.

But when Wenham appeared, the curses died instantly. Dolphin dropped his fist, bowed, and grasped the ambassador’s hand.

“How do, Mr. Wenham? How’s his lordship tonight? Looks uncommonly well,” he said, smoothing his wig like a man who’d just survived a crisis.

And so the visit to Chatteris was arranged. Dolphin wrote to Steyne, informing him that he had seen Miss Fotheringay, was “deeply impressed” by both her talent and appearance, and had already engaged her for London.

Pendennis read the announcement in the *Chatteris Chronicle*, where the editor praised her “unparalleled charm and consummate artistry,” predicting a brilliant career in the capital.

Bingley immediately began advertising: **“The Last Night of Miss Fotheringay’s Engagement!”**

Poor Pen and Sir Derby Oaks attended every performance. Sir Derby threw flowers like a romantic fool, receiving glances like a prize-winning dog. Pendennis, meanwhile, sat in the near-empty boxes, gaunt and hollow-eyed, as if he’d been abandoned by both love and sanity.

Only one other person seemed to care: Mr. Bows, the orchestra leader. One evening, he left his post, walked to Pendennis’s box, and offered his hand.

“Come walk with me,” he said.

They strolled down the street, then sat on Chatteris Bridge under the moonlight, talking about her.

“We may sit on the same bridge,” said Bows, “but we’ve been in the same boat for years. You’re not the only one who’s made a fool of himself over her. And I have less excuse—I’m older, I know her better. She has no heart. Not one. She’s colder than this stone you’re leaning on. She wouldn’t care if you drowned. Or if I did. Or if the bridge itself collapsed.”

“But she cares for you,” said Pendennis.

“She does,” admitted Bows, “because she needs me. I teach her. Without me, she’d be lost. She’ll send for me when she gets to London. She always does. But she won’t do it out of affection. Out of necessity.”

He flicked the end of his cigar into the river. It sparked, then vanished.

“And when she marries,” he continued, “she’ll throw me over—just like I’ll throw this cigar butt away.”

Pendennis rode home that night, thinking—not of himself, not of his broken heart—but of something else. Something bigger. Something… human.

And for the first time in weeks, he felt less alone.
