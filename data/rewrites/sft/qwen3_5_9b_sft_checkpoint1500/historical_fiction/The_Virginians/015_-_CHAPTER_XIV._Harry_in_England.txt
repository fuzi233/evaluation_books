When Queen Dido had bade the wandering Trojans recount their labors and terrors, she gave ear with a rapt attention that turned her royal heart to stone for the sorrow of others. Now, little Harry Warrington sat before his elderly Aunt Beatrix, telling his tale with a simplicity that lacked the tragic grandeur of Aeneas, yet possessed a warm, artless charm that pierced the hard shell of his listener. Mrs. Bernstein, a woman whose life had been a vast, arid plain of solitude, found her heart beating with a strange, unfamiliar rhythm as the boy spoke. To her, his stories were not merely entertainment; they were the sweet, refreshing waters of a fountain she had forgotten existed in her desert existence. She looked at him with a tenderness she had never known, a soft, flushed joy rising in her cheeks that she quickly tried to mask with a stern countenance. Yet, within, a dark voice whispered warnings: *Wait, Beatrix,* it scoffed. *You will tire of this boy as you have tired of everything else. He may perish, and you will find no tear for him.* And yet, she answered the whisper with a smile, deciding to seize the shade under the tree while it lasted, knowing that tomorrow would demand another march into the wilderness.

As Harry continued, weaving tales of his mother and her past, Aunt Beatrix listened with a mixture of amusement and maternal pride. She noted the echoes of the Esmond bloodline in his words—the gentle courtesy inherited from Colonel Washington, whom Harry described not with the fiery passion some expected, but with a quiet, steady friendship. "My mother vowed a thousand times she held no such feelings," Harry admitted, a blush creeping to his face as he recalled his own youthful, absurd jealousy of the Colonel. "But I fear my half-sister will never forgive him," Beatrix mused, her eyes twinkling. "And surely, sir, you do not blame women for finding affection for a younger kin?" she teased gently. "Do I not dote upon you? And do not the ladies of Castlewood all envy me for this favor?"

Despite the underlying currents of jealousy within the household, the surface remained smooth as glass. Within days of his arrival, the formal "Cousin" became simply "Harry" to the young and old alike. The Countess of Castlewood, in particular, showered him with gracious attention, praising his manners and marveling at how a boy raised in the wilds of Virginia could possess such polish. To the Countess, this elegance was proof of the Colonel's enduring influence; to the Baroness, it was evidence of a noble upbringing passed down through the blood. They exchanged glances filled with unspoken rivalry and mutual admiration, a silent dance of wit and flattery where compliments served as the sharpest weapons and the deepest insults were hidden behind smiles. "How exquisite!" the Countess would declare regarding Harry's grace, while the Baroness countered with praises of his natural warmth, each seeking to prove that their version of truth was the superior one.

Yet, behind the scenes, a different reality unfolded. Through the gossip of servants and the casual eavesdropping of a maid, the Baroness learned the true nature of the Countess's behavior. Later, when the young people gathered in the drawing room, the Countess entered, her demeanor shifting instantly from public benevolence to private venom. As Harry and Lady Fanny danced a minuet, guided by the latter's enthusiastic instruction, the Countess watched with a cruel delight. When William Esmond entered, boots still caked with mud from the kennels, the atmosphere curdled. "Is he teaching Fanny the war-dance?" William sneered, his temper flaring. "Does she intend to try her tricks upon this brute?" His mother's glance, sharp and stinging, confirmed his intent.

Fanny, caught between the pressure of her mother's disdain and her own fascination with Harry, curtsied deeply, causing Harry to stumble slightly in his step and turn crimson. The music faltered as Lady Maria stepped away from the harpsichord, leaving the pair to stand in an awkward silence. The Countess, feigning concern, seated herself again at the instrument, playing a lively tune to cover the tension. "Do not let me spoil the sport," she cooed, urging Fanny to continue. "Teach him properly, dearest!"

"I—I know not how to dance," Harry stammered, his shame evident. "Perhaps when I have learned the steps, I may ask my cousin for a walk."

"That shall happen soon, Cousin Warrington," the Countess assured, her voice dripping with honeyed malice.

Outside the room, the Countess's voice dropped to a harsh, cutting register as she dragged her daughter aside into the tapestry-lined corridor. "You booby! You double fool!" she hissed, shaking Fanny violently. "What mischief are you hatching with that Huron? Do you wish to marry a swine, a squaw, and end up a wife in a wigwam?" She mocked the idea of Harry's lineage, reducing his prospects to a swamp, a cabin, and a barrel of tobacco. "Remember your rank, Frances Esmond!" she screamed, forcing her daughter to recall the weight of her birthright. "Before the world, you must act civil enough to keep appearances, but in private, I forbid you to set foot near him or encourage him!"

"But he is our cousin!" Fanny pleaded, her voice trembling. "And Madame de Bernstein says so!"

"Bah!" the Countess dismissed. "Let her like what she will. She takes up a thing today and discards it tomorrow. In private, you obey me. If I cannot bring you to reason, your brother shall."

The conversation ended abruptly, the informants silenced, but the seeds of discord were sown. Only later did the Baroness reveal fragments of this secret struggle to Harry. For now, he perceived only the outward civility of his hosts. Lord Castlewood remained cool and reserved, indistinguishable from his treatment of others, while William and the ladies offered drinks, rides, and companionship. He accepted their kindness as the natural affection of kindred spirits, blind to the fact that he was being courted not out of genuine love, but as a strategic pawn in a web of familial intrigue and ancient grievances. He drank from their cups and walked beside their hands, unaware that he was walking a path paved with traps, serving schemes he could not yet see and creating angers he did not understand.
