Over steaming cups of tea, they revisited the turmoil that had been plaguing Marianne since the arrival of Willoughby's letter. Elinor offered words of comfort and reassurance, her steady conviction a beacon of calm amidst Marianne's tempestuous feelings.

As they spoke, Marianne's thoughts oscillated between the possibility of Willoughby's innocence and the overwhelming sense of despair that threatened to consume her. One moment she envisioned him as a wronged and penitent suitor, eager to make amends; the next, the cruel reality of his betrayal crushed her spirits. Her emotions swung wildly, like a pendulum, as she struggled to reconcile the conflicting desires to forgive and to punish.

In one matter, however, Marianne's resolve remained unwavering: she was resolute in her determination to avoid Mrs. Jennings whenever possible, and to maintain a stony silence when forced to endure her presence. Her heart hardened against the idea that Mrs. Jennings could genuinely understand her sorrow, convinced that her friend's kindness was merely a shallow façade, a mask for her insatiable appetite for gossip.

"No, no, no," Marianne exclaimed, her voice trembling with anguish. "She cannot truly feel. Her benevolence is a hollow shell, devoid of genuine empathy. She only likes me because I provide her with the latest whispers and scandals."

Elinor had long recognized the tendency of Marianne's sensitive nature to misjudge others, to attribute their motivations solely to their immediate effects on her own emotions. Like many people, Marianne tended to overlook the complexities of the human experience, expecting others to share her own refined sensibilities and polished manners. This inflexibility of spirit often led her to judge others harshly, to assume the worst of their intentions without considering the nuances of their actions.
