“The daughter of debate,
That discord still doth sow,
Shall reap no gain where former rule
Hath taught still peace to grow.
No foreign banish'd wight
Shall anker in this port;
Our realm it brooks no stranger's force;
Let them elsewhere resort.”

Amyas Leigh had returned home and settled into a quiet life, content for the time being with the peace of his hearth and the company of those he loved. For the next twelve months, little stirred in Northam that merited record—save the slow unfolding of a young soul caught between two worlds.

Yeo, once a rough sea-dog, now served as major domo in name only, though his presence was constant and watchful. He followed Amyas like a silent shadow, a lank gray hound at his heels. In the evenings, he sat by the fire, Bible open on his knee, hands busy fashioning trinkets—useful and useless alike—for every member of the household. Most often, they were for Ayacanora, whom he treated with a mixture of reverence and gentle mockery, offering her toys too childish for her dignity. She would pout, Mrs. Leigh would chide, and yet she would take the gift, tuck it away, and never look at it again. Her heart was not in playthings, but in becoming English.

She ran after Mrs. Leigh all day long, eager to learn the secrets of the kitchen, the still-room, the art of sewing—not just for herself, but for everyone in Northam. First, she wanted to be a good housewife, like Mrs. Leigh. Then came a deeper idea: helping others. The boundless generosity of her own people had been part of her nature, but giving without expectation of return—this was new. She watched Mrs. Leigh spend her free hours mending clothes, visiting the poor, bringing food and comfort to those who had nothing. She saw Amyas, after a public thanksgiving in church for his safe return, distribute money, grain, and provisions across Northam, Appledore, and Bideford. He bought cottages and turned them into almshouses for aged sailors, calling it a thank-offering to God.

Ayacanora was puzzled. Her idea of sacrifice had been shaped by the Aztec rites—blood and bones offered to the gods. But when Mrs. Leigh explained gently that “whoever gives to the poor, gives to the Great Spirit,” because the Great Spirit dwelled in them, Ayacanora listened. And she believed. From that moment, she worked tirelessly for the old women of Northam, sewed for the needy, walked beside Mrs. Leigh with a quiet grace that made her beloved by all. Sometimes, beneath her long lashes, she would glance toward Amyas, hoping to catch a smile. When she did, she was sweet and obedient all day. When she didn’t, she became stubborn, willful, and hard to manage—even for Mrs. Leigh’s patient spirit.

But one thing she could not control: her dress. No amount of pleading could keep her from sweeping into church each Sunday in dazzling Spanish finery—feathers, jewels, silks—so bright and loud that the vicar himself had to speak to Mrs. Leigh about the distraction. Ayacanora replied with fiery pride: “I am not thinking of them, nor should they think of me.” If the Piache—the conjuror, as she called the priest—wanted a present, she said, he might have her Mexican feather dresses. They were wild Indian things, and she was now an English maid. So she darted upstairs, brought down the garments, and insisted on dressing the vicar in them. He fled, but not before taking one of the most magnificent mantles. Instead of selling it, he transformed it into an altar cloth. For years afterward, the communion at Northam was celebrated upon a blaze of emerald, azure, and crimson—once worn by an Aztec prince.

So Ayacanora lived—brilliant, impulsive, half-child, half-queen—while Amyas watched her with amusement and pride. He looked around at the villagers, as if to say, *See what a fine bird I have brought home.*

Another source of trouble was her treatment of the ladies of the neighborhood. They came to visit, drawn by curiosity and admiration—some to congratulate Mrs. Leigh, others to glimpse the "fair savage." But Ayacanora snubbed them all, from the vicar’s wife to Lady Grenville herself. She refused their smiles, their invitations, their kindnesses. Mrs. Leigh remonstrated, and Ayacanora wept: “They come only to stare at a poor wild girl. I was once a queen. Everyone obeyed me. Here, everyone looks down on me.” When asked if she would rather return to the forests, she clung to Mrs. Leigh like a child, begging not to be sent away. “I’d rather be a slave in this kitchen than go back to those bad people.”

Month after month passed—storms and sunsets in equal measure. But always under the calm influence of Mrs. Leigh, whose steady grace tempered the wildness within Ayacanora. The outbursts grew fewer, the moods more even. Though she remained restless, she was growing wiser.

And slowly, details emerged that confirmed her identity—though not to the world, only to those who knew her best. After seeing houses, she remarked that she had seen such before. The red cattle seemed familiar. Sheep confused her at first, until she said, “Too small.” Yeo seized on this: “Ah, madam, she must have known the great camel-sheep—llamas—in Peru.”

But her true delight was horses. The idea of riding one thrilled her beyond words. She begged Amyas for one, but he refused—fearing for her safety. Then she whispered to Yeo about stealing one. When that failed, she went to the vicar and offered all her finery for his broken-kneed pony. The vicar refused, and so Amyas bought her a jennet. Within two weeks, she rode like a Gaucho.

Then came a dream—one night at Lady Grenville’s estate, where the family had gone to visit. Ayacanora slept in the same room as Mrs. Leigh. Suddenly she screamed, then sobbed out a story: the “Old Ape of Panama” had come to her bed, dragged her into the courtyard, and she had mounted a horse and ridden with an Indian across moors and mountains into a dark wood. There, the Indian and the horses vanished—and she woke, transformed again into a small savage child.

The impression was so vivid she could not be convinced it hadn’t happened. Mrs. Leigh, moved, told the company the next morning that God had revealed Ayacanora’s past in a vision: she had been exposed in the forests by her jealous stepfather, Don Xararte, who may have believed her to be Oxenham’s child. He had taken vengeance by abandoning her to die. Yeo added that he had seen Don Xararte aboard the Lima ship at Guayaquil, near Quito—a fact that helped explain how she ended up beyond Cotopaxi, in the Eastern Andes.

More clues surfaced—each tied to animals or natural things. Her memories were fragmented, emotional, tied to sensory impressions. She could not recall her mother, nor any early religious teaching. That changed one day.

Sir Richard Grenville had brought home an Indian from Virginia—possibly Wanchese, mentioned alongside Manteo. This man, named Raleigh, was baptized in Bideford Church, sponsored by Raleigh himself and Sir Richard. The whole town came to witness: mayor, corporation, common folk—all to see the first “Red man” to set foot on British soil receive the Gospel.

Mrs. Leigh attended, and Ayacanora insisted on going too. She was curious about the “Carib.” “Why don’t they make him a Christian?” she asked. “Why not me?”

Because, they told her, she already was. They were certain she had been baptized at birth. But she wasn’t sure. She pouted, resentful that a “ugly red Carib” might be spiritually ahead of her.

At the ceremony, as the service progressed, Ayacanora suddenly gasped, then fainted into Mrs. Leigh’s arms. When she recovered, she told another dream-like memory: the church had grown immense, the priest’s robes richer. The walls were covered with paintings. Above the altar stood a woman in jeweled robes, holding a child. Soft music filled the air. The scent—sweet, heavy, like incense—drifted through the church like mist. She saw not one, but many Indians at the font. A lady held her hand, and she was a little girl again.

Yeo, listening closely, declared: “She is christened, madam—if Popish baptism counts. And she has seen Indians baptized in the Cathedral of Quito. I know that church well. I sat there three hours in a San Benito, listening to a friar preach lies, wondering if I’d burn the next day.”

Thus, Ayacanora returned to Burrough. Raleigh the Indian went to Sir Richard’s house. His baptism is recorded in the Bideford register, written in crooked letters:

“Raleigh, a Winganditoian: March 26, 1587.”

A year later, another entry appears:

“Rawly, a Winganditoian: April 1589.”

But not under baptisms. The forest-born man had missed his homeland—its cedar woods, its lagoons, its magnolias and vines. He died, longing for the old ways. Now he rests in the narrow churchyard of Bideford, buried beneath dingy houses where no one remembers him. Yet the two entries remain—silent witnesses, tragic symbols of the fate of the Native peoples: civilization came too late to save them, but not too late to destroy them.

Meanwhile, though Amyas stayed idle, England stirred. That spring, Raleigh sent another colony to Virginia, led by John White. He had urged Amyas to command it—had he done so, perhaps the United States might have begun twenty years earlier. But Amyas’s mother had sworn him to stay home for at least a year. So instead of leading the fleet, he sent five hundred pounds—money that likely remains in Virginia, lost to history, never to return.

But soon, a deeper trial came. Amyas began to feel restless, bitter. Others fought Spaniards abroad, while he sat idle. His hatred for Don Guzman grew fierce. He was losing his “single eye”—his inner light. Darkness crept over him, the darkness of hatred. And Yeo, ever more fanatical, fed it. Together, they debated whether they had sinned by sparing the Spanish prisoners on the galleon.

Yet the truth must be said: if Englishmen harbored such rage, Spain had earned it. The Spanish were planning more aggression.

We now approach the year 1588—foretold by an astronomer of Konigsberg a century before as “admirable,” and by German chronologers as the climacterical year of the world.

Prophecies may be myths, but events fulfilled them. That year decided the fate of Europe and America.

Some sensed it—a dread feeling that the Day of the Lord was near, that angels and demons were locked in battle for the souls of men. Astrologers dreamed of planetary alignments signaling doom.

But wise men needed no stars to know the hour had come. The long, scattered war between Spain and England would end in one final clash. Until then, conflict had been limited to the Netherlands, the West Indies, Africa—places Spain claimed unjustly. But Spain itself had remained untouched. Trade continued freely—until 1585, when Spain suddenly imposed an embargo on all English ships arriving on its shores. They were to be seized, used to build a vast armada.

Spain claimed it was to crush the rebellious Dutch. But England suspected otherwise. The queen, her ministers, and the people saw it clearly: England was the real target. So instead of negotiating, they fought to force negotiation.

Drake, Frobisher, Carlisle swept the Spanish Main, cutting off Indian supplies. Walsingham, cunning yet honest, blocked Venetian loans that might have saved Spain. No Armada came that year.

Yet the Jesuits, both here and abroad, spoke openly—among their followers—of the true purpose: to destroy the “impious heretics”—the Drakes, Raleighs, Grenvilles, Cavendishes, Hawkinses, Frobishers—who had dared invade the pope’s domain. Their barks would sink beneath Spanish cannon blessed by Rome. England would fall. The proud islanders, who rejected St. Peter and Mary, would kneel again. Elizabeth, illegitimate, excommunicate, would be chained and sent to Rome. Spain would reclaim the throne.

“What nobler cause? What greater glory? Spain was invincible. Heaven itself called her to fulfill her mission. The saints called for a new crusade. The Virgin Mary, wronged and dishonored, demanded justice. Let all valiant knights rise for her!”

So said Parsons, Allen, and others. But their audience was unmoved. The English Roman Catholics, though loyal to Rome, could not betray their country. They preferred survival to martyrdom.

The Jesuits turned to another queen: Mary, Queen of Scots. She was Catholic, beautiful, unfortunate—perfect for their cause. They played her card till it tore. She plotted against Elizabeth, aided by foreign powers, and sought to overthrow the government. Her beauty and charm won hearts, but her schemes doomed her.

And so the people of England answered with one voice—from noble to peasant: “Seventeen years of terror are enough. This woman must die—or England dies.”

We know which choice was made.

And so the stage was set. The fleet that would carry Virginia’s future was halted—not by storm, but by royal command. The queen had spoken. The age of exploration paused, awaiting a greater reckoning.
