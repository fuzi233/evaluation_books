The ascent was a solitary procession of three men against the indifferent slope: Dr. Armitage, burdened by age and white beard; Professor Rice, a stocky figure of iron-gray resolve; and Dr. Morgan, lean and younger. Below, the remaining group clung to the road, their faces pale masks of terror. With patient, methodical instruction, the scholars had transferred the telescope to the frightened locals, leaving the glass as a tether between the mundane world and the vertical abyss. As the trio climbed, the observers below tracked their progress with a nervous intensity, the telescope passing from hand to hand like a sacred relic. The terrain offered resistance, forcing Armitage to rely on the support of his companions more than once.

High above, the Great Swath trembled. Its creator moved across it with the slow, deliberate patience of a snail, a hellish rhythm that signaled the pursuers were closing the gap. Curtis Whateley, representing the undecayed line, held the telescope as the Arkham party executed a radical detour. He explained to the crowd that the men sought a subordinate peak overlooking the path ahead—a tactical maneuver that proved accurate moments later, as the invisible blasphemy swept past the bend in the shrubbery just as the scholars gained the minor elevation.

Wesley Corey, now gripping the lens, shouted that Armitage was adjusting the sprayer held by Rice. Something critical was imminent. The crowd stirred, remembering the sprayer's intended function: to render the unseen horror momentarily visible. Two or three men closed their eyes against the anticipation. Curtis Whateley snatched the telescope back, straining his vision until his eyes watered. Through the glass, he saw Rice positioned perfectly above and behind the entity, poised to unleash the powder with devastating effect. To the naked eye, there was only an instant's flash of gray cloud, roughly the size of a large house, blooming near the summit.

Curtis dropped the instrument. It fell with a sharp crack into the ankle-deep mud. He reeled, collapsing where he stood, saved only by the grasping hands of bystanders. His voice emerged as a broken moan, barely audible: "Oh, oh, great Gawd... that... that..."

Pandemonium erupted, a cacophony of confused shouting. Only Henry Wheeler, amidst the chaos, managed to retrieve the fallen telescope and wipe the mud from its lenses. Curtis was beyond coherence; even fragmented answers dissolved instantly. "Bigger 'n a barn," he gasped, his speech slurring. "All made o' squirmin' ropes. Hull thing sort o' shaped like a hen's egg bigger'n anything, with dozens o' legs like hogsheads that haff shut up when they step... nothin' solid abaout it—all like jelly, an' made o' sep'rit wrigglin' ropes pushed clost together... great bulgin' eyes all over it... ten or twenty maouths or trunks a-stickin' aout all along the sides, big as stovepipes, an' all a-tossin' an' openin' an' shuttin'... all gray, with kinder blue or purple rings... an' Gawd in Heaven—that haff face on top!"

That final image shattered him. Before he could speak further, Curtis collapsed completely. Fred Farr and Will Hutchins lifted him onto the damp grass. Trembling, Henry Wheeler turned the rescued telescope toward the peak. Through the lenses, three tiny figures ran toward the summit, moving as fast as the steep incline permitted. Nothing else was visible.

Then, a strangely unseasonable noise filled the deep valley behind them, drifting up from the underbrush of Sentinel Hill itself. It was the piping of countless whippoorwills, but in their shrill chorus lurked a note of tense, malignant expectancy. Earl Sawyer took the telescope and reported the three figures standing on the highest ridge, nearly level with the altar-stone yet distant from it. One figure appeared to raise its hands above its head at rhythmic intervals. As Sawyer spoke, the crowd seemed to hear a faint, half-musical sound from the distance, as if a loud chant accompanied the gestures.

The silhouette on the remote peak must have been a spectacle of infinite grotesquerie, yet no observer possessed the capacity for aesthetic appreciation. "I guess he's sayin' the spell," Wheeler whispered, snatching the telescope back. The whippoorwills piped wildly, their rhythm curiously irregular, discordant with the visible ritual. Suddenly, the sunshine seemed to diminish without the intervention of clouds—a peculiar phenomenon noted by all. A rumbling brewed beneath the hills, mixed with a concordant vibration coming from the sky. Lightning flashed aloft, and the crowd searched in vain for the portents of a storm.

The chanting of the Arkham men became unmistakable. Wheeler saw through the glass that they were raising their arms in rhythmic incantation. From a farmhouse far away came the frantic barking of dogs. The quality of daylight shifted, and the crowd gazed at the horizon in wonder. A purplish darkness, born of a spectral deepening of the sky's blue, pressed down upon the rumbling hills. Lightning flashed again, brighter this time, revealing a certain mistiness around the altar-stone on the distant height. No one used the telescope at that exact moment.

The whippoorwills continued their irregular pulsation. The men of Dunwich braced themselves against an imponderable menace, the atmosphere surcharged with static. Without warning, deep, cracked, raucous vocal sounds tore through the air. These were not born of human throats; the organs of man cannot yield such acoustic perversions. If one had to assign a source, it would be the pit itself, yet the origin was unmistakably the altar-stone on the peak. To call them sounds is almost erroneous, for their ghastly, infra-bass timbre spoke to dim seats of consciousness and terror far subtler than the ear. Yet their form was indisputably that of half-articulate words.

They were loud—louder than the rumblings and thunder above which they echoed—and yet they emanated from no visible being. Imagination conjured a source in the realm of non-visible entities, causing the huddled crowd at the mountain's base to press closer together, wincing as if expecting a physical blow.

"_Ygnaiih... ygnaiih... thflthkh'ngha... Yog-Sothoth..."_ rang the hideous croaking out of space. "_Y'bthnk... h'ehye... n'grkdl'lh..."_

The speaking impulse faltered here, as if a frightful psychic struggle were underway. Henry Wheeler strained his eye at the telescope, seeing only the three grotesquely silhouetted human figures on the peak, moving their arms furiously in strange gestures as the incantation neared its culmination. From what black wells of Acherontic fear, from what unplumbed gulfs of extra-cosmic consciousness or obscure, long-latent heredity, were those half-articulate thunder-croakings drawn?

Presently, they gathered renewed force and coherence, growing in stark, utter, ultimate frenzy. "_Eh-ya-ya-ya-yahaah... e'yaya-yayaaaa... ngh'aaaa... ngh'aaaa... h'yuh... h'yuh... HELP! HELP!... ff--ff--ff--FATHER! FATHER! YOG-SOTHOTH!..."_

And then, silence. The pallid group in the road, still reeling from the indisputably English syllables that had poured thickly and thunderously down from the frantic vacancy beside the altar-stone, were never to hear such sounds again. Instead, they jumped violently at the terrific report that seemed to rend the hills—the deafening, cataclysmic peal whose source, whether inner earth or sky, no hearer could ever place.

A single lightning bolt shot from the purple zenith to the altar-stone. A great tidal wave of viewless force and indescribable stench swept down from the hill to all the countryside. Trees, grass, and underbrush were whipped into a fury. The frightened crowd at the mountain's base, weakened by the lethal fetor that seemed about to asphyxiate them, were almost hurled off their feet. Dogs howled from the distance; green grass and foliage wilted to a curious, sickly yellow-gray. Over field and forest were scattered the bodies of dead whippoorwills. The stench left quickly, but the vegetation never recovered. To this day, there remains something queer and unholy about the growths on and around that fearsome hill.

Curtis Whateley was only just regaining consciousness when the Arkham men descended the mountain, walking slowly in the beams of a sunlight once more brilliant and untainted. They were grave and quiet, seemingly shaken by memories and reflections even more terrible than those which had reduced the native group to a state of cowed quivering. In reply to a jumble of questions, they only shook their heads, reaffirming one vital fact.

"The thing has gone forever," Armitage said. "It has been split up into what it was originally made of, and can never exist again. It was an impossibility in a normal world. Only the least fraction was really matter in any sense we know. It was like its father—and most of it has gone back to him in some vague realm or dimension outside our material universe; some vague abyss out of which only the most accursed rites of human blasphemy could ever have called him for a moment on the hills."
