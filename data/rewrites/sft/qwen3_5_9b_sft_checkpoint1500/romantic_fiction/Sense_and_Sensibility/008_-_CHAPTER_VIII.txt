Mrs. Jennings sat comfortably in her parlor, a widow whose ample jointure freed her from the necessity of work, save for one singular ambition: to arrange marriages for everyone within her reach. Having successfully wedded her two daughters, she viewed the rest of the world through the lens of potential unions. Her mind worked quickly, spotting the flicker of attachment before anyone else, delighting in raising the blushes and vanity of young ladies by hinting at their sway over certain gentlemen. Upon arriving at Barton Cottage, her discernment was swift and decisive. Within days, she declared that Colonel Brandon was deeply, hopelessly in love with Miss Marianne Dashwood. The evidence, to her mind, was irrefutable: the colonel's rapt attention during Marianne's songs, first at the park and then again when the Middletons visited, spoke volumes. She saw no obstacle; he was wealthy, and she was beautiful. An excellent match, she concluded, and indeed, she had longed for the colonel's happiness since Sir John introduced him to her circle.

To Mrs. Jennings, the immediate joy of this prospect lay in the endless ammunition it provided for her wit. At the Park, she laughed gently at the colonel; at the cottage, she teased Marianne relentlessly about her feelings. While the colonel likely found her raillery harmless, Marianne struggled to comprehend it. Initially baffled, she eventually realized the sting behind her mother-in-law's jokes—that they mocked not just her emotions, but the colonel's apparent age and his lonely existence as a bachelor.

Mrs. Dashwood, however, stood firm against the notion that a man five years younger than herself could possibly appear ancient. "At least, Mama," Marianne protested, "you cannot deny the absurdity of the accusation, even if you don't intend malice. Colonel Brandon is older than Mrs. Jennings, yes, but he is old enough to be *my* father. If he ever possessed the courage to fall in love, surely those sentiments have long since faded into dust. It is preposterous!" She lamented how little protection age offered from such biting humor.

"Infirmity!" cried Elinor sharply. "Do you truly believe Colonel Brandon is infirm? You may think his age exaggerated, but you cannot mistake his physical strength."

"But did you not hear him speak of rheumatism?" Marianne countered. "Is that not the common ailment of aging?"

"My dearest child," Mrs. Dashwood laughed softly, "if you proceed at this rate, you shall fear my own decline constantly. It seems a miracle to me that I have reached the advanced age of forty without collapsing."

"Mama, you do me no justice," Elinor interjected calmly. "Colonel Brandon is not so elderly that his friends dread losing him naturally. He may live another twenty years. Yet, thirty-five and seventeen are not meant for matrimony."

"Perhaps," Elinor mused, "thirty-five and seventeen should avoid each other entirely. However, if a woman remains single at twenty-seven, I should find no fault with Colonel Brandon marrying her."

"A woman of twenty-seven," Marianne paused thoughtfully, "has lost the capacity to feel or inspire deep affection. If her home is wretched or her fortune meager, she might endure the duties of a nurse simply for the security of a wife. In his marrying such a person, there would be no impropriety. It would be a contract of convenience, satisfying the world's eyes. To me, it would not be a marriage at all, but merely a commercial exchange where each party seeks benefit at the expense of the other."

"It would be impossible," Elinor replied gently, "to convince you that a woman of twenty-seven could love a man of thirty-five enough to make him a cherished companion. Yet, I must object to your sentencing Colonel Brandon and his hypothetical wife to a lifetime of sickness chambers merely because he complained yesterday—a cold, damp day—of a slight ache in his shoulder."

"But he spoke of flannel waistcoats!" Marianne insisted. "In my mind, flannel is forever linked with aches, cramps, rheumatisms, and every misery that plagues the aged and weak."

"If he had been suffering from a violent fever, you would not despise him half so much," Elinor observed. "Confess, Marianne, is there not something fascinating to you in the flushed cheek, the hollow eye, and the rapid pulse of a fever?"

As soon as Elinor left the room, Marianne turned to her mother, her face pale with sudden worry. "Mama, I have an alarm regarding illness that I cannot hide from you. I am certain Edward Ferrars is unwell. We have been here nearly a fortnight, and he has not arrived. Only genuine indisposition could cause such an extraordinary delay. What else keeps him at Norland?"

"Had you expected him sooner?" Mrs. Dashwood asked. "I had no expectation, and if I felt any anxiety, it stemmed from recalling his lack of enthusiasm when I previously invited him here. Did Elinor expect him already?"

"I never mentioned it to her, but surely she must have guessed."

"I doubt you are correct," Mrs. Dashwood said. "Yesterday, when we discussed fitting a new grate for the spare bedroom, Elinor remarked that there was no urgency, implying the room would not be needed for some time."

"How strange!" Marianne exclaimed. "What could account for this? Their behavior toward one another has been utterly inexplicable! How cold, how composed were their final farewells! How listless their conversation the night before! In Edward's goodbye, there was no distinction between Elinor and myself; it was merely the warm wishes of an affectionate brother. Twice I left them alone together that morning, yet each time he followed me out of the room with uncanny speed. And Elinor... when she left Norland and Edward, she did not cry as I did. Her self-control is unbroken. When is she dejected? When does she seek solitude or show signs of unrest?"
