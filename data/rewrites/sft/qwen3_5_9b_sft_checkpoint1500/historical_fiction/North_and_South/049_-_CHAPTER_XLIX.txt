The room in London was quiet, the heavy shadows of the journey to Oxford having finally settled around them. After the news, Edith leaned forward, pulling her husband's tall frame down until their faces were near. Standing on tiptoe, she implored him not to be shocked, her voice trembling slightly as she voiced the question that had haunted her since the revelation. "Is not Margaret the heiress?" she whispered.

Captain Lennox blinked, the news hitting him with the force of a sudden fog. If he had ever heard rumors, they had long since evaporated from his mind. A fellow of a small college? Leaving behind nothing more substantial than a pension? Two hundred and fifty pounds a year, considered in the context of a girl who barely touched wine, sounded absurd, almost comical. Yet, Edith's smile faded, replaced by a melancholy that crushed the romantic illusions she had built around her friend's future. Her vision of a grand, sweeping destiny had been blown to pieces by the mundane arithmetic of a lawyer's account.

A week later, however, the mood shifted dramatically. Edith bounded into the room, curtsying low with a radiant energy that belied her previous despondency. "I am right, and you are wrong, most noble Captain," she announced, her voice dripping with triumph. "Margaret has received a letter from a lawyer. She is the residuary legatee. The specific legacies total about two thousand pounds, but the remainder amounts to roughly forty thousand, calculated at current property values in Milton."

"Indeed!" Captain Lennox stammered, his composure cracking. "And how does she take her good fortune?"

"Oh, it seems she suspected it all along," Edith explained, her eyes dancing. "She simply didn't realize the magnitude. She looks terribly pale and white, claiming she is frightened by it, but that is nonsense. It will vanish soon enough. I left Mother pouring congratulations down her throat and slipped away to bring you this tidings."

To the general consensus of the circle, it became inevitable that Mr. Lennox assume the role of Margaret's legal advisor. Entirely unversed in the machinery of commerce, Margaret found herself constantly referring to him for guidance. He selected her attorney, presented documents requiring her signature, and derived a peculiar joy from instructing her in the arcane symbols of the law. He treated the process not merely as work, but as a courtship of sorts, delighting in teaching her the alphabet of business.

One afternoon, Edith teased him, her gaze arch and playful. "Henry, do you know what I hope and expect all these long conversations with Margaret to eventually lead to?"

"No, I do not," he replied, flushing slightly, though he quickly masked the emotion. "And I pray you do not tell me."

"Oh, very well; then I need not warn Sholto to stop inviting Mr. Montagu so frequently."

"Just as you wish," he responded, feigning a cool detachment that failed to reach his eyes. "Whatever you envision may or may not come to pass. However, before I stake my position, I must ensure the ground beneath me is firm. Go ahead and ask whomever you please. It may not seem polite, Edith, but if you interfere, you will ruin it. She has remained fiercely resistant to me for quite some time; she is only just beginning to thaw from her 'Zenobia' mannerisms. There is a Cleopatra within her, waiting to emerge, provided she allows a touch of paganism to surface."

"For my part," Edith countered with a touch of malice, "I am delighted she remains a Christian. I know so few who truly understand the faith!"

That autumn held no Spain for Margaret. Though she clung to the slender hope that a fortunate circumstance might summon Frederick to Paris, where a convoy awaited, reality grounded her to Cromer instead of Cadiz. It was here that Aunt Shaw and the Lennoxes intended to reside. Having always desired her company, they had exerted only sluggish efforts to facilitate her separate wishes, a trait characteristic of their collective nature. Perhaps Cromer, in a certain sense, was the ideal sanctuary. It offered physical fortification, bracing air, and the necessary rest that her spirit required.

Amidst the vanishing hopes, one particular trust lingered with painful weight: the belief that Mr. Bell might have disclosed the simple truths surrounding the family circumstances preceding Leonards' tragic death to Mr. Thornton. Regardless of how his opinions had shifted since the event, Margaret had wished for any judgment he formed to be rooted in a genuine understanding of her actions and motives. It would have brought her peace, a resolution to a point on which she would otherwise remain perpetually restless throughout her life. Now, so many years had passed that explanation was impossible save through the lens of Mr. Bell's demise. She could submit to being misunderstood, resigning herself to a common human lot, yet her heart ached with a longing that persisted. She prayed that, in time—perhaps years hence, before his eventual passing—he might come to know how deeply she had been tempted. She did not necessarily seek the truth laid bare if she could be assured he would know it, but that prayer proved vain, akin to many others. Thus, she schooled herself into acceptance, turning her entire heart and strength toward the immediate future, vowing to strive and make the best of whatever lay ahead.

Her days at Cromer were often spent sitting for hours upon the beach, staring intently as the waves battered the pebbly shore in their ceaseless motion, or gazing toward the distant horizon where water sparkled against the sky. Unconsciously, she heard the eternal psalm rising from the sea, soothing her without her realizing why. She sat listlessly on the sand, hands clasped around her knees, while Aunt Shaw engaged in minor shopping expeditions and Edith and Captain Lennox roamed far and wide across the coast and countryside. Nurses sauntered past with their charges, whispering in wonder at what could possibly hold Margaret's gaze so transfixed, day after day. When the family gathered for dinner, Margaret was so silent and absorbed that Edith declared her "moped," seizing upon her husband's proposal with great satisfaction: they would invite Mr. Henry Lennox to stay for a week upon his return from Scotland in October.

Yet, amidst the distraction of seaside idleness, Margaret began to reorder the events of her life, placing them correctly regarding their origins and significance, both for her past and future. Those solitary hours by the sea were not wasted; anyone perceptive enough to read the gradual transformation in Margaret's countenance would have seen it clearly. Mr. Henry Lennox was profoundly struck by the change.

"The sea has done Miss Hale an immense deal of good, I should imagine," he remarked upon her first departure from the room following his arrival. "She appears ten years younger than she did in Harley Street."

"That's the bonnet I procured for her!" Edith interjected triumphantly. "I knew instantly it would suit her."

"I beg your pardon," Mr. Lennox replied, adopting a tone that blended half-contempt with indulgence—a habit he usually reserved for Edith. "However, I believe I distinguish between the charms of a dress and the charms of a woman. No mere bonnet could render Miss Hale's eyes so lustrous yet so soft, nor her lips so ripe and red, nor fill her face with such profound peace and light. She resembles, and indeed surpasses," he lowered his voice, "the Margaret Hale of Helstone."

From that moment, the clever and ambitious man devoted all his faculties to winning Margaret's heart. He adored her sweet beauty and discerned the latent breadth of her mind, convinced it could easily be guided to embrace every object upon which he had set his affections. He viewed her fortune merely as a component of her complete and superior character and position, yet he acutely understood the social elevation it would immediately afford him, a poor barrister seeking advancement. Ultimately, he believed he would earn such success and honors as would allow him to repay her, with interest, the initial financial advance he owed her. He had recently visited Milton on business concerning her property, returning from Scotland, and with the keen eye of a seasoned lawyer attuned to contingencies, he observed that additional value accrued annually to the lands and tenements she owned in that thriving, expanding town. He was pleased to note that the current dynamic of client and legal advisor was gradually supplanting the memory of that ill-fated, botched day at Helstone. This arrangement afforded him unusual opportunities for intimate intercourse with her, complemented by the existing connection between their families.

Margaret was all too willing to listen, provided he spoke of Milton. Although he had encountered none of the individuals she particularly knew, the tone of her aunt and cousin—who spoke of Milton with dislike and contempt—was something Margaret was ashamed to recall expressing upon first settling there. Yet, Mr. Lennox surpassed even her in his appreciation of Milton and its inhabitants. Their energy, their power, and their indomitable courage in struggle captivated him. The vivid, lurid intensity of their existence arrested his attention. He never tires of speaking of them, failing to perceive how many of their ends were merely selfish and material until Margaret, even amidst her gratification, possessed the candor to point out this tainting sin in what was otherwise noble and admirable.
