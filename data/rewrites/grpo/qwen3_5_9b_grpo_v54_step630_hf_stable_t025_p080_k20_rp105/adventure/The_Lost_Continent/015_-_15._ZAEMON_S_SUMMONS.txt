From the moment man was first shaped by the hands of the Gods, there have been corners of the earth unfit for his keeping. None are more hostile than the stretch of Atlantis facing the savage coasts of Europe and Africa. The common folk shun this place, driven by a superstition that the spirits of the wicked dead wander its daylight hours, slaughtering anyone the wilder dangers might spare. It is a grim irony that many criminals, fleeing justice, have chosen to surrender voluntarily to the tormentors rather than face the region's fabulous terrors.

To the educated, many of these tales are known to be myths; yet enough unease remains to make existence here a gauntlet of peril. The mountains are perpetually feverish, their contents boiling; earthquakes are a daily occurrence. Invisible, malevolent vapors creep with cunning speed, often choking victims before they can flee. Rivers rise with poison that leaves their color unchanged, while great fissures tear across the ground, drawing water down to the fires below only to shoot it back forth in devastating explosions. Boiling mud and molten rock are constant threats.

Yet amidst this chaos, sombre forests thrive, populated by trees of unimaginable age where fires among the herbage are rare. Beneath the canopy lies water, and the air hangs heavy with warm steam and wetness. To live in such constant, suffocating damp is mortifying to the human frame. Strength and cunning beyond the ordinary are required to survive, for these lands are the domain of lizards—the most enormous and fearsome beasts of all.

Countless families and species of these reptiles exist. With some, a man might contend with a prospect of success; with others, whose hugeness defies human force, survival is impossible. I witnessed one emerge from a lake after feeding, causing the wet land to shake and pulse with its tread. It could have swallowed Phorenice's mammoth whole, and even a charging beast could not have harmed it. Great horny plates armored its head and body, while spines along its back, tail, and limbs tore great slivers from the black trees as it moved.

Occasionally, these monsters become trapped in vast ground fissures, but rarely. Their speed is great, and their sagacity keen. They seem to sense the worst boilings of the mountains and seek safety in deeper lakes or muddy wallows. Moreover, they are better constituted than man to withstand the region's greatest danger: the heat of water harms them not. They lie peacefully in pools where steam bursts send water leaping into boiling fountains, and I have seen one run swiftly across a flow of molten rock, emerging not singed.

Thrown into such a neighborhood, my new life became perilous and hard from the outset. I came near to knowing the true nature of Fear. Only a fervent trust in the Most High Gods, and a firm belief that my life was under Their fostering care, prevented me from succumbing to that horrid knowledge. For long enough, until I learned the ways of this steaming, sweltering land, I was in as miserable a state as Phorenice could have wished to see me. My clothes rotted from my back with the constant wetness until I was as naked as a savage from Europe. My limbs were racked with agues, and I found no herbs to relieve them. For days, my only sustenance was tree-grubs and leaves; often, when I killed beasts knowing little of their qualities, I ate those that brought me pain and sickness.

But as man is born to adapt to his surroundings, so did I, as the months dragged on, learn the limitations of this new life and gather knowledge of its resources. I found a great black tree with a hollow core and a hole near its roots. There I harbored until one night a monstrous lizard, whose sheer weight made the tree rock like a sapling, endeavored to suck me forth as a bird picks a worm from a hollow log. I escaped by the will of the Gods—I could as easily have harmed a mountain as injure that horny tongue with my weapons—but I gave myself warning that such a chance must never happen again.

So I cut a ladder of footholes inside the trunk, ascending to a point ten man-heights from the ground. There I carved other notches and built a floor of tree branches to rest upon. Later, for luxury, I carved arrow-slit windows into the chamber walls and carried up sand for a hearth, allowing me to cook my victuals high above the dangers of the open below.

By degrees, I began to find how the large-scaled fish of the rivers and lesser turtles might be captured, so my ribs threatened less to start through their skin. But the lack of salads and gruels I could never overcome. All green meat was tainted with the taste of tar, a flavor I could never force my palate to accept. Of course, the peril of the greater lizards and other native dangers remained.

As the months mounted into years, and the brute part of my nature became more satisfied, other longings arose that were harder to provide for. From the ivory of a river horse's tooth, I had endeavored to carve a representative of Nais as I last saw her. Though my fingers were loving and my will good, my art was dull, and the result—despite repeated attempts—was always clumsy and pitiful. Still, in my eyes, it carried some suggestion of the original—a curve here, an outline there—and it made my old love glow anew within me as I sat and gazed upon it. Yet it did little to satisfy my longing for the woman I had lost; rather, it whetted my cravings to be with her again, or at least to know her fate.

Other men of the Priests' Clan have come out and made an abode in these Dangerous Lands, mortifying their flesh to gain intimacy with the Higher Mysteries, carrying them far past what mere human learning could teach. Some who have returned to the abodes of men have brought knowledge that earns them a reputation for magic and miracles among the vulgar, adding materially to the ardor with which common people pursue the cult of the Gods. But for myself, I could not free my mind to the necessary clearness for such abstruse studies. During my voyage home from Yucatan, I had communed with them with growing insight; but now my mind was not my own. Nais held a lien upon it, refusing to be ousted. In truth, her sweet trespass was my chief solace.

At last, my longing could no longer be denied. Through one of the arrow-slits of my tree-house, I could see far away a great mountain top whitened with perpetual snow, which our Lord the Sun dyed with blood every night of His setting. Night after night, I watched that ruddy light with wide, straining eyes. Night after night, I remembered that in days gone by, when I entered the priesthood, it had been my duty to adore our great Lord as He rose for His day behind the snows of that very mountain. Always, the thought followed: from that distant crest, I could see across the continent to the Sacred Mount, where the city lay below, and where I had buried my love alive.

So at last, I gave way and set out. A perilous journey it proved. In the heavy mists that hung always on the lower ground, my way lay blind before me, and I constantly lost it. To say I traversed three times the direct distance is a low estimate. Throughout the swamps, the great lizards hunted, and as the country was new to me, I knew no places of harbor. A hundred times I was within an ace of being spotted and devoured at a single mouthful. But the High Gods still desired me for Their own purposes, blinding the great beasts' eyes as I slunk to cover. Twice, rivers of scalding water roared boiling across my path, forcing me to delay until I could collect enough black timber to build rafts for dry ferriage.

The journey was infinitely tedious, yet to me, after those years of waiting, time passed on winged feet. I had been separated from my love until I could bear the strain no longer. Let me but see from a distance the place where she lay, feast my eyes upon it for a while, and then I could return to my abode in the tree and wait patiently for the will of the Gods.

The air grew chillier as I emerged above the region of trees onto higher ground that glares down on the rest of the world. I made buskins and a coat of woven grasses to protect my body from the keen wind. Later, where the snow lay eternally, blown into gullies and frozen into solid banks and bergs of ice, I had hard work making progress among its perilous mazes. The chill numbed me, weakening my natural strength. Overhead, following me with forbidding swoops and occasionally coming so close I had to threaten them with my weapons, were huge man-eating birds that live by pulling down any creature their instincts deem weak and likely to die soon.

But the lure ahead was strong enough to make these difficulties seem small. Though the mountain air disagreed with me, causing sickness and panting, I pressed on with all the speed I could muster toward the elusive summit. Time after time, I thought the next spur would surely bring me to the view my soul yearned for, but always there seemed another bank of snow and ice to climb.

Finally, I reached the crest and gave thanks to the Most High Gods for Their protection and favor. Far, far away, I could see the Sacred Mountain with its ring of fires burning pale under the day. Although the splendid city nesting at its foot could not be seen from where I stood, I knew its position and its plan. My soul went out to that throne of granite in the square before the royal pyramid, where once, years before, I had buried my love. Had Phorenice left the tomb unviolated?

I stood there leaning on my spear, filling my eye with the prospect, warming even to the smoke of mountains I recognized as old acquaintances. Gods! How my love burned within me for this woman. My whole being seemed gone out to meet her, leaving no room for anything beside. For a long time, a voice seemed dimly to call me, but I gave it no regard. I had come to that hoary mountain top for communion with Nais alone, wanting no others to interrupt.

But at length, the voice calling my name grew too loud to be neglected. I pulled myself out of my sweet musing with a start, realizing that here, for the first time since parting with Tob and his company, I should see another human being. I gripped my weapon and asked who called. The reply came clearly from up the slopes, and I saw a man coming toward me over the snows. He was old and feeble, his body bent, his hair and beard white as the ground beneath him. Presently, I recognized him as Zaemon. He came with incredible speed for a man of his years and feebleness, carrying in his hand the glowing Symbol of our Lord the Sun, from which holy strength would greatly augment his powers.

He came close and made the sign of the Seven, which I returned to him with due form and ceremony. Then he saluted me as a messenger appointed by the High Council of the Priests seated before the Ark of the Mysteries, and I made humble obeisance.

"In all things I will obey the orders you put before me," I said.

"Such is your duty, my brother. The command is that you return immediately to the Sacred Mountain. If human means may still prevail, you, as the most skilled general Atlantis owns within her borders, may still save the country from final wreck and punishment. The woman Phorenice persists in her infamies. The poor land groans under her heel. Now she has laid siege to our Sacred Mountain itself, swearing that not one soul shall be left alive in all Atlantis who does not bend humbly to her will."

"It is a command, and I obey it. But let me ask of another matter intimate to both of us. What of Nais?"

"Nais rests where you left her, untouched. Phorenice knows by her arts—she has stolen nearly all the ancient knowledge—that you still live. She keeps Nais unharmed beneath the granite throne in the hopes that some time she may use her as a weapon against you. Little does she know the sternness of our Priests' creed, my brother. Why, even I, that am the girl's father, would sacrifice her blithely if her death or ruin might do a tittle of good to Atlantis."

"You go beyond me with your devotion."

The old man leaned forward, his brow glowering. "What!"

"Or my old blind adherence to the ancient dogma has been sapped and weakened by events. You must buy my full obedience, Zaemon, if you want it. Promise me Nais—and your arts I know can snatch her—and I will be a true servant to the High Council of the Priests, dying in the last ditch if need be to carry out order. But let me see Nais given over to the fury of that wanton woman, and I shall have no inward left except to take my vengeance and see Atlantis piled up in ruins as her funeral stone."

Zaemon looked at me bitterly. "And you are the man the High Council thought to trust as they would trust one of themselves? Truly we are in an age of weak men and faithless ones. But, my lord—nay, I must call you brother still—we cannot be too nice in our choosing today. You are the best there is, and we must have you. We little thought you would ask a price for your generalship, having once taken oath on the walls of the Ark of the Mysteries itself that always, come what might, you would be a servant of the High Council of the Clan without fee and without hope of advancement.
