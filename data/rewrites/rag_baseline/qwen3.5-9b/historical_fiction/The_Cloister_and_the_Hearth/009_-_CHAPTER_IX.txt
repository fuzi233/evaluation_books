The moonlight fractured across the garden wall as Margaret severed a massive chunk of venison, tossing it outward with a decisive flick of her wrist. The meat sailed through the air, landing squarely before the emerald eyes of the leopard. With a savage snarl that vibrated through the night, the beast lunged, tearing and crunching with primal hunger. Suddenly, a hound bayed—a sound so piercing and close it echoed violently within the timbered halls—and the three animals at the window recoiled in unified terror. Fear seized the leopard; her supper secured, she melted away into the shadows of the woods with stolen grace. Instantly, the chaos erupted outside: horses reared, men shouted, and dogs barked, forming a frenzied pursuit that swept past the window. Inside, Martin and his companions exhaled, their chests rising in relief. The leopard was swift; she would vanish beyond a league of their reach before dawn. Seizing the brief respite, Margaret allowed herself a small, trembling sob. Gerard stepped forward, pressing a kiss to wipe the tears from her cheeks, his touch gentle amidst the lingering adrenaline.

They returned to the table, the tension replaced by a fragile warmth. Gerard raised his cup, toasting the unique fortitude of the female spirit. "'Tis stronger than man's force," he declared, his voice thick with admiration.

"Aye," Margaret replied, her gaze steady, "only when those she loves stand in peril. Not otherwise."

That evening, Gerard lingered with her longer than custom dictated, departing home intoxicated by a pride that felt regal, his heart light as a prince's. Yet, mere distances from the sanctuary of his childhood home, beneath the canopy of ancient oaks, two figures emerged to block his path.

It was his father and mother.

*So late,* Gerard thought, a chill seizing his spine. *What could possibly drive them here?*

He halted, staring at them. They stood rigid, grim faces carved from stone, silence hanging between them like a physical weight. He stammered, offering tentative inquiries.

"Why ask?" his father growled, the question itself an accusation. "You know why we are here."

"Oh, Gerard!" his mother interjected, her voice a complex tapestry of reproach and buried affection.

Gerard's heart hammered against his ribs; he fell silent, the weight of their presence crushing his resolve. Then, his father softened, sensing the boy's confusion.

"Nay, do not bow your head," the elder said gently. "You are not the first young fool ensnared by a rosy cheek and a pair of azure eyes."

"Nay, nay!" Catherine chimed in, her tone shifting to superstition. "It was witchcraft! Peter the Magician is renowned for such sorcery."

"Come, Sir Priest," the father resumed, addressing Gerard with paternal authority. "You know you must not consort with women of ill repute. But grant us this promise: do not set foot in Sevenbergen again. Let this be the end. We shall not punish you severely for one lapse."

"I cannot promise that, father."

"Not promise it, you young hypocrite!" the father snapped, his patience fraying.

"Nay, father, do not misname me," Gerard pleaded, his voice cracking. "I lacked the courage to tell you what I knew would grieve you. I am eternally grateful to that good friend, whoever he may be, who revealed your truth to you. It is a burden lifted from my soul. Yes, father, I love Margaret. Call me not a priest, for a priest I shall never be. I would sooner die."

"That we shall see, young man," the father retorted, his composure returning like a shield. "Come, deny me no more; you will learn the price of disrespecting a father."

Gerard fell silent. The trio walked home enveloped in a gloomy quiet, broken only by the occasional, heavy sigh of Catherine.

From that hour, the modest dwelling at Tergou ceased to be a haven of peace. The following day, Gerard was summoned before the entire family, subjected to a barrage of accusations. Every voice rose in condemnation save for little Kate's and the dwarf's, who instinctively mirrored her silence without understanding why. Cornelis and Sybrandt were fiercer than their sire. Dismayed by the sudden proliferation of enemies, Gerard cast a longing glance at his younger sister. Her eyes swelled with tears as harsh words rained down upon him, a boy who had been the universal favorite just yesterday. Yet, she offered no solace. Turning her head away, she spoke with chilling finality: "Dear, dear Gerard, pray to Heaven to cure you of this folly!"

"What, are you against me too?" Gerard asked, his voice hollow with despair. He rose, letting out a deep, ragged sigh, and fled the house, walking straight toward Sevenbergen.

The genesis of a quarrel, where parties remain bound by affection despite opposing interests and sentiments, is relatively innocuous. Often, both sides hold partial truths at the outset, waiting for a calm, judicious friend—one capable of perceiving the full spectrum of the conflict—to intervene as a divine gift. However, as discord endures, it widens and deepens, fed by the inherent fallibility and irritability of human nature. These flaws are not exclusive to either faction; inevitably, the conclusion arrives where both stand equally in the wrong.

The combatants were mismatched: Elias burned with righteous anger, while Cornelis and Sybrandt festered with spite. Gerard, possessing a broader and more cultivated mind, perceived the nuances both sides missed. He suffered from fits of irresolution, feeling neither wrath nor triumph, but merely profound unhappiness. He was utterly alone in this struggle, unable to confide in anyone. Margaret was a spirited girl; he dared not reveal the torment he endured at home. She might well side with his relations by demanding his resignation, sacrificing her own happiness to preserve the family bond. Though Margaret Van Eyck had once been a great comfort to him, he now feared making her his confidante. Her own history was well-known: in her youth, she had rejected numerous suitors for the sake of her art, a pursuit incompatible with the duties of wife and mother. Consequently, she remained single, painting alongside her brothers. How could he confess that he had declined the ecclesiastical benefice she had secured for him, doing so precisely because of the very thing she had once despised and sacrificed so lightly?

Gerard appeared poised to succumb to despair. But the opposition harbored a terrible ally in Catherine the Elder. This good-hearted yet uneducated woman could not, unlike her daughter, act with quiet firmness or strategic planning. At times, she irritated Gerard, inadvertently strengthening him, for anger serves as a potent sustainer of courage. At other moments, however, she would suddenly pivot and launch attacks upon her own household. Consider this single instance among many: one day, gathered at home, Cornelis declared, "Shall our Gerard wed Margaret Brandt? Why, it is hunger marrying thirst."

"And what will happen when you marry?" Catherine cried, her voice sharp. "Gerard can paint, Gerard can write, but what can you do to keep a woman, ye lazy loon? Nothing but wait for your father's shoes. Oh, we see clearly why you and Sybrandt refuse to let the poor boy marry. You fear he will come to us for a share of our substance. Say he does, say we give it to him—it is not yours we part with, and perhaps never will be."

On such occasions, Gerard would smile slyly, regaining his composure, leaving Catherine's unfortunate allies momentarily confused. But eventually, after more than half a year of simmering irritation, the situation reached its climax. The father announced to the assembled family that he had ordered the burgomaster to imprison Gerard in the Stadthouse rather than allow him to marry Margaret. Gerard turned pale with rage, yet through sheer effort, he maintained his silence. His father pressed on, "And before the year is out, you shall be a priest, nilly-willy."

"Is it so?" Gerard cried, his voice breaking. "Then listen to me, all. By God and St. Bavon, I swear I will never be a priest while Margaret lives. If force is to decide this, and not love and duty, then try force, father. But force shall fail you. The day the burgomaster comes for me, I leave Tergou forever, and Holland too, and my father's house, where it seems I have been valued these many years not for myself, but for what can be extracted from me."

With that, he stormed out of the room, his face flushed white with anger and desperation.

"There!" Catherine exclaimed, her voice trembling. "That comes of driving young folks too hard. But men are crueler than tigers, even to their own flesh and blood. Now, Heaven forbid he should ever leave us, married or single."

As Gerard emerged from the house, his cheeks pale and his heart pounding, he encountered Reicht Heynes. She bore a message: Margaret Van Eyck desired to see him. He found the elderly woman seated, grim as a judge. She wasted no time on pleasantries, querying coldly why he had not visited recently. Before he could respond, she spoke in a sarcastic tone, "I thought we had been friends, young sir."

At this, Gerard looked the very picture of doubt and consternation.

"It is because you never told her you were in love," Reicht Heynes said, her expression softening with pity for his confusion.
