Beneath the trembling hand that penned these final words, a letter rests, heavy with ink and silence. It bears the seal of Dr. Hesselius, a scholar whose mind has long been the labyrinth through which we navigate the uncanny. In his own treatise, he approached this matter with the cold precision of a surgeon dissecting a corpse, stripping away the flesh of superstition to reveal the bone beneath—a duality of existence where the living and the dead walk together, blurred by shadows that refuse to fade. He called it "arcana," those profound secrets hidden within the fabric of our souls, yet here, stripped of his academic armor, the truth feels less like a discovery and more like a confession.

I hold this manuscript now, a relic from a time when the world felt wider and stranger. My first impulse was to reach out, to resurrect the ghost of the woman who first told him her tale, to question her about the nights when the moon hung low and the air grew thick with something ancient. But fate, cruel and swift, had already cut the thread. She is gone, taken by a fever that burned away the light from her eyes, leaving behind only a silence as vast as the grave. Her voice, once so clear, is lost to the dust of the past.

So, I am left alone with what remains. This narrative, written with a conscience that trembled at every step, stands as the only witness to what happened. It is a story of a girl who did not age, of a love that fed on blood and shadow, and of a friendship that turned into a nightmare. There is no need for me to explain the theories of the doctor who studied it; they would only clutter the horror with jargon. The terror is simple, raw, and terrible enough without the weight of philosophy. Let the story speak for itself, unburdened by the learned musings of men who try to cage the infinite. For some doors, once opened, cannot be closed again, and some stories, once read, change the reader forever.
