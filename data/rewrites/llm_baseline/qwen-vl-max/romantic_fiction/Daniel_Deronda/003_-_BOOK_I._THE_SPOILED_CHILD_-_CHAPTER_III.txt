“Let no flower of the spring pass by us; let us crown ourselves with rosebuds before they be withered.”—BOOK OF WISDOM.

It was a pity Offendene had not been the home of Miss Harleth’s childhood, or cherished in her heart by family memories. A human life, I believe, ought to take root somewhere—in a corner of native soil where one can grow fond of the earth’s face, the labors men pursue, the voices and accents that linger in the air, and all those small things that make a place unmistakably one’s own amid the widening world. There should be a spot where early impressions are woven into affection, where familiarity with neighbors—even the dogs and donkeys—comes not from sentimentality, but as a quiet habit of the blood. At five years old, children aren’t ready to be citizens of the world, nor stirred by abstract ideals. That blind preference for milk we begin with is, in truth, a sign of how body and soul must first be nourished. The best way to learn astronomy, perhaps, is to think of the night sky as just a little cluster of stars belonging to your own backyard.

But such rootedness had never come to Gwendolen. It was only a year before her return from Leubronn that Offendene had been chosen as her mother’s home—not for its charm or history, but simply because it lay near Pennicote Rectory. And so Mrs. Davilow, Gwendolen, and her four half-sisters (with governess and maid following behind) had arrived on a late October afternoon, when rooks cawed overhead and yellow elm leaves swirled through the air.

The season matched the house’s mood: an old, long red-brick building, too eagerly adorned with stone along every line—from the narrow windows to the large square portico. The stone nurtured a greenish lichen, the brick a dusty gray, so though the structure was rigidly rectangular, its expression softened with age. It stood behind three avenues cutting through a hundred yards of ancient plantation, yet one could wish it lifted upon a knoll, so it might look beyond its modest domain to distant thatched roofs, church towers, scattered homesteads, and the gentle rise of woods and parkland that made the landscape so lovely in this part of Wessex. But even tucked away in flat pastures, it offered one view—the grand sweep of chalk downs, their lofty curves rising like steadfast sentinels beneath shifting skies.

The house was barely large enough to be called a mansion, moderately rented, without a manor attached, and difficult to let due to its somber furnishings and faded upholstery. Yet inside and out, it bore none of the marks of retired tradespeople—no one would suspect such inhabitants. That certainty alone was worth much to tenants who valued tradition over new finery and lived on the edge of rank, where status mattered deeply. To settle in a house once occupied by dowager countesses gave Mrs. Davilow a quiet satisfaction, a sense of legitimacy she had long lacked. This, though mysterious to Gwendolen, became possible only after the death of her stepfather, Captain Davilow, who for nine years had visited his family only briefly and sporadically—just enough to keep them reconciled to his absences. But Gwendolen cared little for the reason; she welcomed the change with open arms. Her prospects had brightened dramatically. She had disliked their former life—drifting from foreign spas to Parisian apartments, always encountering unfamiliar furniture and strangers who treated her as inconsequential. Even two years at a fashionable school, where she was placed front and center during every performance, had only deepened her conviction that someone as exceptional as herself deserved more than ordinary circumstances or a social standing less than distinguished. Any fear of falling short was now gone, for she knew her mother would soon have an establishment of her own. On matters of birth, Gwendolen felt secure. She didn’t know how her maternal grandfather had earned the fortune passed to his daughters—he’d been a West Indian, which seemed to end the inquiry—but she did know her father’s family was so high-born they ignored her mother entirely. Still, her mother proudly kept a miniature of Lady Molly, a relic of that connection. Gwendolen might have learned more about her father had it not been for a moment when she was twelve.

Mrs. Davilow had brought out, as she rarely did, memorials of her first husband. While showing Gwendolen his portrait, she spoke with fervor, clearly expecting a special bond of sympathy: “Dear papa died when his little daughter was still in long clothes.”

Gwendolen, thinking instantly of the unlovable stepfather who had shaped most of her life while her dresses were still short, asked quietly, “Why did you marry again, mamma? It would have been nicer if you hadn’t.”

Mrs. Davilow flushed deeply. A tremor passed across her face. Without hesitation, she shut away the relics and said, in a tone rare for her, “You have no feeling, child!”

Gwendolen, who adored her mother, felt both hurt and ashamed. From then on, she dared not ask another question about her father.

This was not the only time she had drawn guilt upon herself. Usually, when possible, she slept in a small bed beside her mother’s. Mrs. Davilow’s affection clung most fiercely to her eldest daughter, born during her happier years. One night, suffering from pain, she realized the medicine meant to be by her bedside had been forgotten. She begged Gwendolen to get up and fetch it. But the young woman, snug and warm in her tiny bed like a contented infant, refused. She lay perfectly still, grumbling her refusal. Mrs. Davilow went without the remedy and said nothing. But the next day, Gwendolen sensed what her mother must be thinking—and tried to make amends with tender gestures, though they cost her nothing. Always the pet of the household, waited on by mother, sisters, governess, and maids as if she were a princess in exile, she found it hard to see others’ needs as more important than her own. When thwarted, she reacted with startled anger—once, in a fit of rage, strangling her sister’s canary for its relentless singing. She later bought a white mouse as recompense, and though she justified herself by claiming a heightened sensitivity—a mark of her superiority—the memory of that act still made her flinch. Gwendolen was not cruel by nature; she simply preferred easy penance. Now, at twenty, some of her raw energy had hardened into self-control, shielding her from public shame. Her fire remained, but beneath it now ran calculation.

On the day of their arrival at Offendene—unknown even to Mrs. Davilow, who had left its arrangements to her brother-in-law, Mr. Gascoigne—the party stepped from the carriage and gathered under the porch. The door stood open, revealing a stone hall and staircase draped in dark paintings, warmed by a glowing hearth. No one spoke. Mamma, the four sisters, and the governess all turned to Gwendolen, as if their hopes rested entirely on her judgment. The girls—Alice, sixteen, to Isabel, ten—were plain, their black dresses worn. Miss Merry was elderly, expressionless. Mrs. Davilow’s beauty, though faded, looked more poignant for the appeal she cast toward her daughter, who surveyed the house, grounds, and interior with swift, critical eyes. Picture a young racehorse in a paddock among untrimmed ponies and weary hacks.

“Well, dear,” Mrs. Davilow finally said, gently, “what do you think of the place?”

“I think it’s charming,” Gwendolen replied quickly. “Romantic. Anything delightful might happen here. It would make a perfect backdrop for any story. No one need be ashamed to live here.”

“There’s certainly nothing common about it.”

“Oh, it would suit fallen royalty—or any kind of noble poverty. We should properly have lived in splendor and descended to this. It would be wonderfully romantic. But I thought Uncle and Aunt Gascoigne would be here to meet us—and my cousin Anna,” she added, her tone shifting to surprise.

“We’re early,” Mrs. Davilow said, stepping into the hall. “And you expect Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne?” she asked the housekeeper.

“Yes, madam. They were here yesterday, giving orders about the fires and dinner. As for fires, I’ve had them lit in every room for a week. Everything’s aired. I wish the furniture paid better for all the cleaning it’s received, but I *think* the brasses have been well attended to. I *think* when Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne arrive, they’ll say nothing has been neglected. They’ll be here at five, for certain.”

This reassured Gwendolen, who could not bear indifference to their arrival. After glancing up the matted stone stairs, she returned, followed by the girls, and inspected each room off the hall—the dining room, dark oak and worn red satin damask, with a painting of snarling dogs above the sideboard and Christ breaking bread over the mantel; the library, thick with the scent of old leather; and finally, the drawing room, entered through a small antechamber crowded with antique curiosities.

“Mamma, mamma, come here!” Gwendolen called, Mrs. Davilow still speaking with the housekeeper. “There’s an organ! I’ll be Saint Cecilia. Someone should paint me as Saint Cecilia. Jocosa—let down my hair. See, mamma?”

She had removed hat and gloves, seated herself before the organ in a striking pose, gazing upward. Jocosa, submissive and melancholy, untied the comb holding her hair and released the cascade of light-brown locks, which fell far below her waist.

Mrs. Davilow smiled. “A charming picture, my dear!”

Gwendolen rose, laughing with delight. All seemed fitting in a new home so rich in potential.

“What a curious, quaint, picturesque room!” she exclaimed, looking around. “I love these embroidered chairs, the garlands on the wainscot, the pictures—any of them could be anything. That one with the ribs—nothing but ribs and darkness—I think it’s Spanish, mamma.”

“Oh, Gwendolen!” cried little Isabel, opening a hinged panel at the far end of the room.

Everyone rushed to see. Behind the panel was a painting of a dead face turned upward, from which a shadowy figure fled with outstretched arms.

“How horrible!” said Mrs. Davilow, recoiling.

Gwendolen shuddered silently. Isabel, plain and oddly perceptive, whispered, “You’ll never stay in this room alone, Gwendolen.”

“How dare you open things meant to be shut, you perverse child?” Gwendolen snapped, snatching the panel and slamming it shut. “There’s a lock—where’s the key? Find it, or have one made. No one opens it again. Or rather—bring the key to me.”

Her voice rang with command. Turning, flushed from the chill of the moment, she said, “Let’s go to our room, mamma.”

The housekeeper found the key in a nearby cabinet drawer and handed it to Bugle, the lady’s-maid, murmuring, “For Her Royal Highness.”

“I don’t understand you, Mrs. Startin,” Bugle replied, offended. She’d been busy upstairs during the scene.

“I mean the young lady who will rule us all—and well deserves it, for looks and bearing,” Mrs. Startin said, softening. “She’ll know which key it is.”

“Go check on the others, Bugle,” Gwendolen said as they entered their black-and-yellow bedroom, where a delicate white couch sat beside the ornate bed known as the best. “I’ll help mamma.”

Her first move was to the tall mirror between the windows, which reflected her and the room in full. Her mother sat nearby, also studying the image.

“That’s a flattering glass, Gwendolen,” she said. “Or is it the black and gold that suits you?”

Gwendolen stood obliquely, brushing back her hair with one hand. “I’d make a decent Saint Cecilia with white roses in my hair—only, what about my nose, mamma? Saints’ noses never turn up. I wish you’d given me yours—straight, perfect, good for any role. Mine’s just a happy nose. Not suited for tragedy.”

“Oh, my dear,” Mrs. Davilow sighed, throwing her bonnet onto the table. “Any nose will do for sorrow in this world.”

“Now, mamma,” Gwendolen said, turning away from the mirror, vexed. “Don’t start being dull here. It ruins everything. Life could be so happy now. What’s troubling you?”

“Nothing, dear,” Mrs. Davilow said, beginning to undress. “Just seeing you happy is enough for me.”

“But you should be happy too,” Gwendolen insisted, helping her with gentle touches. “Can no one be happy after youth? You’ve made me feel sometimes that nothing matters. With the girls so troublesome, Jocosa so stiff and ugly, everything makeshift—and you so tired—what was the point of my being anything? But now you *could* be happy.”

“So I shall, dear,” Mrs. Davilow said, patting her daughter’s cheek.

“Yes, really,” Gwendolen pressed. “Not just pretending. Look at your hands—so beautiful. Anyone can see you were more beautiful than I am.”

“No, no,” Mrs. Davilow said. “I was heavier. Never half as charming.”

“But why does charm matter if it ends in dullness and indifference? Is that what marriage always becomes?”

“No, child,” she said. “Marriage is the happiest state for a woman—as I trust you’ll prove.”

“I won’t accept it if it isn’t happy,” Gwendolen declared. “I’m determined to be happy. At least, not to waste my life like others do—doing nothing remarkable. I’ve decided not to let anyone control me anymore. Here’s warm water for you, mamma,” she added, removing her own dress and waiting for her mother to wind her hair.

Silence fell for a moment. Then Mrs. Davilow, coiling her daughter’s hair, said softly, “I’ve never crossed you, Gwendolen.”

“You often want me to do things I dislike.”

“You mean teaching Alice?”

“Yes. I’ve done it because you asked. But why should I? It bores me. She’s slow—no ear for music, language, anything. It would be better for her to be ignorant, mamma. It’s her *role*. She’d play it well.”

“That’s harsh, Gwendolen,” Mrs. Davilow said. “She’s so kind to you, waits on you hand and foot.”

“I don’t see why it’s wrong to name things rightly. The hardship is mine—wasting my time.”

“Then let me fix your hair, mamma.”

“We must hurry. Your uncle and aunt will be here soon. For heaven’s sake, don’t be scornful to them, my dear. Or to Anna. Promise me, Gwendolen. You know—you can’t expect Anna to match you.”

“I don’t want her to,” Gwendolen said, tossing her head and smiling. The conversation ended there.

When Mr. and Mrs. Gascoigne and their daughter arrived, Gwendolen was anything but scornful. She greeted them with grace and charm. She was reintroducing herself to relatives she hadn’t seen since sixteen, and she was resolved—yes, resolved—that they would admire her.

Mrs. Gascoigne resembled her sister, but was darker, slimmer, her face untouched by grief, her movements sharper, her expression more alert—fit for a rector’s wife accustomed to quiet authority. Their deepest similarity was a tendency to defer, to imitate, to obey. But circumstance had led them to different lives. The younger sister had been reckless in marriage; the elder believed herself blessed, and her pliability had shaped her into surprising firmness. Many of her opinions—on church governance, Archbishop Laud—seemed too fixed to have formed naturally, suggesting instead a wifely receptiveness. And indeed, her husband’s influence was strong. He had virtues: agreeable, capable, admired. His flaws leaned toward success, not failure.

One of his strengths was his presence—perhaps even more striking at fifty-seven than earlier. His face bore no clerical stiffness, no affected ease. In his Inverness cape, he looked merely a gentleman—handsome, dark-eyed, with a nose that began aquiline but straightened abruptly, iron-gray hair. Perhaps his freedom from professional pretense came from having once been Captain Gaskin—only recently taking holy orders and a diphthong before marrying Miss Armyn. If criticized for inadequate preparation, friends might ask: who made a better figure in clerical robes? Who preached with greater authority? He had a natural gift for administration—tolerant of opinions and conduct because he felt able to manage them. He smiled at tastes he didn’t share—floriculture, antiquarianism—while preferring military campaigns or political speculation. His thinking had settled into an ecclesiastical tone, not modern Anglican, but what he called “sound English”—clear, free of nonsense. He saw religion in daylight, balanced against other truths. No magistrate carried more weight at sessions, nor more practical wisdom in worldly affairs. The worst charge against him was worldliness—though he never abandoned the poor, his friendships favored those likely to benefit his six sons and two daughters. Some bitter souls in Wessex once remarked his views shifted with his interests. But cheerful, successful worldliness appears more selfish than the acrid, failed kind—whose secret is summed up in the words: “Sold, but not paid for.”

Gwendolen wondered why she hadn’t remembered how impressive her uncle was. At sixteen, she’d been too careless to notice. Now, it thrilled her to have a dignified male relative close by—to break the insipid femininity of her life. She didn’t intend to be controlled by him, but she relished the idea of him being proud to introduce her as his niece. And already, signs pointed to pride. He looked at her with admiration as he said, “You’ve outgrown Anna, my dear,” placing his arm gently around his daughter, whose shy face was a miniature of his own. “She’s not quite a year younger, but her growth is finished. I hope you’ll be excellent companions.”

He glanced between them, and though he might have seen Anna’s limitations, he also saw that her timidity and smallness appealed to a different taste—one not drawn to Gwendolen’s boldness. The girls wouldn’t be rivals. Gwendolen understood this, and kissed Anna warmly, saying, “A companion is exactly what I want. I’m so glad we’re here. And mamma will be much happier now she’s near you, Aunt.”
