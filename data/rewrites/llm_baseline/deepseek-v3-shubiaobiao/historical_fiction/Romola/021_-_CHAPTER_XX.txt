The Carnival’s final week had seized Florence in its riotous grip. The streets pulsed with life—masked processions wove through the throng, voices rose in bawdy songs, and the *rigoletto* spun dancers beneath the crisp winter sky. Yet beneath the revelry lurked a darker current: boys, emboldened by the season’s chaos, barricaded alleys with poles, demanding tribute for their bonfires. Stones flew, mischief turned to menace, and the laughter of the crowd carried an edge.

A more refined observer might have winced at the spectacle—had a painter dared to capture its truth, the canvas would demand to be turned to the wall. But Florence’s blood ran quick, her mirth sharp as her quarrels; there was no northern sluggishness here, no southern stealth with a dagger. The city’s joy was bright, reckless, and fleeting.

Through this tumult strode Tito Melema, swift and purposeful, Bernardo Dovizi at his side. Their conversation was animated, their gestures lively, yet Tito’s eyes flickered intermittently over the crowd. At the turn toward the Ponte Rubaconte, his step faltered—there, weaving through the throng, was a face he had hoped not to see. *Tessa.*

The betrothal loomed that evening, an event meticulously prepared. Rooms in Bardo’s home had been refurbished with Tito’s florins; the date, moved forward at his insistence. Rome called him away—Cardinal Giovanni’s patronage was not to be refused—and he would not leave without the security of Romola’s pledge. The ceremony would bind them before law and custom, defying even Bernardo del Nero’s reservations.

Yet now, here was Tessa, her blue eyes wide with forlorn hope. Tito quickened his pace, steering Dovizi deftly toward the Via de’ Bardi. If he could reach Bardo’s door without her accosting him—

But fate was unkind. As he descended the steps afterward, a small hand clutched his sleeve. He turned sharply. "Tessa! You mustn’t follow me. Not here."

Her lips trembled. "The *patrigno* beats me," she whispered. "I waited—you never came—"

The street was too quiet, too perilous. A single witness—Bernardo, or worse—could unravel everything. Yet her tears pricked at him. He led her to the bridge, spoke in softened tones. "Listen, my little Tessa. I go to Rome, but I *will* return. Be patient. No one must know of us—*no one*—or I’ll be lost to you forever. Promise me."

She nodded, swallowing sobs. He pressed a kiss to her brow, then turned away without looking back.

---

The betrothal procession moved with stately grace toward Santa Croce, Bardo’s frail form guided by Tito and Romola. She was resplendent in white and gold, a lily crowned with pearls, her joy luminous. Tito, cloaked in velvet, carried a secret gift—a painted tabernacle, Piero’s work.

"Look," he murmured in the frescoed *salotto*, unveiling the triptych. Bacchus and Ariadne, entwined in gold. "Your sorrows are buried now. The crucifix is hidden—see? Only joy remains."

Romola’s fingers traced the images—the ship on calm seas, the ivy that never faded. "You’ve crowned my life," she whispered.

Yet as they stepped into the piazza, a funereal chant rose. A macabre procession loomed—Time on a black-shrouded car, the Hours wailing behind him. Romola stiffened. For a breath, Dino’s vision seemed to writhe to life.

Tito squeezed her hand. "A Carnival grotesquerie, nothing more."

But the chill lingered. As the torches flared, casting their glow on the newly betrothed, Romola’s gaze flickered backward—toward shadows not so easily dispelled.

*Forward*, Tito had urged. Yet the past, like the crucifix in its gilded tomb, was merely concealed. Not erased.

And the night deepened.
