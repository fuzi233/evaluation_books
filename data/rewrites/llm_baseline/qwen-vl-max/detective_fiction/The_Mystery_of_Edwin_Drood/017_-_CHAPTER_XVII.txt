Half a year had passed, and Mr. Crisparkle sat in the waiting room of the London headquarters of the Haven of Philanthropy, awaiting an audience with Mr. Honeythunder.

In his youth, during his college days, Mr. Crisparkle had been acquainted with professors of the Noble Art of boxing—men who trained in the ring and gathered for bouts under gloves and rules. Now, as he observed the men around him in this charitable institution, he found a striking resemblance between these so-called Professors of Philanthropy and those pugilists. The shape of their skulls, particularly at the back, bore a remarkable similarity—the same bulging contours that phrenologists associated with aggression, dominance, and a readiness to “pitch into” others. These philanthropists moved through the room with the same combative air he remembered from the old prizefighting circles: eyes sharp, shoulders squared, as if prepared for confrontation at any moment.

There was talk of organizing a moral campaign somewhere on the rural circuit—what might have been called a “moral mill.” Other members debated which speaker would deliver the most effective oratory, just as sporting publicans once discussed the merits of boxers before a match. One man, notably celebrated for his rhetorical flair on platforms, wore a black suit and struck Mr. Crisparkle as the living image of a long-dead benefactor known in his time as Frosty-faced Fogo—a man who once managed the ropes and stakes of the boxing ring. Only three differences stood out between these two kinds of "professors."

First, the philanthropists were poorly conditioned—overweight, flabby, their faces and figures bloated with what boxing experts call “suet pudding.” Second, they lacked the discipline and temper of true fighters; their language was coarse and bitter. Third—and most damning—their code of conduct was far more brutal than any fighting rulebook. They not only allowed themselves to wear down their opponents but also to attack them when down, strike them anywhere, kick, stomp, gouge, and slander them behind their backs without mercy. In these respects, the real pugilists were far nobler.

Mr. Crisparkle lost himself in thought, comparing the two worlds, while watching the constant flow of people entering and exiting—always seeming to snatch something from someone else, never giving anything in return. He didn’t notice his name being called until it was repeated twice.

When he finally responded, he was led by a shabby, underpaid assistant—so ill-dressed and dispirited that he seemed to serve an enemy of humanity rather than its champion—to Mr. Honeythunder’s office.

“Sir,” said Mr. Honeythunder in a voice like a schoolmaster reprimanding a disobedient pupil, “sit down.”

Mr. Crisparkle obeyed.

While Mr. Honeythunder signed the final few hundred of thousands of circulars demanding that poor families “stump up” and become philanthropists—or face damnation—a second shabby assistant collected the papers and carried them away.

"Now, Mr. Crisparkle," said Honeythunder, turning his chair half toward him, arms folded, brows furrowed, as though he meant to make short work of the matter, “we hold different views, you and I, on the sanctity of human life.”

"Do we?" asked the Minor Canon.

"We do, sir."

"Would you care to explain your view on that subject?"

"That human life is sacred, sir."

"And yours?"

"By George!" replied Honeythunder, leaning forward sharply, “that's best known to yourself.”

"Granted," said Crisparkle calmly. "But you began by claiming we differ. That implies you believe you know my views. So pray—what are they?"

"There is a young man," said Honeythunder, as if the youth made the tragedy worse, “swept off the face of the earth by violence. What do you call that?"

"Murder," said Crisparkle.

"And the one who did it?"

"A murderer."

"I'm glad you admit that much," retorted Honeythunder, stiffening further. "I didn’t expect it." He glared.

"Then perhaps you could clarify what you mean by those unjustifiable terms."

"I don’t sit here," said Honeythunder, raising his voice, "to be browbeaten."

"As the only other person present," replied Crisparkle quietly, "no one knows better than I do. But I interrupt your explanation."

"Murder!" thundered Honeythunder, falling into a kind of theatrical reverie, folding his arms, nodding slowly after each word. "Bloodshed! Abel! Cain! I hold no terms with Cain. I recoil from the red hand when it’s offered me!"

Instead of leaping to his feet and shouting as the Brotherhood would have done, Crisparkle simply uncrossed his legs and said, “Don’t let me interrupt your explanation—when you begin it.”

"The Commandments say: No murder. NO murder, sir!" continued Honeythunder, pausing dramatically as if Crisparkle had dared suggest murder was permissible in moderation.

"And they also say: You shall bear no false witness," observed Crisparkle.

"Enough!" bellowed Honeythunder, with such solemn severity that it would have silenced a crowd. "E—e—nough! My late wards are now of age. I am released from a trust I cannot contemplate without horror. Here are the accounts you’ve agreed to accept on their behalf, and here is the balance statement you’ve undertaken to receive—and should receive without delay. And let me tell you, sir, I wish you were better employed—as a man and a Minor Canon." He nodded once. "Better employed." Another nod. "Bet—ter em—ployed!" A third nod, all added together.

Crisparkle rose, slightly flushed but composed.

"Mr. Honeythunder," he said, picking up the papers, "whether I am better or worse employed is a matter of opinion. You might think me better off enrolling as a member of your Society."

"Ay, indeed, sir!" snapped Honeythunder, shaking his head threateningly. "It would have been better for you if you had done that long ago!"

"I disagree."

"Or," he continued, shaking his head again, "I might think one of your profession better occupied in uncovering guilt than leaving it to laymen."

"My profession," replied Crisparkle, "teaches me that its first duty lies with those in need, those oppressed, desolate, and suffering. But since I’ve concluded it’s not my role to make professions, I’ll say no more. Yet I owe it to Mr. Neville, to his sister—and to myself—to say this: I *know* I understood Mr. Neville’s mind and heart at the time of this event. I see nothing to conceal or excuse in him that requires correction, and I am certain his story is true. Because I believe that, I stand by him. As long as that belief holds, I will stand by him. If any force could shake me, I’d be ashamed of my weakness—so ashamed that no one’s approval, man or woman, could compensate for the loss of my own self-respect."

A good man. A brave man. And modest too. There was no boastfulness in him—only quiet, steadfast loyalty, like a schoolboy keeping wicket in a field. True souls are always thus. Always have been. Always will be. Greatness of spirit has no smallness.

"So then," asked Honeythunder, turning abruptly, "who do you think committed the deed?"

"Heaven forbid," said Crisparkle, "that in seeking to clear one man I should lightly accuse another. I accuse no one."

"Tcha!" Honeythunder spat with contempt. This was not how the Brotherhood operated. "And remember, sir—you are not an impartial witness."

"How am I biased?" Crisparkle asked, genuinely puzzled.

"There was a stipend paid to you for your pupil," said Honeythunder bluntly, "which may have influenced your judgment."

"Perhaps I expect to keep it?" Crisparkle replied, catching on. "Do you mean that too?"

"Well, sir," said Honeythunder, rising and stuffing his hands into his trousers pockets, "I don’t go about measuring people for caps. If they find one that fits, they can wear it. That’s their business, not mine."

Crisparkle looked at him with growing indignation.

"Mr. Honeythunder," he said, "I hoped to avoid commenting on the intrusion of platform tactics into private conversation. But you've given me no choice. Your behavior is detestable."

"They don’t suit you, I suppose," Honeythunder muttered.

"They are," Crisparkle continued, ignoring the interruption, "detestable. They violate both Christian justice and gentlemanly restraint. You assume a great crime was committed by someone I, knowing the circumstances and having strong reasons, firmly believe innocent. Because I disagree with you on this point, what is your response? Instantly turn on me, accusing me of not understanding the crime’s enormity—and of aiding it! Next time, you set up a platform credulity: a motion passed unanimously in favor of some absurd delusion. I refuse to believe it, and you declare I believe nothing—because I won't bow to your false god, I deny the true God! Then you claim war is a calamity and propose abolishing it with a series of empty resolutions. I reject your claim and your remedy, and you accuse me of reveling in battlefields like a fiend! Or you condemn the sober for the drunken, and when I defend the sober, you proclaim I want to turn God’s creatures into swine! In every case, your supporters—your regular Professors—run wild like mad Malays, attributing the worst motives recklessly, quoting statistics skewed as badly as a ledger with only one side. Therefore, Mr. Honeythunder, I consider the platform a bad influence even in public life—but in private, it becomes unbearable."

"These are strong words, sir!" exclaimed Honeythunder.

"I hope so," said Crisparkle. "Good morning."

He left the Haven quickly, but soon settled into his usual brisk stride. A smile touched his lips as he walked, wondering what the china shepherdess would have said if she'd seen him take on Honeythunder. He had just enough harmless vanity to believe he’d struck hard—and to glow with pride that he’d trimmed the Philanthropic Jacket neatly.

He went to Staple Inn—not to P. J. T. or Mr. Grewgious—but climbed many creaking stairs to reach attic rooms in a corner. He turned the latch of the unbolted door and stepped inside, finding Neville Landless at his table.

The rooms had an air of retreat and solitude. Both the space and its occupant were worn. Sloping ceilings, rusty locks, heavy beams—all slowly decaying—gave the place a prison-like feel. Neville had the haggard look of a prisoner. Yet sunlight streamed through the ugly garret window, which jutted out among the tiles. On the cracked, smoke-blackened ledge outside, sparrows hopped rheumatically, like tiny crippled birds who’d left their crutches behind. Nearby, leaves rustled gently, creating a faint music that would have been melody in the country.

The rooms were sparsely furnished but filled with books. Everything spoke of a poor student. Crisparkle’s presence among them—whether as chooser, lender, or donor—was evident in the warm glance he cast upon them as he entered.

“How goes it, Neville?”

“I’m in good heart, Mr. Crisparkle, working steadily.”

“I wish your eyes weren’t so large and so bright,” said Crisparkle, releasing the hand he’d taken.

“They brighten at the sight of you,” Neville replied. “If you were to leave me, they’d soon grow dull.”

“Rally, rally!” urged Crisparkle, encouragingly. “Fight for it, Neville!”

“If I were dying,” said Neville, “a word from you would revive me. If my pulse stopped, your touch would make it beat again. But I’ve rallied, and I’m doing well.”

Crisparkle turned Neville’s face toward the light.

“I want to see a ruddier flush here,” he said, pointing to his own healthy cheek. “More sun must shine on you.”

Neville drooped slightly.

“I’m not strong enough for that yet,” he whispered. “I may become so, but I can’t bear it now. If you’d walked those Cloisterham streets as I did—if you’d seen the averted eyes, the silent distance people gave me so I wouldn’t touch them—you wouldn’t think it unreasonable that I still avoid daylight.”

“My poor fellow,” said Crisparkle, his voice full of sympathy. “I never thought it unreasonable. But I’d like you to try.”

“And that would give me reason to,” Neville said. “But I can’t yet. I can’t convince myself that strangers’ eyes don’t suspect me. I feel marked—even at night. But darkness hides me. It gives me courage.”

Crisparkle laid a hand on his shoulder, looking down.

“If I could change my name,” said Neville, “I would. But as you wisely told me, I can’t—it would seem like guilt. If I could flee to another land, I might find peace. But that’s impossible—same reason. Hiding would be seen as guilt. It feels harsh to be tied to a stake, innocent though I am. But I don’t complain.”

“And you mustn’t expect a miracle, Neville,” said Crisparkle, kindly.

“No, sir. I know that. Only time and circumstance can help me.”

“It will right you, Neville.”

“I believe it. I hope I live to see it.”

Seeing that his mood was slipping and sensing the hand on his shoulder tremble slightly, Neville lifted his spirits.

“Excellent conditions for study, anyway!” he said. “And you know how much I need it. Not to mention your advice to study law—especially law. I follow your guidance, of course. Such a good friend and helper!”

He took Crisparkle’s hand from his shoulder and kissed it. Crisparkle smiled at the books—though not as brightly as when he’d entered.

“I gather from your silence that my late guardian opposes you, Mr. Crisparkle?”

“The man is utterly unreasonable,” replied Crisparkle. “Whether he’s adverse, perverse, or reversed matters not to anyone sensible.”

“Well for me,” sighed Neville, “that I can live on economy while I learn and wait to be cleared. Else I might prove the proverb: while the grass grows, the steed starves!”

He opened his books and soon immersed himself in annotated pages, while Crisparkle sat beside him, guiding, correcting, advising. His Cathedral duties made these visits rare—coming only every few weeks—but they were invaluable to Neville.

After their studies ended, they leaned on the windowsill, looking down at the garden patch.

“Next week,” said Crisparkle, “you’ll cease to be alone. You’ll have a devoted companion.”

“And yet,” replied Neville, “this seems an unsuitable place for my sister.”

“I don’t think so,” said Crisparkle. “There’s duty here. And a woman’s courage and sense are needed.”

“I meant,” explained Neville, “the surroundings are dull, unwomanly. Helena has no proper society here.”

“You only need to remember,” said Crisparkle, “that you’re here—and she has to draw you into the sunlight.”

They fell silent. Then Crisparkle began again.

“When we first spoke, Neville, you told me your sister had risen above your past, as the tower of Cloisterham Cathedral towers over the chimneys of Minor Canon Corner. Do you remember?”

“Perfectly.”

“I thought it enthusiastic then. I don’t care what I think now. What matters is this: under the head of Pride, your sister is a powerful example.”

“Under all heads that form a noble character, she is.”

“Say so. But take this one. She has learned to govern her pride. Even when wounded by sympathy for you, she masters it. She has suffered deeply in the same streets you did. Her life is darkened by the same shadow. Yet she bends her pride into quiet dignity—not haughtiness, but steady confidence in you and in truth. She walks those streets now with honor, respected by all. Every day since Edwin Drood vanished, she has faced malice and folly—for you—only a brave soul can endure. She will continue so. A weaker pride might break—but hers never will. It knows no fear, and no power over her.”

The pale cheek beside him flushed with emotion.

“I’ll do all I can to imitate her,” said Neville.

“Do so,” replied Crisparkle firmly. “Be a truly brave man, as she is a truly brave woman. It’s growing dark. Will you walk with me when it’s fully dark? Mind—I’m not the one waiting for darkness.”

Neville agreed. But Crisparkle said he had a brief courtesy call to make on Mr. Grewgious, and would cross to his chambers and return to Neville’s door.

Mr. Grewgious sat upright as ever, drinking wine at his open window. His glass and decanter rested on a round table beside him; his legs stretched out on the windowsill. He had only one hinge in his body—like a bootjack.

“How do you do, reverend sir?” said Grewgious, offering hospitality which Crisparkle declined with equal warmth. “And how is your charge getting on in the rooms I recommended to you?”

Crisparkle replied appropriately.

“I’m glad you approve,” said Grewgious. “Because I have a fancy for keeping an eye on him.”

As Grewgious had to tilt his head upward to see the chamber, the phrase was clearly metaphorical.

“And how did you leave Mr. Jasper, reverend sir?”

“Pretty well.”

“And where?”

“At Cloisterham.”

“And when?”

“This morning.”

“Umps!” said Grewgious. “He didn’t say he was coming?”

“Coming where?”

“Anywhere, for instance?”

“No.”

“Because here he is,” said Grewgious, who had been scanning the street outside. “And he doesn’t look agreeable, does he?”

Crisparkle leaned toward the window just as Grewgious added:

“Look.”
