Wall Street, the next day, breathed easier about Beaufort.

Nothing definite—only that vague, well-fed confidence New York reserved for men who knew whom to send for when the ledgers turned ugly. The rumor went round that he had “tided over,” and in the evening, when Mrs. Julius Beaufort appeared in her opera box with her practiced smile set like icing and a new emerald necklace catching the gaslight in cold green sparks, society let out the breath it had been holding.

New York could be merciless about money, about the clean, unspoken code that was as strict as any catechism; but it could also be selfishly relieved. The Beauforts, whatever else they were, had been useful—an easy center of heat and motion in the winter season, the best ballroom, the most obliging hospitality. Their fall would be painful; it would also be inconvenient.

Archer, meanwhile, had made up his mind to go to Washington. He had been waiting for the opening of the lawsuit he had mentioned to May, so he might braid the dates together and give his journey the proper professional look; but on Tuesday Mr. Letterblair told him the case might be postponed several weeks. Archer went home that afternoon with a decision that tasted like iron at the back of his throat: postponed or not, he would leave the next evening. May knew so little of his work, and cared so little for its minutiæ, that the postponement would pass by her like a dish carried through a room she was not hungry to enter. And he could no longer endure the delay of seeing Madame Olenska. There were too many things in him, unsaid, pressing upward like heat under a lid.

But on Wednesday morning, as soon as Archer reached his office, Mr. Letterblair met him with a face gone slack and gray, as if all the color had been wrung out of it.

Beaufort, after all, had not managed to tide over.

The “reassuring reports” had been the cleverest, most poisonous seasoning—sprinkled to keep depositors calm while money poured in. For a day the bank had seemed to fatten; then, the previous evening, the air had changed. Disturbing whispers began to outnumber hopeful ones. When the doors opened, a run began. Before the day was done, the doors would likely close for good.

Now the ugliest words were being spoken of Beaufort’s maneuver—words that stuck to the palate and made one want to spit. His failure promised to be among the most discreditable in Wall Street history, and the sheer breadth of the calamity left Mr. Letterblair shaken, almost helpless.

“I’ve seen bad things in my time,” he said, and his voice sounded like paper dragged over a rasp. “But nothing as bad as this. Everybody we know will be hit, one way or another. And what will be done about Mrs. Beaufort? What can be done about her? I pity Mrs. Manson Mingott as much as anybody—coming at her age, there’s no knowing what effect this affair may have. She always believed in Beaufort—she made a friend of him! And there’s the whole Dallas connection: poor Mrs. Beaufort is related to every one of you. Her only chance would be to leave her husband—yet how can any one tell her so? Her duty is at his side; and luckily she seems always to have been blind to his private weaknesses.”

There was a knock. Letterblair turned sharply, as if sound itself were an insult. “What is it? I can’t be disturbed.”

A clerk brought in a letter for Archer and withdrew.

Archer knew the hand at once. May’s writing, with its round, young confidence, always looked to him like something piped onto a cake—pretty, decided, not to be argued with. He broke the seal and read:

“Won’t you please come up town as early as you can? Granny had a slight stroke last night. In some mysterious way she found out before any one else this awful news about the bank. Uncle Lovell is away shooting, and the idea of the disgrace has made poor Papa so nervous that he has a temperature and can’t leave his room. Mamma needs you dreadfully, and I do hope you can get away at once and go straight to Granny’s.”

He handed the note to Mr. Letterblair. A few minutes later he was crawling northward in a crowded horse-car, the wheels grinding over frozen ruts with a jarring rhythm that climbed into his bones. At Fourteenth Street he climbed up to one of the high, swaying omnibuses of the Fifth Avenue line. The city’s midday clatter—hooves, iron rims, calls, the harsh squeal of harness—seemed louder than usual, as if panic in the financial district had sharpened every edge of sound and carried it uptown.

It was after twelve when the lumbering vehicle dropped him at old Catherine’s.

The ground-floor sitting-room window where Mrs. Mingott usually sat enthroned was occupied by the inadequate figure of her daughter, Mrs. Welland, who made him a haggard sign of welcome the moment she saw him. May met him in the doorway.

The hall had that unnatural look well-kept houses take on when illness breaks in—like a spotless kitchen suddenly turned into a sickroom. Wraps and furs lay in heaps on chairs as if flung down without thought; a doctor’s bag and overcoat rested on the table; beside them, cards and letters already began to pile up, ignored like cooling dishes no one had appetite for. Even the silence had a different quality here: not peace, but a strained quiet in which every distant footfall and door-click carried too far.

May looked pale but smiling; Dr. Bencomb, who had just come for the second time, took a more hopeful view, and Mrs. Mingott’s bulldog will to live and get well was already beginning to steady her household.

May led Archer into the old lady’s sitting-room. The sliding doors that opened into the bedroom had been drawn shut and covered by heavy yellow damask portières that held the light back as though it were too loud. There, in horrified undertones, Mrs. Welland gave him the details.

Something dreadful and mysterious, it seemed, had happened the night before.

At about eight o’clock, just after Mrs. Mingott had finished the game of solitaire she always played after dinner—an old, domestic ritual as fixed as the pouring of tea—the door-bell rang. A lady, so thickly veiled the servants did not at first recognize her, asked to be received. The butler, catching a familiar voice beneath the veil, flung open the sitting-room door and announced, with the solemnity of an ill-omened ingredient added to a broth: “Mrs. Julius Beaufort”—and closed the door again on the two women.

They were together, he thought, about an hour.

When Mrs. Mingott’s bell rang, Mrs. Beaufort had already slipped away unseen, like a scent that vanishes as soon as you turn to name it. And there the old lady sat—white, vast, terrible—in her great chair, and signed to be helped into her room. She had seemed, at that time, though plainly distressed, in full command of her mind and body. The mulatto maid put her to bed, brought her her usual cup of tea—tea brewed with the same measured care as always, because households like this clung to routine the way hands clung to a rail in a storm—straightened everything, and went away.

But at three in the morning the bell rang again. The summons was so unusual that both servants hastened in at once, for old Catherine ordinarily slept “like a baby.” They found their mistress sitting up against her pillows, a crooked smile on her face, one small hand hanging limp from its huge arm.

The stroke had clearly been slight; she could articulate and make her wishes known, and after the doctor’s first visit she began to regain control of her face. But the terror had been real; and when it was pieced together, from her thickened fragments of speech, why Mrs. Beaufort had come, indignation rose in the room like steam.

Regina Beaufort had come to ask her—effrontery beyond belief—to back up her husband, to “see them through,” not to “desert” them, as she called it. In short: to induce the whole family to cover and condone their monstrous dishonour.

“I said to her: ‘Honour’s always been honour, and honesty honesty, in Manson Mingott’s house, and will be till I’m carried out of it feet first,’” the old woman had stammered into her daughter’s ear, her words thick, her breath coming with effort.

“And when she said: ‘But my name, Auntie—my name’s Regina Dallas,’ I said: ‘It was Beaufort when he covered you with jewels, and it’s got to stay Beaufort now that he’s covered you with shame.’”

So much, with tears and gasps, Mrs. Welland delivered, blanched and demolished by the obligation—so unfamiliar to her—of having to look directly at what was ugly, discreditable, and loud in its consequences.

“If only I could keep it from your father-in-law,” she wailed, hands trembling as if she could not hold her own thoughts steady. “He always says: ‘Augusta, for pity’s sake, don’t destroy my last illusions’—and how am I to prevent his knowing these horrors?”

“After all, Mamma, he won’t have seen them,” May suggested, trying to set her voice like a cup carefully back on a saucer.

Mrs. Welland sighed. “Ah, no; thank heaven he’s safe in bed. And Dr. Bencomb has promised to keep him there till poor Mamma is better, and Regina has been got away somewhere.”

Archer had sat down near the window and was looking out at the deserted street. Even the avenue’s usual music seemed hushed at that hour, as if the very traffic kept its distance from illness and scandal. He could feel that he had been summoned less for anything practical he could do than to stand there and lend, by sheer presence, a kind of masculine steadiness to the frightened women.

Mr. Lovell Mingott had been telegraphed for; messengers were out with notes and telegrams to the family members in New York. Meanwhile there was nothing to do but whisper about consequences—about Beaufort’s dishonour, about Mrs. Beaufort’s unjustifiable visit.

Mrs. Lovell Mingott, who had been writing notes in another room, came back and joined the discussion. In their day, the elder ladies agreed, when a man had done something disgraceful in business the wife had but one idea: to efface herself, to disappear with him.

“There was the case of poor Grandmamma Spicer; your great-grandmother, May,” Mrs. Welland said, eager, as always, to wrap the present in the gauze of precedent. “Of course,” she added hastily, “your great-grandfather’s money difficulties were private—losses at cards, or signing a note for somebody—I never quite knew, because Mamma would never speak of it. But she was brought up in the country because her mother had to leave New York after the disgrace, whatever it was: they lived up the Hudson alone, winter and summer, till Mamma was sixteen. It would never have occurred to Grandmamma Spicer to ask the family to ‘countenance’ her, as I understand Regina calls it; though a private disgrace is nothing compared to the scandal of ruining hundreds of innocent people.”

“Yes,” Mrs. Lovell Mingott agreed, “it would be more becoming in Regina to hide her own countenance than to talk about other people’s. I understand that the emerald necklace she wore at the Opera last Friday had been sent on approval from Ball and Black’s in the afternoon. I wonder if they’ll ever get it back?”

Archer listened, unmoved by the relentless chorus. The law of financial probity—clean accounts, clean hands—was in him like a native tongue: not learned, but inherited, spoken without translation. No sentimental pity could make a Beaufort excusable in that dialect. An adventurer like Lemuel Struthers might make his Shoe Polish millions on any number of questionable tricks; but old New York, for all its hypocrisies, still expected its own kind of honesty from the men who claimed to belong to it.

Nor did Mrs. Beaufort’s fate greatly stir him. He felt more sorry for her than did her outraged relatives; yet it seemed to him that the tie between husband and wife, even if society quietly treated it as breakable in prosperity, should be indissoluble in misfortune. A wife’s place was at her husband’s side when he was in trouble. But society’s place was not at his side; and Mrs. Beaufort’s cool assumption that it should be—her attempt to enlist the Family as a shield against the one thing it could not forgive—made her seem almost an accomplice.

The mulatto maid called Mrs. Lovell Mingott into the hall. She returned in a moment, frowning.

“She wants me to telegraph for Ellen Olenska,” she said. “I had written to Ellen, of course, and to Medora; but now it seems that’s not enough. I’m to telegraph immediately, and tell her she’s to come alone.”

Silence received this, a silence that had its own sound—like the pause in a room when a kettle stops singing and the ear strains, waiting. Mrs. Welland sighed as if surrendering to the inevitable. May rose and began gathering up newspapers scattered on the floor, smoothing them as though tidiness could press down calamity.

“I suppose it must be done,” Mrs. Lovell Mingott continued, almost hoping someone would contradict her.

“Of course it must be done,” May said, and the name she spoke had a peculiar distinctness, as if she had tapped two small silver bells to make sure everyone heard. “Granny knows what she wants, and we must carry out all her wishes. Shall I write the telegram for you, Auntie? If it goes at once Ellen can probably catch tomorrow morning’s train.”

“Well, it can’t go at once. Jasper and the pantry-boy are both out with notes and telegrams.”

May turned to Archer with a smile that was both dutiful and bright. “But here’s Newland, ready to do anything. Will you take the telegram, Newland? There’ll be just time before luncheon.”

He rose with a murmur of readiness. She seated herself at old Catherine’s rosewood bonheur du jour and wrote out the message in her large, immature hand. When it was finished she blotted it carefully and handed it to him.

“What a pity,” she said, “that you and Ellen will cross each other on the way!—Newland,” she added, turning toward her mother and aunt, “is obliged to go to Washington about a patent law-suit that is coming up before the Supreme Court. I suppose Uncle Lovell will be back by tomorrow night, and with Granny improving so much it doesn’t seem right to ask Newland to give up an important engagement for the firm—does it?”

She paused as if waiting for the correct response to be served to her. Mrs. Welland, prompt with approval, declared: “Oh, of course not, darling. Your Granny would be the last person to wish it.”

As Archer left the room with the telegram in his hand, he heard his mother-in-law add—presumably to Mrs. Lovell Mingott—“But why on earth she should make you telegraph for Ellen Olenska—” and May’s clear voice answer at once: “Perhaps it’s to urge on her again that after all her duty is with her husband.”

The outer door closed behind Archer. He walked quickly toward the telegraph office, the message in his pocket feeling heavier than paper, and the city’s noon noises rising again around him, rough as a tide he had to push through.
