Colonel Brandon's abrupt departure from the Park, coupled with his unyielding resolve to hide the true nature of his business, occupied Mrs. Jennings' thoughts with relentless fascination for several days. A natural speculator by temperament, her mind was a fertile ground for every possible calamity. With barely a moment's pause between theories, she convinced herself that something dreadfully wrong must have occurred. "Some terrible news," she murmured, tracing lines of sorrow across his retreating figure. "Perhaps his finances are ruined. That estate at Delaford yielded little, and I heard his brother left a mess behind. He must have been summoned to bail out some disaster!" Yet, another thought quickly took root: "It concerns Miss Williams." The memory of his startled look when her name was dropped fueled this suspicion. "Maybe she is ill back in London," she speculated, "or perhaps his sister at Avignon is worse. Whatever it is, I hope he finds relief soon, and that he secures a worthy wife before long."

Her musings swayed like wind-blown grass, shifting from financial ruin to romantic misfortune with every fresh conjecture. Elinor listened, her heart truly drawn to Colonel Brandon's welfare, yet she could not share the depth of her mother's bewilderment regarding his sudden exit. In her view, the circumstance demanded neither lasting amazement nor a kaleidoscope of wild guesses. Instead, her curiosity was fixated on a far stranger mystery: the profound silence surrounding the relationship between Willoughby and Marianne.

For days, neither young man had uttered a word to confirm what their actions clearly proclaimed. To Elinor, this inexplicable quietude seemed entirely at odds with their natures. If marriage lay beyond their immediate reach due to Willoughby's limited income—a mere six or seven hundred a year, squandered on a lifestyle far above his means—they might need time to arrange affairs. But why conceal their engagement so thoroughly from those who cared most for them? The contradiction gnawed at her, creating a shadow of doubt in her mind about whether they were truly affianced at all. This hesitation acted as a shield, preventing her from pressing Marianne for answers.

Yet, Willoughby's devotion to the family was undeniable. To Marianne, he offered a tenderness that transcended ordinary friendship; to the wider household, he was a model of filial and fraternal care. The cottage became his sanctuary, a space he cherished more deeply than his own residence at Allenham. Whether driven by duty or love, his mornings invariably ended there, leaving him to spend the day beside Marianne, with her faithful pointer dog resting at her feet.

One evening, roughly a week after Colonel Brandon's mysterious flight, Willoughby's heart appeared more transparent than usual. When Mrs. Dashwood casually suggested renovating the cottage in the spring, Willoughby sprang to his feet, his opposition fierce and immediate.

"What!" he exclaimed, his voice ringing with indignation. "Improve this dear cottage? Never! Not a single stone shall be added to these walls, not an inch to its size, if you value my feelings."

"Do not be alarmed," Elinor gently interjected. "There is no intention to alter a thing; Mother simply lacks the funds to undertake any project anyway."

"I am genuinely relieved," Willoughby cried, his relief palpable. "May she remain poor forever if it spares her from spending her riches on such changes."

"Thank you, Willoughby," Elinor smiled. "Rest assured, I would never sacrifice your sentiment of local attachment, nor that of anyone I hold dear, for the grandeur of the world. Whatever surplus remains after settling accounts this spring, I would rather leave it idle than invest it in a way that pains you. However, surely you admit the house has flaws?"

"I do," he confessed earnestly. "To me, it is flawless. Indeed, I regard it as the only architectural form capable of housing happiness. Were I wealthy enough, I would tear down Combe Abbey instantly and rebuild it exactly in the likeness of this humble cottage."

"With its dark, narrow stairs and a kitchen that fills with smoke?" Elinor teased lightly.

"Yes!" he shouted, his enthusiasm undampened. "With every imperfection intact! In no single convenience or discomfort would I allow a variation. Only under such a roof, surrounded by these familiar defects, could I perhaps find contentment at Combe Abbey equal to what I've known at Barton Cottage."

"I trust," Elinor replied, her gaze softening, "that even with superior accommodations and a broader staircase, you will eventually find your own home just as perfect as this one."

"There are certainly aspects of it that charm me greatly," Willoughby admitted, his expression turning tender. "But this place holds a unique claim on my affection—one that no other location can ever share."

Mrs. Dashwood glanced with evident delight at Marianne, whose large, expressive eyes were locked onto Willoughby, silently echoing his sentiments.

"How frequently," Willoughby continued, lowering his voice as he addressed Marianne directly, "did I wish, when I last stayed at Allenham twelve months ago, that Barton Cottage was inhabited! I passed it daily, marveling at its setting and mourning the emptiness within. Who could have guessed that the first piece of news I received upon returning to the countryside would be that Barton Cottage was finally occupied? And what a surge of satisfaction and deep personal interest I felt! Nothing short of a premonition of the joy I have since experienced can explain it. Is that not so, Marianne?"

Turning back to Mrs. Dashwood, his tone shifted again, laced with a gentle reproach. "And yet, you would spoil this home? You would strip it of its simple beauty under the guise of 'improvement!' You would degrade this parlor—the very chamber where our acquaintance began, and where countless happy hours have since been shared—to the status of a mere antechamber. Soon, everyone will trample through the room that has offered more genuine comfort and warmth than any opulent apartment in existence."

Mrs. Dashwood, reassured by his words, promised firmly that no such alterations would ever take place.

"You are a kind soul," Willoughby declared, his warmth returning. "Your assurance brings me peace. Extend it further, and you will bring me true happiness. Promise me that not only will your house remain untouched, but that I shall always find you and your family as unchanged as your dwelling. Promise that you will continue to regard me with the kindness that has rendered everything connected to you so precious to me."

The promise was granted willingly, and throughout the evening, Willoughby's behavior radiated a mixture of deep affection and profound contentment.

"As we part, may I ask if I am to dine with you tomorrow?" Mrs. Dashwood inquired as he prepared to leave. "I cannot invite you to breakfast, for we intend to walk to the Park to pay our respects to Lady Middleton."

"It would be my honor," Willoughby replied, bowing low. "I shall be with you by four o'clock."
