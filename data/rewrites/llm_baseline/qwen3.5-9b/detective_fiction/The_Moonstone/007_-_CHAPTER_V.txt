The moment we were left alone on the beach, my first impulse was to rise from my seat in the sand. Mr. Franklin, however, held me back.

"There is one comfort in this dreadful spot," he remarked, "that we have the entire place to ourselves. Remain seated, Betteredge; I require a word with you."

As he spoke, I studied him, searching for traces of the boy I remembered within the man standing before me. The resemblance was gone. Where once there had been rosy cheeks and a trim little jacket, there was now a pale complexion and a face obscured by a curly brown beard and mustache. He possessed a lively, erratic manner that was pleasant enough, yet it lacked the easy charm of his youth. Worse still, he had failed to keep his promise regarding his height; though neat, slim, and well-made, he fell far short of the middle stature he had boasted of. In short, he baffled me completely. The years had stripped him of almost everything, save for the bright, direct light in his eyes. There, amidst the changes, I finally found the essence of the old boy, and decided to cease my scrutiny.

"Welcome back to the old place, Mr. Franklin," I said.

"All the more welcome, sir, since you arrived several hours ahead of schedule," I added.

"I have reasons for arriving early," Franklin replied. "I suspect, Betteredge, that I have been followed and watched in London for the past three or four days. Consequently, I traveled on the morning train rather than the afternoon one, hoping to lose sight of a particular dark-complexioned stranger."

These words struck me with force, instantly recalling the three jugglers and Penelope's fears that they intended harm to Mr. Franklin.

"Who is watching you, sir—and why?" I asked.

Before I could pursue the line of inquiry, Franklin shifted the topic abruptly. "Tell me about the three Indians who visited your house today," he commanded, ignoring my question. "It is merely possible, Betteredge, that my mysterious stranger and your three jugglers are pieces of the same puzzle."

"How do you happen to know about the jugglers, sir?" I stammered, piling question upon question—a breach of etiquette, I grant you—but human nature is rarely perfect, nor am I.

"I saw Penelope at the house," Franklin explained. "Penelope informed me. Your daughter promised to be a lovely girl, Betteredge, and she has fulfilled that vow. Penelope possesses a small ear and a small foot. Did the late Mrs. Betteredge share these inestimable assets?"

"The late Mrs. Betteredge possessed many flaws, sir," I retorted. "One of them, if you'll forgive my candor, was an inability to stay on the subject at hand. She was more akin to a fly than a woman; she could not settle on anything."

"She would have suited me perfectly," Franklin admitted. "Neither do I. Betteredge, your 'edge' is sharper than ever. Your daughter noted as much when I requested details regarding the jugglers. 'Father will tell you,' she said. 'He is a remarkable man for his age, and he expresses himself beautifully.' Penelope's own words, delivered with divine blushing. My respect for you did not prevent me from—well, never mind. I knew her as a child, and she remains unchanged. Let us be serious. What exactly did the jugglers do?"

A flicker of dissatisfaction crossed my mind—not because Franklin kissed my daughter, a gesture I welcomed wholeheartedly—but because he forced me to recount her foolish tale at second hand. Yet, there was no alternative but to speak. As I began, Franklin's earlier cheerfulness vanished. He knitted his brows and twisted his beard. Upon hearing my account, he repeated two of the questions posed by the chief juggler to the boy, seemingly to cement them in his memory.

"'Is it on the road to this house, and on no other, that the English gentleman will travel today?' 'Has the English gentleman got *It* about him?'"

Franklin paused, then produced a small, sealed packet from his pocket. "I suspect," he declared, "that '*It*' refers to *this*. And '*this*', Betteredge, refers to my Uncle Herncastle's famous Diamond."

"Good heavens, sir!" I exclaimed. "How do you find yourself in charge of the wicked Colonel's Diamond?"

"My father's will, acting as the wicked Colonel's executor, entrusted the Diamond to me as a birthday gift for my cousin, Rachel," Franklin stated calmly.

If the sea, slowly encroaching over the Shivering Sands, had transformed into dry land before my eyes, I doubt I could have been more astonished.

"The Colonel's Diamond left to Miss Rachel!" I cried. "And your father, sir, the Colonel's executor! I would wager my head that your father would not so much as touch the Colonel with a pair of tongs!"

"Strong language, Betteredge! What was there against the Colonel? He belongs to your era, not mine. Recount what you know of him, and I shall explain how my father became his executor, along with other matters. I have made disturbing discoveries in London concerning my uncle Herncastle and his Diamond. I wish for you to corroborate them. You just called him the 'wicked Colonel.' Search your memory, my old friend, and tell me why."

Seeing his seriousness, I obliged. For your sake, I shall write out the substance of what I told him below. Please pay close attention, lest you become lost when we delve deeper into the narrative. Clear your minds of children, dinners, new bonnets, politics, horses, city prices, and club grievances. I trust you will not resent this liberty; it is simply my method of addressing the gentle reader. Have you not often seen yourselves engrossed in the works of great authors, only to find your attention wandering when the book demands focus rather than conversation?

I previously mentioned my lady's father, the old Lord with the short temper and the long tongue. He had five children: two sons initially, followed later by three daughters, including my mistress, the youngest and finest of the trio. Of the sons, the elder, Arthur, inherited the title and estates. The younger, the Honourable John, received a substantial fortune from a relative and entered the army.

They say an ill bird fouls its own nest. I consider the noble Herncastle family my nest, and I shall be grateful if I am spared the necessity of detailing the Honourable John's conduct. I honestly believe he was one of the greatest blackguards ever to live. He joined the Guards but was expelled before reaching twenty-two—their discipline proving too rigid for him. He then traveled to India, seeking equal strictness and active service. Regarding bravery, he was a mixture of bulldog and game-cock, with a dash of savagery. He participated in the capture of Seringapatam. Shortly thereafter, he transferred regiments twice more, eventually rising to Lieutenant-Colonel. This promotion coincided with a severe sunstroke, leading him back to England.

He returned with a reputation that barred him from every door in his family. My lady, newly married, took the lead, supported by Sir John, in declaring that her brother should never enter her home. There were numerous slurs attached to the Colonel that made people avoid him, but the stain of the Diamond is the only detail requiring mention here. Rumors suggested he had acquired his Indian jewel through means too bold for him to acknowledge openly. He never attempted to sell it—he was not in need of funds, nor did he prioritize wealth. He never gifted it away, nor did he show it to a living soul. Some claimed he feared legal repercussions from military authorities; others, less knowledgeable about his true nature, whispered he feared losing his life if displayed. Perhaps a grain of truth lay in the latter claim. It was untrue that he feared; however, it was a fact that his life had been twice threatened in India, and the Moonstone was widely believed to be the cause. Upon returning to England and finding himself ostracized, the suspicion that the Moonstone caused his isolation resurfaced.

The mystery of the Colonel's life hindered his existence, effectively outlawing him among his own people. Soldiers denied him entry to clubs; women he sought to marry refused him; acquaintances and relatives pretended not to see him in the street. Most men in such a predicament would strive to clear their names. But to submit, even when unjustly accused and facing universal opposition, was not the path of the Honourable John. Having kept the Diamond in open defiance of assassination attempts in India, he maintained it in defiance of public opinion in England. Thus stands the portrait of the man: a character who dared all, possessing a handsome face that appeared possessed by the devil.

Rumours varied regarding his habits. At times, he was described as addicted to opium and antiquarian book collecting; at others, experimenting with strange chemical processes; occasionally, he was seen carousing among the lowest dregs of London society. Regardless, his life was solitary, vicious, and subterranean.

Once, and only once, after his return to England, did I witness him face-to-face. Approximately two years prior to the current events, and about eighteen months before his death, the Colonel arrived unexpectedly at my lady's house in London. It was the night of Miss Rachel's birthday, June twenty-first, and a party was underway as tradition dictated. A servant summoned me, informing me that a gentleman wished to see me. Ascending to the hall, I found the Colonel: wasted, worn, aged, shabby, yet as wild and wicked as ever.
