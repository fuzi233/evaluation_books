Every traveler who has journeyed through East Anglia knows the scattered country houses that dot the landscape—those somewhat gloomy, diminutive structures, typically built in the Italian manner, encircled by parks ranging from eighty to a hundred acres. To me, they hold an irresistible allure: the weathered grey pales of split oak, the majestic ancient trees, the meres fringed with reeds, and the distant lines of woodland. I find fascination in the pillared porticos, often clumsily affixed to red-brick Queen Anne homes merely to mask them with stucco and align them with the "Grecian" fads of the late eighteenth century. Inside, I admire the soaring halls, ideally equipped with galleries and small organs, as well as libraries containing treasures from thirteenth-century Psalters to Shakespeare quartos. Of course, I cherish the paintings, but above all, I delight in imagining the lives lived within these walls during their founding eras, amidst the booming fortunes of prosperous landlords, and equally in contemplating how varied and intriguing existence remained even when funds grew scarce. I harbor a deep desire to possess one of these estates, coupled with sufficient wealth to maintain it and host friends with modest dignity.

Yet, this is a mere diversion. I must recount a bizarre sequence of events that unfolded within a residence fitting this exact description: Castringham Hall in Suffolk. While significant renovations have altered the exterior since the era of my narrative, the core architecture remains intact—the Italian portico, the stark white block, the interior antiquity masking the facade, the park bordered by woods, and the mere. However, one defining feature, unique among a dozen similar estates, has vanished. Standing within yards of the wall, almost brushing the building with its sprawling limbs, grew a colossal old ash-tree. It likely dates back to when Castringham shed its fortifications and the moat was filled, reaching near-maturity by the year 1690.

That specific year, the region surrounding the Hall became the stage for numerous witch trials. It will take centuries to formulate a fair assessment of the solid logic, if any existed, behind the pervasive fear of witches in those days. Did the accused truly believe they possessed supernatural powers? Did they harbor the will, if not the ability, to harm their neighbors? Or were all the harrowing confessions extracted solely through the brutal cruelty of the witch-finders? These remain unsolved riddles, and the following narrative forces me to pause. I cannot dismiss it as pure fabrication; the reader must form their own judgment.

Castringham yielded a victim to the *auto-da-fé*. Her name was Mrs. Mothersole. She differed from the typical village witch only in her relative prosperity and social influence. Several reputable local farmers attempted to save her, offering testimony to her character and expressing profound anxiety regarding the jury's verdict.

What ultimately sealed Mrs. Mothersole's fate was the deposition of Sir Matthew Fell, the proprietor of Castringham Hall. He testified that on three separate occasions, observed from his window at the height of the moon, he had witnessed her gathering sprigs "from the ash-tree near my house." Clad only in her shift, she had climbed the branches, snipping small twigs with a peculiarly curved knife, muttering to herself throughout the ordeal. Each time, Sir Matthew had rushed to intervene, but every attempt to approach her caused her to flee in alarm, scattering into the garden where he could only spot a hare fleeing toward the village. On the final night, he tracked her at top speed to her doorstep, waiting fifteen minutes to batter down her door. She emerged abruptly, cross and seemingly sleep-deprived, offering no coherent explanation for her nocturnal activities.

Predominantly based on this evidence, alongside lesser testimonies from other parishioners, Mrs. Mothersole was convicted and sentenced to death. She was hanged a week later at Bury St Edmunds, accompanied by five or six other unfortunate souls. Sir Matthew Fell, serving as Deputy-Sheriff, presided over the execution. It was a damp, drizzly March morning as the cart ascended the rough hill outside Northgate, where the gallows stood. The other victims appeared apathetic or utterly broken by misery; Mrs. Mothersole, however, displayed a temperament consistent with her life. Contemporary accounts describe her "poisonous Rage" as working so intensely on the bystanders—even the hangman themselves—that she was universally perceived as embodying the living aspect of a mad devil. Remarkably, she offered no resistance to the officers of the law, gazing upon those who bound her with a direful, venomous expression so terrifying that one officer later confessed the mere thought of it haunted his mind for six months afterward.

The only words attributed to her were seemingly meaningless phrases repeated in an undertone: "There will be guests at the Hall."

Sir Matthew Fell was deeply unsettled by her demeanor. Following the assizes, he discussed the matter with his parish vicar, with whom he returned home. Though his evidence at the trial had been reluctantly given, and he was not particularly consumed by the witch-finding hysteria, he insisted he could provide no account other than what he had stated and that he could not possibly have been mistaken. The entire affair repelled him, for he valued cordial relations with his neighbors, yet he felt a solemn duty fulfilled. The vicar, Mr. Crome, applauded this sense of obligation.

A few weeks later, under the full moon of May, the Vicar and the Squire met again in the park and walked to the Hall together. Lady Fell accompanied her dangerously ill mother, leaving Sir Matthew alone at home, thus persuading Mr. Crome to join them for a late supper.

Sir Matthew proved to be poor company that evening. Conversation revolved around family and parish affairs. By chance, Sir Matthew jotted down a written memorandum of certain intentions regarding his estates, a document that would prove exceedingly useful later.

Around half-past nine, when Mr. Crome considered departing, the two men took a preliminary stroll along the gravel walk behind the house. The sole incident that struck Mr. Crome occurred as they approached the ash-tree, which I described as growing near the windows. Sir Matthew halted and asked, "What is that running up and down the stem of the ash? Is it not a squirrel? They should all be in their nests by now."

The Vicar looked and saw the moving creature, yet the moonlight rendered its color indistinct. However, the sharp outline visible for an instant was etched into his brain. He swore, though it sounded absurd, that regardless of whether it was a squirrel, it possessed more than four legs.

Nevertheless, little could be made of this fleeting vision, and the two parted ways. They may have crossed paths again, but it was not for twenty years.

The next morning, Sir Matthew Fell failed to descend at six, seven, or eight o'clock, as was his custom. Servants, growing anxious, knocked upon his chamber door, listening intently before resuming their pounding. Eventually, the door was opened from the outside, revealing his master dead and blackened. Violence appeared to have played no role initially, but the casement window stood wide open.

One servant fetched the parson, who then rode swiftly to summon the coroner. Mr. Crome arrived promptly and was shown the deceased. His private notes, preserved among his papers, reveal the genuine respect and sorrow felt for Sir Matthew, along with a crucial passage illuminating the nature of the crime and contemporary beliefs:

"There was not the least trace of force used to enter the chamber; the casement stood open, as my poor friend would invariably have it in this season. Upon his bedside table lay a silver vessel of approximately a pint measure containing his usual evening draught of small ale; tonight, it remained undrinken. This beverage was examined by the physician from Bury, Mr. Hodgkins, who, despite his oath before the coroner's court, could detect no venomous substance within it. Naturally, given the tremendous swelling and blackening of the corpse, rumors of poisoning circulated among the neighbors. The body lay in a state of extreme disorder, twisted in a manner suggesting that my worthy friend expired in great pain and agony. Most inexplicable, and to me indicative of some horrid and artful design by the perpetrators of this barbarous murder, was this: the women entrusted with laying out and washing the corpse, both respected and sorrowful in their mournful profession, came to me in immense distress of mind and body. They declared, confirmed immediately upon viewing the body, that the moment their bare hands touched the breast of the corpse, they felt a violent smart and aching in their palms and forearms. Within a short time, these areas swelled immoderately, the pain persisting for weeks, forcing them to abandon their calling. Yet, no marks were visible on the skin.

"Upon hearing this, I summoned the physician, who remained in the house, and we conducted a careful examination aided by a small crystal magnifying lens. We detected nothing of importance beyond a couple of minute punctures or pricks, which we concluded were the spots through which the poison might have been introduced, recalling the infamous rings of Pope Borgia and other known specimens of the horrid arts of Italian poisoners of the previous age.

"This concludes the account of the symptoms observed on the corpse. Regarding what follows, it is merely my personal experiment, left to posterity to judge if it holds value.
