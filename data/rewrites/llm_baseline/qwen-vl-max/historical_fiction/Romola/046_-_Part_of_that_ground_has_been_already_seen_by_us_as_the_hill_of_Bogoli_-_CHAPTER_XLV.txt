CHAPTER XLV. At the Barber’s Shop.

After his unexpected triumph as the bearer of the olive branch—a gift from fortune not sought but gratefully received—Tito had other duties to attend to, carefully planned and deliberately executed. He paused at the Palazzo Vecchio, waiting for the return of the Ten, the council entrusted with foreign affairs and matters of war, so that he might deliver the results of his private mission to Pisa. This mission was meant as a preliminary step toward an official embassy, one in which Bernardo Rucellai would lead the delegation, aiming to establish peace between Florence and Emperor Maximilian and the League. Tito’s talents in diplomacy were well known, and when he presented his findings with clarity and precision, even Bernardo del Nero, then one of the Ten, could not suppress his admiration—though he wished he could.

Bernardo’s initial dislike of Tito had resurfaced, intensified by the sale of the library. Romola had said nothing to him about it, and he took her silence as a sign she did not wish to speak of it. Yet he was certain the act had deeply wounded her, violating her father’s last wishes. The old man watched her closely and saw signs that her marriage was not fulfilling. “Ah,” he mused inwardly, “this must be why she has taken up with Fra Girolamo and the Piagnoni—something I never imagined of her.” Women without children, he thought, often turned either to folly or to excessive piety, believing themselves burdened with the salvation of souls. As for Romola, he reflected, she had been over-educated—filled with Latin and Greek until she seemed no more truly feminine than if she’d spent her days only threading needles. And her husband—so smooth, so useful, so universally employed because he was so pliable—he hoped Tornabuoni and the others would not regret their trust. “Well, well,” Bernardo muttered, “a crooked furrow may still yield a full sack; and whoever insists on having only honest men in service will find himself paying little.”

This long-held belief—that political agents could not be judged solely by moral standards—kept Bernardo from interfering in Tito’s disfavor. Aside from what must remain sacred for Romola’s sake, Bernardo had no real accusation against the Greek scholar beyond the fact that he was Greek—and that Bernardo simply did not like him. The duplicity of pretending loyalty to the popular government while secretly favoring the Medici was common among half the Medicean faction. But Tito practiced it with greater skill than most. That was all. So Bernardo remained cold toward him, and Tito returned the coldness with courteous distance.

Still, in Florence, the old bond between Bernardo and Bardo endured. Any service done to Romola’s husband was seen as an honor paid to her godfather.

Having delivered his report at the Old Palace, Tito felt the formal duties of the day completed. He was weary, dust-covered from long riding, yet he did not go home. There were matters in his satchel and in his mind that needed settling—quickly, discreetly, without drawing attention. He walked slowly from the Palazzo toward the Piazza del Duomo, moving with a casual air, though his eyes scanned the surroundings with quiet purpose.

The procession had ended, but the bells still rang, and the streets buzzed with restless energy. People craved a deeper expression of joy, something more tangible than prayers or processions. If the Frate had stood in the square and preached, they might have found contentment. But now, despite the new rules declaring Christ the special king of Florence and demanding all pleasures be Christian in nature, many young men who shouted “Viva Gesù!” secretly longed for a bit of stone-throwing—just to release their exuberance.

As Tito passed, some recognized him as the olive-branch messenger and pressed around him with eager questions. To escape the unwanted attention, he told them Meo di Sasso, the true envoy from Leghorn, was likely arriving soon and might be met near Porta San Frediano. That explanation, cleverly plausible, freed him from further pursuit.

He reached the Piazza del Duomo, glancing about with feigned indifference, though he was actually searching for someone specific. The end of the procession had drawn crowds to the square, especially near Nello’s barber shop. His instinct proved correct: a group leaned against the railings near the north gate of the Baptistery—exactly the kind of gathering he sought.

He approached, appearing to notice the tallest figure in the group only by chance, turning slightly to greet him with a nod as he tossed his hat back over his shoulder. Yet this man—broad-shouldered, dressed in a richly embroidered tunic, linen displayed in the latest French fashion, sword and dagger at his belt, a red plume in his hat—was no ordinary citizen. His bearing was bold, almost defiant, a mockery of Florentine sobriety under the influence of the Piagnoni. Youthful features gave way to coarseness in his wide face and thick waist, and his eyes held a crude, intrusive gaze that unsettled refined minds like a harsh light or foul smell.

Beside him stood others—equally young, expensively dressed, armed—laughing loudly, as if to prove their amusement was genuine, even if some doubted it. Their laughter echoed anew as Tito joined them.

Leaning against the doorpost was Niccolò Macchiavelli, a sharp-eyed young man already adept at reading the hidden motives behind public words. “Messer Dolfo’s head,” he remarked, “is more pumpkin than I thought. I judge a man’s dullness by the tricks he uses to fool others. The dullest are those who grin after being kicked in the shins. If I were just a little slower,” he added, smiling as the circle opened for Tito, “I’d pretend to love Melema—now that he’s got a secretaryship perfectly suited to me—as if Latin poorly paid could ever love Latin better paid! Melema, you’re a pestilently clever fellow, very much in my way. I’m sorry to hear you’ve had another stroke of luck today.”

“Questionable luck, Niccolò,” Tito replied, tapping him lightly on the shoulder. “All I’ve gained is being grabbed and breathed upon by wool merchants, as filthy and battered as a letter-carrier from Bologna.”

“Ah! You need my art, Messer Oratore,” said Nello, stepping forward at the sound of Tito’s voice. “Your chin bears yesterday’s stubble. Come, come—submit yourself to the priest of all the Muses. Sandro, lather quickly!”

“In truth, Nello,” Tito said, seating himself, “that is precisely what I desire. It was why I came here instead of home after my business at the Palazzo.”

“Yes indeed,” Nello agreed. “It wouldn’t do for you to appear before Madonna Romola with a rough beard and tangled hair. Nothing coarse should approach the Florentine lily—though I see her walking daily like a sunbeam through our rags—if indeed she’s not more like a moonbeam now. Yesterday, when I met her, she looked pale and worn, like that fainting Madonna in Fra Giovanni’s painting. You must see to it, my learned friend: she fasts too often, keeps vigils too long in your absence.”

Tito sighed. “It is true, Nello. She denies herself half her food every day during this famine. What can I do? Her heart burns with devotion. A husband’s influence is powerless against the Frate’s.”

“As every other influence likely is,” said Domenico Cennini, who had entered with Tito. “Have you heard anything at Pisa about how things stand in Rome, Melema?”

“Secrets of the council chamber, Messer Domenico,” Tito replied, palms raised in mock innocence. “An envoy must be as silent as a confessor.”

“Of course,” Cennini said. “No breach of that rule. Still, I believe that if the Holy Father pushes the Frate too far, the Frate would stir heaven and earth to call a General Council—and succeed too. And I, for one, would not mind, though I’m no Piagnone.”

“With respect to your experience, Messer Domenico,” Macchiavelli interjected, “I differ—not in wishing for reform, but in thinking the Frate can outmaneuver the Pope. His game is impossible. Had he limited himself to preaching against Rome’s sins and predicting Italy’s scourge without naming how, Pope Alexander would have let him preach as long as he had listeners. Such spiritual storms don’t topple walls. But the Frate wants more than a trumpet—he wants leverage. And he *has* leverage. He seeks to spread Christ’s word by supporting the popular government in Florence, while the Pope, as I know from reliable sources, desires the opposite.”

“Then Florence will stand by the Frate,” Cennini insisted. “I myself wish he’d stop prophesying—but if our right to choose our own government is attacked, I am a son of the Church, yes—but I’d vote to resist Pope Alexander VI, as our ancestors resisted Gregory XI.”

“But forgive me, Messer Domenico,” Macchiavelli said, hands tucked into his belt, speaking with calm authority, “have you grasped the Frate’s true position? How did he become such a lever? Because he holds the people’s ear. He offers them threats and promises they believe come straight from God—not just about hell and paradise, but about Pisa and the Great Council. But if events turn against him, shaking the people’s faith, his power becomes his downfall. He draws three kinds of hatred: the common man’s resentment of strict virtue; the jealousy of stronger Italian powers who want to control Florence; and the people’s anger, whom he promised earthly blessings, not just heavenly ones. A prophet must have an army behind him, like Mahomet, to revive faith when it falters.”

“Better to sum it all into one,” Francesco Cei broke in hotly. “He has earned the hatred of every sensible and honest man by spreading hypocritical lies. His place is with the false prophets in Hell, walking backward.”

“You’re too angry, Francesco,” Macchiavelli said gently. “Poets cut clouds in wrath. I’m no follower of the Frate, nor would I risk a finger for his truth. But truth is a flower of paradise—its seeds rarely bloom outside its walls. You, too, tell poetic lies—some from passion, some to please. But you object to lies in prose. The Frate differs only in where he draws the line between poetry and fact. In the pulpit, he has fervor—and an audience to satisfy. Ecco!”

“You’re somewhat lenient there, Niccolò,” Cennini said gravely. “I believe in the Frate’s integrity, though not his prophecies. As long as that isn’t disproved, we have a strong popular party to protect him and resist foreign meddling.”

“A party that seems strong,” Macchiavelli replied, shrugging, his glance barely flickering toward Tito, who was now enjoying Nello’s combing and scented oils. “But how many Mediceans are among you? How many won’t shift allegiance for personal grudges?”

“As to the Mediceans,” Cennini said, “there’s little genuine loyalty left to the Medici. Who would risk much for Piero de’ Medici? A few old friends, perhaps—like Bernardo del Nero. But even those tied to the family support the popular government and would defend the Frate. I spoke to Giannozzo Pucci earlier. I’m convinced he’d oppose any attempt to overturn the new order.”

“You’re right, Messer Domenico,” Tito said, rising from the chair with a smile in his eyes. “And I suspect tender feelings helped hard theory there. I think jealousy sparked Pucci’s break with Piero. Else, a man as gentle as he is wouldn’t show such bitterness. Was he in the procession?”

“No,” Cennini replied. “He’s at his villa—left three days ago.”

Tito adjusted his cap, pretending not to care, but in truth, he had just secured vital information. In his satchel lay a crushed gold ring, entrusted to him by an envoy of Piero de’ Medici, whom he’d met by chance on the Siena road. Since Pucci was absent, Tito would send the ring via Fra Michele, a Carthusian brother serving the Medici. Its receipt would bring Pucci back to hear the rest of the message.

“Behold him!” Nello declared, waving his comb. “The most handsome scholar in the world—or the marshlands! A trifle thinner than when he first came to Florence—eh? And I swear, there are faint lines forming around your mouth, Messer Oratore! Ah, the mind is beauty’s enemy! Once, I was considered handsome by women—when I wore swaddling clothes. Now—oimè! My unwritten poems are ciphered across my face!”

Laughing with the others, Tito waved farewell and left.

“I’m with Piero di Cosimo,” Francesco Cei said. “I don’t fully trust Melema. That smile grows stronger—no wonder he has lines at the mouth.”

“He’s too successful,” Macchiavelli teased. “There must be something wrong with him—else why would he have that secretaryship?”

“He’s capable,” Cennini said fairly. “My brother and I have always found him useful with our Greek manuscripts. He satisfies the Ten. I like to see a man rise by merit. And Secretary Scala, who helped him from the start, still speaks highly of him.”

“Certainly,” a notary murmured from the background. “He writes Scala’s letters—or corrects them—and earns well for it.”

“I wish Bartolommeo would pay me to cure his gouty Latin,” Macchiavelli said, shrugging. “Did *he* tell you about the pay, Ser Ceccone, or was it Melema?”

“Melema? No,” the notary answered. “He’s as tight as a nut. Never brags. That’s why he’s everywhere. They say he’s growing rich doing underhand work.”

“It *is* a bit unfair,” Macchiavelli said, “with so many skilled notaries idle.”

“Well,” Cei said, “I remember a nasty story from a year or two ago—the man who claimed Melema stole jewels. It was hushed up, but Piero di Cosimo said he believed it, for he saw Melema’s face when the man grabbed him—‘painted with fear,’ as Dante says.”

“Enough venom, Francesco,” Nello snapped. “Else I’ll consider it my duty to cut your hair crooked next time I hold your scissors. That jewel story was a lie. Bernardo Rucellai and the Magnificent Eight knew the truth. The man was a madman—properly locked away. And Piero di Cosimo’s mind runs wild—he’d mistake a lizard for a crocodile. That tale is buried deep—our noses revolt at it.”

“It is true,” Macchiavelli said. “You forget the danger of precedent. Next, a beggar might accuse you of stealing verses—or me, God help me, of stealing his coppers. Ah!” he added, turning to the door, “Dolfo Spini has carried his red feather out of the square. That swaggering captain would rejoice if Florence lost Pisa—just to see the people tear the Frate’s robes off. With your pardon, Francesco—I know he’s your friend—but few things would please me more than to see him play Capo d’Oca, who blew his trumpets to the tournament and returned with them in a bag.”
