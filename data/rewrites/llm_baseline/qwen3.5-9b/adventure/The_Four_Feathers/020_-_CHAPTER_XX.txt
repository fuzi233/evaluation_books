Durrance found his personal attendant waiting in the hallway when he emerged from the fields toward his home, "Guessens."
"You may extinguish the lights and retire," Durrance instructed, stepping past the servant and into the corridor leading to his study.

The term 'study' felt ill-fitting for the room; it had always served more as a gunroom. Durrance sank into his chair, then began to pace slowly through the darkness. Scattered across the floor lay numerous cups and goblets—trophies from his younger days. He knew each vessel intimately by its silhouette and placement, finding solace in their familiar textures. One by one, he picked them up, running his fingers over them, questioning silently whether, since his blindness, they remained polished as they once were.

He recalled a slender-stemmed goblet won in a regimental steeplechase at Colchester. His memory transported him instantly to that day: the heavy clouds, the grey sky, the somber appearance of the ploughed fields stretching between the hedgerows. Another object, a piece of pewter serving as a penholder on his writing desk (when pens were still useful), dated back to his freshman year at Oxford, earned during a college rowing race. On the mantelpiece rested a silver ornament shaped like the hoof of a beloved horse. Together, these artifacts transformed the room into a living diary. Durrance traced his history of triumphs until his hands finally settled on his firearms.

Lowering them from their racks, he treated the weapons with the same reverence Ethne reserved for her violin. To him, they whispered stories audible only to his touch. Sitting with a Remington across his lap, he relived a scorching afternoon in the hills west of Berenice, where he had tracked a lion across barren, rocky terrain and dispatched it at three hundred yards just before sunset. Another rifle spoke of his first ibex hunt in the Khor Baraka and antelope drives in the mountains north of Suakin. There was also a small Greener gun, used on freezing winter nights aboard a boat in the Salcombe estuary, with which he had bagged his maiden mallard. He lifted it, sliding his left hand beneath the barrel until the stock nestled perfectly into the hollow of his shoulder.

Yet, as he manipulated the triggers, realizing he could no longer align the sights, the weight of his losses pressed down upon him with undeniable clarity. The noise of his memories became overwhelming, mirroring how Ethne's violin had once sounded too piercingly loud in the early days after Harry Feversham's departure, leaving her isolated.

Putting the guns back, Durrance was suddenly gripped by a fierce impulse to deny his blindness—to act as if it posed no obstacle, to convince himself so thoroughly that it ceased to matter. This desire surged through him like a fever, propelling him mentally from the realm of dim stars directly to the East. The scent of the East, its cacophony, the domes of its mosques, the searing sun, the chaotic throngs in its streets, and the steel-blue heavens above seized hold of him. He was yanked from his chair, pacing restlessly.

In his reverie, he imagined himself in Port Said, joining the endless line of steamers descending the canal. The chants of Arab coalers echoed in his mind so vividly he could visualize them moving up and down the planks between barges and decks—an unbroken chain of naked figures glowing red in the light of their braziers. He traveled onward, past the crimson headlands of the Sinai Peninsula, into the cool breezes of the Gulf of Suez. He zigzagged down the Red Sea as the Great Bear hung low in the northern sky and the Southern Cross blazed in the south. He touched anchor at Tor and Yambo; he beheld the towering white houses of Yeddah rising from the sea, admiring the weathered, dark wood of their carved windows. Walking through the twilight of its roofed bazaars, he felt the profound joy of a homesick traveler returning after years abroad. From Yeddah, he crossed the narrowing coral reefs into the inland harbor of Suakin.

Westward from Suakin stretched the desert—a landscape fraught with meaning for a man who had been exiled by it. He envisioned the soft padding of camel hooves in the sand, the massive rock cones rising abruptly like waves frozen in a calm ocean, endlessly approaching yet never reachable. He saw the brilliant flash of sunset colors in the west, the rustling wind during the brief twilight when the horizon split into pale green and deep blue, and the descent of the planets from the void onto the earth.

Lost in this mental pilgrimage, forgetting his blindness and burning with a thirst akin to fever, he wandered back and forth until the sudden chirping of blackbirds and the fluttering of swallows in the garden broke his trance. Outside his window, the world was turning white with dawn.

He woke from his dream at the familiar sound. No further journeys awaited him; affliction had imprisoned him, chaining his leg. Feeling his way along the balustrade to the stairs, he climbed to his bed and fell asleep as the sun rose.

Meanwhile, at Dongola, on the great bend of the Nile south of Wadi Halfa, the sun was already blazing, and life stirred. Sport awaited the townsfolk that morning beneath the sparse palm trees before the residence of Emir Wad El Nejoumi. A white prisoner, captured a week prior near the wells of El Agia on the main Arbain road by a band of Arabs, had been brought in during the night and now awaited judgment. News of the event spread rapidly through the town; crowds of men, women, and children gathered for this rare spectacle.

Before the palms, an open space extended to the Emir's gate; behind them, a sandy slope dropped flat and bare toward the river. Harry Feversham stood beneath the trees, guarded by four Ansar soldiers. Stripped of his clothing, he wore only a tattered, ragged *jibbeh* and a twisted cloth around his head to shield him from the heat. His bare shoulders and arms bore scars and blisters. His ankles were shackled, his wrists bound with palm fiber rope, and an iron collar locked tightly around his neck, tethered by a chain held by one of the guards.

Feversham stood smiling at the jeering crowd, appearing strangely content, almost manic. This was the persona he had adopted. If he could maintain it—if he could confuse his captors into uncertainty regarding whether he was truly insane or merely an agent promising aid to rebellious tribes in Kordofan—perhaps they would hesitate to execute him personally, instead sending him onward to Omdurman. Yet, sustaining such a ruse proved arduous.

Inside the house, the Emir and his advisors debated his fate; outside, visible to him, a high gallows loomed black and menacing against the yellow sand. Feversham welcomed the chain and fetters. They prevented panic by confirming their uselessness. These hours of waiting, as the sun climbed higher and silence persisted from the gate, were the most grueling he had endured. Throughout his fortnight in Berber, hope of escape had buoyed him, but when that lantern had illuminated him amidst the ruins, action demanded immediate execution, leaving no room for fear or contemplation. Now, however, there was time—and far too much of it. Time to anticipate, to foresee.

His heart sank until he felt faint, reminiscent of those distant days when he had heard hounds scrambling in a cover while he sat trembling on his horse. He glanced nervously at the gallows, imagining vultures perching on his shoulders, circling his vision. But the man had changed during his years of trial. Fear of physical pain was secondary, as was the terror of walking unmanly to the scaffold. His greatest dread was that dying here at Dongola would mean Ethne would never retrieve the fourth feather, and his cherished hope for redemption would remain unfulfilled.

Grateful for the collar and chains that anchored him, he rallied his faculties. Standing alone, sharing no burden with another, he laughed, scraped, and grimaced at his tormentors. An elderly woman danced and gestured wildly before him, singing a monotonous tune. Her movements mimicked violence, threatening grotesque mutilations, while her words described, in crude detail, the agonizing death awaiting him and the eternal torture of hell. Feversham understood and shrank inwardly, yet outwardly he mirrored her gestures, nodding and bobbing as if she sang of paradise.

Others approached with war trumpets, pressing the instruments to his ears and blowing furiously. "Can you hear us, Kaffir?" shouted a child, dancing gleefully nearby. "Hear our *ombeiyehs*? Blow harder! Blow louder!" The prisoner merely clapped his hands, declaring the music delightful.

Finally, a tall warrior bearing a long, heavy spear arrived. A cry erupted, clearing a path. He positioned himself before the captive, swinging the spear back and forth to loosen his muscles, preparing to thrust like a bowler winding up for a delivery. Feversham scanned wildly for an escape route, found none, and suddenly threw his chest forward to meet the blow.

But the spear never struck. As the warrior lunged, one of the four guards jerked the neck-chain violently from behind, hurling the prisoner backward, half-strangled, onto the sand. Three times, to the roaring delight of the mob, this game was repeated. Then, a soldier appeared in the gateway of Nejoumi's house.

"Bring him in!" he commanded.
