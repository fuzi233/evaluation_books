The morning of my father's birthday arrived, and with a curious, silent precision, he ceased to be. We had not anticipated the end for some time; indeed, the finality of it escaped us entirely until the silence itself became a presence.

"I have left him sleeping," my mother announced, her voice barely rising above a whisper as she emerged from the bedroom in her dressing gown. "Washburn administered a draught last night. We shall not disturb him."

Thus, we gathered around the breakfast table, speaking in hushed tones, acutely aware that the house was a fragile vessel of thin partitions where every syllable echoed like a shout. Later, my mother crept upstairs, and I followed, prying the door open just enough to peer inside. The blinds remained drawn, casting the room into a heavy, suffocating twilight. My mother stood there for a long duration, her ear pressed firmly against the wooden frame, listening to the hollow rhythm of the house. Outside, the city's clamor seeped in—the shrill cry of a costermonger selling watercress, the hoarse wail of a milkman—but within, the air grew still. Inch by inch, she widened the aperture, and we stole forward.

He lay there, eyes sealed shut, lips parted in a slack surrender. I had never witnessed death before; the concept was abstract, a theoretical shadow rather than a tangible reality. All I perceived initially was that he appeared younger than I had ever seen him, preserved in a stillness that defied the passage of time. Slowly, agonizingly, the truth settled over me: he was gone. For days, for weeks, the phantom of his presence haunted my mind—I heard his footsteps trailing behind me in the street, his voice summoning me from the crowd, his face turning toward me in recognition. I would rush forward, only to stand bewildered as the apparition dissolved into the ether. Yet, paradoxically, in the beginning, I felt no pain.

To my mother, however, it was merely a brief separation. Her placid faith had never known the corrosion of fear or doubt. He was simply waiting for her; in God's good time, they would reunite. What necessity was there for sorrow? Without him, the days stretched out sluggishly, the house inevitably dimming when the good man departed. But that was all. Consequently, she spoke of him constantly—not with tears, but with smiles—recalling his dear, kind mannerisms, his eccentricities and follies that we cherished. She thought of him not as a relic of the past, but as someone beckoning from the future.

We continued to inhabit the old house, though we were perpetually plotting our departure, for the great brick monster of the city had crept inexorably closer, year by year, devouring all that was fair. Fields, gardens, trees, cottages, and time-worn homes suggesting stories of yore—all had been scrunched between its ugly teeth. This East End of London, a ghetto of the poor expanding endlessly, added layer upon layer of dreariness to its existing gloom. Hopelessness stretched its shriveled arms across the horizon, encompassing endless rows of reeking cells where London herded its slaves. Often, on misty afternoons when we sensed life stirring in the sunshine outside this city of the dead, we would venture forth to hunt for homes in pleasant suburbs. We visited Highgate, then a tiny town linked by coach to leafy Holloway; Hampstead, with its ancient red-brick rows overlooking wind-blown heath and distant London domes; Wood Green, nestled among pastures where smock-coated laborers debated politics beneath wide-spreading elms; and Hornsey, then merely a village defined by an ivy-choked church and a single grass-bordered lane. Though we frequently encountered "the very thing for us," discussing its merits from every angle, we invariably delayed.

"We must think it over," my mother would insist. "There is no hurry; for some reasons, I shall be sorry to leave Poplar."

"For what reasons, mother?"

"Oh, well, no particular reason, Paul. Only we have lived here so long, you know. It will be a wrench leaving the old house."

Like the instinct of the clinging vine, all things contribute to the making of man. We fling our tendrils around the nearest castle keep or pigsty wall, fastened tighter by rain and sunshine alike. Dying Sir Walter Scott, hastening home from Italy, feared he might not reach the damp mists of the barren hills to breathe them one last time. An ancient dame I knew was carried from her attic in the slums so she might be fanned by sea breezes, only to pine desperately for what she called "home." Wife, mother, widow, she had lived there until the alley's stench smelled sweet to her nostrils, until its riotous noise was the chorus of her people. Who can understand us save He who fashioned us?

So the old house held us to its dismal bosom. We did not depart until, within its homely yet unlovely arms, first my aunt, and later my mother, had died. It was only then, amidst the littered emptiness, that I bid farewell to Amy.

My aunt died as she had lived: grumbling.

"You will be glad to get rid of me, all of you!" she declared, dropping for the first and last time I could recall into a direct retort. "And I can't say I shall be very sorry to go myself. It hasn't been my idea of life."

Poor old lady! That was only a couple of weeks before the end. I doubt she realized it was so certain, or perhaps she might have been more sentimental.

"Don't be foolish," my mother said gently. "You're not going to die!"

"What's the use of talking like an idiot," my aunt snapped back. "I've got to do it some time. Why not now, when everything's all ready for it? It isn't as if I was enjoying myself."

"I am sure we do all we can for you," my mother replied.

"I know you do," my aunt countered. "I'm a burden to you. I always have been."

"Not a burden," my mother corrected softly.

"What does the woman call it then," my aunt retorted sharply. "Does she reckon I've been a sunbeam in the house? I've been a trial to everybody. That's what I was born for; it's my métier."

My mother wrapped her arms around the poor old soul and kissed her forehead. "We should miss you very much," she said.

"I'm sure I hope they all will!" my aunt answered. "It's the only thing I've got to leave 'em, worth having."

My mother laughed.

"Maybe it's been a good thing for you, Maggie," my aunt grumbled. "If it wasn't for cantankerous, disagreeable people like me, gentle, patient people like you wouldn't get any practice. Perhaps, after all, I've been a blessing to you in disguise."

I cannot honestly say we ever wished her back, though we certainly missed her—we missed many a joke at her oddities, many a laugh at her corner-cutting ways. It takes all sorts, as the saying goes, to make a world. Possibly, if only the perfect folk remained, we would find existence uncomfortably monotonous.

As for Amy, I believe she truly regretted her loss.

"One never knows what's good for one till one's lost it," Amy sighed.

"I'm glad to think you liked her," my mother said.

"You see, mum," Amy explained, "I was one of a large family; and a bit of a row now and again cheers one up, I always think. I'll be losing the power of my tongue if something doesn't come along soon."

"Well, you are going to be married in a few weeks now," my mother reminded her.

But Amy remained despondent. "They're poor things, the men, at a few words, the best of them," she replied. "As likely as not, just when you're getting interested, you turn round to find that they've put on their hat and gone out."

After my aunt's death, my mother and I were very much alone. Barbara had gone abroad to complete her education—to learn the tricks of the upper class' trade, as old Hasluck phrased it—and I had left school to take employment with Mr. Stillwood, without salary, intending to study for the law.

"You are in luck's way, my boy, in luck's way," old Mr. Gadley had assured me. "To commence your career in the office of Stillwood, Waterhead and Royal will be a passport for you anywhere. It will stamp you, my boy."

Mr. Stillwood himself was an extremely old and feeble gentleman—so aged and weak that it seemed strange a wealthy man had not retired long ago.

"I am always meaning to," he explained to me one day shortly after my arrival. "When your poor father came to me, he told me very frankly the sad fact—that he had only a few more years to live.
