They lunched slow, salt wind seeping through the cottage’s warped kitchen window to ruffle the edge of the linen tablecloth, long soft stretches of silence filling the gaps between their rushes of speech. Now that the fragile spell of their long separation had finally broken, they had a thousand tangled things to say, but half the time the words faded away entirely, leaving only that quiet, throbbing duet of shared quiet that needed no translation. Archer never once turned the conversation to his own tangled affairs with May, not out of any conscious plan, but because he dared not miss a single syllable of the story of the year and a half they had spent apart. Ellen leaned across the table, chin resting on her laced-clasped hands, and told him everything.

She had grown tired of what New York insisted on calling “society.” The city had been so kind to her, almost oppressively warm in its welcome when she came home, but once the first novelty wore off, she had found herself too fundamentally “different” to care for the trivialities everyone else there chased. So she had decided to try Washington, where rumor said you met all sorts of people, all sorts of opinions that didn’t fit the staid New York mold. More than likely, she would settle there for good, make a quiet little home for poor Medora, who had worn out the patience of every other relation just when she most needed someone to watch over her, to yank her out of the path of the foolish predatory marriages she kept stumbling into.

“But Dr. Carver—aren’t you afraid of him? I hear he’s been staying with you out at the Blenkers’.” Archer frowned.

Ellen laughed, bright and unconcerned. “Oh, the Carver danger’s long past. He’s a very clever man, you know. He wants a rich wife to fund all his grand social experiments, and Medora’s just a handy, good-looking advertisement as a newly converted follower, nothing more.”

“Converted to what?”

“All sorts of wild, bright new social schemes. And do you know, they interest me far more than the blind, unthinking conformity to someone else’s old traditions that I see all our New York friends clinging to. It seems so stupid, doesn’t it? Men sailed all the way across the ocean to discover a whole new continent, only to turn it into a pale, cheap copy of the old one they left behind.” She smiled across the table at him. “Do you really think Christopher Columbus would have braved all that storm and danger just to go to the opera with the Selfridge Merrys?”

Archer’s face burned. “And Beaufort—do you say these sorts of things to Beaufort?” he asked, abrupt and sharp with sudden unnameable jealousy.

“I haven’t seen him in months. But I used to, once. And he understood.”

“Ah, that’s what I’ve always known. You don’t really like us. You like Beaufort because he’s nothing like us.” He glanced around the bare, whitewashed room, out at the empty stretch of beach, the stark row of village houses strung along the shore like discarded beads. “We’re so damnably dull. No character, no color, no spark at all. I wonder,” he burst out before he could stop himself, “why you don’t just go back to Europe.”

Her dark eyes darkened further, and he braced for an indignant, angry retort. But she sat quiet, turning the words over slowly in her head, and a cold lump of fear settled in his throat that she would say she wondered that too.

At last she spoke, soft and even. “I believe it’s because of you.”

There was no vanity in the confession, no deliberate flattery to stroke his pride. It was as calm and matter-of-fact as if she had told him the tide came in at noon. Archer flushed all the way to the roots of his hair, dared not shift an inch or breathe too loud—her words felt like a rare, fragile butterfly that the smallest movement would send fluttering away, lost forever, if he startled it.

“At least,” she went on, “you’re the one who made me see that under all that dull, stuffy New York surface, there are things so fine, so sensitive, so delicate, that even the people I cared for most back in my old life abroad feel cheap next to them. I don’t know how to put it into words right,” she frowned, her brow furrowed with quiet distress, “but it feels like I never understood before how much that is hard, and shabby, and base we pay for even the most exquisite little joys in life.”

*Exquisite pleasures—just to have known them at all is something!* He ached to say it, but the quiet, aching appeal in her eyes kept the words locked behind his teeth.

“I want to be perfectly honest with you,” she said, “and perfectly honest with myself. I’ve hoped for this chance for so long, to tell you how much you’ve helped me, what you’ve made me see in myself that I never knew was there—”

Archer stared at her, brows drawn tight, and laughed a rough, bitter laugh that cut her off. “And what do you think you’ve made of me?”

She paled, just a little. “Of you?”

“Yes. Because I’m far more your making than you could ever be mine. I’m the man who married one woman because another one told him he should.”

A faint, fleeting flush rose to her cheeks. “I thought—you promised—you wouldn’t say things like that today.”

“Ah, you are just like every other woman! None of you will ever see a bad, messy business all the way through!”

She dropped her voice soft, gentle. “Is it a bad business, though—for May?”

He stepped to the window, drumming his fingers against the raised wooden sash, and every nerve in his body hummed with the tender wistfulness she had put into her cousin’s name.

“That’s the thing we have to always think of, isn’t it?” she pressed on, the words coming slow and pained, as if she was dragging them up from somewhere deep. “By your own showing, anyway. If it’s not worth giving things up, missing things, just to keep other people from disillusionment and misery—then everything I came home for, everything that made my old life abroad feel so bare and empty because no one there cared for those soft, fine little things—all of that is just a sham. Just a dream.”

He turned, not moving an inch from where he stood by the window. “And in that case, there’s no reason on earth you shouldn’t go back, is there?”

Her eyes clung to his, desperate, begging. “Oh—isn’t there any reason at all?”

“Not if you staked everything on my marriage working out,” he said, savage and raw. “My marriage isn’t going to be some pretty, perfect sight that’s worth you staying here for.”

She said nothing, and he pushed on. “What’s the point? You showed me my first glimpse of what a real, honest life could be, and at the very same second you told me I had to go on living the sham one. It’s more than any human can bear. That’s all there is to it.”

“Oh, don’t say that—when I’m bearing it too!” she burst out, tears brimming over her lids. Her arms lay loose along the edge of the table, and she sat there holding her face up to his gaze, reckless, as if she was standing at the edge of a precipice and no longer cared what she let him see. That face laid her whole soul bare, no masks, no pretense. Archer stood frozen, speechless, overwhelmed by the truth that had just dawned on him.

For answer, she let the tears spill over and roll slow down her cheeks.

Half the width of the small room still lay between them, neither of them making the smallest move to cross it. Archer felt a strange, quiet indifference to touching her right then—he would hardly have noticed the shape of her body at all, if one of her hands, splayed out on the tabletop, hadn’t drawn his gaze, the same way it had once back in that little Twenty-third Street house, when he had stared at it so he wouldn’t let himself look at her face. Now his imagination spun around that hand like water circling the edge of a vortex, but he still made no move to step closer. He had known the sort of love that fed on kisses and caresses, but this passion he felt now, this passion that was stitched right into his bones, was far too deep to be satisfied by something as superficial as a touch. His only terror was doing anything at all that might wipe the memory of her words from the air between them. His only thought was that he would never have to feel so alone again.

But after a moment, the heavy weight of waste and ruin settled over him. They were here, together, shut away from the whole world, safe for the first time in years—and yet chained so tight to the separate destinies they had chosen, they might as well have been half a world apart.

“What’s the use, if you’re just going to go back eventually?” he broke out, the words hiding a raw, desperate cry beneath them: *How am I ever supposed to keep you here?*

She sat perfectly still, lids lowered over her wet eyes. “Oh, I’m not going anywhere yet.”

“Not yet? So some day, then? Some day you’ve already counted out in your head?”

At that, she lifted her eyes, clear and bright as the sun on the sea. “I promise you. Not as long as you hold out. Not as long as we can look straight at each other, like this.”

He dropped back into his chair. The unspoken part of her answer was crystal clear, as if she had spoken it out loud: *If you so much as lift a finger to cross this line, you’ll drive me straight back to all the horrors of my old life in Europe, all the temptations you can only half guess at*. He understood it perfectly, and the thought kept him anchored to his side of the table, held him in a kind of soft, sacred, moved submission.

“What a life you’re going to have,” he groaned.

“Oh,” she smiled, soft and wobbly from the tears, “as long as it’s a part of yours.”

“And mine a part of yours?”

She nodded.

“And that’s all we’ll ever get? For either of us?”

“Well,” she said, gentle, “it’s all there is, isn’t it?”

At that, he sprang to his feet, forgetting everything but the soft sweet shape of her face. She stood up too, not to meet him or to run away, just quiet, as if the hardest, scariest part of their talk was done, and now she only had to wait. She was so still that when he drew close, her outstretched hands didn’t push him away, they guided him. Her hands fell into his, her arms loose, keeping him just far enough away that her surrendered, unmasked face could say all the rest that didn’t need words.

They might have stood that way for minutes, or hours—neither of them could tell. But it was long enough for her silence to communicate every last thing she had to say, for him to understand that the only thing that mattered was not to let this be the last time they ever saw each other like this. He would leave their future in her hands, he decided, only asking that she hold tight to it, for both of them.

“Don’t—don’t be unhappy,” she said, her voice cracking as she pulled her hands gently away.

“You won’t go back? You won’t leave and go back to Europe?” he asked, as if that was the one possibility he could not survive.

“I won’t go back,” she promised. She turned, pulled the door open, and led the way out into the public dining room.

The loud, chattering schoolteachers were gathering up their parcels, getting ready to straggle down the path to the wharf. Across the stretch of sunlit beach, the white steamboat waited tied to the pier, and far across the glinting blue water, the rooftops of Boston loomed, soft and hazy on the horizon.
