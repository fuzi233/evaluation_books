The history of mankind would have been written differently if the weight of a guilty conscience were measured strictly by the magnitude of the crime committed. In such a world, the architects of greedy wars and the leviathans of the financial markets would stand in the same penitent ranks as the petty thief, the purse-snatcher, and the butcher who kills with his own hands. Wickedness, it is true, distributes its rewards; yet, to win in this devil's game, one must inevitably become baser, crueler, and more brutal than the average human soul can sustain. Most people are born with a form of conscience—a fear that is the shadow of justice, a pity that is the shadow of love—which prevents them from claiming the prize of serene wickedness, a state of being difficult to maintain in our complex, composite nature.

On the twenty-ninth of December, Deronda knew that the Grandcourts had arrived at the Abbey, though he had not seen them until he went to dress for dinner. A magnificent snowfall had recently blanketed the grounds, offering the children the rare joy of snowball fights and snow forts. During the Christmas holidays, the Mallinger girls were content with no amusement unless it was orchestrated by "cousin," as they invariably called Deronda. Following this outdoor exertion, he had played billiards, and the hours slipped by without his dwelling on the prospect of meeting Gwendolen at dinner. Yet, the anticipation was present. As he retired to his room, slightly tired and heated from his games, he began to speculate on the influence her marriage to Grandcourt would exert upon her. He wondered if there would be discernible shifts in her manner since he had last seen her at Diplow, just as there had been since his first encounter with her at Leubronn.

"I fancy there are some natures one could see growing or degenerating every day, if one watched them closely," Deronda thought. "Some of us move faster than others. I am certain she is a creature who retains strong traces of anything that once impressed her. That little affair with the necklace, and the notion that someone thought her gambling improper, had evidently taken root. But such impressibility works both ways: it can drive one to desperation just as easily as it can lead to something better. Whatever fascinations Grandcourt may hold for capricious tastes—who can believe he would summon tender affections in daily companionship? One might be tempted to horsewhip him just to provoke a show of passion in his face and speech. I fear she married him out of ambition, to escape poverty. But why did she initially avoid him? The poverty came later, though. Poor thing! She may have been urged into it. How can one feel anything but pity for a young creature, full of unused life, ignorantly rash, hanging all her blind expectations on that remnant of a human being?"

Deronda acknowledged that the phrases he applied to the bridegroom were less complimentary than the excuses and pity in which he clothed the bride. His view of Grandcourt as a "remnant" stemmed not from specific knowledge, but from the impression of polite society that Grandcourt had worn out all his natural, healthy interest in things. Generally, when a marriage of note occurs, male acquaintances pity the bride and female acquaintances pity the groom; each is thought to have done better. Especially when the bride is charming, young gentlemen often conclude she has no real attachment to a husband so uninteresting to them, assuming she married on other grounds. Under such circumstances, who pities the husband? Even his female friends often view his position as retributive, believing he should have chosen someone else. Perhaps Deronda could be excused for withholding pity for Grandcourt, who had never struck acquaintances as a man likely to suffer more than he inflicted. Conversely, for Gwendolen—young, headlong, eager for pleasure, and fed with flattery that makes a lovely girl believe in her divine right to rule—how quickly might life turn from expectancy to a bitter realization of the irremediable? After what he had witnessed of her, he must have had rather dull feelings not to look forward with some interest to her entrance into the room. Still, since the honeymoon was already three weeks distant and Gwendolen had been enthroned at both Ryelands and Diplow, she was likely to have composed her countenance with suitable manifestation or concealment, not being one to indulge the curious with a helpless exposure of her feelings.

A varied party had been invited to welcome the new couple. The old aristocracy was represented by Lord and Lady Pentreath; the old gentry by young Mr. and Mrs. Fitzadam of the Worcestershire branch; politics and the public good, specialized in the cider interest, by Mr. Fenn, member for West Orchards, accompanied by his two daughters; Lady Mallinger's family by her brother, Mr. Raymond, and his wife; and the useful bachelor element by Mr. Sinker, the eminent counsel, and Mr. Vandernoodt, whose acquaintance Sir Hugo had found pleasant enough at Leubronn to warrant his adoption in England.

All had assembled in the drawing-room before the new couple appeared. Meanwhile, time was spent chiefly in observing the children—various little Raymonds, nephews and nieces of Lady Mallinger's, alongside her own three girls, who were always permitted to appear at this hour. The scene was delightful, enlarged by full-length portraits with deep backgrounds inserted in the cedar paneling, surmounted by a ceiling glowing with the rich colors of coats of arms. It was illuminated almost as much by the red fire of oak-boughs as by pale wax-lights, stilled by the deep-piled carpet and the high English breeding that subdued all voices. The mixture of ages, from the white-haired Lord and Lady Pentreath to the four-year-old Edgar Raymond, gave a varied charm to the living groups. Lady Mallinger, with her fair matronly roundness and mildly prominent blue eyes, moved about in black velvet, carrying a tiny white dog on her arm as a finishing touch to her costume. The children were scattered among the ladies, while most of the gentlemen stood somewhat aloof, conversing with a moderate vivacity typical of the long minutes before dinner. Deronda was slightly apart from the circle, engaged in a dialogue fixed upon him by Mr. Vandernoodt, a man of the best Dutch blood imported at the revolution. He was one of those commodious persons in society who are nothing particular themselves but are understood to be acquainted with the best in every department: close-clipped, pale-eyed, nonchalant, serving as a perfect foil to the intense coloring and vivid gravity of Deronda.

They were discussing the bride and bridegroom, whose appearance was awaited. Mr. Vandernoodt was an industrious gleaner of personal details, capable of telling everything about a great philosopher or physicist except their theories or discoveries. He now implied that he had learned many facts about Grandcourt since meeting him at Leubronn.

"Men who have seen a good deal of life don't always end by choosing their wives so well. He has had rather an anecdotic history—gone rather deep into pleasures, I fancy, lazy as he is. But, of course, you know all about him."

"No, really," Deronda replied in an indifferent tone. "I know little more of him than that he is Sir Hugo's nephew."

Just then, the door opened, deferring any satisfaction of Mr. Vandernoodt's communicativeness. The scene was one to set off any figure of distinction that entered, and certainly when Mr. and Mrs. Grandcourt appeared, no beholder could deny that their figures possessed distinction. The bridegroom wore neither more nor less easy perfection of costume, nor more nor less well-cut impassibility of face, than before his marriage. It was to be supposed that he would accept nothing less than the best in outward equipment, wife included; and the bride was what he might have been expected to choose. "By George, I think she's handsomer, if anything!" said Mr. Vandernoodt. Deronda agreed but said nothing. The white silk and diamonds—strange as it seemed, she did wear diamonds on her neck, in her ears, and in her hair—might have contributed to the new imposingness of her beauty, which flashed upon him as more unquestionable, if not more thoroughly satisfactory, than when he had first seen her at the gaming table. Some faces peculiar in their beauty are like original works of art; for the first time, they are almost always met with question. But in seeing Gwendolen at Diplow, Deronda had discerned in her more than he had expected of that tender, appealing charm we call womanly. Was there any new change since then? He distrusted his impressions, but as he saw her receiving greetings with what seemed a proud, cold quietude and a superficial smile, there seemed to be at work within her the same demonic force that had possessed her when she took him in her resolute glance and turned away a loser from the gaming table. There was no time for further conclusion, no time even for him to offer his greeting before the summons to dinner.

He sat not far from opposite to her at the table and could sometimes hear what she said in answer to Sir Hugo, who was at his liveliest in conversation with her. Though he looked toward her with the intention of bowing, she gave him no opportunity for some time. Finally, Sir Hugo, who might have imagined they had already spoken, said, "Deronda, you will like to hear what Mrs. Grandcourt tells me about your favorite Klesmer."

Gwendolen's eyelids had been lowered, and Deronda, already looking at her, thought he discovered a quivering reluctance as she was obliged to raise them and return his unembarrassed bow and smile, her own smile being one of the lips merely. It was but an instant, and Sir Hugo continued without pause, "The Arrowpoints have condoned the marriage, and he is spending the Christmas with his bride at Quetcham."

"I suppose he will be glad of it for the sake of his wife, else I dare say he would not have minded keeping at a distance," Deronda remarked.

"It's a sort of troubadour story," said Lady Pentreath, an easy, deep-voiced old lady. "I'm glad to find a little romance left among us. I think our young people now are getting too worldly wise."

"It shows the Arrowpoints' good sense, however, to have adopted the affair, after the fuss in the paper," said Sir Hugo. "And disowning your own child because of a *mésalliance* is something like disowning your one eye: everybody knows it's yours, and you have no other to make an appearance with."

"As to *mésalliance*, there's no blood on any side," said Lady Pentreath. "Old Admiral Arrowpoint was one of Nelson's men, you know—a doctor's son. And we all know how the mother's money came."

"If they were any *mésalliance* in the case, I should say it was on Klesmer's side," Deronda interjected.

"Ah, you think it is a case of the immortal marrying the mortal. What is your opinion?" Sir Hugo asked, looking at Gwendolen.

"I have no doubt that Herr Klesmer thinks himself immortal. But I dare say his wife will burn as much incense before him as he requires," Gwendolen replied. She had recovered any composure she might have lost.

"Don't you approve of a wife burning incense before her husband?" Sir Hugo asked with an air of jocoseness.

"Oh, yes," said Gwendolen, "if it were only to make others believe in him." She paused a moment and then said with more gaiety, "When Herr Klesmer admires his own genius, it will take off some of the absurdity if his wife says Amen."

"Klesmer is no favorite of yours, I see," said Sir Hugo.

"I think very highly of him, I assure you," said Gwendolen. "His genius is quite above my judgment, and I know him to be exceedingly generous."

She spoke with the sudden seriousness often meant to correct an unfair or indiscreet sally, harboring a bitterness against Klesmer in her secret soul which she knew herself unable to justify. Deronda wondered what he should have thought of her if he had never heard of her before: probably that she put on a little hardness and defiance by way of concealing some painful consciousness—if, indeed, he could imagine her manners otherwise than in the light of his suspicion. But why did she not recognize him with more friendliness?

Sir Hugo, changing the subject, said to her, "Is not this a beautiful room? It was part of the refectory of the Abbey. There was a division made by those pillars and the three arches, and afterward they were built up. Else it was half as large again originally. There used to be rows of Benedictines sitting where we are sitting. Suppose we were suddenly to see the lights burning low and the ghosts of the old monks rising behind all our chairs!"

"Please don't!" said Gwendolen, with a playful shudder. "It is very nice to come after ancestors and monks, but they should know their places and keep underground. I should be rather frightened to go about this house all alone. I suppose the old generations must be angry with us because we have altered things so much."

"Oh, the ghosts must be of all political parties," said Sir Hugo. "And those fellows who wanted to change things while they lived and couldn't do it must be on our side. But if you would not like to go over the house alone, you will like to go in company, I hope. You and Grandcourt ought to see it all. And we will ask Deronda to go round with us. He is more learned about it than I am." The baronet was in the most complaisant of humors.

Gwendolen stole a glance at Deronda, who must have heard what Sir Hugo said, for he had his face turned toward them, helping himself to an *entrée*; but he looked as impassive as a picture.
