Good and evil balance themselves in a manner both remarkable and universal. The steel bow of the arbalest, strapped to the rescuer's back and raised high, had nearly claimed the victim; yet, in that very position, it fell into Gerard's grasp. One hand seized the weapon, the other slipped between the bow and its cord, securing the tool of salvation. With a desperate, eager jerk, Denys was hauled from the water by his crossbow until half his body bobbed free.

"Now, grip me not! Grip me not!" Gerard cried, his heart hammering against his ribs in mortal terror of the fatal mistake he had almost made.

"Pas si bete," Denys gurgled, his voice thick with water.

Seeing the sturdy stuff he had to work with, Gerard's fear vanished, replaced by a sharp, commanding calm. "On thy back," he ordered. Seizing the heavy arbalest, he stroked forward, aiding the soldier's ascent. "Hand on my shoulder! Slap the water with the other! No—downward motion. Do nothing more than I bid thee." Gerard had clamped his teeth onto the end of Denys's long hair, twisting it tight, and with the strong muscles of his youthful neck, he held the soldier's head aloft while striking out lustily against the current. For a moment, he hesitated, unsure which bank to aim for, little realizing the terrible importance of that simple choice. Seeing the west shore slightly nearer, he steered toward it, refusing to swim straight to jail like a good boy and thus providing a novel incident for the authorities. Driven by the river's force, they slanted considerably. After covering nearly a hundred yards, Denys murmured uneasily, "How much more of this?"

"Courage," Gerard mumbled. "Whatever a duck knows, a Dutchman knows; thou art safe as in bed."

Suddenly, to their surprise, they found themselves in shallow water and waded ashore. Once on solid ground, they stared at one another from head to toe as if eyes could devour, then, by one impulse, flung arms around each other's necks and panted there, hearts too full for speech. In this sacred moment, life was sweet as heaven to both; sweetest perhaps to the exiled lover who had just saved his friend. Oh, joy to whose height has any poet yet soared? To save a human life, and that life a beloved one. Such moments are worth living for, yes, three score years and ten. Calmer now, they took hands and walked along the bank, hand in hand like sweethearts, scarce knowing or caring whither they went.

The boatmen were all safe on the overturned craft, Herr Turnip-face, the "Inverter of things," sitting in the middle. The entire commotion seemed not to have deranged his habits; he greeted them as they shot into the Rhine and again as they emerged.

"Shall we wait till they right the boat?"

"No, Denys, our fare is paid; we owe them nought. Let us go, and briskly."

Denys assented, noting they could walk the whole way to Cologne on this bank.

"I fare not to Cologne," came the calm reply.

"Why, whither then?"

"To Burgundy."

"To Burgundy? Ah, no! That is too good to be true."

"Sooth 'tis, and sense into the bargain. What matters it to me how I go to Rome?"

"Nay, nay; thou but sayest so to please me. The change is too sudden. Think me not so ill-hearted as to take thee at thy word. Did I not see thy eyes sparkle at the wonders of Cologne? The churches, the images, the relics..."

"How dull art thou, Denys; that was when we were to enjoy them together. Churches! I shall see plenty, go Rome-ward how I will. The bones of saints and martyrs; alas! the world is full of them; but a friend like thee, where on earth's face shall I find another? No, I will not turn thee farther from the road that leads to thy dear home, and her that pines for thee. Neither will I rob myself of thee by leaving thee. Since I drew thee out of the Rhine, I love thee better than I did. Thou art my pearl: I fished thee, and must keep thee. So gainsay me not, or thou wilt bring back my fever; but cry courage, and lead on; and hey for Burgundy!"

Denys gave a joyful caper. "Courage! Va pour la Bourgogne. Oh! Soyez tranquille! Cette fois il est bien décidément mort, ce coquin-là." And they turned their backs on the Rhine.

As this decision became clear, a commotion arose across the river among the little party watching the discussion. They had not taken many steps before a voice rang out over the water. "HALT!"

Gerard turned, seeing one of the four men holding out a badge of office and a parchment slip. His heart sank; for he was a good citizen, accustomed to obey the voice that now bade him return to Düsseldorf—the Law's.

Denys shared no such scruples. He was a Frenchman, despising every other nation, its laws, inmates, and customs. He was a soldier, viewing the situation through a military lens: superior force opposed, a river between, the rear open; retreat was made easy. He saw at a glance that the boat still drifted in mid-stream and no ferry was nearer than Düsseldorf. "I shall beat a quick retreat to that hill," he said, "and then, being out of sight, quick step."

They sauntered off.

"Halt! In the bailiff's name," cried a voice from the shore.

Denys turned, ostentatiously snapped his fingers at the bailiff, and proceeded.

"Halt! In the archbishop's name."

Denys snapped his fingers at his Grace and proceeded.

"Halt! In the emperor's name."

Denys snapped his fingers at his Majesty and proceeded.

Gerard watched this needless pantomime with regret. As soon as they passed the brow of the hill, he declared, "There is now but one course; we must run to Burgundy instead of walking," and set off, running the best part of a league without stopping.

Denys was fairly blown and inquired what had become of Gerard's fever. "I begin to miss it sadly," he said drily.

"I dropped it in the Rhine, I trow," was the reply.

Presently they reached a small village, where Denys purchased a loaf and a huge bottle of Rhenish wine. "For," he said, "we must sleep in some hole or corner. If we lie at an inn, we shall be taken in our beds." This was no more than common prudence on the old soldier's part. The official network for catching law-breakers, especially plebeians, was very close in that age, though the cooperation of the public was almost null, at least upon the Continent. Innkeepers were everywhere under close surveillance regarding their travelers, for whose acts they were even in some degree responsible, more so it would seem than for their sufferings.

Both friends were glad when the sun set, and delighted when, after a long trudge under the stars—for the moon, if memory serves, did not rise until about three in the morning—they arrived at a large barn belonging to a house at some distance. A quantity of barley had recently been thrashed; the heap of straw on one side of the floor was almost as high as the unthrashed corn on the other.

"Here be two royal beds," said Denys. "Which shall we lie on, the mow, or the straw?"

"The straw for me," said Gerard.

They sat on the heap, ate their brown bread, and drank their wine. Then Denys covered his friend in straw, heaping it high above him, leaving only a breathing hole. "Water, they say, is death to fevered men; I'll make warm water on it, anyhow."

Gerard bade him make his mind easy. "These few drops from the Rhine cannot chill me. I feel heat enough in my body now to parch a kennel, or boil a cloud if I were in one." With this epigram, his consciousness faded so rapidly he might truly be said to "fall asleep."

Denys, lying awake a while, heard that which made him nestle closer. Horses' hoofs came ringing up from Düsseldorf, and the wooden barn vibrated as they rattled past, howling in a manner too well known and understood in the fifteenth century, but as unfamiliar in Europe now as a Red Indian's war-whoop.

Denys shook where he lay.

Gerard slept like a top.

It all swept by, and troop and howls died away.

The stout soldier drew a long breath, whistled in a whisper, closed his eyes, and slept like a top too.

In the morning, he sat up and put out his hand to wake Gerard. It lighted on the young man's forehead and found it quite wet. Denys, in his quality of nurse, forbore to wake him. "It is ill to check sleep or sweat in a sick man," he said. "I know that far, though I ne'er minced ape nor gallows-bird."

After waiting a good hour, he felt desperately hungry; so he turned, and in self-defense went to sleep again. Poor fellow, in his hard life he had often been driven to this maneuver. At high noon, he was waked by Gerard moving and found him sitting up with the straw smoking round him like a dung-hill. Animal heat versus moisture. Gerard called him "a lazy loon." He quietly grinned.

They set out, and the first thing Denys did was give Gerard his arbalest and mount a high tree on the road. "Coast clear to the next village," he said, and on they went.

Drawing near the village, Denys halted and suddenly inquired of Gerard how he felt.

"What! Can you not see? I feel as if Rome were no further than yon hamlet."

"But thy body, lad; thy skin?"

"Neither hot nor cold; and yesterday 'twas hot one while and cold another. But what I cannot get rid of is this tiresome leg."

"Le grand malheur! Many of my comrades have found no such difficulty."

"Ah! There it goes again; itches consumedly."

"Unhappy youth," said Denys solemnly, "the sum of thy troubles is this: thy fever is gone, and thy wound is—healing. Sith so it is," he added indulgently, "I shall tell thee a little piece of news I had otherwise withheld."

"What is't?" asked Gerard, sparkling with curiosity.

"Oh!"
