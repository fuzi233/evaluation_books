To any gentleman who has ever stepped inside the Wheel of Fortune public-house—remembering well that it was here Mr. James Morgan's Club convened, and where Sir Francis Clavering once held court with Major Pendennis—one knows the layout of the ground floor. Beyond the bar, where the landlady presides, lie three distinct realms for guests. There is the public parlour, open to all; a second room reserved for gentlemen in livery; and finally, the third apartment, marked boldly with "Private" on its door, hired exclusively by the "Confidentials," a club of which Messrs. Morgan and Lightfoot were proud members.

It was in this shadowed sanctum that the noiseless Morgan sat, having followed his master from the Major's lodgings to the pub. He had eavesdropped on the Major's earlier conversation with Strong, gathering enough private speculation to fuel a lifetime of curiosity. Now, while Pendennis and Clavering argued heatedly in the adjacent parlour, Morgan occupied a strategic corner of the Confidential room from which the entire discourse drifted in. The tone was angry, the voices high, and Morgan, a man of sharp ears, caught almost every syllable. What he heard only confirmed the suspicions already festering in his mind.

"He knew Altamont the moment he spotted him in Sydney!" Morgan whispered to himself, his eyes widening behind his spectacles. "Clavering isn't married to my Lady at all! Altamont is the man! A convict! Young Harthur is set to enter Parliament, and the Governor promises not to split the party. By Jove, what a sly old rogue that Governor is! No wonder he's pushing the match between Blanche and Harthur. Why, she'll bring a hundred thousand pounds to the table, plus a seat in Parliament for her husband."

Nobody witnessed the transformation on Mr. Morgan's face as this intelligence crystallized, but a physiognomist would have marveled at the shift. "But for my age and the confounded prejudices of society," he muttered, eyeing his reflection in the glass, "damn it, James Morgan, you might marry her yourself." Yet, marriage was out of the question. Morgan quickly pivoted to a more lucrative strategy: the possession of this secret could mend his fortunes in ways far more immediate. Of all those touched by the scandal, the majority would pay dearly to keep it buried. Sir Francis Clavering, whose fortune hung in the balance, would wish silence; Colonel Altamont, whose neck was implicated, would prefer oblivion; and that young upstart, Mr. Harthur—who was as proud as a duke despite being a convict's son trading secrets for a parliamentary seat—would pay any price to hide his origins. As for Lady C., whether she wanted to escape Clavering or protect her frightened son, she would pay. And Miss Blanche? She would certainly reward the man who restored her rightful inheritance.

"Damn it," concluded the valet, calculating the wonders of his windfall, "with such cards, James Morgan, you are a made man. It may be a regular eternity of profit. Everyone must subscribe. With what I've already made, I might cut business, warn my old Governor, turn gentleman, and hire a servant of my own, begad." Despite these feverish calculations that threatened to perturb his spirit, Morgan displayed remarkable self-command, maintaining a calm exterior while his inner world spun with ambition.

Colonel Altamont, however, was currently absent from London. The valet knew of Sir Francis's haunt at Shepherd's Inn and arrived an hour or two after the Baronet and Pendennis had parted, only to find the bird had flown. Altamont had cashed his Derby winnings and departed for the Continent. This absence was a profound vexation to Morgan. "He'll drop all that money at the gambling dens on the Rhine," Morgan fretted. "I might have had a good slice of it. It's confounded annoying he's gone and couldn't wait a few days." Yet, hope deferred or triumphant, victory or ambush, Morgan bore it all with an equable countenance. Until the proper day arrived, the Major's boots remained varnished, his hair curled, his tea brought to his bedside, and his oaths endured with silent, obsequious fidelity. Who would guess, seeing him pack trunks and assist at table at country houses, that Morgan was richer than his employer, knowing secrets of his master and others alike? In his profession, Morgan was revered, his reputation for wealth and wisdom earning him renown at supper tables. The younger gentlemen dismissed him as stupid, a fogey with no ideas, yet none would deny the heartfelt prayer of the serious-minded: "When I die, may I cut up as well as Morgan Pendennis!"

As befitted a man of fashion, Major Pendennis spent the autumn moving from house to house among country friends. If the Duke was abroad, he stayed with the Marquis in Scotland; if not, with Sir John or the plain Squire. Truth be told, the old gentleman's reputation was waning. Many of his contemporaries had died, and the current holders of their titles knew him not, caring little for his tales of "the wild Prince and Poins" or the heroes of a vanished age. It struck the good man with melancholy as he walked past London doors that were rarely opened for him, recalling the banquets and welcomes of a score of years prior. He began to admit he was no longer of the present age, dimly sensing that the young men laughed at him. Such musings plague many a Pall Mall philosopher. "The men are not what they were," he thought. "The old grand manner is gone. What is Castlewood House now compared to the magnificence of the old mansion? The late lord arrived with four postchaises and sixteen horses; the North Road hurried to see him. The present lord travels in a railway carriage with five bagmen, sneaking away in a brougham. The late lord filled Castlewood with guests drinking claret till midnight; the present man hides in a hut on a Scotch mountain or a closet in Paris. What a contrast between Lady Lorraine of the Regent's time—gorgeous, magnificent in diamonds, daring in rouge, with the wits of the world bowing at her feet—and today's Lady Lorraine, a little woman in a black silk gown like a governess, talking of astronomy and laboring classes!"

Abbots-Lorraine, once the noblest house in the county, had become a monastery, a regular La Trappe. They didn't drink two glasses of wine after dinner; every other guest was a country curate discussing Polly Higson's school progress or Widow Watkins's lumbago. "And the other young men, those lounging guardsmen and lazy dandies—sprawling over sofas, stealing off to smoke in bedrooms, reverencing nothing, not even an old gentleman who has known their fathers—what a difference!" thought the Major. "They're replaced by cotton-spinners and utilitarians, and young parsons with hair combed down their backs. I'm getting old; they're getting past me." He was not far wrong. The times and manners he admired were nearly gone; the gay young men larked him irreverently, while the serious youth viewed him with a grave pity that would have been more painful had he known its extent. But the Major was simple; his moral examinations had never been deep. He hadn't realized until recently that he was merely a respectable, rather fortunate man. Was there no old age without reverence? Did youthful folly never jeer at other bald pates? For the past few years, he had sensed his day was ending, and the new reign had begun.

After an unsuccessful autumn, during which Morgan faithfully trailed him, Major Pendennis returned to London at the dismal end of October, when fogs and lawyers descend upon the city. Who hasn't watched those loaded cabs, piled with boxes and children, rattling through the streets on dun October evenings? Yesterday was France and sunshine; today is work and yellow fog. And what a heap of bills lies in the master's study! The clerk brings the lawyer's papers; the printer's boy waits in the passage; Mr. Smith arrives with that particular little account, symbolizing duty, difficulty, and struggle. Old Pendennis had no special labors or bills to fear, for he had money in his desk and little affection at home to soothe him. Being indifferent to others' wants, he rarely felt disturbed by them. Yet, a gentleman may be out of temper without owing a shilling; he must occasionally feel dispirited and lonely. He had suffered twinges of gout in the country; the birds were shy, the walks fatiguing, and the young men had laughed at him. He hadn't played whist, and he was glad to return. In all his dealings with Morgan, he had been exceedingly sulky and discontented. He had sworn at him, abused him for days, scalded his mouth with bad soup at Swindon, and left his umbrella in the railroad carriage—a piece of forgetfulness that drove him to curse Morgan more freely than ever. Both chimneys smoked furiously in his lodgings; when he flung open the windows, he swore so acrimoniously that Morgan was inclined to fling him out through the casement. The valet swore after his master as Pendennis marched down the street to the Club.

Bays's was not pleasant. The house smelled of varnish and turpentine, and a large streak of white paint marred the back of the Major's fur-collared coat. The dinner was poor. The three most odious men in London—old Hawkshaw with his cough, old Colonel Gripley clutching his newspapers, and the irreclaimable bore Jawkins, who described every inn-bill from his foreign tours—contributed to the Major's misery. Even the Club waiter trod on his toe bringing coffee. Never alone do the Immortals appear; the Furies hunt in company. They pursued Pendennis from home to the Club and back.

While the Major was away, Morgan sat in the landlady's parlour, drinking freely of hot brandy-and-water and pouring out his master's abuse onto Mrs. Brixham. Mrs. Brixham was Morgan's slave. He was her landlord; he had bought her lease, secured her son's acceptances, and held a bill of sale on her furniture. Her son, a clerk in an insurance office, was subject to Morgan's whims. Mrs. Brixham, a clergyman's widow, found herself fetching bootjacks and slippers for the man who performed his duties on the first floor. The little black profiles of her son and daughter, the very picture of Tiddlecot Church where she was married, now hung over the mantelpiece of Morgan's back-parlour as his property. Morgan sat in the ex-curate's old horse-hair study-chair, making Mrs. Brixham bring supper and refill his glass.

The liquor was bought with the poor woman's own coin, which made Morgan indulge the more freely. He had eaten his supper and was drinking a third tumbler when old Pendennis returned from the Club and went upstairs. Morgan swore savagely at the bell, finishing his brandy before answering the summons.

He received the ensuing abuse in silence, nor did the Major read the anger in the flushed face and glaring eyes of the man beneath him. The foot-bath waited by the fire; his gown and slippers were ready. Morgan knelt to remove the boots with due subordination, keeping up a growl of maledictions below while the Major raged above. "Confound you, sir, mind that strap—curse you, don't wrench my foot off," cried the Major, while Morgan whispered below a wish to strangle, drown, and punch his head off.

Once the boots were off, the coat had to come off, requiring Morgan to approach very near. Pendennis immediately perceived the scent of brandy and, in that simple, forcible phraseology men use with domestics, informed him he was a drunken beast.

At this, the man broke out, losing all patience and subordination. "I'm drunk, am I? I'm a beast, am I? You infernal old miscreant! Shall I wring your old head off and drown you in that pail of water? Do you think I'm going to bear your confounded old harangue, you old Wigsby? Chatter your old ivory teeth at me, do you, you grinning old baboon! Come on, if you're a man, and can stand to a man. Ha! You coward, knives, knives!"

"If you advance a step, I'll send it into you," said the Major, seizing a knife from the table. "Go downstairs, you drunken brute, and leave the house. Send for your book and wages in the morning, and never let me see your insolent face again. This impertinence has been growing for months. You've grown too rich. You're not fit for service. Get out."

"And where would you wish me to go, pray, out of the house?" asked the man, feigning confusion. "Won't it be equal convenient tomorrow morning? Tootyfay mam's hose, sir, sir?"

"Silence, you beast, and go!" cried the Major.

Morgan began to laugh, a rather sinister sound. "Look here, Pendennis," he said, seating himself. "Since I've been in this room, you've called me beast, brute, dog, and damn me. How do you suppose one man likes that talk from another? How many years have I waited on you, and how many damns and curses have you given me along with my wages? Do you think a man is a dog that you can talk to him this way? If I choose to drink a little, why shouldn't I? I've seen many gentlemen drunk formerly, and perhaps have the habit from them. I'm not going to leave this house, old fellow. Shall I tell you why? The house is my house; every stick of furniture in it is mine, except your old traps, your shower-bath, and your wig-box. I've bought the place with my own industry and perseverance. I can show a hundred pounds where you can show fifty, or your damned supercilious nephew either.
