When Romola brought Tessa home with the children, April had already thinned toward its end; the evenings were longer, and the city’s many tongues seemed to take advantage of the extra light to wag the more. Yet it was not the noisy happiness of little feet in her rooms that made Romola’s heart beat quicker, nor the small domestic tasks she set herself—measuring out bread, warming milk, making sure the beans were well rinsed of grit, that the rosemary and bay did not scorch in the pot. It was another hunger that had been growing in her, sharpened by every rumor and every contradiction—a hunger to know the truth of Fra Girolamo, and to taste in it, if she could, something that was not corrupted.

That hunger had been driven to its keenest edge by a printed thing that had begun to pass from hand to hand like a piece of contraband spice—Fra Girolamo’s Trial, or, as everyone called it with a grim sort of relish, his Confession: the words said to have been drawn from him by the sixteen Florentine citizens commissioned to question him. The Signoria had ordered it put forth, and then, as if the city’s ear had proved too quick for their liking, as if the sound of those pages crackling in a thousand rooms had turned into a roar of suspicion, they had tried to call it back with severe measures. But paper has its own cunning. Copies were “mislaid.” A second edition—careful to proclaim that it was not by order of the Signoria—soon slipped into eager hands as quietly as a loaf passed at night to a starving neighbor.

Romola, who had begun to despair of ever speaking with the Frate, read the printed evidence again and again. She read it the way a careful cook will taste a broth in which too many hands have dipped—again and again, searching for what is native to the thing itself and what is foreign, what is the true savor of the ingredients and what is the taint of the vessel. She could not be content with the sharp assertions traded in the streets and under the loggie—one man swearing the Frate had been proved a liar, another swearing the paper was pure forgery, each voice louder than the last, as if volume could replace knowledge. The city’s soundscape itself seemed diseased: the clatter of hooves on stone, the vendors’ cries, the shrill quarrels that rose and broke like waves—noise piled on noise until thought was choked as surely as a flame under wet ash. Florence had always been full of sound; now the sounds pressed like a fever.

Among the more devout followers of Savonarola, his reported want of constancy under torture, and his retraction of prophetic claims, had produced a consternation so deep it could not be pushed aside at once. Yet even in their grief there came, soon enough, a counter-taste—suspicion that would grow into a certainty for them—that any reported words which stood in inexplicable contradiction to their faith had not come from the prophet’s lips at all, but from the falsifying pen of Ser Ceccone, that notary of evil repute who had made the digest of the examination. They spoke of that name as if it were a bitter herb bruised into an otherwise wholesome dish.

And indeed there were facts that threw immediate discredit on the printed document, even for those who were not ready to swear to outright falsification. Was not the list of sixteen examiners half made up of the prophet’s bitterest enemies? Was not the notorious Dolfo Spini one of the new Eight, prematurely elected, as if to load the dice against a man whose ruin had been determined? It was a murder with slow formalities, everyone could see it: a knife hidden under velvet, a cord drawn out with legal phrases. In the Old Palace, beneath the echoing corridors and the murmur of officials’ feet, the Signoria had resolved to drive a bargain with the Pope and with the Duke of Milan by extinguishing the man who was a molestation alike to vicious citizens and to greedy foreign tyrants, as well as to a corrupt clergy. The Frate had been doomed beforehand. The only question now pretended to exist was whether the Republic, in return for permission to lay a tax on ecclesiastical property, should deliver him alive into the hands of the Pope, or whether the Pope should further concede what Florentine dignity demanded—the privilege of hanging and burning its own prophet on its own piazza.

Under such circumstances, who could give full credit to this so-called confession? If the Frate had denied his prophetic gift, was it not plausible that the denial had been wrenched from him by the agony of torture—agony that, in his sensitive frame, must quickly produce raving? What if these wicked examiners declared he had only had the torture of the rope and pulley thrice, and only on one day, and that his confessions had been made when he was under no bodily coercion—was that to be believed? Many said no; and the “many” were made more certain by the measure of their pain. He had been tortured more, they insisted; he had been tortured in proportion to the distress his confessions had created in the hearts of those who loved him. It was a strange arithmetic, but grief counts that way.

Other friends of Savonarola—less ardent partisans—did not doubt the substantial genuineness of the confession, however much the notary might have colored it with transpositions and additions. Yet they argued, with indignation that was calmer only because it had steadier roots, that even so there was nothing warranting death, not even grave punishment. If this examination were the only evidence against him, then he would die not for any crime, but because he had made himself inconvenient—to the Pope; to the rapacious Italian States that wanted to dismember their Tuscan neighbor; to unworthy citizens hungry to gratify private ambition against the common weal. Not a shadow of political crime had been proved. Not one stain had been detected on his private conduct. His fellow-monks, including one who had once been his secretary and was cultivated enough to criticize his rule as Prior, bore witness—even after the shock of his retraction—to an unimpeachable purity and consistency of life that had commanded their unsuspecting veneration.

Even the Pope had been unable to raise a charge of heresy against Fra Girolamo except on the ground of disobedience to a mandate and disregard of the sentence of excommunication. It was difficult to justify that breach of discipline by argument; but there was a moral uprising in grave minds against the Court of Rome which tended to confound the tidy distinction between the Church and churchmen, and so to lighten the scandal of disobedience. Men of ordinary morality and public spirit felt that the triumph of the Frate’s enemies was the triumph of gross license. Keen Florentines like Soderini and Piero Guicciardini may well have smiled angrily at a severity which dispensed with law in order to hang and burn a man in whom the seductions of a public career had warped the strictness of his veracity; may well have remarked that if the Frate had mixed a deeper fraud with a zeal and ability less inconvenient to high personages, the fraud would have been regarded as a useful oil for ecclesiastical and political wheels.

Nevertheless, such shrewd men were forced to admit that however poor a figure the Florentine government made in its clumsy pretense of judicial warrant for what had been predetermined as policy, the measures of the Pope against Savonarola were measures of self-defense. Not to try to rid himself of a man who wanted to stir the Powers of Europe to summon a General Council and depose him would have been adding ineptitude to iniquity. There was no denying that toward Alexander the Sixth Savonarola was a rebel—and, much more, a dangerous rebel. Florence had heard him say, and had understood, that he would not obey the devil. It was inevitably a life-and-death struggle between the Frate and the Pope; but it was less inevitable that Florence should make itself the Pope’s executioner.

Romola’s ears were filled all day with these suggestions: faith, still ardent under its wounds, and worldly discernment, measuring human nature by what it called possibility. She could be satisfied with neither. In the pauses of her household—when she had sent Tessa with a basket to fetch greens, when she herself had listened to the small bubbling of the pot as the beans softened, when she had broken stale bread into a bowl and let it drink broth until it turned again to tenderness—she brought her mind back to the printed document with a steady, painful attention. She had observed things through the years of her discipleship—things recorded half consciously, like smells that lodge in cloth—that whispered a presentiment Savonarola’s retraction was not merely a spasm to escape torture. But against that whisper rose the cry of her own soul for some explanation that would still let her believe the main striving of his life had been pure and grand.

She remembered, too, her recent season of selfish discontent—the blighting wind that had come with the loss of her trust, drying her inward world until good seemed tasteless. The recollection produced in her that strange reaction known to those who have been down into despair: a kind of faith springing up from the depths, not because it is easy, but because without it life becomes unendurable. It was impossible, she told herself now, that the negative, disbelieving thoughts which had made her soul so arid could be founded in the truth of things. Impossible that it had not been a living spirit, and no hollow pretense, that once breathed in the Frate’s words and kindled new life in her. Whatever falsehood there had been in him had been a fall, not a purpose: an entanglement that tightened gradually, in which he struggled, not a contrivance nourished by success.

Looking at the printed confessions, she saw sentences that bore the stamp of bungling fabrication—heavy emphasis, crude repetition in self-accusation, such as none but the lowest hypocrites use when speaking to other men. And precisely because those sentences struck so harshly against the rest—against the man she had known, and against the general tone of what was printed—she was strengthened in the impression that the remainder represented, in the main, what had really fallen from his lips. Hardly a word was dishonorable to him except what turned on his prophetic annunciations. He was unvarying in his statement of the ends he had pursued for Florence, for the Church, for the world; and apart from the mixture of falsity in the claim to special inspiration by which he had sought to lay hold of men’s minds, there was no admission of unworthy means. Even in this confession, even with the notary’s malign phrases left standing like grit under the teeth, Fra Girolamo shone as a man who had sought his own glory indeed, but sought it by laboring for the highest end—the moral welfare of men—not by vague exhortations, but by striving to turn beliefs into energies that would work through all the details of life.

One passage fixed itself in her memory as a taste does—sharp, lasting, impossible to rinse away:

“Everything that I have done, I have done with the design of being for ever famous in the present and in future ages; and that I might win credit in Florence; and that nothing of great import should be done without my sanction. And when I had thus established my position in Florence, I had it in my mind to do great things in Italy and beyond Italy, by means of those chief personages with whom I had contracted friendship and consulted on high matters, such as this of the General Council. And in proportion as my first efforts succeeded, I should have adopted further measures. Above all, when the General Council had once been brought about, I intended to rouse the princes of Christendom, and especially those beyond the borders of Italy, to subdue the infidels. It was not much in my thoughts to get myself made a Cardinal or Pope, for when I should have achieved the work I had in view, I should, without being Pope, have been the first man in the world in the authority I should have possessed, and the reverence that would have been paid me. If I had been made Pope, I would not have refused the office: but it seemed to me that to be the head of that work was a greater thing than to be Pope, because a man without virtue may be Pope; but such a work as I contemplated demanded a man of excellent virtues.”

That blending of ambition with belief in the supremacy of goodness was no new tone to Romola. She had been used to hear it ring through the Duomo—the same metal struck, the same resonance. It was Savonarola’s habit to conceive great things and to feel himself the man to do them. Iniquity should be brought low; justice, purity, love should triumph; and they should triumph by his voice, his work, his blood. In moments of ecstatic contemplation, doubtless, the sense of self melted into the sense of the Unspeakable, and there lay the elements of genuine self-abasement. But in the presence of fellow-men whom he must move, pre-eminence seemed a condition of life itself.

Perhaps, Romola thought, even when the confession described a doubleness conscious and deliberate, it implied no more than that wavering of belief about one’s own impressions and motives to which any human being not made stupid by inflexible self-confidence must be liable under a marked change of outward conditions. In the Frate’s tumultuous life, what room there was for altered self-judgment when, instead of eyes that venerated and knees that knelt, instead of a great work visibly on its way to fulfillment and stamping its agent as chosen, there came hooting and spitting and curses; then the hard faces of enemies sitting as judges; then the horrible torture; and with the torture, the irrepressible cry: It is true, what you would have me say—let me go—do not torture me again—yes, yes, I am guilty. O God! Thy stroke has reached me!

As Romola thought of the anguish that must have followed—whether, in the subsequent solitude of the prison, conscience retracted or confirmed the self-taxing words—that anguish seemed to press upon her own heart and draw out slow, bitter tears. Every vulgar, self-ignorant person in Florence was pronouncing glibly on the man’s demerits, while he was knowing a depth of sorrow that can be known only by the soul that has loved and sought the most perfect thing and beholds itself fallen.

She had not then seen—what she would see later—the evidence of the Frate’s state of mind after he had thus laid his mouth in the dust. As the days went by, the reports of new unpublished examinations—reports that had drawn the city’s curiosity as the smell of roasting draws a crowd—ceased. Savonarola was left alone in his prison, and for a while they allowed him pen and ink, that he might use his bruised and strained right arm to write. He wrote. But what he wrote was no vindication, no protest, no clever argument to shake his enemies’ hold. It was a continued colloquy with the divine purity to which he sought complete reunion; an outpouring of self-abasement; a long cry for inward renovation. No lingering echoes of the old vehement self-assertion—Look at my work, for it is good, and those who set their faces against it are children of the devil. Instead, the voice of Sadness told him: God placed thee in the midst of the people even as if thou hadst been one of the excellent. In this way thou hast taught others and hast failed to learn thyself. Thou hast cured others, and thou thyself hast been still diseased. Thy heart was lifted up at the beauty of thy own deeds, and through this thou hast lost thy wisdom and art become, and shalt be to all eternity, nothing. After so many benefits with which God has honored thee, thou art fallen into the depths of the sea; and after so many gifts bestowed on thee, thou, by thy pride and vainglory, hast scandalised all the world.

And when Hope spoke and argued that divine love had not forsaken him, it said nothing now of a great work to be done, nothing of Florence saved and nations moved. It only said: Thou art not forsaken—else why is thy heart bowed in penitence? That too is a gift.

There is no worthy evidence that from the time of his imprisonment to the supreme moment Savonarola thought or spoke of himself as a martyr. Martyrdom had been for him a passion dividing the dream of the future with the triumph of beholding his work achieved. And now, in place of both, had come a resignation which he called by no glorifying name. But therefore he may the more fitly be called a martyr by his fellow-men to all time. For power rose against him not because of his sins but because of his greatness—not because he sought to deceive the world, but because he sought to make it noble. And through that greatness he endured a double agony: not only reviling, torture, and the death-throe, but the agony of sinking from the vision of glorious achievement into that deep shadow where he could only say, I count as nothing; darkness encompasses me; yet the light I saw was the true light.
