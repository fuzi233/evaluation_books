The hum of holiday crowds eddied around Tito like warm surf long after Fra Luca fell silent, but he remained rooted in place beside the friar, caught fast in a tangle of irresolution. It was only when the press of passing pilgrims and pleasure-seekers thinned, and the old Dominican pushed himself stiffly to his feet to shuffle through the open doors of Santa Felicita’s cool, incense-dim nave, that Tito at last turned his steps down the sun-warmed cobblestones of the Via de’ Bardi.

“If this monk is a Florentine, if he means to settle here long,” he muttered under his breath, fingers tightening unconsciously around the crumpled scrap of parchment Fra Luca had pressed into his hand, “I shall have to lay the whole truth bare.”

A new crisis had risen up before him, but he schooled his features so carefully no hint of his inner turmoil showed by the time he reached Bardo’s house. He had already missed his promised visit the evening before, and he slipped through the second-floor door where Romola and her father sat surrounded by scrolls of parchment and half-carved marble fragments, as aloof from the raucous holiday revelry of the streets as they were on the quietest workday. The regret in his face was barely dimmed from its usual bright, easy charm—he even told them he had skipped the famous Corso carnival procession to call on them, a gesture that needed no further proof of his contrition. He threw himself into the evening’s talk with extra animation, laughing at Bardo’s grumbling jests, leaning over to point out a rare phrase in the Greek manuscript spread before them, while deep below the surface of his words his mind churned like a complex, whirring loom, weaving threads of thought that bore no relation to their conversation. By the time he descended the worn stone stairs and stepped out under the frosty starlight, his purpose had already hardened into a definite shape.

The next day, as soon as his lectures at the humanist academy wrapped for the afternoon, he turned his feet down the Via del Cocomero toward the convent of San Marco. He meant to find Fra Luca, to learn exactly how much the friar guessed of Baldassarre’s fate, what clues had led him to those guesses, and how long he intended to remain in Florence. With that full knowledge, he would craft a story that would spare him the necessity of fleeing the city, the life of respect and easy pleasure and Romola’s love that he had built for himself.

He had never needed to spin an elaborate, purposeful lie before—but the moment had come, the moment that always rises unbidden to shore up small, unacknowledged falsities, and his ingenuity was sharp and ready. He had already convinced himself he owed no duty to go chasing after Baldassarre. He had once carelessly said that if he got fair proof his adoptive father was alive, he would seek him out unhesitatingly—but when he turned the thought over in his mind, why was he bound to do that? Looked at clearly, what was the point of all human life but to draw out the greatest possible sum of pleasure? His own young, blooming life promised far more joy, for himself and for everyone around him, than the withered, wintry life of an old man long past the time for sharp, keen enjoyment, whose ideas had hardened into stiff, barren rigidity. This was the natural order, he told himself: maturity was nothing but a nest for the new life of youth. Baldassarre had had his turn, drunk his fill of life’s cup. It was Tito’s turn now.

The quest stretched ahead as nothing but a vague, exhausting prospect: a long dangerous voyage to Antioch, months or even years spent searching with no guarantee he would ever find the old man at all. He would have to leave all his new status, all the love that was already close to his lips, to return at last, if he succeeded, to the same exacting, joyless companionship he had known by rote as a boy. The gems Baldassarre had left him, and the florins he had converted them to, were technically Baldassarre’s, by the narrow rules that governed ordinary legal possession—but in the far truer, more natural order of things that gave the world to youth and strength, they belonged to the man who could wring the most joy out of them. He was aware that the rest of Florentine society would expect him to spend every last florin to rescue his benefactor, but what was society’s so-called moral sentiment, anyway? Nothing but a tangled mess of random, illogical traditions and opinions no wise man would follow, unless it suited his own comfort. He did not even care for the gold itself, he told himself; he would give up every florin for Romola’s sake without a second thought. But he would not fling away the sweet, full happiness that was within his reach, to wander the world thirsty for joy. Any rule that demanded a man throw away the good things that made life worth living was nothing but human selfishness turned inside out: made by people who wanted everyone else to sacrifice their own pleasure for their benefit. He did not want Baldassarre to suffer—he hated to see anyone suffer—but no philosophy could prove he was obligated to care more for another man’s pain than his own. That would only be required of him if he had loved Baldassarre devotedly, and he did not love him. Was that his fault? Gratitude, when you broke it down, made no real valid claim: Baldassarre’s life would have been dreary and empty without Tito in it. Did anyone owe a man a debt for the pleasure he got out of doing something for you?

Once he had started picking apart the old obligations that bound him to Baldassarre, his thoughts moved as fast and as corrosively as strong acid, eating clean through every last thread of sentiment that might have held him back. He felt no trace of that vague, unnameable dread of wrong-doing that even pagan worshippers had felt for the Nemesis, the quiet guardian of the soul that the old Greek plays sang of, the fear that forces men to wisdom even when no mortal law is there to punish them. Tito was too cultured, too sceptical for such old superstitions; he had been raised on mockery of priests whose lives were as notorious for their corruption as their fairy tales, and deep study of hundreds of philosophical debates over the Highest Good that he had long ago decided was nothing more than a matter of personal taste. The only fear that stirred in him was the fear of anything that might rob him of his pleasure, and he was very much afraid Fra Luca might be the thing that drove him out of Florence for good.

He stepped through the outer cloister of San Marco, breathing in the sharp scent of laurel and beeswax candles, and asked the porter for Fra Luca. The man who answered him was a lay brother, a raw contadino fresh from the farms outside Florence, his accent thick, his gaze dull and incurious.

“Fra Luca? Oh, he was taken up to the Dominican house at Fiesole at dawn, on a litter. The poor brother is very ill, they say the cool hill air might help him rest. Would you like to leave a message for him?”

“Thank you—my business can wait.” Tito turned away, a flood of relief so heavy it made his shoulders slack rolling through him. “That friar is not long for this world,” he murmured to himself. “I saw he was worn to nothing but skin and bone. No one at Fiesole will be reminded of me anytime soon. And even if he does come back one day, the explanation I have already crafted will serve just as well then as it would now.” One last faint, lingering thought pricked at him, unplaceable: he still could not put his finger on why Fra Luca’s face had seemed so hauntingly familiar, from some half-forgotten moment long ago.
