As surely as a man carries his burdens, so too does a woman bear hers, though the chroniclers of history, being predominantly male, often sketch these sorrows only with faint, cold lines, leaving the female heart to fill in the gaps with its own intuition. Thus, I shall merely point toward the storm that swept over Margaret Brandt until that fateful hour when she stepped into Eli's home as an adversary, yet emerged, having read her lover's letter, as a friend.

Now, however, a trial greater than any woman is fated to endure approached, while Gerard remained a ghost across the sea.

Margaret made scant use of Eli's newfound favor. For this reserve, she possessed a thousand plausible excuses, though the truth lay hidden deep within her: two men haunted that household, figures before whom she shrank with an instinctive, primal dread. In their countenances, she had seen wickedness and hate, along with the silent, conspiratorial glances of enemies bound by secret intelligence. Consequently, she preferred the sanctuary of her own hearth. There, Catherine arrived daily, consumed by the feverish anticipation of the expected birth.

Catherine belonged to that order of women whose sole divine mandate seems to be multiplication—the bearing forth, then hovering anxiously over the "fair scourges" still in motion, clucking and fussing as if she herself were multiplying by proxy. It was in this spirit that she beseeched Eli to remain in Rotterdam while she ventured back to Tergou.

"The poor lass stands alone," Catherine argued, her voice thick with worry. "What good are a pair of soldiers? Such beasts ought to be cast out upon the first breath of trouble."

Needless to say, these words offered Margaret profound relief.

Alas, the soul within her was a vessel brimming with anxiety as the appointed day neared. She feared death, and worse, she feared solitude without Gerard. Yet, the scales of fate balanced themselves. The poverty that had once nearly crushed her, alongside her father's frailty, now became her shield. Adversity's iron grip had forged in her a strength denied to many of her sex, forcing her to combat the natural lassitude that overtakes the wealthy, keeping her perpetually on her feet, laboring. She maintained this vigilance, guided by Catherine's counsel.

So it came to pass that on a fine evening, just as the sun dipped below the horizon, Margaret lay weak as water, yet secure. Beside her rested a tiny face, and above them opened the vast, gentle heavens of maternity.

"Why dost thou weep, sweetheart? All of a sudden?" Catherine asked gently.

"He is not here to behold it," Margaret whispered.

"Ay, well, lass, he shall return ere the babe is weaned. Meanwhile, consider how kindly God hath dealt with thee compared to any woman born. Had it not been for the threat my own Kate made against me, we might never have secured this bonny boy, a sturdy lad indeed—for which the Saints give thanks!"

"Yes, Mother. I am but a sorry, ungrateful wretch to weep. If only Gerard were here to witness it. 'Tis strange; I bore him well enough to feel no sorrow in his absence, but oh, it does seem so cruel that he may not share this joy. Prithee, prithee, come to me, Gerard! Dear, dear Gerard!" She stretched out her feeble arms toward the empty air.

Catherine bustled about, deliberately averting her gaze from Margaret's tear-stained face, for she could not restrain her own tears at hearing her own lost son invoked with such earnest love.

Soon, however, turning back, she found Margaret regarding her with a singular, piercing expression. "Didst thou hear nothing?"

"No, my lamb. What?"

"I did cry out for Gerard, but now..."

"Ay, ay, sure I heard that."

"Well, he answered me."

"Tush, girl: say not that."

"Mother, as sure as I lie here with his boy by my side, his voice returned to me. 'Margaret!' So. Yet methought 'twas not his happy voice. Perhaps that is because of the distance; all voices grow sad like when carried afar. Why art thou not happy, sweetheart? And I am so joyful this night?" Margaret's sweet eyes turned, gazing with rapt attention upon the sleeping infant in silence.

It was that same night that Gerard threw himself into the Tiber. And it was within that very hour that she heard him speaking her name, crying aloud amidst the jaws of death and despair.

"Margaret!"

Let those who can account for such mysteries try their hand; I cannot.
