I don’t want to wear out your patience, what? None of that ponderous literary tosh, I promise. I’m no wizard of the pen, mind, but I’ve a chappie who writes light romances tucked away on my Christmas card list, and I’ll get him to buff this whole thing up later so it reads like something out of a proper book.

Old Freddie Meadowes has been my best pal since we got caught tucking a frog in the headmaster’s teapot at Eton, so you can imagine my alarm when I trundled into the club one fine morning and found him hunched in the darkest corner, staring at his empty whiskey glass like it’d personally insulted his grandmother. Normally that bloke’s the life of every party—does a dead-on impression of a parrot arguing with a street musician, top-hole at charades, never lets the fun flag. That day he looked like the last leftover scone at a 7 AM breakfast, all squashed and lifeless.

Jimmy Pinkerton was with me, you know Jimmy, the playwright who churns out farces that make West End crowds snort lemonade out their noses. We sat down, plied him with two more whiskeys, and pried the story out of him.

Naturally, it was a girl. Angela West, his fiancée, had called the whole thing off. Wouldn’t take his calls, sent every letter he posted back unopened, slipped across the street when she saw him coming. Freddie wouldn’t say exactly what the row was about, but it was clear she’d had her fill of him.

My heart ached for the poor chump. I know that exact sinking feeling—once I was head over heels for Elizabeth Shoolbred, who told me to my face I was “the sort of man who’d forget his own head if it wasn’t glued on” and went off to marry a golf pro. I knew exactly what Freddie needed.

“Change of scene, old scout,” I told him. “I’ve taken a little cottage down at Marvis Bay. Jimmy’s joining us on the 24th. We’ll lark about on the beach, no awkward run-ins, no nothing. You’ll be right as rain in a fortnight.”

“Couldn’t be sounder,” Jimmy chipped in. “I knew a chap once whose girl dumped him, he went off to Egypt for two months, and by the time he came back she was wiring him begging to come home. Trouble was, he’d forgotten her surname, so he never even answered.”

Freddie wasn’t buying it, but he tagged along anyway, said he might as well mope in Dorset as in London.

Marvis Bay’s no Monte Carlo, let’s be honest. It’s the sort of place where the most exciting event of the week is the vicar’s cat getting stuck up the oak tree. You spend all day splashing about in the briny or dozing on the sand, then the evening gets eaten alive by gnats so fierce they’d bite through a woolen jumper if you stood still long enough. You get back to the cottage at 9, slather calamine lotion all over the bites, and crash.

At first Freddie lapped it up. The second the moon came up he was trotting down to the beach, and the gnats adopted him as their official patron. They’d buzz past perfectly nice passersby without a second glance, saving all their stinging energy for old Freddie’s exposed ankles.

Peaceful as it was, by the end of the first week I was dying for Jimmy to show up. Freddie wasn’t exactly the ideal houseguest. Half the time he was glowering at the carpet smoking his pipe so thick you couldn’t see the mantelpiece, the other half he was prodding the cottage piano with one finger, bashing out *The Rosary*. He couldn’t play a single other note, and every single time he got to the third bar he’d mess up and have to start all over again.

He was mid-tune one morning when I got back from a dip. His voice was so hollow it echoed.
“Reggie,” he said. “I’ve seen her.”
“Angela? Out here?”
“Ran straight into her at the post office door. She cut me dead. Looked right through me like I was a pane of glass.” He banged the piano key again, messed up the second bar this time. “We ought to leave. Now.”

“Rot,” I said. “This is the best possible thing that could have happened. You’ve got a second chance right here. All you need to do is get her to owe you a favour, right? Make her all soft and grateful, and she’ll forget the whole quarrel.”
“Grateful for what?”
“Save her from drowning,” I said, quick as a flash.
“Can’t swim.”

Typical Freddie. Top bloke, can polo like a champion, but zero initiative when you need it. He went back to hammering the piano, and I legged it out the door to pace the sands and think. I was rounding a pile of jagged rocks when I spotted that blue dress—sixteen photos of her plastered all over Freddie’s bedroom wall, I’d recognise Angela West anywhere. She was sat on the sand building sandcastles with a round, jolly-looking blob of a kid, and an elderly aunt was dozing on a deck chair a few feet off. I heard her call the kid “Tootles,” and he was the sort of chubby bulging toddler who looks like he’s been inflated with a bicycle pump.

After the third sandcastle got washed away, Tootles got bored and wandered off, smacking a jellyfish with his spade. No one else was in sight. And that’s when the brainwave hit me. It was so brilliant I almost smacked my own forehead. Even Sherlock Holmes wouldn’t have thought of this one. I’d grab Tootles for half an hour, right? Panic Angela half out of her wits, then send Freddie trotting up leading the little nipper by the hand, telling her he’d found the poor lost toddler wandering near the dangerous rocks, saved him from falling in the sea. She’d be so overwhelmed with gratitude she’d forgive Freddie every single thing he ever did. The reconciliation would be soft, and warm, and there’d be no more broken engagements.

I picked Tootles up, slung him over my shoulder, and beat it back to the cottage. He started wailing so loud the windows rattled the second we got over the threshold. Freddie stared at him like I’d just brought home a stray alligator.
“Reggie,” he said, “what in the name of sanity have you done?”

I grabbed a jar of honey from the kitchen, slathered a spoonful on it, and the wailing stopped dead. Tootles stuck his whole face in the jar, quiet as a mouse. I laid out my genius plan to Freddie, and slowly the light dawned.
“You’re not quite as big an idiot as you look sometimes, Pepper,” he said, and hauled Tootles out of the honey and dragged him off to find Angela.

I leaned back on the veranda smoking my cigarette, feeling like the cleverest man alive. Any second now Freddie would be back, engaged again, all sunshine and roses. Then I spotted them trundling back down the road ten minutes later, and Freddie looked like he’d just had his puppy run over.
“Couldn’t find her?” I said.
“Found her all right,” he said, with a hollow, bitter laugh. “Reggie, you complete and utter chump. That child isn’t her cousin. She’s never met him before in her life. She just stopped to help a random stranger’s kid on the beach. She looked at me like I was a certified lunatic rambling about rescuing some kid she’d never seen five minutes prior. I looked like a bigger fool than that American bloke who claimed he’d reached the North Pole. And now I’m stuck with him.”

Turns out I’d become an accidental kidnapper. I traipsed all over Marvis Bay with Tootles for hours asking after his parents, and no one claimed him—you’d have thought he was a lone hiker with his own little rented bungalow, for all the fuss anyone made. Finally the sweet stall guy told me he was Tootles Medwin, his family lived at Ocean Rest on Beach Road.

I knocked on the door, and no one came. Then a round pink face with grey whiskers popped out the upstairs window.
“Hi!” he yelled.

I yelled back that I’d brought Tootles home. He peered at me, asked if I was related to Edward Pepper the coal mine owner, who was my uncle.
“Old Ted! Great pal of mine!” he whooped. “Perfect timing! We’ve got the mumps in the house. Our daughter Bootles has just come down with it, we can’t risk Tootles catching it. We’ve been tearing our hair out trying to find someone to look after him till my brother can come from London to pick him up. You’re the very man! You’ll look after him for a week, won’t you? I’ll pay you handsomely!”

Before I could say I’d rather house a nest of vipers, he’d slammed the window shut. Five seconds later he popped back up, dropped a whole bag of breakfast cereal on my head, yelled “Make sure he gets Bailey’s Granulated Chips for breakfast!” and vanished again.

I dragged Tootles back towards the cottage, and who should we run into but Angela. Tootles yelped, pointed at her, and waddled over.
“Oh, hello little one,” she said, smiling, then turned to me. “You’re his daddy, aren’t you? We had such fun building sandcastles this morning.”

She was gone before I could even splutter a denial. Freddie heard the story and went so purple in the face I thought he’d have a stroke. He ranted for ten solid minutes, using words I didn’t even know he knew. I calmed him down eventually, by reminding him we were old school pals, he owed me ten quid, and I’d done the whole daft thing for his sake. He agreed not to run off back to London, but he refused point blank to be grateful.

We bribed Tootles with a mountain of sweets to keep him quiet the rest of the day, fumbled him out of his clothes when he passed out on the sofa, and carted him up to bed. The next morning we borrowed the nurse from the bungalow next door, who dressed Tootles in eight minutes flat. We were just starting to think things might be manageable when Jimmy Pinkerton turned up, hauling a suitcase. He spotted Tootles mid-yell, and backed towards the door.
“Hotel for me,” he said. “Can’t write a word with that racket going on. Whose infant is this?”

We told him the whole story, the kidnapping, the mumps, Angela thinking Reggie was Tootles’ dad. Jimmy’s eyes went wide. He squatted down on the sand, scribbling notes on the back of a play script.
“This is perfect,” he said. “This is a West End farce, ready made. We don’t even need to write it. All we have to do is coach little Tootles to run up to Angela the next time we see her, and yell ‘Kiss Freddie!’ She’ll be so charmed, she’ll forget the whole quarrel and fall back into his arms. It’s foolproof!”

We spent the next three days trying to teach Tootles the line, bribing him with acid drops every time he got it half right. The little bloke was a disaster. Half the time he’d yell “Kick Freddie!” instead, which would have torpedoed the whole plan. Jimmy said we weren’t ready, we needed another day of rehearsals.

Fate, of course, had other plans. The next afternoon, Freddie was sat at the piano, bashing away at *The Rosary*, and I was just herding Tootles out onto the veranda, when Angela came strolling up the path.
“Good morning,” she said, grinning at Tootles. “Hope you don’t mind if I pop in? My aunt’s been trying to find a piano tuner for weeks, and I can hear yours working away. I’ll just pop in and ask him to stop by our bungalow when he’s done.”

I froze. Six feet away, Freddie was still hammering that piano, blissfully unaware. There was no way to stop her. She sat down on the veranda step, chatting to Tootles, and announced she’d wait right there till the tuner was done. I babbled something about going down to the beach, but she didn’t hear me. She was digging in her pocket for something.
“The beach,” I babbled.
