Shirley Keeldar's sincerity regarding her desire for Caroline's companionship was evident, for she actively sought it. Yet, Miss Helstone, usually slow to forge new acquaintances, was hesitant; she harbored the belief that no one desired her company, that she lacked the capacity to amuse. To her, a radiant, joyful young heiress like Shirley seemed entirely too detached from the mundane world she inhabited to genuinely appreciate it. However, Shirley, though likely brilliant and happy, could not exist independently of warm social interaction. Within a month, she had acquainted herself with most local families—the Misses Sykes, the Misses Pearson, and the formidable Misses Wynne of Walden Hall—yet she found none of them truly congenial. She refrained from fraternizing with them, a sentiment she later voiced to her former governess, Mrs. Pryor. Speaking to Mrs. Pryor, Shirley confessed that even if she were truly Lord Keeldar of Briarfield, there was not a single eligible maiden in the neighboring parishes she would invite to become Lady Keeldar. Mrs. Pryor received this pronouncement with quiet composure, advising Shirley to abandon the habit of referring to herself as a gentleman, lest it appear as an affectation to outsiders.

Shirley never mocked her former teacher, respecting even her minor formalities. Thus, she accepted Mrs. Pryor's admonition silently. Standing near the window, she gazed at a magnificent cedar tree, observing a bird perched on a low branch. Soon, she began to chirrup at the bird, transitioning into a clear whistle that evolved into a sweet, deft melody. Startled, Mrs. Pryor exclaimed, "My dear! Were you whistling?"

"I forgot," admitted Shirley, bowing slightly. "I intended to avoid whistling before you."

"But where did you acquire this habit, Miss Keeldar? You never did it before arriving in Yorkshire."

"Oh, I learned it long ago."

"By whom?"

"No one. I picked it up by listening and eventually abandoned it. However, yesterday evening, as I walked up the lane, I heard a gentleman whistling that exact tune across the hedge in the field. It jogged my memory."

"Which gentleman?"

"There is but one gentleman in this region, ma'am, who is not gray-haired: Mr. Moore. Though Mr. Helstone and Mr. Yorke are fine old beaux, infinitely superior to the dull youths around us."

Mrs. Pryor fell silent.

"Do you dislike Mr. Helstone, ma'am?"

"His official position shields him from criticism."

"You typically excuse yourself when he is announced."

"Do you intend to walk today, my dear?"

"Yes, I shall visit the rectory to locate Caroline and encourage her to exercise. She requires a brisk walk across Nunnely Common."

"If you proceed in that direction, please ensure Miss Helstone wraps warmly; a fresh wind is blowing, and she appears susceptible to illness."

"You need not worry, Mrs. Pryor. Will you accompany us?"

"No, my love; I would hinder you. I am heavy-footed and cannot match your pace."

Shirley successfully persuaded Caroline to join her. Once they were upon the quiet road, traversing the expansive, solitary sweep of Nunnely Common, conversation flowed easily. After overcoming initial reserve, Caroline found herself eager to speak with Miss Keeldar. Their exchange of casual observations revealed much about each other instantly. Shirley expressed her fondness for the lush green turf and the heath atop the ridges, noting that the latter reminded her of the Scottish moors she had traversed during travel. She recalled a specific journey from noon until sunset on a sultry, sunless summer afternoon—a seemingly endless waste of deep heath populated only by wild sheep and the cries of wild birds.

"I know exactly how the heath would appear on such a day," Caroline observed. "Purple-black, a deeper shade than the sky, appearing almost livid."

"Precisely livid," Shirley agreed, describing brassy-edged clouds and intermittent white gleams that seemed more ghastly than the lurid tints, threatening to ignite into blinding lightning. "Did it thunder?"

"It murmured distant peals, but the storm did not break until evening, after we reached our inn—an isolated house at the foot of a mountain range."

"Did you watch the clouds descend over the mountains?"

"I stood at the window for an hour. The hills seemed rolled into a sullen mist, and when the rain fell in whitening sheets, suddenly they vanished, wiped clean from the world."

"I have witnessed similar storms in Yorkshire's hilly districts. At their riotous peak, with the sky turned to cataracts and the earth to floods, I have recalled the Biblical Deluge."

"It is strangely refreshing to feel the calm return after such a hurricane, receiving a consoling gleam from the opening clouds, softly testifying that the sun remains unquenched."

"Miss Keeldar, stand still and gaze down at Nunnely Dale and the woods."

They halted on the verdant brow of the common, overlooking the deep valley robed in May's attire. Varied meadows sparkled with daisies and king-cups, bathed in sunlight where transparent emeralds and amber gleams danced. Nunnwood—the sole remnant of ancient British forest amidst a landscape once covered in woodland—lay shrouded in a cloud's shadow. Distant hills were dappled, the horizon shaded like mother-of-pearl, blending silvery blues, soft purples, evanescent greens, and rose-tones into fleecy white clouds, pure as azure snow, drawing the eye toward the foundations of heaven. The air on the brow was fresh, sweet, and invigorating.

"Our England is a beautiful island," Shirley declared, "and Yorkshire is one of its fairest corners."

"Are you a Yorkshire girl as well?"

"I am—in blood and birth. Five generations of my lineage sleep beneath the aisles of Briarfield Church. I drew my first breath in the old black hall behind us."

Caroline extended her hand, which Shirley took and shook. "We are compatriots," she said.

"Yes," Shirley nodded gravely. Pointing to the forest, she asked, "That is Nunnwood?"

"It is."

"Were you ever there?"

"Many times."

"In the heart of it?"

"Yes."

"What is it like?"

"It resembles an encampment of forest sons of Anak. The trees are immense and ancient. Standing at their roots, the summits seem to belong to another realm. The trunks stand still and firm as pillars, while the branches sway with every breeze. Even in the deepest calm, their leaves are never perfectly hushed; in high winds, a flood rushes, a sea thunders overhead."

"Was it not a haunt of Robin Hood?"

"Yes, and remnants of him still exist. To enter Nunnwood, Miss Keeldar, is to step far back into the dim days of antiquity. Can you see a break in the forest near the center?"

"Yes, distinctly."

"That break reveals a dell—a deep, hollow cup lined with turf as green and short as the common itself. Ancient, gnarled mighty oaks crowd the brink. In the bottom lie the ruins of a nunnery."

"We will go there, you and I alone, Caroline, on a fine summer morning. We can bring pencils, sketchbooks, and books for reading. Naturally, we shall take food. My housekeeper, Mrs. Gill, could pack provisions in two little baskets, which we could each carry. Would it tire you too much to walk so far?"

"Oh, no! Especially if we rest the entire day in the wood. I know all the finest spots. I know where to gather nuts in autumn and where wild strawberries abound. I know certain lonely, untouched glades carpeted with strange mosses—some yellow as if gilded, some a sober gray, others gem-green. I know clusters of trees that ravish the eye with perfect, pictorial contrasts: rude oaks, delicate birches, glossy beeches; ash trees stately as Saul standing alone; superannuated wood-giants clad in bright shrouds of ivy. Miss Keeldar, I could guide you."

"You would be dull with me alone?"

"Not at all. I believe we would suit. What third person could exist whose presence would not spoil our pleasure?"

"Indeed, I know of no lady of our age, and as for gentlemen—"

"An excursion changes entirely when gentlemen are present," Caroline interrupted.

"I agree—it becomes quite a different affair from what we proposed."

"We intended merely to view the old trees and ruins, to spend a day in old times, surrounded by ancient silence and above all, quietude."

"You are right. The presence of gentlemen dispels the last charm. If they are of the wrong sort, like your Malones, the young Sykes, or the Wynnes, irritation replaces serenity. If they are of the right sort, there is still a change—one easy to feel, difficult to describe."

"We forget Nature, *imprimis*."

"And then Nature forgets us, covering her vast calm brow with a dim veil, concealing her face, and withdrawing the peaceful joy she would have filled our hearts if we had worshipped her alone."

"What does she offer instead?"

"More elation and more anxiety; an excitement that steals the hours away rapidly, and a trouble that ruffles their course."
