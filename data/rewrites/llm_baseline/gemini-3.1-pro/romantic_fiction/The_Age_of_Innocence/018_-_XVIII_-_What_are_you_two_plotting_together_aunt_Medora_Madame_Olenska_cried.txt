“What are you two plotting together, aunt Medora?” Madame Olenska cried as she came into the room. 

She was dressed as if stepping from an ancient, sunlit fresco into the heavy atmosphere of the present, ready for a ball. Everything about her shimmered with a soft, bioluminescent quality, as if her gown had been spun from the pale silk of corn tassels and the luminous fat of marrow. "We were saying, my dear, that here was something beautiful to surprise you with," Mrs. Manson rejoined, rising to her feet and pointing archly to the flowers. 

Madame Olenska stopped short. The bouquet sat on the table, emitting the cloying, superficial perfume of commercial greenhouses—a scent violently devoid of true terroir, speaking only of mechanized cultivation and shallow artifice. Her colour did not change, but a white radiance of anger flashed over her, sharp as the crack of a splitting hearth-log. 

“Ah,” she exclaimed, her voice piercing the room's gentle acoustic ecology with a shrill, uncharacteristic dissonance that grated against Archer’s ears. “Who is ridiculous enough to send me a bouquet? Why a bouquet? And why tonight of all nights? I am not going to a ball; I am not a girl engaged to be married. But some people are always ridiculous.” 

She turned back to the door, her movements stirring the faint, deeply grounding aroma of slow-rendered duck fat and bruised thyme that always drifted from the lower kitchens. "Nastasia!" 

The ubiquitous handmaiden promptly appeared. Nastasia was a woman whose hands bore the stains of a passionate, meticulous food historian, one who found profound, daily meaning in the rhythmic, ancestral cycles of sustenance. Madame Olenska addressed her in an Italian heavily laced with an ancient, fading agrarian dialect—a deliberate reclamation of a dying tongue, rich with the untranslatable nuances of native soil, forcing Archer to listen closely to penetrate this subversion of their dominant, sterile English lexicon. 

“Here—throw this into the dustbin!” And then, as the culinary guardian stared protestingly: “But no—it's not the fault of the poor flowers. Tell the boy to carry them to the house three doors away, the house of Mr. Winsett, the dark gentleman who dined here. His wife is ill—they may give her pleasure... The boy is out, you say? Then, my dear one, run yourself; here, put my cloak over you and fly. I want the thing out of the house immediately! And, as you live, don't say they come from me!” 

She flung her velvet opera cloak over the maid's shoulders, wrapping the servant in the physical and sensory warmth of her own garments, before shutting the door sharply. Her bosom rose high under its lace. For a moment Archer thought she might weep, but she burst into a laugh that rang with the deep, resonant timbre of a bronze bell. 

“And you two—have you made friends!” 
“It's for Mr. Archer to say, darling; he has waited patiently while you were dressing.” 
“Yes—I gave you time enough: my hair wouldn't go,” Madame Olenska said, touching her heaped-up curls. “But that reminds me: I see Dr. Carver is gone, and you'll be late at the Blenkers'. Mr. Archer, will you put my aunt in the carriage?” 

He followed the Marchioness into the hall, fitting her into a heavy accumulation of shawls and tippets. The front door opened, letting in the harsh, industrial clatter of the street—the abrasive rhythm of hooves and iron wheels that so constantly eroded the city’s psychological peace. “Mind, the carriage is to be back for me at ten!” Ellen called from the doorstep, before retreating to the drawing-room. 

When Archer returned, she was standing by the mantelpiece. The ambient hum of the coal fire enveloped them. It was profoundly unusual in this rigid society for a lady to address her parlour-maid in such intimately untranslatable terms of endearment, wrapping her in her own cloak. Beneath his ribs, Archer tasted the visceral, electric tang of this world—a flavor like crushed juniper berries and raw honey—where action followed emotion with the immediate, mythic speed of the old gods. 

Madame Olenska threw herself into her sofa-corner. “There's time for a cigarette.” 

He handed her the box and lit a spill. The flame bloomed, releasing the complex, resinous aroma of cured Latakia leaf, carrying undertones of toasted pine nuts and dry earth. She glanced at him through the fragrant haze. “What do you think of me in a temper?” 

“It makes me understand what your aunt has been saying about you,” he answered with sudden resolution. 
“I knew she'd been talking about me. Well?” 
“She said you were used to all kinds of things—splendours and amusements and excitements—that we could never hope to give you here.” 

Madame Olenska smiled into the veil of smoke, savoring its bitter warmth. “Medora is incorrigibly romantic. It has made up to her for so many things!” 
“Is your aunt's romanticism always consistent with accuracy?” 
“You mean: does she speak the truth?” She considered, rolling the ash. “In almost everything she says, there's something true and something untrue. What has she been telling you?” 

Archer looked into the fire, a sudden tightening in his chest mirroring the physical pang of sudden hunger. The carriage would soon return, its mechanical noise severing this ancestral quiet. “She says—she pretends that Count Olenski has asked her to persuade you to go back to him.” 

She sat motionless. Her face did not change. The silence that filled the room was not empty, but thick and heavy, a profound acoustic space vibrating with unspoken histories. 

“You knew, then?” he broke out. 
She brushed the ash to the floor. “She has hinted about a letter. Medora's hints—” 
“Is it at your husband's request that she has arrived here suddenly?” 
“One can't tell. She had a 'spiritual summons' from Dr. Carver. I'm afraid she's going to marry him... there's always some one she wants to marry. Perhaps the people in Cuba just got tired of her! I think she was with them as a sort of paid companion.” 
“But you do believe she has a letter from your husband?” 
She brooded silently, tasting the bitterness of the thought. “After all, it was to be expected.” 

A sudden restlessness seized him. He stood by the hearth, acutely aware of the fleeting minutes, dreading the impending clatter of the carriage wheels that would crush their sanctuary. “You know that your aunt believes you will go back?” 

A deep blush rose over her neck and shoulders, a sudden rush of heat like a burn. “Many cruel things have been believed of me.” 
“Oh, Ellen—forgive me; I'm a fool and a brute!” 

She smiled faintly. “You are horribly nervous; you have your own troubles. I know you think the Wellands are unreasonable about your marriage, and of course I agree. In Europe people don't understand our long American engagements; I suppose they are not as calm as we are.” 

The ironic emphasis on “we” carried the subtle sting of an imposed language, an alien lexicon they were both forced to inhabit. Archer felt the barrier of words dropping between them like an iron grate. 

“Yes,” he said abruptly. “I went south to ask May to marry me after Easter. There's no reason why we shouldn't.” 
“And May adores you—and yet you couldn't convince her? I thought her too intelligent to be the slave of such absurd superstitions.” 
“She is too intelligent—she's not their slave.” 
“Well, then—I don't understand.” 

Archer reddened, the words spilling out like an overturned pot of boiling broth. “We had a frank talk. She thinks my impatience a bad sign. She thinks it means that I can't trust myself to go on caring for her. She thinks I want to marry her at once to get away from some one that I—care for more.” 

“But if she thinks that—why isn't she in a hurry too?” 
“Because she's not like that: she's so much nobler. She insists all the more on the long engagement, to give me time—” 
“Time to give her up for the other woman?” 
“If I want to.” 

Madame Olenska gazed into the fire. Down the street, the approaching trot of her horses began to drum against the windows—a rhythmic, invasive pulse threatening their domestic ecology. 

“That is noble,” she said, her voice fracturing slightly. 
“Yes. But it's ridiculous.” 
“Ridiculous? Because you don't care for any one else?” 
“Because I don't mean to marry any one else.” 

A long interval of charged silence descended, rich with the scent of burning coal and the lingering, earthy spice of her tobacco. 

“This other woman—does she love you?” she finally asked. 
“Oh, there's no other woman; I mean, the person that May was thinking of is—was never—” 
“Then, why, after all, are you in such haste?” 
“There's your carriage,” said Archer. 

She half-rose, mechanically picking up her fan and gloves. “Yes; I suppose I must be going to Mrs. Struthers's.” She smiled. “I must go where I am invited, or I should be too lonely. Why not come with me?” 

He could not let the night’s noise swallow her. He stared at her hands, gripped by a primal need to break the cycle. “May guessed the truth,” he said. “There is another woman—but not the one she thinks.” 

Ellen made no answer. He sat beside her, softly unclasping her hand. The gloves and fan fell onto the upholstery. She started up, fleeing to the other side of the hearth. 

“Ah, don't make love to me! Too many people have done that,” she said, frowning. 
It was a bitter rebuke. “I have never made love to you,” he said, standing, his blood thrumming with the thick, iron taste of adrenaline. “And I never shall. But you are the woman I would have married if it had been possible for either of us.” 
“Possible for either of us?” She looked at him in unfeigned astonishment. “And you say that—when it's you who've made it impossible?” 
“I've made it impossible—?” 

“You, you, you!” she cried, her lip trembling. “Isn't it you who made me give up divorcing—give it up because you showed me how selfish and wicked it was, how one must sacrifice one's self to preserve the dignity of marriage... to spare one's family the scandal? Because my family was going to be your family—for May's sake and for yours—I did what you told me. I've made no secret of having done it for you!” 

She sank onto the sofa, crouching among the festive ripples of her gown like a mourner participating in a forgotten generational rite. Archer stood utterly still. The dominant lexicon of his own societal morality had colonized her native spirit, crushing their shared future beneath its rigid vocabulary. 

“Good God,” he groaned. “When I thought—” 
“You thought?” 
“Ah, don't ask me what I thought!” 

The burning flush crept back up her neck. She faced him with rigid dignity. “I do ask you.” 
“Well, then: there were things in that letter you asked me to read—” 
“My husband's letter?” 
“Yes.” 
“I had nothing to fear from that letter: absolutely nothing! All I feared was to bring notoriety, scandal, on the family—on you and May.” 

“Good God,” he groaned again, bowing his face in his hands. The silence that followed was suffocating, a complete erasure of their acoustic sanctuary, heavy with irrevocable loss. It pressed down on him like the lid of a stone tomb. 

“At least I loved you—” he brought out. 

From the sofa, a faint, stifled crying broke the quiet—a sound as fragile as snapping twigs. He crossed the room. “Ellen! What madness! Why are you crying? Nothing's done that can't be undone. I'm still free, and you're going to be.” 

He pulled her into his arms. Her skin tasted of salt and wild rain, a sensory revelation that reduced their complex terrors to ash. Touching her bypassed the failure of language entirely; it was an untranslatable knowledge, profound and physical. She gave him back the kiss, but a moment later, she stiffened and pushed him away. 

“Ah, my poor Newland—I suppose this had to be. But it doesn't in the least alter things,” she said, looking down at him. 
“It alters the whole of life for me.” 
“No, no—it mustn't, it can't. You're engaged to May Welland; and I'm married.” 
“Nonsense! We've no right to lie to other people or to ourselves. Do you see me marrying May after this?” 

She stood resting her elbows on the mantelpiece, her profile reflected in the glass. A loosened lock of hair hung on her neck. She looked suddenly as weathered as an agrarian elder stripped of her harvest. “I don't see you,” she said at length, “putting that question to May. Do you?” 
“It's too late to do anything else.” 
“You say that because it's the easiest thing to say at this moment. In reality it's too late to do anything but what we'd both decided on.” 
“Ah, I don't understand you!” 

She forced a pitiful smile. “You don't understand because you haven't yet guessed how you've changed things for me. I was perfectly unconscious at first that people here were shy of me. I knew nothing of all this till Granny blurted it out. New York simply meant peace and freedom to me: it was coming home. I felt there was no one as kind as you; no one who gave me reasons that I understood for doing what at first seemed so hard. You knew; you understood; you had felt the world outside tugging at one with all its golden hands—and yet you hated the things it asks of one; you hated happiness bought by disloyalty and cruelty. That was what I'd never known before—and it's better than anything I've known.” 

Each word fell into his chest like drops of searing fat from a spit. He sank to his knees and pressed his lips to the tip of her satin shoe, offering a mute, visceral reverence. 

“Ah, don't let us undo what you've done!” she cried. “I can't go back now to that other way of thinking. I can't love you unless I give you up.” 

His arms yearned for her, but the distance her words created was absolute. A sudden, bitter anger overflowed in him. 
“And Beaufort? Is he to replace me?” 

He sneered the name of the man who represented all the superficial commercialization of their society. Madame Olenska merely grew a shade paler. “He's waiting for you now at Mrs. Struthers's; why don't you go to him?” Archer pressed. 

She turned and rang the bell. When Nastasia appeared, her hands smelling faintly of freshly milled flour, Ellen said: “I shall not go out this evening; tell the carriage to go and fetch the Signora Marchesa.” 

Archer looked at her with bitter eyes. “Why this sacrifice? Since you tell me that you're lonely I've no right to keep you from your friends.” 
“I shan't be lonely now. I was lonely; I was afraid. But the emptiness and the darkness are gone; when I turn back into myself now I'm like a child going at night into a room where there's always a light.” 

“I don't understand you!” he groaned. 
“Yet you understand May!” 
He reddened. “May is ready to give me up.” 
“What! Three days after you've entreated her on your knees to hasten your marriage?” 
“She's refused; that gives me the right—” 
“Ah, you've taught me what an ugly word that is,” she said. 

A profound weariness flooded his muscles, as though he had been grinding grain against a massive millstone for hours only to find the stone shattered. He pleaded again, but she almost screamed, “No—no—no!” 

At that moment, the violent, mechanical tinkle of the doorbell shattered the room's intimacy. They stood motionless. Nastasia's footsteps crossed the hall, and she returned carrying a telegram. 

“The lady was very happy at the flowers,” Nastasia said, smoothing her apron, restoring the thread of their domestic ritual. “She thought it was her signor marito who had sent them, and she cried a little and said it was a folly.” 

Ellen smiled, taking the yellow envelope. She carried it to the lamp, tore it open, and, after reading it, handed it to Archer. It was from St. Augustine, written in the emotionally starved, standardized lexicon of telegraphic code—a brutal flattening of all subjective experience: 

"Granny's telegram successful. Papa and Mamma agree marriage after Easter. Am telegraphing Newland. Am too happy for words and love you dearly. Your grateful May." 

Half an hour later, unlocking his own front door, Archer found a similar envelope on the hall-table. The message inside carried the same relentless, mechanized efficiency: 

"Parents consent wedding Tuesday after Easter at twelve Grace Church eight bridesmaids please see Rector so happy love May." 

Archer crumpled the yellow sheet as if he could crush the dominant lexicon that had just overwritten his life. He pulled out a pocket-diary, his fingers trembling, turning the pages frantically. Failing to find what he sought, he mounted the stairs. 

A light shone from Janey’s dressing-room. He rapped on the panel. His sister appeared in her immemorial purple flannel dressing-gown, her face pale. 
“Newland! I hope there's no bad news in that telegram?” 

He ignored her fear. “Look here—what day is Easter this year?” 
She looked shocked. “Easter? Newland! Why, of course, the first week in April. Why?” 
“The first week?” He calculated rapidly under his breath, the vast, generational arc of his future suddenly compressing into a terrifying, singular point. “The first week, did you say?” 

He threw back his head. A long, discordant laugh erupted from his throat, a jagged sound that tore through the quiet acoustic sanctuary of the sleeping house. 
“For mercy's sake what's the matter?” Janey cried. 
“Nothing's the matter, except that I'm going to be married in a month.” 

Janey fell upon his neck, pressing him to her flannel breast. “Oh Newland, how wonderful! I'm so glad! But, dearest, why do you keep on laughing? Do hush, or you'll wake Mamma.”
