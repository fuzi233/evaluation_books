The sun beat down upon the dusty veldt, casting a golden glow over the landscape as the three columns crept forward, their footsteps silent on the parched earth. It was as if time itself had slowed, the only sound the soft rustle of the grasses and the distant hum of the wind. Sir Henry, ever the strategist, had devised a plan to deliver the assault simultaneously, the threefold force converging like the tendrils of a great vine.

Good's eyes scanned the horizon, his mind racing with the possibilities. "Oh, for a Gatling gun," he muttered, his voice low and urgent. "We could clear that plain in twenty minutes." But Sir Henry remained unmoved, his gaze fixed on the approaching forces. "We have no such luxury, my friend," he said dryly. "But try a shot, Quatermain. See how close you can get to that tall fellow in command."

I raised my rifle, feeling the familiar weight of the express in my hands. The target stood tall, his features etched against the blue sky. I lay down behind a nearby boulder, taking aim at the spot where the general's neck met his shoulder. The rifle, like all expresses, was sighted to three hundred and fifty yards, so I calculated the trajectory, allowing for the drop. My finger tightened on the trigger, and I squeezed off the shot.

The puff of smoke cleared, and I felt a jolt of disappointment. The general stood untouched, while his orderly, mere yards away, crumpled to the ground. A cry went up from the regiments, cheering at the display of marksmanship. But I seethed with frustration, my pride wounded by the miss. In a moment of rashness, I raised my rifle once more, taking aim at the general as he fled. This time, my shot found its mark, and the man stumbled, throwing up his arms before falling to the ground.

The impact was palpable, the other regiments faltering in their advance. Sir Henry and Good joined the fray, their rifles crackling as they fired into the enemy ranks. I added my own shots, the Winchester repeater firing steadily as the battle raged on. The enemy lines wavered, their numbers dwindling as our fire took its toll. But the tide of the battle was turning, the other two divisions closing in from either flank.
