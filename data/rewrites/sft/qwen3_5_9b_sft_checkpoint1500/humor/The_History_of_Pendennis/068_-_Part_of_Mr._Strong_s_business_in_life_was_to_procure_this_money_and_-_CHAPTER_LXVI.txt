In Which Pen Begins His Canvass

If Clavering Park had ever known a gloom darker than the days preceding Pen's marriage, it was not nearly so cheerless now as it became under the tenure of Sir Francis Clavering. The vast majority of the mansion stood locked and silent, a monument to a bankrupt proprietor currently exiled to foreign shores. Inside, the Baronet was confined to a few parlors on the ground floor, where his housekeeper and her assistant from the lodge waited upon him with the dutiful servitude of forced exile. Here, amidst the dust of unoccupied halls, he spent his dreary mornings shooting at game, which they then boiled into a meal fit for a refugee.

Lightfoot, his former man, had wisely crossed over to serve "My Lady," and as Pen learned via a letter from Mr. Smirke—who had officiated at the ceremony—the young fellow had successfully executed his prudent design of marrying Mrs. Bonner, My Lady's woman. This union was a triumph of nature; Mrs. Bonner, though of mature years, possessed a charm that struck the youth with irresistible force, and she endowed him with both her savings and her substantial frame. To become joint landlords of the Clavering Arms was the shared ambition of the pair, and it was settled that they would remain in service until quarter-day, at which point they would take possession of their hotel. Pen graciously pledged to hold his election dinner there once the Baronet vacated his seat, and indeed, as arranged by his Uncle, Arthur descended upon Clavering Park in September. The owner, desperate for companionship to alleviate his solitude—and perhaps hoping to borrow some ready cash—was delighted to have a visitor.

Pen, true to his word, furnished his host with a couple of pounds within days of Arthur's arrival. The moment these funds landed in Sir Francis's pocket, however, the Baronet found urgent business waiting for him at Chatteris and various neighboring watering-places. He promptly departed to attend to affairs that were likely transacted at county racecourses and billiard rooms. Arthur, possessing ample mental resources, managed quite well alone. He walked with the gamekeeper in the mornings and consumed books and cigars by the fire in the evenings, proving to be a literary genius who needed little else to pass the night. Yet, within two or three days, the society of Sir Francis Clavering became utterly intolerable to Arthur. With a mischievous eagerness, he offered the Baronet the little pecuniary aid the latter customarily solicited, thereby supplying him with the perfect excuse to flee his own house.

Moreover, Pen found himself tasked with ingratiating himself with the townsfolk of Clavering and the voters of the borough he hoped to represent. Remembering how unpopular he had previously been among the simple folk, he resolved to vanquish the odium he had inspired. A natural humorist, Pen relished this task. Transforming overnight from a reserved, somewhat sullen dandy into a figure of boisterous joviality, he became as frank and easy as Captain Strong. He laughed with everyone willing to share a chuckle, shook hands with dexterous cordiality, and made appearances at market-days and farmers' ordinaries. He acted as a consummate hypocrite, precisely the sort of high-born, spotless gentleman who feigns warmth to win over constituents for personal gain. How easily do we allow ourselves to be fooled? We know it is false coin, flattery distributed at no cost, yet we prefer it to silence. Friend Pen moved through Clavering laboriously simple and adroitly pleased, a stark contrast to the scornful young man the villagers recalled from ten years prior.

Meanwhile, the Rectory stood shuttered. Dr. Portman had fled to Harrogate with his gout and family—an event Pen deplored deeply in a letter, expressing regret for missing his old friend whose advice he might need. Consoled by this absence, Pen turned his attention to Mr. Simcoe, the opposition preacher, the partners of the local cloth factory, and the Independent minister, all of whom he encountered at the Clavering Athenaeum. Established by the Liberal party as a beacon of modern spirit, this new institution stood in proud opposition to the aristocratic old reading-room, where even the *Edinburgh Review* had struggled for entry and tradesmen were strictly forbidden. Pen propitiated the younger cloth-partner by inviting him to dine at the Park, complimented the Honorable Mrs. Simcoe with game from the estate, and requested permission to read her husband's latest sermon. When he fell slightly unwell one day, the rascal Simcoe seized the opportunity to display his tongue to Mr. Huxter, who duly sent medicines and called the following morning. Old Pendennis would have been thrilled by his pupil's antics, and Pen himself found the sport delightful, inspiring a wicked good-humor.

Yet, as Pen walked out of Clavering at night, fresh from presiding over an Athenaeum meeting or entertaining Mrs. Simcoe—who, along with her husband, was awestruck by the young Londoner's reputation—he crossed the familiar bridge over the rushing Brawl. Hearing the well-known roar of the waters beneath and glimpsing his cottage of Fairoaks silhouetted against the starlit sky, different thoughts stirred within him, awakening pangs of grief and shame. A faint light still burned in the window of the room he remembered so well, where the Saint who loved him had spent countless hours in care, yearning, and prayer. He turned his gaze away from the wan, reproachful glow, feeling as though his mother's spirit watched and warned from beyond. The night air was keen, the stars bright, and the waters flowed ceaselessly. The old trees whispered, waving their dark branches over the cottage roof. Beyond lay the terrace where, as a boy, he had wandered in summer evenings, ardent and trustful, unspotted and ignorant of passion or doubt, sheltered in the pure bosom of love. Suddenly, the town clock tolled midnight with a clang, shattering his reverie and sending him onward toward his lodging through the lodge and the dark lime avenues.

By the next afternoon, the cottage smiled in the sunset. The bedroom windows were open, where a light had burned the night before. Pen's tenant, Captain Stokes of the Bombay Artillery—whose mother, old Mrs. Stokes, resided nearby—received his landlord with great cordiality. Stokes showed Pen over the grounds and a new pond he had constructed in the back garden using materials from the stables. They spoke confidentially of repairs to the roof and chimneys, and Stokes begged Pen to name a date for visiting them both. Pen, having been in the country for a fortnight, excused his delay by frankly admitting he lacked the heart to call sooner. "I understand you, sir," Stokes said. Mrs. Stokes, who had slipped away at the ring of the bell—a gesture that seemed oddly formal to Pen—descended in her best gown, surrounded by her children. The youngsters clambered over Stokes; the boy jumped into an armchair that had belonged to Pen's father. Arthur recalled days when he would have thought sitting in that chair as absurd as mounting the King's throne. He asked if Miss Stokes, who was the image of her mother, could play the piano. She did, playing notes on an instrument enfeebled by age. Yet Pen did not listen to the music; he listened to Laura singing as in their youth, and saw his mother bending, beating time over the girl's shoulder.

The dinner at Fairoaks, hosted in Pen's honor by his tenant, proved initially stupid and melancholy for him. However, the mood shifted when the waiter from Clavering—a street boy Pen remembered, now a barber there—dropped a plate over Pen's shoulder. Mr. Hobnell, who also employed the lad, chuckled, "I suppose, Hodson, your hands are slippery with bear's-grease. He's always dropping the crockery about, that Hodson is—haw, haw!" Hodson blushed, looking disconcerted, and Pen burst into laughter. Good humor and hilarity became the order of the evening. For the second course, a hare and partridges were served, and when Pen later told the Vicar of Tinckleton, "I think, Mr. Stooks, you should have asked Hodson to cut the hare," the joke was instantly caught. Minutes later, Captains Stokes and Glanders joined in, followed by Mr. Hobnell, who arrived late but with an immense guffaw.

While Pen engaged in these country schemes, the lady of his choice, if not his deepest affection, came up to London from Tunbridge Wells on shopping expeditions. Accompanied by old Mrs. Bonner, her mother's maid—who had lived and quarreled with Blanche since infancy but now, preparing to quit Lady Clavering's service for the hymeneal state, wished to bestow tokens of kindness before departing to become the wife of Lightfoot and landlady of the Clavering Arms—the pair ventured into the city.

The honest woman utilized Miss Amory's taste to make her purchases, requesting that Blanche choose something she liked, serving as a reminder of a nurse who had attended her through wakeful nights, teething, and childhood fevers, loving her almost as a child of her own. An immense Bible was purchased for Blanche, prompting the suggestion that Mrs. Bonner buy a large Johnson's Dictionary for her mother, a gift both women could surely profit from.
