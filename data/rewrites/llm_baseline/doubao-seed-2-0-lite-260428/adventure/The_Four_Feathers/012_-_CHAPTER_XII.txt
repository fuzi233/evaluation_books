The full May moon hung low over Wadi Halfa, bleaching all but the brightest planets from the sky and gilding the sluggish Nile until it spread like a sheet of burnished silver below the riverbank. Three officers had dragged wicker lounge chairs out to a grassy knoll above the bank, smoking in thick, companionable silence while bullfrogs boomed from a reed-choked mid-river island. The desert across the water stretched endless, its white sand frosted under the moon, scattered pebbles glinting like shards of broken glass. Behind them, the mess verandah’s shadow pooled thick and jet-black, a wall of darkness cut off from the pearly glow of the night.

Colonel Dawson, commanding a Soudanese battalion, struck a match to relight his cigar, the flame guttering across his lined, troubled face.
“I can’t shake the feeling something’s gone very wrong for him,” he admitted, tossing the burnt match stub into the sand below the knoll.

Lieutenant Calder of the Engineers, whose face still held the unlined sharpness of youth even if the brutal Soudan sun had silvered streaks through his hair, nodded grimly. He was Durrance’s closest friend on the garrison. “He left Halfa eight weeks ago to the day.”

The third man, lanky Major Walters from the Army Service Corps, huffed a laugh and puffed at his pipe, gesturing out at the empty desert east of the river. “C’mon, it’s far too early to write Durrance off. You know the bloke—stick him in the middle of nowhere with two bored tribal sheikhs and a campfire, and he’ll spin them adventure stories until they’re begging him not to leave. He hates nothing more than desk work, letters, endless admin—he’s probably intentionally dragging his feet because he doesn’t want to come back to the office grind.”

“Three weeks overdue,” Dawson said, tugging sharply at his moustache. “He’s methodical, for all his wanderlust. I’m not writing him off, but I’m worried.”

Calder leaned forward, knocking ash out of his pipe bowl slow. “There’s something I’ve never told either of you. I didn’t connect the dots until this week, but… the night before he rode out? He was acting strange. Very strange.”

Dawson hitched his chair closer.

“His bags were all packed hours before dinner ended,” Calder went on. “He left the mess early, but he didn’t head to his quarters. He walked a full mile north along the river to Tewfikieh, that scruffy little frontier border town full of Greek storekeepers, native food stalls, crowds of bare-footed Dinka herders and Delta fellahs all yelling in half a dozen different dialects. I popped up to his quarters at 11pm to ask about a last-minute supply detail for his trip, and he still wasn’t back. I waited. He stumbled in fifteen minutes later, fizzing with some kind of barely contained excitement—one minute he was furious, the next he was laughing to himself like he couldn’t help it. Bundled me right out the door, told his servant to turn in, said he was expecting a secret visitor and would let the man in himself.”

“Did the visitor show?” Dawson asked, his voice sharp.

“Nope. His servant found him at 4am, still sitting on his verandah steps, waiting. No one had turned up. Durrance never said a word about it to me, either. He climbed onto his camel at sunrise, sat there staring down at me for a full ten seconds like he was going to spill everything… then he whacked the camel’s flank with his stick, and rode out into the desert head down, no goodbye. I’ve been wondering ever since if that non-existent visitor was a trap, a lure to get him riding out into the badlands distracted.”

Silence settled over the knoll. Walters shook his head stubbornly, said he still refused to believe Durrance was dead. Dawson announced he’d send a full armed search party out at first light.

By the next morning, the three were sitting at breakfast on the verandah, Dawson already sketching out the search patrol roster on a scrap of paper, when Walters leaned across the table at Calder.
“Wait—did you ever ask around Tewfikieh to find who Durrance was talking to that night?”

“I did,” Calder said, and then his head snapped up, his eyes going wide like he’d just seen a ghost. His hands curled tight around the edge of the table, half ready to leap to his feet, before he froze, confused. The rest of the mess officers were too busy passing plates of toast and jam to notice. He leaned back slow, staring over Walters’ shoulder. “I only found one person he spoke to. A Greek wandering musician, playing a zither for spare change outside a dirt-floor café. Durrance tossed him a whole sovereign, stopped to chat for five minutes. I thought nothing of it… until this exact second.” He paused, grinning faint. “Turns out the man I was just talking about is standing right in the doorway behind you.”

Every head snapped around. Durrance was there, dust caked thick on his uniform, his face burnt almost brick-red by the desert sun, leaning in the doorframe listening intently.

“Durrance!” Dawson yelped, leaping up to yank a chair out for him. “We expected you three weeks ago!”

“Couldn’t be helped,” Durrance said, sinking into the chair. His voice was level, but there was a tight, careful edge to it no one but Calder caught.

“Was that secret visitor you were waiting for that Greek musician?” Calder asked.

Durrance laughed, easy. “That’s exactly who it was. Curious about how he’d ended up stranded playing for coppers in a desert border café, that’s all. Nothing exciting.”

“Come on, tuck in,” Dawson said, reaching for the cocoa pot. “What do you want? Tea? Coffee?”

But Calder watched, his stomach turning cold, as Durrance turned his head vague, and called over the mess waiter instead. “Moussa, bring me something cold. Brandy and soda, if you would.” The waiter had to physically guide the glass right into his outstretched palm, because Durrance couldn’t see it resting on the table.

He rambled for an hour, talking about the Ababdeh Arabs out in the eastern mountains, the plentiful ibex, the perfect weather, the quiet tribes that swore loyalty to neither Egypt nor the Mahdi. Calder didn’t believe a single word of it. There were new deep furrows around Durrance’s mouth that hadn’t been there eight weeks before, streaks of grey in his sun-bleached hair that weren’t just dust. When the other officers left for their patrol duties, Calder lingered. Durrance suddenly remembered he had eight weeks of unopened mail waiting in his office, stood up sharp, and excused himself.

Calder waited five minutes, then grabbed his helmet and tramped across the hot sand to Durrance’s small office shack. He eased the door open, and saw his friend hunched over his desk, his face buried in his arms, the stack of unopened letters sitting untouched at his elbow.

Calder stepped inside, slamming the door loud to announce his presence. Durrance’s head snapped up.
“I’ve got that barracks alteration authorization you need to sign,” Calder said, walking over, and held out his lit pipe. He didn’t hand it to him. He set it right in Durrance’s open palm. The hot burning bowl seared his skin. Durrance yelped, yanking his hand back, and the pipe clattered to the floor and shattered.

Neither man spoke for a long beat. Calder put a gentle hand on his shoulder.
“How did it happen?” he whispered.

Durrance buried his face in his hands, his whole body starting to shake. The tight, cheerful mask he’d been holding up for hours shattered completely. “How did you figure it out?”
“You stood in the mess doorway staring right at my face, and you didn’t recognize me,” Calder said soft. “You wouldn’t reach for the food on the table, you couldn’t find your own glass. I knew before you even sat down.”

Durrance laughed, harsh and ragged. “A sandstorm wind blew my helmet off. I was in my shirtsleeves, no collar, running after it across the sand. That stupid helmet kept skipping away from me every time I stooped to grab it. I must have run half a mile, straight into the full glare of the midday sun. I collapsed face down in the dirt right when my fingers closed on the helmet. When I woke up, I couldn’t see a thing. I made my men promise not to tell anyone. I wanted to get all the way back here before anyone found out. I thought I could hide it for at least a day.”

Calder immediately fetched the clever Syrian garrison doctor, who shook his head grimly, and told Durrance he needed to sail for Cairo immediately to see an eye specialist—there was no telling if the sun damage to his eyes was permanent. When the doctor left, Calder sorted through the mail, and found two letters in a looping feminine hand, postmarked from a small village in Donegal, Ireland. Durrance had told him about Ethne Eustace, the woman he loved, once over late night whiskey months before.

“Shall I read these to you?” Calder asked.

Durrance flinched, then laughed that same harsh, ragged laugh. “God, yes. You have to help me answer them. Don’t you get it? I’m blind now. I can’t read a line.”

Calder opened them. They were warm, gentle letters, full of stories of the Donegal hills, the village festivals, the local fishermen, every line brimming with quiet affection for him. When he finished, Durrance let out a long, shaky breath of relief.
“Thank God she turned me down,” he said. “Twice, in fact. Can you imagine what a burden I’d be to her if we were engaged? I knew a man back home who went blind. A great bloke, before. A year later? He was so selfish, so jealous of everyone who could walk and see, so demanding of his wife’s time, you wouldn’t have recognized him. I’m not clever. I can’t sit in a chair all day and amuse myself with thoughts. My whole life is outdoors, hiking, riding, exploring. I’d turn into that same bitter, useless creature. That girl’s free. I dodged a bullet.”

Calder’s heart sank. He’d seen the way Durrance talked about Ethne, once, like she was the only bright spot he dreamed of when he was out patrolling the desert. He wondered if she really had no idea how much he loved her, or if she’d turned him down for a stupid, silly reason she’d regret for the rest of her life.

“Let’s start dictating those replies to her,” Calder said quiet, and sat down beside his friend at the desk.
