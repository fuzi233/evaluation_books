"God will relent, and quit thee all thy debt, Who ever more approves, and more accepts Him who imploring mercy sues for life, Than who self-rigorous chooses death as due..."

More than a fortnight had passed in grueling toil, yet nothing compared to the hardships they had previously endured. Having bid a final farewell to the verdant oceans of the eastern plains, the expedition had crossed the formidable Cordillera mountains. They had cast a longing gaze upon Santa Fe, nestled amidst lush gardens on its high plateau, only to realize it was far too fortified and populous for their ambitions. Yet, their efforts had not been wasted. Their Indian guide had uncovered intelligence: a gold train was descending from Santa Fe toward the Magdalena River.

Consequently, they had established their camp beside a treacherous, muddy rut serving as a road, hidden within a dense oak forest. The setting was deceptive; the towering oaks and fern-filled undergrowth evoked memories of Europe, save for the deep gorges yawning beneath them. From their elevated position, cooled by temperate breezes, they could peer down through a perpetual mist of tropical heat to behold the vibrant, chaotic splendor of the jungle floor.

Pitched among the tree-ferns, overlooking a steep hillside where a ruined path wound dangerously close to a sheer cliff hundreds of feet deep, the group waited. Once, during the reign of the civilized Kingdom of Cundinamarca, this route had been paved with aqueducts, bridges, and orderly post-houses. Now, Spanish misrule had reduced it to a muddy ditch where stones slipped from their places. Like the gardens and llama herds of that strange empire, all arts of civilization had crumbled in the mad scramble for gold, leaving only the magnificent cathedrals rising from the ruins of the Sun Temple—a "milder" pantheon that still demanded human sacrifice, a stark contrast to the gentle nature-worship of the Incas.

Nature had swiftly reclaimed its domain. Amyas and his crew felt as isolated as explorers in the depths of the Orinoco or the Amazon, mere miles from a significant Spanish settlement. Attempts to locate sulfur and saltpeter had failed, forcing them to rely solely on their swords and bows—a prospect that disgusted Yeo. Yet, history offered precedent; Drake had seized Nombre de Dios and its gold train with similar limited weaponry. If they could not find explosives, they would fight.

To secure their advantage, they had felled a massive tree across the road above, creating a natural blockade. Sitting among the flowers, chewing coca to quell their hunger and thirst, they pondered a mysterious roar that had haunted their nights since leaving the Meta riverbanks. It was neither jaguar nor monkey, yet it persisted. Being in a land of wonders, they dismissed the noise as secondary to the impending capture of the gold train.

Suddenly, a sharp crack echoed from below, followed immediately by a piercing cry.

"That was a whip's crack," Yeo observed, "and a woman's wail. They are close!"

"A woman?" Amyas asked. "Do they drive women in their gangs?"

"Why not, the brutes? Look! Did you see their basnets glitter?"

"Men!" Amyas commanded softly. "Trust no one to shoot until I give the order. Then, one arrow, then swords. Pass the word."

From below emerged a ragged procession. First came roughly twenty soldiers, half on foot and half seated in chairs carried on the backs of exhausted Indians. Those marching had surrendered their heavy armor and arquebuses to attendant slaves, who were goaded forward by the pikes of the soldiers behind them.

"The men are mad to leave their ordnance unguarded," someone muttered.

"An Indian will pray to an arquebus not to fire upon him," Yeo replied cynically. "They consider the artillery perfectly safe."

"Look at the proud villains," another whispered, "turning human beings into dumb beasts!"

Amyas counted methodically: "Ten shot, ten pikes. Will, deal with those above."

Trailing the soldiers was an inferior officer, also riding in a chair. As he ascended the hill, facing the suffering gang behind him, he periodically removed his cigar to offer pious ejaculations to various deities—divine, human, anatomical, wooden, and textile. These fervent, often blasphemous invocations earned sixteenth-century Spaniards the reputation of being both the most fetish-ridden idolaters and the worst swearers in Europe.

"The blasphemous dog!" Yeo fumbled with his bowstring, ready to loose an arrow. Amyas restrained him just as a second, far more tragic procession appeared.

It was a heartbreaking and horrific sight, yet tragically common in these remote regions where royal decrees protecting indigenous peoples were ignored. Even the wise Inquisitor Pedro de la Gasca's reforms in Peru had proven futile and delayed against such entrenched cruelty. On the frontiers, where black slaves were imported to perform labor fatal to Indians, and tribes accused of cannibalism were hunted down for "salvation," such scenes remained routine. Indeed, as Humboldt noted, conditions showed little improvement even centuries later in Jesuit missions, where military tyranny compounded monastic rule, and the Gospel was preached with fire and sword.

A line of Indians, Negroes, and Zambos climbed the slope, naked, emaciated, and scarred by whips and fetters. Chained by their left wrists, they labored upward, panting and sweating under the weight of baskets strapped across their foreheads. Among them were elderly men, youths, and women—including slender girls and mothers carrying children. A low murmur of indignation rose from the concealed Englishmen, echoing the righteous hearts of an era when Raleigh defended the "outraged heathens" based on shared humanity, before the brutality of the seventeenth century hardened English consciences alongside the institution of slavery.

The first forty carriers bore burdens that nearly eclipsed the misery of the humans bearing them. Each carried a square package of corded hide, recognizable instantly to Amyas.

"What's in 'em, Captain?"

"Gold!"

At the magic word, eyes strained forward greedily. The rustling of movement grew so intense that Amyas, fearing discovery, whispered urgently, "Be men, be men, or you will spoil everything!"

The final twenty Indians carried lighter loads of manioc and maize bread. Behind them came twenty more soldiers and the commanding officer, smiling smugly from his chair and twirling his mustaches, oblivious to the English arrows itching to pierce his ribs. The ambush was set; only the timing remained.

Amyas felt a visceral hesitation. Drawing a bow against men who were unsuspecting and defenseless, even while committing acts of diabolical cruelty, weighed heavily on him. Cruelty it was, indeed, as drivers wielding whips moved through the staggered line, punishing every stumble with lashes of manati-hide that cracked like pistol shots against bare skin.

Then, fate intervened.

The penultimate figure in the chain was an old, gray-haired man, followed by a slender, graceful girl of about eighteen. Amyas's heart ached for them as they approached. Just as they passed, the lead carrier rounded the bend above. A commotion ensued, and a voice shouted, "Halt, señores! There is a tree blocking the path!"

"A tree across the path?" the officer bellowed, launching into a torrent of passionate appeals to the Virgin Mary, demons, St. James of Compostela, and other saints. The trembling line of Indians, halted above and driven onward by blows below, surged chaotically over the ruined steps until the poor old man collapsed face-down.

The officer leaped from his chair and hurried up. Finding the prostrate elder, he screamed, "Sin pecado concebida! Grandfather of Beelzebub, is this a place to worship your fiends?" He thrust the point of his sword into the man's side.

The old man struggled to rise, but the weight of the basket crushed him back down. He lay motionless. The driver struck his loins with the whip, once, twice, with terrifying force, but it was useless.

"Gastado, Señor Capitán," the driver shrugged. "Worn out. He has been failing for three months!"

"What does the Intendant mean by sending me out with broken cattle like these? Forward!" the officer roared. "Clear the tree, señores, and I'll soon clear the chain. Hold it up, Pedrillo!"

Pedrillo raised the chain attached to the old man's wrist. The officer stepped back, flourishing a Toledo blade renowned for its beauty—a weapon so fine it tempted Amyas to break the Tenth Commandment merely by looking at it.

The officer was tall, handsome, and broad-shouldered. Amyas assumed he intended to demonstrate his strength by severing the chain in a single stroke.

However, he underestimated the eccentric violence of a Spanish adventurer, heir to conquerors who tested their swords on living Indians and feasted on roasted caciques.

The blade gleamed, swung once, then twice, and fell—not on the chain, but directly onto the man's fettered wrist.

There was a shriek, a flash of crimson, and the chain and its prisoner were severed.

For a heartbeat, Amyas's arrow flew true, destined for the murderer's throat. But vengeance did not come from him.
