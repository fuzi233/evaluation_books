Margaret stepped into the vast, unfinished nave of St. Laurens as the sermon had already begun. The church was a grand, half-shaped thing—its side aisles roofed, its central vault open to the sky, the pillars stark and whitewashed against the rough stone. Only one window held glass; the rest were jagged gaps in the walls, letting the summer afternoon pour through in shafts of gold. The light danced across the uneven surfaces, painting the cool shadows with rivers of fire, turning the pillars into glowing sentinels. Above, the blue sky arched close, as if heaven itself leaned down to listen.

The crowd was a sea of white caps, motionless, hushed. The preacher’s voice—gentle, earnest, melodic—held them in thrall. Margaret slipped behind a pillar in the north aisle, where the words barely reached her, but the rhythm of his speech was enough. A quiet peace settled over her, the beauty of the place and the murmur of devotion smoothing the restless waves of her heart. She let her eyes drift half-shut, lulled into soft reverie.

Then she noticed a lady shifting from her place, retreating from the sun’s glare. Quick and silent, Margaret took the vacated spot beside a pillar in the south aisle, now close enough to hear every word. Yet her attention soon snagged on a shadow—a man’s head and shoulders bobbing in odd rhythm. She glanced back through the open window and saw the sexton, Jorian Ketel, digging. A faint smile tugged at her lips.

But then—

A single tone from the preacher’s voice struck her like a physical blow. Her breath caught; her hand flew to her chest as if to steady the sudden tremor inside her. She turned sharply toward the pulpit, but all she saw was the back of his head, the smooth tonsure gleaming in the light. A sigh escaped her. That shaven crown seemed to deny the strange familiarity of his voice. Still, she leaned forward, listening, waiting for that note again—the one that had stirred something deep and half-remembered.

It did not come. But his voice, rising and falling, wove through her like a thread of old music, tugging at forgotten joys. She kept her eyes down, unwilling to break the spell.

Then—silence.

The preacher had stopped mid-sentence. Margaret’s heart lurched. Had he finished? No—the crowd sat still, expectant. Then she noticed the turning of faces, the ripple of murmurs. She looked behind her, thinking some disturbance had drawn their attention. But there was nothing.

Only when she turned back did she see it.

The friar’s face was deathly pale, his eyes—wide, unnatural—fixed on her. Not past her, not near her, but *on* her. As if she were a ghost. A shudder ran through the congregation. Women gasped; men whispered. Margaret shrank behind the pillar, her pulse hammering. One fleeting glance had been enough. In that stricken face, she had seen something that froze her blood.

The sermon stumbled on, the preacher’s voice now fevered, exalted, as if he fought to outrun his own shock. When it ended, the crowd spilled out in murmuring clusters. Margaret moved with them, but at the door, she turned aside, slipping into the churchyard.

She made her way to Peter’s grave—her father’s resting place. Though poor, she had given him a headstone. Now she knelt on the slab, pressed her lips to the cold stone, then drew her apron over her head like a veil.

*"Father,"* she whispered, *"you always said I was strong. But these waters run deeper than I knew. I’ll follow that friar to the ends of the earth if I must—but at arm’s length. And he will tell me why he looked at me as if I were the dead returned."*

Her fingers curled against the stone. *"Speak for me, father. For I am wading in deep waters now."*

From where she sat, she could see the church door. And there she waited, face hidden, watching—a woman turned hunter, patient as the grave.
