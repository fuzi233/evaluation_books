Word of Rosanna Spearman’s disappearance had rippled fast through the house’s outdoor staff. They’d spent the last hour poking through the grounds for clues, and had just collared a scrawny, quick-witted urchin nicknamed Duffy—hired on odd days to weed the garden—who swore he’d seen the missing girl barely half an hour prior. Duffy was dead certain she hadn’t been walking through the fir plantation: she’d been sprinting, flat out, heading straight for the shoreline.
“Does this boy know every inch of this coast?” Sergeant Cuff asked me at once.
“Born and bred on the bay,” I told him. “Knows every tide pool and hidden sandbank better than he knows his own prayers.”
“Duffy,” the Sergeant called, “fancy earning a whole shilling? Then come with me. Keep the pony-chaise hitched and waiting, Mr. Betteredge, till I get back.”
He was off for the Shivering Sand at a lope my seventy-year-old legs could never hope to match. Duffy, true to the feral high spirits of local lads, whooped loud enough to scare every rook out of the fir trees and trotted hot on the detective’s heels.
I can’t rightly parse the restlessness that seized me while they were gone. I fumbled through a dozen meaningless, half-finished chores in and out of the house, none of which I can now recall, my mind buzzing like a hive of angry bees. I had no sense of how much time had passed before Duffy came skidding back down the drive, crumpling a scrap torn out of Cuff’s pocket notebook in his fist. The pencil scribble read: Send me one of Rosanna Spearman’s boots, and hurry.
I dispatched the first maid I spotted to fetch it from Rosanna’s room, then sent Duffy back ahead to say I’d follow with the boot myself. It wasn’t the fastest way to obey Cuff’s order, I’ll own—but I was still stubbornly determined to look after that girl if I could, to see for myself what new dark mystery the detective had stumbled on before I handed over the last of her things. That protective streak, tangled up with the same detective fever that had gripped the whole house for days, had me setting off for the shore at the fastest hobbling run a man of my years could manage.
By the time I hit the dunes, the sky had gone black as pitch. Horizontal rain lashed my face in great stinging sheets, and I could hear the roar of the surf booming over the sandbank at the bay’s mouth long before I caught sight of the water. A little further on, I found Duffy huddled for shelter behind the lee of a sandhill, then spotted Sergeant Cuff: the only dark, solitary figure standing on the empty expanse of storm-lashed beach.
He waved me sharply to the north. “Stay on that hard sand!” he shouted. “Come down to me slow.”
I reached him gasping, chest heaving so hard I could not force a single one of my hundred unspoken questions past my lips. The look on his face curdled my blood: there was raw, unmasked horror in his eyes I’d never seen on that usually stony, composed face before. He snatched the boot out of my hand, pressed it into a half rain-blotted footprint sunk deep in the sand, pointing due south toward the jagged rocky ledge they called the South Spit. It fit to a hair, not a single line of the tread out of place.
Cuff did not speak. He just pointed down at the boot in the print, then turned to follow the track all the way to where the sand met the rock. The South Spit was already half awash with the rising tide, and the glistening, churning surface of the Shivering Sand quicksands heaved higher by the second. Cuff paced back and forth in grim, dogged patience, pressing the boot to every subsequent footprint, but the trail never turned back. There was no sign at all of steps walking away from the Spit.
At last he gave up. He stared first at me, then out at the dark water swallowing the quicksand deeper and deeper, and a cold, dumb, trembling numbness crawled all over my skin. I dropped to my knees in the wet sand.
“She came back to whatever hiding place she’d found,” Cuff muttered to himself. “She must have slipped on the rocks. A terrible accident.”
But I knew better. I replayed the last hour I’d seen Rosanna, blank and slow-faced, sweeping the corridor, her eyes hollow. I remembered her telling me months before that the Shivering Sand felt like it was pulling at her, that she was sure her grave was waiting for her there. My own daughter was just her age—if life had dealt her the same hard, lonely hand Rosanna had drawn, she might have ended up right here too. The thought choked me.
Cuff lifted me gently to my feet, turned me away from the sight of the quicksand, just as all the outdoor servants and the local fisherman Yolland came pelting down the beach toward us, faces white with alarm. In a handful of plain words, Cuff showed them the footprints, laid out what he knew had happened, then turned to Yolland.
“Tell me,” he said, nodding at the raging sea. “Could any boat have reached those rocks to pull her off, in this weather?”
Yolland jerked his chin at the giant piling rollers, sending plumes of foam twenty feet high against the headlands on either side of the bay. “No boat built by mortal man could cut through that surf. She’d be swamped before she got within a hundred yards of the Spit.”
Cuff glanced back at the footprints, which the rain was now fast blurring out of existence. “That’s proof she couldn’t have left by land. That’s proof she couldn’t have escaped by sea.” He paused a minute, working out the timelines. “Rosanna was seen running for this shore an hour ago. How high was the water on the outer side of the Spit—the side not covered by quicksand—at that time?”
“On today’s tide?” Yolland shook his head. “An hour back, there wasn’t enough water there to drown a kitten. Barely ankle deep.”
“On the Shivering Sand side?”
“Less,” the fisherman said. “The quicksands were just barely awash, no deeper than that.”
“Then the accident happened in the quicksand,” Cuff said, turning to me.
My tongue finally came loose. “It was no accident!” I burst out. “She came here because she was sick of her life. She meant to end it here.”
Cuff recoiled, shocked. “How can you be so certain?”
The rest of the men crowded around, but Cuff herded them back gently, said I was an old man, the shock had unhinged me, give me a minute to catch my breath. He turned back to Yolland. “Is there any chance of dragging her back, once the tide goes out?”
“None,” Yolland said, quiet. “What the Shivering Sand takes, the Shivering Sand keeps forever.” He stepped closer to me then, his voice soft. “Mr. Betteredge, I’ve fished these waters my whole life. Four feet out from the Spit, there’s a solid shelf of rock half a fathom under the sand. If she’d slipped by accident, she would have landed on that shelf, water barely up to her waist. She could have stood up, waded back. She had to wade out, or jump out, into the deep quicksands past that shelf, to vanish like this. No accident, sir. She went there on purpose.”
No one argued with him. The man knew these sands better than anyone alive, and we all fell silent. We’d barely turned to trudge back up the beach slope when the under-groom came running down the sandhills, his face somber, holding out a crumpled little note to me.
“Penelope sent me, Mr. Betteredge,” he said. “She found this locked in Rosanna’s desk drawer.”
It was her last note, scrawled in her thin, scratchy handwriting, addressed only to me.
You have forgiven me a hundred times for my mistakes, sir. When you next pass the Shivering Sand, forgive me one last time. I have found the grave I always knew was waiting for me here. I lived, and I died, grateful for every bit of kindness you ever showed me.
That was all. It was little enough, but it broke me. Tears come easy when you’re young, just starting out in the world. Tears come just as easy when you’re old, and nearing the end of yours. I burst out crying.
Sergeant Cuff took a step toward me, meaning to offer comfort, I don’t doubt. I flinched back from him.
“Don’t touch me,” I said. “Your hounding of that poor girl drove her to this.”
“You’re wrong, Mr. Betteredge,” he said, quiet and steady, unoffended. “We can talk it all through once we are back under cover.”
I leaned on the groom’s arm to steady myself, and we all trundled back through the lashing rain, up the beach, toward the house. I did not know then that far worse trouble, far darker secrets, were waiting for us the second we stepped back over the threshold.
