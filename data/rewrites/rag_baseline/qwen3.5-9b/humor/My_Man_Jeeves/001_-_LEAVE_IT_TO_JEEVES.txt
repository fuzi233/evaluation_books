"Leave it to Jeeves," I declared, the phrase hanging in the air with the weight of a tactical directive.

Jeeves—my man, you understand—is a specimen of such rare competence that his existence borders on the miraculous. In terms of broad utility, he resembles those enigmatic figures stationed at the marble balustrades of Pennsylvania Station's inquiry desk. You approach them, bewildered, asking, "When is the next departure for Melonsquashville, Tennessee?" and they respond instantly, without hesitation, "Two-forty-three, Track Ten, connection required in San Francisco." And invariably, they are correct. Jeeves projects this same aura of omniscient certainty.

Consider the incident involving Monty Byng in Bond Street. I encountered him wearing a grey check suit that epitomized sartorial perfection. I resolved that my own happiness was contingent upon acquiring identical attire. Within sixty minutes, I had commissioned the tailors.

"Jeeves," I instructed later that evening, "I am commissioning a check suit similar to Mr. Byng's."

"Injudicious, sir," he replied, his tone leaving no room for debate. "It will not become you."

"What absolute nonsense!" I protested. "It is the finest garment I have encountered in years."

"Unsuited to your frame, sir."

The outcome was predictable. The suit arrived, I donned it, and gazing into the mirror, I nearly suffered a fainting spell. Jeeves was undeniably correct. I resembled a caricature of a music-hall comedian paired with a disreputable bookmaker. Yet, Monty had looked impeccable in the exact same fabric. Such contradictions are merely the inexplicable mysteries of life.

However, Jeeves's infallibility extends far beyond fashion. His knowledge base is exhaustive. Recall the wager regarding the horse "Wonderchild" on the Lincolnshire racecourse. I possessed insider information of the highest caliber—a genuine hot tip.

"Jeeves," I said, motivated by affection and a desire to assist, "if you seek profit, back Wonderchild for the Lincolnshire."

He shook his head decisively.

"I decline, sir."

"But it is verified intelligence. I intend to ride him to victory."

"I strongly advise against it, sir. The animal is not positioned to win. The stable's objective is clearly second place."

I dismissed this as absurdity. How could Jeeves possibly possess such specific data? Nevertheless, fate intervened. Wonderchild led the field until he was mere inches from the wire, only to be overtaken by Banana Fritter. I returned home immediately and summoned Jeeves.

"From this moment forward," I stated, "no action shall be taken without your counsel. Consider yourself the intellectual cornerstone of this household."

"Very good, sir. I shall endeavor to provide total satisfaction."

And he has, by heavens! My own cognitive faculties are somewhat limited; the old bean appears designed more for decoration than function. Yet, after five minutes of consultation with Jeeves, I am prepared to offer expert advice on any matter. Consequently, when Bruce Corcoran arrived bearing his burdens, my initial response was to summon the bell and present the dilemma to the youth with the prominent forehead.

"Leave it to Jeeves," I commanded.

I first encountered Corky during my tenure in New York. He was an associate of my cousin Gussie, who frequented the circles of Washington Square. My relocation to America stemmed from my Aunt Agatha's mission to prevent Gussie from marrying a vaudeville performer. Having become thoroughly entangled in the affair, I deemed it prudent to establish a temporary exile in the States rather than returning to England for further domestic interrogation. I dispatched Jeeves to secure suitable accommodation, and thus began my expatriate life. I must concede that New York is an exceptional locale for exile. The populace was remarkably hospitable, the cultural scene vibrant, and given my financial status, everything proceeded smoothly. I was introduced to various individuals, including those rolling in dollars near Central Park and the artistic intelligentsia residing in modest quarters around Washington Square. Brilliant minds, they were.

Corky belonged to this latter group. He identified as a portrait painter, though he had produced no portraits. He remained on the sidelines, wrapped in a blanket, awaiting an opportunity to enter the profession. The paradox of portrait painting is that commissions do not arrive until one has established a portfolio, creating a Catch-22. Corky subsisted by illustrating cartoons for periodicals and designing furniture for advertisements. However, his primary sustenance derived from exploiting a wealthy uncle, Alexander Worple, a magnate in the jute industry. While I remain unclear on the specifics of jute production, it evidently captivated the public imagination, allowing Mr. Worple to accumulate a fortune of obscene proportions.

Many assume inheriting from a wealthy relative is effortless; Corky disagreed. His uncle was a robust individual seemingly destined for immortality, aged fifty-one and appearing ready to reach parity. Corky harbored no resentment toward his longevity; his grievance lay in the manner in which Mr. Worple treated him. The elder Worple opposed his nephew's pursuit of art, viewing it as a waste of potential. He relentlessly urged Corky to abandon creativity and enter the jute trade, starting from the bottom. Jute had become an obsession for him, imbued with quasi-religious significance. Corky, possessing an intuitive understanding of the industry's grueling nature, recognized it as a task too terrible to describe. Despite his doubts, Corky maintained faith in his artistic destiny, planning a future triumph. For now, he employed immense tact to extract a meager quarterly allowance from his uncle.

This arrangement relied entirely on Mr. Worple's eccentricities. Unlike typical industrialists who retreat into comatose states after closing their offices, Worple spent his leisure time as an ornithologist. He had authored *American Birds* and was currently composing *More American Birds*, intending to continue until avian species were exhausted. Corky visited him approximately quarterly, enduring hours of monologue on American birds. Paradoxically, yielding to this obsession secured his allowance temporarily. Yet, the psychological toll was severe. Beyond the suspense, the subject matter itself bored him rigidly, save for the culinary application of poultry.

Furthermore, Mr. Worple possessed a volatile temperament and viewed Corky as inherently inept, interpreting any independent initiative as definitive proof of stupidity. I suspect Jeeves shares this sentiment regarding me.

One afternoon, Corky entered my apartment, ushering a young woman ahead of him. "Bertie, I require your assistance. This is my fiancée, Miss Singer."

My immediate reaction bypassed the romantic announcement and focused on the underlying crisis. "Corky, how fares your uncle?"

The young man emitted a hollow laugh, his expression mirroring someone who has successfully committed a crime but lacks a plan for disposal of the evidence.

"We are terrified, Mr. Wooster," the girl interjected. "We hoped you might propose a method to break the news to him."

Muriel Singer possessed a quiet, compelling demeanor, gazing upward with large, trusting eyes that suggested she regarded the observer as a savior. Her posture conveyed a sense of vulnerability, evoking a protective impulse akin to comforting a frightened child. Her presence inspired a surge of chivalric resolve; I felt compelled to defend her honor by any means necessary. She radiated an innocence that demanded intervention, transforming me into a knight-errant determined to succeed.

"I fail to comprehend why your uncle would not be delighted," I assured Corky. "He will view Miss Singer as the ideal spouse."

Corky remained despondent.

"You misunderstand him. Even if he admired Muriel, he would deny it. He is obstinate. It would be a matter of principle for him to object. He would perceive my marriage as an unauthorized step and react with fury. This is his consistent pattern."

I engaged my intellect to formulate a strategy.

"Arrange a scenario wherein Mr. Worple encounters Miss Singer unaware of your prior relationship. Subsequently, you intervene..."

"That is impossible," Corky countered.

I acknowledged the validity of his concern.

"There is only one viable option," I stated.

"And what is that?"

"Leave it to Jeeves."

I rang the bell.

"Sir?" Jeeves appeared, materializing as if emerging from thin air. One of his peculiar attributes is his ability to traverse spaces invisibly, assembling himself precisely where needed. A cousin of mine, a Theosophist, claimed to have attempted similar feats but failed, allegedly due to childhood dietary indiscretions involving animals killed in anger.

Upon seeing Jeeves register his attentive presence, a profound sense of relief washed over me. Like a lost child spotting a guardian, I felt anchored. Jeeves is a tall man with a dark, shrewd visage, his eyes glowing with pure intelligence.

"Jeeves, we require your counsel."

"Very good, sir."

I distilled Corky's predicament into concise terms. "Essentially, you seek a method for Mr. Worple to meet Miss Singer without realizing Mr. Corcoran already knows her. Understood?"

"Perfectly, sir."

"Devise a solution."

"I have already conceived one, sir."

"You have?"

"The proposed scheme guarantees success but entails a significant financial expenditure."

"He implies," I translated for Corky, "that he possesses a brilliant idea, albeit one requiring capital."
