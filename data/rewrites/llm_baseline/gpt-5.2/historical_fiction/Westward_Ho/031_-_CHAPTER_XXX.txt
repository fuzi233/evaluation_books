“Oh, where be these gay Spaniards,
Which make so great a boast O?
Oh, they shall eat the gray-goose feather,
And we shall eat the roast O!”

If some curious soul—one of those who in later centuries will come down to Plymouth for an hour’s idle looking, and then whisk home again before the meat is cold—had been turned about by a trick of time, and found the nineteenth century wiped away as clean as crumbs from a board, he would have stood blinking in the bright July of 1588, and scarcely known the place.

Mount Edgecombe still rose green and courteous above the Sound; the same slopes took the same sunlight as if no years could touch them. But all the great iron-roofed docks were not; Devonport was a dream not yet dreamt; the breakwater that will one day lie like a giant’s arm across the water had not been laid there. Drake’s Island showed only a small raw battery, newly bitten out of the turf; Mount Wise was a solitary gentleman’s house among fields where the wind could still be heard moving through grass instead of through rigging and chains. The citadel was a toy fort, a child’s rattle beside the thunder that later engines will learn to make.

And for shipping—where a later age will choke the Hamoaze with floating towns—there were only a few lime-hoys creeping, and, packed thick in the Catwater, the lean-bellied wooden creatures that must to-morrow begin the greatest sea-fight the world has seen. Paltry enough they would look to eyes schooled by iron and steam: hulls no larger than a modest frigate of later days; metal that could not be flung a quarter so far; ropes and timber that asked for men’s muscles and men’s courage, not the turn of a valve.

Yet if the beholder were no mere gape-and-go, but one who knew by his own hands what “progress” costs—slow learning, sour disappointment, the patient tasting and re-tasting of failure until it becomes knowledge—he would not smile with contempt. He would feel, rather, that filial tenderness which the wise bear toward rough beginnings. For there was in those small ships the germ of a mightier sea-power; and to fashion even these, with the tools and charts and half-lit science of the time, demanded minds as inventive and spirits as stubborn as any that will boast afterward.

But if he were a student not of machines but of men—of voices, tempers, loyalties—he would have found no nobler field than the little terrace bowling-green behind the Pelican Inn on the afternoon of the nineteenth of July.

The Armada had been scattered by storm. Lord Howard had run out as far as the Spanish coast to seek it; then the wind shifted south, and he returned to Plymouth, uncertain whether the Dons would come on at all or slip by. The captains waited as lions might wait in their lair for the herd’s passing—yet they did not wait in silence. The Sound below gave back its living music: the clack and sing of blocks, the slap of small waves on timber, the low thunder of surf beyond, and, over all, the thin, constant piping of wind through cordage. No harsh din of industry dulled it—only the true sea-sounds that keep a man’s thoughts braced and wake.

Slip on, for a moment, the drawer’s apron, and come in through the rose-clad door from the tavern with a tray of long-necked Dutch glasses and a silver tankard of wine. The inn’s breath meets you at once—stale smoke, ale-sweetness, sweat of tarry coats drying in sun, and the sharp clean bite of salt blown in at every opening. On the terrace, men lounged over the low wall that looked down on the ships, or gathered in rings, talking earnestly, then breaking into sudden laughter as if laughter itself were a weapon.

There, in the center of one such ring, stood Walter Raleigh—soft long eyes, pointed chin, the look of a man who can taste words as others taste wine. A fair young lord in a flame-colored doublet—Lord Sheffield—had his arm about Raleigh’s neck with boyish warmth. Opposite them, stately as if a mast had learned to walk, stood Sir Richard Grenville; beside him, with a grave sailor’s authority that did not need finery to prove itself, Lord Charles Howard of Effingham, Lord High Admiral of England. Near him was his son-in-law, Sir Robert Southwell, captain of the Elizabeth Jonas.

And a little apart—short, sturdy, plainly dressed—stood the man to whom all turned when he spoke: Francis Drake. He held his cap in his hands, and the bullet head of crisp brown hair and wrinkled forehead showed plain. The cheekbones were high; the square face, broad-templed; thick lips set firm as rock. There was a coarse plebeian stamp; yet the stance—legs planted a little apart, hands clasped behind his back—said boundless determination and a self-possession that could outstare wind and cannon both. When at last he gave a few blunt words, the ring fell quiet to hear; even the gulls’ cries seemed to thin out above them.

Then, like a gust that brings laughter with it, a burly elder in greasy sea-stained garments came waddling up as if he had been born in a gale and never once reached calm water. The upper half of his sharp, dogged face looked like brick-red leather; the lower, like badger’s fur. A huge gold chain lay absurdly splendid against his tarry coat. He clapped Drake on the back and shouted in a broad Devon twang, “Be you a coming to drink your wine, Francis Drake, or be you not?—saving your presence, my lord;” and Lord Howard only laughed and bade Drake go and drink, for John Hawkins, Port Admiral, patriarch of Plymouth seamen, was in a temper of overflowing good-humor such as even his rival Drake could not resist. The prospect of Armageddon on the morrow had shaken him out of crabbed reserve, and made him loud and merry as a man who has already tasted victory.

They pushed through the crowd; and there were faces enough to have made a later historian forget his own name with longing. Martin Frobisher and John Davis sat on a bench, smoking tobacco from long silver pipes, the smoke hanging blue and sweet in the warm air. Near them were Fenton and Withrington—men who had tried to follow Drake round the world and failed, through no cowardice. George Fenner, Leicester’s pet, pledged them better luck next time. That short prim man in the huge yellow ruff, with sharp chin, minute imperial, and self-satisfied smile, was Richard Hawkins, Admiral John’s son, hereafter famous and hapless. He talked with his uncle William—of whom one day a monument will speak fair words in Deptford Church: “a worshipper of the true religion, an especial benefactor of poor sailors, a most just arbiter in most difficult causes, and of a singular faith, piety, and prudence.”

There, too, was John Drake, Sir Francis’ brother; there George, his nephew, not overwise, who had been round the world with Amyas. And there was Amyas himself, speaking low in a corner to Captain Barker of Bristol—answering him in fierce curt sentences, as a man answers when he counts injuries like coins and means to spend them. Barker, brother to hapless Andrew Barker who perished in Honduras twelve years ago because mutiny made him weak, now captained the Victory, one of the queen’s best ships; he had accounts with the Spaniards to settle. Amyas had as many, and more personal. While others jested and drank, those two growled together under the vine’s shade as dogs do before a fight, saving their teeth.

Against the sunny south wall of the tavern sat an aged man on a bench, as much an object of wonder as any ship in harbor. His long white beard flowed almost to his waist; his hands lay on his knees; his palsied head moved slowly from side to side to catch scraps of talk. A little maid of six, his great-grandchild, had laid her curly head on his knees; and his granddaughter, a buxom black-eyed woman of thirty, stood by tending him—half nurse, half showman—fending off well-meaning hands with the same plea again and again: “Bless you, sir, please now, don’t give him no liquor, poor old soul, the doctor says.”

It was old Martin Cockrem, father of the ancient host—aged beyond the common years of man—who could remember the bells of Plymouth ringing for Henry the Eighth’s coronation, and who had been, perhaps, the first Englishman to set foot in the New World. He sat there like a primeval stone tor among rich crops: the living memory of a vanished tongue of time. Around him the captains drifted and stared, as men look at a relic and yet cannot quite read the inscription.

Old Martin seemed uneasy. His rheumy eyes peered through the groups; his head tilted, listening, as if his life depended on a particular sound.

“There ’a be again! Why don’t ’a come, then?”

“Quiet, gramfer,” said the granddaughter, smoothing his shoulder, “and don’t trouble his worship.”

“Here an hour, and never speak to poor old Martin! I say, sir—” and the old man feebly plucked at Amyas Leigh’s cloak as he passed. “I say, captain, do ’e tell young master old Martin’s looking for him.”

“Marcy, gramfer, where’s your manners?” the woman murmured, laughing and blushing with the embarrassment of affection. “Don’t be vexed, sir; he’m a’most a babe, and tejous at times, mortal.”

“Young master who?” asked Amyas, bending, smiling to the nurse to let the old man have his way.

“Master Hawkins; he’m never been a-near me all day.”

Amyas, of course, caught at young Richard Hawkins’ sleeve; but the nurse caught at his, too, laughing now outright.

“No, sir, not Mr. Richard, sir; Admiral John, sir—his father. He always calls him young master, poor old soul!” And she pointed to the grizzled beard and weather-bitten face of the Port Admiral.

Amyas went to Hawkins and gave the message.

“Mercy on me! Where be my wits? Iss, I’m a-coming,” cried the old hero in his broadest Devon. He waddled off to Martin Cockrem, fumbling in a pocket with comic helplessness. “Here, Martin, I’ve got mun, I’ve got mun, man alive; but his Lordship keept me so. Lookee here, then! Why, I do get so lusty of late, Martin, I can’t get to my pockets!”

Out came, one after another, as if the pocket were a pantry stuffed without order: a piece of tarred string; a bundle of papers; a thimble; a lump of pudding-tobacco; and at last—prized above all—a little paper of Muscovado sugar, then a delicacy fit to make a poor mouth think itself in heaven. He thrust it into old Martin’s trembling hand.

Old Martin dipped his finger, rubbed the brown sweetness on his toothless gums, smiling and nodding his thanks; and the little maid at his knee, unrebuked, took her share with serious devotion, as children take sacrament when they do not know the word for it.

“There, Admiral Leigh,” Hawkins said, looking from the old man to the child, “both ends meet—gramfers and babies! You and I shall be like to that one day, young Samson!”

“We shall have slain a good many Philistines first, I hope,” said Amyas.

“Amen! so be it; but look to mun! so fine a sailor as ever drank liquor; and now greedy after a hit of sweet trade! ’Tis piteous like. But I bring mun a hit whenever I come, and he looks for it. He’s one of my own flesh like, is old Martin. He sailed with my father, Captain Will, when they was both two little cracks aboard of a trawler; and my father went up, and here I am—he didn’t, and there he is. We’m up now, we Hawkinses. We may be down again some day.”

“Never, I trust,” said Amyas.

“’Tain’t no use trusting, young man: you go and do. I do hear too much of that there from my lad. Let they ministers preach till they’m black in the face—works is the trade!” And he nudged Amyas in the ribs. “Faith can’t save, nor charity nether. There, you tell with him, while I go play bowls with Drake. He’ll tell you a sight of stories. You ask him about good King Hal, now—just—”

And off he went, leaving behind him the soft, granular scent of sugar and the quieter music of old age breathing.

“You have seen good King Henry, then, father?” Amyas asked, sitting down beside Martin Cockrem.

The old man’s eyes lit as if someone had opened a shutter in his head; he stopped mumbling his sugar.

“Seed mun? Iss, I reckon. I was with Captain Will when he went to meet the Frenchman there to Calais—at the Field, the Field—”

“The Field of the Cloth of Gold, gramfer,” the granddaughter supplied.

“That’s it. Seed mun? Iss, fegs. Oh, he was a king! The face o’ mun like a rising sun, and the back o’ mun so broad as that there—” and he held out his palsied arms—“and the voice of mun! Oh, to hear mun swear if he was merry—oh, ’tas royal!—Seed mun? Iss, fegs! And I’ve seed mun do what few has; I’ve seed mun christle like any child.”

“What—cry?” Amyas said, startled.

“I shouldn’t have thought there was much cry in him.”

“You think what you like—”

“Gramfer, gramfer, don’t you be rude, now—”

“Let him go on,” said Amyas gently; for there was a gravity in the old man’s voice beneath the rustic words, as if the very soundscape of memory—cries, splash, wind—had been preserved in him when other things had rotted away.

“I seed mun christle; and, oh dear, how he did put hands on mun’s face; and ‘Oh, my gentlemen,’ says he, ‘my gentlemen! Oh, my gallant men!’ Them was his very words.”

“But when?”

“Why, Captain Will had just come to the Hard—that’s to Portsmouth—to speak with mun, and the barge Royal lay again the Hard—so; and our boot alongside—so; and the king he standth as it might be there, above my head, on the quay edge, and she come in near abreast of us, looking most royal to behold, poor dear! and went to cast about. And Captain Will, saith he, ‘Them lower ports is cruel near the water;’ for she had not more than a sixteen inches to spare in the nether overloop, as I heard after. And saith he, ‘That won’t do for going to windward in a say, Martin.’ And as the words came out of mun’s mouth, your worship, there was a bit of a flaw from the westward, sharp like, and overboard goeth my cap, and hitth against the wall; and as I stooped to pick it up, I heard a cry, and it was all over!”

“He is telling of the Mary Rose, sir,” the granddaughter said softly, as if naming a dead person.

“I guessed so,” said Amyas.

“All over: and the cry of mun, and the screech of mun! Oh, sir, up to the very heavens! And the king he screeched right out like any maid, ‘Oh my gentlemen, oh my gallant men!’ and as she lay on her beam-ends, sir, and just a-settling, the very last souls I seen was that man’s father, and that man’s. I knowed mun by their armor.”

He pointed—shaking finger—toward Sir George Carew and Sir Richard Grenville.

“Iss! Iss! Drowned like rattens. Drowned like rattens!”

“Now; you mustn’t trouble his worship any more,” the granddaughter murmured.

“Trouble? Let him tell till midnight; I shall be well pleased,” Amyas answered; and called, “Drawer! ale—and a parcel of tobacco.”

The drawer brought ale that smelled of malt and cask-wood and the faint sour of yesterday; and tobacco whose leaf, when crumbled, gave a pungent green-brown odor that clung to the fingers. Amyas settled himself to listen while old Martin, comforted by attention and sweetness, purred half to himself:

“Iss. They likes to hear old Martin. All the captains look upon old Martin.”

“Hillo, Amyas!” cried Cary, coming up. “Who’s your friend? Here’s a man been telling me wonders about the River Plate. We should go thither for luck there next time.”

“River Plate?” said old Martin, lifting his head like a dog that hears its name. “It’s I knows about the River Plate; none so well. Who’d ever been there, nor heard of it nether, before Captain Will and me went, and I lived among the savages a whole year; and audacious civil I found ’em if they’d had but shirts to their backs. And so was the prince o’ mun, that Captain Will brought home to King Henry; leastwise he died on the voyage; but the wild folk took it cruel well, for you see, we was always as civil with them as Christians, and if we hadn’t been, I should not have been here now.”

“What year was that?” Amyas asked.

“In the fifteen thirty: but I was there afore, and learnt the speech o’ mun; and that’s why Captain Will left me to a hostage, when he tuked their prince.”

“Before that?” Cary said. “Why, the country was hardly known before that.”

Old Martin’s eyes flashed in triumph, as if some deep, half-forgotten dialect of pride had been spoken to him.

“Knowed? Iss, and you may well say that! Look ye here! Look to mun!” He waved his hand round at the captains. “There’s captains! and I’m the father of ’em all now, now poor Captain Will’s in gloory; I, Martin Cockrem! Iss, I’ve seen a change.”

His voice wandered, catching at memories by their sounds: bells, battle-cries, the crackle of burning ships.

“I mind when Tavistock Abbey was so full o’ friars, and goolden idols, and sich noxious trade, as ever was a wheat-rick of rats. I mind the fight off Brest in the French wars—Oh, that was a fight, surely!—when the Regent and the French Carack were burnt side by side, being fast grappled, you see, because of Sir Thomas Knivet; and Captain Will gave him warning as he ran a-past us, saying, says he—”

“But,” Amyas said, gently steering him back, “what do you mind about America?”

“America? I should think so! But I was a-going to tell you of the Regent—and seven hundred Englishmen burnt and drowned in her, and nine hundred French in the Brest ship, besides what we picked up. Oh dear! But about America.”

“Yes, about America. How are you the father of all the captains?”

“How? you ask my young master! Why, before the fifteen thirty, I was up the Plate with Cabot (and a cruel fractious ontrustful fellow he was, like all they Portingals), and bid there a year and more, and up the Paraguaio with him, diskivering no end; whereby, gentles, I was the first Englishman, I hold, that ever sot a foot on the New World, I was!”

“Then here’s your health, and long life, sir!” said Amyas; and Cary echoed it.

“Long life? Iss, fegs, I reckon, long enough a’ready!” Old Martin chuckled, and the sound was like dry leaves. “Why, I mind the beginning of it all, I do. I mind when there wasn’t a master mariner to Plymouth, that thought there was aught west of the Land’s End except herrings.”

He spoke now like a man tasting old words—words that belonged to a Plymouth that was gone, a Plymouth that spoke a rougher music, with turns and vowels that no courtier could swallow.

“Why, they held them, pure wratches, that if you sailed right west away far enough, you’d surely come to the edge, and fall over cleve. Iss—’Twas dark parts round here, till Captain Will arose; and the first of it I mind was inside the bar of San Lucar, and he and I were boys about a ten year old, aboord of a Dartmouth ship, and went for wine, and there come in over the bar he that was the beginning of it all.”

“Columbus?” said Amyas.

“Iss, fegs, he did, not a pistol-shot from us; and I saw mun stand on the poop, so plain as I see you. No great shakes of a man to look to nether; there’s a sight better here, to plase me. And we was disappointed, we lads, for we surely expected to see mun with a goolden crown on, and a sceptre to a’s hand, we did, and the ship o’ mun all over like Solomon’s temple for gloory.”

He licked the last grains of sugar from his finger, as if the sweetness helped the memory along.

“And I mind that same year, too, seeing Vasco da Gama, as was going out over the bar, when he found the Bona Speranza, and sailed round it to the Indies. Ah, that was the making of they rascally Portingals, it was! And our crew told what they seen and heerd: but nobody minded sich things. ’Twas dark parts, and Popish, then; and nobody knowed nothing, nor got no schooling, nor cared for nothing, but scrattling up and down alongshore like to prawns in a pule.”

His voice rose into a kind of rough chant, half Scripture, half seaman’s exultation, and the sounds of the harbor seemed to answer him—the wind in the vines, the distant hammering on shipboard, the creak of leather as a captain shifted his stance.

“Iss, sitting in darkness, we was, and the shadow of death, till the day-spring from on high arose, and shined upon us poor out-o’-the-way folk—The Lord be praised! And now, look to mun!” His hand swept round again, trembling with joy. “Look to mun! Look to the works of the Lord! Look to the captains! Oh blessed sight! And one’s been to the Brazils, and one to the Indies, and the Spanish Main, and the North-West, and the Rooshias, and the Chinas, and up the Straits, and round the Cape, and round the world of God, too, bless His holy name; and I seed the beginning of it; and I’ll see the end of it too, I will!”

He leaned forward, peering seaward as if his eyes could reach beyond the Sound to the very edge of Spain.

“I was born into the old times: but I’ll see the wondrous works of the new, yet, I will! I’ll see they bloody Spaniards swept off the seas before I die, if my old eyes can reach so far as outside the Sound. I shall, I knows it. I says my prayers for it every night; don’t I, Mary? You’ll bate mun, sure as Judgment, you’ll bate mun! The Lord’ll fight for ye. Nothing’ll stand against ye. I’ve seed it all along—ever since I was with young master to the Honduras. They can’t bide the push of us! You’ll bate mun off the face of the seas, and be masters of the round world, and all that therein is. And then, I’ll just turn my old face to the wall, and depart in peace, according to His word.”

Then, as if the whole prophecy were no more than steam off a pot-lid, he blinked, looked down, and the childlike bathos of hunger and sweetness took him again.

“Deary me, now, while I’ve been telling with you, here’ve this little maid been and ate up all my sugar!”

“I’ll bring you some more,” Amyas said; and though some laughed, his own throat tightened, for he heard in the old man’s speech the sound of a whole lifetime that could not be translated into any other mouth—not into court English, not into Latin, not into the brave printed words of chronicles. It was Martin Cockrem’s alone, and it was vanishing even as it was spoken.

“Will ye, then?” said Martin, brightening. “There’s a good soul, and come and tell with old Martin. He likes to see the brave young gentlemen, a-going to and fro in their ships, like Leviathan, and taking of their pastime therein. We had no such ships to our days. Ah, ’tis grand times, beautiful times surely—and you’ll bring me a bit sugar?”

“You were up the Plate with Cabot?” Cary asked, after a pause. “Do you mind the fair lady Miranda, Sebastian de Hurtado’s wife?”

“What! her that was burnt by the Indians?” Old Martin’s face changed as if a shadow had passed over sun. “Mind her? Do you mind the sun in heaven? Oh, the beauty! Oh, the ways of her! Oh, the speech of her! Never was, nor never will be! And she to die by they villains; and all for the goodness of her! Mind her? I minded naught else when she was on deck.”

“Who was she?” Amyas asked Cary.

“A Spanish angel, Amyas.”

“Humph!” Amyas said. “So much the worse for her, to be born into a nation of devils.”

“They’em not all so bad as that, yer honor,” old Martin muttered, with a stubborn fairness that had outlived storms. “Her husband was a proper gallant gentleman, and kind as a maid, too, and couldn’t abide that De Solis’s murderous doings.”

“His wife must have taught it him, then,” Amyas said, rising. “Where did you hear of these black swans, Cary?”

“I have heard of them, and that’s enough,” Cary answered, and would not stir the sad recollection further.

“And little enough,” Amyas said sharply. “Will, don’t talk to me. The devil is not grown white because he has trod in a lime-heap.”

“Or an angel black because she came down a chimney,” Cary returned; and the talk might have died there, as such talks do, with the taste of bitterness left on the tongue.

But across the terrace it was cut short by a sudden explosion from old John Hawkins—like a culverin fired too close, startling birds from the vines.

“Fail? Fail? What a murrain do you here, to talk of failing? Who made you a prophet, you scurvy, hang-in-the-wind, croaking, white-livered son of a corby-crow?”

“Heaven help us, Admiral Hawkins—who has put fire to your culverins in this fashion?” Lord Howard called, half laughing, half warning.

“Who? my lord! Croakers! my lord! Here’s a fellow calls himself the captain of a ship, and her majesty’s servant, and talks about failing, as if he were a Barbican loose-kirtle trying to keep her apple-squire ashore! Blurt for him, sneak-up! say I.”

“Admiral John Hawkins,” said the offender, stiff with affront, “you shall answer this language with your sword.”

“I’ll answer it with my foot; and buy me a pair of horn-tips to my shoes, like a wraxling man. Fight a croaker? Fight a frog, an owl! I fight those that dare fight, sir!”

“Sir, sir, moderate yourself,” Lord Howard said, stepping in with the easy authority of a man used to quarrels among brave men. “I am sure this gentleman will show himself as brave as any, when it comes to blows: but who can blame mortal man for trembling before so fearful a chance as this?”
