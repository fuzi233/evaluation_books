From the seventh year of her life, Rosa Bud had known no home other than the Nuns' House and no mother other than Miss Twinkleton. Her memory of her own mother was vivid yet tragic: a tiny creature, scarcely older than Rosa, brought home in her father's arms, drowned during a festive gathering. The image burned indelibly in Rosa's mind—the folds and colors of her pretty summer dress, the long, wet hair tangled with scattered petals of ruined flowers, the dead young figure lying in a "sad, sad beauty" upon the bed. Alongside this lay the wild despair and the subsequent, bowed-down grief of her father, who broke his heart on the first anniversary of that terrible day.

Her betrothal to Edwin Drood sprang from an attempt to soothe his own year of mental distress following the loss of his wife; he, like her father, had been left a widower in his youth. But fate, that relentless guide, led both young couples onto the silent road where all earthly pilgrimages eventually merge, sooner or later. Thus, the pair found themselves as they were.

An atmosphere of pity had surrounded little Rosa since her arrival in Cloisterham, never fully clearing away. As she grew older, happier, and prettier, these hues shifted from golden to roseate to azure, yet always adorned her with a soft, intrinsic light. The community's desire to console and caress her resulted in her being treated as younger than her years, a tendency that persisted even as she matured. Rivalries flared gently within the Nuns' House—who should be her favorite, who bring gifts, who perform services, who take her home for holidays, or who writes most frequently when apart? These gentle contests, hidden beneath veils and rosaries, harbored slight dashes of bitterness. Yet, Rosa remained an amiable, giddy, wilful, and winning creature. She was spoiled in the sense of expecting kindness from all around, but not in repaying it with indifference. Possessing an inexhaustible well of affection, her sparkling waters had refreshed the Nuns' House for years without disturbing their depths. What might transpire when those depths finally moved, or what changes might strike her heedless head and light heart, remained to be seen.

News of a quarrel between two young men overnight, involving a physical onslaught by Mr. Neville upon Edwin Drood, permeated Miss Twinkleton's establishment before breakfast. Whether brought by birds, blown in with the air, kneaded into bread, or delivered by the milkman, it was certain that the rumor filled every gable before Miss Twinkleton rose. Through Mrs. Tisher, the news reached her while she was dressing—a sacrifice to the Graces.

Rumors swirled wildly: Miss Landless's brother had thrown a bottle, then a knife, then a fork, and finally a decanter at Mr. Edwin Drood. Psychologically, it was deemed essential to determine *why* he had thrown them. The truth, as revealed by the cook, was that Miss Landless's brother had claimed to admire Miss Bud. Mr. Edwin Drood had replied that he had no business admiring her. Thereafter, the brother "up'd"—launching a barrage of bottles, knives, forks, and decanters at Mr. Drood.

Poor Rosa, hearing these rumors, put fingers in her ears and retreated to a corner, begging not to hear more. However, Miss Landless, seeking Miss Twinkleton's permission to speak with her brother, ultimately decided to visit Mr. Crisparkle for accurate intelligence. Upon returning, after a private consultation with Miss Twinkleton to filter any objectionable details, she shared with Rosa only the provocation her brother had faced, dwelling on the gross affront but omitting the origin of the dispute: her lover's tendency to take things too easily. With a petition from her brother asking for forgiveness, Miss Landless concluded the matter.

It fell to Miss Twinkleton to tone down the public mind of the Nuns' House. Entering the "apartment allotted to study," she called "Ladies!" causing everyone to rise. Standing beside her as a historical emblem, Mrs. Tisher watched as Miss Twinkleton addressed the assembly. She invoked Shakespeare, the "Swan of Avon," noting that Rumour, like the bird associated with the poet, sings sweetly on the approach of death—an observation unsupported by ornithology. Citing the Bard, Miss Twinkleton declared that a recent *fracas* between two young gentlemen, occurring within a hundred miles of their peaceful walls, had been grossly exaggerated. While initial anxiety arose from sympathy for a sweet young friend connected to one of the combatants, responsible inquiries confirmed the event was merely one of the "airy nothings" the Poet had described. Consequently, the subject was discarded, and minds concentrated on the day's labors.

Yet, the subject survived. Later, Miss Ferdinand incurred new trouble by surreptitiously applying a paper moustache at dinner and attempting to aim a water-bottle at Miss Giggles, who defended herself with a table spoon.

Deep within Rosa, thoughts of this quarrel lingered with an uncomfortable feeling that she was implicated, either as cause or consequence, through her precarious position regarding her marriage engagement. Never free from uneasiness when with her affianced husband, it was unlikely she would be free when apart. Cast alone upon herself, deprived of free talk with her new friend Helena—who avoided the subject as delicate and difficult—Rosa awaited the announcement of her guardian's arrival.

Mr. Grewgious arrived, a man of incorruptible integrity but of singular unappealing appearance. An arid, sandy individual, he resembled grain fed to a millstone destined for high-dried snuff. His hair was a scanty, flat crop of color and consistency akin to mangy yellow fur; so unlike hair was it that it must have been a wig, barring the stupendous improbability of anyone voluntarily wearing such a head. His features were carved deep into his face by hard curves, giving it the aspect of work. Notches in his forehead suggested that Nature had begun to fashion sensibility or refinement before impatiently abandoning the chisel, declaring, "Let him go as he is." With a too-long throat, excessive ankle-bones, an awkward, hesitating manner, a shambling walk, and what was termed "near sight"—perhaps preventing him from seeing how much white cotton stocking he exposed against his black suit—Mr. Grewgious nonetheless possessed a strange capacity to make an agreeable impression.

Found in Miss Twinkleton's sacred room, Mr. Grewgious was discomfited by her presence, dim forebodings of examination oppressing him. "My dear, how do you do?" he greeted warmly, offering a chair. Miss Twinkleton, rising gracefully, politely requested to retire, but he insisted she stay, wheeling her desk to a corner window. Left alone with Rosa by the fire, he repeated his greeting, sitting down as she seated herself.

"My visits," Mr. Grewgious noted, comparing them to those of angels, though he hastened to deny the comparison. "Few and far between." He produced a pocket-book and a stub of black lead, revealing a guiding memorandum. First, "Well and happy." He confirmed that Rosa was well and happy, attributing her state to the "maternal kindness and constant care" of Miss Twinkleton, whom he honored. Rosa, blushing slightly, acknowledged his observations.

Next, he turned to "Pounds, shillings, and pence." He inquired if her allowance was sufficient and if she was in debt. Rosa laughed at the notion of debt, finding it a comical vagary. Satisfied, Mr. Grewgious crossed out the financial entries and moved to the crucial item: "Marriage."

"I now touch," he said, lowering his voice and moving closer, "upon the point that is the direct cause of my troubling you. Otherwise, being a particularly Angular man, I should not have intruded." He likened himself to a bear with cramp in a youthful cotillion, a simile that elicited hearty laughter from Rosa. He affirmed that Mr. Edwin Drood had visited as arranged, that they liked one another, and that they corresponded. Regarding the future, he explained that at Christmas, it would become necessary to give formal business notice to Miss Twinkleton of Rosa's departure in the ensuing half-year. Though their relationship was far more than business, a residue of business remained. "If some competent Proxy would give you away," he suggested, mentioning the dancing master as a candidate for propriety, "I should take it very kindly." Rosa indicated that a substitute might be found if needed, remaining silent as her mind lagged on the prospect of the ceremony.

Then came the "Will." Mr. Grewgious produced a certified copy of her father's will. "Although I have before possessed you with its contents," he explained, "I think it right to leave this copy in your hands... and likewise to place a certified copy in Mr. Jasper's hand."

"Not in his own!" Rosa exclaimed, looking up quickly. "Cannot the copy go to Eddy himself?"

"Why, yes, my dear, if you particularly wish it; but I spoke of Mr. Jasper as being his trustee," Mr. Grewgious replied. "I do particularly wish it," Rosa said hurriedly and earnestly, "I don't like Mr. Jasper to come between us, in any way."
