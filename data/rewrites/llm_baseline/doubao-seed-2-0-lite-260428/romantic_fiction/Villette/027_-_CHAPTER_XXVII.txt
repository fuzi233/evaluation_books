The dawn that followed unfolded far brighter, busier, and more full of unexpected turns than I had dared to anticipate. It was the birthday of the eldest young prince of Labassecour, the Duc de Dindonneau, and every school across the city had been granted a public holiday in his honor—none more bustling than the principal Athénée college, where the students had drafted a formal loyal address to present before the royal party. The ceremony was to be held in the great public hall where yearly examinations and prize distributions took place: after the address was read, one of the college’s most renowned professors would deliver a grand public oration.

Many of M. de Bassompierre’s learned savant friends were closely affiliated with the Athénée, and he had been invited to join their party alongside the burgomaster, the city council, and every parent and relative of the college’s students. His beloved daughter Paulina would of course attend too, and she sent us a little handwritten note bidding Ginevra Fanshawe and me come early to join her group.

As we dressed side by side in the Rue Fossette dormitory, Ginevra suddenly paused mid-brushstroke and burst into peals of laughter.
“What’s got into you now?” I asked.
“It seems absurd, doesn’t it?” she said, with her usual unapologetic mix of frankness and cheeky insolence. “That you and I are now on equal footing, moving in the exact same social circle, counting the same people as friends.”
“I don’t disagree,” I told her. “Though I never had much patience for the crowd you used to run with—Mrs. Cholmondeley and her set would never have suited me at all.”
“Who even are you, Lucy Snowe?” she asked, her curiosity so unguarded and unvarnished that it was my turn to laugh. “You used to call yourself a nursery governess! When you first came to this house I saw you carry little Georgette in your arms like a common bonne—hardly any governess would have stooped that low. Now Madame Beck treats you with more courtesy than she gives that fancy Parisian teacher St. Pierre, and my proud little cousin Paulina acts like you’re her dearest bosom friend!”
“It is quite the marvel,” I agreed, highly entertained by her utter confusion. “Who am I, indeed? Maybe I’m some grand personage in disguise. Shame I don’t look the part, isn’t it?”
“I’m surprised you aren’t more flustered by all this attention,” she went on. “You take it all so weirdly calmly. If you really are the nobody I once thought you were, you’ve got nerves of steel.”
“The nobody you once thought me!” My cheeks flushed a little, but I refused to take offense. What did a schoolgirl’s silly crude labels of “nobody” and “somebody” matter at all? I only told her I had simply been met with basic kindness, and asked what about that kindness was supposed to throw me into a fever of embarrassment.
“Some things are just impossible not to wonder at,” she insisted.
“Wonder at myths you’ve made up in your own head. Are you finally ready to leave?”
“Yes. Let me take your arm.”
“I’d rather we walked side by side.” Whenever she took my arm, she leaned her entire weight on me, and since I was neither a gentleman nor her suitor, I found the habit irksome.
“There you go again!” she cried. “I was trying to pay you a compliment! I wanted to say I approve of how you look today, that I’m proud to be seen with you.”
“Ah. You meant you weren’t ashamed to walk down the Rue Crécy on my arm, in case Mrs. Cholmondeley was cooing over her lapdog in some window, or Colonel de Hamal picking his teeth on a balcony, and spotted us together?”
“Exactly,” she said, with that blunt honesty that was her only redeeming quality—the trait that even made her outright lies feel unfeigned, the only thing keeping her flighty, shallow character from rotting through entirely.

I let my amused, half-scornful smile answer for me, and she grumbled all the way across the great square and into the quiet, pleasant park that was our shortest route to the Rue Crécy. “No one in the whole world is such a tyrant to me as you are!” she complained.
“You bring it on yourself. Leave me alone, be quiet, and I’ll leave you alone too.”
“How can I leave you alone when you’re so weird and mysterious?”
“All that mystery is maggots in your own brain. Be good enough to leave them out of my sight.”
“But are you actually someone important?” She pushed her hand under my arm anyway, and I pressed my arm tight to my side to try to shake her off, but she clung on tight, squeezing until I had to stop in the middle of the path and laugh. The whole walk home she rambled on about her silly theory that I was hiding some grand secret identity, proving over and over that she could not fathom how a person without noble birth, family wealth, or a famous name could possibly hold their head high and live with unassuming integrity. For my part, I cared nothing for pedigree or social rank: the parts of my life that truly mattered were the people who knew me well, the rest I could take or leave. I had learned long ago the world places far more value on status, and I do not blame it. There are people whose social position is the very guardrail that keeps them morally steady, people who would lose all their self-respect if their humble origins were laid bare. Who are we to judge them for clinging to those small defenses that keep them from debasement?

We reached the Hôtel Crécy to find Paulina already fully dressed and waiting, with Mrs. Bretton at her side. Under the chaperonage of Mrs. Bretton and M. de Bassompierre, we were soon escorted to the ceremony hall, and shown to excellent seats well within view of the orator’s Tribune. The Athénée students were lined up before us, the city council and burgomaster sat in their places of honor, the young princes and their tutors occupied a prominent raised platform, and every corner of the hall was packed with Villette’s aristocracy and wealthiest burghers.

I had not cared at all who would be delivering the scheduled oration. I vaguely assumed some stuffy old savant would stand up and drone through half dogmatic lectures to the students, half fawning flattery of the royal princes. The Tribune stood empty when we first entered, but ten minutes later, a head, broad chest, and pair of broad shoulders popped up suddenly above the crimson desk.

I knew that head immediately. The tight black curls, the wide pale brow, the sharp bright blue fire in his eyes—every single detail was so familiar, tied to a hundred silly, chaotic little memories of the Rue Fossette classroom, that I burst out laughing so hard I grew warm, hiding my face behind my handkerchief and lowered veil so no one could see my unseemly mirth.

It was M. Paul Emanuel. I realized I was glad to see him. It felt far more pleasant than awkward to watch him stand up there, fierce and frank, sharp and unapologetic, testy and fearless, exactly the same as he was when he ruled over his classroom estrade. He filled the role so perfectly I’d never once thought to expect him, even though I knew he held the Belles Lettres chair at the Athénée. With him at the Tribune, I knew we would be spared all the hollow formality and empty flattery—but nothing could have prepared me for what he actually said.

He spoke to princes, nobles, magistrates, and ordinary burghers with the exact same unflinching ease, the same sharp, fiery earnestness he used to lecture his three unruly classroom divisions. He did not talk down to the college students like schoolboys—he spoke to them as future citizens, as patriots in the making. His words rang with a political courage I had never expected to sprout from the flat, complacent soil of Labassecour. He trampled over empty Utopian fantasies underheel, dismissed foolish whims with scorn, and when he turned to speak of tyranny, a light sparked in his eyes that was almost breathtaking. When he spoke of injustice, his voice was no soft murmur—it blared like a military trumpet echoing across the park at twilight. His whole audience might not have caught the full fire of his convictions, but the young students ate his words up, and gave him a long, thundering, ringing cheer when he finished. He was their undisputed favorite professor, sharp edges and all.

When our party left the hall, he was standing by the entrance. He spotted me at once, lifted his hat, and pressed my hand as we passed, uttering that typical, impulsive little question: “Qu’en dites vous?” What do you think? Even in this moment of his greatest public triumph, he could not help that restless, inquisitive streak that sometimes made him lack the calm self-control he should have cultivated. He should not have cared what any random person thought of his speech at that moment—but he did, and he was too natural, too impulsive to hide it. If I found his over-eagerness a little silly, I loved his total unpretentious naiveté. My heart was full of praise for him, but the words would not come—who ever finds the right words at the exact right moment? I stammered through a few awkward, lame phrases, and was deeply relieved when other well-wishers swarmed around him with gushing congratulations to cover for my floundering.

Someone introduced him to M. de Bassompierre, and the Count, who had been equally moved by the speech, invited him to join their party and dine with them at the Hôtel Crécy. He declined the dinner—he was always a little shy of wealthy people’s overtures, carrying a quiet, unobtrusive, rock-solid strain of independent pride that was all the more charming for never being flaunted. He did promise, however, to drop by later that evening with his friend the French Academician M. A—.

At dinner that day, Ginevra and Paulina were each beautiful in their own entirely distinct way. Ginevra might have had the edge in raw, obvious physical charm: her deep crimson dress set off her pale curls perfectly, matching the rose glow of her cheeks. But Paulina shone with a far more subtle, spiritual grace: her eyes were bright and eloquent, her bearing soft and graceful, her expression shifting with a thousand delicate winning nuances. Her dress was simple, close-fitting, and made of pure, clear white that made her soft complexion glow, her hair darker than Ginevra’s Saxon blonde, her brows and long lashes rich dark brown, every feature finished with a delicate, careful hand that had only sketched faint, careless outlines on Ginevra’s face.

Paulina was a little awed by the room full of learned savants, but she was not struck completely mute. She spoke softly, modestly, a little shyly, but with such sweet warmth and sharp, gentle good sense that her father paused his own conversations more than once just to look at her, his face glowing with proud delight. It was the polite, courtly learned M. Z— who drew her into talk, and her French was perfect: every sentence structure correct, every idiom natural, her accent pure as a native’s. Even Ginevra, who had spent half her life living on the Continent, could never match that flawless fluency, and M. de Bassompierre, who was very particular about the language, could not have been more pleased.

There was one more observer at the table: Dr. John Bretton, who had arrived late, detained by last-minute work at his practice. He glanced quietly at both young women the moment he sat down, and returned that careful, lingering survey more than once. His arrival perked Ginevra right up: she stopped looking listless, began smiling and chattering nonstop, though almost nothing she said was relevant to the intellectual conversation around the table. I could not help noticing that while Graham’s eyes were on her and his ears were listening, his sharp, lively, intelligent taste was not being fully engaged. Still, he was perfectly courteous, gave her almost all his attention through the whole meal, and she left the table for the drawing room in the highest of spirits.

No sooner had we stepped into the drawing room than Ginevra grew bored again, throwing herself on a couch to complain that the oration and the entire dinner party were dreadfully stupid, and asking Paulina how she could stand to spend time around such a pack of stuffy, prosaic old fogeys. But the second we heard the gentlemen’s footsteps heading our way, she stopped her complaints cold, jumped up, and dashed over to the piano to play with a great show of spirit. Dr. Bretton was the first to enter the room, and he went straight to stand by her side. I had expected he would soon drift over to the hearth where Paulina sat, but he only glanced that way, and it was the learned French savants who clustered around her, charmed by her quiet grace, her soft courtesy, her sharp, quiet tact. They did not bore her with talk of dry science—they spoke of literature, art, daily life, and soon it became clear she had read widely and thought deeply on all of it. I knew Graham was listening, even from across the room: his senses were sharp, and he was absorbing every word of that gentle, clever conversation, looking so pleased it almost hurt. Under that soft, delicate exterior, Paulina held far more strength of feeling and character than anyone guessed—strength that would keep her grace and beauty steady through any storm, long after shallow, unrooted prettiness faded.

While he waited for a gap in the group around Paulina, his wandering glance fell on me, sitting in a quiet nook not far from Mrs. Bretton and M. de Bassompierre, who were as usual deep in their easy, intimate tête-à-tête. Graham smiled in recognition, crossed the room to ask how I was, and told me I looked pale. I smiled to myself: it had been almost three whole months since he had spoken to me one on one, a lapse he had not even noticed. He sat down beside me, quiet, content to look around the room more than talk. He could gaze at Ginevra and Paulina both, study every detail of both their faces, as much as he liked.

I had caught glimpses of M. Paul hovering in the back inner saloon earlier, too, dark and severe, his sharp professor’s profile half-hidden by the doorway. He knew most of the gentlemen in attendance, but almost none of the ladies except me, and he started to walk over to say hello—until he spotted Dr. Bretton sitting beside me. He changed his mind, hung back, puckered his brows, stuck his lip out in that familiar grumpy scowl, and looked so absurdly cross that I averted my eyes to hide my smile. His brother Joseph Emanuel was there too, and he was just then taking over the piano from Ginevra, filling the room with grand, rich, masterful notes that made the old piano sing far better than it ever had under Ginevra’s schoolgirl fingers.

“Lucy,” Graham said, breaking the silence with a smile, as Ginevra glided past us and threw him a quick glance, “Miss Fanshawe is undeniably a remarkably fine girl.”
I nodded in agreement.
“Is there anyone else in the room as lovely as she is?”
“I don’t think there’s anyone here quite so classically handsome.”
“I agree with you. You and I so often share the same opinions, the same taste, I think.”
“Do we?” I said, a little doubtful.
“I believe if you had been a boy, Lucy, instead of a girl—mamma’s godson instead of her goddaughter—we would have been the very best of friends. Our opinions would have fit together perfectly.” He was half bantering, half gentle, half ironic, that familiar warm light glinting in his eye. I could not help the small, sad little ache in my chest: if I had been born wealthy, high born, pretty in the way other women were, would he have ever dismissed me as a quiet, inoffensive shadow the way he so often did? I pushed the feeling away, and asked him calmly what points we were so perfectly aligned on.
“Both of us are sharp observers. You probably don’t think I notice anything, but I do.”
“But we might see the exact same thing and judge it completely differently, mightn’t we?”
“Let’s test it. Of course you admire Miss Fanshawe. What do you think of the other people here? My mother, for example, or those two learned lions Messieurs A— and Z—, or that little pale lady, Miss de Bassompierre?”
“You know how much I think of your mother. I haven’t spared any thought for Messieurs A and Z.”
“And the pale little lady?”
“I think she is exactly what you say: a pale little lady, especially right now, fatigued from all the excitement.”
“Don’t you remember her as a child?”
“I wonder sometimes if you do.”
“I had completely forgotten her. But it’s strange, the way old people, old moments, old words that had slipped your mind can rise up again, under the right circumstances.”
“That’s certainly true.”
“But those old memories are fuzzy, half like a dream, half like a fantasy. You need a witness to confirm they’re real. Weren’t you staying at Bretton house ten years ago, when Mr. Home brought his little daughter, little Polly, to stay with mamma?”
“I was there the night she arrived, and the morning she left again.”
“Such a strange little child, wasn’t she? I wonder how I treated her. Was I even kind to children back then, big reckless schoolboy that I was? But you don’t remember me back then, do you?”
“You’ve seen your own portrait at La Terrasse. It looks exactly like you. Your manners are almost exactly the same now as they were then.”
“That only makes me more curious. What am I like today? What was I like ten years ago?”
“Gracious to everything that pleased you, never intentionally cruel to anything.”
“You’re wrong. I think I was almost a brute to you, for instance.”
“A brute? No, Graham. I would never have patiently put up with brutality.”
“But I do remember this: quiet Lucy Snowe never got any of that kindness from me.”
“Nor any of your cruelty, either.”
“Even Nero himself could not have tormented someone as harmless, as inoffensive as a shadow.”
