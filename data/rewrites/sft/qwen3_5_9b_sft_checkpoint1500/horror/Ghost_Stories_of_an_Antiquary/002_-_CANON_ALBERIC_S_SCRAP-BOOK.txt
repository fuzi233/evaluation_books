St. Bertrand de Comminges is a rotting outpost clinging to the spine of the Pyrenees, a shadow of a town nestled between Toulouse and Bagnères-de-Luchon. Once a seat of a bishopric, it survives today as a pilgrimage spot for tourists seeking the ghosts of the Revolution within its crumbling cathedral. In the spring of 1883, an Englishman named Dennistoun arrived at this forsaken place. A scholar from Cambridge, he had traveled specifically to study the ancient church that dominated the hill of Comminges, leaving two less devoted friends behind at a hotel in Toulouse with a promise to join them the following day. While half an hour would suffice for his companions, Dennistoun intended to spend the entire day documenting every corner of the "wonderful" church, filling notebooks and exposing dozens of photographic plates to capture its decaying grandeur.

To achieve this, he required exclusive access to the sacristy. He summoned the keeper of the keys, the *verger*, through the somewhat brusque proprietor of the nearby *Chapeau Rouge* inn. When the old man arrived, Dennistoun found him to be a figure of unsettling intensity. The sacristan was a small, wizened creature, physically unremarkable save for a demeanor that suggested he was being hunted. He possessed a perpetual, furtive air, his back and shoulders hunched in a constant, nervous contraction, as if expecting the jaws of an enemy to snap shut around him at any second. He glanced behind him constantly, his movements jerky and abrupt. Dennistoun struggled to categorize the man: was he deluded, guilt-ridden, or perhaps the victim of an unbearable tyrant? The odds leaned toward the latter, yet the sheer weight of the persecution implied by the old man's gaze suggested a foe far more terrible than a nagging wife.

Dennistoun, however, quickly buried himself in his work, his notebook and camera becoming his shields. Yet, whenever he risked a glance backward, the sacristan was always there, huddled against the walls or crouching in the ornate stalls, watching with wide, terrified eyes. After some time, Dennistoun grew restless, plagued by suspicions that the old man was withholding his lunch or guarding the sacred ivory crozier and the stuffed crocodile hanging over the font with jealous vigilance.

"You cannot stay here forever," Dennistoun finally urged, trying to break the spell. "I am perfectly capable of finishing my notes alone. You need not lock me in, though you may try. I require at least two more hours, and it must be freezing for you out there, yes?"

"Good heavens!" the little man shrieked, thrown into a panic by the mere suggestion. "That cannot be thought of! To leave Monsieur alone in the church? No, no! Whether it be two hours, three, or all day, I remain. I have eaten, I am not cold, thank you, Monsieur."

"Very well, little man," Dennistoun muttered to himself. "You have been warned; take your consequences."

For the next two hours, Dennistoun meticulously cataloged the dilapidated organ, the choir screen of Bishop John de Mauléon, the shattered remnants of stained glass, and the artifacts in the treasure chest. Throughout, the sacristan trailed at his heels, snapping around like a whipped dog whenever the strange, muffled noises that haunt empty cathedrals drifted on the air. Sometimes, as Dennistoun recounted later, he swore he heard a thin, metallic laughter echoing from high within the tower. When he looked, the sacristan was pasty-white, trembling. "It is he—that is it. No one else. The doors are locked," the old man stammered, staring blankly ahead for a full minute.

A peculiar incident further unnerved Dennistoun. He was examining a dark painting behind the altar, depicting one of St. Bertrand's miracles. Beneath it, a Latin legend read: *Qualiter S. Bertrandus liberavit hominem quem diabolus diu volebat strangulare* ("How St. Bertrand delivered a man whom the Devil long sought to strangle"). Dennistoun turned to the sacristan, intending to make a jesting remark, only to find the old man on his knees before the canvas. The man gazed at the image with the agony of a supplicant, hands clasped tight, tears streaming down his face like rain. Dennistoun feigned ignorance, yet the question gnawed at him: why did such a crude daub evoke such visceral terror? He suspected the man was a monomaniac, but the nature of his obsession remained a mystery.

By five o'clock, twilight was descending, casting the church into a labyrinth of shadows. The strange noises—the soft footfalls, the indistinct whispers—became more frequent, amplified by the failing light and the heightened sensitivity of the ears. The sacristan suddenly displayed signs of impatience, sighing with relief as Dennistoun finally packed his gear. He hurried Dennistoun toward the western door beneath the tower to ring the Angelus. The great bell, Bertrande, tolled with a resonant, mournful voice that swelled through the pine forests and down into the valleys, calling the lonely hill-dwellers to prayer. With the sound, a profound silence fell over the town, and the two men emerged from the sanctuary.

On the threshold, conversation resumed. The sacristan mentioned Dennistoun's interest in the old choir books and asked if a local library existed. When the answer was negative, a strange hesitation seized him, followed by a sudden plunge of resolve. "But if Monsieur is an amateur of old books," he blurted out, "I have something at home that might interest you. It is not a hundred yards."

Dennistoun's hopes for hidden manuscripts flared, then dimmed. Surely, a place so close to Toulouse had already been ransacked by collectors? Still, refusal seemed impossible. He agreed to follow, attempting to distract the sacristan by mentioning his friends arriving the next morning. To his astonishment, this news relieved the old man's anxiety. "That is well," the sacristan said brightly. "Monsieur travels with friends; they will be near him. It is a good thing to travel thus... sometimes." The final word hung in the air, bringing back a wave of gloom to the poor man's face.

They reached the house, a stone dwelling slightly larger than its neighbors, bearing a carved shield of Alberic de Mauléon, a canon from the late seventeenth century. The upper windows were boarded, the entire estate radiating the air of decaying age. At the door, the sacristan paused, hesitating again. "Perhaps, after all, Monsieur has not the time?"

"Not at all," Dennistoun assured him. "Plenty of time. Let us see what you have."

A younger face appeared in the doorway—the sacristan's daughter. She shared her father's distress, though hers seemed rooted in acute anxiety for another rather than fear for herself. Her expression brightened at the sight of the robust stranger, and she exchanged a few words with her father. Dennistoun caught only one phrase from the sacristan: "He was laughing in the church." The girl answered with a look of pure terror.

Inside the sitting room, a high chamber with a stone floor, a wood fire flickered on a great hearth, casting dancing shadows against a towering crucifix that reached toward the ceiling. Beside it stood a solid, aged chest. When a lamp was lit and chairs arranged, the sacristan approached the chest. His movements grew increasingly frantic, his nerves visibly frayed, as he unwrapped a large volume bound in white cloth embroidered with a crude red cross.

Dennistoun felt a surge of professional curiosity. The book was too massive for a missal and oddly shaped for an antiphoner. As soon as it was opened, however, his sentiment shifted to awe. It was a large folio, bound in leather stamped with the golden arms of Canon Alberic de Mauléon. Inside, nearly every page held a leaf torn from an illuminated manuscript. Ten leaves from a Genesis, illustrated with images dating back to the seventh century; a complete set of Psalter pictures executed in England during the thirteenth century of the highest quality; and twenty leaves of uncial Latin script, clearly from an unknown patristic treatise. Was it a fragment of Papias' "Words of Our Lord"? Regardless, Dennistoun resolved to transport this treasure back to Cambridge, even if he had to liquidate his entire fortune to pay for it.

"If Monsieur will turn to the end," the sacristan whispered.

Dennistoun complied, discovering new marvels with every leaf. Finally, he reached the conclusion of the book, where two sheets of paper from a much later era awaited him. They were undoubtedly the work of the unscrupulous Canon Alberic, who had likely plundered the St. Bertrand library to create this scrapbook. The first sheet bore a careful, recognizable plan of the south aisle and cloisters of St. Bertrand's. Marked upon it were curious symbols resembling planetary sigils, a few Hebrew characters in the corners, and a cross drawn in gold paint in the north-west angle of the cloister. Below, in Latin, were lines reading: *Responsa 12mi Dec. 1694. Interrogatum est: Inveniamne? Responsum est: Invenies. Fiamne dives? Fies. Vivamne invidendus?
