They say that knowledge is power, but who has ever truly weighed the crushing weight of ignorance? While knowledge patiently constructs over centuries, ignorance can dismantle a lifetime's work in a single hour. Knowledge refines the senses and multiplies needs, transforming life into a vibrant tapestry of new creations; ignorance arrives drunk on the seventh day, wielding a match and a barrel of oil, declaring "Let there not be," and shriveling the colorful world into a suffocating blackness. Knowledge operates with a conscience, restrained by scruples regarding what *must* be and what *may* be. Ignorance, however, is a blind giant; if left unchecked, he would treat the pillars supporting humanity's greatest achievements as toys, plunging all places of joy into the darkness of a buried Babylon.

When examining the trajectory of a single life, one sees clearly how ignorance of the true bonds between events, coupled with a delusion of control, precipitates the soul toward ruin. Like faulty eyesight that mistakes distant horizons for steps away, this blindness drives the misguided forward into destruction.

It was half-past ten in the morning when Gwendolen Harleth arrived at the station bound for Offendene, having traveled from Leubronn. No carriage awaited her; in a telegram sent from Dover, she had requested a later train, driven by an impatience to escape the London delay. She had not pictured the solitude of arriving unannounced at a remote halt, situated not for convenience but as an equidistant point from everywhere. Deposed as a *femme sole* amidst her towering trunks, she waited in the Railway Inn's waiting room for a vehicle. The scene was dreary: dirty walls, a dusty decanter of flat water, and large, glaring texts urging repentance. These elements seemed woven into the fabric of her family's troubles. Hurried to the outer door, she gazed at the lane and fields, yet even the sunlight appeared melancholic. Autumn leaves shivered, and the wind ruffled the feathers of a cock and two hens, likely parentless and bewildered.

A railway official observed her with an innocent yet condescending gaze, his lack of recognition suggesting he deemed her insignificant. Soon, a filthy old barouche arrived, being prepared by an elderly laborer. These contemptible details often form the bedrock of a life's narrative, accumulating until they shape a mood into a theory or a motive. Philosophy itself cannot escape such determinants. Dropped alone at an ugly, irrelevant spot, burdened by a sense of no income, one is prone to discouraging speculation on the origins of existence. How much heavier these trifles fell on a young lady of fastidious taste, draped in an Indian shawl, surrounded by twenty cubic feet of luggage, and suddenly acutely aware of poverty? They crushed Gwendolen's resistance.

"What is the good of enduring hardship, ugliness, and humiliation?" she asked herself. This was the beginning of returning home, a preview of her future. During the slow, jolting drive, with one trunk squeezing the driver and another lashed to the seat before her, her ruling vision shifted. From Leubronn, she had expected the family to return abroad, assuming a small income remained. She feared a dull exile, watching her sisters grow up in tediousness while her mother grew more melancholy. She refused to submit. Weariness and disgust replaced her uneasy dreams; the self-delight of admiring her reflection in the mirror had vanished, replaced by the futility of being charming, clever, or resolute. Men were hateful. Yet, in these final hours, the nature of that hatred changed. It was one thing to hate stolen goods; it was another to hate them because their theft prevented one from using them. Gwendolen began to rage against Grandcourt specifically—for being the obstacle that barred her from marrying him, the architect of her current misery.

As the vehicle crested the hill toward the house, a figure appeared under the portico. A rush of less selfish emotion swept through Gwendolen. Seeing her mother's beautiful, sorrowful face, she threw her arms around her neck, momentarily aligning her own pain with her mother's. Behind them stood the four "superfluous" girls, each possessing a private world of no consequence to others, yet all sensing that Gwendolen's presence signaled a reprieve from misfortune. Even her brisk dismissal—"Now go away, girls"—carried the comfort of authority that weakness craves.

Miss Merry, usually a picture of meek depression, busied herself with the trunks and the coachman's pay, expecting no greeting. Mrs. Davilow and Gwendolen ascended the stairs to the black-and-yellow bedroom.

"Never mind, mamma dear," Gwendolen whispered, pressing a handkerchief to her mother's weeping cheeks. "I don't mind. I will do something. I will be something. Things will come right. It seemed worse because I was away. Be glad I am here."

Every word rang true. A surge of compassionate tenderness ignited her capacity for resolve. Vague projects from her journey crystallized instantly. She suddenly perceived how she could be "something." Her mother, forgetting their dire straits, looked at her with adoration. "Bless you, my good, good darling! I can be happy, if you can!"

But later, the tide receded. The old slippery rocks and weedy places returned. As misfortune ceased to be a mere announcement and became a tyrannical inhabitant, courage shrank. Initially, the comforts of the large house—the roomy silence, the ready luxuries of her toilette, the private tray of favorite foods—soothed her. She had insisted, "Keep them all away from us today. Let you and me be alone."

Descending into the drawing-room, fresh as a newly dipped swan, Gwendolen leaned against the cushions beside her mother, prepared to hear everything. "What have you thought of doing, exactly, mamma?" she asked deliberately.

"Oh, my dear, the next step is to move from this house," Mrs. Davilow replied softly. "Mr. Haynes is delighted to have it now. Lord Brackenshaw's agent, Bazley, will arrange everything."

"I cannot help thinking Lord Brackenshaw would let you stay here rent-free," Gwendolen interjected, her talents unused to business but keen on discerning admiration.

"My dear child, Lord Brackenshaw is in Scotland and knows nothing of us. Neither your uncle nor I would wish to trouble him. Besides, what could we do here without servants or money to heat it? The sooner we leave, the better. We have nothing to carry but our clothes."

"I suppose you mean to go abroad, then?" Gwendolen asked, accepting this familiar scenario.

"Oh, no, dear. How could we travel? You never learned about income and expenses," Mrs. Davilow smiled sadly, taking Gwendolen's hand. "That makes it so much harder for you, my pet."

"But where are we to go?" Gwendolen pressed, a new current of fear coursing through her.

"It is decided. We shall take a little furniture from the rectory." Mrs. Davilow hesitated, dreading the shock she must inflict. "We are to go to Sawyer's Cottage."

At first, Gwendolen remained silent, paling with justified anger. Then, with haughtiness, she declared, "That is impossible. Something else ought to have been thought of. My uncle ought not to allow that. I will not submit to it."

"My sweet child, what else could be done? Your uncle is suffering; he has a family to raise. Do you understand? We have nothing. We shall have absolutely nothing except what he and my sister provide. They have tried wisely and actively, and we must try to earn something. I and the girls are going to work a table-cloth border for the Ladies' Charity at Winchester and a communion cloth for Pennicote Church."

Mrs. Davilow spoke timidly, hoping to convey the gravity of their position to a daughter who, alas, must submit for now, though the mother secretly hoped for a better background.

"But surely somewhere else than Sawyer's Cottage might have been found," Gwendolen persisted, haunted by the image of the house where an exciseman had lived.

"No, indeed, dear. Houses are scarce. We are thankful for anything so private. It is not so very bad. Two little parlors and four bedrooms. You shall sit alone whenever you like."

The sympathy Gwendolen had felt for her mother evaporated completely. She ignored the deprecatory words. "I cannot conceive that all your property is gone at once, mamma. How can you be sure in so short a time? It is not a week since you wrote."

"The first news came much earlier, dear. But I would not spoil your pleasure until it was necessary."

"Oh, how vexatious!" Gwendolen colored with fresh anger. "If I had known, I could have brought home the money I won. For wanting to know, I stayed and lost it. I had nearly two hundred pounds; it would have sustained us until I could execute some plan." She paused, then added impetuously, "Everything has gone against me. People have come near me only to blight me."

Among the "people" included Deronda. Had he not interfered, she might have returned to the gaming tables with a few napoleons and recovered her losses.
