There exists a limit to human compassion, a boundary where the anemia of the soul renders empathy impossible. When the pitch of life drops so low that the spirit turns inside out, reversing every sweetness into a horror, the connection between human beings dissolves. The sane soul recoils in terror, not merely for its own preservation, but because the proximity to madness threatens to infect sanity itself. We are not gods; we lack the divine authority to expel demons. Our primary, selfish duty is to ensure these entities do not take root within our own hearts.

Yet, this vigilance must be maintained even when Love floods us so completely that we feel ourselves nearly divine. In such states, words of honor and duty become hollow echoes. A letter slipped beneath a darkened door lies ignored, or is glimpsed for a fleeting second between two abyssal gaps of time before being forgotten. Telegrams remain undelivered; the whistling messenger, guided by a wisdom deeper than his own, walks away unaware that a drawn blind has been nudged aside by a trembling finger and then hastily restored. Let the wretched man wrestle with his own shadows; let him, if he truly is mad, clutch and strain and entwine the succubus. But let him do so within a house where no air of heaven penetrates, where no bright finger of the sun pierces the filthy twilight. The lost must remain lost. Humanity has other business.

For Oleron, the handwriting of two letters he had seen on his kitchen floor—letters meant to clear his sitting-room of an armful of fetid, decaying flowers—held no meaning greater than a dim, distant dream. When the telegraph boy pounded on his door, mere feet from the bed where he lay paralyzed, Oleron ground his teeth and clamped his hands over his ears. He visualized the boy standing just beyond his partition, surrounded by packets of provisions and bundles of dead, rotting blooms. His outer landing was strewn with this refuse. Oleron had feared opening his door to admit them. After a week, the errand boys reported a mistake regarding the order and ceased to return. Inside, bathed in a red twilight, the old flowers turned brown, collapsed, and rotted where they lay.

Slowly, his power drained away. The Abomination fastened onto Oleron's vitality. The steady sapping often left him prostrate for hours, gazing vacantly at his red-tinged ceiling, idly suffering fantasies that took possession of him without resistance. Even his strongest memories held only a precarious grip on his attention. Sometimes a flickering half-memories of a novel he must write—a novel vital to his existence—tantalized him briefly before vanishing. Other times, entire novels, perfect and splendid, rose magically before his mind. At other intervals, the memories were absurdly remote and trivial: garrets he had inhabited, lodgings that had sheltered him, the dust and grime of a life long past.

Oleron had known much of these things in his time, but now it was all history. He had finally found a place he did not intend to leave until they dragged him out—a realm some might deem slightly green-sick, others consider too redolent of long-dead, morbid things for a living man to be imprisoned in. Yet it was irresistible, wielding an authority of its own, accompanied by an associate of its own, offering delights once a man ceased struggling against its inexorable will. A novel? Someone ought to write a book about a place like that! There must be plenty to uncover if one could but reach the bottom of it! It had likely already been chronicled by a man named Madley who resided there... but Oleron did not know this Madley. He possessed a strong, chilling intuition that he would not have liked him—that he would have preferred the fellow live elsewhere—and that he hated him, Madley, in fact. (Ah! That was a joke!). He seriously questioned whether Madley had led the life he ought; Oleron was torn between telling the long-nosed guardian of public morals across the street about him and suspecting that the man already knew and had raised his holy hullabaloo for him. That was Madley's trade. Indeed, Oleron himself had had a dust-up with him over something—or someone... Elsie Bengough. Her name surfaced now.

Oleron experienced moments of profound unease regarding Elsie Bengough. Or rather, he was less uneasy about her person and more restless regarding the things she did. Preeminent among these was her persistence in thrusting herself into his thoughts. Whenever he was swift enough, he dispatched her packing the moment she materialized there. The truth was, she was not merely a bore; she had always been that. Now, her mere presence in his fancy became inimical to the full enjoyment of certain experiences. She lacked tact; she surely ought to have known that people are not at home to everyone's thoughts at all times; she ought, in mere politeness, to allow him seasons quite to himself. And she was monstrously ignorant if she did not know, as she seemed not to, that there were special hours when a man's veins ran with fire and daring and power, in which... well, in which he had a reasonable right to treat folk as he had treated that prying Barrett—to shut them out completely. But no: up she popped, the thought of her, and ruined everything. Bright, towering fabrics, by the side of which even those perfect, magical novels of which he dreamed appeared dull and grey, vanished utterly at her intrusion. It was as if a fog should suddenly quench some fair-beaming star, as if at the threshold of some golden portal prepared for Oleron a pit should suddenly gape, as if a bat-like shadow should turn the growing dawn to murky darkness again. Therefore, Oleron strove to stifle even the nascent thought of her.

Nevertheless, there came an occasion when this woman Bengough absolutely refused to be suppressed. Oleron could not say exactly when this happened; he only knew by the glimmer of the street lamp on his blind that it was some time during the night, and that for some duration she had not presented herself.

He received no warning, none, of her coming; she simply arrived—she was there. Strive as he would, he could not shake off the thought of her, nor the image of her face. She haunted him.

But for her to come at that precise moment of all moments!... Really, it was past belief! How she could endure it, Oleron could not conceive! Actually, to look on, as it were, at the triumph of a rival.... Good God! It was monstrous! Tact—reticence—he had never credited her with an overwhelming amount of either: but he had never attributed mere... oh, there was no word for it! Monstrous—monstrous! Did she intend thenceforward.... Good God! To look on!...

Oleron felt the blood rush up to the roots of his hair with anger against her.

"Damnation take her!" he choked....

But the next moment, his heat and resentment transformed into a cold sweat of cowering fear. Panic-stricken, he strove to comprehend what he had done. For though he knew not what, he knew he had done something, something fatal, irreparable, blasting. Anger he had felt, but not *this* blaze of ire that suddenly flooded the twilight of his consciousness with a white, infernal light. *That* appalling flash was not his—not his *that* open rift of bright and searing Hell—not his, not his! His had been the hand of a child, preparing a puny blow; but what was *this other* horrific hand drawn back to strike in the same place? Had *he* set that in motion? Had *he* provided the spark that had ignited the whole accumulated power of that formidable and relentless place? He did not know. He only knew that that poor igniting particle within himself was blown out, that—Oh, impossible!--a clinging kiss (how else to express it?) had changed on his very lips to a gnashing and a removal, and that for very pity of the awful odds he must cry out to her against whom he had lately raged to guard herself... guard herself....

"Look out!" he shrieked aloud....

The revulsion was instant. As if a cold, slow billow had broken over him, he woke to find himself lying in his bed. The mist and horror that had enwrapped him for so long had departed. He was Paul Oleron, and he was sick, naked, helpless, and unutterably abandoned and alone. His faculties, though weak, answered at last to his calls upon them; and he knew that it must have been a hideous nightmare that had left him sweating and shaking thus.

Yes, he was himself, Paul Oleron, a tired novelist, already past the summit of his best work, and slipping downhill again empty-handed from it all. He had struck short in his life's aim. He had tried too much, had over-estimated his strength, and was a failure, a failure....

It all came to him in the single word, enwrapped and complete; it needed no sequential thought; he was a failure. He had missed....

And he had missed not one happiness, but two. He had missed the ease of this world, which men love, and he had missed also that other shining prize for which men forgo ease, the snatching and holding and triumphant bearing up aloft of which is the only justification of the mad adventurer who hazards the enterprise.
