The Wife of Dives

Rain lashed against the glass of Mrs. Manderson's sitting room at White Gables, turning the garden below into a blurred wash of greys and greens. It was a bleak afternoon in June, the kind that clings to the skin with a chill that no fire could quite banish. Outside, the sea lay sullen and mist-shrouded, sending drifting wreaths of rain up the fields in a constant, mournful rhythm. Inside, the air was still, heavy with the weight of a solitary grief. Mrs. Manderson stood by the window, her silhouette sharp against the gloom, her face a mask of weary resignation. A life without purpose, she thought, was a prison built of invisible walls.

A knock shattered the silence. "Come in," she called, straightening her spine with an automatic, brittle movement. Her maid had announced Mr. Trent, noting his apology for the hour but emphasizing the urgency of his visit. "Very well," Mrs. Manderson murmured, walking to the mirror. She stared at the olive complexion reflected back, shaking her head at the faint, grimacing tremor that betrayed her composure. When Trent entered, she noted immediately that the easy, half-smiling warmth of his usual demeanor had vanished. Instead, he wore the hollow, jaundiced look of a man who had not slept, his expression guarded, devoid of his customary cheer.

"Shall I get to the point at once?" he asked, taking her proffered hand. "I have a train at Bishopsbridge at noon, but I cannot board until I resolve this matter concerning you, Mrs. Manderson. I spent half the night working and the rest thinking, and now I know what I must do."

"You look utterly exhausted," she offered gently, guiding him to a plush armchair. "Please sit. This is a comfortable chair. Naturally, it concerns this dreadful business and your duties as a correspondent. Ask me whatever you deem fit to know, Mr. Trent. I trust you will not make matters worse for me than necessity demands. If you insist on speaking, I assume it is because it is your duty."

"Mrs. Manderson," Trent said, pausing to weigh his words carefully, "I promise not to make it worse for you than I can help. However, I am bound to make it difficult for you—in confidence, I hope. Regarding what you may tell me: you will judge that yourself. But on my honor, I shall ask only enough to determine whether I must publish or suppress certain grave revelations regarding your husband's death. These are facts unknown to anyone else, and unlikely to be suspected. What I have uncovered, what I believe I have proven, will shock you regardless. Yet, it might devastate you further. If you give me cause to believe that publication would be cruel, then I will burn this manuscript." He placed a long envelope on the small table between them. "It contains a brief private note to my editor and a lengthy dispatch intended for the *Record*. You need not answer now. If you refuse, my professional obligation dictates that I take this document to London today and leave it to my editor's discretion. I do not claim the right to censor based on a mere hypothetical fear. However, if I discern from you—and from no other source—that this imagined horror holds substance, then as a gentleman and one who wishes you well, I shall withhold the dispatch. There are areas where I refuse to aid the police. Does this align with your understanding?"

"I comprehend perfectly," Mrs. Manderson whispered, drawing a sharp breath. "Though I remain ignorant of the dreadful discovery you suspect, or the specific nightmare haunting your mind, I acknowledge your integrity in approaching me directly. Pray, reveal it."

"I cannot disclose it," Trent replied firmly. "That secret belongs to my newspaper unless it is rightfully yours. If I discover it pertains solely to you, you shall read and destroy the manuscript. Trust me," his voice softened momentarily, betraying a flash of genuine distress, "I abhor creating mysteries from the depths of my soul. This is the most agonizing hour of my existence, and you exacerbate it by withholding answers." His tone hardened again, colorless and clinical. "My sole request is this: Is it true, as you testified at the inquest, that you had absolutely no concept of why your late husband altered his attitude toward you, becoming mistrustful and withdrawn during his final months?"

Mrs. Manderson's dark eyebrows arched, her eyes igniting with sudden fire. She rose abruptly. Trent stood simultaneously, retrieving the envelope from the table; his posture signaled the conclusion of the interview. But she raised a hand, her cheeks flushing, her voice trembling with rapid, urgent cadence. "Do you realize what you demand, Mr. Trent? You ask me if I lied under oath!"

"I do," he answered, unmoved. After a beat, he added, "You were already aware I did not seek to maintain polite fictions, Mrs. Manderson. The notion that a respectable individual, sworn to the truth, could ever withhold a fragment of reality is itself a polite fiction." He stood ready for dismissal, yet she fell silent. She moved to the window, leaving him to watch the slight, jerky movements of her shoulders until they stilled. Turning away, gazing out at the dismal deluge, she finally spoke with crystalline clarity.

"Mr. Trent," she said, "you command respect, and I feel confident that what I wish to conceal remains safe with you. I sense a profound gravity to your actions, though I lack insight into its nature. Perhaps revealing the truth serves justice. To grasp it, you must understand the prelude to my marriage. Many could recount to you as well as I that it was... not a triumphant union. At twenty, I was captivated by his force, his courage, his absolute certainty. He was the strongest man I had ever encountered. Yet, it took little time to realize he cherished his business above me. I suspect I deceived myself far sooner, blind to my own feelings, intoxicated by the illusion of unlimited wealth—a fantasy no ordinary English girl could dream of. I have despised that youthful folly for five years. As for his affection..." She paused, unable to articulate it. "...what I must convey is this: alongside everything else, he harbored a firm conviction that I was destined for prominence in high society. He believed I would embrace that world with relish, becoming a social figurehead reflecting credit upon him. That vision persisted even as other illusions faded. I was merely an extension of his ambition. His deepest bitterness stemmed from my failure as a social asset. I believe he was too astute not to recognize, in his heart, the peril inherent in uniting a man of his stature and relentless industry with a girl raised on music, literature, and impractical ideals, accustomed to living solely for personal pleasure. Nevertheless, he calculated on me performing the honors of his position. I simply could not."

Mrs. Manderson had spoken herself into a fevered state, far beyond her previous reserve. Her words cascaded freely, her voice ringing with a natural expressiveness that seemed to break through the layers of shock and repression she had worn for days. Suddenly, she turned from the window to face him. Her beautiful countenance was flushed, her eyes gleaming, her hands making slight, emphatic gestures as she surrendered to the impulse to unleash a lifetime of pent-up emotion.

"The people!" she exclaimed, her voice rising. "Can you conceive what it entails for someone raised in a sphere defined by creative labor, dignity, intellectual pursuits, and ideals worthy of debate? Men and women of profession and art, some wealthy, some destitute, all bound by beliefs larger than themselves. How does one survive stepping out of that world into a realm where one *must* possess vast, shameful fortunes merely to exist? Where currency is the sole metric of value, dominating every thought? Where the millionaires are so exhausted by toil that sport becomes their only refuge, and the idlers are often vainer and more vicious than the workers? And the women, living exclusively for display, frivolous diversions, and shallow immorality? Do you understand how horrific that existence becomes? Admittedly, there are individuals of intellect and taste within that circle, but they are drowned and corrupted by the milieu. Ultimately, it is all void, empty! Oh! I confess I exaggerate, and I did forge friendships and experience fleeting joys. Yet, looking back, it feels like a singular nightmare. The seasonal gatherings in New York and London—the yachting parties, the cruises—all populated by the same faces, radiating the same hollowness."

"And observe," she continued, her gaze piercing, "your husband could not possibly comprehend this. His life was never barren. He navigated it through business strategies and difficulties, rarely venturing into society. When he did, his mind was occupied by affairs. He harbored no suspicion of my inner turmoil, and I never allowed him to guess; it would have been unjust. I felt compelled to justify my existence as his wife, to share his station and fortune. The only path forward was to strive, relentlessly, to embody the social qualities he envisioned. I endeavored. I played my part to the utmost. Yet, it grew harder year by year. I was never a popular hostess; how could I be? I was a failure. Still, I persevered. Occasionally, I stole away. I felt guilty, as if I were defaulting on a bargain—a thought that seems monstrous, yet it was the truth—whenever I departed with an old school friend, unable to afford travel, wandering Italy for months in poverty, or staying in London with quiet acquaintances who knew me from childhood.
