The Pennsylvania terminus in Jersey City was a vast, dimly lit cavern, the gas lamps casting a faint orange glow on the cold stone floor. Archer stood amidst the throng, his eyes fixed on the far wall as the Washington express lurched into the station. A vision of the future flickered in his mind – a tunnel beneath the Hudson, trains hurtling into New York, ships crossing the Atlantic in mere days. He smiled wryly to himself, recalling the fantastical predictions of his fellow dreamers. The notion of a tunnel, however, unsettled him. It would change everything.

As he paced the platform, his thoughts turned to Madame Olenska. He had meant to surprise her, to arrive in Washington and sweep her off her feet. But now, standing alone in the dim light, he felt a pang of uncertainty. What would she think of his spontaneity? Had he merely delayed the inevitable, or had he somehow managed to surprise her after all?

The train's whistle pierced the air, and Archer pushed forward, his elbows nudging aside the crowd. He scanned the windows of the high-hung carriages, searching for a glimpse of her pale face. And then, suddenly, he saw her – her eyes wide with surprise, her features etched in a delicate line. He felt a familiar flutter in his chest, a sense of having forgotten something essential.

They met, their hands touching, and he guided her through the crowd. "This way," he said, his voice low and steady. The world around them began to recede, and they fell into a rhythm, their footsteps synchronized as they made their way to the carriage.

The brougham, its dark blue paint still bearing the remnants of its wedding varnish, awaited them. Archer helped her in, her bags settling softly at her feet. He recalled the words he had meant to say, the reassurances he had planned to offer. But as he glanced at her, he saw only a faint smile, a fleeting glance before she turned away.

"If only it doesn't mean—poor Granny!" she whispered, her voice barely audible above the din of the station.

He smiled, attempting to reassure her, though his words felt hollow. "No, no, she's much better – she's all right, really." But as they lurched across the gangplank onto the ferry, he felt a pang of doubt. Had he truly believed those words, or had he simply spoken them to comfort her?
