Elinor found herself seated beside Mrs. Jennings in a carriage, embarking on a journey to London as her guest, yet she could not suppress a profound sense of wonderment at her own position. Their acquaintance had been so brief, their ages and temperaments so disparate, and her objections to such an arrangement so numerous just days prior! Yet, swept away by that youthful fervor shared by Marianne and her mother alike, those objections had been effortlessly dismissed. Despite lingering doubts regarding Willoughby's fidelity, Elinor watched Marianne's face—a vessel brimming with ecstatic anticipation—and felt a stark contrast in her own spirit. While Marianne radiated hope, Elinor's prospects seemed bleak and cheerless. In comparing their states, she realized how desperately she would trade her own emptiness for the animating hope that filled her sister's heart.

However, fate was poised to reveal Willoughby's true intentions within a mere handful of days; he was likely already in town. Marianne's impatience to depart confirmed her belief that he awaited her there. Consequently, Elinor resolved to pursue every available clue regarding his character through her own observation and the reports of others. She intended to scrutinize his behavior toward her sister with intense vigilance, aiming to discern his true nature and motives before many meetings occurred. If her findings proved unfavorable, she vowed to enlighten her sister immediately. Conversely, if the outlook remained promising, her efforts would shift: she must learn to suppress self-comparison and banish any regret that might dim her joy in Marianne's potential happiness.

The journey lasted three days. Marianne's demeanor during travel offered a glimpse of her future companionship with Mrs. Jennings: she sat in near-silence, lost in reverie, speaking only when a picturesque sight prompted an exclamatory address solely to her sister. To compensate, Elinor assumed the role of civility she had assigned herself. She attended to Mrs. Jennings with utmost care, engaging her in conversation, sharing laughter, and listening patiently whenever possible. In turn, Mrs. Jennings treated them with boundless kindness, fussing over their comfort and expressing only mild frustration that she could not dictate their meals or extract preferences between salmon and cod, or fowl and veal.

They arrived in London at three o'clock on the third day, eager to escape the carriage's confinement and bask in the warmth of a good fire. Their lodging was handsome and comfortably appointed; the young ladies were settled in a spacious apartment that had previously belonged to Charlotte. Above the mantelpiece hung a landscape painted in colored silks, a testament to Charlotte's seven years spent at a prestigious school in the city. With dinner delayed by two hours, Elinor decided to write to her mother and took her seat. Moments later, Marianne joined her.

"I am writing home, Marianne," Elinor remarked. "Would you not prefer to wait a day or two?"

"I am *not* going to write to my mother," Marianne replied sharply, clearly wishing to end the topic.

Elinor said nothing further, but the implication struck her instantly: Marianne must be writing to Willoughby. The conclusion that followed was unavoidable—they were engaged. Though this realization lacked total certainty, it brought Elinor a surge of pleasure, and she resumed her letter with renewed vigor. Marianne finished hers in moments; it was merely a note, folded, sealed, and directed with frantic speed. Elinor believed she could discern a large 'W' in the address. Before the task was complete, Marianne rang the bell and instructed the footman to send the letter via the two-penny post. The mystery was solved.

Marianne's spirits soared, yet a nervous flutter kept her expression from bringing true joy to her sister. This agitation intensified as evening approached. She barely touched her dinner and, upon returning to the drawing-room, seemed to listen intently to the clatter of every approaching carriage. Elinor was relieved that Mrs. Jennings, absorbed in her own room, witnessed little of the turmoil. When tea was served, Marianne had already been thwarted several times by knocks at neighboring doors. Then, a sharp, unmistakable rap echoed through the air. Elinor felt certain it signaled Willoughby's arrival. Marianne sprang up and hastened toward the door.

Silence reigned. Unable to endure it, she opened the door, stepped onto the landing, and listened for half a minute before retreating into the room, trembling with the conviction that she had heard him. Overcome by ecstasy, she cried out, "Oh, Elinor, it is Willoughby, indeed it is!" appearing ready to rush into his embrace, until Colonel Brandon appeared.

The blow was too severe to withstand calmly, and Marianne fled the room. Elinor felt a pang of disappointment, yet her respect for Colonel Brandon ensured his welcome. She was deeply wounded that a man so fond of her sister perceived only her grief and despair. Instantly recognizing that he noticed her distress as she left, observing her with such astonishment and concern that he barely remembered the courtesy due her, Elinor felt a fresh ache.

"Is your sister ill?" he asked.

Answering with evident distress, Elinor spoke of headaches, low spirits, and exhaustion, attributing Marianne's behavior to these ailments. He listened with earnest attention but, seemingly recalling himself, changed the subject to his pleasure at seeing them in London, asking standard questions about their journey and old acquaintances. Both women, lacking spirits and distracted by their respective thoughts, conversed in a calm, perfunctory manner.

Elinor longed to ask if Willoughby was in town but feared causing pain by inquiring about her rival. Finally, seeking conversation, she asked if he had been in London since their last meeting.

"Yes," he replied, with noticeable embarrassment, "almost ever since. I visited Delaford once or twice for a few days, but I have never been able to return to Barton."

These words, delivered with that specific tone, instantly resurrected the memory of his departure from that estate and the unease it had caused Mrs. Jennings. Elinor feared her question revealed far more curiosity than she possessed.

Soon, Mrs. Jennings entered. "Oh! Colonel," she exclaimed with her characteristic boisterous cheerfulness, "I am monstrously glad to see you—so sorry I couldn't come sooner! Pray forgive me, but I've been forced to tidy up and settle my affairs; it's been ages since I was home, and one always has a world of odd tasks after an absence. Besides, I've had Cartwright to deal with. Lord, I've been as busy as a bee since dinner! But pray, Colonel, how did you guess I'd be in town today?"

"I learned of it at Mr. Palmer's, where I dined."

"Oh, you did! And how are they all? How does Charlotte fare? I wager she's grown quite large by now."

"Mrs. Palmer looked perfectly well, and I'm sent to inform you that you shall certainly see her tomorrow."

"Ay, to be sure, I thought as much. Well, Colonel, I've brought two young ladies with me—you see, well, only one of them is here now, but there's another somewhere. Your friend, Miss Marianne, too—which you won't be sorry to hear. I don't know what you and Mr. Willoughby will manage between you regarding her. Ay, it's a fine thing to be young and handsome. Well! I was young once, but never very handsome—bad luck for me. However, I secured a very good husband, and I don't know what the fairest woman could achieve more. Ah! Poor man! He's been dead these eight years, and better. But Colonel, where have you been since we parted? And how fares your business? Come, come, let's share no secrets among friends."

He responded to her inquiries with his customary mildness, offering no clear answers. Elinor then prepared the tea, forcing Marianne to reappear. Once inside, Colonel Brandon grew increasingly thoughtful and silent, and Mrs. Jennings failed to persuade him to stay long. No other visitors arrived that evening, and the ladies unanimously agreed to retire early.

The following morning, Marianne rose with restored spirits and a radiant countenance. The previous night's disappointment vanished, replaced by the anticipation of the day's events. Hardly had breakfast concluded when Mrs. Palmer's carriage pulled up, and shortly thereafter, she burst into the room laughing. She was so delighted to see everyone that it was difficult to tell if she derived more pleasure from reuniting with her mother or the Dashwood sisters. Surprised by their visit to town, though she had suspected it, she feigned anger at their acceptance of her mother's invitation after rejecting her own, yet admitted she would never have forgiven them had they not come.

"Mr. Palmer will be so happy to see you," she said. "What do you suppose he said when he heard you were coming with Mama? I forget exactly, but it was something quite droll!"

After an hour or two of what her mother termed "comfortable chat"—essentially endless inquiries about mutual acquaintances mixed with baseless laughter from Mrs. Palmer—it was suggested that they all accompany her to some shops where she had business. Mrs. Jennings and Elinor readily agreed, needing purchases themselves, and Marianne, initially reluctant, was persuaded to join them.
