Friday evening found Newland Archer arriving at the Chiverses' estate, and Saturday unfolded with the meticulous, dutiful rhythm of a weekend at Highbank. He began the day with a brisk ride in the ice-boat alongside his hostess and the season's most spirited guests. By afternoon, he accompanied Reggie on a tour of the farm, spending hours in the opulent stables listening to grandiloquent lectures on equine excellence. Following tea, he found himself cornered in the fire-lit hall by a young woman who had once claimed her heart was shattered by his engagement, only to pivot eagerly to her own romantic prospects. As the clock struck midnight, the festivities escalated into absurdity: he helped place a goldfish in a guest's bed, dressed a nervous aunt's brother as a burglar in the bathroom, and joined a chaotic pillow fight that swept from the nurseries down to the basement. Yet, by Sunday lunch, the spell of social obligation broke. Borrowing a cutter, Archer drove across the snow to Skuytercliff.

The house at Skuytercliff was a legend, often mistaken for an Italian villa. Built by Mr. van der Luyden in his youth after his grand tour, it was a massive wooden structure painted pale green and white, featuring a Corinthian portico and fluted pilasters. From its high perch, terraces lined with urns cascaded like a steel engraving toward a small, irregular lake, while weedless lawns dotted with specimen trees rolled down to the hollow where the original Patroon's stone house sat. Against the bleak winter sky, the villa loomed with a grim majesty; even in summer, nature seemed afraid to approach its formidable front.

As Archer rang the bell, the sound echoed through a mausoleum. The butler's response was one of startled disbelief, as if summoned from the grave. Fortunately, Archer was of the family, and the butler quickly informed him that the Countess Olenska had departed for afternoon service with Mrs. van der Luyden just forty-five minutes prior. The butler suggested Mr. van der Luyden might be napping or reading the *Evening Post*, offering to eavesdrop in the library to confirm. Archer politely declined, thanking him and stepping out to meet the ladies, leaving the butler to close the door with a sigh of relief.

Striking out through the park, Archer kept to the high road, knowing Mrs. van der Luyden never walked. Soon, however, he spotted a slender figure in a vibrant red cloak, a large dog bounding ahead. He hurried forward, and Madame Olenska stopped, her face lighting up.

"Ah, you've come!" she exclaimed, pulling her hand from her muff.

The red cloak transformed her, making her appear gay and vivid, reminiscent of the Ellen Mingott of old. Archer laughed as he took her hand. "I came to see what you were running away from."

Her expression darkened instantly. "Ah, well—you will see, presently."

The ambiguity puzzled him. "Do you mean you've been overtaken?"

She shrugged with a gesture that recalled Nastasia, then lightened her tone. "Shall we walk? I'm freezing after the sermon. Besides, what does it matter now that you're here to protect me?"

Blood rushed to Archer's temples as he caught a fold of her cloak. "Ellen—what is it? You must tell me."

"Oh, presently! Let's run a race first; my feet are stuck to the ground," she cried, gathering her cloak and fleeing across the snow, the dog barking playfully. For a moment, Archer watched the flash of red against the white, delighted, before starting after her. They met at a wicket leading into the park, panting and laughing.

"I knew you'd come!" she smiled.

"That proves you wanted me to," he replied, his joy disproportionate to their nonsense. The white glitter of the trees filled the air with a mysterious brightness, and the ground seemed to sing beneath their feet.

"Where did you come from?" she asked.

He explained, adding, "Because I received your note."

After a pause, a chill touched her voice. "May asked you to take care of me."

"I didn't need any asking."

"You mean... I'm so evidently helpless and defenseless? What a poor thing you must all think me! But women here seem not—never—to feel the need: any more than the blessed in heaven."

He lowered his voice. "What sort of a need?"

"Ah, don't ask me! I don't speak your language," she retorted petulantly.

The words struck him like a physical blow. He stood still, looking down at her. "What did I come for, if I don't speak yours?"

"Oh, my friend—" She placed a light hand on his arm, and he pleaded earnestly, "Ellen—why won't you tell me what's happened?"

She shrugged again. "Does anything ever happen in heaven?"

Silence stretched between them as they walked. Finally, she spoke. "I will tell you—but where, where, where? One cannot be alone for a minute in that great seminary of a house, with doors wide open and servants constantly intruding with tea or logs or newspapers! Is there no corner in an American house for solitude? You are so shy, yet so public. I always feel as if I were in a convent—or on a stage before a dreadfully polite audience that never applauds."

"You don't like us!" Archer exclaimed.

They passed the house of the old Patroon, its squat walls and small windows glowing with the light of a fresh fire.

"Why—the house is open!" Archer said.

"No; only for today. I wanted to see it, and Mr. van der Luyden had the fire lit so we might stop here on our way back from church." She ran up the steps and tested the door. "It's still unlocked! Come in; we can have a quiet talk. Mrs. van der Luyden has gone to Rhinebeck, and we won't be missed for an hour."

Inside the narrow passage, Archer's spirits soared with an irrational leap. The homely house, with its shining panels and brasses, seemed magically created to receive them. In the kitchen, a bed of embers glowed beneath an iron pot hanging from an ancient crane. Rush-bottomed chairs faced each other across the tiled hearth, rows of Delft plates lining the shelves. Archer threw a log onto the fire.

Madame Olenska dropped her cloak and sat in a chair. Archer leaned against the chimney, watching her.

"You're laughing now, but when you wrote, you were unhappy," he observed.

"Yes." She paused. "But I cannot feel unhappy when you are here."

"I sha'n't be here long," he replied, his lips stiffening with the effort to say no more.

"No; I know. But I am improvident; I live in the moment when I am happy."

The words stole through him like a temptation. To close his senses to it, he moved away from the hearth, gazing out at the black tree trunks against the snow. Yet, it seemed she had shifted her position too, appearing between him and the trees, drooping over the fire with an indolent smile. Archer's heart beat insubordinately. What if she had been running away from him, waiting to confess only when they were alone in this secret room?

"Ellen, if I am truly a help to you—if you really wanted me to come—tell me what is wrong. Tell me what you are running from," he insisted.

He spoke without turning, keeping the whole width of the room between them, his eyes fixed on the outer snow.

For a long moment, she was silent. In that silence, Archer imagined her stealing up behind him to throw her arms around his neck. While he waited, soul and body throbbing with the miracle to come, his eyes mechanically registered the image of a heavily coated man with a fur collar advancing along the path. It was Julius Beaufort.

"Ah—" Archer cried, bursting into laughter.

Madame Olenska sprang up, moving to his side and slipping her hand into his. But after a glance through the window, her face paled, and she shrank back.

"So that was it?" Archer said derisively.

"I didn't know he was here," Madame Olenska murmured. Her hand still clung to his, but he drew away and walked out into the passage, throwing open the front door.

"Hallo, Beaufort—this way! Madame Olenska was expecting you," he announced.

During the journey back to New York the next morning, Archer relived his final moments at Skuytercliff with fatiguing vividness. Beaufort, though annoyed to find them together, had carried the situation with his usual high-handed ease. His ability to ignore inconvenient people gave them a strange sense of invisibility. Archer, aware of this disembodiment, felt a humbling ghostly advantage of observing unobserved.

Beaufort had entered the little house with easy assurance, though a vertical line marred his brow. It was clear Madame Olenska had not known he was coming, yet her words to Archer had hinted at the possibility. Her unexplained departure from New York had clearly exasperated him. Ostensibly, he had come because he had discovered a "perfect little house" the night before—a property that would vanish instantly if she didn't claim it. He loudly mocked the dance she had led him in, running away just as he found it.

"If only this new dodge for talking along a wire had been a little nearer perfection," he grumbled, disguising real irritation under pretense, "I might have told you all this from town and been toasting my toes before the club fire." At this, Madame Olenska twisted the conversation toward the fantastic possibility of conversing from street to street, or even town to town, invoking Edgar Poe and Jules Verne. These platitudes carried them safely back to the big house.

Mrs. van der Luyden had not yet returned. Archer took his leave to fetch the cutter, while Beaufort followed the Countess indoors. It was probable Beaufort expected an invitation to dinner and a ride back to the station, but it would be inconceivable to his hosts that a gentleman traveling without luggage wished to spend the night, and distasteful to them to propose it to someone with whom they held only limited cordiality. Beaufort knew this and must have foreseen it; his long journey for such a small reward measured his impatience.

He was undeniably in pursuit of the Countess Olenska, and Beaufort had only one object in view in his pursuit of pretty women. His dull, childless home had long palled on him, and he was always in quest of amorous adventures. This was the man from whom Madame Olenska was avowedly fleeing. The question remained: had she fled because his importunities displeased her, or because she did not wholly trust herself to resist them? Unless, indeed, all her talk of flight had been a blind, and her departure merely a maneuver.

Archer did not believe this. Though he had seen little of Madame Olenska, he was beginning to think he could read her face, and if not her face, her voice; both had betrayed annoyance and dismay at Beaufort's sudden appearance. But, after all, if this were the case, was it not worse than if she had left New York expressly to meet him? If she had done that, she ceased to be an object of interest, throwing her lot with the vulgarest of dissemblers: a woman engaged in a love affair with Beaufort "classed" herself irretrievably.

No, it was worse a thousand times if, judging Beaufort and probably despising him, she was yet drawn to him by all that gave him an advantage over other men: his habit of two continents and two societies, his association with artists and actors, and his careless contempt for local prejudices. Beaufort was vulgar, uneducated, and purse-proud; but the circumstances of his life and a certain native shrewdness made him better worth talking to than many men, morally and socially his betters, whose horizons were bounded by the Battery and Central Park. How should anyone coming from a wider world not feel the difference and be attracted by it?

Madame Olenska had told Archer in a burst of irritation that they did not speak the same language. The young man knew this was true in some respects. But Beaufort understood every turn of her dialect and spoke it fluently; his view of life, his tone, his attitude were merely a coarser reflection of those revealed in Count Olenski's letter. This might seem to be to his disadvantage with Count Olenski's wife, but Archer was too intelligent to think a young woman like Ellen Olenska would necessarily recoil from everything that reminded her of her past. She might believe herself wholly in revolt against it, but what had charmed her in it would still charm her, even against her will.

Thus, with painful impartiality, did the young man construct the case for Beaufort and for Beaufort's victim. A longing to enlighten her was strong in him; there were moments when he imagined all she asked was to be enlightened.

That evening, he unpacked his books from London. The box was full of things he had waited for impatiently: a new volume of Herbert Spencer, a collection of Alphonse Daudet's tales, and a novel called *Middlemarch*, recently praised in the reviews. He had declined three dinner invitations for this feast, yet though he turned the pages with the sensuous joy of a book-lover, he did not know what he was reading. One book after another dropped from his hand. Suddenly, among them, he lit on a small volume of verse he had ordered because the name attracted him: *The House of Life*. He took it up and found himself plunged in an atmosphere unlike any he had ever breathed in books—so warm, so rich, yet so ineffably tender, that it gave a new and haunting beauty to the most elementary of human passions. All through the night, he pursued through those enchanted pages the vision of a woman who had the face of Ellen Olenska.
