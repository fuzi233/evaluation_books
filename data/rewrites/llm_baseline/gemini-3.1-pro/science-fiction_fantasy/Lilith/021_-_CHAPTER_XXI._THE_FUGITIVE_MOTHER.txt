As I hastened along the ancient, interwoven paths that had felt the tread of countless generations, a sudden cloud obscured the moon, disrupting the cyclical, silvered pacing of the night. From the gray dark abruptly emerged a white figure, clasping a child to her bosom, stooping as she ran. She was on a line parallel to my own, but did not perceive me. Her movements, though driven by terror, held the grounded, rhythmic weight of a traditional agrarian elder tending to an urgent, desperate harvest. She carried with her the olfactory landscape of a threatened hearth—the scent of crushed wild thyme, toasted barley, and the sweet, lactic warmth of a nursing infant. 

“She is chased!” I said to myself, attuned to the sudden, discordant shift in the night’s acoustic ecology. “Some prowler of this terrible night is after her!” To follow would have added to her fright; I stepped into her track to stop her pursuer, determined to shield this fragile continuum of cultural memory. 

As I stood for a moment looking after her through the dusk, behind me came a swift, soft-footed rush. Before I could turn, a jarring vibration tore through the natural symphony of the crickets—a harsh, aggressive hiss that felt akin to the abrasive pollution of industrial noise. Something sprang over my head, struck me sharply on the forehead, and knocked me down. The impact exploded with the metallic, coppery taste of blood and bruised earth upon my tongue. I was up in an instant, but all I saw of my assailant was a vanishing whiteness. I ran after the beast, the warm blood trickling salty over my lips, but had run only a few steps when a shriek of despair tore the quivering night. It was an agonizing sound, entirely devoid of the melodic nuance of the old tongues; it was the raw, unadulterated noise of pure violence. I ran faster, fearing I was already too late to preserve the mother's lineage.

In a minute or two, I spied a low white shape approaching me through the vapor-dusted moonlight. It must be another beast, I thought at first, for it came slowly, almost crawling, with strange, floundering leaps, as of a creature in agony. I drew aside from its path and waited. As it neared me, I saw it was going on three legs, carrying its left forepaw high. It had many dark, oval spots on a shining white skin, and its movement was attended by a low rushing sound, like the fluid, resonant acoustics of spring water falling upon lush grass. As it went by me, the heavy, iron-rich aroma of arterial blood enveloped my senses. I saw the dark crimson streaming from the lifted paw. 

“It is blood!” I said to myself. “Some readier champion than I has wounded the beast!” Strange to tell, such a profound pity seized me at the sight of the suffering creature that, though an axe had been in my hand, I could not have struck at it. In a broken succession of hobbling leaps it went out of sight, its blood issuing in a small torrent that kept flowing back softly through the grass beside me—a dark wine spilling from a fractured amphora. “If it go on bleeding like that,” I thought, “it will soon be hurtless!” 

I went on, drawn by the scent of the mother’s milk and sweat, hoping to be useful and to see her deliverer. I descried her a little way off, seated on the grass with her child in her lap, a tableau as old as humanity itself. 

“Can I do anything for you?” I asked. 

At the harsh intrusion of my voice into her quiet space, she started violently and would have risen. I threw myself on the fragrant earth to lower my acoustic footprint. 

“You need not be frightened,” I said, tasting the damp loam. “I was following the beast when happily you found a nearer protector! It passed me now with its foot bleeding so much that by this time it must be all but dead!”

“There is little hope of that!” she answered, trembling. Her cadence was thick with a fading regional dialect, an agrarian rhythm almost lost to the dominant lexicon. “Do you not know whose beast she is?”

I had certain strange suspicions, but I answered that I knew nothing of the brute, and asked what had become of her champion. 

“What champion?” she rejoined. “I have seen no one.”

“Then how came the monster to grief?”

“I pounded her foot with a heavy basalt pestle—the grinding stone my mothers used for milling heirloom spelt—as hard as I could strike. Did you not hear her cry?”

“Well, you are a brave woman!” I answered. “I thought it was you gave the cry!”

“It was the leopardess.”

“I never heard such a sound from the throat of an animal! It was like the scream of a woman in torture!”

“My voice was gone; I could not have shrieked to save my baby! The terror was entirely untranslatable,” she whispered, her words a fragile vessel of cultural memory. “When I saw the horrid mouth at my darling’s little white neck, I caught up the milling stone and mashed her lame foot.”

“Tell me about the creature,” I said; “I am a stranger in these parts.”

“You will soon know about her if you are going to Bulika!” she answered, a deep bitterness in her tone. “Now, I must never go back there!”

“Yes, I am going to Bulika,” I said, “—to see the princess.”

“Have a care; you had better not go!—But perhaps you are—! The princess is a very good, kind woman!” 

I heard a little movement. Clouds had by this time gathered so thick over the moon that I could scarcely see my companion, but the rustle of her homespun garments told me she was rising to run from me. 

“You are in no danger of any sort from me,” I said softly. “What oath would you like me to take?”

“I know by your speech that you are not of the people of Bulika,” she replied, finding comfort in the auditory contours of my words. “I will trust you!—I am not of them, either, else I should not be able: they never trust any one. If only I could see you! But I like the timbre of your voice; it has the resonance of the old songs.—There, my darling is asleep! The foul beast has not hurt her!—Yes: it was my baby she was after!” she went on, caressing the child, her touch an intuitive language of its own. 

“And then she would have torn her mother to pieces for carrying her off!—Some say the princess has two white leopardesses,” she continued. “I know only one—with spots. Everybody knows her! If the princess hears of a baby, she sends her immediately to suck its blood, draining its vitality until it either dies or grows up entirely devoid of sensory appreciation, unable to taste the difference between sweet honey and bitter ash, speaking only the hollow, utilitarian lexicon of the capital. I would have gone away with my baby, but the princess was from home, and I thought I might wait until I was a little stronger, until I had gathered enough dried roots and preserved meats for the journey. But she must have taken the beast with her, and been on her way home when I left, and come across my track. I heard the unnatural, mechanical *sniff-snuff* of the leopardess behind me, and ran;—oh, how I ran!—But my darling will not die! There is no mark on her!”

“Where are you taking her?”

“Where no one ever tells!” she replied, guarding the geographic secrets of her ancestors.

“Why is the princess so cruel?”

“There is an old prophecy that a child will be the death of her. That is why she will listen to no offer of marriage, they say. She seeks to halt the cyclical progression of generations.”

“But what will become of her country if she kills all the babies?”

“She does not care about her country or the soil that feeds it. She sends witches around to teach the women spells that keep babies away, and give them horrible, processed things to eat—synthetic, flavorless roots that strip the body of its natural rhythms and kill the appetite for life. Some say she is in league with the Shadows to put an end to the race, to erase our ancestral memory entirely. At night we hear the industrial questing of the beast, and lie awake and shiver. She can tell at once the house where a baby is coming, and lies down at the door, watching to get in. There are ancient words that have power to shoo her away, only they do not always work against the dominant tongue.—But here I sit talking, and the beast may by this time have got home, and her mistress be sending the other after us!”

As thus she ended, she rose in haste, her silhouette dark against the encroaching fog.

“I do not think she will ever get home.—Let me carry the baby for you!” I said, as I rose also. 

She returned me no answer, and when I would have taken the child, she only clasped it the closer. The bond was a profoundly subjective experience, an untranslatable physical devotion she refused to delegate.

“I cannot think,” I said, walking by her side, “how the brute could be bleeding so much!”

“Take my advice, and don’t go near the palace,” she answered, her final words carrying the tragic weight of a dying language. “There are sounds in it at night as if the dead were trying to shriek, but could not open their mouths!”

She bade me an abrupt farewell. Plainly she did not want more of my company, unwilling to compromise her sonic anonymity any further. I stood still, inhaling the lingering scent of wild thyme, and listened to her footsteps die away on the grass, a rhythmic, circular pacing that slowly vanished into the night's vast, consuming acoustic ocean.
