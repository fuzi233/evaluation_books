“John Carter—John Carter,” Dejah Thoris sobbed, her dear head pressed to my shoulder, the faint, familiar sandalwood of her Barsoomian hair tangled against the tattered crimson of my warrior tunic. Even now I can scarce believe my own eyes, she told me. When Thuvia of Ptarth first babbled that you had somehow crossed the gulf of space back to Barsoom, I could not let myself hope. Too many long cycles of silent, lonely imprisonment had worn away every last scrap of faith that happiness could ever find me again. When I finally accepted the news was true, I thought even then that no man, not even you, could ever breach the hidden, guarded walls of the Temple of Issus to reach me.

Moon after moon drifted by, no rumour of rescue reaching my cell, and I resigned myself to being a plaything for the mad false goddess forever. For an hour before you burst through that door, I heard the clash of fighting echo through the halls. I did not know what it meant, but I clung to the faintest, foolish hope that the men of Helium were coming, led by their prince. Tell me—what of our son, Carthoris?”

“He fought beside me less than an hour ago, my Princess,” I answered, pressing my lips to her forehead. “It was his warriors you heard cutting their way through the temple precincts. Where is Issus now?”

Dejah Thoris’s shoulders lifted in a weary, bitter shrug. “She dragged me under guard to this room right as the first fighting broke out, saying she would send for me later. She was shaking, John Carter—wild with anger and raw, unmasked fear. In half a year of watching her cruel, capricious reign, I never saw her act so unhinged. Now I know she learned you were coming to call her to account for stealing Helium’s princess.”

The din of battle rolled through the halls: the clash of obsidian blades against steel, the war cries of red Heliumites, the thunder of booted feet over polished marble. I knew my men needed me on the front lines, but I could not bring myself to drag Dejah Thoris into that roiling, bloody chaos, and I could not bear to leave her unguarded out in the open. Then I thought of the shadowed pits I had just climbed out of, hidden away from the main paths of the temple. I told her my plan to tuck her there until the fighting ended, so I could come back for her and carry her away from this cursed place forever.

For a heartbeat, she clung tighter to me. “I cannot bear to be parted from you, even for a single second,” she whispered. “I shudder to think of being alone here, where that monster Issus could find me. You have no idea of the cruelty she has shown, day in and day out, for half a year. No one who has not seen it for themselves could believe it possible.”

“Then I will not leave you at all,” I said, and began to turn toward the door to join the fighting with her at my side.

She was silent for a long moment, then she pulled my face down to hers and kissed me. “Go, John Carter,” she said, her voice steady even as her eyes glistened. “Our son is out there, fighting for the Princess of Helium beside our soldiers. Your duty is with them. I will not stand in the way of what you must do. Hide me in the pits, then go.”

I led her to the low door leading down to the tunnels below. I crushed her to my chest for one long, desperate moment, my gut twisted with a terrible, unplaceable foreboding, then guided her across the threshold, kissed her one last time, and pulled the heavy door shut behind her.

I did not hesitate a second longer, sprinting down the halls toward the loudest roar of battle. I had barely crossed six chambers before I rounded a corner and found the full force of the Black First Born massed at the mouth of a great hall, blocking the advance of a column of red warriors pushing toward Issus’s inner sacred precincts. Emerging from the tunnels as I did, I found myself directly at their rear. I did not stop to count their numbers, or consider how foolhardy it was for one man to charge a hundred armed black warriors alone. I drew my long steel sword, screamed “For Helium!”, and cut through their ranks before they even knew I was there.

Blow after blow rained down on their unprotected backs. The red men outside heard my shout, roared my name so loud the walls shook, and redoubled their assault so fiercely that the black ranks shattered in minutes, letting the Heliumites pour into the chamber. That fight would go down in the annals of Barsoom as one of the grimmest tests of her warlike people’s ferocity—five hundred men, red against black, no quarter asked, no quarter given, as if they had all agreed to fight to the death to settle once and for all whether the cruel old superstition of Issus would outlive the outer world’s advance. The floor ran ankle-deep in Barsoom’s crimson blood, dead bodies piled so thick that half the time we fought standing on the corpses of the fallen.

When we surged toward the great windows overlooking Issus’s walled gardens, a sight hit me that sent a wave of wild exultation surging through my chest. “Look!” I shouted. “Men of the First Born, look!”

Fighting stopped dead. Every head turned, and every pair of eyes stared at a sight no black warrior of Issus had ever dreamed they would see: a wavering line of black soldiers being slowly forced back across the gardens by a seething horde of green Thark warriors, mounted atop their massive, twenty-foot thoats. At their head, fiercer and grimmer than any warrior there, rode Tars Tarkas, Jeddak of Thark, roaring a command to his legion. He couched his forty-foot, metal-shod lance, and every green warrior behind him did the same.

Twenty yards separated the green horde from the black line. Tars Tarkas shouted once more, and the green men charged with a wild, earth-shaking battle cry. The black line held for no more than a heartbeat before the great thoats and their terrible riders tore straight through their ranks, shattering them completely. Utan after utan of red men poured through the breach behind the green horde, the green warriors fanning out to secure the outer gardens, the red men surging inward to clear the temple halls. Our remaining black defenders had vanished entirely.

My first thought was Dejah Thoris. I called over my shoulder to Carthoris that I had found his mother, then broke into a run toward the chamber I had left her in, my son hot on my heels, our surviving warriors trailing after us. The second I stepped through the chamber door, I knew someone had been there after I left. A scrap of her favourite silk sash lay crumpled on the floor, a heavy ceremonial dagger and half a dozen of her royal metal ornaments scattered across the marble as if torn from her in a struggle. Worst of all, the heavy door leading down to the pits where I had hidden her was standing ajar.

I lunged for it, threw it open, and rushed down the tunnels. Dejah Thoris was gone. I screamed her name over and over, no answer coming back but the echo of my own voice. For a second I teetered on the edge of madness, a wild, maniacal rage burning through every vein in my body. “Issus!” I roared. “Find Issus! No man harms her but John Carter. Carthoris—show me the way to her private apartments, now.”

Carthoris did not wait to confirm I had heard him, sprinting full tilt deeper into the temple’s stone bowels. I kept pace right beside him, pushing him to run faster, until we skidded to a stop before a great carved sandalwood door. Carthoris burst through a step ahead of me, and we found the throne room of Issus laid out before us just as I had seen it months prior: the goddess’s reclining slave attendants, the ranks of her royal guard lining the walls, the great sorapus wood dais holding her gilded throne. We gave them no time to draw their weapons. My first swing of the sword cut down two of the front rank guards, and the weight of my charge carried me straight through the rest of the ranks to leap onto the dais right beside Issus’s throne.

The repulsive old false goddess squatted there, wild with terror, and tried to dart around me to dive into the hidden trapdoor behind her throne. This time I would not let her escape. Before she could get halfway to the door, I had her by the wrist, my dagger pressed hard against her foul, shrivelled breast. “Back!” I screamed at the guards, who were surging toward the dais. “The first black man who steps foot on this platform dies before I drive this blade through Issus’s heart.”

They hesitated. An officer barked an order to fall back, just as a thousand red warriors under Kantos Kan, Hor Vastus, and Xodar the former First Born high priest poured into the throne room behind us.

“Where is Dejah Thoris?” I snarled, shaking the old crone in my grip.

Her eyes rolled wildly in her head. It took her long seconds to process that her temple had fallen, that the men she had claimed were all condemned to eternal joyless slavery in her afterlife had broken down her walls. The realization of what that meant hit her like a blow—her power gone, her fraud exposed, all the eons of lies crumbling to dust around her. Then the highest priest of her old cult stepped forward, his voice shaking. “Issus, Goddess of Death and Life Eternal!” he shrieked. “Rise in your wrath, smite these blasphemers dead with one wave of your omnipotent hand! You are all-powerful—save your people!”

That was the final snap. The false goddess went completely, raving mad. She shrieked and gibbered in my grip, biting and clawing at my arms, then let loose a weird, unearthly cackle that turned my blood cold. She lunged at her terrified slave girls, gnashing her teeth, froth dripping down her chin. I shook her hard, trying to yank her back to lucidity long enough to get an answer. “Where is Dejah Thoris?” I screamed again.

A sudden flicker of cunning sparked in her close-set, hideous eyes. “Dejah Thoris?” she cackled. “And Thuvia, and Phaidor, daughter of Matai Shang! All three of them love John Carter, isn’t it funny? They’re locked inside the Temple of the Sun, to meditate for a full year. When the food runs out, they’ll have nothing left to eat but each other. Ha-ha! Divine entertainment, isn’t it?”

The horror of it nearly paralyzed me. I shook her like a terrier shakes a rat, my rage so fierce I could barely speak. “Countermand the order! Call them back, or you die right now.”

“It’s too late!” she screamed, dissolving back into cackling gibberish.

My dagger flew up over her chest of its own accord, but I held back. Killing a raving mad old woman with my own hands would have fouled my soul forever. I had a far fitter fate for her. “Men of the First Born,” I called out to the black warriors watching, “you have seen Issus is no god. She is a cruel, lying old wretch who has deceived you for ages. John Carter of Helium will not sully his hands with her blood. She is yours.” I shoved the raving creature off the dais, right into the waiting clutches of the people she had betrayed and tormented for centuries.

I spotted Xodar in the crowd of red officers, and called him over to lead me to the Temple of the Sun immediately. We did not stop to see what vengeance the First Born visited on their fallen goddess, sprinting out of the throne room with Xodar, Carthoris, Kantos Kan, Hor Vastus, and a score of Helium’s finest nobles at my heels. Xodar led us through winding inner passages until we burst out into a vast circular central courtyard, paved with translucent, pure white marble, the great golden Temple of the Sun rising before us. Its walls were inlaid with diamonds, rubies, sapphires, and the thousands of rare, iridescent Barsoomian gems that outshine every precious stone on Earth, glinting in the torchlight.

“This way!” Xodar shouted, leading us to a dark tunnel mouth set right beside the temple’s gilded base. We were about to climb down when a deep, thunderous roar burst out from the direction of Issus’s main temple, and a young padwar named Djor Kantos came sprinting through the courtyard gate screaming for us to stop. “The blacks have set fire to the temple!” he yelled. “It’s burning in a thousand different places! If you don’t get back to the outer gardens now, you’ll all burn alive.”

Smoke was already pouring from a dozen windows overlooking the courtyard, a great roiling black pall rising over the temple’s highest minarets. “All of you go back!” I ordered my warriors. “Xodar—show me the way into the Temple of the Sun tunnels, then leave me. I am not leaving here without my Princess.”

“Follow me!” Xodar shouted, already sprinting down the tunnel. I ran at his heels through half a dozen tiers of galleries, until we reached a flat, straight corridor at the end of which I could see a lit chamber blocked off by thick iron bars. Beyond the bars, I saw her: my Dejah Thoris, standing beside Thuvia and Phaidor. When she saw me, she ran straight for the bars separating us.

The great rotating chamber of the Temple of the Sun had already turned so that only a narrow sliver of its outer opening lined up with the corridor’s end. The gap was shrinking faster by the second. Within hours it would close entirely, and the chamber would slowly, silently revolve for 687 long Barsoomian days before the aperture would line up with the corridor once more. A full year locked inside, no way in or out, for the three women inside.

“Xodar!” I shouted. “Is there no way to stop this terrible turning mechanism? No one knows how to open these bars fast enough?”

“The keeper of the keys burned to death in the fires above, his body lost under tons of falling rubble,” Xodar answered. “But I will try to find another way. Wait here for me.”

While he was gone, I pressed my face to the bars, holding Dejah Thoris’s hand as she stretched it through the shrinking gap. Thuvia stepped back to the far side of the small chamber to give us privacy, but Phaidor, the Princess of the Yellow Martian city of Zodanga, hung back a few feet away from us. “John Carter,” she said, her voice soft and sharp with longing, “this is the last time you will ever see any of us. Tell me you love me, so that I may die happy.”

“I love only Dejah Thoris, Princess of Helium,” I answered gently. “I have told you that from the very beginning. I am sorry.”

She bit her lip hard, turned away, and I caught a glimpse of a black, ugly scowl twisting her beautiful face. For a few minutes more we talked in low, quiet tones, the gap between the bars shrinking smaller and smaller. Smoke began curling down the corridor, thick and acrid, the distant roar of the burning temple growing louder behind us. Then our warriors came sprinting down the tunnel toward us, coughing, the smoke so thick we could barely see ten feet in front of our faces.

“There is no other way, John Carter!” Carthoris yelled, grabbing my arm. “The pits are flooding right now. If we don’t leave in three minutes, we will all drown or burn alive.” Xodar had tossed half a dozen sealed tins of concentrated rations through the narrow gap to the three women inside the chamber. The opening was barely an inch wide now. Dejah Thoris was pressed as close to it as she could get, whispering for me to save myself.

Then I saw Phaidor, behind her, her face twisted with unbridled hatred. “You will never hold Dejah Thoris in your arms again!” she shrieked, raising a sharp dagger high over her head. “When the year ends, it will be my arms that welcome you, not hers!”

Before I could scream a warning, Thuvia lunged between them. A great billow of smoke blotted out the chamber completely. A single, high shriek cut through the roar of the fire. When the smoke cleared, the gap was gone. A blank, seamless gilded wall faced us. The chamber had turned fully sealed.

My men dragged me away, fighting and screaming in my madness, half-conscious as they carried me up the tunnels and out into the gardens to safety. I do not remember the rest of that day, and no one who was there has ever spoken of it to me, for fear of reopening the terrible wound in my heart.

Ah, if I could only know one thing! Which woman did that dagger find, in that last split second before the door slammed shut? Only time, the long, slow year of waiting ahead, will ever tell.
