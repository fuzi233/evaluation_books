"Rise, my daughter," Fra Girolamo commanded finally, his voice cutting through the heavy silence. "Your servant waits with the mules not far off. It is time I press onward toward Florence."

Romola rose from her knees. That posture of absolute stillness had functioned as a sacrament, sealing her entry into a state of yearning passivity. By the single act of renouncing her resolve to flee her husband, her will had been shattered so thoroughly that she required direction even for trivialities. She lifted the edge of her cowl, revealing her face, and spotted Maso and the second Dominican standing with their backs to her on the hill's precipice, roughly ten yards distant. Yet she fixed her gaze upon Savonarola once more, remaining silent, as if the command to recall Maso must originate solely from him, not from her own diminished agency.

"I shall go and summon them," he answered her look of quiet desperation. "And I commend you, my daughter, to the Brother accompanying me. You seek to submit yourself to guidance and acquire that wisdom which has previously appeared as folly to you. A primary gateway to this wisdom is the sacrament of confession. You require a confessor, my daughter, and I intend to place you under the care of Fra Salvestro, one of the brethren of San Marco, in whom I place my utmost confidence."

"I would prefer no guidance save your own, Father," Romola replied, anxiety tightening her features.

"My daughter, I do not act as your confessor. My vocation withdraws me from offices necessitating frequent contact with the laity, interfering with my specific duties."

"Then shall I not be permitted to speak to you privately? Should I falter, should—" Romola broke off, overcome by a sudden surge of agitation. A primal alarm seized her: the fear that the nascent strength born of her renunciation would evaporate the moment Savonarola's direct personal influence receded.

"My daughter, if your soul requires the spoken word from my lips in private, inform me through Fra Salvestro, and I shall meet you in the sacristy or the choir of San Marco. I shall not cease to watch over you. I will instruct my brother regarding you, directing him to lead you onto the path of labor for the suffering and the hungry—a calling to which you are destined as a daughter of Florence in these times of dire necessity. I wish to behold you among the weaker and less learned sisters as the apple tree stands among the forest trees, so that your beauty and all your natural gifts serve merely as a lamp through which the Divine light shines with greater purity. I shall now depart to summon your servant."

Once Maso had been dispatched ahead, Fra Salvestro stepped forward, and Savonarola guided Romola toward him. She had anticipated an internal recoil from a new guide entirely alien to her; however, to resist Savonarola's counsel at this juncture would have been to project an attitude of independence precisely when every ounce of her strength depended on the renunciation of such autonomy. Her entire mental orientation was now directed toward performing what was painful rather than what was easy. She bowed reverently to Fra Salvestro before meeting his gaze directly; yet when she raised her head and beheld him fully, her hesitation crystallized into a palpable doubt.

There exist men whose mere presence instills trust and reverence; then there are those to whom we must transport our trust and reverence, forged and ready-made. This distinction struck Romola as the connection to Savonarola severed and Fra Salvestro Maruffi filled the void. It was not that Fra Salvestro possessed anything manifestly repulsive in his visage or demeanor—no hint of hypocrisy, no tinge of coarseness. His face was actually more handsome than Fra Girolamo's, his stature slightly taller. He served as the long-standing confessor to many of Florence's leading figures, possessing extensive experience as a spiritual director. Yet his countenance bore the vacillating expression of a mind incapable of concentrating intensely within the channel of a single great emotion or belief—an expression fatal to influencing an ardent nature like Romola's.

Such an expression is not necessarily the mark of insincerity; rather, it stamps a shallow soul. One often finds such individuals sincerely striving to fulfill a lofty vocation, earnestly composing their faces to utter sublime formulas, only to find their muscles twitch or relax in defiance of their beliefs, much as prose intrudes where poetry belongs in the mind lacking divine frenzy. Fra Salvestro suffered a peculiar susceptibility to visions, seemingly dependent on a constitution prone to somnambulism. Savonarola accepted the supernatural character of these visions, whereas Fra Salvestro had initially resisted such interpretations, even rebuking Savonarola for his prophetic preaching. Another proof, if evidence were needed, that the relative magnitude of men cannot be gauged by their propensity to dismiss the superstitions of their age. Between these two, there could be no ambiguity regarding which was the great man and which the small. The disparity was measured accurately by the shift in Romola's feelings as Fra Salvestro began addressing her with words of exhortation and encouragement.

After her initial angry resistance to Savonarola had subsided, she had forgotten the ancient dread that any influence might drag her into the circle of fanaticism and sour, monkish piety. But now, once again, the chilling breath of that terror stole over her. It held no decisive power against the momentum her mind had just acquired; it was akin to the gathering of grey clouds over a sunrise, rendering her return path monotonous and sombre. Perhaps of all sombre journeys, the one undertaken after traversing a path with strong resolution is the one that most severely tests the fervor of renunciation.

As they re-entered the city gates, light snowflakes descended around them. As the grey-clad sister hastened homeward from the Piazza di San Marco, crossing the bridge again and turning into the grand doorway of the Via de' Bardi, her footsteps left dark impressions on the thin carpet of snow, and her cowl fell laden and damp across her face. She ascended to her chamber, cast aside her serge habit, destroyed the parting letters, restored all her precious trifles to their places, loosened her hair, and donned her customary black dress. Instead of undertaking a long, exhilarating journey, she was to sit in her accustomed spot. Snow battered against the windows, and she was alone. She felt the dreariness, yet her courage remained high, reminiscent of a seeker who has discovered new signs of gold. She intended to navigate life by a fresh clue. She had poured the entirety of her will's energy into renunciation. The empty tabernacle remained locked, and she placed Dino's crucifix outside it. Nothing disrupted the outward monotony of her solitary existence until night arrived like a white ghost at the windows. Nevertheless, for Romola, this Christmas Eve of 1494 proved to be the most memorable of her life.
