II. A Guest

It is not often that one finds oneself confronted with a tale so extraordinary that it challenges the very limits of belief—yet I speak now of something I witnessed with my own eyes, and which, though strange beyond imagining, is no fiction. It was a summer evening, golden and still, when my father invited me to walk with him along the forest path that stretches before our schloss, a stretch of woodland so beautiful it seemed almost enchanted.

As we strolled beneath the tall trees, he told me that General Spielsdorf would not arrive as soon as he had hoped. The general had been expected the next day, bringing with him his niece and ward, Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt—a young woman I had never met but whom I had heard described as bright, graceful, and full of charm. Her presence had become the centerpiece of my quiet dreams for weeks; I had looked forward to her arrival with all the eagerness of youth confined by solitude and routine. Now, that hope was dashed.

“Not until autumn,” my father said. “Perhaps two months from now.” He paused, then added softly, “And I am glad, dear, that you never knew Mademoiselle Rheinfeldt.”

I turned to him, startled. “Why?” I asked, my heart suddenly heavy.

“Because,” he replied, “she is dead.”

The words struck me like a blow. I had known she had been unwell—General Spielsdorf had mentioned it in a letter some weeks prior—but nothing had hinted at such a fate. I took the letter from my father’s hand. His handwriting trembled on the page, each line more frantic than the last.

“I have lost my darling daughter,” it began. “For as such I loved her. During the last days of dear Bertha’s illness, I could not write. Before then, I had no idea of her danger. She died in peace, in innocence, in the hope of eternal life. But the fiend who betrayed our hospitality—our trust—has done this. I thought I was welcoming virtue, joy, a companion for my lost Bertha. Heaven! What a fool I have been! I thank God she died without knowing the truth. She did not suspect what plagued her, nor the cursed passion of the creature responsible. I swear I will hunt down and destroy this monster. I am told there may be hope. For now, there is only darkness. I curse my pride, my blindness, my refusal to believe. Too late. I cannot think or speak clearly. I am undone. As soon as I recover, I shall begin my search—perhaps as far as Vienna. In autumn, two months hence, or earlier if I live, I will come to you—if you allow it. Then I shall tell you all that I dare not write now. Farewell. Pray for me, dear friend.”

I read it twice, then aloud to my father. The letter was a storm of grief and fury, torn between mourning and vengeance. Yet even as I listened, I felt the weight of something deeper—something unnatural—hanging over the words.

By the time I returned the letter, twilight had fallen. The sun had dipped behind the trees, leaving the sky streaked with crimson and violet, and the moon rose above the treetops, casting silver light across the valley. We walked slowly, the silence thick with unease.

At the drawbridge, we met Madame Perrodon and Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, both out without bonnets, drawn by the beauty of the night. Their voices floated ahead of us, animated and poetic. They stood beside us, admiring the scene—the river winding through the mist, the bridge arched over the water, the ruins of an old tower silhouetted against the sky.

Madame Perrodon sighed dramatically. “Ah, how the moonlight sparkles on the windows of the schloss! Like fairy lights!”

Mademoiselle De Lafontaine, ever the mystic, leaned forward. “It is no ordinary moon tonight. Such brilliance—it is said to stir spirits, to awaken hidden forces. It affects dreams, madness, the nervous mind. Once, my cousin, sleeping on deck under such a moon, dreamed of an old woman clawing his face. When he woke, his features were twisted—never fully restored.”

She gestured toward the castle. “Look—see how the moon makes the windows gleam? As if unseen hands lit them for guests from another world.”

I smiled faintly, though my thoughts lingered on the letter. My father broke the silence.

“I feel… restless,” he murmured, quoting Shakespeare. “‘In truth I know not why I am so sad.’ It wearies me. I suppose it’s the general’s letter. Something terrible seems to hang over us.”

Just then, a sound cut through the quiet—hooves pounding on stone, wheels rolling fast. From the high ground above the bridge came a carriage, four horses, two riders ahead, two behind. It was a noble equipage, unmistakably of rank. We watched, transfixed.

Then, as the carriage crested the bridge, one horse reared, panicked. The others followed. The team exploded into a wild gallop, thundering down the road toward us, tossing the carriage like a leaf in a storm. From the window came a scream—long, clear, agonized.

We rushed forward. The horses, terrified, veered sharply at the lime tree by the roadside. The wheel caught the roots. I closed my eyes. A cry pierced the air.

When I opened them, chaos unfolded. Two horses lay on the ground. The carriage lay on its side, wheels spinning in the air. Men scrambled to untangle the harness. And from the wreckage, a woman emerged—tall, commanding, dressed in black velvet. Her hands were clasped, her face pale, yet her eyes burned with intensity.

Through the door, they lifted a young girl—slender, still, her skin unnaturally pale. She seemed unconscious, perhaps worse.

My father hurried to the woman, hat in hand. “Madame, I offer my home, my care—anything you need.”

The woman looked up, her voice trembling. “I must go. This journey is one of life and death. Every hour counts. My child cannot continue. I must leave her. How far is the nearest village?”

I stepped forward, heart racing. “Father, please—ask her to let her stay here! With us! It would be wonderful!”

He turned to the woman. “If you would entrust your daughter to my daughter and Madame Perrodon, and allow her to remain under my protection until your return, it would be a great honor. We would care for her as if she were our own.”

The woman hesitated. “That would be too much kindness,” she said, voice breaking. “Too much.”

“But you have just suffered a cruel disappointment,” my father insisted. “Your daughter has lost a cherished visit. If she stays with us, it may ease her sorrow. The nearest village offers no proper lodging. She cannot travel further tonight. You must part with her—and nowhere would she be safer, more cared for, than here.”

She studied him, then nodded slowly. There was a dignity about her—so regal, so composed—that I could not doubt her importance.

The carriage was righted. The horses calmed. She cast a glance at her daughter—brief, cold, almost distant. Then she beckoned my father aside. They spoke in hushed tones, her face stern, her expression hard.

I watched, puzzled. Why did she seem so detached? Why the sudden change?

After a few moments, she returned. Kneeling beside her daughter, she whispered something—Madame Perrodon thought a blessing. Then she kissed her quickly, climbed into the carriage, and the doors shut.

The footmen leapt into place. The outriders spurred their horses. The postilions cracked their whips. The carriage surged forward, racing away into the night, followed by the two riders.

And as it vanished into the shadows, I could not shake the feeling that something was terribly wrong.

The girl—still unconscious—was laid gently on the bank. Her breath was shallow. Her lips, slightly parted, revealed a hint of blood.

But as I looked at her, I saw something else—something I could not name.

Her eyes, though closed, seemed to follow me.

And in the silence after the carriage left, the moon shone brighter than ever.
