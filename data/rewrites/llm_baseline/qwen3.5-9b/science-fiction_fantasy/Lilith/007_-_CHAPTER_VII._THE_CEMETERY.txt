The air inside felt less like a room and more like the interior of an ice-house. As I crossed the threshold, the heavy door swung shut behind us with a finality that sealed off the world outside.

"Something to say to his wife," the sexton muttered, turning toward her. "Look at the change."

And truly, a transformation had taken root within her. It was as though the brilliance of her eyes had become too intense to contain, sinking inward to ignite her entire countenance with a radiance reminiscent of Beatrice amidst a white rose of redemption. From her emanated a luminosity—life itself, eternal and immortal—that flowed like unbroken lightning. Even her hands seemed to glow, every pearl-shell helmet gleaming with the soft luminescence of a moonstone. Her beauty was so overwhelming that I was relieved when she finally turned her gaze away from me. Yet, the candle's feeble light barely penetrated the gloom, leaving the vast expanse before us shrouded in mystery.

Then, the beam caught something glimmering slightly above the floor. Was it a bed? Could any living creature endure such mortal cold while sleeping? Perhaps that was precisely why none stirred. Beyond that faint point, other glimmers emerged, scattered and uncertain. A few steps brought us to the first object: a human form beneath a sheet, rigid and motionless. Whether male or female, the light seemed to shy away from the face as we passed. Soon, I realized we were traversing an aisle lined with countless couches. Upon almost every one, heads resting against the passage, lay figures asleep—or perhaps dead—all draped in sheets as white as fresh snow.

A profound silence settled over my soul, heavy with dread. Aisle after aisle stretched out before us, filled with innumerable couches. I could only glimpse a handful at a time, yet they surrounded me on all sides, vanishing into an infinity that defied comprehension. *Is this where I choose my bed?* I wondered, panic rising. *Must I sleep among the unwaking, with no one to rouse me? Is this the sexton's library, and are these his books?* This was certainly no halfway house; it was a chamber of the dead.

"One of the cellars I am charged to watch!" Mr. Raven whispered, his voice low, as if fearing to disturb his silent guests. "Much wine is set here to ripen... but it is dark for a stranger!" he added.

"The moon is rising," his wife replied, her clear, sweet voice sounding like ancient sorrow finally released. "She will be here soon."

As she spoke, the moon peered through an opening in the wall, and a thousand pale gleams responded to her presence. Still, the extent of the couches remained elusive. They stretched away endlessly, as if providing beds for the dispersed world. Along the narrowing paths receding into the distance, each couch stood solitary, occupied by a lone sleeper. Initially, I mistook their slumber for death, but quickly discerned something deeper—a state unknown to me.

The moon climbed higher, casting its light through other apertures, yet the sheer scale of the place remained obscured. Now it resembled a long cathedral nave; moments later, a massive barn converted into a dwelling of tombs. The moon appeared colder than any frosty night in our world, casting a bluish, icy sheen upon the white sheets and pallid faces. Or perhaps it was the faces themselves that chilled the moonlight. Among those visible, all shared a brotherhood of death, yet each bore a unique history etched upon their features.

Here lay a man who had perished in the prime of his strength. Though this was not death—I lacked a better term—his dark beard flowed like a liberated stream from the glacier of his frozen visage. His forehead was smooth as polished marble, and a lingering shadow of pain touched his lips, though only a shadow. Beside him rested the form of a girl, passing lovely to behold. The sadness of departure had not yet dissolved into perfect peace, yet absolute submission held her placid features. There was no sign of wasting disease or crushing grief; if pain existed, it had been charmed into eternal sleep.

Many beautiful souls lay very still—some mere children—but I saw not a single infant. The most striking was a lady whose white hair alone marked her age when she first fell asleep. Upon her stately countenance rested not submission, but a noble acquiescence, an assurance as firm as the universe's foundations that all was as it should be. Some faces bore the faint scars of strife, the marks of hopeless loss, the fading shadows of sorrows that once seemed inconsolable. The dawn of the great morning had not yet fully melted them away. Yet, these were few. Every face bearing such a brand of pain seemed to plead, *"Pardon me: I died only yesterday!"* or *"Pardon me: I died but a century ago!"*

Others had been gone for ages, known not merely by their unutterable repose, but by a quality for which I possess no word or symbol.

We arrived eventually at three empty couches. Immediately beyond them lay the form of a beautiful woman, just past the height of her life. One arm lay outside the sheet, her hand palm-upward, centered around a dark spot. Beside her stood the stalwart figure of a middle-aged man. His arm also protruded, his strong hand nearly closed, as if gripping the hilt of a sword. He must have been a king who died fighting for the truth.

"Shall I bring the candle closer, wife?" the sexton whispered, leaning down to inspect the woman's hand.

"It heals well," he murmured to himself. "The nail found in her caused no harm!"

Finally, I dared to speak. "Are they not dead?" I asked softly.

"I cannot answer you," he replied in a subdued tone. "I almost forget what 'dead' means in the old world. If I said a person was dead, my wife would understand one thing, and you would imagine another. This is but one of my treasure vaults, and not all my guests are laid in vaults. Out there on the moor, they lie thick as the leaves of a forest after the first blast of winter—or rather, as if the great white rose of heaven had shed its petals across the land. All night, the moon reads their faces and smiles."

"But why leave them in the corrupting moonlight?" I questioned.

"Our moon," he corrected, "is not like yours—the old cinder of a burnt-out world. Her beams embalm the dead; they do not corrupt them. Observe: here, the sexton lays his dead upon the earth. He buries very few beneath it! In your world, he piles huge stones upon them, as if to weigh them down. I wait for the hour to ring the resurrection bell and wake those who still sleep. Your sexton checks the clock to know when to call the dead-alive to church; I listen for the cock on the spire to crow: 'Awake, thou that sleepest, and arise from the dead!'"

I began to suspect the self-styled sexton was an insane parson; the whole scenario was madness! Yet, how could I escape? Helpless in this realm of the dead, with the raven and his wife as the only living souls I had encountered, where could I turn? I was lost in a space exceeding imagination. If two objects could occupy the same space here, why not twenty, or ten thousand? But I dared not pursue such thoughts further.

"You seem to perceive differences in your dead that elude my understanding," I ventured.

"None of those you see are truly dead yet," he answered. "Some have only just begun to come alive and die. Others had started dying long before they reached us; when they are truly dead, they instantly wake and depart. Almost nightly, some rise and go. But I shall say no more, for my words only mislead you. This is the couch that has been waiting for you," he concluded, pointing to one of the three empty ones.

"Why this one specifically?" I asked, trembling, desperate to delay through conversation.

"For reasons you will one day be glad to know," he replied.

"Why not know them now?"

"That also you will discover when you wake."

"But these are all dead, and I am alive!" I protested, shuddering.

"Not much," the sexton rejoined with a smile. "Not nearly enough! Blessed be the true life wherein the pauses between its throbs are not death!"

"The place is too cold to allow one to sleep!" I declared.

"Do these find it so?" he countered. "They sleep well—or will soon. Cold feels not a breath to them; it heals their wounds. Do not be a coward, Mr. Vane. Turn your back on fear and face whatever comes. Surrender to the night, and you will rest truly. No harm will come to you, only a good you cannot foresee."

The sexton and I stood beside the couch, while his wife, holding the candle, positioned herself at its foot. Her eyes brimmed with light, yet her face had reverted to a still whiteness, no longer radiant.

"Would they have me make of a charnel-house my bedchamber?" I cried aloud. "I refuse! I will lie abroad on the heath; it cannot be colder there!"

"I have just told you that the dead reside there too," the librarian said. "'Thick as autumnal leaves that strow the brooks in Vallombrosa.'"
