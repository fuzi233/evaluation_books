It was the season between Lammas and the autumn equinox of 1928 when the horror of Dunwich truly began to coalesce, and Dr. Armitage stood as a witness to its terrifying prologue. Even before the night broke, whispers had reached him of Wilbur Whateley's grotesque pilgrimage to Cambridge, of his feverish, desperate attempts to scour the Widener Library for copies or excerpts from the *Necronomicon*. These endeavors had failed, thwarted by the fierce, urgent warnings Armitage himself had hurled at every librarian guarding the forbidden tome. Yet, Wilbur remained a study in jarring anxiety; he was a man vibrating with a need for the book, simultaneously terrified by the thought of returning home after such an absence, as if the silence of his journey might bring a doom far worse than the knowledge he sought.

In early August, the anticipated catastrophe arrived. In the small, suffocating hours of the third day, Dr. Armitage was ripped from his slumber by the wild, primal screams of the college's savage watchdog. The snarls were deep, terrible, and half-mad, growing in volume until they shattered the quiet of the campus, punctuated by pauses that held a hideous, ominous significance. Then, a second voice cut through the darkness—a scream drawn from a throat that could belong to no creature of Earth, nor entirely to it. It was a sound that would haunt the dreams of Arkham for years to come, a vocalization of pure, non-earthly agony.

Driven by a panic that superseded reason, Armitage threw on clothes and burst onto the street, racing toward the college buildings. Others were already there, but the echoing shrill of a burglar alarm still tore at the air, coming from the library. An open window gaped wide against the moonlight, a black void. The intruder had made its entry complete; the chaotic noise within—the barking, the screaming, now dissolving into a low, guttural growling and moaning—could only proceed from inside. A cold instinct warned Armitage that what transpired there was not fit for unshielded eyes. With an authoritative sweep of his arm, he pushed back the gathering crowd and unlocked the vestibule door. There, he found Professor Warren Rice and Dr. Francis Morgan, men to whom he had previously voiced his deepest fears and conjectures. He motioned for them to enter with him.

Inside, the sounds had largely subsided, save for a watchful, droning whine from the dog. But as Armitage stepped forward, a sudden, jarring realization struck him: the shrubbery outside had erupted into a chorus of whip-poor-wills, piping with a damnable, rhythmic precision, as if mimicking the final breaths of a dying man. The air in the building was thick with a stench so foul and specific that Armitage knew it intimately. Together, the three men rushed across the hall to the genealogical reading-room, the source of the low, wet whining. For a heartbeat, no one dared touch the switch to illuminate the scene. Finally, summoning a courage that felt like physical pain, Armitage flipped the lever.

One of the three men shrieked, the voice lost somewhere between a sob and a gurgling choke. Whatever had sprawled before them, amidst overturned chairs and scattered debris, had caused him to lose consciousness instantly, though he did not fall. Lying half-bent on its side in a fetid pool of greenish-yellow ichor and tar-like slime was a thing almost nine feet tall. The dog's teeth had stripped away most of its clothing and much of its skin. It was not dead, yet it twitched silently and spasmodically, its chest heaving in a monstrous, jerky unison with the frenzied piping of the birds outside. Shreds of leather and fabric littered the floor, and near the broken window lay an empty canvas sack where the intruder had presumably discarded his garments. Beside the central desk sat a revolver, a dented weapon with a single cartridge that had never been discharged, explaining the lack of gunfire.

But the image of the creature itself crowded out all else, rendering language inadequate. To claim it was indescribable is a cliché, though truthfully, no mind grounded in the common forms and contours of our three-dimensional reality could visualize it. It bore undeniable traces of humanity: hands and a head that were distinctly manlike, and a face that was goatish, chinless, and stamped with the madness of the Whateleys. Yet, below the waist, biology dissolved into a fantasy so terrible it defied categorization. While the upper torso retained a semblance of form—though the chest, where the dog's paws clung vigilantly, was covered in the leathery, reticulated hide of a crocodile—the lower body was a nightmare. Thick, coarse black fur blanketed the skin, from which a dozen long, greenish-gray tentacles with red, sucking mouths protruded limply. Their arrangement followed a symmetry alien to Earth, a cosmic geometry unknown to our solar system. Deep within pinkish, ciliated orbits on each hip sat rudimentary eyes, blind and vacant. Instead of a tail hung a trunk-like feeler marked with purple rings, an undeveloped appendage that hinted at a secondary mouth or throat. The limbs, rough and veined, resembled the hind legs of prehistoric saurians, ending in pads that were neither hooves nor claws. As the beast breathed, its tail and tentacles shifted color in a slow, pulsating rhythm, draining of life and regaining it in hues of sickly gray and yellow, devoid of genuine blood, leaving only the curdling ichor that stained the floorboards.

As the presence of the three men seemed to jar the dying entity, it began to murmur, lips moving without lifting the head. Dr. Armitage noted later that no words of English escaped the thing. At first, the syllables were unintelligible, a chaos of clicks and gutturals, but toward the end, disjointed fragments emerged, clearly drawn from the *Necronomicon*, the blasphemous grimoire that had driven the boy to ruin. Armitage recalled them faintly: *"N'gai, n'gha'ghaa, bugg-shoggog, y'hah; Yog-Sothoth, Yog-Sothoth..."* The fragments trailed off into nothingness as the whip-poor-wills shrieked in a crescendo of unholy anticipation.

Then, a halt came to the gasping. The dog lifted his head, letting out a long, lugubrious howl. A transformation swept over the yellow, goatish face of the prostrate thing; the great black eyes fell, sinking deep with an appalling finality. Outside, the shrilling of the birds ceased abruptly, replaced by the sound of panicked flapping and whirring. Against the moonlit sky, vast clouds of feathered creatures rose and raced from sight, frantic at the prey they had sought. Suddenly, the dog bolted, leaping nervously out the window by which it had entered. A cry rose from the crowd, and Dr. Armitage shouted to those outside that no one must approach until the police or a medical examiner arrived. He was grateful the windows were too high for prying eyes and drew the heavy curtains down over each pane.

By this time, two policemen had arrived, and Dr. Morgan met them in the vestibule, urgently pleading for them to delay entering the stench-filled room until the examiner could cover the remains. Meanwhile, frightful changes were consuming the figure on the floor. Before the eyes of Armitage and Professor Rice, the process of shrinkage and disintegration unfolded with terrifying speed. Aside from the remnants of the face and hands, the truly human elements of Wilbur Whateley were vanishing, reduced to insignificance. When the medical examiner finally arrived, there was little more than a sticky, whitish mass clinging to the painted boards, the monstrous odor nearly gone. It appeared Whateley possessed no skull or stable bony skeleton; he had taken, in death, something very much like his unknown father.
