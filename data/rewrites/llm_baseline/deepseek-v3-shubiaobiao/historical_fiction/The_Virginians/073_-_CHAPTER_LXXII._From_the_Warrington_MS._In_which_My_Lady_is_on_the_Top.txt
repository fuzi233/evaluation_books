As I sit by the hearth, gazing across the fire at the dear companion who has shared my joys and sorrows these many years, I often find myself wondering—with the fond repetition of middle age—whether any two young fools were ever so reckless as we when we married in the year of the old King’s death. My son Miles, who has taken many a wild leap in pursuit of a fox, confesses that he looks back on those perilous gaps and rivers with a shudder, amazed at his own audacity. Yet none rides harder than he does now. He has broken bones and given his mother many a fright, just as his father—lost in verse or quarter-session speeches—has been flung more than once from his drowsy mare. But Miles’s daring has been his delight, as my marriage has been my blessing, and I thank Heaven for both.

Not that I would set myself up as an example. I do not advise young lovers to wed on a pittance—our vestry would frown upon such imprudence. Yet my wife, bless her, remains an incurable matchmaker. When some rustic pair appears before me pleading for parish relief, we counsel them to save their half-crowns, add a trifle of our own, and summon the vicar.

My dear wife’s thoughts are ever in harmony with mine; I need only wear a certain expression, and her answer is already mirrored in her eyes. If I remark, *"My love, does not the moon tonight resemble a wheel of cream cheese?"* she will reply, *"Indeed, papa, your similes are as droll as ever."* Should I observe, *"Mr. Pitt’s speech was fine, but he is no match for his father,"* she agrees at once: *"No one could equal Lord Chatham."* Then one of our girls will cry, *"But I’ve heard you call Lord Chatham a charlatan!"*—to which my wife sighs, *"How like her Aunt Hetty!"*

As for Miles, he cares little for politics or poetry. The sporting columns of the *Norwich Chronicle* are his sole literature. He has never read my verses—much less my tragedies—and when I mentioned *Pocahontas*, the rascal thought her a Virginian river. My Latin epics lie unread beneath my spectacles, though they are left in plain sight. I have even sketched unflattering caricatures of him in these pages, yet he neither notices nor minds—save once, when he replaced my drawing of him asleep with a crude sketch of myself tumbling from my mare, labeled *"The Squire on Horseback, or Fish out of Water."* The rogue roared with laughter, and the girls giggled as I turned the page. My wife swears she never endured such terror as when I opened the book—but I can bear a jest, even at my own expense.

Yet I love Miles none the less for his lack of wit or beauty. His mother sees in him the likeness of Apollo—or the Prince of Wales, born in the same year. She keeps a print of Mr. Coates’s portrait of His Royal Highness in her dressing-room, insisting upon the resemblance. *[Note, in a feminine hand: "My son is no spendthrift, nor a breaker of hearts, as some gentlemen are; but that he bore a striking likeness to H.R.H. in infancy is certain, for the Duchess of Aneaster herself remarked upon it in St. James’s Park, where Gumbo and my dear Molly often took him for air. Th. W."]*

That same year also saw the birth of Lord Esmond, Lord Castlewood’s heir—though under far different circumstances. My lord and I were already estranged by then, and no golden pap-boat graced my Miles’s christening. Yet fortune has since smiled upon us—*laus Deo!*—for neither he nor I could have mended our lot by mere wit.

Castlewood House now wears a face quite altered from the venerable one I knew in my youth—its scars of time smoothed, its ancient cracks repaired. Yet I prefer to remember it as it was when young George Warrington rode down at his lordship’s invitation to witness his marriage to Miss Lydia Van den Bosch, *"an American lady of noble Dutch lineage,"* as the county papers declared. Then, the towers stood as Colonel Esmond—my grandfather—had known them. The woods, though thinned, still rustled with the same rooks, perhaps, that had cawed in his day. His portrait hung in the hall—a man who had chosen love over title—and as I wandered those corridors, I fancied myself one of Mr. Walpole’s cavaliers, rapier at my side, half-expecting a Jacobite conspirator to step from the shadows.

Strange, sad, sweet fancies filled me then. I dreamed of old tragedies, intrigues, serenades beneath moonlit windows—of Roundheads battering the gates, or Hal’s beefeaters drilling on the lawn. I was courting a certain lady then—ah, madam, your eyes needed no spectacles in those days!—and I filled reams with romantic letters, which she devoured eagerly. Now, she complains I send but three lines when I am away. *"’Tis to spare your eyes, my dear!"*

For the wedding, I commissioned a fine new suit and presented myself at Castlewood with Monsieur Gumbo in attendance—my faithful Sady having been sent home to Virginia, his lungs too frail for English winters. The house overflowed with guests: sulky Lady Fanny as bridesmaid, neighboring virgins in tow, and the Baroness Beatrix, widowed bishop’s wife, bringing a reverend brother-in-law to tie the knot. For a time, the old halls rang with merriment long unheard.

The talk, of course, was of the bride’s fortune—seventy thousand pounds, by the papers, though rumor doubled it. The country folk cared little for town gossip about jilted suitors; they welcomed the reopening of Castlewood’s kitchens, the stables filled, the spits turning once more. Her ladyship assumed her place with dignity, her wit as sharp as her accounts.

Her grandfather, that quiet, keen-eyed Dutchman, set to work at once, mending rents, trimming waste, scrutinizing every flagon of beer, every yard of lace. The servants grumbled, but were paid; the horses thrived on less grain; the gardens flourished. From his turret chamber, the old man ruled invisibly, his thrift steady as time.

Yet not all rejoiced in this new order. The dowager Countess and Lady Fanny soon found themselves outmatched by the young American’s will and wit. They retreated to their jointure house in Kensington—unmourned by my lord, who seldom grieved for absent faces.

And what of cousin Warrington? Did I expect Lord Castlewood to sorrow at my departure, my misfortunes, my death? I knew better. A man of skeptical temper, I observed my neighbors with grim amusement, liking this one despite his hollow core. What are courage and heart to the world? A man may lack both, so long as he is agreeable, keeps a decent figure, and pays his way.

I often studied the portrait of Colonel Esmond—my grandfather—who might once have held these very titles. *"He must have been touched here,"* my lord once remarked, tapping his temple. There are acts some men deem madness—renouncing wealth, title, ambition—that others cannot fathom.

*"I do you justice, cousin,"* I told him once, *"you would surrender no advantage for any man."*

*"Selfish? But am I worse than the world?"* he replied with a shrug, taking snuff.

Once, in a scarlet coat, he fled a cow with schoolboy agility, then laughed at his own cowardice. *"I daresay you respect me no more than I do myself, George,"* he said, and launched into a wry discourse on human frailty—why Heaven had not made us all brave, tall, and rich.

And I? Though I loved to play the king among my company, I could not help but like him—this polished, witty, high-born kinsman, so utterly my inferior. His look seemed to say, *"Do not beat me—but if you must, I will not resist."* And what man, save a bully, strikes a creature so defenseless?
