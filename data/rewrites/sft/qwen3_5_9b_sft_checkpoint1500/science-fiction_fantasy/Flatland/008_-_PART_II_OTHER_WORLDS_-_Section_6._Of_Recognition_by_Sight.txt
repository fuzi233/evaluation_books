I stand before you now, and I fear I shall appear profoundly contradictory. For in prior sections, I asserted that every figure in Flatland presents merely as a straight line, rendering visual distinction between social classes impossible. Yet here, I intend to demonstrate to my Spaceland observers precisely how we achieve recognition through sight.

If you consult the records regarding "Recognition by Feeling," you will note a specific caveat: this method is universal *only* among the lower classes. Visual recognition, conversely, is an art cultivated exclusively within the higher strata and in our temperate latitudes. This ability arises solely from the presence of Fog. While you, inhabitants of Spaceland, regard fog as a malignancy that obscures vision and saps vitality, we venerate it as a benevolent element, second only to air itself, serving as the mother of our sciences and the nurse of our arts.

Were fog absent, all lines would manifest with identical, indistinguishable clarity—a state found only in those wretched, arid regions devoid of atmospheric moisture. However, where fog abounds, it creates a gradient of visibility. Objects situated at varying distances—say, three feet versus twenty-nine inches—appear with distinct degrees of dimness. Through rigorous training and constant empirical observation of these comparative shades, we deduce the precise configuration of an approaching entity.

Consider a practical scenario. Two figures approach me, and I seek to determine their rank. Let us assume one is a Merchant (an Equilateral Triangle) and the other a Physician (a Pentagon). How do I differentiate them?

Imagine my gaze bisects the angle of an approaching stranger. My视线 falls squarely between the two sides nearest to me. Consequently, those two adjacent sides appear equal in magnitude. Now, observe the difference in fading.

In the case of the Merchant, whose form is triangular, I perceive a central line segment that glows brightly near my eye, but its extremities vanish rapidly into the mist. Because the triangle's angles recede sharply away from the viewer, the tips (points D and E) become exceedingly faint, barely perceptible against the gray backdrop.

Conversely, the Physician, being pentagonal, presents a line where the center remains brilliant, yet the extremities fade much more gradually. The sides of a polygon recede less steeply into the fog than those of a triangle. Thus, the Physician's outer edges remain discernibly brighter than the Merchant's vanishing points.

Through extensive education and relentless practice, the upper classes can thus distinguish, with fair precision, between the middling and the lowest orders purely by sight. If my Spaceland patrons accept this premise as plausible rather than dismissing it as absurd, I have succeeded in conveying the core concept. To delve deeper into technical minutiae would only confuse you.

However, let me caution the novice. Do not mistake this simplified example for the reality of daily life. Recognizing one's father or son under ideal conditions is trivial; navigating a crowded street is a Herculean task.

Suppose my father, the Triangle, approaches presenting a flat side rather than an angle. Until I request him to pivot or shift my own position, I am momentarily paralyzed with doubt: Is he a Triangle, or is he a Straight Line? And a Straight Line, to us, signifies a Woman. Similarly, when observing one of my hexagonal grandsons head-on, the geometry becomes intricate. I see one dominant line of relative brightness flanked by two smaller, deeply shadowed lines that dissolve completely at their termini.

To manage these complexities amidst a throng of rotating polygons in a ballroom or conversazione requires the intellectual agility of the highest order. It justifies the existence of the prestigious Professors of Geometry at the University of Wentbridge, where the static and kinetic arts of sight recognition are taught to the elite. Only the wealthy scions of our nobility possess the resources to master this discipline. Even I, a mathematician of some standing and grandfather to two regular hexagons, often feel bewildered in such crowds. For a tradesman or serf, the scene is utterly unintelligible.

To you, a Spaclander, such a crowd would appear as a chaotic array of seemingly straight lines, fluctuating wildly in luminosity. Even a student versed in the theories of the Pentagon and Hexagon classes would require decades of experience to navigate a fashionable assembly without jostling a superior. It is considered bad form to "feel" a person in public, yet without the refined spatial awareness gained from sight-recognition training, one cannot anticipate the movements of others. To conduct oneself with propriety in Polygonal society, one essentially must be a Polygon oneself.

The mastery of sight recognition is akin to an instinct honed by habit, specifically by abstaining from the crude custom of feeling. Consider the deaf and mute of Spaceland: if permitted to gesture, they never acquire lip-reading. Likewise, those who rely on tactile sensation in youth never attain the perfection of visual discrimination. Therefore, among the higher classes, "feeling" is strictly prohibited. Children bypass elementary schools, where feeling is taught, and attend exclusive seminaries. At the University, to "feel" is a grave offense punishable by expulsion.

Among the lower classes, however, sight recognition is deemed an unattainable luxury. A tradesman cannot spare his son the leisure for abstract study. Consequently, the poor are left to rely on feeling from infancy, granting them a natural vivacity and precocious adaptability that initially contrasts favorably with the rigid, listless demeanor of the academically trained youth. Yet, upon completing their university education, the former undergo a transformation so profound it resembles a rebirth. Suddenly, they surpass their triangular rivals in every scientific and social endeavor, their fluid understanding of the world having been forged in the absence of formal geometric instruction.

Tragically, a small minority of the Polygonal class fails the final examination. Their plight is miserable. Rejected by the nobility and despised by the laborers, they lack the systematic intellect of the masters and the innate versatility of the tradesmen. Careers and public offices remain closed to them. Though legal barriers to marriage are low, social stigma ensures that unions with such "irregulars" rarely produce viable offspring, perpetuating a cycle of misfortune.

It is from this discarded stratum—the failed intellectuals—that the tumults and seditions of history have frequently drawn their leaders. The chaos they unleash is so devastating that a growing faction of progressive statesmen argues that true mercy lies in total suppression, advocating for the imprisonment or painless extinction of anyone who fails the final university exam.

Thus, I pause, having skirted the dangerous waters of "Irregularities," a topic of such vital consequence that it demands its own dedicated discourse.
