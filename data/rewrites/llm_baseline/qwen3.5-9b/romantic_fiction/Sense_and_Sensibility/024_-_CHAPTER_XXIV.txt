Elinor approached the conversation with a steady, albeit guarded, composure. "I would feel unworthy of the confidence you've placed in me," she began, "if I harbored no wish to maintain it, or no further curiosity regarding its subject. Therefore, I offer no apology for raising it once more."

"Thank you!" Lucy cried warmly, her relief palpable. "You have broken the ice; you have set my heart at ease. I confess I was terrified I had offended you back on Monday."

"Offend you?" Elinor replied with genuine warmth. "How could you possibly imagine that? Believe me, nothing was further from my intent than to suggest such a thing. Could you have any motive for trusting me that wasn't honorable and flattering?"

"And yet," Lucy countered, her sharp eyes gleaming with hidden meaning, "I felt a distinct coldness and displeasure in your manner that left me utterly uncomfortable. I was convinced you were angry, and I have been tormenting myself ever since over the liberty I took in troubling you with my affairs. But how glad I am to discover it was merely my own imagination, and that you truly hold no grudge. If only you knew how consoling it was to unload my heart onto someone who listens, or how much I think of you every moment of my life, your compassion would surely forgive everything else."

"I can easily believe it brought you great relief to speak freely," Elinor said gently. "Be assured you shall never regret it. Your situation is undeniably unfortunate; you appear surrounded by difficulties, and you will require all your mutual affection to sustain you. Regarding Mr. Ferrars, I believe he relies entirely on his mother's provision."

"He possesses only two thousand pounds of his own," Lucy explained. "It would be sheer madness to marry on such a sum, though personally, I could surrender every prospect of wealth without a sigh. I have always known a very small income and could endure any poverty for his sake. Yet, I love him too deeply to become the selfish instrument of robbing him of whatever his mother might bestow if he married solely to please her. We must wait—it may be for many years. For almost any other man, this would be an alarming prospect, but Edward's affection and constancy are treasures nothing can strip from me. I know that."

"That conviction must be everything to you," Elinor observed carefully, ensuring her face remained unreadable. "And he is undoubtedly sustained by the same faith in yours. Had the strength of your reciprocal attachment failed—as it often does between many couples under similar circumstances during a four-year engagement—your position would indeed be pitiable."

Lucy looked up expectantly, but Elinor held her gaze firmly. "Edward's love for me," Lucy declared, "has already undergone a severe test. Our engagement has endured a very long period of separation since we first promised, and it has passed this trial so successfully that I would be unpardonable to doubt it now. I can safely assert he has never given me a single moment of alarm."

Elinor struggled internally, unsure whether to smile or sigh at such a confident assertion. Lucy pressed on. "I am naturally somewhat jealous, and given our disparate social standings, his immersion in the world compared to my seclusion, and our constant separations, I was prone to suspicion. Had there been the slightest change in his behavior toward me upon our meetings, any inexplicable drop in spirits, or excessive talk of another lady, I would have discovered the truth instantly. I do not claim to be particularly observant in general matters, but in such a critical instance, I am certain I could not be deceived."

*All this,* Elinor thought, *is very pretty, but it cannot deceive either of us.*

After a brief silence, Elinor ventured, "But what are your plans? Or do you view nothing but waiting for Mrs. Ferrars' death—a melancholy and shocking extremity? Is her son resolved to submit to this, and to the tedious years of suspense it may entail, rather than risk her displeasure temporarily by revealing the truth?"

"If we could be certain it would be only temporary! But Mrs. Ferrars is a headstrong, proud woman. Her initial fury upon hearing the truth would likely see her secure everything for Robert. The mere thought of that, for Edward's sake, deters me from taking any hasty measures."

"And for your own sake as well, or you are pushing your disinterest beyond reason." Lucy glanced at Elinor, then fell silent.

"Do you know Mr. Robert Ferrars?" Elinor asked.

"Not at all; I have never seen him. However, I fancy he is very unlike his brother—silly and a great coxcomb."

"A great coxcomb!" repeated Miss Steele, having caught the phrase amidst a sudden pause in Marianne's piano playing. "Oh, they are discussing their favorite beaux, I suppose."

"No, sister," Lucy corrected sharply. "You are mistaken; our favorite beaux are *not* great coxcombs."

"I can vouch for it that Miss Dashwood's beau is not," Mrs. Jennings laughed heartily. "He is one of the most modest and well-behaved young men I have ever encountered. As for Lucy, she is such a sly little creature; there is no telling whom she actually likes."

"Oh," cried Miss Steele, casting significant glances around the room, "I dare say Lucy's beau is quite as modest and well-behaved as Miss Dashwood's."

Elinor blushed involuntarily. Lucy bit her lip and shot an angry look at her sister. A heavy silence ensued until Lucy broke it in a lowered tone, despite Marianne providing a musical shield with a magnificent concerto.

"I will honestly disclose one scheme recently conceived to bring matters to a resolution. Indeed, I am bound to share this secret, for you are a party concerned. You have likely seen enough of Edward to know he prefers the clergy to any other profession. My plan is for him to take holy orders as soon as possible. Then, through your influence—which I am sure you would kindly exert out of friendship for him, and hopefully out of some regard for me—your brother might be persuaded to grant him the Norland living. I understand it is a very good rectory, and the current incumbent is unlikely to survive much longer. This would provide sufficient income for us to marry upon, leaving us to trust in time and chance for the remainder."

"I should always be happy," Elinor replied, "to demonstrate any mark of esteem and friendship for Mr. Ferrars. But do you not perceive that my interest in such a matter would be entirely superfluous? He is the brother of Mrs. John Dashwood—that connection alone must suffice for his father-in-law."

"But Mrs. John Dashwood would scarcely approve of Edward entering the clergy."

"Then I rather suspect my influence would achieve very little."

They lapsed into silence again for many minutes. Finally, Lucy sighed deeply. "I believe the wisest course is to dissolve the engagement outright. We seem besieged by difficulties on every side. Though it would render us miserable for a time, we might ultimately be happier. But you will not give me your advice, Miss Dashwood?"

"No," Elinor answered with a smile that masked her agitated feelings. "On such a subject, I certainly will not. You know very well that my opinion would carry no weight with you unless it aligned with your own desires."

"You wrong me greatly," Lucy responded with grave solemnity. "There is no one whose judgment I hold in higher esteem than yours. I truly believe that if you were to say, 'I advise you by all means to end your engagement with Edward Ferrars; it will be more beneficial for the happiness of both of you,' I would resolve to act immediately."

Elinor blushed at the insincerity of the future wife-to-be and replied, "Such a compliment would effectively deter me from offering any opinion had I formed one. It elevates my influence far too high; the power to divide two people so tenderly attached is too great a burden for an indifferent observer."

"'Tis precisely because you are an indifferent person," Lucy retorted with some pique, emphasizing the word heavily, "that your judgment might justly carry such weight with me. Were you suspected of bias by your own feelings, your opinion would be worthless."

Elinor deemed it wiser to remain silent, fearing they might provoke each other into an unsuitable display of familiarity and candor. She was even partially resolved never to revisit the topic. Consequently, a prolonged silence followed, and Lucy was once again the first to break it.

"Will you be in town this winter, Miss Dashwood?" she asked with her customary air of complacency.

"Certainly not."

"I am sorry to hear that," the other replied, her eyes brightening at the news. "It would have given me such pleasure to meet you there! But I suppose you will go regardless. After all, your brother and sister will invite you."

"It will not be within my power to accept their invitation if they do."

"How unfortunate!" Lucy exclaimed. "I had fully relied on meeting you there. Anne and I are traveling to visit some relations late in January—they have been urging us to pay them a visit for years! But I go solely for the sake of seeing Edward. He will be there in February; otherwise, London holds no charm for me. I lack the spirit for it."
