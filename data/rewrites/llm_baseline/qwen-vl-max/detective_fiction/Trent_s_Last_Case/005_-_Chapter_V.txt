There are moments in life—few, but unforgettable—when the quiet machinery within us suddenly whispers a truth we cannot explain. A sense of certainty, unbidden and profound, rises like a bird from hidden heather: not the reckless assurance of a gambler, nor the blind optimism of the hopeful, but a deep, wordless knowing that something important is about to unfold. The general feels it at dawn, sensing victory; the golfer knows, before the putt is struck, that the green will yield. As Trent climbed the stairs outside the library door, such a feeling washed over him—a clear, unwavering conviction that the mystery was nearing its end. His mind swirled with guesses and half-formed theories, observations too fragmented to fit into any coherent picture. Yet, as he ascended, he felt certain that light would soon break through.

The bedrooms lined a wide, carpeted corridor, lit by a tall window at one end. It ran the full length of the house, turning sharply into a narrower passage where the servants’ rooms opened. Martin’s room was an exception—it branched off from a small landing halfway up. As Trent passed, he glanced inside. A modest, tidy space, nothing remarkable. He continued upward, stepping carefully, his body close to the wall, each footfall measured and soft. Still, the old floor groaned beneath him, betraying his presence with a series of audible creaks.

He knew Manderson’s room was the first on the right when he reached the bedroom floor. He approached it immediately, testing the latch and lock—both functioning normally—and examined the key’s wards. Then he turned to the room itself.

It was small, oddly bare. The man’s toiletries were minimal, almost austere. Everything remained exactly as it had been the morning the body was found in the grounds. The bed was unmade, sheets and blankets tangled over a narrow wooden frame. Sunlight streamed through the window, illuminating the mess. On a plain table beside the bed sat a shallow glass bowl filled with water—inside, the delicate metalwork of dentures gleamed. A wrought-iron candlestick stood nearby. Clothes lay carelessly draped over one of two rush-bottomed chairs. On the chest of drawers, used as a dressing table, objects were scattered haphazardly—evidence of haste. Trent scanned them with a keen eye. He noted that the occupant had neither washed nor shaved. With a fingertip, he lifted the dental plate from the bowl, frowning at its inexplicable presence.

The emptiness of the room, drenched in sunlight, stirred a sense of unease in Trent. His imagination conjured a vision: a pale, anxious man rising before dawn, dressing in silence, eyes darting toward the inner door where his wife slept, consumed by dread.

Trent shivered. To steady himself, he opened the two tall wall cupboards flanking the bed. They held clothing—plenty of it, clearly a necessity for comfort. And shoes. An extraordinary collection of polished footwear lined long low shelves against the wall. No boots among them. Trent, a connoisseur of fine leather, studied them with interest. The shoes were narrow, round-toed, impeccably crafted—all from the same last, suggesting a man who took pride in his well-shaped feet.

Then his gaze fixed on a pair of patent-leather shoes on the upper shelf.

These were the ones the inspector had described—the ones Manderson wore the night before his death. They were worn, but recently polished. Something about their uppers caught Trent’s attention. He leaned closer, comparing them to the others. Then he picked them up, examining the seam where the upper met the sole.

As he did so, a faint, precise melody began to hum from his lips—an air Inspector Murch, if he’d been there, would have recognized instantly.

Many men who master self-control have a tell—a small, involuntary habit that reveals suppressed emotion. Murch had once observed that when Trent sensed a breakthrough, he whistled softly, almost imperceptibly, a tune from Mendelssohn’s *Lied ohne Worte* in A Major.

Trent turned the shoes over, measured them with a marked tape, and scrutinized the soles. In the angle between heel and instep, he detected a faint trace of red gravel.

He placed the shoes back on the floor and walked to the window, hands behind his back, still whistling faintly. He stared out, though his eyes saw nothing. Once, his lips parted, releasing the Englishman’s classic expletive of sudden insight. After a moment, he returned to the shelves and examined every shoe with swift, careful precision.

Next, he collected the clothes from the chair, inspected them thoroughly, and set them down again. He searched the wardrobes, then revisited the cluttered dressing table. Finally, he sat on the empty chair, head in hands, staring at the carpet. Minutes passed. Then he rose, opened the inner door leading to Mrs. Manderson’s room.

At once, it was clear the room had been hastily cleared. The usual accoutrements of a woman’s boudoir—dressing table, hats, bags, ribbons, gloves—were gone. The bed, chairs, tables—nothing bore the signs of daily use. It looked like a guest chamber left vacant. Yet every detail spoke of refined taste—unconventional, yes, but meticulous. Trent, trained in aesthetics, noted the harmony of color and form. He realized this woman, however ill-matched her husband, possessed artistic sensibility. His curiosity deepened. He thought of the burdens she carried, and the unfolding story now taking shape in his mind.

He went to the tall French window facing the door, opened it, and stepped onto a small iron-railed balcony. Below stretched a broad lawn, beginning just beneath the house, separated only by a narrow flowerbed. It sloped gently toward the orchard. Another window opened above the library’s garden entrance. In the far corner, a second door led to the passage—the one the maid used in the mornings, the one Mrs. Manderson might take when leaving.

Trent sat on the bed and quickly sketched a plan in his notebook. The bed stood in the angle between the connecting door and the sash window, its head against the wall dividing the rooms. He stared at the pillows, then lay down deliberately, looking through the open doorway into Manderson’s room.

Satisfied, he rose and added details to his sketch: two small tables with covers, one farther from the door holding a copper electric lamp connected by a free wire to the wall. He studied the switches—on the wall near the door, out of reach from the bed. He tested the lights, confirmed they worked, then turned abruptly and walked into Manderson’s room.

He rang the bell.

“I need your help again, Martin,” he said as the butler appeared, upright and impassive. “I want you to speak to Mrs. Manderson’s maid. I need to talk to her.”

“Certainly, sir,” Martin replied.

“What’s she like? Is she level-headed?”

“She’s French, sir,” Martin said, pausing. “Not long with us, but I’ve noticed she knows more of the world than is good for her—since you asked.”

“You think butter might melt in her mouth?” Trent asked with a smile. “Well, I’m not afraid. I want to ask her some questions.”

“I’ll send her up at once, sir.” The butler withdrew.

Trent paced the room, hands behind his back. Sooner than expected, a small, neat figure in black appeared.

The maid, Célestine, had seen Trent earlier across the lawn and hoped he would summon her. She needed to vent—her nerves were frayed. But the other servants dismissed her outbursts, and Inspector Murch had silenced her with cold authority. Trent, from afar, had seemed different—not like a policeman, but *sympathique*.

Yet as she entered, instinct told her coquetry would be misplaced. Instead, she offered an amiable, candid smile. “Monsieur desires to speak with me,” she said. “I am called Célestine.”

“Naturally,” Trent replied, calm and businesslike. “Now, Célestine, I need to know: When you brought tea to your mistress yesterday at seven o’clock, was the door between these two rooms open?”

Célestine became animated. “Oh yes!” she exclaimed, using her favorite English phrase. “Always open, monsieur. I shut it as always. But let me explain. When I enter madame’s room from the other door—ah! If monsieur will step in there, all becomes clear.” She guided him into the larger bedroom. “See! I come in with the tea. I approach the bed. Before I reach it, here is the door—always open—to my right. But monsieur can see I cannot see into Mr. Manderson’s room. The door opens toward the bed, not toward me. I shut it without seeing inside. That is the routine. Yesterday was no different. I see nothing of the next room. Madame sleeps like an angel—she sees nothing. I shut the door. I place the tray—I open the curtains—I prepare the toilette—I leave—voilà!”

Trent watched her gestures closely, nodding. “I understand perfectly now. Thank you, Célestine. So Mr. Manderson was assumed to be still in his room while your mistress dressed and ate breakfast?”

“Oui, monsieur.”

“No one missed him,” Trent remarked. “Well, Célestine, thank you very much.” He reopened the door.

“It is nothing, monsieur,” she said, crossing the room. “I hope you catch the assassin of Monsieur Manderson. But”—she paused, hand on the outer door—“I do not regret him at all. Not at all!” Her voice cracked. She clenched her teeth, color rising in her cheeks. English fled. “Je ne le regrette pas du tout, du tout! Madame—ah! je me jetterais au feu pour madame—une femme si charmante, si adorable! Mais un homme comme monsieur—maussade, boudeur, impassible! Ah, non! De ma vie! J’en avais par-dessus la tête! Ah, vrai! Est-ce insupportable, tout de même, qu’il existe des types comme ça? Je vous jure que—”

“Finissez ce chahut, Célestine!” Trent interrupted sharply. Her outburst triggered memories of his student days. “En voilà une scène! C’est rasant, vous savez. Faut rentret ça, mademoiselle. Du reste, c’est bien imprudent, croyez-moi. Hang it! Have some common sense! If the inspector heard you say that, you’d be in trouble. And don’t wave your fists—someone might get hit. You seem,” he added, softer now, “to be even happier than most that Mr. Manderson is gone. I could almost suspect, Célestine, that he didn’t pay you the attention you believed you deserved.”

“A peine s’il m’avait regardé!” she answered simply.

“Ça, c’est un comble!” Trent observed. “You’re quite a character for a small tea-party, I must say. A star burned on your birthday whose fierce, serene, red, pulseless planet never yearned in heaven, Célestine. Mademoiselle, I’m busy. Bon jour. You certainly are a beauty!”

Célestine took it as a compliment—unexpected, perhaps, but welcome. Surprise steadied her. With a flash of her eyes and teeth, she opened the door and vanished.

Left alone, Trent muttered two forceful words in French and returned to his thoughts. He placed the shoes he’d examined on one chair, sat opposite, hands in pockets, eyes fixed on them. Occasionally, he whistled a few bars—almost inaudibly. The room was still. A soft twittering came from the trees beyond the open window. A breeze rustled the thick creeper along the sill. But Trent sat unmoving, face grim, lost in thought.

He remained like that for half an hour. Then he rose, replaced the shoes on the shelf, and stepped onto the landing.

Two doors faced him across the hall. He opened the one directly opposite—a bedroom far from orderly. Fishing rods leaned in a corner, books piled elsewhere. The housemaid hadn’t managed to tame the chaos: pipes, knives, pencils, keys, golf balls, letters, photos, boxes, tins, bottles littered the dressing table and mantelshelf. Two fine etchings and watercolors hung on the walls. Unhung engravings leaned against the wardrobe. A row of shoes and boots stood beneath the window.

Trent crossed the room, studied the footwear, then measured several with his tape, whistling softly. Satisfied, he sat on the edge of the bed, eyes scanning the room.

The photographs on the mantel caught his attention. He rose and examined one—Marlowe and Manderson riding horses. Two others showed Alpine peaks. A faded print depicted three youths—one unmistakably the man with the haggard blue eyes—dressed in sixteenth-century soldier’s garb. Another was a portrait of a stately elderly woman, vaguely resembling Marlowe.

Trent mechanically took a cigarette from an open box, lit it, and stared at the photos. Then he noticed a flat leather case beside the box.

He opened it. Inside lay a small revolver, finely crafted, with a dozen loose cartridges. Engraved on the stock: “J. M.”

A step echoed on the stairs. As Trent opened the breech and peered into the barrel, Inspector Murch appeared in the doorway.

“I was wondering—” he began, then stopped, seeing what Trent was doing. His eyes widened slightly. “Whose revolver is that, Mr. Trent?” he asked casually.

“Clearly the occupant’s,” Trent replied lightly, pointing to the initials. “Mr. Marlowe. I found it on the mantelpiece. Neat little pistol. Looks like it’s been cleaned recently. But I know little about guns.”

“Well, I know a lot,” Murch said quietly, taking the revolver. “Firearms are a specialty of mine, as you know. But even a novice can tell one thing.” He placed the gun back in its case, pulled out a cartridge, and laid it in his palm. Then he took a small object from his pocket—a lead bullet, slightly battered, with fresh scratches.

“That’s the one?” Trent murmured, leaning in.

“That’s him,” Murch said. “Lodged in the bone at the back of the skull. Dr. Stock removed it an hour ago and sent it to me. Those bright scratches? Made by the doctor’s tools. The others? From the rifling of the barrel—just like this one.” He tapped the revolver. “Same make, same caliber. No other weapon marks a bullet quite like this.”

They stood in silence, the revolver between them. Trent broke it first. “This mystery is all wrong,” he said. “It’s madness. The symptoms of delusion are clear. Let’s see where we stand. We agreed, I think, that Manderson killed Marlowe in the car on the way to Southampton, and that Marlowe returned late last night—hours after the murder.”

“There’s no doubt about that,” Murch said, emphasizing the verb.

“And now,” Trent continued, “this clean, elegant firearm invites us to believe this: that Marlowe never went to Southampton; that he returned in the night; that he somehow woke Manderson, made him dress, and lured him into the grounds; that he shot him with this pistol; that he cleaned it, returned it to the mantel—without waking anyone—and hid for the rest of the day—with a large motor car; and then reappeared, pretending ignorance—at what time?”

“A little after nine p.m.,” Murch said, still watching Trent. “Yes, that’s the theory suggested by this find. And it’s wild enough—though it collapses at the start. When the murder happened, Marlowe was fifty to a hundred miles away. He *did* go to Southampton.”

“How do you know?”

“I questioned him last night. Took down his story. He arrived in Southampton around 6:30 Monday morning.”

“Come off it,” Trent snapped. “What do I care about his story? What do you care? How do you *know* he went?”

Murch chuckled. “I thought I’d get a rise out of you, Mr. Trent. Well, no harm in telling. After I arrived, I got the story from Mrs. Manderson and the staff. The first thing I did was wire our people in Southampton. Manderson had told his wife he changed his mind and sent Marlowe to get information from someone on the next boat. Seemed plausible—but Marlowe was the only one not under my watch. He didn’t return until later. So I wired Southampton. This morning I received this.” He handed Trent a series of telegraph slips.

> Person answering description in motor answering description arrived Bedford Hotel here 6.30 this morning gave name Marlowe left car hotel garage told attendant car belonged Manderson had bath and breakfast went out heard of later at docks inquiring for passenger name Harris on Havre boat inquired repeatedly until boat left at noon next heard of at hotel where he lunched about 1.15 left soon afterwards in car company’s agents inform berth was booked name Harris last week but Harris did not travel by boat Burke Inspector.

“Simple and satisfactory,” Murch said, as Trent read it twice. “His story matches every detail. He told me he waited at the docks for half an hour, hoping Harris would arrive late. Then he went back, had lunch, decided to return immediately. He wired Manderson—‘Harris not turned up missed boat returning Marlowe’—which was delivered here in the afternoon and placed among the dead man’s letters. He drove back fast, arrived exhausted. When Martin told him of the murder, he nearly fainted. Sleepless, tired, he was barely coherent when I interviewed him last night—but he was perfectly logical.”

Trent picked up the revolver, twirled the cylinder idly. “It was unfortunate for Manderson that Marlowe left his pistol and cartridges so carelessly,” he said at last, placing it back in the case. “It was like throwing temptation in someone’s path, wouldn’t you say?”
