The sermon had barely commenced when Margaret stepped into the vast, unfinished cathedral of St. Laurens. It was a colossus of stone, halted in its ascent, a testament to the slow pace of divine architecture. The side aisles bore roofs, yet the central nave and the chancel remained open to the sky. Pillars stood proud and arches were carved with care, some already washed in white plaster, but the great windows were mere jagged wounds in the masonry, save for a single pane of glass that caught the light.

Yet, in this midsummer afternoon, the imperfections transformed into splendor. The sun, breaking through the irregular gaps, cast fractured beams of gold that danced across the rough surfaces, painting the dark side aisles with rivers of glory and setting the white pillars ablaze. The central aisle became a checkerboard of light and shadow, the darkness cool and soothing, the light shimmering like amber diamonds infused with celestial fire. Above, the blue vault of the sky pressed close against the high roof, while the white hoods of the congregation formed a serene sea against the vivid azure.

Though the great central aisle was packed, a profound silence held sway. The preacher's words, delivered in a mellow, earnest voice, bound the crowd in mute reverence. Margaret stood transfixed by the sheer beauty and the collective devotion, a "great calm" settling over her. Seeking refuge from the glare, she slipped behind a pillar in the north aisle. There, amidst the loveliness of the place and the music of the voice, a sweet languor washed over her, smoothing the waves of her heart until she leaned against the stone, eyes half-closed, lost in a dreamy reverie.

It felt right to be there.

Soon, she noticed a lady vacating a prime spot opposite her to escape the scorching sun pouring from a high window. Margaret moved swiftly and silently, securing the seat. Now positioned beside a pillar in the south aisle, less than fifty feet from the pulpit, she was close enough to hear every syllable, standing just behind and to the side of the speaker.

Her concentration, however, was briefly shattered by the bobbing shadow of a man's head and shoulders, moving with such peculiar, almost comical rhythm that she struggled to suppress a smile. Yet, it was no cause for mirth. It was the sexton, diligently digging. She confirmed this by glancing through the open window to see the source of the shadow.

As her gaze lingered on Jorian Ketel working below, a specific tone of the preacher's voice struck her ear with such distinct force that it seemed to vibrate through her very bones. Her hand flew to her bosom, startled by the sudden thrill. Turning sharply, she sought the source, but only saw the back of the man and his tonsure. She sighed; the visual image of the monk contradicted the powerful resonance of his voice.

Still, she leaned forward, eyes cast down, waiting for that accent to return. It did not. Yet, the voice itself grew strangely potent, rising and falling as the preacher warmed to his theme, awakening faint echoes of a thousand happy memories. She refused to look up, unwilling to dispel the melancholy pleasure the sound evoked.

Then, in the midst of an eloquent passage, the preacher stopped abruptly.

Margaret almost sighed; the soothing melody had ceased. Was the sermon over? She glanced around, but the congregation remained still. Several faces turned toward her. She spun sharply, but the space behind her was empty. Startled expressions nearby fixed on the preacher. Following their gaze, she saw him in the pulpit: a face like a staring corpse. The friar's naturally large eyes, widened by the hollows of his cheeks, were dilated to a supernatural size, glaring directly at her from a bloodless visage.

She cringed, turning fearfully, convinced some terrible specter stood behind her. There was nothing; she was merely an outsider among the listeners.

Suddenly, the church erupted into commotion. Figures surged upward, craning necks; hundreds of agitated faces swung between the friar and Margaret. The rustling of caps created a deafening noise, followed by shrieks from nervous women and the buzzing murmur of men. Seeing so many eyes leveled at her, Margaret shrank terrified behind the pillar, casting one frantic, hurried glance at the preacher.

In that fleeting moment, she caught an expression on his stricken face that made her shiver. She turned faint and sat down on a heap of chips left by the workmen, burying her face in her hands. The sermon resumed, but she heard only the sound, not the sense. Her mind spun in a whirl, fixated on a single image: the prodigy-stricken face bearing a look stamped upon it. The memory of that gaze made her quiver from head to toe.

That look was *Recognition*.

After wavering, the sermon concluded with a strain of feverish eloquence that nearly drowned out the memory of the preacher's strange pause and ghastly stare. Margaret hurriedly merged with the departing crowd and exited the church.

Outside, they dispersed toward their homes, but she turned at the threshold and walked into the churchyard, heading straight for Peter's grave. Though poor, she had secured him a slab and a headstone. Sitting upon the stone, she kissed it, then pulled her apron over her head to hide her hair from any prying eyes.

"Father," she whispered, "you have often heard me say I am wading in deep waters; but now I begin to think God alone knows the bottom of them. I will follow that friar round the world, but I will keep him at arm's length. He must tell me why he looked toward me like a dead man wakened, with no one else behind me. Oh, Father, you often praised me here; speak a word for me there. For I am wading in deep waters."

Her father's tomb offered a commanding view of the church door. From there, with her face concealed, she waited, waylaying the holy preacher.
