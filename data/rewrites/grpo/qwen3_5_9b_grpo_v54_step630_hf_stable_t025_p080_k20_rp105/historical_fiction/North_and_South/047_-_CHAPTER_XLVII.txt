"Experience, like a pale musician, holds / A dulcimer of patience in his hand; / Whence harmonies we cannot understand, / Of God's will in His worlds, the strain unfolds / In sad, perplexed minors." — *Mrs. Browning.*

Just as the dust of the strike began to settle, Dixon returned from Milton, bringing with her the heavy, gossip-laden air of the town. She settled into her post as Margaret's maid, eager to fill the silence with the trivialities of the world she had left behind. Her reports were a tapestry of petty grievances and social observations: Martha's departure to live with Miss Thornton following the marriage; the elaborate details of the bridesmaids' gowns and the breakfasts that followed; the whispered judgment that Mr. Thornton had thrown away too much dignity on a wedding, given his financial losses from the failed contracts. Dixon relished recounting the auction of the Thorntons' furniture, noting with satisfaction how little the items Dixon herself had coveted had fetched, a fact she found deeply unjust considering the wealth of Milton's inhabitants. She recounted Mrs. Thornton's shrewd bargains and Mr. Thornton's foolish attempts to outbid himself, a spectacle Dixon found delightful as it leveled the playing field.

She spoke of Mr. Bell's confusing correspondence regarding books, his particularity making letters seem like riddles compared to a personal visit, and offered a vague, aristocratically filtered account of the Higginses. Her memory betrayed her when it came to those beneath her station; she recalled Nicholas as "very well," the only one besides Mr. Thornton to inquire after Miss Margaret, while Mary was dismissed as a "great, stout, slatternly thing." There was a rumor, perhaps a dream, that Mary had taken work at the mill to learn cooking—a notion Margaret found incoherent enough to be dismissed as fantasy. Yet, it was a relief to have Dixon to discuss Milton, even if Dixon preferred to keep that part of her life in shadow. Instead, she dwelled on Mr. Bell's speeches, convinced they hinted at a grand design: making Margaret his heiress. Margaret, however, offered no encouragement, shutting down Dixon's insinuations with polite indifference.

Beneath the chatter, a strange, undefined longing gnawed at Margaret. She waited for Mr. Bell to return to Milton, for they had agreed at Helstone that the explanation she craved must come from him, delivered only to Mr. Thornton, and never forced. Though he wrote occasionally, his letters were now short, complaining, and laced with an unusual bitterness. He seemed to regret the past and weary of the present, mentioning an "old-fashioned complaint called the spleen," leaving Margaret to wonder if it was physical or mental. She stopped asking, accepting the silence as a barrier she could not breach.

The only spark of hope came accidentally from Edith. During a conversation with Mr. Bell in London, he had half-thought of taking Margaret to Cadiz to meet her new sister-in-law and hear Frederick's account of the mutiny. Edith, weary of Margaret's cross-questioning, admitted it was merely a fleeting fancy, yet the idea captivated Margaret. It promised a bright escape from the monotony of her current existence. But Edith, sensitive to any joy that excluded her, felt slighted. Margaret had to soothe her, hiding her own delight behind the safety valve of asking Dixon if she would enjoy seeing Master Frederick and his new wife.

"She's a Papist, Miss, isn't she?" Dixon asked, her voice dropping.

"I believe—oh yes, certainly!" Margaret replied, dampened instantly by the thought.

"And they live in a Popish country?"

"Yes."

"Then I'm afraid my soul is dearer to me than even Master Frederick," Dixon declared, feigning terror lest she be converted. "I should be in perpetual dread."

"Oh," Margaret said gently, "I do not know that I am going. And if I do, I am not such a fine lady as to travel without you. You shall have a long holiday."

"But I think it's a long 'if,'" Dixon countered, her curiosity piqued despite her protestations. She asked if avoiding priests and churches would truly save her from conversion, noting that Master Frederick had gone over unaccountably.

"I fancy it was love that first predisposed him to conversion," Margaret sighed.

"Indeed, Miss!" Dixon exclaimed. "Well, I can preserve myself from priests and churches; but love steals in unawares! I think it's as well I should not go."

Margaret feared dwelling too much on the Spanish plan, for it distracted her from her desperate need to speak plainly with Mr. Thornton. Mr. Bell remained stationary at Oxford, bound by some secret restraint that prevented him from visiting Milton or confirming the trip to Spain.

Her greatest solace lay in Edith's son. He was a storm of will and emotion, a creature of strong passions that drove Edith to despair. Yet, Margaret found him more endearing in his tantrums than in his good moods. She would carry him off to a private room, where they battled it out alone. With a firm power that subdued him into peace, she countered his every wile with charm and reason until he would bury his hot, tear-smeared face in her neck, kissing her until sleep claimed him. These were Margaret's sweetest moments, fleeting tastes of a maternal warmth she feared was denied to her forever.

Mr. Henry Lennox also brought a new element to the household, colder but more brilliant than before. His conversation was flavored with varied knowledge and a slight contempt for his brother's frivolous life. He questioned his brother sharply about relinquishing his profession, and when Captain Lennox replied that he had enough to live upon, Mr. Lennox curled his lip with disdain: "And is that all you live for?"

Despite the friction, the brothers remained attached, the clever leading the patient. Mr. Lennox was calculating, far-seeing, and proud, pushing ahead in his career. Since their first deep conversation in Mr. Bell's presence, their interactions had cooled, drifting apart in opinion and taste. Yet, whenever Mr. Lennox spoke with epigrammatic brilliance, his eyes would seek Margaret's countenance first, listening to her opinion with a deference that was both reluctant and concealed.
