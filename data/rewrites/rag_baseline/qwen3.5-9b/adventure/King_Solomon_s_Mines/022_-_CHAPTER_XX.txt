The dust of the desert, fine as powdered bone, coated everything in sight, turning the horizon into a shimmering mirage of heat and delusion. Yet, amidst this vast, swallowing expanse, I found myself walking alone, a solitary figure detached from the trail behind me, following the silent ribbon of a stream that wound its way from the distant oasis before vanishing into the throat of the dunes. It was in this moment of quiet solitude, far ahead of Sir Henry and Good, that fate decided to intervene with a brutality that bordered on the theatrical.

I halted abruptly, shielding my eyes against the glare, rubbing them as if trying to scrub away a hallucination born of dehydration. There, barely twenty paces ahead, nestled in a pocket of deceptive charm beneath the gnarled branches of a wild fig tree, stood a structure that defied the logic of the wastes. It was a hut, constructed on the rough principles of the Kafirs—wattle and daub, woven grasses—but distinguished by a proper, full-length doorway rather than the narrow bee-holes typical of nomadic shelters. Facing the water, it seemed an invitation to civilization in a land of death.

"What on earth," I muttered to the empty air, "could possibly justify a dwelling here?"

Before the echo of my confusion could fade, the door swung open. Stepping out onto the scorched earth was a man who belonged nowhere and everywhere simultaneously: a white man, draped in animal skins, his face obscured by a beard so jet-black it looked like ink spilled on parchment. He moved with a pronounced limp, dragging one leg as if it weighed a ton.

A wave of vertigo washed over me. Had the sun struck me with such force that my vision had fractured? Was this a trick of the heat? No. The impossibility of the sight grounded me instantly. No hunter ventured this deep; certainly, no hunter would choose to make a permanent home among the vultures. I stared, transfixed, until the movement of the stranger caught the eye of the man trailing behind me. Just then, the rhythm of our footsteps changed as Sir Henry and Good rounded the bend.

"Stop!" I commanded, my voice cracking slightly. "Is that a white man, or have I finally succumbed to madness?"

Sir Henry squinted, his brow furrowing in disbelief. Good leaned forward, peering through the haze. Then, the stranger spoke. It was a sound that shattered the silence of the desert—a raw, guttural cry that echoed off the dunes. He began to hobble toward us, his movements erratic, driven by a desperation that transcended pain.

As he closed the distance, he collapsed, a heap of skin and bone upon the sand. Without hesitation, Sir Henry launched himself forward, catching the man before he hit the ground.

"Great Powers!" Sir Henry gasped, his face pale with shock. "It is my brother, George!"

The commotion drew another figure from within the hut. Clad similarly in skins, clutching a rifle, he emerged with a speed belied by his earlier caution. Upon spotting me, he let out a shriek of recognition.

"Macumazahn!" he hailed, stumbling toward me. "Don't you recognize me, Baas? I'm Jim, the hunter! I lost the note you entrusted to me for the Baas, and we have been stranded here for nearly two years!"

Overcome with emotion, the man threw himself at my feet, rolling in the dirt, sobbing uncontrollably.

"You careless scoundrel!" I shouted, though my anger was tempered by the sheer absurdity of the reunion. "You deserve a thorough sjambocking—that is, a hiding—for your incompetence!"

Meanwhile, the man with the black beard, George, had regained his footing. He and Sir Henry engaged in a frantic embrace, pumping each other's shoulders in a display of affection that suggested a past grievance—perhaps involving a woman, though I never pressed the matter—had been utterly erased by the miracle of survival.

"My dear old fellow," Sir Henry burst out, tears streaming down his cheeks. "I thought you were dead! I trekked over Solomon's Mountains specifically to find you. I had surrendered all hope of ever seeing you again, and now here you are, perched in the desert like an old *aasvoël*."

"I tried to cross those mountains nearly two years ago," George replied, his voice hesitant, unused to speech after such a long silence. "But when I reached this spot, a boulder tumbled from the ridge and crushed my right leg. Since then, I have been unable to move forward or backward."

I stepped closer, extending a hand. "How do you do, Mr. Neville? Do you remember me?"

"Why," George stammered, looking between us, "isn't it Hunter Quatermain, and Good too? Hold on, gentlemen, I feel dizzy again. It is all so incredibly strange, and yet, when a man has ceased to hope, happiness arrives with such violent force!"

That evening, gathered around the crackling campfire, George recounted his ordeal. His story, in its own right, rivaled ours in drama. Less than two years prior, he had departed Sitanda's Kraal attempting to reach Suliman's Berg. The note I had dispatched via Jim had been lost, unknown to him until this miraculous convergence. Acting on local intelligence, he had bypassed Sheba's Breasts, choosing instead the ladder-like descent of the mountains we had recently traversed—a route superior to the one mapped by the deceased Dom Silvestra.

Their journey had been a gauntlet of suffering. Eventually, they stumbled upon this oasis, where tragedy struck. While sitting by the stream, Jim had climbed a bank above to harvest honey from a stingless bee's nest. In his exertion, he dislodged a massive boulder that crashed down, shattering George's leg. Trapped between the advancing desert and the stagnant waters, they survived by hunting the abundant game drawn to the oasis at night, trapping them in pits, and eventually wearing their clothes down to the bare hides of the animals themselves.

"We lived like a second Robinson Crusoe and his Friday," George concluded, staring into the flames. "We hoped against hope that natives might appear to rescue us, but none came. Last night, we resolved that Jim should attempt to reach Sitanda's Kraal for aid. He was to depart tomorrow, but I had little expectation of returning. And now, *you*, of all people in the world, *you*, whom I presumed had long forgotten me and lived comfortably in old England, appear in a chaotic fashion to find me exactly where you least expected. It is the most wondrous and merciful coincidence I have ever witnessed."

Sir Henry then detailed our own adventures, recounting the trials and triumphs until the night wore on. When I presented George with a selection of the diamonds we had secured, his reaction was one of stunned gratitude.

"By Jove!" he exclaimed. "Well, at least you have acquired something tangible for your efforts, besides my worthless self."

Sir Henry chuckled. "They belong to Quatermain and Good. It was stipulated in our agreement that they should split any spoils discovered."

This statement sparked a reflection in me. Turning to Good, I voiced our collective sentiment: that Sir Henry should receive a third portion of the diamonds. Should he decline, his share was to be transferred to his brother, who had endured greater hardship than we had in the pursuit of these gems. We successfully persuaded him to accept this arrangement, though George remained unaware of the generosity until later.

At this juncture, I believe my chronicle should draw to a close. The trek back to Sitanda's Kraal proved arduous, particularly with George's weakened leg, which constantly shed fragments of bone. Yet, we accomplished the impossible. Six months after our return to Sitanda's, where we reclaimed our guns and supplies—much to the chagrin of the old custodian, disgusted that we had survived to claim them—we finally reached safety at my residence on the Berea, near Durban. From there, I bid farewell to all who had shared in this strangest expedition of my long and varied career.

*Postscript:* Just as I penned the final word, a Kafir ascended my avenue of orange trees, bearing a letter wedged in a cleft stick, delivered from the post office. It was from Sir Henry, and since it speaks for itself, I transcribe it in full.

I send you a line a few mails back to confirm that the three of us—George, Good, and myself—arrived safely in England. We disembarked at Southampton and proceeded to town. You should have witnessed the transformation of Good the very next day: impeccably shaved, sporting a frock coat that fit like a second skin, complete with brand-new spectacles, and so forth. I strolled through the park with him and encountered acquaintances, immediately regaling them with the tale of his "beautiful white legs." He is currently furious, especially since some ill-natured individual has printed the anecdote in a Society paper.

Turning to business, Good and I took the diamonds to Streeter's for valuation, as agreed. I hesitate to disclose the sum they appraised, as the figure seems astronomical. They noted that, naturally, it involves considerable estimation, as such stones have never previously appeared on the market in such quantities. Apparently, with the exception of one or two of the larger specimens, they possess the finest water and are equal in every respect to the best Brazilian stones.
