For three days, Feversham drifted in a feverish haze, wandering aimlessly in his talk. For three days, Trench fetched water from the Nile, shared his scant food, and tended to his needs. For three nights, Trench stood guard alongside Ibrahim, fighting off attackers in front of Feversham within the shadowy confines of the House of Stone.

On the fourth morning, Feversham woke to clarity. Looking up, he saw the familiar face of Trench bending over him. At first, the vision seemed part of his lingering delirium—a nightmare visage that had grown grotesquely large in the dark nights of his childhood. He waved a weak arm to dismiss it, but as he scanned his surroundings, reality crashed down upon him. He lay in the deep shadows of the prison compound, beneath a harsh blue sky, on dry, trampled earth. Around him, the ragged figures of fellow prisoners dragged their heavy chains or lay prostrate in the throes of sickness. The meaning settled over him with chilling certainty.

Turning to Trench, he grabbed the man as if fearing he would vanish in the next instant, then broke into a smile.

"I am in the prison at Omdurman," Feversham declared, his voice trembling with disbelief. "Actually *in* the prison! This is Umm Hagar, the House of Stone. It seems too good to be true."

Leaning back against the rough wall, he exhaled with profound relief. To Trench, the tone of satisfaction sounded almost ironic—like the words of a man posing as indifferent to pain and pleasure, someone who claimed such vast experience that joy and sorrow could no longer move him. Yet, Trench knew better. Feversham was not that stoic actor; his madness had proven otherwise. The relief was genuine. The danger of Dongola was over. He had found Trench. He was finally in Omdurman.

That stone enclosure was his longed-for destination, and he had arrived. He could have been hanging on a gibbet hundreds of miles downstream, vultures circling his shoulders, his purpose unfulfilled. Instead, he was within the walls of the House of Stone.

"You've been here a long while," Feversham noted. "Three years."

Feversham glanced around the *zareeba*. "Three years of it," he murmured. "I was terrified I wouldn't find you alive."

Trench nodded grimly. "The nights are the worst. It's a miracle anyone survives a week of them, yet I have endured a thousand." Even to Trench, whose endurance was legendary, the figure seemed impossible.

"A thousand nights in the House of Stone!" Feversham gasped.

"But we can go down to the Nile by day," Feversham suggested abruptly, springing up with alarm as he stared at the thorny fence surrounding them. "Surely we're granted some liberty? An Arab at Wadi Halfa told me so."

"And it is true," Trench confirmed, pointing to the clay bowl of water resting beside him. "I filled that from the Nile this morning."

"I must go," Feversham insisted, lifting himself onto his elbows. "I must leave this morning." His voice rose with excitement, prompting Trench to whisper urgently, "Hush! There are many prisoners here, and among them, many informants."

Feversham slumped back to the ground, driven by both weakness and obedience. "But they won't understand what we say," he argued, his excitement fading instantly. "They can see we speak earnestly. Idris would know within the hour; the Khalifa before sunset. If we speak, we'll get heavier chains and the *courbach*. Lie still. You're weak, and I'm exhausted. We'll sleep, and later today, we'll walk to the Nile together."

Trench lay down beside him and was asleep within moments. Feversham watched him, noting the deep lines etched into Trench's face—the physical toll of those three years.

By noon, Feversham awoke. "Is no one bringing you food?" he asked.

"Yes," Feversham replied. "A boy should come. He brings news too."

They waited until the gate of the *zareeba* swung open and friends or wives of the prisoners entered. Instantly, the enclosure transformed into a cage of wild beasts. The guards distributed their daily ration immediately. The *aseeda*—the damp, pounded cake of sorghum that sustained the townspeople—was given in such meager quantities that it barely kept life going. Strong men fought the weak for scraps, and groups clashed violently. From every corner, emaciated figures hopped and leaped toward the entrance, limited only by the weight of their chains. Some stumbled and fell, lying prone in stolid despair, knowing they would starve that day. Others lunged at the food-bearers, tearing bread from their hands despite the lashes of the guards opening their backs.

Thirty guards, armed with rhinoceros-hide whips, patrolled the area, yet on this one daily occasion, the *courbach* was neither feared nor felt.

Amidst the chaos, a young boy hid behind the crowd, hesitating. Soon, he was spotted. Knocked down, his food snatched away, he screamed. His cries summoned Idris-es-Saier himself, who shoved the attackers aside.

"For whom do you come?" Idris demanded, pushing the prisoners away.

"For Joseppi, the Greek," the boy answered, pointing to the corner where Feversham lay.

The boy stepped forward, holding out empty hands to explain his lack of provisions. Squatting beside Feversham, he began to speak rapidly. As he talked, he loosened a gazelle-skin pouch tied around his waist beneath his robe and dropped it by Feversham's side. Inside was a live chicken. On that bird, Feversham and Trench ate breakfast, lunch, and dinner.

An hour later, they were permitted to exit the *zareeba* and walk toward the Nile. They moved slowly, stopping frequently. During one pause, Trench said, "We can talk here."

Below them, at the water's edge, prisoners unloaded boats (*dhows*) or waded knee-deep in mud. The shore was a cacophony of shouting men, seemingly excited for no reason. The guards were visible but out of earshot.

"Why have you come?" Trench asked.

"I was captured in the desert on the Arbain road," Feversham admitted slowly. "Masquerading as a mad musician who wandered out of Wadi Halfa with a zither."

"Yes," Trench countered. "But you were captured by your own deliberate choice. You came to join me in Omdurman."

"How do you know?"

"You told me. During the last three days, you've told me everything," Trench said, his voice dropping. "You came because five years ago, I sent you a white feather."

"And was that all you told me?" Feversham asked anxiously.

"No," Trench replied, drawing out the word. He sat up while Feversham remained on his side, turning his gaze toward the Nile, hiding his face in his hands so Feversham couldn't see him. "No, that wasn't all. You spoke of a girl—the same girl you mentioned when Willoughby, Durrance, and I dined with you in London. I know her name now. Her Christian name. She was with you when the feathers arrived. I hadn't considered that possibility. She gave you a fourth feather to add to ours. I am sorry."

A long silence followed, broken only by Feversham's slow reply. "For my part, I am not sorry. Nor am I sorry she was there when the feathers came. In fact, I am rather glad. She gave me the fourth feather, yes, but I am glad of that too. Without her presence, without that fourth feather snapping from her fan, I might have surrendered right then and there. Who knows? I doubt I could have survived the three long years in Suakin. I used to see you, Durrance, Willoughby, and former friends going about the duties I had abandoned. You can't imagine how appealing the mere routine of a regiment became to me, even one I cursed. I could have run away so easily. Slipped aboard a boat and returned to Suez. The chance I waited for never came—for three years."

"You saw us?" Trench asked.

"And gave no sign?"

"How would you have reacted if I had?" Trench remained silent.

"No, I saw you, but I ensured you didn't see me. I doubt I could have endured it without the memory of that night at Ramelton, without the touch of the fourth feather keeping that memory vivid. I never would have gone down from Obak into Berber. I certainly never would have joined you in Omdurman."

Trench turned sharply to his companion. "She would be glad to hear you say that. I have no doubt she regrets giving the fourth feather, just as I regret the other three."

"There is no reason for her to regret it, or for you," Feversham said quietly. "I don't blame you, and I don't blame her."
