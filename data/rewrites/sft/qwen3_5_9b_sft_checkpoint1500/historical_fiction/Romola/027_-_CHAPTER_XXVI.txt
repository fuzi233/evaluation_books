By six o'clock, the majority of Florence breathed a sigh of relief as the spectacle of the new Charlemagne finally concluded. While the rolling drums, blaring trumpets, and thundering hooves along the Pisan road mingled with the ringing of excited bells, creating a majestic crescendo for those perched on turreted rooftops, the scene itself was shrouded in twilight. Without sunlight to illuminate the banners, spears, and silken surcoats, nor a thick dust cloud to obscure them, the approaching troops stood out with stark, terrifying clarity. Tall Scottish archers, ponderous Swiss halberdiers, agile Gascon climbers, and heavy cavalrymen who seemed embodiments of knight-errantry—all appeared formidable yet purposeful. To the onlookers, there was solace in knowing this array of strength existed solely to punish the enemies of God. With hearts fortified by this conviction, viewing the splendor of the nobility, the jewel-adorned hilts, the embroidered scarves bearing symbols of piety and valor, the golden chains, and the transcendent canopy borne aloft by chosen youths above the Most Christian King became a distinct, albeit solemn, pleasure. As an old chronicler once noted, simply, "_it was great magnificence._"

For the Signoria, however, standing on their platform against the gates, the grandeur was obscured by a rising unease. They waited to march forth, led by their orator, to greet the mighty guest. Had Messer Luca Corsini been able to deliver his prepared Latin welcome without obstruction, the moment would have been triumphant. Yet, rain began to fall, breeding impatience in both men and horses that shattered the delivery of his carefully studied periods. The scholarly representatives were forced to abandon their formal address, offering a clumsy, impromptu greeting in French. This sudden chaos, however, proved fortuitous for Tito. Stationed behind the Signoria among the secretaries, he found himself thrust forward by the press of horses and officials.

"Somebody step forward and speak a few words in French," Soderini urged.
No man of high standing dared risk another failure. "You, Francesco Gaddi—you can manage it." But Gaddi, doubting his own composure, hesitated. Pushing Tito aside, he whispered, "You, Melema."

Tito stepped forward instantly. With the natural grace of deference that suited him as easily as walking, he delivered the necessary few words in the name of the Signoria. Then, with a fluid courtesy, he yielded the space for the King to pass. His presence of mind, which had deserted him during the terrifying crisis of the morning, served him perfectly now. It was the mark of an excellent liveried servant to remain steadfast when danger was invisible. When later commended for his timely service, Tito laughed off the praise as trivial, allowing Gaddi to claim the credit for the improvised welcome. There was no wonder at Tito's popularity; the standard by which men judge others is most often their own vanity.

Yet, other aspects of the day's reception had proven far more disastrous than anticipated. If the Florentine procession of clergy and layfolk had followed their intricate plans, they would not have found themselves choked into a makeshift route through back alleys, meeting the King only at the Cathedral. Furthermore, if the young monarch, seated beneath his canopy with lance resting on his thigh, had resembled a true Charlemagne rather than a hastily modeled grotesque, the imaginations of his admirers might have been better served. One wished the scourge of Italian wickedness and "Champion of the honor of women" possessed a less miserable leg, fewer than the normal number of toes, a mouth less reptilian in its slit, and a nose and head of less exorbitant proportion. Nevertheless, the thin leg rested upon cloth of gold and pearls, and the face was merely a small interruption amidst black velvet, gold, blazing rubies, and the brilliant embroidery of the canopy. Still, "_great magnificence_" it was.

The people cried out "_France, France!"_ with an enthusiasm matched only by their subsequent tearing of the canopy to pieces, an act performed according to immemorial custom. Royal lips duly kissed the altar, and despite the myriad mishaps, the King and his retinue were lodged in the Palace of the Via Larga. The nobles and gentry dispersed to their great houses, while the terrible soldiery encamped in the Prato and surrounding fields. The business of the day was done.

But the streets still presented a startling aspect, unseen by Florentines before under the November stars. Instead of a gloom broken only by feeble lamps before saintly images or streams of reddish light from open doorways, every house window glowed with suspended lamps. Men walked along with security and ease comparable to daylight—"great magnificence."

Through these illuminated thoroughfares, Tito Melema walked at approximately eight o'clock, returning home. He had labored throughout the day under the weight of hidden anxieties, eventually slipping away unnoticed from the post-prandial gaiety. Now, alone, he hoped to confront his circumstances and adjust himself to the probable outcomes, thereby shedding his childish fears. If only he had not lacked the presence of mind to recognize Baldassarre during that shocking encounter! It would have been happier for him in every regard. He still winced at the knowledge that he was deliberately inflicting suffering upon his father; he would have vastly preferred that Baldassarre prosper and find happiness. But there remained no second path now; conflict was impossible. All that remained was to protect himself.

As he moved from the Piazza di Santa Croce along the Via dei Benci, nearing the turn into the Borgo Santa Croce, his ear caught a sound that was not the merriment of evening revelry, but the vigorous labor of the anvil. Tito gave a slight start and quickened his pace, the sounds suggesting a welcome thought. He knew they emanated from the workshop of Niccolò Caparra, a favorite haunt for all Florentines who admired curious and beautiful ironwork.

"What keeps the giant at work so late?" Tito wondered. "All the better for me. I can finish this little piece of business tonight instead of tomorrow morning."

Preoccupied, he paused momentarily in admiration as he approached the workshop. The wide doorway, situated at the truncated angle of a great block of houses, was surmounted by a loggia roofed with fluted tiles and supported by stone columns with roughly carved capitals. Framed in red light by the outlines of the tiles and columns stood the grand figure of Niccolò in black relief, his huge arms moving in a rhythmic rise and fall, alternately hiding and revealing the profile of his firm mouth and powerful brow. Two smaller, ebony-like figures—one at the anvil, the other at the bellows—accentuated his superior massiveness.

Tito darkened the doorway, standing in silence, for it was futile to speak until Niccolò should deign to pause and notice him. Only after the smith had hammered the head of an axe to the proper sharpness and dismissed it did Niccolò acknowledge him.

"What is it, Messer Tito? Business?"

"Assuredly, Niccolò; otherwise, I should not venture to interrupt you while working past hours, as I interpret that as a sign that your work is pressing."

"I've been at the same work all day—making axes and spear-heads. Every fool who passes my shop puts his pumpkin-head inside to ask, 'Niccolò, will you not come and see the King of France and his soldiers?' And I answer, 'No: I do not wish to see their faces—I wish to see their backs.'"

"Are you forging arms for the citizens, then, Niccolò, so they may have something better than rusty scythes and spits in case of an uproar?"

"We shall see. Arms are good, and Florence is likely to need them. The Frate tells us we shall regain Pisa, and I agree with the Frate; but I would gladly know how that promise is to be fulfilled if we do not secure plenty of good weapons. The Frate sees far ahead, I believe. But he does not see birds caught by winking at them, as some of our folk attempt to imagine. He sees sense, not nonsense. But you are a bit of a Medicean, Messer Tito Melema. Ebbene! I too have been one in my time, before the cask began to run sour. What is your business?"

"Merely to inquire regarding the price of that fine coat of mail I observed hanging here recently. I intend to purchase it for a certain personage who requires protection of that sort beneath his doublet."

"Let him come and buy it himself," Niccolò replied bluntly. "I am particular about what I sell and to whom. I prefer to know my customer."

"I understand your scruples, Niccolò. But this is defensive armor; it cannot hurt anyone."

"True: but it may give the wearer a false sense of security, prompting him to desire to hurt someone if given the chance. No, no; it is not my own work. It is the fine craftsmanship of Maso of Brescia. I would be loath for it to cover the heart of a scoundrel. I must know who is to wear it."

"Well, then, to be plain with you, Niccolò mio, I want it myself," Tito admitted, sensing persuasion was futile. "The truth is, I am likely to undertake a journey—and you know what travel entails in these times. Do you suspect _me_ of treason against the Republic?"
