Time, that relentless editor, had moved our cast of characters forward by a full month. In this interim, a significant portion of our familiar troupe had reconvened at the very provincial town where our saga first unfolded. Among them was Frederic Lightfoot, formerly the *maitre d'hôtel* to Sir Francis Clavering, who had recently secured a promotion of sorts. Having formally petitioned the local nobility and gentry of the county, he announced his acquisition of the "Clavering Arms," a establishment of noted comfort and antiquity. His public manifesto declared that the historic inn had undergone a renovation of the highest caliber, boasting spacious stabling for hunters riding the Dumplingbeare hounds, a newly fitted billiard room, and cellars stocked with the finest vintages, curated without fiscal restraint by C.L. Furthermore, he assured commercial travelers that the pricing structure had been meticulously adjusted to align with the prevailing spirit of economy.

Indeed, the atmosphere within the Clavering Arms was electric. The gateway had been freshly painted, the coffee-room windows gleamed with holiday cheer adorned by Christmas holly, and the Petty Sessions were convened in the card-room of the former Assembly Hall. The traditional Farmers' Ordinary drew larger crowds than ever, delighted by Mrs. Lightfoot's culinary innovations. Her Indian curries and Mulligatawny soup became instant hits, earning high praise from Major Stokes of Fairoaks Cottage, Captain Glanders, and other local dignitaries. These dishes were savored repeatedly at private gatherings and the grand dinner celebrating the inauguration of the Clavering Institute's reading-room. The event was chaired by Sir Francis Clavering, seconded by the Reverend Dr. Portman, with the Vice-Chairmanship ably handled by Barker, Esq.—a man of enterprise who headed the local ribbon factory and directed the Clavering and Chatteris branch of the Great Western Railway, whose tracks were currently being laid with industrious fervor.

Local press, specifically the *Chatteris Champion, Clavering Agriculturist, and Baymouth Fisherman*, a publication renowned for its unwavering loyalty to the British oak and its suitability for advertising, weighed in on the social shifts. They reported that rumors swirled regarding Arthur Pendennis's withdrawal from parliamentary candidacy. Whispers suggested that should Sir Francis Clavering's declining health necessitate a resignation, the seat would pass to a young man of colossal wealth and high aristocratic lineage, soon to wed a lady closely tied to the Clavering Park family. Indeed, Lady Clavering and Miss Amory had arrived for the Christmas festivities, promising a New Year's season of unprecedented celebration.

Through these announcements, the reader may deduce the events of our brief hiatus. While Lady Rockminster grumbled slightly at Laura's preference for Pendennis over her suitor Bluebeard, those privy to the latter's secrets knew the young woman had no other option. The kindly dowager, seeing her ward fulfill the ultimate destiny of young ladies, was secretly pleased. That same night, she confided in her maid, Mrs. Beck—a woman thoroughly briefed by Martha of Fairoaks—who reacted with delight. "Mr. Pendennis's income is substantial," Beck calculated aloud, adding projections for the railway expansion. "Miss Bell has her share, and perhaps more later. We shall manage comfortably. I shall speak to my nephew, George Pynsent, now Chief Secretary of the Tape and Sealing Wax Office, and arrange for Mr. Pendennis to secure a position. Tomorrow, Beck, deliver my compliments to Major Pendennis; I shall visit him at one o'clock." With a resigned mutter, the old lady added, "The Major must be reconciled, and he must ensure his fortune passes to Laura's children."

True to her word, at precisely one o'clock, Dowager Lady Rockminster presented herself at Major Pendennis's residence. The Major, expecting news of a broken engagement between Pen and Miss Amory, received his visitor with courtly bows and genuine warmth. He had just dispatched a letter to his uncle via his new servant, Mr. Frosch, explaining that Miss Amory had happily united with Harry Foker, a match yielding fifteen thousand pounds annually. "Tatham informs me," Pen's note read, "that he has sold three of my Fairoaks fields to the Railway Company at a premium. More details await our reunion."

Lady Rockminster, however, possessed different tidings. "I trust you appreciate my kindness in delivering this news," she stated gracefully. "How well you look! How benevolent you have always been toward that young man!" When the Major expressed relief at the resolution, noting Pen's increased fortune, she gently steered the conversation toward consolation. "We must secure him a wife," she urged. The Major's mind immediately grasped the true purpose of her embassy.

No lengthy negotiation was required. Given the mutual wishes of Pen and his mother, and bolstered by Lady Rockminster's subtle hints—which carried immense weight given her age and status—the Major was swiftly persuaded. Overcome by her determined graciousness and evident affection for Laura, he agreed. During a fleeting moment when the Major lamented that Pen was "throwing himself away," Lady Rockminster delivered a passionate defense of Miss Bell, declaring her a paragon fit for a king. Instantly, the Major's admiration shifted entirely to the young lady.

Thus, when Herr Frosch summoned Miss Bell and Arthur Pendennis to the Major's presence, Laura appeared radiant, clinging to Pen's arm. The Major exchanged cordial greetings with both, showering Laura with additional attention that elicited a deeper blush. "Happy blushes!" the narrator observed, hoping future audiences might share such luminous joy.

With Pen retreating amicably and Blanche bestowing her affections upon her fiancé, Harry Foker, a wave of happiness swept through Lady Clavering's household. The good Begum, unseen for years, rejoiced alongside Blanche. Foker, eager to shorten his period of mourning, pressed for the wedding date. Lady Clavering returned from Tunbridge Wells, and milliners and jewelers sprang into action. Even Sir Francis, buoyed by his wife's good humor, reconciled fully with her, returning to London to preside over his own table and resume his pastimes at the billiard rooms and gaming houses.

One evening, dining at Grosvenor Place with Major Pendennis and Arthur, they encountered Mr. James Morgan, now serving as major-domo. Dressed in black, he offered them champagne with grave politeness. The Chevalier Strong was also present, recounting his continental adventures in high spirits. Under his breath, Strong whispered to Arthur, "My Lady invited me. That fellow Morgan looked as black as thunder when I entered. He is no good here. I shall depart first and meet you at Hyde Park Gate." As the Major departed, Morgan assisted him with his coat, muttering about accepting a temporary role with the Clavering family while avoiding eye contact.

"I have a paper of yours, Mr. Morgan," the Major noted.
"Feel free to show it to Sir Francis, sir," Morgan replied, eyes downcast. "Thank you for your kindness, Major."

Arthur overheard the exchange and the look of hatred accompanying it. Pretending to forget his handkerchief, he fled upstairs. Finding Blanche still lingering near her "siren" (Foker), Pen exchanged a meaningful glance with her. Upon retrieving his handkerchief and exiting, Blanche called out, "Oh, Arthur—Mr. Pendennis! I need you to tell dear Laura something!"

"What?" she asked, closing the door against prying ears.
"Have you told Harry? Does Morgan know everything?"
"I know it," she admitted.
"Have you told Harry?"
"No, no. Won't you betray me?"
"Morgan will," Pen insisted.
"No, he won't. I've promised him—*n'importe*. Wait until after our marriage... Oh, how wretched I am!" she exclaimed, her earlier gaiety vanishing.
Arthur pleaded, "Tell him tonight! It is not your fault. He will forgive anything."
"And give her this—" Blanche handed him a note—*"Il est là"*—with my love. Please ask if she can meet me at Madame Crinoline's at half-past three, provided Lady Rockminster can spare her. I'd love to drive with her in the park." Singing softly, she slipped inside as Morgan ascended the stairs.

As Pen descended to rejoin his uncle, Blanche's piano erupted into brilliant music. Arthur recounted the encounter. "What is to be done?" the Major asked.
"Leave it alone," the old gentleman shrugged. "Let us be thankful we are out of the business and let those concerned handle it."
"I hope to heaven she tells him," Pen worried.
"She'll take her own course," the Major dismissed. "Miss Amory is a wide-awake girl; she plays her own cards. I'm glad you're out of it."

Just then, Strong attempted to interject, but the Major deftly changed the subject to the moonlight on Apsley House and the weather, steering clear of the scandal. He clung to his nephew's arm, walking down St. James's Street, reiterating his advice to avoid further meddling. "It nearly cost you dearly, sir. Take my counsel."
