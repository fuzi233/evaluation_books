"Your beauty was the first that won the place
And scal'd the walls of my undaunted heart,
Which, captive now, pines in a captive case,
Unkindly met with rigour for desert:—
Yet not the less your servant shall abide,
In spite of rude repulse or silent pride."

The following morning, Margaret dragged herself upright, grateful that the night had finally ended. Though she felt unrefreshed, she possessed a strange, lingering restfulness. Everything within the house had proceeded smoothly; her mother had awakened only once during the sleepless hours. A gentle breeze stirred the stagnant heat outside. Although there were no trees nearby to display the playful tossing of leaves, Margaret knew that somewhere—a roadside, a copse, or a dense green wood—a pleasant, murmuring dance of wind must exist. The mere thought of that rushing, falling noise echoed a distinct gladness in her own heart.

She sat working at Mrs. Hale's desk. Once this brief forenoon slumber passed, she intended to assist her mother with dressing. After dinner, she planned to visit Bessy Higgins. She resolved to banish all memory of the Thornton family entirely; there was no need to summon them until they physically stood before her in flesh and blood. Yet, naturally, the very effort to suppress the thought only drove it closer to the surface. From time to time, a hot flush swept across her pale cheeks, coloring them as swiftly as a sunbeam breaks through watery clouds to strike the sea.

Dixon entered the room with extreme softness, approaching on tiptoe. "Mr. Thornton, Miss Margaret. He is in the drawing-room."

Margaret dropped her embroidery instantly. "Did he ask for me? Has Papa returned?"

"He requested you, miss; Master is still out."

"Very well, I will come," Margaret said quietly. Yet she lingered strangely near the shaded window.

Mr. Thornton stood beside one of the panes, his back ostensibly turned toward the door as if absorbed in watching the street. In truth, however, he was terrified of himself. His heart hammered violently at the prospect of her arrival. He could not erase the sensation of her arms around his neck, which he had felt impatiently the previous day; now, the recollection of her clinging defense thrilled him through and through, melting every resolution and stripping away all power of self-control as if it were wax before a flame. He dreaded advancing to meet her with arms outstretched in mute entreaty—that she would come and nestle against him as she had the day before, unheeded then, but never again to be ignored. His heart throbbed loudly and quickly. Despite his strength as a man, he trembled at the anticipation of what he needed to say and how it might be received.

Perhaps she would droop, flush, and flutter into his arms as to her natural home and resting place. One moment he burned with impatience at the thought that she might do exactly this; the next, he feared a passionate rejection, an idea so devastating it withered his future with a deadly blight, causing him to refuse even to entertain it.

He was startled by the sudden awareness of another presence in the room. Turning, he found she had entered so silently that he had not heard her footsteps; the street noises had been far more distinct to his distracted ear than her slow movements in her soft muslin gown.

She stood by the table, refusing to sit. Her eyelids were lowered half over her eyes; her teeth were closed, not compressed; her lips were slightly parted, revealing the white line between their curves. Her slow, deep breaths expanded her chin and her noble nostrils—it was the only motion visible on her countenance. The fine-grained skin, the oval cheek, the rich outline of her mouth with its deep-set corners and dimples—all appeared wan and pale today. The loss of their usual healthy color was accentuated by the heavy shadow of her dark hair, drawn down over her temples to hide all trace of the blow she had suffered. Her head, despite her drooping eyes, was thrown back slightly in her old proud posture. Her long arms hung motionless at her sides. Altogether, she resembled a prisoner falsely accused of a crime she loathed and despised, one from which she was too indignant to defend herself.

Mr. Thornton made a hasty step forward, then recovered himself, walking with quiet firmness to the door—which she had left ajar—and shutting it. He returned and stood opposite her for a moment, absorbing the general impression of her beautiful presence before daring to disturb it, perhaps even repelling it, with whatever words lay in store.

"Miss Hale, I was very ungrateful yesterday—"

"You had nothing to be grateful for," she interrupted, raising her eyes to look at him fully and steadily.

"You mean, I suppose, that you believe I ought to thank you for what I did."

In spite of herself, in defiance of her anger, thick blushes spread across her face, burning into her very eyes, yet she kept her gaze grave and unwavering.

"It was only a natural instinct; any woman would have acted precisely the same. We all feel the sanctity of our sex as a high privilege when we witness danger. I ought rather," she added hastily, "to apologize to you for those thoughtless words which sent you down into the peril."

"It was not your words; it was the truth they conveyed, expressed as pungently as it was. But you shall not drive me off on that ground alone, thereby escaping the expression of my deep gratitude, my—" He paused, standing on the verge of speech. He would not speak in the haste of his hot passion; he would weigh every syllable. His will proved triumphant, and he stopped mid-sentence.

"I do not attempt to escape from anything," she declared. "I simply state that you owe me no gratitude; and I may add that any expression of it would be painful to me, for I do not feel I deserve it. Nevertheless, if it will relieve you from even a fancied obligation, please proceed."

"I do not wish to be relieved from any obligation," he retorted, goaded by her calm demeanor. "Fancied or real, I question neither. I choose to believe that I owe my very life to you—ay, smile if you deem it an exaggeration. I believe it, because it adds value to that life to think—oh, Miss Hale!" He lowered his voice to a tenderness of passion so intense she shivered and trembled beneath it. "To think that circumstances were so wrought that whenever I exult in existence henceforward, I can tell myself, 'All this gladness in life, all honest pride in doing my work in the world, all this keen sense of being, I owe to her!' It doubles the gladness, it makes the pride glow, it sharpens the sense of existence until I hardly know whether it is pain or pleasure to realize that I owe it to one—nay, you must, you shall hear—to one whom I love as I do not believe any man has ever loved a woman before."

He seized her hand tightly, panting as he awaited her response. With indignation, he flung the hand away upon hearing her icy tone; for cold it was, though the words stumbled forth as if she searched desperately for them.

"Your manner of speaking shocks me. It is blasphemous. I cannot help it if that is my first reaction. It might not be so, I dare say, if I understood the nature of the feeling you describe. I do not wish to vex you; moreover, we must speak gently, for Mother is asleep. However, your entire manner offends me—"

"How!" he exclaimed. "Offends you! I am indeed most unfortunate."

"Yes!" she replied, regaining her dignity. "I do feel offended; and I think, justly. You seem to imagine that my conduct yesterday"—once more the deep carnation blush rose, but this time accompanied by eyes kindling with indignation rather than shame—"was a personal act between you and me; and that you may come to thank me for it, instead of perceiving, as a gentleman would—yes! a gentleman," she repeated, alluding to their earlier debate regarding that specific title, "that any woman, worthy of the name of woman, would step forward to shield, with her revered helplessness, a man in danger from the violence of a multitude."

"And the gentleman thus rescued is forbidden the relief of thanks!" he interjected contemptuously. "I am a man. I claim the right of expressing my feelings."

"And I yielded to that right; simply stating that you gave me pain by insisting upon it," she answered proudly. "But you seem to have imagined that I was not merely guided by womanly instinct, but"—here the passionate tears, held back for so long, struggled vehemently to the surface and choked her voice—"but that I was prompted by some particular feeling for *you*—you! Why, there was not a man—not a single poor, desperate soul in that entire crowd—for whom I did not possess more sympathy; for whom I would not have done what little I could more heartily."

"You may continue, Miss Hale. I am aware of these misplaced sympathies of yours. I now believe it was only your innate sense of oppression—(yes; I, though a master, may also be oppressed)—that compelled you to act so nobly. I know you despise me; permit me to say, it is because you do not understand me."
