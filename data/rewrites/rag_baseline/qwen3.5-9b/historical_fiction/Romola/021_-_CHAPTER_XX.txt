The final week of the Carnival transformed Florence into a chaotic theater of noise and violence. Under the pale, frosty sky, the streets teemed with masked processions singing the mandatory hymns introduced by Lorenzo the Magnificent, while the *rigoletto*—a frenzied round dance—stamped out in the piazzas. But the true spectacle was the mob itself, particularly the boys and striplings who formed its most volatile element. At Carnival's peak, they became as loud and uncontrollable as cicadas, wielding poles to block every passerby until a tribute was extorted for supper and bonfire. This culminated in the standing entertainment of stone-throwing, a practice far from monotonous given the variety of injuries inflicted; often, a single victim was not enough, and death claimed multiple targets. The pleasures of the season were thus checkered with grossness and barbarity. A painter attempting to depict them truthfully would create a canvas so repulsive it required mounting face-down, save for the rare historical justification needed to condemn the narrow-minded zealots ignorant of such facts. Yet, amidst this, persisted a more innocent, picturesque merriment inherent to a people of quick animal spirits and sensitive nerves. It lacked the heavy stupor of the northern blood and the stealthy ferocity of the south that turns a brawl into a dagger-stroke.

High morning found the carnival spirit still lingering in lazy recollection of the previous night's jests. Tito Melema moved at a brisk, purposeful pace toward the Via de' Bardi, accompanied by Young Bernardo Dovizi—the keen-eyed Cardinal da Bibbiena of later portraits. Their conversation was animated, seemingly unrelated to the sights and sounds rushing past them along the Por' Santa Maria. Yet, as they gestured and smiled, both men periodically scanned their surroundings with sharp, tactical awareness. Near the turn onto the Lung' Arno, leading to the Ponte Rubaconte, Tito registered a presence he desperately wished to avoid recognition from at that specific moment. His mind was consumed by a singular, high-stakes objective: his betrothal, scheduled for the evening. The decision had arrived abruptly. While preparations for the marriage had been underway—primarily Tito pouring his florins into refurbishing Bardo's sparsely furnished dwelling, excluding the library—the union was originally slated to occur post-Easter, after Tito's year of probation mandated by Bernardo del Nero concluded. However, an urgent summons from Cardinal Giovanni to Rome, offering Tito a chance to assist Bernardo Dovizi in organizing a library using his superior Greek, presented an undeniable path to advancement. Tito had seized the opportunity, urgently petitioning that the betrothal proceed immediately. Delaying the marriage until his return would minimize the separation; being outwardly and inwardly pledged to Romola would render his claim irrefutable against Messer Bernardo or any rival. In the legal framework of the time, the exchange of rings and signing of mutual contracts constituted more than half the validity of marriage, with the nuptial benediction completing the ritual later. Romola's feelings aligned perfectly with his, and the elders' consent was secured.

Now, Tito hastened to snatch a morning visit from Romola, needing to exchange necessary final words before their evening commitment. Recognition was undesirable, especially from Tessa. Unmistakably, he spotted her moving with a timid, forlorn expression toward the very bend of the Lung' Arno he was rounding. As he maintained his discourse with the young Dovizi, an uncomfortable undercurrent confirmed Tessa had seen him and would undoubtedly pursue him. Escape along the direct route by the Arno and over the Ponte Rubaconte was impossible. She dared not approach while he was accompanied, so Tito kept Dovizi close, quickening his stride and weaving new topics into their conversation. Yet, the phantom sensation of Tessa trailing him intensified, becoming increasingly irritating. Perhaps it was exacerbated by a flicker of tenderness and pity for the poor creature, making the decision to evade her notice without acknowledgment a distasteful necessity. Tito persevered, guiding his companion to the door, skillfully executing his farewell without turning his face toward the potential line of sight for a pair of importunate blue eyes. Ascending the stone steps, he attempted to drown unpleasant thoughts in self-deception, convincing himself Tessa might not have seen him, or if she had, might not have followed.

However, reliance on such probabilities was flawed. Upon exiting the doorway, Tito adopted a brisk, oblivious demeanor, unaware of figures to his right or left. Human vision, however, operates beyond voluntary control, capturing a wide field of view. Tito knew instantly—a small figure in a white hood stood near the entrance. Before the physical contact occurred, his mind registered the presence. Then, a hand grasped his arm—not a tentative tap, but a desperate, forceful clutch. Poor Tessa, startled by his rapid exit, had surged forward with a final, frantic effort. When Tito halted and turned, her face betrayed a terrified dread of her own audacity.

"Tessa!" Tito's voice cut through her with a sharpness she had never known. "Why are you here? You must not follow me—you must not loiter at doorways waiting for me."

Her blue eyes widened, filling with tears, yet she remained silent. Tito feared the worst: being seen in the Via de' Bardi with a peasant girl staring pathetically at him. It was a street of high, silent residences, devoid of traffic, yet Bernardo del Nero—or someone equally dangerous—could appear at any moment. Even absent the day of his betrothal, the incident would be awkward and damaging. Yet, driving Tessa away with harsh words was brutality; it was impossible. That cursed folly regarding the *cerretano*, buried quietly for months, had erupted now as an unseasonable crop of vexation. Unable to speak harshly, he spoke hurriedly instead.

"Tessa, I cannot—must not talk to you here. I will go to the bridge and wait for you. Follow me slowly."

He turned, walking rapidly to the Ponte Rubaconte, leaning against the wall of a quaint house spaced evenly along the span, facing the approach Tessa would take. Seeing the little figure advance, her round face pale and saddened since their parting at the Nunziata, would have softened a harder heart than Tito possessed. Fortunately, it was the least-frequented bridge, nearly empty at that hour. He wasted no time addressing her as she drew near.

"Now, Tessa, I have very little time. Do not cry. Why did you follow me this morning? Never do so again."

"I thought," Tessa whispered, struggling against a sob rising at the timbre of his voice, "I thought you wouldn't be so long before coming to take care of me again. And the *patrigno* beats me, and I can't bear it anymore. Every holiday I walk to find you, but I can't. Oh, please don't send me away again! It has been so long, and I cry because you never come. I can't help it; the days are endless, and I don't mind the goats or kids... I can't—"

Sobs tore through her quickly, great tears streaming. Tito felt compelled to comfort her. Sending her away was a necessity, yes; he *had* to act immediately. But telling her anything that would plunge her into hopeless grief was impossible. He sensed deeper troubles looming, yet the immediacy of the moment demanded action over foresight.

"Tessa, my little one," he said, adopting his old, caressing tone, "do not cry. Endure the cruel *patrigno* a little longer. I will return to you. But I am leaving now for Rome—a very long journey. I shall return in a few weeks, and then I promise to come see you. Promise me to be good and wait."

It was the familiar voice; the sound alone was half sufficient to soothe Tessa. She looked up with trusting eyes, still glittering with tears, sobbing despite her utmost efforts to comply. He repeated gently, "Promise me, my Tessa."

"Yes," she whispered. "But you won't be long?"

"No, not long. But I must *leave now*. Remember what I told you, Tessa. No one must know you see me, or you will lose me forever. Go straight home now, never follow me again. Wait for me to come. Good-bye, my little Tessa: I *will* come."

There was no alternative; he had to turn and depart without looking back to gauge her reaction, for he had no time to spare. When he glanced around, he found himself in the Via de' Benci, where the bridge was invisible. Tessa, too trusting and obedient to disobey, would do exactly as instructed.

For that day, the difficulty was resolved. Yet, this resurgence of Tessa's presence, occurring when he could not deceive her into believing the truth, was an unpleasant burden to carry. Tito's mind, however, was currently saturated with the hopeful fervor of his first love, intertwined with ambitious prospects. The future necessity of grieving Tessa was akin to hearing the faint cry of a suffering animal hidden in a thicket while riding a merry cavalcade across a sunlit plain. On his second crossing of the Ponte Rubaconte that day, the thought of Tessa caused no perceptible dimming of his happiness. Well-muffled in his mantle, perhaps less for protection against the chill than to avoid drawing attention to his dainty attire, he leaped up the stone steps two at a time.
