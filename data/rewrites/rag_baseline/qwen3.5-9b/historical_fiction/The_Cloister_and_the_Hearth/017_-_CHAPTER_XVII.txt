Jorian Ketel pressed forward without hesitation, driven by a singular purpose, until he reached the threshold of Margaret's chamber. There, however, the reality of his quest shattered against a tableau of profound stillness. In his place, he found the very man he sought, Gerard, rendered ghostly and inert, his head resting in the lap of Margaret. She knelt over him, a figure carved from marble by grief, utterly mute and petrified. Her eyes, once vibrant, were now dilated pools of glass, reflecting neither the flickering candlelight nor the presence of the intruder; she possessed no awareness of the earthly realm save for the pallid countenance nestled beneath her hands.

Jorian stood transfixed, the candle trembling violently in his grip, casting dancing shadows that seemed to mock the silence. "Where was he, then, all this time?" he demanded, his voice breaking the heavy air. Margaret did not stir, her spirit suspended in a paralysis of terror. Ignoring her, Jorian approached the empty chest, his mind racing to connect the dots of the unfolding tragedy. As he examined the hollow space, comprehension dawned upon him, heavy and grim. The girl's dumb, frozen despair struck him with the force of a physical blow. "This is a sorry sight," he muttered, the weight of the situation settling on his shoulders. "It is a black night's work—all for a few skins! Far better to have gone with us than to end thus. She is past answering me, poor wretch."

He paused, his instinct shifting from observation to action. "Stop! Let us try whether—" Reaching out, he retrieved a small, circular mirror, no larger than his palm, and pressed it gently against Gerard's lips and nostrils, holding it steady against the stillness. When he withdrew it, the surface remained dull, devoid of the mist of exhalation. Yet, in that moment of inspection, a spark ignited within Jorian. "THERE IS LIFE IN HIM!" he cried out, the realization exploding from his chest.

The words, though spoken aloud, seemed to bypass the ears and strike directly at Margaret's consciousness. Instantly, she reacted, a low mutter escaping her lips, as if a statue suddenly infused with blood and passion. With a surge of energy that defied her previous rigidity, she rose and flung her arms around Jorian's neck. "Oh, bless the tongue that tells me so!" she exclaimed, clutching the large, rough-hewn man with an intensity that bordered on ferocity. "There, there! Let us lay him warm," Jorian urged, guiding her frantic movements. In a heartbeat, he lifted Gerard and deposited him onto the bedclothes.

From his belt, Jorian produced a flask, pouring a measure of Schiedamse brandy into his hand twice, and flinging the pungent liquor sharply into Gerard's face. The harsh alcohol acted as a catalyst for recovery; Gerard gave a faint, ragged sigh. Never had such a sound been more joyful to the human ear. Margaret lunged toward him, but halted abruptly, quivering with the fear that her touch might crush the fragile life returning to him. She had lost all confidence in her own strength. "That is right—let him alone," Jorian instructed firmly. "Do not go cuddling him as you did me, or you'll drive his breath back again. Let him alone: he is sure to come to. 'Tisn't like as if he was an old man."

Gerard sighed deeply, and a faint streak of color began to steal across his lips. Seizing the opportunity, Jorian made for the door. Hardly had he reached the threshold when his legs were seized from behind. It was Margaret! She curled around his knees like a serpent, kissing his hand and fawning upon him with desperate devotion. "You won't tell? You have saved his life; you have not the heart to thrust him back into his grave, to undo your own good work?"

"No, no! It is not the first time I have done you two a good turn; 'twas I told you in the church whither we had to take him," Jorian replied smoothly. "Besides, what is Dierich Brower to me? I'll see him hanged ere I'll tell him. But I wish you'd tell me where the parchments are! There are a hundred crowns offered for them. That would be a good windfall for my Joan and the children, you know."

"Ah! They shall have those hundred crowns," Jorian promised. "What! Are the things in the house?" he asked eagerly.

"No; but I know where they are; and by God and St. Bavon I swear you shall have them tomorrow. Come to me for them when you will, but come alone."

"I were made else," Jorian declared, his resolve hardening. "What! Share the hundred crowns with Dirk Brower? And now may my bones rot in my skin if I let a soul know the poor boy is here."

With that, he bolted from the room, fleeing lest any further delay arouse suspicion and draw the authorities to them both. Left alone, Margaret knelt, quivering from head to toe, praying fervently beside Gerard and for Gerard.

Outside, the dynamic shifted again. "What is to do?" Dierich Brower queried, his tone laced with suspicion. "Why, we have scared the girl out of her wits. She was in a kind of fit."

"We had better all go and doctor her, then," someone suggested.

"Oh, yes! And frighten her into the churchyard," Jorian countered with an offhand ease that disarmed all doubt. "Her father is a doctor, and I have roused him, and set him to bring her round. Let us see the fire, will ye?"

His casual manner worked wonders, dissolving the tension. Soon, the group agreed that the kitchen of the "Three Kings" offered far greater warmth than Peter's house, and they departed, having first untied Martin. At the door, Dierich Brower remarked, "Take note, mate, that I was right, and the burgomaster wrong; I said we should be too late to catch him, and we were too late."

Thus, Gerard escaped the twin terrors of prison and grave. How did he achieve freedom? Not through cunningly contrived hiding places nor Margaret's ready wit, but by a sudden impulse in one of his captors—a fragment of humanity remaining in a somewhat reckless heart, aided by a desire for gain. So mixed and seemingly incongruous are human motives, so shortsighted our shrewdest counsels. Those whose moderate natures or gentle fates keep them from the fierce extremes of joy and anguish that our nature is capable of, are perhaps the best, and certainly the happiest of mankind. But to such readers, I should try in vain to convey the bliss unspeakable that now settled upon these persecuted lovers. Even to those who have enjoyed greatly and suffered greatly, my feeble art can present but a pale reflection of Margaret's and Gerard's ecstasy. To sit and watch a beloved face return from the grave to the world, to health and beauty, by swift gradations; to see the roses return to the loved cheek, love's glance to the loved eye, and his words to the loved mouth—this was Margaret's joy, a happiness to balance years of sorrow. It was Gerard's to awaken from a trance, finding his head pillowed on Margaret's arm, to hear the woman he adored murmur new words of eloquent love, and to be showered with tears, tender kisses, and caresses. He never knew, until this sweet moment, how ardently, how tenderly, she loved him. He thanked his enemies. They wreathed their arms sweetly round each other, and trouble and danger seemed a world, an age behind them. They called each other husband and wife. Were they not solemnly betrothed? Had they not stood before the altar together? Was not the blessing of Holy Church upon their union—and her curse on all who would part them?

But as no woman's nerves can bear with impunity so terrible a strain, presently Margaret turned faint and sank on Gerard's shoulder, smiling feebly but quite, quite unstrung. Then Gerard grew anxious and sought to seek assistance. But she held him with a gentle grasp, imploring him not to leave her for a moment. "While I can lay my hand on you, I feel you are safe, not else. Foolish Gerard! Nothing ails me. I am weak, dearest, but happy, oh! so happy!"

Then it was Gerard's turn to support that dear head, with its great waves of hair flowing loose over him, to nurse her and soothe her, quivering on his bosom, with soft encouraging words and murmurs of love and gentle caresses. Sweetest of all her charms is a woman's weakness to a manly heart. Poor things! They were happy. Tomorrow they must part. But that was nothing to them now. They had seen Death, and all other troubles seemed light as air. While there is life there is hope; while there is hope there is joy. Separation for a year or two, what was it to them, who were so young, and had caught a glimpse of the grave? The future was bright, the present was Heaven: so passed the blissful hours. Alas! Their innocence ran other risks besides the prison and the grave. They were in most danger from their own hearts and their inexperience, now that visible danger there was none.
