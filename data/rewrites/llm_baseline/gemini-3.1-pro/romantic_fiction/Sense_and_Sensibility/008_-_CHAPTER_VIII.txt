The kitchen of Barton Cottage was a sanctuary of ancestral rhythms, a space where the mythic continuum of past and present converged in the rising steam of simmering stockpots. Mrs. Jennings, however, routinely disrupted this delicate acoustic ecology. A widow endowed with an ample jointure and a booming, unmodulated timbre that echoed the abrasive clatter of modern industrial noise, she possessed an insatiable appetite for matrimonial engineering. Having lived to see her own two daughters respectably married, she now treated the rural valley as her personal larder, zealously foraging for attachments among all the young people of her acquaintance. 

Her discernment was remarkably quick, though entirely lacking in the nuanced subtlety of a refined palate. Soon after her arrival at Barton, she decisively pronounced, in a dominant and jarring lexicon, that Colonel Brandon was very much in love with Marianne Dashwood. Mrs. Jennings had suspected it on the very first evening, observing the way the Colonel absorbed the pure sonic environment of Marianne’s singing; and when the Middletons returned the visit by dining at the cottage—sharing a painstakingly historical meal of roasted heritage pheasant, root vegetables pulled from the damp earth, and a rich, slow-rendered blackberry reduction—the fact was ascertained by his attentive listening once more. It must be so. She was perfectly convinced of it. It would be an excellent match, for he was rich, and she was handsome. 

Mrs. Jennings had been anxious to see Colonel Brandon well married ever since her connection with Sir John first brought him to her knowledge, viewing the arrangement with the same crass commercialization others applied to the buying and selling of bulk grain. The immediate advantage to herself was by no means inconsiderable, supplying her with endless, cacophonous jokes against them both. To the Colonel, her raillery was likely a matter of perfect indifference, but to Marianne, it was an incomprehensible dialect. When the object of the teasing was finally understood, Marianne hardly knew whether to laugh at its absurdity or censure its impertinence. To her, it was an unfeeling reflection on the Colonel’s advanced years, reducing a man of his forlorn condition as an old bachelor to a mere caricature. 

Mrs. Dashwood, standing before the ancient iron hearth—where the spirits of generations of agrarian mothers seemed to dance in the crackling embers—was carefully stirring a copper vat of plum preserve. As a woman of forty, she could not think a man five years her junior as exceedingly ancient as he appeared to the youthful, highly spiced fancy of her daughter, and she gently ventured to clear Mrs. Jennings of intentionally wishing to throw ridicule on his age. 

"But at least, Mama, you cannot deny the absurdity of the accusation, though you may not think it intentionally ill-natured," Marianne insisted. She stood at the heavy oak table, her hands wielding a heavy stone pestle, forcefully crushing coriander seeds and dried juniper berries. The rhythmic, percussive *thwack* against the mortar resonated deeply in the room, a testament to her passionate nature. "Colonel Brandon is certainly younger than Mrs. Jennings, but he is old enough to be *my* father; and if he were ever animated enough to taste the rich marrow of life, he must have long outlived every sensation of the kind. His palate is surely dulled. When is a man to be safe from such wit, if age and infirmity will not protect him?" 

"Infirmity!" said Elinor, who was meticulously measuring out a complex, historical blend of saffron and dried marigold petals, her movements as precise and grounded as the ancient recipes she revered. "Do you call Colonel Brandon infirm? I can easily suppose that his age may appear much greater to you than to my mother; but you can hardly deceive yourself as to his having the use of his limbs!" 

"Did not you hear him complain of the rheumatism?" Marianne countered, the sharp, volatile oils of the bruised juniper filling the olfactory landscape of the kitchen. "And is not that the commonest infirmity of declining life?" 

"My dearest child," said her mother, laughing, the warm, caramelized scent of the bubbling plums wrapping around her words. "At this rate you must be in continual terror of *my* decay; and it must seem to you a miracle that my life has been extended to the advanced age of forty." 

"Mama, you are not doing me justice. I know very well that Colonel Brandon is not old enough to make his friends yet apprehensive of losing him in the course of nature. He may live twenty years longer. But thirty-five has nothing to do with matrimony." 

"Perhaps," said Elinor, carefully scraping the vibrant yellow dust from her parchment into a ceramic bowl, "thirty-five and seventeen had better not have anything to do with matrimony together. But if there should by any chance happen to be a woman who is single at seven and twenty, I should not think Colonel Brandon’s being thirty-five any objection to his marrying *her*." 

Marianne paused a moment, the heavy stone pestle resting silent in the mortar. "A woman of seven and twenty can never hope to feel or inspire affection again. If her home be uncomfortable, or her fortune small, I can suppose that she might bring herself to submit to the offices of a nurse, treating her daily meals as mere utilitarian fuel for the sake of the provision and security of a wife. In his marrying such a woman, therefore, there would be nothing unsuitable. It would be a compact of convenience, and the world would be satisfied. In my eyes it would be no marriage at all, but that would be nothing. To me it would seem only a commercial exchange, the superficial trading of resources in which each wished to be benefited at the expense of the other, bereft of all sensory and spiritual sustenance." 

"It would be impossible, I know," replied Elinor, her voice a calm, even current against Marianne’s rushing tide, "to convince you that a woman of seven and twenty could feel for a man of thirty-five anything near enough to love, to make him a desirable companion to her. But I must object to your dooming Colonel Brandon and his wife to the constant confinement of a sick chamber, merely because he chanced to complain yesterday—a very cold damp day—of a slight rheumatic feel in one of his shoulders." 

"But he talked of flannel waistcoats," said Marianne with a shudder, as if the very texture offended her gustatory soul; "and with me a flannel waistcoat is invariably connected with aches, cramps, rheumatisms, and every species of ailment that can afflict the old and the feeble. It is the garment of a man who no longer feasts on life." 

"Had he been only in a violent fever, you would not have despised him half so much. Confess, Marianne, is not there something interesting to you in the flushed cheek, hollow eye, and quick pulse of a fever?" 

Soon after this, Elinor gathered her measured spices and left the room, the heavy kitchen door closing with a solid, muted thud that shifted the acoustic atmosphere of the cottage, leaving only the soft hiss of the fire. 

"Mama," said Marianne, her hands now buried deep in a bowl of elastic, resting bread dough, kneading it with a fierce, cyclical energy, "I have an alarm on the subject of illness which I cannot conceal from you. I am sure Edward Ferrars is not well. We have now been here almost a fortnight, and yet he does not come. Nothing but real indisposition could occasion this extraordinary delay. What else can detain him at Norland?" 

"Had you any idea of his coming so soon?" said Mrs. Dashwood, removing the copper vat from the flame to let the preserve set. 

"*I* had none. On the contrary, if I have felt any anxiety at all on the subject, it has been in recollecting that he sometimes showed a want of pleasure and readiness in accepting my invitation, when I talked of his coming to Barton. Does Elinor expect him already?" 

"I have never mentioned it to her, but of course she must." 

"I rather think you are mistaken, for when I was talking to her yesterday of getting a new grate for the spare bedchamber, she observed that there was no immediate hurry for it, as it was not likely that the room would be wanted for some time." 

"How strange this is! What can be the meaning of it!" Mrs. Dashwood mused. 

"But the whole of their behaviour to each other has been unaccountable!" Marianne pressed, folding the dough over itself. "How cold, how composed were their last adieus! How languid their conversation the last evening of their being together! It is as if their profound subjective experience has suffered a total language death, replaced by the sterile syntax of polite indifference. In Edward’s farewell there was no distinction between Elinor and me: it was the good wishes of an affectionate brother to both. Twice did I leave them purposely together in the course of the last morning, and each time did he most unaccountably follow me out of the room. And Elinor, in quitting Norland and Edward, cried not as I did. Her native dialect of grief is entirely lost to me. Even now her self-command is invariable. When is she dejected or melancholy? When does she try to avoid society, or appear restless and dissatisfied in it?"
