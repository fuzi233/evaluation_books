The maternal grandfather of our Virginians, the Colonel Esmond, who had long ago traded the bustle of England for the quiet expanses of the New World, had spent a significant portion of his American leisure composing memoirs of his early life. Within these volumes, Madame de Bernstein—then known as Miss Beatrice Esmond before her marriage—played a role of considerable importance. George had devoured his grandfather's manuscripts countless times, learning to know his kinswoman intimately long before he ever laid eyes upon her. He knew, at least, the lady of half a century prior: young, radiant, and wilful. Now, he encountered her in the twilight of her days.

One wonders, when the cheeks have faded and the eyes grown dim, whether it is a sorrow or a delight for a woman who is no longer a beauty to recall the zenith of her bloom. When the heart has withered, does it still cherish memories of its former freshness and warmth? When the spirit grows languid and weary, do we truly wish to remember how brightly it once burned, how buoyant our hopes, how ready our sympathies, and how keen our enjoyment of life? Thus do they fall—the buds of spring, the roses of youth, the golden harvests of summer—until only bare branches shiver in the winter wind.

"And that was a beauty once!" George Warrington mused as his aunt emerged from the rout, powdered and diamond-studded. "And that ruin was once a splendid palace. Crowds of lovers have sighed before those decrepit feet and been bewitched by the brilliance of those eyes." His mind drifted to a fireworks display at Williamsburg on the King's birthday, recalling the skeletal wheels and empty sockets of exploded Roman candles. The dazzling radiance of Aunt Beatrice's early career flashed before him as he reflected on his grandsire's journals.

Honest Harry had witnessed those same spectacles, yet he was no scholar of books and had not pored over the manuscript with such scrutiny. Even if he had, he likely would not have indulged in the moralizing speculations of his melancholy senior. Mr. Warrington felt no compulsion to inform his aunt of his deep familiarity with her early history, so he remained silent on the matter.

After their meal, the old lady tapped her cane toward her writing desk and instructed her attendant to retrieve a letter resting beneath the inkwell. George recognized the handwriting instantly; it was the correspondence he had carried from home.

"It appears from this letter," the old lady observed, fixing a sharp gaze upon her nephew, "that since your return, there have been certain disagreements between you and my sister."

"Indeed?" George replied smoothly. "I was unaware that Madam Esmond had alluded to them."

The Baroness adjusted a large pair of spectacles onto eyes that once had kindled passions unknown, glanced briefly at the document, and handed it to George. He began to read aloud:

*I have received, and thankfully acknowledged, your ladyship's favor via the Rose packet of October 23rd. I answer you now at a season meant for goodwill and peace among men, though Heaven has decreed that we must still bear our share of earthly sorrow and trouble. My reply shall be delivered by my eldest son, Mr. Esmond Warrington, who returned to us so miraculously from the Valley of the Shadow of Death—as our previous letters informed my poor Henry—and who desires, with my full consent, to visit Europe, despite his brief stay among us.*

*I grieve to think that my dearest Harry appeared at home—in England—under false colors, as it were. He was presented to His Majesty, to our family, and to his own kin as his father's heir, while my dear son George was still alive, though dead to us. Ah, Madam! During the eighteen months of his captivity, what anguish his mother's and his brother's hearts endured! My Harry possesses the tenderest heart of any man living. In the joy of seeing Mr. Esmond Warrington restored to life, he will surely forget the worldly misfortune that befalls him. He will return to comparative poverty without a pang. The most generous and obedient of human beings, of sons, he will gladly yield to his elder brother that inheritance which was rightfully his but for the accident of birth and the providential return of my son George.*

*Your beneficent intentions toward dearest Harry will be more than ever welcome, now that he is reduced to a younger brother's slender portion! Many years ago, an advantageous opportunity arose to provide for him within this province; by this time, he would have been master of a noble estate and numerous negroes, capable of making a figure among the elite, had my wishes been honored and his father's small portion, currently yielding little interest in British funds, been invested in this most excellent purchase. Yet, legal formalities and, I grieve to admit, my elder son's scruples prevailed, causing this admirable opportunity to slip through my fingers! Harry will find that the savings of his income have been carefully accumulated—may he live long to enjoy them! May Heaven bless you, dear sister, for whatever addition your ladyship makes to his modest store!*

*As I gather from your letter that the sum allotted to him has proven insufficient for his expenses in the refined society he has frequented—and considering he is the grandson of the Marquis of Esmond, one who stood near to inheriting his lordship's title—he surely claims equality with any other nobleman in Great Britain—I entrust a sum I had always intended for the poor child's establishment to my eldest son. To do him justice, he holds a most sincere regard for his brother, and I charge him to invest this for Harry's best advantage.*

*This remittance, I trust, will amply cover any expenses incurred by the mistake regarding his station. I wish I could trust his elder brother's prudence as confidently as I trust my Harry's! But I fear that, even during his captivity, Mr. Esmond W. has learned little of the humility becoming all Christians, a virtue I have striven to instill in my children. Should you happen to show him these lines when, by the blessing of Heaven upon those who sail the seas, the Great Ocean divides us, he will know that a fond mother's blessings and prayers follow both her sons. There is no act I have ever taken, nor desire I have ever expressed (however little he may have been inclined to obey it!), but what was dictated by the fondest wishes for my dearest boys' welfare.*

*There is a scratch with a penknife and a great blot upon the letter, as if water had fallen on it. Your mother writes well, George. I suppose you and she had a difference?*

*'Twas a question of money—a ransom I promised to the old lieutenant of the fort who aided my escape. I told you he had a mistress, a poor Indian woman who showed me kindness. Six weeks after my arrival home, the poor creature appeared in Richmond, having navigated the woods along roughly the same path I had taken, bearing the token Museau promised to send upon his connivance at my flight. A commanding officer and a substantial reinforcement had arrived at Duquesne. Charges of unspecified peculation were brought against this Museau; he was arrested, attempted escape, and, less fortunate than myself, was shot on the ramparts. On his deathbed, he sent the Indian woman to me with my grandfather's watch and a line scrawled in his prison cell, begging me to send *ce que je savais* to a notary in Havre de Grace, France, to be forwarded to his relatives in Caen, Normandy. My friend Silverheels, the hunter, assisted my poor Indian companion on her journey. I cannot fathom how she would have escaped scalping otherwise. But at home, they received her harshly. They scarcely offered her a welcome. I will not detail the suspicions they harbored regarding her and me. The poor wretch fell to drinking whenever she could procure means. I ordered her food and shelter, yet she became the sport of our negroes and the subject of scandalous gossip among the elderly fools of our little town. Fortunately, our Governor was a man of sense. I made representations to him and secured a pass to return her to her people. Her profound grief at parting from me only served to confirm the suspicions against her. A fellow preacher denounced me from the pulpit; I was forced to administer a beating to another. Furthermore, I engaged in a violent dispute with Madam Esmond—a rift not yet healed—because I insisted upon paying the heirs designated by Museau the money I had promised for my deliverance. You see, scandal flourishes at the borders of the wilderness and in the New World as readily as in the Old.*

*I have suffered from it myself, my dear!* [interjected George]
