I had just completed my tenure at Oxford, stepping aside for a brief respite before formally assuming command of the estate. My lineage was a graveyard of early succession; my father died in infancy, and my mother followed within a year, leaving me orphaned and solitary before I had truly drawn breath. I possessed little knowledge of my ancestry save for a singular, recurring trait: a compulsive devotion to study. This inheritance manifested primarily in the physical sciences, though I confess my approach has been somewhat desultory. What drives me is not mere data, but the visceral wonder of discovery—the constant hunt for analogies bridging disparate fields, linking physical laws to the metaphysical dreams that plague the rational mind. I suffer from a dangerous proclivity to elevate hypothesis to theory prematurely, a mental quirk best left unexamined.

The house, like the family, is ancient, requiring no detailed inventory to understand its atmosphere. It houses a formidable library, a collection predating the printing press and expanding organically to the present day, shaped by centuries of shifting intellectual tides. Nothing instills a deeper sense of impermanence than inheriting such a legacy; it feels like watching a moving panorama drift past one's eyes, now slowly fading into obscurity. The library, treated as an encroaching state, has swallowed room after room, dominating the ground floor. Its central chamber is vast, walls lined with books nearly to the ceiling, spilling into adjacent spaces of irregular geometry connected by arches, staircases, and hidden passageways.

My primary domain is the main hall, where I spend my hours poring over scientific texts, ancient and modern alike. My interest lies in the trajectory of the human intellect regarding purported knowledge. Figures like Ptolemy, Dante, the two Bacons, and Boyle resonate with me more profoundly than Darwin or Maxwell; they represent the vanishing edge where light breaks into the darkness of ignorance.

On a gloomy August evening, seated in my accustomed position with my back to a window, I was engrossed in reading. Rain had battered the afternoon, but as sunset approached, the clouds fractured, allowing the sun to pierce the gloom. I rose to inspect the view. Outside, the fountain column stood bathed in crimson radiance. Turning back to my chair, my gaze snagged on a single portrait set within a niche amidst the shelves—a likeness of an ancestor. It hung alone, excluded from the gallery, a mystery I had never bothered to solve. The direct sunlight rendered the image vivid; for the first time, the painting seemed to acknowledge my presence.

Compelled by an inexplicable urge, I glanced toward the far end of the room. There, silhouetted against the dimming light, a tall figure reached upward toward a bookshelf. Instantly, the comparative dusk corrected my vision; the figure vanished. I assumed a momentary optical failure. Yet, as I returned to my text, a specific volume required consultation. Upon locating the shelf, I found a void where the book should stand. Simultaneously, the memory of the phantom figure searching for a tome surged back. A frantic scan of the area yielded nothing.

The following morning, the missing volume was precisely where it belonged. No one in the household showed interest in such obscure texts.

Three days later, the anomaly repeated with greater strangeness. On a wall stood the low, narrow door of a closet housing the library's rarest artifacts. The door was thick, framed by a protruding structure crossed with shallow shelves displaying only book spines—fake covers bearing humorous or lost titles, a prank by some eccentric forebear. I favored this deception. To sell the illusion, a craftsman had inserted a genuine, thin volume atop one row, cutting it diagonally so that a limp vellum corner projected beyond the fake spines. Opening this corner revealed manuscript on parchment.

While reading, my eyes drifted to the door. The "book" was gone. Furious, I summoned the butler. He paled, denying any knowledge of the incident. As a lifelong servant, his credibility was absolute, yet his hesitation suggested suppressed information.

Later that afternoon, seeking a passage demanding reflection, I lowered my book. Immediately, I perceived the silhouette of a slender old man in a worn, dark coat, slipping through the masked door into the closet. I sprinted across the room, threw the door open, and peered inside. Empty. Concluding I was suffering a recurrence of hallucination, I returned to my seat. Nervousness gnawed at me. Rising abruptly to verify my solitude, I rushed to the door—and the mutilated volume was back in place! I gripped it; it was immovable.

Bewildered, I alerted the butler. He confirmed he had never seen the object move. "I hoped the old gentleman would fade into oblivion," he admitted quietly. "Few besides yourself have witnessed him. When I first entered service, tales abounded, but they ceased decades ago. Grandfather forbade mention of it under penalty of dismissal, dismissing it as a pretext for the maids' infatuations. Only one footman fled due to the 'haunting.' An elderly villager recounted a legend of Mr. Raven, long-time librarian to Sir Upward, the man whose portrait hangs here. She claimed Raven read forbidden, evil texts, encouraging Sir Upward until both vanished. Sir Upward was never seen again, yet Raven persisted, appearing at irregular intervals. Some believed he was immortal; others, that a ghost revisits the world more plausibly than a centenarian remains alive. The woman described him exactly as I saw him."

"I trust his visit was benign," the butler concluded, smiling nervously.

"I welcome whatever company Mr. Raven brings," I replied, "but discretion is paramount regarding the staff." I then queried whether he had ever noticed the volume displaced. "Never," he insisted, treating it as a permanent fixture. He stepped forward and tugged; the book remained stubbornly fixed.

Nothing more occurred for several days. By the seventh day, the silence of the study had curdled into a tension that demanded release. I had contemplated the manuscript fragment for weeks, attempting to extract it from its invisible bonds, yet every method I employed proved futile. The obstruction remained absolute, a paradoxical lock that defied both force and finesse.

My attention shifted instead to the closet behind the desk. Its stagnant atmosphere had grown oppressive, threatening the structural integrity of the volumes within. What began as a vague intention to organize the collection hardened into a resolve. As I rose from my chair to initiate the overhaul, movement caught my eye near the closet door.

It was not necessarily the old librarian I perceived, though the silhouette matched his description perfectly. It was a shadowy entity, a slight figure stooped under the weight of a shabby dress coat that dragged nearly to the floor. As the figure moved, the tails of the garment parted slightly, exposing thin legs encased in black stockings and terminating in oversized, slipper-like shoes. Without hesitation, I pursued. Whether I chased a phantom or a man made of smoke mattered less than the certainty that I was tracking something tangible.

The figure exited the library, traversed the hall, and descended to the base of the grand staircase. Ascending to the first floor—the domain of the primary living quarters—I followed closely. We passed the principal chambers, continuing through a broad corridor until we reached the threshold of a narrower stairwell leading to the second level. The figure climbed without pause. When I finally emerged onto the landing, I realized I had entered a sector of the house entirely alien to my memory. Deprived of siblings to guide me through childhood explorations, and removed from this home since infancy, I knew the layout only from a brief visit a month prior.

We navigated a series of passages until arriving at a door at the base of a winding wooden spiral. With every step I took, the timber groaned under my weight, yet my guide produced no sound whatsoever. Halfway up the ascent, the figure vanished from view. Reaching the summit, I scanned the darkness, but the shadow was gone. It was not merely absent among the pervasive glooms; it was nonexistent.

I found myself in the main garret, a cavernous space dominated by massive timber beams and soaring rafters. Great voids surrounded me, punctuated only by distant doors and long corridors where the darkness was pierced by the faint, cobweb-filtered light of high windows and small skylights. Awe and a peculiar thrill mingled in my chest; this vast expanse was mine to claim, untouched by previous occupancy.

In the center of the room stood an unpainted enclosure of rough planks, its door standing ajar. Suspecting Mr. Raven might be concealed within, I pushed it open and stepped inside. The chamber was bathed in a pale, desolate luminescence, the kind that inhabits forgotten places, casting a look of profound utility-loss as if the room itself regretted its existence. Dim shafts of sunlight, cutting through a suspended cloud of dust motes, illuminated a tall mirror. It possessed an antique, narrow profile—an ordinary glass framed in ebony. Perched atop the frame sat a black eagle with wings spread wide, clutching a golden chain in its beak, from which dangled a single black sphere.

I had been observing the surface rather than gazing into it when the reflection ceased to correspond with reality. The image of the room dissolved. Was it a trick of the optics, mistaking a canvas for glass? Before me lay a rugged landscape, torn and heather-strewn. In the middle distance rose desolate hills of unusual contour; further still, the jagged peaks of a distant mountain range defined the horizon. Closest to me stretched a tract of moorland, flat and mournfully silent.

Struggling with poor vision, I advanced to inspect the texture of a stone in the immediate foreground. As I leaned in, a large, ancient raven hopped toward me with solemn deliberation. Its plumage was a deep, purplish-black, softened here and there by patches of gray. It appeared intent on finding worms. Unstartled by the intrusion of a living creature into a static scene, I took another step forward to observe it more clearly. I stumbled—likely over the edge of the mirror's frame—and collided directly with the bird. Nose to beak, I realized with jarring clarity: I was no longer in the garret. I stood in the open air, upon a houseless heath.

I turned and scanned the space behind me, but the boundary between solid matter and atmospheric distortion had dissolved. Fog merged indistinguishably with the skyline; clouds draped the contours of mountains that should not exist. The only certainty remained negative: I saw nothing I recognized. Suspecting a profound optical failure, I reached out blindly, stretching my arms to anchor my vision through tactile confirmation. I probed the air in every direction, hoping that where sight failed, touch might succeed, yet my search yielded only void.

Instinct drove me toward the sole animate presence nearby: the raven. It stood a short distance away, observing me with an expression that oscillated between deference and mockery. The absurdity of soliciting counsel from such a creature struck me with sudden clarity, and I spun away, consumed by bewildered dread. Had I drifted into a domain where the fundamental laws of physics and psychology had collapsed? Could a man slip beyond the bounds of order at any moment, becoming prey to chaos? Yet here I was, grounded on soil, hearing the rustle of wind through low-lying vegetation.

"How *did* I arrive here?" I asked—aloud, apparently, for the query was instantly answered.

"You entered through the door," a voice replied. It was harsh, rasping, yet oddly distinct.

I glanced backward, then surveyed the horizon, finding no human silhouette. Panic seized me—the primal terror that sanity was fracturing. Must I abandon trust in my senses and my own consciousness? In that same heartbeat, I realized the speaker was the raven. It stood fixed, gaze upward, awaiting my response. Though the sun was absent, the bird cast a sharp, defined shadow that seemed intrinsic to its form.

"I implore you to assist me in articulating this experience—if comprehension remains viable between us," I began. "I inhabit a state of affairs, an economic system of conditions, a mode of existence so alien to our known world that any vocabulary I employ is merely a sketch of the truth I seek to convey. I fear I have attempted the impossible: narrating the ineffable using language ill-suited to the shapes forming in my mind. I have already recorded statements I wish to retract, for closer approximations threaten to dissolve the very realities they attempt to capture. I feel like one waking from a nightmare, watching a familiar object mutate rapidly through successive forms until its essence becomes unrecognizable."

A bird capable of addressing a man possessed the right to a civilized reply; perhaps, as a bird, a superior claim. A natural croak lent his speech a rough edge, but his tone was not unpleasant, nor were his words rude.

"I did not enter through any door," I countered.

"I witnessed you pass through it!" the raven insisted, positive yet devoid of malice. "With my own ancient eyes!"

"I saw no door!" I persisted.

"Of course not!" he retorted. "Every door you have encountered thus far—and you have seen few—was an entrance. Here, you arrived via an exit! The peculiar aspect of your situation," he continued, musing, "is that the more doors you traverse outwardly, the deeper you penetrate inwardly!"

"Explain my location."

"That is impossible. You possess no concept of 'whereness.' The only method to ascertain your position is to establish residence."

"How do I commence habitation amidst such strangeness?"

"By acting."

"What action?"

"Any. Initiate immediately; delay compounds the difficulty. Until you are settled, exiting will prove as arduous as entering."

"I have found entry too facile; upon escape, I shall never return!"

"You have stumbled in and may, eventually, stumble out. Whether your arrival constitutes misfortune remains to be determined."

"Do you never depart, sir?"

"When I choose, though rarely and briefly. Your world is a half-baked construct, simultaneously childish and arrogantly self-satisfied. It lacks the developmental maturity required for an elder raven—at your service!"

"Is my presumption incorrect—that humanity surpasses avian intelligence?"

"As circumstances dictate. We do not squander intellect on generalization; we accept entities as they manifest. Now, it is my turn to inquire."

"You hold the prerogative," I conceded. "In fact, you possess the absolute right to do so!"

"Well reasoned!" he acknowledged. "Identify yourself, if you possess such knowledge."

"How can I ascertain my identity? I am myself, and therefore must know!"

"If you acknowledge you are yourself, you admit you are not another. But do you truly know you are yourself? Are you certain you are not your own father—or, forgive the crude phrasing, your own fool? Who are you, pray?"

Instantly, I realized I could offer no definition. Indeed, who was I? To declare "I am who!" was tautological. I understood then that I lacked self-knowledge, possessing no basis to distinguish one entity from another. Regarding the name I bore in my previous world, I had forgotten it and cared little to recall it; it signified nothing here, and its origin was irrelevant. I had nearly forgotten that naming was a custom of my former reality. Silence proved wise. What could I say to a creature that pierced accidental appearances to reveal essential truths?

"Observe me," he commanded. "Then identify me."

As he spoke, he turned his back. Instantly, recognition flooded me. He was no longer a raven, but a man of medium height, stooped, emaciated, clad in a long black tailcoat. He rotated again, and the bird reasserted its form.

"I have encountered you previously, sir," I admitted, feeling foolish rather than startled.

"How can you claim familiarity from viewing my posterior?" he rejoined. "Have you ever observed yourself from behind? You have never seen yourself at all! Tell me now, then, who I am."

"I humbly apologize," I answered. "I believed you were once the librarian of our household, though I know not *who* you truly are."

"Why the apology?"

"Because I mistook you for a raven," I explained, perceiving him as clearly as any observer sees a bird or a man.

"You committed no error," he replied. "Whether calling me a raven or believing me one, you granted me existence, which is the totality of what one demands of a fellow-being. In return, I impart a lesson: No one can claim to be himself until he first comprehends that he *IS*, and subsequently defines what *HIMSELF* entails. In truth, nobody is himself, and himself is nobody. There is more to this than you perceive currently, yet no more than you require. You have entered this region prematurely, yet you must acclimate; for home, as you may or may not realize, is the sole locus where one may both exit and enter."

He turned to depart, and once more I beheld the librarian. He appeared unchanged, save that he had assumed his shadow. I knew this seemed nonsensical, yet I could not suppress the observation. I watched him recede until he vanished—whether obscured by distance or dissolving into the heather, I could not discern.

Had I died, unaware? Was I wandering the realm beyond the grave, searching for my station? How might I locate my home? The raven demanded action; what deed awaited me here? Would it elevate me to significance? For now, alas, I was nobody.

I followed the path the Mr. Raven had taken, moving slowly. Soon, a grove of tall, slender pines emerged, and I turned toward them. Their scent greeted me, compelling me to hasten into their embrace. Deep within their twilight gloom, I spotted an object shimmering between two trunks. It possessed no color, resembling the translucent tremor of heat rising from sun-baked earth on a midsummer noon, vibrating like struck strings of an instrument. Its nature remained obscure as I approached; nearing closely, I ceased to see it entirely, the trees beyond appearing strangely indistinct.

Attempting to pass between the stems, I received a sharp jolt, stumbled, and fell. Rising, I confronted the wooden wall of a garret chamber. Turning, I saw the mirror atop which the black eagle had seemingly perched moments prior. Terror gripped me, and I fled.

Outside the chamber, the vast garret spaces exuded an uncanny atmosphere. They seemed to have waited eons for something; it had arrived, and they waited anew. A shudder racked me on the winding stairs; the house had become alien. Something lurked ready to spring from behind! I darted down the spiral, collided with the wall, fell, rose, and ran. On the next floor, I lost my bearings, traversing several passages twice before locating the stairhead. Reaching the summit of the great staircase, I regained composure, sitting to recover my breath in the library. Never again would I ascend that final, terrifying flight! The garret at the apex dominated the entire structure, pressing down as if threatening to crush me out of existence. It was the brooding brain of the edifice, teeming with mysterious inhabitants, any of whom might emerge into the library at any instant. Safety was nonexistent. I resolved to sell this dreadful place, where an aerial portal remained perpetually open to entities of non-human vitality. I would purchase a crag in Switzerland and construct a single-story wooden nest, guarded by a grand peak sending down nothing worse than tons of crushing rock!

Deformity and sickness were taxed heavily; indeed, no legislation of their Princess was more enthusiastically embraced than those measures designed to make poverty subservient to wealth.

Desperate, I took to my heels. No one pursued me beyond the gate. However, a lumbering figure sitting nearby, chewing on a hunk of bread, picked up a stone to throw after me. In his stupid eagerness, he hurled not the stone, but the bread. I snatched it; he dared not follow to reclaim it. Beyond the walls, they were cowards. I retreated a few hundred yards, collapsed onto the grass, devoured the bread, and fell into a sound sleep. The hot sunlight renewed my strength.

Night fell. The moon descended, casting a friendly glow, as if claiming an old acquaintance. It was the same silver orb that had witnessed my terror during my first night in this strange world. A cold wind drifted from the gate, carrying an evil odor, yet it failed to chill me, for the sun had plumped me with stored warmth. I crept back into the city. There, I found the few souls remaining outdoors crouching in corners to escape the biting blast.

Walking slowly down the long, narrow street, a huge white shape bounded across my path, flashing briefly in the moonlight before vanishing. Eager to track it, I turned down the next opening—a lane so narrow it barely accommodated passage—which led into a wider thoroughfare. The moment I entered, I spotted the creature on the opposite side, lurking in the shadow. It was following me like a dog, trailing what I initially mistook for a man. Every few moments, the figure glanced over his shoulder at the beast, yet neither spoke nor attempted to drive it away.

Where the figure crossed a patch of direct moonlight, I observed a chilling phenomenon: he cast no shadow. He was himself a flat, superficial shadow, existing in two dimensions. Yet, he remained an opaque void; he did not merely darken objects behind him, he rendered them invisible. Within his own shadow, he was blacker than darkness itself. In the moonlight, he appeared as though he had drawn his shadow up around him; not a hint of it moved beside or beneath him. Meanwhile, the gleaming leopardess that trailed so close at his heels, appearing as the white shadow of his blackness, dragged her own gliding shadow—a solid black smear—across the ground alongside her.

When they passed together from the shade into the moonlight, the Shadow deepened into absolute blackness, while the animal flared into radiant brilliance. I walked abreast of them on the opposite side, my bare feet striking the flat stones. The leopardess never turned her head or twitched an ear. The Shadow seemed to glance at me; for an instant, I lost his profile, seeing only a sharp, upright line. That exact moment, the wind caught me, blowing through my clothes and flesh. I shuddered from head to toe, and my heart hammered against my ribs like a pebble rattling in a child's toy.

I diverted into a service alley, seeking refuge within a stone archway. As I stepped into the gloom, I paused to survey the scene illuminated by the pale, indifferent moonlight. Before I could process the geometry of the space, a figure emerged—a woman, trembling violently, who froze mid-step to stare back at me. The silence stretched, taut and brittle, until a massive leopard erupted from the shadows. Its coat was a chaotic map of white fur marred by dark, irregular blotches, and it surged across the archway with predatory grace.

Instinctively, the woman pressed herself against my side, her breath hitching in ragged gasps. Pity, sharp and sudden, flooded my chest. I wrapped an arm around her rigid frame. "If that brute comes closer," I stated, my voice level despite the chaos, "I will engage it. You must flee."

"Thank you!" she whispered, her voice cracking. "Have you encountered this before?"

"Several times," she admitted, her knees buckling slightly under the weight of terror. "This is the Princess's pet. You are a stranger; otherwise, you would recognize her."

"I am a stranger," I confirmed. "Yet, is she permitted to roam freely?"

"She is confined to a cage, muzzle locked tight, paws encased in gloves of crocodile leather. She is chained, yet escapes with alarming frequency, draining the blood of any child she can catch. Fortunately, Bulika lacks many mothers." Her composure shattered instantly. "I wish I were at home!" she sobbed. "The Princess returned only last night, and the leopardess is already loose! "All strangers are not enemies," I countered, maintaining a defensive posture. "The beast cannot reach you until I have dealt with it. By then, you will be inside. You possess a roof over your head! What a ferocious gale it is!"

"Escort me home safely, and I shall grant you shelter from this storm," she pleaded.

"We must wait briefly," I instructed.

Her answers revealed a society built on stagnation and absurdity. She explained that her people subsisted solely on digging for precious stones in their cellars, remaining perpetually wealthy because labor was deemed a disgrace. Their ancestors had hoarded resources centuries ago, creating a static economy where wealth was inherited rather than earned. When funds ran low, they liquidated gemstones, convinced the earth held infinite reserves.

"But surely poverty exists?" I pressed.

"It is an abstraction we ignore," she dismissed. "When someone falls into destitution, we simply cease to acknowledge them. This is how we maintain our status. We intend to remain rich forever."

"And when the mines are exhausted? When the stockpile depletes?"

"We have untold quantities, and the ground yields endlessly," she insisted, her eyes fixed on something beyond my shoulder. "Besides, no foreign horde dares approach. They are paralyzed by fear of our Princess. She commands the air, the water, the earth—and presumably the fire. She rules absolutely, accountable to no one."

Periodically, she halted, scanning the darkness behind her with paranoid intensity. I questioned the xenophobia that permeated the city. "Why such animosity toward outsiders?"

"Their presence defiles the sanctity of our city," she declared. "We are ancient and noble compared to any other nation. Therefore, we expel all strangers before nightfall."

"How, then, can you admit me?"

"I shall make an exception for you."

"If no provision exists for housing travelers, why not build one?"

"Such a structure would be demolished, and its owner consumed by flames. How can purity be maintained unless we keep the base masses at a proper distance? Dignity is a fragile construct!"

She recounted that the Princess had reigned for millennia, wielding dominion over the elements themselves. Finally, she resolved to lead me to safety. We navigated a labyrinth of narrow passages, reaching her residence without encountering a single living soul. The building stood in a broader thoroughfare, flanked by towering structures, accessed via a steep, winding staircase.

As we ascended, her confidence evaporated. Fear seized her, and she bolted upward, scrambling up the final flights with frantic haste. I arrived moments later, only to find the door slamming shut in my face. I stood confounded on the landing, trapped in a narrow gap between opposing doors—just enough space for a man to lie prone. Exhausted and unwilling to soil the pristine air of Bulika further, I accepted the cramped shelter, resigned to the indignity of my position.

At the base of the stairs, the moonlit street churned under the weight of an unwholesome, inhospitable wind. Inside my retreat, however, the air remained stagnant and still. Composing myself for rest, I closed my eyes, only to snap them open moments later. Rising above the uppermost step was the head of the shining creature I had tracked—the White Leopardess—following the Shadow.

The moment our gazes locked, she halted. Her posture shifted instantly from pursuit to retreat; she lowered her haunches, presenting her tail first, signaling submission. I surged upward, cutting off her escape route. With nowhere to turn, she executed a desperate somersault, throwing herself backward head-over-tail before scrambling to her feet and vanishing down the stairwell in a blur of motion.

I descended after her, scanning the street from end to end. Empty. I returned to my hard couch, the realization settling heavily: two predators prowled Bulika now—one spotted, one pristine. While I lacked the inclination to risk my life for most citizens, the preservation of a child's existence warranted the sacrifice of my own comfort. I resolved to stand vigil at the door until dawn.

Silence held for a long interval, broken only by the slow, deliberate click of a latch. I looked up to find the door ajar. Sliding inward silently, I found not the woman I had befriended, but the Muffled Woman of the Desert. She said nothing, guiding me several paces into an empty, stone-paved chamber and indicating a rug on the floor. Wrapped within its fibers, I collapsed once more. As the door sealed behind me, I heard the outer threshold open and close again. Darkness reclaimed the room, illuminated only by the pale spill of moonlight.

Sleep eluded me. Soon, a stifled moan drifted through the air, persisting for some time before erupting into the cry of a child, followed immediately by a shriek that tore through the quiet. I launched myself into the passage. From a side door emerged the White Leopardess, clutching a newborn infant in her jaws, carrying it with the same possessive ferocity she reserved for her own cubs.

I tackled her, forcing her to release the bundle. The infant struck the stone slabs with a piteous wail. Instantly, the Muffled Woman appeared. Stepping over the writhing beast and myself, she scooped up the child and vanished into the darkness. Moments later, she returned, lifting me from the animal's grasp, opening the door, and pushing me gently outside. Trailing at my heels was the leopardess.

*She has abandoned me,* I thought, the cynicism sharp in my mind. *Left me to settle accounts with the beast at her leisure. But we shall have a reckoning.*

Fear drove me down the stairs, anticipating a leap onto my back. Instead, she followed quietly. At the foot of the stairs, I turned to seize her, but she sprang effortlessly over my head. When I pivoted to face her, she was already crouched at my feet. I stooped, running a hand over her sleek, white coat. She responded by licking my bare feet with a hard, dry tongue. I patted her, a surge of genuine tenderness flooding my chest. Even if this affection was a mask, how else could I distinguish truth from fabrication in a world so devoid of it?

We rose together. Suddenly, a bulky object hit the cobblestones with a heavy, wet squelch, landing mere yards from us. Rushing to inspect it, I found a pulpy mass, barely retaining the form of a woman. Thrown from a neighboring window, undoubtedly.

Scanning the surroundings, I saw the Shadow walking along the opposite side of the street, flanked once more by the White Leopardess. I moved to intercept, reasoning that she was likely acting under compulsion rather than free will. Yet, as I closed the distance, she spun and lunged with a hideous snarl. Instinct recoiled me. Immediately, she resumed her position behind the Shadow.

I advanced again; she attacked again, her eyes blazing like live emeralds. A third attempt yielded the same result: she snapped at me like a rabid dog, biting my leg. My resolve shattered. I cried out, and the creature paused, turning her gaze with a silent accusation: *Why force me?*

Angry at my own failure, I turned away. I had wasted precious time in this place. Nightfall demanded action; I would go straight to the Palace. High above the city's heart, circled by formidable defenses, it loomed—a fortress disguised as a residence. Its fortifications, however, mirrored the city's decay: neglected, crumbling, and seemingly useless for centuries. Great gates stood open across a rocky chasm, though the hollow before them showed no trace of water.

A suffocating silence pervaded the structure, making the mere thought of noise terrifying. Sleep seemed to seep into the stones, rendering the moonlight discordantly awake. Like an outcast dog, I paced the walls until I reached a small recess containing a stone bench. Taking refuge from the wind, I lay down and, despite the chill, succumbed rapidly to sleep.

Waking abruptly, I felt something leap upon me, licking my face with the rough tongue of a feline. *The White Leopardess,* I realized. *She intends to drain my blood—and why not? It costs less to yield than to fight.*

I lay still, bracing for pain. None came. Instead, a pleasant warmth diffused through my limbs. Stretched along my back, she pressed as close as anatomy allowed, her body heat penetrating mine. Her breath, utterly lacking the scent of wild prey, swathed my head and face in a genial atmosphere. Conviction seized me: her intent was benevolent. Turning like a sleepy child, I draped an arm over her and sank into profound unconsciousness.

When consciousness returned, I imagined lying warm and soft in a familiar bed. *Am I home?* The scents of a known garden crowded my senses. Rubbing my eyes, I looked out. I lay on bare stone in the heart of a hateful city. I sprang up. Had I truly shared a bed with a leopardess, or was it a fever dream? The residual warmth of her fur confirmed the former. She had just departed.

Leaving the recess, I carried a hope both potent and undefined. One fact remained absolute: I must find the Princess. I possessed leverage over her, forged in the debt of saving her life and sustaining it at the cost of my own vitality. This reflection ignited the courage needed to confront her, regardless of her nature.

Making a circuit of the fortress, I returned to the open gates, crossed the ravine-like moat, and emerged onto a paved courtyard. Towering poplars stood at regular intervals, their massive trunks creating a rhythmic barrier. In the center rose a specimen distinct from the rest; its upper branches spread wide, mimicking the fronds of a palm. Through the gaps between these colossal stems, I gained glimpses of the palace—a structure alien to European design yet unmistakably Indic in origin. It was elongated and low, crowned by lofty corner towers and a singular, immense dome that pierced the roofline to half the height of the spires. The main entrance dominated the facade: a low arch, nearly an ellipse, standing wide and unguarded.

Unchallenged, I passed through into a vast hall shaped like a long ellipse. To one side stood a cage housing a massive leopardess. Chained by a thick steel collar, her muzzle and paws were bound, rendering her immobile save for her gaze. Her coat was snow-white, marked with dark ovals, and her eyes—wide, unblinking orbs with canoe-shaped pupils and verdant irises—fixed upon me. Yet, not a muscle twitched; not a whisker shifted. Her tail extended behind her, rigid as an iron rod. She appeared suspended between life and death, a statue of predatory potential. From this vestibule, two low passages branched into a labyrinth of narrow, irregular corridors.

At a choke point barely wide enough for two men, a page collided with me. He recoiled in terror, only to scan my features and instantly swap fear for insolent bravado. "To see the Princess?" he demanded, puffing out his chest.

"A likely request," he scoffed. "I haven't seen Her Highness this morning!"

Seizing him by the back of the neck, I shook him violently. "Take me to her immediately, or I will drag you until I locate her. Let her know precisely how her servants receive her guests."

He glared, then began to lead me like a blind man's guide, pulling me toward a large kitchen where several servants moved with sluggish, dreamlike efficiency. I expected them to swarm and eject me, but instead, they froze, eyes widening as they stared—not at me, but at something behind my back. Their expressions grew ghastly. Turning, I saw the white leopardess regarding them with an intensity that would have shattered lesser spirits. One servant, realizing the threat was passive, slowly recovered his composure. I released the boy.

"Take me to the Princess," I commanded.

"She has not yet left her chambers, my lord," he replied nervously. "Inform her that I await an audience."

"Will you give me your name?"

"Tell her that one who knows the White Leech seeks her."

"She will execute me for delivering such a message. I dare not."

"You refuse?"

He cast a wary glance at my companion and retreated. The others remained transfixed by the beast. I turned to the creature, now standing with her muzzle lowered to my heel, radiating a warm splendor in the gloom. I stooped and patted her flank. She looked up; the mere movement of her head caused the remaining servants to scatter in panic. Rising on her hind legs, she placed her paws on my shoulders. I embraced her. She pricked her ears, broke free, and vanished into the shadows in an instant.

The man I had dispatched earlier reappeared. "Please, come this way, my lord."

My heart hammered, bracing for impact. I followed him through numerous passages until we entered a chamber so vast and dark that the walls dissolved into obscurity. A single patch of light illuminated the floor, surrounded by absolute blackness. Looking upward, I spotted an oval aperture high in the ceiling, framed by the joints of black marble blocks. The light source revealed close-fitting slabs of the same material covering the floor. Later, I deduced the elliptical walls were also clad in light-absorbing black marble, while the roof formed the long half of an ellipsoid, with the opening positioned directly over one focus of the floor's ellipse. Faint reddish lines flickered briefly before vanishing.

Suddenly, a radiant form materialized in the center of the darkness, casting a brilliance in every direction. Draped in soft white robes, her hair cascaded like a cataract, jet-black against the marble. Her eyes burned with a luminous void; her limbs resembled warm ivory. She greeted me with the innocent smile of a girl, appearing freshly crossed the threshold of womanhood.

*Alas,* I thought, *my calculations of danger were flawed. Is this the woman I rescued—the one who struck me, scorned me, and abandoned me?*

I stood gazing into the dark; she returned the stare, scanning the void as if hunting for me. Then, she vanished.

"She rejects me!" I concluded.

Instantly, her eyes flashed from the gloom, locking directly onto mine. She had found me.

"You have found me at last!" she declared, placing a hand on my shoulder. "I knew you would!"

My frame quivered under conflicting impulses, each sensation indistinguishable from the other. I was simultaneously drawn to and repelled by her.

"You shiver," she observed. "This place is cold for you. Come."

Silent, struck dumb by her beauty and sweetness, I allowed her to lead me to the pool of light. She lingered an instant. "You have grown brown since I last saw you," she noted.

"This is almost the first roof I've been under since you left me," I replied.

"Whose was the other?"

"I do not know the woman's name."

"I would gladly learn it! The instinct of hospitality is foreign to my people!"

Taking my hand again, she guided me past a curtain of black fabric to reveal a white staircase. Ascending, she led me to a magnificent chamber.

"How you must miss the hot, flowing river!" she remarked. "Yet, there is a bath in the corner, free of White Leeches. Beneath your couch lies a garment. When you descend, I shall be in the room to your left, at the foot of the stairs."

Left alone, I accused my own presumption. How could I judge this lovely woman as an agent of evil when she treated me with sisterly affection? Where had the marvelous transformation occurred? She had struck me down previously, yet now welcomed me with an embrace. Had she known my doubts? Could she explain the shift? Could I trust her explanation? Regarding her hospitality, I had earned it and might accept it tentatively until forming a definitive judgment. Could such beauty coexist with such wickedness? If so, how? Unable to resolve the paradox, I deferred the question.

Clear as crystal, the water in the great white bath, sunk into the marble floor, sparkled invitingly. Since leaving home, I had seen only the crushed paw of the leopardess, the pools in the track of the wounded beast, and the pitcher-plant's secretion. This water looked celestial. I plunged in.

Immediately, my brain filled with a strange, delicate odor—one I did not entirely relish. Doubt resurfaced. Had she medicated the water? Enchanted it? Was she manipulating me unlawfully? Furthermore, how did water exist within the palace while the city outside remained parched? Remembering the leopardess's crushed paw, I sprang from the bath. What had I been washing in?

Again I saw the fleeing mother, again the howl, again the limping beast. But what mattered the source? Was not the water sweet? Was it not simply the pitcher-plant storing moisture for the weary traveler? Water came from heaven; the wellspring was irrelevant. Yet, I did not re-enter.

Donning the white woolen robe, embroidered with intricate patterns at the neck and hem, I descended the stairs to the room designated for me. It was circular, constructed entirely of alabaster, devoid of windows. Light permeated the space everywhere, a soft, pearly shimmer rather than harsh brightness. Vague, shadowy forms drifted along the walls and low dome, resembling loose rainclouds over a grey-blue sky.

The Princess awaited me, draped in a robe woven with argent rings, discs, rectangles, and lozenges—a silver mail that fell unbroken from her neck to hide her feet, though its long, open sleeves left her arms bare. On an ivory table rested cakes, fruit, an ivory jug of milk, a crystal jug of pale rose-colored wine, and a white loaf.

"Here we do not kill to eat," she stated. "But I believe you will appreciate what I offer."

I expressed that nothing could surpass what I saw. Seating herself on a couch beside the table, she gestured for me to join her. Pouring a bowl of milk, she handed me the loaf, asking me to tear off a piece. Then, filling two silver goblets of grotesquely graceful craftsmanship from the wine jug, she offered them.

"You have never tasted wine like this!" she proclaimed.

I drank, marveling. Every flower of Hybla and Hymettus seemed to have sent its spirit to swell the soul of that vintage.

"Now that you are prepared to listen," she continued, "I must strive to make myself intelligible. Our natures differ profoundly, making this difficult. Men and women live but to die; we, however, live to continue living. Old age is a horror to you; to us, it is a cherished desire. The older we grow, the closer we approach perfection. Your

I collapsed onto the mattress, the springs screaming under my weight, and forced my mind to dissect the narrative she had just spun. In her feverish recounting, she had inadvertently stripped away the ambiguity surrounding her state in the forest. The leopardess leapt; the princess lay prone by the stream; the water had shattered her enchantment. Her own testimony confirmed the peril of the Little Ones was now imminent—I had inadvertently secured the life of their sole predator! This deduction held firm until the wine claimed me.

When consciousness returned, night had swallowed the world. A single lamp, suspended from the ceiling, cast a sterile, ghostly luminescence through the chamber. A narcotic lethargy seized me, lifting me from the earth as if drifting on a twilight ocean. Pain was absent. Pure existence was bliss. Or so I thought.

Then, the agony struck—a sudden, violent injection of mortal suffering that pierced straight through my heart. Sharpness itself! A sickening sting that paralyzed every muscle. I tried to raise my hand to clutch my chest, but an invisible force pinned my limb to the sheet. The pain receded slightly, yet my entire body remained locked in rigid suspension. Something malevolent sat atop me—hateful, crushing. I wanted to fight, to scream, but my will screamed in vain against the paralysis. I resigned myself to passivity.

A soft pressure touched my cheek, forcing my head back into the pillow, followed by the heavy weight of a body collapsing across my torso. My breathing expanded instantly; the suffocating weight lifted. I opened my eyes.

The princess stood over me on the bed, staring into the room with the vacant intensity of a dreamer. Her great eyes were clear, unnervingly calm. Her lips curled in a expression of satisfied passion, smeared with a streak of crimson. She met my gaze, leaned down, and struck me across the face with a handkerchief. It felt like a blade drawn across my eyelids; for moments, absolute blindness descended.

I heard a dull, heavy impact—the sound of a large, soft-footed beast landing mid-stride. Opening my eyes, I watched the massive sweep of a long tail disappear through the half-ajar doorway. I launched myself after it. Gone. Completely vanished.

I sprinted down the stairs and burst into the alabaster hall. The moon hung high, bathing the interior in a pale, sun-faded radiance. The princess was nowhere to be seen. I needed her proximity; without it, I was prey, a living fountain for a thirst demon. She tolerated me only to consume me easier. My waking eyes dared not fear her, but closing them invited her arrival. Unable to see her, I felt her omnipresence, lurking in every secret cavern of sleep. Safety existed only in direct visual contact.

Outside the alabaster hall, total darkness reigned. I navigated blindly, hands and feet scraping against the void, until I brushed against a curtain. Pushing it aside, I entered a vestibule dimly lit by moonlight. Inside, the leopardess's cage served as the stage for a desperate, silent combat. Two vastly different forms—one human, one bestial—were entangled in a chaotic knot of limbs, writhing in tight embrace.

The struggle had lasted mere seconds when the leopardess emerged from the cage, walking calmly toward the open door. As I hurried to follow, I glanced back. The spotted leopardess remained in the cage, crouching motionless as before. High in the sky, the moon shone bright and clear. The bodiless shadow I had witnessed the previous night walked through the trees toward the gate, trailed closely by the leopardess, her tail swinging lazily.

I followed at a distance, moving with silent precision. Neither paused to acknowledge me. We passed through the open gate, descending toward the city, which lay quiet beneath the moonlight. The lunar face was utterly still, its tranquility radiating an aura of expectant anticipation.

The shadow moved directly to the staircase where I had lain the night prior. He ascended without hesitation, and the leopardess followed. I quickened my pace, but a split second later, a cry of pure horror tore through the air. Followed by the thud of something soft and heavy falling between me and the stairs, I looked down. A body lay crushed and frightfully blackened at my feet, yet unmistakably the woman who had brought me home and barred the door.

Petrified, I stood frozen as the spotted leopardess bounded down the stairs, a baby clamped firmly in her jaws. I lunged to snatch the infant before she could turn at the bottom. In that exact instant, a flash of brilliant white erupted from behind me. The white leopardess, resembling a bar of molten silver, shot through the moonlight and seized the spotted one by the throat.

She dropped the child. I grabbed it and stepped back to witness the duel.

What a spectacle. One dominated, then the other; both so focused that the only sounds were low growls, whimpering cries, and snarls of hatred, punctuated by the frantic scrambling of claws as they fought for purchase on the pavement. The spotted leopardess was larger, and my instinct screamed for her protection. Yet, I quickly realized that while equal in strength and agility, the white leopardess possessed superior endurance. She never loosened her grip on the other's neck.

From the spotted beast's throat erupted a howl of agony, rapidly ascending through swift gradations into the long-drawn crescendo of a woman's ultimate wail. The white leopardess released her jaws; the spotted one recoiled, rising onto her hind legs.

Erect in the moonlight stood the princess. A chaotic vortex of shadows swirled over her pristine whiteness—the leopard's spots crowded together, fleeing toward the refuge of her eyes, where they merged and vanished. The last few, outrun and delayed, blended into the cloud of her streaming hair, leaving her radiant as the moon after a legion of vapor spirits has been scattered by the wind. Save for a thin thread of blood trickling down the white column of her throat from the wounds of her adversary's terrible teeth.

She turned, taking a few steps with the gait of Hecate, then fell, instantly obscured by returning spots, and fled at a long, stretching gallop. The white leopardess turned as well, springing upon me, tearing my arms apart, snatching the baby as it fell, and flying with it down the street toward the gate.

"Turn," the command came, sharp and devoid of warmth.

I pivoted, tracking the spotted leopardess as she surged upward, tearing across the brow of the hill toward the palace gates. My vision caught her only in fleeting glimpses—a blur of muscle and fur against the stone—before she vanished into the courtyard beyond.

Reaching the entrance hall, I found the Princess mid-motion, hastily wrapping a robe around her frame where it had pooled on the floor. The blood had already congealed on her skin, dried stiff by the chill of her frantic flight. Upon seeing me, a flash of irritation crossed her features, causing her to turn her head sharply away. Then, forcing a brittle smile, she faced me again.

"I have encountered a minor mishap!" she declared, her voice tight. "Hearing rumors that the Cat-Woman has returned to the city, I descended to expel her. But she brought one of her abominable creations along. It leapt upon me, driving its claws into my throat before I could strike!"

She shivered violently. I felt a surge of pity, though my intellect screamed that she was lying; the wounds were genuine, and the pallor of her face mirrored the terror I had witnessed in the cave. My conscience reproached me instantly for allowing her to fight alone, and my expression betrayed the compassion rising within me.

"Child of folly!" she snapped, the smile dissolving into something sharper. "--Are you weeping? Do not cry! Wait here. I am entering the Black Hall momentarily. Fetch me something for these scratches."

I moved to follow, keeping pace just behind her. Fear prickled at the base of my spine; I dreaded the moment she disappeared from my line of sight. The instant she crossed the threshold, the air filled with a low, vibrating hum, akin to thousands of whispers merging into a single drone. Simultaneously, the vast assembly began to shift, illuminated intermittently by rays of light that traveled erratically from spot to spot. Groups of figures would flare into visibility, glowing with unnatural intensity, only to sink back into the gloom as another section of the crowd ignited.

Some scenes played out before me that I recognized intimately—I had lived them, or watched them unfold, alongside the Princess. Now, witnessing them simultaneously, the illusion shattered.

In the Forest-Hall, the skull-headed dancers trod the grass. There, at the doorway, stood the Princess, observing! In the Evil Wood, the battle raged; there, she urged the combatants onward! Yet physically, she remained motionless beside me, her head bowed onto her breast, a statue carved from grief.

The chaotic murmur continued, a swirling confusion of colors and shapes, while the shifting light persisted. Finally, the beam settled on a hollow in the heath. There, the Princess paced anxiously, attempting in vain to wrap vapor around her form.

Then, the distortion occurred. The Old Librarian stepped forward, regarding her silently. She collapsed. Her limbs abandoned her, fleeing outward as her physical form dissolved into mist. A wild shriek tore through the echoing chamber. With the vanishing of her eidolon—the phantom projection—the Princess herself, who had stood rigid before me, crumpled heavily to the ground, lying still.

I turned immediately and exited. Never again would I attempt to restore her.

Standing trembling beside the cage, I realized with chilling clarity that within the black ellipsoid, I had occupied the Princess's own mind. I saw the tail of the leopardess twitch once, a final spasm of malice.

While struggling to regain my composure, the Princess's voice cut through the silence beside me. "Come now," she commanded, her tone regaining its edge. "I require your assistance."

She led me into the court. I followed in a state of dazed compliance. The moon hung near the zenith, its silver radiance appearing far brighter than the golden light of the absent sun. She guided me through the trees to the tallest specimen, the central sentinel. Its branches drew inward at the apex, forming a cluster that resembled a fir-cone viewed from below.

The Princess stood directly beneath it, gazing upward. "Upon the summit of that tree grows a tiny blossom capable of healing my scratches instantly," she murmured, addressing the canopy rather than me. "I could transform into a dove and retrieve it, but I perceive a small serpent coiled in the leaves. Its bite would prove far deadlier to a bird than a tiger's bite would be to me... How I detest that Cat-Woman!"

She spun to face me, her expression softening into one of her sweetest smiles. "Can you climb?"

The smile evaporated as quickly as it appeared, replaced by a mask of profound suffering. I should have allowed her pain to consume her, yet the gesture of her hand pressing against her wounded neck struck a chord in my heart. I assessed the tree. Projections jutted from the trunk like the remnants of fallen palm fronds, offering potential footholds.

"I can ascend that tree," I stated.

"Not with bare feet!" she retorted.

In my haste to pursue the leopardess, I had discarded my sandals in my quarters.

"It matters not," I replied. "I have long since gone barefoot."

My gaze drifted upward, following the rough bark until my vision blurred at the highest branches. Moonlight shimmered like silvery foam across the rugged bole, and a faint breeze rustled the treetop with a sound reminiscent of water trickling softly into a pool. I stepped forward to begin my ascent.

The Princess halted me. "I cannot permit you to attempt this with exposed feet!" she insisted. "A fall from such height would be fatal."

"So would a bite from the snake!" I countered, admitting privately that I doubted the existence of any serpent.

"It would not harm *you*!" she shot back. "--Wait."

She tore from her garment the two wide borders meeting at her waist. Kneeling on one knee, she instructed me to place my left foot, then my right, onto the fabric strips. She bound them tightly around my ankles with the thick embroidery.

"You have left the ends dangling, Princess!" I observed.

"I possess no means to trim them," she admitted. "However, they are too short to snag on the branches."

Turning to the tree, I began to climb.

In Bulika, the cold after sunset was less severe than in other regions—particularly near the sexton's cottage—but as I ascended, the temperature plummeted. After climbing a mere few meters, I felt a biting chill that intensified with every step, reaching its peak among the upper branches. Shivers racked my frame, and I felt as though I had lost sensation in my extremities entirely.

There was virtually no wind, and the branches remained perfectly still. Yet, as I neared the summit, a peculiar instability seized me. Every limb I touched or stepped upon seemed poised to snap under my weight.

When my head finally cleared the canopy, bathed in the open moonlight, searching for the blossom, I was suddenly drenched from crown to toe. The next instant, as if cast into a violent tempest, I was thrashed about wildly, feeling myself plunge downward. Tossed up and down, flung side to side, rolling over and over, checked and reversed, I sank deeper and deeper. Gasping, gurgling, choking on the thin air, I crashed finally onto a solid surface.

"I told you so!" a voice rasped in my ear.

I wiped the condensation from my optics, focusing on the silhouette perched atop the rim of the massive hydro-accumulator basin. Dawn's pale luminescence fractured against his iridescent feathers, casting a sharp, critical glare downward. I lay submerged in the coolant fluid, propped on my elbows, lifting my face just enough to register the threat.

I was positioned within the primary containment reservoir of the fountain system my progenitor had engineered centuries ago—a relic of hydraulic engineering situated in the center of the manicured lawn. Above me, the main injector shaft, a thick column of polished alloy, pierced the atmosphere, erupting a torrent of pressurized mist a hundred meters high before dissipating into a chaotic spray of droplets.

Stung by the avian entity's assessment—"You told me nothing!"—I retorted, my voice cracking slightly in the humid air. "I instructed you to ignore anyone you deemed untrustworthy!"

"Tut! How was a mortal mind expected to retain that directive?"

"You will not forget the cascading consequences of such negligence," Mr. Raven replied. The creature shifted, extending a clawed talon across the basin's edge. I grasped it, and instantly, the laws of physics bent to accommodate the transfer; I found myself standing on the grass, dripping wet, the sensation of gravity returning with a jolt.

"Immediate decontamination is required," he commanded, his tone devoid of malice but heavy with expectation. "Moisture accumulation is negligible in your origin sector, though here, in this controlled environment, it presents significant logistical complications."

He resumed his natural form, a raven of imposing stature, striding with deliberate precision toward the residence. Its entrance stood agape.

"I require minimal adjustment," I chuckled, stripping off my outer tunic to reveal the sleek, scaled integument beneath. "It has been an eternity since I shed a layer of armor."

"It is a long interval since you molted," the bird observed, pausing at the threshold.

Inside, the facility remained in a state of suspended animation. I proceeded to my quarters, donning a thermal regulation gown, and descended to the central archive. Upon entry, the custodian emerged from a storage alcove. I collapsed onto a reinforced sofa. Mr. Raven pulled a chair close, settling opposite me. Silence stretched between us, dense and heavy.

I broke the quiet first. "Explain the parameters of this situation."

"A pertinent inquiry," he responded. "Human cognition rarely grasps intrinsic meaning; it only interprets utility based on application. Whether understanding occurs depends entirely on how one leverages available data."

"I have applied no utility thus far."

"Scant progress, yes. Yet awareness of stagnation constitutes a baseline metric. Most species spend eons realizing they possess zero functional output. At minimum, you harbor the intent to contribute."

"My objective was to assist the Children—the Precious Little Ones."

"I acknowledge your motivation, yet your vector was flawed."

"I lacked the correct methodology."

"That is accurate, though culpability lies in your failure to seek it."

"I accept your premise, provided I comprehend the underlying mechanics."

"Acceptance of our summons would have aligned your trajectory. When an agent refuses action within their current locus, they must traverse vast distances to locate their function."

"I have traversed extensively and achieved null results. I failed to identify my function. I abandoned the Children to discover how to serve them, only to realize the magnitude of the peril threatening them."

"When co-located with them, you possessed the capacity to intervene. By departing to search for your role, you abdicated responsibility. Wisdom dictates knowing when to withdraw; folly compels immediate retreat."

"Do you imply I could have aided the Little Ones by remaining?"

"Did you attempt instruction by abandoning them?"

"No. However, what methodology existed? Furthermore, they surpassed my capabilities."

"That is factual. Yet you lacked the metric to gauge them. Had they possessed your knowledge—or potential insights—they would have eclipsed you exponentially. Instead, they exhibited stasis, or growth so imperceptible it suggested regression. They feared expansion. You have never witnessed a species capable of maintaining juvenile status indefinitely."

"But I possessed no agency to force maturation."

"You could have eliminated impediments to development."

"What obstacles? I identified none. I suspected perhaps a hydration deficit."

"Naturally. They lack the medium for vocalization."

"Undoubtedly. The hallmark of incompetent altruism. Consider, Mr. Vane: were it not for the cacophony of distress, your civilization would never have warranted salvation. You hypothesized a water requirement; why did you not excavate aquifers?"

"That consideration never arose."

"I recall a singular instance. However, I feared the Giants for their sake. That terror drove me to endure such abuse from the beasts myself!"

"In essence, you conditioned the noble offspring to dread the Stupid Bags. While they nourished, soothed, and revered you, you submitted to servitude under brutish overlords. You could have orchestrated the Giants into manual labor roles for the Little Ones."

"I concede the validity of your assertion, Mr. Raven. Yet I feared advanced knowledge might corrupt them, diminishing their innocence and aesthetic purity."

"They offered no justification for such apprehension."

"Is not limited knowledge inherently hazardous?"

"One of the foundational fallacies of your epoch. Does superior cognition render humanity greater, or does it introduce disproportionate risk? The notion that knowledge possesses inherent magnitude suggests any degree of enlightenment exceeds the danger of total ignorance. Omniscience is not greatness."

"At least my submission stemmed from affection, not timidity."

"Accepted. Nevertheless, you should have served the Little Ones, not the Giants. Providing them with water would have swiftly revealed the Giants' subordinate status. Simultaneously, you could have directed the Giants to prune two-thirds of their coarse flora to accommodate the delicate saplings. You squandered your opportunity with the Lovers, Mr. Vane. You engaged in speculation rather than execution."

Silence and shame settled heavily upon me. His words struck with the weight of absolute truth: I had failed the Little Ones. Mr. Raven continued, his voice devoid of pity. "You wronged them doubly. You insulted the stupid creatures themselves. For beings of such limited cognition, slavery would have been a step toward progress. To them, a few harsh lessons administered with a stick from their own trees would have been invaluable wisdom."

"What difference does that make?" he countered, his gaze piercing. "The man who grounds his actions on another's cowardice is, in essence, a coward himself. I fear worse consequences await us. By this hour, the Little Ones might have developed the capacity to defend themselves against the Princess, or even the Giants—they were always formidable enough for that task. As it stands, they laughed at them. But now, through your treacherous relations with her..."

"I hate her!" I cried out, the emotion tearing from my throat.

"Did you let her know you hated her?" he asked softly.

Again, I fell silent. "Not even to her have you been faithful!" he accused, his tone sharp. "But hush! We were followed from the fountain. I fear it."

"No living creature did I see," I insisted, scanning the gloom. "Except a disreputable-looking cat that bolted into the shrubbery."

"It was a magnificent Persian," Mr. Raven corrected, his voice dropping to a grave register. "Though so wet and draggled, she looked worse than disreputable."

"What do you mean, Mr. Raven?" I demanded, a fresh wave of horror seizing my throat.

"There was a beautiful blue Persian about the house," he explained, "but she fled at the mere sound of water. Could she have been after the goldfish?"

"We shall see," the librarian replied, rising with deliberate calm. "I possess knowledge of various feline breeds, and there exists within this room a mechanism that will unmask this creature, provided I am not mistaken in her nature."

He moved to the door of the small closet, retrieved the mutilated volume, and seated himself beside me. I stared at the book in his hands; miraculously, it was whole, entire, and sound. "Where was the other half of it?" I gasped.

"Sticking through into my library," he answered simply.

I held my peace. A single further question would have been a plunge into a bottomless sea, and there might be no time remaining.

"Listen," he commanded. "I am going to read a stanza or two. There is one present who, I imagine, will find the recitation most unpleasant."

He opened the vellum cover, turning a leaf or two. The parchment was discolored with age, and one leaf bore a dark stain covering two-thirds of its surface. He slowly turned this page as well, appearing to search for a specific passage within what seemed a continuous poem. Somewhere near the middle, he began to read. However, what follows represents not the exact words spoken, but the profound impression they made upon my consciousness.

The poem seemed composed in a language I had never encountered before, yet I understood it perfectly, though I could not transcribe the words or define their meanings save in poor approximation. These fragments are the shapes those verses assumed as they passed again through my mind:

"But if I found a man that could believe

He turned a leaf and read again:

I understood nor what I was, nor where;
I knew what I had been: still on my brow

A life that flouted life with mop and mow!'

And sometimes wore a splendour on my head

Once more, the ugly cry of feline pain pierced the air. Again I looked, but saw neither shape nor motion. Mr. Raven seemed to listen for a moment, but again turned several pages and resumed:

"Hideously wet, my hair of golden hue
Fouled my fair hands: to have it swiftly shorn
I had given my rubies, all for me dug new—
No eyes had seen, and such no waist had worn!
For a draught of water from a drinking horn,
For one blue breath, I had given my sapphires blue!
'Nay, I had given my opals for a smock,
A peasant-maiden's garment, coarse and clean:
My shroud was rotting! Once I heard a cock
Lustily crow upon the hillock green
Over my coffin. Dulled by space between,
Came back an answer like a ghostly mock.'"

Once more arose the bestial wail. "I thought some foul thing was in the room!" the librarian exclaimed, casting a glance around him; but instantly he turned a leaf or two and again read:

"For I had bathed in milk and honey-dew,
In rain from roses shook, that ne'er touched earth,
And ointed me with nard of amber hue;
Never had spot me spotted from my birth,
Or mole, or scar of hurt, or fret of dearth;
Never one hair superfluous on me grew.
'Fleeing cold whiteness, I would sit alone—
Not in the sun—I feared his bronzing light,
But in his radiance back around me thrown
By fulgent mirrors tempering his might;
Thus bathing in a moon-bath not too bright,
My skin I tinted slow to ivory tone.'
'But now, all round was dark, dark all within!
My eyes not even gave out a phantom-flash;
My fingers sank in pulp through pulpy skin;
My body lay death-weltered in a mash
Of slimy horrors—'"

With a fearsome yell, her clammy fur staring in clumps, her tail thick as a cable, her eyes flashing green as a chrysoprase, her distended claws entangling themselves so that she floundered across the carpet, a huge white cat rushed from somewhere and made for the chimney. Quick as thought, the librarian threw the manuscript between her and the hearth. She crouched instantly, her eyes fixed on the book. Yet his voice went on as if he were still reading, and his eyes seemed also fixed on the text:

"'Ah, the two worlds! so strangely are they one,
And yet so measurelessly wide apart!
Oh, had I lived the bodiless alone
And from defiling sense held safe my heart,
Then had I scaped the canker and the smart,
Scaped life-in-death, scaped misery's endless moan!'"

"We must be vigilant," Adam stated, his voice cutting through the tension like a scalpel. "Or she will outwit us once more. She would deceive even the Elect!"

"How do we achieve vigilance?" I asked, seeking a foothold in the chaos.

"Every conceivable way," he replied, his gaze fixed on the sealed closet. "She fears her offspring, and thus she hates them. She stands within this house intent on their destruction. To her, childbirth signifies the death of the parent; every new generation is an existential threat to the previous one. Her daughter represents an open conduit through which her immortality—a power she believes is intrinsic—flows rapidly away. To stem this loss, she has pursued the girl with relentless animosity since infancy. Yet, her schemes have inadvertently resulted in a thriving colony of children, led by that daughter, whom she views as both heart and shield. My Eve yearned for the child, ready to embrace her as a mother, but we were ill-equipped to guide her. Instead, she was cast into the wilderness, her fate unknown to us for eons. Divinely nurtured and accompanied by angelic companions, she remained untouched by sorrow until she discovered an infant in the woods, awakening a maternal instinct that has never since been satisfied. Her family is her sole obsession; she mothers them with unparalleled devotion. While her authority over them is absolute, she remains unaware of its extent, manifesting only through constant watchfulness and service. She has forgotten her existence prior to them, convinced she emerged from the forest as their primal ancestor."

"You have spared the lives of her enemies and theirs," Adam continued, turning to me. "Therefore, your life now belongs to them. The Princess was advancing to annihilate them, but as she crossed the stream, vengeance intercepted her. Had you not intervened, she would have perished. Consequently, she would have ravaged the Little Ones had she dared to cross the threshold again. However, a path existed between the three-dimensional world and their realm; previously, her slaying of her former body barred her entry unless she maintained direct contact with someone belonging to that dimension. You provided the opening. Never before had she possessed such an opportunity. Her hand rested lightly upon your muffled feet with every step of your ascent. Within that small chamber, she watches, poised to escape the moment she deems it safe."

"She cannot comprehend the door!" I insisted, though doubt gnawed at my resolve. "She certainly doesn't know how to open it!"

"Hush!" Adam whispered, raising a hand to silence me. "She perceives through all barriers. You must depart immediately and proceed to my wife's cottage. I shall remain to guard her."

"Let me reach the Little Ones!" I cried out.

"Beware, Mr. Vane. Go to my wife and heed her instructions." His counsel offered no comfort; why hasten toward inevitable delay? If the goal was protection, why flee? Even now, I trusted him only enough to demand answers rather than obedience.

"Mr. Raven," I pressed, "why confine her specifically there? The night I fled your home, I entered directly into that closet!"

"The closet is equidistant from our cottage as any other location."

"But," I countered, struggling to grasp the illogical, "when you retrieve a complete book from that door, I perceive only a fragment. Conversely, you've just revealed that the missing portion protrudes into your library. Upon returning the volume to the shelf, will it not again extend into the closet? Do the two locations containing simultaneous segments of the same object not necessarily lie adjacent? Or can one part of a book occupy space while the other exists nowhere?"

"I regret that I cannot elucidate this for you," Adam answered, his expression weary. "There is simply no cognitive framework within you to comprehend it. Not only is the phenomenon inexplicable to you, but its very nature is beyond your apprehension. Indeed, I only partially grasp it myself. Yet, you constantly experience realities you neither understand nor can understand. You believe you do, but your familiarity breeds complacency, not comprehension. You accept them not through insight, but necessity; they exist, and their connection to you is unavoidable. Truthfully, no man truly understands anything. Recognizing one's ignorance is the first stumbling block—not toward knowledge, but toward the potential for it. Since you are unused to such phenomena, you mistake habit for understanding. Neither I nor anyone else can force this understanding upon you, but I may assist you in believing."

Adam moved to the closet door and emitted a low whistle. Moments later, a soft whirring filled the air. Looking upward, I saw a white dove perch briefly atop the shelves above the portrait before descending to rest its head against Adam's cheek. Their heads moved in silent conversation; I heard nothing. Before I could shift my gaze, the bird vanished, and Adam returned to his seat.

"Why did you whistle?" I asked. "Sound here is not sound there!"

"You are correct," he replied. "I whistled to signal her presence. It was not the acoustic wave, but the meaning behind it that reached her."

"There is no time to waste. You must go!"

"I will depart immediately!" I responded, reaching for the door.

"You will spend tonight at my inn," he commanded, his tone carrying a mild but firm authority.

"My heart lies with the children," I replied. "But if you insist—"

"I do insist. Without your departure, nothing changes. I will accompany you as far as the mirror and ensure your safe passage."

He rose. Suddenly, a violent impact shook the closet. The leopardess had thrown herself against the heavy door. Glancing at my companion, I urged him forward. Before we reached the library entrance, a blood-curdling scream erupted, mixed with the shriek of claws tearing into the hardwood floor. I hesitated, turning halfway.

"To think of her lying there alone," I murmured, "with that grievous wound!"

"Nothing will ever heal that wound," Adam sighed. "It must consume her heart. Annihilation is not death for evil. Evil persists until goodness replaces it. Only then is evil truly slain."

I fell silent as an incomprehensible sound overtook us.

"If she breaks free!" I shouted.

"Hurry!" Adam rejoined. "I will descend the moment you are gone; I have already disturbed the mirrors."

We sprinted, bursting into the wooden chamber gasping for air. Adam seized the chains, adjusting the hood, then aligned the mirrors in their precise configuration. Standing before the central pane, he joined me. Through the glass, the mountain range began to emerge from the mist. Between us, wedging our paths apart, surged the massive form of the spotted leopardess, screaming like a demon. She leaped through the mirror as effortlessly as through an open window, instantly transforming into a low, rapid gallop across the landscape.

I exchanged a look of dread with Adam and sprang through to pursue her. He followed at a leisurely pace.

"You need not run," he called out. "You cannot catch her. This is our path."

Speaking, he turned in the opposite direction.

"She possesses more arcane power at her fingertips than I care to acknowledge," he added softly. "We must do what we can!"

"Doubtless we must," I agreed, running ahead until the beast dwindled into the distance. Sickened by the sight, I halted and turned back to him.

"Naturally we must," he answered. "However, my wife has alerted Mara, and she will fulfill her role. You must rest first; you promised me that much!"

"I have no intention of breaking my word. But surely rest is not the priority! Action must precede repose!"

"A man achieves nothing unless he is fit to act. See! Did I not tell you Mara would do her part?"

He indicated a point in the distance. I looked and spotted a white speck moving at an acute angle relative to the leopardess's trajectory.

"There she is!" Adam cried. "Though the spotted leopardess is formidable, the white entity is superior!"

"I have witnessed their battles; the outcome did not seem decisive."

"How can such eyes judge victory when they have never known sleep? The Princess did not admit defeat—that she never does—but she fled! When she acknowledges the loss of her last hope, realizing the futility of resistance, her day begins to dawn. Come! He who cannot act must hasten to sleep!"

I stood and watched the last gleam of the white leopardess melt into the void, then turned to follow my guide—reluctantly. What purpose served sleep when the hour demanded vigilance? Reason was constant across the cosmos; to succumb to unconsciousness while the living called was illogical. Yet, no one would rouse me, and certainty of awakening was a fantasy. In that house, morning bled indistinguishably into noon, and noon into eternal night; the inhabitants were statues of suspended animation. I murmured my protest, but compliance was the only variable remaining.

The librarian advanced in silence, a lean, long-coated silhouette against the encroaching gloom. I matched his cadence, moving through a landscape where time and space dissolved into a uniform gray. Sunset descended, casting the air thick with the chilling radiance of the Chamber of Death. My resolve eroded with every step. The figure ahead vanished into the heather, his swishing stride swallowed by the dark. Soon, only the slow, rhythmic flapping of a raven's wings pierced the silence, punctuated occasionally by the erratic rise of a firefly or a luminous butterfly drifting into the rayless atmosphere.

Then, the moon ascended, a slow, deliberate crossing of the distant horizon.

"You are weary, Mr. Vane?" the raven asked, landing heavily on a stone. "You must acquaint yourself with the beast that will bear you tomorrow!"

A strange whistle tore from his long black beak. Simultaneously, a blemish marred the lunar surface. Instantly, the sound of swift, soft-galloping hooves drummed against the ether. Within minutes, the terrible steed erupted from the very disc of the moon. His mane streamed behind him like the crest of a wind-defying wave, torn seaward in hoary spray, while the lash of his tail blinded the lunar gaze. Nineteen hands high, massive in bone, taut in skin, dense in muscle—he was a mount worthy of Holy Death himself to ride into slaughter. Even the moon regarded him with awe; in her spectral light, he appeared a skeletal framework, loosely lashed together by gravity. Yet, terrifyingly large, he moved with the deceptive lightness of a winged insect.

As he closed the distance, his velocity moderated, his flowing mane and tail settling into a chaotic drift. I was no mere admirer of equine form; I possessed every horse I encountered. My currency had always been spent solely on steeds, never withdrawn. This creature, monstrous in appearance, ignited a primal craving within me. It was pure greed, rank covetousness—an evil impulse transcending worlds. I did not intend theft, but possession regardless of propriety; I would have purchased him if the market allowed.

I placed my hands upon him, stroking the protuberant bones that humped a hide smooth, thin, and glossy as satin. So polished was the coat that the moon's reflection warped within it. I caressed his sharp, pointed ears, whispering endearments, and breathed the warm air of humanity into his red nostrils. In response, he exhaled the raw, musk-laden breath of a horse's life into my lungs. We locked gazes. What eyes he possessed! Blue-filmy, like the sockets of the dead, yet behind each orb burned a glowing coal. The raven, wings half-extended, observed our bonding with evident satisfaction.

"That is well," the bird croaked. "Forge a friendship; he will carry you further tomorrow. Now, haste toward the cottage!"

My desire to mount grew feverish. "May I not ascend at once, Mr. Raven?" I cried.

"By all means!" he answered. "Ride him home."

The horse bowed his head over my shoulder with affectionate submission. I twisted my fingers into his mane, scrambling onto his back, aided by the very ridges of his spine. "He would outrun any leopard in creation!" I shouted.

"Not that path at night," the raven corrected sharply. "The terrain is treacherous. Loss incurred now becomes gain later; waiting is harder than running, and its reward is fuller. Proceed, my son—straight to the cottage. Your mother's heart will rejoice to see her son astride such a beast!"

I remained motionless. The horse stood rigid as a block of marble. "Why do you hesitate?" the raven demanded.

"I yearn so desperately to pursue the spotted leopardess," I admitted, "that I cannot restrain myself."

"You have pledged your word!"

"My debt to the Little Ones outweighs my bond to you."

"Yield to this temptation, and you shall bring ruin upon them—and upon yourself."

"What consequence matters to me? I love them; love commits no evil. I shall proceed."

The truth was, I had forgotten the children, intoxicated by the animal. Shadows flickered in the darkness, and I recognized Adam standing in his own form beside me. His voice carried a suppressed indignation, barely contained.

"Mr. Vane," Adam said, "do you not comprehend why you have accomplished nothing of value thus far?"

"Because I have been a fool."

"In what respect?"

"In every aspect."

"What counts as your most indiscreet act?"

"Awakening the Princess. I should have permitted her to meet her destined end."

"Nonsense! You could not have known she was malevolent. Furthermore, you never resurrected anyone! How could you, given that you are already dead?"

"I dead?" I exclaimed.

"Yes," he replied calmly. "And you shall remain dead so long as you refuse to accept it."

"Back to the riddles!" I retorted scornfully.

"Be persuaded and return with me," Adam continued gently. "The single, nearly sole folly you committed was fleeing from our dead."

I pressed the horse's ribs, and he surged forward like a sudden gust of wind. I pat the side of his neck as he executed a sharp, driven curve—"close to the ground, like a cat wheeling after a mouse"—leaning sideways until his mane brushed the tops of the heather. Through the obscurity, I heard the raven's wings beating five rapid times before he settled atop the horse's head.

The horse halted instantly, digging his hooves into the earth.

"Mr. Vane," the raven croaked, "consider your actions. Evil has struck you twice—once from fear, once from negligence. Breach of promise is far worse; it is a crime."

"The Little Ones face imminent peril, and I caused it!" I cried. "Yet I cannot break my vow to you. I shall return and endure whatever nights, days, or years you require."

"I warn you once more: going tonight brings harm to them," the raven insisted.

A false sense of power, vibrating from the sheer strength of the steed, had rendered me deaf to reason. "Will you deny me my last opportunity for reparation?" I demanded. "This time, there shall be no evasion! It is my duty, and I shall go—even if I perish!"

"Go then, foolish boy!" the raven returned, anger coloring his croak. "Take the horse and ride to failure! May it lead to humility!"

With a beat of his wings, he took flight. I pressed the lean ribs beneath me. "After the spotted leopardess!" I whispered in his ear.

He turned his head, sniffing the air, then paused in a slow, indecisive walk. Suddenly, he quickened to a trot, breaking into a gallop whose speed became tremendous. He navigated the darkness with uncanny vision, stumbling not once, faltering not once, hesitating not once. I sat balanced on the ridge of a wave, feeling the distinct play of every muscle beneath me. His joints were elastic; his movements glided seamlessly into one another, creating zero jar. His accelerating swiftness transformed him into something flying rather than running. The wind roared past us like a tornado.
